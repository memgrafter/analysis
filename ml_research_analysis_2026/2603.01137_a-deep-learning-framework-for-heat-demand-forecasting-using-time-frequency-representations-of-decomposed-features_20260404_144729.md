# A Deep Learning Framework for Heat Demand Forecasting using Time-Frequency Representations of Decomposed Features

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.01137  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01137  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Reduces Mean Absolute Error by 36%–43% relative to best baselines, reaching up to 95% accuracy.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a deep learning framework for multi-hour ahead heat demand forecasting in District Heating Systems (DHS). The method transforms historical consumption, weather, and temporal features into time-frequency scalograms via Continuous Wavelet Transform (CWT) after additive decomposition into trend, seasonal, and residual components. A convolutional neural network then processes these scalograms to predict the next 24 hours. Evaluated on five real-world datasets from Danish and German cities, the approach achieves substantial MAE reductions (36–43%) compared to state-of-the-art baselines, including Transformers and foundation models, and demonstrates robust tracking of volatile demand peaks.

## Method Summary
The pipeline begins with additive decomposition (e.g., STL) of each input series—consumption lags (24 h, 168 h), weather variables, and calendrical features—into trend, seasonal, and residual components. Each 24‑point component vector is converted into a 24×24 scalogram via CWT, yielding a time‑frequency representation. All scalograms are stacked depth‑wise to form a 3‑D input tensor. This tensor passes through a CNN with three convolutional layers (32/64/128 filters, 3×3 kernels, ReLU, same padding, no pooling) to learn hierarchical features. The output is flattened and fed into two dense layers (1024 units each, Leaky ReLU) with dropout (p=0.1) to produce a 24‑hour forecast. Training uses Adam (lr=0.001), MSE loss, ReduceLROnPlateau, and early stopping.

## Key Results
- **MAE reduction:** 36%–43% lower than best baselines (SARIMAX, Transformers, Mixer models, foundation models).
- **High accuracy:** Up to 95% accuracy on test sets across five districts.
- **Robust peak tracking:** Reliably captures sharp morning peaks and volatile transitions, critical for DHS operation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Time-Frequency Localization via CWT
Transforming decomposed series into scalograms via CWT enables the CNN to learn hierarchical temporal‑periodic patterns, especially high‑frequency volatility, that are inaccessible in the raw time domain. CWT convolves the signal with scaled wavelets, producing a 2‑D representation where rows are frequency scales and columns are time steps. This explicitly localizes when specific frequencies occur, allowing 2‑D kernels to detect localized time‑frequency motifs and cross‑feature interactions. Evidence includes ablation studies showing decomposed features with CWT yield significantly lower errors (e.g., DMA A MAE 101.7 vs 122.7 for raw), and stable performance across wavelet families. A break condition would be drastic wavelet preference variation per feature/dataset, but the paper found consistent results.

### Mechanism 2: Disentanglement via Trend‑Seasonal‑Residual Decomposition
Additive decomposition separates long‑term trends (e.g., efficiency improvements), fixed seasonal cycles (daily/weekly), and irregular residuals (weather shocks). Each component is transformed via CWT separately and concatenated, reducing noise and feature entanglement. This structure aligns with correlation analysis (Figure 5) showing strong trend‑demand vs. trend‑temperature coupling but weaker seasonal links, allowing the model to assign different convolutional filters to different temporal scales. Decomposed models uniformly outperform raw‑feature models (Table 8), and the performance spread narrows, indicating robustness. Potential failure would be phase misalignment from poor decomposition, but outlier handling and stable gains mitigate this.

### Mechanism 3: High‑Resolution CNN without Pooling
The CNN stack excludes pooling layers to preserve exact temporal phase information in the scalograms. Pooling downsamples spatial resolution, blurring the timing of events like morning peaks. With stride‑1 convolutions and same padding, the 24×24 spatial fidelity is retained, letting the subsequent MLP learn precise mappings from time‑frequency patterns to the 24‑hour forecast. This design is validated by ablation results (pooling degrades performance) and qualitative plots showing sharp peak capture versus smoothed or lagged baselines. Overfitting risk from high‑dimensional flattening is countered with dropout (0.1) and early stopping, as training/validation loss stability indicates.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Time‑Series Decomposition (STL/Additive)**  
  *Why needed:* The entire pipeline relies on splitting raw series into components for separate CWT processing; prevents mixing of temporal scales.  
  *Quick check:* If seasonal temperature correlates near‑zero with seasonal demand (Figure 5), why exclude weather seasonal? Because the paper includes only trend and residual for weather, aligning with weak seasonal coupling.
- **Concept: Continuous Wavelet Transform (CWT)**  
  *Why needed:* Core transformation creating scalograms; provides continuous scale localization without downsampling, preserving phase.  
  *Quick check:* Why CWT over Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT)? CWT offers high‑fidelity time‑frequency representations without dyadic downsampling/aliasing.
- **Concept: CNN Processing of 2D Scalograms**  
  *Why needed:* Model interprets scalograms as multi‑channel images; 2‑D kernels capture local time‑frequency patterns and cross‑feature interactions.  
  *Quick check:* What does a 3×3 kernel see on a scalogram? A local patch in time (columns) and scale (rows), representing a specific frequency burst at a specific time.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input Preprocessing → CWT Module → CNN Feature Extractor → Flatten & MLP  
- **Critical path:** Decomposed feature vectors → CWT scalograms (stacked depth‑wise) → CNN depth‑wise processing (learns cross‑feature time‑frequency motifs) → flatten → MLP (integrates global patterns) → 24‑hour forecast. Removing any convolution block harms performance; spatial hierarchy from CNN is vital.  
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *No pooling vs. overfitting:* Chosen to preserve phase; mitigated with dropout (0.1) and early stopping.  
  - *Fixed wavelet vs. feature‑specific:* Single wavelet per dataset for simplicity; corpus shows minor impact across wavelets.  
  - *Decomposed vs. raw:* Decomposition adds complexity but yields consistent gains and narrows performance spread, enhancing operational robustness.  
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - *Smoothed forecasts:* Underfitted volatility; peaks rounded (seen in Preformer/Autoformer).  
  - *Phase lag:* Forecast peaks shifted 1–2 hours (seen in SARIMAX/XGBoost); due to resolution loss or lack of phase modeling.  
  - *Holiday outlier spikes:* Large errors on holidays; model reverts to typical weekly pattern.  
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Experiment I:** Compare [c24] vs. [c24, c168]. c168 stabilizes forecasts on pattern‑disruption days (weekends/holidays). **Action:** Always include c168.  
  2. **Experiment II:** Add weather variables to [c24, c168]. t_amb strongest; combinations dataset‑specific. **Action:** Start with t_amb; test others if marginal gain justifies complexity.  
  3. **Experiment III:** Compare raw vs. decomposed feature sets. Decomposed always wins. **Action:** Decompose demand into trend, seasonal, residual; decompose weather into trend, residual; exclude weather seasonal.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How can models accurately predict rare, non‑recurring events (holidays, anomalies)? Sparse representation causes smoothing; memory‑augmented or few‑shot learning suggested.
- Would adaptive wavelet selection per feature improve accuracy over fixed wavelet? Current hyperparameter search uses one wavelet per dataset.
- Can integrating granular external features (industrial schedules, school calendars, event logs) bridge statistical patterns and real‑world causality?
- What is the full potential of foundation models? Basic fine‑tuning lags behind purpose‑built models; systematic benchmarks with hybrid strategies needed.
- How does long‑term concept drift affect accuracy over decades? Limited to ≤7 years; multi‑decadal data and continual learning required.

## Limitations
- Decomposition algorithm and seasonal periods unspecified; risk of incorrect component separation on new data.
- CWT implementation details (scale selection, normalization) not fully documented; affects scalogram fidelity.
- Holiday embedding strategy district‑specific; no general rule for new regions.
- Geographic scope limited to European datasets; generalization to other climates unvalidated.
- Computational cost of CWT and CNN flattening not analyzed.

## Confidence
- **Performance gains (36–43% MAE reduction):** High within evaluated districts, Medium for unseen regions.
- **CWT time‑frequency representation:** Medium (supported by ablations but sensitive to unstated parameters).
- **Decomposition disentanglement:** Medium (strong correlation evidence but algorithmic gaps in multi‑seasonal handling).
- **No‑pooling CNN for phase preservation:** High (validated via ablations and qualitative plots).
- **Generalization beyond paper’s context:** Low (no tests on non‑European districts or extreme climates).
