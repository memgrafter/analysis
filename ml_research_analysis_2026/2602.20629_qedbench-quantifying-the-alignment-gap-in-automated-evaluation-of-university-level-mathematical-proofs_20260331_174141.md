# QEDBENCH: Quantifying the Alignment Gap in Automated Evaluation of University-Level Mathematical Proofs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.20629  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20629  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: Frontier LLM judges systematically inflate scores compared to human experts; Gemini 3.0 Pro achieves best alignment (0.91 overall), while Llama 4 Maverick shows highest inflation (+0.36) and pass rate (90.2% vs 67.7% human).

## Executive Summary
QEDBENCH introduces a rigorous benchmark for auditing LLM-based evaluators of university-level mathematical proofs. The benchmark comprises 272 problems across 10 disciplines, with solutions generated by five frontier models and annotated by domain experts for over 1,000 hours. Using a crossed 7-judge × 5-solver matrix and dual rubrics (Expert vs. Course‑Specific), the study measures alignment gaps between AI judges and human consensus. Key findings reveal universal grade inflation among frontier evaluators, with mean score deltas ranging from +0.18 (Claude Opus 4.5) to +0.36 (Llama 4 Maverick). Gemini 3.0 Pro attains state‑of‑the‑art alignment (0.91 average human score), while GPT‑5.2 Pro and Claude Sonnet 4.5 suffer sharp drops in discrete mathematics. The authors also identify a “Sycophancy Trap” where judges reward superficial coherence and penalize correct logical refusals, and demonstrate rubric insensitivity—prompt constraints have negligible effect on alignment. Contamination tests confirm no significant performance boost on publicly available solutions. The work highlights severe limitations in using LLMs as autonomous judges for mathematical proofs and calls for architectural innovations beyond prompt engineering.

## Method Summary
The study employs a factorial design in which seven judge models evaluate all solutions produced by five solver models across 272 problems, under two distinct rubrics. Human experts provided granular scores (0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.9, 1.0) and justifications, forming a gold‑standard ground truth. LLM judges were queried via a two‑step JSON pipeline: first outputting a numeric score, then generating a justification given the score. Alignment was quantified by the mean difference between AI and human scores (Δ), pass rates at a ≥0.9 threshold, and error decomposition into Leniency (false positive) and Harshness (false negative) rates. To assess rubric sensitivity, the same judges were run under both the Expert (allowing advanced machinery) and Course‑Specific (pedagogical constraints) rubrics. The dataset was also split into online (solutions potentially memorized) and offline subsets to test for data contamination using Welch’s t‑tests. This design isolates judge bias from solver skill and allows systematic measurement of alignment gaps across domains and model families.

## Key Results
- Universal grade inflation: All seven frontier judges score above human strictness; mean deltas range +0.18–+0.36, with Llama 4 Maverick highest (+0.36) and pass rate 90.2% vs human 67.7%.
- Mixed domain performance: Gemini 3.0 Pro leads overall (0.91 average) and in Graph Theory (0.89), but GPT‑5.2 Pro and Claude Sonnet 4.5 drop to 0.72 and 0.63 in Discrete Math, revealing a discrete‑continuous divide.
- No contamination effect: Welch’s t‑tests show no statistically significant performance difference between online and offline problems (p > 0.05 for all solvers).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Crossed Evaluator-Solver Matrix with Dual Rubrics
The fully crossed 7×5 design ensures that each judge evaluates every solver’s output on the same problem set, allowing statistical separation of judge‑specific bias from solver skill. Dual rubrics (Expert vs. Course‑Specific) provide orthogonal dimensions: the former accepts advanced mathematical machinery, while the latter enforces pedagogical constraints. Human expert consensus serves as an unbiased ground truth. The paper’s evidence includes the successful marginalization of individual model bias and the consistency of bias patterns across rubrics (heatmaps). A break condition would be low inter‑rater reliability among human experts, mitigated by >1,000 hours of PhD‑level annotation.

### Rubric Insensitivity Detection
By comparing each judge’s alignment under both rubrics, the authors demonstrate that prompt constraints have negligible impact on internal evaluation logic. For example, GPT‑5.2 Pro’s correlation with human scores changes by only 0.02 (r=0.69 vs 0.67) and MAE by 0.01. This indicates that LLM judges rely on pre‑trained priors rather than instruction following, limiting the effectiveness of prompt engineering. The core assumption is that the rubrics meaningfully differ in strictness; the paper shows they do by design. Break condition would be if judges perceived the rubrics as identical; the noticeable wording differences and expert validation argue against this.

### Sycophancy Trap Identification via Error Mode Decomposition
The analysis decomposes judge errors into Leniency Rate (false positives) and Harshness Rate (false negatives) using a binary pass threshold (≥0.9). High Leniency Rates (e.g., Llama 4 Maverick 74.8%) reveal a systematic tendency to approve flawed proofs, while some models (DeepSeek‑V3 12.3% Harshness) are overly conservative. Qualitative case studies confirm judges reward “hallucinated rigor” (e.g., empty error messages, irrelevant proofs) and penalize solvers who correctly refuse false premises, exposing a sycophancy bias toward superficially coherent but logically invalid outputs. The break condition would be an arbitrary threshold; the authors used expert‑informed thresholds and showed consistency across judges.

## Foundational Learning
- **Dual‑Rubric Calibration**  
  Why needed: Distinguishes pedagogical correctness (course constraints) from expert‑level logical rigor; a proof using advanced theorems may be correct but not acceptable in an undergraduate context.  
  Quick check: For a proof using the Residue Theorem in a complex analysis course, which rubric would penalize it and why?

- **Factorial Design for Bias Isolation**  
  Why needed: Separates judge effects from solver effects; without crossing, a lenient judge might evaluate stronger solvers by chance, confounding bias with skill.  
  Quick check: If Judge A passes 80% of solutions and Judge B passes 60%, what could explain the difference without assuming Judge A is “worse”?

- **Rubric Insensitivity**  
  Why needed: Demonstrates that improving LLM judges requires architectural changes (e.g., process supervision) rather than better prompting; prompt engineering has diminishing returns.  
  Quick check: If adding “DO NOT USE ADVANCED THEOREMS” to the prompt doesn’t change scores, what does that tell us about the model’s internal evaluation mechanism?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Problem Curation → Solver Generation → Expert Annotation → Judge Evaluation → Analysis Engine

**Critical path**  
1. Human ground truth establishment (1000+ hours of PhD‑level annotations) – cannot be bypassed.  
2. Complete 7×5 matrix evaluation (35 combinations × 272 problems = 9520 individual evaluations); must handle API failures with retries.  
3. Bias calculation and statistical validation (Welch’s t‑test for contamination; correlation/MAE for alignment).  
4. Qualitative case study analysis of failure modes (especially solutions scoring <0.9).

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Scale vs depth: 272 problems is modest but annotation‑intensive; scaling with synthetic judges loses human calibration.  
- Rubric complexity: Dual rubrics double evaluation cost but reveal pedagogical vs expert dimensions.  
- Judge diversity: 7 judges covers major families but misses newest releases; tradeoff between coverage and recency.

**Failure signatures**  
- Judge-level: Pass rate >75% with human baseline 67.7% indicates sycophancy; Leniency Rate >60% is pathological (Llama Maverick 74.8%).  
- Solver-level: Performance drop >20% from continuous (ODEs ~100%) to discrete (Combinatorics ~30%) indicates reasoning gap.  
- System-level: Rubric correlation >0.95 between conditions suggests insensitivity; equal inflation across judges may mask degradation.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. Human reliability check: Compute inter‑rater agreement (Cohen’s κ/ICC) on a held‑out subset; expect κ > 0.8.  
2. Contamination validation: Compare performance on online vs offline problems (Table 2); expect no significant gap (p > 0.05).  
3. Rubric sensitivity test: Run GPT‑5.2 Pro on both rubrics; measure delta in alignment metrics; expect minimal change (<0.02 correlation drop).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Quantifying and mitigating the “poisoning effect”** of training LLM judges on sycophantic signals (e.g., rewarding empty error messages, irrelevant proofs). Evidence: LLM judges award partial credit for such failures.  
2. **Architectural changes beyond prompt engineering** needed to overcome rubric insensitivity. Evidence: changing rubrics yields negligible alignment improvement; process supervision may be required.  
3. **Discrete‑continuous reasoning divide**: Why do frontier models excel in continuous domains (ODEs) but struggle with discrete mathematics (Combinatorics)? Hypothesis: template retrieval vs constructive state search.  
4. **Effect of model scale** on the alignment gap: Current frontier models still inflate; systematic scaling experiments needed.  
5. **Designing robust benchmarks** that resist known failure modes (e.g., rewarding empty proofs, falsehoods). QEDBENCH itself remains vulnerable to leniency.

## Limitations
- Human ground truth aggregation method (average/median/consensus) not disclosed.  
- Availability of all judge evaluation logs uncertain; some models may be inaccessible for reproduction.  
- Limited benchmark size (272 problems) and arbitrary pass‑rate threshold (≥0.9) may affect generalizability.
