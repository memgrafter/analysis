# Towards Robust Scaling Laws for Optimizers

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.07712
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07712
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Proposed a unified scaling law with shared exponents across optimizers and optimizer‑specific rescaling factors (ρ<sub>N</sub>, ρ<sub>D</sub>), yielding stable parameter estimates and a >2× reduction in extrapolation MSE.

## Executive Summary
Optimizer choice is a key factor in large language model (LLM) training, but conventional approaches that fit independent Chinchilla‑style scaling laws per optimizer produce brittle, highly correlated parameter estimates. This paper introduces a unified law where the power‑law exponents (α, β) and coefficients (A, B, E) are shared across all optimizers, while each optimizer is characterized by two rescaling factors, ρ<sub>N</sub> and ρ<sub>D</sub>, that multiplicatively adjust the effective model size and data budget. Tested on OLMo and Llama models with five optimizers, the method yields significantly improved stability and interpretability. A theoretical analysis on convex quadratics with power‑law spectra supports the law’s form, linking approximation and optimization errors to the shared exponents. The result offers a robust, quantitative way to compare optimizer efficiency.

## Method Summary
Training runs were collected for OLMo‑2 and Llama models spanning parameter counts from 50M to 1.5B and token‑to‑parameter ratios D/N ∈ {30, 50, 100, 200}, using five optimizers (AdamW, Muon, Scion, Shampoo, SOAP). For each run, the final test loss was recorded. Two families of scaling laws were fitted: (1) independent Chinchilla laws (L = A/N<sup>α</sup> + B/D<sup>β</sup> + E) per optimizer, and (2) the proposed unified law (L = A/(ρ<sub>N</sub>N)<sup>α</sup> + B/(ρ<sub>D</sub>D)<sup>β</sup> + E) where α, β, A, B, E are first fitted on AdamW data only and then fixed; for each other optimizer only ρ<sub>N</sub> and ρ<sub>D</sub> are fitted. Stability was evaluated via leave‑one‑out cross‑validation (parameter variance) and extrapolation accuracy (training on smaller models and predicting loss for larger ones). The unified law consistently achieved lower variance and reduced extrapolation MSE by more than a factor of two compared to independent fits.

## Key Results
- Independent Chinchilla fits per optimizer are ill‑conditioned: parameters (A, α) and (B, β) are highly correlated and unstable across resampling.
- The unified law with shared exponents and optimizer‑specific ρ factors yields far more stable estimates and cuts extrapolation MSE by >2×.
- Empirically, non‑AdamW optimizers (e.g., Muon) are generally more data‑efficient (ρ<sub>D</sub> > 1) while parameter efficiency remains similar to AdamW (ρ<sub>N</sub> ≈ 1), enabling clear, interpretable comparisons.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Sharing exponents removes parameter trade‑offs.** Independent fits suffer from strong correlation between (A, α) and (B, β) because limited data ranges allow multiple combinations to produce similar loss curves. Fixing α and β globally reduces each optimizer’s degrees of freedom from five to two (ρ<sub>N</sub>, ρ<sub>D</sub>), eliminating the trade‑off and stabilizing estimates. This relies on the assumption that optimizer choice does not change the underlying power‑law decay of loss with N or D; evidence is the observed correlation in independent fits (Fig. 1) and the abstract’s statement.

**Rescaling factors disentangle parameter and data efficiency.** The unified law casts optimizer effects as multiplicative adjustments to effective model size (ρ<sub>N</sub>) and data budget (ρ<sub>D</sub>). ρ<sub>N</sub> > 1 means the optimizer behaves as if it had a larger model (parameter‑efficient); ρ<sub>D</sub> > 1 means it reaches the same loss with fewer tokens (data‑efficient). This clean separation allows direct comparison. Empirically, most optimizers improve ρ<sub>D</sub> while ρ<sub>N</sub> stays near 1 (Tables 3–4).

**Spectral theory naturally yields the unified form.** Gradient descent on a convex quadratic with eigenvalues decaying as a power‑law decomposes excess loss into approximation error (∝ N<sup>-(α+β-1)</sup>) and optimization error (∝ D<sup>-β</sup>). The exponents are set by the spectral properties (source/capacity conditions), not by optimizer details; faster optimizers can be modeled by rescaling D. The theoretical derivation (Section 6, Theorem 6.3) maps directly onto the empirical law, providing a mechanistic justification.

## Foundational Learning
- **Chinchilla scaling law (L = A/N<sup>α</sup> + B/D<sup>β</sup> + E)**  
  • *Why needed*: Serves as the baseline that separates model‑capacity effects from data‑coverage effects; the paper builds on it.  
  • *Quick check*: What does the term B/D<sup>β</sup> represent, and why might its exponent β become correlated with A and α when fitting per optimizer?

- **Modern optimizers (AdamW, Muon, Shampoo, SOAP)**  
  • *Why needed*: Knowing their differences (first‑order vs. second‑order, orthogonalization) helps interpret ρ<sub>N</sub> and ρ<sub>D</sub> outcomes (e.g., most improve data efficiency but not parameter efficiency).  
  • *Quick check*: How does Muon’s orthogonalized update differ from AdamW’s adaptive momentum, and why might this lead to higher ρ<sub>D</sub> but similar ρ<sub>N</sub>?

- **Spectral dimension and power‑law spectra**  
  • *Why needed*: The theory uses spectral dimension ω to link Hessian eigenvalue decay to scaling exponents via ω = 1 + β<sup>-1</sup>/α under power‑law assumptions.  
  • *Quick check*: In the quadratic model, what does the spectral dimension ω represent, and how does it relate to the empirical exponents α, β?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map (plain text):  
Data collection -> Fitting module -> Validation

**Critical path**:  
1. Gather training runs for each optimizer covering a wide span of N (e.g., 10⁸–10⁹) and D/N ratios (30–200).  
2. Log‑transform N, D, L; choose robust Huber loss (δ = 10⁻³).  
3. Select AdamW as reference optimizer.  
4. Fit shared parameters (A, α, B, β, E) on AdamW runs using nonlinear least squares.  
5. For each other optimizer, fix shared parameters and fit only ρ<sub>N</sub>, ρ<sub>D</sub> (initialized at 1,1).  
6. Evaluate stability via leave‑one‑out CV (standard deviation of ρ) and extrapolation MSE.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- *Reference optimizer*: AdamW is default; another baseline only changes ρ interpretation, not the law itself.  
- *Shared exponents*: Assuming exponent invariance across optimizers may hide benefits if false; residuals should be checked.  
- *Data span*: Sufficient coverage in N and D is required to identify exponents; the paper uses D/N up to 200.  
- *Fitting scale*: Log‑fitting stabilizes but may bias if noise is multiplicative; Huber loss mitigates outliers.

**Failure signatures**:  
- High leave‑one‑out variance in ρ<sub>N</sub>, ρ<sub>D</sub> (>10% relative) indicates underidentification.  
- Large train/test error mismatch suggests misspecification (e.g., need optimizer‑specific exponents).  
- Strong correlation between ρ<sub>N</sub> and ρ<sub>D</sub> across optimizers might imply an unmodeled trade‑off (e.g., compute axis).  
- Extrapolation MSE higher than naive fits signals that shared exponents are invalid for some optimizers.

**First 3 experiments**:  
1. **Unified law replication**: Fit the unified law on the provided OLMo data; verify ρ<sub>D</sub> > 1 for non‑AdamW optimizers and ρ<sub>N</sub> ≈ 1.  
2. **Leave‑one‑out stability**: For each optimizer, omit one (N,D) point, refit ρ<sub>N</sub>, ρ<sub>D</sub>, and report standard deviation; expect <0.05 for both.  
3. **Extrapolation accuracy**: Fit laws using only models with N < 1B; predict loss for N = 1.5B; compute MSE and confirm it is >2× lower than the MSE from independent per‑optimizer fits.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1**: Does the convex quadratic theoretical analysis extend to non‑convex neural network training?  
*Basis*: Theory is restricted to convex quadratics; the paper admits it is an approximation for LLMs but lacks a non‑convex justification. *Unresolved*: Non‑convexity may introduce additional error terms. *Resolution*: Develop scaling theory under NTK or mean‑field approximations and validate on large models.

**Open Question 2**: Are the exponents α and β truly optimizer‑independent?  
*Basis*: The unified law fixes α, β across all tested optimizers, but accelerated or second‑order methods might alter scaling exponents. *Unresolved*: Current formulation cannot capture a change in β from acceleration. *Resolution*: Test optimizers with provably different convergence rates (e.g., Nesterov) and check whether shared‑exponent fits remain stable; if not, allow exponents to vary.

**Open Question 3**: How accurately do ρ<sub>N</sub> and ρ<sub>D</sub> extrapolate to much larger scales (e.g., >10B parameters, D/N > 500)?  
*Basis*: Authors caution that ρ values are valid only within the fitted range (up to 1.5B parameters, D/N≤200). *Unresolved*: Scaling laws often exhibit crossovers; mid‑scale estimates may not hold at extreme compute. *Resolution*: Train models at significantly larger scales, refit the law, and track any drift in ρ.

## Limitations
- The shared‑exponent assumption might fail for optimizers with fundamentally different update rules (e.g., second‑order methods that change the scaling form).
- Theoretical justification relies on a convex quadratic model with a power‑law Hessian spectrum; LLM loss landscapes may deviate, especially near convergence.
- Empirical evidence is limited to two model families (OLMo, Llama) and D/N ≤ 200; generalizability to other architectures or massive compute regimes is uncertain.

## Confidence
- **Stable shared‑exponent fits**: High (empirical variance reduction and extrapolation gains are robust).
- **ρ factors as efficiency metrics**: Medium‑High (interpretable and consistent across experiments, but contingent on shared exponents).
- **Theoretical derivation mapping to empirical law**: Medium (plausible but not directly verified for non‑convex LLMs).

## Next Checks
1. Test generalizability by training additional architectures (e.g., encoder‑decoder, MoE) with diverse optimizers (Adafactor, 8‑bit Adam) and refit unified laws.
2. Statistically test exponent invariance by fitting independent (α, β) per optimizer and checking overlap of 95% confidence intervals; significant differences would challenge the shared‑exponent premise.
3. Explore extreme scales: train models with D/N = 500–1000 and sizes >10B parameters to assess whether ρ values remain constant and power‑law trends persist.
