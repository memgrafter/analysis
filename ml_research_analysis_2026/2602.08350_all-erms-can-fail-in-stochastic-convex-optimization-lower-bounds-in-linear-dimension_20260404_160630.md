# All ERMs Can Fail in Stochastic Convex Optimization Lower Bounds in Linear Dimension
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.08350
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08350
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Generic Empirical Risk Minimizers (ERMs) can fail to generalize in Stochastic Convex Optimization when the dimension is linear in the sample size, and Gradient Descent suffers a generalization lower bound of Ω(√(ηT/m^{1.5})).

## Executive Summary
This paper resolves a long‑standing open question by proving that generic Empirical Risk Minimizers (ERMs), even approximate ones with error Θ(m^{-3/2}), can fail to generalize in Stochastic Convex Optimization (SCO) when the problem dimension is linear in the number of samples m. The core of the construction is a modification of Feldman’s function, which relies on asymptotically good binary codes. By adding a sample‑dependent noise term and a penalizing link function, the authors force the empirical risk to have a unique minimizer that overfits, establishing that the sample complexity of ERM is linear in dimension. Extending the construction to Gradient Descent (GD), they prove a new lower bound of Ω(√(ηT/m^{1.5})) on the generalization error, where η is the learning rate and T the horizon—exponentially narrowing the gap with the best known upper bound O(ηT/m) for constrained GD.

## Method Summary
The paper studies a stochastic convex optimization instance where the input space is [k] with k=2m and the loss in dimension d is defined by several components: a convex Feldman term hζ(wc,i) built from an asymptotically good binary code G, a linear noise term −⟨wm, δ_i⟩ that depends on the sample, a penalizing max‑link p(w) that aligns w with sample‑dependent vectors, and quadratic regularizers. For a sample S of size m, the vectors v_S and v_s_S encode the sample. Under a no‑collision event (all indices distinct, constant probability when d=Θ(m)), the empirical risk FS(w) has a unique minimizer (determined by first‑order conditions) that aligns with (G(v_s_S), v_S) up to scaling and incurs constant population excess risk. For GD, projected updates on FS with step size η and T iterations make the iterates track similar alignment; when ηT > √m the final iterate (or suffix average) inherits the constant excess risk, yielding the lower bound Ω(√(ηT/m^{1.5})). A minimal reproduction involves: (1) implementing a small binary code with constant relative distance; (2) setting parameters satisfying the paper’s inequalities; (3) sampling S without collisions; (4) computing the closed‑form ERM and its risk; (5) optionally simulating GD to verify the scaling.

## Key Results
- **Theorem 1:** For SCO with dimension d=Θ(m), there exists a distribution where any (exact or approximate) ERM with error ε=Θ(m^{-3/2}) fails to generalize, incurring a constant excess risk Ω(1).
- The failure holds even for strongly convex losses with strong convexity λ ≤ 1/√m, contrasting with the known O(1/(λ m)) upper bound.
- **Theorem 4:** For constrained Gradient Descent on the empirical risk, if ηT > √m then the generalization error is at least Ω(√(ηT/m^{1.5})), narrowing the exponential gap with the previous best lower bound.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1:** Feldman’s function leverages an asymptotically good binary code (constant relative distance ρ) to create a loss landscape with both generalizing (e.g., w=0) and overfitting empirical minimizers. For a random sample of indices, there exists a codeword G(v_s_S) that correlates well with unseen indices, yielding low empirical loss but high population loss, while w=0 maintains moderate loss on both.
**Mechanism 2:** The sample‑dependent noise term (−⟨wm, δ_i⟩) and the penalizing link p(w)=max_v[γ_m⟨v,wm⟩ − γ_c⟨G(v),wc⟩] force all (ε‑)ERMs to align wm with v_S and wc with G(v_s_S). Under strong convexity and the no‑collision event, the unique ERM becomes wc=(γ_c/λ_c)G(v_s_S), wm=(1/λ_m)(v_S/m − γ_m v_s_S), which overfits because hζ_D(wc) is large by the code’s distance property.
**Mechanism 3:** For GD, the loss is regularized only on wc (λ_c‖wc‖^2). The update recursion shows that wc_t converges to a scaled version of G(v_s_S) while wm_t tracks v_S/m. When ηT > √m, wc_T is close to the overfitting alignment, and its population risk hζ_D(wc_T) is constant, giving the Ω(√(ηT/m^{1.5})) lower bound.

## Foundational Learning
- **Stochastic Convex Optimization (SCO)**  
  *Why needed:* The paper’s setting; learner outputs w after seeing m i.i.d. samples to minimize expected loss.  
  *Quick check:* What distinguishes population risk F(w) from empirical risk FS(w), and why might minimizing FS not guarantee small F even with many samples?
- **Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM)**  
  *Why needed:* Central solution concept; the paper proves that even the best ERM can fail to generalize.  
  *Quick check:* How does an ε‑ERM relax exact minimization, and why does allowing a small ε make the failure result more surprising?
- **Asymptotically good binary codes**  
  *Why needed:* Feldman’s function uses codes with constant relative distance to create a gap between good and bad solutions.  
  *Quick check:* What property ensures that for a random subset of indices, a codeword exists that is far from all codewords associated with those indices?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Feldman function (code G) → Loss components (hζ(wc,i), noise −⟨wm,δ_i⟩, penalizing max p(w), regularizers λ_m/2‖wm‖^2, λ_c/2‖wc‖^2) → Loss f(w,i) → Empirical risk FS(w) = (1/m) Σ f(w,z_i) → Optimization (Exact ERM: ∇FS=0; or Gradient Descent: w_{t+1}=Π(w_t−η∇FS(w_t)) with suffix averaging) → Predictor w_S.

**Critical Path**  
1. Select an asymptotically good binary code G with constant relative distance ρ (e.g., from Justesen–Sipser–Spielman).  
2. Choose parameters λ_m, λ_c, γ_m, γ_c, ζ satisfying inequalities (e.g., γ_c ≤ λ_c/3, λ_m ≥ 9/√m, γ_m ≤ 1/(2m), ζ = γ_c/λ_c).  
3. Condition on the no‑collision event in S (holds with constant probability when d=Θ(m)).  
4. Show that strong convexity and the p(w) term force the unique (approximate) ERM to satisfy wc = ζ·G(v_s_S), wm = (1/λ_m)(v_S/m − γ_m v_s_S).  
5. Use code distance to lower bound hζ_D(wc) by a constant, yielding constant excess risk.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- **Dimension:** Full proof uses d=6m; simplified versions may need d=O(m²) to ease collision analysis.  
- **Strong convexity λ:** Construction works for λ ≤ 1/√m; larger λ speeds optimization but changes the lower bound’s λ‑dependence.  
- **Approximate ERM error ε:** Extending to ε=Θ(m^{-3/2}) relies on strong convexity to keep the ERM close to the unique minimizer.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Many collisions in S reduce ∥v_S∥, weakening the p(w) term and potentially allowing non‑overfitting ERMs.  
- Insufficient code distance ρ makes the hζ separation too small to guarantee constant excess risk.  
- Learning rate η too large activates projection in GD, breaking the linear recurrence used in the analysis.

**First 3 Experiments**  
1. **Feldman baseline:** For small m (e.g., m=4), compute hζ_D(w) for w=0 and w=G(v_s_S) to confirm the expected gap ~ζρ/4.  
2. **ERM verification:** Sample many S without collisions, compute the closed‑form ERM (Eq. 20), evaluate population risk F(w_S) and F(0), and check that the average gap ≥ constant; also verify the fraction of no‑collision trials.  
3. **GD scaling:** For m=20, set η=1/(m√m), T=m² (ηT = m^{1.5}), run projected GD on FS (Eq. 31), record suffix‑averaged population loss, and confirm it matches Ω(√(ηT/m^{1.5})) scaling; vary ηT to locate the overfitting threshold.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Approximate ERM regime (ε between m^{-3/2} and m^{-1/2})**  
   The construction fails for ε = O(m^{-1/2}) (generalizing ERMs always exist) but works for ε = Θ(m^{-3/2}). It is open whether every ε‑ERM fails for ε in the intermediate range.
2. **Strongly convex ERM sample complexity**  
   For λ ≤ 1/√m, the tight worst‑case generalization error of exact ERMs is unknown: lower bound Ω(1/(λ m^{3/2})) vs. upper bound O(1/(λ m)).
3. **Smooth objectives**  
   The results rely on non‑smooth losses; for smooth convex losses, GD enjoys an improved O(ηT/m) bound. Extending the overfitting phenomenon to the smooth setting remains an open challenge.
4. **Tight GD bounds**  
   The proven lower bound Ω(√(ηT/m^{1.5})) and existing upper bound O(ηT/m) have a polynomial (√m) gap; closing it to a constant‑factor gap is open.

## Limitations
- Code constructions for moderate m may not achieve the theoretical constant relative distance, hampering empirical validation.
- The argument conditions on the no‑collision event, which only occurs with constant probability, effectively reducing sample size.
- The parameter ranges (e.g., λ_m ∈ [9/√m, 27/(2√m)]) are tight and derived from technical inequalities; satisfying all simultaneously across m may require delicate tuning.

## Confidence
- **ERM failure in linear dimension (Theorem 1):** High
- **Unique overfitting ERM for approximate cases (ε=Θ(m^{-3/2})):** Medium
- **GD generalization lower bound (Theorem 4):** Medium
