# CryptoAnalystBench: Failures in Multi-Tool Long-Form LLM Analysis
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.11304
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11304
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** State-of-the-art LLM analyst agents exhibit low hallucination (<6%) and high citation precision (86–97%) on a crypto-specific multi-tool benchmark, yet fail on depth and data consistency due to seven higher-order error types (e.g., source reconciliation, missing risk context).

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces CryptoAnalystBench, a benchmark of 198 production-aligned crypto analyst queries designed to stress-test LLM agents that orchestrate multiple tools (price feeds, on-chain analytics, news). A layered evaluation—combining LLM-as-a-judge scoring with automated citation verification—reveals that while modern models avoid obvious fabrication and cite sources precisely, they struggle with deeper synthesis, temporal reasoning, and consistency across heterogeneous data. A human-annotated taxonomy identifies seven recurring failure modes beyond simple factuality. Targeted mitigations improve top models but harm weaker ones, underscoring the need for model-aware interventions in high-stakes, data-dense domains.

## Method Summary
The authors built an agentic harness using a ReAct-style loop where an LLM plans and executes tool calls (crypto APIs, web search) to answer long-form queries. Evaluation uses two complementary layers: (1) an LLM judge scores responses on temporal relevance, data consistency, depth, and relevance; (2) a citation verification pipeline extracts claims, links them to tool outputs, and classifies support as exact, derived, or fabricated. A human-annotated error taxonomy of seven higher-order failure types is used to analyze outputs and train an automated classifier (93.45% accuracy). Full reproduction requires implementing the tool environment, running the harness with a chosen LLM, and applying the provided judge/verification prompts.

## Key Results
- State-of-the-art models show low hallucination (<6% fabricated claims) and high citation precision (86–97%).
- Persistent failures in depth of analysis and cross-source data consistency.
- LLM judges achieve fair-to-moderate agreement with humans (Cohen’s κ 0.35–0.42) but reliably flag critical failures.
- Targeted mitigations (e.g., explicit temporal prompting) improve stronger models but regress weaker ones, highlighting model-capability interactions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** CryptoAnalystBench effectively stress-tests multi-tool analyst agents by leveraging the crypto domain’s extreme data density, temporal volatility, and high-stakes decision context.
- **Mechanism:** Queries require integration of dozens of heterogeneous tool outputs under tight time constraints, forcing failures like source reconciliation, staleness, and missing risk context to surface.
- **Core assumption:** Crypto is representative of other high-stakes, data-intensive domains (e.g., finance, law); failures here generalise.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 1 describe crypto’s extreme data density, high temporal volatility, diverse modalities, and decision stakes. Corpus context shows growing interest in multi-tool agent vulnerabilities.
- **Break condition:** Benchmarks in other domains (e.g., medical) fail to reproduce similar patterns, or crypto-specific pitfalls (e.g., adversarial data) dominate.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** A layered evaluation—LLM-as-a-judge plus citation verification—produces complementary signals that capture holistic quality and fine-grained factual grounding, exposing higher-order errors missed by factuality checks alone.
- **Mechanism:** The LLM judge scores relevance, temporal relevance, depth, and consistency; citation verification extracts claims, maps to tool outputs, and classifies support. Discrepancies reveal structural issues.
- **Core assumption:** Neither metric alone is sufficient; the combination yields a more complete diagnostic.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 6 describe the layered framework and judge’s role in identifying critical failures despite moderate human agreement.
- **Break condition:** Citation precision begins to correlate perfectly with judge scores; the second layer’s marginal value disappears.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A human-annotated error taxonomy, coupled with an automated LLM classifier, isolates recurring qualitative failures that standard metrics do not capture.
- **Mechanism:** Experts annotate failures, distilling seven abstract categories. An LLM is trained to detect these, achieving 93.45% accuracy, enabling scalable identification.
- **Core assumption:** Structured error attribution is more actionable than scalar scores and can be reliably automated.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 5–6 define the taxonomy and report classifier validation.
- **Break condition:** The classifier’s accuracy drops significantly on out-of-distribution queries or across domains, limiting scalability.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Multi-Tool Agent Orchestration (e.g., ReAct)**  
  - **Why needed here:** The harness uses a ReAct-style loop where the LLM plans tool calls, parses outputs, and iterates. Understanding this reasoning–acting cycle is key to grasping how tool results are integrated.  
  - **Quick check question:** What are the three core challenges when an LLM must orchestrate many heterogeneous tools for a single query?

- **Concept: LLM-as-a-Judge Evaluation**  
  - **Why needed here:** The core evaluation relies on an LLM judge scoring responses on rubrics. Awareness of known biases (length, position, self-preference) and fairness techniques (balanced ordering) is essential to interpret scores correctly.  
  - **Quick check question:** How does position bias affect LLM judges, and how can it be mitigated in pairwise comparisons?

- **Concept: Citation & Attribution in Knowledge-Augmented Generation**  
  - **Why needed here:** The paper measures factual grounding via claim extraction, citation precision, and verification. Distinguishing coverage (claims cited) from attribution accuracy (citations support claims) is central.  
  - **Quick check question:** What is the difference between claim-to-citation coverage and attribution accuracy?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Query Understanding → Tool Execution Environment → Reasoning LLM (ReAct loop) → Response Generator → Citation Verification Pipeline → LLM-as-a-Judge → Error Classifier → Aggregator

**Critical path:**  
Query → Query Understanding → (ReAct loop: plan → call tool → ingest) → Response → Citation verification → LLM judge scoring → Error classification → Metrics aggregation.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- LLM judge vs. human: scalability (hours vs. weeks) traded off against moderate agreement (κ 0.35–0.42); judge reliably flags critical failures.  
- Citation verification leniency: allowing paraphrasing/derivation reduces false negatives but may inflate precision; strictness risks missing valid claims.  
- Taxonomy granularity: 7 categories balance specificity and automation; finer categories may reduce inter-annotator agreement.  
- Intervention scope: model-aware mitigations improve strong models but regress weak ones, necessitating adaptive selection.

**Failure signatures (examples):**  
- Source reconciliation: Conflicting values (e.g., all-time high) across cited sources, unresolved.  
- Staleness/missing time bounds: “Current TVL” without date, or data older than implied freshness window.  
- Missing risk/mechanism context: Recommendation without discussing smart-contract risk or volatility assumptions.  
- Shallow synthesis: Lists influencers but no analysis of motives or verification.  
- Partial/misframed answer: Addresses only a subset of query aspects.  
- Overconfident prediction: Exact price target with no uncertainty bounds.  
- Inconsistent claims: Contradictory statements (e.g., bullish pattern vs. bearish pattern cited).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Harness sanity check:** Run 5 diverse queries through the full pipeline (GPT-4-level model); manually verify tool calls match intent and response structure (citations, multi-source integration) is correct.  
2. **Citation verification validation:** Take a response with known claim-citation pairs; run the verifier and confirm precision/completeness match manual counts. Test with fabricated claims to check detection.  
3. **Judge calibration:** Score 20 benchmark outputs with the LLM judge and have a human expert rate the same responses on the 4 rubrics; compute quadratic-weighted Cohen’s κ. If agreement is low (<0.3), iterate on judge prompt (e.g., add exemplars, balanced ordering).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can LLM-as-a-judge reliability be improved for multi-tool long-form analysis beyond current fair-to-moderate human agreement?
- **Basis in paper:** "improving LLM-as-a-judge reliability remains an open challenge; future work could explore hybrid judging approaches that combine rubric-based scoring with structured failure detection or uncertainty-aware calibration." (Page 11)
- **Why unresolved:** Current LLM judges show only fair-to-moderate agreement with human experts (Cohen’s κ 0.35–0.42), limiting fine-grained diagnostic feedback despite reliably flagging critical failures.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Development and empirical validation of hybrid judge architectures (e.g., integrating rubric scores with structured error classifiers) on diverse analyst tasks, with performance correlated against expanded human annotations across multiple rubrics.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can the seven-category failure taxonomy be refined into finer-grained, domain-specific subcategories to enhance diagnostic precision for analyst agent errors?
- **Basis in paper:** "while our failure taxonomy captures dominant qualitative error patterns, extending it to finer-grained or domain-specific subcategories may further improve diagnostic resolution." (Page 11)
- **Why unresolved:** The taxonomy distinguishes high-level failure modes but may mask nuanced variations (e.g., different reconciliation conflict types) that require targeted mitigation.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Multi-domain human annotation studies (e.g., legal, biomedical) to identify recurring subpatterns within each category, followed by training and validating automated classifiers for these subcategories.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How can mitigation strategies for higher-order failures be made adaptive to avoid regressions in weaker models while benefiting stronger ones?
- **Basis in paper:** "adaptive mitigation strategies—where interventions are dynamically selected based on model capability, task complexity, or real-time tool traces—represent a promising avenue for reducing regressions in weaker planners." (Page 11)
- **Why unresolved:** Fixed interventions (e.g., explicit temporal prompting) improve top models but degrade weaker ones, suggesting unaddressed capacity constraints in planning or reasoning.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments with dynamic intervention selection (e.g., based on model size-confidence profiles or tool-trace complexity) tested across model tiers, measuring performance delta and failure mode shifts.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** To what extent do the observed failure modes generalize beyond cryptocurrency to other high-stakes, data-intensive domains?
- **Basis in paper:** "applying CryptoAnalystBench-style evaluation to other settings such as legal analysis, enterprise intelligence, or scientific research would help assess the generality of the observed failure modes." (Page 11)
- **Why unresolved:** Crypto’s extreme data density and volatility may produce unique failure patterns; domains with different data modalities (e.g., legal documents) could exhibit distinct error profiles.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replication of the benchmark methodology (query curation, tool harness, multi-level evaluation) in at least two non-crypto domains, comparing error taxonomy distributions and model performance hierarchies.

## Limitations
- Tool implementation details (API endpoints, schemas, rate limits) are not fully specified, hindering exact replication.
- LLM judge and classifier specifications (e.g., Deepseek V3.1 version, training data splits) are incomplete.
- Generalizability beyond crypto is unverified; crypto-specific dynamics may dominate failure patterns.
- Benchmark scale (198 queries) may not capture full diversity of crypto analyst tasks.
- Moderate human-LLM judge agreement (κ 0.35–0.42) affects reliability of rubric-based scores.

## Confidence
- **CryptoBench stress-tests multi-tool agents effectively:** Medium (plausible but unvalidated in other domains).
- **Layered evaluation exposes higher-order errors:** Medium (ablation supports, but citation leniency may bias metrics).
- **Error taxonomy and classifier are robust:** Medium (taxonomy solid, classifier generalizability uncertain without full training details).
- **Model performance findings (low hallucination, high citation precision, shallow depth):** High (direct empirical results).
- **Mitigation regressions for weaker models:** High (clear experimental evidence).
