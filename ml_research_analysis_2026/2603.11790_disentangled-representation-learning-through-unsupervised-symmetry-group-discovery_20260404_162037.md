# Disentangled Representation Learning through Unsupervised Symmetry Group Discovery

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.11790
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11790
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Proposes an unsupervised method that discovers the symmetry group decomposition from interaction data and learns a linear symmetry-based disentangled representation, achieving near-perfect disentanglement scores and superior generalization.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles a key limitation in symmetry-based disentangled representation learning: the need for prior knowledge of the symmetry group structure. It introduces a three-stage unsupervised pipeline. First, an Action-based VAE (A-VAE) learns an entangled latent representation and linear action matrices from transition data. Second, a novel pseudo-distance computed from these matrices clusters actions into subgroups, effectively recovering the group decomposition without supervision. Third, a Group-Masked A-VAE (GMA-VAE) enforces a block-diagonal structure on the action matrices via learned masks, yielding a Linear Symmetry-Based Disentangled (LSBD) representation. Theoretical guarantees are provided under minimal assumptions. Extensive experiments on synthetic datasets (Flatland, COIL, 3DShapes, MPI3D) show the method outperforms existing LSBD approaches on standard disentanglement metrics and demonstrates robust long-term prediction and out-of-distribution generalization.

## Method Summary
The approach consists of a two-phase training procedure. In Phase 1, the A-VAE is trained on transitions \((x, g, x')\) using a loss combining reconstruction (like a standard VAE) and an action consistency term that encourages the encoder to satisfy equivariance \(h(x') \approx \rho(g) h(x)\). The learned action matrices \(\rho(g)\) and encoder \(h\) are then used to compute a pseudo-distance \(d_G(g,g')\) between actions, which reflects whether two actions belong to the same subgroup under mild assumptions. Agglomerative clustering on this distance yields a decomposition of the action set into subgroups \(\hat{G}_k\). Phase 2 trains the GMA-VAE from scratch, introducing binary assignment masks \(\pi_k\) (one per subgroup) that force the action matrix for each action \(g\) to be block-diagonal: the masked matrix \(\tilde{\rho}(g)\) is identity except on the block corresponding to \(g\)’s subgroup. A disentanglement loss encourages the masks to be nearly binary and ensures each latent dimension aligns with at most one subgroup. Under the assumptions (injective observation, disjoint subgroups, and a generative relation among actions), the resulting representation is provably LSBD. The method requires no prior knowledge of the group structure and works with finite, possibly partially observed, action sets.

## Key Results
- Achieves near-perfect scores on disentanglement metrics (Independence, Modularity, DCI) on multiple datasets, significantly outperforming baselines such as LSBD-VAE and SOBDRL.
- Demonstrates superior long-term prediction accuracy and generalization, particularly in out-of-distribution scenarios where actions or object configurations are unseen during training.
- Provides theoretical identifiability guarantees for both action clustering (Theorem 2) and LSBD representation learning (Theorem 3) under minimal assumptions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Learning Equivariant Representations with A-VAE
The A-VAE optimizes an evidence lower bound (ELBO) that includes a reconstruction term and an action consistency term. This directly enforces the equivariance condition \(h(x') \approx \rho(g) h(x)\) in the latent space. Under Assumption 1 (injective observation function), the learned encoder \(h\) and action matrices \(\rho(g)\) capture the group action up to the representation mapping. The action loss must converge; otherwise equivariance fails, breaking the subsequent steps.

### Mechanism 2: Unsupervised Subgroup Discovery via Pseudo-Distance
A pseudo-distance \(d_G(g,g')\) is defined using the learned action matrices and the semi-norm \(\|A\|_h = \mathbb{E}_x[\|A h(x)\|]\). The distance measures whether two actions’ matrices are equivalent up to a subgroup transformation, leveraging Assumption 3 (a specific generative relation among actions of the same subgroup). Under Assumptions 2 (actions form a disjoint union of subgroups) and 3, Theorem 2 proves that \(d_G(g,g')\) is small if and only if \(g\) and \(g'\) belong to the same subgroup. Agglomerative clustering with a threshold \(\eta\) then recovers the true decomposition. If the assumptions fail or the A-VAE matrices are not accurate, the distance matrix will not exhibit clear block structure and clustering breaks.

### Mechanism 3: Enforcing Disentanglement via Masking in GMA-VAE
The GMA-VAE introduces assignment masks \(\pi_k\) (one per discovered subgroup) that are used to mask each action matrix \(\rho(g)\). The masked matrix \(\tilde{\rho}(g)\) is set to the identity everywhere except on the block corresponding to \(g\)’s subgroup: \(\tilde{\rho}(g) = \pi_k\pi_k^\top \odot \rho(g) + (1-\pi_k\pi_k^\top)\odot I\). The loss adds a disentanglement term \(\lambda_{\text{DIS}} \sum_i |H(\pi_i) - C|\) that pushes the per-dimension assignment entropy \(H(\pi_i)\) to low values (binary assignments). Combined with the action consistency loss and a reconstruction loss, the optimization drives the representation to be LSBD. Theoretically, Theorem 3 shows that under Assumptions 1–2 and with a finite dataset covering all transitions, any minimizer of the loss yields an LSBD representation. Failure signatures include non-binary masks, off-block entries in \(\tilde{\rho}(g)\), or a drop in prediction accuracy.

Concepts like subgroup, generated monoid \(\lceil S\rceil\), direct sum decomposition, and linear representation \(\rho: G\to GL(\mathbb{Z})\) are central. Assumptions about the action set being a union of subgroups rely on these definitions.
  - *Quick check*: Why is a direct product group decomposition necessary for a linear action to be block‑diagonalizable?

- **Equivariance & Disentanglement Metrics**
  - *Why needed*: The target is an LSBD representation (Definition 1). Metrics such as Independence, Modularity, DCI, SAP assess whether latent factors are isolated and modular. Understanding how block‑diagonal \(\rho(g)\) corresponds to factor-wise independence is key.
  - *Quick check*: How does the block‑diagonal structure of \(\rho(g)\) ensure that each latent subspace corresponds to a single factor of variation (i.e., satisfies point 3 of Definition 1)?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: A-VAE -> Action Clustering -> GMA-VAE

**Critical path**:
1. Train A-VAE until action loss converges, obtaining encoder \(h\) and action matrices \(\rho(g)\).
2. Compute the pairwise pseudo‑distance \(d_G(g,g')\) using \(h\) and \(\rho\); run agglomerative clustering with threshold \(\eta = 0.1\) to obtain subgroups \(\hat{G}_k\).
3. Train GMA-VAE from scratch with masks \(\pi_k\) initialized randomly, using the same hyperparameters and annealing schedule for \(C\).

**Design tradeoffs**:
- *Two‑stage vs. end‑to‑end*: The sequential design simplifies theoretical analysis but requires full retraining and may propagate errors. An end‑to‑end approach could improve sample efficiency but would need a differentiable clustering surrogate.
- *Assumption strength*: Relies on Assumption 2 (actions uniquely belong to a subgroup) and Assumption 3 (generative relation). Violations (e.g., actions mixing factors) can cause failure.
- *Scalability*: The clustering step is \(O(|G|^2)\) and becomes expensive for large or continuous action sets; the current method handles only finite, modest‑size groups.

**Failure signatures**:
- A‑VAE action loss does not decrease → equivariance not learned → clustering distance matrix lacks block structure.
- Clustering yields ambiguous partitions (no clear threshold) → likely Assumption 2/3 violation or poor A‑VAE training.
- GMA‑VAE masks remain soft (high entropy) after training → disentanglement loss weight too low or annealing too fast; increase \(\lambda_{\text{DIS}}\) or slow down \(C\) decay.
- Disentanglement metrics remain low despite low reconstruction error → masks not aligned with true subgroups; check block‑diagonality of \(\tilde{\rho}(g)\).

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Action clustering validation** on Flatland (FLC/FLP) and COIL2/COIL3: compute \(d_G\) matrix, cluster, and compare to ground‑truth partition via Adjusted Rand Index. Expected ARI = 1.0.
2. **Disentanglement metrics** on the same datasets: compare Independence, Beta‑VAE, MIG, DCI, Modularity, SAP scores against baselines (LSBD‑VAE, SOBDRL). Expect near‑perfect scores ( >0.95) on all metrics.
3. **Generalization test** (Section 5.5): train on a partial set of actions (e.g., only one object rotatable in COIL) and evaluate i.i.d. and OOD prediction error. Expect minimal degradation (<5% relative increase) when testing on held‑out actions, indicating robust disentanglement.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### 1. End‑to-End Unification
**Basis in paper**: The conclusion suggests initializing GMA‑VAE with the A‑VAE encoder or developing a joint optimization that merges action clustering and representation learning.
**Why unresolved**: The current pipeline is sequential; errors in early stages propagate. A unified loss would need to incorporate the clustering criterion differentiably, which is non‑trivial.
**Evidence needed**: Implement a joint loss combining A‑VAE action loss, a differentiable version of \(d_G\), and the GMA‑VAE disentanglement term. Compare sample efficiency, disentanglement scores, and prediction accuracy against the sequential method.

### 2. Robustness to Partial Observability
**Basis in paper**: Theorem 1 requires an injective observation function \(b\) for LSBD existence. The paper acknowledges this strong assumption and notes experiments only on fully observable synthetic environments.
**Why unresolved**: Real‑world scenarios often involve occlusions or hidden variables that violate injectivity. It is unclear whether the method can recover correct group structure or learn disentangled representations under partial observability.
**Evidence needed**: Run experiments on modified datasets (e.g., 3DShapes with occluded objects, COIL with masked regions). Measure action clustering accuracy (ARI) and disentanglement metrics to assess degradation. If performance drops, investigate remedies such as recurrent context or multi‑step prediction.

### 3. Handling Entangled Actions
**Basis in paper**: Assumption 2 (each action belongs to a unique subgroup) is central. Appendix H.2 tests settings where actions are products of subgroup elements and shows disentanglement degrades for baseline LSBD methods.
**Why unresolved**: It is unknown whether GMA‑VAE itself fails when actions mix multiple subgroups, or if the masking mechanism can adapt. The method’s theoretical guarantees rely on Assumption 2, so empirical behavior under violations is not fully explored.
**Evidence needed**: Systematically vary the proportion of actions that are products of multiple subgroup elements in environments like Flatland and COIL. Report ARI for clustering and disentanglement metrics for GMA‑VAE. Identify whether the pseudo‑distance \(d_G\) remains discriminative or whether the masking can still learn a useful factorization.

### 4. Generalization to Unseen Actions or Environments
**Basis in paper**: The theoretical guarantees assume all actions in \(G\) are available in the dataset. Generalization experiments in Section 5.5 test within the same action set but not with entirely new actions.
**Why unresolved**: Real agents encounter novel transformations over time. It is unclear whether the discovered group decomposition or the learned representation can extrapolate to actions not seen during training, or transfer to new environments sharing the same group structure.
**Evidence needed**: After training on a fixed action set, evaluate prediction error when applying unseen transformations. Alternatively, train on one environment (e.g., Flatland with a specific group) and directly test on another environment with an isomorphic group (e.g., COIL with \(S_2 \times \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\)) without retraining the encoder.

### 5. Sample Complexity and Scalability
**Basis in paper**: The paper evaluates groups up to \(S_4\) and \(SO(2)^2\). The clustering algorithm requires a finite action set and computes all pairwise distances. The number of action matrix parameters grows linearly with \(|G|\) but quadratically with latent dimension \(d\).
**Why unresolved**: No analysis of how many transitions are needed for correct clustering and disentanglement, nor tests on larger groups (e.g., \(S_n\) with \(n>4\) or products of many cyclic groups). Continuous groups are not handled by the clustering step.
**Evidence needed**: Conduct experiments with synthetic environments having groups of increasing size (e.g., \(S_n\) for \(n=5,6,\dots\), \(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\) with \(n\) up to 100). Measure clustering accuracy and disentanglement as a function of dataset size. For continuous groups, extend the method (e.g., via discretization) and evaluate scalability in terms of time, memory, and performance.

## Limitations
- The method relies on strong assumptions (Assumption 2: actions form disjoint subgroups; Assumption 3: generative relation among subgroup actions). Violations in real‑world tasks with coupled or composite actions may lead to failure.
- Hyperparameters (λ_ACT, λ_DIS, annealing of \(C\)) are underspecified in the paper, making reproduction and tuning difficult.
- The clustering step scales quadratically with the number of actions, limiting applicability to large or continuous action spaces.

## Confidence
- **High**: Theoretical guarantees (Theorems 2–3) and strong empirical results on synthetic benchmarks validate the core approach.
- **Medium**: The assumptions appear plausible for controlled environments, but their necessity and robustness in more complex domains remain to be proven.
- **Low**: The method’s sensitivity to hyperparameter choices and its extension to continuous or large groups are uncertain and require further empirical evaluation.

## Next Checks
1. **Assumption Violation Stress Test**: Deploy the method on environments with non‑factorial (entangled) actions and quantify the degradation in clustering ARI and disentanglement metrics.
2. **Hyperparameter Sensitivity Ablation**: Systematically vary λ_ACT, λ_DIS, and the annealing schedule for \(C\); report stability of mask binaryness and final disentanglement scores.
3. **Scalability to Continuous Actions**: Apply the approach to discretized continuous transformation groups (e.g., 2D rotations with increasing resolution) and analyze clustering runtime, subgroup recovery, and disentanglement as the action set grows.
