# A Multilayered Approach to Classifying Customer Responsiveness and Credit Risk

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.01970
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.01970
- **Reference count**: 0
- **Primary result**: Extra Trees achieved 79.1% recall for response identification; Random Forest achieved 84.1% specificity and $5.3M estimated profit for risk minimization; combined response-risk model achieved 83.2% accuracy with inquiry counts as top features.

## Executive Summary
The study tackles a core business problem in credit card marketing: how to target mail campaigns to customers who are likely to respond while simultaneously avoiding those at high risk of default. Leveraging a proprietary Atlanticus dataset with a severe class imbalance (75.2% of customers never opened a card), the authors construct three predictive models—a response model (open vs. not open), a risk model (non‑delinquent vs. delinquent), and a combined multi‑class model—through a rigorous preprocessing and feature engineering pipeline. Key methodological steps include aggressive handling of missing values and outliers, dimensionality reduction via hierarchical clustering and variance inflation factor (VIF) filtering, and careful mitigation of class imbalance using ADASYN oversampling applied strictly to the training set. Each model is hyperparameter tuned to optimize a task‑specific metric: recall for response, specificity for risk, and accuracy for the multi‑class problem. Results show Extra Trees leading for response recall (79.1%), Random Forest dominating risk specificity (84.1%) and profit ($5,309,400), and Random Forest again top for combined accuracy (83.2%). Across all models, inquiry count features (ams3002, ams3124) consistently rank as the most important predictors, suggesting their strong association with both responsiveness and credit risk.

## Method Summary
The authors begin with the Atlanticus dataset containing 541 continuous predictors and a three‑class label (0: opened, not delinquent; 1: opened, delinquent; 2: did not open). Initial cleaning removes features with a single unique value or >50% missing values, replaces coded missing values (e.g., 2–9, 92–99999) with NaN, and imputes remaining missing values with the median. Outliers are capped at ±3 standard deviations and replaced by the median. All features are then scaled to a [0,1] range. Feature reduction proceeds in two stages: hierarchical clustering groups correlated variables, and from each cluster the feature with the highest within‑cluster R‑squared is retained; subsequently, any feature with VIF > 5 is dropped. The reduced dataset is split 70% training and 30% validation. ADASYN oversampling is fit and applied only to the training set to balance the classes for the respective binary tasks. For each task, multiple classifiers (Extra Trees, Random Forest, XGBoost, etc.) are trained; hyperparameter tuning is performed to maximize the target metric (recall, specificity, or accuracy) using cross‑validation on the training set. Models are evaluated on the validation set using confusion matrices, ROC/AUC curves, and a profitability matrix for the risk model.

## Key Results
- **Response model**: Extra Trees classifier achieved the highest recall of 79.1%.
- **Risk model**: Random Forest achieved the highest specificity of 84.1% and generated the maximum estimated profit of $5,309,400.
- **Combined response‑risk model**: Random Forest achieved the highest accuracy of 83.2%.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The approach’s effectiveness stems from three synergistic design choices. First, ADASYN oversampling directly addresses the extreme class imbalance (75.2% non‑openers) by synthesizing minority‑class examples, thereby improving sensitivity for both response and risk detection while keeping the validation set pristine. Second, ensemble methods (Random Forest, Extra Trees) inherently reduce overfitting through bootstrap aggregation and feature randomness, delivering robust performance even with high‑dimensional reduced feature sets. Third, task‑specific metric optimization aligns each model with its business objective: maximizing recall captures more potential responders, maximizing specificity ensures low default rates among approved customers, and the combined model balances both concerns. Feature reduction via clustering and VIF mitigates multicollinearity and reduces noise, while median imputation and outlier capping enhance model stability.

## Foundational Learning
- **ADASYN Oversampling**: *Why needed*: To handle severe class imbalance and improve detection of minority classes (responders, delinquents). *Quick check*: After oversampling, verify the training class distribution is balanced; ensure the validation set remains untouched.
- **Ensemble Methods (Random Forest, Extra Trees)**: *Why needed*: They combine many weak learners to achieve high accuracy and reduce overfitting; they also provide feature importance. *Quick check*: Compare performance against a single decision tree on a holdout set; the ensemble should significantly outperform.
- **Hierarchical Clustering for Feature Selection**: *Why needed*: Groups highly correlated features to reduce redundancy and dimensionality, improving interpretability and generalization. *Quick check*: Calculate pairwise correlation among selected features; expect low average correlation. Test stability by varying the clustering cut height.
- **Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) Filtering**: *Why needed*: To eliminate multicollinearity among predictors, which can destabilize estimates and harm performance. *Quick check*: After filtering, all remaining features should have VIF < 5; re‑compute to confirm.
- **Hyperparameter Tuning via Cross‑Validation**: *Why needed*: To optimize model settings for the target metric and prevent overfitting to a fixed validation set. *Quick check*: Use a CV loop on the training set; ensure the metric improves consistently across folds.
- **Outlier Capping and Median Imputation**: *Why needed*: Capping reduces the influence of extreme values; median imputation robustly fills missing data without assuming normality. *Quick check*: After capping, the absolute z‑score should be ≤ 3; after imputation, verify no remaining NaNs.

## Architecture Onboarding
The data processing and modeling pipeline follows a linear sequence:

Raw Data → Missing Value Imputation → Outlier Capping → Min‑Max Scaling → Hierarchical Clustering → VIF Filtering → Train/Validation Split → ADASYN Oversampling (training only) → Model Training & Hyperparameter Tuning → Evaluation on Validation Set

**Critical Path**: The longest sequence of dependent tasks runs from data ingestion through to final evaluation; the most computationally intensive stages are hyperparameter tuning and, to a lesser extent, the feature reduction (clustering). Project duration is highly sensitive to tuning efficiency.

**Design Tradeoffs**:
- *Median vs. Mean Imputation*: Median is robust to outliers but may distort the feature distribution; mean preserves shape but is outlier‑sensitive. The authors favoured robustness.
- *Hierarchical Clustering vs. Manual Selection*: Clustering automates redundancy reduction but depends on linkage method and cut height; different choices can yield different feature sets.
- *ADASYN vs. SMOTE*: ADASYN generates synthetic samples near the decision boundary, which may boost recall but risks overfitting; SMOTE is simpler but might be less effective for extreme imbalance.
- *Metric‑Specific Optimization*: Optimizing a single metric (recall, specificity, accuracy) may neglect other business‑relevant trade‑offs (e.g., profit); a custom loss function could better capture the overall objective.

**Failure Signatures**:
- Validation performance dramatically higher than typical benchmarks suggests data leakage (e.g., oversampling or feature selection applied before splitting).
- Selected features exhibit high pairwise correlation, indicating ineffective VIF filtering.
- Large gap between training and validation metrics (e.g., perfect training recall but low validation recall) signals overfitting or leakage.
- Risk model confusion matrix shows swapped labels (e.g., more false positives than true negatives), implying an incorrect positive‑class definition for specificity.

**First Three Experiments**:
1. **Baseline without oversampling**: Train a Random Forest on the original imbalanced training set and compare metrics to the ADASYN‑augmented version to quantify oversampling impact.
2. **Feature selection stability test**: Vary VIF threshold (e.g., 4, 5, 6) and clustering linkage (Ward, complete); measure overlap in selected features to assess sensitivity.
3. **Leakage detection**: Intentionally apply ADASYN *before* the train/validation split and compare validation metrics to the correct pipeline; a significant performance increase confirms the necessity of split‑after‑oversampling.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Does the proposed multi‑model framework (separate response and risk classifiers followed by a multi‑class response‑risk model) outperform a single integrated model that directly optimizes for both response likelihood and low default risk?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state, “In this study, we hope to explore different machine learning models that can identify potential credit users who are more likely to respond to a credit card offer and minimize any chances of delinquency… no studies have fully explored the use of machine learning models to identify potential customers who are more likely to respond… and while also not being delinquent.” They implement three separate models rather than a single joint objective.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper compares three modeling approaches but does not benchmark against a unified model that simultaneously optimizes for both business objectives (maximizing response while minimizing risk) in a single training process. It is unclear if the sequential approach is optimal.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A comparative experimental study where a unified model (e.g., a multi‑output model or a custom loss function combining response and risk objectives) is trained and evaluated against the paper’s three‑model framework using the same dataset and business profit metric.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** To what extent do the consistently important inquiry features (ams3002, ams3124) reflect causal financial behavior versus being proxies for other underlying risk or responsiveness drivers?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors note, “two features appear to be ranked highly in all three models. These features are ams3002 and ams3124 which represent the number of inquiries made within 24 months and 6 months respectively.” They also state it “would be interesting to see if these features become highly ranked in ensemble models.”
- **Why unresolved:** While feature importance is reported, the paper does not investigate the *reason* for their dominance. High inquiry counts could signal active shopping (increasing response likelihood) or financial distress (increasing risk), but the causal mechanism and potential interaction with other variables are not explored.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Behavioral or domain expert analysis correlating inquiry counts with external financial health indicators, or a causal inference study (e.g., using matched samples or quasi‑experimental design) to isolate the effect of inquiry volume on response vs. default, controlling for confounding factors like credit score or income.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How robust are the reported model performances (especially the high recall/specificity) to the specific choices in the preprocessing pipeline, such as the ADASYN oversampling technique and the hierarchical clustering‑based feature selection?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The methodology relies heavily on specific steps: 1) ADASYN for handling the extreme class imbalance (75.2% class “did not open”), 2) hierarchical clustering with R² to reduce from 465 to an unspecified number of features, and 3) VIF >5 for multicollinearity. The sensitivity of final results to these thresholds and techniques is not evaluated.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper establishes a single pipeline but does not conduct ablation studies or robustness checks. For instance, different oversampling methods (SMOTE, SMOTE‑ENN, no oversampling) or feature selection criteria (different clustering linkage, variance thresholding) could significantly alter the feature set and model performance, potentially changing which classifier is deemed “optimal.”
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A systematic sensitivity analysis where key preprocessing parameters (e.g., ADASYN k‑neighbors, VIF threshold, clustering distance metric and cut height) are varied, and the resulting model performance distributions (recall, specificity, profit) are compared to the baseline reported in the paper.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Is the chosen primary metric for each model (recall for response, specificity for risk, accuracy for multi‑class) truly aligned with maximizing long‑term profitability, or does it inadvertently create suboptimal trade‑offs?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors explicitly optimize each model for a single metric: “the Extra Trees classifier demonstrates the highest recall level (79.1%)… the Random Forest classifier exhibits remarkable specificity of 84.1%… the Random Forest classifier achieves the highest accuracy (83.2%).” Profit is only calculated post‑hoc for the risk model.
- **Why unresolved:** Optimizing for a single metric may neglect other aspects of the confusion matrix crucial for profit. For example, maximizing recall in the response model may increase false positives (wasted mail costs), and maximizing specificity in the risk model may increase false negatives (undetected defaults). The paper does not demonstrate that the metric‑optimized models actually maximize the *net profit* metric they define, which depends on a specific cost matrix.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A direct optimization and comparison of models using the defined profit function ($200 for true positive good risk, -$600 for false negative bad risk, etc.) as the training/validation objective for all three modeling tasks, rather than optimizing for recall, specificity, or accuracy first and then computing profit.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Do the predictive relationships and feature importances discovered on the Atlanticus dataset generalize to other credit card issuer populations or time periods, particularly given the dataset’s specific class distribution (75.2% non‑openers) and potential temporal economic context?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The study uses a single proprietary dataset (Atlanticus). The extreme class imbalance (75.2% class “never opened a card”) is noted but not discussed in terms of its generalizability. The economic context (U.S. consumer debt at “all‑time peak” as of Dec 2023) is mentioned, but the dataset’s temporal alignment is unclear. No external validation or discussion of dataset representativeness is provided.
- **Why unresolved:** The findings are based on one snapshot of data from one institution. The importance of inquiry features, the effectiveness of ADASYN, and the performance hierarchy of classifiers (Extra Trees for response, RF for risk) may be specific to this population’s demographics, the economic cycle at the time of data collection, or the issuer’s specific marketing practices.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replication studies on similar but independent datasets from different issuers or different time periods (e.g., pre‑ and post‑economic shifts). A formal assessment of transportability, perhaps using domain adaptation techniques or testing for covariate shift between the Atlanticus data and a target population.

## Limitations
- The final feature set after hierarchical clustering and VIF filtering is not reported, making exact replication impossible.
- Hyperparameter search spaces and validation strategies (e.g., cross‑validation folds) are unspecified, preventing verification of optimal model configurations.
- Potential data leakage if feature reduction (clustering, VIF) was performed on the full dataset before the train/validation split, which would inflate performance estimates.
- The paper does not explicitly define which class is considered "positive" for the risk model’s specificity calculation, introducing ambiguity in interpreting the 84.1% figure.

## Confidence
- **Preprocessing pipeline execution**: Medium
- **Model performance figures** (e.g., 79.1% recall, $5.3M profit): Low
- **Feature importance** (inquiry counts as key drivers): Medium
