# Hybrid Federated Learning for Noise-Robust Training

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.04483  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04483  
- Reference count: 14  
- Primary result: HFL achieves superior test accuracy at low SNR (-20 dB and -15 dB) compared to standalone FL or FD on MNIST classification.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces Hybrid Federated Learning (HFL), a framework that combines the strengths of federated learning (gradient‑based) and federated distillation (logit‑based) to improve robustness against noise in wireless channels. In HFL, each user equipment (UE) adaptively chooses to transmit either gradients or logits based on the instantaneous channel condition, as determined by the base station (BS). The BS uses a noise‑enhancement factor and Jenks clustering to assign UEs to either FL or FD groups, and then dynamically combines the received updates with a weight optimized per round via a damped Newton method. Experiments on MNIST show that HFL outperforms both FL and FD at very low SNRs, effectively balancing robustness and learning speed.

## Method Summary
HFL operates in a wireless system with K single‑antenna UEs and an N‑antenna BS. Each round begins with the BS estimating channel matrix H(t) and computing for every UE a post‑equalization noise enhancement factor q_k. Using Jenks optimization, the BS clusters q_k values into two groups; UEs with low q_k (reliable channels) are assigned to FL mode (transmit gradients), while those with high q_k (unreliable channels) are assigned to FD mode (transmit logits). The BS broadcasts the global model and the assignment. FL‑UEs compute gradients on their local data; FD‑UEs first perform one local SGD epoch on private data and then compute logits on a public dataset. All signals are preprocessed (pairing, standardization, ∞‑norm scaling) and transmitted over L time slots. The BS applies zero‑forcing equalization, reconstructs the individual gradient and logit vectors using stored metadata, and aggregates them with weights proportional to local dataset sizes. Two descent directions are formed: d_fl = -η1ˆg and d_fd = -η2∇Q, where Q is a knowledge‑distillation loss between the aggregated logits and the current model’s logits on the public data. The BS then solves a scalar optimization min_s L(s) = F(D_pub; θ(t) + σ(s)d_fl + (1-σ(s))d_fd) by a damped Newton method (30 iterations per round) to obtain s* and set α=σ(s*). The global model is updated as θ(t+1) = θ(t) + α d_fl + (1-α) d_fd. The framework’s convergence is analyzed under assumptions of strong convexity, bounded noise, and small logit errors, showing that HFL generalizes both FL and FD.

## Key Results
- At very low SNR (-20 dB, -15 dB), HFL achieves significantly higher test accuracy than both standalone FL and FD on MNIST.
- Adaptive UE clustering based on channel reliability effectively assigns high‑dimensional gradients to reliable links and low‑dimensional logits to unreliable links, reducing noise corruption.
- Per‑round dynamic weighting via Newton optimization further improves convergence and final accuracy, demonstrating the benefit of tailoring the update direction to current channel and aggregation conditions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Adaptive UE Clustering
Claim: Assigning UEs to transmit gradients (FL) or logits (FD) based on instantaneous channel reliability mitigates noise corruption by matching data type to channel conditions.  
Mechanism: The BS computes each UE’s post‑equalization noise enhancement factor `q_k = 1/(ρ[H(t)H(t)^H]_{kk})`. Using Jenks natural breaks optimization (one‑dimensional k‑means), it splits the sorted q_k values into two clusters. UEs with low q_k (reliable channels) are placed in the FL group (`I_k=0`); those with high q_k (poor channels) go to the FD group (`I_k=1`).  
Core assumption: CSI is accurate and stable over the round, and q_k reliably predicts the relative impact of noise on the two data types.  
Evidence anchors: Abstract states “user equipment (UE) adaptively transmits either gradients or logits based on channel conditions”; Section III‑C1 details the computation of q_k and the Jenks assignment.  
Break condition: If the channel coherence time is shorter than the time needed to acquire CSI and perform clustering, the assignment becomes outdated, negating the adaptation benefit.

### Mechanism 2: Adaptive Weight Selection
Claim: Dynamically optimizing the convex combination weight α between the FL gradient step and the FD distillation step each round tailors the update direction, improving convergence speed and final accuracy.  
Mechanism: After reconstructing the aggregated gradient ˆg(t) and logits ˆz(t), the BS defines two descent directions: d_fl = -η1ˆg(t) and d_fd = -η2∇θ Q(D_pub; θ(t), ˆz(t)), where Q is a KL‑divergence‑based distillation loss. The weight α is found by minimizing L(s) = F(D_pub; θ(t) + σ(s)d_fl + (1-σ(s))d_fd) over a scalar s via a damped Newton method (30 iterations per round). The final s* yields α = σ(s*) and is recomputed every communication round.  
Core assumption: The public dataset D_pub is sufficiently representative of the underlying task, and the loss L(s) is smooth enough for Newton to converge quickly.  
Evidence anchors: Abstract mentions “the base station (BS) selects the per‑round weights of FL and FD updates”; Section III‑C2 describes the damped Newton optimization.  
Break condition: If D_pub is small or biased, L(s) becomes a poor proxy for the true objective, leading to detrimental α values. Non‑convexity or poor initialization may also cause suboptimal convergence.

### Mechanism 3: Generalization of Convergence Bound
Claim: The HFL update rule encompasses both FL and FD, and under bounded noise (especially small logit error) the expected squared distance to optimum contracts linearly, providing a theoretical guarantee that includes both extremes.  
Mechanism: The parameter update (5) incorporates noise terms e_g (gradient noise) and e_z (logit noise). Assuming strong convexity, bounded gradient variance, bounded gradients, and Lipschitz continuity in logits, the convergence proof shows E[‖θ(t+1)-θ*‖²] ≤ (1-μ̄)E[‖θ(t)-θ*‖²] + A, where μ̄ depends on learning rates and α, and A aggregates noise and variance contributions. Setting α=1 yields FL bound; α=0 yields FD bound (when e_z=0).  
Core assumption: The logit noise e_z is small enough to permit a first‑order Taylor expansion (δ_Q ≈ e_z · derivative), and all noise terms are zero‑mean with bounded second moments (σ_g, σ_z).  
Evidence anchors: Abstract notes convergence derivation; Section III‑B and Proposition III.1 present the full proof with assumptions III.1–III.4.  
Break condition: In very low SNR regimes, e_z may not be “small”, violating Assumption III.4. The derived bound then does not strictly apply, although empirical results still show convergence.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map (plain text):  
UEs ──Uplink (noisy wireless)──> BS ──Downlink (control, model broadcast)──> UEs

Critical path:
1. BS obtains instantaneous CSI H(t).  
2. For each UE k, compute q_k based on post‑equalization noise.  
3. Apply Jenks optimization to binary‑assign each UE to FL (I_k=0) or FD (I_k=1).  
4. BS broadcasts global model θ(t) and assignments I_k to all UEs.  
5. FL‑UEs: compute gradient g_k on local data; FD‑UEs: perform one epoch local SGD then compute logits z_k on public data.  
6. All UEs preprocess their vectors (pairing, standardization, ∞‑norm scaling) into complex signals and transmit simultaneously.  
7. BS applies zero‑forcing equalization, decodes the aggregated signals, and reconstructs each UE’s gradient or logit using stored metadata.  
8. BS aggregates: ˆg = Σ (|D_k|/Σ|D|) g_k (FL group); ˆz = Σ (|D_k|/Σ|D|) z_k (FD group).  
9. Compute descent directions d_fl = -η1ˆg, d_fd = -η2∇Q; run damped Newton to find α.  
10. Update global model: θ(t+1) = θ(t) + α d_fl + (1-α) d_fd.

Design tradeoffs:
- Computational overhead: Jenks clustering (O(K log K)) and 30 Newton iterations add latency; must fit within channel coherence time.
- Public dataset dependence: FD logits and weight optimization require a fixed public set D_pub; its size and representativeness directly affect performance.
- CSI freshness: Clustering accuracy depends on CSI reflecting current channel conditions; outdated CSI leads to misassignment.

Failure signatures:
- Poor clustering: If q_k threshold q* is miscalibrated (e.g., due to noisy CSI), many UEs are assigned to the wrong group, causing gradient corruption or wasted robustness.
- Weight misselection: If Newton optimization converges to a local minimum or L(s) is not smooth, α may be suboptimal, slowing convergence.
- Zero‑padding mismatch: Incorrect signal length L relative to vector dimensions causes distortion during mapping, degrading reconstruction quality.

First 3 experiments:
1. Baseline comparison at low SNR: Run HFL, FL, FD at ρ = -20 dB and -15 dB on MNIST; verify HFL achieves higher final test accuracy and/or faster convergence.
2. Ablation of degrees of freedom: Compare four configurations: (a) adaptive clustering + adaptive weight (proposed), (b) reverse clustering + adaptive weight, (c) adaptive clustering + fixed weight (α=0.5), (d) reverse clustering + fixed weight. Measure accuracy gap to validate the contribution of each DoF.
3. Noise variance sensitivity: Sweep SNR from -25 dB to 0 dB for all schemes; plot accuracy vs. SNR. Confirm HFL’s advantage peaks at low SNRs and fades as noise diminishes.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Beamformer design beyond zero‑forcing**: The conclusion suggests exploring alternative beamformers (e.g., MMSE, regularized ZF) for over‑the‑air HFL.
- **Additional degrees of freedom**: Introducing parameters that jointly leverage channel conditions and local data distributions could further improve performance.
- **Convergence under low‑SNR with large logit errors**: The current analysis assumes small logit error; a tighter bound or empirical study across SNRs is needed to understand convergence when this assumption is violated.

## Limitations
- The paper lacks full specification of local training hyperparameters (number of SGD steps, batch size, local learning rate), hindering exact reproduction.
- The expression for q_k is ambiguous; it is unclear whether it uses the diagonal of HᴴH or of (HᴴH)⁻¹, which leads to different clustering outcomes.
- The theoretical convergence bound relies on a small logit error assumption that may be violated at the low SNRs where HFL shows empirical success, limiting the guarantee’s practical relevance.
