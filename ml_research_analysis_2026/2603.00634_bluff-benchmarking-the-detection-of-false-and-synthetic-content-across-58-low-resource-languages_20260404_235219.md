# BLUFF: Benchmarking the Detection of False and Synthetic Content across 58 Low-Resource Languages

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.00634
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00634
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** BLUFF is the first large-scale multilingual falsehood detection benchmark with 202K+ samples across 79 languages (58 low-resource), revealing up to 25.3% F1 degradation on low-resource languages and showing multilingual training reduces resource gaps to 0.1–7.9%.

## Executive Summary
BLUFF tackles the critical English-centric bias in false and synthetic content detection by introducing a comprehensive benchmark spanning 79 languages, with deliberate emphasis on 58 low-resource languages. It combines 122K+ human-written, fact-checked samples with 79K+ LLM-generated samples produced via the AXL-CoI agentic framework and filtered through the mPURIFY pipeline. Evaluations show that state-of-the-art detectors exhibit significant performance drops on low-resource languages, while multilingual joint training and models like S-BERT (LaBSE) achieve strong, balanced results. The benchmark enables equitable research and exposes vulnerabilities in both detector models and LLM safety mechanisms.

## Method Summary
The BLUFF dataset integrates two sources: human-written (HWT) samples from IFCN-certified fact-checkers and machine-generated samples from 19 multilingual LLMs using the AXL-CoI framework. AXL-CoI orchestrates sequential agents to apply 36 disinformation tactics to real news or legitimate edits to fake news, with full change logging. The mPURIFY pipeline employs asymmetric thresholds across 32 automatic metrics (e.g., BERTScore, COMET) to filter out defects, hallucinations, and manipulation failures, retaining 43.1% of generated samples. The final corpus is stratified by language, topic, and label, and used to fine-tune encoder models (AdamW, lr=2e-5) and evaluate decoder models zero-shot, with macro-F1 as the primary metric across binary and multiclass veracity/synthetic detection tasks.

## Key Results
- State-of-the-art detectors suffer up to **25.3% F1 degradation** on low-resource versus high-resource languages in cross-lingual transfer.
- **S-BERT (LaBSE)** achieves the best overall performance (97.2% average macro-F1) with minimal big-head/long-tail gaps (1.2–4.5 points).
- **Multilingual joint training** reduces the resource performance gap to **0.1–7.9%**, demonstrating the benefit of balanced training data.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The AXL-CoI agentic framework produces controlled, diverse multilingual false and real news samples while maintaining generation quality and providing transparent edit trails.
- **Mechanism:** AXL-CoI orchestrates specialized agents (Analyst, Manipulator/Editor, Translator, etc.) in sequential chains, each performing a defined transformation. For fake news, two of 36 disinformation tactics are injected at a chosen severity; for real news, legitimate editing techniques are applied. The framework uses structured JSON outputs to document every change, enabling downstream validation and filtering.
- **Core assumption:** LLMs can reliably follow long, structured prompts to execute multi-step transformations across languages without deviating from intended edits, and the change logs accurately reflect the modifications.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "We present AXL-CoI (Adversarial Cross-Lingual Agentic Chain-of-Interactions), a novel multi-agentic framework for controlled fake/real news generation." [section] Section 3.2 details the 10‑chain fake and 8‑chain real pipelines, dynamic persona impersonation (ADIS), and bidirectional translation. [corpus] BLUFF contains 30,240 unique fake news configurations and 144 real ones per language, with documented change logs and evaluation scores (Section 3.4, Table 30).
- **Break condition:** If LLMs drop or alter instructions, manipulations may be incomplete or invalid, leading to inconsistent mPURIFY scores and reduced benchmark reliability.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The mPURIFY quality filtering pipeline ensures dataset integrity by automatically verifying consistency, translation quality, manipulation presence, and structural correctness using multiple complementary metrics.
- **Mechanism:** mPURIFY combines standard automatic evaluation metrics (e.g., BERTScore, AlignScore, COMET) with LLM‑as‑judge scoring across 32 features. Asymmetric thresholds (e.g., ≥4.0 for real news consistency, ≤3.0 for fake) enforce high fidelity for real content while tolerating deliberate distortions in fake samples. Sequential filters remove defects, hallucinations, mistranslations, and manipulation failures.
- **Core assumption:** Automated metrics reliably approximate human judgment for multilingual quality and that the defined thresholds effectively separate genuine from manipulated text.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "...paired with mPURIFY, a quality filtering pipeline ensuring dataset integrity." [section] Section 3.4 and Appendix C describe the four‑stage filtering (defect identification, LLM‑AEM, S‑AEM, thresholding) and report pass rates (e.g., 97.9% combined). [corpus] After filtering, 78,443 samples remain (43.1% retention), with real news at 95.6% and fake at 84.3% (Table 16). Manipulation detection achieves near‑perfect class separation (real mean 1.04, fake 4.80) (Figure 17).
- **Break condition:** If thresholds are mis‑calibrated, genuine samples may be discarded or low‑quality fakes retained, contaminating the benchmark and harming downstream model training.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Comprehensive multilingual coverage—especially the inclusion of 58 low‑resource languages—provides the first large-scale testbed for evaluating cross‑lingual transfer and reduces detector performance gaps between high‑ and low‑resource settings.
- **Mechanism:** BLUFF balances the long‑tail distribution by actively targeting low-resource languages and using bidirectional translation (English ↔ X) to generate content. The dataset’s linguistic diversity (12 families, 9 scripts, 6 syntax types) enables systematic analysis of transfer patterns. Multilingual joint training on this balanced corpus minimizes catastrophic forgetting and negative transfer.
- **Core assumption:** Model performance disparities are primarily due to data scarcity during pretraining and fine‑tuning; providing sufficient labeled examples for low‑resource languages mitigates this.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "BLUFF uniquely covers both high‑resource 'big‑head' (20) and low‑resource 'long‑tail' (59) languages." [section] Section 4 shows up to 25.3 % F1 degradation on low‑resource languages in cross‑lingual transfer, but multilingual training reduces the gap to 0.1–7.9% (Table 4, Table 5). [corpus] The final dataset comprises 201,279 samples across 78 languages, with 58 long‑tail (e.g., Amharic, Guarani, Kurdish) and balanced topic/organization coverage (Section E, Table 52).
- **Break condition:** If low‑resource coverage remains insufficient or models still suffer from tokenization issues (e.g., CJK, Arabic), performance gaps will persist despite larger datasets.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Data sourcing -> AXL-CoI generation -> mPURIFY filtering -> Training/evaluation

- **Critical path:**  
  Raw news -> Reputation filtering -> AXL-CoI manipulation/editing -> mPURIFY quality checks -> Final BLUFF dataset -> Model training -> Cross-lingual & multilingual evaluation -> Analysis by family/script/syntax.  
  Key dependency: mPURIFY must complete before training; generation must cover all language‑tactic‑degree combinations.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  • Scale vs. quality: Only 43.1% of generated samples survive filtering; aggressive thresholds improve integrity but reduce size.  
  • Balance vs. feasibility: Long‑tail languages are intentionally oversampled to ensure minimum representation, but some still have few samples.  
  • Diversity vs. control: AXL-CoI permits 30,240 fake configurations; ensuring each appears at least once increases dataset size but may lead to uneven quality.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  – Very low retention for certain languages (e.g., < 50%) indicates poor LLM generation quality or translation brittleness.  
  – Large performance swings between similar language families suggest inadequate transfer or typological mismatch.  
  – Decoder models scoring below random baseline on multiclass tasks indicate zero‑shot limitations.  
  – High variation in mPURIFY’s manipulation scores between expected and evaluated degrees (Table 22) can compromise label reliability.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Binary veracity classification (Real/Fake) – Train encoder models multilingual and cross‑lingual; compute macro‑F1; compare big‑head vs. long‑tail gaps.
  2. Cross‑script transfer – Train on Latin‑script languages, test on Arabic/Ethiopic; measure performance drop and isolate tokenization effects.
  3. Synthetic text detection (Human/Machine) – Evaluate models’ ability to distinguish HWT, MGT, MTT, HAT; analyze confusion matrices for HAT↔MGT ambiguity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can falsehood detection performance be improved for low-resource languages, especially those with under‑represented scripts (e.g., Arabic, Ethiopic, Indic) and non‑SVO syntax (e.g., VSO, Creole)?
2. Can decoder‑only models achieve competitive performance on fine‑grained synthetic text detection tasks (e.g., 8‑class veracity, 4‑class authorship) through fine‑tuning or advanced prompting, rather than zero‑shot?
3. What techniques can mitigate the universal vulnerability of LLMs to the ADIS jailbreak attack across diverse model families and languages?
4. How can the BLUFF benchmark be extended to address its identified limitations: geographic bias in human‑written data, lack of temporal dynamics, and incomplete coverage of indigenous and Creole languages?
5. Does incorporating explicit manipulation tactic labels (the 36 disinformation tactics in BLUFF) into model training improve falsehood detection accuracy and enable cross‑lingual transfer of tactic understanding?

## Limitations
- Geographic bias: Human-written data is concentrated in Europe and South Asia due to IFCN fact‑checker distribution, underrepresenting African and indigenous languages.
- Temporal dynamics: The dataset captures a static snapshot, missing the evolving nature of disinformation campaigns over time.
- Incomplete coverage: Some regions and language groups (e.g., indigenous American languages beyond Guarani, Creole languages) remain underrepresented.
- Script/syntax gaps: Performance on under‑represented scripts (Arabic, Ethiopic) and non‑SVO syntax (VSO Arabic alone, Creoles) remains poor.

## Confidence
- **Dataset Scale & Coverage:** High – Directly supported by corpus statistics and language lists.
- **AXL-CoI & mPURIFY Mechanisms:** Medium – Detailed but depends on unreported components (ADIS prompt library, proprietary LLM versions in mPURIFY); core assumption of automated metrics approximating human judgment is partially validated.
- **Performance Claims & Gaps:** Medium – Based on provided splits and configurations, but dataset integrity hinges on mPURIFY thresholds not cross-validated with humans; long-tail sample counts may still be insufficient for robust learning.

## Next Checks
1. **Independent low-resource evaluation:** Train a standardized model (e.g., XLM-R) on BLUFF’s multilingual split and benchmark on a held-out, human-annotated low-resource test set to verify if reported 0.1–7.9% gap persists on out-of-distribution data.
2. **mPURIFY threshold audit:** Re-run the filtering pipeline using only open-source judges (e.g., Mistral, Llama) and compare sample retention rates, manipulation-score distributions, and downstream model performance against the reported proprietary-model results.
3. **Typology ablation:** Replicate cross-script transfer experiments while controlling for tokenization efficiency (e.g., using vocabulary size matched per script) to isolate whether performance gaps stem from data scarcity or fundamental model bias.
