# Decision-Focused Sequential Experimental Design: A Directional Uncertainty-Guided Approach

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.05340  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05340  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Proposes a directional uncertainty-guided sequential design that aligns with downstream decision loss (SPO), achieving fewer experiments and earlier stopping than decision-blind methods under an optimality gap assumption.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses sequential experimental design within the predict-then-optimize (PtO) paradigm, where the goal is to minimize a downstream decision loss (the SPO loss) rather than prediction error. Traditional decision-blind designs that maximize predictive accuracy are misaligned with this objective. The proposed method, IWSD-DU, quantifies predictive uncertainty using only the *direction* of cost vectors—by normalizing predictions to unit vectors—thereby avoiding costly optimization oracle calls and focusing sampling on variations that actually change the downstream optimizer’s decision. The approach enjoys strong consistency and convergence guarantees. Theoretically, under an optimality gap assumption, it attains an earlier stopping time than decision-blind designs. Empirical validation on a cardiovascular diagnosis task and an LLM job allocation problem shows it reaches a target SPO loss with substantially fewer experiments, demonstrating its efficiency and decision-focused advantage.

## Method Summary
The algorithm operates on a finite hypothesis class **H**. It first preprocesses **H** to satisfy a *directional margin condition* (Assumption 1) by replacing any degenerate predictions (those lying near decision boundary cones) with non-degenerate alternatives, ensuring a lower bound η on normalized distance to degeneracy. At each iteration *t*, it computes directional uncertainty for each design *x* as the maximum disagreement in normalized predictions among the current confidence set **H<sub>t-1</sub>**: *p<sub>t,i</sub>* = max<sub>h₁,h₂∈H<sub>t-1</sub></sub> || h₁(x)/||h₁(x)|| – h₂(x)/||h₂(x)|| ||. Designs are sampled with probability proportional to *p<sub>t,i</sub>*. Upon observing a label, the algorithm updates an importance-weighted empirical SPO loss: for each hypothesis *h*, the loss contribution from design *x* is scaled by μ(x)/π<sub>t,i</sub> (inverse sampling probability). A confidence set **H<sub>t</sub>** is then updated by eliminating hypotheses whose reweighted loss exceeds the best loss plus a time-decaying slack *r<sub>t</sub>*. The loop stops when all directional uncertainties vanish (∑ p<sub>t,i</sub> = 0) or only optimal hypotheses remain.

## Key Results
- Reduces the number of experiments needed to achieve a target SPO loss compared to decision-blind designs (ℓ₂ uncertainty or squared loss).
- Provides finite-time consistency and convergence guarantees under a directional margin condition (Assumption 1) and an optimality gap (Assumption 2).
- Achieves earlier stopping than decision-blind benchmarks when a uniform positive gap in SPO risk separates optimal from suboptimal predictors.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Normalizing predictions to unit vectors before measuring uncertainty better aligns the experimental design criterion with downstream SPO loss compared to using raw ℓ₂ differences.
- **Mechanism:** SPO loss is scale-invariant (Fact 1: *w*(αĉ) = *w*(ĉ)), so only the *direction* of the predicted cost vector affects the optimizer. The proposed directional uncertainty metric discards scale information, focusing exclusively on directional disagreement that can change decisions. This avoids wasting samples on uncertainty that does not impact the downstream optimizer.
- **Core assumption:** SPO loss’s scale invariance holds for linear optimization.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] "quantifies predictive uncertainty using normalized (direction-only) cost vectors"  
  [section 2.1] Introduces Metric 3 (directional) vs. Metric 1 (ℓ₂); Figure 5 shows directional uncertainty correctly identifies designs where predictions lead to different optimizers.
- **Break condition:** If the downstream decision problem is not scale-invariant (e.g., nonlinear objectives), the normalization step could discard relevant information.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The importance-weighted sequential design (IWSD-DU) using directional uncertainty produces an unbiased SPO risk estimate and converges to the optimal predictor under a directional margin condition.
- **Mechanism:** Designs are sampled with probability proportional to directional uncertainty. The observed labels are re-weighted by the inverse sampling probability in the loss calculation. This re-weighting yields an unbiased estimator of the SPO risk. The confidence set **H<sub>t</sub>** shrinks based on this re-weighted loss, eventually isolating the optimal predictor.
- **Core assumption:** **Assumption 1 (Directional Margin Condition):** ∃η > 0 such that for all *h ∈ H*, *x ∈ X*, ν<sub>S</sub>(h(x)) / ||h(x)|| ≥ η. This ensures a Lipschitz-like property of SPO loss w.r.t. directional differences (Lemma 1).
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [section 2.3] Formal statement of Assumption 1 and Lemma 1 proving |ℓ<sub>SPO</sub>(h₁, c) – ℓ<sub>SPO</sub>(h₂, c)| ≤ L · || normalized difference ||.  
  [section 4.1] Proposition 2 shows any finite hypothesis class can be pre-processed (Algorithm 2) to satisfy Assumption 1.
- **Break condition:** If η is too small, Lipschitz constant L becomes large, degrading finite-sample convergence rates. Extreme degeneracy (predictions near cone boundaries) violates the assumption.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Under an optimality gap assumption, the directional uncertainty design achieves an earlier stopping time than decision-blind designs (ℓ₂ uncertainty with squared loss).
- **Mechanism:** The optimality gap (Assumption 2) guarantees a non-zero minimum difference in SPO risk between optimal and suboptimal predictors. Directional uncertainty focuses samples on the finite set of "decision-relevant" directions, while decision-blind methods may waste samples on scale variations irrelevant to the optimizer. This leads to faster elimination of all suboptimal hypotheses.
- **Core assumption:** **Assumption 2 (Optimality Gap):** ∃α > 0 such that R<sub>SPO</sub>(h) – R<sub>SPO</sub><sup>*</sup> ≥ α for all *h ∈ H \ H<sup>*</sup>*.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [section 3.1] Theorem 3 proves IWSD-DU converges to H<sup>*</sup> in finite time, whereas a decision-blind benchmark may fail to eliminate suboptimal predictors with positive probability.  
  [section 4.2] Proposition 3 constructs an example satisfying Conditions 1–3 that imply Assumption 2.
- **Break condition:** If the optimality gap α is arbitrarily small (e.g., continuous hypothesis classes or near-degenerate predictions), decision-blind methods may asymptotically match directional methods, eroding the finite-sample advantage.

## Foundational Learning
- **Predict-then-Optimize (PtO):** A framework where a predictor’s output is used as input to a downstream optimization. *Why needed:* The entire problem is framed within PtO; performance is measured by decision loss (SPO), not prediction error. *Quick check:* Why might a predictor with lower ℓ₂ error yield higher SPO loss? (Answer: SPO loss is scale-invariant; only directional accuracy matters.)
- **SPO Loss and Scale Invariance:** ℓ<sub>SPO</sub>(ĉ, c) = cᵀ(w*(ĉ) – w*(c)). *Why needed:* It is the target metric; Fact 1 (w*(αĉ)=w*(ĉ)) justifies normalizing predictions. *Quick check:* If you multiply a predicted cost vector by 100, how does SPO loss change? (Answer: Unchanged.)
- **Distance to Degeneracy (ν<sub>S</sub>):** For a cost vector ĉ, ν<sub>S</sub>(ĉ) is the minimum ℓ₂ distance from ĉ to the boundary of the normal cone of the feasible set S at the optimal solution w*(ĉ). *Why needed:* Central to Assumption 1; measures robustness of the optimizer to perturbations. *Quick check:* What does a large ν<sub>S</sub>(ĉ) imply about w*(ĉ)? (Answer: Small perturbations to ĉ won’t change the optimizer.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**
Hypothesis class H -> directional uncertainty p<sub>t</sub> -> sampling distribution π<sub>t</sub> -> sample design x<sub>t</sub> -> observe label c<sub>t</sub> -> reweighted loss update -> confidence set H<sub>t</sub> update -> stop if ∑ p<sub>t,i</sub>=0.

**Critical path:**
1. For each design *x<sub>i</sub>*, compute *p<sub>t,i</sub>* = max<sub>h₁,h₂∈H<sub>t-1</sub></sub> || normalized h₁(x<sub>i</sub>) – normalized h₂(x<sub>i</sub>) ||.
2. Normalize to get sampling probabilities: π<sub>t,i</sub> = *p<sub>t,i</sub>* / ∑<sub>j</sub> *p<sub>t,j</sub>*.
3. Sample design *x<sub>t</sub>* ∼ π<sub>t</sub>; query true cost *c<sub>t</sub>*.
4. For each hypothesis *h*, update cumulative reweighted loss:  
   hat{l}<sub>t</sub>(h) = (1/t) ∑<sub>s=1</sub><sup>t</sup> [ μ(x<sub>s</sub>)/π<sub>s,i<sub>s</sub></sub> · ℓ<sub>SPO</sub>(h(x<sub>s</sub>), c<sub>s</sub>) ].
5. Find best hypothesis *h<sub>t</sub>* = argmin<sub>h∈H<sub>t-1</sub></sub> hat{l}<sub>t</sub>(h); denote minimum hat{l}<sub>t,</sub>*.
6. Update confidence set: H<sub>t</sub> = { h ∈ H<sub>t-1</sub> : hat{l}<sub>t</sub>(h) ≤ hat{l}<sub>t,</sub>* + r<sub>t</sub> }.
7. Update slack: r<sub>t+1</sub> = r<sub>t</sub> · sqrt( t/(t+1) · log(2(t+1))/log(2t) ).
8. If ∑<sub>i</sub> *p<sub>t,i</sub>* = 0, stop; else *t* ← *t*+1.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Directional vs. ℓ₂ uncertainty:* Directional avoids costly optimization oracles and aligns with SPO but requires Assumption 1; ℓ₂ is simpler but decision-blind and may waste samples on scale variations.
- *SPO vs. ℓ₂ loss for elimination:* Using SPO loss directly in the confidence set update is necessary for the theoretical advantage under an optimality gap, though it may be non-smooth; ℓ₂ loss is smooth but misaligned.

**Failure signatures:**
- **Degenerate predictions:** Many *h(x)* lie near cone boundaries → ν<sub>S</sub>(h(x)) ≈ 0 → violates Assumption 1 → Lipschitz constant L explodes → poor convergence.
- **No optimality gap:** Suboptimal predictors have SPO risk arbitrarily close to optimal → Theorem 3’s advantage vanishes → decision-blind may perform similarly.
- **Numerical instability:** ||h(x)|| ≈ 0 during normalization → divide-by-near-zero → add small epsilon (e.g., 1e-12).
- **Degenerate feasible set S:** S not polyhedral or optimizers not unique → definitions of w* and ν<sub>S</sub> ill-defined.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Synthetic validation:** Construct a 2D linear optimization problem with known normal cones. Verify that directional uncertainty samples designs where predictions fall into different cones (changing the optimizer), while ℓ₂ uncertainty samples based on scale magnitude.
2. **Assumption 1 check:** Preprocess a hypothesis class using Algorithm 2. Compute min<sub>h,x</sub> ν<sub>S</sub>(h(x))/||h(x)|| and confirm it exceeds a chosen η > 0. If not, adjust preprocessing aggressiveness.
3. **Stopping time comparison:** Under a controlled distribution satisfying Assumption 2 (e.g., from Proposition 3), run IWSD-DU and a decision-blind benchmark (ℓ₂ uncertainty + ℓ₂ loss). Compare iteration counts until H<sub>T</sub> ⊆ H<sup>*</sup>. Expect IWSD-DU to stop strictly earlier.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How do the theoretical guarantees (consistency, convergence rates) of IWSD-DU extend to infinite or nonparametric hypothesis classes?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper mentions that for infinite *H*, the analysis could extend by replacing |*H*| with a covering number, but detailed guarantees are not provided.
- **Why unresolved:** The proofs rely on a finite hypothesis class; for infinite classes, uniform convergence and confidence set construction require additional arguments (e.g., Rademacher complexities) that are not developed.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Derive explicit finite-sample bounds for IWSD-DU under infinite hypothesis classes using metric entropy or Rademacher complexities, and validate via simulations with kernelized predictors.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Under what conditions does the optimality gap (Assumption 2) hold in practice, and can the algorithm achieve earlier stopping without a uniform positive gap?
- **Basis in paper:** Theorem 3 requires Assumption 2 for the earlier stopping time guarantee; the paper provides examples where it holds but notes empirical performance even without it.
- **Why unresolved:** The assumption may fail when suboptimal predictors yield decision losses arbitrarily close to optimal. Understanding its necessity and robustness is crucial for real-world applications.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Empirically measure the distribution of decision risk gaps across datasets; theoretically analyze the algorithm’s behavior under small or vanishing gaps, possibly deriving adaptive stopping rules.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can the directional uncertainty approach be extended to nonlinear or discrete decision problems, and what would be an appropriate decision-focused uncertainty metric in those settings?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper explicitly restricts to linear optimization (Section 1.1), and the directional metric relies on linearity (scale invariance, normal cones).
- **Why unresolved:** For nonlinear decision problems, the SPO loss does not have the same scale-invariant property, and the geometry of the feasible set is more complex.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Extend the framework to a specific class of nonlinear decision problems (e.g., logistic regression with decision thresholds or a small integer program) and propose a new uncertainty measure that captures decision-relevant variations, supported by theory and experiments.

## Limitations
- **Scale Invariance Scope:** The core justification for directional uncertainty (Fact 1) is strictly valid only for linear SPO loss with a linear optimizer. The paper does not establish that the approach remains effective or theoretically sound for nonlinear or discrete decision problems (e.g., integer programs), where scale changes *can* alter decisions.
- **Directional Margin Practicality:** While Algorithm 2 provides a preprocessing fix, the required margin parameter η depends on the geometry of the feasible set *S* and hypothesis predictions. In high dimensions or with poorly conditioned sets, achieving a non-trivial η may force aggressive preprocessing that alters predictor behavior and harms generalization.
- **Optimality Gap Fragility:** The theoretical stopping time advantage hinges on a strictly positive risk gap α. This is non-trivial for continuous hypothesis classes (e.g., neural networks) or problems where many predictors yield near-identical decisions. Empirical results use finite, pre-generated pools where gaps exist, but this may not generalize to standard DFL training loops.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (Directional Alignment):** High for linear SPO problems (scale invariance mathematically sound); Low for extension to nonlinear PtO.
- **Mechanism 2 (Consistency under Assumption 1):** Medium—theory rigorous but practical satisfaction of directional margin without excessive preprocessing is uncertain; depends heavily on geometric condition ν<sub>S</sub>/||h(x)|| bounded away from zero.
- **Mechanism 3 (Earlier Stopping under Assumption 2):** Medium—proof clear, but confidence in universal identifiability of a non-zero gap in real sequential design is lower; advantage may vanish in dense, continuous hypothesis spaces.

## Next Checks
1. **Test Nonlinear Extension:** Apply IWSD-DU to a PtO problem with a nonlinear objective (e.g., quadratic cost or a small integer program). Compare stopping time and final SPO loss against a decision-blind design. A significant performance drop would confirm the linearity boundary.
2. **Measure η Sensitivity:** On the cardiovascular dataset, compute the empirical min<sub>h,x</sub> ν<sub>S</sub>(h(x))/||h(x)|| before and after preprocessing (Algorithm 2). Plot the distribution. A long tail near zero post-preprocessing indicates fragile margin satisfaction and potential inconsistency.
3. **Optimality Gap Audit:** After IWSD-DU converges on the LLM task, compute empirical SPO risk for all surviving hypotheses in the final confidence set H<sub>T</sub>. Report the range (max – min). A near-zero range suggests stopping was driven by design exhaustion (∑ p<sub>t,i</sub>=0) rather than a true optimality gap, questioning Assumption 2’s role.
