# Plasticity vs. Rigidity: The Impact of Low-Rank Adapters on Reasoning on a Micro-Budget

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.06677  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06677  
- **Reference count**: 6  
- **Primary result**: High-rank (r=256) LoRA adapters unlock plasticity in instruction-tuned models, achieving 40.0% Pass@1 on AIME24 (+11.1% absolute) for DeepScaleR-1.5B; low-rank (r=8) adapters fail across models; high-rank adapters cause degradation in math specialists.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether small language models (≤1.5B) can develop strong reasoning capabilities under extreme computational constraints (single A40 GPU, <24 hours) using Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) and Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA). The core finding reveals a crucial interaction between adapter capacity and model initialization. High-rank LoRA (r=256) grants generalist instruction‑tuned models the plasticity needed to elongate reasoning chains and maximize reward, leading to state‑of‑the‑art gains on math competition benchmarks. Conversely, low‑rank LoRA (r=8) consistently fails to capture the optimization dynamics. Critically, heavily math‑aligned specialist models suffer catastrophic performance collapse with high‑rank adapters, indicating that noisy, low‑budget RL updates act as destructive interference for policies already near a task‑specific optimum.

## Method Summary
The study systematically evaluated small models (1–1.5B) in a micro‑budget regime: ≤300 GRPO steps on a single NVIDIA A40 within 24 hours, updating only LoRA parameters. The independent variable was LoRA rank (r=8 vs r=256) across three model categories: generalist instruction‑tuned (e.g., Qwen2.5‑1.5B‑Instruct), math specialists (Qwen2.5‑Math‑1.5B), and an RL‑optimized baseline (DeepScaleR‑1.5B). Training maximized a verifiable reward combining format adherence (weight 0.2) and answer accuracy. Performance was measured on AIME24, AMC23, and MATH500 using Pass@k, while monitoring reward curves, response lengths, and policy entropy. This design isolates how adapter capacity influences the stability‑plasticity trade‑off under severely constrained RL.

## Key Results
- High‑rank LoRA (r=256) enabled DeepScaleR‑1.5B to reach 40.0% Pass@1 on AIME24 (11.1% absolute gain over baseline) and 70.0% Pass@16.
- Low‑rank LoRA (r=8) failed for all models, showing negligible reward improvement and no meaningful behavioral change.
- Math specialist models (e.g., Qwen2.5‑Math‑1.5B) exhibited reward instability, sharp entropy reduction (mode collapse), and degraded accuracy when trained with r=256.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: High‑Rank Adapters Enable Plasticity in Generalists
High‑rank LoRA (r=256) provides enough trainable capacity to approximate complex gradient updates from RLVR, allowing generalist models to explore new reasoning paths (e.g., longer CoT) and maximize reward. Evidence: monotonic reward curves for generalists with r=256 (Section 3.1); DeepScaleR‑1.5B’s +11.1% absolute Pass@1 (Section 3.4). Break condition: if the base model lacks strong reasoning priors (e.g., Llama‑3.2‑1B) or training steps are too few, entropy collapse may occur without gains.

### Mechanism 2: Low‑Rank Adapters Lack Capacity to Capture Optimization Dynamics
Low‑rank adapters (r=8) mechanically restrict policy deviation from the pre‑trained manifold, anchoring the model to its initialization and preventing internalization of RL gradients. Evidence: all models show poor or no reward growth with r=8 (Abstract, Section 3.1); relative entropy change is minimal (Section 3.5). Break condition: ranks r≥64 may become effective; simpler tasks or less noisy rewards might allow lower ranks to succeed.

### Mechanism 3: Destructive Interference in Specialist Models
Specialists have converged to a narrow, high‑reward manifold. Noisy low‑budget RL updates with high‑rank adapters perturb the policy away from this optimum; the fragile manifold collapses into low‑entropy safe behaviors (e.g., short responses). Evidence: reward instability and degradation in Qwen2.5‑Math‑1.5B (Abstract, Section 3.1); accuracy drop at Pass@1 but slight improvements at Pass@8/16 (Section 3.4); sharp entropy reduction (Section 3.5). Break condition: more conservative RL (e.g., Dr. GRPO) or longer training might prevent collapse; less‑optimized specialists may behave like generalists.

## Foundational Learning
- **Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)**  
  *Why needed*: RLVR optimizes models using a reward that verifies final answer correctness and format, eliminating reliance on human preference labels.  
  *Quick check*: How does RLVR differ from traditional RL, and why is a verifiable reward essential for mathematical reasoning tasks?

- **Low‑Rank Adaptation (LoRA)**  
  *Why needed*: LoRA is the parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning method used to adapt models under strict memory limits, central to the micro‑budget experiments and the plasticity/rigidity trade‑off.  
  *Quick check*: In LoRA, what does the rank parameter control, and how does it balance memory usage with adaptational capacity?

- **Chain‑of‑Thought (CoT) Reasoning**  
  *Why needed*: Models are trained to generate CoT with `<think>` tags; changes in CoT length are a key mechanism by which RLVR shapes reasoning behavior.  
  *Quick check*: Why might elongating the chain‑of‑thought improve reward in this setup, and what are the risks of excessive length?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Base Models (Qwen2.5‑1.5B‑Instruct, Llama‑3.2‑1B, DeepScaleR‑1.5B, Qwen2.5‑Math‑1.5B, Qwen3‑0.6B) → LoRA adapters (inserted into Q, K, V, O, and MLP layers with rank r) → GRPO training (group size k=8, no value network) → Reward (0.2*format + accuracy) → Evaluation (MATH500, AIME24, AMC23).

### Critical Path
1. Load base model and tokenizer; wrap transformer layers with LoRA at chosen rank r.
2. Tokenize Open‑RS training prompts (7000 problems) with maximum response length 3584 tokens.
3. For each step: per prompt sample k=8 responses, compute per‑token rewards (format + accuracy), calculate GRPO loss, update only LoRA parameters.
4. Periodically evaluate zero‑shot Pass@1 on MATH500 validation.
5. After 24 hours or convergence, select checkpoint with best validation Pass@1.
6. Run final evaluation on held‑out benchmarks (AIME24/25, AMC23) with Pass@8/Pass@16.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Rank r**: higher increases capacity/plasticity but raises memory and risk of collapse in specialists; lower is safer but may not learn.
- **Group size k**: larger improves baseline estimation but linearly scales memory; fixed k=8 balances memory.
- **Token limit**: 3584 tokens balances memory and CoT exploration; too low stifles, too high risks OOM.
- **Learning rate & alpha**: fixed across models for budget; tuning could mitigate collapse.
- **Reward weighting**: format weight 0.2; changing shifts balance between formatting and accuracy.

### Failure Signatures
- Training reward drops or oscillates wildly (especially specialists with high r).
- Response length contracts sharply or becomes unstable.
- Validation MATH500 accuracy declines after initial steps.
- Entropy drops significantly without corresponding reward gain (mode collapse).
- Generalist models show no reward improvement with low r.

### First 3 Experiments
1. Train Qwen2.5‑1.5B‑Instruct with r=8 → expect flat reward/accuracy (low‑rank generalist baseline).
2. Train Qwen2.5‑1.5B‑Instruct with r=256 → expect reward growth, length increase, validation gains (high‑rank generalist).
3. Train Qwen2.5‑Math‑1.5B with r=256 → expect reward instability, accuracy drop, length contraction (high‑rank specialist collapse).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Does a brief supervised fine‑tuning (SFT) phase before RLVR warm‑start generalist models, mitigating the cold‑start problem under micro‑budget constraints?  
2. Can alternative RL algorithms (e.g., Dr. GRPO, DAPO) prevent the destructive interference seen in specialist models when using high‑rank LoRA under micro‑budget RL?  
3. Does the plasticity vs. rigidity dichotomy generalize to larger language models (>1.5B parameters)?  
4. What is the scaling law of high‑rank LoRA (r=256) compared to full‑parameter fine‑tuning for reasoning alignment across model sizes?

## Limitations
- Compute constraints (single A40, 24h) may restrict convergence and hyperparameter exploration.
- Only small models (≤1.5B) were evaluated; findings may not scale to larger architectures.
- Reward design is fixed (format weight 0.2, binary accuracy); sensitivity and optimal weighting remain unknown.

## Confidence
- **High‑Rank Adapters Enable Plasticity in Generalists**: High confidence (monotonic reward growth, +11.1% absolute Pass@1 on AIME24).
- **Low‑Rank Adapters Lack Capacity**: High confidence (consistent failure across models with r=8, minimal entropy change).
- **Destructive Interference in Specialists**: Medium confidence (supported by instability/collapse in Qwen2.5‑Math‑1.5B, but limited specialist variety).

## Next Checks
1. **Rank sweep study**: Train each model with intermediate ranks (r=16,32,64,128) to identify precise capacity thresholds separating success and failure.
2. **Cross‑domain evaluation**: Replicate experiments on non‑math reasoning tasks (e.g., logical deduction, commonsense QA) to test generalizability.
3. **Algorithmic robustness**: Replace GRPO with a more stable RL algorithm (e.g., PPO with value network) and/or increase training steps to assess whether specialists can avoid collapse.
