# APEX: Probing Neural Networks via Activation Perturbation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.03586
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03586
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** APEX introduces activation perturbation to probe neural network representations, enabling a transition from sample-dependent to model-dependent behavior and revealing training-induced biases such as backdoors.

## Executive Summary
APEX is a novel framework for probing neural networks by directly perturbing hidden activations during inference. Unlike input or parameter perturbations, activation perturbation accesses internal representational structures. The paper shows theoretically that adding Gaussian noise to activations induces a transition: as noise magnitude increases, predictions shift from being sample-dependent to model-dependent. In the small-noise regime, APEX yields an “escape noise” metric that correlates with sample regularity (e.g., memorization). In the large-noise regime, stationary output distributions expose training-induced biases such as backdoor attacks, which concentrate probability on a target class. Extensive experiments on CIFAR‑10/100, ImageNet, and multiple architectures (ResNet, VGG, DeiT) validate these findings.

## Method Summary
APEX performs inference‑time Gaussian noise injection into ReLU activations of a fixed model. For each input, multiple stochastic forward passes (Monte Carlo) are run. Two regimes are analyzed: (1) small noise – compute the “escape noise” per sample, defined as the minimal noise scale σ where the top‑1 class probability falls below 0.5; (2) large noise – compute normalized entropy of the averaged output distribution across many inputs, which becomes input‑independent at high σ. The implementation requires selecting a noise scale and Monte Carlo sample count T. Models (benign or backdoored) are trained with standard procedures; APEX operates without altering parameters.

## Key Results
- Activation perturbation theoretically transitions behavior from sample‑dependent to model‑dependent as noise magnitude increases, generalizing input/parameter perturbations as constrained special cases.
- Escape noise (small σ) strongly correlates with memorization and consistency scores and distinguishes models trained with structured vs. random labels.
- Under large σ, backdoored models exhibit near‑zero normalized entropy (concentration on target class), a signal not reliably revealed by input or parameter perturbations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
- **Mechanism 1: Transition via noise decomposition**  
  Adding Gaussian noise to activations decomposes the forward signal into a noise‑driven term (σUvL) and an input‑dependent residual (rℓ(x;σ)). As σ grows, the residual’s influence vanishes due to boundedness assumptions (Lipschitz networks, bounded inputs), causing predictions to be governed by the model’s linear readout rather than the specific input.
- **Mechanism 2: Escape noise for sample regularity**  
  In the small‑noise regime, minor activation noise only alters predictions for samples in fragile regions of representation space. Escape noise (minimum σ causing predicted probability ≤ 0.5) measures local sensitivity. It correlates with memorization scores and decreases monotonically as training randomness increases, because random labels fragment representations.
- **Mechanism 3: Stationary distributions reveal bias**  
  At large σ, the noise term dominates and the output distribution becomes input‑independent, determined solely by the model’s readout matrix UvL. This stationary distribution reflects how representation space is allocated across classes. Backdoored models show near‑zero normalized entropy because the attack biases internal representations strongly toward the target class.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Pretrained model (fixed parameters) → Noise injector (adds Gaussian noise to selected activations) → Monte Carlo estimator (runs T stochastic forward passes) → Output distribution analyzer (computes empirical class probabilities, escape noise thresholds, normalized entropy)
- **Critical path:**  
  Input x → noise injected at hidden layers → stochastic forward passes → prediction histogram per input → (aggregated across inputs) → sample‑level escape noise or model‑level stationary distribution
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Noise magnitude σ: small (∼0.01–0.2) for sample regularity; large (∼10–100) for model‑level biases. Must be tuned per dataset/architecture.
  - Layer selection: paper uses all layers; single‑layer injection shows deeper layers yield higher entropy dispersion. Tradeoff between computational cost and sensitivity.
  - Noise type: Gaussian assumed; Laplace/uniform yield similar stationary distributions, but Gaussian is simplest.
  - T (Monte Carlo samples): 1,000 for small datasets; 100 for ImageNet due to cost. More samples reduce estimation variance.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Non‑monotonic escape noise vs. σ: indicates poor noise scaling or unstable model.
  - No entropy difference between benign/backdoored models under large σ: could mean attack failed, noise insufficient, or architecture‑specific (e.g., DeiT).
  - Input perturbation mimicking APEX behavior: suggests model is linear or has extremely wide decision regions; unlikely for deep nets.
  - Unbounded residual in Theorem 3.1: indicates numerical instability or non‑Lipschitz activations (e.g., unbounded ReLU variants).
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Two‑regime validation:** For a fixed model, vary σ from small to large and plot (a) average prediction success rate vs. σ (should drop for irregular samples) and (b) normalized entropy of input‑averaged output distribution vs. σ (should drop to near zero for backdoored models but stay > 0 for benign).
  2. **Semantic alignment check:** Train a model where two classes share identical input distribution. Under activation perturbation, the prediction probability for the reassigned class should increase monotonically with σ, while input/parameter perturbations show no consistent trend.
  3. **Backdoor detection benchmark:** Train/evaluate backdoored models (BadNets, Blended, etc.) and benign models on CIFAR‑10/100. Compute average normalized entropy at large σ (e.g., σ = 100). Expect near‑zero entropy for backdoored, > 0.2 for benign.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- What underlying geometric property of the representation space does escape noise quantify?
- Which model‑ or training‑specific factors control the noise scale at which predictions become input‑independent in APEX?
- Can the low normalized entropy of stationary output distributions under large activation noise serve as a universal, reliable indicator of backdoor attacks?
- Does APEX’s two‑regime behavior and the observed large‑noise bias collapse generalize to non‑vision domains (e.g., language models, graph nets) and non‑ReLU activations?

## Limitations
- Theoretical transition relies on bounded inputs and Lipschitz layer transformations; violations (unbounded data, non‑Lipschitz activations) may break the residual bound.
- Noise magnitude σ is dataset‑ and architecture‑dependent with no universal scaling rule, limiting out‑of‑the‑box application.
- Escape noise correlations with sample regularity are demonstrated on a narrow set of models; behavior for poorly calibrated or extremely high‑capacity networks remains unknown.
- Large‑noise entropy signals for backdoor detection could be confounded by other biases (e.g., spurious correlations) and show attenuated performance on transformers.
- Exclusive use of Gaussian noise is not fully justified; other distributions might yield different sensitivities.

## Confidence
- **Theoretical transition (sample‑ to model‑dependent behavior):** High  
- **Escape noise as a sample‑regularity metric:** Medium  
- **Large‑noise stationary distributions revealing biases (e.g., backdoors):** Medium
