# PARHAF, a human-authored corpus of clinical reports for fictitious patients in French

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.20494
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20494
- **Reference count**: 0
- **Primary result**: 6,185 public clinical reports (CC‑BY) for 4,254 fictitious patients; total corpus of 7,394 reports for 5,009 patients with 1,209 embargoed; covers 18 specialties and includes dedicated subsets for oncology, infectious diseases, and coding.

## Executive Summary
PARHAF is a large-scale corpus of clinical reports written for entirely fictitious patients, designed to overcome the privacy barriers that prevent sharing of real French medical records. The project engaged 104 medical residents across 18 specialties to produce realistic discharge summaries, operative reports, and other document types. Clinical scenarios were sampled from the French National Health Data System (SNDS) to mirror the epidemiological distribution of hospitalizations, while structured templates ensured consistency. Each report underwent peer review and automated validation, resulting in a low 3.6% rejection rate. The final dataset comprises 7,394 documents for 5,009 patients, with 6,185 documents (4,254 patients) released under a CC‑BY license and the remainder temporarily embargoed to safeguard future benchmark integrity.

## Method Summary
The corpus construction began with epidemiological sampling: frequencies of DRGs, primary ICD‑10 diagnoses, age, sex, and length‑of‑stay groups were extracted from SNDS. To balance common and rare conditions, a square‑root transformation was applied to raw frequencies and capped at a maximum probability of 0.1%, after which the distribution was renormalized. Operational adjustments were made to match resident availability. Scenarios were assigned to medical residents, who authored reports following specialty‑specific templates via an online editor. A second resident peer‑reviewed each submission for clinical coherence and adherence. Automated checks verified document counts, length‑of‑stay consistency, and presence of required codes. The final product is distributed as a JSON index linking patient metadata to plain‑text files by specialty, with an accompanying Hugging Face Parquet dataset for convenient loading.

## Key Results
- Broad clinical coverage: 18 specialties, with a general component reflecting hospitalization distributions and four specialized subsets (oncology biomarkers, treatment response, infectious diseases, diagnostic coding).
- Scalable release: 6,185 documents (4,254 patients) publicly available under CC‑BY; 1,209 documents (755 patients) temporarily embargoed to prevent large language model contamination.
- Quality control: human peer review and automated sanity checks rejected 3.6% of submissions and corrected an additional 120 documents.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Anonymous synthetic data
- **Claim:** Reports about fictitious patients are legally and ethically shareable because they contain no real personal health information.
- **Mechanism:** All patient details (identifiers, demographics, diagnoses, procedures) are invented and do not correspond to any real individual, thus avoiding re‑identification risk and circumventing privacy regulations that bind real medical records.
- **Core assumption:** Fictional scenarios are realistically plausible yet statistically disconnected from any actual patient record.
- **Evidence:** [abstract] “PARHAF comprises expert‑authored clinical reports describing realistic yet entirely fictitious patient cases, making it anonymous and freely shareable by design.” [section 2.2.1] “Patient name was randomly fixed.” The JSON metadata stores only fictional identifiers (`id`, `local_id`) and paths to external text files with no embedded PHI.
- **Break condition:** Inadvertent incorporation of too many specific details (e.g., a rare combination of symptoms that matches a real patient) could raise re‑identification probability and violate anonymity‑by‑design.

### Mechanism 2: Epidemiological sampling for representativeness
- **Claim:** Sampling scenarios from SNDS distributions yields a corpus that approximates the real‑world spectrum of French hospitalizations.
- **Mechanism:** SNDS provides exhaustive nationwide hospitalization claims. Frequencies of DRG‑ICD‑10‑age‑sex‑LOS combinations are used to create a target distribution. A square‑root transformation (`pi = min(pmax, √fi) / Σ min(pmax, √fj)`) prevents over‑representation of extremely common conditions, while a cap (`pmax = 0.1%`) ensures diversity. The sampled cases are then allocated to authors according to specialty capacity.
- **Core assumption:** SNDS accurately reflects the target hospital population and the transformation‑cap adjustment preserves useful representativeness while boosting diversity.
- **Evidence:** [abstract] “…epidemiological guidance from the French National Health Data System (SNDS), ensuring broad clinical coverage.” [section 2.3.1] details the sampling database of 100,000 cases covering ~4,000 ICD‑10 primary diagnoses and the formula applied. The `suggested_scenario` field in each patient JSON encodes the sampled parameters.
- **Break condition:** If SNDS data are outdated or biased (e.g., coding shifts, regional skew), or if the adjustment distorts key prevalence patterns, the corpus may misrepresent the target population.

### Mechanism 3: Clinician authorship with peer review
- **Claim:** Medical residents writing structured reports produce documents that are clinically authentic and stylistically similar to real clinical notes.
- **Mechanism:** Residents draw on their training and familiarity with French hospital documentation practices to generate free‑text reports within predefined templates. Each report is independently reviewed by a second resident, who checks for clinical coherence and compliance with the scenario, thereby catching inconsistencies.
- **Core assumption:** Residents possess sufficient expertise to create realistic content and the review process effectively maintains quality and consistency.
- **Evidence:** [abstract] “A total of 104 medical residents across 18 specialties authored and peer‑reviewed the reports…” [section 5] “All contributors… completed standardized online training… Human validators… assessing clinical coherence and adherence to the instructions. This step resulted in a rejection rate of 3.6% of submitted documents.” The corpus JSON includes `author` and `reviewer` trigrams.
- **Break condition:** If author motivation is low, reviewers are perfunctory, or training is inadequate, reports may contain inaccuracies or deviate from typical clinical language, reducing NLP utility.

## Foundational Learning
### Synthetic data generation for privacy‑preserving NLP
- **Why needed here:** PARHAF’s value lies in its synthetic nature, which sidesteps privacy constraints but introduces trade‑offs in realism. Understanding these trade‑offs is essential to interpret the corpus’s limitations.
- **Quick check:** What aspects of clinical realism might be compromised in synthetic data to maintain privacy, and how could that affect downstream NLP model performance?

### Epidemiological sampling for corpus design
- **Why needed here:** The corpus’s representativeness stems from SNDS‑guided sampling. Grasping the sampling adjustments (square‑root transformation, cap) is key to identifying over‑ and under‑represented clinical scenarios.
- **Quick check:** Why use a square‑root transformation on condition frequencies instead of proportional sampling, and what effect does the cap `pmax` have on the final distribution?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** `parhaf.json` (JSON index) → plain‑text report files (organized by medical specialty directories); `parhaf.json` also derives a Hugging Face Parquet dataset.

**Critical path:** 
1. Load `parhaf.json`.
2. Iterate over the `data[]` array.
3. For each document in a patient’s `documents[]`, construct the full file path by appending `path` to the base directory of the text files.
4. Read the plain‑text file and process according to its `type` (e.g., `discharge_summary`, `operative_report`). Optionally use the `structured_abstract` field for a quick overview.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Plain‑text storage:* Human‑readable and language‑agnostic, but requires external parsing logic and provides no markup or annotations.
- *Separate metadata and content:* Enables independent updates and minimal file rewriting, but introduces file I/O overhead and potential path mismatches.
- *Embargoed split:* Protects against LLM contamination for future benchmarks, but reduces available training data and may introduce selection bias if the holdout criteria are non‑random.

**Failure signatures:**
- Missing text files: `path` entries in JSON do not match the actual filesystem layout.
- Inconsistent document counts: The number of documents for a patient deviates from the expected count based on their scenario (e.g., 1–3).
- Encoding issues: Improper UTF‑8 decoding leading to garbled French characters (e.g., accented letters).

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Integrity check:** Load `parhaf.json` and verify total patient and document counts match the paper (5,009 patients, 7,394 documents). Check the release/embargo split (4,254 released patients, 755 embargoed).
2. **Template adherence:** Randomly sample 20 discharge summaries and confirm the presence of expected sections (e.g., “Motif d’hospitalisation”, “Antécédents”, “Examen clinique”, “Conclusion”). Measure coverage of mandatory fields.
3. **Distribution validation:** Compute specialty‑wise patient counts from the JSON and compare against Figure 2 of the paper. Verify the age/sex pyramid matches Figure 3; significant discrepancies may indicate sampling or processing errors.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** To what extent does the PARHAF corpus’ epidemiological distribution, adjusted for author capacity and capped condition frequencies, preserve the statistical properties of real‑world French hospitalizations for downstream NLP model generalization?
**Basis in paper:** (inferred) The authors describe using SNDS data to guide scenario selection, applying a square‑root transformation and a cap (`pmax=0.1%`) to condition frequencies to avoid over‑representation, followed by “iterative adjustment” due to operational constraints. The paper states the general component “approximates real‑world hospitalization distributions” but does not quantify the fidelity of this approximation or its impact on model performance.
**Why unresolved:** The final distribution after adjustments is not disclosed, nor is any divergence metric reported. The potential impact of these deliberate distortions on model utility remains unevaluated.
**What evidence would resolve it:** A comparative statistical analysis (e.g., Jensen‑Shannon divergence) between the age/sex/DRG/ICD‑10 distributions in the released PARHAF general component and the full SNDS 2024 dataset. Subsequent benchmark studies on real‑world French datasets would test whether performance generalizes.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** How authentic is the linguistic and pragmatic style of resident‑authored reports compared to authentic clinical documents produced under real‑world operational constraints (e.g., time pressure, interruptions, EHR copy‑paste)?
**Basis in paper:** (explicit) “The corpus does not capture the operational constraints of real clinical environments (e.g., time pressure, workload, interruptions) and should not be used for stress‑testing models under realistic clinical conditions.” Authors note reports were authored by medical residents, not fully practicing clinicians, and AI tool use was discouraged.
**Why unresolved:** The degree of stylistic deviation from real clinical text is not measured; authenticity is assumed based on expert authorship but not validated against a gold‑standard corpus of real French clinical notes.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Quantitative stylistic analysis comparing PARHAF to a sample of real French clinical notes (where ethically permissible) on metrics like sentence complexity, abbreviation density, hedging language, temporal references, and copy‑paste artifacts. Human evaluation by practicing clinicians could also assess perceived authenticity.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** Do the specialized subsets (oncology biomarkers, treatment response, infectious diseases) provide sufficient signal and diversity to effectively train and evaluate fine‑grained information extraction models, or are they too narrow/synthetic for robust performance claims?
**Basis in paper:** (explicit) Four specialized subsets are introduced for specific IE tasks, but their size and diversity are not detailed in the main text. Limitations state the corpus “does not cover all specialties and variations.”
**Why unresolved:** Utility for IE tasks depends on frequency, variability, and contextual complexity of target entities. For oncology subsets, it is unclear how many unique biomarkers or response descriptions are included and whether they cover the full clinical spectrum, or if the synthetic nature leads to formulaic language.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Characterization of entity and relation distributions within each subset (e.g., count of unique biomarker types, mutation names, response phrases). Benchmark results using these subsets for the stated IE tasks, reporting performance variance across target concepts, would assess whether the data supports learning or merely memorization of synthetic patterns.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** Is the temporary embargo of 1,209 documents (755 patients) an effective strategy to mitigate large language model (LLM) contamination for future benchmark development, and what is the optimal embargo duration?
**Basis in paper:** (explicit) “A subset of the dataset is temporarily embargoed to enable future evaluations under controlled conditions, thereby limiting the risk of large language model contamination through prior exposure to the data.” This is a methodological decision without empirical justification.
**Why unresolved:** Effectiveness of data embargoes against LLM contamination is an open research problem. The paper does not analyze the proportion embargoed, the likelihood that future foundational models will have seen the released portion, or establish a release timeline. The assumption that a 17% holdout is sufficient is untested.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Contamination risk assessment modeling the probability that a given future model (with known or estimated training data scope) would have seen the released PARHAF portion. Longitudinal monitoring of benchmark performance on the embargoed set after model releases could measure if performance degrades, indicating contamination.

### Open Question 5
**Question:** Does the human‑authored, template‑driven creation process introduce systematic biases or blind spots (e.g., over‑emphasis on textbook‑perfect presentations, under‑generation of comorbidities or social determinants) compared to real patient records?
**Basis in paper:** (inferred) The protocol provided structured templates and clinical scenarios based on SNDS aggregates. Authors were “encouraged to develop the clinical case details” but within a guided framework. The corpus is not for “epidemiological or population‑level inference” and doesn’t reflect “real‑world prevalence.” The 3.6% rejection rate indicates quality control for coherence but not for diversity.
**Why unresolved:** The top‑down scenario assignment and template constraints may skew content toward “classic” textbook presentations and away from messy, atypical, or multi‑morbid cases common in real practice. The reliance on residents may bias toward academic phrasing. This could reduce model robustness to the full spectrum of real clinical language.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Comparative analysis of the range and co‑occurrence of diagnoses, procedures, and social history elements in PARHAF versus a real‑world dataset. For instance, are rare comorbidities present? How often do reports mention non‑adherence, social issues, or diagnostic uncertainty? Expert review by seasoned clinicians could identify under‑represented real‑world elements.

## Limitations
- Synthetic realism: Fictitious cases may inadvertently resemble rare real combinations, posing a (unquantified) re‑identification risk.
- Sampling distortion: Square‑root transformation and frequency cap may over‑suppress common conditions; final distribution after capacity adjustments is undisclosed.
- Quality control opacity: Peer‑review rubric and complete author guidelines are missing; reviewer rigor could vary across specialties.
