# Measuring Stability Beyond Accuracy in Small Open-Source Medical Large Language Models for Pediatric Endocrinology

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.11567
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.11567
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Small medical LLMs show significant output instability across prompt variations, temperature sampling, self-assessment, and hardware changes, even when aggregate accuracy remains stable, indicating that accuracy metrics alone are insufficient for clinical deployment.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates six small open-source medical large language models (LLMs) on a pediatric endocrinology multiple-choice question (MCQ) dataset, moving beyond traditional accuracy metrics to assess stability under real-world variations. The authors test the models with different prompt formulations, stochastic sampling temperatures, self-assessment tasks, and hardware/software environments. While one model (HuatuoGPT-o1-8B) achieves the highest accuracy (~0.35), all models exhibit statistically significant shifts in output distributions with minor prompt changes, despite stable McNemar accuracy. Furthermore, consistency of responses across temperature settings does not correlate with correctness, and models display a bias toward selecting their own incorrect reasoning over gold-standard explanations. Even changing CUDA versions produces output shifts without affecting accuracy. These findings reveal that accuracy alone obscures critical reproducibility and reasoning flaws, underscoring the need for broader diagnostic frameworks before clinical deployment.

## Method Summary
The researchers used 91 MCQs from the Pediatric ESAP 2021–2022 exam, each comprising a clinical case, a question, and five options. Six small medical LLMs (HuatuoGPT-o1-8B, Diabetica-7B, Diabetica-o1, Meditron3-8B, MedFound-7B, ClinicalGPT-base-zh) were evaluated under three prompt conditions: a base prompt, a syntactically varied prompt, and the base prompt with option letters removed. For deterministic runs (temperature 0), outputs were human-evaluated to extract selected options and reasoning; for stochastic runs (temperatures 0.3, 0.6, 1.0), ten samples per item were aggregated to compute majority vote consistency. Self-assessment bias was probed by asking models to choose between their own reasoning and a gold-standard explanation. Hardware reproducibility was tested by comparing outputs on two NVIDIA GPUs with different CUDA versions. Statistical analyses included McNemar’s test for paired accuracy, Stuart-Maxwell for distributional shifts, Cohen’s κ for agreement, and correlation of consistency with correctness.

## Key Results
- All models show significant output distribution shifts with minor prompt variations (Stuart-Maxwell p < 0.05), while McNemar’s test indicates stable aggregate accuracy (p > 0.4).
- Consistency defined as majority vote across temperature settings does not guarantee correctness; for example, Diabetica-7B at T=0.3 exhibits high consistency for incorrect answers.
- Models frequently favor their own erroneous reasoning over gold-standard explanations in self-assessment, with bias dependence on presentation order.
- Changing CUDA versions yields statistically significant output shifts (Cohen’s κ = 0.31–0.68) without altering accuracy.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Prompt Sensitivity
Minor prompt variations cause significant output distribution shifts in small medical LLMs, even when aggregate accuracy remains stable. The mechanism is reliance on shallow pattern matching and lexical cues (e.g., option labels) rather than deep semantic reasoning; changing prompt syntax or removing letters disrupts these cues, leading to categorical response shifts. The core assumption is that small, medically fine-tuned LLMs lack robust internal representations of clinical concepts, making them sensitive to superficial input features. Evidence: Stuart-Maxwell tests yield p < 0.05 for all models across prompt variants, while McNemar’s p > 0.4 shows stable accuracy. Cross-domain literature on LLM stability supports this pattern.

### Mechanism 2: Consistency–Correctness Decoupling
Output consistency (majority vote across temperature settings) does not correlate with answer correctness; high consistency can reinforce incorrect answers. The mechanism is that stochastic sampling explores the probability distribution, but for poorly calibrated small models, the distribution’s mode may peak around incorrect yet entrenched misconceptions. Consistency then reflects confidence in flawed reasoning. Core assumption: small LLMs have uncalibrated probability estimates where high likelihood does not equate to clinical validity. Evidence: Diabetica-7B at T=0.3 shows many items with majority vote incorrect; HuatuoGPT-o1-8B shows more correct majority votes. Frequency-response analyses in other domains probe similar parametric stability.

### Mechanism 3: Self-Assessment Bias
LLMs exhibit self-assessment bias, preferentially selecting their own erroneous reasoning over gold-standard clinical explanations when asked to compare. The mechanism is that models trained on self-generated data or with limited exposure to authoritative clinical text develop a preference for their own output distribution, mistaking familiarity for correctness. Positional effects amplify this bias. Core assumption: self-preference arises from overfitting to the model’s own stylistic and probabilistic patterns during training or instruction tuning. Evidence: HuatuoGPT-o1-8B and Diabetica-o1 often fail to select gold-standard, especially when their reasoning is presented first. Prior work on LLM evaluators favoring their own generations supports this bias.

## Foundational Learning

**Concept 1: MCQ-based evaluation limitations**  
*Why needed:* The paper demonstrates that accuracy on MCQs obscures instability and reasoning flaws, preventing misuse of benchmark scores.  
*Quick check:* Can two models have nearly identical MCQ accuracy but fundamentally different reliability under prompt variation?  
*Answer:* Yes, as shown by Stuart-Maxwell tests: accuracy stable (McNemar p > 0.4) but distributions shift (p < 0.05).

**Concept 2: Statistical tests for categorical stability (McNemar vs. Stuart-Maxwell vs. Cohen’s κ)**  
*Why needed:* McNemar only assesses paired accuracy changes; Stuart-Maxwell detects distributional shifts across multiple categories; Cohen’s κ measures agreement beyond chance, revealing bias.  
*Quick check:* Why might a high match-rate but low Cohen’s κ occur?  
*Answer:* Match-rate includes chance agreement and shared option preferences; κ adjusts for these, indicating true consistency.

**Concept 3: Self-assessment bias in LLMs**  
*Why needed:* Models that cannot discern superior external reasoning risk generating plausible but incorrect clinical suggestions and rejecting valid expert input.  
*Quick check:* What does a model’s preference for its own explanation, even when incorrect, imply about its training?  
*Answer:* It suggests overfitting on self-generated data or lack of exposure to high-quality reference reasoning, leading to familiarity-over-correctness bias.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
ESAP dataset -> Prompt variants (A, B, A without letters) -> Model inference (AutoModelForCausalLM.generate) -> Human output evaluation -> Statistical analysis (McNemar, Stuart-Maxwell, κ, majority vote, self-assessment) -> Hardware cross-validation (CUDA 11.8 vs 12.8)

### Critical Path
1. Load model checkpoint with fixed seed and hyperparameters.
2. For each ESAP item, generate response under each prompt condition.
3. Human-evaluate output to extract selected option.
4. Compute accuracy (vs. gold standard), pairwise agreement (κ, match-rate), and Stuart-Maxwell distribution tests.
5. For stochastic runs, aggregate 10 samples to compute majority vote and correctness.
6. For self-assessment, run Experiment 3 prompts with swapped explanation orders.
7. Cross-validate on second CUDA system for numerical stability.

### Design Tradeoffs
- Accuracy vs. stability: A model may have higher accuracy but also higher sensitivity to prompt changes (HuatuoGPT-o1-8B).
- Deterministic vs. stochastic inference: T=0 ensures reproducibility but may not reflect real-world deployment where some randomness is used; stochastic runs reveal consistency-correctness decoupling.
- Automated vs. human evaluation: Automated parsing fails on hallucinations; human review is expensive but necessary for clinical nuance.

### Failure Signatures
- High hallucination rate (>20% “Hallucinate/None”) in MedFound-7B and ClinicalGPT-base-zh.
- Option letter bias: Accuracy drops significantly when option labels are removed (Cohen’s κ ≤0.4).
- Temperature sensitivity: Sharp decline in correct majority votes at T≥0.6 (Diabetica-7B, Meditron3-8B).
- Self-assessment bias: Model selects own incorrect reasoning >50% of the time.
- CUDA sensitivity: Statistically significant Stuart-Maxwell shifts (p<10⁻⁴) despite identical weights and libraries.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Prompt variation stress test:** Run each model under Prompt A, Prompt B, and Prompt A without letters; compute Stuart-Maxwell p-value and Cohen’s κ. Hypothesis: All models will show significant distributional shifts (p<0.05) even if McNemar’s accuracy is stable.
2. **Stochastic consistency vs. correctness:** For top models from Exp1, run 10 samples at T=0.3,0.6,1.0. Plot consistency (majority vote ≥7/10) against correctness (correct selections). Expectation: Consistency does not guarantee correctness; some models will show high consistency for wrong answers.
3. **Self-assessment bias probe:** For items answered incorrectly in Exp1, prompt model to choose between its own reasoning and gold-standard explanation (order counterbalanced). Measure frequency of selecting gold-standard. Expectation: Models will often prefer their own incorrect reasoning, with positional dependence.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How do other inference hyperparameters (e.g., top_p, repetition_penalty) beyond temperature affect output stability and accuracy in small medical LLMs?  
   *Why unresolved:* The study only varied temperature and prompt structure; other parameters may significantly influence consistency but were not tested.
2. What specific implementation differences between Hugging Face’s pipeline() and AutoModelForCausalLM.generate() cause output divergence under deterministic decoding?  
   *Why unresolved:* Divergence was noted qualitatively but not isolated; the root cause in library internals remains unknown.
3. What mechanisms underlie LLMs’ self-assessment bias, where models preferentially select their own incorrect reasoning over gold-standard explanations?  
   *Why unresolved:* The study demonstrates the bias but does not investigate whether it stems from prompt formatting, internal confidence scores, or training objectives.
4. Are reasoning errors in top-performing small medical LLMs primarily due to omission of key clinical features or inclusion of factual inaccuracies?  
   *Why unresolved:* Expert evaluation was limited to 12 cases; the predominant error mode lacks statistical generalizability.
5. How do variations in hardware/software stacks (e.g., GPU architecture, CUDA versions, deep learning libraries) propagate to clinically meaningful output shifts in small medical LLMs?  
   *Why unresolved:* The study compared only two systems; the scope of instability across different GPU families, driver versions, or library updates is unknown.

## Limitations
- The ESAP 2021–2022 question bank is not publicly available, preventing exact replication.
- Exact model checkpoints and fine-tuning details (e.g., Diabetica’s internal data) are unspecified, risking implementation drift.
- Hardware/software confounding (only two CUDA versions compared) may affect numerical stability results.
- Sample size of 91 MCQs limits statistical power for subgroup analyses.

## Confidence
- Prompt sensitivity (Stuart-Maxwell shifts): High
- Consistency–correctness decoupling: Medium
- Self-assessment bias: Medium
- Hardware sensitivity: Medium

## Next Checks
1. Generalizability test – Replicate core stability analyses (Stuart-Maxwell, κ) using a public medical MCQ dataset (e.g., MedQA-USMLE) with the same models to rule out ESAP-specific artifacts.
2. Consistency calibration probe – Expand stochastic runs to 50 samples per temperature (T=0.1–1.0) for HuatuoGPT-o1-8B and Diabetica-7B, plotting consistency vs. correctness to quantify the inflection point where high confidence aligns with accuracy.
3. Self-assessment ablation – Systematically vary explanation quality (gold-standard vs. perturbed incorrect versions) and presentation order to isolate whether bias stems from familiarity, positional priming, or intrinsic preference for self-generated text.
