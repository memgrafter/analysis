# Information-theoretic analysis of world models in optimal reward maximizers

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.12963
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12963
- Reference count: 19
- Primary result: The mutual information between an unknown environment (modeled as a controlled Markov process) and the optimal deterministic policy is exactly \(n \log m\) bits, independent of the reward‑aggregation scheme, establishing a lower bound on the world model any optimal agent must contain.

## Executive Summary
Optimal reinforcement learning agents implicitly construct world models that enable them to maximize reward. This paper asks: how much information about an unknown environment is necessarily encoded in an optimal policy? Treating the environment’s dynamics as a random variable \(X\) uniformly distributed over the space of controlled Markov processes with \(n\) states and \(m\) actions, and considering a non‑constant reward function, the authors prove that the mutual information \(I(X;\Pi)\) between the environment and the optimal deterministic policy \(\Pi\) is exactly \(n \log m\) bits. This bound holds for finite‑horizon, discounted, and average‑reward objectives. The result demonstrates that optimal performance cannot be achieved without implicitly representing a world model with at least that much information.

## Method Summary
The analysis adopts a Bayesian perspective: the environment \(X\) is a continuous random variable over the product of probability simplices defining transition probabilities for each state‑action pair. A uniform prior is placed on \(X\). For each fixed deterministic policy \(\pi\), its value function \(V^\pi(x)\) (whether discounted sum, finite‑horizon return, or average reward) is shown to depend only on the induced transition matrix \(M^\pi(x)\) and to be real‑analytic in \(x\). Because the reward is non‑constant, the difference in values between any two distinct policies is a non‑identically‑zero analytic function, hence non‑zero almost everywhere—ensuring a unique optimal policy for almost all environments. The core geometric insight: for any two policies \(\pi_i, \pi_j\), there exists a measure‑preserving bijection \(g_{ij}\) that swaps the transition rows corresponding to actions favored by each policy. If \(\pi_i\) is optimal in environment \(x\), then \(\pi_j\) is optimal in \(g_{ij}(x)\). This implies each policy occupies an equal‑volume region of the environment space. Under a uniform prior, the entropy of the policy is \(H(\Pi)=n\log m\), so \(I(X;\Pi)=n\log m\). The argument extends across reward‑aggregation schemes due to the shared analytic structure.

## Key Results
- \(I(X;\Pi) = n \log m\) bits, regardless of whether the objective is finite‑horizon, discounted, or average‑reward.
- The partition of the environment space into sets where each deterministic policy is optimal consists of \(m^n\) regions of equal volume.
- The mutual information provides a tight information‑theoretic lower bound on the world model embedded in any optimal agent.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Equal‑volume partition via measure‑preserving swaps
**Claim:** The space of environments partitions into equal‑volume subsets where each deterministic policy is uniquely optimal, enabling entropy reduction.
**Mechanism:** Construct bijections \(g_{ij}\) that swap transition probabilities for actions taken by policies \(\pi_i\) and \(\pi_j\). Because \(V^\pi\) depends only on \(M^\pi\), if \(\pi_i\) is optimal in \(x\), then \(\pi_j\) is optimal in \(g_{ij}(x)\). The Jacobian determinant of \(g_{ij}\) equals 1, so it preserves Lebesgue measure. Thus all policy‑optimal regions have equal volume.
**Core assumption:** The value function depends solely on the transition matrix induced by the policy, not on other environmental details.
**Break condition:** If the value function depends explicitly on action choices or historical data (e.g., action‑dependent rewards), the swap argument fails.

### Mechanism 2: Unique optimality almost everywhere via analyticity
**Claim:** For non‑constant reward functions, ties in policy performance occur only on a measure‑zero set.
**Mechanism:** Show \(V^\pi(x)\) is real‑analytic in the interior of \(X\). Then for two distinct policies, \(f_{ij}(x)=V^{\pi_i}(x)-V^{\pi_j}(x)\) is analytic and not identically zero. By the identity theorem for analytic functions, its zero set has Lebesgue measure zero.
**Core assumption:** Reward function is non‑constant; consider environments in the interior of \(X\) (where all transition probabilities are positive).
**Break condition:** Constant reward makes all differences identically zero; discontinuous rewards may destroy analyticity.

### Mechanism 3: Mutual information from equal‑volume partition
**Claim:** From the equal‑volume partition and unique optima a.e., the mutual information is \(I(X;\Pi) = n \log m\) under a uniform prior.
**Mechanism:** The uniform prior gives entropy \(h(X)=\log V\). Conditioning on \(\Pi=\pi_i\) restricts \(X\) to a set of volume \(V/m^n\), so \(h(X|\Pi=\pi_i) = \log(V/m^n)\). Thus \(h(X)-h(X|\Pi)=n \log m\), which equals \(I(X;\Pi)\) because \(\Pi\) is discrete.
**Core assumption:** Uniform prior over environments; partition covers almost all of \(X\).
**Break condition:** Non‑uniform prior changes the entropy reduction; unequal partition volumes would also change it.

## Foundational Learning
- **Controlled Markov Process (CMP)**
  - Why needed: The environment is modeled as a CMP, specifying transition probabilities for each state–action pair. The space \(X\) is the product of \(n \cdot m\) probability simplices.
  - Quick check: For a state \(s\) and action \(a\), the transition probabilities \(P(s'|s,a)\) must be non‑negative and sum to 1 over \(s'\). What other global constraints define the space \(X\)?
- **Mutual Information and Differential Entropy**
  - Why needed: Quantifies how much the optimal policy reveals about the continuous environment under a uniform prior.
  - Quick check: If \(X\) is continuous with differential entropy \(h(X)\) and \(\Pi\) is discrete, how is \(I(X;\Pi) = h(X) - h(X|\Pi)\) computed when \(\Pi\) is a deterministic function of \(X\) almost everywhere?
- **Real Analytic Functions**
  - Why needed: To prove that differences in policy values have measure‑zero ties, using the fact that non‑zero analytic functions are non‑zero on a dense open set.
  - Quick check: Why does an analytic function on \(\mathbb{R}^d\) that is not identically zero have a zero set of Lebesgue measure zero? (Hint: Use its Taylor expansion and the fact that zeros cannot contain an open ball unless the function is identically zero.)
- **Transition Matrix \(M^\pi(x)\)**
  - Why needed: The value function \(V^\pi(x)\) depends only on the transition matrix induced by policy \(\pi\) in environment \(x\), enabling the swap argument.
  - Quick check: For a deterministic policy \(\pi\) and an environment \(x\), how is the entry \([M^\pi(x)]_{ss'}\) defined from the underlying transition probabilities \(P(s'|s,a)\)?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Environment space \(X\) (product of simplices, uniform measure) → For each \(\pi\), induces transition matrix \(M^\pi(x)\) → Value function \(V^\pi(x)\) (analytic) → Optimal policy \(\Pi(x) = \arg\max_\pi V^\pi(x)\) → Partition \(X_i = \{x : \Pi(x)=\pi_i\}\) (equal volume) → Mutual information \(I(X;\Pi) = n \log m\).

Critical path: 1. Formalize CMPs and the uniform prior over \(X\). 2. Express \(V^\pi(x)\) in terms of \(M^\pi(x)\) and prove analyticity (e.g., via geometric series for discounted case). 3. Show that for any two distinct policies, the set where they tie has measure zero. 4. Construct the measure‑preserving bijections \(g_{ij}\) and prove they map \(X_i\) onto \(X_j\), establishing equal volumes. 5. Apply Lemma 3.1 (equal‑volume partition under uniform prior) to compute the mutual information as \(n \log m\).

Design tradeoffs: The analysis trades generality for tractability: deterministic policies exclude stochasticity; uniform prior ignores structured environments; interior exclusion simplifies analysis but discards boundary sets (which have measure zero); the reward must be non‑constant. These assumptions limit direct applicability to realistic agents but yield a clean analytic result.

Failure signatures: The proof breaks if (a) the reward is constant → no unique optimal policy; (b) the value function depends on actions directly, invalidating the \(M^\pi\)‑only dependence; (c) analyticity fails (e.g., discontinuous reward); (d) the prior is non‑uniform → the entropy reduction is no longer \(n \log m\); (e) stochastic policies are allowed → policy space becomes continuous and the finite partition fails.

First 3 experiments:
1. **Numerical volume verification:** For small \(n,m\) (e.g., \(2\times2\)), sample \(10^6\) CMPs uniformly. Compute optimal deterministic policies. Estimate the volume of each policy’s region and verify they are equal within Monte Carlo error (e.g., \(\chi^2\) test for uniformity).
2. **Reward/aggregation robustness:** Test various non‑constant reward functions (random values, linear, step‑based) and reward‑aggregation schemes (discount \(\gamma\in\{0.5,0.9\}\), finite‑horizon \(T\), average‑reward iteration). Compute the empirical entropy of the policy distribution; it should be within 0.01 bits of \(n\log m\).
3. **Boundary sensitivity:** Sample environments with transition probabilities concentrated near the boundary (e.g., using Dirichlet(\(\alpha=0.5\))) and enforce numerical positivity with a small \(\epsilon\). Compare the policy entropy to the baseline to ensure no systematic drop, confirming that boundary exclusion is benign.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Does the bound \(I(X;\Pi)=n\log m\) extend to optimal stochastic policies \(\pi:S\to\Delta A\) under the uniform prior?
2. How does allowing policies to depend on the full history (memory) affect the mutual information bound?
3. In partially observable environments (POMDPs) with observations \(O=f(S)\), what is the mutual information between the environment and the optimal policy \(\pi:O\to A\)?
4. For policies that are not optimal but achieve performance within \(\delta\) of optimum, can we bound \(I(X;\Pi)\) as a function of \(\delta\)?
5. Does the \(n\log m\) bound hold when the reward function depends explicitly on actions as well as states?

## Limitations
- The uniform prior over the space of CMPs is unrealistic for natural structured environments; the transfer of the bound to more realistic priors remains unclear.
- The analysis is restricted to deterministic policies; allowing stochastic policies would enlarge the policy space and likely increase the mutual information.
- The core swap argument assumes the value function depends only on the policy‑induced transition matrix; this assumption fails for action‑dependent rewards or history‑dependent objectives.

## Confidence
- **Equal‑volume partition (Theorem 3.2):** High – rigorous measure‑theoretic proof with explicit volume‑preserving bijection.
- **Uniqueness a.e. (Lemmas 3.3–3.6):** Medium – analyticity is well‑established for common objectives, but could break for discontinuous rewards or near boundaries.
- **Mutual information \(I(X;\Pi)=n\log m\):** High – direct consequence of equal‑volume partition and uniform prior, and proven for all three reward‑aggregation schemes.

Compare the resulting entropy to the baseline; a systematic drop >0.01 bits would indicate that near‑boundary effects are not negligible.
