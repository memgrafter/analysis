# TimeRadar: A Domain-Rotatable Foundation Model for Time Series Anomaly Detection

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.19068
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19068
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Introduces a domain-rotatable foundation model for unsupervised time series anomaly detection, achieving average gains of 10.5% in AUC-R and 29.4% in AUC-P over the previous generalist DADA in zero-shot settings.

## Executive Summary
Current time series foundation models (TSFMs) are designed for supervised forecasting and fail at unsupervised anomaly detection because they learn regular patterns in fixed domains where anomalies may resemble normals. TimeRadar addresses this by introducing a domain-rotatable foundation model specifically for generalist time series anomaly detection (TSAD). Its core innovation is Fractionally modulated Time-Frequency Reconstruction (FTFRecon), which uses a learnable fractional order to rotate each input into an optimal continuous time-frequency angle for accurate reconstruction, thereby enhancing the salience of anomalies. Additionally, a Contextual Deviation Learning (CDL) component models local deviations. Evaluated on eight benchmark datasets, TimeRadar consistently outperforms state-of-the-art TSFMs and conventional TSAD methods, demonstrating significant improvements in zero-shot settings and strong few-shot adaptation capabilities.

## Method Summary
The method employs two key components: (1) Fractionally modulated Time-Frequency Reconstruction (FTFRecon), which applies a learnable fractional transformation to the input time series, effectively rotating it in the continuous time-frequency domain to achieve more accurate reconstruction of normal patterns; and (2) Contextual Deviation Learning (CDL), which captures local contextual deviations that may be overlooked by global reconstruction. The reconstruction error from FTFRecon, combined with deviation scores from CDL, yields an anomaly score. This approach allows the model to adapt to different time series domains and enhances the distinction between normal and anomalous points.

## Key Results
- **Superior performance**: TimeRadar outperforms existing TSFMs and conventional TSAD methods across eight benchmark datasets.
- **Significant zero-shot gains**: Achieves average improvements of 10.5% in AUC-R and 29.4% in AUC-P over the previous generalist method DADA in zero-shot settings.
- **Effective few-shot adaptation**: Demonstrates strong performance when adapted with limited labeled anomalies.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
TimeRadar works by rotating each time series into an optimal time-frequency representation via a learnable fractional order. This rotation aligns the data such that normal patterns become easier to reconstruct accurately, while anomalies, which do not conform to the learned regularities, yield higher reconstruction errors. The fractional modulation provides a continuous spectrum of transformations, allowing the model to adapt per input rather than relying on fixed time-frequency bins. Furthermore, Contextual Deviation Learning (CDL) complements this by explicitly modeling local deviations from expected context, capturing anomalies that might be missed by reconstruction errors alone. The synergy between global reconstruction fidelity and local deviation modeling enhances anomaly salience.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Input -> FTFRecon (Fractional Modulator + Reconstruction Network) -> Reconstruction Error; Input -> CDL (Context Encoder + Deviation Estimator) -> Deviation Score; [Reconstruction Error, Deviation Score] -> Fusion -> Anomaly Score.

Critical path: The input time series first passes through FTFRecon to obtain a reconstruction and error. Simultaneously, CDL processes the input (or its latent representation) to produce a deviation score. The two scores are combined (e.g., weighted sum) to yield the final anomaly score. Training involves minimizing reconstruction error on normal data and possibly a contrastive loss for deviations.

Design tradeoffs: The learnable fractional order adds a hyperparameter and computational cost but enables domain adaptation. CDL introduces additional parameters and may require careful tuning of context window. The two-module design increases complexity but aims to capture both global and local anomalies.

Failure signatures: 
- If the fractional order collapses to a constant (e.g., 0 or 1), domain rotation degenerates, reducing adaptability.
- If reconstruction error on normal validation data is high, the model may be underfitting or the rotation may be suboptimal.
- If CDL produces high scores on normal data, it may be overfitting to noise.

First three experiments to run:
1. **Single-dataset sanity check**: Train on a normal-only subset of a dataset (e.g., SMD) and evaluate on its test set; check that AUC is high and that reconstruction error correlates with anomalies.
2. **Ablation of FTFRecon**: Replace the fractional modulator with a fixed transformation (e.g., identity or standard STFT) and compare performance to the full model, measuring the impact on zero-shot generalization.
3. **Component contribution analysis**: Remove CDL entirely and evaluate how AUC changes, especially on datasets with contextual anomalies; also try varying the context window size for CDL to see sensitivity.

## Limitations
- **Validation scarcity**: The paper has zero citations and no independent reproduction notes, raising concerns about the robustness and generalizability of reported gains.
- **Benchmark scope**: Evaluation is limited to eight datasets; it is unclear how well the model performs on diverse real-world scenarios (e.g., multivariate vs. univariate, seasonal vs. non-seasonal).
- **Theoretical opacity**: The learnable fractional order in FTFRecon lacks theoretical guarantees for converging to an optimal time-frequency rotation, risking overfitting to training anomalies.

## Confidence
- Performance claims (AUC-R/P gains over DADA): **Low**
- Methodological novelty (FTFRecon + CDL framework): **Medium**
- Generalizability as a “generalist” TSAD model: **Low**
