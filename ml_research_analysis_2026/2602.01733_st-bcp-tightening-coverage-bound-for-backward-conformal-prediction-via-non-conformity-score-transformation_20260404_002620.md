# ST-BCP: Tightening Coverage Bound for Backward Conformal Prediction via Non-Conformity Score Transformation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.01733  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.01733  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: ST-BCP reduces the average coverage gap of Backward Conformal Prediction from 4.21% to 1.12% (73% reduction) and from 5.38% to 0.72% under T=2 on CIFAR-10, while lowering MSE and STD of the LOO estimator.

## Executive Summary
Backward Conformal Prediction (BCP) provides marginal coverage guarantees but suffers from excessive conservatism due to looseness in Markov's inequality, leading to a large gap between theoretical and empirical coverage. ST-BCP addresses this by introducing a data‑dependent transformation of non‑conformity scores: \(h(s) = w(D,X) \cdot \mathbb{I}(s \geq w(D,X))\), where \(w(D,X)\) is a threshold derived from the size constraint \(T(D,X)\). This step function reshapes scores into a two‑point distribution, the case where Markov’s bound is tight, thereby shrinking the coverage gap. The transformation is symmetric, preserving exchangeability and ensuring consistency of the leave‑one‑out estimator at the same \(O(1/\sqrt{n})\) rate as standard BCP. Experiments on CIFAR‑10, CIFAR‑100, and Tiny‑ImageNet with multiple architectures confirm substantial gap reductions (average 73%) and improved estimation precision.

## Method Summary
ST‑BCP modifies BCP by first computing a threshold \(w(D,X)\) as the \((T(D,X)+1)\)-th smallest non‑conformity score among all candidate labels for a given context. The score transformation \(h(s) = w(D,X) \cdot \mathbb{I}(s \geq w(D,X))\) is then applied. The method relies on a symmetric leave‑one‑out construction: for each calibration point \(i\), a dataset \(D_i\) (calibration without \(i\)) is used to compute \(w_i\) and \(h_i\); for a test point, a pseudo‑dataset \(D_{n+1}\) (full calibration) yields \(w_{n+1}\) and the prediction set \(C = \{ y : S(X,y) < w_{n+1} \}\). The conformal level is \(\tilde{\alpha} = \frac{1}{n+1}\left( \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{h_i}{w_{n+1}} + 1 \right)\). The leave‑one‑out estimator \(\hat{\alpha}_{\text{LOO}}\) averages the per‑fold \(\tilde{\alpha}_i = \frac{1}{n}\left( \sum_{j\neq i} \frac{h_j}{w_i} + 1 \right)\). The transformation preserves exchangeability, enabling a tighter Markov bound while retaining \(O(1/\sqrt{n})\) consistency.

## Key Results
- Average coverage gap reduced from **4.21% to 1.12%** (73% decrease) across CIFAR‑10, CIFAR‑100, Tiny‑ImageNet using ResNet50, MobileNet, EfficientNet, DenseNet.
- Under constant \(T=2\) on CIFAR‑10, gap shrank from **5.38% to 0.72%** (over 7× improvement).
- ST‑BCP also achieved **lower MSE and standard deviation** of the LOO estimator, indicating higher precision and stability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Aligning with the Tight Case of Markov’s Inequality
- **Claim**: The step transformation \(h(s)=w(D,X)\mathbb{I}(s\geq w(D,X))\) aligns the non‑conformity score distribution with the two‑point structure that makes Markov’s inequality tight, thereby reducing the coverage gap.  
- **Mechanism**: Markov’s inequality \(P(Z\geq t) \leq \mathbb{E}[Z]/t\) is loose unless \(Z\) is essentially a two‑point random variable. ST‑BCP’s step function forces transformed scores to be either 0 or the constant \(w(D,X)\). Since \(w(D,X)\) derives from the size constraint \(T\), the resulting e‑variable approaches the tight Markov bound. The expectation \(\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\alpha}]\) becomes smaller and its distribution concentrates, directly shrinking the gap between empirical coverage and the LOO estimator.  
- **Core assumptions**: (i) Original scores are exchangeable; (ii) \(w(D,X)\) is well‑defined as the \((T(D,X)+1)\)-th order statistic; (iii) Conditional distribution of \(S\) given \(D,X\) concentrates around \(w\).  
- **Evidence anchors**: Section 3.2 (Probability Theory Insight) states the tightness condition; abstract reports the 73% gap reduction; Figure 2 shows empirical concentration.

### Mechanism 2: Symmetric Parameterization Preserves Exchangeability
- **Claim**: The symmetric design (using \(D_i^y = D\{(X_i,Y_i)\} \cup \{(X_{n+1}, y)\}\)) ensures that transformed scores remain exchangeable, which is essential for the e‑variable’s validity and the LOO estimator’s consistency.  
- **Mechanism**: Because \(h\) depends on the full dataset, a naive pointwise transformation would break permutation invariance. The symmetric construction guarantees that for any candidate \(y\), each data point appears exactly once across the collection of parameters, maintaining exchangeability. This allows Markov’s inequality to be applied correctly and the LOO estimator to be unbiased.  
- **Core assumptions**: i.i.d. data; \(h\) measurable; candidate label \(y\) can be treated as a pseudo‑observation.  
- **Evidence anchors**: Section 3.1 formally defines the symmetric parameterization; abstract mentions “formalize theoretical conditions under which data‑dependent transformations preserve score exchangeability”.

### Mechanism 3: Improvement Operator and Asymptotic Efficiency
- **Claim**: Applying the improvement operator \(G\) to the identity transformation yields a strictly lower expected miscoverage while retaining the \(O_p(1/\sqrt{n})\) convergence rate of the LOO estimator.  
- **Mechanism**: Framework 3.2 reformulates gap minimization as \(\min_h \mathbb{E}[\tilde{\alpha}(h)]\) under monotonicity. Theorem 3.5 shows the optimal \(h\) is a step function based on unknown conditional probabilities. The operator \(G\) formalizes that for any admissible \(h\), \(G(h)\) is preferred. Since identity is admissible, \(G(\text{identity}) = w\mathbb{I}(s\geq w)\) is provably better. Theorem 3.1 guarantees that any \(h\) satisfying its conditions (including this step) yields a LOO estimator with the same convergence rate as standard BCP, thus achieving a tighter bound without sacrificing asymptotic efficiency.  
- **Core assumptions**: Conditions (P1)-(P3) of Theorem 3.1 (boundedness, Chernoff‑Hoeffding concentration, Lipschitz condition); monotonicity.  
- **Evidence anchors**: Corollary 3.6 defines \(G\) and its idempotence; Theorem 3.1 gives \(O_p(1/\sqrt{n})\) rate; Table 1/Figure 2 show lower MSE/STD in experiments.

## Foundational Learning
- **Exchangeability**  
  *Why needed*: ST‑BCP’s validity rests on transformed scores being exchangeable; without this, the e‑variable construction and LOO estimator consistency break down.  
  *Quick check*: “What property of the data points makes the distribution of non‑conformity scores invariant to permutations?” (Answer: They must be i.i.d., i.e., exchangeable.)

- **Markov’s inequality and its tightness**  
  *Why needed*: The coverage gap in BCP originates from Markov’s looseness; ST‑BCP tightens it by shaping scores into a two‑point distribution where Markov is sharp.  
  *Quick check*: “Give a simple distribution where \(P(Z\geq t) = \mathbb{E}[Z]/t\) exactly for a given \(t>0\).” (Answer: A distribution that puts mass \(1-p\) at 0 and \(p\) at \(c\) with \(c\) chosen so that \(t=c\); then \(P(Z\geq t)=p\) and \(\mathbb{E}[Z]=pc\) so ratio equals \(p\).)

- **Leave‑One‑Out estimator consistency**  
  *Why needed*: The LOO estimator \(\hat{\alpha}_{\text{LOO}}\) approximates the intractable expectation \(\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\alpha}]\); its convergence rate determines how many calibration points are needed for a reliable coverage bound.  
  *Quick check*: “What is the asymptotic convergence rate of the LOO estimator in conformal prediction under exchangeability?” (Answer: \(O(1/\sqrt{n})\).)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Calibration Data Loader -> Non‑conformity Score Function -> Size Constraint Rule -> Threshold Compute (\(w\)) -> Transformation Compute (\(h\)) -> LOO Precomputation (loop over calibration points) -> Test Processing (loop over candidate labels, recompute \(w_i^y\)) -> Set Construction -> LOO Estimator
- **Critical path**: The dominant cost is the nested loop over candidate labels \(y\) and calibration points \(i\), especially when \(T(D,X)\) depends on \(D\) (so \(w_i^y\) must be recomputed per \(y\)). Optimizations: if \(T\) depends only on \(X\), \(w_i\) does not change with \(y\); precomputing sorted score lists speeds up \(w\) lookups.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - *Accuracy vs. cost*: ST‑BCP achieves tighter bounds but incurs \(O(|\mathcal{Y}| \cdot n)\) work per test point, which may be prohibitive for large label spaces.
  - *Robustness*: In low‑coverage regimes (small \(T\)), the step transform may overfit; Appendix D suggests using an indicator‑only or corrected coverage level.
  - *Memory*: Storing all \(w_i^y\) values reduces recomputation at the cost of \(O(|\mathcal{Y}| \cdot n)\) memory.
  - *Adaptive \(T\)*: Using \(T(D,X)\) improves size adaptation but adds computational overhead (PCA, k‑NN) and hyperparameters.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Coverage gap remains >2%: suspect incorrect \(D_i^y\) (missing pseudo‑test point) or off‑by‑one in order statistic.
  - LOO estimator unstable (high variance): calibration set too small relative to \(|\mathcal{Y}|\); increase \(n\) or simplify \(T\).
  - Prediction sets unexpectedly empty: \(w_{\text{test}}\) exceeds all scores; check \(T\) and handle ties.
  - Runtime is prohibitive: \(|\mathcal{Y}|\) or \(n\) too large; consider approximations (e.g., reuse \(w_i\) without recomputation).
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Sanity check**: Run ST‑BCP on CIFAR‑10 (ResNet50, \(n=200\), constant \(T=2\)). Verify coverage gap ~0.7% (Table 1) and that prediction sets match BCP in size.
  2. **Stress test**: Use Tiny‑ImageNet (200 classes) with \(T=40\). Check that ST‑BCP still reduces gap and that runtime scales modestly with label space.
  3. **LOO consistency**: Sweep \(n \in \{100,500,1000\}\) and plot MSE vs. \(n\) on a log‑log scale. Ensure both methods’ MSE decays as \(1/\sqrt{n}\), with ST‑BCP lower constant factor (Figure 2).

## Limitations
- Training details (hyperparameters, checkpoints) are not released, making exact reproduction difficult.
- The adaptive size constraint \(T(D,X)\) is underspecified (k‑NN size, entropy binning ranges, PCA per‑trial), leading to implementation variance.
- Computational complexity \(O(|\mathcal{Y}| \cdot n)\) per test point may be prohibitive for large label spaces; this limitation is not deeply analyzed.

## Confidence
- Gap reduction: **High** (empirical over 3 datasets, 4 models, consistent 73% average decrease)
- Exchangeability preservation: **High** (formal proof + symmetric construction)
- Statistical efficiency (\(O(1/\sqrt{n}\)): **Medium** (theory requires boundedness/Lipschitz; practical violations possible)
- Generality across tasks: **Low** (evaluation limited to image classification; dependencies untested)

## Next Checks
1. **Scalability sweep**: Vary calibration size \(n \in \{100,500,1000\}\); confirm both methods’ MSE scales as \(1/\sqrt{n}\) and ST‑BCP’s gap remains lower.
2. **Symmetry ablation**: Remove the pseudo‑test point from \(D_i^y\) (i.e., set \(D_i^y = D\setminus\{i\}\)) and demonstrate coverage degradation, confirming the necessity of the symmetric construction.
3. **Adaptive T stress**: Test extreme size constraints \(T \in \{1,50\}\); expect gap widening for very small \(T\) (due to \(w\) instability) but still observe ST‑BCP improvement over BCP.
