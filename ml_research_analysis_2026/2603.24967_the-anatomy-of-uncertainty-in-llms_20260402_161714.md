# The Anatomy of Uncertainty in LLMs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.24967  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24967  
- **Reference count**: 11  
- **Primary result**: Introduces a three-way decomposition of LLM uncertainty (input ambiguity, knowledge gaps, decoding randomness) that shifts with model scale and task, enabling targeted reliability interventions.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the limited diagnostic value of existing LLM uncertainty quantification methods by proposing a novel framework that splits uncertainty into three semantically distinct components: input ambiguity (from underspecified prompts), knowledge gaps (from insufficient training data), and decoding randomness (from stochastic sampling). The authors isolate each component by fixing other generation factors and measuring semantic entropy over clustered responses. Empirical evaluations on factual QA (TriviaQA) and reasoning (GSM8K) across models of varying scales (Llama 3 8B, Gemma 3 270M–27B) show that the dominant uncertainty source changes with model size and task: decoding uncertainty best predicts failures for smaller models, while input ambiguity becomes stronger for larger ones. Stochastic decoding strategies (top‑k, temperature) outperform deterministic ones (greedy, beam) in failure prediction. Moreover, models are most poorly calibrated when both input and decoding uncertainties are low, indicating dangerous overconfidence. The decomposition opens the door to targeted mitigations such as prompt clarification, retrieval augmentation, or adaptive decoding.

## Method Summary
The method operationalizes the three‑component decomposition by estimating each uncertainty source independently. For **input ambiguity**, the system generates *K* meaning‑preserving paraphrases of a prompt using an external paraphraser, obtains a greedy response from the target LLM for each paraphrase, clusters the responses via a bidirectional entailment model, and computes entropy over the resulting semantic clusters. For **knowledge gaps**, an ensemble of *M* LoRA adapters (different random seeds) is trained; each adapter generates a greedy response for the fixed prompt, and semantic clustering plus entropy yields knowledge uncertainty. For **decoding randomness**, the same model and prompt are used with a stochastic decoding strategy (e.g., temperature, top‑k) to produce *N* responses; clustering and entropy compute decoding uncertainty. Semantic clustering filters superficial lexical variation, making entropy a robust measure. The resulting uncertainty scores are evaluated by their ability to predict generation failures (AUROC) and calibration (ECE) on held‑out examples.

## Key Results
- On TriviaQA, decoding uncertainty is the strongest failure predictor for smaller models (Llama 3 8B, AUROC 0.731), while input ambiguity dominates for larger ones (Gemma 3 27B, AUROC 0.761).
- Stochastic decoding strategies (top‑k, temperature) yield higher AUROC than deterministic ones (greedy, beam), confirming the importance of capturing decoding variability.
- Models exhibit the worst calibration (highest ECE) when both input and decoding uncertainties are low, suggesting overconfidence in seemingly unambiguous, deterministic settings.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Input Ambiguity
- **Claim**: Input ambiguity is a distinct source of uncertainty arising from ambiguous or underspecified prompts.
- **Mechanism**: Generate *K* meaning‑preserving paraphrases of the prompt (using an external paraphraser). For each paraphrase, obtain a response from the same LLM using deterministic decoding. Cluster responses into semantic equivalence classes (via entailment). Compute the entropy of the cluster probability distribution; high entropy indicates input ambiguity.
- **Core assumption**: The paraphrases adequately cover plausible rephrasings, and semantic clustering correctly groups responses by meaning.
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] includes “input ambiguity, arising from ambiguous prompts” as a component. [section 3] Equation (2) formalizes *U_input* and Figure 2 illustrates the pipeline.
- **Break condition**: If paraphrases introduce semantic shifts or clustering misgroups answers, *U_input* may not reflect true input ambiguity.

### Mechanism 2: Knowledge Gaps
- **Claim**: Knowledge gaps represent uncertainty due to insufficient or outdated parametric knowledge captured via model ensemble disagreement.
- **Mechanism**: Train an ensemble of *M* model variants (LoRA adapters with different seeds). For fixed input and greedy decoding, generate one response per ensemble member. Cluster responses semantically and compute entropy over clusters (Equation 3). High entropy signals knowledge uncertainty.
- **Core assumption**: LoRA ensemble approximates the posterior over parameters; clustering accurately captures answer equivalence.
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] lists “knowledge gaps, caused by insufficient parametric evidence.” [section 3] details the LoRA ensemble construction and Equation (3).
- **Break condition**: If LoRA variants are not diverse enough (e.g., low rank, insufficient seed variation), *U_knowledge* may be underestimated.

### Mechanism 3: Decoding Randomness
- **Claim**: Decoding randomness is uncertainty introduced by stochastic sampling during generation.
- **Mechanism**: Fix model and prompt; generate *N* responses using a stochastic decoding strategy (temperature, top‑k, etc.). Cluster responses semantically and compute entropy (Equation 4). Higher entropy indicates greater decoding‑induced uncertainty.
- **Core assumption**: The decoding strategy’s randomness is the only varying factor; semantic clustering filters superficial differences.
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] includes “decoding randomness, stemming from stochastic sampling.” [section 3] describes *U_dec* and evaluates multiple decoding strategies.
- **Break condition**: If decoding is too deterministic (e.g., greedy) or clustering fails, *U_dec* may not capture true decoding variability.

## Foundational Learning

- **Semantic Entropy**  
  *Why needed*: Token‑level entropy poorly correlates with answer correctness in LLMs due to multiple valid expressions; semantic clustering aligns uncertainty with meaning.  
  *Quick check*: How does computing entropy over semantic clusters improve upon using the model’s token probability distribution for uncertainty estimation?

- **Uncertainty Decomposition**  
  *Why needed*: A single uncertainty score cannot guide whether to clarify the prompt, retrieve more information, or adjust decoding.  
  *Quick check*: For each component, describe a concrete system‑level intervention that could mitigate it.

- **Semantic Clustering**  
  *Why needed*: Lexical variation should not affect uncertainty; clustering ensures entropy reflects semantic diversity of answers.  
  *Quick check*: What are the limitations of using NLI models for clustering, and how might clustering errors propagate to uncertainty estimates?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**:  
  The system comprises three parallel pipelines for the three uncertainty sources:  
  (1) *Input Ambiguity*: Paraphrase Generator (*K*) → Model (greedy) → *K* responses.  
  (2) *Knowledge Gaps*: LoRA Trainer (*M* adapters) → Each adapter → Model (greedy) → *M* responses.  
  (3) *Decoding Randomness*: Decoding Executor (*N* samples, chosen strategy) → Model → *N* responses.  
  All response sets are processed by shared **Semantic Clustering** (NLI‑based) and **Entropy Calculator** modules, producing respective uncertainty scores. The **Evaluation** module then computes AUROC and ECE against failure labels.

- **Critical path**:  
  To compute a component’s uncertainty score: generate the required set of model responses → apply semantic clustering to obtain clusters → compute entropy over the cluster probability distribution. Correctness labels (determined via NLI or ROUGE) are only needed later for evaluating predictive performance (AUROC/ECE), not for the uncertainty estimation itself.

- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - *Number of paraphrases (K)*: More *K* improves coverage of prompt variations but increases API cost and latency.  
  - *Ensemble size (M)*: Larger *M* yields a better approximation of the parameter posterior but requires training and storing more LoRA adapters.  
  - *Decoding samples (N)*: More samples smooth entropy estimates but slow inference; *N*=5 is a compromise.  
  - *Clustering quality*: A strong NLI model (e.g., DeBERTa) is more accurate but computationally heavier than heuristics like ROUGE; it also affects overall reliability.  
  - *Deterministic vs. stochastic decoding*: Stochastic sampling reveals uncertainty but may produce incoherent outputs; deterministic strategies are faster but can underestimate uncertainty.

- **Failure signatures**:  
  - Very low AUROC (<0.5) across all components suggests a bug in clustering or correctness labeling.  
  - High correlation between *U_input* and *U_dec* may indicate that input ambiguity is conflated with decoding variability, requiring re‑examination of paraphrase diversity.  
  - Poor calibration (high ECE) when both *U_input* and *U_dec* are low matches the paper’s “overconfidence” danger zone; confidence scaling might be needed.  
  - Homogeneous LoRA outputs (identical responses across ensemble members) signals insufficient adapter diversity; increase LoRA rank or training variation.  
  - Incoherent paraphrases (meaning drift) invalidate *U_input*; inspect random samples and adjust the paraphraser.

- **First 3 experiments**:  
  1. **Replication of main results**: Compute failure prediction AUROC and ECE for all three uncertainty components on TriviaQA and GSM8K using Llama 3 8B and Gemma 3 27B. Verify that decoding uncertainty dominates for Llama 3 on TriviaQA while input ambiguity dominates for Gemma 3.  
  2. **Scaling analysis**: For the Gemma family (270M to 27B), compute *U_input* and *U_dec* on TriviaQA. Plot AUROC versus model size to confirm that input uncertainty becomes a stronger predictor as models scale.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **Efficient coverage of prompt variations**  
   - **Question**: How can we estimate input ambiguity in LLMs by efficiently covering a more complete space of semantically equivalent prompt variations, rather than relying on a small set of manually or automatically generated paraphrases?  
   - **Basis**: The authors state that input‑based uncertainty is estimated using a finite set of paraphrases rather than considering the full space of possible prompt variations, which remains open for improvement.  
   - **Why unresolved**: The current approach may miss many plausible paraphrases that could reveal additional ambiguities, leading to an incomplete estimate of input‑induced uncertainty. Scaling to a larger or complete set of variations is computationally expensive and may require novel methods to ensure coverage without redundancy.  
   - **What evidence would resolve it**: A new method that either (a) generates a diverse and representative set of paraphrases with theoretical or empirical guarantees of coverage, or (b) directly models the distribution of prompt variations and computes uncertainty without explicit enumeration. Evaluation would compare the new method’s failure prediction performance (e.g., AUROC, ECE) against the baseline and assess computational efficiency.

2. **Better approximation of parameter posterior**  
   - **Question**: How can we more accurately quantify knowledge gaps in LLMs by approximating the posterior distribution over model parameters more effectively than small LoRA ensembles?  
   - **Basis**: The authors note that knowledge‑based uncertainty is approximated using a small ensemble of LoRA‑adapted model realizations instead of performing full posterior inference over model parameters, which is an open challenge.  
   - **Why unresolved**: LoRA ensembles capture only a limited set of fine‑tuned variations and may not fully represent the uncertainty due to missing training data or outdated information. Full Bayesian inference is computationally prohibitive for large LLMs, so alternative approximations are needed.  
   - **What evidence would resolve it**: Development of scalable Bayesian inference techniques for LLMs (e.g., variational inference, posterior ensembles with diverse initialization, or dropout as Bayesian approximation) that can be applied to large models. Evaluation would measure the correlation between the new uncertainty estimate and actual knowledge gaps, perhaps by comparing with held‑out data or known factual gaps, and assess computational feasibility.

3. **Improving semantic clustering reliability**  
   - **Question**: How can we improve the reliability of semantic clustering for uncertainty estimation, given that it depends on the accuracy of the underlying entailment model and may propagate errors?  
   - **Basis**: The authors state that their estimates depend on the quality of semantic clustering, which may be affected by errors in the underlying entailment model, and this remains open for improvement.  
   - **Why unresolved**: Current semantic clustering uses NLI models to group responses, but NLI models themselves can be inaccurate, especially on ambiguous or out‑of‑domain text. Errors in clustering directly affect the computed semantic entropy and thus the uncertainty estimates.  
   - **What evidence would resolve it**: Robustness analysis showing the sensitivity of uncertainty estimates to NLI model errors, and development of more robust clustering methods (e.g., using multiple NLI models, ensemble clustering, or alternative semantic similarity measures). Evaluation would demonstrate reduced variance in uncertainty scores under different NLI models and improved correlation with failure rates.

## Limitations
- **Paraphrase generation substitution**: Replacing “GPT‑5‑nano” with an open‑source paraphraser may alter diversity and meaning preservation, impacting *U_input* reliability (Confidence: Medium).
- **Semantic clustering specifics**: Undisclosed NLI model, threshold, and clustering algorithm introduce implementation variability that can affect entropy calculations (Confidence: Medium).
- **Correctness metric inconsistency**: The main text cites ROUGE‑L ≥ 0.3 while the appendix uses bidirectional NLI (γ=0.5); it is unclear which metric drove the reported AUROC/ECE values (Confidence: Low).
