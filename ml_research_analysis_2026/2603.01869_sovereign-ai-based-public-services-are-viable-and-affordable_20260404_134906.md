# Sovereign AI-based Public Services are Viable and Affordable

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.01869  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01869  
- Reference count: 0  
- Primary result: A sovereign RAG-based chatbot using Gervásio 70B matched or exceeded a commercial baseline in accuracy (4.14 vs 4.02) and served 100–500 concurrent users on a single multi‑GPU node with acceptable latency.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates sovereign, on‑premise AI solutions for public services, using a Portuguese RAG‑based chatbot built with open‑weight models (Gervásio 8B/70B). The top configuration (Gervásio 70B + RAG) achieved higher accuracy than a commercial national chatbot baseline and demonstrated that modest hardware can handle hundreds of concurrent users. The findings suggest that governments can deploy independent AI services that are both technically feasible and economically sustainable, reducing reliance on external commercial providers.

## Method Summary
The authors created a chatbot for Portuguese public administration by implementing a retrieval‑augmented generation (RAG) pipeline. They scraped and processed approximately 2,300 official government web pages to build a vector‑based knowledge base. Two locally fine‑tuned open‑weight models, Gervásio 8B and 70B, were evaluated. Performance was measured automatically using Llama 3.3 70B Instruct as a judge, comparing generated answers against those of a commercial national chatbot on a set of direct questions. Load testing with a load balancer assessed the system’s ability to handle concurrent users on a single multi‑GPU node.

## Key Results
- The sovereign system (Gervásio 70B + RAG) achieved an accuracy score of 4.14 on a 0–5 scale, slightly exceeding the commercial baseline (4.02).  
- With load balancing, a single multi‑GPU node served 100 concurrent users (95th‑percentile latency <30 s) and 500 users (<89 s).  
- Error analysis revealed a cautious tendency: 6% of in‑domain questions were incorrectly classified as out‑of‑domain and left unanswered, a trade‑off framed as protecting the provider’s reputation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The approach succeeds because retrieval‑augmented generation grounds answers in an official document corpus, reducing hallucinations and ensuring compliance with public‑service information. Small, open‑weight models allow fine‑tuning to the specific language and domain while being efficient enough to run on modest on‑premise hardware. Load balancing across multiple GPUs on a single node distributes inference requests, enabling the system to handle hundreds of concurrent users with acceptable latency. Together, these elements provide a viable alternative to cloud‑based commercial services.

## Foundational Learning
**Retrieval‑Augmented Generation (RAG)**: Why needed: To provide factual, document‑grounded answers and mitigate model hallucination. Quick check: Does the system retrieve relevant passages before generating?  
**Open‑weight Models**: Why needed: To enable local hosting, customization, and independence from proprietary AI providers. Quick check: Are model weights publicly available and licensable for on‑premise use?  
**Sovereign AI**: Why needed: To maintain full control over data, models, and infrastructure for national security and regulatory compliance. Quick check: Is the entire AI stack owned and operated by the government?  
**Load Balancing**: Why needed: To distribute inference load across multiple GPU workers, ensuring low latency under concurrency. Quick check: Does the system dynamically allocate requests to available GPU instances?  
**Fine‑tuning**: Why needed: To adapt a base model to a specific domain (Portuguese public services) and improve performance. Quick check: Was the model trained on in‑domain data?  

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:
User -> Load Balancer -> API Server -> Retriever -> Vector Database; API Server -> Generator (Gervásio 70B) -> GPU

Critical path:
User query is sent to the load balancer, forwarded to an API server, the retriever fetches relevant documents from the vector database, the generator (running on GPU) produces an answer, and the response is returned to the user. Latency is dominated by the generator inference step.

Design tradeoffs:
- Model size: 70B offers higher accuracy but requires multiple GPUs; 8B reduces resource use at the cost of accuracy.  
- RAG: Improves factual grounding but adds retrieval latency and depends on document quality.  
- Caution policy: Over‑classifying out‑of‑domain queries avoids incorrect answers but increases the unanswered rate.  
- Single‑node deployment: Simpler and cheaper than a distributed cluster, but limits fault tolerance and horizontal scaling.

Failure signatures:
- Latency spikes when retrieval returns many/large documents or GPU memory is saturated.  
- Load balancer health‑check failures if all generator workers are busy, leading to dropped requests.  
- Misclassification of in‑domain queries as out‑of‑domain, causing user frustration.  
- Vector index corruption returning irrelevant context and degrading answer quality.

First experiments:
1. Compare accuracy of Gervásio 8B/70B with and without RAG against the commercial baseline on a held‑out question set.  
2. Perform load testing with incremental concurrent users (10, 50, 100, 200, 500) to identify the 95th‑percentile latency and maximum sustainable load on the single node.  
3. Simulate a GPU failure by disabling one GPU in the multi‑GPU setup to verify load balancer failover and measure performance degradation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What are the long-term administrative efficiency, inclusiveness, and trust outcomes of deploying sovereign AI chatbots in public administration, as opposed to the current focus on usability and citizen satisfaction metrics?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper states: "Understanding how chatbots affect administrative efficiency, inclusiveness, and trust in government remains a critical research gap." and "Empirical studies tend to focus on usability, citizen satisfaction, or task automation rather than long-term institutional or economic outcomes."
- **Why unresolved:** The authors' study provides a snapshot of technical viability but does not measure longitudinal impacts on administrative processes, equity of access, or public trust over time.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Longitudinal, mixed-methods studies tracking metrics like process automation rates, citizen adoption/equity data, and trust surveys before and after sovereign AI deployment in multiple government agencies.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How do the total cost of ownership (TCO), including hardware, energy, maintenance, and staff, for a sovereign on-premise RAG system compare to the subscription and API costs of leading commercial cloud‑based AI services over a 5‑year operational period?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper argues sovereign AI is "economically sustainable" and "affordable" based on a load test showing feasibility on a "single multi‑GPU node," but provides no detailed cost model or comparison to commercial service pricing tiers, scaling costs, or hidden operational expenses (e.g., cooling, power, sysadmin).
- **Why unresolved:** The economic claim is supported by technical feasibility (latency under load) but lacks a comprehensive financial analysis that accounts for the full spectrum of ownership costs versus the predictable operational expenditure of cloud services.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A transparent, itemized TCO model for the described hardware setup compared against documented pricing for equivalent commercial chatbot services (e.g., per‑query costs, monthly/user fees) under projected scaling scenarios.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** To what extent does the performance advantage of fine‑tuned, small, culturally‑specific models (like Gervásio) hold when the domain scope is expanded beyond Portuguese public services to multi‑lingual, multi‑national, or more diverse administrative contexts?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The study's success is demonstrated on a monolingual (European Portuguese), single‑nation (Portugal) dataset of ~2.3k web pages. The claim of general suitability "for organizations and governments worldwide" is made based on technical independence from language/national settings, but this generalizability is not empirically tested.
- **Why unresolved:** The core result is context‑specific. Performance gains from language‑specific fine‑tuning and a narrow RAG corpus may not transfer to scenarios with broader language coverage, more complex bureaucratic structures, or noisier source documents.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replication studies deploying the same architecture on public service datasets from other countries and languages, followed by comparative analysis of accuracy metrics and RAG retrieval effectiveness.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What are the specific failure modes and error propagation risks of using an automatic LLM‑as‑a‑judge (Llama 3.3 70B Instruct) for evaluating chatbot answers in a high‑stakes public service context, and how might human‑in‑the‑loop validation alter the reported performance gaps?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The evaluation relies solely on an automated judge model: "The answers generated by the different systems were evaluated automatically using Llama 3.3 70B Instruct as a judge model." The paper acknowledges the judge but does not assess its own reliability, bias, or alignment with human judgments for this specific, nuanced task.
- **Why unresolved:** Using an LLM judge introduces potential for assessment bias (e.g., preferring verbosity, specific phrasing) that may not correlate with actual answer utility, correctness for citizens, or compliance with administrative rules. The small performance differences (e.g., 4.14 vs 4.02) could be within judge error margins.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A human evaluation study where domain experts (public administration officials) rate a sampled subset of the judge's evaluations, measuring inter‑rater reliability and identifying systematic discrepancies between automated and human judgment on factors like completeness, safety, and policy adherence.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** How does the "caution" tendency observed in the top sovereign system (over‑classifying questions as out‑of‑domain) impact citizen task completion and satisfaction in real‑world usage compared to a commercial baseline that may offer more speculative but engaging answers?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The error analysis notes: "Gervásio 70B + RAG...completely failed to provide correct answers to 6% of [in‑domain] questions, because these happened to be incorrectly classified as out‑of‑domain." It also states the system "eventually favors caution in its responses, helping to protect the reputation of the chatbot provider, at the cost of leaving a small number of in‑domain questions unanswered."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper frames caution as a protective feature but does not measure its user‑level consequence. An unanswered question may be more detrimental to citizen experience than a speculative but helpful answer, especially in complex administrative queries.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** User studies (e.g., task‑based experiments, A/B testing) where citizens interact with both the cautious sovereign bot and the commercial baseline, measuring success rates in resolving queries, frustration levels, and fallback to human agents.

### Open Question 6
- **Question:** What are the practical engineering and governance challenges in maintaining cultural and linguistic sovereignty for AI systems when the underlying open‑weight models (e.g., Llama derivatives) are primarily developed and controlled by non‑European entities?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper champions "digital and cultural sovereignty" through local deployment of open‑weight models fine‑tuned for Portuguese. However, it does not address the dependency on base models (Llama) with training data, objectives, and potential update cycles controlled by external corporations (Meta). True sovereignty may require more than just local fine‑tuning and hosting.
- **Why unresolved:** The model's foundational capabilities, biases, and evolution are determined by the original developer. The paper treats the open‑weight model as a neutral substrate, but its origin introduces a hidden dependency that could conflict with long‑term sovereignty goals if licensing, data provenance, or roadmap control change.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Analysis of the provenance, licensing terms, and update policies of the base open‑weight models used for fine‑tuning, coupled with a framework for assessing "model sovereignty" that includes factors like training data transparency and governance influence.

## Limitations
- **Baseline ambiguity:** The commercial national chatbot used as a baseline is not named or described, limiting interpretation of the accuracy comparison.  
- **Scale and generalizability:** Results are based on a monolingual Portuguese dataset and a single multi‑GPU node; national‑scale deployment and costs remain unvalidated.  
- **Economic analysis missing:** Claims of affordability and sustainability lack a comprehensive total cost of ownership (TCO) model compared to commercial alternatives.
