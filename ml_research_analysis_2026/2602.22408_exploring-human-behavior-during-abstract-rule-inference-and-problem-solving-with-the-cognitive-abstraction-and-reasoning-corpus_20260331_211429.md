# Exploring Human Behavior During Abstract Rule Inference and Problem Solving with the Cognitive Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.22408  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22408  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** High but variable accuracy (~90% Exp1, ~80% Exp2) with problem difficulty strongly correlated with experimenter-rated rule complexity, deliberation time, and convergent error patterns.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces CogARC, a human-adapted subset of the Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus (ARC), to investigate abstract rule inference. Across two experiments (total n=260), participants solved 75 visual reasoning problems by editing grids, with their edit sequences and response times logged. Results show that problem difficulty varies systematically: harder rules require longer deliberation and yield more divergent edit strategies, while incorrect solutions often converge on shared misinterpretations. These findings highlight structured inductive biases in human abstract reasoning and provide a new behavioral benchmark for comparing human and AI performance.

## Method Summary
Participants solved 75 ARC-style problems on a browser-based interface. For each problem, they viewed 2–6 input-output example pairs, then generated a correct output for a novel test input by editing a grid (placing colored tiles). All actions (edits with coordinates and colors, view switches, submissions) were timestamped. Two experiments differed in setting: Exp1 (n=40) used supervised Zoom calls with a fixed problem order; Exp2 (n=220) used MTurk with randomized order, a 2-hour limit, and a performance bonus. Data were quality-controlled by excluding trials where examples were never viewed and participants who solved fewer than 10 problems. Key metrics computed per problem included accuracy, a difficulty score (1–4 based on attempts needed), mean deliberation time (latency to first edit), and mean pairwise Jaccard similarity of first-attempt edit sequences (treated as unordered sets of tile placements).

## Key Results
- Accuracy was high but variable across problems: ~90% in Exp1, ~80% in Exp2.
- Problem difficulty (mean attempts needed) correlated positively with experimenter-rated rule complexity (r=0.61) and deliberation time (r=0.52–0.69), and negatively with edit sequence similarity (r=-0.83).
- Many incorrect solutions were highly convergent across participants (≥5 identical submissions per problem), revealing systematic misinterpretations despite varied edit trajectories.
- Across the task, participants initiated responses faster over time but showed a slight accuracy decline.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Rule complexity drives difficulty
- **Claim:** Problem difficulty is systematically driven by the complexity of the abstract rule.
- **Mechanism:** Experimenter-rated rule complexity (based on number of core knowledge concepts and conditional interactions) correlates with participant difficulty scores (1–4 scale).
- **Core assumption:** Human raters can reliably estimate the cognitive load of a rule from its structural properties.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Performance varied widely across problems and was highly related to rule complexity.
  - Difficulty increased with experimenter-rated rule complexity (r=0.61, p<.001) (Page 14).
- **Break condition:** If rule complexity ratings fail to predict difficulty in an independent sample or with different raters.

### Mechanism 2: Deliberation time indexes inference effort
- **Claim:** Longer pre-response deliberation reflects greater effort in rule inference and hypothesis generation.
- **Mechanism:** Mean deliberation time (trial onset to first edit) positively correlates with problem difficulty score.
- **Core assumption:** Time spent viewing examples before editing indexes the cognitive process of extracting and testing candidate rules.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Harder problems elicited longer deliberation times.
  - Mean deliberation time was positively correlated across problems with the mean difficulty score (r=0.52–0.69) (Pages 11, 14).
- **Break condition:** If deliberation time does not vary with problem difficulty or if participants deliberate equally long on easy/hard problems.

### Mechanism 3: Convergent errors reveal shared inductive biases
- **Claim:** Systematic errors reveal shared inductive biases that constrain the hypothesis space during rule learning.
- **Mechanism:** Many incorrect solutions are highly convergent across participants (≥5 identical submissions per problem), even when edit trajectories vary, suggesting a limited set of plausible misinterpretations.
- **Core assumption:** Convergence in errors stems from similar prior expectations about transformations, not random guessing or disengagement.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Even incorrect solutions were often highly convergent, revealing shared systematic misinterpretations.
  - Common errors (identical outputs by ≥5 participants) were highly structured and interpretable (Pages 9, 17–18).
- **Break condition:** If common errors disappear when task format changes (e.g., multiple-choice) or if error patterns are completely idiosyncratic across participants.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Inductive biases**  
  *Why needed here:* They explain why humans generalize from sparse examples and why errors cluster—biases narrow the hypothesis space.  
  *Quick check:* What core knowledge prior might cause participants to misapply an "objectness" rule to a geometry problem?
- **Concept: Edit sequence dynamics**  
  *Why needed here:* Captures real-time problem-solving strategy, not just outcome; reveals convergence/divergence in how participants reach solutions.  
  *Quick check:* How would you interpret a bimodal distribution in "normalized extra steps" for a common error?
- **Concept: Difficulty scoring (behavioral)**  
  *Why needed here:* Provides a continuous measure of rule inference challenge grounded in actual participant performance, supplementing expert complexity ratings.  
  *Quick check:* Why might a problem have high experimenter complexity but low behavioral difficulty score?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Frontend (grid editor, example viewer, attempt submission) -> Backend (timestamped action logger, state tracker) -> Analysis (difficulty scorer, Jaccard similarity calculator, trajectory metrics).
- **Critical path:** Participant interaction -> raw action log -> per-trial state reconstruction -> per-problem aggregates (mean difficulty, mean similarity) -> correlation with rule properties.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Logging granularity:* Tile-level edits capture strategy but increase data volume; submission-only reduces noise but loses dynamics.
  - *Problem order:* Fixed order controls for sequence effects but may confound learning; randomization reduces confounds but adds inter-participant noise.
  - *Attempt limits:* Three attempts allow recovery but may permit strategic guessing; unlimited could extend task fatigue.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Low edit similarity across all problems: suggests instructions unclear or participants use random exploration.
  - No correlation between deliberation time and difficulty: indicates task not engaging rule inference (e.g., perceptual-only).
  - High extra steps without convergence: may signal motor inefficiency or exploration, not rule misunderstanding.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. *Replication with controlled order:* Test whether random vs. fixed problem order affects within-participant difficulty trends.
  2. *Example count manipulation:* Vary number of input-output examples (2, 4, 6) to test impact on deliberation time and accuracy.
  3. *Think-aloud validation:* Record verbal protocols on a subset to qualitatively link edit sequences to explicit rule hypotheses.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** What specific inductive biases guide human rule inference in CogARC tasks, leading to both successful solutions and systematic errors?  
  *Basis in paper:* The paper shows that errors are highly convergent across participants, suggesting shared biases, but does not identify the specific biases responsible.
- **Open Question 2:** Why does participant accuracy decline slightly over time despite faster response initiation, and why is this decline not directly correlated with individual changes in deliberation speed?  
  *Basis in paper:* Results show group-level trends: faster initiation but small accuracy drop, with no significant correlation between individual slopes in speed and accuracy.
- **Open Question 3:** What cognitive processes occur during the pre-edit deliberation period, and how does the duration of deliberation mechanistically contribute to successful rule inference?  
  *Basis in paper:* Deliberation time correlates with problem difficulty and predicts success, but the paper does not analyze what participants do or think during this period.
- **Open Question 4:** How do specific core knowledge systems (e.g., objectness, geometry, number) individually and interactively influence performance on CogARC problems, especially for rules combining multiple concepts?  
  *Basis in paper:* Problems are categorized by core knowledge types and experimenter-rated complexity, but the paper does not test whether these categories differentially predict difficulty or error patterns.
- **Open Question 5:** Can computational models that explicitly incorporate human-like inductive biases and error structures from CogARC achieve better generalization and interpretability compared to current AI systems on abstract reasoning tasks?  
  *Basis in paper:* The discussion highlights that human errors are structured and informative, and cites prior work (Do et al., 2025) using such data to inform models, but this study does not present new model comparisons.

## Limitations
- The exact 75-problem set and experimenter-assigned complexity rubric are not publicly available, limiting replication fidelity.
- Complexity ratings rely on subjective judgments of "core knowledge concepts" and their interactions, with unreported inter-rater reliability.
- Protocol differences between experiments (supervised vs. MTurk, fixed vs. random order) may confound difficulty trends and within-participant learning effects.
- Deliberation time’s causal role in rule inference remains inferential; it could reflect other processes like perceptual encoding or motor planning.

## Confidence
- **Rule complexity → difficulty:** Medium (robust correlation, but rating reliability unclear)
- **Deliberation time → difficulty:** Medium (consistent pattern, alternative explanations possible)
- **Convergent errors → shared biases:** High (direct observation of identical incorrect outputs)

## Next Checks
1. **Complexity rating reliability:** Recruit new expert raters to independently score the 75 problems using the published criteria. Compute inter-rater reliability and correlate with behavioral difficulty.
2. **Example count manipulation:** Run a within-subject experiment varying the number of input-output examples (2, 4, 6). Test whether deliberation time and accuracy change systematically, isolating the rule extraction stage.
3. **Think-aloud convergence analysis:** Record verbal protocols during problem solving for a subset of items. Code explicit rule hypotheses and map them to final edit sequences to confirm that similar errors stem from similar misinterpretations.
