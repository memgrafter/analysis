# From Static Benchmarks to Dynamic Protocol: Agent-Centric Text Anomaly Detection for Evaluating LLM Reasoning

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.23729  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23729  
- **Reference Count:** 40  
- **Primary Result:** ATAD reduces average model accuracy by ~37.3 percentage points from base to final validated problems, systematically exposing reasoning failures missed by conventional static benchmarks while maintaining high problem quality and stable cross-model comparisons.

## Executive Summary
Static LLM benchmarks suffer from data contamination and saturation, leading to inflated scores that fail to track evolving model reasoning. This paper introduces ATAD, an agent-centric dynamic evaluation protocol where a Teacher generates anomaly detection problems, an Orchestrator validates them for clarity and fairness, and a Student model attempts solutions. Difficulty scales adaptively: upon Student success, the Teacher produces harder variants; upon failure, the problem is finalized. Using cross-sentence logical inference tasks across seven anomaly types, ATAD yields benchmarks that consistently challenge models, revealing substantial reasoning gaps. Across model families, accuracy drops significantly from base to final validated versions, confirming non-trivial difficulty scaling and offering a sustainable path for LLM evaluation.

## Method Summary
ATAD instantiates a three-agent loop for dynamic benchmark generation. For each task instance, a Teacher agent first produces a candidate problem, which an independent Orchestrator agent validates against criteria including well-formedness, logical coherence, task adherence, and fairness. Once approved, a Student model (the model under evaluation) attempts the problem. If the Student fails, the problem is added to the benchmark. If it succeeds, the Orchestrator provides feedback to increase difficulty, and the Teacher generates a harder variant, which must again be validated. This cycle repeats until the Student fails or a maximum iteration count is reached, finalizing the hardest valid problem the Student cannot solve. The protocol scales difficulty per-instance based on the specific Student’s demonstrated capabilities, creating a tailored learning curve. Key unknowns include the precise Orchestrator decision logic, the Teacher’s difficulty escalation algorithm, and whether Student reasoning must be explicit.

## Key Results
- Across seven anomaly types and multiple model families, average accuracy drops by ~37.3 percentage points from initial base problems to final validated problems.
- Finalized problems maintain high quality (validity, coherence, fairness) as enforced by the Orchestrator, with ablation studies showing that removing validation lowers both scores and quality.
- The protocol yields stable cross-model rankings and resists saturation, as stronger models still fail on problems finalized after their own successes.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Adaptive difficulty scaling exposes latent reasoning failures by continuously pushing beyond a model's current competence. A Teacher generates progressively harder variants after each correct Student solution, creating a per-instance learning curve calibrated to that model's limitations. The final item is the hardest valid problem the Student fails. This assumes models have untested reasoning capacities that static datasets cannot exhaustively probe. Evidence: the protocol definition describes iterative success-driven escalation; Section 2.2.2 details the cycle ending upon failure. A break condition occurs if the Student solves a problem but the Orchestrator rejects all harder variants, potentially under-scaling difficulty.

### An independent Orchestrator validates problems for clarity and fairness, preventing difficulty inflation through adversarial or ambiguous design. The Orchestrator applies multi-criteria checks (well-formedness, logical coherence, task adherence, fairness) to every Teacher-generated problem, providing structured feedback for rejected items and authorizing escalation only for validated variants. This separation is necessary because a Teacher incentivized solely to increase difficulty will eventually produce unsolvable or flawed problems. Evidence: the abstract mentions rigorous verification; Section 2.1 defines validation criteria; Section 4.4 shows benchmarks without an Orchestrator have lower scores and quality. A break condition: if the Orchestrator consistently rejects harder variants after initial success, escalation halts prematurely, possibly under-estimating model limits.

### The protocol avoids data contamination and saturation by generating novel, non-memorizable instances on-demand during evaluation. Problems are created from LLM-driven generative processes, not pre-curated. The final benchmark is a product of the specific interaction between generator and tested model at a given time, making memorization impractical. Evidence: the abstract contrasts static benchmarks; Section 1 discusses contamination; Section 4.5’s hypothetical future model scenario demonstrates resistance to saturation. A break condition: if the Teacher samples from known public content, novelty could be compromised; Orchestrator checks may not detect semantic plagiarism.

## Foundational Learning
- **Adversarial Curriculum Learning:** The Teacher actively creates problems the Student will fail, based on past successes. This "teaching by failure" drives dynamic difficulty scaling. *Why needed:* It calibrates difficulty to the specific model’s limits. *Quick check:* How does the protocol ensure harder variants test the same underlying reasoning skill, not just different topics?
- **Validation-Driven Generation:** The Orchestrator independently judges problem quality, separate from the Teacher’s generation goal. *Why needed:* Separation prevents the Teacher from producing ambiguous or unfair items in pursuit of difficulty. *Quick check:* What specific validation criteria cause rejection, and how does feedback guide the Teacher’s next attempt?
- **Instance-Level Difficulty Calibration:** Difficulty is determined per problem instance via the interaction trajectory with a specific Student model. *Why needed:* Static benchmarks assign global difficulty; ATAD tailors "hard" to the model’s demonstrated capability. *Quick check:* If a stronger or weaker Student were used during generation, how would the final benchmark’s difficulty profile change?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Teacher agent -> Orchestrator agent -> Student agent, with a non-LLM Protocol Controller managing the state machine, loop counters, and data storage. The Controller mediates all transitions and enforces `max_init_loops` and `max_student_loops`.
- **Critical path:**  
  1. Controller calls Teacher to generate a base problem.  
  2. Controller calls Orchestrator to validate; repeat Teacher→Orchestrator until valid or `max_init_loops` exhausted.  
  3. Controller calls Student to solve validated problem.  
  4. If Student fails: store problem, end trajectory.  
  5. If Student succeeds: Controller asks Orchestrator for feedback to increase difficulty.  
  6. Controller calls Teacher with feedback to generate a harder variant.  
  7. Controller calls Orchestrator to validate harder variant; if invalid, regenerate (possibly with reduced difficulty) until valid or `max_student_loops` hit; if still invalid, finalize previous problem.  
  8. If variant valid, set as current problem and loop back to step 3.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Same-model vs cross-family agents: Using one LLM family for all roles simplifies setup but may embed heuristics; cross-family configurations increase diversity but risk inconsistent validation styles.  
  - Validation strictness: Rigorous Orchestrator ensures quality but slows generation; lenient Orchestrator increases throughput but risks low-quality items.  
  - Single-instance vs batch: Processing one instance fully ensures clean difficulty calibration but is compute-intensive; batch parallelization loses tight Teacher-Student feedback.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - High rejection rate at initialization (> `max_init_loops`): Teacher cannot generate valid base problems (overly restrictive specs or poor prompt).  
  - Early termination in adaptive phase (Student fails first attempt): Base problem too hard (Orchestrator approved borderline item or Teacher skipped to high difficulty).  
  - Orchestrator rejects all harder variants after one success: Teacher cannot increment difficulty without losing validity (insufficient feedback or inherent task ceiling).  
  - Inconsistent model rankings across generator families: Orchestrator may not fully neutralize generator-specific artifacts (though bias index suggests minimal effect).
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Baseline validation test:** Run protocol with same model for all roles; report % of base problems approved on first try and avg. validation loops per problem. Expect moderate initial rejection (~30-50%).  
  2. **Difficulty scaling sanity check:** For a fixed generator family (e.g., GPT-4o), evaluate a held-out model (e.g., Claude-3.5-Sonnet) on base vs. final problems. Must observe significant accuracy drop (~37 pp).  
  3. **Orchestrator ablation:** Generate benchmark without Orchestrator’s difficulty validation (Teacher directly increases difficulty after success). Evaluate same models on this set and the full protocol’s set. Expect lower scores and lower quality (validity/coherence) on the no-Orchestrator set.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Can the ATAD protocol be extended to evaluate reasoning tasks that require external knowledge (e.g., science, math) without undermining the Orchestrator’s ability to maintain clarity and fairness?  
2. How effectively does ATAD generalize to other reasoning domains beyond text anomaly detection, such as mathematical reasoning, code, or tool-use, and what modifications are necessary?  
3. Can the Orchestrator’s validation process be automated or learned rather than relying on manually crafted prompts, and what is the impact on benchmark quality and reliability?  
4. How do heterogeneous agent configurations (different model families for Teacher, Orchestrator, Student) affect the stability, difficulty scaling, and potential bias of the generated benchmarks?

## Limitations
- Orchestrator decision logic (criteria weighting) is underspecified, risking inconsistent validation.
- Teacher’s difficulty escalation algorithm lacks concrete parameters for increment adjustments.
- Scope is limited to seven anomaly types; generalizability to other reasoning domains remains unproven.
- Potential early termination if Orchestrator rejects harder variants, possibly under-scaling difficulty.
- LLM agent biases may persist despite reported low bias index.

## Confidence
- Adaptive difficulty scaling exposes failures: **Medium** (empirical support but underspecification).
- Orchestrator quality control is essential: **Medium** (ablation evidence but opaque logic).
- Protocol avoids data contamination: **Medium** (novelty plausible, no direct test).
- Generates stable cross-model comparisons: **Medium** (consistent rankings, limited scope).
