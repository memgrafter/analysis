# Evaluation of Deontic Conditional Reasoning in Large Language Models: The Case of Wason's Selection Task

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.06416  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06416  
- **Reference count:** 16  
- **Primary result:** LLMs achieve 5.0%–41.2% higher accuracy on deontic vs. descriptive conditional reasoning tasks and exhibit a strong lexical matching bias, selecting cards matching the rule’s explicit terms even when logically incorrect.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates whether large language models (LLMs) exhibit domain-specific conditional reasoning using a modified Wason Selection Task. A new dataset of 160 problems systematically varied rule modality (deontic vs. descriptive) and polarity (handling negation) to distinguish matching bias from confirmation bias. Across five model families and multiple prompting strategies (zero-shot, few-shot, chain-of-thought), LLMs consistently performed better on deontic rules. Error analysis revealed a robust preference for selecting cards that lexically matched the antecedent and consequent of the rule, even when negated—indicating a matching bias rather than a confirmation bias. These findings suggest that LLM reasoning is shaped by statistical patterns in training data and may share processing mechanisms with human intuition.

## Method Summary
The researchers created a dataset of 160 Wason Selection Task problems, varying rule modality (deontic/descriptive) and four polarity conditions to manipulate negation. Five model families were tested under zero-shot, few-shot, and chain-of-thought prompts. Model outputs were parsed to extract selected card numbers, which were compared against gold-standard logical answers via exact-match scoring. Accuracy and selection patterns were aggregated by condition to measure domain effects and identify bias types (matching vs. confirmation). This design allowed systematic isolation of modality and negation handling.

## Key Results
- Deontic rules yield significantly higher accuracy than descriptive rules, with improvements ranging from 5.0% to 41.2% across models and prompts.
- LLMs show a strong matching bias: they preferentially select cards matching the literal terms *p* (antecedent) and *q* (consequent) even when those terms are negated in the rule, particularly for descriptive rules.
- Chain-of-Thought prompting partially mitigates matching bias and boosts deontic reasoning, especially in smaller models, though bias persists.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Deontic Domain Specialization
- **Claim:** LLMs perform better on deontic conditionals because pre-training exposes them to statistical associations between deontic modality (e.g., “must”, “obliged”) and correct falsification patterns.
- **Mechanism:** In legal, moral, and normative texts, deontic rules are frequently checked by seeking counterexamples (selecting *p* and ¬*q*), reinforcing a heuristic that aligns with the Wason task’s requirements.
- **Core assumption:** LLMs approximate logical deduction via surface-level correlations; deontic domains provide richer, more consistent signals for the needed pattern.
- **Evidence anchors:** Systematic accuracy gains for deontic rules across all models and prompts (Table 2, abstract); supporting literature on normative reasoning in LLMs.
- **Break condition:** If training data systematically excludes deontic normative contexts, the performance gap would collapse.

### Mechanism 2: Lexical Matching Bias
- **Claim:** LLMs default to matching bias (selecting lexically matching items) rather than confirmation bias due to token-level pattern matching inherent in Transformer attention.
- **Mechanism:** Conditional rules are parsed as token sequences; negation (“not”) is often a separate token with weak logical binding, leading models to prefer cards matching the affirmative forms (*p*, *q*) even when the rule contains negated antecedents or consequents.
- **Core assumption:** Attention prioritizes local co-occurrence statistics over global logical form, especially under short-context, zero-shot conditions.
- **Evidence anchors:** Strong preference for *p* over ¬*p* and *q* over ¬*q* across polarity patterns, especially in descriptive rules (Table 4, abstract); experimental design explicitly contrasts matching vs. confirmation bias.
- **Break condition:** Fine-tuning on negation-heavy datasets or prompting for logical formalization would reduce the bias.

### Mechanism 3: Chain-of-Thought Mitigation
- **Claim:** Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting partially reduces matching bias and enhances deontic reasoning by externalizing intermediate reasoning steps.
- **Mechanism:** The reasoning trace may activate higher-level pattern matching over logical structure, helping models reorient from lexical to logical considerations; for deontic rules, it could also trigger norm-related schemas.
- **Core assumption:** CoT serves as a cognitive scaffold that decouples card selection from immediate lexical associations, though effectiveness depends on the model’s instruction-following capability.
- **Evidence anchors:** CoT improves accuracy (e.g., Gemma3-4b on deontic from 17.5% to 38.8%) and shifts selection patterns (Supplemental Table 16), though matching bias remains.
- **Break condition:** Poorly designed CoT prompts or non-instruction-tuned models may not yield improvement and could amplify biases.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Dataset Generator → Prompt Manager → Model Inference Client → Response Parser & Scorer  
- **Critical path:** Define rule polarity & modality → instantiate dataset item → apply prompt → query model → parse response → score → aggregate by condition → analyze selection percentages vs. logical/biased expectations.  
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Dataset size vs. coverage:* 160 items (20 per polarity-modality cell) balances power and construction effort; more would improve reliability but require additional validation.  
  - *Prompt simplicity vs. guidance:* Zero-shot is clean but may not elicit structured reasoning; few-shot with incorrect exemplars prevents memorization but may confuse; CoT allows long reasoning but increases cost and variability.  
  - *Scoring strictness:* Exact-match is conservative but may penalize logically equivalent but differently phrased selections; softer scoring could better separate reasoning from bias.  
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - High matching bias: persistent selection of *p* and *q* even in negated rules (Table 4 patterns).  
  - Prompt insensitivity: similar error patterns across zero-shot, few-shot, and CoT.  
  - Deontic benefit absence: accuracy difference between deontic and descriptive <5%.  
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Replicate domain specificity: Run all models with zero-shot prompts; compute deontic minus descriptive accuracy per model. Expect positive delta, larger for reasoning models.  
  2. Isolate negation handling: For descriptive rules, compare selection rates of *p* vs. ¬*p* (FA column) and *q* vs. ¬*q* (TC column) across polarities. Strong *p*/*q* preference indicates matching bias.  
  3. Test CoT effect: Compare matching bias metrics (*p*/¬*p* and *q*/¬*q* ratios) between zero-shot and CoT for the same model (e.g., Gemma3-4b). Reduction shows CoT mitigation.

## Limitations
- Dataset is synthetic (LLM-generated + human adjustment); ecological validity for real-world deontic reasoning is unclear.
- Only five model families (2024–2025) tested; generalizability to newer or closed-source models remains unknown.
- Exact-match scoring may penalize logically equivalent but differently phrased selections, potentially understating true reasoning capability.
- Mechanisms are inferred from patterns without direct causal manipulation (e.g., attention analysis).

## Confidence
- Deontic advantage: **High**  
- Matching bias over confirmation bias: **High**  
- CoT mitigation: **Medium**  
- Proposed mechanisms (statistical association, token-level bias): **Medium**  

## Next Checks
1. **Replicate with expanded model set:** Test newer open/closed models (e.g., GPT-4.1, Claude 4) to confirm deontic benefit generalizes.  
2. **Implement fuzzy scoring:** Accept selections that logically imply correct cards (e.g., selecting both *p* and ¬*q* when only ¬*q* is required) to assess sensitivity to scoring strictness.  
3. **Prompt for formalization:** Add prompts requiring models to output the rule in logical notation (e.g., `p → q`) before selecting cards; measure reduction in matching bias.
