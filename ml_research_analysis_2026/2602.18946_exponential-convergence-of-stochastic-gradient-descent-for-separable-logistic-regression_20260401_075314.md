# Exponential Convergence of (Stochastic) Gradient Descent for Separable Logistic Regression
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.18946
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18946
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: A simple, non‑adaptive, increasing step‑size schedule for GD yields exponential‑like convergence for separable logistic regression; an adaptive step‑size for SGD achieves fast expected hitting time to ε‑suboptimality.

## Executive Summary
The paper studies gradient descent (GD) and stochastic gradient descent (SGD) for binary logistic regression under the assumption that the data are linearly separable. It introduces a non‑adaptive, increasing step‑size schedule for GD that depends only on the initial loss and the margin γ. This schedule ensures the logistic loss never exceeds 1/η_t and achieves a convergence rate L(w_t) ≤ C t^{2/3} exp(-c t^{1/3}), where c and C are determined by γ. The method is anytime and does not require knowledge of the optimization horizon or target precision. For SGD, the authors propose an adaptive per‑iteration step‑size η_t = min{1/ε, 1/L_{i_t}(w_t)} based on the local Lipschitz constant of the sampled loss. This yields an expected hitting time to ε‑suboptimality bounded by 2n/γ² ln²(4n/ε). A block‑adaptive variant removes the dependence on ε and attains total iterations O(n/γ² ln³(n/ε)). Theoretical findings are supported by experiments on synthetic data and MNIST.

## Method Summary
For GD, the step‑size is set to increase with the iteration counter (e.g., η_t ∝ t^{1/3}), which counteracts early large gradients and leverages the curvature of the logistic loss under separability to keep the loss bounded. For SGD, the step‑size is chosen as the reciprocal of the current sample’s loss Lipschitz constant, capped at 1/ε; this automatically scales the step according to local curvature, ensuring stability and fast progress. The block‑adaptive version applies this idea only after a block of samples, eliminating the need to specify ε. All schedules are straightforward to implement; the GD schedule requires only a lower bound on the margin γ, while the SGD schedules are fully automatic.

## Key Results
- GD with increasing step‑size: L(w_t) ≤ C t^{2/3} exp(-c t^{1/3}) with c, C ∝ 1/γ; the method is anytime and non‑adaptive.
- SGD with adaptive step‑size: Expected iterations to reach ε‑suboptimality ≤ (2n/γ²) ln²(4n/ε).
- Block‑adaptive SGD: Total iterations O((n/γ²) ln³(n/ε)) without prior knowledge of ε.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Separability ensures a positive margin γ, which lower‑bounds the projection of data points onto the separating hyperplane. Under logistic loss, the gradient norm scales with the loss, and the Hessian’s eigenvalues are tied to the data distribution. The increasing step‑size for GD grows the step as the iterates approach the optimum, preventing large early updates that could blow up the loss; the specific schedule maintains the invariant L(w_t) ≤ 1/η_t, leading to an exponential‑like decay when combined with a Lyapunov potential. For SGD, using η_t = 1/L_{i_t}(w_t) scales each step inversely to the local curvature of the sampled example, providing implicit preconditioning. This adaptivity avoids overshooting and yields a super‑linear decrease in expected suboptimality. The martingale‑based analysis bounds the hitting time; the block version averages curvature over blocks to remove the ε‑dependence.

## Foundational Learning
- **Separable data**: Existence of a weight vector with zero training error; necessary for a positive margin. Quick check: Train a linear SVM; if it achieves 100% accuracy, data is separable.
- **Margin (γ)**: Minimum distance of training points to the decision boundary; dictates convergence constants. Quick check: For a separating w, compute min_i y_i (w·x_i)/||w||.
- **Logistic loss**: L(w)=Σ log(1+exp(-y_i w·x_i)); smooth and convex with well‑behaved gradients/Hessians. Quick check: Verify smoothness constant L via data norm.
- **Increasing step‑size schedule**: Step‑size grows with iteration (e.g., ∝ t^{1/3}); counters early large gradients. Quick check: Implement η_t = η₀ (t+1)^{1/3} and monitor loss behavior.
- **Adaptive per‑sample step‑size**: η_t = min{1/ε, 1/L_{i_t}(w_t)} where L_{i_t}(w) ≈ |y_i x_i|²/4. Quick check: Compute L_i(w) on the fly and use its reciprocal as step‑size.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Data → Model → Loss → Gradient → Update (GD); Data stream → Sample → Loss → Gradient → Adaptive step‑size → Update (SGD).  
**Critical path**: Initialize weights w. For each iteration t: (GD) compute full gradient ∇L(w); (SGD) draw random index i_t and compute gradient ∇ℓ_i(w)(w). Compute step‑size η_t according to the chosen schedule. Update w ← w - η_t ∇ℓ. Repeat until convergence criterion met.  
**Design tradeoffs**:  
- *Complexity vs adaptivity*: GD’s increasing step‑size is simple but needs γ; SGD’s adaptive step‑size is automatic but requires per‑sample loss evaluations, raising per‑iteration cost.  
- *Anytime vs horizon‑dependent*: GD schedule is anytime; block‑adaptive SGD removes ε‑dependence at the price of block‑level granularity.  
- *Practical acceleration*: The polynomial factor t^{2/3} in GD’s rate may reduce the observed exponential gain; SGD’s high‑probability bounds may be loose in practice.  
**Failure signatures**:  
- Data not truly separable → margin γ = 0, invalidating guarantees; GD may diverge, SGD may never reach ε‑optimality.  
- Under‑estimated γ → overly aggressive steps, loss spikes, instability.  
- Outliers with huge curvature → SGD step becomes extremely tiny, causing stagnation.  
- Numerical issues: per‑sample Lipschitz computation may overflow or suffer precision loss.  
**Three first experiments**:
1. **Synthetic separable test**: Generate linearly separable data with known γ. Run GD with the proposed increasing step‑size and compare convergence to constant step‑size GD; verify the predicted decay law.
2. **MNIST binary classification**: Choose digits 0 vs 1. Apply adaptive SGD and block‑adaptive SGD; measure iterations to reach a given ε and compare to standard tuned SGD.
3. **Margin estimation robustness**: On the synthetic data, compute an empirical margin using an SVM instead of the true γ. Use this estimate in the GD schedule and evaluate convergence speed and stability relative to the true‑γ case.

## Limitations
- The theoretical analysis hinges on perfect separability, an assumption rarely met in real‑world datasets.
- Convergence rates and step‑size schedules depend on the true margin γ, which is typically unknown and may be hard to estimate accurately.
- The adaptive SGD variant requires per‑sample loss evaluations, increasing per‑iteration computational overhead compared to simpler schemes.
