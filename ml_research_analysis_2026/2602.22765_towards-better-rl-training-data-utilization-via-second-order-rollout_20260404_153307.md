# Towards Better RL Training Data Utilisation via Second-Order Rollout

## Quick Facts

- **ArXiv ID**: 2602.22765
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22765
- **Reference count**: 36
- **Primary result**: GC-RL (joint generation-critique training via second-order rollout) achieves 59.3% generation accuracy and 78.6% critique accuracy on Qwen2.5-7B, surpassing both G-RL and C-RL.

## Executive Summary

The paper argues that standard reinforcement learning (RL) fine-tuning for large language models (LLMs) underutilises training data by relying solely on first-order rollouts (generating multiple responses) and ignoring the training of critique capabilities. To address this, it introduces second-order rollout: generating multiple critiques for stored (question, response) pairs, and proposes GC-RL, a unified framework that jointly trains generation and critique models within a single RL loop. A data filter ensures balanced critique labels by retaining one correct and one incorrect response per question when possible, preventing label bias. Mixed rollouts (responses and critiques) are used to update the policy model via GRPO. Evaluations on Qwen2.5 (1.5B–7B), Llama, and Mistral across Math-500, GSM8k, and other benchmarks show consistent gains: GC-RL outperforms both generation-only (G-RL) and critique-only (C-RL) baselines in their respective metrics. Further analysis highlights the data filter's essential role, the benefit of reward denoising via self-correction sampling, and trade-offs between dynamic and static data.

## Method Summary

GC-RL extends standard RL fine-tuning by adding a critique training phase. Starting from a cold-start model obtained by supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on a small set of high-quality GPT-5–distilled critiques, the training proceeds in alternating steps. First, a batch of questions is sampled and first-order rollouts generate *n* responses each. These responses are evaluated via a verifier to determine correctness. A data filter discards questions where all responses are uniformly correct or incorrect; for the remaining mixed questions, one correct and one incorrect response are randomly selected and stored in a cache. Second, from this cache (without replacement), pairs of (question, response) are sampled and second-order rollouts generate *n* critiques each. Critiques are rewarded based on outcome: 0.7 if the final judgment matches the response's correctness, else 0; an optional denoising step scales this reward by the fraction of self-correction samples that become correct. The policy model updates on a mixed mini-batch containing trajectories from both rollouts, with advantages computed by GRPO. The process repeats, gradually improving both generation and critique abilities under the same training data.

## Key Results

- On Qwen2.5-7B, GC-RL reaches 59.3% average generation accuracy, compared to 56.7% for G-RL and 48.3% for C-RL.
- GC-RL also achieves 78.6% critique accuracy, compared to 73.8% for C-RL and 65.1% for G-RL.
- Similar relative improvements hold for smaller models (1.5B) and other architectures (Llama3.1-8B, Mistral-7B) across Math-500, GSM8k, and other math benchmarks.
- Ablation studies show that removing the balanced data filter causes a large drop in both generation and critique accuracy, confirming its importance.
- Reward denoising via self-correction sampling further boosts performance, particularly on the challenging Math-500 dataset.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Joint Training Synergy

- **Claim**: Joint training via second-order rollout improves both generation and critique capabilities more than training either alone, by exploiting complementary learning signals.
- **Mechanism**: First-order rollout (multiple responses) trains generation. Second-order rollout (multiple critiques for stored pairs) trains critique. The policy updates on a mixed batch, allowing gradients from critique to inform generation and vice versa.
- **Core assumption**: Generation and critique capabilities are synergistic.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract states joint training achieves better performance under same data; Table 1 shows GC-RL outperforms G-RL and C-RL; neighbour "Critique-Coder" suggests critique training aids task performance.
- **Break condition**: If critique capability is too weak/noisy, gradients from second-order rollout may mislead generation, causing instability (observed without filter or denoising).

### Mechanism 2: Balanced Data Filter for Stable Training

- **Claim**: The data filter that enforces a 1:1 ratio of correct to incorrect responses per question prevents label imbalance and stabilizes joint training.
- **Mechanism**: The filter discards questions where all *n* responses are uniformly correct or incorrect. For mixed questions, it randomly selects one correct and one incorrect response to store, guaranteeing balanced binary labels for second-order rollout.
- **Core assumption**: Binary classification training is sensitive to label distribution; extreme imbalance causes majority-class bias.
- **Evidence anchors**: Section 3 describes the filter; Table 2 shows random sampling (imbalanced) yields worst accuracy, while filter gives best; neighbour "Not All Rollouts are Useful" discusses down-sampling but not balance.
- **Break condition**: If most questions yield uniformly correct/incorrect responses (e.g., very easy/hard datasets), the filter may discard too much data, reducing sample size.

### Mechanism 3: Reward Denoising via Self-Correction Sampling

- **Claim**: Reward denoising mitigates noise in outcome-based rewards for critiques, improving both critique and subsequent generation performance.
- **Mechanism**: For a critique, sample *n* refined responses based on it. Use the accuracy of these refinements (*k/n*) to scale the outcome reward, providing finer granularity.
- **Core assumption**: Critique quality correlates with correctness of refined responses; a good critique enables successful self-correction.
- **Evidence anchors**: Section 5.2 defines denoising reward; Figure 3 shows denoising improves metrics; neighbour "Confidence v.s. Critique" decomposes self-correction.
- **Break condition**: If self-correction yields low diversity or refinement ability is poor (base model cannot correct even with good critiques), *k/n* becomes uninformative and denoising fails.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
- **Policy Model**: generates both responses (first-order) and critiques (second-order).
- **Data Cache**: stores filtered <question, correct response> and <question, incorrect response> pairs.
- **Data Filter**: ensures balanced labels (1 correct, 1 incorrect per mixed question).
- **Rollout Modules**: First-order (question → multiple responses), Second-order (cached <q,r> → multiple critiques).
- **Reward Functions**: R(r) for responses (binary correctness via verifier), R(c) for critiques (outcome-based, optionally denoised).
- **Mixed Update**: combines all trajectories, computes advantages via GRPO, updates policy.

Flow: Questions → First-order Rollout → Data Filter → Data Cache → Second-order Rollout → Rewards → Mixed Update → Policy Model.

### Critical Path

1. Sample a batch of questions from the training set.
2. Perform first-order rollout: generate *n* responses per question.
3. Apply the data filter: keep only questions with mixed correctness; from each, randomly select one correct and one incorrect response.
4. Insert selected pairs into the data cache.
5. Sample a batch of cached <q,r> pairs (without replacement).
6. Perform second-order rollout: generate *n* critiques per pair.
7. Assign rewards: response reward R(r) = 1 if verifier says correct else 0; critique reward R(c) = 0.7 if final judgment matches response correctness else 0 (optionally scaled by self-correction accuracy *k/n*).
8. Mix all trajectories, compute group advantages via GRPO, and perform a policy update step.

### Design Tradeoffs

- **Dynamic data**: Self-generated responses/critiques adapt to current policy, beneficial for GC-RL, but risk reward hacking if used alone for C-RL (model learns to produce easily critiqued wrong answers).
- **Static data**: Pre-curated pairs avoid hacking in C-RL but may not align with evolving policy in joint training; less effective for GC-RL.
- **Data filter**: Essential for label balance and stable training, but may reduce cache size if many questions yield uniform correctness (e.g., trivial or impossible tasks).
- **Self-correction denoising**: Improves reward quality at additional compute cost; effectiveness depends on model's refinement ability.

### Failure Signatures

- **Training instability**: Sudden loss spikes or divergence, typically indicating missing or ineffective data filter (label imbalance).
- **Degraded critique accuracy**: Low precision/recall due to noisy outcome rewards; monitor critique validation accuracy.
- **Reward hacking in C-RL**: Generation accuracy collapses while critique accuracy remains high; indicates model is generating intentionally wrong answers to get easy critique rewards.
- **Cache starvation**: The data filter discards all responses for many questions, leading to insufficient second-order data and stalling progress.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **Generalisation beyond GRPO and small models**  
   *Question*: Does GC-RL generalise to RL algorithms other than GRPO and to models larger than 10B parameters across multiple domains?  
   *Basis*: Limitations: "For simplicity, we only employ the GRPO algorithm… applicability to other RL algorithms (such as PPO) remains to be investigated. Moreover, experiments are confined to the mathematical domain using models with fewer than 10B parameters, and extending our approach to larger-scale RL training with multi-domain training data and larger models is considered an important direction."  
   *Why unresolved*: Only evaluated with GRPO on 1.5B–7B models on math tasks; unknown with other algorithms and larger scales.  
   *Resolution evidence*: Comprehensive experiments comparing GC-RL with PPO/other RL on large models (e.g., Llama3-70B) across multiple domains to measure sample efficiency and final accuracy.

2. **Second-order rollout for rubric-based tasks**  
   *Question*: How can second-order rollout and critique rewards be designed for RL tasks without rule-based verifiable correctness (e.g., rubric-based evaluation)?  
   *Basis*: Limitations: "Our approach also requires that responses can be rule‑based verified, making it less straightforward to apply to RL tasks where responses are harder to evaluate (e.g., rubric‑based RL training). How to perform second‑order rollout and assign rewards to critiques in such settings is also worth further exploration."  
   *Why unresolved*: GC-RL depends on binary outcome rewards; many real-world tasks use soft rubrics or human preferences where automatic verification is noisy or impossible.  
   *Resolution evidence*: Propose a framework where critiques are rewarded via learned reward models or LLM‑as‑judge with proper calibration; evaluate on rubric‑based tasks (e.g., summarization, instruction following) to show joint training improves both generation and critique metrics over vanilla RL.

3. **Convergence speed of GC-RL**  
   *Question*: What causes the slower convergence of GC-RL compared to vanilla RL, and how can convergence be accelerated without sacrificing performance gains?  
   *Basis*: Limitations: "We have observed that our approach converges more slowly compared to vanilla RL, essentially trading computational resources for improved performance."  
   *Why unresolved*: The paper does not diagnose the source of slow convergence—whether from joint objective interference, data mixing, or critique training instability—nor does it offer solutions, limiting practical efficiency.  
   *Resolution evidence*: Ablation studies analysing training dynamics (e.g., gradient norms, KL divergence, loss curves) and testing modifications like adaptive mixing ratios, curriculum learning, or separate warm‑up phases for critique training to speed up convergence while preserving accuracy.

4. **Optimal self‑correction sampling budget**  
   *Question*: What is the optimal number of self‑correction samples for denoising critique rewards under varying computational budgets?  
   *Basis*: Inferred: §5.2 introduces denoising and notes: "Theoretically, a larger number of samples *n* leads to better noise reduction and more accurate reward values, but at a higher computational cost." Only *n*=1 was tested.  
   *Why unresolved*: The optimal sampling number likely depends on model size, task difficulty, and available compute; without systematic study, practitioners cannot balance noise reduction and cost effectively.  
   *Resolution evidence*: Controlled experiments varying *n* (e.g., 1, 2, 5, 10) on multiple datasets and model scales, reporting generation/critique accuracy vs. compute time; possibly derive a heuristic to select *n* based on desired performance or resource constraints.

## Limitations

- Distillation source (GPT‑5) data is unavailable; substituting other models may alter cold‑start quality and final performance.
- Rule‑based verifier and answer‑parsing logic are unspecified, leading to potential implementation discrepancies and affecting reward assignment/evaluation.
- The balanced data filter may discard many questions in edge cases (e.g., very easy/hard datasets), reducing cache size; dynamic data in C‑RL alone can cause reward hacking; denoising effectiveness depends on the model’s self‑correction ability.
