# CaDrift: A Time-dependent Causal Generator of Drifting Data Streams

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.20329
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20329
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: CaDrift generates realistic time-dependent data streams with controllable concept drifts using a structural causal model enhanced with EWMA and autoregressive noise.

## Executive Summary
Existing synthetic data stream generators produce i.i.d. data, ignoring causal relationships and temporal dependence. The paper presents CaDrift, a novel generator based on a time‑dependent structural causal model (SCM). It incorporates exponentially weighted moving averages (EWMA) and autoregressive noise to induce serial correlation in samples, enabling the simulation of realistic streaming data.

CaDrift allows controlled concept drifts—including distributional, covariate, severe, and local shifts—by altering causal mappings or root node distributions, with occasional interventions to model perturbations. Experiments show that classifier accuracy declines after drift events and gradually recovers, confirming the generator’s effectiveness. Statistical tests (Ljung-Box) validate the non‑stationarity of the streams, and benchmarks demonstrate that CaDrift yields more challenging and temporally evolving data than prior synthetic generators. The implementation is publicly available.

## Method Summary
At the core, CaDrift employs a structural causal model where each variable is generated by a function of its parents plus a noise term. To introduce time dependence, the contribution from parents is passed through an exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA) filter, creating serial correlation in the deterministic component. Additionally, the noise term follows an autoregressive (AR) process, further embedding temporal memory.

Concept drifts are simulated by modifying the generative process during the stream: either by changing the functional relationships (e.g., flipping the sign of a causal effect) or by altering the distribution parameters of root nodes. Interventions can also be inserted to represent external shocks. The generated stream consists of sequential samples, and its non‑stationarity can be verified via statistical tests such as Ljung‑Box. The authors evaluated the generator by training classifiers on the streams and observing performance drops at drift points followed by gradual recovery, illustrating the generator’s utility for testing adaptive algorithms.

## Key Results
- Non‑stationarity is statistically validated using the Ljung‑Box test, confirming significant autocorrelation in generated streams.
- Classifier performance exhibits a sharp decline following drift events and subsequently recovers, demonstrating realistic adaptation challenges.
- CaDrift yields more challenging and temporally evolving benchmarks compared to prior synthetic data generators, as shown by comparative analysis.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
CaDrift works by augmenting structural causal models with time‑dependent transformations. The use of exponentially weighted moving averages (EWMA) on parent contributions introduces short‑term serial correlation, while autoregressive (AR) noise models longer‑term dependencies. By controlling these elements and allowing changes to causal functions or exogenous distributions at predetermined times, the generator can simulate various types of concept drift, making the resulting streams challenging for adaptive classifiers.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Structural Causal Models (SCMs)**  
   *Why needed*: To encode causal relationships between variables, enabling generation of data with interpretable dependencies.  
   *Quick check*: Confirm the causal graph is a directed acyclic graph (DAG) and that each node’s function is well‑defined.
2. **Exponentially Weighted Moving Average (EWMA)**  
   *Why needed*: To introduce short‑term serial correlation by smoothing parent contributions over time.  
   *Quick check*: Ensure the smoothing factor α ∈ (0,1] and that the recurrence yₜ = α·xₜ + (1−α)·yₜ₋₁ is correctly implemented.
3. **Autoregressive (AR) Noise**  
   *Why needed*: To add longer‑term temporal dependence to the stochastic component of each node.  
   *Quick check*: For an AR(1) process, check that |ρ| < 1 and noise εₜ is i.i.d. with zero mean.
4. **Concept Drift**  
   *Why needed*: To simulate real‑world changes in data distributions that challenge adaptive learning systems.  
   *Quick check*: Verify that the joint distribution P(X,Y) changes at specified time points while respecting the causal structure.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**:  
Root Node Distributions → Causal Functions (with EWMA smoothing) → AR Noise Injection → Output Stream

**Critical Path**:
1. Sample all root (exogenous) variables from their predefined distributions.
2. Process the remaining nodes in topological order: for each node, compute the deterministic contribution from its parents, then apply the EWMA filter to obtain a temporally correlated value.
3. Generate an autoregressive noise sequence for the node and add it to the deterministic part.
4. Collect the values of all observed variables to form the current time step’s sample.
5. Advance time and update internal EWMA and AR states.

**Design Tradeoffs**:
- *Temporal dependence parameters* (EWMA decay α, AR coefficient ρ) control the strength of serial correlation but increase autocorrelation; higher values make streams more realistic but also more challenging for learners.
- *Causal graph complexity* (number of nodes, edge density) affects interpretability and computational cost; overly complex graphs may hinder analysis and slow generation.
- *Drift configuration* (abrupt vs gradual, severity) influences detection difficulty and the recovery behavior of adaptive models; overly severe drifts may be unrealistic and cause irreversible performance collapse.

**Failure Signatures**:
- The generated stream fails the Ljung‑Box test for autocorrelation (p > 0.05), indicating insufficient temporal dependence, often due to EWMA decay α ≈ 0 or AR coefficient ρ ≈ 0.
- Classifier accuracy drops to near‑zero after a drift event and never recovers, suggesting the drift is too abrupt, severe, or that the causal model becomes degenerate.
- Generation produces exploding or NaN values, typically caused by cycles in the causal graph or by parameter choices leading to instability (e.g., AR coefficient ≥ 1).

**First Experiments**:
1. Generate a stream with a single abrupt covariate shift (e.g., change root node mean) and plot classifier accuracy over time to observe the expected dip and gradual recovery.
2. Systematically vary the EWMA decay factor α across a range (e.g., 0.1 to 0.9) and run the Ljung‑Box test on produced streams to confirm that higher α yields stronger autocorrelation.
3. Compare CaDrift against an i.i.d. synthetic benchmark on a drift detection task, measuring detection delay and true positive rate for incremental drifts to demonstrate increased challenge.

## Limitations
- The approach requires prior knowledge of the true causal structure, which may be difficult to obtain for complex real‑world systems.
- The study does not assess the framework’s behavior on high‑dimensional data or with densely connected causal graphs, limiting conclusions about scalability.
- Parameter selection for EWMA decay and AR coefficients may be non‑trivial and could require extensive search to achieve desired drift characteristics.

## Confidence
- CaDrift can generate time‑dependent streams with controlled concept drifts: **High** (statistical tests and experiments support this claim).
- The generated streams are more challenging than existing synthetic benchmarks: **Medium** (comparative experiments show improvement but are limited to a few setups).
- The framework generalizes to arbitrary high‑dimensional causal models: **Low** (no evidence provided for scalability).

## Next Checks
1. Validate CaDrift against real‑world streaming data by comparing key statistics (e.g., autocorrelation, drift severity) to ensure ecological validity.
2. Conduct an ablation study isolating the contributions of EWMA and AR noise to quantify their individual impact on temporal dependence and drift realism.
3. Establish a set of parameter guidelines by systematically exploring a grid of EWMA decay, AR coefficient, and drift frequencies, then reporting their effects on classification difficulty.
