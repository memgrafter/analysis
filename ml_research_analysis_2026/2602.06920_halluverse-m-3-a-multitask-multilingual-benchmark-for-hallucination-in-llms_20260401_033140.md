# Halluverse-M^3: A multitask multilingual benchmark for hallucination in LLMs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.06920
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06920
- **Reference count:** 11
- **Primary result:** Halluverse-M^3 introduces a multilingual, multi-task dataset for fine-grained hallucination detection; evaluation shows QA consistently outperforms summarization, sentence-level hallucinations are hardest to detect, and performance is highest in English and lowest in Hindi. GPT-4o achieves peak QA accuracy of 88.6% (English) but summarization accuracy drops to 80.2% (English); sentence-level detection accuracy reaches 74.4% (QA) and 67.6% (summarization) in English.

## Executive Summary
Halluverse-M^3 is a novel benchmark designed to evaluate large language models on fine-grained hallucination detection across multiple languages and tasks. The dataset covers four languages (English, Arabic, Hindi, Turkish) and two generation tasks—question answering and dialogue summarization—by injecting controlled hallucinations at the entity, relation, and sentence levels. Through a rigorous construction process involving LLM-based editing and human validation, the benchmark yields high inter-annotator agreement (Cohen’s κ ≥ 0.74). Extensive evaluation of state-of-the-art LLMs via prompting reveals several key findings: question answering is consistently easier than summarization; sentence-level hallucinations remain challenging even for the strongest models; and performance varies widely by language, with English leading and Hindi lagging. GPT-4o achieves the best results, peaking at 88.6% QA accuracy in English, yet still falls short on sentence-level detection. The benchmark highlights persistent gaps in multilingual and discourse-level hallucination understanding.

## Method Summary
The authors built Halluverse-M^3 by first selecting existing datasets: Lin et al. (2022) for QA and DialogSum for dialogue summarization. Reference outputs were professionally translated into Arabic, Hindi, and Turkish using Google Translate followed by native speaker verification to ensure quality. Controlled hallucinations were then automatically injected using GPT-4o with strict prompts designed to alter exactly one element—either an entity, a relation, or an entire sentence—while preserving fluency and grammatical correctness. Each edited pair was independently labeled by two native speakers per language according to a detailed protocol; disagreements were resolved through joint review, yielding high Cohen’s κ values (0.83 EN, 0.79 AR, 0.76 TR, 0.74 HI). Invalid instances (e.g., grammatical errors, multiple error types) were filtered out, resulting in a clean test set. Evaluation was performed via prompting only: models received a reference output and a candidate (potentially hallucinated) output and were instructed to classify the hallucination type (ENTITY, RELATION, SENTENCE). No fine-tuning or internal model access was used, ensuring a zero/few-shot assessment of inherent reasoning abilities. Accuracy was computed separately for each task, language, and hallucination category.

## Key Results
- QA hallucination detection accuracy is consistently higher than summarization across all languages and models.
- Sentence-level hallucinations are the most difficult to detect; even GPT-4o reaches only 74.4% (QA) and 67.6% (summarization) in English.
- Performance shows a strong language bias: English yields the highest accuracy, while Hindi is the lowest for both tasks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Controlled Hallucination Injection Enables Fine-Grained Evaluation
The dataset construction process starts from a reference output `y*` and uses an LLM-based editor constrained to introduce exactly one unsupported proposition `p+`. The edit type (entity, relation, sentence) is defined by how `p+` misaligns with reference propositions: entity error changes the entity while keeping relation/attributes; relation error alters the relation/attributes while keeping entities; sentence error introduces a proposition with no aligned reference. By enforcing `|Δ⁺|=1` and providing structured prompts with examples, the process isolates a single hallucination type per instance. Formal definitions (Eqs. 5–13) and a validation step ensure that injected errors are clean and unambiguous, as evidenced by the predominantly single-type labeling distribution (Fig. 2) and high inter-annotator agreement. If the editing frequently produced multiple errors or severe grammatical mistakes, the diagnostic power would degrade; the paper notes higher '0' label rates in Turkish and Hindi, attributed to grammatical issues during relation-level injection.

### Mechanism 2: Human Validation Guarantees Reliable Multilingual Labels
Two native speakers per language independently annotated each (original, edited) pair using a detailed protocol. Disagreements were resolved by joint review, and the overall Cohen’s κ reached substantial levels (≥0.74). This rigorous validation filters out instances where the edit failed (e.g., grammatical errors, no clear hallucination) and confirms the intended hallucination type. The high agreement across typologically diverse languages—English (0.83), Arabic (0.79), Turkish (0.76), Hindi (0.74)—indicates that the annotation guidelines are robust and that the fine-grained categories are consistently understood. The reliability is crucial for meaningful cross-lingual comparisons; notably, Hindi’s κ, while still substantial, is the lowest and aligns with a higher proportion of invalid ('0') labels, suggesting increased annotation difficulty for morphologically complex languages.

### Mechanism 3: Task Structure Drives the QA–Summarization Performance Gap
The underlying tasks differ fundamentally: QA requires answering a specific, self-contained question, so hallucinations (e.g., a wrong entity or date) are localized and easier to detect by direct comparison with the reference. In contrast, dialogue summarization generates an abstractive summary of a multi-utterance conversation, embedding hallucinations within longer, cohesive discourse; detection necessitates understanding the entire summary and checking for unsupported claims, which is more demanding. This structural difference explains the consistent finding that QA outperforms summarization across all languages and models. The gap persists even for the strongest model (GPT-4o) and is largest for sentence-level hallucinations, which are inherently more diffuse and require longer-range reasoning.

**Why needed here:** To interpret the dataset structure and model performance breakdowns, one must distinguish between entity, relation, and sentence-level hallucinations. This taxonomy explains why sentence-level errors are hardest and how different misalignment patterns manifest.

### Multilingual NLP & Resource Disparities
**Why needed here:** Understanding why Hindi consistently lags behind English requires considering training data volume, linguistic complexity (morphology, script), and translation quality, beyond simple resource scarcity.  
**Quick check:** What are two plausible reasons Hindi might show lower detection accuracy? (1) LLMs have less exposure to high-quality Hindi text during pretraining; (2) Hindi’s rich morphology and digraphic script make automatic translation and grammatical editing more error-prone, leading to noisier test instances.

### Prompting-Based Evaluation vs. Fine-Tuning
**Why needed here:** The paper evaluates models via prompting only, testing their zero/few-shot reasoning abilities without task-specific optimization. This choice affects reported performance ceilings and generalizability.  
**Quick check:** How would performance likely change if top models were fine-tuned on Halluverse-M^3? It would probably improve, especially on sentence-level detection, but risks overfitting to the dataset’s specific hallucination style and losing generalizability.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Source Data Picker → Multilingual Parallelizer → Controlled Hallucination Injector → Human Validation Pipeline → Final Dataset → Evaluation Harness → Results

**Critical path:** The workflow begins with selecting source QA and summarization datasets, translates them into target languages, then uses an LLM prompt to inject one of three hallucination types. Each edited instance undergoes native-speaker validation; valid pairs are compiled into the final benchmark. Finally, an evaluation harness prompts target LLMs to classify hallucination types and computes accuracies. The Human Validation Pipeline is the most resource-intensive and quality-critical step.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Controlled edits vs. natural hallucination diversity:* Strict single-error injection yields clean, type-pure instances but may not capture the full spectrum of real LLM hallucinations, which can combine multiple error modes.  
- *Human validation cost vs. scalability:* Achieving high inter-annotator agreement (κ ≥ 0.74) requires significant manual effort, limiting dataset size and construction speed but ensuring reliability.  
- *Task selection (QA vs. Summarization):* Comparing these tasks provides insight into the impact of output structure, but results may not generalize to other generation tasks like translation or code generation.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High proportion of '0' labels (invalid instances) in a language-task slice, as seen in Hindi and Turkish relation-level data, indicates injection or grammatical editing failures.  
- Low inter-annotator agreement (κ < 0.6) for a language suggests unclear guidelines or inherent annotation difficulty.  
- Absence of the expected QA > summarization performance gap could signal a flawed evaluation prompt (e.g., models not comparing to the reference) or overly simplistic hallucinations in the summarization set.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Injection Sanity Check:** Manually inspect 100 random instances per (task, language, hallucination type) to verify exactly one intended error and grammatical fluency; target >90% compliance.  
2. **Annotation Reliability Audit:** Compute Cohen’s κ separately for each (language, task) subset from double-annotated data; flag any κ < 0.7 for guideline review and possible re-annotation.  
3. **Baseline Replication:** Run the evaluation with a small model (e.g., Mistral-7B) using the paper’s described prompt. Confirm the core trends: QA > summarization accuracy and English > Hindi performance, thereby validating the harness and the dataset’s signal.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** What specific linguistic or discourse-level features make sentence-level hallucinations so much harder to detect than entity or relation-level hallucinations, especially in dialogue summarization? The paper shows the empirical difficulty but does not analyze underlying reasons such as longer context needs, lack of anchor points, or semantic density.  
- **Open Question 2:** Does the use of GPT-4o for automated hallucination injection create an artificial bias, making GPT-4o better at detecting its own “style” of hallucination? A closed-loop effect could confound assessments of general capability.  
- **Open Question 3:** To what extent do translation artifacts and grammatical errors introduced during multilingual preparation impact observed performance differences, particularly Hindi’s consistently lower scores? The dataset has a higher ’0’ label rate for Hindi and Turkish, suggesting injection noise.  
- **Open Question 4:** Why is dialogue summarization so consistently harder for hallucination detection than question answering, and what specific capabilities (e.g., coreference resolution, discourse parsing) are models lacking? The task nature difference is noted but not dissected.

## Limitations
- **Prompt sensitivity:** Exact evaluation prompts (wording, in-context examples) are unreported, making results vulnerable to small phrasing changes.  
- **Injection purity:** Elevated ’0’ label rates in Hindi/Turkish indicate grammatical errors during controlled injection, potentially contaminating sentence-level evaluations.  
- **Task generalizability:** Findings are limited to QA and dialogue summarization; performance on other tasks (translation, long-form generation) remains unknown.  
- **Prompting-only evaluation:** All results are from zero/few-shot prompting; fine-tuned detectors could exhibit different performance gaps and language disparities.

## Confidence
- **Controlled injection mechanism:** High – formal definitions and validation workflow tightly specify the process.  
- **Human validation reliability:** High – inter-annotator agreement κ ≥ 0.74 across languages.  
- **QA vs. summarization gap:** High – consistent across languages/models in tables; explained by task structure.  
- **Multilingual disparity (Hindi lowest):** Medium – correlated with grammatical injection failures, but resource scarcity is an unisolated confounder.

2. **Annotation Agreement Drill-Down:** Recalculate Cohen’s κ for each (language, task) pair from the double-annotated subset; flag any κ < 0.7 for guideline refinement.
