# Learning Shrinks the Hard Tail: Training-Dependent Inference Scaling in a Solvable Linear Model

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.03764
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03764
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Training increases the effective inference exponent βeff(N) until it saturates at the intrinsic tail index β, linking generalization improvements to steeper pass@k scaling and informing compute allocation.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates how training progress influences test-time compute scaling (pass@k) in tasks with heterogeneous instance difficulty. It introduces the Latent Instance Difficulty (LID) model, where each data point's difficulty follows a heavy-tailed distribution. Theoretically, the pass@k failure rate follows a power law with an effective exponent βeff(N) that increases with training sample size N and saturates at an intrinsic limit β determined by the tail of the difficulty distribution. This reveals a coupling: as training reduces generalization error (scaling as ~1/N), the inference scaling steepens until limited by irreducible instance noise. The authors derive a compute-allocation rule: prioritize training until βeff(N) ≈ β, then allocate additional resources to inference. Empirical validation through simulations and real-data proxies (CIFAR-10H, GSM8K distillation) confirms the predicted growth and saturation of βeff(N).

## Method Summary
The study analyzes a linear regression setting with latent instance difficulties modeled as heavy-tailed (Gamma-distributed precision). The failure probability for pass@k, Linf(k;N), is shown to consist of two power-law tails: an intrinsic tail from extremely hard instances (exponent β) and a bias-induced tail from finite training (exponent γ(N) ∝ 1/Lgen(N)). Consequently, the effective exponent βeff(N) = min(β, γ(N)) + o(1) grows with N until saturation. For compute allocation under a fixed budget, the total loss Ltot = R Lgen(N) + P̃(N) k^{-βeff(N)} yields an optimal split that shifts from training to inference once βeff(N) stabilizes. Experiments use synthetic data and two real-world proxies: (i) CIFAR-10H with linear heads on ResNet features, leveraging human label variances as difficulty; (ii) GSM8K with a Flan-T5 teacher and LoRA-finetuned student. Both show the predicted βeff(N) increase and saturation.

## Key Results
- The pass@k failure rate follows a power law whose exponent βeff(N) increases with training sample size N and saturates at an intrinsic limit β set by the difficulty distribution's tail.
- The failure distribution is a two-tail mixture: Linf(k;N) ≈ C1 k^{-β} + C2(N) k^{-γ(N)}, with γ(N) ∝ 1/Lgen(N). The effective exponent is the smaller of the two.
- Optimal compute allocation follows a threshold policy: train until βeff(N) ≈ β, then allocate additional compute to inference (more attempts). Empirical βeff(N) curves from CIFAR-10H and GSM8K show the predicted growth and saturation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core of the paper's explanation is that training reduces model bias, which in turn sharpens the pass@k failure tail. Specifically, three interconnected mechanisms are identified:

1. **Training-dependent effective exponent**: In the Latent Instance Difficulty (LID) model, the pass@k failure rate follows a power law with effective exponent βeff(N). As training sample size N increases, the generalization error Lgen(N) decays (roughly as 1/N), causing the bias-induced tail exponent γ(N) ∝ 1/Lgen(N) to rise. Consequently, βeff(N) = min(β, γ(N)) + o(1) increases with N until it reaches the intrinsic tail index β of the difficulty distribution, at which point further training cannot steepen the curve.

2. **Two-tail mixture**: The failure distribution Linf(k;N) is the average over instances of the per-instance failure probability. For an instance with precision τ_x, the success probability in one trial behaves like 1 - cδ/√τ_x for very small τ_x (hard tail) and like exp(-B_N(x)² τ_x/(2σ_η²)) when the model bias B_N(x) is large. Averaging over τ_x (which is regularly varying near zero with index β/2) and over the (sub-Gaussian) distribution of B_N(x) yields a sum of two power-law tails: C1 k^{-β} + C2(N) k^{-γ(N)}. The intrinsic tail stems from the hardest examples, while the bias tail arises from typical examples where the model is still inaccurate.

3. **Compute-allocation shift**: When optimizing total loss Ltot = R Lgen(N) + P̃(N) k^{-βeff(N)} under a fixed compute budget, the first-order optimality condition contains a term -β'eff(N) log(k). If βeff(N) is still rising (β'eff(N) > 0), this term increases the marginal benefit of additional training relative to the case of constant β. Hence the optimal policy first pushes N until βeff(N) saturates at β, then allocates surplus compute to increasing k.

## Foundational Learning
- **High-dimensional linear regression scaling laws**: Understanding the baseline scaling of generalization error L_gen(N) is crucial because the bias tail exponent γ(N) scales as 1/L_gen(N). The paper shows that in overparameterized regimes (N < d) with feature eigenvalues σ_j² ∝ j^{-(1+α)}, L_gen(N) ∝ N^{-α}, while in underparameterized regimes (N ≫ d) it scales as d/N.  
  *Quick check*: If σ_j² ∝ j^{-(1+α)}, what is the scaling of L_gen(N) for ridgeless interpolation when N < d?
- **Regular variation and heavy-tailed analysis**: The intrinsic tail index β arises from the small-τ_x behavior of the difficulty distribution. Specifically, τ_x ∼ Gamma(β/2, 1) implies F_τ(t) ∼ t^{β/2} as t → 0. Using a Tauberian theorem on the Laplace–Stieltjes transform of the success probability yields the k^{-β} asymptotic for Linf.  
  *Quick check*: If F_τ(t) = t^{β/2} L(t) with L slowly varying, what is the asymptotic form of E[e^{-c/√τ_x}] for large c?
- **Power-law mixture and effective exponents**: The empirically fitted β_eff(N) is not a single true exponent but an effective value that changes with the k-window due to the mixture of two power-law tails. In regular variation theory, when multiple regularly varying terms are summed, the term with the smaller exponent dominates asymptotically, but over finite ranges the observed slope is a weighted average of exponents.  
  *Quick check*: For f(k) = A k^{-3} + B(N) k^{-5}, what effective exponent would you measure by fitting a power law over k ∈ [10^3, 10^4] versus k ∈ [10^5, 10^6]?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map:
Feature extractor (pre-trained backbone) → Difficulty proxy (human label variance or teacher variability) → Learner (linear regression or LoRA) → Inference engine (sampling multiple outputs) → Metrics pipeline (compute L_gen, pass@k, fit βeff)

### Critical path:
1. Prepare dataset with instance difficulty (human uncertainty for CIFAR-10H or teacher sample variance for GSM8K).
2. Train model across increasing training set sizes N (log-spaced).
3. Compute generalization loss L_gen(N) on held-out data.
4. For each N, compute inference loss L_inf(k;N) by drawing k samples per test instance and evaluating pass@k.
5. Estimate effective exponent βeff(N) via linear regression of log(L_inf) vs log(k) over a central window.
6. Validate saturation: plot βeff(N) vs N and compare to estimated tail index of difficulty distribution.
7. Compute total loss L_tot for a fixed budget and compare optimal N* to analytic prediction.

### Design tradeoffs:
- Tolerance δ: Too small increases variance of pass@k; too large may break small-window expansion assumption. Choose δ relative to noise scale (e.g., δ ≈ 0.1 σ_η).
- Ridge regularization λ: Must be small enough for ridgeless limit in overparameterized regime; too large introduces extra bias.
- k-window for βeff estimation: Should lie in region where L_inf is purely power-law. Avoid small-k prefactor distortion and large-k saturation. Use middle half of k-range typically.

### Failure signatures:
- No saturation of βeff(N): either difficulty distribution lacks heavy tail or N not large enough; check L_gen(N) trend.
- Non-monotonic βeff(N): suggests experimental noise or model worsening with more data; inspect L_gen(N).
- Discrepancy between fitted βeff and empirical difficulty tail index: may indicate τ_x not independent of x or violation of Gaussian label noise; check feature-variance correlation.

### First 3 experiments:
1. Synthetic LID sanity check: Generate Gaussian features with power-law spectrum, τ ∼ Gamma(β/2,1), linear teacher, noisy targets variance σ_η²/τ. Train ridge (λ→0) for N from small to N≫d. Compute L_gen(N) and L_inf(k;N). Verify L_gen scaling and βeff(N) growth toward β.
2. CIFAR-10H validation of difficulty tail: Compute empirical distribution of human label variances; fit Pareto tail to estimate β_tail. After training linear models at large N (e.g., N > 5000), estimate saturated βeff. Compare the two.
3. Compute allocation threshold test: With fixed budget C = 10^6, costs c_N=1, c_k=0.01, vary R and find grid-optimal N*. Compare to analytic prediction from Eq. (21). Optimal N should lie left of constant-β prediction (using saturated β), confirming the shift toward more training.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Extension to multi-layer networks**: Does the LID theory extend to jointly learned representations in deep networks? (The paper suggests random-feature or kernel models as a bridge.)
2. **Invariance of intrinsic β**: Is the difficulty tail index β fixed for a task, or does it decrease with model capacity as stronger models simplify the task?
3. **Validation in autoregressive LM pre-training**: Does β_eff(N) increase and saturate in large-scale LLM training, and does the compute-allocation rule hold?
4. **Broken scaling regimes**: How does the theory apply when L_gen(N) is non-monotonic (e.g., double descent)? Does the two-tail mixture law still hold?

## Limitations
- Assumptions such as τ_x ⊥ x, sub-Gaussian bias, and regular variation may not hold in real datasets, potentially invalidating the mixture law.
- Empirical validations are limited to linear heads with frozen features; it is unclear whether results extend to end-to-end training or deeper architectures.
- The estimation of βeff(N) is sensitive to the choice of k-window and lacks principled uncertainty quantification, making reported saturation values potentially window-dependent.

## Confidence
- βeff(N) growth & saturation: **High** for synthetic experiments, **Medium** for real-data proxies.
- Two-tail mixture for Linf: **High** theoretically, **Medium** empirically due to overlapping tails.
- Compute-allocation threshold rule: **Medium**; relies on accurate βeff(N) curves and unbounded k.

## Next Checks
1. **Tail index consistency**: On CIFAR-10H, fit a Pareto tail to human label variances to estimate β_tail and compare to saturated βeff from large N; consistency would strengthen the link.
2. **Window-robustness test**: Re-estimate βeff(N) across multiple k-windows (e.g., different percentiles) and compute stability; report confidence intervals.
3. **Nonlinear extension test**: Train a small ReLU network on synthetic LID data and check if βeff(N) still increases and saturates, probing robustness to feature learning.
