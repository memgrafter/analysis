# A Task-Centric Theory for Iterative Self-Improvement with Easy-to-Hard Curricula

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.10014  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10014  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** A theoretical framework for iterative self-improvement (ISI) in LLMs with binary rewards, proving that easy-to-hard curricula provably outperform fixed-mixture training under moderate difficulty separation and sufficient sample budget, and empirically validating the theory on synthetic and math benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
The paper develops a theory for iterative self-improvement (ISI) where an LLM fine-tunes on its own reward-verified outputs over multiple rounds. Each iteration is modeled as maximum-likelihood training on a distribution filtered by a binary reward threshold. The analysis reveals a feedback loop: as the model improves, its acceptance rate increases, yielding more training data per iteration and sustaining progress until saturation. For multiple task difficulties, the authors prove that an easy-to-hard curriculum—progressively training on harder questions—strictly outperforms a fixed mixture of difficulties, provided the separation between difficulty levels is moderate and the question budget exceeds a critical threshold. Theoretical finite-sample guarantees are derived for expected reward (Pass@1). Empirical experiments on a synthetic shortest-path task and two math reasoning datasets (GSM8K, DeepMind Mathematics) confirm the predictions: ISI succeeds only when the initial task difficulty lies in a specific interval, the advantage of easy-to-hard curricula exhibits a phase transition with sample budget, and larger question and answer budgets improve outcomes.

## Method Summary
The core method is iterative self-improvement (ISI). Starting from a pretrained model θ₀, each iteration t samples n questions from a task distribution p_t, generates m answers per question using θ_t, and retains only the first answer that meets the reward threshold τ (binary correctness: s(q,a)=1). The accepted (question, answer) pairs form a filtered dataset, on which the model is fine-tuned via maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) to obtain θ_{t+1}. Two scheduling strategies are considered: (1) fixed-mixture, where p_t is a uniform mixture over all task difficulty levels at every iteration; (2) easy-to-hard curriculum, where p_t = p_{t+1} (a single difficulty level) that increases in hardness across iterations. The theoretical analysis assumes a finite model class Π and a neighborhood Θ around the initialization. Key assumptions link the per-question acceptance rate to the expected reward (Assumption 4.3) and relate expected rewards across difficulty levels via power-law separations (Assumption 5.1). Finite-sample lower bounds on expected reward after T iterations are derived, proving monotonic improvement only when the initial expected reward V_{p₀}(θ₀) lies in a feasible interval I(1,ν). For curricula, composed improvement maps show a provable gap over fixed-mixture when feasibility conditions (Mi<0, N<0) hold. Empirically, the authors implement ISI with LoRA finetuning on Llama, Qwen, and other models, using synthetic graph problems and math datasets with controlled difficulty splits.

## Key Results
- ISI exhibits a feedback loop: better models accept more training samples per iteration, enabling sustained improvement until saturation, where the expected reward upper bound is strictly less than 1.
- Monotonic improvement via ISI is guaranteed only when the pretrained model’s expected reward on the initial task lies within a specific interval I(1,ν) determined by sample budget ν and constants; outside this interval, progress may degrade or collapse.
- For multiple difficulty levels, an easy-to-hard curriculum provably yields a higher expected reward lower bound than fixed-mixture training when (i) difficulty separation is moderate, (ii) question budget exceeds a critical threshold, and (iii) the initialization satisfies a feasibility condition.
- Experiments confirm: (a) on a synthetic shortest-path task, only models with initial Pass@1 in the predicted moderate range show monotonic gains; (b) increasing question (n) and answer (m) budgets improves final performance; (c) the curriculum advantage exhibits a phase transition as difficulty separation varies, aligning with the theory.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Positive feedback via data quantity  
Better models have a higher acceptance rate α(θ,q), leading to more accepted samples n_t per iteration. This increase in data amplifies the MLE update, creating a feedback loop that sustains improvement until the acceptance rate saturates (when the filtered distribution becomes nearly deterministic). Core assumption (4.3): for a neighborhood Θ of the pretrained model, the per-question acceptance rate is tightly coupled to the expected reward V_p(θ), ensuring that n_t increases with model quality. Break condition: saturation occurs when V_p approaches the fixed point x^+ < 1−γ (Remark 4.6.3).

### Mechanism 2: Moderate initial difficulty requirement  
ISI is effective only if the initial expected reward V_{p₀}(θ₀) lies in an interval I(1,ν). If too easy, acceptance is already near 1, leaving little room for improvement; if too hard, acceptance is too low to provide sufficient training data, risking collapse. Mechanism: the improvement map F(x) is strictly increasing only within I(1,ν) (Proposition 4.5). Break condition: outside I(1,ν), the lower bound sequence becomes non-monotonic or decreases.

### Mechanism 3: Easy-to-hard curriculum advantage  
When training across a spectrum of difficulties, an easy-to-hard schedule yields tighter lower bounds than repeatedly applying the fixed-mixture improvement map. The mechanism involves two steps: (i) starting with easy tasks yields high acceptance rates and abundant data to build a strong model; (ii) later, the improved model can tackle harder tasks. Under power-law difficulty separation (Assumption 5.1) and when feasibility conditions Mi<0 and N<0 hold (Theorem 5.2), the composed maps H_t for the curriculum dominate the repeated F for fixed mixture. Break conditions: if β' is too small/large relative to β, sample budget n falls below a phase-transition threshold ν_c, or initialization not in feasible region IN(β',β,ν), then N≥0 and the curriculum does not provably beat the baseline.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept 1: Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)**  
*Why needed:* Each ISI iteration is modeled as MLE on the reward-filtered distribution. Understanding MLE is essential to derive the update and finite-sample bounds.  
*Quick check:* Given accepted pairs (q_i,a_i)~D'_{p,θ_t}, what is the empirical MLE objective and its population optimum θ_{t+1}^*?

**Concept 2: Binary Rewards and Pass@1**  
*Why needed:* The paper specializes to binary rewards (s∈{0,1}) where Pass@1 (expected correct probability) is the metric. This simplifies the acceptance rate to the expected reward V_p(θ).  
*Quick check:* For binary s, show that Z_p(θ)=E_{q∼p}[α(θ,q)] equals V_p(θ).

**Concept 3: Power-Law Difficulty Separation**  
*Why needed:* The easy-to-hard analysis uses exponents β',β to bound ratios of expected rewards between adjacent difficulty levels, capturing task separation.  
*Quick check:* For adjacent tasks p_i and p_{i+1}, according to Assumption 5.1, what interval contains V_{p_i}(θ)/V_{p_{i+1}}(θ)?

**Concept 4: Neighborhood Θ of Initialization**  
*Why needed:* All key assumptions (4.3, 5.1) are stated only for θ in a small neighborhood Θ around the pretrained model; the theory applies only in this regime.  
*Quick check:* What does Assumption 4.3 state about the per-question acceptance rate α(θ,q) relative to V_p(θ)?

**Concept 5: Finite-Sample Confidence Parameters δ,δ'**  
*Why needed:* The bounds (e.g., Theorem 4.1) involve constants cδ, cδ' that depend on δ,δ' and |Π|, controlling confidence.  
*Quick check:* What are cδ and cδ' in terms of δ, δ' and model class size |Π|?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** θ₀ (pretrained model) → p_t (question distribution) → sample n questions per iteration → generate m answers per question → accept pairs with s≥τ (binary) → buffer of accepted pairs → MLE fine-tune (LoRA) → θ_{t+1} → repeat L times. For curriculum: p_t changes to next difficulty level each iteration; for fixed-mixture: p_t constant.

**Critical path:** 1. Sample n questions from p_t. 2. For each q, generate up to m answers sequentially until one is accepted (s=1). 3. Add first accepted (q,a) to buffer. 4. Perform one epoch of MLE finetuning (LoRA) on buffer to obtain θ_{t+1}. 5. Evaluate Pass@1 on a held-out test set from the target distribution p₀. Loop for T iterations.

**Design tradeoffs:** 
- m vs n: Larger m increases chance of obtaining an accepted answer per question but costs more generation; larger n directly scales accepted data. Balance affects efficiency and monotonicity.
- τ: For binary correctness, τ=1 is typical. Lower τ may include wrong answers, potentially harming performance.
- Curriculum difficulty gap: Too small separation (β'≈β) makes curriculum effect negligible; too large may cause the model to fail on hard tasks early if initial model is weak. Requires moderate separation.

**Failure signatures:** 
- Collapse: Accepted sample size n_t plummets or test Pass@1 drops sharply after an iteration.
- No progress: The improvement map F^∘t(V_p₀(θ₀)) is non-increasing from the start.
- Curriculum fails: Easy-to-hard final Pass@1 is less than or equal to fixed-mixture for same total budget.
- High variance across seeds: Often due to insufficient accepted samples; remedy by increasing m.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. *Single-task ISI:* Choose a moderate-difficulty task (initial Pass@1 ~0.35-0.45). Run L=3 iterations with n=2000, m=1, τ=1. Expect monotonic increase in Pass@1.
2. *Budget sensitivity:* Vary n (1000,2000,4000) and m (1,2,4,8) across L=3 iterations on a fixed task. Plot final Pass@1; should improve with larger n and m.
3. *Curriculum vs fixed-mixture:* Construct two tasks: easy (initial Pass@1 ~0.6) and hard (~0.2) using same model. Compare (a) fixed-mixture: 50% easy + 50% hard each iteration; (b) easy-to-hard: iterations 1-2 only easy, then switch to hard. Use equal total budget. Expect easy-to-hard yields higher final Pass@1 on the hard task if separation moderate and n large.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:** Under what exact conditions does multi-step self-improvement yield strictly increasing expected reward, and are these conditions necessary?  
*Basis:* Theorem 4.1 and Corollary 4.4 provide sufficient conditions (initialization in interval I(1,ν)). The necessity and applicability to infinite model classes are unknown.  
*Evidence needed:* A matching lower bound showing that outside I(1,ν) improvement fails under the same assumptions, or extending the theory to broader model classes (e.g., via Rademacher complexity).

**Open Question 2:** What are necessary and sufficient conditions for an easy-to-hard curriculum to provably outperform fixed-mixture training, and how robust are these conditions to uncertainty in difficulty ordering?  
*Basis:* Theorem 5.2 gives sufficient conditions (Mi<0, N<0) based on power-law separation (β',β). The tightness and robustness are not established.  
*Evidence needed:* Proving necessity of some conditions, analyzing alternative difficulty models (e.g., arbitrary orders with bounded swaps), and Monte‑Carlo mapping across β',β,ν.

**Open Question 3:** How does the theory change when rewards are non-binary, noisy, or estimated from imperfect verifiers?  
*Basis:* The analysis heavily uses binary reward properties (α(θ,q)=V_p(θ) for binary). For continuous rewards, the coupling in Assumption 4.3 may fail and concentration bounds differ.  
*Evidence needed:* Extend the framework to general [0,1] rewards, derive finite‑sample bounds analogous to Theorem 4.1, and test with synthetic continuous rewards.

**Open Question 4:** Can adaptive curriculum scheduling—where the question distribution is updated based on the current model’s performance—achieve provably better guarantees than fixed easy-to-hard schedules?  
*Basis:* The paper only analyzes a predetermined difficulty progression; adaptive methods like AdaStar are empirical.  
*Evidence needed:* Extend the theoretical framework to allow p_t to depend on past performance, derive lower bounds for adaptive strategies, and compare with fixed schedules via simulations.

**Open Question 5:** What additional training components (e.g., reinforcement learning, distillation, multi‑step reasoning) can break the inherent saturation limit of ISI predicted by the theory?  
*Basis:* Theoretical upper bound x^+(1,ν) < 1−γ suggests a saturation ceiling; the authors propose incorporating RL but do not quantify potential gains.  
*Evidence needed:* Analyze alternative objectives (e.g., policy gradient, contrastive learning) under the ISI loop, derive new finite‑sample guarantees possibly with higher ceilings, and empirically test combinations on challenging tasks.

## Limitations
- Theoretical assumptions (e.g., Assumption 4.3, 5.1) are restricted to a small neighborhood Θ around the pretrained model; the practical size of Θ for large language models is unclear.
- Key implementation details are missing, such as systematic methods to achieve desired initial performance, precise construction of difficulty-separated curricula, and the CP‑SAT solver formulation for training set generation.
- Validation is limited to synthetic graphs and a few math reasoning datasets; generalizability to domains like code or open‑ended generation remains unknown.
- The analysis assumes binary, perfectly verifiable rewards; extensions to noisy or continuous rewards are not provided.

## Confidence
- **Feedback loop (better models accept more data):** Medium (derivation relies on restrictive assumptions; empirical evidence suggestive but not exhaustive).
- **Moderate initial difficulty requirement (V_{p₀}(θ₀) ∈ I(1,ν)):** High (theory is explicit and experiments confirm the predicted interval).
- **Easy-to-hard curriculum advantage:** Medium (provable under specific conditions; experiments show phase transition but depend on accurate difficulty estimation and sufficient budget).
