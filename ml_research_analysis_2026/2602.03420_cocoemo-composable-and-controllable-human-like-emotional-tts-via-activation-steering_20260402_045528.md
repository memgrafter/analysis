# CoCoEmo: Composable and Controllable Human-Like Emotional TTS via Activation Steering

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.03420  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03420  
- **Reference count**: 35  
- **Primary result**: Enables controllable mixed-emotion TTS via activation steering in the speech language model, improving emotion similarity (E-SIM up to 0.795) and handling text‑emotion mismatch while preserving naturalness.

## Executive Summary
CoCoEmo tackles the limitation of current emotional text‑to‑speech (TTS) systems, which produce only a single, utterance‑level emotion and suppress nuanced, mixed, or text‑mismatched expressions. The method introduces activation steering within the speech language model (SLM) of a hybrid TTS pipeline. Steering vectors are extracted from mid‑to‑late transformer layers where emotional representations are linearly separable. These vectors can be arithmetically combined to represent mixed emotions and injected at inference time to modulate prosody independently of the textual content. A cross‑conditioning diagnostic reveals that emotional prosody is primarily synthesized by the SLM, not the flow‑matching acoustic decoder. Evaluations show that steering yields high‑quality, controllable mixed‑emotion synthesis (E‑SIM up to 0.795, rank correlation ρ up to 0.399), effectively handles text‑emotion mismatches, and maintains natural speech quality.

## Method Summary
CoCoEmo is built on a hybrid TTS architecture comprising an autoregressive speech language model (SLM) and a flow‑based acoustic decoder. The core idea is to steer the SLM’s intermediate activations toward desired emotional states without retraining. First, the authors identify layer‑operations (e.g., attention outputs) in the SLM where emotional classes are most linearly separable, using linear probes on training activations. For each emotion, a steering vector is computed as the mean difference between activations of that emotion and matched neutral utterances (same speaker, same transcript). Mixed‑emotion vectors are formed by a weighted sum of single‑emotion vectors according to target proportions. At inference, given text and a neutral reference speech, the SLM generates speech tokens autoregressively. At each selected layer/operation, the last‑token activation is shifted by adding α·v (v = steering vector) and then renormalized to its original norm. The modified token sequence is passed to the flow‑matching module and vocoder to produce the final waveform. This training‑free approach decouples emotional prosody from linguistic content and allows continuous control.

## Key Results
- **SLM as primary prosody driver**: Cross‑conditioning diagnostic shows that conditioning the SLM on emotion yields highly distinct prosodic contours, whereas conditioning only the flow‑matching module does not.
- **Effective mixed‑emotion control**: Steering achieves emotion similarity (E‑SIM) up to 0.795 and rank correlation (ρ) up to 0.399, significantly outperforming instruction‑based and emotion‑vector baselines.
- **Robustness to mismatch**: The method successfully imposes a target acoustic emotion even when the text suggests a different one, without degrading naturalness (N‑MOS comparable to unsteered baseline).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### 1. Steering in the SLM vs. Flow‑Matching Module
Emotional prosody is primarily encoded in the latent speech token sequence generated by the SLM, not in the flow‑matching acoustic decoder. This is demonstrated by a cross‑conditioning diagnostic: when emotion conditioning is applied only at the SLM (“SLM‑driven”), prosodic features (F0, energy, speaking rate) show clear differentiation across emotions; when applied only at the flow‑matching module (“Flow‑driven”), the contours overlap heavily. The hybrid architecture cleanly separates linguistic content generation (SLM) from acoustic rendering (flow‑matching). Therefore, steering vectors injected into the SLM directly manipulate the emotional plan, while the flow‑matching faithfully renders whatever token sequence it receives.

**Evidence anchors**:  
- Abstract: “Results show steering enables controllable mixed‑emotion synthesis… handling text‑emotion mismatch and preserving speech naturalness.”  
- Section 2.1, Figure 2 visualizes energy contours; Table 1 quantifies lower CCC and higher SR STD for SLM‑driven vs. flow‑driven.  
- No neighboring papers systematically analyze this separation; CoCoEmo provides the first evidence.

### 2. Linear Separability Enables Vector Composability
Mid‑to‑late transformer layers (especially attention outputs) exhibit high linear separability of emotion classes, as measured by linear probe accuracy. This property allows extraction of reliable steering directions: the mean‑difference vector between activations of a given emotion and neutral captures a consistent “emotion shift” in the activation space. Because the classes are approximately linearly separable, such a direction vector predictably moves activations toward the target emotion class. Moreover, these directions superimpose additively; weighted sums of single‑emotion vectors yield meaningful mixed‑emotion targets.

**Evidence anchors**:  
- Abstract: “Steering vectors are extracted from mid‑to‑late layers (especially attention outputs) where emotional representations are linearly separable.”  
- Section 2.2, Figure 3 shows discriminability peaking in later layers; Section 4.5, Figure 7 shows layer‑wise TEP correlates with probe accuracy (ρ=0.5078).  
- Related steering work assumes linearity; CoCoEmo validates it systematically for TTS.

### 3. Acoustic‑Only Vectors Enable Text‑Emotion Misalignment
Steering vectors derived from speaker‑matched, transcript‑matched neutral‑emotion pairs isolate purely acoustic emotional variation. Injecting these vectors at SLM layers biases the generated speech token sequence toward the target acoustic emotion, overriding any emotional tendency implied by the text. This relies on the SLM’s internal disentanglement of linguistic content from acoustic/prosodic planning.

**Evidence anchors**:  
- Abstract: Results include “handling text‑emotion mismatch and preserving speech naturalness.”  
- Section 3.1 details the pairing protocol; Section 4.3, Figure 5 and Table 3 show E‑SIM and TEP gains increase with mismatch severity and steering strength α.  
- Prior work (Peng et al., 2025) handles mismatch via guidance; CoCoEmo shows a simpler, training‑free latent steering approach works.

## Foundational Learning

**Activation Steering**  
*Why needed*: Provides a lightweight, training‑free method to direct model behavior by adding pre‑computed direction vectors to intermediate activations.  
*Quick check*: Why is renormalization after injection necessary? Without it, the added vector could significantly change the activation norm, potentially destabilizing subsequent layers and degrading output quality.

**Hybrid TTS Architecture**  
*Why needed*: Understanding the two‑stage pipeline (SLM → flow‑matching → vocoder) is crucial to locate where emotional prosody is synthesized and thus where steering should be applied.  
*Quick check*: In the cross‑conditioning diagnostic, the vocoder is the same across conditions; thus it cannot explain observed prosodic differences. What role does the vocoder play, and why is it not a confounding factor? (Answer: vocoder is a fixed, high‑quality synthesizer that faithfully renders any mel‑spectrogram; differences in prosody must arise earlier.)

**Linear Discriminability as Proxy for Steerability**  
*Why needed*: The method selects steering sites based on probe accuracy, assuming that linearly separable representations admit reliable directional shifts.  
*Quick check*: What does a high linear probe accuracy indicate about the geometric organization of emotion classes in that layer’s activation space? (Answer: It suggests that a linear hyperplane can separate the classes, implying the existence of a consistent direction that moves activations from neutral toward a target emotion.)

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**  
Text tokens + Neutral reference speech tokens → SLM (steering vectors injected at selected layer/operations) → Speech token sequence → Flow‑matching → Mel‑spectrogram → Vocoder → Waveform

**Critical path**  
1. Encode text and neutral reference speech into tokens.  
2. Autoregressively generate speech tokens with the SLM.  
3. At each selected layer/operation, modify the last‑token activation: h ← h + α·v, then renormalize to original norm.  
4. Process the steered token sequence with flow‑matching to produce a mel‑spectrogram.  
5. Convert mel‑spectrogram to waveform with the vocoder.  
Emotion control is confined to the SLM; flow‑matching and vocoder remain unchanged.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Steering strength α: larger values improve emotion alignment but risk increased WER and speaker drift; safe range ≈[0, 4.5], up to 6.0 with caution.  
- Number of steering layers K: more layers can strengthen control but may accumulate perturbations; the paper selects top‑2 (CosyVoice2) or top‑3 (IndexTTS2) under a WER constraint.  
- Layer selection: must balance linear separability with robustness to overwriting; very shallow layers may be dominated by input conditioning.  
- Mixed vs. single: Weighted sums of vectors enable continuous control but may slightly reduce E‑SIM compared to pure directions; per‑emotion α tuning may be needed.

**Failure signatures**  
- WER increases >0.5 above no‑steering baseline → excessive perturbation corrupting linguistic content.  
- S‑SIM drops significantly → speaker identity drift.  
- N‑MOS decreases → unnatural prosody or artifacts introduced.  
- Mixed‑emotion ρ or H‑Rate low → linear combination invalid, weights mis‑specified, or suboptimal layer choice.  
- No improvement in high‑mismatch scenario → steering vector may not fully disentangle acoustic emotion from text in that layer.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. Cross‑conditioning diagnostic: Compare prosodic differentiation when emotion is applied at SLM vs. flow‑matching.  
2. Layer‑wise discriminability analysis: Train linear probes on activations from each layer/operator to predict emotion; identify top‑K sites.  
3. Mixed‑emotion steering evaluation: Use multi‑rater soft labels to construct mixed vectors; measure E‑SIM, TEP, ρ, H‑Rate against baselines.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Generalization of SLM dominance across TTS architectures**: Is the SLM the primary driver of emotional prosody for all hybrid TTS systems? The diagnostic was only run on CosyVoice2 and IndexTTS2; different architectures (e.g., VITS, DiffTTS) might allocate emotion processing differently.
- **Sufficiency of linear mixing for mixed emotions**: Do weighted sums of steering vectors produce perceptually accurate mixed emotions, or are non‑linear methods needed? Human emotional blends are often non‑additive; the linearity assumption remains untested.
- **Cross‑cultural and demographic transferability**: Does activation steering generalize to diverse cultural and demographic contexts where emotional expression norms vary? All evaluations were on English; cultural biases in emotion‑labeled data could lead to uneven performance or stereotyped affect.

## Limitations
- Hook implementation details missing: exact layer names, tensor shapes, and insertion points for activation extraction are unspecified, hindering exact replication.
- Speaker‑specific vectors: Steering vectors require speaker‑ and transcript‑matched neutral recordings; obtaining these for new speakers is costly, limiting zero‑shot speaker transfer.
- Linearity assumption: Mixed‑emotion control via weighted sums may fail for subtle or non‑linear emotional blends, especially when emotions are not well represented in the training data.

## Confidence
- **SLM as primary driver of emotional prosody**: High  
- **Linear separability enabling vector composability**: Medium  
- **Acoustic‑only vectors enabling text‑emotion mismatch**: Medium  

## Next Checks
1. **Architectural generalization**: Re‑run the cross‑conditioning diagnostic on a VITS‑based TTS with a separate prosody predictor to test the universality of the SLM‑primary claim.  
2. **Non‑linear steering comparison**: Derive emotion directions via linear probes versus a small autoencoder; compare mixed‑emotion fidelity and naturalness to assess whether linearity suffices.  
3. **Zero‑shot cross‑speaker steering**: Apply vectors derived from a seen speaker to an unseen speaker; measure emotion similarity and speaker identity drift to evaluate the portability of acoustic‑only vectors.
