# Dissecting Quantization Error: A Concentration-Alignment Perspective

## Quick Facts

- arXiv ID: 2603.04359
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04359
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: CAT jointly optimizes concentration and alignment, achieving SQNR gains up to 10 dB and W4A4 performance rivaling W6A6 quantization.

## Executive Summary

This paper introduces a novel framework for analyzing quantization error in large language models by decomposing signal-to-quantization-noise ratio (SQNR) into two interpretable factors: concentration (outlier severity) and alignment (directional similarity between weights and activations). The analysis reveals that prior function-preserving transforms, such as rotations or Hadamard matrices, only improve concentration while leaving alignment unchanged. Motivated by this insight, the authors propose the Concentration-Alignment Transform (CAT), a block-diagonal linear transform that uses calibration data to jointly optimize both terms. CAT approximates the SQNR-maximizing solution with a manageable computational budget. Experiments across multiple LLMs at 4-bit precision show that CAT consistently matches or outperforms state-of-the-art transform-based methods (QuaRot, FlatQuant etc.), with gains up to 10 dB SQNR on problematic layers and W4A4 accuracy rivaling 6-bit quantization.

## Method Summary

CAT is a post-training quantization transform for linear layers. It estimates activation covariance $\Sigma_x$ and weight autocorrelation $\Sigma_w$ from a small calibration set. The SQNR-optimal alignment transform is the matrix geometric mean $M = (\Sigma_w \# \Sigma_x^{-1})^{1/2}$. To reduce cost, CAT approximates $M$ with a block-diagonal matrix where channels are partitioned into blocks of size $k$ (default 128). This block-diagonal approximation is then composed with a Hadamard matrix $H$, which improves concentration by spreading outliers while preserving alignment. The final transform is $T = H M_{\text{block}}$. Deploying CAT requires fusing $T^{-1}$ into weights and inserting $T$ before linear activations. The method works without training but can be optionally fine-tuned on a calibration set for further gains. CAT is compatible with standard quantization schemes like RTN and GPTQ.

## Key Results

- SQNR decomposition reveals quantization error depends separately on concentration and alignment, not just outlier reduction.
- Orthogonal transforms (e.g., Hadamard) improve concentration but leave alignment unchanged.
- CAT jointly optimizes both, achieving up to 10 dB SQNR improvement on problematic layers and W4A4 accuracy rivaling W6A6 quantization across multiple LLMs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

1. **SQNR Decomposition into Concentration and Alignment**  
   **Claim:** The SQNR of a quantized linear layer can be decomposed into a bit-width term, an activation concentration term, a weight concentration term, and an alignment term.  
   **Mechanism:** Theorem 2.4 approximates SQNR as $\frac{1}{2} N(b)^2 (C(x) \parallel C(W)) A(x,W)$, where $N(b)=2^b-1$, concentration $C(x)=E[\|x\|^2]/E[r(x)^2]$ captures outlier severity (range $r(x)$), and alignment $A(x,W)=E[\|Wx\|^2]/(\|W\|_F E[\|x\|^2])$ measures directional similarity.  
   **Core assumption:** Negligible clipping error and de-correlated quantization noise.  
   **Evidence anchors:** Theorem 2.4, Lemmas 2.2, 2.3; Figure 2 validates approximation. Prior works focus only on concentration.  
   **Break condition:** Fails when clipping error is significant or quantization noise correlates with signal.

2. **Orthogonal Transforms Improve Only Concentration, Not Alignment**  
   **Claim:** Orthogonal linear transforms (e.g., Hadamard rotations) improve concentration but leave alignment unchanged.  
   **Mechanism:** Rotations mix channels, spreading outliers and making distributions more Gaussian-like, thereby increasing concentration. Alignment is invariant under orthogonal transformations: $A(Rx, WR^T) = A(x,W)$.  
   **Core assumption:** Transform is orthogonal and applied consistently to weights and activations.  
   **Evidence anchors:** Abstract; Section 3 states invariance; Figure 5 shows alignment unchanged by Hadamard.  
   **Break condition:** Non-orthogonal components (e.g., per-channel scaling) can alter alignment.

3. **CAT Jointly Optimizes via Block-Diagonal Alignment Transform with Hadamard**  
   **Claim:** The alignment-optimal transform is the matrix geometric mean of weight and activation autocorrelations; approximating it with a block-diagonal structure (CAT) jointly optimizes concentration and alignment, achieving higher SQNR than rotation-only methods.  
   **Mechanism:** Maximizing $A(Mx, W M^{-1})$ yields $M = (\Sigma_w \# \Sigma_x^{-1})^{1/2}$. Composing $M$ with a Hadamard matrix $H$ preserves alignment while improving concentration. CAT uses block-diagonal $M_{\text{block}}$ (blocks of size $k$) to reduce cost, yielding $T = H M_{\text{block}}$.  
   **Core assumption:** Block-diagonal approximation adequate; calibration data provides accurate covariances.  
   **Evidence anchors:** Abstract; Section 4 derivation; Figures 5,6 show CAT surpasses Hadamard and channel scaling.  
   **Break condition:** Approximation suboptimal if cross-block correlations strong or covariance estimates noisy.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Quantization**  
  Why needed: Quantization reduces precision and introduces error; understanding uniform integer quantization is prerequisite.  
  Quick check: "For a tensor quantized symmetrically to $b$ bits, what is the number of quantization levels $N(b)$? How is the quantization step size $\Delta$ defined?" (Answer: $N(b)=2^b-1$; $\Delta = \frac{\max-\min}{N(b)}$ for symmetric, $\Delta = \frac{2\max}{N(b)}$ if min=0?)

- **Concept: SQNR**  
  Why needed: SQNR is the primary metric for quantization error; analysis and experiments revolve around it.  
  Quick check: "Write the definition of SQNR for a quantized linear layer as in Equation 1. What does a higher SQNR value indicate about the quantization quality?" (Answer: $\text{SQNR} = \frac{\mathbb{E}[\|y\|^2]}{\mathbb{E}[\|y-\hat{y}\|^2]}$ where $y=Wx$, $\hat{y}$ quantized; higher means lower error.)

- **Concept: Concentration**  
  Why needed: One of two SQNR factors; captures outlier severity.  
  Quick check: "What does a low concentration value (higher dB) tell you about a tensor’s distribution? How is concentration mathematically defined?" (Answer: $C(x) = \mathbb{E}[\|x\|^2] / \mathbb{E}[r(x)^2]$; low concentration means more outliers/larger range relative to energy.)

- **Concept: Alignment**  
  Why needed: The other SQNR factor; measures directional similarity between weight and activation spaces.  
  Quick check: "Why is alignment invariant under rotations? What does a high alignment value indicate?" (Answer: Invariant because both weights and activations transform equivalently under orthogonal transforms; high alignment means weight columns align with activation principal components.)

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**: Calibration data loader -> Activation covariance estimator & Weight autocorrelation calculator -> Block transform builder (with Hadamard generator) -> Composite transform builder -> Model adapter (fuses $T^{-1}$ into weights, inserts $T$) -> Quantization modules -> Evaluator

- **Critical path**:
  1. Forward calibration data through model to collect activation statistics per layer.
  2. Compute per-layer activation covariance $\Sigma_x$ and weight autocorrelation $\Sigma_w = W^T W$.
  3. Partition channels into blocks of size $k$; compute per-block matrix geometric mean to form block-diagonal $M_{\text{block}}$.
  4. Generate Hadamard matrix $H$ (fixed, deterministic).
  5. Construct composite transform $T = H M_{\text{block}}$.
  6. Fuse $T^{-1}$ into weights: $W' = W M_{\text{block}}^{-1} H^T$; insert $T$ before each linear layer's activation.
  7. Save transformed model; during inference, apply $T$ to activations, then quantize; use $W'$ quantized.

- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Block size $k$: Smaller (e.g., 32) reduces compute/memory but misses cross-channel correlations; larger (e.g., 512) improves alignment but costs more. Default $k=128$ balances.
  - Hadamard vs. learnable rotations: Hadamard is fast and data-independent; learned rotations (e.g., SpinQuant) may improve concentration but require training.
  - Calibration data: More samples improve covariance estimates but increase time; typical 128 sequences of length 2048.
  - Training: CAT works without training; optional fine-tuning on language modeling loss yields small gains at extra cost.

- **Failure signatures**:
  - Performance drop on layers with strong head-wise dependencies (e.g., attention projections) if blocks split across heads; align blocks with attention heads.
  - Numerical instability in matrix geometric mean for ill-conditioned $\Sigma_x$; add small diagonal regularization (e.g., $10^{-3} I$).
  - Overfitting to calibration domain; evaluate on multiple validation sets and increase calibration diversity if performance degrades out-of-distribution.
  - High memory during calibration for large models; use activation checkpointing or reduce batch size.

- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Concentration analysis**: Using Llama 3.2 1B, compute concentration $C(x)$ and $C(W)$ for each linear layer under no transform, channel scaling, Hadamard, and CAT ($k=128$). Plot to confirm CAT achieves concentration gains comparable to Hadamard.
  2. **Alignment analysis**: Compute alignment $A(x,W)$ for same layers/transforms. Plot to verify CAT significantly improves alignment while Hadamard does not.
  3. **End-to-end quantization**: Implement CAT ($k=128$) for W4A4 on Llama 3 8B. Perform PTQ (with optional GPTQ) and evaluate Wikitext perplexity and LM-Harness 0-shot accuracy. Compare against QuaRot, FlatQuant; expect CAT to match or outperform.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. Which transforms provide the best speed-accuracy trade-off when approximating the CAT optimal transform? (The paper does not compare various approximation structures like Kronecker or permutations.)
2. How can we design more effective approximations of CAT using mergeable rotations or permutations? (Future work: explore such structured approximations.)
3. How does block size affect the trade-off between alignment gains and computational cost in block-diagonal CAT? (Paper uses fixed $k=128$; optimal size unknown and may vary by layer.)
4. Does the concentration-alignment SQNR decomposition remain valid at very low bit-widths where clipping error is non-negligible? (Theorem 2.4 assumes negligible clipping; validity at 2-bit or lower is unknown.)

## Limitations

- Block assignment strategy (contiguous vs. permuted vs. clustered blocks) is unspecified and critically affects the block-diagonal approximation's alignment improvement.
- Training procedure for "CAT with additional training" (loss function, optimizer, learning rate, epochs) is not detailed, making reproduction of reported gains speculative.
- The SQNR decomposition assumes negligible clipping and decorrelated noise; its validity under aggressive 4-bit quantization or highly non-Gaussian activations is not fully characterized.

## Confidence

- **High**: SQNR decomposition into concentration/alignment and alignment invariance under orthogonal transforms (theoretically derived, empirically validated).
- **Medium**: Optimality of matrix geometric mean and sufficiency of block-diagonal approximation (evidence strong but sensitive to block partitioning).
- **Low**: Training protocol details for optional fine-tuning (no implementation specifics provided).
