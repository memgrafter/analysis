# Making Large Language Models Speak Tulu: Structured Prompting for an Extremely Low-Resource Language

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2602.15378
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15378
*   **Reference count:** 16
*   **Primary result:** Structured prompting reduces vocabulary contamination from a related high-resource language (Kannada) by 75 percentage points and achieves 85% grammatical accuracy in Tulu without model fine-tuning.

## Executive Summary
This study tackles the challenge of enabling large language models (LLMs) to generate output in an extremely low-resource language (Tulu) without any fine-tuning. The authors develop a structured, multi-layer prompting technique that combats severe vocabulary contamination from a closely related high-resource language (Kannada). Their approach combines a standardized romanization system, explicit grammatical documentation, targeted negative lexical constraints, and self-verification prompts. Evaluated across three modern LLMs with validation from native speakers, the full five-layer prompt reduces Kannada token interference from ~80% to 5% and yields 85% grammatical accuracy. The method demonstrates that careful prompt engineering can partially overcome data scarcity for language generation.

## Method Summary
The research employs a zero fine-tuning, prompt-engineering pipeline. The core deliverable is a fixed five-layer prompt (~2800 tokens) designed for any query. Its layers are: (1) an identity role assignment, (2) a list of 50 explicit negative constraints prohibiting specific high-probability Kannada words and mandating their Tulu equivalents, (3) exhaustive grammar rules (verb paradigms, case markers, SOV syntax), (4) 10-15 few-shot exemplars of validated Tulu Q-A pairs, and (5) a self-verification checklist for the model to audit its output. Inputs are first romanized using a custom scheme to disambiguate from Kannada script and improve tokenization. The system is evaluated on a held-out test set of 100 manually curated questions. Performance is measured primarily by (a) contamination rate (presence of prohibited Kannada words) and (b) grammatical accuracy (rule-based compliance checked by an automated validator and native speakers). Ablation experiments systematically remove prompt layers to isolate their contributions.

## Key Results
*   The complete five-layer prompt reduces vocabulary contamination from a baseline of ~80% to 5% and achieves 85% grammatical accuracy.
*   Negative lexical constraints provide consistent, architecture-agnostic improvements of 12–18 percentage points in reducing contamination.
*   Explicit grammar documentation yields significant gains (8–22 percentage points) that vary by model; a falsification test with incorrect rules causes a 47 percentage point drop, confirming active model utilization.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Negative Lexical Constraints for Token Suppression
*   **Claim:** Explicit prohibition of high-probability tokens from a related high-resource language (Kannada) consistently reduces vocabulary contamination by 12–18 percentage points across diverse LLM architectures.
*   **Mechanism:** At each generation step, the model samples from a probability distribution. Without constraints, distributions are heavily weighted toward frequent Kannada tokens (e.g., `naanu` for "I"). The prompt directly names prohibited tokens and their Tulu alternatives, creating a strong, localized signal that appears to downweight the competing high-probability tokens in practice, rather than relying on a weak positive instruction ("respond in Tulu").
*   **Core assumption:** Models can follow precise lexical prohibitions even when those prohibitions target tokens with extremely high pretraining likelihood, and this effect generalizes beyond the explicitly listed words to some degree.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [abstract] "negative constraints to suppress high-probability tokens from related languages... provide consistent improvements (12–18 percentage points)."
    *   [section 3.3] Ablation: removing constraints increased contamination by a mean of 15pp. "Negative constraints worked similarly well across all architectures."
    *   [section 5] "The phrase 'never use naanu, always use y¯an'... influences token probabilities more strongly than general instructions."
    *   [corpus] No direct corpus evidence; related work on NeuroLogic decoding (Lu et al., 2022) uses logit manipulation, but this paper's approach uses prompt-based constraints.
*   **Break condition:** Effectiveness would diminish if the target and interfering language share very few lexical items, or if the model's tokenizer merges the prohibited and target tokens into the same subword unit.

### Mechanism 2: Script Romanization for Disambiguation & Tokenization
*   **Claim:** A carefully designed romanization system reduces vocabulary contamination (from 88% to 5% in one experiment) and improves grammatical accuracy by providing a distinct signal from the interfering language and reducing subword fragmentation.
*   **Mechanism:** Romanization prevents script-based confusion where Tulu written in the Kannada script is misidentified as Kannada. It also aligns input text with the model's subword vocabulary more efficiently (1.4 vs. 3.2 tokens/word), reducing context window pressure and improving the model's ability to maintain language boundaries.
*   **Core assumption:** The model's internal representations are sensitive to script differences and tokenization efficiency, and a standardized romanization can serve as a more separable input signal than a shared script.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [abstract] "romanization standardization... reduces vocabulary contamination from 80% to 5%."
    *   [section 3.1] Controlled experiment: Kannada script with Tulu instruction yielded 88% Kannada responses; their romanization yielded 5%. Tokenization efficiency correlated with contamination (r=0.78, p<0.001).
    *   [section 5] "Explicit romanization may therefore better align input with the subword units... reducing interference."
    *   [corpus] Prior work (Dhamecha et al., 2021) supports tokenization benefits for Indic languages; this paper extends with a disambiguation-focused scheme.
*   **Break condition:** If the target language has no orthographic overlap with a high-resource relative, the disambiguation benefit is negated. If the language's phonology cannot be faithfully represented in romanization without ambiguity, the approach may introduce new errors.

### Mechanism 3: Explicit Grammar Documentation as an Instantiator
*   **Claim:** Providing exhaustive, explicit grammatical rules (paradigm tables, case markers, syntax) allows models to apply pretrained, language-general grammatical operations to a new language, achieving 85% accuracy; falsification with incorrect rules drops accuracy by 47pp, proving active reliance.
*   **Mechanism:** During pretraining, models develop abstract representations of grammatical functions (e.g., "mark tense," "mark case"). The prompt supplies the concrete morphological exponents for Tulu (e.g., which suffix marks dative). The model appears to treat the documentation as a lookup table to instantiate these abstract operations.
*   **Core assumption:** LLMs encode reusable, cross-linguistically applicable grammatical operations during pretraining that can be bound to new language-specific forms via natural language instructions.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [abstract] "explicit grammar documentation... effects vary by architecture (8–22 points)."
    *   [section 4.4] Falsification: "With incorrect grammar, accuracy dropped to 38% (vs. 85%), a 47pp decrease."
    *   [section 5] "We interpret this as evidence that models learn language-general grammatical operations... that can be instantiated with language-specific morphology when provided."
    *   [corpus] Related work (Wang et al., 2023) shows formal grammars in prompts help structured generation, supporting the mechanism.
*   **Break condition:** For languages with grammatical features absent in the model's training data (e.g., polypersonal agreement, tonal morphology) or with wildly different typological parameters, the model may lack the necessary abstract operations to instantiate. Variation in effect size across models (GPT-4o: +22pp, Llama: +8pp) suggests dependence on pretraining data diversity.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Tokenization & Subword Fragmentation**
    *   **Why needed here:** Tokenization efficiency directly impacts contamination. The romanization reduced tokens/word from 3.2 to 1.4, correlating with lower contamination (r=0.78). An engineer must understand how a model's tokenizer partitions text to predict interference and design efficient inputs.
    *   **Quick check question:** `Given a sentence in Kannada script vs. your romanized Tulu, estimate the token count difference and explain why the latter might help the model maintain a "Tulu" representation.`
*   **Concept: Constraint Satisfaction in Autoregressive Decoding**
    *   **Why needed here:** The core debiasing mechanism (negative constraints) operates by influencing the probability distribution at each step. An engineer must grasp that without logit control, constraints are "soft" and rely on the model's instruction-following capacity to downweight prohibited tokens.
    *   **Quick check question:** `What is the key difference between a decoding-time method like logit bias and this paper's prompt-based negative constraint in terms of API accessibility and mechanism?`
*   **Concept: Cross-Linguistic Transfer of Abstract Grammar**
    *   **Why needed here:** The 47pp drop in the falsification experiment shows models actively use the provided rules. This implies the model isn't just parroting examples but applying a general "grammatical processor" to new morphological exponents. An engineer must recognize that grammar documentation's value is in providing instantiations, not teaching grammar from scratch.
    *   **Quick check question:** `If providing incorrect case markers causes a large drop, what does that suggest about the internal state of the model when processing the grammar documentation layer?`

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:** Input Normalization -> Prompt Assembly -> Generation -> Output
*   **Critical path:** Romanization (input) -> Prompt Layer Order (Constraints *before* Grammar for salience) -> Self-Verification (final layer to activate checking).
*   **Design tradeoffs:**
    *   **Prompt Length vs. Efficacy:** The ~2800-token prompt is highly effective but costly. Shorter prompts failed to suppress contamination.
    *   **Ordering vs. Naturalness:** Placing constraints early maximizes salience but may make the prompt feel unnatural to the model, potentially harming fluency (evidenced by 3.6/5.0 fluency score).
    *   **Generality vs. Specificity:** The 50-word constraint list is language-specific but provides consistent gains. A more generic "avoid Kannada" instruction was ineffective.
*   **Failure signatures:**
    *   **Contamination spike:** Model output contains prohibited Kannada words (`naanu`, `yenu`). Indicates constraints were ignored or forgotten.
    *   **Grammatical decay:** Incorrect verb conjugation or case marking despite rules. Suggests rules were not properly instantiated or were overwhelmed by token probabilities.
    *   **"Translatese":** Output scores 3.6/5.0 on fluency, sounding unnatural. Indicates pragmatic/sociolinguistic competence is not captured by the structural prompt.
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1.  **Baseline Validation:** Run minimal prompt ("Respond in Tulu") on all target models. Expect ~80% contamination, <25% grammar. Confirms the severity of the interference problem.
    2.  **Component Isolation - Constraints:** Add *only* the negative constraints layer (with identity). Measure change in contamination. Expect 12–18pp reduction. Validates the core debiasing mechanism.
    3.  **Falsification Test:** Replace the correct grammar documentation with intentionally incorrect rules (e.g., swap dative/accusative markers). Expect ~47pp drop in grammar accuracy. Provides causal evidence that models actively use the provided grammar rules.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
*   **Question:** To what extent does the structured prompting approach generalize to languages that lack a closely related high-resource "donor" language (like Kannada for Tulu) or that are typologically distant (e.g., polysynthetic, tonal, or isolate languages)?
*   **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state in the Limitations (Section 6): "Generalization beyond Tulu... remains uncertain and may require substantially different prompting strategies." They also note their evaluation was limited to one Dravidian language and generalization to "typologically distant languages remains untested."
*   **Why unresolved:** The paper's success hinges on exploiting systematic interference from a specific, closely related high-resource language (Kannada). Languages without such a clear relative or with profoundly different grammatical structures may not benefit from negative constraints targeting lexical contamination, and the grammar documentation strategy may not capture their typological needs.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** A replication study applying the identical five-layer prompting framework to a diverse set of low-resource languages from different families (e.g., a Mayan language, a Siberian isolate, a Niger-Congo tone language) with minimal digital presence. Success would be measured by comparable reductions in contamination and gains in grammatical accuracy, using native speaker validation.

### Open Question 2
*   **Question:** What is the precise mechanistic scope of "negative constraints"? Do they create a localized, token-level suppression effect only for explicitly listed constraints, or does their inclusion induce a broader, structural shift in the model's output distribution that reduces the probability of other, unlisted tokens from the interfering language?
*   **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors discuss the 12-18 pp consistent benefit from constraints but explicitly note: "our analysis does not reveal whether this suppression transfers to other Tulu tokens beyond those explicitly listed." They propose analyzing logit changes for common phrase pairs as future work.
*   **Why unresolved:** Understanding the mechanism is crucial for efficient prompt design. If the effect is highly localized, prompts would need exhaustive negative lexicons for full disambiguation. If it induces a broader distributional shift, a smaller, strategic set of constraints might suffice, making the approach more scalable.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** A detailed logit-level analysis during generation with/without constraints. This would track probability changes for (a) explicitly constrained Kannada tokens, (b) unconstrained Kannada tokens in the watchlist, and (c) Tulu equivalents. A systemic decrease in probability for category (b) would indicate a broader suppressive effect.

### Open Question 3
*   **Question:** What architectural or training factors explain the significant variation in how different LLMs (GPT-4o vs. Llama 3.1 70B) benefit from explicit grammar documentation (22pp vs. 8pp improvement)?
*   **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The results show a dramatic, model-dependent effect for grammar rules: "effects vary by model architecture (8–22 points)." The ablation table (Table 3) shows removing grammar hurts GPT-4o far more (24pp loss) than Llama (8pp loss). The authors speculate this capability is "data-driven rather than architecturally innate" but do not investigate.
*   **Why unresolved:** This variability is a critical practical consideration. If some model families are innately better at utilizing explicit linguistic rules, it would guide model selection for prompting-based approaches to new low-resource languages. The underlying cause (e.g., pre-training data composition, fine-tuning objectives, internal representation capabilities) is unknown.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled probing experiments across a wider range of model families (e.g., Claude, Mistral, earlier vs. later GPT versions). Correlating the magnitude of grammar benefit with known training data characteristics (e.g., proportion of grammatical text, linguistic task fine-tuning) or with internal metrics (e.g., perplexity on held-out grammar rules provided in-context) could identify predictive factors.

### Open Question 4
*   **Question:** Can the framework's reliance on a manually curated, single-dialect grammar (Mangalore) and a fixed negative lexicon be automated or scaled for languages where such expert elicitation is not feasible?
*   **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The grammar was created via "targeted elicitation sessions" with three native speakers. The negative constraints list (50 word pairs) was manually constructed based on observed failure modes. The authors acknowledge the risk of "linguistic standardization" from codifying one variety.
*   **Why unresolved:** The paper presents a successful but expert-heavy pipeline. For thousands of other low-resource languages, such dedicated linguistic expertise and native speaker time for prompt crafting is unavailable. The core value of the work is its generalizable *procedure*, but its *instantiation* for a new language currently requires this scarce resource investment.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** Developing and evaluating automated or semi-automated alternatives. For grammar: using existing, possibly imperfect, descriptive grammars or web-crawled text to generate rule candidates via a high-resource LLM, then filtering with minimal human input. For negative constraints: algorithmically extracting high-frequency interference tokens by comparing a related high-resource language's lexicon against a small seed set of target language words. Success would be defined as performance within 5-10pp of the manually-crafted Tulu system.

### Open Question 5
*   **Question:** How can the prompting framework be extended to address the qualitative gap in pragmatic competence and natural fluency (scoring 3.6/5.0), where errors like register mismatches (25% of errors) and unnatural code-switching persist?
*   **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors identify fluency as the lowest-scoring dimension and state: "This gap between grammatical correctness and pragmatic naturalness reflects the difficulty of encoding sociolinguistic competence, discourse conventions, and idiomatic usage through prompting alone." They note register mismatches were the most common error category.
*   **Why unresolved:** The current system excels at syntax and core lexicon but fails at the pragmatics of *how* to speak naturally in context. This limits real-world utility. The five-layer prompt structure seems optimized for grammatical constraint-following, not for modeling sociocultural nuance, which may require fundamentally different prompt components (e.g., persona narratives, discourse-mode examples, cultural context layers) or post-hoc rewriting strategies.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic A/B testing of new prompt layers designed to target pragmatic gaps. For register: adding examples and explicit rules for formality levels across social relationships (e.g., elder vs. peer). For idiomatic usage: in-context demonstrations of natural Tulu phrases vs. literal translations. For discourse: modeling multi-turn conversational flow with pragmatic markers. Fluency gains would be measured via human evaluation, specifically targeting the previously low-scoring dimensions, without sacrificing the hard-won grammatical accuracy.

## Limitations
*   The exact, verbatim prompt content (50-word constraint list, full grammar tables, examples, verification checklist) is not released, requiring reconstruction that risks implementation drift.
*   The held-out evaluation dataset of 100 Q-A pairs and the code for the automated grammar checker are not public, hindering exact replication of reported metrics.
*   The approach's effectiveness is demonstrated only for the specific Tulu-Kannada language pair; generalizability to other low-resource languages with different interference patterns or typologies is uncertain.

## Next Checks
1.  **Exact Material Replication**: Obtain the verbatim five-layer prompt (50 constraints, full grammar tables, examples, verification checklist) from the authors. Run the full pipeline on the same three models with the original test set to confirm the 85% grammar / 5% contamination numbers. Failure here points to missing implementation details.
2.  **Cross-Linguistic Generalization Test**: Apply the identical prompt architecture to another extremely low-resource language with a high-resource relative (e.g., **Sardinian vs. Italian**, **Manx vs. English**). If contamination reduction and grammar gains are significantly lower, the mechanism is Tulu/Kannada-specific and relies on particular lexical/grammatical overlap.
3.  **Tokenization-Interference Causality Probe**: For a fixed set of Tulu sentences, generate multiple romanization variants of differing tokenization efficiency (e.g., with/without diacritics, different granularity). Measure (a) tokens/word per model’s tokenizer and (b) contamination rate in constrained generation. A weakened correlation (r < 0.78) would challenge the proposed tokenization mechanism.
