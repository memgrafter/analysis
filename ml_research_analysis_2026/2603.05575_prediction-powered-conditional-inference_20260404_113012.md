# Prediction-Powered Conditional Inference

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.05575  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05575  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** PPCI achieves near-nominal coverage with substantially sharper confidence intervals than labeled-only or global prediction-powered methods for conditional inference with scarce labels and abundant unlabeled covariates.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Prediction-Powered Conditional Inference (PPCI), a framework for pointwise inference on a conditional target (e.g., a conditional mean or quantile) when only a small labeled sample is available alongside a large pool of unlabeled covariates and a pre-trained machine‑learning predictor. PPCI combines an RKHS‑based localization technique that converts the conditional moment at a test point into an unconditional weighted moment with a prediction‑powered decomposition that injects the predictor’s information via a residual‑plus‑plug‑in structure. Two‑fold cross‑fitting decouples weight estimation from the contributions of unlabeled data, yielding sharp non‑asymptotic bounds and asymptotic normality. The resulting confidence intervals attain near‑nominal coverage and are notably narrower than those from existing methods in both simulations and real‑data applications (census income, BlogFeedback).

## Method Summary
PPCI addresses the problem of estimating a conditional functional \(\theta_0(x_0)\) defined by a conditional moment condition \(\mathbb{E}[\ell(Y; \theta) \mid X=x_0]=0\). Given labeled data \((X_i,Y_i)_{i=1}^n\), unlabeled covariates \((\tilde X_u)_{u=1}^N\), and a black‑box predictor \(f\), the method first approximates the conditional moment via an RKHS‑based weight \(w_{x_0,\lambda} = (T_K+\lambda I)^{-1}K_{x_0}\), where \(T_K\) is the kernel integral operator and \(\lambda\) a regularization parameter. The moment at \(x_0\) is then decomposed into a residual term (estimated from labeled data) and a plug‑in term (estimated from unlabeled data using \(f\)). Cross‑fitting on the unlabeled sample ensures independence between weight estimates and the scores they weight. The estimator \(\hat{\theta}(x_0)\) solves the resulting empirical moment equation; its asymptotic variance is estimated from the two components, yielding a confidence interval that enjoys validity irrespective of predictor accuracy and gains efficiency when \(f\) is informative.

## Key Results
- PPCI achieves pointwise asymptotic normality with consistent variance estimation, enabling construction of Gaussian‑based confidence intervals.
- Non‑asymptotic error bounds establish minimax‑optimal convergence rates under RKHS smoothness conditions.
- In simulations and real data, PPCI produces substantially narrower intervals than labeled‑only localization or global prediction‑powered inference while maintaining near‑nominal coverage.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: RKHS‑based localization
The conditional moment \(\eta(x_0;\theta)=\mathbb{E}[\ell(Y;\theta)\mid X=x_0]\) is approximated by an unconditional weighted moment \(\eta_\lambda(x_0;\theta)=\mathbb{E}[w_{x_0,\lambda}(X)\ell(Y;\theta)]\), where the weight \(w_{x_0,\lambda}\) is obtained via Tikhonov regularization in the RKHS associated with the kernel. This converts the pointwise conditional problem into an unconditional one, at the cost of a small bias controlled by \(\lambda\) and the smoothness of \(\eta(\cdot;\theta)\). The approximation error scales with the pointwise leverage \(\mathbf{D}(x_0;\lambda)\), which for interior \(x_0\) behaves like \(\lambda^{-d/(2m)}\).

### Mechanism 2: Prediction‑powered decomposition
The localized moment is algebraically split into two pieces:
\[
\eta_\lambda(x_0;\theta) = \mathbb{E}\!\bigl[ w_{x_0,\lambda}(X)\bigl(\ell(Y;\theta)-\ell(f(X);\theta)\bigr) \bigr] + \mathbb{E}\!\bigl[ w_{x_0,\lambda}(X)\ell(f(X);\theta) \bigr].
\]
The first term uses the labeled sample and captures the residual variance; the second term plugs in the predictor on the unlabeled covariates, with variance that shrinks as \(1/N\). By combining these estimators, the method reduces variance when \(f\) is informative while remaining valid even if \(f\) is poor.

### Mechanism 3: Two‑fold cross‑fitting
The unlabeled data are split into two folds. For each fold \(m\), the weight \(\hat w^{(m)}_{x_0,\lambda}\) is estimated using only the other fold. The labeled contribution uses the average weight \(\bar w = (\hat w^{(1)}+\hat w^{(2)})/2\), while the unlabeled contribution in fold \(m\) uses the out‑of‑fold weight \(\hat w^{(3-m)}_{x_0,\lambda}\). This decoupling prevents dependence between weight estimation and score contributions, simplifying the variance analysis and ensuring that the asymptotic variance equals the oracle (known‑weight) variance.

## Foundational Learning
- **Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS)**  
  *Why needed:* The localization weight and target conditional moment are defined via the RKHS norm; smoothness assumptions on \(\eta(\cdot;\theta)\) in this space control bias.  
  *Quick check:* What does the eigenvalue decay rate \(\mu_j \asymp j^{-2m/d}\) imply about smoothness of functions in \(\mathcal{H}\)?

- **Prediction‑Powered Inference (PPI)**  
  *Why needed:* PPCI extends the global PPI idea (decompose moment into residual + plug‑in) from unconditional to conditional settings, enabling variance reduction via an auxiliary predictor.  
  *Quick check:* In global PPI, how is the population moment \(E[\ell(Y;\theta_0)]=0\) split, and which data sources estimate each part?

- **Cross‑fitting**  
  *Why needed:* Estimating the localizing weights from the same unlabeled points that later appear in the unlabeled scores introduces dependence; cross‑fitting eliminates it, yielding sharp non‑asymptotic bounds and asymptotic normality.  
  *Quick check:* Why does using the same data to estimate \(\hat w\) and compute \(\sum w(X)\ell(f(X);\theta)\) lead to a more complicated variance structure?

- **Tikhonov regularization & pointwise leverage**  
  *Why needed:* The weight \(w_{x_0,\lambda}\) and the bias–variance trade‑off are governed by \(\mathbf{D}(x_0;\lambda)=\langle K_{x_0}, (T_K+\lambda I)^{-1}K_{x_0}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\). Its scaling \(\asymp \lambda^{-d/(2m)}\) for interior points directly appears in rates.  
  *Quick check:* How does \(\mathbf{D}(x_0;\lambda)\) scale with \(\lambda\) when \(\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}^m(\mathcal{X})\)? Express exponent in terms of \(d\) and \(m\).

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Labeled data + unlabeled data + predictor \(f\) + kernel → Localization module (computes fold‑specific weight functions) → Prediction‑powered estimator (solves cross‑fitted moment equation) → Inference module (computes Jacobian & variance estimate) → Confidence interval.

**Critical path:**  
1. Split unlabeled data into two equal folds \(I_1, I_2\).  
2. For each fold \(m\): compute Gram matrix \(\Sigma^{(m)}\) and target vector \(k_0^{(m)}\); select \(\hat\lambda_m\) via L‑curve; obtain weight \(\hat w^{(m)}_{x_0,\hat\lambda_m} = (\Sigma^{(m)}+\lambda I)^{-1}k_0^{(m)}\).  
3. Set \(\bar w = (\hat w^{(1)}+\hat w^{(2)})/2\) for labeled scores; use out‑of‑fold \(\hat w^{(3-m)}\) for unlabeled scores.  
4. Form empirical moment \(\hat\eta_\lambda(x_0;\theta)\) and solve \(\hat\eta_\lambda(x_0;\hat\theta)=0\).  
5. At \(\hat\theta\), compute score contributions for labeled and unlabeled parts; estimate residual and prediction variances; combine into \(\hat V(x_0)\). Compute Jacobian \(\hat J_\lambda(x_0)\).  
6. Construct CI: \(\hat\theta \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\hat V/|\hat J|}\).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Cross‑fitting vs. non‑split:** Cross‑fitting demands \(N\) large enough for stable per‑fold weight estimates and discards half the data for weight learning, but simplifies theory and avoids dependence bias. Non‑split uses all unlabeled data for weight estimation but introduces dependence between \(\hat w\) and unlabeled scores, requiring more delicate arguments and sometimes poorer finite‑sample coverage.
- **Kernel choice:** Matérn kernels are used for their smoothness properties; Gaussian kernels are more flexible but may need stronger regularization. Bandwidth (if applicable) is tuned via LOOCV on a pilot labeled sample.
- **Regularization \(\lambda\):** Selected per fold by L‑curve; too small leads to high variance (large leverage), too large increases bias. Grid range and fineness affect stability.
- **Loss \(\ell\):** Assumed smooth and bounded; non‑smooth losses (e.g., quantile) may break theoretical guarantees without smoothing.
- **Budget allocation:** When labeling and unlabeled collection have different costs, optimal \(n,N\) can be chosen to minimize width (Proposition 2).

**Failure signatures:**  
- **Undercoverage:** Caused by ignoring weight–score dependence (non‑split) or overly aggressive \(\lambda\) (small \(\lambda\) makes Jacobian noisy). Diagnose by checking if coverage falls below nominal, especially with small \(n\) or poor \(f\).
- **Overly wide intervals:** Occur when \(f\) is uninformative (\(\sigma^2_{Y-f}\approx\sigma^2_Y\)) or \(\lambda\) is too large (high bias). Monitor relative width compared to labeled‑only method.
- **Numerical instability:** Solving \((\Sigma+\lambda I)^{-1}k_0\) can become ill‑conditioned if \(\lambda\) is very small; watch condition number and add jitter if needed.
- **Boundary effects:** Theory assumes interior \(x_0\); near boundaries the constant \(C_0\) in Lemma 1 degrades, potentially increasing variance. Flag test points with very few neighbors within kernel bandwidth.
- **Predictor overlap:** If \(f\) is trained on data that includes the labeled responses, dependence invalidates the decomposition. Always use sample‑splitting or cross‑prediction to ensure \(f(X_i)\) is independent of \(Y_i\).

**First three experiments:**  
1. **Synthetic validation:** Simulate \(\eta(x)=\sum_{k=1}^3 \beta_k \sin(\omega_k^\top x)\) in \(d=3\), noise \(\sigma_\varepsilon=2.0\), and a biased/noisy predictor \(f\). Compare PPCI (two‑fold) against labeled‑only and global PPI for test points inside \([0.7,0.85]^3\). Record RMSE, 95% coverage, and average width.
2. **Census income application:** Use California census data (age 70–100) with a pre‑trained XGBoost predictor for log‑income. Compare conditional mean estimation across ages for males/females. Use Nadaraya–Watson as an oracle. Verify PPCI maintains coverage while LO is conservative and PPI undercovers.
3. **Stress test of \(\lambda\) selection:** Fix \(n=200, N=5000\) in the synthetic setting. Run PPCI over a wide log‑grid of \(\lambda\) values. Plot coverage and width vs. \(\lambda\); confirm the L‑curve selects a \(\lambda\) near the width minimizer and that coverage remains ≈95% across a reasonable range. Also run with a fixed poor \(\lambda\) to show degradation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Adaptive weighting of ML predictions:** The current construction uses a fixed decomposition (ω=1). Developing principled, data‑driven methods to weight the predictor’s contribution could further improve efficiency, especially when \(f\) is weak or biased.
- **Extension to nonsmooth targets:** The theory requires smooth estimating functions. Extending PPCI to conditional quantiles or other nonsmooth targets without resorting to ad‑hoc smoothing is an open challenge.
- **Boundary test points:** The analysis assumes \(x_0\) lies in the interior of \(\mathcal{X}\). Theoretical and practical properties near the domain boundary (where edge effects may degrade performance) remain unexplored.
- **Predictor trained with overlapping data:** The paper assumes sample‑splitting to keep \(f\) independent of the labeled responses. In practice, one might train \(f\) on all available data. A theoretical analysis of the resulting dependence and possible corrections is lacking.

## Limitations
- **Smoothness assumption:** The theory requires the conditional moment function \(\eta(\cdot;\theta)\) to lie in an RKHS with smoothness \(m > d/2\). Violations (discontinuities, boundaries) may increase bias and variance.
- **Practical specification ambiguity:** The L‑curve corner detection, kernel bandwidth selection, and handling of predictor training splits are not fully specified, leaving room for implementation errors that could harm finite‑sample performance.
- **Finite‑sample regime:** Empirical validation is limited to relatively low‑dimensional settings (\(d\le 3\)) and specific kernels. Performance in higher dimensions or with more complex kernels remains uncertain.

3. **Predictor degradation robustness:** Systematically degrade the predictor \(f\) (add Gaussian noise, introduce constant bias, or reverse predictions) and confirm that PPCI maintains nominal coverage while the width gradually approaches that of the labeled‑only estimator as predictor quality worsens.
