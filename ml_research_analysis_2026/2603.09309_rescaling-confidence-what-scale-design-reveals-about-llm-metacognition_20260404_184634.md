# Rescaling Confidence: What Scale Design Reveals About LLM Metacognition

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.09309
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09309
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** A 0–20 confidence scale consistently improves metacognitive efficiency (higher Mratio) over the standard 0–100 format across multiple models and datasets.

## Executive Summary
This study examines how the design of numerical scales used to elicit confidence statements from large language models (LLMs) systematically distorts reported uncertainty. It finds that the common 0–100 scale induces severe "round-number" discretization, with over 78% of responses clustering on integers like 50, 80, 90, or 100. This clustering compromises the quality of the confidence signal for calibration. Through controlled experiments manipulating scale granularity, boundary placement, and range regularity, the authors demonstrate that a simpler 0–20 scale yields measurably better metacognitive sensitivity. The work establishes scale design as a critical, non-trivial experimental variable in LLM confidence research.

## Method Summary
The evaluation systematically varies three properties of numeric response scales: **granularity** (e.g., 0–100 vs. 0–20), **boundary placement** (e.g., standard [0,100] vs. compressed [60,100]), and **range regularity** (e.g., continuous vs. irregular intervals). For each configuration, models generate answers to QA tasks alongside a confidence rating. Metacognitive performance is quantified using **meta-d'**, a signal detection measure of how well confidence ratings discriminate between correct and incorrect responses. Higher meta-d' relative to the theoretical optimum (Mr ratio) indicates more efficient use of the confidence scale. Experiments span six LLMs and three diverse QA datasets.

## Key Results
- A **0–20 scale** yields significantly higher metacognitive efficiency (Mr ratio) than a 0–100 scale across all tested models and datasets.
- **Boundary compression** (e.g., a [60,100] range) degrades performance, as confidence reports cluster near the upper limit, reducing discriminative power.
- A strong **round-number bias** persists even with irregularly spaced scale options, indicating models inherently prefer neat numeric values.
- **Narrower ranges** (e.g., 0–20) increase the frequency of responses that technically fall outside the specified range, suggesting boundary adherence difficulties.

- **Critical Path:** The numerical confidence is produced via the same autoregressive decoding process as the answer text, not through a separate calibration head.
- **Design Tradeoffs:** Using the primary text-generation pathway for confidence reporting trades off flexibility (any number can be generated) for control (no guaranteed adherence to scale constraints).
- **Failure Signatures:** High frequency of round numbers (e.g., 50, 100) and out-of-range violations indicate the model's token selection policy overriding scale constraints.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- What is the underlying cognitive or architectural mechanism behind the round-number bias and the impact of scale granularity on metacognitive efficiency?
- How do these results generalize across different model architectures, parameter sizes, languages, and tasks beyond question answering?
- What are the effects of non-numeric confidence scales (e.g., verbal descriptors, sliders) on the accuracy and bias of uncertainty reporting?
- How does scale design influence other calibration metrics (e.g., Brier score) and practical applications that rely on confidence scores?

## Limitations
- Scope is limited to six models and three QA datasets; generalizability to other architectures, sizes, languages, and tasks (e.g., summarization) is unknown.
- Evaluation relies solely on meta-d'; other crucial calibration metrics like the Brier score are not reported.
- Only explicitly numeric prompt scales were tested; the impact of verbal descriptors (e.g., "very sure") or UI elements is unexamined.
- The underlying cognitive or architectural **mechanism** for the round-number bias is not diagnosed.

## Next Checks
1.  **Generalize to new settings:** Replicate the core experiment on encoder-decoder models (e.g., T5), multilingual models, and small models (<1B parameters), and on tasks like code generation or closed-ended reasoning.
2.  **Diagnose the bias mechanism:** Correlate confidence outputs with token frequencies in the training data or model's internal representations, and test if supervised fine-tuning on non-round confidence data reduces the effect.
