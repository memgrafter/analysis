# Unsupervised Anomaly Detection in NSL-KDD Using $β$-VAE: A Latent Space and Reconstruction Error Approach

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.19785
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19785
- **Reference count**: 18
- **Primary result**: Latent-space k‑NN (β=10⁻⁵, k=5000) attains mean AUROC 97.90% on NSL‑KDD, outperforming reconstruction error (96.78%).

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles unsupervised anomaly detection for network intrusion detection using the NSL‑KDD dataset. It employs a β‑Variational Autoencoder trained exclusively on normal traffic and evaluates two anomaly scoring strategies: reconstruction error and Euclidean distance to k‑nearest neighbors in the latent space. The latent‑space method achieves a mean AUROC of 97.90% with β=10⁻⁵ and k=5000, surpassing the best reconstruction error result (96.78% at β=0). The two approaches are complementary, and the latent‑space technique offers additional benefits such as better interpretability and potential for incremental learning.

## Method Summary
The study uses a β‑VAE with a symmetric fully‑connected architecture (encoder: 64→32→16; decoder: 16→32→64) and an 8‑dimensional latent space. The loss combines a reconstruction component (categorical cross‑entropy for one‑hot features, binary cross‑entropy for booleans, mean squared error for continuous features) and a KL divergence term scaled by β. Training occurs on normal‑only data from NSL‑KDD (Xtrain) using Adam (lr=0.001, batch=2048) across a range of β values. For evaluation, two anomaly detectors are constructed: (1) Lrec‑classification uses the reconstruction error as an anomaly score; (2) Zk‑classification projects the training set into the latent space, then for each test sample computes the average Euclidean distance to its k nearest neighbors among the normal training projections. AUROC on the union of normal test and attack samples serves as the primary metric. Results indicate that the latent‑space k‑NN approach consistently outperforms reconstruction error, especially at low β values.

## Key Results
- **Zk‑classification (β=10⁻⁵, k=5000)** yields a mean AUROC of **97.90%**.
- **Lrec‑classification (β=0)** achieves **96.78%** AUROC.
- The two scoring methods are complementary; the latent‑space approach also provides improved interpretability and supports incremental updates.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper attributes the superior performance of the latent‑space method to three interrelated mechanisms:

1. **Latent Space Neighborhood Density**: The β‑VAE encoder maps normal traffic samples into a compact, high‑density region in the 8‑dimensional latent space. Anomalous inputs, being out‑of‑distribution, project to low‑density areas far from the normal clusters. The average Euclidean distance to the k nearest neighbors among the normal training set thus serves as an effective anomaly score. Evidence includes formal definitions in the paper (Section V‑B) and the observation that Zk‑classification degrades when β is too high (Table II), suggesting the latent structure is crucial.

2. **Reconstruction Error Inadequacy**: While a VAE trained on normal data should reconstruct normal patterns accurately and yield high error for anomalies, the β‑VAE’s reconstruction fidelity is limited by the KL regularisation. The composite reconstruction loss Lrec (catering to heterogeneous feature types) proves less discriminative than the latent‑space distance, especially when β > 0, because the decoder’s capacity is constrained and anomalies can sometimes be reconstructed with moderate error. The peak Lrec‑AUROC occurs at β=0 (no KL penalty), confirming its reliance on perfect reconstruction.

3. **β as a Disentanglement‑Reconstruction Dial**: The hyperparameter β controls the trade‑off between latent‑space smoothness (promoted by the KL term) and reconstruction accuracy. For Zk‑classification, a small positive β (10⁻⁵) yields optimal performance by slightly regularising the latent space, making normal clusters denser and more separable without sacrificing reconstruction quality too much. For Lrec‑classification, the optimal β is 0. Experimental results (Table II) clearly show the inverse relationship between the two metrics, validating this mechanism. The break condition for Zk is excessive disentanglement (high β) that collapses the latent structure or under‑regularisation that leaves normal samples scattered.

## Foundational Learning
- **Variational Autoencoder (VAE)**: A generative model that learns a probabilistic latent representation by optimizing an evidence lower bound (ELBO). It consists of an encoder that outputs distribution parameters and a decoder that reconstructs inputs from latent samples.  
  *Why needed*: The entire methodology relies on the VAE framework; understanding its components (reparameterization, ELBO loss) is essential.  
  *Quick check*: What is the purpose of the Kullback‑Leibler (KL) divergence term in the standard VAE loss?

- **β‑VAE**: A variant of the VAE that introduces a weighting factor β on the KL divergence term, allowing explicit control over the trade‑off between latent‑space regularization and reconstruction fidelity.  
  *Why needed*: β is the primary tuning knob that differentially affects the two anomaly scoring methods.  
  *Quick check*: How does increasing β affect the latent space distribution and the reconstruction error?

- **NSL‑KDD Dataset & Network Intrusion Context**: NSL‑KDD is a benchmark dataset for network intrusion detection, containing 41 features (mixed types) labelled with normal traffic and four attack categories (DoS, Probe, U2R, R2L). The unsupervised setting trains only on normal samples.  
  *Why needed*: Defines the problem domain, data characteristics, and the evaluation protocol.  
  *Quick check*: Why is it common in unsupervised IDS to train only on normal network traffic samples?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Preprocessor → β‑VAE (Encoder → Sampling → Decoder) → Loss Calculator → (Lrec‑classifier / Zk‑classifier) → Evaluator

**Critical path**:
1. Split NSL‑KDD into Xtrain (normal only), Xtest (normal only), Xattack (all attacks).  
2. Preprocess all sets using statistics from Xtrain (one‑hot categorical features, encode booleans, standardize continuous features).  
3. Train β‑VAE on Xtrain for a grid of β values (e.g., 0, 10⁻⁵, …, 0.5) with Adam (lr=0.001, batch=2048).  
4. For each trained model:  
   a. Compute Lrec scores on Xtest ∪ Xattack.  
   b. Pre‑compute Ztrain (latent projections of Xtrain). For each test/attack sample, compute average Euclidean distance to its k nearest neighbors in Ztrain (for a grid of k).  
5. Compute mean AUROC over multiple seeds for all (β, k) pairs.  
6. Select best configuration.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- **Reconstruction vs. Latent‑space**: Lrec is simpler and faster (no neighbor search) but less accurate; Zk requires storing Ztrain and performing k‑NN searches but yields higher AUROC.
- **β Tuning**: Low β favors reconstruction quality but may yield a less separable latent space; high β enhances latent structure at the cost of reconstruction. Optimal β depends on the chosen scoring method.
- **k Selection**: Larger k provides a smoother density estimate and better performance up to a point (paper: k=5000, ∼80% of Xtrain size), but may blur local anomalies.

**Failure signatures**:
- **Latent space collapse**: Ztrain points cluster tightly (low variance), causing Zk to lose discriminative power. Check: standard deviation of Ztrain across latent dimensions is near zero.
- **Under‑fit model**: Both scores are high for normal samples; AUROC near 0.5. Check: mean Lrec on Xtest is very high.
- **Over‑regularized (high β)**: Lrec AUROC drops sharply; Zk may remain stable or improve slightly. Validated by Table II: for β=0.5, Lrec AUROC ~96.28% (down from 96.78%) while Zk AUROC falls to 75%.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Baseline reproduction**: Train β‑VAE with β=0. Compute Lrec‑ and Zk5000‑classification. Expect Lrec AUROC ~96.78% and Zk AUROC ~97.70% (as in paper’s β=0 row).
2. **Optimal β for Zk**: Train models for β ∈ {10⁻⁵, 10⁻⁴, 10⁻³}. Run Zk5000. Expect peak AUROC at β=10⁻⁵ (~97.90%).
3. **k sensitivity**: Using β=10⁻⁵ model, sweep k from 1 to 5000. Expect AUROC to increase with k, confirming that using a large fraction of training neighbors is beneficial.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Score fusion**: How can reconstruction error and latent‑space distance be optimally combined in an unsupervised β‑VAE framework? The paper shows complementarity but does not implement any fusion strategy.
- **Mahalanobis distance**: Does replacing Euclidean with Mahalanobis distance in the latent space improve performance? The authors suggest benchmarking it to account for covariance structure.
- **Transferability**: Does the β‑VAE latent‑space approach generalize to other network intrusion datasets (e.g., CIC‑IDS2017, UNSW‑NB15) beyond NSL‑KDD?
- **Incremental learning**: Can the reference set Ztrain be updated incrementally to adapt to streaming data and track evolving clusters for behavior analysis?

## Limitations
- **Data split ambiguity**: The paper does not precisely specify how NSL‑KDD files map to the Xtrain/Xtest/Xattack splits, which could affect sample counts and class distribution.
- **Missing training details**: Epochs, early‑stopping, and weight initialization are unreported, potentially impacting convergence and reproducibility.
- **Dataset specificity**: Results are limited to NSL‑KDD, which is synthetic and may not represent modern network traffic; generalizability is uncertain.

## Confidence
- **High**: The core methodology (β‑VAE training and the two scoring paths) is clearly described and internally consistent.
- **Medium**: The claim that latent‑space k‑NN outperforms reconstruction error holds within the reported β=10⁻⁵, k=5000 configuration; minor implementation variations might shift the exact hyperparameters.
- **Low**: The assertion of “better interpretability” for the latent‑space method is qualitative and not quantitatively validated.

## Next Checks
1. **Split verification**: Re‑run experiments using at least two canonical NSL‑KDD splits (e.g., original KDDTrain+.txt/KDDTest+.txt vs. common pre‑processed versions) and confirm that the best Zk configuration (β=10⁻⁵, k=5000) yields AUROC within ±1% of the reported 97.90%.
2. **Training robustness**: For β=10⁻⁵, train with 10 random seeds, varying epochs (100–500) and including early stopping. Compute mean and standard deviation of Zk5000 AUROC; expect stability with std < 0.5%.
3. **Mechanism check**: For the β=10⁻⁵ model, compute average nearest‑neighbor distances for normal test samples versus each attack class (DoS, Probe, U2R, R2L). Expect all classes to show significantly larger distances than normal; if U2R/R2L overlap, the advantage may be class‑dependent.
