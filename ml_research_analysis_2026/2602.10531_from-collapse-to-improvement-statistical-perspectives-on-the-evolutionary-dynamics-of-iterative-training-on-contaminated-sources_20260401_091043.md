# From Collapse to Improvement: Statistical Perspectives on the Evolutionary Dynamics of Iterative Training on Contaminated Sources

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.10531
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10531
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** With any non-trivial proportion of fresh real data (\(\alpha_t > 0\)) and appropriately increasing sample sizes (\(n_t\), e.g., \(n_t \sim t^2\)), iterative training on contaminated data can achieve consistency (risk \(\rightarrow 0\)), thereby preventing model collapse.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates model collapse in generative models arising from iterative training on synthetic data. The authors develop a statistical framework in which each training stage utilizes a mixture of the true data distribution and outputs from the previous model. They derive a recurrence for the expected squared error risk \(R_t = \mathbb{E}\|\hat\theta_t - \theta^*\|^2_2\) and show that, in the absence of fresh real data (\(\alpha_t = 0\)), improvement is impossible and collapse occurs. However, as long as a non-trivial proportion of fresh data is present (\(\alpha_t > 0\)), even if this proportion decays over time, the risk can be driven to zero provided the sample sizes grow sufficiently fast. Specifically, the conditions \(\sum_t \alpha_t = \infty\) and \(\sum_t 1/n_t < \infty\) are sufficient for consistency, with concrete schedules such as \(\alpha_t \propto 1/\log t\) and \(n_t \propto t^2\). The theoretical findings are supported by simulations on multinomial and Gaussian models and by experiments with GPT-2.

## Method Summary
The study formalizes iterative training as follows: start with an initial set of \(n_0\) i.i.d. samples from the true distribution \(\theta^*\); compute the empirical estimator \(\hat\theta_0\). For each subsequent iteration \(t \ge 1\):
1. Construct a mixture parameter \(\theta_{\text{mix}} = \alpha_t \theta^* + (1-\alpha_t) \hat\theta_{t-1}\), where \(\alpha_t \in [0,1]\) controls the fresh data proportion.
2. Draw \(n_t\) i.i.d. samples from the categorical distribution \(\text{Cat}(\theta_{\text{mix}})\).
3. Update \(\hat\theta_t\) as the normalized counts from these samples (or from all accumulated samples if accumulation is used).

The expected squared error \(R_t\) is tracked across repetitions. The authors derive a recurrence for \(R_t\) and analyze its asymptotic behavior under various schedules for \(\alpha_t\) and \(n_t\). The main theoretical contribution is identifying the necessary and sufficient conditions for consistency, namely the divergence of \(\sum \alpha_t\) and the convergence of \(\sum 1/n_t\). Simulations explore constant, decaying, and increasing schedules, both with and without accumulation of past data.

## Key Results
- **Necessity of fresh data:** If \(\alpha_t = 0\) for all \(t\) (no real data), the risk cannot decrease; model collapse is inevitable.
- **Sufficient conditions for consistency:** When \(\sum_t \alpha_t = \infty\) and \(\sum_t 1/n_t < \infty\), the risk converges to zero, i.e., \(\hat\theta_t \rightarrow \theta^*\) in mean square.
- **Practical schedules:** For example, \(\alpha_t \propto 1/\log t\) combined with \(n_t \propto t^2\) satisfies the conditions and yields monotonic risk decrease in simulations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The mechanism hinges on the risk recurrence derived by the authors. Under the mixture model, the update for the expected squared error can be expressed as:
\[
R_t = (1-\alpha_t)^2 R_{t-1} + \text{Var}(\hat\theta_t \mid \theta_{\text{mix}}),
\]
where the variance term is on the order of \(1/n_t\) (scaled by the number of categories). If \(\alpha_t\) is too small or decays too fast, the multiplicative factor \((1-\alpha_t)^2\) remains close to 1, causing the risk to stagnate or even grow due to variance. However, by ensuring that the sum of the mixture weights diverges (\(\sum \alpha_t = \infty\)), the product \(\prod (1-\alpha_t)^2\) eventually goes to zero, providing a contraction. Simultaneously, the variance contributions must be summable (\(\sum 1/n_t < \infty\)) so that cumulative error vanishes. Thus, the interplay between a persistent influx of fresh data and increasingly precise empirical estimates drives the risk to zero. Even if \(\alpha_t\) decays, a sufficiently fast growth of \(n_t\) (e.g., polynomial) compensates by reducing the variance term.

## Foundational Learning
- **Mixture contamination model:** The core assumption that each training batch is generated from \(\alpha_t \theta^* + (1-\alpha_t)\hat\theta_{t-1}\). *Why needed:* Captures the effect of synthetic data injection. *Quick check:* Write the mixture distribution for a given \(\alpha_t\) and \(\hat\theta_{t-1}\).
- **Expected squared error risk:** \(R_t = \mathbb{E}\|\hat\theta_t - \theta^*\|^2_2\). *Why needed:* Provides a tractable measure of estimator accuracy. *Quick check:* Express \(R_t\) in terms of bias and variance for a multinomial model.
- **Risk recurrence:** The equation linking \(R_t\) to \(R_{t-1}\). *Why needed:* Enables asymptotic analysis. *Quick check:* Derive the recurrence for the multinomial case under exact mixing.
- **Consistency conditions:** \(\sum \alpha_t = \infty\) and \(\sum 1/n_t < \infty\). *Why needed:* These are the threshold conditions for risk to vanish. *Quick check:* Check whether \(\alpha_t = 1/t\) and \(n_t = t^2\) satisfy both.
- **Model collapse:** Divergence of risk when training on only synthetic data. *Why needed:* Contrasts the beneficial regime. *Quick check:* Show that with \(\alpha_t=0\), \(R_t \ge R_{t-1}\) for all \(t\).

## Architecture Onboarding
The iterative training pipeline can be viewed as a loop with the following components:

**Component map (plain text):**  
TrueDistribution(\(\theta^*\)) -> MixtureController(\(\alpha_t,\hat\theta_{t-1}\)) -> Sampler -> AccumulationBuffer(optional) -> EmpiricalEstimator -> \(\hat\theta_t\) (feedback to MixtureController)

**Critical path:** Each iteration:
- Compute mixture \(\theta_{\text{mix}} = \alpha_t \theta^* + (1-\alpha_t)\hat\theta_{t-1}\).
- Generate \(n_t\) samples from \(\text{Cat}(\theta_{\text{mix}})\).
- Either (a) use only these \(n_t\) samples to compute \(\hat\theta_t\) (no accumulation) or (b) append them to a buffer containing all previous samples and compute \(\hat\theta_t\) from the entire buffer.
- Update \(\hat\theta_t\) and proceed to next iteration.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Accumulation vs. constant memory:* Accumulation improves data efficiency but leads to linear memory growth; non-accumulation keeps memory bounded but requires larger \(n_t\) to achieve comparable accuracy.
- *α_t decay schedule:* Smaller \(\alpha_t\) reduces reliance on true data but necessitates larger \(n_t\) to keep variance in check.
- *Sample size growth:* Faster growth (e.g., \(n_t \propto t^2\)) increases computational cost but guarantees consistency under decaying \(\alpha_t\); constant \(n_t\) fails unless \(\alpha_t\) is sufficiently large.

**Failure signatures:**
- **Risk divergence:** \(R_t\) increases over iterations, often due to \(\alpha_t = 0\) or \(\sum 1/n_t = \infty\).
- **Memory blow-up:** With accumulation, the buffer size becomes \(\sum_{i=1}^t n_i\); cubic growth quickly exceeds memory.
- **Variance explosion:** For small \(\alpha_t\) and modest \(n_t\), the variance term dominates, causing high variance in \(\hat\theta_t\) across repetitions.
- **Non-convergence:** When conditions are borderline, risk may plateau instead of decaying to zero.

**First three experiments to run:**
1. **Baseline constant schedules:** Set \(\alpha_t = 0.1\) and \(n_t = 5\) (or \(n_t = 5t\)); verify that \(R_t\) stabilizes to a constant >0, matching the analytical limiting risk.
2. **Decaying \(\alpha_t\) with growing \(n_t\):** Use \(\alpha_t = 1/\log(t+1)\) and \(n_t = 5 t^2\); observe monotonic decay of \(R_t\) toward zero, confirming the sufficiency conditions.
3. **Accumulation vs. non-accumulation:** Compare two runs with identical schedules (e.g., \(\alpha_t = 0.1\), \(n_t = 5t\)): one using only current batch for \(\hat\theta_t\) and one using all past samples; accumulation should yield faster risk reduction at the cost of memory.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Estimating mixture weight \(\alpha_t\):** How can \(\alpha_t\) be estimated from mixed data during iterative training? This is crucial for applying corrections or assessing contamination levels.
- **Static true distribution assumption:** Does the assumption of a fixed true distribution \(P^*\) hold in the long run as AI-generated content influences human language? Co-evolution could alter dynamics.
- **Self-correcting behavior:** Does the mixture weight \((1-\alpha_t)\) naturally decrease with model risk \(R_{t-1}\) in practice? If so, the required sample size growth may be less strict.
- **Extension to non-convex deep networks:** Can the consistency results (e.g., \(\alpha_t\) decaying with \(n_t \sim t^2\)) be generalized to non-convex deep networks trained with SGD? Overparameterization and implicit regularization may break the risk recurrence.

## Limitations
- The true distribution \(\theta^*\) used in the paper’s figures is not specified; assuming a uniform distribution may not match their specific random draw.
- Details of the accumulation protocol (e.g., whether \(n_t\) schedules apply to new samples only) are ambiguous.
- The GPT-2 experiments lack essential hyperparameters (learning rate, batch size, generation settings) and dataset information, preventing faithful replication.
- The theoretical framework assumes i.i.d. data and a simple mixture model, whereas real language model training involves non-i.i.d. sequences and architectural updates.

## Confidence
- **Claim:** Fresh real data (\(\alpha_t > 0\)) is necessary to avoid collapse. **Confidence:** High (theoretically proven).
- **Claim:** Conditions \(\sum \alpha_t = \infty\) and \(\sum 1/n_t < \infty\) guarantee consistency. **Confidence:** Medium (proven for multinomial exponential family; empirical evidence on GPT-2 is suggestive but not conclusive).
- **Claim:** The theoretical results extend to modern large language models. **Confidence:** Low (the paper’s GPT-2 experiments lack reproducibility details; non-convexity and sequential dependencies may invalidate the recurrence).
