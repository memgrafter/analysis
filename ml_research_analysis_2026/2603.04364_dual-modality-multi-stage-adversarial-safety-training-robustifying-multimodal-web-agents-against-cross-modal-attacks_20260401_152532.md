# Dual-Modality Multi-Stage Adversarial Safety Training: Robustifying Multimodal Web Agents Against Cross-Modal Attacks

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.04364
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04364
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: DMAST reduces attacker success on VisualWebArena from 41.2% to 21.4% and doubles task completion from 6.2% to 10.2%; with prompt defense it achieves 7.2% ASR while surpassing baseline defenses.

## Executive Summary
Multimodal web agents that process both screenshots and accessibility trees are susceptible to cross‑modal attacks where a single DOM injection creates a consistent deceptive narrative across both observation channels. This paper introduces DMAST, a framework that formalizes the agent‑attacker interaction as a two‑player zero‑sum Markov game and employs three training stages: (1) imitation learning from a teacher model, (2) oracle‑guided supervised fine‑tuning using zero‑acknowledgment task‑focused Chain‑of‑Thought (CoT) under attack, and (3) adversarial reinforcement learning via Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) self‑play. On out‑of‑distribution VisualWebArena tasks, DMAST lowers the attacker success rate from 41.2% to 21.4% while increasing task success from 6.2% to 10.2%. Combined with a simple safety prompt, it attains 7.2% ASR and preserves utility, outperforming prior adversarial training and prompting defenses. Ablation studies confirm that each training stage provides cumulative gains, with the oracle‑guided SFT instilling persistent task priors and the RL co‑evolution generating diverse, context‑aware attacks.

## Method Summary
**Stage 1 – Imitation Learning:** The student model (Gemma‑3‑12B‑IT) is supervised‑fine‑tuned on 4 k clean and 4 k successful adversarial trajectories generated by the teacher (Gemma‑3‑27B‑IT). The loss combines a negative log‑likelihood for actions and a KL penalty toward the reference policy.

**Stage 2 – Oracle‑Guided SFT:** For each clean golden trajectory step, the teacher attacker produces a concrete HTML/CSS injection. The oracle teacher (with privileged access to both clean and attacked views) then generates a zero‑acknowledgment CoT that reasons exclusively about legitimate UI elements and completely ignores injected content. The student is fine‑tuned on a mixture of these (attacked observation, oracle CoT, original action) pairs together with clean data, again with KL regularization.

**Stage 3 – Adversarial RL via GRPO Self‑Play:** The student acts as both agent and attacker, distinguished only by role‑specific prompts. Attacker checkpoints from earlier iterations are stored in a population; at each training iteration, current attacker samples from this population (recency‑weighted). Episodes (max 5 steps) are run in the headless browser; after termination, rewards (+1 for safe task completion, –1 for data leak or failure) are assigned. Group‑relative advantages are computed within each group of episodes (no value network), and the shared policy is updated via GRPO with clipping and a KL penalty toward the Stage 2 checkpoint. The cosine learning rate schedule spans all iterations. After ~10 iterations, the agent is ready for deployment, optionally augmented with a safety prompt.

## Key Results
- On VisualWebArena, full DMAST cuts ASR from 41.2% to 21.4% and raises TSR from 6.2% to 10.2%.
- With an additional prompt defense, DMAST reaches 7.2% ASR while maintaining utility, beating SPAG, ART, online SFT, and pure prompt baselines.
- Ablation shows Stage 2 alone lifts TSR from 5.1% to 8.2%, and Stage 3 further reduces ASR from 36.7% to 21.4% and increases TSR to 10.2%.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Multimodal web agents are especially vulnerable to coordinated cross‑modal attacks because a single DOM injection simultaneously corrupts both the visual screenshot and the accessibility tree, creating a consistent deceptive narrative that is harder to detect than single‑modality perturbations.
- **Mechanism:** The agent receives dual inputs derived from the same DOM. An attacker injects HTML/CSS via JavaScript; this payload appears in both observation channels, aligning textual structure with visual overlays (e.g., fake alerts). Empirical results (MiniWob++) confirm that attacks with a visual component achieve 34.4% attacker success versus 24.1% for text‑only.
- **Core assumption:** The agent relies on both modalities with comparable weight and lacks a cross‑modal consistency check; injected elements remain semantically coherent and task‑plausible enough to mislead.
- **Break condition:** The attack fails if the injection becomes inconsistent across modalities (e.g., separate sanitization layers) or if the visual overlay is effectively obscured.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The oracle‑guided supervised fine‑tuning stage (zero‑acknowledgment CoT) instills a persistent prior to keep the agent focused on task goals despite adversarial content, thereby improving task completion under attack.
- **Mechanism:** The oracle (with privileged access to both clean and attacked views) generates a reasoning trace that strictly ignores injected elements and reasons only about legitimate UI components from the AXTree. These denoised trajectories augment the student’s SFT data. Ablation shows Stage 2 alone increases TSR on VisualWebArena from 5.1% to 8.2%.
- **Core assumption:** The genuine task‑relevant UI controls remain identifiable and correctly labeled in the attacked AXTree; the attack adds distractors but does not alter or mask the required elements.
- **Break condition:** If the attack replaces/obscures all task‑relevant elements, the oracle‑guided CoT cannot produce a valid action, leading to failure or refusal; also, if the oracle inadvertently references the attack, the prior may reinforce attention to injected content.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The adversarial reinforcement learning stage (GRPO self‑play) drives co‑evolution of agent and attacker policies, yielding an agent robust to a wide spectrum of attacks while maintaining or improving task performance.
- **Mechanism:** Agent and attacker share VLM weights but differ via role prompts. During RL, each episode runs against a population of historical attacker checkpoints (recency‑weighted). GRPO computes group‑relative advantages for tokens without a value network. Results: Stage 3 reduces ASR on VisualWebArena from 36.7% to 21.4% and raises TSR from 8.2% to 10.2%. Diversity metrics confirm that attacks become more varied over iterations.
- **Core assumption:** Shared‑weight design enables transfer of attack awareness to defense; population sampling prevents overfitting to a single attacker; group‑relative scaling stabilizes learning without a critic.
- **Break condition:** If the attacker population collapses (diversity plateaus) or if the KL penalty toward the SFT checkpoint is too weak, the agent may forget clean‑task behavior, causing utility degradation or refusal collapse.

## Foundational Learning
- **Chain‑of‑Thought (CoT) Reasoning** – Why needed: Stages 1 and 2 generate intermediate reasoning traces that guide action selection; the oracle’s zero‑acknowledgment CoT shapes the agent’s focus away from distractions. Quick check: What distinguishes the oracle’s CoT from a standard agent CoT, and why is this distinction critical for training?
- **Reinforcement Learning with Group Relative Advantages (GRPO)** – Why needed: Stage 3 relies on GRPO to perform policy updates without a separate value network, using relative performance within a group of episodes; this is essential for efficient co‑evolution in the zero‑sum game. Quick check: In GRPO, how are advantages computed, and what is the benefit of using group‑relative instead of standard advantage estimates?
- **Multi‑Modal Fusion (Vision + Text from DOM)** – Why needed: The agent’s dual‑modality input (screenshot + accessibility tree) is central to the attack surface; understanding how these modalities are combined informs why cross‑modal attacks are effective. Quick check: If the attacker injects HTML that modifies the DOM, how does this change both the screenshot and the accessibility tree, and why is this synchrony problematic?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:  
Teacher VLM (Gemma‑3‑27B‑IT) -> (demonstrations, oracle CoT) -> Student VLM (Gemma‑3‑12B‑IT)  
HTML Injector -> Headless Browser (produces screenshot & AXTree) -> Student VLM  
GRPO Trainer -> (collects rollouts, computes GRPO updates) -> Student VLM (shared weights)  
Reward evaluator -> (task success, data leak flags) -> GRPO Trainer  
Buffers store trajectories for SFT stages.

Critical path:  
1. Imitation learning: SFT on teacher demos (clean & successful adversarial).  
2. Oracle‑guided SFT: For each clean golden step, generate attack, obtain attacked observation, produce oracle CoT, mix with clean data, fine‑tune student.  
3. Adversarial RL loop (K≈10 iterations): sample tasks; per episode, attacker proposes injection, browser injects, agent acts, rewards computed, GRPO update with population‑sampled attacker checkpoints.  
4. Deployment: Agent receives task + observations; optional safety prompt.

Design tradeoffs:
- Shared weights reduce memory and enable co‑evolution but may limit capacity or attacker strength.
- Teacher‑student setup leverages a larger model for data generation but introduces a capability gap.
- Population‑based attacker sampling improves robustness at the cost of increased compute/storage.
- GRPO without a value network simplifies training yet may increase variance; group size requires tuning.
- Zero‑acknowledgment CoT prevents distraction but risks overlooking task‑relevant changes if attacks mimic legitimate content.

Failure signatures:
- High ASR (>30%) despite training → attacker diversity insufficient or oracle guidance weak.
- Low TSR (<5%) → refusal collapse, often from over‑regularization or excessive KL penalty.
- Stagnant Distinct‑n / Self‑BLEU → attacker not exploring new patterns.
- Large KL drift from SFT reference → catastrophic forgetting, clean performance drops.

First 3 experiments:
1. Vulnerability analysis on MiniWob++ (Section 3.2, Table 1) – quantifies ASR for different attack modalities.
2. Stage‑wise ablation on unseen MiniWob++ and VisualWebArena (Section 5.3, Table 3) – isolates contribution of each stage.
3. Co‑evolution & diversity – cross‑evaluation heatmap (Figure 3) plus lexical/strategic diversity metrics (Figure 4) confirms mutual improvement and attack diversification.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Alternative adversarial objectives** – Can DMAST’s framework be extended to control‑flow hijacking or misinformation dissemination beyond sensitive data leakage? The current experiments focus solely on data leakage; the Markov game formulation is general but its efficacy for other objectives remains untested.
2. **Scaling the student model** – How does increasing student capacity (e.g., >27B parameters) affect the safety‑utility trade‑off? The paper notes that the 12B student limits absolute task success; applying DMAST to larger VLMs is an important future direction.
3. **Shared vs. separate architectures** – Does the shared‑weight co‑design significantly contribute to robustness, or would independent agent/attacker models yield similar or better results? No ablation compares these choices.
4. **Zero‑acknowledgment strategy for subtle attacks** – Is always ignoring attacks optimal, or could explicit attack recognition sometimes improve robustness? The approach has not been tested on attacks that are indistinguishable from legitimate task content.

## Limitations
- Insufficient disclosure of teacher/oracle prompts, web environment injection/rollback mechanics, and reward shaping specifics.
- Evaluation confined to synthetic benchmarks (MiniWob++, VisualWebArena), raising questions about real‑world transfer.
- Shared‑weight agent‑attacker design may limit capacity and cause attacker diversity to diminish over time.

## Confidence
- Cross‑modal attacks severely increase vulnerability: High.
- DMAST significantly reduces ASR and boosts TSR: High.
- Oracle‑guided SFT and GRPO self‑play drive robustness via persistent priors and co‑evolution: Medium.
