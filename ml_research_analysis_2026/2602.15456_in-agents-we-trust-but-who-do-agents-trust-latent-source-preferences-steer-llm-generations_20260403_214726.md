# In Agents We Trust, but Who Do Agents Trust? Latent Source Preferences Steer LLM Generations

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.15456
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15456
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** LLMs exhibit strong, predictable source preferences that vary by model size and context, persist despite anti-bias prompts, and often override content relevance.

## Executive Summary
This paper reveals that LLM agents harbor latent preferences for information sources—such as news outlets, research journals, and e-commerce platforms—that systematically bias their selections even when content is identical. Through indirect experiments (swapping source labels on the same content) and direct source rankings across three domains, the authors demonstrate that these preferences are robust, correlate with model size, and remain unaffected by explicit anti-bias instructions. In real-world scenarios like news aggregation and product recommendations, source bias dominates decisions, frequently diverging from human or platform norms, and model source rankings are highly correlated, suggesting shared training-data influences.

## Method Summary
The study employs a dual evaluation framework: direct tasks where models compare two source identities (e.g., brand names or URLs) and indirect tasks where identical content is paired with different source labels. Across news, research, and e-commerce domains, 12 LLMs (including GPT-4.1, Llama, and Mistral variants) generate pairwise preferences, measured via preference percentages and Kendall Tau rank correlations. Datasets include synthetically generated content (e.g., paraphrased articles) and real-world aggregations (AllSides, Amazon), with structured outputs enforcing parseable responses. This design isolates source effects from content quality, quantifying systematic biases.

## Key Results
- LLMs show strong, predictable source preferences that intensify with model size and shift by domain context.
- Preferences persist despite anti-bias prompts and often override content relevance in selection tasks.
- In real-world deployments (e.g., AllSides, Amazon), source bias drives choices and misaligns with platform algorithms; model source rankings are highly correlated (Tau up to 0.99), indicating shared training-data origins.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1:** LLMs develop latent preferences for sources through statistical co-occurrence patterns in pre-training data, where source names correlate with quality or popularity cues. These patterns become embedded in model weights, biasing decisions when sources are mentioned. Evidence: Consistent preference percentages across models; persistence despite prompts. Break condition: Uniform or counter-balanced source distributions during training.
**Mechanism 2:** LLMs unify different identifiers (URLs, handles, names) into a single internal source representation, applying consistent preferences across them. Evidence: High Kendall Tau correlations in rankings across identity forms (e.g., GPT-4.1-Mini in Fig. 4). Break condition: Ambiguous or novel identities fail to map correctly.
**Mechanism 3:** Latent preferences are entrenched in model parameters, resisting override by standard debiasing prompts, especially in larger models. Evidence: Anti-bias prompts in AllSides case study failed to reduce skew. Break condition: Fine-tuning with adversarial debiasing or retrieval augmentation may mitigate persistence.

## Foundational Learning
- **Latent Source Preference:** Why needed: To understand why LLMs favor certain sources despite identical content. Quick check: What is a latent source preference and how might it affect an LLM agent’s output?
- **Cross-Identity Consistency:** Why needed: To recognize that source identifiers (URL, handle, name) trigger the same bias, impacting audits. Quick check: How does an LLM treat “nytimes.com” versus “New York Times” in ranking tasks?
- **Prompt Inoculation Limits:** Why needed: To set realistic expectations for alignment; simple instructions may not erase ingrained biases. Quick check: Will instructing an LLM to ignore source information eliminate its latent preferences? Why or why not?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Input parser -> Source annotator -> LLM engine -> Output formatter
**Critical path:** User query -> retrieve candidates -> annotate sources -> LLM generates preference-adjusted scores -> select top candidate -> produce response.
**Design tradeoffs:** Transparency vs. performance (exposing source tags aids auditability but may amplify bias); user control vs. system complexity (overrides add UX overhead); debiasing cost (mitigation may degrade capabilities or increase latency).
**Failure signatures:** Over-concentration on few sources; robustness to source-swap manipulations; no improvement after “be unbiased” prompts; high correlation of rankings across disparate identities.
**First 3 experiments:** 1. Direct ranking: compare two source identities head-to-head. 2. Indirect evaluation: present identical content with swapped source labels. 3. Context-specific preference test: evaluate if preferences vary across domains (e.g., computational linguistics vs. computer vision).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** What causes latent source preferences? Do they emerge from pre-training data statistics or post-training processes? Basis: Authors state they "have not explored the causal origins." Why unresolved: Without knowing the origin, effective mitigation is impossible. What evidence: Ablation studies on models with identical pre-training but varying post-training; tracking preference emergence during training; correlating preferences with source-related corpus statistics.
- **Open Question 2:** How can we build practical auditing tools to measure and communicate latent source preferences? Basis: Paper advocates for "greater transparency, new auditing tools." Why unresolved: Stakeholders lack ways to assess systematic source favoritism. What evidence: Develop standardized benchmarks extending the authors' datasets; open-source tooling that outputs preference rankings; user studies on interpretability.
- **Open Question 3:** Can alignment techniques (e.g., RLHF, contrastive finetuning) grant users dynamic control over source preferences while preserving task performance? Basis: Need for "controllable alignment mechanisms." Why unresolved: Simple prompting often fails; more robust controls needed. What evidence: Experiments with rewards for source neutrality; modular architectures separating reasoning from bias; interactive user interfaces for setting preference weights.
- **Open Question 4:** Do latent source preferences generalize to other high-stakes domains (e.g., legal, medical) and how do they interact with domain-specific credibility signals? Basis: Study limited to three domains; authors suggest broader investigation. Why unresolved: Critical domains may exhibit similar or novel biases. What evidence: Replicate indirect/direct evaluation methodology in new domains; collect real-world data; test persistence or new dominating factors.

## Limitations
- Mechanism 1 (pre-training co-occurrence) lacks direct evidence; no training data analysis.
- Only 12 models tested; results may not generalize to newer architectures.
- E-commerce platform details and Amazon dataset underspecified, hindering replication.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1** (pre-training co-occurrence): Medium (indirect evidence; needs corpus validation).
- **Mechanism 2** (cross-identity consistency): High (strong cross-identity correlations across models).
- **Mechanism 3** (prompt inoculation limits): Medium (few prompts evaluated; broader space unexplored).

## Next Checks
1. Compute source co-occurrence with quality markers (e.g., "journalistic standards") in actual pre-training corpora (e.g., Infini-gram) and correlate with observed preferences.
2. Evaluate additional recent models (GPT-4o, Claude 3.5, Llama-3.3) to test generalizability of source preferences.
