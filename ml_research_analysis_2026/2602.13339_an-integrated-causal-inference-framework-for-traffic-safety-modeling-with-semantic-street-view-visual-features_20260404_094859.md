# An Integrated Causal Inference Framework for Traffic Safety Modeling with Semantic Street-View Visual Features

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.13339
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13339
- **Reference Count:** 13
- **Primary Result:** A higher proportion of street-level greenery significantly reduces traffic crashes (Average Treatment Effect = -6.38, p = 0.005), with the strongest effects in densely populated, socially vulnerable urban cores and for angle and rear-end crashes.

## Executive Summary
This study develops a causal inference framework to move beyond observational associations in traffic safety modeling by quantifying the effect of street-level visual environment features. Using semantic segmentation on 220,000 Google Street View images from Miami, it estimates the causal impact of greenery on crash frequency via Double Machine Learning (DML). The analysis finds that increased greenery reduces crashes overall, with spatially heterogeneous effects strongest in urban cores. The work demonstrates how high-dimensional visual data can be integrated with robust causal methods to inform urban planning for traffic safety.

## Method Summary
The analysis constructs a 2 km × 2 km spatial grid over three Florida counties and aggregates crash counts, street-view visual features (from semantic segmentation), and socioeconomic covariates into each cell. A Double Machine Learning (DML) framework estimates the Average Treatment Effect (ATE) of greenery on crashes by orthogonalizing residualized outcomes and treatments using cross-fitted machine learning nuisance models (Random Forest, XGBoost, GBM, LightGBM). SHAP values interpret the nuisance models. A Causal Forest then estimates Conditional Average Treatment Effects (CATEs) to map spatial heterogeneity in the greenery effect. Robustness checks include multi-learner comparison and subgroup analyses by crash type.

## Key Results
- Greenery proportion has a significant negative ATE on total crashes (ATE = -6.38, p = 0.005), indicating a protective effect.
- The effect is strongest in densely populated, socially vulnerable urban areas and for angle and rear-end crashes; it is limited for vulnerable road user crashes.
- Causal Forest analysis reveals substantial spatial heterogeneity in the effect, confirming that the global ATE masks important local variation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Double Machine Learning for Unbiased ATE
- **Claim:** The DML framework provides an unbiased estimate of the Average Treatment Effect (ATE) of greenery on crash frequency by orthogonalizing the outcome and treatment models.
- **Mechanism:** DML uses two nuisance models—for outcome `g(X)` and treatment `m(X)`—estimated via cross-fitting with ML algorithms. It regresses residualized outcomes on residualized treatments, removing confounding bias from high-dimensional covariates `X` without requiring parametric forms.
- **Core assumption:** Conditional independence (greenery assignment is as-good-as random given observed covariates `X`) and overlap (sufficient variation in greenery across similar `X`). The paper strengthens this with a rich covariate set and focuses on common support.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "...applied a Double Machine Learning framework to estimate average treatment effects." [section 4.2] "We estimate 𝜃 using a 5-fold cross fitting algorithm... This formulation isolates the causal parameter 𝜃..."
- **Break condition:** Violation of overlap (extremely high/low greenery in all similar locations) or unmeasured confounding (e.g., driver behavior not captured by covariates) would bias the ATE. The paper mitigates this via covariate screening and spatial grid design.

### Mechanism 2: Causal Forests for Spatial Heterogeneity
- **Claim:** Causal Forests estimate spatially heterogeneous Conditional Average Treatment Effects (CATEs), revealing where greenery's safety benefit is strongest.
- **Mechanism:** An ensemble of honest regression trees partitions the covariate space to maximize heterogeneity in treatment effect estimates. Within each leaf, a locally linear regression of residualized outcome on residualized treatment yields the CATE, `τ(x)`. This nonparametric approach captures effect variation across urban contexts.
- **Core assumption:** Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA) for spatial units—one grid's greenery does not affect crashes in another. The paper argues this is plausible due to grid scale (2 km) and high-dimensional controls.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "Causal Forest analysis confirmed substantial spatial heterogeneity in the effect." [section 4.3] "The Causal Forest estimates τ(x) by growing an ensemble of honest regression trees... recursively partitions the covariate space with the objective of maximizing heterogeneity..."
- **Break condition:** Strong spatial spillovers (e.g., greenery in one grid affecting neighboring crashes) violate SUTVA. Dependence on correct "honest" tree splitting—if covariate space poorly captures effect modifiers, heterogeneity may be missed.

### Mechanism 3: SHAP for Nuisance Model Interpretation
- **Claim:** SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values interpret the nonlinear influence of confounders in the DML nuisance models, validating that the model captures plausible mechanisms linking environment to crashes.
- **Mechanism:** SHAP computes each feature's marginal contribution to model predictions. For nuisance models `g(X)` and `m(X)`, SHAP rankings show which covariates most strongly predict greenery distribution and crash frequency, and SHAP dependence plots reveal threshold/nonlinear effects.
- **Core assumption:** The ML models are correctly specified to capture true functional relationships; SHAP explanations are faithful approximations.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 4.2.2] "We employed SHAP for model interpretation... quantifying the marginal contribution of each covariate..." [section 5.2] Figures 8-10 show SHAP results: car proportion drives greenery distribution; population and car proportion drive crashes; nonlinear thresholds exist.
- **Break condition:** If SHAP reveals counterintuitive drivers (e.g., higher poverty increasing greenery in a way inconsistent with urban ecology), it may indicate model misspecification or omitted variable bias, questioning the DML confounder adjustment.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Double Machine Learning (DML) for Partial Linear Models
- **Why needed here:** To obtain a causal effect estimate (`θ`) when treatment (greenery) and outcome (crashes) are both confounded by many nonlinear, high-dimensional covariates (e.g., population, car proportion). Standard regression fails; DML uses ML for nuisance estimation while maintaining `√n`-consistency for `θ`.
- **Quick check question:** What are the two nuisance functions in the DML partially linear model, and how does cross-fitting prevent overfitting bias in the ATE estimate?

### Concept: Causal Forests for Heterogeneous Treatment Effects
- **Why needed here:** The global ATE masks important variation—greenery may only reduce crashes in dense, vulnerable areas. Causal Forests estimate the Conditional Average Treatment Effect (CATE) `τ(x)` for any location with covariates `x`, identifying high-impact zones for targeted interventions.
- **Quick check question:** How does "honesty" in Causal Forests (using separate samples for splitting and estimation) differ from standard random forests, and why is it crucial for valid causal inference?

### Concept: Semantic Segmentation & Feature Extraction from Street View Imagery
- **Why needed here:** The treatment (greenery proportion) is not directly observable from traditional GIS data. Semantic segmentation (using a model like Segmenter Transformer) converts raw images into pixel-level category proportions (e.g., greenery, road, building), creating a quantitative, objective measure of the visual environment.
- **Quick check question:** How is the pixel proportion for "greenery" calculated from a segmented image, and how are multiple images per grid cell aggregated?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Data Ingestion & Spatial Alignment -> Feature Engineering (Semantic Segmentation + Covariate Screening) -> Causal Identification (Stage 1: DML for ATE) -> Interpretability (SHAP) -> Heterogeneity Analysis (Stage 2: Causal Forest for CATE) -> Spatial Mapping -> Validation & Robustness

### Critical path
The linear dependency is: **Segmentation → Covariate Screening → DML (ATE) → Causal Forest (CATE) → Spatial Mapping**. DML must complete first to identify the robust treatment (greenery). Causal Forest depends on the same covariate set `X` and treatment variable. SHAP runs in parallel on DML nuisance models.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Grid size:** 2 km chosen to balance local heterogeneity vs. spatial spillover/SUTVA. Finer grids may increase autocorrelation; coarser grids may mask local effects.
- **DML learner choice:** Four learners used for robustness; however, each has hyperparameters (e.g., tree depth) that can influence `g(X)`, `m(X)` estimation and thus ATE variance. Simpler models may underfit; complex models may overfit residuals.
- **CATE estimation:** Causal Forest trades global efficiency for local flexibility. It provides unit-level CATEs but with higher variance than global ATE. Bandwidth/leaf size choices affect smoothness of spatial heterogeneity maps.

### Failure signatures
1. **ATE instability across DML learners:** If RF, XGBoost, GBM, LightGBM yield wildly different ATEs (beyond confidence intervals), it suggests the signal is not robust or covariates are inadequate.
2. **Uniform CATE distribution:** If the spatial map of CATEs shows no variation (all similar values), it implies no meaningful heterogeneity—or the forest failed to find effect modifiers (check `X` includes key moderators like population density, SVI).
3. **SHAP contradiction:** If SHAP shows that a primary confounder (e.g., car proportion) has a negligible or counterintuitive effect on the outcome model `g(X)`, the confounder adjustment may be incomplete, risking bias.

### First 3 experiments
1. **DML Consistency Check:** Run the DML procedure on a synthetic dataset where the true ATE is known (e.g., `Y = 2*T + 3*X1 + ε`). Verify that all four learners recover an ATE close to 2 with correct coverage.
2. **Covariate Sensitivity Analysis:** Remove one top SHAP-ranked confounder (e.g., population size) and re-run DML. If the greenery ATE changes substantially (>20%), the model is sensitive to that confounder; revisit covariate set completeness.
3. **Spatial Autocorrelation Test on CATEs:** Compute Moran's I on the CATE map. Significant positive autocorrelation suggests remaining spatial structure in effects—possibly unmeasured spatial confounders or violation of SUTVA. Consider adding spatial lag covariates or adjusting grid size.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How generalizable are the causal effects of street-level greenery on traffic crashes to other geographic regions with different urban forms and cultural contexts?
- Do different types or configurations of greenery (e.g., trees, shrubs, grass) have distinct causal impacts on traffic crash frequency?
- How do temporal dynamics in street-level greenery (e.g., seasonal changes, urban development) affect traffic safety outcomes over time?

## Limitations
- Exact ML hyperparameters for DML learners and Causal Forest are not reported, hindering exact replication.
- Semantic segmentation specifics (checkpoint, resolution, panorama aggregation method) are unclear.
- SVI-to-grid allocation method (weighting formula, partial overlaps) and crash subtype classification logic are unspecified.
- Potential unmeasured confounding (e.g., driver behavior) and spatial spillovers could bias estimates; SUTVA justification is informal without formal spatial autocorrelation tests.

## Confidence
- **Global ATE (greenery → crash reduction):** *Medium*. DML properly orthogonalizes nuisance models and uses rich covariates, but bias may persist from unmeasured confounders or overlap violations.
- **Spatial heterogeneity (CATEs):** *Medium*. Causal Forest captures effect variation, yet relies on SUTVA and adequate coverage of effect modifiers; spatial spillovers could distort local estimates.
- **SHAP mechanistic interpretations:** *Low*. SHAP values explain ML model predictions, not causality; they are vulnerable to model misspecification and do not confirm true causal pathways.

## Next Checks
1. **DML consistency on synthetic data:** Simulate a partially linear data generating process with known ATE and run the exact DML pipeline (all four learners). Verify recovery of the true effect and confidence interval coverage.
2. **Covariate sensitivity analysis:** Sequentially drop the top SHAP‑ranked confounders (e.g., population size, car proportion) and re‑estimate the greenery ATE. A substantial change (>20%) would indicate sensitivity to omitted variables.
3. **Spatial autocorrelation diagnostics:** Compute Moran’s I on DML residuals and on the final CATE map. Significant positive autocorrelation suggests remaining spatial structure—potentially unmeasured confounders or SUTVA breaches—and may require adding spatial lags or adjusting the grid size.
