# The Intelligent Disobedience Game: Formulating Disobedience in Stackelberg Games and Markov Decision Processes

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.20994
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20994
- Reference count: 30
- Primary result: Introduces the Intelligent Disobedience Game (IDG) to model selective override in shared autonomy, characterizes optimal strategies (obey non-harmful, disobey harmful; leader proposes goal actions), and identifies safety traps where the follower indefinitely avoids harm but fails to achieve the goal.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents the Intelligent Disobedience Game (IDG), a Stackelberg game framework for shared autonomy in which an assistive agent selectively overrides harmful human instructions. The human leader proposes actions, while the assistant follower decides to obey or disobey with asymmetric information about action consequences. The authors derive optimal strategies: the follower should obey actions that are not harmful and override harmful ones; the leader, anticipating this, should propose goal-reaching actions whenever possible. In multi-step settings, they reveal “safety traps”—recurrent state subsets where the follower can indefinitely avoid harm but also permanently prevent the leader from reaching any goal. The IDG is translated into a shared-control Multi-Agent MDP, creating a testbed for reinforcement learning experiments and for studying human trust in disobedient AI systems.

## Method Summary
The IDG is formalized as an extensive-form Stackelberg game with information asymmetry: the follower perfectly classifies leader actions into goal‑reaching, harmful, or other, while the leader cannot distinguish harmful from other actions. Under deterministic transitions and specific utility definitions (leader gains +1 on goal, –1 on harm; follower gains +1 for preventing harm, –1 for erroneous disobedience), the authors analytically characterize optimal strategies for both players. To enable computational exploration, the game is mapped to a multi-agent MDP: the leader faces a POMDP due to partial observability, while the follower has full state observability. Both agents train independently using reinforcement learning to maximize their respective rewards. This decomposition allows empirical investigation of disobedience dynamics, safety trap avoidance, and human‑AI interaction without centralized control.

## Key Results
- Follower’s optimal strategy: obey non‑harmful actions and disobey harmful ones, assuming perfect action classification.
- Leader’s optimal strategy: persistently propose goal‑reaching actions when available to ensure follower obedience and progress toward the goal.
- Safety traps: state subsets with no goal actions where the follower’s optimal policy leads to infinite disobedience loops, blocking goal achievement while indefinitely avoiding harm.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Selective Disobedience
- **Claim:** The follower’s optimal strategy is to obey non‑harmful actions and to disobey harmful actions.
- **Mechanism:** The follower perfectly classifies leader actions into goal‑reaching (A_g), harmful (A_h), and other (A_o). Given its reward structure (preventing harm), it maximizes utility by obeying A_g and A_o and overriding A_h. The leader, knowing this, cannot gain by proposing A_h.
- **Core assumption:** Follower possesses deterministic, perfect classification of actions into the three sets.
- **Evidence:** [abstract] “characterize optimal strategies: the follower should obey non‑harmful actions and disobey harmful ones…”; [section 3.1] derives the policy (A_g obey, A_h disobey, A_o obey) when A_g ≠ ∅.
- **Break condition:** Imperfect classification (e.g., false negatives) may cause the follower to obey harmful actions, breaking optimality.

### Mechanism 2: Leader’s Commitment Pressure
- **Claim:** The leader’s optimal strategy is to propose goal‑reaching actions (A_g) whenever possible.
- **Mechanism:** As the Stackelberg leader, it commits to repeatedly offering A_g actions. The follower, following Mechanism 1, will obey these, enabling goal progress. This credible commitment deters the follower from entering a safety trap.
- **Core assumption:** The leader can perfectly identify A_g actions at each state and a finite‑horizon path to a goal exists.
- **Evidence:** [section 3.2] “the leader can persistently propose goal‑reaching actions, it can effectively pressure the follower into obeying…”; [abstract] “the leader should propose goal‑reaching actions when possible.”
- **Break condition:** If no A_g actions exist from the current (or reachable) state, the leader cannot apply pressure, potentially leading to Mechanism 3.

### Mechanism 3: Safety Traps
- **Claim:** Safety traps are recurrent state subsets where the follower can indefinitely avoid harming the leader but also permanently block goal achievement.
- **Mechanism:** A safety trap S_trap satisfies: (1) no A_g(s) for s ∈ S_trap; (2) all A_o(s) transitions stay within S_trap; (3) closure under A_o. Within S_trap, the follower’s optimal policy (disobey any A_h) yields an infinite stream of harm‑prevention rewards for the follower, while the leader receives zero (no goal). Both players are stuck in a non‑cooperative equilibrium.
- **Core assumption:** Follower’s utility infinitely values harm prevention over the leader’s finite goal reward; environment is deterministic and stationary.
- **Evidence:** [section 3.2] full definition and strategic consequences; [abstract] “states where the follower indefinitely avoids harm but fails to achieve the goal.”
- **Break condition:** Finite‑horizon or discounted follower utility, stochastic transitions that can exit the trap, or side‑payments could dissolve the trap equilibrium.

  *Why needed:* The leader cannot distinguish harmful from other actions, making their decision process partially observable. Translating the IDG into a POMDP for the leader is necessary for computational modeling and RL training.  
  *Quick check:* How does partial observability for the leader change the optimal policy computation compared to a fully observable MDP?

- **Multi‑Agent Reinforcement Learning (Independent Learners)**

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
State Space (S) → Leader Action Partition (A_L = A_g ∪ A_h ∪ A_o) → Follower Action Set (A_F = {obey, disobey}) → Deterministic Transition Function T(s, a_L, a_F) → Reward Functions (R_L, R_F) → Leader Observation Model (O_L)

### Critical Path
1. Define the domain: enumerate all environment states and deterministic successor functions for leader actions.
2. Implement the action partition logic: for every state s, label each a_L(s) as A_g, A_h, or A_o (the asymmetric information).
3. Construct the two MDPs (M_L as POMDP, M_F as MDP) using shared S, A, T and the defined reward/observation functions.
4. Choose RL algorithms (e.g., DQN or PPO) for each agent; for the leader’s POMDP use a recurrent architecture or belief state tracking.
5. Train concurrently or in isolation, monitoring for emergence of the predicted strategic behaviors.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Determinism vs. Stochasticity:** Assuming deterministic transitions simplifies the follower’s harm calculation and safety trap definition. Adding stochasticity (probabilistic outcomes) complicates analysis and may require redefining traps as recurrent classes.
- **Reward Shaping:** The follower’s reward is sparse (only on disobedience events). Adding a small per‑step penalty could encourage faster goal attainment if possible but might alter the optimal “safety trap” strategy.
- **Observation Granularity:** The leader’s observation model is underspecified. A simple model (signal “goal‑action” vs. “non‑goal”) may be learnable; introducing noise or more ambiguous signals would increase difficulty and could break the pure‑strategy equilibria.

### Failure Signatures
- **Follower:** Learns to obey harmful actions (false negative on A_h) or disobeys goal actions (over‑generalization). Check policy accuracy on a held‑out set of (a_L, true label) pairs.
- **Leader:** Fails to commit to A_g proposals, instead randomizing or proposing harmful/other actions, resulting in consistently low payoff and no goal achievement.
- **System:** Enters an infinite loop not corresponding to a formally defined safety trap (e.g., due to stochasticity or a poorly defined partition), wasting computation without progress.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Tabular Verification of Equilibria:** For a small manually defined IDG instance (e.g., 5 states), compute the exact follower policy via the lookup rule and the leader’s best response. Verify predicted payoffs and check for safety traps; use as a sanity check for the MDP implementation.
2. **Learning the Follower’s Policy:** Train the follower agent in isolation (with a fixed, random leader policy). Hypothesis: it should quickly converge to a policy that perfectly classifies A_g/A_h/A_o and obeys non‑harmful/disobeys harmful actions. Measure classification accuracy.
3. **Emergence of Leader Commitment & Trap Avoidance:** Train both agents jointly from scratch in a medium‑sized graph (e.g., 20 states) with at least one goal path and one safety trap. Hypothesis: the leader learns to seek and propose A_g actions, the follower obeys them, and episodes avoid infinite loops. Track goal attainment rate vs. trap entries.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Dynamic Environments:** How can the IDG framework be extended to model environments where hazards or goal states appear or disappear over time? (Conclusion mentions investigating “dynamically changing environments in which agents can alter the environment’s state through their actions.”)
2. **Imperfect Perception/Action:** What strategies can prevent the follower from entering irreversible safety traps when its perception or action execution is imperfect? (Paper notes AI systems are faulty; false negatives on harm could lead to obeying dangerous actions.)
3. **Human Trust Impact:** How does the frequency and context of intelligent disobedience affect human trust in the assistive system, especially when disobedience leads to safety traps (infinite non‑goal loops)? (IDG enables empirical study of trust, but specific factors remain unexplored.)

## Limitations
- Deterministic transition assumption and follower’s perfect, deterministic action classification may not hold in real stochastic environments.
- The follower’s infinite‑horizon utility model (harm prevention infinitely outweighs the leader’s goal) is unrealistic and could break safety‑trap properties.
- The leader’s observation model is underspecified; noise or partial signals could hinder learning and alter equilibria.
- No empirical protocol details (RL algorithms, hyperparameters) are provided, leaving reproducibility dependent on approximations.

## Confidence
- Follower’s optimal disobedience strategy (obey non‑harmful, disobey harmful): **High**
- Leader’s commitment to goal‑reaching actions: **Medium**
- Safety trap identification and consequences: **Medium**
