# Residual Koopman Spectral Profiling for Predicting and Preventing Transformer Training Instability
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.22988
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22988
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A single forward pass at initialization predicts training divergence with AUROC 0.995; subsequent spectral regularization reduces divergence from 66.7% to 12.5% and permits 50–150% higher learning rates.

## Executive Summary
Training divergence in large transformers is costly and typically discovered only after expensive runs. This paper introduces Residual Koopman Spectral Profiling (RKSP), a method that uses a single forward pass at initialization to collect layer-wise residual snapshots. By applying whitened dynamic mode decomposition, RKSP estimates local Koopman operators and computes the near-unit spectral mass (M≈1)—the fraction of eigenvalues near the unit circle—as a predictor of instability. RKSP achieves an AUROC of 0.995 for divergence prediction, substantially outperforming gradient-based baselines. Building on this diagnosis, the authors propose Koopman Spectral Shaping (KSS), a regularizer that reshapes eigenvalues away from unstable regions during training. Empirically, KSS reduces divergence rates in challenging no-normalization settings and enables significantly higher learning rates. The approach generalizes across language modeling, vision transformers, and emerging architectures like MoE, Mamba, and KAN.

## Method Summary
RKSP operates in two phases. First, at initialization, it performs a single forward pass on a small batch (N=2048) to collect residual vectors (hℓ, hℓ⁺¹) for each layer. These snapshots are zero-phase whitened using the input covariance to ensure scale invariance. Whitened Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD) then solves a least-squares problem to estimate the local linear operator ˆAℓ, whose eigenvalues characterize the layer’s dynamic stability. Reliability filtering via residual DMD discards spurious eigenvalues. The key metric, near-unit spectral mass M≈1 (fraction of eigenvalues with |λ|∈[0.90,1.05]), strongly correlates with later divergence. Second, for mitigation, Koopman Spectral Shaping (KSS) periodically (every 10–20 steps) recomputes DMD on a subset of layers during training and adds a differentiable spectral loss. This loss combines a penalty pushing eigenvalues away from the unit circle (τᵤ=1.05) and a guidance term pulling M≈1 toward a target band (γ∈[0.3,0.5]) to avoid over-damping.

## Key Results
- RKSP predicts training divergence from a single initialization forward pass with AUROC 0.995, a 31% relative improvement over the best gradient-based baseline.
- Koopman Spectral Shaping reduces the divergence rate from 66.7% to 12.5% in no-normalization transformer training.
- KSS enables learning rates 50% to 150% higher than baseline before divergence occurs.
- The approach generalizes across architectures: GPT-2 up to 7B, vision transformers, MoE, Mamba-style SSMs, and KAN.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** A higher near-unit spectral mass (M≈1) at initialization correlates with increased training divergence risk.
- **Mechanism:** When eigenvalues cluster near the unit circle (|λ|≈1), the layer’s local linearization is near-isometric and weakly damping. This allows perturbations and optimization noise to propagate across depth without sufficient attenuation, especially under high learning rates, raising instability probability.
- **Core assumption:** The layer transition linearizations are approximately normal (eigenvectors not highly non-orthogonal). Non-normality can cause transient growth even if M≈1 is low.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "the near-unit spectral mass (M≈1) ... serves as the key instability predictor. RKSP achieves an AUROC of 0.995." [section 3.2.3] "our central diagnostic, the near-unit spectral mass, quantifies the fraction of modes concentrated near the unit circle, which captures instability risk." [section 4, Theorem 1] Under near-normality, larger M≈1 implies higher expected signal energy preservation. [corpus] Corpus evidence is weak; related Koopman-DMD papers focus on representation learning, not instability prediction.
- **Break condition:** When κ(V) ≫ 1 (high non-normality), eigenvalues near the unit circle may be perturbation-sensitive; transient growth can trigger instability even with moderate M≈1. In such cases, M≈1 alone is insufficient.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Whitened Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD) applied to layer-wise residual snapshots yields a coordinate-invariant spectral estimate that reliably predicts divergence from a single forward pass.
- **Mechanism:** Residual snapshots (hℓ, hℓ⁺¹) are collected across N inputs. Zero-phase whitening using the covariance of Xℓ normalizes scales, making spectra comparable across architectures. DMD then solves a least-squares problem in whitened coordinates to estimate the local linear operator ˆAℓ. Reliability filtering via residual DMD (ResDMD) discards spurious eigenvalues.
- **Core assumption:** The snapshot set is representative of the data distribution and the local linear approximation is meaningful; the whitening regularization ϵ ensures numerical stability.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "From a single forward pass at initialization, RKSP extracts Koopman spectral features by applying whitened dynamic mode decomposition (DMD) to layer-wise residual snapshots." [section 3.2.2] Details whitening, DMD, and ResDMD filtering; states that whitening "ensures cross-model comparability and yields coordinate-invariant spectral estimates." [section 5.1] "RKSP achieves an AUROC of 0.995 at initialization, representing a 31% relative improvement over the best gradient-based method." [corpus] Corpus shows active Koopman-DMD research but not the specific whitening or instability prediction application.
- **Break condition:** When the normalized fit error ηnl is high (e.g., >0.7 in late layers), the linear approximation is poor, making DMD eigenvalues less reliable for instability prediction (see Figure 4, "Start Linear, End Nonlinear" pattern).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Koopman Spectral Shaping (KSS) reduces divergence by regularizing the spectra during training, moving eigenvalues away from the unit circle and guiding near-unit mass toward a target band.
- **Mechanism:** KSS adds a differentiable loss Lℓ_KSS per sampled layer, combining an unstable penalty (softplus(|λ|−τᵤ)² weighted by sigmoid) to push |λ| away from τᵤ=1.05, and a near-unit guidance term (msoft_ℓ) pulled toward a target γ∈[0.3,0.5] to avoid over-contraction. Applied periodically (e.g., every 10−20 steps) to 50% of layers.
- **Core assumption:** The spectral regularization computed from current DMD estimates reflects the ongoing training stability; the target band γ balances damping and expressivity.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "KSS reduces the divergence rate from 66.7% to 12.5% in challenging no-normalization settings and enables learning rates 50% to 150% higher." [section 3.3] Defines Lℓ_KSS with unstable penalty and near-unit guidance; default hyperparameters T=20, τᵤ=1.05, τₗ=0.90, γ∈[0.3,0.5]. [section 5.3, Table 3] Shows KSS (α=0.15) lowers divergence from 66.7% to 12.5% in No-Norm; Figure 2 visualizes dose-response. [section 5.3, Table 6] Ablation: Both penalty and guidance are necessary; full KSS reduces divergence 5.3× vs generic regularization. [corpus] Corpus lacks direct evidence; related Koopman work does not address spectral regularization for stability.
- **Break condition:** If α is too high, spectra become overly contracted, harming expressivity and final accuracy; if too low, instability persists. Also, KSS assumes DMD estimates remain reasonably reliable (ηnl not too high); in layers with very high nonlinearity, spectral shaping may not align with true instability sources.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Koopman operator theory
  - **Why needed:** Provides linear eigenvalue interpretation of nonlinear dynamics; eigenvalues directly indicate expansion/contraction.
  - **Quick check:** What does an eigenvalue with |λ|>1 imply for forward signal propagation?
- **Concept:** Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD)
  - **Why needed:** Data-driven method to approximate Koopman operators from snapshots; RKSP uses whitened DMD per layer.
  - **Quick check:** Why apply whitening before DMD, and how does it affect eigenvalue interpretation?
- **Concept:** Transformer residual formulation as discrete dynamical system
  - **Why needed:** Each layer transition hℓ⁺¹ = hℓ + fℓ(hℓ) defines a map; its local linearization determines stability.
  - **Quick check:** If a layer’s DMD eigenvalues are all within |λ|<0.9, what does that imply about signal damping?
- **Concept:** Near-unit spectral mass (M≈1)
  - **Why needed:** Compact scalar summary of how many modes are near-isometric; primary instability predictor.
  - **Quick check:** Why not simply use spectral radius ρ? What extra information does M≈1 capture?
- **Concept:** Non-normality (κ(V), Kreiss constant)
  - **Why needed:** Non-orthogonal eigenvectors can cause transient amplification even if ρ≤1, so κ(V) contextualizes M≈1.
  - **Quick check:** How does a high condition number κ(V) modify the interpretation of near-unit eigenvalues?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Single forward pass → per-layer whitened DMD → eigenvalue filtering → M≈1 calculation → risk decision. For KSS: periodic DMD during training → spectral regularization applied → divergence reduction.
- **Critical path:** The shortest path from data to instability prediction is: collect residual snapshots (N=2048 inputs) → compute whitened DMD per layer → filter reliable eigenvalues → compute M≈1 and κ(V) → aggregate across layers → estimate divergence probability.
- **Design tradeoffs:** Randomized DMD rank r (default 32): higher r improves accuracy but costs O(dN r + r³). Thresholds (ϵu,ϵn,δc) define spectral bins; tighter bins may reduce noise but miss borderline cases. KSS hyperparameters: α (regularization strength), τᵤ/τₗ (band edges), γ (target near-unit mass). Overhead ~10% for typical settings.
- **Failure signatures:** High ηnl (>0.7) indicates unreliable DMD estimate; ignore M≈1 in those layers or retry after a few training steps. High κ(V) (>10) suggests non-normality-driven instability; consider combining with Kreiss constant K or using gradient-based monitoring. If KSS fails, check that DMD reliability is acceptable and that α is within [0.1,0.2] for No-Norm.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Normalization sweep: Train GPT-2-style models with Pre-LN, Post-LN, RMSNorm, DeepNorm, SubLN, No-Norm on associative-recall; compute M≈1 and divergence rate. Expect No-Norm: high M≈1 (~0.8) and high divergence (~96%).
  2. AUROC evaluation: Collect M≈1 from ~100 runs across diverse LR, normalization, depth; compute AUROC vs binary divergence label. Expect AUROC ≈0.995, significantly above gradient-norm baselines.
  3. KSS ablation: In No-Norm setting, compare full KSS (unstable penalty + near-unit guidance) vs each component alone vs generic ℓ₂/Jacobian penalties. Expect full KSS reduces divergence >5×; removing either component degrades performance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What mechanisms cause the universal increase in nonlinearity ratio ηnl with transformer depth (“Start Linear, End Nonlinear” pattern)?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Figure 4 shows ηnl rising monotonically across layers in multiple pretrained models, but the paper does not explain why.
- **Why unresolved:** This pattern impacts RKSP reliability in deeper layers and may relate to fundamental depth limitations.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Depth-ablated architecture studies; theoretical analysis linking layer-wise nonlinearity accumulation to depth.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How does RKSP perform on extremely large models (>100B parameters) and highly nonlinear architectures where ηnl is consistently high?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper tests up to 7B and notes ηnl increases with scale (Figure 3). KAN shows ηnl≈0.8 and DMD reliability as low as 52% (Table 18), raising doubts about reliability in extreme regimes.
- **Why unresolved:** If RKSP fails, it cannot predict instability for state-of-the-art models.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Profiling larger models (e.g., GPT-4, PaLM) and highly nonlinear variants; developing nonlinearity-robust DMD extensions.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can the KSS hyperparameters (τu, τl, γ) be determined automatically from the initial spectral profile instead of manual sweeps?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 3.3 uses fixed defaults τu=1.05, τl=0.90, γ∈[0.3,0.5] and sweeps α; no adaptive method is proposed.
- **Why unresolved:** Manual tuning is costly and may not transfer across architectures or tasks.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Deriving stability bounds that yield optimal target bands; adaptive algorithms that adjust γ based on initial M≈1 and κ(V).

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Why does RKSP’s predicted divergence probability show moderate calibration (ECE=0.283), and how can we improve it for reliable early-stopping?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Figure 5 reports an ECE of 0.283 and notes "moderate calibration."
- **Why unresolved:** Poor calibration undermines confidence in probability estimates for risk-averse pruning decisions.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Applying calibration methods on diverse architectures; analyzing miscalibration sources (e.g., overconfidence on high-M≈1 models).

## Limitations
- The link between M≈1 and instability critically assumes approximately normal linearizations; high non-normality (κ(V) ≫ 1) can cause transient growth even with moderate M≈1, and the proposed κ(V) correction is a heuristic.
- Zero-phase whitening, while enabling cross-model comparability, may distort true spectral properties if the input covariance estimate is poor or unrepresentative.
- Generalization bounds remain unknown; evidence for MoE, Mamba, and KAN is limited in scale/diversity, and KSS hyperparameters were tuned on standard Transformers.

## Confidence
- **RKSP prediction efficacy (AUROC 0.995):** High – strong empirical result with consistent outperformance of baselines, though test set distribution is unclear.
- **Mechanism: M≈1 as key predictor:** Medium – theoretical link via near-normality is sound, but the normality assumption is frequently violated; break condition acknowledges this limitation.
- **KSS efficacy for reducing divergence:** Medium – convincing 66.7%→12.5% reduction in no-norm setting, but relies on careful α-tuning and overhead; optimal target γ chosen empirically.
- **Cross-architecture generalization:** Low-Medium – evidence spans several architectures but each has limited configurations; scaling to larger models and more complex tasks remains unvalidated.

## Next Checks
1. Stress-test non-normality handling: Train Transformer variants with induced high non-normality (e.g., structured low-rank attention projections). Validate whether the `M≈1 × log10(κ(V))` correction maintains high AUROC, or if a more sophisticated metric (e.g., Kreiss constant) is needed.
2. Ablate the whitening transform: For a fixed set of divergent/non-divergent runs, compare RKSP performance using (a) whitened DMD, (b) standard DMD on raw snapshots, and (c) DMD on per-layer activation-normalized snapshots. Isolate whitening’s contribution to invariance vs. information loss.
3. Scale KSS to large models & diverse objectives: Apply KSS during pre-training of a >1B parameter model (e.g., GPT-2 1.5B or Mamba) on a standard corpus (e.g., The Pile). Measure (a) final perplexity vs. baseline at identical LR, (b) divergence rate over multiple seeds, and (c) compute overhead. Test if benefits persist at scale and on real-world language modeling loss landscapes.
