# Finite-time analysis of Multi-timescale Stochastic Optimization Algorithms

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.29380  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.29380  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** First finite-time convergence guarantees for multi-timescale zeroth-order stochastic optimization algorithms, with explicit rates O(T⁻⁰·⁴) for GSF1 and O(T⁻⁰·⁴⁺ᵟ) for NSF1.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents the first finite-time analysis for two multi-timescale zeroth-order stochastic optimization algorithms: a two-timescale gradient method (GSF1) and a three-timescale Newton method (NSF1). Both algorithms use smoothed functional perturbations to estimate gradients and, for NSF1, the Hessian from noisy function samples. The analysis derives explicit mean-squared error bounds for the Hessian and gradient estimators and establishes finite-time convergence guarantees on the expected squared gradient norm. Near-optimal step-size exponents yield convergence rates O(T^{-0.4}) for GSF1 and O(T^{-0.4+δ}) for NSF1. These rates are experimentally validated on the Continuous Mountain Car benchmark, demonstrating the benefit of incorporating curvature information via the Newton method.

## Method Summary
The algorithms operate in a stochastic approximation setting where the objective J(θ) is defined as the long-run average of a cost function h(θ, X) with X following a Markov chain. Both GSF1 and NSF1 rely on smoothed zeroth-order estimators: for a random perturbation η ~ N(0,I) and smoothing parameter β, the gradient estimator is ĝ = η/β · h(θ+βη, X) and the Hessian estimator elements are built from η_iη_j/β² · h(...). GSF1 employs two timescales: a slow parameter step-size a(m) and an intermediate gradient step-size c(m); it tracks a moving average Z(m) of ĝ and updates θ via Γ(θ – a(m) Z(m)). NSF1 adds a fastest timescale with Hessian step-size b(m) to recursively average the Hessian estimator Ĥ, maintains a positive-definite matrix M(m) = (P PD(H(m)))^{-1} (e.g., H+λI), and updates θ via Γ(θ – a(m) M(m) Z(m+1)). For the Continuous Mountain Car experiments, the authors use β=0.05, step-size exponents σ=0.6 (a(m) ≈ (1+m)^{-0.6}), α=0.4 (c(m) ≈ (1+m)^{-0.4}), and ν=0.4 for NSF1 (b(m) ≈ (1+m)^{-0.4}); a linear policy u = θᵀx with θ ∈ ℝ² is employed to align with theoretical dimensionality assumptions.

The finite-time analysis expands the squared error recursions for H and Z into five components (telescoping, noise cross-term, drift, martingale variance, deterministic drift), then sums over time using polynomial step-size decay to obtain explicit bounds that vanish under 0<ν<α<σ<1. The resulting convergence rate min_{m≤T} E∥∇J(θ(m))∥² ≤ O(T^{σ-1}) + O(T^{-ν}) + O(T^{-2(σ-α)}) for NSF1 (and O(T^{α-1}) for GSF1) highlights the trade-offs between timescale separation and estimation error.

## Key Results
- The paper provides the first finite-time analysis for multi-timescale zeroth-order stochastic optimization, deriving explicit mean-squared error bounds for gradient and Hessian estimators that capture the interaction of timescales.
- For the three-timescale Newton method (NSF1), the convergence guarantee is: min_{0≤m≤T} E‖∇J(θ(m))‖² ≤ O(T^{σ-1}) + O(T^{-ν}) + O(T^{-2(σ-α)}); for the two-timescale gradient method (GSF1): same with O(T^{α-1}) instead of O(T^{σ-1}).
- With near-optimal step-size exponents (σ=0.6, α=0.4, ν→0), the rates become O(T^{-0.4}) for GSF1 and O(T^{-0.4+δ}) for NSF1, where the δ accounts for residual Hessian estimation error. These rates are validated experimentally on the Continuous Mountain Car benchmark.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Multi-timescale step-size separation controls error propagation from noisy Hessian estimation, enabling finite-time convergence.
- **Mechanism:** The algorithm uses three diminishing step-sizes: a(m) for parameters (slowest), c(m) for gradient (intermediate), b(m) for Hessian (fastest). The hierarchy a(m)=o(c(m))=o(b(m)) ensures the Hessian estimator tracks ∇²J(θ(m)) rapidly enough that its error does not dominate the slower parameter updates. The finite-time bound (Theorem 2) explicitly shows terms like O(T^{-ν}) from Hessian error and O(T^{-2(σ-α)}) from coupled gradient-Hessian error, which vanish when 0<ν<α<σ<1.
- **Core assumption:** Step-size exponents satisfy 0<ν<α<σ<1 (Assumption 3) and iterates remain bounded (Assumptions 1-2, 4).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "explicitly characterizes the interaction between multiple time-scales and the propagation of estimation errors."
  - [section 3.3-3.5] Describes the three recursions with step-size ordering a=o(c)=o(b.
  - [section 5.1, Theorem 1] Hessian MSE bound E[∥H(t)-H*(t)∥_F²] ≤ O(t^ν)+O(t^{1-ν})+O(t^{1-2(σ-ν)}).
  - [corpus] Related papers (e.g., "Non-Expansive Mappings in Two-Time-Scale Stochastic Approximation") study multi-time-scale SA but not with zeroth-order Hessian estimation or finite-time bounds for Newton methods—this paper fills that gap.
- **Break condition:** If step-sizes are not properly separated (e.g., σ ≤ α or α ≤ ν), the error terms O(T^{-ν}) or O(T^{-2(σ-α)}) may not decay, leading to no convergence guarantee or instability.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Smoothed functional zeroth-order estimators provide unbiased (in limit β→0) gradient/Hessian estimates from noisy function samples, enabling derivative-free optimization.
- **Mechanism:** Random perturbations η(m)~N(0,I) and smoothing parameter β>0 are used. The gradient estimator bg(m)=η(m)/β * h(θ(m)+βη(m),X_m) and Hessian estimator elements (e.g., Hat_H_{i,i}(m)=η_i(m)²/β² * h(...)) approximate ∇J and ∇²J of the Gaussian-smoothed objective. The recursion (e.g., H(m+1)=H(m)+b(m)(Hat_H(m)-H(m))) recursively averages these noisy estimates.
- **Core assumption:** Smoothing parameter β>0 is fixed; h(θ,X) has uniformly bounded fourth moments (Lemma 2); θ↦P_θ, h_θ are C³ (Assumption 2) ensuring smoothness of J.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "smoothed functional perturbations to estimate gradients and, for NSF1, the Hessian from noisy function samples."
  - [section 3.2] Defines the gradient and Hessian estimators explicitly.
  - [section 4, Lemma 2] Bounded fourth moment sup_θ E[h(θ,X)⁴]<∞ ensures boundedness of estimator second moments.
  - [corpus] Smoothed functional methods trace back to Bhatnagar (2005-2007) and SPSA; this paper provides the first finite-time Hessian error bounds in a multi-timescale setting.
- **Break condition:** If β is too large, smoothing bias dominates; if too small, variance increases. The analysis assumes β is constant and absorbed into constants; in practice, β may require tuning. Also, if h(θ,X) has heavy tails (violating Lemma 2), moment bounds fail.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Recursive averaging with diminishing step-sizes yields explicit mean-squared error bounds for gradient and Hessian estimates, which feed into the parameter convergence bound.
- **Mechanism:** The gradient and Hessian recursions are linear stochastic approximations with time-varying step-sizes. The finite-time analysis expands the squared error recursion into five components (C1–C5): telescoping, noise cross-term, drift from moving true parameter, martingale variance, and squared deterministic drift. Summing from m=τ to t and using step-size decays (a(m)~(1+m)^{-σ}, etc.) yields O(t^ν), O(t^{1-ν}), O(t^{1-2(σ-ν)}) type bounds. These are plugged into the smoothness-based parameter descent inequality.
- **Core assumption:** Step-sizes are polynomially decaying (Assumption 3); Lipschitz continuity of ∇J and ∇²J (Lemma 1); boundedness of iterates via projection (Γ, P PD).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 5.1, Step 3] "Expansion into five components" and the constants C1–C5 capture different error sources.
  - [appendix A.5] Detailed term-wise bounds leading to F(t)=O(t^ν)+O(t^{1-ν})+O(t^{1-2(σ-ν)}).
  - [section 5.1, Theorem 2] Final gradient bound min_{0≤m≤T} E∥∇J(θ(m))∥² ≤ O(T^{σ-1}) + O(T^{-ν}) + O(T^{-2(σ-α)}).
  - [corpus] Finite-time SA analysis typically uses similar telescoping and martingale techniques (e.g., in "Fast Two-Time-Scale Stochastic Gradient Method"), but applying it to zeroth-order multi-timescale Newton is novel.
- **Break condition:** If step-sizes do not decay polynomially (e.g., constant), the sums X b(m) diverge, and estimates may not converge. If Lipschitz constants are too large (violating Lemma 1), drift terms (C3, C5) blow up.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Stochastic Approximation**
  - Why needed here: The algorithms are SA methods: iterative updates using noisy samples to find θ minimizing J(θ)=lim_{N→∞} 1/N E[∑ h(θ,X_m]]. Unlike stochastic gradient descent, SA handles long-run average costs with Markovian noise.
  - Quick check: "What is the key difference between stochastic gradient descent (SGD) and stochastic approximation (SA) as used here?" (SGD typically assumes i.i.d. data and known gradient form; SA here deals with dependent Markov samples and zeroth-order gradient estimates.)

- **Concept: Multi-timescale Stochastic Approximation**
  - Why needed here: NSF1 has three recursions (Hessian, gradient, parameter) with different step-sizes. Understanding timescale separation (a=o(c)=o(b) is crucial to analyze how estimation errors propagate without destabilizing parameter updates.
  - Quick check: "In a two-timescale algorithm, if the fast timescale (e.g., critic in actor-critic) updates too slowly relative to the slow timescale (actor), what goes wrong?" (The fast timescale cannot track the moving target, causing bias in the slow update.)

- **Concept: Smoothed Functional Zeroth-Order Optimization**
  - Why needed here: The objective J(θ) is accessible only via noisy samples h(θ,X). No explicit gradient/Hessian. Smoothed functionals use random perturbations and smoothing β to estimate derivatives from function values.
  - Quick check: "How does the smoothing parameter β trade off bias vs. variance in the gradient estimator bg(m)=η/β * h(θ+βε,X)?" (Larger β increases smoothing bias but reduces variance from h's noise; smaller β reduces bias but increases variance.)

- **Concept: Finite-Time vs. Asymptotic Analysis**
  - Why needed here: Asymptotic results (e.g., "θ(m)→θ* a.s.") do not specify how many iterations T are needed to achieve a desired accuracy. Finite-time bounds give explicit rates like O(T^{-0.4}) for min_{m≤T} E∥∇J(θ(m))∥², which is essential for practical deployment and hyperparameter selection.
  - Quick check: "What does the term O(T^{σ-1}) in Theorem 2 represent, and why does it dominate when σ=0.6?" (It comes from the parameter step-size a(m)~(1+m)^{-σ}; summing a(m) from 1 to T gives O(T^{1-σ})=O(T^{0.4}), so the average gradient norm is lower-bounded by something like O(T^{σ-1})=O(T^{-0.4}).)

- **Concept: Projection and Boundedness in SA**
  - Why needed here: The parameter update includes projection Γ to a compact convex set C, and Hessian is projected to positive definite matrices (P PD). These ensure iterates stay in a region where assumptions (Lipschitz, bounded moments) hold and M(m)=(H(m)+λI)^{-1} is uniformly bounded (Assumption 4).
  - Quick check: "Why is it necessary to project the Hessian estimate to the positive definite cone (e.g., via H←H+λI)?" (To ensure the Newton-type update θ←θ-a M z is a descent direction; if H is not PD, M could point uphill or be undefined.)

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:**  
  Inputs (current θ(m), step sizes a, c, b, smoothing β, sample X_m, perturbation η(m)) → Gradient estimator bg(m)=η/β·h(θ+βη,X) and Hessian estimator Hat_H(m) (using η_iη_j/β²·h(...)) → Recursions: H(m+1)=P PD(H(m)+b(m)(Hat_H(m)-H(m))), z(m+1)=z(m)+c(m)(bg(m)-z(m)) → Parameter update: M(m)=(H(m)+λI)^{-1}, θ(m+1)=Γ(θ(m)-a(m) M(m) z(m+1)) → Projections (Γ to C, P PD) → next iterate.
- **Critical path:**  
  1. Initialize θ(0)∈C, H(0) (e.g., identity), z(0)=0.  
  2. For m=0 to T-1:  
     a. Sample η(m)~N(0,I); compute θ̃=θ(m)+βη(m).  
     b. Generate X_m from simulator with policy θ̃.  
     c. Compute h_val = h(θ̃,X_m).  
     d. Compute bg(m)=η(m)/β * h_val.  
     e. Compute Hat_H(m) element-wise.  
     f. Update H: H←H + b(m)*(Hat_H - H); then H←H+λI.  
     g. Update z: z←z + c(m)*(bg - z).  
     h. Compute M = (H+λI)^{-1}.  
     i. Update θ: θ←Γ(θ - a(m)* M * z).  
  3. Return θ(T).
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Step-size exponents: Must satisfy 0<ν<α<σ<1. Optimal near σ=0.6, α=0.4, ν→0 (Theorem 2), but ν too small increases Hessian error propagation; α too close to σ increases gradient error coupling.  
  - Smoothing β: Larger β reduces variance of bg and Hat_H but introduces bias in approximating true ∇J, ∇²J. Paper absorbs β into constants; in practice, β may need to decrease slowly (e.g., β∼1/√m) but analysis assumes fixed β.  
  - Hessian approximation: Full Hessian (d×d) costs O(d²) storage and O(d³) inversion. Experiment uses diagonal approximation to reduce cost, but theory assumes full Hessian. Trade-off: diagonal loses off-diagonal curvature but scales better.  
  - Projection λ for Hessian: Must be large enough that H+λI is always PD. Too large λ distorts Newton direction; too small risks non-PD. Often set adaptively via eigenvalue monitoring or Levenberg-Marquardt style.  
  - Projection Γ for θ: Ensures θ stays in compact C where assumptions hold. Choice of C affects constant bounds; typically a large box or ball.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Gradient norm stagnates/increases: Check step-sizes—if a(m) not diminishing (σ≤0) or too large, updates overshoot. Also check Hessian conditioning: if H(m) has small eigenvalues, M(m) becomes huge and amplifies noise.  
  - Hessian estimate diverges or becomes non-PD: b(m) may be too small (slow tracking) or too large (high variance). Monitor min eigenvalue of H(m); if negative, λ is insufficient. Also check β: if β too small, Hat_H variance huge.  
  - Parameter θ leaves feasible set C: Projection Γ may be missing or incorrect. Also, if M(m)z(m) norm is huge due to poor Hessian estimate, the step may overshoot projection.  
  - High variance in performance across seeds: Indicates step-size separation not sufficient (e.g., α and ν too close), causing error coupling. Also check Markov chain mixing: if underlying chain mixes slowly (Assumption 1 violated), sample correlations degrade bounds.  
  - NaN or Inf values: Typically from numerical instability in Hessian inversion. Use Cholesky with jitter (add λI) or use pseudo-inverse with threshold.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Synthetic quadratic test:** J(θ)=½ θᵀ A θ with A PD (e.g., diagonal [1,100]). Compare NSF1 vs GSF1 with same total samples. Verify Hessian estimate error decays as O(m^ν) and NSF1 converges faster. Sweep σ,α,ν to see effect on final gradient norm.  
  2. **Ill-conditioned non-convex test:** e.g., J(θ)=θ₁⁴ - θ₁² + θ₂² (has saddle). Check if NSF1 escapes saddle faster due to curvature. Monitor frequency of finding first-order stationary point (∥∇J∥<ε).  
  3. **Continuous Mountain Car replication:** Reproduce paper’s Figure 1. Use exactly their hyperparameters (β=0.05, L=400, a(0)=0.1, b(0)=1 (NSF1) or 0.5, c(0)=0.05, σ=0.6, α=0.4, ν=0.4). Verify empirical decay rate of E[∥∇J∥²] matches O(T^{-0.4}) for GSF1 and O(T^{-0.4+δ}) for NSF1. Then ablate: set ν=α (violates separation) and observe slower convergence.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Are the step-size exponents σ=0.6, α=0.4, ν→0 provably optimal for achieving the finite-time convergence rate O(T^{-0.4}) in NSF1 and GSF1?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper identifies these exponents by balancing dominant error terms in Theorem 2 but describes the rates as "near-optimal" without providing a lower bound to confirm optimality.
- **Why unresolved:** The analysis yields upper bounds, but no lower bounds exist for multi-timescale zeroth-order stochastic optimization to show that O(T^{-0.4}) is the best achievable rate under the given assumptions.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Prove a matching lower bound for the convergence rate under the same oracle model and assumptions, demonstrating that any choice of step-size exponents cannot yield a faster rate than O(T^{-0.4}).

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can the finite-time analysis be strengthened to provide high-probability convergence guarantees, such as P( max_{0≤m≤T} ||∇J(θ(m))|| > ε ) ≤ δ, instead of only bounds in expectation?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper focuses on expected squared gradient norm (E[||∇J(θ(m))||^2]) and does not derive any tail bounds or high-probability statements.
- **Why unresolved:** Expectation bounds are weaker for safety-critical applications where rare failures matter. The coupled multi-timescale recursions involve dependent martingales, making standard concentration techniques challenging to apply directly.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Adapt martingale concentration inequalities (e.g., Freedman's inequality) to the specific structure of the NSF1 and GSF1 recursions, deriving high-probability bounds with explicit dependence on T and δ.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Do the finite-time convergence guarantees extend to objective functions that are not Lipschitz smooth or have non-smooth characteristics, such as those arising in reinforcement learning with discontinuous rewards?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Assumptions 1-2 and Lemma 1 require C^3 smoothness and Lipschitz continuity of gradients and Hessians, which may not hold for many practical optimization problems.
- **Why unresolved:** The proofs rely critically on smoothness to bound drift terms like ||H^*(m+1) - H^*(m)|| via ||θ(m+1) - θ(m)||. Without smoothness, these terms could grow uncontrollably, invalidating the error propagation analysis.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Develop new analysis techniques for non-smooth objectives, perhaps using subgradients or generalized smoothing, and validate on benchmark problems with non-smooth objectives to confirm convergence rates.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How does the smoothing parameter β affect the finite-time convergence constants and rates, and what is the optimal choice of β relative to the step-sizes to minimize the expected squared gradient norm?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The smoothing parameter β is used in the gradient and Hessian estimators (equations 1,2,4) but does not appear in the final big-O bounds of Theorems 2 and 4, implying its effect is absorbed into constants. The interplay between β and step-sizes for finite-time performance is not analyzed.
- **Why unresolved:** In practice, β must be tuned, but the theory provides no guidance on how to scale β with the step-size exponents (σ, α, ν) to minimize estimation error and improve finite-time convergence.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Derive explicit bounds that include β as a parameter, and theoretically or empirically determine the optimal scaling of β with the step-sizes to minimize the convergence bound, possibly showing that β should decrease at a specific rate relative to a(m), b(m), c(m).

## Limitations
- Theoretical analysis relies on strong, unverified assumptions (ergodicity, C³ smoothness, uniformly bounded fourth moments, uniform Hessian lower bound) that are not validated in the experiments.
- The Continuous Mountain Car simulator is underspecified (exact dynamics, noise level, horizon), preventing exact replication.
- The experimental policy is a neural network while the theory assumes direct updates on θ ∈ ℝᵈ; this mismatch may invalidate the finite-time rates for the reported results.
- Step-size exponents are tuned to specific values without comprehensive sensitivity analysis; the "near-optimal" claim lacks a lower bound confirmation.
- Hidden constants in the O(·) bounds are not provided, making quantitative comparison between theory and experiment impossible beyond qualitative decay rates.
