# ComplLLM: Fine-tuning LLMs to Discover Complementary Signals for Decision-making
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.19458
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19458
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** ComplLLM fine‑tunes an LLM to extract complementary signals from unstructured text, improving decision accuracy beyond an existing agent across medical, moderation, and reviewing tasks.

## Executive Summary
ComplLLM tackles the challenge of surfacing complementary information from unstructured text to enhance decisions made with an existing AI agent. It introduces a decision‑theoretic fine‑tuning objective that maximizes the increase in best‑attainable decision payoff when the LLM’s extracted signals are combined with the agent’s recommendation. Evaluated on synthetic and three real‑world tasks—radiology diagnosis, content moderation, and paper reviewing—the method consistently produces signals that improve accuracy, with physician interviews confirming clinical relevance.

## Method Summary
ComplLLM proceeds in four stages. First, it estimates the data‑generating joint distribution π(s,z,y) using a reference LLM (e.g., Qwen3‑8B) to define a space of binary signals and to annotate each text instance with signal presence via majority vote, yielding a binary matrix s_i^(0). Second, it derives “complementary labels” s_i^(1) by selecting basic signals that both occur and improve the best‑attainable decision payoff (estimated via a regression model on s_i^(0), z_i, y_i) by more than a threshold ε. Third, the base LLM undergoes supervised fine‑tuning (SFT) to output s_i^(1) given (t_i, z_i) with chain‑of‑thought prompting. Fourth, it further optimizes the policy using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), where the reward directly reflects the complementary value of the extracted signals as measured by π. The final model thus generates signals that, when combined with the agent’s recommendation Z, decision‑theoretically increase expected payoff.

## Key Results
- Synthetic data: recovers known complementary signals with surface similarity 0.98 and F1 0.67, beating baselines.
- Radiology diagnosis: accuracy improves from 0.819 (agent) to 0.839 with 12 statistically significant signals.
- Content moderation: accuracy improves from 0.847 to 0.878 with 4 signals; paper reviewing: from 0.627 to 0.692 with 8 signals.
- Physician interviews confirmed signal relevance; using signals with an LLM reviewer raised accuracy from 76.8% to 79.7%.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** ComplLLM optimizes for signals that improve the *best-attainable decision performance* given an existing agent recommendation, not just predictive or frequent signals.
- **Mechanism:** It defines complementary value via a decision-theoretic objective (Equation 1): the expected increase in optimal decision payoff when extracted signals S are combined with agent output Z, compared to using Z alone. This value is computed from an estimated joint distribution π(s,z,y).
- **Core assumption:** The best-attainable payoff (maximizing expected utility over decisions conditional on information) is the correct measure of a signal's decision-theoretic worth.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "...fine-tunes an LLM via a decision-theoretic objective: maximizing the increase in best-attainable decision payoff..."
  - [section 3] Formal definition of `V_U,Z(LLM)` as the improvement in `hat_U` from observing `LLM(T)` and Z versus Z alone.
  - [corpus] Weak direct evidence; neighbor papers focus on multi-agent coordination, not this specific decision-theoretic framing.
- **Break condition:** If the estimated posterior `π(y|s,z)` is highly inaccurate (e.g., due to limited data for rare signals), the computed complementary value will be misleading, causing the model to optimize for spurious signals.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** A two-stage fine-tuning pipeline (SFT followed by GRPO) effectively shapes the LLM to output signals with high complementary value.
- **Mechanism:** 
  1. **SFT stage:** Generates "ground-truth" complementary signals `s_i^(1)` for each instance by selecting basic signals that occur and improve `hat_U` by more than a threshold (Equation 2). The LLM is fine-tuned to mimic these selections, with chain-of-thought traces enforcing reasoning about evidence and complementarity.
  2. **GRPO stage:** Uses reinforcement learning with a reward function `R(s, z_i, y_i)` (Equation 3) that directly scores signals based on their realized complementary value (normalized by the SFT signal's value). GRPO's group-relative advantage (Equation 4) encourages outputs that outperform the batch average.
- **Core assumption:** The SFT-generated labels are a sufficiently good approximation of optimal complementary signals to initialize policy learning, and the reward function accurately distinguishes high-value signals.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4.2] "We generate the training data for SFT as the complementary signals... by selecting those basic signals that both occur and have larger best-attainable payoff..."
  - [section 4.3] "We fine-tune LLMθ using the GRPO algorithm... The reward function R... rewards signals only when they increase best-attainable payoff..."
  - [corpus] Neighbors discuss multi-agent LLM systems; no direct evidence on this SFT+RL pipeline for complementarity.
- **Break condition:** If the SFT labels are systematically biased (e.g., due to threshold `ε` or regression errors), the model may converge to a poor local optimum from which GRPO cannot recover, especially if the reward landscape is sparse or deceptive.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Estimating the full data-generating joint distribution `π(s,z,y)` via LLM prompting enables the computation of the complementary value objective without requiring ground-truth complementary signals.
- **Mechanism:** Uses a reference LLM in two rounds:
  1. **Signal space discovery:** Prompts to extract all candidate basic signals across the dataset, then filters by frequency to ensure stable estimation.
  2. **Signal occurrence annotation:** For each candidate signal and instance, prompts to determine presence (majority vote over samples), yielding binary matrix `s_i^(0)`.
  The posterior `π(y|s,z)` is then estimated by fitting a regression model (Algorithm 1) on `{s_i^(0), y_i}` (and interactions), which is used to compute `hat_U`.
- **Core assumption:** The reference LLM can reliably and consistently extract a comprehensive set of clinically/domain-relevant signals and their occurrences from unstructured text.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4.1] "We estimate the data-generating process by prompting an LLM model in two rounds... In the first round, we identify the space of possible signals... In the second round, we identify whether a signal occurs in each instance..."
  - [section 5.2] Details on using Qwen3-8B as `LLMref` for this estimation.
  - [corpus] Neighbors use LLMs for extraction/hypothesis generation but not specifically for building a joint distribution for a decision-theoretic reward.
- **Break condition:** If the reference LLM has low precision or recall in signal extraction (e.g., missing subtle but important signals, or hallucinating spurious ones), the estimated distribution will be biased, leading to incorrect value estimates and mislabeled SFT data.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Decision-Theoretic Value of Information**
  - *Why needed here:* ComplLLM's core innovation is framing signal extraction as maximizing *complementary* value, defined by improvement in *best-attainable* decision payoff. This requires understanding that the value of a signal depends on the decision problem (utility) and existing information (Z).
  - *Quick check question:* "How does the value of a signal change if the decision-maker's utility function changes (e.g., from accuracy to weighted F1-score)?"

- **Concept: Reinforcement Learning from Human/Environment Feedback (RLHF) Style Tuning**
  - *Why needed here:* The complementary value objective is non-differentiable and instance-specific. GRPO is used to directly optimize the LLM policy for this objective after SFT provides a reasonable starting point.
  - *Quick check question:* "Why might SFT alone be insufficient, necessitating an RL stage with a reward based on the decision outcome?"

- **Concept: Joint Distribution Estimation for Counterfactual Evaluation**
  - *Why needed here:* To compute `V_U,Z(LLM)`, we need `π(y|s,z)`, which is never directly observed. The method estimates this from historical data `(t_i, z_i, y_i)` by extracting signals `s_i` from `t_i`. This is a form of *off-policy* evaluation of a new information source (the extracted signals).
  - *Quick check question:* "What are the risks of using a regression model estimated on `s_i^(0)` to represent `π(y|s,z)` for future `s` generated by the fine-tuned LLM?"

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  1. **Reference LLM (`LLM_ref`)**: Qwen3-8B. Role: Generates candidate signal space and annotates signal occurrences (Section 4.1). Input: text `t_i`. Output: list of signal names.
  2. **Distribution Estimator**: Logistic/regression model (Algorithm 1). Role: Fits `π(y|s,z)` using `{s_i^(0), z_i, y_i}`. Output: posterior probabilities for computing `hat_U`.
  3. **SFT Module**: Fine-tuning script (e.g., Hugging Face `Trainer`). Role: Trains base LLM (`LLM_θ`) on `(t_i, z_i, s_i^(1))` pairs with CoT. Input: prompt with `t_i, z_i`; output: `s_i^(1)`.
  4. **GRPO Module**: RL training loop (based on Shao et al., 2024). Role: Optimizes `LLM_θ` to maximize reward `R(s, z_i, y_i)`. Input: `(t_i, z_i)`; generates multiple `s` candidates per instance; computes advantages from rewards.
  5. **Reward Calculator**: Function implementing Equation 3. Role: Computes scalar reward for each candidate signal set using the pre-fitted `π(y|s,z)` and `s_i^(1)` normalizer.
  6. **Validation/Test Harness**: Scripts to compute final metrics (Surface Similarity, F1, Complementary Information Value) by fitting a fresh model on `LLM(t_i)` outputs.

- **Critical path:** Data `(t_i, z_i, y_i)` → [1] Signal extraction (`s_i^(0)`) → [2] Fit `π(y|s,z)` → [3] Generate `s_i^(1)` (SFT labels) → [4] SFT on `(t_i, z_i, s_i^(1))` → [5] GRPO: for batch, generate `s` candidates → [6] Compute rewards using `π(y|s,z)` → [7] Update `LLM_θ`. Path breaks if any component fails (e.g., `s_i^(0)` extraction fails, regression overfits).

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **LLM vs. Traditional Models for `π` estimation:** Using an LLM for extraction is flexible but introduces noise and cost. A traditional topic model (e.g., BERTopic) was a baseline but performed worse, suggesting domain-specific signal extraction needs LLM reasoning.
  - **SFT then RL vs. End-to-End RL:** SFT provides a stable prior; RL then fine-grained optimization. Skipping SFT led to instability in early trials (per Appendix D). The tradeoff is added complexity and compute.
  - **Signal Space Size:** Filtering by frequency `N_τ` reduces dimensionality and stabilizes regression but risks dropping rare but true complementary signals (Limitations section). Setting `ε, δ` controls this.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Synthetic recovery failure:** Low Surface Similarity/F1 (<0.9/0.6) indicates the model cannot even recover known complementary signals, suggesting fundamental issues in the SFT labeling or GRPO reward.
  - **No significant signals in real tasks:** If `Breadth` metric (count of significant coefficients) is zero, the extracted signals add no unique information beyond Z. This could be due to poor signal diversity (SFT) or reward mis-specification (GRPO).
  - **Degradation vs. Benchmark:** If accuracy with signals is worse than the non-interpretable benchmark (Figure 3), the extraction is destroying predictive information.
  - **Qualitative mismatch:** Domain experts (physicians) flagging signals as irrelevant or already captured by Z indicates a breakdown in the "complementarity" definition during training.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Synthetic data recovery:** Train on MIMIC-CXR-derived synthetic data where ground-truth complementary signals (Edema, Pleural Effusion) are withheld from Z. Evaluate recovery via Surface Similarity/F1 and complementary value. This tests if the *mechanism* can recover known signals.
  2. **Radiology diagnosis (real):** Train on full MIMIC-CXR with real `z` (vision model scores) and `y` (cardiac dysfunction from blood tests). Evaluate: (a) number of statistically significant signals in `LLM(t)` (controlling for `z`), (b) qualitative physician validation of signal relevance, (c) improvement in best-attainable accuracy.
  3. **Content moderation:** Train on DICES dataset where `z` is a specific demographic group's toxicity labels and `y` is majority-vote label. Evaluate complementary value and breadth. Tests generalizability to non-medical, text-only task.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can ComplLLM reliably identify rare but important complementary signals that fall below the frequency threshold Nτ used during data-generating process estimation?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state in the Limitations section: "however, there is a risk that COMPLLLM drops rare but important signals, i.e., signals that have frequency lower than Nτ in the dataset but would be considered important to domain experts."
- **Why unresolved:** The current approach uses a frequency cutoff to ensure stable estimation of posterior distributions, which may discard rare but critical signals. In high-stakes domains like medicine, rare findings can be highly significant. The method lacks a mechanism to recover such signals systematically.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An empirical study on datasets with known rare signals, perhaps via synthetic injection or expert annotation, measuring ComplLLM's recall of rare signals under varying Nτ. Adaptations that incorporate confidence intervals or Bayesian methods to handle sparsity could be evaluated.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** In settings where the recommending agent and supervisor are the same (e.g., AI-assisted human decision-making), how should ComplLLM be adapted to account for the learning and belief updating that occurs when the human supervisor reviews the complementary signals?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors note: "when the recommending agent and supervisor are the same, as in AI-assisted human decision workflows where a human first arrives at an independent judgment, then consults an AI, providing complementary signals may induce learning such that the predicted complementary value of signals no longer holds in hindsight. Future work could extend this by formalizing a continual learning problem that accounts for changes to human beliefs about the state after viewing the complementary signals and updates the LLM model correspondingly."
- **Why unresolved:** The current formulation assumes a static relationship between signals and the payoff state; after humans see signals, they may incorporate that information, changing the decision outcome. This dynamic effect is not captured in the static reward function, potentially limiting real-world effectiveness.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A user study where participants make decisions before and after seeing complementary signals, measuring how the value of signals changes over time. A modified framework that models human learning (e.g., using a dynamic Bayesian model) could be implemented and compared.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How robust is ComplLLM's reliance on a reference LLM (LLMref) for estimating the data-generating joint distribution? If the reference model is biased or inaccurate in its signal extraction, how does that propagate to the fine-tuned model's performance?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The method uses an LLM to first extract the signal space and then to label signals per instance (Section 4.1). The authors do not analyze sensitivity to errors in this reference model; they simply state they use it to estimate π. The fine-tuning and RL stages use the estimated distribution to compute rewards and generate training labels, so any systematic error could be amplified.
- **Why unresolved:** The choice of reference LLM may affect the quality of signal definitions and incidence estimates. In domains where LLMs have limited domain expertise or hallucinate, this could degrade the complementary value. The paper does not explore the impact of reference model quality or propose alternatives.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Experiments comparing different reference LLMs (e.g., larger models, domain-tuned models) or using human annotations as ground truth for the signal space. Measuring the variance in ComplLLM's extracted signals and complementary value across reference models would quantify sensitivity. Possibly developing a self-correcting framework that iteratively refines the signal space.

## Limitations
- Reference LLM–driven joint distribution estimation may be noisy; errors propagate to SFT labels and GRPO rewards, potentially biasing the model toward spurious signals.
- Frequency‑based filtering of the signal space risks discarding rare but valuable complementary cues, and the chosen thresholds (ε, δ, Nτ) are not thoroughly justified.
- Real‑task evaluation relies on post‑hoc linear models to assess complementary value; this may not capture complex interactions and assumes correct specification of the utility function.
- The method requires ground‑truth outcomes Y and an agent’s recommendation Z, limiting applicability to settings where both are observable.

## Confidence
- **Optimizing for best‑attainable payoff yields complementary signals.**
  Confidence: *Medium* (synthetic evidence strong; real‑task evidence depends on accurate π estimation).
- **SFT + GRPO pipeline is necessary and sufficient.**
  Confidence: *Medium* (ablation studies limited; GRPO adds value in synthetic but not fully isolated in real tasks).
- **Reference LLM can reliably construct π(s,z,y).**
  Confidence: *Low‑Medium* (no direct validation of the estimated joint distribution; indirect success only).

## Next Checks
1. **Validate the estimated posterior:** Hold out a test set, compute π(y|s,z) using the reference LLM and regression, then compare predicted probabilities to actual outcomes using calibration (Brier score) and discrimination (AUC). Significant deviation would indicate mis‑estimation.
2. **Ablate the GRPO stage:** Train SFT‑only models on the same data and compare complementary value (accuracy improvement) against the full ComplLLM on all three real tasks. A substantial drop would confirm GRPO’s contribution.
3. **Stress the signal space filter:** Systematically vary Nτ (e.g., from the 97.5% quantile down to a more permissive threshold) and measure performance. A steep decline when allowing more signals suggests that rare true complements are being dropped; recover them manually to see if they are indeed valuable.
