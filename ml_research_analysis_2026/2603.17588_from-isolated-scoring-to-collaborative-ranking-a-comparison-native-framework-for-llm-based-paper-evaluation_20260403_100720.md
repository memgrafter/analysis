# From Isolated Scoring to Collaborative Ranking: A Comparison-Native Framework for LLM-Based Paper Evaluation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.17588  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17588  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** CNPE-7B achieves a 21.8% average relative improvement over DeepReview-14B on ICLR-2025, leading in F1 (0.6732), AUC (0.7408), Spearman’s ρ (0.4091), MAP@20 (0.7076), and NDCG@20 (0.8153), with robust generalization to five unseen conferences.

## Executive Summary
LLM-based paper evaluation traditionally relies on isolated absolute scoring, which does not generalize across variable score scales. This paper introduces a comparison-native framework (CNPE) that reframes evaluation as a ranking problem. CNPE integrates graph-based pair sampling (GBR-BR) to select informative paper pairs with overlapping research contexts, a two-stage training pipeline of supervised fine-tuning on reasoning chains followed by reinforcement learning with comparison-based rewards (RLVR), and aggregation of pairwise preferences via the Bradley-Terry model. On the ICLR-2025 dataset, CNPE-7B outperforms DeepReview-14B by 21.8% average relative improvement across decision and ranking metrics. The framework also demonstrates strong generalization to five unseen conference datasets, establishing a new state-of-the-art in automated paper assessment.

## Method Summary
The framework operates on titles and abstracts to balance efficiency and information richness. For training, GBR-BR constructs a weighted sparse graph from bidirectional retrieval rankings: each paper’s top embedding neighbors are reranked, edges are weighted by reciprocal retrieval ranks, and connectivity is enforced (adjusting parameters if needed). The resulting pairs are filtered by score difference thresholds and occurrence limits. The base LLM (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) first undergoes supervised fine-tuning on pairwise comparison tasks, trained to output reasoning chains (pros/cons) generated by a larger model. Then RLVR fine‑tunes the model using GRPO, where the reward is a sparse binary signal indicating whether the model’s preference matches the ground-truth order derived from average human scores. During inference, a fraction of test‑set pairs is sampled via GBR‑BR; the trained model predicts pairwise preferences; these are aggregated by maximum likelihood estimation of the Bradley‑Terry model to produce a global ranking. The top 31.4% are accepted, mirroring the conference’s acceptance rate.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Graph-based pair sampling (GBR-BR) yields informative pairs
- **Claim:** GBR-BR generates more informative paper pairs than random or domain‑clustered sampling, enhancing the model’s ability to learn nuanced quality distinctions.
- **Mechanism:** Constructs a weighted sparse graph from bidirectional retrieval rankings; edges encode semantic similarity and prioritize overlapping research contexts; connectivity is enforced and noisy/overly similar pairs are filtered (via $d_{min}$).
- **Core assumption:** Papers with overlapping areas provide more meaningful comparative signals than randomly paired or strictly domain‑separated papers.
- **Evidence:** Algorithm 1 (Section 3.2) details the process; it ensures graph connectivity and weights favor shared context.
- **Break condition:** If repeated increases of $k_e,k_r$ fail to connect the graph, the sampling may not yield sufficient cross‑domain pairs, limiting generalization.

### Mechanism 2: SFT+RLVR aligns preference learning
- **Claim:** Supervised fine‑tuning with reasoning chains followed by RLVR with comparison‑based rewards yields more reliable and calibrated pairwise preferences than pointwise scoring or zero‑shot comparison.
- **Mechanism:** SFT cold‑starts the model with explicit comparative reasoning (pros/cons). RLVR (GRPO variant) optimizes for correct binary preferences via a sparse reward, avoiding score regression pitfalls; asymmetric clip thresholds ($\varepsilon_{low}=0.2, \varepsilon_{high}=0.28$) and removed KL loss stabilize policy updates.
- **Core assumption:** Training on preference outcomes learns a transferable relative‑quality function generalizing across scales and contexts.
- **Evidence:** Ablation (Table 2) shows both SFT and RL are necessary; removing either stage degrades performance significantly.
- **Break condition:** Overly strict filters ($d_{min}, c_{max}$) make training pairs too sparse/noisy; mis‑specified reward (e.g., noisy ground‑truth scores) misguides RL.

### Mechanism 3: Bradley‑Terry aggregation produces robust global ranking
- **Claim:** Aggregating pairwise preferences via Bradley‑Terry MLE yields a more stable and accurate global ranking than direct score prediction or graph‑centrality methods.
- **Mechanism:** Estimates latent quality scores $\theta_i$ by maximizing log‑likelihood of observed binary preferences, assuming transitivity; synthesizes many noisy pairwise comparisons into a coherent order.
- **Core assumption:** Transitivity holds approximately for paper quality judgments; MLE converges to a meaningful ranking from sparse comparisons.
- **Evidence:** Table 9 and Appendix F show MLE Bradley‑Terry consistently outperforms alternative aggregators (Eigenvector, PageRank, Thurstone‑Mosteller, Bayesian BT).
- **Break condition:** Intransitive cycles or strong position bias in predictions can distort $\theta_i$.

- **Concept:** Graph‑based sampling and connectivity constraints.  
  **Why needed:** GBR‑BR constructs a connected graph from semantic similarity to ensure every paper participates in meaningful comparisons, avoiding isolated nodes that yield no ranking signal.

- **Concept:** Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) variants, specifically GRPO.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Data Module: Corpus → Embedding model (Embed) → Reranker (Rerank) → GBR‑BR algorithm → filtered pair sets (Φ_train, Φ_infer)  
Training Module: Base LLM (Qwen2.5‑7B‑Instruct) → SFT cold‑start (reasoning chains) → RLVR (GRPO) → trained comparator  
Inference Module: Test papers → GBR‑BR pair sampling (fraction α) → trained LLM pairwise predictions (ŷ_ij) → Bradley‑Terry MLE → global ranking → acceptance threshold (31.4%)

**Critical path:**  
1. Pair sampling (GBR‑BR) must yield a connected graph with semantically meaningful edges; failure invalidates downstream steps.  
2. Training (SFT → RL) provides initial comparative reasoning and aligns preferences with ground‑truth score differences; both stages are required per ablation.  
3. Aggregation (Bradley‑Terry) converts pairwise outputs to ranking; alternative methods underperform (Table 9).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Metadata‑only vs. full text: reduced compute vs. potential loss of methodological details.  
- Sampling fraction (α) vs. number of pairs (n): larger α/n improves ranking but quadratic compute cost.  
- Similarity vs. random sampling: both needed; over‑reliance on either harms performance (Table 2).

**Failure signatures:**  
- Graph disconnected: GBR‑BR cannot connect all papers; check `is_connected` loop. Some papers never compared → ranking incomplete.  
- Positional bias persists: correlation between paper ID order and predicted rank percentiles remains.  
- RL does not improve over SFT: monitor reward; compare SFT‑only vs. SFT+RL on held‑out pairs.  
- Bradley‑Terry MLE fails to converge or yields extreme scores: check preference matrix sparsity; ensure minimum degree >1.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Validate GBR‑BR connectivity: run on a toy corpus (e.g., 50 papers). Vary $k_e$, $k_r$ and log connectivity attempts. Ensure graph becomes connected and edge weights correlate with embedding similarity.  
2. Ablate training pipeline: train three models – base only, SFT only, SFT+RL. Compare on validation set using Spearman’s ρ. Expect SFT+RL > SFT > base (Table 2).  
3. Compare aggregation methods: on fixed pairwise predictions, apply Eigenvector Centrality, PageRank, Thurstone‑Mosteller, and Bradley‑Terry (MLE). Compute MAP@20 and NDCG@20. Expect Bradley‑Terry (MLE) to win (Table 9).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question:** To what extent does the comparison‑native framework generalize to non‑computer science domains, given that the study only evaluates on AI/ML conference papers?
  **Basis in paper:** "Our experiments rely exclusively on computer science conference papers, which limits the generalizability of our findings."
  **Why unresolved:** The framework’s reliance on semantic similarity sampling and domain‑specific review criteria may not transfer to fields with different publication cultures, evaluation dimensions, or writing conventions.
  **What evidence would resolve it:** Train and evaluate CNPE on datasets from diverse fields (biology, physics, social sciences), measuring performance against human reviews and examining whether similarity‑based sampling remains effective.

- **Question:** How does the exclusion of full‑text content (using only titles/abstracts) impact the accuracy and fairness of pairwise quality judgments, especially for papers where key contributions are in methodology or experiments?
  **Basis in paper:** "Using only titles and abstracts significantly reduces computational cost... but it inevitably constraints information available in full papers, which may cause mild performance degradation."
  **Why unresolved:** The paper acknowledges potential bias against papers with strong technical content but weak abstracts but does not quantify this effect. The comparison‑native paradigm may be particularly sensitive to missing details.
  **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct experiments on subsets where full‑text analysis reveals critical distinctions not captured in abstracts, measuring divergences in rankings and analyzing error patterns by paper type.

- **Question:** Why does training‑time diversity (via mixed similarity and random sampling) yield greater performance gains than test‑time diversity, and what does this imply about the optimal balance between domain‑specific and cross‑domain comparisons during learning?
  **Basis in paper:** Ablation shows removing random sampling at training causes a 12.1% average drop, while at test the drop is only 1.1%.
  **Why unresolved:** The paper does not analyze why training‑time diversity is more beneficial; the interplay between exposure during learning and inference‑time coverage is unexplored.
  **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled analyses varying the ratio of similarity‑to‑random pairs during training, tracking calibration across domains, position bias, and noise robustness; also test if different aggregators exploit test‑time diversity differently.

- **Question:** What are the long‑term dynamics of using comparison‑native review systems on scientific publication ecosystems, particularly regarding reinforcement of prevailing paradigms and marginalization of unconventional research?
  **Basis in paper:** Ethical risks include "reinforcing biases... marginalize unconventional research... trained largely on top‑tier conference practices, its implicit standards may disadvantage unconventional research directions."
  **Why unresolved:** The paper notes the risk but does not study it empirically; the Bradley‑Terry aggregation and similarity‑based sampling might amplify consensus and penalize novelty.
  **What evidence would resolve it:** Simulate ecosystem effects by iteratively ranking papers with CNPE, then resampling future submissions based on accepted distributions; measure shifts in topic/method diversity over time and compare with human review trends; audit model preferences for high‑risk/high‑reward papers.

- **Question:** How does the design of the comparison‑based reward in reinforcement learning (using score differences as ground truth) interact with the inherent noise and subjectivity of human review scores, and could this introduce subtle biases?
  **Basis in paper:** The reward treats mean scores as verifiable labels without addressing their reliability.
  **Why unresolved:** Human scores are noisy, context‑dependent, and often inconsistent; thresholding continuous scores into binary preferences discards information and may amplify existing biases.
  **What evidence would resolve it:** Analyze reward consistency by comparing multiple reviewers’ binary preferences vs. consensus; measure model performance when training on high‑agreement vs. low‑agreement pairs; introduce probabilistic rewards (e.g., based on score variance) and evaluate stability.

## Limitations
- Exact DeepReview train‑test split and score normalization details are not provided.
- Critical model implementations (Qwen3 embedding/reranker), SFT prompt templates, and GRPO rollout settings remain unspecified.
- Evaluation is restricted to ICLR‑2025; generalizability to other domains and publication venues is unverified.
