# WASD: Locating Critical Neurons as Sufficient Conditions for Explaining and Controlling LLM Behavior

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.18474  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18474  
- **Reference count:** 12  
- **Primary result:** WASD significantly outperforms the Circuit Tracer baseline, achieving much higher precision (e.g., 91.92% vs. ~30‑50%), lower instability, and more concise explanations.

## Executive Summary
Large language models (LLMs) often require precise, low‑cost methods to influence their outputs without compromising semantic coherence. The WASD framework addresses this by identifying minimal sets of neuron‑activation predicates that serve as sufficient conditions for a target output, robust to input perturbations. Using prompt perturbation to generate candidate predicates and an attribution‑guided greedy search, WASD finds the smallest rule set that meets a high precision threshold (τ). Experiments on Gemma‑2‑2B with SST‑2 and CounterFact show WASD achieving over 90% precision, far surpassing the Circuit Tracer baseline (30‑50%), while producing more concise explanations. In a cross‑lingual control case study, WASD enforced Chinese generation with 89.4% success compared to 14.8% for Circuit Tracer and 60.4% for in‑context learning, while maintaining higher semantic similarity and lower perplexity. These results demonstrate WASD’s potential for explainable and controllable LLM behavior.

## Method Summary
WASD operates in two main phases. First, for a given input prompt *x*, it constructs a local neighborhood *N(x)* by applying neutral edit operations (e.g., prepending neutral prefixes). For each perturbed prompt *x'* in *N(x)*, it uses Circuit Tracer’s `WtExt` to obtain per‑neuron activations Φ_v(*x'*) and contributions C_v(*x'*). Each neuron *v* receives a candidate predicate with threshold λ·max_{x'∈N(x)} Φ_v(*x'*) and ranking score equal to mean_{x'∈N(x)} C_v(*x'*).  

Second, WASD sorts these candidate predicates by descending ranking score and performs a greedy search: starting with an empty rule, it tentatively adds predicates one by one only if they improve the rule’s precision, which is estimated by intervening (hard‑coding activations) on a sample from *N(x)* and checking if the output remains unchanged. The search stops when precision reaches the target τ (default 0.9). A pruning pass then removes any redundant predicates while preserving the precision threshold. The resulting minimal sufficient rule can be used for intervention, reliably enforcing the original output across perturbations.

## Key Results
- **Precision:** Achieved 91.92% on SST‑2 and CounterFact, dramatically higher than Circuit Tracer’s ~30‑50%.
- **Cross‑lingual control:** 89.4% success in generating Chinese text vs. 14.8% (Circuit Tracer) and 60.4% (in‑context learning), with superior semantic similarity (LaBSE 0.2867) and lower perplexity (250.79).
- **Explanation conciseness:** WASD identifies smaller neuron sets than top‑K Circuit Tracer selections, yielding more compact and stable explanations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Perturbation‑driven predicate generation
WASD creates candidate predicates by perturbing the input and analyzing neuron behavior across the perturbed set. A neighborhood *N(x)* is formed via neutral edits (e.g., deletions, replacements). For each perturbed *x'*, Circuit Tracer yields activations Φ_v and contributions C_v. Each neuron *v* gets a threshold λ·max_{x'∈N(x)} Φ_v and a ranking score mean_{x'∈N(x)} C_v. The core assumption is that neurons consistently high‑activating and contributing across neutral variants are functionally important. Risks arise if the perturbation set doesn’t cover the neuron’s true maximum activation, leading to thresholds that are too low; also, a large λ may overemphasize noise from a single perturbation.

### Mechanism 2: Greedy search for minimal sufficient rules
Candidates are sorted by descending ranking score. Starting from an empty rule, predicates are added one by one only if they improve the rule’s precision (estimated via intervention on sampled perturbations). The process stops when precision reaches τ, then pruning removes any redundant predicates. The greedy order assumes that higher‑contribution predicates are more likely to be part of a minimal sufficient set. Limitations include possible suboptimality if a low‑contribution predicate is needed early, and stochastic precision estimates due to finite samples may cause premature or delayed stopping.

### Mechanism 3: Intervention for precise control
Intervening on the neurons in the identified rule (by hard‑coding their activations to the predicate values) enforces the original output across the neighborhood. If the rule satisfies Prec(r, x) ≥ τ, then for any *x'* ∈ *N(x)*, the output token matches the original with probability ≥ τ. This relies on the rule being causally sufficient and the neighborhood capturing relevant variations. Failure can occur if the rule is not truly sufficient or if fixed activations conflict with other necessary computations, leading to incoherent outputs.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Perturbation Generator → Circuit Tracer Module → Predicate Constructor → Greedy Searcher → Precision Evaluator → Intervention Module  

**Critical path:**  
1. Generate *N(x)* (e.g., ~50 perturbed prompts).  
2. For each *x'* ∈ *N(x)*, run Circuit Tracer → (Φ_v, C_v) ∀v.  
3. Build candidate predicates *P*.  
4. Sort *P* by mean contribution.  
5. Initialize *r* = ∅; compute precision(∅).  
6. For *p* in *P*: if adding *p* improves precision, keep; else skip. Stop when precision ≥ τ.  
7. Prune: for each *p* ∈ *r*, if precision stays ≥ τ, delete.  
8. Output *r*.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **λ vs. τ:** Larger λ yields stricter thresholds (fewer predicates) but risks missing necessary neurons; τ controls sufficiency strength (higher τ → smaller, more precise rules but may require more predicates).  
- **Neighborhood size vs. cost:** More perturbations improve precision estimate stability but increase Circuit Tracer calls.  
- **Greedy optimality:** Local search may miss a smaller sufficient set if a low‑contribution predicate is necessary early on. Precision estimates are stochastic due to finite perturbations; with few samples, the stopping condition may be triggered prematurely or delayed. The neighborhood definition must be representative; otherwise, precision on unseen perturbations may collapse.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Precision on held‑out perturbations << τ (overfitting to sampled *N(x)*).  
- Instability (high Jaccard distance) when *N(x)* is regenerated (rule not robust).  
- Rule size grows linearly with τ without convergence (indicates insufficient discriminative predicates).  
- Intervention yields incoherent or off‑constraint outputs despite τ being met (neighborhood not capturing relevant variations).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Replication of Table 1: For SST‑2 and CounterFact, compute WASD rules on a fixed test set of prompts. Report precision, instability (Jaccard vs. neutral prefixes), and rule size; compare against Circuit Tracer Top‑K baselines.  
2. Cross‑lingual control (case study): Extract Chinese‑generation rules from Chinese prompts. Apply intervention to English CounterFact prompts. Measure FastText LID success rate, LaBSE similarity, and Qwen2.5‑7B perplexity.  
3. Hyperparameter sensitivity sweep: Vary λ ∈ [4.0, 8.0] and τ ∈ [0.8, 0.95] on a subset. Plot rule size and precision tradeoff to validate chosen defaults (λ=6.5, τ=0.9). Check for brittle regimes.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:** Can WASD scale effectively to much larger language models (>10B parameters) while maintaining precision and efficiency?  
*Basis:* Explicitly mentioned in conclusion as future work.  
*Why unresolved:* Experiments only on Gemma‑2‑2B; heuristic search and Circuit Tracer may not scale.  
*Resolution:* Replicate core experiments on a >10B model (e.g., Llama‑3‑70B) and compare metrics.

**Open Question 2:** To what extent is WASD’s identified rule set a globally optimal minimal sufficient condition versus locally optimal from greedy search?  
*Basis:* Limitations section admits heuristic approach cannot guarantee global optimality.  
*Why unresolved:* No ablation comparing to exhaustive search.  
*Resolution:* For tractable subsets, perform brute‑force search to find true minimal rules and compare to WASD’s output.

**Open Question 3:** How dependent is cross‑lingual control efficacy on the choice of external LLM evaluator?  
*Basis:* Case study uses an external LLM as evaluator; its biases not analyzed.  
*Why unresolved:* The rule is optimized for that evaluator; may overfit to its notion of “Chinese”.  
*Resolution:* Repeat extraction with multiple diverse evaluators (FastText, other LLMs, humans) and measure variance in neuron sets and intervention success.

**Open Question 4:** What is the fundamental reason behind WASD’s higher stability (lower Jaccard instability) compared to Circuit Tracer’s top‑K neurons?  
*Basis:* Results show higher stability but no dissection of why.  
*Why unresolved:* Unclear if stability stems from precision metric, pruning, or deeper property.  
*Resolution:* Correlate individual predicate stability with inclusion; disable pruning to isolate its effect.

**Open Question 5:** Are the neuron‑level sufficient conditions transferable across different model architectures?  
*Basis:* Method contingent on Circuit Tracer’s replacement model; not tested for transfer.  
*Why unresolved:* Same neuron indices in different models may not function similarly.  
*Resolution:* Identify rules in Model A and intervene on same indices in Model B; measure output change.

## Limitations
- Dependence on Circuit Tracer’s replacement model, whose training and fidelity are not detailed, introduces a black‑box dependency.
- Neighborhood construction (neutral prefixes) is underspecified and may not generalize to other tasks or models.
- Evaluations limited to small (2B) models and narrow tasks leave scalability to larger, more complex LLMs unproven.

## Confidence
