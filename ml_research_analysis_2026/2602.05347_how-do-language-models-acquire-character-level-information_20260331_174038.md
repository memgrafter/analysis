# How Do Language Models Acquire Character-Level Information?

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.05347
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05347
- Reference count: 10
- Primary result: Language models acquire character-level information via (1) BPE merge rule statistics, (2) orthographic constraints within words, (3) semantic bigram associations, and (4) syntactic affix functions.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates how language models (LMs) retain character-level information despite being trained exclusively on subword tokens. The authors design a systematic set of controlled pretraining experiments, manipulating tokenizers (BPE, WordPiece, and custom variants) and training corpora (including character-perturbed, word-substituted, lemmatized, and stemmed versions of a 10B-token English web corpus). A probing task—binary classification of whether a token contains a specific character—is used to measure the amount of character-level knowledge encoded in token embeddings. The results reveal that tokenization itself provides two major cues: the statistical regularities arising from BPE merge rule priorities and orthographic constraints within multi-subword words. Additionally, independent of tokenization, semantic associations of character bigrams and syntactic functions of inflectional/derivational affixes contribute significantly. The study thus dissects the multiple, partly overlapping mechanisms by which LMs implicitly learn character-level patterns from subword-only data.

## Method Summary
The research employs a probing framework with small-scale language models (nanoGPT, decoder-only; BERT-Tiny, encoder-only) trained on a 10B-token subset of the FineWeb corpus. To isolate the sources of character-level information, the authors apply three controlled data transformations: (1) CharPert replaces each alphabetic character with a random one, preserving case and token boundaries, thereby disrupting orthographic and semantic regularities while keeping merge statistics intact; (2) WordSub substitutes each subword with a random string of identical length, preserving the exact subword segmentation and thus merge rule regularities but destroying lexical semantics; (3) lemmatization and stemming remove affixal characters, targeting the syntactic cue from morphological markers. Tokenizer variants include standard BPE/WordPiece, a controlled 1–3 character BPE with shuffled merge priorities, and a fixed three-character segmentation (TCS) tokenizer. For each pretraining condition, the final-layer token embeddings are frozen and used to train a shallow MLP probe that predicts the presence of a given character. Positive and negative examples are length-matched to control for token-length biases. By comparing probing accuracy across conditions—e.g., natural vs. randomized merge rules, tokens with vs. without leading whitespace, and baseline vs. ablated corpora—the authors quantify the distinct contributions of merge rule statistics, orthographic transitions, semantic bigram clustering, and affixal syntax.

## Key Results
- BPE merge rule statistics contribute ~12.3 points: accurate character inclusion (62.4%) under a controlled tokenizer drops to random baseline (50.1%) when merge priorities are randomized.
- Orthographic constraints within words yield a large 30.2‑point gap: with a three‑character segmentation tokenizer, tokens without leading whitespace achieve 80.3% accuracy, while those with leading whitespace fall to 50.1%.
- Semantic bigram associations cause a ~3.9‑point drop when the probe is prevented from using preceding/following character context; syntactic affix information accounts for a 3.0‑point drop after lemmatization and a 3.9‑point drop after stemming.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: BPE Merge Rule Statistical Biases
- **Claim**:  
  The priority order of BPE merge rules creates statistical regularities at subword boundaries that correlate with character presence, even when linguistic meaning is destroyed.
- **Mechanism**:  
  Lower-priority merges cause more frequent splits between specific character pairs (e.g., `(β, X)` in `αβXγδ`). The model learns that when a subword ends with `αβ`, the next subword is more likely to start with certain characters `X` depending on the relative merge strengths `m(β, X)`, `m(X, γ)`. This boundary regularity indirectly signals whether a target character (e.g., the third character `X`) appears in the following subword.
- **Core assumption**:  
  Merge rule priorities are fixed after tokenizer training and applied deterministically during tokenization, creating predictable split patterns across the corpus.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "merge rules from BPE segmentation contribute significantly (62.4% accuracy under a controlled tokenizer vs. 50.1% with rules randomized)."
  - [Section 5.1.2]: Controlled tokenizer experiment achieves 62.4% accuracy on CharPert data; randomizing rule order drops accuracy to 50.1%.
  - [corpus]: Neighbor papers (e.g., "Broken Tokens? Your Language Model can Secretly Handle Non-Canonical Tokenizations") study robustness to non-canonical tokenizations, implying that boundary regularities from merge rules are learnable.
- **Break condition**:  
  If merge rule priorities are randomized or if all merges have equal priority, the boundary regularities vanish, and character inclusion accuracy converges to random baseline (~50%).

### Mechanism 2: Orthographic Transition Constraints within Words
- **Claim**:  
  Within a multi-subword word, orthographic patterns (e.g., `ck` after short vowels) constrain which characters can follow a given subword, enabling inference of characters in subsequent subwords.
- **Mechanism**:  
  For non-initial subwords (no leading whitespace), the preceding subword limits the space of likely character sequences. The model learns transition probabilities like `P(third_char | prefix_subword)` from the training corpus. The first subword of a word (with leading whitespace) lacks this constraint, making character prediction harder.
- **Core assumption**:  
  Natural language orthography imposes non-uniform transition probabilities between adjacent character sequences within a word, independent of semantics.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "orthographic constraints within words yield a 30.2-point accuracy gap."
  - [Section 5.2.2]: Three-character segmentation (TCS) tokenizer shows 80.3% accuracy for subwords without leading whitespace vs. 50.1% with leading whitespace (controlled for merge rules).
  - [corpus]: Neighbor "The Strawberry Problem" discusses failure on character-level tasks due to tokenization, highlighting that subword boundaries disrupt orthographic continuity.
- **Break condition**:  
  If all within-word character transitions are made equally probable (e.g., via character perturbation or random word substitution), the orthographic cue is eliminated, and accuracy drops to baseline for non-initial subwords.

### Mechanism 3: Semantic Bigram Clustering and Syntactic Affix Functions
- **Claim**:  
  Independent of tokenization, models exploit (a) semantic relatedness among words sharing character bigrams (sound symbolism) and (b) the functional load of inflectional/derivational affixes to infer character presence.
- **Mechanism**:  
  (a) Words with similar bigrams (e.g., `sl-` for sliding) cluster in embedding space; a probe can use the bigram context (previous/next character) as a cue. (b) Affixes like `-ed`, `-ly` have consistent syntactic roles, making their characters highly predictive. Removing these via lemmatization/stemming degrades probing accuracy.
- **Core assumption**:  
  Semantic fields are systematically associated with certain phoneme-grapheme patterns, and morphemes have discrete syntactic functions that are reflected in token embeddings.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "semantic associations of character bigrams (∼3.9-point drop when contextualized) and syntactic information from affixes (∼3.0-point drop after lemmatization, ∼3.9 after stemming)."
  - [Section 6.1.2]: Grouping tokens by preceding/following character reduces accuracy by ~3.5 points from 61.2% baseline, indicating bigram context matters.
  - [Section 6.2.2]: Lemmatization (-3.0) and stemming (-3.9) lower accuracy relative to baseline.
  - [corpus]: Neighbor "Small Language Models Also Work With Small Vocabularies" probes linguistic abilities with grapheme-based tokens, indirectly supporting that affix function aids substring awareness.
- **Break condition**:  
  If semantically related bigram groups are mixed uniformly in the training data (e.g., via word substitution) and all inflectional/derivational morphology is removed (lemmatized + stemmed), these cues vanish and accuracy approaches the tokenization-only baseline.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Tokenizer (BPE/WordPiece/TCS/controlled) → Pretraining Corpus (FineWeb + manipulated variants) → Language Model (nanoGPT/BERT-Tiny) → Frozen Token Embeddings → Probing Classifier (MLP) → Character Inclusion Accuracy
- **Critical path**:
  1. Train tokenizer on pretraining corpus (or define merge rules for controlled tokenizer).
  2. Apply tokenizer to corpus → subword sequences.
  3. Train LM on subword sequences (MLM or autoregressive).
  4. Extract final-layer token embeddings for vocabulary tokens.
  5. Train probe on embeddings → report accuracy per character / condition.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Small model vs. generalizability: Using nanoGPT/BERT-Tiny reduces compute but may not capture mechanisms in large models.
  - Corpus choice: FineWeb is high-quality web text; results may differ for conversational or code corpora.
  - Controlled vs. natural tokenizers: Controlled tokenizer isolates merge rules but lacks real-world frequency distributions.
  - Length matching: Necessary to avoid length confound but reduces natural token distribution variance.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Probe accuracy near 50% on CharPert + controlled tokenizer with randomized rules → merge rule mechanism failed.
  - No accuracy gap between leading-whitespace and non-leading-whitespace tokens in TCS → orthographic constraint mechanism failed.
  - No drop after lemmatization/stemming → syntactic affix cue not captured.
  - Probe accuracy high on very long tokens (≥7 chars) but low on short tokens (≤4) → over-reliance on token length heuristic rather than character patterns.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Replicate Table 4: Train on FineWeb, WordSub, CharPert with BPE vs. Word tokenization. Expect: CharPert+BPE > 50% (merge rules), WordSub drops vs. FineWeb (semantic cues).
  2. Controlled tokenizer test (Section 5.1): Use CharPert + controlled BPE, and its randomly substituted variant. Expect: ~62% vs. ~50%.
  3. Orthographic gap (Section 5.2): Use TCS tokenizer. Compare accuracy for tokens with/without leading `Ġ`. Expect: ~80% (no `Ġ`) vs. ~50% (with `Ġ`).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: What is the contribution of explicit lexical resources to character-level learning beyond tokenization and linguistic cues?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors admit they have not fully controlled for lexical information and its contribution remains uneliminated.
- **Why unresolved**: Current perturbations (WordSub, CharPert) do not fully isolate lexical resources from statistical cues.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Train LMs on corpora with and without explicit word lists or spelling rules, while holding other factors constant, and compare probing accuracy differences.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: How does model scale affect the relative importance of tokenization-derived versus independent factors for character-level learning?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors note: “the nanoGPT model used is relatively small; it remains unclear what cues contribute in larger models.”
- **Why unresolved**: Small models may not capture the same factor contributions as larger ones; scaling could change reliance on specific cues.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Run the same factor-isolation experiments (controlled tokenizers, WordSub/CharPert) on larger models (e.g., GPT-3) and compare accuracy drops.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Do the mechanisms of character-level acquisition generalize to languages with different orthographies and morphological complexity?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The paper states: “it remains an open question whether the mechanisms generalize to other languages with different orthographic systems, morphological complexity, or writing scripts.”
- **Why unresolved**: The study only examined English; orthographic constraints, tokenization behavior, and morphology vary widely across languages.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Apply identical experiments to multilingual datasets covering diverse scripts (e.g., Chinese, Arabic, Finnish) and probe character inclusion.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: How does pretraining corpus variation (domain, genre, quality) influence character-level information acquisition?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The limitations note: “we used only FineWeb … different pretraining corpora … could potentially yield different patterns.”
- **Why unresolved**: Corpora differ in spelling consistency, lexical diversity, noise, which may interact with the identified factors.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Train models on multiple corpora (news, social media, scientific) and compare factor contributions using the same analysis.

## Limitations
- Small model scale: All experiments used nanoGPT/BERT-Tiny; mechanisms might differ in larger LMs.
- Controlled tokenizer limitations: Simulated tokenizers (e.g., fixed 3-char segmentation) isolate cues but may not generalize to natural BPE/WordPiece.
- Corpus and language specificity: Results on English FineWeb may not apply to other domains or languages with different orthographies.

## Confidence
- BPE merge rule statistical biases: High
- Orthographic transition constraints within words: High
- Semantic bigram clustering & syntactic affix functions: Medium
- General transfer to large LMs: Low
