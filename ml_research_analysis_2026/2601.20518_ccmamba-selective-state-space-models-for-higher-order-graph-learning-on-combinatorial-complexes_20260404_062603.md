# CCMamba: Selective State-Space Models for Higher-Order Graph Learning on Combinatorial Complexes

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.20518
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.20518
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: A linear‑complexity Mamba‑based framework for higher‑order graph learning on combinatorial complexes, achieving state‑of‑the‑art accuracy with up to 85% memory reduction.

## Executive Summary
CCMamba introduces a unified state‑space modeling approach to overcome the quadratic complexity and limited long‑range dependency modeling of attention‑based topological deep learning on higher‑order structures. By reformulating message passing on combinatorial complexes as selective state‑space processes, the method linearizes multi‑rank incidence relations into rank‑aware sequences processed by bidirectional SSMs (BiMamba). This enables adaptive directional propagation in linear time while preserving geometric fidelity across ranks. The architecture integrates intra‑neighborhood SSM filtering, inter‑neighborhood rank‑level aggregation, and deep residual refinement. Extensive experiments on graph‑ and node‑level classification benchmarks show CCMamba outperforms attention‑based and other topological baselines, reduces GPU memory by up to 85% and training time by 88% relative to GAT, and remains robust to depth. Theoretical analysis bounds its expressive power by the 1‑dimensional combinatorial complex Weisfeiler–Lehman test, providing a clear characterization.

## Method Summary
CCMamba operates on combinatorial complexes (CCs) that unify graphs, hypergraphs, and simplicial complexes. The input graph is lifted to a CC via a parameterized procedure (Algorithm 1) that discovers higher‑order cells (cliques, cycles) up to a maximum length \(L\). Incidence matrices \(B_1\) (node‑edge) and \(B_2\) (edge‑face) encode neighborhood relations. Each CCMamba layer processes cells rank‑wise. For a given rank \(k\), the method first constructs a structured sequence by concatenating the features of cells in that rank with signals from incident neighbors (e.g., nodes with edges via \(B_1\) or edges with faces via \(B_2\)). These sequences are projected and fed through a bidirectional selective SSM block (BiMamba), which adaptively filters information in both forward and reverse directions. After intra‑rank updates, inter‑rank aggregation fuses cross‑rank context: each cell pools features from its incident neighbors in adjacent ranks, combines them via an MLP, and sums the result. The aggregated output is added residually, passed through LayerNorm and a feed‑forward network to produce the final cell embeddings for that layer. The process repeats over multiple layers, with rank‑specific linear projections, SSMs, and MLPs accounting for heterogeneity. This design achieves linear time complexity in the number of cells per rank, avoids pairwise attention, and adapts directional propagation to the topology.

## Key Results
- **State‑of‑the‑art accuracy** on multiple benchmarks (e.g., 85.11% on MUTAG, 89.22% on Cora) for both graph‑ and node‑level tasks.
- **Massive efficiency gains**: up to 85% reduction in GPU memory and 88% reduction in training time compared to GAT.
- **Linear complexity and depth robustness**: Computational cost scales linearly with the number of cells; performance remains stable even with many layers, unlike many deep GNNs that suffer from over‑smoothing.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Replacing attention‑based aggregation with selective state‑space modeling reduces computational complexity from quadratic to linear while preserving long‑range dependency modeling.
- **Mechanism**: The SSM processes sequences via input‑dependent state transitions: \(h_t = \widehat{A} h_{t-1} + \widehat{B} x_t\), \(y_t = C h_t\), where parameters are functions of the input. This allows adaptive information filtering along the sequence.
- **Core assumption**: The linearized neighborhood sequence retains sufficient topological structure for message passing; the SSM can dynamically attend to relevant positions without pairwise attention.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - Abstract: “CCMamba reformulates higher‑order message passing on combinatorial complexes as selective state‑space modeling… enabling adaptive, directional, and long‑range information propagation in linear time without self attention.”
  - Section IV.B: “Each Mamba block is formulated as a learnable linear‑time recurrent filter… This formulation yields stable propagation dynamics for each rank, with linear‑time complexity in the number of cells.”
  - Corpus: Related works (e.g., Graph‑Mamba, Topological Deep Learning with State‑Space Models) explore SSMs for structured data, but CCMamba uniquely integrates them with combinatorial complex neighborhoods.
- **Break condition**: If the sequence linearization discards critical topological cues (e.g., incidence directions), the SSM may fail to capture necessary relational patterns.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Bidirectional SSM (BiMamba) processes rank‑aware sequences from both forward and reverse directions, preserving geometric fidelity of incidence relations despite arbitrary linearization.
- **Mechanism**: For each rank, two SSMs process the sequence in opposite orders; their outputs are combined to produce direction‑aware representations. This captures both incoming and outgoing topological flows.
- **Core assumption**: The non‑causal neighborhood of a CC can be linearized into a sequence such that bidirectional traversal covers all incident relations without loss.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - Section IV.C: “To guarantee that information propagation is robust to this linearization, CCMamba integrates a bidirectional state‑space module (BiMamba). This ensures that message passing captures structural dependencies from both directions, preserving the geometric fidelity of the incidence relations.”
  - Section IV.C, Equation (8) explicitly applies BiMamba to concatenated features \([h_k, incident\_neighbors]\) for each rank.
  - Corpus: Bidirectional processing is standard in sequence models; the adaptation for topological neighborhoods is novel in CCMamba.
- **Break condition**: Extremely long sequences may cause state collapse in one direction; also, if the sequence ordering strongly biases one direction, reverse processing may not fully compensate.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Inter‑neighborhood rank‑level aggregation via incidence operators fuses cross‑rank contextual signals, explicitly preserving hierarchical geometry of the CC.
- **Mechanism**: After intra‑rank SSM updates, each cell aggregates features from neighboring cells of adjacent ranks (e.g., an edge aggregates from its incident nodes and faces). This uses the boundary/coboundary incidence matrices to pool \(\sum_{r\in R(\sigma)} Combine(h_{intra,\sigma}, h_{intra,neighbors})\) then applies an MLP.
- **Core assumption**: Incidence relations (lower/upper star) are the natural way to propagate information across ranks in a CC; summing pooled neighbor features is sufficient for fusion.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - Section IV.D: “Following the rank‑wise selective state‑space propagation… CCMamba aggregates information across different topological ranks to generate contextually enriched embeddings for each cell.”
  - Section IV.D, Equation (9) formalizes the aggregation: \(h_k^{l+1} = \phi_1(h_k^l, MLP(\sum_{r\in R(\sigma)} Combine(...)))\).
  - Corpus: Other TDL models (e.g., CCNNs, TopoTune) use similar multi‑stage aggregation, but CCMamba integrates it with SSM outputs.
- **Break condition**: Sparse or noisy incidence relations may introduce irrelevant neighbors; the summation may dilute discriminative signals if rank adjacency is large.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Input → Lifting (Algorithm 1) → Incidence matrices \(B_1, B_2\) → For each layer: (Linear projection → BiMamba per rank → Intra‑rank output) → Inter‑rank aggregation (summation over incident ranks) → Residual + LayerNorm + FFN → Output cell embeddings → Task head (pooling/classification).
- **Critical path**:
  1. Construct CC from raw graph (choose lifting parameters: max rank \(K\), max cell length \(L\)).
  2. For each layer, for each rank \(k\): concatenate \([h_k, B_k h_{k+1}]\) or \([h_k, B_k^T h_{k-1}]\) depending on direction, then apply linear projection and BiMamba.
  3. For each cell, aggregate neighbor features from adjacent ranks using incidence matrices and combine via MLP and summation.
  4. Add residual and pass through FFN with layer norm.
  5. After \(L\) layers, readout (node/graph) for task.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - *Max cell length*: Larger captures higher‑order cliques but may fragment sequences; smaller loses global structure.
  - *SSM state dimension*: Small limits memory for long‑range dependencies; large increases parameters and may overfit.
  - *Number of heads*: More heads improve capacity but fragment per‑head signal; optimal varies by topology (graph vs. simplex).
  - *Rank input*: Including higher ranks (0,1,2) adds expressive power but increases sequence length; may be unnecessary for some tasks.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Performance degrades sharply if max cell length is poorly tuned (see Figure 4(b)).
  - On deep layers, if SSM state dimension is too small, over‑smoothing may reoccur despite residual connections (though robustness is high).
  - For simplex topology, accuracy can collapse with many heads (Table VII).
  - Memory/time scales linearly with total cells; extremely large CCs may still be prohibitive if state dimension is huge.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Complexity validation**: Run CCMamba and GAT on a dataset like PROTEINS, measure GPU memory and training time vs. number of cells; confirm linear vs. quadratic scaling.
  2. **Bidirectional ablation**: Replace BiMamba with unidirectional Mamba (forward only) and compare performance on a dataset where directionality matters (e.g., Roman Empire). Expect drop due to loss of reverse propagation.
  3. **Inter‑neighborhood ablation**: Remove the cross‑rank aggregation (only use intra‑rank SSM updates) and evaluate on MUTAG; expect significant drop as higher‑order interactions are lost.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: Can the expressive power of CCMamba exceed the proven 1‑CCWL bound, or is this bound inherent to any selective state‑space model applied to combinatorial complexes?
- **Basis in paper**: Theorem 1 establishes that the expressive power of CCMamba is upper‑bounded by the 1‑dimensional combinatorial complex Weisfeiler–Lehman test.
- **Why unresolved**: The theorem holds for the current architecture and its injectivity assumptions; it remains unknown whether alternative SSM‑based designs could break this bound, e.g., by incorporating higher‑dimensional WL tests or non‑linear readouts.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Either a proof that all linear‑time SSM‑based message passing schemes are bounded by 1‑CCWL, or the construction of a concrete Mamba‑variant that distinguishes pairs of complexes where 1‑CCWL fails.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: How does CCMamba perform on extremely large‑scale combinatorial complexes (e.g., millions of cells) and at higher maximum ranks (e.g., 3‑cells and beyond)?
- **Basis in paper**: The paper demonstrates linear time complexity and reports memory savings on standard benchmarks, but all datasets are relatively small (tens of thousands of cells) and the implementation only handles up to rank 2.
- **Why unresolved**: Real‑world higher‑order structures (e.g., scientific collaboration hypergraphs, molecular dynamics) often contain many more cells and higher‑dimensional facets; the scalability of the incidence‑aware SSM pipeline under such conditions has not been tested.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Empirical evaluation on massive synthetic or real higher‑order graphs, including measurements of memory usage, computation time, and potential bottlenecks (e.g., sequence construction) as the number of cells and maximum rank increase.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Can CCMamba be extended to model temporal or streaming combinatorial complexes where the incidence structure evolves over time?
- **Basis in paper**: The current framework assumes static combinatorial complexes; all experiments are on fixed graphs lifted into fixed complexes.
- **Why unresolved**: Many applications (e.g., evolving social networks, dynamic traffic systems) involve time‑varying higher‑order interactions. The ability to process sequences of complexes without re‑computing the entire structure is crucial for online learning.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A temporal variant of CCMamba that integrates state‑space updates across time steps, validated on dynamic graph benchmarks (e.g., TGB or evolving hypergraph datasets), showing improved accuracy or efficiency compared to re‑training from scratch.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: To what extent does the choice of lifting hyperparameters (e.g., maximum cell length, number of ranks) affect model quality, and can these be learned automatically rather than manually tuned?
- **Basis in paper**: The methodology describes a lifting operation (Algorithm 1) with parameters like maximum cell length L, and a sensitivity analysis (Figure 4b) shows performance varies dramatically with these settings.
- **Why unresolved**: The experiments manually set these parameters per dataset; a practitioner without extensive validation may obtain sub‑optimal complexes, limiting the model’s practical usability and generalizability.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Development of a differentiable lifting module that optimizes cell construction end‑to‑end, or a meta‑learning approach that predicts suitable lifting settings from graph statistics, demonstrated to reduce the need for manual tuning while maintaining or improving accuracy.

## Limitations
- **Lifting specification gaps**: The algorithm for identifying cells (cliques, cycles) and translating max cell length \(L\) into combinatorial structures is not fully described, impeding faithful replication and potentially altering topological features.
- **BiMamba fusion ambiguity**: The exact mechanism for merging forward and reverse SSM states (e.g., concatenation, summation) and the definition of the `Combine` function in inter‑neighborhood aggregation are underspecified, leaving critical implementation choices open.
- **Sequence order sensitivity**: The linearization order of neighborhood sequences for SSM processing is not detailed; since SSMs are order‑dependent, this could significantly affect performance and may require careful engineering.
