# Boule or Baguette? A Study on Task Topology, Length Generalization, and the Benefit of Reasoning Traces

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.14404
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14404
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Reasoning traces (RTs) improve generalization on broad, shallow tasks but underperform direct prediction (DP) on narrow, deep tasks, with theoretical scaling showing RT depth scalability limited by signal dilution (∼√H vs. linear) while breadth scaling is comparable.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the impact of incorporating intermediate reasoning traces (RTs) on length generalization in neural sequence models. It introduces a task topology framework distinguishing two axes: depth (number of reasoning steps) and breadth (number of distinct examples/symbols). Using the large-scale PITA dataset of propositional logic proofs in Lean and a synthetic transitive inference (TI) task, the authors compare models trained with explicit reasoning traces (RT) against those trained for direct prediction (DP). Results reveal a clear dichotomy: RT models excel on broad, shallow (“boule”) tasks, where generalization across many examples is required, while DP models perform better on narrow, deep (“baguette”) tasks that involve long chains of reasoning. Theoretical analysis based on max-margin principles explains these findings as an inductive bias: RTs enforce structured enumeration that aids breadth generalization but suffer from signal dilution as trace length increases, limiting depth scalability (∼√H vs. linear). The work provides both empirical and theoretical evidence for how task topology interacts with training format.

## Method Summary
The study contrasts two training paradigms: reasoning traces (RT), which include intermediate logical steps (e.g., Lean tactics), and direct prediction (DP), which maps inputs directly to a success/failure token. For empirical evaluation, two reasoning tasks are used: (1) PITA, a diverse corpus of propositional logic proofs (23M+ statements) split by proof depth and breadth into “boule” (FULL, IMPLY, OR) and “baguette” (PHP) conditions; (2) a controlled transitive inference (TI) task parametrized by breadth B (number of symbol branches) and depth D (chain length). Models are fine-tuned via QLoRA on large language models (Qwen2.5, Gemma3, Llama3) for PITA, and small single-layer Transformers (no positional encodings) for TI. Training examples are truncated around the median proof length; generalization is measured on longer test instances. The TI theoretical analysis assumes uniform attention and Gaussian embeddings, deriving max-margin solutions to explain observed scaling laws.

## Key Results
- RT models significantly outperform DP on broad, shallow tasks (boule splits), while DP surpasses RT on narrow, deep tasks (baguette splits).
- Theoretical max-margin analysis reveals RTs provide an inductive bias that improves breadth generalization, but suffer from signal dilution in long contexts: RT depth scalability is limited to ∼√H (H = hidden dimension), whereas DP scales linearly.
- Breadth scaling is similar for both paradigms (B ∝ H²), but DP accuracy decays as B⁻², whereas RT maintains higher accuracy; however, when breadth becomes extremely large relative to model width (B ≫ H²), RT generalization also deteriorates.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Inductive Bias via Structured Enumeration
- **Claim:** Reasoning traces (RTs) provide an inductive bias that improves generalization on broad, shallow ("boule") tasks by reducing overfitting to spurious correlations in the training distribution.
- **Mechanism:** RTs enforce a structured intermediate computation (enumeration of symbols/branches) that directly mirrors the proof topology. This biases the model toward solutions that transfer to new examples via permutations, as the model lacks positional encodings and is invariant to input order up to the query token.
- **Core assumption:** The max-margin solution under sample-coordinate representation yields sign uniformity within task branches for positive activations, and uniform attention ensures learned features depend only on the set of input symbols, not their order.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states RTs improve breadth generalization; Section 3.3 shows RT accuracy remains stable as breadth B increases (Fig. 3b) while DP decays as B⁻²; Section B.4.1 reports DP high false negative rates on longer distances; Appendix B.3.2 shows RT decay when B >> H².
- **Break condition:** When task breadth is very large relative to model width (B >> H²), sample coordinates become too sparse to maintain sign uniformity; generalization decays even for RTs.

### Mechanism 2: Long-Context Signal Dilution
- **Claim:** RT performance degrades on deep, narrow ("baguette") tasks because the required long reasoning trace dilutes the relevant signal (whether the query symbol belongs to the enumerated set), making the classification statistically harder.
- **Mechanism:** As depth D increases, RT input length L = Θ(D) grows. The positive class signal becomes O(1/L) weaker relative to the negative class baseline. Formally, total variation distance between positive/negative distributions is O(√H/L), bounding classification accuracy.
- **Core assumption:** Symbol embeddings are i.i.d. Gaussian, and the model’s decision depends on the aggregated sum of embeddings (uniform attention).
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract notes depth scales as √H for RT vs. linear for DP; Section 3.3 shows RT accuracy decays with depth following √H scaling (Fig. 3c); Section B.3.1 derives statistical indistinguishability; Figure 10 shows RT errors on PHP dominated by non-termination.
- **Break condition:** If the model had explicit symbolic membership operators or positional encodings, dilution might be mitigated; otherwise depth scaling remains sublinear.

### Mechanism 3: Spurious Correlation Overfitting
- **Claim:** Direct prediction (DP) models overfit to spurious correlations in broad training distributions, leading to brittle generalization that fails when test examples require longer inference chains.
- **Mechanism:** DP models learn shortcuts by exploiting statistical patterns in the training set (e.g., negative examples with distance ≤ k show small/negative sums among nearby symbols). This pattern breaks for distances > k, causing false negatives. For breadth increases, the logit margin scales as O(B) negative, degrading accuracy as B⁻².
- **Core assumption:** The max-margin solution for DP weights allocates positive/negative symbols per branch but without enumeration cannot guarantee consistent sign patterns for unseen distances.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 2.3 shows DP underperformance on boule splits; Section B.4 shows false negative rate → 1 for test distances > k (Fig. 7); Section B.2 reveals bimodal positive/negative proportions in DP weight sample coordinates (Fig. 5).
- **Break condition:** If training distribution matches test distribution (same distance bounds), overfitting may be avoided, but for length generalization the shift is inherent.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Max-margin theory in neural networks**
  *Why needed here:* Explains the normative solution structure (sign uniformity, sample coordinate distribution) that RT/DP models converge to, linking training dynamics to generalization.
  *Quick check question:* Why does the max-margin solution require half the branches to have uniformly positive sample coordinates for positive readout units?
- **Concept: Sample-coordinate representation**
  *Why needed here:* Allows analyzing weight vectors in the span of training embeddings, revealing how models allocate capacity across task branches/depths.
  *Quick check question:* Under what condition does 1/H w·x approximate the sample coordinate c?
- **Concept: Total variation distance for classification bounds**
  *Why needed here:* Provides a statistical limit on the RT model’s ability to distinguish positive/negative classes as enumeration length grows.
  *Quick check question:* How does TV(T,F) scale with hidden dimension H and context length L?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Input formatting (PITA: XML tactics/states; TI: symbol pairs + optional enumeration) → Tokenization (preserve special tokens) → Transformer (no positional encodings; uniform attention in TI models) → Output head (generates `<success/>`/`<failure/>`). RT path processes full trace before classification; DP path classifies immediately.

**Critical path:**
1. **Data generation:** For PITA, generate propositional statements → focused proof search → Lean translation. For TI, sample symbol branches/depths → construct pairs.
2. **Formatting:** Insert XML delimiters, separate prompt/completion with `||`.
3. **Finetuning:** QLoRA on next-token prediction; only completion tokens contribute loss. Typical hyperparameters: LR=2e-4, batch=32, 2500 steps.
4. **Evaluation:** Autoregressive generation until classification token; measure accuracy on test lengths > training median.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- RT vs. DP: RTs impose longer sequences (higher memory/compute) but improve breadth generalization; DP is efficient but brittle on broad tasks.
- Task split design: Boule (broad/shallow) favors RT; baguette (narrow/deep) favors DP. Choose based on expected deployment distribution.
- Trace length: Longer traces help breadth but hurt depth due to signal dilution; truncation harms RT performance (Fig. 11).

**Failure signatures:**
- RT on deep tasks: Non-termination (exceeding context limit) or incorrect classification after long trace (PHP errors dominated by non-termination).
- DP on broad tasks: High false positive/negative rates on out-of-distribution lengths (e.g., false negatives on longer distances).
- Both: Degradation when model width H is too small relative to task parameters (B ∝ H², D ∝ √H for RT).

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Replicate boule/baguette split comparison: Train RT/DP on PITA OR (boule) vs PHP (baguette) for 1B-7B models; measure generalization gap vs. test proof length.
2. Verify attention uniformity: Train minimal TI model (single-layer, single-head), compute total variation distance of attention weights per position; expect ≈0.
3. Test scaling predictions: For TI, sweep breadth B at fixed depth D for RT/DP; fit accuracy decay to B⁻² for DP. For depth D at fixed B, fit D scaling to H vs. √H.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
What is the precise relationship between reasoning trace length, signal dispersion in the context, and generalization performance? The theoretical analysis shows RT accuracy bound 1/2 + O(√H/D), suggesting dilution, but empirical validation across tasks and architectures is lacking. Evidence: measurement of context length, information concentration, and accuracy across controlled signal dispersion tasks.

### Open Question 2
Do the observed advantages of reasoning traces for breadth-dominated tasks extend to tasks with both high breadth and high depth (the "diagonal" case)? The paper studies pure breadth and pure depth separately; the interaction remains unknown. Evidence: create tasks varying both depth and breadth systematically and map RT vs. DP performance surface.

### Open Question 3
Why do direct prediction models fail to generalize on breadth-dominated tasks despite being free from long-context issues? The max-margin analysis suggests DP “overfits to the training distribution” and finds “suboptimal minima,” but the nature of these minima is not fully characterized. Evidence: analyze decision boundaries of DP vs RT on breadth tasks via probing or representation analysis.

### Open Question 4
Can modifications to training objectives or data augmentation strategies improve DP model generalization on breadth-dominated tasks without requiring RTs? RTs help but may be costly; if DP limitations are not fundamental, better training might suffice. Evidence: train DP on breadth tasks with regularization, curriculum, or augmentations designed for robust feature learning.

### Open Question 5
How do the findings generalize to multi-step reasoning tasks that require non-linear, branching, or recursive reasoning structures beyond linear chains? PITA and TI involve essentially linear chains; real-world tasks often have complex graph-like structures. Evidence: extend paradigm to tasks with tree-like, graph traversal, or multi-path reasoning and test if boule/baguette pattern persists.

## Limitations
- Unspecified PITA generation parameters (e.g., proof search depth, beam size) may cause train/test split distributions to differ from reported.
- µP implementation details (scaling factors) not fully described, affecting theoretical scaling predictions (B ∝ H², D ∝ √H).
- Core assumptions (uniform attention, i.i.d. Gaussian embeddings, no positional encodings) may not hold exactly in fine-tuned LLMs, limiting applicability of mechanisms.
- Low external corroboration: average neighbor FMR 0.406, zero citations—findings lack independent replication.

## Confidence
- RT improves breadth generalization on boule tasks: Medium (empirical support from Fig. 3b but relies on uniform attention and order invariance).
- RT limits depth generalization on baguette tasks: Medium-High (theoretical TV bound matches √H scaling in Fig. 3c; signal dilution plausible).
- Direct prediction overfits to spurious correlations: Medium (false-negative analysis and B⁻² decay consistent, but assumes max-margin convergence).
