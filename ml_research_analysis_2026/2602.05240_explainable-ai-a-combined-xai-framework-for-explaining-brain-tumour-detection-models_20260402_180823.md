# Explainable AI: A Combined XAI Framework for Explaining Brain Tumour Detection Models
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.05240
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05240
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Custom CNN with integrated XAI framework (GRAD-CAM, LRP, SHAP) achieved 91.24% test accuracy on BraTS 2021 and provided layered explanations for brain tumor detection.

## Executive Summary
Deep learning models for brain tumour detection often suffer from a "black box" nature, which impedes clinical trust. This study trained a custom convolutional neural network on the BraTS 2021 dataset, reaching a test accuracy of 91.24%. To improve interpretability, the authors combined three explainable AI techniques—GRAD-CAM, LRP, and SHAP—into a unified framework. The integrated approach delivered multi-level explanations (regional focus, pixel-level relevance, feature quantification) and reportedly offered superior explanatory power for borderline cases compared to any single method. While the results are promising, the claims remain unvalidated by external benchmarks or clinical user studies, limiting confidence in real-world deployment.

## Method Summary
The authors developed a custom CNN architecture tailored for brain tumour detection using the BraTS 2021 dataset. After training the model to optimal performance, they applied three post‑hoc XAI methods: GRAD-CAM to generate coarse activation maps, LRP to produce pixel‑level relevance scores, and SHAP to quantify feature contributions. These explanations were integrated into a single framework intended to give clinicians a more complete understanding of the model’s decisions. The paper does not detail the exact fusion mechanism or implementation specifics, but the core idea is that the complementary perspectives enhance transparency, particularly for ambiguous cases.

## Key Results
- The custom CNN achieved 91.24% test accuracy on the BraTS 2021 dataset.
- The integrated XAI framework produced layered explanations that successfully identified both full and partial tumours.
- For borderline cases, the combined framework delivered superior explanatory power relative to any single XAI method alone.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The combined XAI framework leverages the complementary strengths of its constituent techniques. GRAD-CAM quickly highlights the most discriminative image regions, directing attention to potential tumour areas. LRP goes deeper by assigning relevance to individual pixels, clarifying fine‑grained details. SHAP adds a quantitative layer, computing Shapley values to measure each feature’s marginal contribution to the prediction. Together, these perspectives create a more holistic and robust explanation, especially in challenging scenarios where a single method might be ambiguous. The paper attributes the improved explanatory power to this multi‑faceted analysis, though a formal empirical comparison is not provided.

## Foundational Learning
- **GRAD-CAM**  
  *Why needed:* To visualize class‑specific activation maps without modifying the model architecture.  
  *Quick check:* Ensure the highlighted regions align with tumour annotations from experts.

- **Layer‑wise Relevance Propagation (LRP)**  
  *Why needed:* To propagate prediction relevance back to input pixels, providing fine‑grained attribution.  
  *Quick check:* Verify that high relevance scores concentrate on tumour tissue in the MRI.

- **SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)**  
  *Why needed:* To quantify each feature’s contribution to the output in a theoretically grounded way.  
  *Quick check:* Compare SHAP values against known tumour biomarkers; positive values should correspond to tumour‑indicative features.

- **BraTS Dataset**  
  *Why needed:* A standardized benchmark for brain tumour segmentation and detection that enables reproducibility and comparison.  
  *Quick check:* Confirm that preprocessing (e.g., normalization, resampling) follows the BraTS challenge protocol.

- **Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)**  
  *Why needed:* Well‑suited for image tasks due to hierarchical spatial feature learning.  
  *Quick check:* Validate model generalisation on a held‑out set to avoid overfitting.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component Map** (plain text):  
  MRI Input → Preprocessing → Custom CNN → Tumor probability.  
  In parallel: CNN → GRAD‑CAM → heatmap; CNN → LRP → pixel relevance map; CNN → SHAP → feature importance values.  
  The three explanation streams are combined into a unified dashboard for clinician review.

- **Critical Path**:  
  1. Load and preprocess BraTS MRI scans.  
  2. Train the custom CNN to detect tumours.  
  3. For each test image, obtain the model’s prediction.  
  4. Generate GRAD‑CAM heatmap.  
  5. Compute LRP attribution.  
  6. Estimate SHAP values (computationally intensive).  
  7. Integrate the three explanations into a single output.  
  8. Present the combined explanation to the end‑user.

- **Design Tradeoffs**:  
  - *Computational cost vs. richness*: Running three XAI methods increases inference time but yields more comprehensive insights.  
  - *Complexity vs. usability*: Presenting multiple explanation types may overwhelm users; careful UI design is required.  
  - *Generality vs. fidelity*: SHAP is model‑agnostic and approximate, while LRP and GRAD‑CAM are model‑specific; balancing trust in their outputs is challenging.

- **Failure Signatures**:  
  - Inconsistent heatmaps (e.g., GRAD‑CAM and LRP highlight disjoint regions) indicating model instability or erroneous explanations.  
  - High variance in SHAP values across perturbed samples, suggesting unreliable feature importance estimates.  
  - Excessive runtime, especially from SHAP, leading to timeouts or impractical deployment.

- **Three First Experiments**:  
  1. *Baseline model evaluation*: Train the CNN on BraTS 2021, tune hyperparameters, and report accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity on the test set.  
  2. *Individual XAI assessment*: Apply each XAI method separately on a diverse test subset; have domain experts judge the plausibility of the explanations.  
  3. *Combined framework demonstration*: Integrate the three XAI outputs into a dashboard, test on borderline cases, and collect expert feedback on utility compared to single‑method explanations.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The provided summary does not indicate that the authors explicitly raised open questions. However, the study's limitations and the proposed next checks imply several important questions for future research:
- How does the model perform on external datasets from different institutions and scanner manufacturers?

## Limitations
- The reported 91.24% test accuracy lacks external validation against the official BraTS 2021 leaderboard or an independent hold‑out set, limiting reproducibility confidence.
- The claim of superior explanatory power for borderline cases is qualitative; no quantitative metrics or statistical comparisons against single XAI methods are presented.
- The study does not address model robustness to variations in scanner manufacturers, acquisition protocols, or population shifts, nor does it include clinical user evaluation, undermining claims of enhanced transparency and reliability.
