# Mechanistically Interpreting Compression in Vision-Language Models
## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.25035
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25035
- **Reference Count:** 17
- **Primary Result:** Wanda pruning preserves circuit topology but rotates/attenuates features, causing larger utility drops and severe safety degradation (genuine refusal drops from 39% to 22% at 50% sparsity due to incoherent outputs); INT4 quantization alters circuits more but retains feature alignment, preserving refusal rates better (33.9% at 4‑bit).

## Executive Summary
Vision‑language models (VLMs) are often compressed for efficiency, yet the internal consequences and safety implications remain poorly understood. This paper mechanistically compares two common compression methods—pruning (via Wanda) and quantization (INT4/NF4)—on BLIP‑VQA and LLaVA models. Using Edge Activation Patching and crosscoder feature comparisons, they find that pruning retains the overall circuit structure (high Jaccard/Spearman similarity) but rotates and attenuates internal feature representations (low shared similarity), causing substantial drops in both task performance and safety. Quantization, in contrast, alters circuit edges more drastically but preserves feature geometry (high shared similarity). To assess safety, the authors introduce VLMSafe‑420, a counterfactual benchmark of 420 harmful/benign image‑question pairs across 38 categories. On LLaVA‑v1.6‑13B, 50% sparsity pruning reduces genuine refusal rates from 39% to 22% while increasing incoherent outputs (failures) to 22%; 4‑bit quantization maintains refusal at 33.9% with minimal failures. Safety‑critical components concentrate in the projector and mid‑to‑late attention layers, yet activation patching and targeted ablations reveal that refusal mechanisms are distributed rather than localized.

## Method Summary
The authors evaluate compression effects through three complementary analyses. First, Edge Activation Patching (EAP) identifies circuit components essential for VQA performance: for each counterfactual sample, clean and corrupted runs are contrasted, and activations of vision‑encoder and projector modules are patched to measure recovery of correct‑answer logit differences. Circuit similarity between uncompressed and compressed models is quantified via Jaccard overlap and Spearman correlation of component importance. Second, crosscoders are trained on residual‑stream activations (CLS token after vision encoder, averaged Q‑Former outputs) from both uncompressed and compressed models. Crosscoder metrics—Feature Sharing Ratio (FSR), Shared Similarity Score (SSS), and Class‑specific Sensitivity (CSS)—quantify how feature representations are preserved or altered. Third, safety is evaluated using VLMSafe‑420. Compressed VLMs generate responses to 420 harmful/benign image‑question pairs; Claude Haiku judges each for refusal (binary) and coherence (binary). Outcomes are categorized as genuine refusal (coherent refusal), failure (incoherent refusal), or compliance (coherent harmful response). Additional analyses include logit‑lens tracking of refusal‑token probabilities across layers and targeted ablations of top‑ranked safety‑critical components to test the necessity of distributed mechanisms.

## Key Results
- **Circuit vs. feature preservation:** Pruning (Wanda) retains circuit topology (high Jaccard/Spearman) but rotates/attenuates features (low shared similarity); quantization (INT4) changes circuits more but aligns features better (high shared similarity).
- **Safety impact:** On LLaVA‑v1.6‑13B, 50% pruning reduces genuine refusal from 39% to 22% while failures rise to 22%; 4‑bit quantization preserves refusal at 33.9% with minimal failures. Compliance remains stable under pruning (~60%), indicating failures drive the safety drop.
- **Mechanism distribution:** Activation patching highlights the projector and mid‑to‑late attention layers as safety‑critical. However, logit‑lens shows a gradual rise in refusal probability across layers, and targeted ablations of the top‑30 safety‑critical components do not uniquely degrade refusal, suggesting a distributed safety pathway.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Pruning vs. Quantization on Circuits and Features
**Claim:** Pruning (Wanda) preserves the overall circuit topology (high Jaccard/Spearman) but rotates and attenuates internal feature representations (low cosine similarity), while quantization (INT4) alters circuit structures more but retains better feature alignment (high cosine similarity).  
**Mechanism:** Wanda’s pruning approximates layer‑wise distortion minimization: it removes weights with smallest contribution to local output fidelity, computed as `|Wij|·∥xj∥`. This keeps high‑usage edges, preserving circuit overlap, but the resulting sparse perturbations rotate feature directions (lower cosine similarity) or reduce their norm (attenuation). Quantization adds bounded elementwise error; while it may push many edges below attribution thresholds (lower circuit overlap), the surviving features remain geometrically closer to the original (higher alignment).  
**Core assumption:** Circuit similarity metrics (Jaccard, Spearman) and feature alignment metrics (cosine similarity) are valid proxies for mechanistic preservation across compression methods.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract states the contrast; Section 4.3 Tables 3 and 4 show Wanda achieves higher circuit similarity (IoU, ρ) than INT4, while Wanda has lower SSS than INT4. Neighboring works on circuit tracing and sparse autoencoders validate attribution and crosscoder methods.  
**Break condition:** At very high sparsity (e.g., >50% Wanda) or extreme low‑bit quantization, both circuit and feature preservation collapse, limiting generalizability.

### Mechanism 2: Distributed Safety Mechanisms
**Claim:** Safety‑critical mechanisms for refusal behavior in VLMs are distributed across mid‑to‑late layers (projector and attention/MLP blocks), rather than localized to a single component.  
**Mechanism:** Activation patching restores clean activations for individual components and measures recovery of refusal logit difference. Components with higher recovery are deemed safety‑critical. Logit‑lens tracking of refusal‑token probability shows a gradual rise across layers, indicating a distributed pathway that consolidates the refusal decision.  
**Core assumption:** Refusal behavior can be causally attributed to specific components via patching, and the logit‑lens trajectory reflects the decision‑making process.  
**Evidence anchors:** Section 5.3 Table 6 ranks components by patching recovery; projector and layers 13–19 are prominent. Figure 3 shows refusal probability rising from layer 12 to 37. Section 5.4 notes “safety behavior is distributed… tolerates selective perturbations.”  
**Break condition:** If the model’s safety training is only text‑triggered (as noted for VLMSafe‑420 image counterfactuals), visual safety signals may be weaker; or if the architecture lacks a distinct projector (e.g., unified transformer), distribution may differ.

### Mechanism 3: Pruning Degrades Refusal via Incoherent Outputs
**Claim:** Pruning degrades genuine refusal rates primarily by increasing incoherent outputs (model failures) rather than by increasing compliance with harmful requests.  
**Mechanism:** Wanda’s layer‑wise distortion minimization disrupts the coherent generation needed for clear refusals, leading to garbled or incomplete text that may contain refusal‑like tokens but lacks semantic validity. The model thus fails to produce well‑formed refusals or compliance alike; the drop in genuine refusal coincides with a rise in failures, not a rise in coherent harmful responses.  
**Core assumption:** Safety evaluation must disentangle refusal from output coherence, as compressed models can produce refusal‑like fragments that are not functionally safe.  
**Evidence anchors:** Section 5.3 Table 7 shows as Wanda sparsity increases, genuine refusal drops (39%→22% at 50%) while failure rate rises (0%→22%); compliance remains relatively stable (~60%). Section D.1 describes the LLM‑as‑judge protocol distinguishing genuine refusal, failure, and compliance.  
**Break condition:** If evaluation counts any output containing refusal keywords as safe, this mechanism would be invisible; or if pruning is mild (<30% sparsity), failure rates may not rise significantly.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Vision encoder -> Projector (e.g., Q‑Former) -> Language decoder

**Critical path:**  
Image → Vision encoder → Projector → Concatenation with text tokens → Language decoder → Output logits

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Pruning (Wanda):** Retains more circuit edges and feature sharing but rotates/attenuates features, causing larger utility and safety degradation at high sparsity.  
- **Quantization (INT4):** Alters circuit edges more drastically but keeps surviving features aligned, better preserving safety behavior.  
- **Module compression:** Compressing the projector (vs. vision encoder) has stronger impact on VQA performance; safety‑critical components concentrate there and in mid‑late attention layers.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **Pruning:** increased incoherent outputs (“failures”) rather than compliance; genuine refusal rate drops sharply beyond 30% sparsity.  
- **Quantization:** more circuit changes but stable refusal rates; failures minimal.  
- **Over‑compression:** severe degradation in both utility (Table 1) and safety beyond 50% pruning.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Edge Activation Patching:** Run clean/corrupt counterfactual VQA, patch components in vision encoder and projector, measure recovery of correct‑answer logit difference. Compare compressed vs. uncompressed circuit importance maps.  
2. **Crosscoder Feature Comparison:** Train a crosscoder on residual stream activations (CLS token for vision encoder, averaged Q‑Former outputs) from uncompressed and compressed models. Compute feature sharing ratio (FSR) and shared similarity score (SSS).  
3. **Safety Evaluation with VLMSafe‑420:** Evaluate compressed models on 420 counterfactual pairs, use LLM‑as‑judge to classify outputs as genuine refusal, failure, or compliance. Identify safety‑critical components via activation patching and logit‑lens.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How do pruning and quantization fundamentally differ in their impact on the internal circuit structure and feature representations of vision-language models?**  
   *Basis:* The paper explicitly poses RQ1 and finds pruning retains circuit structure (high Jaccard/Spearman) but rotates/attenuates features (low SSS), while quantization changes circuits more but aligns features better (higher SSS).  
   *Why unresolved:* The paper establishes correlational differences via metrics but does not mechanistically explain why these distinct perturbation patterns arise from the mathematical properties of each compression method.  
   *Resolving evidence:* A theoretical analysis deriving the expected perturbation to feature geometry from the optimization objectives of pruning (e.g., Wanda score) and quantization (e.g., rounding error). Validation could involve controlled synthetic experiments where specific weight distributions and activation statistics are manipulated to isolate causes of feature rotation vs. circuit alteration.

2. **What specific internal mechanisms are causally responsible for the degradation in genuine refusal behavior under pruning, and why does quantization preserve it better?**  
   *Basis:* RQ2 asks about safety implications. Results show pruning at 50% sparsity drops genuine refusal from 39% to 22% (mostly turning to failures), while 4‑bit quantization preserves 33.9%. Safety‑critical components concentrate in the projector and mid‑to‑late attention layers, but targeted ablations fail to isolate a unique mechanism.  
   *Why unresolved:* Activation patching identifies components correlated with refusal, but ablating the top‑30 "safety‑critical" components does not uniquely degrade safety, indicating refusal mechanisms are distributed. It remains unclear how the feature‑level changes (e.g., shared feature attenuation under pruning) map to the specific computational pathways that produce refusal tokens.  
   *Resolving evidence:* Causal interventions that patch or ablate features (not just components) identified by the crosscoder, especially those that are "redirected" or "attenuated" under pruning. Logit‑lens trajectories combined with feature attribution could trace how refusal‑token probabilities emerge differently in pruned vs. quantized models across layers.

3. **Why does the safety behavior in VLMs appear distributed across mid‑to‑late layers, and how does this distribution interact with compression to cause failure‑dominant degradation rather than coherent compliance?**  
   *Basis:* The paper states: "safety in VLMs is neither fully localized nor fully diffuse. The behavior relies on a distributed mid‑layer pathway." Logit‑lens shows refusal probabilities rising gradually from layer 12 to 37. Under aggressive pruning, failures increase sharply (22% at 50% sparsity) while compliance does not, suggesting distributed mechanisms degrade into incoherence rather than being bypassed.  
   *Why unresolved:* The distributed nature is established empirically, but the reason for this distribution—e.g., whether it arises from redundancy for robustness, from layered abstraction of harmfulness, or from multimodal integration demands—is not explained. Why pruning disrupts this distribution into incoherence while quantization does not is also not mechanistically linked to the earlier circuit/feature findings.  
   *Resolving evidence:* Ablation studies that selectively disrupt connectivity between mid‑layer components (e.g., via edge patching) to test for critical paths. Analysis of how pruned models’ activation statistics (e.g., entropy, norm) in mid‑layers correlate with output coherence. Comparison with language‑only models to isolate the role of the vision‑language projector in distributing safety signals.

4. **To what extent do the mechanistic findings generalize to larger VLMs (e.g., >13B parameters) and architectures where the language decoder itself is safety‑aligned via methods like RLHF, rather than only the instruction tuning?**  
   *Basis:* The limitations note: "Experiments on model families are restricted to small‑mid sizes models (≤8B). Further, the AI safety implications were evaluated on VLMs where only the decoder is safety trained." The study uses BLIP‑VQA (base) and LLaVA‑v1.5/1.6 (instruct‑tuned but not explicitly RLHF‑aligned), leaving open questions about scaling and alignment method effects.  
   *Why unresolved:* Compression effects may differ in larger, more capable models with richer safety training. Mechanisms like refusal might be more sparse and brittle in RLHF‑aligned models (as suggested in language model studies), which could interact differently with pruning‑induced feature rotation or quantization‑induced circuit changes. The paper’s VLMSafe‑420 includes 50 JailbreakBench‑style prompts, but evaluation is on a 13B model without explicit RLHF.  
   *Resolving evidence:* Replicating the mechanistic pipeline (EAP, crosscoders, VLMSafe‑420) on larger VLMs (e.g., LLaVA‑NeXT, InternVL) and models explicitly alignment‑trained (e.g., with DPO/RLHF). Comparing the concentration and distribution of safety components and feature stability metrics across scales and alignment methods.

5. **How does compressing the language decoder (rather than only vision and projection modules) affect the preserved safety mechanisms, and can compression be made "mechanism‑aware" to protect critical pathways?**  
   *Basis:* The study compresses only vision encoder and Q‑Former/projector modules, explicitly noting this as a limitation: "the AI safety implications were evaluated on VLMs where only the decoder is safety trained," yet they did not compress the decoder. The decoder contains most safety‑aligned parameters; compressing it might drastically alter refusal circuits.  
   *Why unresolved:* The paper’s main safety analysis avoids decoder compression, so it is unknown whether the observed distributed mid‑layer pathway extends into the decoder or if decoder compression would obliterate it. The finding that pruning causes failures rather than compliance suggests degradation is non‑uniform; mechanism‑aware compression (e.g., sparing decoder layers or visual‑projector pathways) is suggested but untested.  
   *Resolving evidence:* Experiments where the decoder is pruned/quantized separately and in combination with vision modules, measuring circuit similarity, feature stability (crosscoder), and VLMSafe‑420 outcomes. Development of compression schedules that prioritize preserving components/features identified as safety‑critical (e.g., via high patching recovery) and evaluation of whether this mitigates safety drops.

## Limitations
- **Scope limited to small‑mid VLMs and specific compression methods:** Experiments used BLIP‑VQA, LLaVA, and Qwen‑VL with Wanda pruning and INT4/NF4 quantization; findings may not generalize to larger models (>13B) or other techniques (e.g., structured pruning, lower‑bit quantization).
- **Safety benchmark focus on image‑triggered harms:** VLMSafe‑420 centers on visual counterfactuals, potentially under‑representing textual safety steering and other risk modalities.
- **Methodological sensitivities:** Edge Activation Patching assumes linear local effects and may miss distributed interactions; crosscoder metrics (FSR, SSS) depend on hyperparameters (expansion factor, λ, top‑k) and can suffer training instability; reliance on Claude Haiku for judgment without human validation introduces potential bias.

## Next Checks
1. **Crosscoder robustness:** Train crosscoders with multiple seeds, varied expansion factors (4×, 8×), and alternative top‑k schemes (per‑stream vs. union) to confirm that FSR and SSS differences between pruning and quantization persist and are not artefacts of a single configuration.
2. **Fine‑grained ablation of safety‑critical heads:** Within the projector and mid‑to‑late attention layers identified, ablate individual attention heads (e.g., via Wanda at 50% sparsity restricted to that head) and measure refusal rates. This tests whether any single head is indispensable, further probing the distributed claim.
3. **External safety benchmark validation:** Run compressed models on a different multimodal safety dataset (e.g., M‑Harm) and evaluate with both an LLM judge and a small human study. This checks whether the refusal/failure pattern generalizes beyond VLMSafe‑420 and whether judge bias explains the observed effects.
