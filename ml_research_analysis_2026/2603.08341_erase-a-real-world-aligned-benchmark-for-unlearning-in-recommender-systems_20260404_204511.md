# ERASE -- A Real-World Aligned Benchmark for Unlearning in Recommender Systems

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.08341
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08341
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Most unlearning algorithms fail to achieve the required ≥1000× speedup over full retraining for practical deployment in recommender systems.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces ERASE, the first large-scale benchmark specifically designed for evaluating machine unlearning in recommender systems. It addresses the gap between research and real-world deployment by simulating operational unlearning scenarios through sequential, small-scale requests. The benchmark systematically tests seven unlearning algorithms across nine datasets and nine models, covering three core recommendation tasks: collaborative filtering, session-based, and next-basket recommendation. Results indicate that recommender-specific methods (e.g., SCIF) demonstrate greater robustness than general-purpose approaches, particularly for attention-based and recurrent architectures. However, a critical efficiency shortfall is revealed: nearly all algorithms fall short of the 1000× speedup over retraining needed for practical use, highlighting a significant barrier to real-world adoption.

## Method Summary
The benchmark evaluates unlearning under two realistic scenarios: (1) removing sensitive item interactions via sequential per‑user batches (total forget interactions = 0.01% of data) and (2) removing injected spam users in batches of 256 (total spam interactions = 1% of training set). For each model–dataset pair, an original model is trained on the full data. Then, unlearning requests are applied sequentially, with utility (nDCG@k, Recall@k, Hit@k), effectiveness (ResItems@k or ResEff@k), and efficiency (wall‑clock time, speedup vs. full retraining) measured after each batch. A retrained model from scratch on the retain set (with equal time budget) serves as the gold standard. The evaluation pipeline is implemented in RecBole, with artifacts including preprocessed data, pretrained checkpoints, forget‑set definitions, and evaluation scripts.

## Key Results
- Recommender‑specific methods (e.g., SCIF) maintain higher utility and effectiveness on attention‑based and recurrent models compared to general‑purpose approximate unlearning algorithms.
- Most algorithms fail to achieve the ≥1000× speedup over full retraining required for production deployment, with only a few narrowly meeting the threshold.
- Sequential unlearning leads to cumulative degradation for noise‑based methods (e.g., Fanchuan, Seif) and divergence for Hessian‑based methods (e.g., GIF, IDEA, CEU) without careful tuning.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
ERASE aligns with real‑world requirements by using *sequential small‑scale unlearning requests* rather than single large forget sets, mirroring how unlearning would actually occur in a live system (e.g., user‑by‑user data removal). The inclusion of *multiple recommendation paradigms* (CF, SBR, NBR) and *diverse datasets and models* ensures findings generalize across the field. By comparing against a *time‑budgeted retraining baseline*, the benchmark measures practical efficiency, not just theoretical approximation quality. This design exposes the trade‑offs between utility preservation, forgetting effectiveness, and computational speed that define real‑world viability.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Data Loader → Model Trainer → Unlearning Module → Evaluator → Metrics Aggregator

**Critical path**: Load preprocessed dataset → Train original model to convergence → For each sequential forget batch: apply unlearning algorithm → Evaluate on fixed test set → Record utility, effectiveness, wall‑clock time → Compare to retrained model (same time budget) → Aggregate results across seeds.

**Design tradeoffs**: 
- *Accuracy vs. speed*: SCIF uses multiple repair iterations for robustness, increasing latency; noise‑based methods (Seif) are fast but degrade utility over time.
- *Memory vs. feasibility*: Hessian‑based methods (GIF, IDEA) require significant memory for second‑order computations; diagonal approximations reduce accuracy.
- *Generality vs. specialization*: Recommender‑specific adaptations (e.g., SCIF’s item‑wise repair) improve effectiveness but may not transfer across architectures.

**Failure signatures**: 
- Numerical divergence (NaNs) in Hessian methods; mitigate with damping and gradient clipping.
- Rapid utility drop in sequential unlearning for noise methods; monitor effectiveness over batches.
- Memory overflow during large‑model retraining or Hessian computation; reduce batch size or use checkpointing.

**First three experiments**:
1. **Baseline sanity check**: Train a small model (e.g., BPR on MovieLens) using the provided scripts; verify that utility metrics (nDCG@10) match reported baselines within variance.
2. **Single‑batch unlearning**: Apply SCIF to remove a tiny forget set (e.g., one user’s interactions) from a pretrained SASRec; confirm that ResItems@k for sensitive items drops near zero while nDCG remains stable.
3. **Efficiency measurement**: Time GIF vs. full retraining for one batch on a medium dataset (e.g., Dunnhumby); compute speedup and verify it is below the 1000× threshold as claimed.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. What is the unlearning effectiveness of approximate unlearning methods?Results show high variability across datasets and models; no single method uniformly effective; effectiveness depends on task and architecture specifics.
2. Do approximate unlearning methods achieve robust, consistent performance across CF, SBR, and NBR recommendation tasks?Figure 2 reveals most algorithms exhibit high variance or degradation under sequential unlearning, except SCIF; consistency across diverse tasks remains unproven.
3. How does model architecture influence approximate unlearning effectiveness?General methods degrade for attention/recurrent models, but causal mechanisms are unclear; SCIF’s architectural adaptations lack deep analysis.
4. Do current approximate unlearning algorithms achieve sufficient efficiency (≥1000x speedup over retraining) for real‑world deployment?ERASE shows most methods fall short of the 1000x speedup target, with only a few meeting it minimally; a practical gap persists.

## Limitations
- Training hyperparameters (learning rates, epochs, regularization) for each model–dataset pair are not fully documented; only tuning summaries appear in the appendix.
- Forget‑set construction is unclear: exact definition of “sensitive items” per dataset and the procedure for injecting spam users are not described in the paper; must be inferred from code.
- Train/validation/test splits used for evaluation are not released; their absence hinders fair replication and may affect utility metric calculations.
