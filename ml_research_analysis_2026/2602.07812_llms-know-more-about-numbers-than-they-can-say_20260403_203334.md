# LLMs Know More About Numbers than They Can Say

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.07812  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07812  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** LLMs linearly encode log-magnitudes and comparison outcomes in hidden states, but verbal accuracy is low; finetuning with an auxiliary classification probe loss improves verbal accuracy by an additional 3.22%, demonstrating that enhancing internal representations can improve numerical reasoning.

## Executive Summary
LLMs often fail to correctly compare numbers written in different notations (e.g., 5.7×10² vs. 580), suggesting a weakness in number sense. This study probes hidden states of 7B–8B models and finds that numeric magnitudes are encoded approximately linearly on a log scale (2.3% relative error on synthetic data), and that the comparison result itself is directly encoded with >90% accuracy via a linear classifier. However, when asked to verbalize the answer, accuracy drops to 50–70%. By incorporating the classifier’s loss as an auxiliary objective during finetuning, the authors boost verbal accuracy by an extra 3.22%. This work demonstrates that LLMs possess numerical knowledge that is inaccessible to generation, and that improving internal representations causally enhances verbal reasoning.

## Method Summary
The authors constructed a synthetic dataset of 11,200 number pairs across mixed notations (integer vs. scientific, decimal vs. scientific) and sampled 5,000 real-world pairs from arXiv preprints. They trained linear probes on frozen model hidden states: a regression probe to predict log₂ magnitude from the last numeral token, and a classification probe to predict which number is larger from the final prompt token. For finetuning, they applied LoRA with an additional loss term equal to the classification probe’s cross-entropy (weight β=0.02) on the 90%-depth layer, while keeping the standard next-token loss. Verbal accuracy was measured by greedy decoding given a one-shot prompt. The probing results quantify the internal encoding, and the causal effect of the auxiliary loss tests whether improving that encoding translates to better verbal performance.

## Key Results
- Linear regression probes predict log-magnitudes with very low error on synthetic data (median relative error ~2.3%, R² > 0.998) but degrade on arXiv data (19% error, R²=0.51).  
- Linear classification probes predict the correct comparison with >90% accuracy, even for pairs with nearly equal magnitudes where magnitude regression fails.  
- Standard finetuning improves verbal accuracy; adding the classification probe loss yields an additional 3.22% average gain (e.g., DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B improves from 84.06% to 95.19%).  
- Early-layer probe performance strongly correlates with verbal accuracy across models, indicating that the gap between internal knowledge and verbalization is linked to early representational quality.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Linear magnitude encoding
The model represents numeric values in a low-dimensional subspace where log₂ magnitude is approximately linearly decodable. A single linear projection on the last token of a numeral achieves high R² on synthetic data, indicating that after a few layers the hidden state preserves magnitude information in a simple form. Evidence: R² > 0.998 on cross-notation pairs; median relative error ~2.3%. However, this linear relationship breaks down on heterogeneous real-world text (arXiv R²=0.51, 19% error), suggesting the encoding is brittle or requires a more complex subspace.

### Mechanism 2: Direct comparison encoding
Beyond independent magnitude representations, the prompt-final hidden state directly encodes the correct comparison outcome. A linear classifier on that state achieves >90% accuracy even for pairs where the difference in log-magnitudes is tiny (|log₂(a/b)| < 0.2), where regression-based comparisons fall to chance. This implies the model’s internal computation for the comparison task produces a robust signal that is more linearly separable than the separate magnitude signals.

### Mechanism 3: Auxiliary loss improves verbalization
Adding the classification probe’s loss during finetuning causally improves the model’s ability to verbalize correct comparisons. Since the probe loss encourages earlier and later layer representations to be more informative for the comparison task, the model learns to leverage its internal knowledge when generating answers. Empirically, this yields an extra 3.22% average improvement in verbal accuracy over standard finetuning, confirming a causal link between enhanced internal representations and downstream performance.

## Foundational Learning

- **Linear Probing**  
  *Why needed:* It is the primary tool to extract information (magnitudes, comparisons) from frozen hidden states. It tests whether a property is represented in a linearly decodable way, which is interpretable but cannot prove the model uses that information during generation.  
  *Quick check:* If a linear probe achieves high accuracy on a task, does that necessarily mean the model uses that information during generation? Why or why not?

- **Log-Magnitude Transformation**  
  *Why needed:* Numeric values span many orders of magnitude; representing them on a log₂ scale compresses the range and makes a linear encoding plausible. The regression probe predicts log₂ instead of raw values to keep the learning problem well-conditioned.  
  *Quick check:* Why might predicting raw numeric values be harder for a linear probe than predicting log₂(magnitude)?

- **Auxiliary Objectives in Finetuning**  
  *Why needed:* The paper uses an auxiliary probe loss to intervene on internal representations and test causality: improving the encoding of comparison improves verbal accuracy. This moves from correlation (probe predicts verbal performance) to causal intervention.  
  *Quick check:* What is the risk of using the same data to train the probe and then as an auxiliary loss during finetuning?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map:** Input Encoder (tokenizer) -> Transformer Stack (layers) -> Probe Heads (external linear probes) -> Finetuning with Aux Loss (LoRA + loss) -> Generation (LM head)

**Critical Path:** The input prompt is tokenized into embeddings. The Transformer Stack (28–32 layers) processes these embeddings via self-attention and MLP. For probing, the hidden state at a specific token position (e.g., the last token of a numeral or the final prompt token) is extracted and fed to an external linear probe head (Regression or Classification). During Finetuning with Aux Loss, a LoRA-modified Transformer is trained with total loss L_LM + β * L_cls, where L_cls is computed from the classification probe attached to a chosen layer (typically 90% depth). At Generation, the final layer’s output passes through the LM head to produce token probabilities; greedy decoding yields the verbal answer.

**Design Tradeoffs**
- *Probe layer depth:* Early layers correlate more strongly with verbal performance but have lower raw probe accuracy; later layers have higher probe accuracy but weaker correlation. Choosing a middle layer (e.g., 90%) for the auxiliary loss balances signal strength and downstream utility.
- *Probe type:* Regression probes are precise on synthetic data but fail for close comparisons; classification probes directly target the verbal task. The paper finds classification loss helps verbalization, while regression loss does not.
- *Data:* Synthetic cross-notation data provides clean signals but may not generalize to real text; arXiv data is noisier and shows degraded linear probe performance, indicating limited capacity of a single linear subspace.

**Failure Signatures**
- High classification probe accuracy (>90%) but verbal accuracy near 50%: indicates a representational-access gap—the model knows the answer internally but cannot verbalize it.
- Finetuning with probe loss yields no verbal improvement: possibly due to poor probe layer choice, incorrect β scaling, or that the generation path cannot leverage the improved representation.
- Regression probe R² is low on arXiv (<0.6): suggests linear probes are insufficient; may require nonlinear probes or multiple subspaces.

**First 3 Experiments**
1. Reproduce magnitude probing: Train linear regression probes for each layer on synthetic cross-notation data; verify the bell-shaped MSE curve (Fig. 2) and identify the optimal layer per model.
2. Compare regression vs. classification for close values: For pairs with |log₂(a/b)| < 0.2, compute accuracy of (a) regression-predicted comparison and (b) classifier probe; confirm classifier superiority (as in Fig. 3b,c).
3. Run finetuning ablation: Finetune one model (e.g., Mistral-7B) under four settings: base, standard finetuning (β=0), + classification loss (β=0.02), + regression loss. Validate that classification loss improves verbal accuracy while regression loss does not (matching Table 2).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
Do LLMs actively utilize their internally encoded magnitude representations during verbal generation of cross-notation comparisons?  
*Basis:* The authors note that probing reveals representation, not usage, and did not perform causal interventions at inference time.  
*Why unresolved:* No experiments directly perturbed or traced the use of magnitude representations during generation.  
*Evidence needed:* Causal mediation or probing interventions that selectively modify magnitude-related activations during decoding and measure changes in verbal comparison accuracy.

### Open Question 2
How generalizable are the linearly encoded log-magnitudes from synthetic prompts to real-world scientific text (e.g., arXiv papers)?  
*Basis:* Performance sharply dropped on arXiv (19.06% median error) versus synthetic data (2.3%), indicating a synthetic-real gap.  
*Why unresolved:* The linear probe’s failure on arXiv suggests the encoding may not be robust or linear in complex contexts; nonlinear probes were not systematically tested.  
*Evidence needed:* Train and compare nonlinear probes (e.g., shallow MLPs) or multi-subspace linear probes on a larger, diverse arXiv corpus to see if they achieve lower error.

### Open Question 3
Does the dissociation between internal numerical knowledge and verbalization persist in larger language models beyond the 7B–8B scale examined?  
*Basis:* The study was limited to 7B–8B open-weight models; larger closed models (e.g., GPT-4.1) were not probed.  
*Why unresolved:* Unknown whether scale improves alignment between internal representations and verbal output.  
*Evidence needed:* Replicate the probing and finetuning pipeline on openly available large models (e.g., Llama 3 70B) to measure probe accuracy and verbalization performance, and compare the correlation to smaller models.

## Limitations
- Prompt template differences (system prompts vs. Q/A) between GPT-4 and open-model evaluations may bias comparisons.  
- Tokenization boundaries for scientific notation vary across tokenizers, affecting probe extraction consistency.  
- Linear magnitude encoding degrades on real-world data (arXiv R²=0.51, 19% error), indicating brittleness outside synthetic distributions.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (Linear magnitude encoding):** Medium – compelling on synthetic data (R²>0.998) but fragile on heterogeneous corpora.  
- **Mechanism 2 (Direct comparison encoding):** High – classifier probes consistently exceed 90% accuracy, even for near-equal magnitudes.  
- **Mechanism 3 (Auxiliary loss improves verbalization):** Medium – causal improvement demonstrated (+3.22%) but may not generalize beyond controlled settings.

## Next Checks
1. Evaluate probe and verbal accuracy on multiple out-of-distribution datasets (e.g., news, social media) to test encoding robustness beyond arXiv.  
2. Systematically vary prompt formats and tokenization strategies for mixed notation to quantify their impact on the representational gap.  
3. Replace the linear classification probe loss with a nonlinear probe or adjust the layer depth used during finetuning to assess the specificity of the auxiliary loss effect.
