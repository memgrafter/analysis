# Beyond identifiability: Learning causal representations with few environments and finite samples

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.25796  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25796  
- **Reference count**: 12  
- **Primary result**: The paper provides finite-sample guarantees for learning causal representations (latent causal graph, mixing matrix, and intervention targets) from a sublinear number of unknown, multi-node intervention environments using only second-order statistics.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the problem of learning causal representations from multi-environment data under a linear latent causal model with unknown interventions. The authors propose a three-step method: (1) recover intervention targets via the rank structure of intersections of column spaces of empirical covariances across environments, (2) recover the mixing matrix by intersecting column spaces where each latent node remains un-intervened, and (3) recover the latent causal graph through a generalized eigenvalue decomposition on de-mixed covariances. Under assumptions of sub-Gaussian data, a strongly separating system, distinct latent noise variances, and a well-conditioned decoder, the method achieves consistent estimation of all components with only \(O(\log d)\) environments and finite-sample high-probability bounds. The results bridge the gap between identifiability theory and practical estimation for causal representation learning.

## Method Summary
The algorithm operates on datasets from \(K+1\) environments (one observational, \(K\) interventional), each with two noise conditions and \(n\) samples.  
**Step 1 (Intervention recovery):** For every subset \(T \subseteq [K]\), compute the projection matrix \(Q(T)\) derived from the product of projectors onto the column spaces of the empirical covariances. The number of eigenvalues of \(Q(T)\) above a threshold equals the dimension of the intersection of those column spaces. The pattern of these dimensions across all \(T\) uniquely determines the intervention sets \(I(k)\) because \(\text{col}(\Sigma_X^{(k)}) = \text{col}(B[:, S^{(k)}])\) with \(S^{(k)} = I(k)^c\).  
**Step 2 (Decoder estimation):** For each latent node \(j\), compute the intersection of column spaces of the covariances from environments where \(j\) is not intervened (including the observational environment). This yields an estimate \(\hat{B}[:,j]\) up to scale and permutation, based on the equality \(\text{col}(B[:,j]) = \bigcap_{k \in \kappa_j} \text{col}(\Sigma_X^{(k)})\) with \(\kappa_j = \{k: j \notin I(k)\} \cup\{0\}\).  
**Step 3 (Graph recovery):** Using \(\hat{B}\), obtain estimates of the latent covariances under the two noise conditions via \(\hat{\Sigma}_Z^{(0),\ell} = \hat{B}^\dagger \hat{\Sigma}_X^{(0),\ell} (\hat{B}^\dagger)^\top\). Solve the generalized eigenvalue problem \(\hat{\Sigma}_Z^{(0),1} t = \lambda \hat{\Sigma}_Z^{(0),2} t\); stack the eigenvectors into \(\hat{T}_Z\). Theoretical relation \(T_Z = (I - A)D\) (with \(D\) diagonal and nonzero) implies that the zero pattern of \(\hat{T}_Z\) reveals the latent causal graph \(A\). Thresholding absolute entries recovers the adjacency matrix.

## Key Results
- **Mixing matrix error**: \(\|\hat{B} - B D\|_F = O\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{r \log(pn)/n}}{1-\rho^*}\right)\) with high probability, where \(r = d\), and \(1-\rho^*\) measures decoder conditioning.
- **Graph recovery**: Succeeds with threshold \(\alpha \asymp \frac{1}{1-\rho^*} \left(\frac{\lambda^+}{\lambda^-}\right)^3 \sqrt{d^2 \log(pn)/n}\) using only \(K = O(\log d)\) environments.
- **Overall consistency**: Under bounded sub-Gaussian data and assumptions (A1)–(A3), the method consistently estimates the latent graph, mixing matrix, representations, and intervention targets.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim**: Unknown multi-node intervention targets can be recovered from the combinatorial rank structure of column space intersections across environments using only second-order statistics.  
**Mechanism**: For any subset \(T \subseteq [K]\), define \(g(T) = \dim(\cap_{k\in T} \text{col}(\Sigma_X^{(k)}))\). This equals the rank of the projection matrix \(Q(T) = (\Pi_{k\in T} P_{\Sigma_X^{(k)}})^\top (\Pi_{k\in T} P_{\Sigma_X^{(k)}})\). The pattern of \(g(T)\) across all \(T\) reveals \(I(k)\) because \(\text{col}(\Sigma_X^{(k)}) = \text{col}(B[:, S^{(k)}])\) with \(S^{(k)} = I(k)^c\).  
**Core assumptions**: (A1) Strongly separating system with \(K \lesssim \log d\) environments; (A2) Distinct noise variance ratios across latent factors; (A3) Decoder \(B\) well-conditioned: \(\lambda_{\min}(B^\top B)/\lambda_{\max}(B^\top B) = 1-\rho^*\) sufficiently large.  
**Evidence anchors**: Abstract mentions projection-based eigen-counting; Section 3.1 defines \(g(T)\) and Lemma 3.1; Section 4.2 defines \(Q(T)\) and Lemma 4.2; neighboring work (arXiv:2406.05937) confirms the mechanism.  
**Break condition**: Failure of (A3) makes intersections too noisy; (A1) fails cause the mapping from \(g(T)\) to \(I(k)\) is not injective; (A2) fails may yield ambiguous intervention recovery.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim**: Each column \(B[:,j]\) of the mixing matrix can be recovered as the intersection of column spaces from environments where node \(j\) is *not* intervened.  
**Mechanism**: Define \(\kappa_j = \{k : j \notin I(k)\}\). Then \(\text{col}(B[:,j]) = \bigcap_{k\in\kappa_j} \text{col}(\Sigma_X^{(k)})\). After estimating \(I(k)\), compute these intersections on sample covariances to obtain consistent estimates up to scale.  
**Assumptions**: Full column rank of \(B\); each \(j\) appears un-intervened in at least one environment (guaranteed by (A1)); accurate recovery of \(I(k)\).  
**Evidence anchors**: Abstract states intersections for decoder recovery; Section 3.2 equation (7) shows the equality; Section 4.3 Theorem 4.6 gives error bound.  
**Break condition**: If \(\kappa_j\) empty (j intervened everywhere), \(B[:,j]\) is unidentified; if \(B\) nearly rank-deficient (small \(1-\rho^*\)), intersection becomes numerically unstable; poor covariance estimates cause spurious intersections.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim**: The latent causal graph structure is encoded in the zero pattern of \(T_Z = (I - A) D\) (with \(D\) diagonal, nonzero), and can be recovered via generalized eigenvalue decomposition on latent covariances after peeling off \(B\).  
**Mechanism**: Given \(\hat{B}\), compute \(\hat{\Sigma}_Z^{(0),\ell} = \hat{B}^\dagger \hat{\Sigma}_X^{(0),\ell} (\hat{B}^\dagger)^\top\). Solve \(\hat{\Sigma}_Z^{(0),1} t = \lambda \hat{\Sigma}_Z^{(0),2} t\); stack eigenvectors into \(\hat{T}_Z\). Theoretically \(T_Z = (I-A)D\) so \((i,j)\) entry zero iff \(A_{ji}=0\). Threshold \(|\hat{T}_Z[i,j]|\) to recover graph.  
**Assumptions**: \(\hat{B}\) sufficiently accurate; latent covariances have well-separated, bounded eigenvalues; graph sparsity (max in-degree \(m_{\text{deg}}\)) to handle noise.  
**Evidence anchors**: Abstract mentions graph via generalized eigen; Section 3.3 derives equations; Section 4.3 Theorem 4.7 gives threshold.  
**Break condition**: If \(\hat{B}\) error exceeds \(\approx 1/\sqrt{d}\), derived latent covariances are distorted; if \(\lambda^+/\lambda^-\) too large, sensitivity increases; high in-degree may violate threshold condition without more samples.

## Foundational Learning
- **Sub-Gaussian random variables**: Needed to obtain finite-sample concentration for covariance estimation, ensuring eigenvalues of empirical covariances concentrate. Quick check: A sub-Gaussian vector \(X\) satisfies \(\mathbb{P}(|\langle X,v\rangle| > t) \le \exp(-c t^2/\sigma^2)\) for all unit \(v\); concentration of empirical covariance involves \(\sqrt{\log p / n}\) via union bound or matrix Bernstein.
- **Eigenvalue perturbation theory (Davis‑Kahan/Wedin)**: Central to bounding changes in eigenvectors/eigenvalues of projected matrices and generalized eigenproblems. Quick check: The Davis‑Kahan \(\sin\theta\) theorem bounds the between true and perturbed eigenspaces by \(\|\text{perturbation}\|\) divided by the eigengap.
- **Column space intersections and rank formulas**: The method relies on dimensions of intersections of column spaces and identities like \(\dim(\cap_i U_i) = \text{rank}(\prod P_{U_i})\). Quick check: For well-separated subspaces, \(\text{rank}(P_U P_V) = \dim(U \cap V)\) and small perturbations in projectors cause small rank changes provided principal angles are bounded away from zero.
- **Identifiability vs. estimation**: Identifiability ensures recovery in infinite samples; finite‑sample guarantees ensure convergence with enough data. Quick check: In \(X = BZ\) with latent \(Z\), \(B\) is not identifiable from a single environment’s covariance because any orthogonal transformation of \(Z\) yields the same covariance; independence of \(Z\)’s components plus non‑Gaussianity may recover \(B\) up to permutation and scale.
- **Generalized eigenvalue problems**: Used to recover \(A\) from \(\Sigma_Z^{(0),1} t = \lambda \Sigma_Z^{(0),2} t\), leading to \(T_Z = (I-A)D\). Quick check: If \(D\) is invertible and \(A\) is a DAG (strictly lower triangular), the pencil is regular and eigenvectors are unique up to scaling when eigenvalues are distinct.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Input buffer → Covariance estimator → Intervention target estimator → Decoder estimator → Latent covariance estimator → Graph estimator → Representation estimator.

### Critical Path
Steps 3 → 4 → 5 → 6 are sequential; error propagates. Step 3 (intervention recovery) is most critical; errors there feed into Step 4, whose accuracy determines Step 5’s stability via the pseudoinverse of \(\hat{B}\).

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Number of environments \(K\) vs per‑environment sample size** \(n_{k,\ell}\): More environments improve intervention recovery but reduce \(n_{k,\ell}\); balance via total sample size \(N = \sum_{k,\ell} n_{k,\ell}\).
- **Threshold choices**: \(\rho\) (for eigen‑counting) and \(\alpha\) (graph sparsity) must be set; too conservative or liberal yields missed or false detections. The paper provides asymptotic scalings but no data‑driven method.
- **Computational cost**: Naïve computation of all \(Q(T)\) costs \(O(2^K d^3)\); with \(K = O(\log d)\) this becomes polynomial in \(d\).
- **Noise variance ratio condition (A2)**: Requires designing environments with different noise variances to achieve distinctness, a constraint on data collection.

### Failure Signatures
- \(\hat{g}_\rho(T)\) deviates from expected; check rank pattern consistency across bootstrap samples.
- Columns of \(\hat{B}\) exhibit unexpected zeros or extreme norms; indicates wrong \(\kappa_j\) sets from mis‑recovered interventions.
- \(\hat{\Sigma}_Z^{(0),\ell}\) nearly singular or eigenvectors very noisy; suggests \(\hat{B}\) error too large.
- Graph recovery unstable to small changes in \(\alpha\); entries of \(\hat{T}_Z\) near threshold.

### First Experiments
1. **Synthetic verification**: Simulate linear SEM with \(d=10\), \(p=50\), random DAG, \(B\) with condition number \(0.1\), intervention sets satisfying (A1), \(n=1000\) per env. Verify \(\mathbb{P}(\hat{I}(k)=I(k) \;\forall k) \approx 1\), \(\|\hat{B}-BD\|_F / \|B\|_F\) matches \(\sqrt{r\log(pn)/n}/(1-\rho^*)\), and graph exact recovery probability approaches 1 as \(n\) grows.
2. **Environment count ablation**: Fix total samples, vary \(K\) from 1 to \(\lceil 2\log_2 d\rceil\). Plot intervention/graph recovery error vs. \(K\). Expect sharp improvement around \(K \approx c\log d\) then plateau; too few environments (\(K < c\log d\)) should fail per the lower bound.
3. **Decoder condition sensitivity**: Vary the condition number of \(B\) (make columns more collinear). Plot recovery errors vs. \(1-\rho^*\). Error should blow up as \(1/(1-\rho^*)\) as predicted by Theorems 4.6 and 4.7. Also test extreme violation of (A3) and observe failure.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Nonlinear extension**: The paper states linear results are a first step toward nonlinear generative models. Finite‑sample guarantees for nonlinear mixing and latent causal mechanisms remain open because nonlinearity introduces non‑convexity and second‑order statistics may be insufficient.  
- **Relaxing assumptions (A1) and (A2)**: The method relies on a strongly separating intervention system and distinct noise variance ratios to produce a unique rank pattern. Whether these can be weakened (e.g., partial interventions, unknown noise scales) while still achieving consistency with sublinear environments is unknown; the proof technique seems tied to these structures.  
- **Optimality and lower bounds**: Upper bounds are provided, but no matching lower bounds exist. The dependence on \(1/(1-\rho^*)\) might be an artifact of the analysis or fundamental. Determining minimax rates and sample‑complexity lower bounds for causal representation learning with \(O(\log d)\) environments is an open theoretical challenge.

## Limitations
- **Restrictive assumptions**: The approach requires sub‑Gaussian data, a strongly separating intervention system (A1), distinct latent noise variances (A2), and a well‑conditioned decoder (A3). These may not hold in many real‑world scenarios.
- **Threshold selection**: The theoretical thresholds \(\rho\) and \(\alpha\) depend on unknown quantities (e.g., \(\lambda^+/\lambda^-\), \(\rho^*\)); the paper does not provide practical data‑driven methods to choose them.
- **Error propagation**: The sequential pipeline amplifies errors; inaccurate intervention recovery or an ill‑conditioned \(\hat{B}\) can cause downstream failure in graph recovery.
- **Algorithmic details**: The poly‑time recovery of intervention patterns from the rank pattern \(g(T)\) is not fully elaborated; degenerate patterns (multiple nodes sharing the same intervention set) may lead to intersections of dimension >1, breaking the top‑eigenvector assumption.

## Confidence
| Claim cluster                 | Confidence |
|-------------------------------|------------|
| Intervention recovery         | High (theory), Medium in practice (due to environment design and distinct noise variance requirement) |
| Decoder (mixing matrix)       | High (theory) but Medium–Low in practice if (A3) violated or interventions mis‑recovered |
| Causal graph recovery         | High only with sufficiently accurate \(\hat{B}\) and sparse graph; otherwise Medium–Low (sensitivity to \(\lambda^+/\lambda^-\) and two‑stage error) |

## Next Checks
- **Robustness to decoder condition**: Simulate across varying \(1-\rho^*\) and sample size; measure mis‑recovery rate of \(I(k)\) to test stability of eigen‑counting under nearly collinear columns.
- **Data‑driven threshold selection**: For many random \(T\), plot eigenvalue distributions of \(\hat{Q}(T)\) to find a spectral gap for \(\rho\). Similarly, histogram of \(|\hat{T}_Z[i,j]|\) to choose \(\alpha\) via FDR control. Compare recovery performance to theoretical scalings.
- **Sensitivity to violation of A2**: Deliberately set equal noise variance ratios for some latent nodes and quantify degradation in intervention and graph recovery. This will expose the necessity (or fragility) of the distinct‑ratio assumption.
