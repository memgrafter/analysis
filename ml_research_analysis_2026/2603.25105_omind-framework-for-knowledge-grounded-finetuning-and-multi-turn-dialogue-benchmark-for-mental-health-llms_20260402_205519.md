# OMIND: Framework for Knowledge Grounded Finetuning and Multi-Turn Dialogue Benchmark for Mental Health LLMs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.25105
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25105
- **Reference count:** 12
- **Primary result:** Introduces a ∼164k knowledge-grounded SFT dataset, specialized oMind-LLMs, and a multi-turn benchmark, with models consistently outperforming baselines (e.g., +9.2% F1 on classification, 80% win rate in head-to-head comparisons).

## Executive Summary
The oMind framework addresses two critical gaps in mental health LLMs: the absence of large-scale, knowledge-grounded training data and the lack of robust multi-turn dialogue evaluation. It introduces a three-part solution: (1) a ∼164k multi-task SFT dataset generated via a retrieval-pruning-review pipeline grounded in UMLS and medical texts, (2) specialized LLM agents (oMind-LLMs) fine-tuned with this data and optionally aligned with DPO, and (3) the oMind-Chat benchmark featuring expert-defined turn-level and conversation-level rubrics. Evaluations show oMind models achieve significant gains across classification, QA, and dialogue tasks, with preference alignment notably improving reasoning and response quality.

## Method Summary
The framework constructs its SFT dataset through a three-stage pipeline: retrieval (BM25 + BERT embeddings over UMLS KG and medical books to gather relevant facts), pruning (an LLM filters and restructures retrieved elements into coherent sentences), and review (RoBERTa NLI filters contradictions using a 0.8 threshold). Seed tasks (MCQA, classification) are expanded into ∼57k supporting QA pairs and ∼37.5k multi-turn conversations (3–5 turns across 5 categories) to teach narrative reasoning and therapeutic progression. Models are fine-tuned via LoRA on the combined ∼164k dataset, with optional DPO alignment using 20k GPT-as-a-judge preference pairs. Evaluation employs both traditional benchmarks (e.g., ANGST, MHQA) and the novel oMind-Chat benchmark, which uses expert rubrics to score individual turns and overall conversation coverage.

## Key Results
- oMind-Mistral improves classification average F1 by 9.2% over its base model.
- oMind-Qwen wins 80% of head-to-head explanation comparisons versus its base model.
- oMind-Qwen-DPO achieves an 84% win rate, with DPO significantly boosting reasoning and dialogue quality.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
*Claim:* The retrieval-pruning-review pipeline creates factually accurate, interpretable explanations by grounding responses in structured medical knowledge.  
*Mechanism:* Retrieval uses BM25 + BERT over UMLS and medical books (∼10 chunks each) to gather relevant facts. An LLM prunes irrelevant elements and restructures them into sentences. A RoBERTa NLI model checks each sentence against grounding, removing contradictions (>0.8 threshold) and regenerating if needed.  
*Core assumption:* Structured retrieval plus rigorous verification yields more reliable explanations than parametric-only generation or simple RAG.  
*Evidence anchors:* [abstract] emphasizes "knowledge grounded" explanations and "factual verification"; [section 3.2] details the three-stage process; win rate analysis shows KG-grounded explanations win 58–67% for Factual Richness.  
*Break condition:* UMLS lacks coverage for emerging conditions, or NLI misclassifies valid paraphrases as contradictions, causing over-filtering.

### Mechanism 2
*Claim:* Supporting long-form QA generation from seed data enables cross-task knowledge transfer, improving multi-turn dialogue coherence.  
*Mechanism:* For each MCQA/classification instance, an LLM identifies non-trivial symptom-disorder relations and generates targeted long-form questions; answers are existing explanations, creating ∼57k QA pairs. These are mixed into SFT training to connect discrete facts into narratives.  
*Core assumption:* Intermediate open-ended tasks bridge the gap between structured classification and free-form conversation by training narrative reasoning.  
*Evidence anchors:* [section 3.3] describes generating supporting QA; [section 6.1] ablation shows oMind-Qwen's conversation performance drops 6% without supporting QA.  
*Break condition:* Supporting QA generation introduces hallucinations or creates weak symptom links that don't generalize.

### Mechanism 3
*Claim:* Turn-level + conversation-level dual rubric evaluation captures both immediate therapeutic quality and cumulative information building.  
*Mechanism:* Experts define required elements per turn (e.g., empathy, factual accuracy, follow-up) and essential information per conversation. Evaluation uses binary coverage, 0–10 scales, and Likert 1–5.  
*Core assumption:* Effective mental health dialogue requires both per-turn therapeutic adherence and progressive coverage of diagnostic/management information.  
*Evidence anchors:* [abstract] introduces the dual rubrics; [section 4] details rubric creation; [table 3] shows models can improve turn-level but not conversation-level scores, revealing distinction.  
*Break condition:* Rubrics become too prescriptive, stifling natural variation, or expert consensus on essential information is low.

## Foundational Learning

**Concept: Knowledge Graph Retrieval for Medical Grounding**  
*Why needed here:* The generation pipeline depends on accurate UMLS triplet retrieval. Engineers must understand that UMLS relations (e.g., "may_treat", "has_symptom") encode clinical knowledge differently than natural language.  
*Quick check:* What's the difference between "has_symptom" and "associated_with" in UMLS for mental health contexts, and how might this affect explanation fidelity?

**Concept: NLI-based Factual Consistency**  
*Why needed here:* The review stage uses RoBERTa NLI to filter contradictions. Engineers need to know NLI models can misclassify valid paraphrases as "neutral" and miss subtle factual errors.  
*Quick check:* If an explanation says "anxiety often involves restlessness" and grounding says "restlessness is a symptom of anxiety", is this entailed, neutral, or contradictory? Why might an NLI model get it wrong?

**Concept: Multi-Turn Dialogue State in Mental Health**  
*Why needed here:* oMind-Chat evaluates both turn-level and conversation-level coverage. Engineers must understand that mental health dialogues have therapeutic progression (validation → exploration → recommendation).  
*Quick check:* In a 3-turn conversation about anxiety, how should the distribution of rubric elements (empathy, factual, follow-up, recommendation) ideally shift across turns?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Seed datasets (MCQA, classification) -> Retrieval queries -> UMLS/Books retrieval (BM25+BERT) -> LLM pruning -> LLM generation -> NLI review -> Validated explanations -> Supporting QA & multi-turn chats -> Integrated SFT dataset (~164k) -> LoRA finetuning (base LLM) -> Optional DPO (20k preference pairs) -> oMind-LLMs -> oMind-Chat evaluation (turn & conversation rubrics)

**Critical path:**  
1. Gather seed datasets and define retrieval queries.  
2. Implement BM25 + BERT retrieval over UMLS/books.  
3. Build LLM pruning/generation prompts; set up NLI review loop.  
4. Generate supporting QA and conversations using GPT-4o-mini.  
5. Combine into final SFT dataset; verify task distribution.  
6. Run LoRA finetuning; validate on core tasks before DPO.  
7. If doing DPO: curate preference pairs (GPT-as-a-judge) and train.  
8. Evaluate on oMind-Chat using rubric prompts; compare against baselines.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Retrieval size: More chunks (n,m) increases coverage but raises pruning cost and noise; paper uses 10 total.  
- NLI threshold: 0.8 removes contradictions but may discard valid rephrasing; lower threshold risks factual drift.  
- Supporting QA volume: ∼57k adds knowledge connectivity but risks diluting task-specific learning if unbalanced.  
- DPO data source: 10k unseen instances prevent overfitting but may misalign if unseen distribution differs.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High turn-level but low conversation-level scores (as with oMind-Mistral): Check conversation training data diversity.  
- DPO degrades MCQA performance (as with oMind-Qwen on ANGST): Audit preference pair quality and distribution.  
- Low factual richness in win rates: Check UMLS query formulation and retrieval quality.  
- Poor empathy/validation scores: Augment training data with counseling dialogue examples.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline replication:** Finetune Mistral-7B on provided oMind-SFT; evaluate on ANGST (classification) and MHQA (MCQA). Expect ~9.2% F1 gain over base.  
2. **Supporting QA ablation:** Train identical models with/without supporting QA subset; evaluate on oMind-Chat conversation-level scores. Expect significant drop without it.  
3. **Retrieval source comparison:** Create three SFT variants: (a) only UMLS triplets, (b) only books chunks, (c) combined. Compare factual richness (win rate) and MCQA. Expect combined best, books alone may harm some tasks.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:** What factors explain the divergent effects of DPO preference alignment across different base model architectures (e.g., improvement for Qwen vs. degradation for Mistral) in mental health LLMs?  
*Basis:* Ablation studies show DPO improves oMind-Qwen on multiple tasks but can degrade oMind-Mistral on some classification tasks; impact of removing Supporting QA also varies by model.  
*Why unresolved:* Authors do not investigate causes, such as architecture differences, pretraining data distribution, or optimization behavior.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Controlled experiments analyzing gradient dynamics, loss surfaces, or representation similarity during DPO; studies on how preference data distribution interacts with model capabilities; systematic comparison of model responses to identify where DPO helps or harms.

**Open Question 2:** What is the marginal benefit of the knowledge-grounded generation pipeline (UMLS retrieval + pruning + review) relative to simply increasing task diversity via supporting QA and multi-turn conversations in the SFT dataset?  
*Basis:* The pipeline ensures factual grounding, but components are not disentangled; paper ablates supporting QA but not knowledge grounding itself.  
*Why unresolved:* Cannot tell how much performance gain comes from knowledge-grounded explanations versus task diversity.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Ablation studies training on datasets with identical task distributions but without retrieval-based grounding (e.g., synthetic explanations without KG), measuring factual accuracy, interpretability, and downstream tasks.

**Open Question 3:** Do the expert-annotated turn-level and conversation-level rubrics in oMind-Chat comprehensively capture quality dimensions of mental health dialogues, and do they align with real-world therapeutic outcomes or expert assessments beyond rubric adherence?  
*Basis:* Rubrics were created by four psychology experts based on therapeutic elements (e.g., empathy, active listening) and used to score models, but validity in predicting actual therapeutic effectiveness or holistic expert judgments is unevaluated.  
*Why unresolved:* Rubrics are prescriptive (what should be included) but not validated against external measures like outcomes in real counseling sessions or independent expert ratings.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Human studies where professionals evaluate conversations using rubrics and provide overall therapeutic quality scores; correlation analyses between rubric adherence and user well-being outcomes; expert workshops to iteratively refine and validate the rubric inventory.

## Limitations
- **Reproduction gaps:** Exact LoRA hyperparameters, prompt templates, and per-conversation rubrics are undisclosed, hindering faithful replication.
- **Evaluation dependency:** Heavy reliance on GPT-4o-mini for data generation and GPT-as-a-judge for win rates may introduce unmeasured bias or instability.
- **Knowledge source constraints:** UMLS/DSM-5 coverage may be incomplete for emerging mental health conditions, and NLI filtering (0.8 threshold) risks over-pruning valid paraphrases.
