# Multi-Perspective LLM Annotations for Valid Analyses in Subjective Tasks

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.21404  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21404  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** PDI improves accuracy for the hardest-to-model demographic group (Age 50+) while maintaining 95% coverage, reducing mean absolute error for offensiveness to 5.24% (from >24% LLM-only), and increases human annotations to that group by 19% via adaptive sampling.

## Executive Summary
Subjective tasks like rating politeness or offensiveness exhibit demographic variation in human judgments. Large language models (LLMs) used as annotation proxies tend to reflect some perspectives better than others, and existing error-correction methods assume a single ground truth, ignoring meaningful disagreement. This paper introduces Perspective-Driven Inference (PDI), which treats the distribution of annotations across demographic groups as the estimand. PDI employs adaptive sampling: it predicts LLM annotation error from annotator demographics and directs a limited human annotation budget to groups where LLM proxies are least reliable. Evaluated on politeness and offensiveness ratings, PDI substantially improves accuracy for the hardest-to-model group (Age 50+) while maintaining overall coverage, demonstrating the value of targeted human input when LLM performance varies across perspectives.

## Method Summary
PDI begins with a small burn-in set of human annotations across demographic groups to train an error predictor (e.g., gradient-boosted trees) that estimates the expected absolute error of the LLM’s annotation for a given item and demographic group. Using these error estimates, PDI allocates the remaining human annotation budget via adaptive sampling: items and groups with higher predicted error receive more human annotations. The final estimator aggregates both LLM and human annotations to approximate the true distribution of perspectives for each item. The method explicitly acknowledges that disagreement across demographic groups is signal, not noise, and optimizes the use of costly human labor to correct the LLM where it is weakest.

## Key Results
- PDI improved accuracy for the hardest-to-model group (Age 50+) while maintaining 95% coverage on both politeness and offensiveness tasks.  
- For offensiveness, PDI reduced mean absolute error for Age 50+ to 5.24%, compared to >24% for LLM-only and 5.64% for uniform PPI.  
- Adaptive sampling increased human annotations allocated to Age 50+ by 19% relative to uniform PPI, demonstrating targeted gains when LLM performance varies across groups.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
PDI works because it explicitly models the heterogeneity of LLM error across demographic perspectives and then focuses limited human annotation resources where they are most needed. By treating the distribution of annotations across groups as the target (rather than a single ground truth), PDI preserves the diversity of opinions. The error predictor, trained on initial human data, identifies which items are likely to be annotated poorly by the LLM for a given group. The adaptive sampling policy then up-weights these high-error combinations, effectively reducing the overall bias in the aggregated judgments. This targeted approach yields greater accuracy for the most disadvantaged groups compared to uniform allocation, without sacrificing coverage for others.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Burn-in human annotations (with demographics) → Error Predictor → Error estimates per (item, group)  
LLM annotations → Final Estimator  
Error estimates + Remaining human annotation budget → Sampling Policy → Additional human annotations (targeted by group)  
LLM annotations + Human annotations → Final Estimator → Perspective distribution  

**Critical Path**  
1. Gather burn‑in human annotations across a representative set of demographic groups.  
2. Train the error predictor to map item features and group demographics to expected LLM error.  
3. For each unlabeled item, compute error estimates for all relevant groups.  
4. Compute sampling weights that prioritize groups with higher predicted error while satisfying coverage constraints.  
5. Allocate the remaining human annotation budget according to these weights.  
6. Combine LLM and collected human annotations to produce the final perspective‑driven estimates.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *Burn‑in size:* Larger burn‑in improves error predictor accuracy but consumes budget that could be used adaptively.  
- *Predictor complexity:* Using only demographics keeps the model simple and interpretable; adding text‑derived features may boost performance at the cost of overfitting or reduced generalizability.  
- *Coverage vs. focus:* Imposing minimum coverage constraints ensures all perspectives are represented but may dilute the focus on high‑error groups.  
- *Labor distribution:* Adaptive upsampling concentrates effort on groups where LLMs perform worst, which are often marginalized, raising ethical concerns about fairness and compensation.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Error predictor misestimates error for one or more groups, leading to misallocation of budget and worse performance for those groups.  
- LLM performance disparities across groups are very small, making adaptive gains negligible and overhead unjustified.  
- Sparse representation of certain demographic cells in the burn‑in leads to high‑variance predictions and unstable sampling.  
- Intersectional effects (e.g., older women) are ignored, causing residual bias even after single‑axis adaptation.

**First Experiments (Exactly 3)**  
1. **Main result replication:** Run PDI on the POPQUORN politeness and offensiveness tasks, compare MAE for Age 50+ against LLM‑only and uniform PPI baselines.  
2. **Burn‑in ablation:** Vary the size of the initial human annotation set (e.g., 5%, 10%, 20% of total budget) and measure effect on final MAE and coverage.  
3. **Feature augmentation:** Enrich the error predictor with text‑based features (readability scores, identity‑related terms, LIWC categories) and assess changes in sampling efficiency and required burn‑in size.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How does PDI's performance generalize to a wider range of subjective tasks, domains, and non-demographic stratification axes (e.g., political ideology, professional background), and does the adaptive advantage persist when the LLM proxy exhibits smaller performance disparities across groups?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "Our evaluation focuses on two subjective rating tasks from a single dataset... Broader evaluation across tasks, domains, and non-demographic perspective axes remains an important direction."  
- **Why unresolved:** The current evaluation is limited to politeness/offensiveness ratings on POPQUORN, stratified by gender, age, and education. The framework's effectiveness on more diverse subjective concepts (e.g., moral judgment, emotional valence) and stratification schemes (e.g., ideology, lived experience) is unknown. The adaptive gains may diminish if LLM performance is more uniform across perspectives.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply PDI to multiple new datasets covering varied subjective tasks (e.g., fairness perception, empathy ratings) and stratify by non-demographic axes (e.g., political affiliation, professional role). Compare adaptive gains (reduction in delta for targeted groups) against uniform PPI across a spectrum of LLM performance disparities.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can incorporating richer textual features (e.g., linguistic complexity, topic, perceived identity markers) into the error predictor improve the efficiency of PDI's adaptive sampling, and does this mitigate the need for large burn-in phases?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "The error predictor is a gradient‑boosted tree trained on demographic features alone; richer features, including linguistic properties of the text, could improve predictions and allocate the budget more efficiently."  
- **Why unresolved:** The current error predictor uses only annotator demographics, ignoring potentially predictive signals from the text itself (e.g., whether a comment contains identity‑related terms, its syntactic structure). Richer features could allow earlier, more accurate identification of high‑error instances, reducing the size of the required burn‑in phase.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Augment the error predictor with text‑derived features (e.g., LIWC categories, readability scores, named entity types). In controlled simulations and real‑data experiments, measure changes in sampling efficiency (delta improvement per annotation) and required burn‑in size compared to the demographic‑only predictor.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How can PDI be extended to effectively model intersectional demographic subgroups (e.g., older women, less‑educated minorities) when such intersections are sparse in the data, without requiring annotation budgets that scale exponentially with the number of intersectional cells?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "An annotator who is older and identifies as a particular gender occupies an intersection that our per‑axis treatment does not capture. Modeling intersectional subgroups directly would require larger annotation budgets and raises challenges for the error predictor when intersectional cells are sparse."  
- **Why unresolved:** The current method stratifies by single demographic axes independently, masking intersectional effects. Directly modeling all combinations is infeasible under limited budgets due to the curse of dimensionality and sparse data for rare intersections, which would also degrade the error predictor's reliability.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Develop hierarchical or Bayesian variants of the error predictor that share strength across related intersectional groups (e.g., borrowing information across age groups within a gender). Evaluate on synthetic data with known intersectional dynamics and on real datasets with sufficient intersectional representation, measuring whether intersectional‑specific deltas improve without excessive budget growth.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What are the ethical and practical trade‑offs of PDI's adaptive upsampling in real‑world deployments, particularly regarding the distribution of labor burden across demographic groups and the potential for reinforcing stereotypes through over‑sampling of already marginalized populations?  
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The Ethical Considerations section notes that adaptive upsampling "concentrates annotation effort on groups where LLMs perform worst... these are often already marginalized groups" and that "those annotators bear more of the annotation labor." This trade‑off is acknowledged but not investigated.  
- **Why unresolved:** While PDI aims to correct for LLM bias by soliciting more human input from underrepresented perspectives, this may disproportionately increase the workload and cognitive burden on those same groups. The long‑term implications for annotator well‑being, compensation equity, and whether repeated upsampling could inadvertently signal that certain groups are "harder to model" are unaddressed.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct participatory design studies and surveys with annotators from groups that are frequently upsampled. Measure perceived fairness of task allocation, satisfaction with compensation, and awareness of the adaptive rationale. Develop operational guidelines for dynamic compensation that accounts for upsampling rates and annotator feedback.

## Limitations
- Generalizability unknown beyond politeness/offensiveness tasks to other subjective domains.  
- Intersectional demographic effects (e.g., age + gender + race) not evaluated; PDI may not handle overlapping group perspectives.  
- No statistical significance testing or variance reporting for error reductions; robustness unclear.  
- Cost‑benefit trade‑offs of the adaptive sampling budget allocation not analyzed.  
- Comparison limited to uniform PPI and LLM‑only; not benchmarked against other disagreement‑aware methods.

## Confidence
- *PDI improves accuracy for hardest‑to‑model group (Age 50+)*: **Medium**  
- *MAE reduction for Age 50+ to 5.24%*: **High**  
- *Adaptive sampling increases human annotations to Age 50+ by 19%*: **High**
