# Learning on the Fly: Replay-Based Continual Object Perception for Indoor Drones

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.13440
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13440
- **Reference count:** 35
- **Primary result:** Forgetting-Aware Replay (FAR) achieves 82.96% mAP50-95 with only 5% replay memory in class-incremental object detection on a new indoor drone dataset.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles class-incremental object detection (CLOD) for resource-constrained indoor drones, where learning new object classes must not catastrophically forget previously learned ones. The authors introduce a novel temporally coherent indoor UAV video dataset (14,400 frames) with pseudo-labels for five classes. They evaluate three replay-based continual learning strategies—Experience Replay (ER), Maximally Interfered Retrieval (MIR), and Forgetting-Aware Replay (FAR)—using a lightweight YOLOv11-nano detector under tight memory budgets (5–10%). The key finding is that FAR, which selects replay samples based on their performance drop, outperforms the others at the smallest budget, achieving an average accuracy (mAP50-95) of 82.96% with only 5% replay. Grad-CAM analysis further reveals that in human-drone mixed scenes, the model’s attention shifts toward humans after learning the human class, correlating with degraded drone localization performance; FAR mitigates this attention diffusion.

## Method Summary
The authors construct a dataset by collecting indoor UAV videos, applying GroundingSAM for pseudo-labeling (confidence ≥0.75, IoU≥0.5), and manually verifying a subset (98.6% first-pass acceptance). The dataset is split into five class-incremental tasks. For continual learning, they maintain a fixed-capacity replay buffer (5–10% of previous task data) and three selection strategies: ER (random sampling), MIR (selecting samples with lowest current recall@0.5), and FAR (selecting samples with largest recall drop from the checkpoint when those classes were learned). The detector is YOLOv11-nano, trained with augmentations (HSV, mosaic, mixup, copy-paste) and early stopping (patience 3). After each task, the model is evaluated on all seen tasks to compute average accuracy (ACC) and backward transfer (BWT). Grad-CAM visualizations analyze attention shifts in mixed scenes and correlate them with localization errors.

## Key Results
- FAR achieves the highest average accuracy (mAP50-95) of 82.96% with a 5% replay budget, outperforming ER and MIR.
- Under tight memory constraints (5–10%), FAR shows the least catastrophic forgetting (most positive BWT) and maintains stable per-task performance across the sequence.
- Grad-CAM analysis shows that in human-drone mixed scenes, attention shifts toward humans after learning the human class, and this shift is associated with increased localization error for drones; FAR mitigates this attention diffusion compared to ER.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Replaying a small subset (5–10%) of prior-task data mitigates catastrophic forgetting in class-incremental object detection on resource-constrained drones.
- **Mechanism:** During sequential task learning, previously seen samples are stored in a bounded memory buffer and interleaved with current-task training. This re-exposure stabilizes weights related to older classes by counteracting distribution shift across tasks.
- **Core assumption:** Forgetting is primarily driven by distribution shift; re-exposure to past data distributions preserves learned representations.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract reports FAR’s high accuracy with low replay; Section 3.2 describes the replay buffer and interleaving; cited literature broadly supports replay as an effective CL strategy.
- **Break condition:** If the replay buffer contains non-representative or noisy samples (e.g., from pseudo-label errors), stabilization may fail.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Forgetting-Aware Replay (FAR) selects replay samples that exhibit the largest performance drop under the current model, thus preserving past knowledge more efficiently under tight memory budgets.
- **Mechanism:** After each task, the system caches image-level recall@0.5 for a pool of prior images using the model checkpoint from when those classes were learned. When a new task arrives, the same images are rescored, and those with the greatest recall decrease (max(0, recall_baseline − recall_current)) are prioritized for replay.
- **Core assumption:** A sample’s drop in retrieval performance correlates with the extent of forgetting for the classes it contains; prioritizing these maximizes retention per unit of memory.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 3.2 detail FAR’s recall-drop criterion and its superior performance at 5% budget; MIR (which uses only current recall) is outperformed by FAR.
- **Break condition:** Recall@0.5 may be a noisy proxy for true task retention due to detector instability, leading to misprioritization.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** In mixed scenes containing humans and drones, the model’s attention shifts toward humans as new tasks are learned, which correlates with degraded drone localization performance.
- **Mechanism:** Grad-CAM visualizations show that after learning the “human” class (Task 5), activation heatmaps in scenes with both humans and drones concentrate on human figures. This saliency shift likely co-opts feature extraction for the high-frequency human class, reducing sensitivity to drone-specific features and increasing localization error for drones.
- **Core assumption:** Attention heatmaps from the final convolutional layer reflect feature importance; shifts indicate representational interference.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions the association; Section 4.1 quantifies attention concentration on humans and shows ER’s attention degrades while FAR maintains localized attention.
- **Break condition:** The association is correlational; other factors (e.g., background clutter, motion blur) could jointly cause both attention shifts and localization errors.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Class-Incremental Learning (CIL)**
  - **Why needed here:** The core problem is that drones must learn new object classes sequentially without forgetting old ones, requiring understanding of catastrophic forgetting, task boundaries, and metrics like ACC and BWT.
  - **Quick check question:** If a model trained on Task A then Task B forgets 30% of Task A’s performance, is this catastrophic forgetting or normal plasticity?
- **Concept: One-Stage Object Detection (YOLO)**
  - **Why needed here:** YOLOv11-nano is the deployed detector; understanding its anchor-free design, speed-accuracy trade-offs, and why lightweight variants are more prone to forgetting (e.g., due to regression noise) is essential.
  - **Quick check question:** Why might a lightweight YOLO variant be more susceptible to forgetting than a heavier two-stage detector?
- **Concept: Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM)**
  - **Why needed here:** Used to diagnose model attention shifts that correlate with performance drops; requires understanding how gradients flow to the final conv layer to produce class-discriminative maps.
  - **Quick check question:** If Grad-CAM shows no change in activation patterns after sequential tasks, does that guarantee no forgetting occurred?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Raw videos → frame sampling → GroundingSAM pseudo-labeling (conf≥0.75, IoU≥0.5) → manual verification (98.6% first-pass accept) → task-split by class → sequential training with replay buffer (ER/MIR/FAR) → YOLOv11-nano training with augmentations (HSV, mosaic, mixup, copy-paste) → early stopping (patience 3) → evaluation (mAP50-95 per task) → Grad-CAM analysis.
- **Critical path:** Data collection and annotation → define task sequence → initialize replay buffer → for each task: train with current data + selected replay → evaluate on all seen tasks → analyze attention via Grad-CAM.
- **Design tradeoffs:** 
  - Memory budget vs. retention: 5% buffer gives 82.96% ACC (FAR) vs. ~88.7% at 50% (all methods converge).
  - Pseudo-label noise vs. annotation cost: GroundingSAM scales labeling but residual 1.4% errors may accumulate.
  - Replay selection complexity: FAR requires storing checkpoints and rescoring pools, minor overhead vs. ER’s simplicity.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Naïve fine-tuning: steep drop in mAP for early tasks.
  - ER at 5%: attention diffusion after Task 3, high negative BWT (-11.65).
  - FAR at 5%: possible replay of outlier samples if recall drop is noisy; monitor per-task mAP variance.
  - Grad-CAM: uniform attention in mixed scenes predicts poor drone localization.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Baseline replay comparison:** Train Naïve/ER/MIR/FAR with 5% and 10% buffer; plot task-wise mAP to confirm FAR’s lead at smallest budget.
  2. **Grad-CAM correlation:** For mixed human-drone scenes, compute attention entropy vs. drone localization error across tasks; expect positive correlation.
  3. **Real-world transfer test:** Deploy the 5% FAR model in unseen environments (different flooring, outdoor) with a PID tracker; measure target re-acquisition rate after occlusion.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
How can class-incremental object detection be effectively scaled to 20+ object categories under extremely tight replay memory budgets (e.g., <5%)? The study evaluates only 5 classes; performance degradation at scale with more subtle inter-class distinctions under minimal replay is unknown.

### Open Question 2
Can replay-based continual learning detectors be adapted to handle significant domain shifts, such as transitioning from indoor to outdoor environments within the same incremental sequence? The dataset and evaluation are solely indoor; it is unclear if catastrophic forgetting is exacerbated when tasks themselves represent distinct domains.

### Open Question 3
Does a hybrid strategy that dynamically allocates replay memory based on measured forgetting (e.g., per-class BWT) outperform fixed-budget heuristics like FAR under the same memory constraints? FAR uses a static, uniform buffer budget and a forgetting-aware selection heuristic but does not allocate more memory to more forgettable classes/samples.

### Open Question 4
Can attention regularization (e.g., constraining Grad-CAM saliency maps) during replay mitigate the observed “human-dominant” attention shift in mixed scenes, thereby improving drone localization? The paper diagnoses the problem but does not test any mitigation.

### Open Question 5
To what extent does residual noise in the semi-automatic pseudo-labels (∼1.4% error) accumulate catastrophically across incremental learning steps, and does it disproportionately affect certain replay strategies? It is unclear if MIR (which selects interfered samples) might be more vulnerable to such noise than ER or FAR.

## Limitations
- **Real-world generalization:** The indoor UAV dataset may not capture the full diversity of real-world deployments (e.g., varied lighting, clutter, outdoor transitions); performance gains may not transfer directly.
- **Replay sample representativeness:** Pseudo-labeling introduces a small noise floor (1.4% error); it is unclear if FAR-selected “high-drop” samples might amplify these artifacts under tight budgets.
- **Causal link for attention shift:** The correlation between Grad-CAM attention shifts and drone localization degradation is compelling but correlational; confounding factors (e.g., scene composition) could explain both.

## Confidence
- **FAR's efficiency under tight memory budgets (5–10%):** High. Supported by clear experimental comparisons against ER and MIR.
- **Dataset contribution (temporally coherent indoor UAV video for CLOD):** High. Addresses a stated gap with a described collection and verification process.
- **Recall-drop as an optimal proxy for sample importance in FAR:** Medium. The mechanism is well-defined and outperforms MIR, but its intrinsic optimality is not theoretically proven.
- **Attention shift (via Grad-CAM) as explanatory for localization degradation:** Low-Medium. Evidence is internal and correlational; Grad-CAM is an established but not definitive diagnostic for detection performance.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-Dataset Generalization Test:** Train the 5% FAR model on the proposed dataset and zero-shot evaluate it on a distinct indoor drone dataset (e.g., subset of VisDrone or UAVDT). Measure mAP drop to quantify overfitting.
2. **Replay Buffer Ablation & Noise Sensitivity:** Replace FAR’s selection metric with random sampling at 5% budget. Additionally, inject controlled label noise (e.g., 5% class flip) into the stored buffer and measure relative degradation of FAR vs. ER.
3. **Interventional Grad-CAM Study:** For a held-out set of mixed human-drone scenes, manipulate model attention (e.g., via occlusion or adversarial perturbation targeting human regions) and measure causal effect on drone localization error. If attention diversion is causal, forcing attention away from humans should degrade drone detection more.
