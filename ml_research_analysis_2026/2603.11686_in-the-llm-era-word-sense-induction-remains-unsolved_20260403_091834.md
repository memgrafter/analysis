# In the LLM era, Word Sense Induction remains unsolved

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.11686  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11686  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** No fully unsupervised WSI method beats the "one cluster per lemma" baseline on a natural corpus; Wiktionary-based semi-supervised augmentation achieves new SOTA (76.0 F‑B3), but WSI remains unsolved for realistic scenarios.

## Executive Summary
This paper identifies a critical evaluation flaw in Word Sense Induction (WSI): standard datasets like SemEval 2010/2013 have unrealistic polysemy and frequency distributions, causing systems to overfit and report over‑optimistic results. The authors introduce SemCor‑WSI, a new benchmark derived from SemCor that respects natural corpus statistics. They evaluate clustering of contextualized embeddings, direct LLM prompting, and data augmentation. The strong "one cluster per lemma" baseline (73.6 F‑B3) reveals that many lemmas are effectively monosemous in natural text. No fully unsupervised approach surpasses this baseline. However, by leveraging the English Wiktionary for fine‑tuning, must‑link constraints, and cluster‑count estimation, they achieve a new SOTA of 76.0 F‑B3. Direct LLM prompting performs poorly, indicating current LLMs cannot solve WSI as framed. The core conclusion is that WSI for realistic corpora remains an open challenge, with lexicon‑based resources currently providing the most effective signal.

## Method Summary
The paper conducts a comprehensive study of WSI under realistic conditions. It first constructs SemCor‑WSI, a full‑corpus WSI dataset from SemCor 3.0 that preserves the original corpus’s sense and lemma frequency distributions, contrasting with the biased SemEval benchmarks. Three families of approaches are evaluated: (1) clustering contextualized embeddings from BERT and fine‑tuned variants, using agglomerative or X‑means clustering; (2) direct prompting of LLMs (GPT‑4o, Llama) to assign cluster labels; and (3) semi‑supervised augmentation using external resources—English Wiktionary for must‑link constraints, sense‑count hints, and fine‑tuning, plus raw corpora (WikiBooks) for densifying the instance space. Performance is measured with instance‑weighted F‑B3, chosen for its sensitivity to cluster quality properties. The results show that simple baselines exploiting natural monosemy are strong, and only when Wiktionary is integrated does performance improve meaningfully.

## Key Results
- The "one cluster per lemma" (1cpl) baseline achieves 73.6 F‑B3 on SemCor‑WSI, indicating many lemmas are monosemous in natural corpora.
- No fully unsupervised method (including prior SOTA PolyLM) exceeds the 1cpl baseline on this realistic dataset.
- Semi‑supervised augmentation with Wiktionary (fine‑tuning, must‑link constraints, cluster‑count from senses) achieves 76.0 F‑B3 on the test set, a 3.3% improvement over previous SOTA.
- Direct LLM prompting yields low and highly variable scores (e.g., GPT‑4o: 43.1 F‑B3), demonstrating LLMs’ current inability to perform WSI as evaluated.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1  
- **Claim:** WSI systems overfitted to the unrealistically high polysemy of SemEval datasets fail on natural distributions because they assume high polysemy, whereas in natural corpora many lemmas are effectively monosemous.  
- **Mechanism:** SemEval 2010/2013 over‑represent polysemous lemmas and fix instance counts per lemma, leading systems to learn patterns that do not generalize. The 1cpl baseline works well on natural data precisely because many lemmas have a single dominant sense.  
- **Core assumption:** Full‑corpus WSI should reflect the true polysemy and lemma‑frequency distributions found in real corpora.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - The paper shows SemEval’s average polysemy (3.79) is much higher than SemCor‑WSI’s (~1.9).  
  - Table 11 demonstrates the 1cpl baseline achieves 73.6 F‑B3 on SemCor‑WSI, confirming widespread monosemy.  
  - Section 3.1 details the biased lemma selection and instance counts in SemEval versus natural corpora.  
- **Break condition:** If the target corpus has abnormally high polysemy (e.g., specialized scientific text), the 1cpl baseline degrades, and systems that explicitly detect polysemy become necessary.

### Mechanism 2  
- **Claim:** Adding unlabeled examples from external corpora (e.g., WikiBooks) to the set of instances to cluster improves WSI performance by densifying the instance space and providing more context for similarity computation.  
- **Mechanism:** Clustering contextualized embeddings benefits from more instances per lemma: it creates additional similarity links, reduces noise, and resolves undefined silhouette scores for small‑n lemmas. Attested corpus examples are more effective than LLM‑generated ones because they reflect natural usage distributions.  
- **Core assumption:** Added examples are drawn from the same underlying sense distribution as the target corpus; otherwise, domain shift introduces noise.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Abstract states data augmentation is beneficial and ~100–150 examples per lemma from raw corpora is optimal.  
  - Tables 4 & 5 show monotonic improvement with more WikiBooks examples (e.g., BERT‑l‑u reaches 73.6 F‑B3 with 150).  
  - Table 16 compares sources; WikiBooks consistently outperforms LLM‑generated sentences.  
- **Break condition:** If the added corpus is topically mismatched (e.g., medical text for newswire), the extra instances may represent different senses and degrade clustering quality.

### Mechanism 3  
- **Claim:** Leveraging English Wiktionary in three ways—fine‑tuning embeddings, providing must‑link constraints, and determining cluster counts—surpasses the 1cpl baseline and achieves a new SOTA (76.0 F‑B3).  
- **Mechanism:**  
  - Fine‑tuning BERT on Wiktionary examples via contrastive learning (BERT‑Wikt) enhances sense‑discriminative power.  
  - Must‑link constraints force instances sharing a Wiktionary sense into the same cluster during agglomerative clustering (by setting pairwise distance to zero).  
  - Using the number of Wiktionary senses to set the number of clusters (AGwikt) better estimates true polysemy than silhouette.  
- **Core assumption:** Wiktionary’s sense groupings, though coarse and potentially mismatched to WordNet, provide a useful signal for the target sense distribution.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Abstract notes Wiktionary capitalizing helps and surpasses previous SOTA by 3.3%.  
  - Tables 4‑6 show the best configuration (BERT‑l‑Wikt + AGwikt + MustLink + 100WB) reaches 76.0 F‑B3 on test.  
  - Consistent boosts when using Wiktionary‑derived cluster counts and constraints across tables.  
- **Break condition:** For languages or domains with sparse Wiktionary coverage or divergent sense granularity, constraints may force incorrect merges and hurt performance. A mismatch between lexicon sense inventory and corpus senses also makes the cluster‑count hint misleading.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Word Sense Induction (WSI) vs. Word Sense Disambiguation (WSD)**  
  - *Why needed:* WSI discovers senses without a predefined inventory, while WSD disambiguates to a fixed dictionary. This distinction explains why evaluation biases arise and why lexicon resources can be repurposed for WSI.  
  - *Quick check:* What is the key difference between WSI and WSD, and why does WSI avoid needing sense‑annotated training data?

- **Concept: Fuzzy B‑Cubed (F‑B3) evaluation metric**  
  - *Why needed:* F‑B3 is uniquely sensitive to homogeneity, completeness, rag‑bag, and size‑vs‑quality properties, making it suitable for full‑corpus WSI where instance‑weighted scoring matters. Understanding it clarifies why earlier SemEval results (using V‑measure/NMI) are not comparable.  
  - *Quick check:* Why is F‑B3 preferred over NMI for evaluating WSI on a full‑corpus scenario with skewed sense distributions?

- **Concept: Semi‑supervised clustering with must‑link constraints**  
  - *Why needed:* The best results use must‑link constraints from Wiktionary. Knowing how such constraints integrate with agglomerative clustering (by setting pairwise distances to zero) is key to reproducing the architecture.  
  - *Quick check:* How do must‑link constraints modify the clustering process, and why might they improve sense induction when derived from a lexicon?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:**  
  Data extraction -> Embedding model -> Clustering engine -> Data augmentation -> Evaluation

- **Critical path:**  
  a. Prepare SemCor‑WSI datasets (run extraction scripts to match distributional properties).  
  b. Select embedding model and layer (tune on dev set).  
  c. Choose clustering algorithm and cluster‑count strategy.  
  d. If using must‑link, preprocess Wiktionary to extract same‑sense example pairs.  
  e. Run clustering on the (augmented) instance set, parse outputs, evaluate.  
  f. Iterate: adjust augmentation, hyperparameters, or constraints.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - **AGsilh vs. X‑means:** AGsilh is deterministic but may be undefined for n ≤ 2; X‑means handles small n but introduces nondeterminism requiring multiple runs.  
  - **Silhouette vs. Wiktionary count:** Silhouette is fully unsupervised but unstable for low‑polysemy lemmas; Wiktionary count introduces lexicon bias but yields consistent gains.  
  - **Corpus source for augmentation:** WikiBooks offers natural, high‑volume examples; LLM‑generated are cheap but less natural; Wiktionary examples are sense‑annotated but literary.  
  - **Fine‑tuning vs. constraint only:** Fine‑tuning BERT on Wiktionary improves embeddings but requires compute; adding only must‑link constraints is cheaper but less effective.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - F‑B3 drops below the 1cpl baseline (~73.6 on dev) → configuration not capturing senses.  
  - High variance across X‑means runs (std > 1.0) → unstable clustering; switch to AGsilh or increase runs.  
  - Silhouette score undefined for many lemmas (n ≤ 2) → default to 1 cluster; consider augmentation to increase n.  
  - Parsing LLM prompts fails (non‑standard outputs) → need robust regex or prompt tuning.  
  - Forgetting to set must‑link distance to 0 in clustering → no improvement.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Baseline replication:** Compute 1cpl F‑B3 on SemCor‑WSI dev to establish the strong baseline (expect ~73.6).  
  2. **Basic clustering:** Use BERT‑large‑uncased with AGsilh (no augmentation, no constraints). Expect ~71 F‑B3; verify layer choice (typically 20–24).  
  3. **Augmentation sanity check:** Add 100 WikiBooks examples per lemma to the previous setup; expect F‑B3 to rise to ~73.5, demonstrating the data‑augmentation effect.

## Limitations
- Evaluation is limited to English (SemCor); no cross‑lingual or multi‑domain evidence.
- Best system depends on English Wiktionary; coverage and sense granularity may not align with other corpora or languages.
- Reproducibility hindered by missing fine‑tuning hyperparameters, LLM prompting specifics, and bootstrap code.
- Only one natural corpus (SemCor) is used as testbed; findings might not generalize.

## Confidence
- **C1 (SemEval bias & 1cpl strength):** High – supported by distribution analysis and robust baseline.
- **C2 (Raw corpus augmentation):** Medium – improvement shown with WikiBooks only; other sources unverified.
- **C3 (Wiktionary‑enhanced WSI):** Medium – gains consistent but resource‑dependent; may fail where Wiktionary is sparse.
- **C4 (LLM underperformance):** Medium – low scores across models but prompt tuning could shift results.
- **Overall (WSI unsolved):** High – comprehensive negative evidence for unsupervised methods.
