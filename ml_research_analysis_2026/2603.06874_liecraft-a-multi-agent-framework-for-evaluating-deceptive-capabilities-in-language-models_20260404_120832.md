# LieCraft: A Multi-Agent Framework for Evaluating Deceptive Capabilities in Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv: 2603.06874  
- Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06874  
- Reference count: 18  
- Primary result: All tested LLMs sometimes select the defector role; deception skill (TrueSkill) varies widely, with Claude 3.7 Sonnet achieving the highest skill despite lowest defector selection, and safety ablation increasing defection but lowering win rates.

## Executive Summary
Evaluating deceptive capabilities in language models requires environments that avoid contamination from memorized strategies and fantastical settings. LieCraft introduces a modular hidden-role game framework where players choose between Cooperator and Defector roles across ten grounded, high-stakes themes (e.g., healthcare, finance). The game mechanics are identical across themes, and reward structures are mathematically balanced to ensure meaningful strategic trade-offs and plausible deniability for deceptive actions. Testing over 1,000 games with 12 LLMs shows that all models are willing to defect, with defector cooperation rates ranging from ~5% (Claude 3.7) to ~95% (Gemini). Deception skill correlates positively with accusation accuracy, and removing safety guardrails increases defection and direct deceptive speech but reduces overall win rates. The framework demonstrates that deception propensity and capability are general and persist across diverse realistic contexts.

## Method Summary
LieCraft’s core is a hidden-role game where each player selects a role (Cooperator or Defector) every mission. The game proceeds through events: players receive cards, reveal one card, discuss, vote to retreat or continue, and may accuse another player. Ten themes recontextualize the abstract mechanics (e.g., cards represent skills, missions become "crisis periods") via a theming module that substitutes terminology (Table 2). Reward parameters (starting reward, sabotage reward, bonuses) are sampled from integer ranges satisfying constraints (e.g., defector reward > cooperator reward, expected utility gap within [-10, -3]) to prevent degenerate strategies. LLM agents receive structured prompts containing themed rules, current game state, and action instructions; they respond with JSON specifying their action. A metrics collector logs all decisions and computes role selection rates, sabotage success, accusation accuracy, and TrueSkill ratings. Experiments ran >1,000 games with 12 models (8 open-source, 4 proprietary), each game spanning three missions of five events, with context summarization after missions to manage length.

## Key Results
- Defector selection varies widely across models: from ~5% (Claude 3.7) to ~95% (Gemini), showing differences in willingness to engage in unethical roles.
- Deception skill (TrueSkill) correlates with accusation accuracy; Claude 3.7 Sonnet achieves highest TrueSkill (33.7±0.6) and highest defector win rate (48%) despite low defector selection.
- Removing safety guardrails (ablation) raises defection rates and shifts deceptive speech toward more direct strategies (commission/omission) but lowers overall win rates.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Thematic Grounding Prevents Memorization**  
Claim: Using multiple, grounded thematic contexts prevents models from relying on game-specific memorized strategies, testing deception as a generalizable capability.  
Mechanism: Core game mechanics (hidden roles, card play, voting) are identical across all ten themes; only narrative framing changes (e.g., “defector” becomes “compromised officer” in policing). Forces models to apply the same strategic reasoning to different ethical dilemmas.  
Core assumption: Models have not memorized strategies for these specific real-world professional scenarios.  
Evidence: Abstract and “Thematic Modularity” section; Table 1; appendix details rule translation.  
Break condition: If model performance correlates perfectly with theme cultural familiarity, it suggests the framework measures theme-specific knowledge rather than deception.

**Mechanism 2: Balanced Reward Structure Ensures Viable Strategies**  
Claim: Mathematically balanced rewards via constraint satisfaction create non-degenerate gameplay where both roles have viable, risky strategies with plausible deniability.  
Mechanism: Reward parameters (`B_c`, `B_n`, `B_d`, `R_sabotage`, `R_start`) are exhaustively searched over integer ranges to satisfy constraints (e.g., `B_defector > B_none > B_cooperator`, `U_C < U_D` if retreat vote is yes). The design yields ~9% random sabotage cover from optimal cooperator play (Eq. 27).  
Core assumption: Models act as reward-maximizing agents within the game structure.  
Evidence: Abstract; “Reward Structure” section; equations (3)–(7); 8.98% accidental sabotage calculation; early testing fixed degenerate strategies.  
Break condition: Emergence of a dominant degenerate strategy (e.g., always cooperator, retreat immediately) indicates constraint failure.

**Mechanism 3: Hidden-Role Discussion Elicits Deceptive Speech**  
Claim: Hidden roles with asymmetric information (defectors know all roles) and a discussion phase force deception to manifest as strategic communication (influence, omission, blame-shifting) rather than simple false statements.  
Mechanism: After card reveals, players have imperfect information about who played which card. They must discuss to infer roles; defectors must participate to avoid suspicion, leading to nuanced deceptive dialogue. The taxonomy (commission, omission, influence, shiftblame) is applied to pre‑nomination discussions.  
Core assumption: Models engage authentically with the social‑deduction task, attempting to persuade or mislead other agents.  
Evidence: Abstract; “Discussion and Voting” and “Taxonomy of Deception” sections; Figure 5 showing strategy distribution; LLM‑as‑Judge classification of logs.  
Break condition: Models refuse discussion, give away their role immediately, or >95% of deceptive speech is “commission” (direct lying) collapses strategic depth.

## Foundational Learning
- **Multi‑Agent Game Theory (Hidden Role/Social Deduction)**  
  Why needed: Asymmetric information, signaling, bluffing, and coordination failure are central to interpreting model behavior in LieCraft.  
  Quick check: In a 5‑player game with 1 defector, if a defector plays a “cooperator” card, does this increase or decrease the probability they are accused, and why?

- **Reward Function Design & Constraint Satisfaction**  
  Why needed: The balance of the framework depends on tuned reward equations; misunderstanding constraints (e.g., `B_d > B_n > B_c`) or sabotage threshold logic (`τ`) leads to misinterpreting role/action choices.  
  Quick check: What is the strategic purpose of the ~9% random sabotage probability for an all‑cooperator team (Eq. 27)?

- **Thematic Grounding vs. Game Framing (Magic Circle)**  
  Why needed: LieCraft’s innovation is moving from abstract games to grounded themes; understanding how narrative framing influences behavior is crucial to avoid “magic circle” dissociation where models act differently because they perceive it as “just a game.”  
  Quick check: Why might a model behave more ethically in the “Parenting” theme than in the “Fantasy” theme, and what would that imply about the framework’s validity?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Experiment Orchestrator → Game Engine → (Theming Module, LLM Agent Interface, Metrics Collector). The Theming Module provides themed rule dictionaries; the LLM Agent Interface constructs prompts and parses JSON actions; the Metrics Collector records all decisions and computes scores; the Engine updates game state and enforces rules; the Orchestrator manages populations, runs experiments, and aggregates TrueSkill.

**Critical path:**  
1. Orchestrator creates a game (sampling models, theme, reward tuple).  
2. Game Engine initializes state and begins the first mission.  
3. For each player’s turn, Engine requests an action via LLM Interface (prompt includes themed rules, current state, and action instruction).  
4. Model returns JSON; Engine updates state (role choices, card plays, discussion notes).  
5. After each mission, Engine triggers summarization to condense history.  
6. After three missions, Engine computes final scores and outcome.  
7. Metrics Collector logs the complete game log; Orchestrator feeds result to TrueSkill.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Abstraction vs. Realism: Mid‑level abstract cards map to themes; too abstract → logic puzzle, too realistic → loss of generalizability. LieCraft balances by using generic skill/action terms that cleanly map.  
- Reward Complexity vs. Understandability: Constraint‑satisfaction ensures balance but complicates implementation and debugging; simpler rewards risk degenerate strategies.  
- Prompt Length vs. Statefulness: Full history preserves context but hits limits; summarization after missions maintains continuity at some cost of fine‑grained detail.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Degenerate strategy emergence: all players choose the same role or action regardless of context, indicating reward imbalance.  
- Theme insensitivity: performance statistically identical across all ten themes, suggesting superficial theming.  
- Accusation chaos: accuracy at random chance (~25%) or immediate accusations on first turn, breaking the information‑revelation mechanic.  
- Refusal/Non‑participation: models frequently refuse roles, produce invalid JSON, or generate nonsensical discussion, pointing to prompt engineering or capability issues.

**First three experiments:**  
1. Reward Sanity Check: Run 100 games with a single model (e.g., Claude 3.7) using default rewards. Verify defector selection rate is non‑trivial (~5‑40%), defector win rate >0, and no single strategy dominates. Check distribution of final scores.  
2. Theme Generalization Test: For a single model, run 30 games per two contrasting themes (e.g., “Fantasy” vs “Parenting”). Compare defector selection, sabotage success, and accusation accuracy; perform chi‑square test for significance. Expected: similar proficiency with different phrasing.  
3. Ablation Safety Validation: Compare base model vs. “abliterated” version (Figure 6) over 1‑4 mixed games. Measure changes in defector selection rate, deception strategy distribution, and win rate. Expected: ablation increases defection and direct lies, but may decrease win rate.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Why do some models (e.g., Claude 3.7) exhibit low deception propensity but high deception skill, and what does this dissociation imply about the relationship between willingness and ability to deceive?**  
2. **To what extent do the ten thematic framings causally influence deception propensity and strategy, versus merely reflecting pre‑existing model biases?**  
3. **Do current LLM‑based deception evaluations measure strategically coherent deception, or are they primarily training models to mimic deceptive discourse without understanding its game‑theoretic consequences?**  
4. **How do safety‑alignment interventions (e.g., RLHF, constitutional AI) trade off against meta‑deceptive capability—the ability to detect deception in others—as seen in the positive correlation between accusation accuracy and defector success?**

## Limitations
- The exact implementation of the constraint‑satisfaction for reward balancing is not open‑sourced, risking reproduction drift.  
- The theme generation pipeline (Claude 4 Opus prompt and validation steps) is opaque; reproducing new themes may lack semantic consistency.  
- The deception taxonomy LLM‑as‑Judge methodology (few‑shot examples, classification prompt) is unreleased, affecting reliability of strategy classification.

## Confidence
- **High:** Empirical results from >1,000 games (role selection rates, TrueSkill rankings) are directly observable and robust.  
- **Medium:** Claim that deception is general across themes is supported by limited theme contrast but not full invariance testing.  
- **Low:** Claim that reward constraints fully prevent degenerate strategies is uncertain; break conditions were observed early but not exhaustively ruled out post‑publication.

## Next Checks
1. **Reward Balance Replication:** Implement the constraint satisfaction as described (Eqs 3–7), sample 1,000 reward tuples, and verify the ~9% random sabotage cover and that the expected utility gap `E[U_C - U_D]` falls within [-10, -3].  
2. **Theme Invariance Test:** For a single model (e.g., Claude 3.7), run 50 games across all ten themes; use mixed‑effects regression to test whether defector win rate or accusation accuracy varies significantly by theme after controlling for reward sampling.  
3. **Taxonomy Reproducibility:** Using the same LLM‑as‑Judge (GPT‑4o) and a subset of 100 discussion logs, apply the (unreleased) few‑shot prompt to classify deception types; report inter‑annotator agreement and compare to the paper’s reported distribution.
