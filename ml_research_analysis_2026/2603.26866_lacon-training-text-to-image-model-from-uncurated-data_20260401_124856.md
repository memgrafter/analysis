# LACON: Training Text-to-Image Model from Uncurated Data

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.26866
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26866
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: LACON-A on Sana-1.6B (512×512) reaches GenEval 71.6 vs. 68.0 for the best filtered baseline (Baseline-B), DPG 78.1 vs. 76.1, and FID 11.2 vs. 13.1.

## Executive Summary
Standard text-to-image training often discards large volumes of uncurated data through aggressive filtering, assuming they degrade quality. LACON challenges this by treating quality signals (aesthetic, watermark, clarity, entropy, luminance) as explicit conditioning labels, enabling a single model to learn the full spectrum from low to high quality. Applied to both diffusion (Sana) and autoregressive (Qwen) backbones, LACON consistently outperforms models trained on filtered subsets or raw data without conditioning. The approach also supports fine-grained quality control during inference via independent guidance per attribute, while reducing knowledge gaps for long-tail concepts.

## Method Summary
Given an uncurated dataset of image–text pairs, LACON first annotates each image with five quality metrics using pre‑trained predictors or simple statistics. For each attribute, a Gaussian‑weighted cluster centroid (GCC) embedding converts the continuous score into a soft token sequence via radial basis function affinities over learnable centroids. These attribute embeddings are concatenated and injected into the generative model (either a diffusion model trained with flow matching or an autoregressive transformer). The model is trained on the full dataset to learn the conditional distribution \(p(x|y,s)\). At inference, LACON‑S fixes the quality vector to a high target, while LACON‑A applies classifier‑free guidance independently to each attribute, letting users adjust specific quality aspects.

## Key Results
- On Sana‑1.6B (512×512), LACON‑A outperforms the best filtered baseline (Baseline‑B) with GenEval 71.6 vs. 68.0, DPG 78.1 vs. 76.1, and FID 11.2 vs. 13.1.
- The method generalizes to autoregressive models (Qwen), showing consistent improvements over filtered baselines.
- LACON provides fine‑grained quality controllability at inference and narrows the performance gap on long‑tail concepts.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: LACON improves text-to-image performance by learning from uncurated data via quality signal conditioning, rather than discarding low‑quality samples.
- **Mechanism**: The filter‑first paradigm discards data based on quality thresholds; LACON instead extracts multiple quality metrics and uses them as continuous condition labels during training. The model learns the joint distribution \(p(x|y,s)\), capturing both high‑ and low‑quality patterns and reducing knowledge gaps.
- **Core assumption**: Quality signals are meaningful and can be automatically computed; conditioning on them disentangles quality from content.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] “Instead of filtering, LACON re‑purposes quality signals… as explicit, quantitative condition labels. The generative model is then trained to learn the full spectrum of data quality, from bad to good.”
  - [section 3.2] Detailed labeling and conditional training pipeline.
  - [corpus] *Ambient Diffusion Omni: Training Good Models with Bad Data* (arXiv:2506.10038) similarly reports benefits from non‑curated data.
- **Break condition**: If quality signals are noisy, biased, or misaligned with human judgment, conditioning may degrade outputs.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: LACON uses a Gaussian‑weighted cluster centroid (GCC) embedding to convert continuous quality scores into soft token assignments, enabling smooth and expressive condition representation.
- **Mechanism**: For each attribute, a set of fixed anchor points partitions the score range. Learnable centroid tokens are associated with each anchor. A sample’s score is mapped via Gaussian RBF affinities to all anchors, producing a weighted sum of centroids as the embedding. This yields overlapping bins and smooth gradients, avoiding the pitfalls of hard binning or linear interpolation.
- **Core assumption**: Modeling score distributions with overlapping Gaussian kernels yields more stable, sample‑efficient learning than discrete assignments.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 3.2] “We propose a flexible embedding strategy… Gaussian Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel… soft vector representation.”
  - [section 4.5] Ablations show GCC outperforms linear interpolation, discrete binning, and Fourier features on GenEval, DPG, FID.
  - [corpus] No directly comparable work in the neighbor set; the soft token assignment via RBF is novel in this context.
- **Break condition**: Too few anchors limit expressiveness; too many may overfit. Bandwidth \(\sigma_k\) must be chosen appropriately.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: LACON‑A (multi‑condition guidance) provides fine‑grained quality control by applying classifier‑free guidance independently to each attribute.
- **Mechanism**: During sampling, parallel predictions are computed for each attribute with its target high‑quality value; the final prediction combines text guidance and per‑attribute guidance vectors with individual scales \(\omega_k\). This lets users dial specific quality aspects.
- **Core assumption**: The model has learned separable representations for each quality attribute such that independent guidance is meaningful and non‑interfering.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 3.3] “LACON‑A: applies CFG to both the text condition \(y\) and each quality attribute \(k\) independently.”
  - [section 4.2] LACON‑A outperforms LACON‑S on most metrics, indicating benefit.
  - [corpus] *MIRO: MultI‑Reward cOnditioned pretraining improves T2I quality and efficiency* (arXiv:2510.25897) explores multiple reward conditionings, supporting the viability of multi‑attribute guidance.
- **Break condition**: If attributes are correlated or not disentangled, independent guidance could cause conflicts or artifacts.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Data labeling pipeline → GCC embedding module → Conditional generative model → Inference engine

**Critical path**:  
1. Compute quality metrics for all raw images offline.  
2. Initialize GCC centroids (random) and train the conditional generative model on the full dataset.  
3. After training, perform inference by specifying target quality values and optionally adjusting individual guidance scales.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- More attributes increase conditioning capacity but also model complexity and inference cost.
- GCC vs. discrete binning: GCC provides smoothness and better gradients (shown in ablation) but introduces hyperparameter \(\sigma_k\).
- Full‑data training leverages all samples but requires the model to learn quality representations; may need longer training or larger capacity.
- LACON‑A offers fine control at the expense of \(K+2\) forward passes per step (~7× compute of LACON‑S).

**Failure signatures**:
- Noisy or biased quality predictors produce misleading labels, leading to artifacts or over‑suppression of certain styles.
- Over‑strong guidance on low‑quality attributes may yield unnatural outputs; tuning \(\omega_k\) per attribute is necessary.
- The model may overfit to the distribution of quality scores in the training set; extrapolation to extreme values can fail.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Baseline comparison** – Train on filtered subsets (varying retention ratios) and on full data without conditioning; evaluate GenEval, DPG, FID to demonstrate data‑quality trade‑off and LACON’s advantage.
2. **Token‑injection ablation** – Replace GCC with linear interpolation, discrete binning, Fourier features; compare quantitative results to validate GCC design.
3. **Architecture generalization** – Apply LACON to an autoregressive model (Qwen) and show consistent improvements over the best filtered baseline.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Which quality signals are most critical?**  
   *Basis*: The paper uses all five signals but no isolation ablation.  
   *Why unresolved*: Knowing relative importance could simplify the labeling pipeline.  
   *Resolving evidence*: Ablation experiments with various signal combinations (e.g., only aesthetic) and statistical analysis of performance changes.

2. **How does LACON scale with model size and dataset size?**  
   *Basis*: Evaluations capped at 1.7B parameters on 110M images; larger scales are common.  
   *Why unresolved*: Unclear whether benefits hold for state‑of‑the‑art large models.  
   *Resolving evidence*: Train LACON across multiple scales (e.g., 0.6B, 1.7B, 3B, 10B) and dataset sizes (100M, 500M, 1B) and compare to filtered baselines at each scale.

3. **Can fine‑grained control be achieved more efficiently than LACON‑A?**  
   *Basis*: LACON‑A requires K+2 forward passes per step, multiplying compute.  
   *Why unresolved*: High cost limits real‑time deployment; more efficient methods are needed.  
   *Resolving evidence*: Propose and evaluate alternative guidance schemes (e.g., a small network predicting adjustments or a caching mechanism) and measure quality‑time trade‑off.

4. **What drives the performance gains: improved alignment or enhanced visual quality?**  
   *Basis*: Metrics (GenEval, DPG, FID) combine both aspects; no disentanglement.  
   *Why unresolved*: Understanding the primary factor could guide future research.  
   *Resolving evidence*: Run breakdown analyses with separate metrics (e.g., CLIPScore for alignment, predicted aesthetic for quality) and compare LACON against baselines.

## Limitations
- **Quality metric noise and bias**: Automatic quality predictors may be misaligned with human judgment, introducing spurious correlations.
- **Generalization beyond curated benchmarks**: Evaluation is limited to MJHQ‑30K and GenEval/DPG; performance on truly in‑the‑wild distributions remains unknown.
- **Scalability of multi‑attribute guidance**: LACON‑A’s K+2 forward passes per step is computationally expensive, especially for large models or high‑resolution generation.

## Confidence
- Core mechanism (quality conditioning over filtering): **Medium‑High** – strong empirical evidence but missing isolation of individual signals.
- GCC embedding design: **Medium** – ablations favor GCC, but sensitivity to anchors and bandwidth is not fully explored.
- LACON‑A fine‑grained control: **Medium** – qualitative demonstrations exist but lack quantitative validation of independent guidance.
- Cross‑architecture generalization: **High** – improvements hold for both diffusion and autoregressive models.
- Reduced long‑tail knowledge gap: **Medium** – analysis based on a small manually selected set of rare concepts.

## Next Checks
1. **Robustness to degraded quality signals**: Systematically corrupt one or more quality labels (e.g., randomize aesthetic scores, invert watermark probabilities) and measure drop in GenEval/DPG to quantify sensitivity and pinpoint which signals drive gains.
2. **Scalability to more attributes and model sizes**: Apply LACON to larger T2I models (e.g., SDXL‑scale) and add attributes (contrast, sharpness, compression artifacts); measure marginal gains versus added conditioning complexity and inference cost.
3. **Zero‑shot generalization to out‑of‑distribution quality shifts**: Train LACON on a high‑quality subset (aesthetic > 7) and evaluate on held‑out data with extreme quality (very low aesthetic, high watermark); compare to a model trained on full uncurated data to test if conditioning allows recovery from quality mismatches.
