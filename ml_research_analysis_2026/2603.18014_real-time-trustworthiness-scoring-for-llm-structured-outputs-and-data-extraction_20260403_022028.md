# Real-Time Trustworthiness Scoring for LLM Structured Outputs and Data Extraction

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.18014
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18014
- **Reference Count**: 40
- **Primary Result**: CONSTRUCT scores trustworthiness of LLM structured outputs in real time using multiple diverse verifier prompts and harmonic/arithmetic aggregation, significantly outperforming LLM-as-a-Judge and token probability baselines across four benchmarks while providing explanations and improved efficiency.

## Executive Summary
Large language models (LLMs) often produce structured outputs (e.g., JSON) that contain undetected errors, hindering reliable automation in enterprise settings. This paper presents CONSTRUCT, a method for real‑time trustworthiness scoring of entire structured outputs and individual fields. CONSTRUCT uses any LLM—including black‑box APIs—as a verifier, issuing several diverse prompts in parallel to generate intermediate confidence scores. These scores are aggregated via harmonic mean for document‑level and simple average for field‑level metrics. Evaluated on four new high‑quality benchmarks spanning finance, PII, insurance, and tabular data, CONSTRUCT consistently beats existing approaches such as single‑call or multiple‑call LLM‑as‑a‑Judge and token‑probability baselines. It achieves higher AUROC, Precision@Num‑Errors, and Confidence Gap for both per‑document and per‑field scoring, operates with lower latency than multi‑call methods, and automatically produces explanations for low scores.

## Method Summary
CONSTRUCT employs a lightweight orchestration layer that validates inputs, constructs five different verifier prompts (Type 1: document‑level numeric score; Type 2: field‑level numeric scores; Type 3: document score with indirect field flagging), and dispatches them concurrently to a verifier LLM. Upon receiving responses, a parser extracts intermediate scores, converting Type 3’s flagged fields into fixed low/high scores (α=0.1, β=0.9). The aggregation engine computes each field’s final trust score as the average of all intermediate field‑level scores. For document scoring, it first derives a Type 2 intermediate score via the harmonic mean of the field scores, then averages all intermediate document scores (from Type 1, Type 2, and Type 3) to produce the final trustworthiness score. An explanation generator selects the verifier response with the lowest intermediate document score to provide a human‑readable justification, and an output formatter packages the results. The approach is model‑agnostic, works with any black‑box LLM, and includes timeouts to bound latency.

## Key Results
- CONSTRUCT significantly outperforms LLM‑as‑a‑Judge (single‑call and multi‑call) and token‑probability baselines on four benchmarks, achieving higher AUROC, Precision@Num‑Errors, and Confidence Gap for both document‑ and field‑level scoring.
- The method yields robust per‑field scores, enabling identification of specific erroneous fields, while the harmonic‑mean aggregation ensures that any single weak field lowers the overall document score.
- Compared to baseline methods that make a number of calls proportional to the number of fields, CONSTRUCT uses a fixed set of parallel verifier calls, reducing wall‑clock time and cost; it also supplies automated explanations for low scores.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Diverse Verifier Prompts Improve Coverage
**Claim**: Using multiple diverse verifier prompts improves error detection coverage by capturing complementary signals.  
**Mechanism**: CONSTRUCT issues five parallel LLM calls with distinct prompt templates (Type 1, 2, 3). The templates vary the verification task (document vs. field scoring), the scoring format (numeric vs. Likert), and the reasoning directive (accuracy vs. confidence). This diversity ensures different failure modes are surfaced.  
**Core assumption**: The error signals from these prompts are not perfectly correlated; the union of their detections approximates the full set of errors.  
**Evidence anchors**: Abstract states “CONSTRUCT employs multiple diverse verifier prompts”; Section 3.2 describes template types and motivation; neighboring papers on ensemble diversity (e.g., Hu et al. on Dipper) provide indirect support.  
**Break condition**: If all prompts share a systematic bias (e.g., all overlook subtle date‑format errors), the ensemble fails to catch those errors.

### Mechanism 2: Harmonic and Arithmetic Aggregation Yield Robust Scores
**Claim**: Aggregating intermediate scores with harmonic mean for documents and arithmetic mean for fields yields robust trust signals that reflect the weakest field.  
**Mechanism**: For each document, intermediate field‑level scores from Type 2 templates are combined via harmonic mean to produce a document‑level intermediate score (soft‑minimum). All intermediate document scores (from Type 1, Type 3, and the harmonic‑mean‑derived ones) are then averaged for the final document score. Per‑field scores are the simple average of all intermediate field‑level scores (from Type 2 and α/β conversions from Type 3).  
**Core assumption**: Document correctness is a union of field correctness; the harmonic mean appropriately penalizes any single low field score without being as extreme as a hard minimum.  
**Evidence anchors**: Abstract mentions aggregation method; Section 3.2 (Score Aggregation) provides formulation and rationale; limited direct prior work on harmonic mean for this purpose.  
**Break condition**: If field scores are systematically overconfident (e.g., all >0.9 even for wrong fields), the harmonic mean will remain high and fail to flag erroneous documents.

### Mechanism 3: Type 3 Templates Efficiently Yield Dual Scores
**Claim**: Type 3 templates (document‑level scoring with indirect field flagging) efficiently yield both document and field scores with minimal extra latency.  
**Mechanism**: A verifier LLM is prompted to first list fields it suspects are incorrect, then provide a document confidence score. The list of flagged fields is mapped to low scores (α=0.1) and unflagged fields to high scores (β=0.9). These scores contribute to per‑field aggregation and to the harmonic mean for document‑level intermediate scores.  
**Core assumption**: The verifier’s binary judgment of field correctness is sufficiently accurate to serve as a proxy for calibrated confidence.  
**Evidence anchors**: Abstract mentions “document‑level scoring + indirect field‑level scoring”; Section 3.2 describes Type 3 templates; Appendix E provides specific prompts.  
**Break condition**: If the verifier’s field flagging has low precision (many false alarms), per‑field scores become noisy, leading to both false positives and false negatives in document scoring.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
The system pipeline is: Input Validator → Verifier Orchestrator → (parallel verifier calls) → Response Parser → Aggregation Engine → Explanation Generator → Output Formatter. Input Validator checks that the generator inputs are present and well‑formed. Verifier Orchestrator constructs five verifier request payloads using predefined templates and dispatches them concurrently with an adaptive timeout. Response Parser extracts intermediate document and field scores; for Type 3 it converts flagged fields into fixed α/β values. Aggregation Engine computes per‑field scores (average of intermediates) and per‑document score (harmonic mean of Type 2 field scores, then average of all intermediate document scores). Explanation Generator selects the verifier response with the lowest intermediate document score and returns its explanation text. Output Formatter packages the results into a dictionary.

### Critical Path
1. Receive generator LLM call details: user prompt, schema, and generated structured output.  
2. Build five verifier request payloads using the appropriate templates.  
3. Issue all verifier LLM calls concurrently (async) and start a timeout monitor.  
4. Upon responses (or timeout expiries), parse each to extract: document‑level score `S_doc` (if present) and field‑level scores `S_i` (if present; for Type 3, assign α=0.1 to flagged fields, β=0.9 to others).  
5. Compute per‑field score for each field *i*: average of all `S_i` values available.  
6. Compute document‑level intermediate score from Type 2 calls: harmonic mean of the per‑field scores. Then collect all intermediate `S_doc` values (from Type 1, Type 3, and the harmonic‑mean‑derived ones).  
7. Final document `trustworthiness_score` = arithmetic average of all intermediate `S_doc` values.  
8. Identify `untrustworthy_fields` as those with per‑field score < threshold (e.g., 0.5), or present all per‑field scores.  
9. Generate explanation: pick the verifier response with the lowest `S_doc` and include its `explanation` text; also include per‑field explanations where available.  
10. Return result dictionary.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Number of verifier calls**: More calls improve coverage but increase latency, cost, and risk of hitting rate limits. CONSTRUCT uses five as a sweet spot; ablation shows marginal gains beyond that.  
- **Verifier model choice**: Using a more capable model (e.g., GPT‑4.1‑mini) yields better scores but may be slower/expensive; cheaper models reduce cost but may degrade detection quality. The method is model‑agnostic.  
- **Fixed α/β values**: Simple and fast, but may not be optimal for all domains; could be learned if calibration data exists, but the paper avoids extra training.  
- **Parallel execution**: Reduces wall‑clock time compared to sequential calls, but requires handling API rate limits and timeouts. The timeout mechanism drops slow calls to bound latency.  
- **Explanation source**: Using the lowest‑scoring verifier provides a consistent explanation, but could be cherry‑picked; however, it reflects the most skeptical perspective.

### Failure Signatures
- High rate of verifier timeouts (>20%) leads to insufficient intermediate scores, causing aggregation instability or fallback to fewer calls.  
- Malformed verifier responses (missing score fields, invalid JSON) indicate prompt misconfiguration; should be logged and perhaps trigger a re‑prompt.  
- All intermediate document scores are very high (>0.95) even on known error cases; suggests verifier model is overconfident or prompts are ineffective.  
- Per‑field scores are uniform across fields (low variance) indicating lack of discriminative power.  
- Sudden drop in precision/recall on recent production data compared to benchmark performance; may indicate distribution shift.

## Limitations
- **Generalization**: Benchmarks cover specific domains (finance, PII, insurance, data tables); performance may degrade on unseen structured formats (e.g., complex nested JSON, code outputs).  
- **Verifier Model Dependency**: Detection quality hinges on the verifier LLM’s own reliability; systematic biases (e.g., overconfidence in numeric fields) are not deeply characterized.  
- **Real‑World Robustness**: Evaluation uses curated error sets; behavior under adversarial attacks, schema drift, or noisy generator outputs remains unknown.
