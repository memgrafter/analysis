# Execution-Verified Reinforcement Learning for Optimization Modeling
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.00442  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00442  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Introduces EVOM, an RL framework that treats a mathematical programming solver as a deterministic verifier, achieving performance comparable to or better than process‑supervised fine‑tuning and enabling zero‑shot solver transfer with large accuracy gains.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the challenge of automatically converting natural‑language problem descriptions into executable optimization code. Traditional approaches suffer from high latency due to multi‑step agentic pipelines or from overfitting to specific solvers via painstaking process‑level supervision. The authors propose Execution‑Verified Optimization Modeling (EVOM), a reinforcement learning framework that uses a solver itself as a verifier: the policy generates code, the solver executes it in a sandbox, and the execution outcome (status, objective value) is transformed into a scalar reward. This outcome‑only signal eliminates the need for intermediate annotations and allows cross‑solver generalization simply by swapping the solver environment. Experiments across multiple benchmarks with Gurobi, OR‑Tools, and COPT confirm that EVOM matches or exceeds process‑supervised fine‑tuning, demonstrates impressive zero‑shot transfer (e.g., 54.31% vs. 3.49% accuracy on OptiBench), and adapts efficiently to new solvers with minimal additional data.

## Method Summary
EVOM operates as a closed‑loop RL system. A policy language model, conditioned on a natural‑language problem and a target solver identifier, produces a reasoning block (`<think>`) followed by solver‑specific code (`<code>`). The code is extracted, executed in a resource‑limited sandbox for the chosen solver, and an observation is returned containing execution status, solver status, objective value, and logs. A reward function combines a format compliance term (`r_fmt`) and an outcome correctness term (`r_abs`) that compares the solver’s result against a ground‑truth answer. Because the reward is derived solely from the final execution result, no intermediate step annotations are required. Policy updates use group‑relative reinforcement learning (GRPO or DAPO), which samples multiple outputs per problem, normalizes advantages within the group, and performs critic‑free optimization. The solver identifier is part of the input, so the same policy can be evaluated on different solvers without retraining, enabling zero‑shot and low‑cost adaptation.

## Key Results
- EVOM (trained on Gurobi) matches or surpasses process‑supervised SFT on NL4OPT, MAMO, IndustryOR, and OptiBench.
- Zero‑shot transfer from Gurobi to OR‑Tools yields 54.31% accuracy on OptiBench, drastically higher than the 3.49% achieved by a process‑supervised model.
- Low‑cost fine‑tuning on a new solver (OR‑Tools) leads to a +28.99% improvement on MAMO‑E, demonstrating efficient cross‑solver adaptation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Outcome‑only supervision replaces process annotations
- **Claim:** Outcome‑only supervision with solver execution feedback can replace costly process‑level annotations for optimization modeling.
- **Mechanism:** The solver acts as a deterministic verifier. Generated code is executed, and the outcome (status, objective value) is converted into a scalar reward that directly supervises the policy. This generate‑execute‑feedback‑update cycle aligns natural language with mathematical logic without requiring intermediate reasoning traces.
- **Core assumption:** Solver execution outcomes provide an unambiguous signal of whether the model captured the optimization structure.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states the framework removes process‑level supervision; Section 3.4 defines the reward based solely on solver outcome; the corpus shows prior methods still incorporate process signals.
- **Break condition:** If solver verification is non‑deterministic (e.g., timeouts, stochastic solvers) or ground‑truth answers are ambiguous (multiple optimal formulations), the reward becomes noisy or insufficient.

### Mechanism 2: Cross‑solver generalization via environment switching
- **Claim:** Cross‑solver generalization is achievable by treating the solver as part of the verification environment, allowing transfer without solver‑specific retraining.
- **Mechanism:** The policy is trained on a solver‑conditioned objective πθ(y|q,s). For zero‑shot transfer, the execution backend Es is swapped while keeping policy parameters fixed. The model learns solver‑agnostic modeling principles; syntax differences are adapted through execution feedback.
- **Core assumption:** The logical structure of optimization models is largely solver‑invariant; differences are mainly syntactic.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract highlights switching verification environments; Section 3.6 defines zero‑shot transfer and low‑cost adaptation; Section 4.4.1 and Table 2 show successful zero‑shot transfer.
- **Break condition:** Transfer to a solver with a fundamentally different paradigm (e.g., constraint programming) may fail without extra adaptation.

### Mechanism 3: Group‑relative policy optimization handles sparse rewards
- **Claim:** Group‑relative policy optimization with critic‑free methods (GRPO/DAPO) effectively handles sparse, binary execution rewards.
- **Mechanism:** For each problem, a group of G outputs is sampled. Rewards are normalized within the group to produce relative advantages, eliminating the need for a value critic. This turns sparse outcome supervision into a denser signal based on intra‑group ranking.
- **Core assumption:** Within‑group diversity in code quality yields meaningful comparisons.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 3.5 details GRPO/DAPO; Section 4.3 shows both methods perform similarly; Section 3.5.2 introduces dynamic sampling to mitigate entropy collapse.
- **Break condition:** Low group diversity (e.g., all outputs syntactically similar) or extremely skewed reward distributions can weaken gradients or destabilize training.

## Foundational Learning
- **Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)**  
  *Why needed:* Provides a framework for learning from outcome‑level signals without process supervision or learned reward models, essential for scalable optimization modeling where intermediate annotations are expensive.

- **Group‑Relative Policy Optimization**  
  *Why needed:* Execution rewards are sparse and binary (success/failure). Group normalization creates a denser gradient signal by comparing outputs within the same problem batch, stabilizing policy updates.  
  *Quick check:* Why is a critic unnecessary in this setting, and what problem does group‑relative normalization solve?

- **Solver‑Conditioned Policy**  
  *Why needed:* Enables a single policy to generate code for multiple solvers by conditioning on the solver identifier. This is the foundation for cross‑solver generalization without retraining.  
  *Quick check:* What changes in the execution environment when switching solvers while keeping the policy fixed?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map (plain text flow):**  
Dataset (q, a) → Solver Sampler (s) → Policy LLM (πθ) → Outputs y = (y_think, y_code) → Code extraction → Execution Environment (E_s) → Observation o → Reward Calculator (R = r_fmt + r_ans) → Group Buffer → Advantage Normalization (group‑relative) → Policy Optimizer (GRPO/DAPO) → Updated πθ.

**Critical path:**
1. Sample (problem q, ground‑truth answer a, solver s) from dataset and solver distribution.
2. Generate a group of outputs y ∼ πθ_old(y|q, s).
3. Parse the `<code>` block from each y; execute in E_s to obtain observation o.
4. Compute reward R(y, o; a) using format and outcome terms.
5. Compute group‑relative advantages A_i = (R_i − μ_R)/(σ_R + δ).
6. Update πθ using the GRPO or DAPO objective.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Strict output protocol (exactly one `<think>` and one `<code>` block) ensures reliable parsing but may limit natural reasoning expression.
- Group size G versus compute cost: larger groups improve advantage estimation but increase execution time and resource usage.
- Reward sparsity: `r_ans` is binary; `r_fmt` offers a denser early signal but risks overfitting to formatting.

**Failure signatures:**
- High parse failure rate: model violates output schema; increase `r_fmt` weight or add formatting examples.
- All rewards zero in a group: indicates either too hard tasks or insufficient group diversity; DAPO dynamic sampling should skip such groups, but if persistent, reduce task difficulty or increase G.
- Reward saturation early: `r_fmt` saturates quickly; monitor `r_ans` curve to ensure genuine modeling improvement continues.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Baseline Reproducibility:** Train EVOM (GRPO) on Gurobi for 1 epoch with default hyperparameters (Qwen2.5‑7B, group size 8, batch 64). Expect accuracy gains on NL4OPT/MAMO/IndustryOR as in Table 1.
2. **Zero‑Shot Transfer Test:** Take the model from experiment 1 and evaluate on OR‑Tools without any adaptation. Expect a drop from in‑solver performance but still far above the SFT baseline (∼54% vs. 3.5% on OptiBench, per Table 2).
3. **Ablation – Reasoning Block:** Train EVOM on Gurobi with a modified prompt that removes the `<think>` block (direct code generation). Expect severe performance degradation on complex benchmarks (IndustryOR, MAMO‑C), confirming the necessity of the explicit reasoning workspace (Figure 2).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Can EVOM avoid any solver‑specific SFT for brand‑new solvers?** The current two‑stage approach still requires SFT for cold‑start (e.g., COPT). Pure RL with sparse rewards on a novel solver remains untested.
- **Does EVOM’s zero‑shot transfer generalize to solvers with different modeling paradigms (e.g., NLP, CP)?** Transfer has only been tested among MILP solvers; outcome reward may be tied to MILP‑specific constructs.
- **Is the `<think>` block indispensable, or can EVOM work with code‑only generation?** Ablation shows performance collapse when it is removed, but it is unclear whether the block enables planning or is merely correlated with better code.

## Limitations
- Reward signals rely on solver determinism; non‑determinism (timeouts, numerical variance) can corrupt the feedback loop.
- Cross‑paradigm generalization (e.g., to constraint programming or nonlinear solvers) is untested and may fail due to incompatible modeling patterns.
- Group‑relative optimization assumes sufficient diversity within sampled groups; a narrow policy may produce low‑diversity rollouts, rendering advantages uninformative.

## Confidence
- Outcome‑only supervision replaces process annotations: **Medium** (strong empirical evidence within controlled settings, but sensitive to solver non‑determinism and ambiguous ground truths).
- Cross‑solver generalization via environment switch: **High** for same‑paradigm solvers (e.g., among LP solvers), **Low** for cross‑paradigm (unevaluated).
- Group‑relative optimization handles sparse rewards: **Medium** (GRPO/DAPO perform similarly, but effectiveness depends on group diversity, which is not thoroughly analyzed).

## Next Checks
1. **Solver consistency test:** For a fixed set of generated programs, execute each multiple times per solver and compute outcome variance. High variance indicates reward instability.
2. **Paradigm‑shift transfer:** Train EVOM on a MILP solver (Gurobi) and evaluate on a CP solver (e.g., MiniZinc). Compare the accuracy drop to same‑paradigm transfer to OR‑Tools.
3. **Group diversity monitoring:** During RL training, compute pairwise code similarity (e.g., AST edit distance) within each group. Correlate diversity metrics with policy improvement to diagnose stagnation due to low diversity.
