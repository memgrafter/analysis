# The Coordinate System Problem in Persistent Structural Memory for Neural Architectures

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.22858  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22858  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: DPPN shows persistent structural memory requires stable coordinates; learned coordinates are unstable, and while learning‑rate modulation enables graceful transfer, coordinate stability remains a fundamental challenge.

## Executive Summary
Persistent structural memory in neural networks demands a stable coordinate system, yet any coordinates learned jointly with the model prove inherently unstable. This paper introduces the Dual‑View Pheromone Pathway Network (DPPN), which routes sparse attention through a persistent pheromone field over latent slot transitions to diagnose the issue. Five experiments reveal several obstacles: pheromone saturation, surface‑structure entanglement, coordinate mismatch (3.5% alignment vs. 3.1% chance), and embedding instability. Although fixed random Fourier features supply extrinsic stable coordinates, routing‑bias pheromone still shows no transfer advantage (10 seeds, p > 0.05). Replacing routing bias with learning‑rate modulation eliminates negative transfer, yielding a small but statistically significant (+0.003) same‑family AULC improvement (7 seeds, p < 0.05). Within the primary task, DPPN outperforms transformer (0.700 vs. 0.680) and random sparse (0.670) baselines. The study concludes that two independent requirements govern persistent memory: coordinate stability and a graceful transfer mechanism.

## Method Summary
The authors propose the Dual‑View Pheromone Pathway Network (DPPN) to study coordinate stability in persistent structural memory. DPPN introduces a persistent pheromone field that operates over latent slot transitions, routing sparse attention. The architecture includes two views: one for memory (the pheromone field) and one for processing (sparse attention). Experiments systematically manipulate coordinate representations (learned vs. fixed random Fourier features) and the transfer mechanism (routing bias vs. learning‑rate modulation). Performance is evaluated both within the primary task and in transfer settings, with comparisons to standard transformer and random sparse attention baselines. Five experiments isolate the identified obstacles: pheromone saturation, surface‑structure entanglement, coordinate mismatch, and embedding instability.

## Key Results
- DPPN surpasses within‑task baselines: 0.700 vs. 0.680 (transformer) and 0.670 (random sparse).  
- Coordinate mismatch is marginal: 3.5% alignment versus 3.1% chance level, indicating poor learning of stable coordinates.  
- Routing‑bias pheromone with fixed random Fourier coordinates shows no transfer advantage (p > 0.05), whereas learning‑rate modulation yields a significant +0.003 AULC improvement (p < 0.05) on same‑family transfers.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The instability of learned coordinates leads to misalignment between memory and processing views, disrupting effective routing. Fixed random Fourier features provide a stable external coordinate system, but the routing‑bias mechanism still fails to leverage it for transfer. Learning‑rate modulation, however, adjusts optimization dynamics rather than attention patterns, enabling graceful adaptation without negative interference. The persistent pheromone field accumulates slot transition statistics, yet suffers from saturation and entanglement; these issues are mitigated by combining stable coordinates with the modulation approach.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map** (plain text): Input Encoder -> Latent Slot Generator -> Pheromone Field (persistent) -> Sparse Attention Router -> Output Head. An auxiliary Learning‑Rate Modulator may replace the routing bias.  
- **Critical path**: Input is encoded into latent slots; these interact with the persistent pheromone field storing transition statistics; sparse attention routing selects relevant slots; the output head produces predictions. Learning‑rate modulation influences optimizer steps during training.  
- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - Stable coordinates (fixed) vs. flexible coordinates (learned): fixed ensures stability but may limit adaptation; learned adapts but risks misalignment.  
  - Routing bias (pheromone‑driven) vs. learning‑rate modulation: the former is interpretable but prone to saturation/entanglement; the latter is less direct but supports transfer.  
  - Sparse attention reduces compute but may omit relevant information; the pheromone field adds memory overhead.  
- **Failure signatures**:  
  - Pheromone saturation: the field becomes overly dense, losing discriminative power.  
  - Surface‑structure entanglement: the field mixes different task structures, impairing separation.  
  - Coordinate mismatch: alignment between learned coordinates and task structure remains near chance.  
  - Embedding instability: embeddings drift during training, breaking consistency.  
- **Exactly 3 first experiments**:  
  1. Baseline DPPN with learned coordinates and routing bias – establishes within‑task performance and reveals obstacles.  
  2. DPPN with fixed random Fourier features (stable coordinates) while retaining routing bias – tests whether stable coordinates alone enable transfer.  
  3. DPPN with learning‑rate modulation instead of routing bias – evaluates the alternative transfer mechanism.

## Limitations
- Evaluation relies on small seed counts (10 and 7), limiting statistical robustness.  
- Reported coordinate alignment (3.5% vs. 3.1% chance) is marginal and non‑significant (p > 0.05), raising doubts about meaningful coordinate learning.  
- The core transfer mechanism remains unexplained; stable coordinates did not improve routing‑bias transfer, whereas learning‑rate modulation gave only a tiny effect (+0.003 AULC).

## Confidence
- Within‑task DPPN superiority (0.700 vs. 0.680/0.670): **High**  
- Coordinate instability as fundamental obstacle: **Medium**  
- Learning‑rate modulation enabling transfer: **Medium‑Low**

## Next Checks
1. Replication with larger samples and diverse tasks: run DPPN with ≥30 seeds on 3+ standard memory/transfer benchmarks (e.g., PGMC, ABA) to confirm statistical power and generalization.  
2. Coordinate system ablation: replace random Fourier features with other stable encodings (e.g., frozen pretrained positions, modulated sinusoidal) and measure alignment/transfer trade‑offs.  
3. Mechanistic dissection of transfer: ablate pheromone field dynamics vs. learning‑rate modulation independently in transfer settings; log gradient norms and attention entropy to diagnose why modulation succeeds where routing bias fails.
