# DSS-GAN: Directional State Space GAN with Mamba backbone for Class-Conditional Image Synthesis

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** `2603.17637`
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17637
- **Citation count:** 40 (as of the write‑up)
- **Primary result:** DSS‑GAN introduces a Mamba backbone generator with Directional Latent Routing (DLR) to achieve lower FID/KID and higher Precision/Recall/Density/Coverage than StyleGAN2‑ADA on AFHQ, FFHQ, LSUN, and CelebA while using only 7.3 M parameters (82% fewer).

## Executive Summary
DSS‑GAN is the first GAN to employ a Mamba backbone as a hierarchical generator for noise‑to‑image synthesis. Its key innovation is Directional Latent Routing (DLR), which splits the latent vector into direction‑specific subvectors (row, column, diagonal) and jointly projects each with a class embedding to produce feature‑wise affine modulations applied before Mamba scans, enabling spatially disentangled control. Experiments on standard class‑conditional image datasets show that DSS‑GAN attains lower Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) and Kernel Inception Distance (KID) as well as higher Precision, Recall, Density, and Coverage than StyleGAN2‑ADA. For instance, on AFHQ 256×256 it reduces FID from 13.16 to 10.29 and increases Density from 0.84 to 1.33. The generator uses only 7.3 M parameters—82% fewer than StyleGAN2‑ADA’s 25.0 M—while maintaining competitive inference latency. Latent space analysis confirms directional specialization: perturbations to individual subvectors yield structured, axis‑correlated changes in generated images.

## Method Summary
DSS‑GAN’s generator receives a noise vector split into a base component `z_base` and a directional component `z_dir`. `z_base` is linearly projected and processed by two Mamba blocks, then reshaped into an 8×8 token grid. Each subsequent scale applies a Directional Latent Routing (DLR) block: the feature map is rotated 180°, serialized along a chosen scan permutation (row, column, diagonal), and modulated by affine parameters `(γ_k, β_k)` derived from concatenating the corresponding subvector `z_k_dir` with a direction‑specific class embedding. The modulated tokens traverse a Mamba state‑space module, are unscrambled, and rotated back. The outputs of the K directions are summed using learned direction weights. After each DLR block the resolution is upsampled (nearest‑neighbor ×2) and passed through a convolutional projection. At the highest resolution (e.g., 256×256 or 512×512) a StyleGAN2‑style convolutional refinement recovers fine details. The discriminator is the unaltered StyleGAN2‑ADA CNN. Training minimizes the non‑saturating GAN loss with R1 gradient penalty, using Adam (β₁=0, β₂=0.99), learning rates 9e‑5 (generator) and 3e‑5 (discriminator), gradient clipping (G=10, D=15), and exponential moving average of generator weights. Hyperparameters (latent dimensions, Mamba configuration, batch size) are dataset‑ and resolution‑dependent as detailed in the appendix.

## Key Results
- On AFHQ 256×256, DSS‑GAN lowers FID to 10.29 (vs. 13.16 for StyleGAN2‑ADA) and Density to 1.33 (vs. 0.84).
- Across AFHQ, FFHQ, LSUN, and CelebA it consistently achieves lower FID/KID and higher Precision, Recall, Density, and Coverage.
- The generator contains only 7.3 M parameters (82% fewer than StyleGAN2‑ADA) while keeping inference latency comparable.
- Latent perturbation experiments verify that each directional subvector controls spatial structure along its corresponding axis.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Directional Latent Routing (DLR)
**Claim:** DLR decomposes the latent vector into direction‑specific subvectors, each jointly projected with a class embedding to produce feature‑wise affine modulations applied to Mamba scans, enabling spatially disentangled control.  
**Mechanism:** The latent vector `z_dir` is split into `K` subvectors (one per scan direction). Each subvector `z_k_dir` is concatenated with a per‑direction class embedding `e_k`, projected via a direction‑specific MLP to affine parameters `(γ_k, β_k)`, and applied to the token sequence *before* the Mamba SSM recurrence for that scan direction. This shapes what spatial relationships the recurrent state integrates along each axis.  
**Core assumption:** Different scan directions (row, column, diagonal) capture orthogonal or complementary spatial structures, and the conditioning signal can be specialized per axis.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- Abstract: "DLR decomposes the latent vector into direction‑specific subvectors… enabling spatially disentangled control."  
- Section 3.2: Detailed architecture of per‑direction affine modulation and residual routing; Figure 3 shows direction‑specific activation patterns.  
- Section 4, Table 4: LPIPS sensitivity analysis shows latent perturbations affect different directions differently; class override experiments (Figures 6, 12‑13) show partial class changes via single‑direction overrides.  
- Corpus: Weak; corpus papers focus on other Mamba/diffusion architectures; no direct validation of DLR.  
**Break condition:** If all `K` directions are identical (e.g., 3× row scan), routing weights fail to specialize (Appendix C, Figure 18), class routing scale `|α|` remains near zero, and training collapses (FID spikes, R1 penalty rises).

### Mechanism 2: Mamba as Hierarchical Generator Backbone
**Claim:** Using Mamba as a hierarchical generator backbone for noise‑to‑image synthesis builds global spatial coherence via linear‑complexity SSM recurrence, while avoiding quadratic attention costs of ViTs and limited receptive fields of CNNs.  
**Mechanism:** The generator processes a tokenized `z_base` through a sequence of Mamba blocks across scales (8×8 → 128×128), where each block’s SSM recurrence aggregates information along a fixed scan permutation. The hierarchical structure progressively refines spatial resolution.  
**Core assumption:** The selective SSM in Mamba can effectively model long‑range dependencies in 2D feature maps when serialized via scan directions, and this global coherence is beneficial for image generation.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- Abstract: "first GAN to employ Mamba as a hierarchical generator backbone for noise‑to‑image synthesis."  
- Section 1: Contrasts with prior Mamba uses in image‑to‑image translation/diffusion; argues for linear complexity vs. ViT’s O(N²).  
- Section 3.1, Table 2: Ablation shows removing Mamba entirely (CNN‑only) reduces recall; Mamba provides global structure.  
- Corpus: Weak; corpus includes VCMamba (discriminative) and Pi‑Mamba (protein design); no prior GAN generator use.  
**Break condition:** At high resolutions (512×512), Mamba capacity (dstate, expand) is reduced (Appendix D), which may limit performance relative to StyleGAN2‑ADA. The sequential nature also limits batch throughput (Table 11).

### Mechanism 3: Intra‑block 180° Rotation for Gradient Stability
**Claim:** Applying and inverting a 180° rotation within each DLR block exposes the SSM recurrence to reversed gradient paths during backpropagation, improved training stability without altering output layout.  
**Mechanism:** The feature map `h` is rotated by 180° before scanning, so each scan direction’s permutation π_k is effectively reversed. The Mamba SSM processes this reversed sequence, then the output is un‑scanned and rotated back. This ensures the recurrent state receives gradients from both forward and backward traversals within the same forward pass.  
**Core assumption:** Bidirectional gradient flow along the SSM recurrence stabilizes learning by providing more consistent gradient signals across training steps.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- Section 3.2.1: Explicit description of rotation as applied/inverted within forward pass.  
- Table 3: Rotation improves FID/KID/recall on AFHQ 128×128; increases mean gradient norms in deeper blocks (1.23× at 64×64) without increasing CV (instability).  
- Corpus: Tang et al. [45] use rotation for coverage in medical segmentation; LBMamba [51] reverses sequence order between layers for bidirectional scanning—related but different purpose.  
**Break condition:** If rotation is omitted, training may be less stable (higher FID, lower recall per Table 3). If rotation is not perfectly inverted within the block, spatial layout would be corrupted, but paper ensures inversion.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: State Space Models (SSM) and Selective SSM/Mamba
**Why needed:** DSS‑GAN’s generator backbone is Mamba, a selective SSM. Understanding how SSMs process sequences (discretization, recurrent state update) and why "selective" (input‑dependent parameters) matters is key to grasping why Mamba can condition on latent inputs.  
**Quick check:** How does the selective mechanism in Mamba allow the SSM parameters to depend on the input, and why is this important for conditioning in DLR?

### Concept: GAN Conditioning Mechanisms (FiLM, SPADE, StyleGAN)
**Why needed:** DLR is a novel conditioning method. Comparing to prior art (global FiLM in BigGAN, spatially‑adaptive SPADE, global StyleGAN style codes) clarifies what DLR changes: per‑direction affine modulation *before* SSM vs. global or spatial post‑hoc modulation.  
**Quick check:** What is the difference between injecting conditioning additively after a layer (like AdaIN) vs. modulating the input to a recurrent module (as in DLR), and what might be the effect on learned representations?

### Concept: Latent Disentanglement and Directional Specialization
**Why needed:** The paper claims latent subvectors control different spatial directions (Figures 7, 8, Table 4). Understanding how to probe latent space (perturbations, swaps, class overrides) and metrics like LPIPS for perceptual sensitivity is essential to evaluate DLR.  
**Quick check:** How would you design an experiment to test if a latent subvector specifically controls, say, horizontal vs. vertical structure, and what metric would indicate success?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
- **Tokenizer:** Linear projection of `z_base` → two Mamba blocks → reshape to 8×8 token grid.
- **DLR Block (per scale):** For each of K scan directions (row, column, diagonal):
  1. Rotate feature map 180°.
  2. Serialize tokens via direction‑specific permutation.
  3. Apply affine modulation `(γ_k, β_k)` from `[z_k_dir || e_k]`.
  4. Process through Mamba SSM.
  5. Unscramble tokens and rotate back.
  6. Sum across directions with learned weights.
- **Upsampling:** Nearest‑neighbor ×2 → convolutional projection.
- **Final CNN refinement:** StyleGAN2‑inspired convolutional block at the highest resolution for high‑frequency detail.
- **Discriminator:** Standard StyleGAN2‑ADA CNN.

Data flow: `z_base` → Tokenizer → (DLR Block + Upsampling) repeated for each scale → CNN Refinement → `toRGB` layer. `z_dir` and class label `y` are injected into every DLR Block.

### Critical Path
The generator first tokenizes `z_base` and then iteratively applies DLR Blocks at increasing resolutions. At each block the same `z_dir` and `y` are re‑injected, allowing the conditioning signal to modulate spatial integration along different axes across multiple scales. The discriminator receives the final image and class label, and is trained independently with the StyleGAN2‑ADA protocol.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Mamba vs. CNN final stage:** Mamba alone lacks high‑frequency detail; removing the final CNN raises Precision but lowers Recall (Table 2). Adding CNN too early displaces latent routing and also reduces diversity.
- **Number of scan directions `K`:** Dataset‑dependent. Rectilinear scenes (LSUN Rooms) prefer `K=2` (row+col); textured datasets (AFHQ) prefer `K=3`; isotropic data (CelebA) can use `K=1`. `K=2` may cause routing instability (Appendix C).
- **Rotation:** Intra‑block 180° rotation improves gradient flow without cost; consistently recommended.
- **Parameter efficiency vs. throughput:** DSS‑GAN uses 7.3 M parameters (82% fewer than StyleGAN2‑ADA) but suffers lower throughput at large batches due to sequential Mamba processing (Table 11).

### Failure Signatures
1. **Routing collapse:** Using identical directions for `K>1` keeps routing weights uniform (~1/`K`), `|α|` near zero, and leads to training collapse (FID >200, R1 >1.0) around epoch 108 (Appendix C, Figure 18).
2. **Recall collapse from early CNN:** Moving CNN refinement to a lower resolution causes monotonic recall drop (Table 2).
3. **Directional indifference:** If LPIPS sensitivity across directions is nearly identical, multiple directions are unhelpful; `K=1` may be optimal (Table 4, CelebA).
4. **Gradient instability without rotation:** Omitting rotation reduces mean gradient norms in deeper Mamba blocks (∼10–20% lower) and worsens FID/Recall (Table 3).
5. **R1 penalty and clipping issues:** Excessive R1 or missing gradient clipping triggers instability; recommended clipping values are G=10, D=15.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Rotation ablation** (Table 3). Train DSS‑GAN on AFHQ 128×128 with and without intra‑block rotation. Expect: rotation improves FID/KID/Recall and raises gradient norms in deeper blocks.
2. **CNN refinement boundary** (Table 2). Relocate final CNN from 128×128 to 64×64, 32×32, and remove it. Expect: Recall drops monotonically as CNN moves earlier; Precision may rise.
3. **Scan directions** (Tables 5‑8). Test `K=1` (row), `K=2` (row+col), `K=3` on AFHQ, LSUN Rooms, CelebA. Expect: `K=2` best for rectilinear, `K=3` best for textured, `K=1` best for isotropic; also monitor routing weights (Figure 4) and class‑override LPIPS (Table 4).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How would incorporating a Mamba‑based discriminator conditioned via DLR affect the performance, training stability, and mode coverage of DSS‑GAN?
- **Basis in paper:** "Incorporating a Mamba‑based discriminator conditioned via DLR is a natural extension not explored in the present work" (Section 5.3).
- **Why unresolved:** The current work uses a standard StyleGAN2‑ADA discriminator (a CNN) to isolate the generator’s contribution; a direction‑aware Mamba discriminator has not been implemented or evaluated.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Train DSS‑GAN with a Mamba‑backbone discriminator that also employs DLR for conditioning. Compare against the baseline in terms of FID, KID, Precision/Recall, density/coverage, and training dynamics. Ablate the impact of DLR in the discriminator by training a Mamba discriminator without directional routing.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can the DLR mechanism be effectively transferred to three‑dimensional generation (e.g., volumetric medical data) and what scan directions should be used for such domains?
- **Basis in paper:** "The directional scanning principle extends naturally to three‑dimensional data by introducing scan directions transverse to the feature map axes — corresponding, for instance, to axial, coronal, and sagittal orientations in volumetric medical imaging — without modification to the DLR routing mechanism" (Section 5.3).
- **Why unresolved:** All experiments are conducted on 2D image datasets; no 3D validation is provided.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement DSS‑GAN for 3D data (e.g., CT/MRI volume synthesis). Define suitable scan permutations (e.g., along anatomical axes or 3D space‑filling curves). Evaluate generation quality using 3D‑adapted metrics (e.g., 3D FID, segmentation accuracy if conditioning on anatomical classes) and compare different direction sets.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can the choice of scan directions be made learnable in an end‑to‑end differentiable fashion to automatically adapt to the geometric structure of the training data?
- **Basis in paper:** "direction selection could therefore be treated as a dataset‑specific design choice or learned end‑to‑end via a differentiable relaxation of the permutation π_k" (Section 5.3).
- **Why unresolved:** The architecture uses fixed, predefined scan permutations (row, column, diagonal); no learning of direction ordering occurs.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replace fixed permutations with a differentiable mechanism, e.g., a weighted mixture over a candidate set of scan orders or a continuous relaxation of permutation matrices. Train the full model end‑to‑end and compare performance against fixed‑direction baselines. Analyze whether learned directions converge to meaningful patterns (e.g., orthogonality, locality) for different datasets.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Would a systematic hyperparameter search (e.g., R1 penalty strength, learning rate, latent dimensionality) significantly improve DSS‑GAN’s results on individual datasets?
- **Basis in paper:** "A systematic grid search over training hyperparameters — in particular R1 regularisation strength, learning rate schedule, and latent space dimensionality — could be expected to yield further improvements on individual datasets" (Section 5.3).
- **Why unresolved:** The paper employs one training configuration per resolution (except a single choice between predefined configs) and does not perform per‑dataset hyperparameter optimisation.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct a thorough hyperparameter optimisation (e.g., Bayesian optimisation) for each dataset/resolution pair, focusing on R1 weight, generator/discriminator learning rates, EMA decay, and latent vector sizes. Evaluate whether tuned models achieve statistically significant gains in FID, KID, Precision/Recall, and training stability compared to the reported results.

## Limitations
- **Hyperparameter details underspecified:** Exact MLP architectures (hidden sizes) for affine modulation and direction weighting, the residual clipping value `δ_ℓ`, and full Mamba parameter settings per resolution are not enumerated and must be inferred from code.
- **Training schedule incomplete:** Total number of iterations, learning rate decay schedule, and precise EMA timing are not provided, hindering exact replication.
- **Inference latency at high resolution:** At 512×512 DSS‑GAN exhibits higher latency than StyleGAN2‑ADA due to sequential Mamba processing, offsetting its parameter‑count advantage.
