# SmartBench: Evaluating LLMs in Smart Homes with Anomalous Device States and Behavioral Contexts

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.06636  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06636  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: Current LLMs fail to reliably detect anomalies in smart homes; best F1 scores are 79.3% (context‑independent) and 67.6% (context‑dependent) with high false positive rates, poor device localization, and weak explanation consistency.

## Executive Summary
Smart home assistants based on large language models (LLMs) lack robust anomaly detection, a critical safety function. To address this gap, the paper introduces **SmartBench**, the first LLM‑focused benchmark for smart‑home anomaly detection. The dataset contains 4,400 samples covering two contexts—context‑independent (single device‑state snapshot) and context‑dependent (temporal sequence)—across 15 anomaly categories. Data is generated via a three‑stage pipeline (normal construction, compliance validation, controlled anomaly injection) and includes fine‑grained annotations of responsible devices and human‑written rationales.

Evaluation of 13 mainstream LLMs reveals severe shortcomings: the best models achieve F1 scores below 80% (CI) and 70% (CD) but with extremely high false positive rates (up to 78.2%). Anomaly localization (AL) scores are low (≤ 0.491 CI, ≤ 0.365 CD), and attribution consistency (AC) – the quality of explanations – is poor, with valid rates below 70% for most models. In‑context learning yields only marginal gains, underscoring that current LLMs are not yet capable of reliable smart‑home anomaly detection and handling.

## Method Summary
SmartBench defines two tasks. **Context‑independent** detection uses a single snapshot of 62 device states and environmental parameters. **Context‑dependent** detection uses a timestamped sequence of device transitions, a household profile, and segment duration. The dataset (1:1 normal/anomalous) spans 155 device types and 15 anomaly categories.

The data generation pipeline: (1) normal samples are produced from public datasets and GPT‑5‑generated virtual scenarios; (2) compliance validation by GPT‑5‑mini checks device validity, temporal consistency, and semantic alignment, with flagged samples manually corrected; (3) anomalies are injected via four minimal edits (insertion, deletion, temporal shift, value adjustment) that preserve overall semantics. Ground truth includes a set of evidence devices and a human‑written rationale.

Thirteen LLMs are evaluated with temperature 0 and model‑specific token limits. Prompts ask the model to decide if an anomaly exists, list anomalous devices, and provide an explanation. Outputs are parsed to compute detection metrics (F1, accuracy, precision, recall, false positive rate), anomaly location (AL) score (Jaccard between predicted and true evidence devices), and attribution consistency (AC) score (GPT‑5‑mini rates explanation similarity on a 1–5 scale; valid if ≥ 4). Additional experiments test in‑context learning and context compression.

## Key Results
- Best **context‑independent** F1: 79.3% (gemini‑3); best **context‑dependent** F1: 67.6% (gemini‑2.5) but with an extremely high false positive rate of 78.2%.
- **Anomaly localization** scores are low: maximum AL 0.491 (CI) and 0.365 (CD), indicating poor identification of responsible devices.
- **Attribution consistency**: most models achieve valid rates below 70%; many explanations are incoherent or inaccurate.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The effectiveness of SmartBench stems from three key design mechanisms.

**Dual‑context isolation** – The benchmark defines two tasks (snapshot vs. sequence) that cleanly separate static reasoning from temporal reasoning. Performance differences between them pinpoint whether failures arise from basic state comprehension or from handling temporal dynamics. This separation is explicitly defined in Section 3.1 and distinguishes SmartBench from prior works like HomeBench, which ignore temporal anomalies.

**Fine‑grained annotation and metrics** – Each anomalous sample includes a set of evidence devices and a human‑written rationale. This support enables metrics beyond binary detection: Anomaly Location (AL) measures Jaccard overlap between predicted and true device sets; Attribution Consistency (AC) uses GPT‑5‑mini to score explanation similarity. Results (Table 2) show that even models with moderate F1 often fail to localize (AL ≤ 0.491) or explain (AC valid < 70%). Such metrics expose weaknesses that accuracy alone would miss, addressing gaps in benchmarks like Sasha that lack explanation evaluation.

**Three‑stage synthetic pipeline** – Normal samples from public data and GPT‑5 scenarios undergo compliance validation (GPT‑5‑mini + manual correction, 98.5% compliance) followed by controlled anomaly injection via four minimal edits (insertion, deletion, temporal shift, value adjustment) that preserve semantics. This yields a large, diverse (4,400 samples, 155 device types, 15 categories), high‑fidelity dataset without costly real‑world deployment. Table 1 and Section 3.2.2 provide evidence of scale and quality.

**Break conditions** – The mechanisms rely on assumptions: (1) static and temporal reasoning are separable; (2) AL and AC validly measure utility; (3) synthetic anomalies with minimal edits suffice. Potential issues include overlapping cues between tasks, ambiguous evidence sets, judge LLM bias, or synthetic anomalies being too simplistic, all of which could limit diagnostic power or generalizability.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Scenario Definition → Normal Sample Generation → Compliance Validation → Anomaly Injection → Dataset Storage → Evaluation Harness → Scoring → Reporting

**Critical Path**  
1. Define anomaly categories and scenario descriptions.  
2. Generate raw normal samples via GPT‑5 from scenarios.  
3. Run compliance validation; manually correct flagged samples.  
4. Inject anomalies using controlled edit operations.  
5. Store final dataset (split into test sets).  
6. For each model, prepare zero‑/few‑shot prompts per task.  
7. Execute inference on all samples.  
8. Parse outputs into predicted label, device set, and reasoning.  
9. Compute F1, AL, AC against ground truth.  
10. Produce result tables/figures.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- Synthetic vs. real data: Synthetic scales but may miss edge cases; real data is costly and privacy‑sensitive.  
- Context compression: Cuts token usage >50% but risks losing subtle cues; paper shows limited performance gain.  
- Automated compliance: Uses GPT‑5‑mini for speed but introduces model bias; manual review is limited to flagged samples.  
- In‑context learning: Improves accuracy but increases prompt length and cost; zero‑shot is cheaper but less accurate.  
- Metric selection: Accuracy/F1 hide localization/explanation quality; AL/AC require extra annotation.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Very high false positive rate (>70%) → model over‑sensitivity.  
- AL score near 0 → model cannot identify correct devices even when it detects an anomaly.  
- AC valid rate < 50% → explanations are often irrelevant or contradictory.  
- Many samples flagged non‑compliant → pipeline generation issues.  
- Performance drop after compression beyond token reduction → loss of critical information.

**First Three Experiments**  
1. **Baseline replication**: Evaluate the 13 models listed in Section 4.1 on the full test set, reproduce Table 2 and Figure 4 to confirm the main findings (poor performance, especially on context‑dependent).  
2. **ICL vs zero‑shot ablation**: For 50 samples per task, test with 0, 1, 3, 5 few‑shot examples; measure F1 improvement and token cost to replicate Section 4.7.  
3. **Context compression impact**: On 50 long context‑dependent samples, compare raw vs. compressed versions as in Section 4.6; record changes in token count, F1, AL, and AC to validate the limited benefit and spot model‑specific behaviors.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Cross‑zone physical dependencies** – How can future benchmarks and LLM training better capture device interactions that extend beyond immediate room contexts? The paper notes constructing samples with cross‑zone effects is particularly challenging (Appendix A.5).
2. **Synthetic data bias** – To what extent does SmartBench’s synthetic nature (LLM‑generated scenarios, LLM‑based compliance checks) introduce subtle distributional biases that inflate performance on synthetic test data but fail to generalize to real‑world noisy deployments?
3. **Context compression degradation** – What specific semantic and temporal features of context‑dependent anomalous sequences are most degraded by the current “minimal semantic impact” compression, and can a learnable, anomaly‑aware compression model preserve critical cues better?
4. **Root causes of high false positive rates** –gemini‑2.5 achieved the best context‑dependent F1 (67.6%) but with an extremely high false positive rate (78.2%). Are these errors due to model over‑sensitivity, dataset annotation artifacts, or a misdefinition of “normal” baselines?
5. **AC metric reliability** – Can the Anomaly Attribution Consistency (AC) score be validated as a reliable proxy for a model’s actual utility in providing actionable user guidance, or does it merely measure surface‑level semantic alignment without capturing reasoning correctness or safety?

## Limitations
- Synthetic data generation may not fully capture real‑world anomaly complexity, threatening ecological validity.
- AC scoring relies on GPT‑5‑mini as judge, introducing potential model bias and inconsistency.
- Context compression and ICL configurations are underspecified, hindering exact replication.
