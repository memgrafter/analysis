# On Sample-Efficient Generalized Planning via Learned Transition Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.23148
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23148
- **Reference count:** 15
- **Primary result:** State-centric transition modeling with WL embeddings yields superior out-of-distribution satisficing success and sample efficiency compared to action-centric Transformers.

## Executive Summary
Generalized planning aims to learn strategies that transfer across problem instances of varying sizes within the same domain. Recent Transformer-based planners directly predict action sequences but suffer from state drift and poor out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization because they lack explicit world dynamics modeling. This paper proposes a state-centric formulation: a neural model explicitly learns a goal‑conditioned transition model by predicting successor state embeddings via a residual delta. Plans are generated by autoregressively rolling out predicted state trajectories and mapping them back to symbolic actions through nearest‑neighbor decoding against valid successors, guaranteeing symbolic validity. Experiments on four IPC domains show that combining this approach with size‑invariant Weisfeiler–Leman (WL) relational graph embeddings achieves higher OOD satisficing success than action‑centric Transformers (e.g., Blocksworld 0.50 vs. 0.13; VisitAll 1.00 vs. 0.64) while using orders of magnitude fewer parameters (~1–2 M vs. ~25–220 M) and training on significantly smaller unaugmented datasets. Fixed‑size factored encodings completely fail on extrapolation, confirming the necessity of permutation‑ and size‑invariant representations. Residual delta modeling consistently improves performance for non‑sequential models (XGBoost). A key limitation is failure in the Logistics domain under strict size extrapolation, indicating challenges with hierarchical long‑range dependencies.

## Method Summary
The method follows a neuro‑symbolic loop. First, symbolic expert trajectories for small training instances are generated using Fast Downward. States and goals are encoded as fixed‑dimensional vectors using Weisfeiler–Leman (WL) graph embeddings, which are permutation‑ and size‑invariant. A transition model \(f_\theta\) (either a 2‑layer LSTM or XGBoost) is trained to predict either the next‑state embedding or the residual \(\Delta = \phi(s_{t+1}) - \phi(s_t)\) given the current state embedding \(\phi(s_t)\) and goal embedding \(\phi(g)\). At inference, given an initial symbolic state \(s_t\), the system computes the predicted next‑state embedding \(v_t = \phi(s_t) + f_\theta(\phi(s_t), \phi(g))\). Then it enumerates all valid symbolic successors \(\text{Succ}(s_t)\) using the domain operators, embeds each candidate successor, and selects the one whose embedding is closest to \(v_t\). The corresponding action is executed symbolically, updating \(s_t\). This closed‑loop grounding corrects neural drift and ensures every step corresponds to a valid operator application.

## Key Results
- **Out‑of‑distribution success:** Significantly higher than action‑centric Transformers (Blocksworld 0.50 vs. 0.13; VisitAll 1.00 vs. 0.64).
- **Efficiency:** ~1–2 M parameters vs. ~25–220 M; works with small unaugmented training sets.
- **Invariance necessity:** Fixed‑size factored (FSF) encodings fail completely on extrapolation; WL essential.
- **Residual delta:** Consistent improvement for XGBoost across domains.
- **Domain limitation:** 0% extrapolation success in Logistics, revealing difficulties with hierarchical long‑range dependencies.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Explicit transition‑model learning prevents state drift**
- **Claim:** Predicts successor state embeddings rather than actions to avoid accumulation of implicit state errors during autoregressive decoding.
- **Mechanism:** The model outputs \(v_t = \phi(s_t) + f_\theta(\phi(s_t), \phi(g))\). After nearest‑neighbor decoding among valid successors \(\text{Succ}(s_t)\), the symbolic state \(s_{t+1}\) is grounded, breaking error propagation.
- **Core assumption:** Deterministic STRIPS dynamics allow exact enumeration of valid successors, enabling neuro‑symbolic correction.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract: “enforces explicit world‑state evolution, enables successor validation.” Section 3: details enumeration and nearest‑neighbor selection.
- **Break condition:** If \(v_t\) is closer to an invalid embedding than any \(\phi(s')\) for \(s' \in \text{Succ}(s_t)\), decoding selects an invalid state; more likely under distributional shift.

**Mechanism 2: Permutation‑ and size‑invariant representations enable extrapolation**
- **Claim:** WL embeddings produce fixed‑dimensional vectors independent of object count, allowing models trained on small instances to generalize to larger ones.
- **Mechanism:** WL color refinement aggregates local neighborhoods in the instance graph, yielding a histogram over a fixed vocabulary. Input dimensionality stays constant even as the number of objects grows.
- **Core assumption:** Domain dynamics are structurally consistent across sizes; local relational patterns scale predictably. The 1‑WL expressivity suffices.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 4: FSF encodings fail extrapolation; Section 5: WL models achieve strictly positive extrapolation in three domains.
- **Break condition:** If the domain requires global constraints beyond 1‑WL (e.g., parity conditions), WL may not capture necessary invariants.

**Mechanism 3: Residual delta modeling improves sample efficiency for sparse STRIPS dynamics**
- **Claim:** Predicting \(\Delta_t = \phi(s_{t+1}) - \phi(s_t)\) focuses learning on changed predicates, easing regression for non‑sequential models.
- **Mechanism:** In STRIPS, most predicates remain unchanged. The delta vector is sparse and lower‑magnitude, reducing regression variance.
- **Core assumption:** WL embedding aligns with STRIPS fluents; unchanged predicates map to near‑zero or predictable dimensions.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 3: residual formulation “explicitly encodes frame axioms”; Section 5: XGBoost delta improves Blocksworld extrapolation (0.25 → 0.50).
- **Break condition:** The benefit diminishes if the embedding is entangled, effects are dense, or sequential models can infer unchanged elements via recurrence.

## Foundational Learning
- **Permutation & Size Invariance**:  
  *Why needed*: Generalization to more objects requires a representation whose dimensionality does not depend on object count; fixed‑slot encodings break.  
  *Quick check*: If adding a block changes the state vector’s length, a network trained on 4 blocks cannot process 10 blocks.

- **STRIPS Sparse Dynamics**:  
  *Why needed*: STRIPS actions modify only a few ground predicates; predicting the full next‑state vector wastes capacity on unchanged dimensions, hurting sample efficiency.  
  *Quick check*: In a typical STRIPS action, what fraction of ground predicates change? Usually very few.

- **Neuro‑Symbolic Decoding**:  
  *Why needed*: Continuous neural predictions must be projected onto a discrete, valid state to ensure plan executability; nearest‑neighbor over \(\text{Succ}(s_t)\) provides this grounding.  
  *Quick check*: Why not construct \(s_{t+1}\) by taking the argmax over all grounded predicates? That may combine incompatible predicates, yielding a state not reachable by any single action.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Symbolic Planner (Fast Downward) → State Encoder (WL) → Transition Model (\(f_\theta\)) → Plan Decoder (nearest‑neighbor over \(\text{Succ}(s_t)\)) → Validator (VAL)

**Critical Path:**  
Generate small‑instance trajectories with Fast Downward → reconstruct full state sequences with VAL → build WL vocabulary from training states → embed all (state, goal) pairs → train \(f_\theta\) (LSTM or XGBoost) → at test time, iterate: embed current state + goal → predict next embedding → enumerate \(\text{Succ}(s_t)\) → pick nearest successor → extract action → update state.

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- *LSTM vs XGBoost*: LSTM captures temporal dependencies (better in Gripper); XGBoost is simpler, faster, and benefits more from delta sparsity (Blocksworld, VisitAll).  
- *State vs Delta*: Residual delta reduces regression complexity for sparse STRIPS effects, especially for non‑sequential models; may hurt LSTM if recurrent state needs full transition.  
- *WL vs FSF*: WL necessary for size invariance but requires graph construction and vocabulary; FSF trivial but fails on extrapolation.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- Logistics domain 0% extrapolation: indicates inability to model hierarchical long‑range dependencies.  
- FSF encodings cause 0% extrapolation: classic failure of fixed‑size representations.  
- Decoding loops without progress: model predicts no change or dead‑ends; \(s_t\) repeats.

**First 3 Experiments:**  
1. *Invariance Smoke Test*: Train WL model on 4‑block Blocksworld, test on 5‑block (in‑distribution) and 9‑block (extrapolation). Expect high success on 5‑block, positive on 9‑block; with FSF, expect 0% on 9‑block.  
2. *Delta Impact*: For Blocksworld, train two XGBoost models (state vs. delta). Compare extrapolation success; expect delta > state.  
3. *Model Class Comparison*: In Gripper, compare LSTM vs. XGBoost. Analyze per‑step validity; test if LSTM maintains symbolic validity longer and if XGB fails early because its one‑step Markovian approximation is insufficient.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How can the state‑centric framework be extended to effectively handle domains with hierarchical and long‑range dependencies, such as Logistics, under strict size extrapolation?** The current one‑step successor matching yields 0% success in Logistics extrapolation, suggesting the need for multi‑step or abstract transitions while preserving symbolic verification.

2. **Under what domain characteristics does LSTM’s sequential memory provide an advantage over non‑sequential models (e.g., XGBoost) for transition learning in generalized planning?** The paper reports divergent results (e.g., LSTM succeeds in Gripper but fails in Blocksworld/VisitAll) but does not characterize the determining domain properties.

3. **Why does residual delta modeling improve extrapolation for tree‑based models (XGBoost) consistently but have mixed effects on LSTM performance across domains?** The effect on LSTM is domain‑dependent (e.g., delta improves Blocksworld but degrades Gripper). The underlying mechanism causing this interaction remains unresolved.

## Limitations
- **Logistics domain failure:** 0% extrapolation success highlights a limitation for hierarchical, long‑range dependency domains.
- **Beam search ambiguity:** The paper mentions beam width 3 during decoding but Algorithm 1 is greedy; the exact integration is unclear from the text.
- **WL expressive ceiling:** Only 2‑step refinements were used; higher \(k\) might capture more invariants but was not explored.

## Confidence
- **Transition‑model grounding prevents state drift:** Medium (internal evidence; limited external corroboration).  
- **WL invariance is necessary for size extrapolation:** High (strong ablation contrast with FSF across multiple domains).  
- **Residual delta improves sample efficiency for non‑sequential models:** Medium (consistent XGBoost gains but mixed for LSTM).

## Next Checks
1. **Hierarchical dependency probe:** Re‑run Logistics with a handcrafted hierarchical decomposition (e.g., separate subproblems for package/vehicle routing) to test whether the one‑step Markovian approximation is the core limitation.
2. **Beam search implementation:** Extract and reproduce the exact beam width 3 decoding from the authors’ codebase to confirm whether greedy search or parallel beam rollouts were used, and measure sensitivity.
3. **WL expressivity stress test:** Train WL models with \(k=3\) and \(k=4\) refinements on Blocksworld/VisitAll extrapolation splits; if success rates increase, it suggests 1‑WL (k=2) may be insufficient for some domain invariants.
