# Anatomy of Unlearning: The Dual Impact of Fact Salience and Model Fine-Tuning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.19612
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19612
- **Reference count**: 15
- **Primary result**: Popular facts are significantly harder to erase than rare ones; SFT models exhibit smoother forgetting dynamics, more stable hyperparameter tuning, and 10–50% higher retention on rare facts compared to pretrained models; pretrained models behave unpredictably, sometimes improving performance on popular forget sets (relearning).

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates machine unlearning (MU) in large language models, challenging the common assumption that all facts are equally forgettable. The authors introduce DUET, a benchmark of 28,600 Wikidata-derived question-answer pairs annotated with fact popularity via Wikipedia link counts and LLM-based salience. Using LLaMA-3.1-8B in both pretrained and supervised fine-tuned (SFT) variants, they evaluate three gradient-based unlearning algorithms (Gradient Ascent, Gradient Difference, Negative Preference Optimization) with ROUGE-L. Results reveal that fact popularity significantly influences forgetting difficulty: popular facts are harder to erase. Moreover, the training stage matters: SFT models exhibit smoother forgetting dynamics, more stable hyperparameter tuning, and better retention of rare facts, while pretrained models show instability and even relearning of popular facts. The study demonstrates that MU outcomes are jointly shaped by fact salience and model training state, with implications for designing reliable unlearning methods.

## Method Summary
The study leverages the DUET benchmark, comprising 28,600 QA pairs derived from Wikidata. Facts are stratified by popularity using a composite score of Wikipedia inbound links and an LLM-based salience assessment. Models include LLaMA-3.1-8B in two configurations: a raw pretrained checkpoint and a variant fine-tuned on the full DUET dataset via LoRA (SFT). Unlearning is performed with three algorithms: Gradient Ascent (maximizing forget loss), Gradient Difference (balancing forget and retain losses), and Negative Preference Optimization (a preference-based approach). Hyperparameters (learning rate 2e-5, 2 epochs) are selected via grid search. Primary evaluation uses ROUGE-L on forget (should decrease) and retain (should remain stable) sets, supplemented by LLM-as-a-Judge and intrinsic memorization metrics. The main experiments use the “places city” domain with 5% forget sets (rare and popular) and a retain subset of 500 examples, analyzing forgetting curves, retention stability, and hidden-state similarity.

## Key Results
- Popular facts are significantly harder to erase than rare ones, across all unlearning algorithms.
- SFT models show smoother forgetting dynamics, more stable hyperparameter tuning, and 10–50% higher retention on rare facts compared to pretrained models.
- Pretrained models exhibit instability, sometimes improving performance on popular forget sets (relearning) and suffering abrupt catastrophic forgetting on retain sets.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Popular facts are significantly harder to erase than rare ones.
- **Mechanism:** The external prominence (Wikipedia link counts) and model-perceived salience of a fact correlate with its resistance to gradient-based unlearning updates. Popular facts are more deeply embedded in model parameters due to higher frequency and distribution during pretraining, requiring a stronger learning signal (higher LR) to shift their activation.
- **Core assumption:** Fact popularity, proxied by Wikipedia link counts, is a valid indicator of in-model memorization strength and resistance to unlearning.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Popular facts are significantly harder to erase than rare ones."
  - [section 3.2, Finding 1] "For popular entities, the Pretrained model displays counter-intuitive behavior: instead of forgetting, it improves performance... SFT model behaves as expected: unlearning consistently decreases ROUGE-L."
  - [corpus] Corpus neighbors discuss related unlearning works but do not directly provide evidence for this claim; evidence is from the paper's own experiments.
- **Break condition:** If the correlation between Wikipedia link counts and internal model salience (validated via LLM judgment) breaks down for a new domain or model family, the popularity effect may not hold.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** An SFT step on the forget data yields smoother forgetting dynamics, more stable hyperparameter tuning, and higher retention quality compared to unlearning directly on a pretrained model.
- **Mechanism:** SFT adapts the model's output head and a subset of parameters (via LoRA) to a supervised QA task, which renormalizes the knowledge distribution. This creates a more disentangled representation where target facts are less entangled with core competencies, making gradient-based unlearning more selective and predictable.
- **Core assumption:** SFT on the full dataset (including both forget and retain facts) creates a more "unlearnable" parameter state than a pure pretrained model, where knowledge is more uniformly distributed and brittle.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "SFT models exhibit smoother forgetting dynamics, more stable hyperparameter tuning, and 10-50% higher retention on rare facts compared to pretrained models."
  - [section 3.2, Finding 2] "SFT models are more robust on the retain set, showing roughly half the risk of catastrophic forgetting compared to rare-fact removal, while pretrained models degrade more abruptly."
  - [section 3.1] Use of LoRA for SFT variant, indicating parameter-efficient adaptation changes the optimization landscape.
- **Break condition:** If SFT is performed with a very small dataset or with a high dropout/regularization regime that severely underfits, the smoother dynamics may not emerge. The effect is contingent on the SFT model having already absorbed the general knowledge distribution.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Unlearning on pretrained models is unstable and prone to relearning or catastrophic forgetting, especially for popular facts.
- **Mechanism:** In a pretrained model, factual knowledge is distributed across many layers and parameters as part of the general language modeling objective. Unlearning gradients interact unpredictably with this distributed representation. For popular facts, the strong pretraining signal can cause the unlearning update to be overridden (relearning) or to trigger a collapse in adjacent knowledge (catastrophic forgetting) because the optimization lacks a stabilizing "anchor" provided by SFT.
- **Core assumption:** The raw pretrained parameter state lacks the task-specific separation of knowledge that SFT induces, making it more sensitive to the direction and magnitude of unlearning updates.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Pretrained models behave unpredictably, sometimes improving performance on popular forget sets (relearning)."
  - [section 3.2, Finding 1] "Pretrained models are unstable, prone to abrupt degradation, and even unintended relearning on popular facts."
  - [section C.4, Hidden-state similarity] "In pretrained models, hidden states remain highly similar even for facts targeted by unlearning, indicating limited internal adaptation." This suggests the unlearning signal fails to locally modify representations effectively.
- **Break condition:** If unlearning is performed with an extremely precise, model editing-style intervention (e.g., MEMIT) rather than gradient-based methods, the instability might be mitigated. The claim is specific to gradient-based MU algorithms evaluated.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Wikidata → Salience computation (Wikipedia sitelinks + LLM) → Stratified DUET splits (rare/popular forget/retain) → Model Variants (Pretrained, SFT) → Unlearning Algorithms (GA, GD, NPO) → Evaluation Metrics (ROUGE-L, LLM-as-Judge, intrinsic)

### Critical path
1. **Data Pipeline:** Wikidata → Filter unique answers → LLM validation (BERT sim >0.6) → Compute popularity → Stratify into rare/popular forget & retain sets → Create compact city subsets.
2. **Model Preparation:** Obtain base pretrained checkpoint → Train SFT variant on full DUET via LoRA.
3. **Unlearning Loop:** For each (algorithm, LR, epoch) combo: initialize from either pretrained or SFT checkpoint → optimize unlearning loss on forget set (and retain set for GD) → evaluate on forget and retain QA splits.
4. **Analysis:** Plot ROUGE-L vs. LR for each (train type, popularity, algorithm) → compare trends, identify relearning/collapse thresholds → run diagnostics (token ranks, hidden states) on selected points.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Benchmark Scale vs. Control:** Using a single large domain (`places city`) reduces noise from domain shifts but limits generalizability; multi-domain subset (Table 8) is used for validation.
- **Metric Choice:** ROUGE-L is efficient for large-scale QA but may miss semantic errors; supplemented by LLM judge and intrinsic analysis.
- **SFT Method:** LoRA chosen for efficiency/stability vs. full fine-tuning; likely contributes to the smoother SFT dynamics.
- **UNlearn Set Size:** Evaluated at 1%, 5%, 10%; 5% used as main setting as a balance between signal strength and catastrophic risk.

### Failure signatures
- **Pretrained + Popular Forget:** ROUGE-L on forget set *increases* with LR/epochs (relearning). Retain set may drop abruptly at high LR.
- **SFT + Popular Forget:** Forget set ROUGE-L decreases effectively, but retain set degrades sharply at moderate LR (catastrophic forgetting of retain).
- **Any + Rare Forget (Pretrained):** Forget set ROUGE-L decreases but retention often collapses earlier and more severely than for SFT.
- **Hyperparameter Instability:** Small LR changes cause large swings in retain performance for pretrained models; SFT curves are smoother.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Baseline Contrast:** Run no-unlearning baselines for both `Pretrained` and `SFT` on `Places City` forget/retain sets to establish initial performance gaps (SFT has higher baseline due to supervised QA tuning).
2. **Popularity Effect (SFT):** Apply NPO at LR=2e-5 for 2 epochs on SFT model, compare `popular_forget_5` vs. `rare_forget_5` forget/retain ROUGE-L. Expect: popular forget drops more, retain drops more for popular forget.
3. **Stability Check:** For `Pretrained` model with `popular_forget_5`, sweep LR from 1e-6 to 5e-5 using Gradient Ascent. Expect: forget ROUGE-L might increase at low LR (relearning) and only drop at very high LR with retain collapse. Contrast with SFT's monotonic forget decrease.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How do the reported interactions between fact popularity and training type generalize to other model architectures (e.g., larger LLMs, different families) and a more diverse set of factual domains beyond the heavily city-focused benchmark?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The "Limitations" section explicitly states the scope is limited to LLaMA-3.1-8B and the *places city* forget/retain sets, noting that "broader domains and larger models are future work."
- **Why unresolved:** The core findings are based on a single model family and a domain-dominated split. Generalizability is unknown; architectural differences (e.g., Gemma vs. LLaMA) or domain-specific knowledge patterns could alter the observed popularity asymmetry and SFT/pretrain divergence.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic replication of the DUET experimental protocol on a diverse suite of models (e.g., varying parameter scales, architectures like dense vs. MoE) and on domain-balanced subsets from DUET or other fact-centric benchmarks. Cross-lingual evaluation using popularity metrics adapted to other Wikipedia languages would also test robustness of the popularity signal.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** To what extent are unlearning outcomes dependent on the specific operationalization of "fact popularity"? Would alternative salience signals (e.g., based on web crawl frequency, model internal activation saliency, or temporal recency) reveal different or stronger popularity effects?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The "Limitations" note that "popularity labels: we rely on Wikipedia signals and model salience; these proxies may drift over time and across languages."
- **Why unresolved:** The benchmark's popularity score is a composite of Wikipedia link counts and a one-time LLM judgment. This may not capture other dimensions of salience (e.g., recency, controversy, or modality-specific prominence). The stability of the "popular vs. rare" dichotomy under different definitions is unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Construct parallel versions of DUET forget/retain splits using alternative popularity metrics (e.g., frequency in a large web corpus, temporal dynamics from Wikipedia page views, or gradient-based saliency maps). Re-running the key unlearning experiments would show if the primary findings (harder forgetting for popular facts, SFT stability) hold or change.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Are the observed differences in retention stability between SFT and pretrained models primarily a consequence of the SFT step itself, or are they an artifact of the LoRA fine-tuning method used to create the SFT variant? Would full-parameter SFT yield the same dynamics?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The experimental setup states the SFT variant was trained "using LoRA instead of full fine-tuning for efficiency and stability," acknowledging this as a methodological choice.
- **Why unresolved:** LoRA introduces parameter-efficient updates that may constrain the model's internal reorganization differently than full fine-tuning. The smoother forgetting and higher retention in SFT models could be specific to the low-rank adaptation subspace. This confounds the pure effect of the "SFT training stage."
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicate the core experiments with an SFT variant obtained via full-parameter fine-tuning on the same DUET data, using identical hyperparameter search grids. A comparative analysis of forgetting curves, retention stability, and hidden-state changes (as in Appendix C.4) between LoRA-SFT and full-SFT would isolate the method's impact from the training stage effect.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What is the precise mechanistic reason behind the "relearning" phenomenon in pretrained models—where unlearning popular facts paradoxically improves performance on the forget set? Is this driven by optimization dynamics (e.g., gradient descent on a non-convex loss landscape amplifying existing strong signals) or by a redistribution of probability mass within the model's parametric knowledge?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Finding 1 reports that for popular entities, "the Pretrained model displays counter-intuitive behavior: instead of forgetting, it improves performance on the forget set." The paper describes the symptom but does not probe the underlying cause, an unstated assumption being that this is an instability.
- **Why unresolved:** The phenomenon suggests unlearning signals on deeply embedded (popular) knowledge can be co-opted by the pretrained model's existing parameter configurations. The root cause—whether it's a feature of the loss surface, the gradient's interaction with pretrained weights, or the lack of a stabilizing SFT step—is not investigated.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Perform a detailed diagnostic analysis during unlearning: track gradient norms, loss curvature, and changes in the log-probability distribution over the entire vocabulary for popular vs. rare facts. Compare the optimization trajectories of pretrained vs. SFT models starting from identical initializations. Ablation studies that isolate the contribution of the retain set loss (in GD) or reference model (in NPO) could pinpoint which algorithm components trigger relearning.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Do the unlearning dynamics observed with gradient-based methods (GA, GD, NPO) and self-distillation (UNDIAL) reflect a fundamental trade-off between "erasability" and "selectivity" that is invariant to the algorithm, or are they algorithm-specific artifacts? Specifically, does the rare/popular asymmetry persist under fundamentally different unlearning paradigms like adversarial poisoning or influence-function-based approaches?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper evaluates three gradient-based algorithms and one distillation-based method (UNDIAL in Appendix C.6), noting that "the rare vs. popular asymmetry persists under self-distillation." However, it does not question whether this persistence implies a universal property of unlearning or if all current methods share a common limitation.
- **Why unresolved:** The evidence only covers a narrow family of optimization-based unlearning techniques. If the asymmetry holds across all paradigms, it points to a deep structural property of how LLMs store and modify knowledge (e.g., popular facts being more distributed). If it fails under a different class, it could indicate that current methods are all similarly biased by the training objective.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement and test at least one fundamentally different unlearning approach—such as those based on machine unlearning with differential privacy, influence function estimation, or model editing (e.g., MEMIT)—on the DUET benchmark with both rare and popular splits. A consistent pattern across all tested paradigms would strongly suggest an inherent model property; divergence would highlight algorithm-dependent biases.

## Limitations
- Limited scope to LLaMA-3.1-8B and the places city domain; generalizability to other models and domains uncertain.
- Popularity metric reliant on Wikipedia link counts and a single LLM salience judgment; may not capture all dimensions of fact salience.
- SFT variant uses LoRA; full-parameter fine-tuning could produce different unlearning dynamics.

## Confidence
- Popular facts are harder to erase: Medium
- SFT models exhibit smoother dynamics and better rare-fact retention: Medium
- Pretrained models are unstable and prone to relearning: Medium
- MU outcomes are jointly shaped by fact popularity and training stage: Medium
