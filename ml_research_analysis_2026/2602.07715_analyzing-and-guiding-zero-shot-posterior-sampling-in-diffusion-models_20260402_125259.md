# Analyzing and Guiding Zero-Shot Posterior Sampling in Diffusion Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.07715
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07715
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Spectral guidance weights for diffusion-based inverse problems improve the perceptual-distortion trade-off over heuristics.

## Executive Summary
This work tackles the problem of selecting guidance parameters in zero-shot diffusion-based inverse problems, which are typically chosen via manual heuristics. Under a Gaussian prior assumption and shift-invariant linear degradation operators, the authors derive closed-form spectral representations for both the ideal posterior sampler and practical diffusion-based methods (e.g., DPS, ΠGDM). This spectral perspective enables a principled, method-agnostic optimization to compute guidance weights that jointly account for prior characteristics, degradation operator, and diffusion dynamics. Experiments on synthetic Gaussian data and real datasets (FFHQ, ImageNet) demonstrate that the optimized spectral weights achieve a better balance between measurement fidelity (higher PSNR/SSIM) and perceptual quality (lower LPIPS/FID) compared to standard heuristics, particularly at larger numbers of diffusion steps. The optimization is made efficient through iterative initialization and dimensionality reduction.

## Method Summary
The approach starts by modeling the image prior as a Gaussian with shift-invariant (circulant) covariance Σ₀ and assuming the degradation operator H is linear and circulant (e.g., convolutional blur). These assumptions allow both Σ₀ and H to be diagonalized by the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT), decoupling the posterior inference into independent scalar operations per frequency. For diffusion-based posterior samplers like DPS and ΠGDM, the authors derive closed-form spectral transfer functions that map the diffusion process to the posterior mean and covariance. They then formulate an optimization problem: choose guidance weights (e.g., ζ for DPS) to minimize the expected squared difference between the estimated posterior’s covariance and the true posterior’s covariance, plus a mean-matching term, using the average Wasserstein-2 distance as the metric. This yields a smooth, step-dependent weight schedule. The optimization is solved using L-BFGS-B, with acceleration via iterative initialization (solve for fewer steps, then warm-start for more steps) and PCA-based dimensionality reduction (project onto top eigenvectors of Σ₀). The resulting weights are plugged into the diffusion update equations, replacing heuristic constants.

## Key Results
- Spectral recommendations achieve lower Wasserstein-2 distances than heuristic weights on synthetic Gaussian experiments, approaching the performance of the analytically derived optimal sampler.
- On real datasets (FFHQ, ImageNet), spectral parameters provide a better trade-off: higher PSNR/SSIM (measurement fidelity) and lower LPIPS/FID (perceptual quality) compared to standard heuristics, especially for larger diffusion step counts.
- The optimization is practical: iterative initialization and dimensionality reduction make it efficient even for high-dimensional images.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core insight is that for image priors with shift-invariant covariance and linear, shift-invariant degradations, both the prior and the measurement operator diagonalize under the DFT. This decouples the problem into independent frequency channels, allowing the posterior mean and covariance to be expressed as scalar transfer functions per frequency. As a result, both the ideal Gaussian posterior sampler and diffusion-based approximation algorithms admit closed-form spectral representations. These representations expose how guidance parameters influence the posterior covariance and mean. By formulating an optimization that minimizes the average Wasserstein-2 distance between the estimated posterior and the true posterior, the authors derive guidance weights that explicitly balance measurement fidelity and perceptual quality. The resulting weights vary with the diffusion step because the uncertainty in the prior estimate evolves over time; early steps have higher uncertainty, requiring stronger guidance, while later steps have lower uncertainty, requiring weaker guidance. This adaptive schedule, learned from the spectral analysis, consistently outperforms static heuristics.

## Foundational Learning
- **Gaussian prior & linear inverse problems**  
  Why needed: The entire analysis hinges on closed-form posterior expressions (Wiener filter) for a Gaussian prior and linear degradation.  
  Quick check: "Given prior covariance Σ₀ and linear observation y = Hx + n (noise variance σ²), what is the posterior mean µ_{x|y}?" Answer: µ₀ + Σ₀Hᵀ(HΣ₀Hᵀ + σ²I)⁻¹(y – Hµ₀).

- **Spectral decomposition via Fourier basis**  
  Why needed: Diagonalizes Σ₀ and H, turning matrix operations into element‑wise manipulations across frequencies, enabling scalar transfer functions and a simplified Wasserstein expression.  
  Quick check: "What property must Σ₀ and H satisfy to be simultaneously diagonalizable by the DFT?" Answer: They must be circulant (or block‑circulant with circulant blocks for multi‑channel data).

- **Diffusion marginal and Tweedie’s formula**  
  Why needed: The forward process (xt = √ᾱₜ x₀ + √1–ᾱₜ ε) gives the relationship between a noisy sample and the clean one; the optimal prior denoiser (Eq. 9) is derived from this marginal and is the building block for the posterior update.  
  Quick check: "For a Gaussian prior and diffusion step t, express the MMSE denoiser E[x₀|x_t] in terms of x_t, ᾱ_t, Σ₀, µ₀." Answer: (ᾱ_tΣ₀ + (1‑ᾱ_t)I)⁻¹(√ᾱ_tΣ₀ x_t + (1‑ᾱ_t)µ₀).

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Prior estimation → Spectral diagonalization of Σ₀ and H → Derive D₁, D₂, D₃ → Formulate objective L(ζ) → Solve optimization → Use weights for sampling.

Critical path: The chain is linear; failure at any stage (e.g., inaccurate covariance estimate) propagates. Key dependencies: diagonalization requires circulant Σ₀ and H; optimization requires a differentiable objective; inference requires a pretrained diffusion model.

Design tradeoffs:
- Per‑obs vs. average optimization: Solving for each observation yields instance‑specific weights and slightly better metrics but costs O(K) solves; average optimization is cheaper but less tailored.
- Dimensionality reduction: PCA projection (keep top d′ ≪ d eigenvalues) reduces optimization time dramatically but may discard high‑frequency details that affect fine reconstruction. Typical d′ ≈ 50–100 for 256×256 images.
- Iterative initialization: Warm‑starting from a solution for a smaller number of steps S′ < S greatly speeds convergence, assuming weights vary smoothly with S. Skipping may cause slow or failed convergence.

Failure signatures:
- Optimization diverges or returns negative weights → likely due to poor starting point; use multi‑restart or tighter bounds.
- No improvement over heuristics (or worse LPIPS) → prior covariance Σ₀ estimate may be inaccurate; re‑estimate from more representative data or adjust stationarity assumption.
- Artifacts in reconstructions when using spectral weights → check that H is genuinely diagonalizable (e.g., for non‑uniform blur, use padded DFT or full‑matrix formulation).
- Runtime excessive for S > 400 → increase PCA reduction ratio or interpolate weights from a smaller S instead of full optimization.

First 3 experiments:
1. Synthetic Gaussian test: Generate circulant Gaussian prior, apply low‑pass H, solve optimization analytically. Compare spectral ζ with manually tuned values using Wasserstein‑2 distance; expect spectral ζ to nearly match the analytically optimal posterior.
2. Real‑image posterior sampling: On FFHQ‑256 or ImageNet‑256, fit Gaussian prior (mean + circulant covariance), run full pipeline for DPS with spectral ζ, compute PSNR/SSIM vs. LPIPS/FID. Expect spectral weights to strike a better balance than fixed ζ′=0.3 (FFHQ) or 0.2 (ImageNet).
3. Ablation on optimization choices: Measure runtime and quality when varying (a) per‑obs vs. average loss, (b) PCA dimensions (e.g., d=196 608 vs. d′=100), and (c) single‑S vs. iterative initialization. Expect large speed‑ups with minimal quality loss.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Extension to non-Gaussian priors**: Can the spectral method be extended to non-Gaussian priors while maintaining closed‑form solutions or efficient optimization? The Gaussian assumption is fundamental to derivations; no pathway is provided.
2. **Appropriateness of Wasserstein‑2**: Is Wasserstein‑2 the most suitable discrepancy measure? The paper does not justify over alternatives like KL or MMD; its relation to perceptual metrics is not established.
3. **Robustness to non‑shift‑invariant assumptions**: How robust are the recommendations when the shift‑invariant assumption is violated? Many degradations are not circulant; the analysis relies on diagonalization, which may fail.
4. **Dynamic within‑trajectory adaptation**: Could guidance weights be adapted dynamically during diffusion based on local sample conditions, rather than being fixed per step? Current weights are static across the trajectory for a given setting.

## Limitations
- Assumes Gaussian prior with shift‑invariant covariance and circulant linear operator; real images and many degradations (e.g., non‑uniform blur) violate these assumptions.
- Requires estimation of Σ₀; dimensionality reduction via PCA may discard important high‑frequency information.
- Optimizes an average Wasserstein‑2 proxy that may be mis‑specified relative to the true diffusion posterior and perceptual quality metrics.

## Confidence
- Spectral decoupling under circulant assumptions: **High**
- Closed‑form posterior expressions for DPS/ΠGDM: **High**
- Optimized weights improve fidelity‑quality trade‑off on real images: **Medium**
- Generalization to non‑Gaussian priors or non‑circulant operators: **Low**
