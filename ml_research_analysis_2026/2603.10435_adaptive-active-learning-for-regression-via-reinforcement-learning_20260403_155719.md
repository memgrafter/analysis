# Adaptive Active Learning for Regression via Reinforcement Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.10435
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10435
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: WiGS-SAC reduces cumulative error relative to iGS on 15 of 20 datasets (median relative AUC < 1) and achieves 4% label savings (median Nrel = 0.96).

## Executive Summary
Active learning for regression must balance feature-space exploration and output-space investigation. The state-of-the-art improved Greedy Sampling (iGS) uses a static multiplicative rule that suppresses high-uncertainty samples in dense regions—a “density veto.” This paper proposes Weighted iGS (WiGS), an additive scoring framework where a reinforcement learning agent (WiGS-SAC) dynamically adapts the weight between exploration and investigation. Across 20 benchmark datasets, WiGS-SAC outperforms iGS on 15, achieves a median relative AUC below 1, and saves 4% of labels (median Nrel = 0.96). The approach remains robust to irregular densities and non-linear models by learning non-stationary policies that avoid the density veto, albeit with a significant computational overhead.

## Method Summary
WiGS computes an additive candidate score \(s = w \cdot \phi(d_x) + (1-w) \cdot \phi(d_y)\), where \(\phi\) normalizes pairwise feature and prediction distances for comparability. The weight \(w(t)\) determines the trade-off between exploration (feature diversity via \(d_x\)) and investigation (prediction uncertainty via \(d_y\)). Baselines include static variants: GSx (pure exploration), GSy (pure investigation), iGS (multiplicative \(\min(d_x \cdot d_y)\)), and adaptive strategies WiGS-MAB (UCB1 over discrete weights \(\{0.25,0.5,0.75\}\)) and WiGS-SAC (soft actor-critic). Experiments follow pool-based active learning: start with 5% random labels, select one sample per iteration until the labeling budget is exhausted. Ridge regression (\(\alpha=0.01\)) and Random Forest (100 trees) serve as base models. Evaluation metrics are Relative AUC (cumulative error vs. iGS) and Relative Label Efficiency (Nrel). The SAC agent’s state comprises the current cross‑validation RMSE, progress \(t/T\), and distributional statistics of the training set; its continuous action \(w \in [0,1]\) aims to maximize cumulative reward \(\Delta\) CV‑RMSE. Hyperparameters include learning rate \(3\mathrm{e}{-4}\), discount \(\gamma=0.99\), replay buffer size \(10^4\), batch size 64, target smoothing \(\tau=0.005\), and entropy coefficient \(0.2\).

## Key Results
- WiGS‑SAC reduces cumulative error relative to iGS on 15/20 datasets (median relative AUC < 1).
- Achieves 4% label savings (median Nrel = 0.96).
- Robustly handles irregular densities and non‑linear models by avoiding the density veto.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Static multiplicative rule causes “density veto”
- **Claim:** The static multiplicative rule in iGS suppresses high‑uncertainty samples in dense feature regions.
- **Mechanism:** In high‑density regions, the normalized feature‑space distance \(d\) approaches zero. Since iGS score is \(S_{\text{iGS}} = d \cdot u\), the product is suppressed regardless of uncertainty \(u\), preventing selection of high‑error samples simply because they lie in dense areas.
- **Core assumption:** Heterogeneous data densities exist where high‑error regions overlap with feature‑space density peaks.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “…the baseline’s multiplicative rule ignores high‑error samples in dense regions.”; [section 3.2] Formal proof of Proposition 3.1 defining the density veto threshold \(\delta\); [corpus] Related active learning work but no direct evidence for this specific failure mode.
- **Break condition:** Fails when data density is uniform or when high‑error regions are also sparse (density veto irrelevant).

### Mechanism 2: Additive framework prevents density veto
- **Claim:** The additive WiGS score \(s = w \cdot \phi(d_x) + (1-w) \cdot \phi(d_y)\) decouples exploration and investigation, allowing high uncertainty to compensate for low density when \(w\) is small.
- **Mechanism:** Unlike multiplication, addition permits a high \(u\) to overcome a low \(d\) for small \(w\). The paper proves there always exists a weight \(w\) such that an additive selector prefers a high‑uncertainty target even in dense regions.
- **Core assumption:** The optimal balance \(w\) is not fixed but dataset‑ and stage‑dependent.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 3.1] Equation 5 defines the additive score and introduces dynamic weight \(w(t)_x\); [section 3.2] Additive robustness proof showing \(w\) can be chosen to overcome density suppression; [corpus] Related work uses static heuristics; no direct support for additive superiority.
- **Break condition:** If the relationship between \(d\) and \(u\) is perfectly multiplicative (rare), additive offers no advantage. Requires proper normalization \(\phi()\).

### Mechanism 3: Reinforcement learning learns dynamic policy
- **Claim:** A reinforcement learning (SAC) agent learns a state‑dependent policy for \(w(t)_x\) that adapts to the evolving learning state, outperforming static schedules.
- **Mechanism:** SAC observes state \(s_t\) (CV error, progress, data distribution) and learns to output continuous \(w\) to maximize cumulative reward (CV error reduction). The entropy term maintains exploration to avoid premature convergence.
- **Core assumption:** The exploration‑investigation trade‑off is non‑stationary; a fixed schedule or weight is suboptimal for complex/heterogeneous datasets.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 3.4.2] Full MDP formulation and SAC optimization; [section 5.1] WiGS‑SAC outperforms iGS on 15/20 datasets; static best‑weight baselines fail on mpg; [section C.2] Policy analysis shows agent maintains high variance throughout learning, never converging to a single weight.
- **Break condition:** Requires sufficient labeling budget to overcome RL warm‑up (Section 5.3). May overfit small datasets with high variance.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Data Flow: Initial training set → Model f → Predict on candidate pool → Compute pairwise distances d_x, d_y → Normalize via φ() → Apply weight w(t)_x → Compute additive min-score → Select top candidate.  
RL Agent (WiGS‑SAC): State = [CV_RMSE, t/T, D_tr stats] → Actor network outputs w → Critic networks evaluate Q‑value → Replay buffer stores transitions.  
Training Loop: f trained on D_tr → Agent observes state, selects w, selects sample → Oracle labels sample → Update D_tr → Compute CV_RMSE → Reward = ΔCV_RMSE → Agent update.

**Critical path:**  
1. Initialize D_tr (5% random).  
2. For each AL iteration: train f on D_tr, compute CV_RMSE.  
3. Agent constructs state s_t from D_tr and t.  
4. Agent outputs w(t)_x.  
5. Compute WiGS scores for all pool candidates using w(t)_x, φ(d_x), φ(d_y).  
6. Select x* with max min‑score, query label, update D_tr.  
7. Compute reward r_t = CV_prev - CV_current.  
8. Store (s_prev, w_prev, r_t, s_t) in replay buffer, update agent.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Computational cost: WiGS‑SAC runtime ~27× iGS due to neural network updates. Trade label efficiency for computation.  
- Warm‑up period: SAC lags early; best for large labeling budgets.  
- Action space: MAB uses coarse discrete weights (cheap, limited granularity); SAC uses continuous (expensive, flexible).

**Failure signatures:**  
- SAC: Early iterations show no improvement over iGS; may never surpass if budget too small (<100 labels in some datasets).  
- MAB: Performance sensitive to arm set and exploration constant c; coarse grid may miss optimal weight.  
- Robustness: Check if φ() normalization choice (standard vs robust scaling) alters ranking; paper shows consistent results (Figure 18).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Synthetic Density Veto Validation**: Run Two‑Regime DGP (Section 4.1). Confirm iGS fails in [0.8,0.9] trap, WiGS‑SAC succeeds. Visualize selected points and agent weights.  
2. **Benchmark Suite Comparison**: Run all 18 datasets with 100 seeds. Compute Relative AUC (Equation 7) and Nrel (Figure 4). Verify WiGS‑SAC median Rel. AUC < 1 on ≥15 datasets.  
3. **Policy Analysis**: On synthetic data, extract average w(t)_x per selected sample across feature space. Expect red (exploration) at high‑curvature points, blue (investigation) in linear/noisy regions (Figures 6,7).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** What labeling budget size (as a fraction of total data) is necessary for WiGS‑SAC to overcome its warm‑up period and consistently outperform static iGS?  
**Basis in paper:** Section 5.3 describes an algorithmic warm‑up period where WiGS‑SAC lags behind iGS early on, and notes that performance parity on small datasets (e.g., Yacht, NO2) suggests budget size matters, but no quantitative threshold is established.  
**Why unresolved:** The paper provides anecdotal evidence across datasets of varying sizes but does not systematically isolate budget size as a variable or define the minimum conditions for adaptivity to pay off.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments where the number of active learning iterations is varied from very small to large on multiple datasets, plotting relative AUC against budget fraction to identify crossover points where WiGS‑SAC becomes superior; include statistical tests for significance at different budget levels.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** How do the RL agent’s weight choices (w_x) correlate with local data characteristics—such as feature density, prediction uncertainty, and functional curvature—in high‑dimensional, real‑world datasets?  
**Basis in paper:** Appendix C.3 visualizes learned policies on 1D synthetic data, showing patterns like high exploration (w_x ≈ 1) at function vertices and investigation (w_x ≈ 0) in linear or noisy regions, but this analysis is restricted to low‑dimensional, synthetic cases.  
**Why unresolved:** The spatial policy analysis is not extended to multi‑dimensional datasets where concepts like “vertices” or “linear regions” are not well‑defined, and it is unclear which data properties drive the agent’s decisions in complex feature spaces.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Apply dimensionality reduction (e.g., t‑SNE, UMAP) to high‑dimensional datasets, color points by average assigned weight from WiGS‑SAC, and correlate with local metrics (e.g., density via KNN, uncertainty via model variance, gradient magnitude); or train interpretable models (e.g., decision trees) to predict w_x from these features and evaluate feature importance.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** Can alternative reinforcement learning algorithms or discretized strategies (like WiGS‑MAB) achieve near‑SAC performance with significantly lower computational cost, making adaptive WiGS practical for time‑sensitive applications?  
**Basis in paper:** Section E reports WiGS‑SAC’s median runtime as 27× that of iGS (416.4 s vs. 15.5 s), while WiGS‑MAB is nearly as fast as iGS, but the paper does not deeply compare their performance‑cost trade‑offs beyond median AUC.  
**Why unresolved:** The paper presents SAC as the adaptive champion but does not explore whether cheaper alternatives (e.g., bandits, simpler policy gradients) can approach its benefits, or quantify the marginal gain per unit compute.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Benchmark a range of RL methods (e.g., DQN for discretized weights, PPO with smaller networks) and heuristic adaptations (e.g., time‑decay tuned via light validation) against SAC on a suite of datasets, plotting relative AUC against runtime to identify Pareto‑efficient methods; include sensitivity analysis of SAC’s hyperparameters to assess robustness.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** Is the reward signal based on K‑fold cross‑validation RMSE reduction the most effective for training the WiGS agent, or could alternative rewards (e.g., based on uncertainty reduction or exploration metrics) improve learning efficiency and final accuracy?  
**Basis in paper:** Section 3.4.2 defines reward as \(r_t = \text{CV}_{t-1}^{\text{RMSE}} - \text{CV}_t^{\text{RMSE}}\) to avoid data leakage, but no comparison to other reward formulations is provided, leaving open whether this proxy aligns best with active learning goals.  
**Why unresolved:** The choice is motivated by validation best practices but not empirically justified; CV RMSE can be high‑variance in small labeled sets, and rewards directly targeting informativeness (e.g., expected error reduction) might accelerate learning.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Implement alternative reward functions (e.g., reduction in prediction variance, increase in diversity of selected points, expected error reduction) and compare WiGS‑SAC’s learning curves, final AUC, and label efficiency across benchmarks; analyze reward correlation with oracle performance to assess informativeness.

## Limitations
- ϕ(·) normalization: Exact per‑iteration normalization scheme is unspecified; variations could alter candidate rankings.
- SAC state definition: “Distributional statistics of D_tr” is vague; precise features (e.g., means, variances, quantiles) are unknown and critical for policy learning.
- Computational cost: WiGS‑SAC median runtime ~27× that of iGS, limiting practical adoption for large‑scale or real‑time applications.
