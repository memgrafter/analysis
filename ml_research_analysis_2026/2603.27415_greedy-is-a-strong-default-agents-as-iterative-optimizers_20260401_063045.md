# Greedy Is a Strong Default: Agents as Iterative Optimizers

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.27415  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27415  
- **Reference count**: 13  
- **Primary result**: Greedy hill climbing with an LLM agent is a strong default, achieving equal or better performance with 2–3× fewer evaluations than simulated annealing and parallel methods across four tasks.

## Executive Summary
This paper explores replacing random proposal generators in classical iterative optimization algorithms (hill climbing, simulated annealing, parallel investigators) with a large language model (LLM) agent that reasons about evaluation diagnostics. Across four tasks—discrete rule discovery, mixed hyperparameter tuning, and continuous XGBoost tuning—the authors find that greedy hill climbing with early stopping matches or outperforms more sophisticated approaches while requiring dramatically fewer evaluations. The LLM’s strong learned prior yields most of the improvement in the first round and eliminates catastrophic failures common in random search. These results suggest that, when paired with an LLM, simple acceptance rules are sufficient for efficient optimization.

## Method Summary
The method instantiates an iterative optimization loop with three core components: an **Investigator** (LLM agent), a **Reviewer** (acceptance rule), and an **Orchestrator** (loop manager). Given the current best configuration and rich diagnostics (e.g., training logs, cross‑validation metrics, error patterns), the Investigator proposes a new candidate. The Orchestrator evaluates the candidate using a task‑specific harness (e.g., training a model), then the Reviewer applies a rule—greedy, simulated annealing, or a parallel variant—to decide whether to update the best configuration. Early stopping triggers after a fixed number of consecutive rejections. This framework reuses classical optimizers’ acceptance logic but leverages the LLM’s prior and diagnostic reasoning to generate high‑quality proposals, reducing the total number of expensive evaluations.

## Key Results
- Greedy hill climbing matched or outperformed simulated annealing and parallel investigators while using **2–3× fewer evaluations** across all four tasks.
- Substantial gains: Breast cancer rule accuracy **86.0% → 96.5%**; LoRA fine‑tuning on SST‑2 **2× more efficient** than Optuna TPE; XGBoost AUC **0.9297 → 0.9317** with **3× fewer** evaluations vs. CMA‑ES.
- The LLM’s prior delivered **most improvement in round 1**, making acceptance‑rule sophistication largely unnecessary, and produced **zero catastrophic failures** compared to 60% for random search.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Greedy hill climbing matches or outperforms simulated annealing when the LLM's proposal distribution is concentrated and the objective is unimodal within that region.
- **Mechanism**: The LLM's learned prior yields a proposal distribution with narrow effective support (Θq). Within this support, the objective function M is approximately unimodal, so greedy ascent converges to the local optimum. Simulated annealing's barrier‑crossing ability is unnecessary because there are no significant energy barriers between distinct basins.
- **Core assumption**: The LLM's prior is strong enough to cluster proposals in a small region, and the underlying objective has a single basin within that region.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - [abstract] "the LLM's learned prior appears strong enough that acceptance‑rule sophistication has limited impact—round 1 alone delivers the majority of improvement, and variants converge to similar configurations across strategies."  
  - [section 2.6] "If the objective M restricted to Θq is unimodal—i.e., Θq contains at most one local optimum—then greedy and SA converge to the same solution."  
  - [section 4.2] "All XGBoost investigators—whether greedy, SA, or parallel with directives—converge to the same 'conventional' region: depth 5–7, colsample 0.35–0.5..."  
- **Break condition**: If the LLM's effective support Θq is wide (diverse proposals) or the objective has multiple distinct basins within Θq (sharp local optima), then simulated annealing's stochastic acceptance may help escape local optima that greedy would miss.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: The first proposal from the LLM agent typically delivers the majority of total improvement because the LLM's prior encodes strong domain knowledge about good configurations.
- **Mechanism**: The LLM, trained on extensive machine learning literature, possesses a prior over high‑performing hyperparameters or rule structures. When given the seed and diagnostics, it immediately proposes a significantly better candidate by applying this prior. Subsequent rounds yield diminishing returns as the search approaches the local optimum.
- **Core assumption**: The seed configuration is far from optimal and the LLM's prior is accurately aligned with the task.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - [abstract] "round 1 alone delivers the majority of improvement"  
  - [section 3] Figure 2 shows round 1 accounts for most gains across all tasks; e.g., LoRA SST‑2 round 1 contributed +3.9–4.5% validation gain.  
  - [section 4.2] "Across all tasks, round 1 contributes the majority of total improvement."  
- **Break condition**: If the seed is already near‑optimal or the LLM's prior is misaligned (e.g., domain shift), round 1 may not yield large gains, requiring more rounds.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: The LLM agent's diagnostic reasoning implicitly constrains the search to viable configurations, eliminating catastrophic failures common in random and some Bayesian methods.
- **Mechanism**: The Investigator analyzes evaluation diagnostics (training curves, per‑fold metrics, error patterns) to reason about issues like overfitting or class imbalance. It then proposes coherent parameter adjustments that maintain training stability, avoiding degenerate combinations that cause divergence or near‑chance accuracy.
- **Core assumption**: The LLM correctly interprets the diagnostics and proposes safe updates.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - [abstract] "the framework produced zero catastrophic failures compared to 60% for random search."  
  - [section 3.2] "The LLM’s domain knowledge also acts as an implicit constraint: it avoids degenerate parameter combinations, producing an IQR of ∼0.02 across all proposals compared to 0.60 for random search."  
  - [section 4.4] "Across all HPO experiments... the agentic method produced zero catastrophic failures (test accuracy below chance level), compared to 60% for random search and 33% for SMAC on STL‑10."  
- **Break condition**: If diagnostics are misleading/incomplete or the LLM misinterprets them, catastrophic proposals may still occur; also in highly sensitive spaces (e.g., GANs), even informed proposals might fail.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Investigator (LLM agent) → Reviewer (acceptance rule) → Orchestrator (loop manager) → Evaluation Harness (training/evaluation) ↔ Storage (persistance)

### Critical Path
1. Orchestrator loads seed configuration and runs initial evaluation to obtain diagnostics.
2. For each round: send current best config and diagnostics to one or more Investigators.
3. Investigator returns proposed config(s).
4. Orchestrator submits each proposal to the Evaluation Harness (potentially in parallel).
5. Collect evaluation metrics and new diagnostics for each proposal.
6. Reviewer compares new metrics to current best using the acceptance rule; if accepted, update current best and diagnostics.
7. Check early stopping condition (e.g., 2 consecutive rejections); if met, terminate.
8. Store final best configuration for reporting.

### Design Tradeoffs
- Single vs. multiple investigators: Parallelism with strategic directives encourages diversity but may converge to the same region due to shared LLM prior; adds coordination overhead.
- Acceptance rule: Greedy is simplest and empirically strong; simulated annealing adds stochastic acceptance but requires temperature tuning and often wastes evaluations.
- LLM model choice: A single strong model (Claude Opus) performed best; introducing a second model (Codex) without moderation led to over‑aggressive proposals and underperformance.
- Diagnostic richness: Providing full training logs and per‑fold metrics enables diagnostic reasoning; limiting to score history (as in OPRO‑lite) reduces effectiveness.
- Evaluation budget: The framework reduces training evaluations but incurs LLM API costs; tradeoff depends on relative cost of LLM calls vs. training.

### Failure Signatures
- Catastrophic failures: Any evaluation yields test accuracy below chance (e.g., <0.5 for binary classification). Monitor per trial.
- High proposal variance: Proposals exhibit large performance swings (high std dev), indicating weak use of diagnostics.
- Rejection streaks: Multiple consecutive rejections (>3) suggest the LLM is stuck and cannot improve.
- Convergence to unconventional poor configurations: Best found config is far from expected (e.g., extreme hyperparameters) with suboptimal AUC.
- LLM parsing errors: Investigator output cannot be parsed into a valid configuration, causing round skip.

### First 3 Experiments
1. Breast Cancer rule discovery (discrete): Fast, interpretable, demonstrates LLM's structural reasoning; shows large improvement over seed.
2. LoRA fine‑tuning on SST‑2 (mixed): Tests coordination of interacting hyperparameters; reveals two‑phase optimization; matches Optuna TPE with fewer evaluations.
3. XGBoost on Adult (continuous): Core ablation comparing greedy vs. simulated annealing vs. parallel; validates greedy as strong default; includes OPRO‑lite baseline to isolate diagnostic reasoning.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: Is the LLM's performance due to memorization or genuine diagnostic reasoning?  
  *Basis*: The authors highlight memorization vs. reasoning as an unresolved limitation. *Why unresolved*: Real datasets may contain known good configurations in pretraining data. *Evidence*: Use synthetic tasks with optima outside training data or probe model reasoning.

- **Open Question 2**: Can heterogeneous LLM ensembles break the performance ceiling with single‑model proposals?  
  *Basis*: Multi‑model tests found no gain, but authors speculate potential for novel architectures. *Why unresolved*: Only one task and one alternative LLM tested; generalizability unknown. *Evidence*: Test multi‑LLM collaboration on diverse, out‑of‑distribution tasks.

- **Open Question 3**: Does greedy hill climbing stay optimal in high‑dimensional spaces (>50 parameters)?  
  *Basis*: All search spaces were modest (8–16 params); scalability untested. *Why unresolved*: Higher dimensions may increase ruggedness, possibly benefiting SA/parallel. *Evidence*: Apply to high‑dimensional HPO (e.g., large NAS) and compare algorithms.

- **Open Question 4**: Under what conditions does simulated annealing benefit LLM proposals over greedy?  
  *Basis*: Authors posit SA may help with sharper optima or weaker LLM priors. *Why unresolved*: Only four tasks with strong priors; factors not varied systematically. *Evidence*: Vary prior strength (e.g., random proposals) or use synthetic multi‑basin landscapes.

## Limitations
- Generalizability beyond Claude Opus and the specific tasks; LLM prior strength may vary across models and domains.
- The unimodality assumption within the LLM’s effective support (Θq) is inferred from convergence patterns, not directly measured.
- Diagnostic reasoning’s specific contributions are not isolated; the agent might still fail with misleading/incomplete diagnostics.

## Confidence
- Greedy hill climbing is a strong default: **Medium** (empirical support across 4 tasks, but limited LLM/task diversity and reliance on concentrated proposal distribution).
- Round 1 delivers majority of improvement: **High** (consistent across all experiments, clearly shown in figures).
- Zero catastrophic failures vs. random/SMAC: **Medium** (only compared to two baselines; catastrophic definition narrow—test accuracy below chance).

## Next Checks
1. **Measure effective support and unimodality**: Cluster all LLM‑proposed configurations from multiple runs; fit a surrogate model (e.g., GP) on the objective within the cluster to assess smoothness. Run independent random restarts inside the cluster to test if they converge to the same optimum.
2. **Cross‑LLM replication**: Repeat the XGBoost greedy experiment with at least two other LLMs (e.g., GPT‑4, Llama 3) using identical prompts and settings to verify prior strength transferability.
3. **Diagnostic ablation**: Remove specific diagnostic components (e.g., error patterns, training curves) from the agent’s input in the XGBoost task; measure changes in catastrophic failure rate and evaluation efficiency to quantify each component’s contribution.
