# SkyNet: Belief-Aware Planning for Partially-Observable Stochastic Games

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.27751
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27751
- **Reference count:** 29
- **Primary result:** SkyNet achieves a 75.3% peak win rate against baseline MuZero on the Skyjo card game, corresponding to a +194 Elo improvement (p < 1e-50).

## Executive Summary
We propose SkyNet, an extension of the MuZero algorithm designed for partially observable, stochastic, multi-player games. SkyNet introduces two key modifications: ego conditioning, which adds player-specific embeddings to the latent state before prediction, and auxiliary heads that predict the final winner and rank. These components encourage the latent representation to retain hidden information relevant to long-term outcomes without explicit belief tracking. Evaluated on the imperfect-information card game Skyjo, SkyNet significantly outperforms standard MuZero, achieving a 75.3% peak win rate in head-to-head matches (+194 Elo, p < 1e-50) and also surpassing heuristic opponents (72.0% vs 46.6%). Notably, the belief-aware model initially underperforms but overtakes the baseline once training throughput is sufficient, highlighting a data-driven threshold for the auxiliary supervision to become beneficial.

## Method Summary
SkyNet retains MuZero’s core architecture—observation encoder, representation network, dynamics network, and prediction network—and augments it with player-adaptive and outcome-focused features. Observations are tokenized (board, discard, global, history, decision tokens) and processed by a transformer to produce an initial latent state `h₀`. The dynamics network rolls the latent state forward given actions. Before making predictions, the latent state is augmented with three learned embeddings: `e_ego` (ego player identity), `e_current` (current actor), and `e_nplayers` (total player count). This ego conditioning allows a single network to output player-specific policies and values, which is crucial for non-zero-sum games. Additionally, two auxiliary MLP heads attached to the conditioned latent predict the per-player win probability (winner) and rank distribution. The total loss combines MuZero’s policy/value/reward cross-entropy with the auxiliary losses, with weights ramped up during early training to avoid interference. Training uses self-play with MCTS, a curriculum of heuristic opponents, and an opponent pool to ensure diverse data.

## Key Results
- SkyNet achieves a **75.3% peak win rate** against baseline MuZero in 1000-game head-to-head matches, a +194 Elo gap with statistical significance (p < 1e-50).
- Against a suite of heuristic bots, SkyNet attains **72.0% win rate**, while the baseline MuZero manages only 46.6%.
- The performance curve shows SkyNet initially lags behind MuZero but crosses over between training iterations 250 and 500, demonstrating that the benefit of auxiliary supervision materializes only after sufficient training throughput.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Auxiliary heads retain hidden information
**Claim:** Auxiliary winner/rank prediction heads shape the latent state to retain outcome-relevant hidden information under partial observability.  
**Mechanism:** By training the shared representation to predict final game outcomes (winner, rank) in addition to immediate policy/value, the latent vectors are regularized to encode features predictive of long-term success despite incomplete observations. This acts as a self-supervised objective that complements the primary MuZero losses.  
**Core assumption:** The latent state has sufficient capacity to encode hidden-state information linearly decodable by the auxiliary heads; the auxiliary loss gradients flow effectively through the shared backbone.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions “encouraging latent states to retain outcome-relevant hidden information”; Section 4.2.2 details winner/rank heads and loss weighting; Section 7.7 linear probe results show positive R² on hidden-card features only for the ego-conditioned belief model.  
**Break condition:** If auxiliary loss weights are too high early in training, they could dominate gradients and destabilize learning before the primary MuZero objectives stabilize, as hinted by the initial underperformance.

### Mechanism 2: Ego conditioning enables player-specific estimation
**Claim:** Ego conditioning enables player-specific value/policy estimation from a shared latent representation, facilitating effective perspective augmentation during multi-player self-play.  
**Mechanism:** Learned embeddings for ego player, current actor, and total player count are added to the latent state before prediction. This allows a single network to output different predictions for different players given the same latent history, ensuring that value estimates reflect each player’s unique payoff rather than a zero-sum inverted value.  
**Core assumption:** The conditioning embeddings are low-dimensional enough not to disrupt latent dynamics but expressive enough to capture player-specific strategic nuances.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract references “ego-conditioned auxiliary heads”; Section 4.2.1 formalizes the conditioning equation; Section 7.5 inference-time ablation shows disabling ego conditioning drastically reduces win rate (81.1% → 19.0% at iter 1000), proving active use at planning time.  
**Break condition:** If the number of players exceeds the capacity captured by the `e_nplayers` embedding, the conditioning might fail to disentangle perspectives adequately, leading to value confusion in >2-player settings.

### Mechanism 3: Throughput threshold for beneficial auxiliary supervision
**Claim:** The benefit of belief-aware auxiliary supervision materializes only under sufficient training throughput, as the latent representation needs diverse data to learn hidden-state features without collapsing.  
**Mechanism:** Auxiliary tasks introduce optimization competition early in training; with low data throughput, noise dominates and the model cannot reconcile the multiple objectives. As data quantity and diversity increase (via scaled self-play, opponent pool), the latent space stabilizes and the auxiliary gradient signal becomes constructive rather than destructive.  
**Core assumption:** There exists a data-threshold phenomenon where multi-task learning in this architecture requires a minimum number of decision steps to separate signal from noise.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract states “belief-aware model initially underperforms the baseline but decisively surpasses it once training throughput is sufficient”; Section 7.3 shows crossover between iter 250 and 500; Section 7.6 notes early experiments with 4 episodes/iter showed high instability, scaling to 32 episodes/iter stabilized both models.  
**Break condition:** If throughput is scaled excessively without adjusting other hyperparameters (e.g., replay buffer size, MCTS sims), overfitting to the self-play opponent pool could occur, reducing generalization to new opponents.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**
1. **Observation Encoder:** Tokenizes board/discard/global/action-history/decision tokens; transformer (6 layers, 8 heads) produces `[CLS]` vector.
2. **Representation Network:** Projects `[CLS]` to initial latent state `h₀ ∈ ℝ⁵¹²`.
3. **Dynamics Network:** MLP with residual: `(hₖ, aₖ) → (hₖ₊₁, rₖ)`; action embedded and concatenated.
4. **Prediction Network (Baseline):** Separate MLP heads for policy (16 actions) and value (categorical).
5. **Ego Conditioning Layer:** Adds three learned embeddings (`e_ego`, `e_current`, `e_nplayers`) to latent state before prediction.
6. **Auxiliary Heads:** Winner (per-player win probability) and rank (per-player rank distribution) MLPs acting on ego-conditioned latent.

**Critical path:** `ot → Transformer → h₀ → (hₖ, aₖ) via Dynamics repeatedly → ego-conditioned hₖ → Policy/Value/Winner/Rank heads → MCTS uses policy/value for planning; auxiliary losses update representation/dynamics during training.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Auxiliary loss weighting: Ramp-up (α: 0.1→0.5, β: 0.1→0.25) prevents early domination but may delay convergence.
- Ego conditioning dimension: Small embeddings (likely ~32–64 dims each) add minimal parameters; too large could overfit player counts.
- Transformer vs. MLP encoder: Token-based transformer handles variable players naturally but is heavier than a fixed-board MLP; chosen for scalability to 2–8 players.

**Failure signatures:**
- Early win rate < 40% against heuristic bots while baseline > 50%, with high gradient norm variance (>5).
- Auxiliary losses (winner, rank) plateau around 1.0 (near random for classification), indicating failed representation shaping.
- Inference-time ablation: disabling ego conditioning drops win rate by <5%, suggesting conditioning isn't being used (maybe loss ramped too slowly).

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Sanity check:** Train baseline MuZero on 2-player Skyjo with 4 episodes/iter; confirm it reaches >60% win rate vs. heuristics within 500 iters.
2. **Main comparison:** Train SkyNet with same hyperparameters + auxiliary heads (ramped), and run head-to-head at matched iterations (e.g., 250, 500, 1000) with 1000 games each. Expect crossover.
3. **Ablation:** At a fixed SkyNet checkpoint (iter 500), evaluate with/without ego conditioning (zero out embeddings) in 1000-game matches; expect >10% win rate drop if conditioning is effective.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **What is the independent effect of the auxiliary supervision (winner/rank heads) versus the ego conditioning mechanism on SkyNet’s performance?**  
   - **Basis:** Section 7.5 states that the inference-time ablation does not separate the individual contributions of training-time auxiliary supervision from ego conditioning during training. A full training ablation (ego-only, heads-only) remains future work.  
   - **Why unresolved:** Disentangling these factors is necessary to understand which component drives the improvement and to guide future architectural decisions for partially observable domains.  
   - **What evidence would resolve it:** Train two full-training ablations: one with auxiliary heads but without ego embeddings, and one with ego embeddings but without auxiliary heads. Compare against full SkyNet and the baseline MuZero in 1000‑game head‑to‑head matches and track training curves (loss, win rate, stability).

2. **Does SkyNet’s advantage persist in multi‑player Skyjo (3–8 players) and how does performance scale with player count?**  
   - **Basis:** Limitations note evaluation is limited to 2‑player Skyjo; the model supports 2–8 players architecturally but scaled multi‑player experiments remain future work. Future Work explicitly proposes evaluating 3–8 player Skyjo, where “win signal sparsity (∼1/N) and opponent modeling complexity increase.”  
   - **Why unresolved:** The game is designed for up to eight players; partial observability and non‑zero‑sum dynamics become more challenging, possibly affecting the auxiliary heads’ efficacy.  
   - **What evidence would resolve it:** Train both baseline MuZero and SkyNet on Skyjo with varying numbers of players (3–8) using the same pipeline. Conduct head‑to‑head evaluations (1000 games per checkpoint) to compute win rates and Elo gaps. Also test against the heuristic opponent suite to check consistency of relative improvement.

3. **Can predicting explicit belief distributions over hidden card values and using them to weight chance nodes in MCTS further improve performance?**  
   - **Basis:** Future Work suggests “training the belief head to predict explicit distributions over face‑down card values and using these beliefs to weight chance branches in MCTS following Stochastic MuZero [7].”  
   - **Why unresolved:** Current auxiliary heads predict only outcomes (winner, rank). Explicit beliefs could provide richer, uncertainty‑aware information for decision‑making under partial observability.  
   - **What evidence would resolve it:** Extend SkyNet with an additional head that outputs a categorical distribution for each face‑down card. During MCTS, bias chance node sampling according to these distributions instead of uniform. Compare the new variant against original SkyNet and baseline in Skyjo (head‑to‑head and heuristic evaluations).

4. **Does the belief‑aware architecture generalize to other imperfect‑information games, such as Hanabi (cooperative) or Hearts (trick‑taking)?**  
   - **Basis:** Future Work includes “applying the belief‑aware framework to other imperfect‑information games (e.g., Hanabi, Hearts) to assess generality.”  
   - **Why unresolved:** Skyjo represents only one type of POMDP; testing on diverse games with different information structures and reward schemes is essential to validate broader applicability.  
   - **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement SkyNet for Hanabi and Hearts, with suitable observation encodings and action decompositions. Train from scratch using self‑play and a curriculum of heuristics. Benchmark against strong existing agents (e.g., standard MuZero where feasible, or known heuristics) and report win rates/scores; compare to human‑level baselines if available.

## Limitations
- Single-domain evaluation (Skyjo only); generalizability to other POMDPs unknown.
- Incomplete specification of heuristic bots and tokenization logic hinders faithful replication.
- Evidence for belief modeling relies on indirect linear probes; explicit reconstruction of hidden information not demonstrated.
