# Quotient Geometry and Persistence-Stable Metrics for Swarm Configurations

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.18041
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18041
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Introduces a quotient formation space \(\mathcal{S}_n(M,G)\) with a formation matching metric \(d_{M,G}\) that relaxation of Gromov–Hausdorff distance and proves Vietoris–Rips persistence diagrams are 1‑Lipschitz with respect to it.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the fundamental problem of comparing unordered swarm configurations in the presence of ambient symmetries (e.g., rotations, translations) and agent relabeling. It constructs a quotient space \(\mathcal{S}_n(M,G) = M^n/(G\times S_n)\) equipped with a metric \(d_{M,G}\) that optimizes over group actions and permutations, providing a physically interpretable upper bound on Gromov–Hausdorff distance. The main theoretical result shows that Vietoris–Rips persistence diagrams are stable under this metric, enabling robust topological comparisons. The geometry of the quotient is analyzed, revealing compactness, completeness, and geodesicity under natural assumptions, while also identifying inherent non-injectivity of persistence signatures due to symmetry mismatch and compression. A conditional inverse theorem for a restricted phase‑circle model demonstrates local bi‑Lipschitz correspondence between the 0‑dimensional diagram and the formation metric.

## Method Summary
The method represents each swarm configuration as an equivalence class in the quotient space \(\mathcal{S}_n(M,G)\). To compare two formations, one computes the formation matching distance \(d_{M,G}([x],[y])\) by optimizing over ambient symmetries \(g\in G\) and relabelings \(\sigma\in S_n\) to minimize the worst‑case pointwise error. Vietoris–Rips persistence diagrams are then derived from the inter‑agent distance matrices. Stability is achieved by proving that \(d_{M,G}\) upper‑bounds the Gromov–Hausdorff distance between the induced metric spaces and composing this with the classical stability theorem for persistence. Implementation requires solving a non‑convex optimization over \(G\) and \(S_n\) (e.g., via sampling and the Hungarian algorithm) and computing persistence with standard TDA libraries. The framework supports approximate computation of \(d_{M,G}\) as long as it remains an upper bound, preserving the stability guarantee.

## Key Results
- The formation matching metric satisfies \(d_{\mathrm{GH}}(X_x,X_y) \le d_{M,G}([x],[y])\), serving as a structured relaxation of Gromov–Hausdorff distance.
- Vietoris–Rips persistence diagrams are 1‑Lipschitz: \(d_B(\Phi_k([x]),\Phi_k([y])) \le d_{M,G}([x],[y])\).
- Under compactness of \(G\) and geodesicity of \(M\), the quotient space \((\mathcal{S}_n(M,G), d_{M,G})\) is compact, complete, and geodesic, with stratified singularities from collisions and symmetries.
- Persistence signatures are not injective; non‑injectivity arises from symmetry mismatch (invariance under larger isometry groups) and persistence compression. However, for the phase‑circle model with a gap‑labeling margin, \(\Phi_0\) is locally bi‑Lipschitz to \(d_{M,G}\) up to factor \(2(n-1)\).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Gromov–Hausdorff Relaxation
- **Claim:** \(d_{M,G}\) upper‑bounds the Gromov–Hausdorff distance between the induced inter‑agent metric spaces.
- **Mechanism:** For formations \([x],[y]\), pick \((g,\sigma)\) achieving near‑optimal \(d_{M,G}\). The correspondence \(R = \{(i,\sigma(i))\}\) after applying \(g\) has distortion at most \(2\max_i d_M(gx_i, y_{\sigma(i)})\) because \(g\) is an isometry and by triangle inequality. Hence \(d_{GH}(X_x,X_y)\le d_{M,G}([x],[y])\).
- **Core assumption:** \(G\) acts by isometries on \((M,d_M)\).
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states the bound; Section 2.3 Lemma 1 gives full proof; neighboring corpus focuses on formation control and persistent homology but not this GH‑relaxation.
- **Break condition:** If \(G\) does not consist of isometries, the identity \(d_M(gx_i,gx_j)=d_M(x_i,x_j)\) fails and the bound may not hold.

### Mechanism 2: Persistence Stability
- **Claim:** Vietoris–Rips persistence diagrams \(\Phi_k\) are 1‑Lipschitz with respect to \(d_{M,G}\).
- **Mechanism:** Combine \(d_{GH}(X_x,X_y)\le d_{M,G}([x],[y])\) (Mechanism 1) with the standard stability \(d_B(\Phi_k(X_x),\Phi_k(X_y))\le d_{GH}(X_x,X_y)\). Composition yields \(d_B(\Phi_k([x]),\Phi_k([y]))\le d_{M,G}([x],[y])\).
- **Core assumption:** Classical stability theorem for Vietoris–Rips persistence under Gromov–Hausdorff perturbations holds.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states the inequality; Section 2.4 Theorem 1 proves it; stability of persistence is well‑established in TDA literature.
- **Break condition:** Failure of either building block (e.g., \(G\) not isometric or persistence stability theorem not applicable) breaks the bound.

### Mechanism 3: Geodesicity of the Quotient
- **Claim:** Under compactness and geodesicity assumptions, \((\mathcal{S}_n(M,G),d_{M,G})\) is geodesic.
- **Mechanism:** The \(\ell^\infty\) product metric on \(M^n\) is geodesic when \(M\) is geodesic (Lemma 4). Compactness of \(G\) ensures the infimum in \(d_{M,G}\) is attained by some \((g^*,\sigma^*)\). The straight‑line geodesic in \(M^n\) from \(x\) to \(g^*\cdot y\) (after permutation) projects via the 1‑Lipschitz quotient map to a path in the quotient. The triangle inequality shows this projected path is length‑minimizing, i.e., a geodesic.
- **Core assumption:** \(M\) geodesic; \(G\) compact; action continuous and isometric.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions geodesicity; Section 3.5 Theorem 4 provides proof; application of \(\ell^\infty\) metric to orbit spaces not present in neighbors.
- **Break condition:** Non‑compact \(G\) may prevent attainment; non‑geodesic \(M\) can cause \(M^n\) with \(\ell^\infty\) to lack geodesics.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Formation encoder → Distance engine → Persistence module → Comparison module → Stability validator

**Critical path:**  
To compare two formations: (i) compute/estimate \(d_{M,G}\); (ii) compute \(\Phi_k\) for each; (iii) evaluate bottleneck distance \(d_B\). Stability guarantees \(d_B \le d_{M,G}\), so step (ii) can be omitted if only a bound is needed. For inverse estimation, Theorem 6 relates \(d_{M,G}\) to \(d_B(\Phi_0)\) under margin assumptions.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Precision vs cost:* Exact \(d_{M,G}\) requires global optimization over \(G\); discretization speeds up but may miss optimum. The bound \(d_B\le d_{M,G}\) remains valid for any feasible upper bound.
- *Invariance vs expressivity:* Enlarging \(G\) increases physical invariance but can cause more collisions in persistence (symmetry mismatch), reducing discriminative power. Tailor \(G\) to true symmetries.
- *Persistence dimension:* \(\Phi_0\) is cheapest and suffices for the inverse theorem on the phase‑circle; higher homology separates more shapes but costs more and may compress worse.
- *Representative selection:* Accelerate pairwise \(d_{M,G}\) by fixing canonical representatives (e.g., minimizing scatter); must be consistent across formations.

**Failure signatures:**  
- *Symmetry mismatch:* Two formations related by an isometry outside \(G\) yield \(d_{M,G}>0\) but identical \(\Phi_k\). Detect via point‑set congruence checks.
- *Persistence compression:* Distinct shapes share \(\Phi_0\) (e.g., collinear vs square on \(\mathbb{T}^2\) for \(n=4\)), giving \(d_B=0\) while \(d_{M,G}>0\).
- *Non‑attainment:* Non‑compact \(G\) (e.g., full Euclidean group) may cause infimum not attained, leading to numerical instability and breaking geodesic construction.
- *Margin violation:* In phase‑circle inverse setting, overlapping gap intervals or configurations leaving the semicircle cause the reconstruction factor \(2(n-1)\) to be meaningless.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Stability verification:** Randomly generate \(x,y\) on \(S^2\) with \(n=5\), apply small random rotations and noise. Compute \(d_{S^2,SO(3)}\) via SO(3) sampling + Hungarian, and \(\Phi_0\) via Ripser. Plot \(d_B\) vs \(d_{M,G}\); verify \(d_B\le d_{M,G}\) and observe cases with strict inequality.
2. **Symmetry mismatch demonstration:** Take a generic configuration on \(S^2\), reflect it (isometry in \(O(3)\setminus SO(3)\)). Compute \(d_{S^2,SO(3)}>0\) and \(\Phi_0,\Phi_1\); confirm diagrams match for both formations, illustrating Proposition 5. Switching to \(G=O(3)\) should yield \(d_{M,G}=0\).
3. **Conditional inverse theorem (phase‑circle):** For \(n=5\) on \(\mathbb{T}^1\) with points confined to an arc \(<\pi\), assign non‑overlapping gaps \(g_i\in I_i\) (e.g., intervals \([0.5,0.6]\), \([0.7,0.8]\), …). Perturb each gap by \(\pm0.02\) inside its interval. Compute \(d_{T,T}\) (by aligning sorted phases) and \(d_B(\Phi_0)\). Verify \(d_{T,T}\le 2(n-1)d_B\); break the margin by allowing gaps from the same interval and show the bound fails.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Generalization to varying agent numbers:** How can \(d_{M,G}\) be extended to configurations with births/deaths (partial matchings or measure‑based variants) while maintaining GH‑type relaxation and persistence stability?
   - *Basis:* Explicitly mentioned in Section 6.
   - *Why unresolved:* Fixed‑size bijection constraint breaks; new distance needed with proven GH bound and persistence stability under it.
2. **Injectivity via enriched signatures:** Can signatures like the persistent homology transform provide injective or locally invertible representations for broader ambient spaces beyond phase‑circle?
   - *Basis:* Mentioned in Section 6; references to transform and distributed constructions.
   - *Why unresolved:* Standard diagrams are non‑injective; conditional inverse only for restricted model; unknown if richer signatures yield two‑sided control generally.
3. **Efficient computation of \(d_{M,G}\):** What are the complexity and approximation algorithms with guaranteed error bounds that preserve persistence stability?
   - *Basis:* Section 6 and "Algorithmic considerations".
   - *Why unresolved:* Exact computation involves non‑convex optimization over \(G\times S_n\); likely intractable; no known polynomial‑time approximations with stability guarantees.
4. **Stratified geometry for reconfiguration planning:** How do singularities from collisions and symmetries affect planning complexity, and can we derive variational formulations for optimal formation transitions?
   - *Basis:* Section 6 suggests studying path geometry on stratified space.
   - *Why unresolved:* Paper only establishes metric properties, not planning; stratified nonsmoothness poses challenges for classical methods.

## Limitations
- Exact computation of \(d_{M,G}\) requires global optimization over \(G\times S_n\); practical implementations may only obtain approximations, risking violation of the stability bound.
- Collision singularities (coincident points) lead to a stratified space that is numerically unstable near diagonals.
- The conditional inverse theorem is restricted to the phase‑circle model with strong gap‑margin assumptions; its robustness beyond this setting is unclear.
- Persistence compression (distinct shapes sharing diagrams) limits discriminative power; prevalence for typical \(n\) and \(M\) is unknown.
- Approximating \(d_{M,G}\) (e.g., discrete sampling of \(G\)) may break the theoretical \(d_B \le d_{M,G}\) guarantee if the approximation exceeds the true metric.

## Confidence
- GH relaxation (\(d_{GH} \le d_{M,G}\)): **High** – direct correspondence proof, isometry assumption clear.
- Persistence stability (\(d_B(\Phi_k) \le d_{M,G}\)): **High** – composition of established results.
- Quotient geodesicity (under compact \(G\), geodesic \(M\)): **Medium** – theoretical existence of minimizers; numerical attainment may be fragile.
- Conditional inverse bi‑Lipschitz (phase‑circle): **Medium** – proof correct but assumptions are restrictive and sensitive.
