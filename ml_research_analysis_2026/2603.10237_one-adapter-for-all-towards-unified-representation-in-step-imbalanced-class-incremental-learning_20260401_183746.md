# One Adapter for All: Towards Unified Representation in Step-Imbalanced Class-Incremental Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.10237  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10237  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** One‑A achieves state‑of‑the‑art last‑step accuracy and average accuracy across four benchmarks under multiple step‑imbalance settings (e.g., 69.94% AT on ImageNet‑R with T=40, γ=0.01).

## Executive Summary
Step‑imbalanced class‑incremental learning (SI‑CIL) addresses realistic scenarios where incoming tasks contain vastly different numbers of classes. This imbalance causes large tasks to dominate gradient updates, while small tasks inject noise and destabilize previously learned knowledge. Existing CIL methods treat all tasks equally, leading to imbalanced updates and performance degradation. One‑A introduces a unified framework that incrementally merges task‑specific adapters into a single adapter with constant inference cost. It employs three key mechanisms: asymmetric subspace alignment via SVD to preserve dominant directions from larger tasks, information‑adaptive weighting based on class counts to balance contributions, and directional gating to stabilize high‑energy directions while allowing plasticity in low‑energy ones. Across four benchmarks (CIFAR100, CUB200, ImageNet‑A, ImageNet‑R) and multiple step‑imbalance settings (γ ∈ {0.001, 0.01, 0.05}, T=10–40), One‑A achieves state‑of‑the‑art last‑step and average accuracy, e.g., 69.94% AT on ImageNet‑R (T=40, γ=0.01), while using far fewer FLOPs than multi‑adapter approaches.

## Method Summary
One‑A adopts a rehearsal‑free, adapter‑based continual learning paradigm on a frozen pre‑trained Vision Transformer backbone. For each task *t*, a new lightweight MLP adapter is inserted into the backbone and trained on the task’s data using a composite loss combining cross‑entropy with a size‑adjusted contrastive objective (λ⁽ᵗ⁾ larger for smaller tasks to boost discriminative power). After training, the task‑specific adapter is merged into the accumulated adapter via the One‑A merge procedure: (1) asymmetric SVD alignment projects the smaller adapter’s update onto the dominant right‑singular subspace of the larger (base) adapter; (2) a global weighting based on class counts balances their contributions; (3) directional gating modulates each singular direction’s update strength, preserving high‑energy directions (old knowledge) while allowing flexibility in low‑energy ones. The merged adapter replaces all previous adapters, and class prototypes for the new classes are computed from features extracted by the merged model. At inference, a single merged adapter is used together with a prototype‑based classifier (cosine similarity), achieving task‑agnostic prediction with constant computational overhead.

## Key Results
- On ImageNet‑R (T=40, γ=0.01) One‑A reaches 69.94% average accuracy (AT), surpassing the best multi‑adapter method (69.32%) and a merging baseline (60.54%) while incurring far lower FLOPs.  
- Across CIFAR100, CUB200, ImageNet‑A, and ImageNet‑R, under γ ∈ {0.001, 0.01, 0.05} and T = 10–40, One‑A consistently achieves the highest last‑step accuracy and average accuracy compared to prior CIL methods and merging baselines.  
- Ablations confirm the benefit of each One‑A component (asymmetric alignment, global weighting, directional gating), and sensitivity analysis shows robustness to gating hyper‑parameters within a reasonable range (τ_q, κ).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Asymmetric Subspace Alignment
**Claim:** Asymmetric SVD alignment preserves the dominant subspace from the large task and restricts the small task’s updates to that subspace, preventing subspace drift under step‑imbalance.  
**Mechanism:** The adapter from the larger task (base) is decomposed via SVD: ΔW_b = U_b Σ_b V_b^⊤. The adapter from the smaller task (align) is projected into the base’s right singular space: V_{a→b} = ΔW_a^⊤ U_b Σ_b^{-1}. The fused representation then uses V_b as the fixed basis and combines the aligned component with adaptive weighting. This forbids the align adapter from rotating the principal subspace.  
**Core assumption:** The dominant subspace of the larger task contains the most essential features; smaller tasks can be integrated within this basis without needing to expand it.  
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] (asymmetric subspace alignment description); [section 4.1] (Eqs. 5–6 formulation); [corpus] comparison to MOS [44] and ACMap [12] (symmetric merging) which underperform in step‑imbalance (Table 4).

### Mechanism 2: Information‑Adaptive Weighting
**Claim:** Information‑adaptive weighting based on class counts balances the contributions of base and align adapters during fusion, counteracting the dominance of large tasks.  
**Mechanism:** Global fusion weights are computed as w_a = ϕ(Info_a)/(ϕ(Info_a)+ϕ(Info_b)) and w_b = 1‑w_a, where ϕ(Info_t) = number of classes in task t. The fused singular vectors become V_fused = w_b V_b + w_a V_{a→b}.  
**Core assumption:** The number of classes in a task directly reflects its information content; more classes imply richer supervision and more reliable gradients.  
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] (information‑adaptive weighting); [section 4.2] (ϕ(Infot) = #classes(t) and formula Eq. 7); [corpus] CL‑LoRA [16] and EASE [58] treat tasks uniformly, leading to biased representations under step‑imbalance (Figure 2, Table 1).

### Mechanism 3: Directional Gating
**Claim:** Directional gating modulates each singular direction’s update strength, stabilizing high‑energy directions (preserving old knowledge) while allowing plasticity in low‑energy ones (injecting new knowledge).  
**Mechanism:** For the base adapter’s singular values Σ_b = diag(s₁,…,sᵣ), compute normalized s̃ᵢ = sᵢ/(s₁+δ). Let θ be the q‑quantile of {s̃ᵢ}. The gate is gᵢ = σ(κ(θ‑s̃ᵢ)) where σ is sigmoid. The final fused basis is V_final = V_b + g ⊙ (V_fused − V_b), where gⁱ≈0 for high‑energy directions and gⁱ≈1 for low‑energy ones.  
**Core assumption:** Principal (high‑energy) directions encode fundamental features critical for previously learned tasks and should be preserved, while minor directions can flexibly adapt to new information.  
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] (directional gating description); [section 4.3] (gating computation Eq. 12 and reconstruction Eq. 13); [corpus] ACMap [12] performs uniform averaging, which cannot preserve important directions selectively; One‑A’s gating improves last‑step accuracy and reduces forgetting (Table 3, Figure 5).

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:** Backbone → Task‑specific MLP adapters → One‑A merge module → Single merged adapter → Prototype‑based classifier.

**Critical path:**
1. For a new task *t*, initialize a fresh adapter ΔW_new_t and train it on D_t using the composite loss (cross‑entropy + contrastive) with task‑size‑adjusted λ⁽ᵗ⁾.
2. After training, merge ΔW_new_t with the current merged adapter ΔW_{t‑1} using the One‑A procedure (asymmetric SVD alignment, information‑adaptive weighting, directional gating).
3. Compute class prototypes for all classes in D_t from features extracted with the merged adapter.
4. Store the prototypes and discard the local classifier; the inference model uses the frozen backbone, the merged adapter, and the prototype bank.
5. Proceed to the next task.
6. At test time, forward pass with the merged adapter and classify by cosine similarity to the appropriate prototypes in a task‑agnostic manner.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Asymmetric alignment protects the large‑task subspace but may discard informative directions from small tasks that are orthogonal to that subspace.
- Class‑count weighting is simple and stable but may misestimate information if within‑task diversity is uneven or class distribution is long‑tailed.
- Directional gating introduces hyperparameters (q, κ) that require tuning; the smooth sigmoid balances stability‑plasticity but may be suboptimal if the singular spectrum is ambiguous.
- Single‑adapter inference reduces memory and FLOPs but merges all knowledge into one module, potentially limiting capacity compared to multi‑adapter routers.

**Failure signatures:**
- Severe forgetting on tail tasks when gating threshold q is too high or asymmetric alignment uses a poorly trained large‑task adapter.
- Under‑fitting on new classes if gating is too conservative (κ large) or the small‑task’s discriminative signal resides in high‑energy directions that get suppressed.
- Instability under long‑tailed mixtures because class‑count weighting disregards intra‑class sample imbalance.
- Performance collapse when the assumption that larger tasks are more informative fails (e.g., a small task contains highly diverse, novel categories).

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Benchmark replication: Reproduce Table 1 (γ=0.01, multiple T, four datasets) and confirm One‑A achieves the highest AT and ¯A while using a single adapter.
2. Ablation study: Reproduce Table 3 by removing Asymmetric SVD Alignment (ASA), Global Weighting (GW), and Directional Gating (DG) individually; observe the performance drop each component causes.
3. Sensitivity analysis: Reproduce Figure 7 by varying q and κ on CIFAR100 (γ=0.01, T=10) and ImageNet‑R; identify stable regions and note degradation at extreme values.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How to extend One-A to handle step-imbalance and long-tailed sample distributions together?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Future work mentioned in Section 10.
- **Why unresolved:** Current method assumes balanced samples per class; long-tails may disrupt alignment and weighting.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Build mixed-imbalance datasets; compare One-A against long-tail CIL baselines.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can One-A work without a strong pre-trained backbone, e.g., from random initialization?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Entirely relies on PTM (Sections 3.2, 8.2); no experiments without PTM.
- **Why unresolved:** PTM dependency limits applicability; method’s behavior from scratch unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Train from scratch on CIFAR100/CUB; compare with PTM-based methods.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can the gating thresholds and weighting be learned automatically instead of manual tuning?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Uses fixed class-count proxy and manual q, κ (Sections 4.2, 4.3, 5.4).
- **Why unresolved:** Manual settings may not generalize; an adaptive scheme would improve robustness.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Incorporate a meta-controller to adjust parameters based on validation loss; test across datasets.

## Limitations
- **SVD rank selection:** The method uses “thin SVD” but does not specify whether singular values are truncated; the rank choice can significantly affect subspace alignment and directional gating.
- **Prototype computation timing:** It is ambiguous whether class prototypes are extracted from the merged adapter (post‑merge) or the newly trained adapter (pre‑merge), which could lead to inconsistency during inference.
- **Gating hyperparameter sensitivity:** The quantile q and sharpness κ require careful tuning and the reported defaults may not generalize across all datasets and extreme imbalance settings.

## Next Checks
1. **Subspace preservation test:** After each merge, compute the average cosine similarity between the base adapter’s right singular vectors before and after fusion. Expect high similarity, especially for large tasks, confirming asymmetric alignment’s protective effect.
2. **Prototype source experiment:** For each task, compute prototypes using both ΔW_t (pre‑merge) and ΔW_m (post‑merge). Evaluate both on the final test set; a significant accuracy gap would indicate a protocol inconsistency that must be resolved.
3. **Gating hyperparameter sweep:** Systematically vary q ∈ [0.3, 0.7] and κ ∈ [5, 20] on CIFAR100 (γ=0.01, T=10) and ImageNet‑R. Plot accuracy heatmaps to determine if the default values lie in a flat stable region; if not, the claimed robustness is overestimated.
