# Central-to-Local Adaptive Generative Diffusion Framework for Improving Gene Expression Prediction in Data-Limited Spatial Transcriptomics

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.26827
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26827
*   **Reference Count:** 40
*   **Primary Result:** A two-stage generative framework that uses synthetic histology patches, produced from minimal local spatial transcriptomics data via gene-conditioned modulation of a pretrained diffusion model, to consistently improve downstream gene expression prediction accuracy.

## Executive Summary
Spatial transcriptomics (ST) data is severely limited due to high costs and privacy constraints, hindering robust model development for predicting gene expression from histology. This paper introduces C2L-ST, a central-to-local framework that addresses data scarcity. It first pretrains a global diffusion model on large-scale histopathology data to learn general tissue morphologies. For a new, data-limited ST site, this central model is efficiently adapted using a small fraction of local paired image-gene spots through lightweight gene-conditioned modulation. The resulting local generator produces synthetic histology patches aligned with the site's molecular profiles. Co-training a downstream predictor on this synthetic data combined with the limited real data yields significant and consistent reductions in gene expression prediction error, offering a scalable solution for molecular data augmentation.

## Method Summary
C2L-ST employs a two-stage approach. Stage one involves unconditional pretraining of a DDPM diffusion U-Net on a large, unlabeled histopathology dataset (SPIDER) to establish a robust morphological prior. Stage two adapts this frozen central model to a specific ST institution. A lightweight gene-conditioned modulation module is introduced, which projects the gene expression vector into parameters (γ, β) that modulate normalization layers within selected ResNet blocks of the U-Net. Only this small module is trained on the local ST spots, preserving the central prior while aligning generation to local morphology-gene relationships. The adapted model then generates synthetic histology patches conditioned on gene vectors. Finally, a ResNet-50 predictor for gene expression is trained on a mixture of the original limited real spots and the generated synthetic pairs (typically at a 10:1 ratio), leading to improved performance.

## Key Results
*   Generated synthetic patches exhibit high visual/structural fidelity, with embedding cosine similarity to real data of **0.79–0.89** across different organs and preserved cell-type compositions.
*   Combining synthetic data with limited real spots (10:1 ratio) consistently **reduces mean absolute error (MAE) by 0.047–0.056** in gene expression prediction.
*   The approach **recovers performance** even when adaptation uses only **5% of real spots**, demonstrating extreme data efficiency.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
*   **Claim:** A global diffusion model pretrained on large-scale histopathology data provides a transferable morphological prior that captures diverse tissue structures.
*   **Mechanism:** The central model undergoes unconditional diffusion pretraining (forward noising and reverse denoising) on hundreds of thousands of unlabeled histopathology patches. This learns a data distribution over tissue morphology, encoded in the U-Net's weights, which serves as a robust starting point for generating realistic histology.
*   **Core assumption:** Morphological patterns in histopathology are largely shared across institutions and tissue types, and learning these from abundant public data is sufficiently generalizable.
*   **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "A global diffusion model is first pretrained on large-scale histopathology data to learn morphological priors." [section 2.1] Details the DDPM framework and U-Net architecture for pretraining on the SPIDER dataset.
*   **Break condition:** If the central model's pretraining data lacks diversity relevant to a target ST organ (e.g., only breast tissue used for a liver ST task), the morphological prior may be misaligned, limiting adaptation effectiveness.

### Mechanism 2
*   **Claim:** Lightweight gene-conditioned modulation adapts the global model to institution-specific molecular contexts using minimal local ST data.
*   **Mechanism:** The frozen pretrained U-Net has its normalization layers in selected ResNet blocks modulated by a gene expression vector. The gene vector is projected to produce scaling (γ) and shifting (β) parameters (akin to Adaptive Instance Normalization). Only this small set of modulation parameters and the projection layer are trained during local adaptation.
*   **Core assumption:** The morphological representations learned centrally are linearly separable or modifiable by a low-dimensional gene embedding to align with local morphology-gene relationships. The gene embedding contains sufficient information to guide generation.
*   **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "Institution-specific local models are then adapted via lightweight gene-conditioned modulation using only a small fraction of paired image–gene spots." [section 2.2] Explicitly defines the modulation operation `h' = γ(G)·h + β(G)` and the frozen parameter strategy. [section 3.1 & 3.2] Shows generated patches correspond to gene clusters and have high embedding similarity (0.79–0.89 cosine) to real data, indicating successful alignment.
*   **Break condition:** If the local ST dataset is extremely sparse (<5% spots) or genetically atypical (e.g., rare mutation profile not represented in the gene set), the modulation may fail to capture correct morphology-gene mappings, generating implausible tissues.

### Mechanism 3
*   **Claim:** Co-training a downstream predictor on a mixture of limited real ST spots and synthetically generated image-gene pairs improves gene expression prediction accuracy and spatial coherence.
*   **Mechanism:** Synthetic pairs (X_H, G) from the adapted local model are combined with the small set of real pairs D_real to form D_train. A predictor (ResNet-50) is trained on D_train to regress gene expression from histology. The synthetic data expands the effective training distribution, regularizing the predictor and filling spatial gaps.
*   **Core assumption:** Synthetic samples are distinguishable from real ones by the predictor but provide valid, diverse examples that improve the learned mapping function f_ϕ. The predictor does not overfit to subtle generative artifacts.
*   **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "When combined with limited real data for downstream gene expression prediction, synthetic samples consistently reduce mean absolute error by 0.047–0.056..." [section 2.3] Defines the co-training loss `Lpred(ϕ) = E(X_H,G)∼Dtrain [∥G−f_ϕ(X_H)∥1]`. [section 3.4 & Table 2] Quantifies consistent MAE reduction across all four organs, especially under extreme sparsity (5% real spots).
*   **Break condition:** If the synthetic data distribution drifts significantly from the real ST distribution (e.g., due to adaptation failure), co-training may introduce distributional shift, harming predictor performance. A synthetic-to-real ratio >10× might cause saturation or minor degradation (seen in Table 2 for MEND40/MEND90 at 10×).

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM)**
    *   **Why needed here:** The entire generative core is a diffusion model. Understanding the forward noising process and reverse denoising objective (`Ldiff = ||ε - ε_θ||^2`) is essential to grasp how the central model learns the data distribution.
    *   **Quick check question:** What is the purpose of the noise prediction network ε_θ in the DDPM framework, and how is it used during inference?
*   **Concept: Conditional Modulation (e.g., FiLM/AdaIN)**
    *   **Why needed here:** The local adaptation mechanism uses scaling/shifting parameters derived from a gene embedding to modulate features. This is a form of feature-wise affine transformation, a core technique for injecting conditioning signals into frozen networks.
    *   **Quick check question:** In the equation `h' = γ(G)·h + β(G)`, if γ(G) is consistently near 1 and β(G) near 0 for a given gene input, what does that imply about the adaptation's effect?
*   **Concept: Transfer Learning & Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT)**
    *   **Why needed here:** The framework's efficiency hinges on freezing the massive central U-Net and only training a tiny fraction of parameters (projection layer + modulation heads). This is a classic PEFT strategy.
    *   **Quick check question:** If instead of modulation, all central U-Net parameters were fine-tuned on the small local ST dataset, what is the most likely outcome regarding performance and data efficiency?

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:**
    1.  Central Model (U-Net) -> 2. Local Adaptation Module -> 3. Synthetic Generator -> 4. Downstream Predictor (f_ϕ)
*   **Critical path:** (1) Pretrain Central U-Net → (2) For each institution: Adapt via Modulation Module using local paired spots → (3) Generate D_syn using adapted model → (4) Train f_ϕ on D_real ∪ D_syn → (5) Deploy f_ϕ for inference on unsampled spots.
*   **Design tradeoffs:**
    *   **Frozen vs. Full Fine-tuning:** Freezing ensures morphological prior preservation and prevents catastrophic forgetting with scarce local data, but limits adaptation capacity. Full fine-tuning risks overfitting and destroying the prior.
    *   **Modulation Placement:** Choosing which ResNet blocks to modulate trades expressivity for parameter efficiency. Earlier layers affect low-level texture; later layers affect high-level semantics.
    *   **Synthetic Data Ratio:** Adding synthetic data helps, but too much may dilute real data signals. The 10:1 ratio is reported optimal; higher ratios show diminishing/negative returns.
*   **Failure signatures:**
    1.  **Poor Generative Fidelity:** t-SNE embeddings of synthetic data do not overlap with real data (cosine similarity <<0.79). Cell-type proportions (HoverNet) deviate significantly (>15% absolute difference in dominant cell type).
    2.  **Co-training Degradation:** MAE of predictor trained on (real + synthetic) is *higher* than real-only baseline, especially at low real-data percentages (e.g., 5%). This indicates synthetic data is out-of-distribution or misleading.
    3.  **Adaptation Instability:** During local adaptation, loss L_cond does not decrease, or the model fails to generate distinct images for different gene conditions (clusters appear visually identical).
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1.  **Baseline Generative Quality Check:** After local adaptation (e.g., 25% spots), generate 100 synthetic patches conditioned on randomly sampled real gene vectors. Compute (a) FID against real patches, (b) cosine similarity of Conch embeddings (expect >0.8), (c) HoverNet cell-type distribution KS-test p-values (expect p>0.05).
    2.  **Synthetic Data Utility Screening:** For a single organ (e.g., ZEN48), train predictor on four setups: (a) 5% real only, (b) 5% real + 5x synthetic, (c) 25% real only, (d) 25% real + 10x synthetic. Plot MAE vs. real data %. Key signature: (b) MAE ≈ or < (c) MAE.
    3.  **Modulation Ablation:** Re-run local adaptation with the modulation module disabled (use frozen central model directly for conditional generation). Compare embedding similarity (cosine) and downstream MAE (with 10x synthetic) against the full modulated model.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Question 1:** How can conditioning be extended beyond top-expressed genes to capture subtle molecular patterns?
    *   **Basis:** The authors state that "future refinements could expand the conditioning space to include additional biological signals such as less expressed genes, alternative molecular markers, or complementary spatial modalities."
    *   **Why unresolved:** The current implementation conditions on a compressed embedding derived from the top 250 genes per center, potentially discarding biologically relevant signals from low-abundance transcripts or other molecular layers.
    *   **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematically evaluate synthetic image quality and downstream gene prediction MAE when incorporating broader gene sets, noncoding RNAs, or multimodal inputs like immunohistochemistry stains.
*   **Question 2:** Do explicit cell-level spatial representations improve molecular-morphological alignment in generated patches?
    *   **Basis:** The authors propose "incorporating single-cell references, cell segmentation maps, and graph-based representations of cellular topology" as a future direction.
    *   **Why unresolved:** C2L-ST conditions on aggregated patch-level gene expression without explicit modeling of cellular layout or intercellular relationships.
    *   **What evidence would resolve it:** Integrate cell segmentation masks or spatial graph features into the conditioning pipeline, then assess improvements in cell-type composition fidelity and spatial coherence of predicted gene expression.
*   **Question 3:** Can the central-to-local generative paradigm be successfully transferred to other spatial omics modalities such as spatial proteomics?
    *   **Basis:** The authors note that "the same principle could be extended to other spatial omics modalities, such as spatial proteomics."
    *   **Why unresolved:** C2L-ST is designed for continuous gene expression vectors; other modalities (e.g., protein abundance, metabolites) have different distributions, dynamic ranges, and spatial resolutions.
    *   **What evidence would resolve it:** Adapt the framework to a spatial proteomics dataset, generating synthetic histology conditioned on protein panels, and evaluate alignment through embedding similarity and downstream task performance.
*   **Question 4:** What factors determine the residual performance gap between synthetic-augmented and real-only training, and can synthetic data alone achieve parity?
    *   **Basis:** Results show synthetic-only training yields higher MAE than real-only training, and combined training still does not fully close the gap to a hypothetical all-real-data ceiling.
    *   **Why unresolved:** The paper does not isolate whether this gap stems from distributional mismatch, limited synthetic diversity, or inherent information loss in the generative process.
    *   **What evidence would resolve it:** Perform ablation studies varying synthetic data diversity, analyze feature-space coverage of synthetic vs. real embeddings, and theoretically characterize the distributional divergence.

## Limitations
*   Central model transferability hinges on SPIDER data diversity; performance may degrade for organs/tissues poorly represented in pretraining.
*   Modulation design remains underspecified (e.g., which ResNet blocks, MLP architecture), risking reproducibility and suboptimal adaptation.
*   Synthetic utility is validated up to a 10:1 ratio; higher ratios may introduce distribution shift and harm predictor training.

## Confidence
*   **Mechanism 1 (global prior):** High for pretraining stability, Medium for cross-institution generalization.
*   **Mechanism 2 (modulation):** High for feasibility with modest data, Medium for robustness under extreme sparsity (<5% spots) or atypical gene profiles.
*   **Mechanism 3 (co-training):** High for consistent MAE reduction at 10:1, Medium for scalability beyond tested organs and ratios.

## Next Checks
1.  **Cross-organ transfer test:** Pretrain central model on one SPIDER organ subset (e.g., breast), adapt to ST data from a different organ (e.g., liver), and compare generation fidelity (embedding similarity) and prediction MAE against same-organ pretraining.
2.  **Modulation component ablation:** Disable scaling (γ) or shifting (β) parameters, or vary modulated block depths; measure impact on synthetic-real embedding cosine similarity and downstream MAE to identify critical design choices.
3.  **Synthetic ratio stress test:** Increase synthetic-to-real ratios to 20:1 and 50:1 during predictor training; track MAE changes to determine saturation points and degradation thresholds across all four organs.
