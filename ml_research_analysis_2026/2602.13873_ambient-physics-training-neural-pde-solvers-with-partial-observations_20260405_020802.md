# Ambient Physics: Training Neural PDE Solvers with Partial Observations

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.13873  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13873  
- **Reference count:** 26  
- **Primary result:** Ambient Physics achieves state-of-the-art reconstruction on PDEs from only 3% observations, reducing average relative L2 error by 62.51% compared to prior diffusion-based methods while using 125× fewer function evaluations.

## Executive Summary
Ambient Physics tackles the long-standing challenge of learning PDE solvers from incomplete data. Traditional neural PDE solvers require fully observed coefficient-solution pairs for training, but in practice only partial measurements are available. The proposed method introduces a simple yet powerful training trick: randomly mask a subset of already-observed measurements while still supervising on the full set. This forces the model to treat artificially masked points the same as truly unobserved ones, compelling it to learn the underlying physical joint distribution and produce coherent predictions across the entire domain. The approach is architecture‑agnostic; it can be applied to both diffusion‑based models (Ambient Flow) and classical solvers like FNOs (Ambient FNO).

In extensive experiments on Darcy flow, Helmholtz, Navier‑Stokes, and Poisson equations at 128×128 resolution with only 3% uniformly random observations, Ambient Flow sets new state‑of‑the‑art results. It reduces average relative L2 error by 62.51% compared to prior diffusion methods (e.g., FunDPS) while requiring 125× fewer function evaluations. Moreover, the authors uncover a striking “one‑point transition”: masking even a single observed point during training yields a dramatic 10–100× error reduction, enabling effective learning across diverse architectures and measurement patterns.

## Method Summary
Ambient Physics reformulates the reconstruction problem as learning the joint distribution \(p(a,u)\) of PDE coefficients \(a\) and solutions \(u\) from partial observations. During training, the model receives a set of available measurements \((A_{a_a}, A_{u_u})\) created by applying fixed binary masks \(A_a\) and \(A_u\) to the full fields. Additionally, random masking operators \(B_a\) and \(B_u\) are applied to these already‑observed points, effectively discarding a fraction (e.g., one‑third) of them. The model is conditioned on the sparser masked observations \((\tilde{B}_{a_a} = B_a A_{a_a}, \tilde{B}_{u_u} = B_u A_{u_u})\) and must predict the full fields \((\hat{a},\hat{u})\). Crucially, the loss is computed on all originally available measurements \((A_{a_a}, A_{u_u})\), not just the ones retained in the conditioning. This mismatch prevents the model from distinguishing truly unobserved points from artificially masked ones, forcing it to produce physically plausible values everywhere. Any neural network architecture can be used; the paper demonstrates both a UNet‑based conditional rectified flow (Ambient Flow) and an adapted FNO (Ambient FNO).

At inference time, the model generates samples by integrating a learned probability flow ODE. Starting from Gaussian noise \((a_0,u_0)\), at each step a fresh random pair \((B_a,B_u)\) is drawn, the current noisy state is masked accordingly, and the network predicts the clean fields. The predicted velocity \((\hat{a}-a)/(1-t)\) is used in an Euler step to advance the state. Repeating for \(K\) steps yields the final reconstructed \((a_K,u_K)\). The same random masking strategy is used during both training and inference, ensuring consistency.

## Key Results
- Ambient Flow reduces average relative L2 error by **62.51%** compared to diffusion‑based baselines (e.g., FunDPS at 500 NFE) while using **125× fewer** function evaluations.
- The method achieves state‑of‑the‑art reconstruction across **four PDE benchmarks** (Darcy flow, Helmholtz, Navier‑Stokes, Poisson) using only **3%** partial observations.
- A **single‑point masking** (i.e., withholding one already‑observed point) triggers a sharp **10–100×** error reduction, demonstrating the “one‑point transition” phenomenon.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Random Masking During Training Forces Joint Distribution Learning
- **Claim:** Randomly masking a subset of already‑observed measurements during training, while still supervising on the full set, forces the model to learn the joint distribution of coefficient‑solution pairs across the entire domain.
- **Mechanism:** The model receives conditioning on a sparser set of observations (via additional masks \(B_a, B_u\)) than the full available set \((A_{a_a}, A_{u_u})\). The loss supervises on all available measurements. The model cannot distinguish between “truly unobserved” points (from the data generation process) and “artificially unobserved” points (from the extra masks). To minimize loss, it must predict coherent values at *all* locations, including the artificially masked ones, implying it has learned the underlying physical joint distribution.
- **Core assumption:** The physical system is governed by a well‑defined joint distribution \(p(a,u)\) that is learnable, and that learning to impute artificially masked points is equivalent to learning to sample from the posterior over unobserved points given the observed ones.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  – Abstract: “The key idea is to randomly mask a subset of already‑observed measurements and supervise on them, so the model cannot distinguish ‘truly unobserved’ from ‘artificially unobserved’, and must produce plausible predictions everywhere.”  
  – Section 3 formalizes \(\tilde{B}x := B x A\) and \(L_{\text{meas}} = \|A_{a_a}-A\hat{a}_a\|^2 + \|A_{u_u}-A\hat{u}_u\|^2\), showing supervision on full observations but conditioning on masked versions.
- **Break condition:** If the additional masks \((B_a,B_u)\) are not sampled independently of the observed set or if the supervision \(L_{\text{meas}}\) does not cover the artificially masked points, the model can still learn to copy observed values and fail to generalize.

### Mechanism 2: One‑Point Transition
- **Claim:** Masking even a single already‑observed point is sufficient to move from catastrophic failure (naive training) to effective learning from partial observations.
- **Mechanism:** In naive training (\(B = I\)), the model minimizes loss by exactly replicating observed values and predicting arbitrarily elsewhere, receiving no signal for unobserved locations. Introducing any non‑zero artificial mask (\(B \neq I\), even masking one point) withholds at least one observed value from the conditioning. The model must now predict that withheld value from the remaining context, introducing a minimal training signal for imputation. This small signal is enough to bootstrap learning of the joint distribution, as the model can no longer trivially satisfy the loss by copying inputs.
- **Core assumption:** The initial failure mode of naive training is a degenerate solution where the model learns an identity‑like mapping at observed points. A single withheld point breaks this degenerate solution space and requires the model to engage its representational capacity to infer missing data.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  – Section 4: “Figure 4 shows a sharp threshold: masking a single already‑observed point leads to an ∼10–100× reduction in error.”  
  – Same section repeats the threshold effect.
- **Break condition:** If the additional mask is too small (zero points) or the model’s architecture/power is insufficient to learn even a single‑point imputation, the transition may not occur. Also, highly structured observation patterns might not provide enough contextual diversity with a single masked point.

### Mechanism 3: Architecture‑Agnostic Adaptation
- **Claim:** The Ambient Physics principle (training with masked conditioning and full supervision) is architecture‑agnostic and can adapt traditional neural PDE solvers (e.g., FNOs) to learn directly from partial observations, not just diffusion/flow‑matching models.
- **Mechanism:** The core training loop (masked conditioning + full supervision) defines a learning objective independent of the parameterization. For a directional mapping architecture (e.g., \(A a \rightarrow u\)), one sets \(B_u = 0\) (no masking on the solution branch) and applies \(B_a\) to the coefficient input. The model is then trained to predict the full solution \(u\) given the masked coefficient observations \(B_a A_{a_a}\), supervised on all available solution points \(A_{u_u}\). This forces the model to learn a mapping that produces physically plausible outputs everywhere, not just at the masked locations.
- **Core assumption:** Traditional neural PDE solvers, when trained with this objective, will shift from learning a deterministic input‑output map for fully observed inputs to learning a conditional generative process that can handle missing inputs, provided they have sufficient capacity.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  – Section 4: “We consider the same experimental setup… start from an FNO backbone… we effectively set \(B_a=I\) and \(B_u=0\)… We refer to this model as Ambient FNO.”  
  – Ambient FNO reduces error by 74.27% compared to vanilla FNO.  
  – Results show that Ambient Physics can adapt traditional solvers, not only diffusion/flow matching.
- **Break condition:** If the architecture is fundamentally incapable of representing a diverse set of plausible completions (e.g., deterministic and under‑parameterized), or if the masking regime is incompatible with the architecture’s input handling (e.g., the model expects fully dense inputs and cannot handle variable masks), adaptation may fail.

## Foundational Learning

### Reconstruction Problem Generalization
**Why needed:** The paper unifies various reconstruction tasks (forward, inverse, coefficient inpainting) into a single joint distribution learning framework using measurement operators \(A_a, A_u\). This abstraction clarifies that Ambient Physics solves a general problem: predicting \((a,u)\) given \((A_{a_a}, A_{u_u})\), regardless of which fields are observed.  
**Quick check:** Given \(A_a = 0, A_u = I\), what classical PDE problem does this represent? (Answer: Inverse problem: \(u \rightarrow a\).)

### Flow Matching / Rectified Flow
**Why needed:** This is the generative modeling backbone for Ambient Flow, defining how a model is trained to transport noise to data via a learned ODE and how conditional sampling is performed.  
**Quick check:** In rectified flow, what does the network \(M_\theta\) typically predict: the instantaneous velocity or the final data point? (Answer: The final state \(x_1\); the paper parameterizes to predict \(x_1\).)

### Training vs. Inference Masking Consistency
**Why needed:** Ambient Physics relies on using the same random masking strategy during training and inference. Understanding this stochastic conditioning is critical for correct implementation.  
**Quick check:** At inference, what inputs are provided to \(M_\theta\) at step \(k\)? (Answer: \((\tilde{B}_{a_a}^k, \tilde{B}_{u_u}^k, \tilde{B}_{a_a}, \tilde{B}_{u_u}, B_a, B_u, t_k)\) where \(\tilde{B}_{a_a} = B_a A_{a_a}\), etc.)

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
1. **Data Loader:** Generates partial observations \((A_{a_a}, A_{u_u})\) via fixed binary masks (e.g., 3% retention) and samples additional random masks \((B_a, B_u)\) that zero‑out a subset of already‑observed points.  
2. **Masking Module:** Applies \(B_a\) and \(B_u\) to produce \(\tilde{B}_{a_a} = B_a A_{a_a}\) and \(\tilde{B}_{u_u} = B_u A_{u_u}\).  
3. **Model Backbone (e.g., UNet, FNO):** Takes as input mid‑flow states \((a_t, u_t)\) constructed from \(\tilde{B}\) and Gaussian noise, plus conditioning channels for masks \((\tilde{B}_a, \tilde{B}_u)\) and original partial observations \((A_{a_a}, A_{u_u})\). Outputs predicted clean fields \((\hat{a}, \hat{u})\).  
4. **Loss Function:** Computes \(L_{\text{meas}} = \|A_{a_a}-A_a\hat{a}\|^2 + \|A_{u_u}-A_u\hat{u}\|^2\) only on the locations specified by the *original* measurement operators \(A_a, A_u\). No PDE residual loss is used.  
5. **Sampling Loop:** At inference, draws Gaussian noise \((a_0,u_0)\), samples new \((B_a,B_u)\) per step, constructs \(\tilde{B}\), integrates the ODE using Euler steps with velocity \((\hat{a}-a)/(1-t)\), and returns \((a_K,u_K)\).

### Critical Path
Data → apply \(B_a,B_u\) to create \(\tilde{B}_{a_a},\tilde{B}_{u_u}\) → build mid‑flow states \((a_t,u_t)\) → Model predicts \((\hat{a},\hat{u})\) → Loss computed on full \((A_{a_a},A_{u_u})\) → Backpropagation.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Masking ratio:** More aggressive masking (smaller fraction of observed points retained in \(\tilde{B}\)) provides stronger training signal for imputation but reduces effective input information. The paper uses withholding ~1/3 of observed points.
- **Backbone choice:** Diffusion/flow‑matching (UNet) gives strong performance but many function evaluations; adapted FNOs are more efficient but may require the “+Joint Dist. Modeling” tweak (i.e., using non‑zero \(B_u\) as well as \(B_a\)) to avoid artifacts.
- **Conditioning channel design:** Simple concatenation of masks and partial observations works but might not be optimal for all architectures.

### Failure Signatures
- **High error at unobserved locations** while observed points are accurate: indicates the model has not generalized beyond copying (naive training failure).  
- **Spatial artifacts** (e.g., shifts, blurring) in reconstructions: seen in Ambient FNO without joint distribution modeling; suggests the model isn’t learning a coherent joint distribution.  
- **Failure of one‑point transition:** if masking a single point does not improve performance, check that masks are truly random subsets of observed points and that the loss is computed on the *full* observation set.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Naive vs. Ambient Baseline:** Train two models on the same partial‑observation dataset. Naive: \(B=I\) (no extra masking). Ambient: sample \(B_a,B_u\) to mask 1/3 of observed points. Compare reconstruction L2 error over the full domain. Expect Ambient to drastically outperform, especially at unobserved points.  
2. **One‑Point Transition:** For a fixed PDE (e.g., Darcy flow), vary the number of additionally masked points \(\{0,1,2,4,…,256\}\). Plot relative L2 error vs. number of masked points. Expect a sharp drop at 1 masked point and improving performance up to a point.  
3. **Architecture Adaptation:** Implement Ambient FNO: set \(B_u=0\), apply \(B_a\) to coefficient input \(A_{a_a}\). Train on partial observations. Compare against vanilla FNO and DiffusionPDE/FunDPS. Check for artifacts and whether “+Joint Dist. Modeling” (i.e., using non‑zero \(B_u\) as well) improves results.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Real‑world deployment** – Can Ambient Physics be effectively deployed in real‑world scientific and engineering scenarios where complete observations are never available, considering practical challenges such as measurement noise, irregular geometries, and highly limited data?
2. **Single‑model generalization** – Is it possible to train a single Ambient Physics model that generalizes across a wide variety of measurement patterns (e.g., random, patch, column, window) and resolutions (e.g., 32×32 to 128×128) without retraining or task‑specific tuning?
3. **Mechanism of one‑point transition** – What is the underlying mechanism that makes masking even a single observed point sufficient to transition from catastrophic failure to effective learning? The paper identifies this empirically but provides no theoretical explanation.
4. **Architectural sensitivity** – Why do certain neural architectures (e.g., FNO) require joint distribution modeling with non‑trivial additional masking to avoid artifacts, while others (e.g., UNet) do not? What architectural properties determine this sensitivity?
5. **Extension to other generative models** – Can Ambient Physics principles be extended beyond flow matching/diffusion to other generative model classes (e.g., GANs, VAEs, autoregressive models) while maintaining reconstruction quality and efficiency?

## Limitations
- **Implementation details:** Exact generation of measurement masks \((A_a,A_u)\) and additional masking operators \((B_a,B_u)\) is not fully specified; reproduction relies on assumptions (e.g., independent per‑point random masking).
- **Generality:** The architecture‑agnostic claim is only validated on FNOs; applicability to other popular neural PDE solvers (e.g., DeepONet, PINNs) remains uncertain.
- **Real‑world robustness:** Experiments use synthetic PDE data with uniform random masks; performance under structured missingness, sensor noise, or non‑uniform measurement patterns is unknown.

## Confidence
- **Core mechanism** (random masking forces joint distribution learning): *Medium* – logically sound and empirically supported by ablations, but relies on ideal conditions (well‑defined joint distribution, sufficient model capacity).
- **One‑point transition** (masking a single already‑observed point yields drastic improvement): *High* – directly observed in experiments across multiple PDEs; threshold effect is robust.
- **Architecture‑agnostic adaptation** (traditional solvers can be retrained to handle partial observations): *Medium* – demonstrated for FNOs, but lacking evidence for a broader range of architectures.

## Next Checks
1. **Ablation over additional masking fraction:** Systematically vary the fraction of additionally masked points (0%, 10%, 33%, 50%, 90%) for each PDE and observation pattern, measuring reconstruction error to confirm optimal ratio and the sharpness of the one‑point transition.
2. **Cross‑architecture validation:** Implement the Ambient Physics training protocol on at least two other neural operator architectures (e.g., DeepONet, Galerkin Transformer) and evaluate on partial‑observation tasks; compare performance against naive training without extra masking.
3. **Real‑world data evaluation:** Test Ambient Flow on a real‑world physical dataset with partial measurements (e.g., fluid velocity fields from PIV or subsurface flow from sparse wells), where ground truth is available, to assess robustness to non‑uniform masks, sensor noise, and distribution shifts.
