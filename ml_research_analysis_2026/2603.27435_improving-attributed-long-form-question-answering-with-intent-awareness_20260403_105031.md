# Improving Attributed Long-form Question Answering with Intent Awareness

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.27435
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27435
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Intent-aware inference yields average gains of +2.9 points for large models and up to +12.3 for small models, with citation metrics improving by as much as +18.7.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses a key limitation in attributed long-form question answering: current LLMs do not explicitly model human writing intents that guide document structure and citation use. The authors introduce an intent-aware writing framework that annotates each paragraph and citation with structured tags representing rhetorical purpose (e.g., [PIT-Cause-Effect], [CIT-Motivation]). For large models, intents are elicited at test time via prompting; for small models, they enable the creation of high-quality synthetic data for supervised fine-tuning. Evaluations on three scientific report generation benchmarks show consistent improvements in both report quality and citation accuracy. A user study further confirms that intent-aware reports are perceived as more readable and transparent.

## Method Summary
The core method defines a schema of intent tags for paragraphs and citations. At inference, large models are prompted to first generate an intent for each paragraph and then produce the paragraph conditioned on that intent. For smaller models, the framework generates synthetic training data by using a large model to produce intent-annotated reports; these are then used for supervised fine-tuning. Three SFT variants are explored: (1) **intent-explicit**, where the model generates the intent tag alongside the paragraph; (2) **implicit**, where intents are used to curate training data but not explicitly generated; and (3) **multiview**, which decomposes intents into separate training pathways.

## Key Results
- Across SQA-CS-V2, DeepScholar Bench, and ResearchQA, intent-aware inference improves large model performance by an average of +2.9 points overall and +3.7 on citation metrics.
- For small models (Qwen3-8B, Llama3.1-8B), intent-aware SFT achieves absolute gains up to +12.3, with citation recall increasing by as high as +18.7, sometimes matching large model performance.
- A user study (N=20) reports higher Likert scores for paragraph guidance (4.47 vs 3.84) and citation utility (4.46 vs 3.62) when intents are used.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Intent tags provide explicit rhetorical scaffolding that helps models organize information flow and select appropriate sources. By decomposing the writing process into discrete, labeled sub-tasks, the framework reduces cognitive load on the generation model and mitigates hallucinations. The structured schema acts as a soft constraint, aligning output with human-like writing strategies. For small models, synthetic data distilled from large models transfers this structured reasoning ability, enabling them to learn effective writing patterns without extensive human annotation.

## Foundational Learning
- **Attributed Long-form QA**: Task of generating comprehensive answers with citations; requires synthesis and accurate source attribution.
- **Intent Modeling**: Capturing rhetorical purpose (e.g., explain, compare) to structure text; moves beyond surface-level generation to coherent discourse.
- **Structured Tagging**: Using explicit tags (e.g., [PIT-*], [CIT-*]) to represent intents; provides interpretable control and facilitates data synthesis.
- **Synthetic Data Generation**: Leveraging large LLMs to create annotated examples; scales intent annotation without human cost.
- **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)**: Adapting smaller models to follow intent patterns; critical for transferring structured writing skills.
- **Evaluation of Long-form Generation**: Metrics for overall quality and citation accuracy; necessary for measuring improvements in report writing.

*Quick check:* Verify that intent tags align with human writing plans; ensure synthetic data retains diversity and avoids model artifacts.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Intent Elicitation (prompting) → Intent-Aware Generator (large models)  
Synthetic Data Generator (using large model) → SFT → Small Model (intent-conditioned)

**Critical path:** Input question → assign paragraph/citation intents → generate paragraph with citations → iterate to produce full report.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Explicit tags boost interpretability but may restrict generation flexibility; implicit conditioning offers more fluidity at the cost of control.  
- Synthetic data reduces annotation cost but risks quality degradation from LLM errors.  
- On-the-fly intent elicitation adds latency for large models; pre-defined intents during SFT reduce runtime overhead for small models.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Intent mismatches leading to incoherent paragraphs or misplaced citations.  
- Over-reliance on intents causing formulaic or repetitive text.  
- Synthetic data distributions diverging from target domain, harming generalization.

**First three experiments:**  
1. Measure consistency of intent elicitation across different prompting strategies.  
2. Ablate explicit vs. implicit intent conditioning to isolate their impact on quality and attribution.  
3. Vary synthetic data volume and quality (e.g., with/without human validation) to determine optimal SFT setup for small models.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can we model hierarchical relationships between paragraph intents (e.g., support, contrast) to improve global narrative coherence in long-form reports?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "Future work could explore tree-structured or graph-based representations of intent to reflect how one paragraph supports, contrasts, or contextualizes another."
- **Why unresolved:** Current flat, independent paragraph intents ignore discourse-level structure that humans use when writing.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A model generating graph-based intent annotations that correlate with improved coherence scores or human assessments of narrative flow.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How should intent schemas be adapted for non-scientific domains (e.g., law, humanities), and can models automatically discover domain-specific intent types?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "To generalize, future work is needed for understanding how intent distributions vary by domain, whether schemas need to be domain-adaptive, and how models might learn new intent categories from domain-specific corpora."
- **Why unresolved:** The current schema is tailored to scientific writing; other domains have different rhetorical conventions and citation purposes.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Cross-domain experiments showing performance gains with adapted schemas; unsupervised induction of intent categories from target-domain text that align with human judgments.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Why do current models severely underuse "Comparison or Contrast" citation intents (5% vs. 17% in human writing), and how can training be improved to foster comparative synthesis?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The analysis states models underuse Comparison or Contrast, highlighting a limitation in synthesis ability.
- **Why unresolved:** This gap suggests models struggle to integrate multiple sources comparatively, a key skill for comprehensive reports; the cause (data, objective, capacity) is unclear.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablation studies pinpointing the cause (e.g., training data bias); interventions like data augmentation or multi-task objectives that increase comparison usage and improve synthesis-heavy metrics.

## Limitations
- Lack of experimental detail: no test set sizes, variance across runs, or statistical significance reported.
- Domain restriction: all evaluations on scientific benchmarks; generalizability to other long-form QA domains (e.g., legal, medical) is unclear.
- User study limitations: only 20 participants, likely convenience sample, no qualitative feedback or diversity metrics.
- Overhead not measured: intent elicitation likely increases latency and cost for large models, but these costs are unreported.
- Synthetic data quality: no human verification of LLM-generated intent-annotated data; risk of propagation of model errors.

## Confidence
- Intent-aware generation improves quality and citation metrics (consistent gains across three benchmarks and multiple models): **Medium** (absence of confidence intervals or significance tests reduces certainty).
- SFT with intents yields large gains for small models (absolute improvements up to +12.3): **Medium** (compelling but risk of overfitting to benchmarks or synthetic data artifacts).
- Human-perceived readability and transparency improve (user study Likert gains): **Low** (small sample, no statistical validation, limited representativeness).

## Next Checks
1. Replicate all experiments with ≥5 random seeds, compute means/standard deviations, and report statistical significance via bootstrapping or ANOVA.
