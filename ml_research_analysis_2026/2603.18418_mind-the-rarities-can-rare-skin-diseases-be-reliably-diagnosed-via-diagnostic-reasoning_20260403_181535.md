# Mind the Rarities: Can Rare Skin Diseases Be Reliably Diagnosed via Diagnostic Reasoning?

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.18418
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18418
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Diagnostic reasoning for rare skin diseases is poor in current LVLMs; supervised fine-tuning on the new DermCase dataset yields dramatic improvements.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses a critical gap in evaluating large vision-language models (LVLMs) for diagnosing rare skin diseases. Existing benchmarks focus on common conditions and only final accuracy, neglecting the clinical reasoning process. The authors introduce DermCase, a dataset of 6,354 rare dermatology cases with multi-modal images and detailed reasoning chains. They propose DermLIP-based metrics that better align with expert assessments of differential diagnosis quality. Benchmarking 22 LVLMs reveals poor performance, with MedGemma-27B achieving only 26.1% final diagnosis accuracy. Supervised fine-tuning on DermCase leads to substantial gains, while preference-based methods like DPO offer marginal improvements. Error analysis shows frequent visual interpretation and reasoning failures.

## Method Summary
The study constructs DermCase from peer-reviewed case reports, extracting complete clinical narratives, images, and expert reasoning chains. Each case comprises a visually grounded question (with lesion descriptions omitted to force image use) and a step-by-step diagnostic rationale. The evaluation uses three components: (1) final diagnosis accuracy judged by DeepSeek-R1; (2) differential diagnosis quality via DermLIP-based semantic similarity metrics; and (3) a rubric-based reasoning score across five dimensions, also assessed by an LLM judge. Fine-tuning experiments include supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on 5,154 cases and direct preference optimization (DPO) using chosen-reject pairs that contrast correct reasoning with flawed reasoning and wrong diagnoses.

## Key Results
- Current LVLMs perform poorly on rare skin disease diagnosis: top accuracy only 26.1% (MedGemma-27B).
- Supervised fine-tuning dramatically improves accuracy; e.g., InternVL2.5-4B gains +95.61%.
- DPO yields marginal gains (≤0.6%) over SFT, indicating knowledge injection is more effective than preference alignment.
- DermLIP metrics correlate with dermatologist ratings (ρ=0.35) while generic text similarity fails (BERTScore ρ=-0.01).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Dataset Construction Addresses Reasoning Gap
**Claim:** Constructing a long-context dataset from peer-reviewed case reports with explicit reasoning chains addresses the evaluation gap for rare skin diseases.
**Mechanism:** By extracting complete clinical narratives, multi-modal images, and step-by-step diagnostic reasoning from case reports, the dataset provides a realistic testbed that requires models to synthesize heterogeneous information rather than rely on pattern matching for common conditions.
**Core assumption:** Rare disease diagnosis depends more on structured clinical reasoning than on memorized visual patterns; thus, evaluating reasoning chains is essential.
**Evidence anchors:** Existing benchmarks overlook reasoning process (abstract); DermCase comprises 6,354 cases with comprehensive information (section 3); neighboring corpora identify data scarcity for rare manifestations.
**Break condition:** If rare disease presentations in real-world clinics diverge significantly from published case reports (e.g., due to reporting bias), the dataset's distribution may not reflect actual diagnostic challenges.

### Mechanism 2: Domain-Specific Metrics Align with Experts
**Claim:** Using DermLIP-based semantic similarity metrics yields stronger alignment with dermatologist assessments of differential diagnosis quality.
**Mechanism:** DermLIP, a vision-language model pretrained on dermatology image-text pairs, encodes clinically meaningful relationships between disease terms, enabling nuanced evaluation of differential lists.
**Core assumption:** Diagnostic similarity in dermatology is a domain-specific semantic relationship best captured by models trained on medical visual contexts.
**Evidence anchors:** DermLIP metrics achieve stronger alignment (abstract); DermLIP embeddings capture clinically meaningful relationships (section 3.3); correlation study shows ρ=0.35 vs. BERT’s -0.01 (section 4.5); domain-specific metrics for dermatology differentials are limited in existing literature.
**Break condition:** If DermLIP’s pretraining data (Derm1M) lacks coverage of rare conditions in DermCase, its embeddings may fail to capture semantics for under-represented diseases.

### Mechanism 3: SFT Outperforms DPO for Knowledge Injection
**Claim:** Supervised fine-tuning on DermCase substantially improves diagnostic accuracy and reasoning, while DPO yields only marginal gains, suggesting knowledge injection outweighs preference alignment for this task.
**Mechanism:** SFT directly trains models on high-quality, stepwise reasoning chains, teaching them to integrate multi-modal information and follow a diagnostic protocol; DPO optimizes for response preferences but provides less signal when the base model already produces plausible reasoning.
**Core assumption:** The primary deficit is lack of domain-specific knowledge and structured reasoning templates, not subtle preference mismatches.
**Evidence anchors:** SFT yields dramatic improvements (abstract); DPO fine-tuning yields marginal gains (≤0.6%) (section 4.4); MPO variant also yields marginal gains (≤0.3 points) and may optimize primarily for visual perception (section 4.3).
**Break condition:** If the SFT data contains subtle systematic errors (e.g., extraction artifacts), models may learn flawed reasoning patterns that DPO could correct if given better preference pairs.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Case report HTML → structured extraction (LLM + rules) → visual-grounded questions → reasoning chains (LLM with evidence constraints) → DermCase dataset.  
Evaluation: DermLIP encoder + greedy bipartite matching for differential metrics; DeepSeek-R1 judge for final diagnosis and reasoning rubric.  
Fine-tuning: SFT (freeze vision encoder, update MLP+LLM) → DPO (using chosen-reject pairs derived from correct vs. flawed reasoning/wrong diagnosis).

**Critical path:**  
1) Curate DermCase from licensed case reports.  
2) Validate extraction/reasoning with dermatologist sampling.  
3) Establish DermLIP threshold from similarity distribution.  
4) Benchmark 22 LVLMs.  
5) Run SFT/DPO ablations.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Dataset: Long context (1,100-token answers) enables reasoning evaluation but limits batch size and increases compute.  
- Metrics: DermLIP improves clinical alignment but depends on pretraining disease coverage; LLM-as-judge is flexible but may have biases.  
- SFT vs. DPO: SFT gives large gains but risks overfitting to case report style; DPO is sample-efficient but provides small additional gains on top of SFT.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High visual interpretation errors (26.9%): model misdescribes or ignores image features.  
- Logical inconsistencies (18.5%) + incomplete steps (17.2%): reasoning chain breakdowns.  
- Factual medical errors (18.3%): incorrect terminology or pathophysiology.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Reproduce final diagnosis accuracy table (Table 2) on a subset of models to verify evaluation pipeline.  
2. Validate DermLIP threshold by computing similarity distributions for a sample of differential diagnosis pairs.  
3. Run SFT on InternVL2.5-4B for 1 epoch on 100 cases and measure delta in DermLIP-based F1 and rubric score.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** What are the root architectural or training data limitations that cause the 26.9% visual interpretation errors in LVLMs diagnosing rare skin diseases?  
**Basis in paper:** Error analysis (Section 4.6, Figure 6) identifies visual interpretation errors as the largest single failure mode (26.9% of errors), but does not investigate specific causes such as vision encoder deficiencies, multimodal misalignment, or dataset biases.  
**Why unresolved:** The paper quantifies the error prevalence but provides no ablative analysis to distinguish whether failures stem from perception, grounding, or data distribution shifts.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments isolating vision components, per-architecture failure analysis, and studies correlating error rates with image modality, quality, or disease rarity; dataset probes to identify visual feature distributions missed during pretraining.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** Why does Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) yield minimal gains in diagnostic reasoning for rare skin diseases, in contrast to its success in general reasoning tasks?  
**Basis in paper:** Section 4.4 states: "DPO fine-tuning yields marginal gains on final diagnosis (≤0.6% across models)" while supervised fine-tuning (SFT) improves accuracy by up to 151.55%. The paper speculates that MPO's BCO loss may hinder nuanced reasoning but does not analyze DPO specifically.  
**Why unresolved:** No diagnostic of whether DPO's reward model fails due to noisy preference pairs, insufficient coverage of reasoning errors, or misalignment between preference objectives and clinical reasoning metrics.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Analysis of DPO loss curves and gradient dynamics on medical reasoning; ablation studies varying preference pair construction (e.g., focusing on reasoning steps vs. final diagnosis); comparison of reward model quality on held-out clinical reasoning benchmarks.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** Can domain-specific similarity metrics like DermLIP be improved to achieve higher correlation with expert differential diagnosis assessments beyond the current ρ=0.35?  
**Basis in paper:** Section 4.5 reports that DermLIP Score correlates with dermatologist similarity ratings at Spearman's ρ=0.35, outperforming BERTScore (−0.01), but acknowledges this is moderate and validation was limited to 3 dermatologists on 50 cases.  
**Why unresolved:** The metric's clinical alignment remains partial; the paper does not explore whether incorporating richer clinical context or multi-modal signals could enhance alignment.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Larger-scale multi-center dermatologist evaluations; error analysis of metric failures on specific disease pairs; iterative refinement of DermLIP embeddings with expert feedback or contrastive learning on differential diagnosis data.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** What architectural innovations are necessary for LVLMs to reliably perform multi-step diagnostic reasoning across the long-context (~1,100 tokens) and multi-modal (4+ images) rare case reports in DermCase?  
**Basis in paper:** DermCase cases average 1,100 tokens and require synthesis of history, images, and labs, yet best model (MedGemma-27B) scores only 56.13/100 on rubric-based reasoning (Table 3). The paper benchmarks existing models but does not propose or test architectural changes tailored to long-context medical reasoning.  
**Why unresolved:** It remains unclear whether limitations arise from context window constraints, reasoning chain coherence, or integration of disparate modal information over extended sequences.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Development and evaluation of architectures explicitly designed for long clinical narratives (e.g., hierarchical attention, memory mechanisms); context window scaling studies; ablation of image count vs. reasoning quality; integration of structured clinical knowledge graphs.

## Limitations
- **Dataset Generalization:** DermCase derives from published case reports, which may exhibit reporting bias (e.g., atypical or especially instructive presentations), limiting real-world clinic applicability.
- **Metric Dependency:** DermLIP’s alignment relies on its pretraining (Derm1M) covering the semantic space of rare diseases in DermCase; poor coverage for ultra-rare conditions could bias differential evaluation.
- **Evaluation Stability:** LLM-as-judge for reasoning rubrics lacks reported robustness checks; prompt phrasing or judge model version could significantly alter scores.

## Confidence
- **Dataset Construction:** Medium confidence. The curation pipeline is well-documented and dermatologist-validated, but distribution shift from published reports to real clinics remains unevaluated.
- **DermLIP Metric Superiority:** Medium confidence. Correlation with dermatologist ratings (ρ=0.35) is statistically meaningful but modest, and coverage gaps in Derm1M pose a significant risk.
- **SFT > DPO for Reasoning:** Medium confidence. Results are consistent across models, but the flawed-reasoning DPO data construction may limit DPO’s potential; alternative preference curation could yield different outcomes.

## Next Checks

2. **SFT Data Artifact Audit:** Perform error analysis on SFT model outputs, specifically tracing incorrect reasoning chains back to the training case reports. Determine if flaws originate from model hallucination or from subtle inaccuracies/noise in the extracted ground-truth chains.
3. **LLM Judge Sensitivity Analysis:** Evaluate the reasoning rubric’s sensitivity by assessing a fixed set of model responses with 3–4 rephrased prompt templates and, if feasible, an alternate judge model (e.g., GPT-4o).
