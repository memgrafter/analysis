# Anterior's Approach to Fairness Evaluation of Automated Prior Authorization System

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.14631
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14631
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** For sex, age, and socioeconomic status, adjusted error-rate differences were ≤1 percentage point with confidence intervals within a ±5 percentage-point tolerance band and sufficient power (≥0.80), indicating no meaningful disparity; for race/ethnicity, results were inconclusive due to low power (0.51) and wide confidence intervals.

## Executive Summary
This study tackles the challenge of evaluating fairness in an automated prior authorization (PA) system, where traditional approval-rate parity is inappropriate because clinical guidelines legitimately vary across demographics. The authors propose evaluating fairness via consistency of model error rates (disagreement with human reviewers) rather than outcomes, using a tolerance-band framework (±5 percentage-point margin), protocol-controlled logistic regression, and statistical power analysis on 7,166 human-reviewed cases. For sex, age, and socioeconomic status, adjusted error-rate differences were minimal, confidence intervals fell within the tolerance band, and power was adequate, indicating no meaningful disparity. For race/ethnicity, point estimates were similarly small but confidence intervals were wide and power was low, rendering results inconclusive due to limited sample size.

## Method Summary
The fairness evaluation uses a static dataset of 7,166 prior authorization cases that underwent human review. Each case includes a protocol identifier (payer-defined medical necessity guideline), the human reviewer’s determination (approval or escalation), the model’s recommendation, and patient demographic attributes (sex, age, race/ethnicity, socioeconomic status) extracted from unstructured clinical text. The primary estimand is the absolute difference in error rates (cases where the model disagrees with the human reviewer) between two groups of a protected attribute. Evaluation employs: (1) unadjusted two-proportion z-tests, (2) 95% confidence intervals for the difference compared against a pre-specified tolerance band of ±5 percentage points (using equivalence/non-inferiority logic), (3) statistical power to detect a 5 pp difference, and (4) adjusted differences via logistic regression with protocol fixed effects and marginal standardization, with bootstrap confidence intervals. The analysis respects partial observability—demographics are only included if explicitly documented, and missing data leads to exclusion per attribute.

## Key Results
- **Sex, Age, Socioeconomic Status:** Adjusted absolute error-rate differences ≤1 percentage point; 95% CIs fully within ±5 pp tolerance; power ≥0.80 → no meaningful disparity detected.
- **Race/Ethnicity:** Small point estimates but 95% CI wide (overlaps tolerance band); power low (0.51) → results inconclusive due to insufficient sample size (n=920, ~87% missing).
- **Protocol Overlap:** >90% of protocols included cases from all comparison groups for sex/age/SES, supporting validity of adjusted analyses.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1:** Fairness is evaluated via consistency of model error rates (disagreement with human reviewers) rather than approval rate parity. This isolates model performance artifacts from legitimate, guideline-driven variation in clinical outcomes across demographics. By framing the human reviewer as the reference standard, the analysis tests whether the model deviates from expert judgment at different rates for different groups, independent of baseline approval eligibility. Core assumption: human reviewer determinations represent a clinically sound and operationally relevant fairness reference, and any systematic difference in model error rates relative to these reviewers indicates a model bias requiring investigation. Break condition: if the human review process itself contains systematic, demographic-dependent error or bias, the reference standard is compromised, invalidating the disparity measure.

**Mechanism 2:** Practical significance of observed error-rate differences is determined via a pre-specified tolerance band (±5 pp), not just statistical significance. This uses an equivalence/non-inferiority testing framework: a 95% confidence interval for the error-rate difference (Δ) is compared against a domain-informed margin (δ). If the CI lies entirely within [-δ, +δ], the observed difference is deemed practically negligible. Core assumption: the ±5 pp margin accurately reflects the smallest operationally meaningful disparity in PA workflow impact. Break condition: the pre-specified tolerance band is either too wide (masking harmful disparities) or too narrow (flagging inconsequential variation), leading to incorrect fairness conclusions.

**Mechanism 3:** Adjusted analyses using logistic regression with protocol fixed effects control for confounding from heterogeneous clinical guidelines. Protocols are the primary driver of case difficulty and base error rates. Including protocol as a fixed effect in the model `error ~ A + C(Protocol) + ...` holds guideline constant, isolating the effect of the protected attribute `A` on error rates from the mix of protocols assigned to each group. Core assumption: protocol fixed effects adequately capture all protocol-level confounding; any remaining disparity after adjustment is attributable to the protected attribute or unmeasured covariates. Break condition: if protocol effects interact with protected attributes (effect modification) or if protocol assignment to cases is itself non-random and related to demographics, fixed effects may not fully eliminate confounding.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Equivalence/Non-Inferiority Testing
  - **Why needed here:** Standard NHST (p-values) is insufficient for fairness; a non-significant p-value does not prove fairness. This framework tests whether an effect size is *small enough* to be irrelevant, which is the operational fairness question.
  - **Quick check:** Why is rejecting the null hypothesis (Δ=0) not the same as concluding the model is fair across groups?
- **Concept:** Fixed-Effects Regression for Confounding Control
  - **Why needed here:** PA decisions are governed by dozens of distinct clinical protocols with varying inherent difficulty and error rates. Without controlling for protocol, differences in which protocols each demographic group's cases belong to could create spurious disparity signals.
  - **Quick check:** What is the key difference between controlling for protocol via fixed effects versus including it as a simple covariate in a fairness analysis of PA?
- **Concept:** Partial Observability & Signal Extraction from Unstructured Text
  - **Why needed here:** The model only "sees" demographic information if it is explicitly documented in clinical notes. The fairness evaluation must respect this partial observability—it assesses fairness based on the information *actually available* to the model, not on ideally complete metadata. Missing demographic data leads to exclusion from analysis, not imputation.
  - **Quick check:** How does the fact that demographic attributes are extracted from clinical text (with missingness) condition the scope of the fairness claims made?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input Processor -> Demographic Signal Extractor -> Protocol Matcher & Contextualizer -> Core Decision Engine -> Human Review Interface -> Audit Log & Fairness Data Pipeline
- **Critical path:** Unstructured Doc -> Text Extraction -> Demographic & Protocol Identification -> Contextual LLM Reasoning -> Binary Recommendation -> Human Adjudication (Ground Truth) -> Fairness Analysis Dataset.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Accuracy vs. Transparency:** Using an LLM for complex protocol reasoning increases coverage but reduces interpretability vs. a purely symbolic rule engine. Tradeoff managed by requiring protocol citations in outputs.
  - **Automation vs. Safety:** The system *only* recommends approval or escalation; it never recommends denial. This limits automation's scope but contains risk by design.
  - **Data Richness vs. Generalizability:** Fairness analysis is constrained by what's documented. Building a better demographic extractor could improve analysis coverage but might not align with model's actual input.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Data Pipeline:** Sudden drop in extracted demographic coverage (race/SES) indicates a change in source documentation format or extractor logic break.
  - **Model Behavior:** Increase in overall error rate (`False Approval` + `Unnecessary Escalation`) or a shift in their ratio. A new protocol introduced without sufficient training data may cause localized error spikes.
  - **Fairness Monitoring:** For a previously "fair" attribute (sex/age), a new CI from fresh data crossing the ±5 pp tolerance band, especially with maintained power, is a red flag. For race, widening CIs without point estimate shift likely indicate worsening data sparsity.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Protocol Overlap Diagnostic:** For each protected attribute, compute the number of protocols with cases from *both* comparison groups. Goal: >90% overlap (as seen in paper) before trusting adjusted analyses.
  2. **Tolerance Band Sensitivity:** Re-run the primary adjusted analysis for a well-powered attribute (e.g., Sex) with alternative δ values (±3 pp, ±7 pp). Observe how the "CI∈δ" conclusion changes. Tests robustness of the fairness conclusion to the tolerance choice.
  3. **Missingness Impact Simulation:** For the SES analysis, artificially induce "missing" on the payer field for a random 20% of High-SES cases. Re-run the analysis. Goal: Quantify how conclusions depend on the documented SES signal, mirroring the paper's complete-case robustness check.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How can missing demographic data (race/ethnicity, socioeconomic status) be reduced in prior authorization submissions to enable robust fairness evaluation?**  
   Basis: The study had very high missingness for race/ethnicity (86.9%) and SES (65.2%), leading to sparse subgroups, wide confidence intervals, and inconclusive results, which the authors attribute to limited documentation in clinical notes.  
   Why unresolved: The current extraction relies entirely on explicit demographic statements in unstructured text, and the paper offers no strategy to improve capture; thus, fairness assessments for these groups remain impossible with available data.  
   What evidence would resolve it: Validate natural language processing methods that infer demographics from contextual cues or pilot structured demographic reporting in PA workflows; then replicate the fairness analysis with fuller, more reliable subgroup data.

2. **Is the predefined ±5 percentage-point tolerance band an appropriate universal threshold for meaningful error-rate disparities, or should it be protocol-specific or risk-adjusted?**  
   Basis: The authors state: "We set the tolerance band to δ=±5 percentage points (pp), reflecting the smallest difference considered material in a prior authorization workflow." No empirical justification or variation by context is discussed.  
   Why unresolved: The choice is based on author judgment without evidence that it aligns with stakeholder perceptions of harm or operational impact; different medical necessity guidelines may tolerate different error margins.  
   What evidence would resolve it: Gather input from clinicians, patients, and payers via surveys or interviews to define meaningful differences; or conduct analyses linking error-rate disparities to downstream outcomes like treatment delays to calibrate acceptable thresholds per protocol.

3. **Do automatically approved decisions (cases not escalated for human review) exhibit demographic disparities in error rates, potentially introducing hidden bias not captured by the current evaluation?**  
   Basis: The fairness evaluation was restricted to the 7,166 human-reviewed cases, and the authors explicitly note: "Fairness claims in this work are conditional on the reviewed-case population." The system also auto-approves cases without human review.  
   Why unresolved: Errors in auto-approved cases are completely unobserved; if these errors differ across demographic groups, the overall fairness picture could be substantially biased, but the current methodology cannot detect such disparities.  
   What evidence would resolve it: Conduct a prospective audit by sampling and manually reviewing a representative subset of auto-approved cases to estimate group-specific error rates, then integrate these with reviewed-case results to produce a comprehensive fairness assessment.

## Limitations
- **Race/ethnicity analysis inconclusive:** Low statistical power (0.51) and wide confidence intervals due to small sample size (n=920) and high missingness (~87%).
- **Human reviewer as gold standard:** Core assumption that human adjudications are bias-free and clinically definitive is untested; systematic reviewer bias would invalidate the error-rate disparity measure.
- **Documented population bias:** Fairness evaluation is restricted to cases where demographics were explicitly documented in clinical text, potentially biasing results if documentation practices correlate with model error.

## Confidence
- **High confidence:** For sex, age, and SES, the framework correctly identified no statistically or practically significant disparity *within the studied population and tolerance definition*. Data sufficiency (power ≥0.80) and narrow CIs support this.
- **Medium confidence:** In the *overall methodological approach* (tolerance-band comparison, protocol-adjusted logistic regression). The logic is sound and transparent, but its real-world validity hinges on the unverifiable "human as reference" and "tolerance band" assumptions.
- **Low confidence:** In any conclusion regarding *race/ethnicity*. The data provided insufficient evidence to judge fairness or bias.

## Next Checks
1. **Tolerance Sensitivity Re-analysis:** Re-run the primary adjusted analysis for a well-powered attribute (e.g., Sex) using alternative δ margins (±3pp, ±7pp). A robust "fair" result should hold under reasonable margin variation; a fragile conclusion would flag over-reliance on the specific ±5pp choice.
2. **Protocol Overlap Diagnostic:** For each protected attribute, calculate the proportion of protocols containing cases from *all* comparison groups (e.g., both Male and Female). For valid adjustment, this should approach 100% for Sex/Age/SES. For Race, low overlap would explain wide CIs and necessitate a different analytical strategy (e.g., meta-analysis of protocol-specific disparities).
3. **Missingness Bias Simulation:** For the SES analysis, simulate a missing-not-at-random scenario: artificially mark 20% of *High-SES* cases as "missing" (assuming wealthier patients have less detailed payer info in notes). Re-run the complete-case analysis. A large shift in the point estimate or CI would quantify the vulnerability of the documented-population analysis to differential missingness.
