# Evaluating AI Grading on Real-World Handwritten College Mathematics: A Large-Scale Study Toward a Benchmark

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.00895
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00895
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** AI grading achieves MAE 0.5–1.06 points vs. TAs, with 68–86% agreement within 1 point and 79.79% full correctness by human review across 3,945 handwritten calculus submissions.

## Executive Summary
This study tackles scalable grading and formative feedback for handwritten calculus in large undergraduate courses. The authors built a system where GPT-4.1-mini transcribes student work into LaTeX, and an LLM evaluates responses using structured prompts with dual rubrics (flexible and fixed) and a max-rule for score aggregation. Validated on 3,945 submissions from nearly 800 students, the AI’s grades closely matched TA grades (mean absolute error 0.5–1.06 points), and independent review found 79.79% of evaluations fully correct. Student survey feedback was largely positive, indicating strong potential for AI-assisted grading at scale.

## Method Summary
The pipeline begins with GPT-4.1-mini OCR converting handwritten responses into LaTeX code. An LLM then assesses the LaTeX using two independent rubrics: a flexible rubric for partial credit and a fixed rubric for binary correctness. A max-rule aggregates the two scores to produce a final grade and natural-language feedback. TA grades serve as the ground truth for evaluation. The system was deployed in a real calculus course, with OCR readability and grading accuracy quantified against human benchmarks.

## Key Results
- Mean absolute error (MAE) of AI grades vs. TA grades: 0.5–1.06 points.
- 68–86% of AI scores within 1 point of TA grades.
- Independent human review: 79.79% of AI evaluations fully correct, 9.55% acceptable; 87.64% of OCR outputs readable.
- Student survey (n=301): 60–61% found feedback accurate, 62–63% clear; 76–78% open to AI grading.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The approach succeeds because modern OCR models like GPT-4.1-mini can handle varied handwritten mathematical notation, converting it to structured LaTeX that LLMs can parse. LLMs excel at applying rubrics to assess mathematical reasoning and calculations, especially when guided by dual rubrics that balance flexibility and consistency. The max-rule aggregation mitigates under-grading risks from any single rubric, and feedback generation leverages the LLM’s ability to explain decisions in natural language tied to rubric criteria.

## Foundational Learning
- **Handwritten Math OCR**: Converts images to LaTeX; needed to transform physical work into digital, machine-readable format. Quick check: Can the OCR correctly recognize common calculus symbols (e.g., integrals, derivatives) in messy handwriting?
- **Structured LLM Prompting**: Uses detailed rubrics to guide evaluation; ensures consistent application of grading criteria. Quick check: Does the prompt include clear point allocations and example responses?
- **Dual Rubric System**: Combines flexible (partial credit) and fixed (binary) rubrics; balances fairness with standardization. Quick check: Are the two rubrics applied independently to avoid cross-contamination?
- **Score Aggregation (Max-Rule)**: Takes the higher score from the two rubrics; reduces under-grading risk. Quick check: Does the max-rule favor one rubric type over the other in edge cases?
- **Human-in-the-Loop Validation**: TAs provide ground truth; essential for training and evaluating the system. Quick check: Is there a statistically significant sample of TA-graded submissions for comparison?
- **Feedback Generation**: LLM produces explanations; needed for formative learning. Quick check: Is feedback tied to specific rubric criteria and errors in the submission?

## Architecture Onboarding
Handwritten Submission -> OCR (GPT-4.1-mini) -> LaTeX Code -> LLM Evaluation (with dual rubrics) -> Score Aggregation (max-rule) -> Final Grade and Feedback.

Critical Path: Image ingestion to final feedback output, with OCR and LLM evaluation as sequential bottlenecks.

Design Tradeoffs:
- OCR accuracy vs. inference speed: More accurate models may be slower.
- Rubric complexity vs. LLM consistency: Overly nuanced rubrics may confuse the LLM.
- Automation vs. human oversight: Reducing TA workload but maintaining quality control.

Failure Signatures:
- Unreadable OCR output (e.g., garbled LaTeX).
- LLM scores deviating significantly from TA grades (>2 points).
- Student complaints about feedback relevance or accuracy.

First Experiments:
1. Benchmark OCR accuracy on a diverse set of handwriting samples, measuring symbol recognition rates.
2. Calibrate LLM scoring by comparing against a subset of TA grades, adjusting prompt phrasing.
3. Conduct a small-scale student survey to evaluate feedback clarity and usefulness before full deployment.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper does not explicitly enumerate open questions. However, based on the limitations and future directions discussed, the following questions are implied for further investigation:
- How does the system generalize to other mathematical domains (e.g., algebra, statistics) and diverse institutional contexts?
- What is the inter-rater reliability among TAs, and how does variability in human grading affect the evaluation of AI performance?
- How can the OCR readability threshold be standardized and improved to reduce the 12.36% exclusion rate?
- What are the specific error patterns in AI scoring (e.g., arithmetic mistakes, rubric misinterpretation), and how can they be systematically addressed?
- How does the system scale with larger class sizes and more varied handwriting quality without degradation in accuracy?

## Limitations
- Generalizability limited to a single calculus course; unknown performance in other math domains or institutions.
- Ground truth based on TA grades without inter-rater reliability measurement.
- Vague "readable" OCR criterion; 12.36% of submissions excluded due to unreadability, potentially biasing results.

## Confidence
- **Grading accuracy:** High (large N, low MAE, high human review correctness).
- **OCR effectiveness:** Medium (87.64% readable but criteria undefined).
- **Feedback acceptance:** Medium (survey shows approval but methodological details lacking).
