# \textit{Versteasch du mi?} Computational and Socio-Linguistic Perspectives on GenAI, LLMs, and Non-Standard Language

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.28213  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.28213  
- **Reference count**: 28  
- **Primary result**: Non-standard languages face systemic marginalization in LLMs due to tokenization biases, benchmark exclusion, and severe data resource disparities.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how large language models (LLMs) systematically marginalize non-standard languages and dialects through computational and sociolinguistic analysis. Focusing on South Tyrolean dialects and Kurdish varieties as case studies, it reveals that tokenization imposes a "tax" of 2–15× higher computational costs compared to English, while major benchmarks like FLORES-200 and Global MMLU exclude most non-standard languages entirely. The authors link these technical biases to deeper issues of linguistic inequality, such as the lack of ISO language codes and culturally skewed evaluation frameworks, ultimately proposing policy interventions like mandatory "dialect gap" reporting and community-led data sovereignty to address the exclusion.

## Method Summary
The paper employs an interdisciplinary case-study methodology, combining sociolinguistic critique with computational experimentation. It evaluates two distinct language groups: South Tyrolean German dialects (e.g., Versteasch) and Kurdish varieties (e.g., Central, Southern, Northern). For each, it measures tokenization efficiency across standard tokenizers, audits representation in key multilingual benchmarks, and quantifies available training resources (tokens, audio hours) from public corpora. This dual lens—sociolinguistic theory and empirical NLP metrics—frames marginalization as a product of both technical design and societal neglect.

## Key Results
- Tokenization inefficiency: Non-standard languages require 2–15× more tokens than English for equivalent text, increasing computational cost.
- Benchmark exclusion: Major multilingual benchmarks (e.g., FLORES-200, Global MMLU) largely omit non-standard languages; only Central/Northern Kurdish appear in FLORES-200.
- Resource disparities: Even included languages like Central Kurdish have minimal resources (<300M tokens, 200h audio), while excluded ones like Southern Kurdish have near-zero (<10M tokens, 10h audio).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Marginalization arises from a feedback loop: tokenizers trained on dominant-language corpora split non-standard words into many subwords, increasing cost and reducing model quality; benchmarks designed for high-resource languages fail to evaluate non-standard performance, disincentivizing development; and the absence of ISO codes or documentation further erases these languages from data pipelines. This technical occlusion mirrors sociopolitical neglect, where non-standard varieties are deemed "not worth" standardizing or supporting.

## Foundational Learning
- **Subword tokenization (BPE/WordPiece)**: Algorithms that split text into frequent subword units; why needed: explains why rare/dialectal forms incur higher token counts. Quick check: How many tokens does "Versteasch" split into vs. standard German "Verstehst du"?
- **Language vs. dialect continuum**: Sociolinguistic concept where varieties form gradients, not discrete categories; why needed: challenges binary ISO coding and benchmark inclusion. Quick check: Is South Tyrolean a language or dialect? What political factors influence this?
- **ISO 639 language codes**: Standard identifiers for languages; why needed: absence of codes for many non-standard varieties blocks their inclusion in datasets and tools. Quick check: What is the ISO code for Central Kurdish? Does Southern Kurdish have one?
- **Benchmark construction bias**: How evaluation datasets reflect the linguistic worldviews of their creators; why needed: explains why culturally specific knowledge is excluded. Quick check: Does FLORES-200 include any Tyrolean sentence pairs?
- **Data sovereignty**: Principle that communities control their own language data; why needed: counters extractive data practices that exclude speakers. Quick check: Who owns the Kurdish audio corpora used in research?
- **Tokenization tax**: The increased computational cost from inefficient tokenization; why needed: quantifies a hidden barrier to inclusion. Quick check: If a 100-word English message uses 120 tokens, how many might a Southern Kurdish equivalent use?

## Architecture Onboarding
The "architecture" here is the existing NLP pipeline that excludes non-standard languages.  
**Component map**: Raw text (dialect) -> Tokenizer (trained on standard corpora) -> Model (trained on dominant-language data) -> Evaluation (on standard-language benchmarks) -> Deployment.  
**Critical path**: Data scarcity in non-standard languages leads to poor tokenization, which degrades model performance, which goes unmeasured due to benchmark absence, reinforcing the cycle.  
**Design tradeoffs**: Efficiency (fast, cheap tokenization for high-resource languages) vs. inclusivity (supporting linguistic diversity); current systems optimize for the former.  
**Failure signatures**: High token counts for non-standard text, near-zero benchmark scores, absence from model card evaluations.  
**First experiments**: 1) Tokenize identical sentences in standard/non-standard variants across BPE, WordPiece, Unigram tokenizers; 2) Audit XGLUE, TydiQA, AfroXLMR for non-standard language coverage; 3) Compare available audio/text corpora sizes for Kurdish varieties against a high-resource baseline like German.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper does not explicitly list open questions beyond its analysis, but its policy proposals implicitly raise questions about implementation: How can "dialect gap" reporting be standardized? What legal frameworks support community data sovereignty? How to design benchmarks that capture cultural nuance without exoticizing?

## Limitations
- Case studies limited to two language groups; broader generalizability uncertain.
- Tokenization tax estimates depend on specific tokenizer versions and model architectures; the 2–15× range may vary.
- Resource disparity data drawn from public corpora likely incomplete and biased toward better-documented varieties.

## Confidence
- **Tokenization tax**: Medium confidence (empirically measured but may not generalize across all tokenizers/LLMs).
- **Benchmark marginalization**: High confidence (directly verifiable via benchmark documentation).
- **Resource disparities**: Medium confidence (based on reported sizes; data availability is fragmented).
