# Generative Model via Quantile Assignment
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.18216
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18216
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: NeuroSQL achieves lower proxy FID (e.g., 0.61 vs VAE 1.26 on MNIST) and higher SSIM (0.616 vs VAE 0.179) than VAEs and GANs, while requiring the least training time; also yields superior downstream classification accuracy (87.7% vs VAE 67.5% on MNIST) and remains effective under limited training data.

## Executive Summary
NeuroSQL is a generative modeling framework that eliminates auxiliary networks (encoders, discriminators) by learning latent variables via a deterministic optimal-transport-based quantile assignment. It solves a linear assignment problem to map each data point to a fixed lattice of prior quantiles, then trains a standalone generator. This approach reduces training instability, computational overhead, and mode collapse, while achieving state-of-the-art results on MNIST, CelebA, AFHQ, and OASIS benchmarks with lower FID, higher SSIM, and better downstream task performance, especially in low-data regimes.

## Method Summary
NeuroSQL constructs a fixed lattice of quantiles in the latent space (e.g., via Sobol points mapped through the prior CDF). For a given dataset, it solves a linear assignment problem (Hungarian or greedy) to match each data point to a quantile by minimizing a reconstruction loss (e.g., SSIM+L1) between the real image and the generator's output conditioned on that quantile. The generator is then trained via supervised regression on these assigned latents. The process alternates: update generator parameters using the current latent assignments, recompute the assignment using the updated generator, and apply a momentum update to smooth latent assignments. This cycle avoids joint training of separate encoder/discriminator networks, leading to faster convergence and improved stability.

## Key Results
- Lower proxy FID: 0.61 on MNIST vs VAE 1.26 and GANs.
- Higher SSIM: 0.616 on MNIST vs VAE 0.179.
- Superior downstream classification: 87.7% on MNIST synthetic data vs VAE 67.5%.
- Most efficient training time among compared methods.
- Robust performance even with limited training data.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** NeuroSQL eliminates the need for an encoder network by learning latent variables through a deterministic, optimal-transport-based quantile assignment instead of amortized inference.
- **Mechanism:** The method approximates latent variables \(Z_i\) as a permutation of fixed prior quantiles \(Q^k_n\). This reduces latent estimation to solving a linear assignment problem (via Hungarian or greedy algorithm) that pairs each data point \(X_i\) with a quantile \(Q_k\) by minimizing a reconstruction loss cost matrix \(C_{i,k} = \ell(X_i, G_\theta(Q_k))\).
- **Core assumption:** As \(n \to \infty\), the discrete grid of quantiles (constructed via optimal transport for multivariate cases) weakly converges to the uniform distribution over the latent support, making the assignment error vanish (Proposition 1).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "...eliminates the need for auxiliary networks by learning low-dimensional latent representations implicitly... learns the latent variables by solving a linear assignment problem."
  - [section 2.2] "...there exists a permutation \(\pi\) such that \(Z \approx Q^n_\pi\)... the latent variables can be approximated by learning an appropriate permutation of the quantiles."
  - [section 2.3] Uses center-outward distribution functions (Hallin et al.) to define multivariate quantiles via optimal transport.
  - [corpus] Weak; neighboring papers focus on VQ, Koopman VAEs, or diffusion, not quantile assignment as a replacement for encoders.
- **Break condition:** The asymptotic guarantee fails if the quantile grid does not weakly converge to the uniform distribution (e.g., high-discrepancy grid) or if the latent distribution is highly non-uniform and the chosen prior \(P_Z\) is misspecified.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** By removing auxiliary networks (encoder/discriminator), NeuroSQL reduces computational overhead, parameter count, and training instability, leading to faster convergence and better performance in low-data regimes.
- **Mechanism:** The training loop alternates between (i) updating generator parameters \(\theta\) via supervised regression on assigned quantiles and (ii) reassigning quantiles via a combinatorial optimization independent of data dimensionality. No separate network processes high-dimensional \(X_i\), avoiding the curse of dimensionality in auxiliary function learning.
- **Core assumption:** The generator \(G_\theta\) is the sole learnable function approximator; the assignment step is a discrete, closed-form operation that does not require gradient-based optimization and thus does not suffer from joint-training instability.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "...eliminates the need for auxiliary networks... training instability, computational overhead, and risks like mode collapse... requires the least training time."
  - [section 2.4] Algorithm 1 shows the alternating scheme; complexity \(O(n \cdot c_G + n^3)\) (or \(O(n^2)\) with greedy) is independent of data dimension \(p\).
  - [section 3.5] Diffusion underperforms due to high-variance score estimates in low-data settings; NeuroSQL's simpler objective scales better.
  - [section 2.5] "The assignment step is independent of the data dimension \(p\), which is why NeuroSQL scales favorably to very high-dimensional observations."
- **Break condition:** If the generator capacity is insufficient to model \(G\) given the fixed quantile lattice, or if the assignment cost (e.g., SSIM+L1) is not a suitable proxy for data likelihood.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The deterministic, quantile-based latent space yields more interpretable and better-separated class representations compared to stochastic VAEs, potentially aiding downstream tasks.
- **Mechanism:** Since latents are assigned from a fixed, high-variance lattice (e.g., Sobol-mapped Gaussian quantiles), the embedding space is used more evenly and without the KL-prior crowding effect seen in VAEs. This creates compact, well-isolated clusters for different data classes (e.g., digits in MNIST).
- **Core assumption:** The permutation recovery via optimal transport preserves semantic structure (e.g., class membership) in the latent space better than a stochastic encoder trained with a regularization term (e.g., KL divergence).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 2.6] Fig. 2: Visual comparison shows NeuroSQL latent space has distinct, non-overlapping digit clusters vs. VAE's "fuzzy cloud." Text: "by approximating latents via a linear assignment to a fixed, high-variance lattice... organizes digit classes into compact, well-isolated ‘islands’."
  - [section 3.4] Downstream classifier trained on NeuroSQL synthetic data achieves 87.7% accuracy vs. VAE's 67.5%, implying better class-conditional representation.
  - [corpus] Weak; related work (e.g., VQ-VAEs) also yields disentangled latents but via learned codebooks, not fixed quantile assignment.
- **Break condition:** If the data manifold is not well-approximated by a permutation of fixed quantiles (e.g., extremely complex, continuous class boundaries), the discrete assignment may force artificial separations that harm generation quality.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Optimal Transport for Multivariate Quantiles**
  - *Why needed here:* NeuroSQL defines multivariate quantiles \(Q_k\) as the push-forward of a uniform grid via the optimal transport map (center-outward distribution function). This provides a canonical, coordinate-free way to partition the latent space into \(n\) regions, essential for the assignment step when \(d > 1\).
  - *Quick check question:* Given a latent distribution \(P_Z\), how would you construct a discrete set of points that act as multivariate quantiles using optimal transport? What property must the grid satisfy as \(n \to \infty\)?
- **Concept: Linear Assignment Problem (Hungarian Algorithm)**
  - *Why needed here:* The core training step solves \(\min_{\pi} \sum_i C_{i,\pi(i)}\) to match data points to quantile codes. Understanding this combinatorial optimization (and its \(O(n^3)\) complexity) is crucial for grasping NeuroSQL's computational trade-offs and scalability solutions (greedy, mini-batch).
  - *Quick check question:* What is the time complexity of the Hungarian algorithm, and why does NeuroSQL's assignment cost matrix size depend on \(n\) but not on data dimension \(p\)?
- **Concept: Asymptotic Approximation in Sieve Methods**
  - *Why needed here:* The justification for replacing true latents \(Z_i\) with a permutation of quantiles \(Q^n_\pi\) relies on a sieve-limit argument: as \(n \to \infty\), the approximation error vanishes (Eq. 3, Proposition 1). This connects to nonparametric statistics (Chen, 2007) and is key to the method's theoretical foundation.
  - *Quick check question:* If the grid converges weakly to uniform, what does Theorem 2.4 in Hallin et al. (2021) guarantee about \(\min_\pi \|Z - Q^n_\pi\|^2\)?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Quantile Lattice Generator -> Generator Gθ -> Assignment Solver -> Momentum Buffer -> Loss ℓ
- **Critical path:** Init random \(\pi^{(0)}\) and \(\hat{Z}^{(0)} = Q^n_{\pi^{(0)}}\) → Loop: (a) Update \(\theta\) via \(\min_\theta \frac{1}{n} \sum_i \ell(X_i, G_\theta(\hat{Z}_i^{(t-1)}))\) (supervised regression), (b) Compute full cost matrix \(C_{i,k} = \ell(X_i, G_\theta(Q_k))\), (c) Solve \(\pi^{(t)} = \arg\min_\pi \text{Tr}(C_\pi)\), (d) Momentum update \(\hat{Z}^{(t)}\). Repeat until convergence.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Assignment algorithm:** Hungarian (\(O(n^3)\)) vs. Greedy (\(O(n^2)\)). Greedy is ~6.5x faster (Section 2.5) with minimal quality drop; recommended for \(n > 2000\).
  - **Cost function \(\ell\):** Pixel L2 is simpler but yields blur; SSIM+L1 improves perceptual metrics. Ablate per dataset (Appendix L).
  - **Latent dimension \(d\):** Larger \(d\) improves expressivity but increases assignment cost indirectly (more epochs?). Experiment sweep (2–128) shows moderate \(d\) (16–64) often best (Tables 5–19).
  - **Mini-batch assignment:** Full-batch assignment ensures global optimality but limits scalability; mini-batch (Algorithm 2) with persistent memory bank and momentum enables large \(n\) but introduces stochasticity.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Poor FID/LPIPS/SSIM:** Generator capacity insufficient, or cost function misaligned with perceptual quality.
  - **Mode collapse / lack of diversity:** Assignment may overfit to a subset of quantiles if \(\rho\) too low or greedy assignment creates biased matches.
  - **Training instability / plateauing:** Momentum \(\rho\) too low; consider increasing to 0.7–0.9. Also check if generator learning rate is too high relative to assignment frequency \(K\).
  - **High memory usage:** Full-batch Hungarian stores \(n \times n\) cost matrix; switch to greedy or mini-batch for \(n > 5000\) (Table 4 shows Hungarian peaks at ~285 MB VRAM vs. greedy ~82 MB).
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Sanity check on MNIST:** Run NeuroSQL with \(d=2\), ConvNet, Hungarian assignment, full batch. Verify latent space plot (like Fig. 2a) shows separated digit clusters. Confirm FID < 1.0 and SSIM > 0.6 (Table 19).
  2. **Assignment algorithm ablation:** Compare Hungarian vs. greedy on a medium dataset (e.g., CelebA, \(n \approx 2500\)). Measure epoch time and FID/LPIPS. Expect ~6x speedup with <5% metric degradation (Section 2.5, Table 3).
  3. **Low-data regime test:** Train on subsets (e.g., 500–2000 samples) of OASIS or AFHQ. Compare against VAE/GAN with same generator and compute budget. Expect NeuroSQL to degrade more gracefully (Section 3.5, "practical... limited training data").

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

(Basis: authors state future work can evaluate scalability to higher resolutions and comparisons against baselines like StyleGAN3.)
2. **Medical imaging benchmarks:** Can NeuroSQL be effectively applied to medical imaging benchmarks like MedMNIST to demonstrate its utility for data augmentation and synthetic data generation in healthcare? (Basis: authors mention "a natural next step is to experiment with the MedMNIST benchmark suite.")
3. **Generalization to non-image modalities:** Can the quantile-assignment mechanism be generalized to non-image modalities such as audio and 3D data, and what generator architectures are most suitable? (Basis: authors list "scaling the assignment mechanism to new modalities (e.g., audio, 3D)" as a key direction.)
4. **Finite-sample theoretical guarantees:** What theoretical guarantees exist for the finite-sample performance of the quantile assignment estimator, and how do they depend on sample size, latent dimension, and data distribution? (Basis: paper provides an asymptotic result but does not address finite-sample rates; authors note expanding theory as future work.)

## Limitations
- Theoretical guarantee of asymptotic convergence for finite n may fail on complex manifolds or with non-uniform priors; break condition unexplored.
- Critical implementation details missing: exact generator architecture (kernel sizes, strides), hyperparameters (LR schedule, early stopping), and random CNN seed for proxy FID.
- Evaluation reliability: downstream task setup ambiguous; SSIM/LPIPS implementations unreported; results may be dataset-specific.

## Confidence
- Quantile assignment replaces encoder/discriminator: High
- Achieves lower FID/higher SSIM on reported datasets: Medium
- Generalizes to low-data regimes and unseen domains: Low
