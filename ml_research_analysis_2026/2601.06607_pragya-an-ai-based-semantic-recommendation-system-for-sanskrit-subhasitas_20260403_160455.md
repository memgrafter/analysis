# Pragya: An AI-Based Semantic Recommendation System for Sanskrit Subhasitas

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.06607
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06607
- **Reference count:** 7
- **Primary result:** Semantic retrieval with RAG improves Top-3 precision from 45% (keyword) to 72% and user satisfaction from 2.8 to 4.3/5 for Sanskrit Subhasita recommendations.

## Executive Summary
Pragya is a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) system designed to overcome barriers in accessing *Sanskrit Subhasitas* (wise sayings). It addresses underutilization by combining **semantic retrieval** from a small, curated 200-verse dataset using **IndicBERT** with a **Mistral LLM** that generates culturally contextual outputs, including transliterations, translations, and explanations. In a pilot evaluation, it significantly outperformed keyword-based baselines on precision, coverage, and user satisfaction, while generating explanations rated higher in clarity and cultural appropriateness than standard dictionary translations.

## Method Summary
The system employs a two-stage RAG pipeline. First, a **semantic retriever** (IndicBERT) encodes the user query and the curated Subhasita corpus to find the top-k most relevant verses based on embedding similarity, replacing or augmenting keyword matching. Second, a **generative LLM** (Mistral) takes the retrieved verses and the query to produce a structured response: a transliteration, a translation, and a contextual explanation designed to convey the verse's wisdom and cultural nuance. The corpus is small (~200 verses) but manually curated for quality and thematic diversity.

## Key Results
- Semantic retrieval (IndicBERT) boosted Top-3 precision from 45% (keyword) to 72% and coverage from 60% to 82%.
- User satisfaction with Pragya's full output averaged 4.3/5 vs. 2.8 for a keyword-only or dictionary baseline.
- Generated explanations scored significantly higher than literal dictionary translations in clarity, cultural appropriateness, and engagement.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core innovation is using **semantic embeddings** to overcome the lexical gap between a user's modern or cross-lingual query and the archaic, often metaphorical Sanskrit verses. Keyword matching fails on synonymy and conceptual similarity; IndicBERT captures these deeper meanings, retrieving relevant verses even without exact word overlap. The **RAG pattern** then grounds the LLM in these high-quality retrieved verses, mitigating hallucination and enabling the generation of context-rich, culturally framed explanations that go beyond literal translation. This combination directly targets the documented barriers of linguistic diversity and lack of contextual guidance.

## Foundational Learning
1.  **IndicBERT** – A pre-trained multilingual BERT model specifically adapted for Indian languages. *Why needed:* Provides robust semantic representations for Sanskrit queries and verses within the Indian linguistic context, outperforming generic multilingual models. *Quick check:* Compare its zero-shot retrieval accuracy on a held-out set of Subhasita-query pairs against mBERT or LaBSE.
2.  **Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)** – A pattern where an LLM generates text conditioned on retrieved documents. *Why needed:* Grounds generative outputs in a trusted, curated knowledge base (the Subhasita corpus), improving factual accuracy and cultural appropriateness. *Quick check:* Assess hallucination rates when the retriever returns irrelevant passages vs. when it returns relevant ones.
3.  **Sanskrit Subhasita Literature** – A genre of aphoristic, poetic sayings from classical Indian texts. *Why needed:* The domain-specific content requires understanding of poetic devices, cultural references, and contextual morality. *Quick check:* Evaluate a sample of verses for multiple interpretations and required cultural knowledge to gauge annotation complexity.
4.  **Semantic Search vs. Keyword Search** – Information retrieval based on meaning vs. lexical overlap. *Why needed:* To handle the evolution of language and metaphorical expressions in Subhasitas. *Quick check:* Manually judge a set of query-verse pairs for semantic relevance and compare to BM25 scores.
5.  **Low-Resource NLP for Sanskrit** – Techniques for languages lacking large digital corpora and standard NLP tools. *Why needed:* Sanskrit presents challenges for standard models due to its morphology and limited modern training data. *Quick check:* Benchmark part-of-speech tagging or dependency parsing accuracy on available Sanskrit corpora.
6.  **Cultural Contextualization in NLG** – Adapting generated text to fit cultural norms and expressive styles. *Why needed:* Literal translations often lose the spirit, humor, or pedagogical intent of Subhasitas. *Quick check:* Conduct a blind evaluation where native speakers/experts rate outputs for "feeling Indian" or "appropriate for the context."

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:** User Query -> [IndicBERT Retriever] -> Top-k Subhasitas -> [Mistral LLM with Prompt Template] -> Structured Output (Transliteration, Translation, Explanation)

**Critical Path:** The retrieval step is the critical path. If the retriever fails to fetch a relevant verse, the subsequent generation is built on a poor foundation, leading to irrelevant or nonsensical explanations. The LLM's prompt engineering is the second critical dependency for quality.

**Design Tradeoffs:**
- *Corpus Size vs. Curation Cost:* A tiny, expertly curated corpus (200 verses) ensures high quality and manageable annotation but limits semantic coverage and retrieval robustness for diverse queries.
- *Model Choice (IndicBERT vs. Others):* IndicBERT offers good Indic language coverage but may be outperformed by newer multilingual models (e.g., LaBSE) or fine-tuned smaller models, trading off performance for simplicity.
- *LLM Control:* Using a generalist LLM (Mistral) allows flexible prompting but makes it harder to guarantee consistent stylistic or philosophical framing compared to a fine-tuned, domain-specific model.

**Failure Signatures:**
- **Retrieval Failure:** Query returns no relevant verses (low recall) or returns top-k verses that are only superficially related (low precision). Manifested as LLM generating explanations for an irrelevant quote.
- **Generation Failure:** LLM produces a fluent but factually incorrect translation, a transliteration with diacritic errors, or an explanation that is generic, culturally tone-deaf, or contradicts the verse's traditional meaning.
- **Systemic Failure:** Performance degrades sharply for metaphorical or philosophical queries versus literal ones, indicating a lack of true conceptual understanding in the embedding space.

**First Three Experiments to Run:**
1.  **Scaling Experiment:** Expand the corpus incrementally (200 -> 1000 -> 5000 verses) from diverse sources (Hitopadesha, Panchatantra, etc.) and measure the change in (a) retrieval precision@k and (b) LLM explanation quality (via automated metrics like BLEU against expert translations and human evaluation).
2.  **Multilingual Query Benchmark:** Create a test set of identical Subhasita queries in English, Hindi, and a non-Indic language (e.g., Spanish). Evaluate retrieval performance (IndicBERT) and final output satisfaction across language groups to quantify cross-lingual robustness.
3.  **Retriever Ablation:** Replace IndicBERT with BM25 (keyword), mBERT, and a sentence-transformer model (e.g., all-MiniLM-L6-v2) in the identical pipeline. Compare final system performance (precision, user satisfaction) to isolate the contribution of the specific semantic retrieval model.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What dataset size and source diversity are optimal for semantic retrieval of Subhasitas, and how can authoritative curation be ensured at scale?
- **Basis in paper:** Future Work states: "Scaling the corpus beyond 200 Subh¯as.itas to include thousands of verses across diverse themes... collaborating with Sanskrit scholars to curate high-quality, annotated datasets from authoritative sources like the Hitopadesha, Panchatantra, and various Puranas."
- **Why unresolved:** The current 200-verse dataset limits semantic coverage and retrieval robustness; curating larger sets faces bottlenecks in reliable annotations and source verification.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A expanded dataset (e.g., thousands of verses) with transparent curation protocols, experimental results showing improved retrieval metrics (precision, coverage), and expert validation of thematic and source authenticity.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Which multilingual embedding models and generative adaptations most effectively support cross-lingual Subhasita queries while preserving cultural nuance in outputs?
- **Basis in paper:** Future Work proposes "extending the system to accept and understand queries in additional Indian and global languages" via fine-tuning IndiBERT or using LaBSE/mBERT, and configuring the LLM for multilingual responses.
- **Why unresolved:** The prototype's language support is unspecified and likely limited; multilingual extension requires addressing model selection, fine-tuning data scarcity, and prompt engineering for cultural context transfer.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Comparative benchmarks of embedding models on multilingual query-verse pairs, coupled with user studies assessing translation accuracy, cultural appropriateness, and engagement across diverse language speakers.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How can natural-sounding TTS and robust ASR be engineered for Sanskrit to enhance voice-based interaction and user engagement in Pragya?
- **Basis in paper:** Future Work aims to "incorporate a high-quality Text-to-Speech (TTS) engine... to generate natural-sounding recitations" and "an Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) model tailored for low-resource Indian languages" to fix the current "robotic" voice output.
- **Why unresolved:** Sanskrit lacks large-scale speech corpora, making high-quality TTS/ASR challenging; existing robotic output reduces engagement for emotional or motivational use cases.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Trained TTS/ASR models with objective metrics (e.g., Mean Opinion Score for naturalness, Word Error Rate for ASR), and user evaluations measuring engagement, usability, and preservation of poetic prosody.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What standardized evaluation framework and large-scale methodology are needed to robustly measure Pragya's semantic relevance, cultural appropriateness, and emotional impact across diverse users?
- **Basis in paper:** Future Work calls for "large-scale user studies with diverse demographic groups, including students and scholars" and "A/B testing on different models" to overcome the "small pilot group" and "subjective" current evaluation.
- **Why unresolved:** The existing 5-user pilot lacks generalizability and benchmarks; no established metrics exist for Subhasita recommendation, making it hard to assess real-world impact.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A comprehensive evaluation protocol with diverse participant cohorts, validated metrics for semantic alignment and emotional resonance, and A/B test results comparing retrieval/generation variants across cultural contexts.

## Limitations
- Evaluation based on a very small pilot user group (n=5) with no statistical reporting (confidence intervals, p-values).
- Corpus is extremely limited (200 verses), raising concerns about overfitting and generalizability to the vast Subhasita literature.
- No involvement of domain experts (Sanskrit scholars) in evaluating the cultural or semantic accuracy of outputs; user satisfaction may be biased.
- No error analysis provided for retrieval failures or LLM hallucinations; scalability and computational costs are not addressed.

2.  **Expert Validation:** Conduct a double-blind evaluation with a panel of Sanskrit scholars and native speakers using a rubric assessing semantic accuracy of retrieval, correctness of transliteration/translation, and cultural appropriateness of explanations; measure inter-rater reliability.
3.  **Baseline Benchmarking:** Compare Pragya's end-to-end performance against strong baselines including BM25, dense retrieval with Sentence-BERT, and zero-shot/fine-tuned LLMs (e.g., GPT-4, Claude) in a standardized test suite.
