# Cross-household Transfer Learning Approach with LSTM-based Demand Forecasting

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.14267
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14267
- **Reference count**: 25
- **Primary result**: DELTAiF reduces total LSTM training time by 67% while achieving R² values of 0.874–0.991 and MAPE of 0.001–0.017 for household hot water demand forecasting.

## Executive Summary
The study addresses the computational expense of training separate machine learning models for each household in residential heat pump systems. It introduces DELTAiF, a transfer learning framework that pre‑trains an LSTM on a representative source household and fine‑tunes it for others, combined with an Isolation Forest for shower event detection. Evaluated on six Swedish households, the approach cuts total training time by 67% (from 54 to 17.55 minutes) while maintaining high predictive accuracy. The system generates weekly demand calendars by identifying shower events from model residuals, enabling scalable adaptive hot water production.

## Method Summary
The method uses minute‑resolution mid‑tank temperature (`t_mid`) data. Preprocessing includes forward‑filling missing timestamps, outlier removal, and resampling to 1‑minute intervals. A source household with stable consumption is selected iteratively by evaluating transfer performance across candidates. An LSTM with 50 units is pre‑trained on the source to predict future `t_mid`. The pre‑tained model is then fine‑tuned on each target household’s data. Finally, an Isolation Forest with a fixed 2% contamination rate is applied to the LSTM’s prediction residuals to detect shower events, which are aggregated into weekly calendars.

## Key Results
- Training time reduction: 67% (from 54 minutes to 17.55 minutes total for six households).
- High predictive accuracy: R² values range 0.874–0.991; MAPE range 0.001–0.017.
- Effective shower event detection: Isolation Forest identifies sharp hot water withdrawals, enabling generation of plausible household‑specific weekly demand calendars.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Transfer Learning reduces computational training time**  
*Claim*: TL avoids full retraining per household by initializing target models with weights from a source model, requiring fewer epochs.  
*Mechanism*: Lower LSTM layers capture generic temporal trends (daily cycles, cooling dynamics) shared across households. Transfer via weight initialization accelerates convergence.  
*Core assumption*: Hot water consumption patterns share underlying temporal structure, and a source with stable patterns provides a beneficial prior.  
*Evidence*: Total training time drops from 54 to 17.55 minutes; measurements show 9 min pre‑training and 8.55 min fine‑tuning for five targets. Poor transfer occurs when source is mismatched (e.g., Household 1 as source yields high RMSE).

**Mechanism 2: LSTM effectively models long‑term temperature dependencies**  
*Claim*: The gated LSTM architecture can learn minute‑level temperature dynamics, including gradual cooling and heating recovery.  
*Mechanism*: Forget gates regulate information over many time steps, capturing slow thermal processes. The model uses sliding windows of past `t_mid` to forecast future values.  
*Core assumption*: `t_mid` dynamics are deterministic and driven by past consumption and system physics, with sufficient signal at 1‑minute resolution.  
*Evidence*: Model uses 50 LSTM units; achieved R² up to 0.991 across households, indicating strong fit to test sequences.

**Mechanism 3: Isolation Forest detects shower events from prediction residuals**  
*Claim*: Shower withdrawals cause sharp drops in `t_mid` that appear as anomalies in LSTM prediction residuals; iForest isolates these events without labeled data.  
*Mechanism*: iForest randomly partitions data; points that are easier to isolate (fewer splits) are flagged as outliers. Applied to residuals, large negative values (actual much lower than predicted) correspond to unexpected heat extraction.  
*Core assumption*: Shower events produce statistically aberrant residuals distinct from normal fluctuations, and a fixed contamination parameter (2%) approximates their prevalence.  
*Evidence*: Manual validation confirmed alignment with sharp withdrawals. Generated weekly calendars show plausible peak hours per household.

## Foundational Learning
- **Transfer Learning (Domain Adaptation)**  
  *Why needed*: Core scalability mechanism; without understanding feature reuse across households, the premise of pre‑training and fine‑tuning may not hold.  
  *Quick check*: If a source household has irregular occupancy (e.g., vacation home), is it a good candidate for pre‑training a model for a standard family? Why or why not?

- **Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM) Networks**  
  *Why needed*: The predictor relies on LSTM’s ability to model long intervals between consumption events and avoid vanishing gradients.  
  *Quick check*: How does the forget gate specifically help model the gradual cooling of a hot water tank between uses?

- **Unsupervised Anomaly Detection (Isolation Forest)**  
  *Why needed*: Shower events are unlabeled; iForest provides a way to extract them from model residuals in a robust manner.  
  *Quick check*: Why might iForest be preferred over a simple threshold on residual magnitude for detecting shower events?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Data Prep -> Source Selector -> LSTM Pipeline (Pre‑train -> Fine‑tune) -> Event Detector (iForest) -> Calendar Generator

**Critical path**: Data Prep → Source Selector (executed once to choose optimal source) → Pre‑train LSTM on that source → Fine‑tune the pre‑trained model on each target household’s training data → Run LSTM predictions on test data → Apply iForest to residuals to flag shower events → Aggregate events into weekly calendars.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Fixed contamination (2%) vs adaptive: Fixed simplifies but may mis‑calibrate for households with atypical shower frequencies; adaptive would require ground‑truth labels.
- Computational savings vs accuracy ceiling: TL drastically reduces compute but may limit maximum accuracy if source domain is suboptimal; full retraining could be more accurate but infeasible at scale.
- iForest threshold sensitivity: Lower contamination increases precision but risks missing events; higher values increase recall but may include false positives.

**Failure signatures**:
- High RMSE on fine‑tuned target (e.g., >1.0) indicates poor transfer due to source‑target mismatch.
- Erratic calendar output (e.g., showers at implausible hours or uniformly distributed) suggests iForest parameters or LSTM predictions are flawed.
- Unstable source selection: multiple candidate sources yield similar average target errors, implying no clear best source and marginal TL benefits.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Source Domain Identification**: Perform leave‑one‑out TL (each household as source); compute average target RMSE. Confirm which household is optimal (paper found Household 5). If no clear winner, analyze consumption regularity metrics (e.g., daily usage coefficient of variation).
2. **Contamination Rate Sensitivity**: For a fixed fine‑tuned model (e.g., H5→H1), vary iForest contamination (0.5%, 1%, 2%, 5%). Manually inspect resulting weekly calendars for plausible event timing and count. Choose rate that balances precision and recall.
3. **TL vs. From‑Scratch Benchmark**: For one target household, compare TL (pre‑train + fine‑tune) to training an LSTM from random initialization on its full dataset. Measure final R²/MPAE and wall‑clock time. Verify TL’s 67% saving does not cause significant accuracy degradation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Automated source selection**: The paper notes that source selection currently requires exhaustive evaluation. Future work should develop an automated metric based on consumption pattern stability to predict transferability without costly trials. Resolving this would streamline deployment across many households.
2. **Handling concept drift**: DELTAiF assumes stable consumption patterns over time. Long‑term behavioral changes (e.g., new residents) would degrade model performance. Evidence needed: a drift detection mechanism and incremental fine‑tuning strategy that maintains accuracy with lower compute than full retraining.
3. **Adaptive iForest contamination**: The fixed 2% contamination rate may not be optimal for all households, as shower frequency varies. A data‑driven or household‑specific adjustment could improve detection accuracy. Evidence: sensitivity analysis or an adaptive method that estimates the true anomaly rate.
4. **Generalization to other tasks and settings**: The framework was evaluated only on residential heat pumps for hot water. Its applicability to space heating/cooling or non‑residential buildings is untested. Evidence: adapted implementations and comparable scalability/accuracy gains in those domains.

## Limitations
- **Method details missing**: LSTM input window length, feature encoding (e.g., cyclical time), and fine‑tuning protocol (layers, learning rate) are unspecified, hindering exact reproduction.
- **Generalization constraints**: Optimal source household is dataset‑specific (H5 in the study); performance may degrade for households with divergent patterns (e.g., vacation homes). Fixed iForest contamination (2%) may not scale across varying shower frequencies.
- **Limited external validation**: The evaluation uses only six Swedish households; broader validation across diverse climates and household types is lacking.

## Confidence
- TL time reduction (67%): **High** (directly measured)
- LSTM predictive accuracy (R² 0.874–0.991): **High** (reported per household)
- iForest shower detection (fixed 2%): **Medium** (relies on manual validation; no quantified precision/recall)
