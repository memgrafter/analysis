# Why the Counterintuitive Phenomenon of Likelihood Rarely Appears in Tabular Anomaly Detection with Deep Generative Models?

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.09593  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09593  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Likelihood inversion is rare in tabular anomaly detection; simple likelihood testing with normalizing flows (NF‑SLT) achieves AUROC up to 0.8575 and fail ratio as low as 0.06, thanks to lower data dimensionality and weaker feature correlations.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the counterintuitive failure mode where normalizing flows, popular deep generative models, assign higher likelihood to anomalous images than to normal ones – a phenomenon that questions their suitability for anomaly detection. The authors define a domain‑agnostic criterion to detect this failure and evaluate it extensively on 47 tabular and 10 embedding datasets using 13 baseline models. They find that the phenomenon almost never occurs in tabular data. Their theoretical and empirical analysis identifies two key factors: (1) low ambient dimensionality of tabular datasets reduces the concentration of Euclidean norms in the latent space, preserving the likelihood gap; (2) weaker feature correlations (higher intrinsic‑dimension ratio) in tabular data prevent flows from latching onto spurious correlation patterns that would mislead the likelihood. As a result, simple likelihood testing with a well‑tuned NICE flow (NF‑SLT) consistently outperforms alternative anomaly detection methods.

## Method Summary
NF‑SLT uses a NICE normalizing flow (additive coupling layers, volume‑preserving) trained solely on normal tabular data. The training data are standardized with RobustScaler (except for two baselines that require different scaling). The flow maximizes the log‑likelihood under a standard Gaussian prior. After training, likelihoods on the test set (comprising held‑out normal samples and all anomalies) are computed; lower likelihood indicates an anomaly. The method is evaluated on the ADBench suite (47 datasets) and 10 embedding datasets, using the standard 50%‑normal‑training split and averaging AUROC/AUPRC over 10 random seeds. Hyperparameters are tuned globally to avoid overfitting: 10 coupling layers, MLP subnetworks with hidden size 256, AdamW optimizer with cosine annealing, batch size 512, weight decay 1e‑4, and 200 epochs. Performance is compared against 13 shallow and deep baselines.

## Key Results
- NF‑SLT attains an average AUROC of **0.8575** across the 47 ADBench tabular datasets, with a **fail ratio** (rank ≥ 9) as low as **0.06**, far below competing methods.
- The counterintuitive likelihood inversion occurs in only **2 out of 47** tabular datasets under the strictest definition, demonstrating its rarity.
- Empirical results confirm the theoretical predictions: datasets with higher ambient dimension and lower intrinsic‑dimension ratio (i.e., stronger feature correlations) are more prone to inversion, while typical tabular data (d < 100, d‑Ratio > 0.1) avoid it.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Dimensionality‑driven norm concentration
As the ambient dimension \(d\) increases, the variance of the squared Euclidean norm \(\|\mathbf{z}\|_2^2\) of the latent vector \(\mathbf{z}=f(\mathbf{x})\) (which ideally follows \(\mathcal{N}(0,I_d)\)) shrinks relative to \(d\). This concentration makes the latent norms of normal and anomalous data nearly identical under slight model misestimimation, causing the likelihood gap \(\mathbb{E}_{P}[\log p_\theta] - \mathbb{E}_{Q}[\log p_\theta]\) to become negative and scale linearly with \(d\) under certain entropy conditions (Theorem 5.4). Consequently, the AUROC upper bound decays with \(d\) (Corollary 5.6). Tabular datasets, with their relatively low dimensionality (\(d < 100\) typically), are less affected by this concentration.

### Mechanism 2: Weak feature correlations yield high intrinsic dimension
Strong feature correlations reduce the intrinsic dimension (ID) relative to ambient dimension, yielding a low \(d\)-Ratio (intrinsic/ambient). Images exhibit extremely low \(d\)-Ratio (∼0.002) because of homogeneous local correlations; normalizing flows, especially with convolutional biases, then overfit to these spurious correlation patterns and assign higher likelihood to out‑of‑distribution (OOD) images that share similar correlations. Tabular data, however, feature heterogeneous attributes and generally have weak correlations, resulting in a high \(d\)-Ratio (typically > 0.1). The flow therefore models the marginal distributions more faithfully and does not produce misleading likelihoods.

### Mechanism 3: Joint effect
The combination of low ambient dimension and weak correlations is what makes NF‑SLT robust. High dimension amplifies any negative likelihood gap, while strong correlations create structured dependencies that flows can inadvertently use as spurious cues. Tabular data avoid both pitfalls: low dimension keeps norm‑concentration effects minimal, and weak correlations prevent the flow from focusing on misleading cues. Real‑world tabular benchmarks therefore rarely exhibit the likelihood inversion phenomenon.

## Foundational Learning
- **Normalizing flows and change‑of‑variables**  
  *Why needed:* The paper’s analysis hinges on the flow’s log‑likelihood formula \(\log p(\mathbf{x}) = \log p(\mathbf{z}) + \log|\det \partial\mathbf{z}/\partial\mathbf{x}|\). Understanding the Jacobian determinant’s role explains how volume‑preserving (e.g., NICE) versus volume‑changing (e.g., RealNVP) architectures affect modeling capacity and stability.  
  *Quick check:* How does the absence of a Jacobian term in a pure autoregressive model change its likelihood behavior compared to a flow with coupling layers?

- **Intrinsic vs. ambient dimension**  
  *Why needed:* Intrinsic dimension (ID) serves as a proxy for feature correlation strength. A low ID relative to ambient dimension indicates data lie on a low‑dimensional manifold with strong correlations, which, as shown in Section 5.2, correlates with likelihood inversion.  
  *Quick check:* If a dataset has ambient dimension \(d=500\) and estimated ID \(d'=50\), what is the \(d\)-Ratio, and what does it imply about feature correlations?

- **Anomaly detection evaluation metrics**  
  *Why needed:* The paper uses AUROC and AUPRC as primary metrics. Understanding AUROC as the probability that a randomly chosen anomaly is ranked lower (less likely) than a random normal sample is key to interpreting the “counterintuitive phenomenon” (Definition 3.3), which requires not just low AUROC but also a significant gap relative to baselines.  
  *Quick check:* If all models on a dataset achieve AUROC ≈ 0.6, does the phenomenon necessarily occur? Why not?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Tabular data → RobustScaler → NICE flow (10 additive coupling layers) → Latent Gaussian \(\mathcal{N}(0,I)\) → Log‑likelihood score (lower = more anomalous)

**Critical path:**  
1. Preprocess each dataset with RobustScaler (fit on training normals).  
2. Train NICE flow on the normal training split (50 % of normal samples) to maximize log‑likelihood.  
3. After training, compute log‑likelihood for each test sample (remaining normals + all anomalies).  
4. Rank test samples by ascending likelihood; lower scores indicate anomalies.  
5. Compute AUROC/AUPRC against ground‑truth labels; repeat 10 times with different seeds and average.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *NICE vs. RealNVP:* NICE’s additive coupling is simpler, volume‑preserving, and yielded best results in the ADBench grid. RealNVP offers affine coupling (more expressive) but can suffer from numerical instability and overfitting on small tabular datasets.  
- *Global vs. per‑dataset hyperparameters:* Global hyperparameters (the same for all datasets) were selected to avoid selection bias; per‑dataset tuning brings marginal gains (Table 16) but risks overfitting and reduces comparability.  
- *Flow depth:* Increasing coupling layers enhances capacity but may overfit when data are scarce; the chosen 10 layers balanced performance and stability.  
- *Activation choice:* The paper notes that ReLU in coupling subnetworks can lead to latent norm convergence in synthetic high‑dimensional experiments; using tanh may mitigate this (Appendix C.3).

**Failure signatures:**  
- High “fail ratio” (≥ 0.3) per Definition 3.3 across many datasets.  
- AUROC near 0.5 while simple shallow baselines (e.g., PCA, OCSVM) achieve significantly better scores.  
- Performance degrades sharply when ambient dimension increases (e.g., via PCA on InternetAds).  
- Training instability (NaNs, exploding gradients) especially with high dimensionality or aggressive learning rates.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Replicate Table 1:** Implement NICE with global hyperparameters, run 10 seeds on all 47 ADBench tabular datasets, verify mean AUROC ≈ 0.8575 and fail ratio ≤ 0.06.  
2. **Dimensionality sensitivity:** On InternetAds (d = 1555), apply PCA to retain 100 %, 95 %, 90 %, 85 %, 80 %, 75 % variance, retrain NF‑SLT for each reduced dimension, and plot AUROC versus retained dimension. Expect performance to improve as dimension decreases.  
3. **Intrinsic dimension correlation check:** Estimate d‑Ratio for a typical tabular dataset (e.g., `magicgamma`) and an image dataset (e.g., CIFAR‑10 features) using TwoNN; confirm tabular d‑Ratio > 0.4 while image d‑Ratio is ∼ 0.002.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Q1:** Can normalizing flow architectures be designed to better capture semantic information in tabular data, thus improving performance on datasets where NF‑SLT is not top‑ranked?
- **Q2:** Do the theoretical findings (dimensionality and correlation effects) extend to high‑dimensional, strongly correlated tabular data (e.g., genomics), where the likelihood inversion might reappear?
- **Q3:** Why do latent norms from flows with ReLU‑like activations become nearly identical for normal and anomalous distributions in high‑dimensional synthetic experiments, and does this phenomenon occur in real tabular data?

## Limitations
- **Exact dataset/split details** are missing, preventing straightforward replication of the 47‑dataset benchmark.
- **Theoretical assumptions** (independent features, entropy gap condition) may not hold for real‑world tabular data with dependencies.
- **Intrinsic‑dimension estimation** is noisy, especially on small tabular datasets; the robustness of the reported d‑Ratio thresholds to estimator choice remains uncertain.

## Confidence
- **Claim A** (Dimensionality‑driven likelihood inversion): **High** – strong theoretical backing and synthetic experiments.
- **Claim B** (Weaker feature correlations in tabular data): **Medium** – supported by ID estimates but relies on noisy proxies.
- **Claim C** (NF‑SLT avoids the phenomenon and outperforms baselines): **High** – consistent results across 47 datasets with low variance.
- **Claim D** (Joint effect of low dimension and weak correlations): **Medium** – empirical correlation present but causal disentanglement incomplete.
- **Claim E** (Generalizability beyond ADBench): **Low** – no evaluation on out‑of‑distribution or high‑dimensional correlated tabular domains.
