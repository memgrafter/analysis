# SAGE: Scalable AI Governance & Evaluation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.07840  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07840  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: A closed-loop governance system that distills a calibrated LLM judge into a production model, reducing evaluation cost by 92× while maintaining human-aligned relevance scoring and driving a 0.25% DAU lift at LinkedIn.

## Executive Summary
SAGE bridges the governance gap between nuanced human oversight and high-throughput production search evaluation. It operationalizes human judgment via a bidirectional calibration loop where natural‑language Policy, curated Precedent, and an LLM Surrogate Judge co‑evolve to resolve ambiguities and create an interpretable multi‑dimensional rubric. Teacher‑student distillation then compresses the calibrated judge into a low‑cost Student Judge that achieves substantial human agreement (linear κ 0.72–0.73). Deployed at LinkedIn for job/people search, SAGE accelerated offline evaluation from two weeks to three days, detected relevance regressions missed by engagement metrics, and increased daily active users.

## Method Summary
SAGE integrates three stages: (1) **Bidirectional calibration**: Human experts curate a small Precedent set; a frontier LLM Teacher Judge applies a natural‑language Policy. Disagreements between Judge and Precedent, or among humans, trigger updates to Policy and Precedent via four feedback vectors, iterating until Teacher–Human κ stabilizes (~0.77). Relevance is decomposed into orthogonal graded attributes (e.g., Title, Seniority) for auditability. (2) **Distillation**: The calibrated Teacher labels a large stratified corpus (312K examples). Full‑parameter fine‑tuning of an 8B model yields a Student Judge with near‑teacher alignment (κ ≥ 0.72). (3) **Online compression**: A second distillation prunes the Student into an ultra‑compact Online Model (<0.6B) for millisecond‑latency serving in the ranking pipeline.

## Key Results
- 92× lower evaluation cost via teacher–student distillation while preserving human alignment (linear κ 0.72–0.73).  
- Offline evaluation runtime reduced from 2 weeks to 3 days; production monitoring detected relevance regressions invisible to engagement metrics.  
- Deployed at LinkedIn, driving a 0.25% lift in daily active users.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: SAGE operationalizes human judgment through a bidirectional feedback loop that co‑evolves Policy, Precedent, and the LLM Surrogate Judge to resolve ambiguities and align evaluation with product intent.  
- **Mechanism**: Human experts annotate a curated Precedent set. An initial Teacher Judge (frontier LLM) applies the natural‑language Policy. Disagreements between the Judge and Precedent, or between human annotators, are systematically analyzed. Judge disagreements with human labels trigger adversarial audits to correct Precedent errors. Human annotator disagreements on specific query patterns indicate Policy underspecification, prompting explicit Policy updates. This creates four feedback vectors (Human→Policy, Human→Human, Judge→Precedent, Judge→Policy) that iteratively refine all three components.  
- **Core assumption**: Human product judgment, while nuanced and initially heterogeneous, can be convergent and codified into an executable rubric through structured iteration and conflict resolution.  
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - Abstract: “bidirectional calibration loop where natural‑language Policy, curated Precedent, and an LLM Surrogate Judge co‑evolve.”  
  - Section 4.2: Describes the four feedback vectors and their interventions (e.g., “Judge→Precedent Corrected human labeling errors”).  
- **Break condition**: If feedback vectors fail to produce converging agreement (e.g., κ plateaus below threshold) or Policy updates become excessively frequent without stabilization, the calibration loop may not resolve fundamental ambiguity, indicating the judgment task may be inherently non‑convergent or Policy scope too broad.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Decomposing relevance into orthogonal, graded attributes (e.g., Title, Seniority) creates an interpretable rubric that eliminates black‑box opacity, enables targeted failure attribution during calibration, and translates abstract quality thresholds into executable suppression rules.  
- **Mechanism**: Instead of a monolithic score, SAGE evaluates multiple relevance dimensions independently. A weighted heuristic combines these into a final 5‑point grade (0–4). This structure lets clinicians isolate which attribute (e.g., “Person Name” matching) causes a disagreement during feedback cycles. It also allows product policy to define clear thresholds (e.g., grades 0–1 trigger automatic suppression).  
- **Core assumption**: Complex, multi‑faceted relevance judgments can be reliably broken down into a set of simpler, orthogonal sub‑judgments without significant loss of holistic validity.  
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - Section 4.1 Decision 1: “we decompose relevance into orthogonal attributes… This composite approach allows us to align the final score with human intuition by strictly prioritizing the signals users care about most.”  
  - Section 6.2: Formalizes metrics (Good Recall, Poor Match Rate) based on these attribute‑derived grades.  
- **Break condition**: If attributes become highly correlated in practice (e.g., a poor Title match always correlates with poor Seniority match), the decomposition loses diagnostic value. Alternatively, if stakeholders cannot agree on the weighting heuristic or orthogonal attribute definitions, the rubric fails to translate policy into actionable scores.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Teacher‑student knowledge distillation compresses the high‑cost, high‑latency frontier LLM Teacher Judge into a production‑scale Student Judge that maintains substantial human alignment (κ ≥ 0.7) while enabling massive throughput (>10⁷ labels/day).  
- **Mechanism**: A calibrated Teacher Judge (GPT‑o3/GPT‑5) generates graded judgments on a large, stratified corpus of production and aspirational queries. This dataset fully fine‑tunes a smaller open‑source 8B parameter model (the Student). Key optimizations include rebalancing underrepresented score classes and reusing the exact prompt structure/output schema. Further distillation into an ultra‑compact “Online Model” for real‑time serving uses structural pruning, reasoning internalization, and context minimization.  
- **Core assumption**: The reasoning patterns and judgment heuristics of a high‑fidelity frontier model can be transferred via supervised fine‑tuning to a smaller architecture without catastrophic loss of alignment, provided the teacher’s outputs are high‑quality and the training data is sufficiently diverse and balanced.  
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - Abstract: “teacher–student distillation… achieving 92× lower cost while maintaining substantial human agreement (0.72–0.73 linear kappa).”  
  - Section 5.2: Details training data construction (312K examples), full‑parameter fine‑tuning, and cost comparison table.  
  - Section 6.1: Describes the second distillation stage into the “Online Model” for <10ms latency.  
- **Break condition**: If the Student’s linear κ against human precedent falls below the ~0.7 production threshold, or if its performance on edge cases (F1‑Poor‑) degrades significantly compared to the Teacher, the distillation has not preserved critical judgment fidelity, likely due to insufficiently representative training data or architectural capacity limits.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: The Governance Gap**  
  - **Why needed**: SAGE is explicitly designed to solve the mismatch between resource‑constrained, nuanced human oversight and the high‑throughput demands of production search evaluation. Understanding this gap explains why engagement proxies and sparse manual review are insufficient.  
  - **Quick check**: Why do engagement metrics (e.g., clicks, applies) fail to capture certain high‑impact relevance failures that a human policy judge would catch?

- **Concept: Bidirectional Calibration**  
  - **Why needed**: This is the core process that transforms static, brittle evaluation into a dynamic, self‑correcting system. It redefines the relationship between humans (policy/precedent) and the LLM judge from unidirectional teaching to mutual refinement.  
  - **Quick check**: What are the four feedback vectors in SAGE’s loop, and what specific problem does each one address (e.g., Judge→Precedent corrects what?)?

- **Concept: Distillation for Scale**  
  - **Why needed**: Enables the transition from an expensive, slow Teacher Judge suitable for calibration to a cheap, fast Student Judge usable for daily development and monitoring. Without this, the calibrated judge remains a research artifact, not an industrial primitive.  
  - **Quick check**: What are the two main stages of distillation described (Teacher→Student, Student→Online Model), and what distinct operational need does each stage address?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Policy (P) -> Precedent (E) -> Teacher Judge (J_teacher) -> Student Judge (J_student) -> Online Model (J_online) -> Simulation & Evaluation Pipeline -> Production Monitoring

- **Critical path**: Policy draft → Expert curation of Precedent → Initial Teacher Judge scoring → Disagreement analysis → (Policy/Precedent updates) → Repeat until Teacher‑Human κ ≥ 0.7 → Generate large‑scale Teacher labels → Distill to Student Judge → Validate Student‑Human κ ≥ 0.7 → Deploy Student for offline candidate selection & A/B test scoring → Distill Student to Online Model → Deploy Online Model as ranking gate. Feedback from Online Model monitoring can restart the loop.

- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - Expert curation vs. Scale: Small, high‑quality Precedent enables rapid iteration but may miss long‑tail patterns. Mitigated by using the Teacher to surface edge cases for expert review.  
  - Decomposition vs. Holistic Matching: Graded attributes improve auditability but may struggle with context‑dependent “soft” matches. Policy heuristics (weighting) attempt to synthesize attributes holistically.  
  - Full fine‑tuning vs. LoRA: Full tuning gave higher stability for graded judgments but was more computationally expensive.  
  - Online Model latency vs. Fidelity: Reasoning internalization and context compression sacrifice some nuanced reasoning for millisecond latency.

- **Failure signatures**:  
  - Calibration stage: Stagnant or declining linear κ between Teacher and Human experts after several loop iterations; persistent high Human→Human disagreement on core attributes.  
  - Distillation stage: Student‑Human κ < 0.7, especially on F1‑Poor‑; large gap between Teacher‑Student and Student‑Human agreement.  
  - Production stage: Sudden, unexplained shifts in policy‑based metrics (e.g., PMR@10) not correlated with known model changes; divergence between policy metrics and engagement proxies indicating a “silent” regression.

- **First 3 experiments**:  
  1. **Bidirectional Calibration Iteration**: Take a batch of 50 contested query‑document pairs from current Precedent. Run Teacher Judge and record disagreements. Conduct adversarial audit: for each Judge rejection, check if it correctly identified evidence human missed. For each human rejection of Judge, debate if Policy was misapplied or underspecified. Update Precedent and/or Policy accordingly. Measure change in Teacher‑Human κ on a held‑out validation set.  
  2. **Student Distillation Ablation**: Train three Student variants on the same 312K dataset: (a) Baseline full fine‑tune, (b) LoRA fine‑tune, (c) Full fine‑tune with augmented class balance. Compare linear κ, F1‑Good+, and F1‑Poor‑ against Human Precedent. This validates the key choices in Section 5.3.  
  3. **Offline Candidate Selection Simulation**: Sample 10k historical real‑user queries. Score outputs from a Candidate Model A and a baseline Model B using the deployed Student Judge. Compute GR@10 and PMR@10. Verify that a known problematic model variant (e.g., one with reduced recall on “foreign language” queries) shows a statistically significant GR@10 drop. This tests the pipeline’s ability to catch a pre‑defined regression before online launch.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1**: Can the teacher–student distillation process be further optimized to more closely approach the teacher's alignment with expert human judges, given the observed gap between student (0.72–0.73 linear κ) and teacher (0.76–0.77) performance?  
  - **Basis**: The paper establishes teacher–human alignment as the effective upper bound for student performance but does not explore whether alternative distillation objectives or architectures could reduce this gap.  
  - **Why unresolved**: The distillation approach uses standard supervised fine‑tuning with a fixed 8B model; it is unclear if more advanced techniques (e.g., logic‑based distillation, process reward modeling) could transfer more of the teacher’s nuanced reasoning.  
  - **Resolving evidence**: Ablation studies comparing different distillation losses or student architectures, measuring student–teacher agreement and student–human agreement on a held‑out set of ambiguous precedents.

- **Question 2**: To what extent does SAGE’s decomposed relevance rubric (e.g., Title, Seniority) generalize to search domains with fundamentally different attribute structures (e.g., e‑commerce, where price or brand may dominate)?  
  - **Basis**: SAGE’s attributes are tailored to professional search intents. The principle of decomposability is advocated, but its applicability to domains with non‑orthogonal or qualitative attributes is not evaluated.  
  - **Why unresolved**: The success of decomposition relies on attributes being interpretable and independently scoreable; in other domains, attributes may be highly correlated or subjective (e.g., “style” in fashion), risking rubric brittleness.  
  - **Resolving evidence**: A replication study deploying SAGE in a different vertical, defining a new attribute rubric, and measuring calibration loop efficacy and judge agreement compared to a monolithic evaluation.

- **Question 3**: What are the operational limits of the bidirectional calibration loop as policy complexity and update frequency increase, and can the reliance on expert curation be partially automated without degrading alignment?  
  - **Basis**: The paper notes the precedent set is “small, curated” and updated within hours, but scalability of this human‑in‑the‑loop process as policies multiply is not addressed.  
  - **Why unresolved**: The loop’s agility depends on rapid expert review; scaling to dozens of product areas or frequent policy churn could create a bottleneck. Automation (e.g., LLM‑assisted precedent filtering) risks introducing noise.  
  - **Resolving evidence**: Longitudinal study tracking calibration velocity (time per iteration) vs. policy version count and domain count; experiment with semi‑automated disagreement triaging, measuring impact on final human–judge agreement.

- **Question 4**: Does SAGE’s reliance on a compact expert‑curated precedent set compromise evaluation fidelity on truly novel, long‑tail queries that fall outside the precedent distribution, and how can coverage be safely expanded?  
  - **Basis**: Precedent is intentionally small for agility, and student judge training uses stratified sampling to broaden coverage. The paper claims “comprehensive coverage” via distillation but does not prove robustness to out‑of‑distribution queries.  
  - **Why unresolved**: A small precedent may not capture the full semantic diversity of production queries; the student judge might overfit to precedent patterns and fail on genuinely new intent formulations.  
  - **Resolving evidence**: Controlled injection of synthetic or real long‑tail queries into the evaluation pipeline, measuring degradation in student–human agreement and correlation with downstream DAU lift compared to head queries.

## Limitations
- **Undisclosed implementation specifics**: Exact LinkedIn search policy rubrics, attribute definitions (Title, Seniority), and weighting heuristics are unreleased, limiting reproducibility and cross‑domain transfer.  
- **Distillation hyperparameters**: Critical training details (learning rate, class balancing, regularization) and Online Model compression techniques (“reasoning internalization”) are not specified, hindering exact replication of the reported 92× cost savings.  
- **Generalizability concerns**: Evidence is confined to LinkedIn’s Job/People Search; it is unclear if the bidirectional loop converges for inherently ambiguous tasks or different domains (e.g., e‑commerce).

## Confidence
- **Bidirectional Calibration Mechanism**: **Medium**. Paper provides internal empirical evidence (κ improvements) but lacks external validation; corpus shows limited direct parallels.  
- **Attribute Decomposition**: **Medium**. Supported by internal diagnostics but orthogonality assumptions and holistic synthesis via weighting are not stress‑tested against alternative rubrics.  
- **Distillation Efficacy**: **High**. Strong internal results (κ ≥ 0.72) and alignment with known distillation literature; cost/latency gains are concretely measured.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross‑Domain Calibration Replication**: Apply SAGE’s loop to a new, distinct search domain (e.g., academic paper search) using a small expert Precedent set (n=200). Test if Teacher‑Human κ converges past 0.7 within five iterations, probing generalizability.  
2. **Distillation Fidelity Ablation**: Train Student Judges with systematic variations: (a) remove class rebalancing, (b) use LoRA instead of full fine‑tuning, (c) reduce training data to 50K examples. Measure linear κ drop against the reported 0.72 baseline to identify critical factors.  
3. **Metric‑Engagement Discrepancy Audit**: On a live traffic slice, compare SAGE’s PMR@10 alerts against traditional engagement metrics (CTR, apply rate). Quantify the fraction of SAGE‑flagged regressions that engagement metrics miss, validating the “invisible failures” claim.
