# Reasoning Gets Harder for LLMs Inside A Dialogue

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.20133
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20133
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Reasoning performance degrades in task-oriented dialogue; average scores drop from ~0.9 in isolated settings to ~0.5-0.7 in dialogue (p<0.001).

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how reasoning performance in large language models (LLMs) degrades when tasks are embedded within task-oriented dialogues compared to isolated settings. To enable systematic evaluation, the authors introduce BOULDER, a dynamic benchmark comprising eight travel-domain reasoning tasks (covering arithmetic, spatial, and temporal reasoning) in both isolated and dialogue formats. They evaluate eight state-of-the-art LLMs and find a substantial and consistent performance drop across models: average scores decline from approximately 0.9 in the baseline isolated condition to between 0.5 and 0.7 in the dialogue condition (statistically significant, p<0.001). Through careful ablations, the study identifies the multi-turn nature of dialogue as the primary driver of this degradation, with additional negative contributions from role conditioning (assigning a travel assistant persona) and tool-use requirements. Notably, even the largest proprietary models suffer these drops, and dialogue responses are significantly shorter, suggesting that the conversational context fundamentally alters reasoning behavior. This work underscores the necessity of evaluating LLM reasoning in realistic, interactive scenarios rather than relying solely on isolated benchmarks.

## Method Summary
BOULDER is a dynamic benchmark built from the MultiWOZ database, conversation templates, and an LLM (Claude Sonnet) for paraphrasing, generating 100 examples per task in two formats: isolated (JSON data + question) and dialogue (multi-turn history with tool calls). Seven tool functions (e.g., search_restaurants, buy_train_tickets) are used. Models are evaluated zero-shot with greedy decoding. An LLM-based parser (Qwen3 30B MoE) extracts answers from responses; these are compared to ground truth using task-specific metrics (accuracy, precision, MAE). Prediction-powered inference (PPI) corrects parser bias using a human-validated sample, and statistical significance is assessed via t-tests. Ablations modify prompts or dialogue structure to isolate factors such as role, tool presence, and turn count.

## Key Results
- Reasoning scores drop significantly in dialogue: from ~0.9 (isolated) to 0.5-0.7 (dialogue), p<0.001.
- Multi-turn interaction is the main cause; single-turn dialogue or merging turns recovers much of the performance.
- Role conditioning and tool-use requirements further impair reasoning, with shorter responses observed in dialogue settings.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Multi-turn disruption
The multi-turn nature of task-oriented dialogue significantly reduces LLM reasoning performance. Models must maintain context and generate incremental responses, which disrupts chain-of-thought reasoning and leads to shorter, less explicit reasoning traces. The core assumption is that LLMs have limited capacity to preserve intermediate reasoning states across turns. Evidence includes ablations showing that adding turns to isolated settings decreases scores, while merging dialogue turns increases scores. Convergent evidence from neighboring work on multi-turn evaluation supports this. Potential mitigation: models that can maintain explicit reasoning traces across turns (e.g., via external memory) or tasks that are decomposable per turn may avoid degradation.

### Mechanism 2: Role conditioning bias
Assigning a travel assistant persona induces a conversational style bias that reduces reasoning depth. Role instructions prime models to generate brief, friendly responses, suppressing step-by-step reasoning and leading to premature conclusions. This relies on LLMs' sensitivity to role specifications, allowing them to shift generation style even when it conflicts with task demands. Evidence: qualitative analysis shows markedly shorter dialogue responses; ablations where dialogue role is added to baseline cause significant drops for half the models; adding reasoning instructions to dialogue barely helps, indicating strong bias. Related work on persona bias corroborates this. The bias may be reduced if role instructions explicitly prioritize reasoning (e.g., "think step by step") and the model is trained to obey such overrides.

### Mechanism 3: Tool-use interference
Requirements to generate and handle structured tool calls add cognitive load that interferes with reasoning. Understanding schemas and parsing tool responses increases context length and complexity, diverting attention from the core reasoning task. This assumes finite context window and attention capacity, so extra non-reasoning content reduces resources for reasoning. Evidence: the "Dialogue without tools" ablation shows performance improvements across models. The "Dialogue with reduced domains" ablation had inconsistent effects, suggesting tool complexity, not just number of domains, is the key factor. Work on tool-integration challenges in LLMs also aligns. Interference may be mitigated by simplifying tool interactions or delegating tool calls to a separate module.

## Foundational Learning
- **Task-oriented dialogue (TOD)**: Why needed? The paper's core comparison is between isolated reasoning and reasoning embedded in TOD, which is multi-turn, goal-driven, and involves tool use. Quick check: What distinguishes a task-oriented dialogue from a general conversation?
- **Ablation studies**: Why needed? The paper uses ablations to isolate factors causing performance gaps (multi-turn, role, tools). Understanding ablations is key to causal inference. Quick check: How does a "Dialogue without tools" ablation help identify the effect of tool use?
- **Dynamic benchmark construction**: Why needed? BOULDER is dynamic to avoid data contamination; static benchmarks risk being memorized. Quick check: Why might a static benchmark be contaminated, and how does dynamic generation mitigate it?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Data generator → Dialogue system → Evaluator → Metrics calculator → Statistical analyzer

**Critical path:**
1. Generate test data (100 examples per task) with both isolated and dialogue prompts.
2. Run target LLMs in specified settings (baseline, dialogue, dialogue-concise, ablations) to collect responses.
3. Parse responses using appropriate parser to extract answer values.
4. Compare extracted values with ground truth to compute raw metrics.
5. Apply PPI bias correction using human-annotated parser accuracy samples.
6. Aggregate metrics across tasks and models, perform statistical significance testing.
7. For ablations, regenerate examples with modified prompts/histories and repeat steps 2–6.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Dynamic generation vs static benchmark: Dynamic avoids contamination but requires more computation and careful validation.
- LLM-based parsers vs rule-based: Parsers handle variability but introduce extraction noise; mitigated by bias correction and human validation.
- Quantized models: Used for efficiency in open-weight models; may affect absolute scores but within-model comparisons remain valid.
- Single end-to-end LLM vs modular pipeline: Chosen for realism and simplicity, but may not be optimal for each reasoning subtype.

**Failure signatures:**
- Parser errors: sudden drops in metric consistency; validate with human annotation.
- Model refusal: outputs like "I don't have access", especially in dialogue settings; track refusal rates.
- Short responses: abrupt reduction in response length in dialogue settings correlates with performance drops.
- Systematic spatial errors: misinterpretation of coordinate systems (e.g., y-axis direction) in directional tasks.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Main replication:** Run all 8 LLMs on baseline, dialogue, and dialogue-concise settings across all 8 tasks (100 examples each) to reproduce Figure 2 and verify pipeline integrity.
2. **Parser validation:** Conduct human evaluation on a random sample (e.g., 30 examples per task per setup) to confirm parser extraction accuracy meets >95% threshold.
3. **Ablation verification:** Execute the single-turn dialogue ablation for at least 3 models to confirm the multi-turn effect; expect significant performance increase over full dialogue.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**What is the primary factor causing the performance degradation when tool use is required in dialogue settings:** the dual role of generating both responses and tool calls, or the extended context length from tool schemas? The paper states this remains a question for future work. Ablations show removing tools helps, but they did not disentangle role from context length. Evidence that could resolve: an experiment where the dialogue setup includes tools but separates the roles (e.g., separate modules) or varies context length independently while keeping role constant.

### Open Question 2
**Does the observed performance gap between isolated and dialogue reasoning tasks generalize to domains beyond travel (e.g., medical advice, financial planning), and does the magnitude of the gap correlate with domain complexity?** The study is limited to travel domains, per the Limitations. It is unclear whether similar degradation occurs in other domains with different reasoning demands. Resolving requires replicating the BOULDER evaluation framework in other high-stakes domains (e.g., medical diagnosis, legal reasoning, finance) and comparing gaps.

### Open Question 3
**To what extent can few-shot prompting or specialized fine-tuning mitigate the reasoning performance drop in dialogue settings, and which approach is most effective?** Experiments were zero-shot; the impact of few-shot prompting or fine-tuning was not investigated. Resolving requires systematic experiments adding few-shot examples (with/without reasoning traces) to the prompt, or fine-tuning models on dialogue-embedded reasoning tasks, and measuring changes in the isolated-vs-dialogue gap.

### Open Question 4
**Why do LLMs generate significantly shorter responses in dialogue settings even without explicit brevity instructions, and how does this reduced output length contribute to reasoning errors?** The paper observes shorter responses in dialogue correlating with performance drops, but the causal link is unexplained. Evidence to resolve: an analysis correlating response length with correctness across models and settings, and experiments that explicitly vary response length constraints (e.g., mandating long vs. short outputs) to see if length alone accounts for the gap.

## Limitations
- The human validation set for PPI bias correction is unreleased; final scores may still reflect parser noise.
- Dynamic benchmark generation lacks fixed random seeds; test-set distribution and difficulty may vary across runs.
- Tool-calling format compliance varies across models, and the pipeline's robustness to deviations is not fully documented.
