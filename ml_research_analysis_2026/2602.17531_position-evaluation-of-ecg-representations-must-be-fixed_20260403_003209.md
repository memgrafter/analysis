# Position: Evaluation of ECG Representations Must Be Fixed

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.17531
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17531
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Current ECG representation evaluation over-relies on three arrhythmia benchmarks and macro‑AUROC, ignoring broader clinical tasks and label imbalance; best practices include expanded downstream evaluation, per‑task AUROC/AUPRC with uncertainty, exclusion of low‑support labels, and a randomly initialized encoder baseline.

## Executive Summary
The paper argues that ECG representation evaluation is flawed due to narrow benchmark selection and an overreliance on macro‑AUROC. A survey of 28 recent papers reveals that 89.29% use macro‑AUROC as the headline metric and 35.71% omit per‑label results, which masks performance on clinically crucial endpoints. Empirical comparisons of four encoders across six tasks show that a random baseline often matches or exceeds pretrained models on standard arrhythmia benchmarks, and rankings shift dramatically when adding structural disease, hemodynamic, and forecasting tasks. The authors呼吁 adopting best practices: report per‑task metrics with confidence intervals, exclude low‑support labels, include random baselines, and evaluate on a wider array of clinical endpoints.

## Method Summary
The authors compare four ECG encoders (CLOCS, MERL, D‑BETA, and a randomly initialized CNN) using linear probing across six downstream tasks: arrhythmia classification on PTB‑XL, CPSC2018, and CSN; echocardiographic prediction (EchoNext); hemodynamic state inference; and 1‑year heart failure forecasting. Each task uses a separate logistic regression classifier trained on frozen encoder embeddings. Performance is measured with AUROC and AUPRC, aggregated via macro‑averaging. 95% confidence intervals are obtained through 1000 bootstrap resamples of the test set, and labels with fewer than 10 positive examples are sometimes excluded to reduce noise. Experiments are repeated at 1%, 10%, and 100% training fractions to assess data efficiency.

## Key Results
- The random baseline matches or exceeds CLOCS on standard arrhythmia benchmarks and remains competitive on structural and forecasting tasks, questioning the assumed value of pretraining.
- Macro‑AUROC rankings change substantially when low‑support labels are removed, per‑task metrics with uncertainty are reported, and new clinical targets are added, indicating instability in current leaderboards.
- MERL and D‑BETA sometimes outperform random on specific tasks (e.g., 1‑year heart failure), but no single representation consistently dominates across all six evaluation settings.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Macro‑AUROC obscures label‑specific performance
Macro‑AUROC equally weights each label’s AUROC, giving disproportionate influence to extremely rare labels with high variance and hiding how models behave on clinically important endpoints. This can produce misleading method rankings. Reporting per‑label metrics with confidence intervals and using AUPRC for imbalanced tasks mitigates the issue. Survey data shows 89.29% of papers rely solely on macro‑AUROC, exacerbating the problem.

### Mechanism 2: Random initialization is a surprisingly strong baseline
Random convolutional filters capture generic temporal and spectral patterns that suffice for many arrhythmia detection tasks. Pre‑training objectives emphasizing arrhythmia invariances offer inconsistent gains, and the random baseline often falls within the 95% CI of pretrained models. Its performance improves further when low‑support labels are removed, suggesting much of the earlier deficit was due to noise.

### Mechanism 3: Different downstream tasks probe distinct ECG information
Structural disease and forecasting rely on ECG features beyond rhythm and waveform morphology, such as signs of remodeling and long‑term risk. Pre‑training on arrhythmia data does not guarantee transfer to these tasks, causing rankings to shift when evaluation is broadened. The necessity of a diverse task suite is underscored by the observed variability in model performance across the six settings.

## Foundational Learning
- **Linear probing as a representation test**  
  *Why needed*: Freezing the encoder and training only a linear classifier isolates the quality of learned features without confounding from fine‑tuning.  
  *Quick check*: If you fine‑tuned the full encoder, how might that mask deficiencies in the pretrained representation?

- **Uncertainty quantification via bootstrapping**  
  *Why needed*: Imbalanced labels and small test sets yield high variance in performance estimates; confidence intervals reveal whether apparent differences are statistically meaningful.  
  *Quick check*: Two models’ macro‑AUROC 95% CIs overlap. Can you declare one superior? Why or why not?

- **Per‑task metrics beyond macro‑averaging**  
  *Why needed*: Macros hide performance on individual clinical endpoints; AUPRC is often more informative than AUROC for rare labels.  
  *Quick check*: When would AUPRC be a more clinically relevant metric than AUROC for a specific ECG diagnosis?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Dataset loaders (PTB‑XL, CPSC2018, CSN, EchoNext, Hemodynamic, Forecasting) with patient‑level splits → Encoder repository (Random, CLOCS, MERL, D‑BETA) with frozen weights → Linear probe module (ℓ2‑regularized logistic regression with hyper‑parameter sweep) → Metric engine (AUROC, AUPRC, macro‑averages) → Bootstrap resampler (1000 iterations) for 95% CIs → Optional label filter (exclude tasks with <10 positive test examples).

**Critical path**  
1. Preprocess raw ECGs (standardize, clip, handle missing).  
2. Generate embeddings by passing each 10‑second recording through the frozen encoder.  
3. For each downstream label, train a separate linear probe using the training set; select hyper‑parameters via validation loss.  
4. Evaluate the chosen probe on the test set, storing predictions.  
5. Aggregate predictions across labels to compute macro‑AUROC and macro‑AUPRC.  
6. Repeat evaluation on bootstrap resamples to obtain confidence intervals.  
7. (Optional) Repeat steps 1‑6 with reduced training fractions (1%, 10%).

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Feature standardization improves convergence but must be applied identically across splits; not standardizing can penalize some encoders unfairly.  
- Class weighting (balanced) helps on rare labels but can increase variance.  
- Low‑support label exclusion reduces noise at the cost of discarding potentially important rare conditions; threshold ≥10 positives is pragmatic but not universally justified.  
- Macro vs micro averaging: macro treats all labels equally (clinical equity), micro reflects overall prevalence and may be dominated by common labels.

**Failure signatures**  
- Macro‑AUROC changes dramatically when low‑support labels are removed.  
- Random baseline performs within CI of pretrained models on many tasks.  
- Huge gaps between AUROC and AUPRC on high‑prevalence tasks suggest poor calibration.  
- CLOCS shows a consistent drop on EchoNext, signaling limited transfer to structural disease.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. Replicate macro‑AUROC comparison on all six evaluation settings at 100% training data to confirm random’s competitiveness.  
2. Conduct per‑task analysis on PTB‑XL SUB to expose where random matches CLOCS and where MERL/D‑BETA excel; compute AUPRC for high‑prevalence tasks.  
3. Run the full pipeline on EchoNext to verify the ranking shift; specifically check that CLOCS underperforms and MERL/D‑BETA show only marginal gains over random.

## Limitations
- Empirical evaluation restricted to four representations and six downstream tasks; generalizability to newer self‑supervised methods or other endpoints is uncertain.  
- All comparisons use linear probing; the impact of full fine‑tuning on relative performance remains unknown.  
- Datasets contain demographic and collection biases; cross‑dataset robustness is not assessed.

## Confidence
- Macro‑AUROC obscures meaningful differences: **High**  
- Random baseline competitiveness: **Medium**  
- Rankings shift with expanded evaluation: **High**

## Next Checks
1. **Broaden model coverage**: Replicate the pipeline with at least five additional recent self‑supervised ECG representations across all six task categories, reporting per‑task AUROC/AUPRC with 95% CIs and the random baseline.
2. **Fine‑tuning vs linear probing**: For each representation, train both a linear probe and a fully fine‑tuned classifier; compare rankings to determine if the random baseline’s competitiveness persists under full fine‑tuning.
3. **Threshold sensitivity analysis**: Systematically vary the minimum positive support threshold (5, 10, 15, 20) for label inclusion; compute macro‑AUROC stability and per‑task performance to identify a robust exclusion criterion.
