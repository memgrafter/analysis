# Act or Clarify? Modeling Sensitivity to Uncertainty and Cost in Communication

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.02843
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02843
- **Reference count:** 7
- **Primary result:** Humans ask clarification questions more frequently when contextual uncertainty and the cost of acting incorrectly are both high, a pattern well-accounted for by an expected regret model.

## Executive Summary
This paper examines the decision to ask a clarification question (CQ) versus acting directly under uncertainty. The authors propose a computational model where agents choose the option (ask or act) that minimizes expected regret—the difference between the utility of the best action given the true state and the utility of the chosen action. Two behavioral experiments manipulate the level of uncertainty in a scenario and the cost of a incorrect action. Results show that CQs increase with uncertainty and error cost, while direct action decreases. The expected regret model, which incorporates both factors, fits the interaction pattern better than ablated versions lacking either parameter, suggesting people integrate uncertainty and cost in a normatively sensitive way when deciding whether to seek clarification.

## Method Summary
The core method is a computational model based on expected regret. The model calculates, for each possible decision (ask a CQ or take a direct action), the expected regret given the agent's beliefs about the true state of the world. It then selects the option with lower expected regret. This model was tested against human behavioral data from two experiments. Experiment 1 used a hypothetical scenario task where participants chose to ask a predefined CQ or act, with uncertainty (ambiguous vs. clear context) and error cost (low vs. high) manipulated between-subjects. Experiment 2 employed a within-subjects rating design, asking participants to rate their likelihood of asking a CQ across different cost and uncertainty levels. Model fits were compared to versions where either the cost or uncertainty parameter was held constant.

## Key Results
- **Uncertainty & Cost Interaction:** Participants asked CQs more frequently as uncertainty increased, and this effect was significantly amplified when the cost of a wrong action was high (Experiment 1: β=1.58 [0.75,2.48]; Experiment 2: β=4.47 [0.38,8.48] for high cost interaction).
- **Direct Action Decrease:** The rate of choosing direct action decreased with higher uncertainty and higher error costs.
- **Model Superiority:** The full expected regret model, with separate parameters for uncertainty sensitivity and cost sensitivity, provided a better fit to the pattern of results than models that omitted one of these factors, capturing the critical interaction between the two.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The expected regret model works because it formalizes a rational trade-off: the value of gaining information via a CQ must outweigh both the inherent cost of asking and the anticipated benefit of reducing the chance of a costly error. By quantifying the expected loss from acting on a false belief, the model predicts that seeking clarification becomes optimal precisely when uncertainty is high *and* mistakes are expensive. This captures the intuitive idea that we tolerate ambiguity for trivial matters but demand clarity for high-stakes decisions. The model's success suggests people's CQ decisions are sensitive to this normative calculus rather than being driven by a simple heuristic like "ask when confused."

## Architecture Onboarding
*How to think about the paper's modeling framework as a system.*

**Component Map:**
Input (Scenario Parameters: Uncertainty Level, Error Cost, CQ Cost) -> Belief Updater (Bayesian? fixed priors) -> Expected Regret Calculator (for "Ask" and "Act" branches) -> Decision Unit (Compare Expected Regrets, Apply Threshold?) -> Output (Choice: Ask CQ or Direct Action)

**Critical Path:**
The central computation is the `Expected Regret Calculator`. It takes the scenario's uncertainty and cost parameters, combines them with the (presumed) utilities of correct/incorrect actions and the cost of asking, and outputs two expected regret values. The `Decision Unit` then simply selects the option with the lower value. The entire system's behavior is determined by how these two factors—information potential and cost structure—interact within this calculation.

**Design Tradeoffs:**
- **Simplicity vs. Ecological Validity:** The model assumes a single, common utility scale for all outcomes (correct/incorrect act, asked/not asked). This enables a clean formalization but may not hold when comparing linguistic (time spent) vs. physical (broken door) costs.
- **Optimality vs. Psychological Plausibility:** It implements a normative optimal choice rule (minimize expected regret). This is strong for prediction but may omit cognitive biases (e.g., recency) that the authors acknowledge.
- **Parameter Parsimony:** It uses just two sensitivity parameters (for uncertainty and cost). This is elegant but risks underfitting if more factors (e.g., social cost of CQs) are at play.

**Failure Signatures:**
- Systematic deviations where CQ rates do not increase with uncertainty, or where the cost manipulation has no effect.
- A pattern where the effect of uncertainty *decreases* when cost is high (opposite of the predicted interaction).
- Persistent recency effects (e.g., preferring the last-listed action) that the model cannot capture, indicating a need for sequential or heuristic processing layers.

**First Three Experiments to Validate/Extend:**
1. **Orthogonal Cost Manipulation:** Replicate Experiment 1 but explicitly manipulate the cost of *asking a CQ* (e.g., time delay, small monetary penalty) alongside uncertainty and error cost. This tests the model's cost parameter decomposition.
2. **Free-Production CQ Quality:** Have participants generate their own CQs in response to the same decision problems. Analyze the information-theoretic quality (expected uncertainty reduction) of the produced questions versus an optimal benchmark. This tests if people ask *good* questions, not just whether they ask.
3. **Real-Time Interactive Setup:** Implement the task in a live, paced dialogue with a confederate or a simple AI agent. This tests the model's generalizability from hypothetical, static scenarios to dynamic communication where turn-taking and contextual evolution matter.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*Based on the provided analysis.*

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can utilities be specified across fundamentally different action types (e.g., linguistic vs. non-linguistic) to allow the expected regret model to generalize to mixed action spaces like those in Experiment 2?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state that adapting the model to Experiment 2 "would require principled ways of specifying utilities across distinct modalities" because costs like "wasting time" versus "breaking down the wrong door" lack an obvious common currency.
- **Why unresolved:** The current model assumes comparable utility scales, but real-world actions involve incommensurate cost types (e.g., epistemic vs. physical), preventing direct application of the same formalism.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A formal framework that defines utility scales across modalities (e.g., via meta-decision procedures or hierarchical utility functions) tested in experiments where participants trade off linguistic and physical actions with experimentally calibrated outcomes.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What are the specific costs of asking clarification questions (e.g., time delay, social imposition) and how do they interact with contextual uncertainty in the decision to seek clarification?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors note that while parameter *c* in their model could represent the cost of asking, they "did not explicitly manipulate this" and suggest "future experiments might do so through time pressure or social context."
- **Why unresolved:** The model includes an undifferentiated cost parameter for asking, but real CQs incur multiple separable costs whose independent effects and interactions with uncertainty remain unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Behavioral experiments that orthogonally manipulate explicit costs of CQs (e.g., time penalties, perceived social hierarchy with the addressee) while measuring CQ frequency, combined with model extensions that decompose *c* into distinct cost components.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Do humans ask clarification questions that are *good* in an information-theoretic sense—i.e., that optimally reduce uncertainty about the decision-relevant state given the available options?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper focuses on *when* to ask CQs via expected regret, but cites Coenen et al. (2019) who list "Do people ask good questions?" as a major open challenge. The authors do not address whether CQs actually maximize expected information gain.
- **Why unresolved:** The model assumes rational information-seeking but does not measure the efficiency of the CQs produced; participants were constrained to pre-defined CQs, so natural production might be suboptimal.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Free-production studies where participants generate CQs in response to the same decision problems, followed by an information-theoretic analysis of how much uncertainty each CQ would eliminate compared to an optimal benchmark.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What cognitive factors beyond expected regret (e.g., recency biases, response noise, heuristic thresholds) influence clarification question choices, and how can they be integrated into the layered decision model?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors acknowledge that their model "does not fully capture the variance in the data," noting biases like "choosing the option mentioned last" and suggesting "factors beyond expected regret such as response noise or recency effects."
- **Why unresolved:** The expected regret model fits the interaction pattern but fails to account for systematic deviations (e.g., recency) and residual noise, indicating missing psychological processes.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Cognitive experiments designed to isolate potential biases (e.g., counterbalancing presentation order, introducing irrelevant distractors) and model comparisons that add parameters for heuristic thresholds or sequential dependencies to the existing layered architecture.

## Limitations
- **Statistical Precision:** The study lacks reported sample sizes and power analyses; confidence intervals for key interactions (e.g., Experiment 2 cost: β=4.47 [0.38,8.48]) are wide, indicating low precision and potential sampling instability.
- **Model Comparison Scope:** Model comparison was restricted to ablated versions of the proposed expected regret model. Alternative computational accounts (e.g., expected utility, reinforcement learning, simple heuristics) were not tested, limiting confidence in the model's unique explanatory power.
- **Ecological Validity:** The experimental tasks use hypothetical, between-subjects or rating-based scenarios, which may not capture the dynamics, social pressures, or real-time constraints of natural communication where CQs are produced.

## Confidence
- **Claim 1:** Humans increase clarification questions when uncertainty and error cost are high, and decrease direct action accordingly. *Confidence: Medium* – supported by significant effects but limited by potential sampling issues and modest precision in estimates.
- **Claim 2:** The expected regret model provides a better fit than models lacking cost or uncertainty parameters. *Confidence: Low* – because the comparison is incomplete (no alternative models tested) and lacks quantitative fit metrics (e.g., AIC, BIC, cross-validated likelihood) for a robust assessment.

## Next Checks
1. **High-powered preregistered replication** of both experiments with large samples (e.g., N≥500 per condition) to obtain narrow confidence intervals and robust effect size estimates.
2. **Expanded model comparison** evaluating the expected regret model against a suite of alternative decision-theoretic models (e.g., expected utility, probability matching, simple threshold heuristics) using information criteria (AIC/BIC) and trial-level cross-validation.
3. **Ecological validity study** deploying the paradigm in a real-time interactive setting (e.g., human–AI or human–robot dialogue) to test generalizability of the uncertainty-cost sensitivity to dynamic, socially embedded communication.
