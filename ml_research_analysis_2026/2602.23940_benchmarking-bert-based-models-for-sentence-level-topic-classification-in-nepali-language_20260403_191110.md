# Benchmarking BERT-based Models for Sentence-level Topic Classification in Nepali Language

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.23940
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23940
- Reference count: 18
- Primary result: MuRIL-large achieved the highest weighted F1-score (90.60%), while NepBERTa delivered a competitive 88.26% F1 with far fewer parameters and training time.

## Executive Summary
This study benchmarks ten BERT-based models for sentence-level topic classification in Nepali, a low-resource language. Using a well-balanced dataset of 25,006 sentences across five domains, the authors fine-tune each model and evaluate using accuracy, weighted F1-score, and AUROC. Results show that Indic models, particularly MuRIL-large, achieve the best performance with a weighted F1 of 90.60%. Remarkably, the monolingual NepBERTa attains 88.26% F1 despite having only 110M parameters and requiring much less training time, demonstrating the efficiency of language-specific pretraining. The work underscores the importance of linguistic alignment in transfer learning for low-resource languages.

## Method Summary
The authors collected 25,006 Nepali sentences from five domains (Agriculture, Health, Education & Technology, Culture & Tourism, General Communication), ensuring balance across classes. The data was split into training (20,005), validation (2,500), and test (2,501) sets. Ten BERT-based models—including multilingual (mBERT, XLM-R, mDeBERTa), Indic (MuRIL, DevBERT, HindiBERT, IndicBERT), and Nepali-specific (NepBERTa)—were fine-tuned using the Hugging Face library. Each model was trained for 10 epochs with a learning rate of 2e-5, batch size 8, gradient accumulation steps 2, and a maximum sequence length of 256. The training was conducted on a desktop with an Intel Xeon CPU and an RTX 4060 Ti 16GB GPU, and training times were recorded. Performance was assessed via accuracy, weighted F1-score, weighted precision, weighted recall, and AUROC.

## Key Results
- MuRIL-large achieved the highest weighted F1-score of 90.60%, outperforming all other models.
- NepBERTa achieved a competitive 88.26% F1-score with significantly fewer parameters (110M) and much shorter training time (34.78 min) compared to MuRIL-large (304M, 65.75 min).
- All models exhibited lower performance on the "General Communication" category relative to the other four domains, indicating its higher semantic heterogeneity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Models pre-trained on linguistically related Indic corpora (e.g., MuRIL) outperform broader multilingual and monolingual models for Nepali text classification.
- **Mechanism:** Multilingual Indic pre-training exposes the model to shared morphological, syntactic, and lexical patterns across multiple Devanagari-scripted Indo-Aryan languages. This allows the model to develop more robust cross-lingual representations for Nepali-specific constructs than a model trained on globally diverse languages (like mBERT) or a single non-Indic language (like RoBERTa).
- **Core assumption:** Linguistic similarity in script and language family leads to beneficial transfer learning during pre-training, which is further leveraged during fine-tuning on a Nepali task.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "...Indic models, specifically MuRIL-large, achieved the highest weighted F1-score of 90.60%, outperforming all multilingual and monolingual baselines."
  - [section V, Result and Discussion]: "These results reaffirm that models trained on linguistically and scriptually related Indic data... outperform broad multilingual models in presenting Nepali text."
  - [corpus]: Neighbor papers on Indic-specific models (e.g., maiBERT for Maithili, MahaBERT for Marathi) show similar patterns of strong performance on related low-resource languages, providing indirect support for this transfer mechanism.
- **Break condition:** This advantage would diminish if the target language (Nepali) had highly unique grammatical structures or script usage not shared with other Indic languages, or if the Indic pre-training corpus lacked sufficient Nepali text.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** A monolingual model (NepBERTa) achieves competitive performance with significantly lower computational cost compared to larger Indic/multilingual models.
- **Mechanism:** Monolingual pre-training on a large Nepali corpus allows the model to optimize its vocabulary and internal representations exclusively for Nepali's specific morphological richness and lexical semantics. This eliminates the "noise" and parameter overhead of representing unrelated languages, leading to a more efficient model for the target task.
- **Core assumption:** Sufficient in-domain pre-training data exists to create a high-quality monolingual model that does not suffer from catastrophic forgetting or under-representation.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "NepBERTa, achieved a competitive 88.26% F1-score with significantly lower parameters and training time (110M, 34.78 min) compared to MuRIL-large (304M, 65.75 min)."
  - [section V]: "The strong performance of MuRIL-large and NepBERTa highlights the effectiveness of region- or language-specific pretraining in low-resource languages like Nepali."
  - [corpus]: Weak direct evidence; neighbor papers (e.g., on MedicalBERT) show domain-specific pretraining benefits, but the monolingual vs. multilingual efficiency trade-off for Nepali is primarily evidenced within this paper's results.
- **Break condition:** The efficiency advantage would reverse if the monolingual pre-training corpus were orders of magnitude smaller or less diverse than the multilingual/Indic corpora, leading to poor base representations.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The "General Communication" category (D5G) is consistently more difficult to classify than domains like Agriculture or Culture & Tourism.
- **Mechanism:** Topic classification performance correlates with semantic/lexical coherence within a domain. Categories derived from "Arts, Literature, and Stories" (D5G) inherently contain more diverse linguistic styles, subjects, and vocabulary than functionally defined domains like "Agriculture" (D1A) or "Health" (D2H). This heterogeneity increases intra-class variance and makes decision boundaries fuzzier for the classifier.
- **Core assumption:** The label "General Communication" aggregates multiple loosely-related sub-topics or genres, unlike the more conceptually unified other domains.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section V]: "all models are able to classify Agriculture, Education & Technology, Health, and Culture sentences more accurately compared to General communication... The reason behind such a difference may be attributed to the fact that General communication data is derived from multiple sub-categories."
  - [section V, Fig 2]: Confusion matrices for top models (e.g., MuRIL-large) show higher misclassification rates specifically for D5G.
  - [corpus]: No strong direct neighbor evidence; the mechanism is inferred directly from the paper's error analysis and dataset description.
- **Break condition:** This pattern would not hold if the "General Communication" corpus were manually curated to be as lexically and thematically homogeneous as the other four domains.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Transformer Fine-tuning for Text Classification
  - **Why needed here:** All evaluated models are pre-trained transformers (BERT variants) that require task-specific fine-tuning. The engineer must understand how to add a classification head, adjust learning rates, and manage gradient flow on a new dataset.
  - **Quick check question:** What are the key differences between fine-tuning the entire transformer versus only the classification head, and which approach is typically used for low-resource target tasks?
- **Concept:** Evaluation Metrics for Multi-class Imbalance (even with balanced data)
  - **Why needed here:** The paper uses accuracy, weighted F1-score, and AUROC. The engineer must know why weighted F1 is crucial for interpreting performance across all classes equally, and what AUROC reveals about the model's ranking confidence per class.
  - **Quick check question:** If a model has high global accuracy but a low AUROC for a specific class, what does that suggest about its predictions for that class?
- **Concept:** Linguistic Script and Morphology in Tokenization
  - **Why needed here:** Nepali uses Devanagari script with rich morphology. The choice of pre-trained tokenizer (WordPiece, SentencePiece) directly impacts how well rare or complex Nepali words are represented. The engineer must understand that tokenization schemes are pre-determined by the chosen model.
  - **Quick check question:** How might a monolingual Nepali tokenizer (like NepBERTa's) differ in sub-word unit creation from a multilingual tokenizer (like mBERT's) when processing a compound Nepali noun?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
The processing pipeline flows as: Raw Nepali sentence → Tokenizer → Pre-trained Encoder → Pooling/Classification Head → Output.
- **Input:** Raw Nepali sentence (Devanagari Unicode string).
- **Tokenizer:** Model-specific tokenizer (e.g., MurilTokenizer, BertTokenizer). Converts text to sub-word IDs, adds [CLS] and [SEP] tokens, creates attention masks and token type IDs.
- **Pre-trained Encoder:** Transformer backbone (e.g., MuRIL-large, NepBERTa-base). Takes token IDs and produces contextualized embeddings for each token.
- **Pooling/Classification Head:** Uses the [CLS] token's final hidden state, passes through dropout and a linear layer to project to 5 classes.
- **Output:** Logits over the 5 classes, softmaxed to probabilities.

### Critical Path
Load Dataset → Tokenize (with padding/truncation to 256) → Forward Pass through (Tokenizer + Encoder + Head) → Compute Cross-Entropy Loss → Backpropagate → Validate/Test.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Model Size vs. Resources:** Larger models (MuRIL-large) capture more nuance but require more memory and time; smaller models (NepBERTa) are faster and lighter with modest performance drop.
- **Linguistic Scope:** Monolingual (Nepali) vs. Indic (multiple) pre-training. Monolingual avoids cross-lingual noise but lacks potential positive transfer from related languages. Indic models balance this but include irrelevant language data.
- **Sequence Length:** 256 tokens trade off context capture against quadratic memory cost of attention.

### Failure Signatures
- **Per-category failure:** Consistently low F1 on D5G (General Communication) indicates data labeling or coherence issues.
- **AUROC near 0.5 for a class:** Model cannot distinguish that class, suggesting under-representation in pre-training or flawed label.
- **Training loss diverges/plateaus early:** Possibly due to inappropriate learning rate, gradient accumulation errors, or faulty data loading.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How does expanding the dataset to include more topic categories affect the performance and comparative effectiveness of BERT-based models for Nepali sentence-level classification?
2. What distinct error patterns (e.g., morphological, syntactic, or domain-specific) explain why Indic models like MuRIL-large outperform monolingual NepBERTa in certain categories, and vice versa?
3. Can ensemble methods combining predictions from multiple BERT-based models (e.g., MuRIL-large, XLM-R, NepBERTa) achieve higher classification accuracy than any single model for Nepali text?
4. How transferable are sentence-level classification models to document-level topic classification in Nepali, and does the model performance hierarchy (e.g., MuRIL-large > NepBERTa) persist at the document level?

## Limitations
- **Dataset Access Unknown:** The balanced 25k-sentence corpus is not publicly available, requiring author contact and risking unavailability or data drift.
- **Tokenization & Preprocessing Ambiguity:** Exact model versions (commit hashes) and any text normalization steps are unreported, potentially leading to token-alignment mismatches.
- **Generalization Limits:** Results are confined to one Nepali topic classification task; transferability to other NLP tasks (e.g., NER, sentiment) or dialects remains unevidenced.
