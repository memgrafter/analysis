# sebis at ArchEHR-QA 2026: How Much Can You Do Locally? Evaluating Grounded EHR QA on a Single Notebook

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.13962
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13962
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** The authors demonstrate that fully local EHR question answering on commodity hardware can achieve above-average leaderboard scores using quantized models and dense embeddings, with best F1 scores of 51.61 (evidence identification) and 74.84 (evidence alignment).

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the feasibility of running grounded EHR question answering entirely on local commodity hardware without cloud infrastructure. The authors implement and evaluate multiple approaches—embedding-based retrieval, fine-tuned BERT classifiers, and quantized small language models—for all four subtasks of the ArchEHR-QA 2026 shared task. Results show competitive performance: their submissions achieve above-average scores on the leaderboard for Subtask 1 (question interpretation) and Subtask 4 (evidence alignment). The work demonstrates that properly configured smaller and quantized models can approach the performance of larger systems, establishing the technical feasibility of privacy-preserving, fully local EHR QA on standard notebooks.

## Method Summary
The authors build a modular pipeline that processes a patient question and a segmented clinical note through four subtasks: (1) question interpretation using quantized LMs (e.g., Qwen3-4B, gpt-oss-120b) with constrained prompting; (2) evidence identification via two parallel methods—dense embedding similarity (Qwen3-Embedding-8B) with a tuned threshold, and fine-tuned BERT-style classifiers augmented with synthetic data; (3) answer generation using quantized LMs; and (4) evidence alignment through embedding thresholding, list-wise prompting (Qwen3.5-35B), or classifier pairs. Synthetic data (200 cases) are generated using a local Llama3.1-70B to stabilize classifier training when real annotated data is extremely limited (20 dev cases). All models are quantized to 4‑bit and run with the MLX framework on Apple Silicon (M4 Pro 48GB or M3 Max 96GB). Performance is evaluated on the ArchEHR‑QA test set, with best configurations achieving micro‑F1 scores of 51.61 (evidence identification) and 74.84 (evidence alignment).

## Key Results
- Out‑of‑the‑box dense embeddings (Qwen3‑Embedding‑8B) surpass fine‑tuned cross‑encoders for evidence identification (51.61 F1 vs. 44.43).
- Quantized small LMs (e.g., Qwen3‑4B) produce competitive question interpretations and answers, but top alignment relies on a 35B model (Qwen3.5‑35B list‑wise prompting) reaching 74.84 F1.
- Synthetic data augmentation improves classifier balance in limited‑data settings but risks overfitting to the development distribution.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Out‑of‑the‑box dense embedding models can outperform fine‑tuned cross‑encoders for evidence identification and alignment in low‑resource EHR‑QA settings.
- **Mechanism:** Embedding models leverage large‑scale pretraining to capture general semantic similarity, providing robust zero‑shot performance when fine‑tuning data is scarce. Their fixed representations avoid overfitting to small, potentially noisy development sets.
- **Core assumption:** The semantic gap between clinical note sentences and clinician‑interpreted questions can be bridged by general‑domain or biomedical pretrained embeddings without extensive task‑specific adaptation.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] "Strong Embedding Baselines: We show that out‑of‑the‑box dense embedding models surprisingly beat fine‑tuned cross‑encoders for evidence extraction and alignment."  
  [section 5.2] "Surprisingly, most methods struggle to outperform [the everything‑is‑relevant] baseline substantially... Qwen3‑Embedding‑8B achieves 51.61 F1 on test vs. fine‑tuned Bio_ClinicalBERT's 44.43."  
  [section 6] "...embedding similarity or cross‑encoder ranking generally increase precision but often at the cost of reduced recall."
- **Break condition:** If target clinical terminology or task‑specific reasoning diverges significantly from the embedding model's pretraining corpus, performance would likely degrade without fine‑tuning.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Synthetic data generated by a local large language model can stabilize the fine‑tuning of BERT‑style classifiers for evidence identification when real annotated data is extremely limited (20 cases).
- **Mechanism:** The LLM‑based synthesis pipeline creates augmented examples with controlled relevance label distributions, expanding the training distribution. This helps the classifier learn more generalizable decision boundaries than training on the tiny real set alone.
- **Core assumption:** The LLM can generate clinically plausible note excerpts and relevance labels that sufficiently approximate the real data distribution, despite the domain's complexity.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [section 3.2] "To address the limited size of the development set for fine‑tuning encoder models, we generate a supplementary synthetic dataset... resulting in 200 new synthetic cases comprising 1,818 sentences... This generation process utilizes a two‑stage pipeline... guided by manually defined quality thresholds."  
  [section 5.2] "Models trained with HYDRA and synthetic data exhibit somewhat more balanced precision–recall trade‑offs... synthetic examples can help establish clearer decision boundaries when real training data is limited."  
  [section 6] "...future research should explore integrating large‑scale proxy datasets from related clinical domains alongside more advanced synthetic data generation methods to better stabilize model fine‑tuning."
- **Break condition:** If the synthetic data generation process fails to capture key clinical complexities (e.g., temporal reasoning, negation, medication dosage relationships), the classifier may learn spurious correlations.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Quantized and small autoregressive language models, when paired with constrained prompting, can handle generative EHR‑QA subtasks (question interpretation, answer generation) with performance approaching larger models, enabling local execution.
- **Mechanism:** Model quantization (4‑bit) drastically reduces memory and compute requirements. Smaller models (e.g., Qwen3‑4B) focused on specific subtasks via strict prompting (word limits, clinical tone) can compensate for reduced capacity by operating within a narrow, well‑defined distribution.
- **Core assumption:** The task distributions for rewriting patient questions and generating short answers from provided evidence are constrained enough that they do not require massive model scale or emergent reasoning capabilities.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] "quantized small language models... Qwen3‑4B method... competitive performance... smaller models can approach the performance of much larger systems when properly configured."  
  [section 2.2] "Recent work has begun to address privacy‑preserving deployment... there is still limited work on how grounded EHR QA methods compare when all training and inference must remain on commodity hardware."  
  [section 6] "Results from Subtasks 1 and 3 indicate that although larger models generally achieve stronger performance, the performance gap compared to smaller models remains moderate."
- **Break condition:** If the questions or required answers become more complex or require deep medical knowledge synthesis across multiple document sections, the smaller models' limited context window and parametric knowledge would likely cause significant failure.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Dense Retrieval with Embedding Models  
  *Why needed here:* The paper's top evidence identification and alignment results come from computing cosine similarity between query and sentence embeddings (Qwen3‑Embedding‑8B). Understanding how embedding models create comparable vector spaces is key to replicating the thresholding approach.  
  *Quick check question:* Why might a general‑domain embedding model fine‑tuned on search logs (MedCPT) underperform compared to a large, modern general embedding model (Qwen3‑Embedding‑8B) on this clinical task?

- **Concept:** Encoder Fine‑tuning for Low‑Resource Classification  
  *Why needed here:* The evidence identification and alignment subtasks are framed as sentence‑level binary classification. The paper uses BERT‑style models (Bio_ClinicalBERT, DeBERTa) with multi‑head (HYDRA) architectures. Knowing how to fine‑tune these with limited data, including strategies like up/downsampling and K‑fold validation, is prerequisite.  
  *Quick check question:* What is the risk of using a 1:1 real‑to‑synthetic data ratio for fine‑tuning, and how does the HYDRA multi‑head design mitigate brittleness?

- **Concept:** Quantization for Local LLM Deployment  
  *Why needed here:* The entire feasibility claim rests on running models (e.g., 35B, 120B) in 4‑bit precision on Apple Silicon (MLX framework). Understanding quantization's impact on memory, speed, and potential quality loss is essential for reproducing the hardware requirements.  
  *Quick check question:* When quantizing a model for a classification or generation task, what are the typical failure modes (e.g., instability, loss of nuanced tokens) and how might they manifest in clinical QA outputs?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Input Processor -> Question Interpreter -> Evidence Identifier (Embedding Retriever || Classifier) -> Answer Generator -> Evidence Aligner (Embedding Thresholding || List‑wise Prompt || Classifier) -> Output Formatter

- **Critical path:** The question `q` is converted to `q_clin` by the Interpreter; this drives the Evidence Identifier to produce `E`; the Answer Generator uses `q` and `D` (or `q_clin`) to produce `A`; the Evidence Aligner then links `A` back to `D` to produce citations `C`. The quality of `q_clin` directly influences downstream components.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Embedding vs. Classifier for Evidence:* Embeddings offer zero‑shot convenience but require threshold tuning on the target distribution. Classifiers can learn task‑specific patterns but overfit easily with scarce real data.  
  - *Synthetic Data:* Augments limited real data but risks distribution shift if generation quality is poor.  
  - *Model Scale vs. Local Execution:* Larger models (35B, 120B) deliver better performance but demand high‑end hardware (≥96GB RAM) even when quantized. Smaller models (4B) run on modest hardware (48GB) but may lack nuance.  
  - *Prompting Complexity:* Few‑shot prompts improve quality but increase token usage and inference time; zero‑shot is more efficient but less reliable.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - *Subtask 1:* `q_clin` exceeds 15 words, retains first‑person pronouns, or introduces unsupported information.  
  - *Subtask 2:* Classifier predicts `not‑relevant` for most sentences (high precision, zero recall) or predicts `essential` for all sentences (recall=100%, low F1). Threshold `t` tuned on dev fails on test due to distribution shift.  
  - *Subtask 3:* Answer contains hallucinations, exceeds 75 words, or includes citation markers.  
  - *Subtask 4:* Fine‑tuned alignment classifier catastrophically fails on test (e.g., F1 drop from 21.47 dev to 8.33 test). List‑wise prompt may output malformed JSON or cite non‑existent sentence IDs.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Baseline Reproduction:** Run Qwen3‑Embedding‑8B evidence identification with the dev‑set‑optimized threshold `t` on 5 test cases. Document F1, precision, recall. Verify the “everything is relevant” baseline F1 is ~44 as reported.  
  2. **Synthetic Data Ablation:** Train the Bio_ClinicalBERT evidence identifier on (a) only real data, (b) real + synthetic (1:1). Compare F1 on dev out‑of‑fold predictions. Expect synthetic to improve the precision‑recall balance.  
  3. **Quantization Benchmark:** Run Qwen3.5‑35B list‑wise alignment (best test F1=74.84) in 4‑bit MLX on a 48GB vs. 96GB machine. Measure peak memory, inference time per case, and output quality (F1) on a 10‑case subset. Expect 96GB runs smoothly; 48GB may OOM or require further optimizations.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How can EHR QA systems be further optimized to run on highly constrained edge devices (e.g., mobile phones, clinical tablets) while maintaining clinically acceptable performance?**  
   *Basis:* Section 6.1 explicitly states the need to explore even smaller, highly optimized models.  
   *Why unresolved:* The current study uses high‑end commodity hardware (48–96GB memory), which remains costly and inaccessible in many resource‑limited settings. The limits of model compression, quantization, and architectural efficiency for truly low‑memory, CPU‑only devices are unexplored.  
   *Resolving evidence:* Systematic benchmarks of sub‑1B parameter models with extreme quantization on actual edge hardware, measuring accuracy, latency, and reliability against clinical usability thresholds. Comparative studies of distillation, pruning, and hardware‑aware NAS for EHR tasks would clarify feasibility boundaries.

2. **To what extent can synthetic data generation and large‑scale proxy datasets from related domains improve the robustness and generalization of fine‑tuned clinical NLP models, especially under distribution shifts?**  
   *Basis:* Section 6.1 proposes integrating large‑scale proxy datasets and advanced synthetic generation to stabilize fine‑tuning.  
   *Why unresolved:* The paper’s synthetic pipeline used a fixed 1:1 ratio and simple quality thresholds, yielding inconsistent results (e.g., helped DeBERTa but hurt Bio_ClinicalBERT). Optimal strategies for synthetic clinical data—including domain coverage, label noise handling, and combination with external corpora—are unknown.  
   *Resolving evidence:* Large‑scale controlled experiments varying synthetic data sources (LLM vs. template‑based), integration methods (curation, filtering, multi‑task learning), and proxy dataset domains (e.g., PubMed, MIMIC‑III). Evaluation should include out‑of‑distribution test sets, calibration metrics, and failure analysis to determine when synthetic data helps or harms.

3. **Why do out‑of‑the‑box dense embedding models (e.g., Qwen3‑Embedding‑8B) consistently outperform fine‑tuned BERT‑style cross‑encoders for evidence identification and alignment in low‑resource EHR QA settings?**  
   *Basis:* The authors highlight this as a key contribution but provide no analysis.  
   *Why unresolved:* Cross‑encoders are theoretically stronger for relevance ranking due to pairwise interactions; their underperformance suggests fine‑tuning issues (overfitting, poor lexical generalizability) on small clinical datasets. Without probing representations or decision boundaries, the relative advantage of zero‑shot embeddings remains unexplained.  
   *Resolving evidence:* Representational similarity analysis (e.g., CCA, SVCCA) between embeddings and fine‑tuned encoders; attention/feature attribution studies to compare exploited signals; controlled fine‑tuning experiments with varying data sizes, augmentation, and regularization; testing on larger clinical datasets to see if fine‑tuning eventually catches up.

4. **What causes the significant performance drop in evidence alignment (Subtask 4) from the development set to the test set, and how can models be made more robust to such distribution shifts in grounded EHR QA?**  
   *Basis:* The Discussion notes a substantial dev/test discrepancy and hypothesizes a distribution shift.  
   *Why unresolved:* The authors do not characterize the shift (e.g., note complexity, question types, evidence patterns) or evaluate adaptation techniques. The low‑resource setting (20 dev cases) may exacerbate overfitting, but without analyzing dev/test divergence, it is unclear whether this reflects dataset artifacts or a broader clinical generalization challenge.  
   *Resolving evidence:* Detailed dataset analysis comparing dev/test distributions along linguistic and clinical dimensions (sentence length, medical terminology, evidence sparsity). Application of domain adaptation methods (e.g., unsupervised adversarial training, test‑time adaptation) and robustness evaluations across multiple hospital datasets or time slices to determine if alignment models generalize beyond the training environment.

## Limitations
- Tiny development set (20 cases) undermines reliable threshold tuning and classifier stability, increasing overfitting risk.  
- Key implementation details are missing (exact cosine thresholds, full prompt templates, synthetic data generation hyperparameters), hindering exact replication.  
- Test labels are not publicly available; leaderboard scores cannot be independently verified, and the findings may be specific to Apple Silicon/MLX environments.

## Confidence
- *Dense embeddings beat fine‑tuned cross‑encoders*: Medium. Evidence from one model (Qwen3‑Embedding‑8B) on a single dataset; not broadly validated.  
- *Synthetic data stabilizes low‑resource classifiers*: Low. Modest dev improvement but severe test degradation (F1 drop ~13) suggests distribution mismatch.  
- *Quantized small LMs achieve near‑large‑model performance*: Medium. Competitive on interpretation/generation, but alignment relies on a 35B model, limiting the “small” claim.
