# The Struggle Between Continuation and Refusal: A Mechanistic Analysis of the Continuation-Triggered Jailbreak in LLMs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.08234
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08234
- **Reference count:** 11
- **Primary result:** Relocating a continuation-triggered instruction suffix after the user prompt marker sharply increases Attack Success Rate (ASR) in aligned LLMs, and this behavior stems from competition between safety and continuation attention heads.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates a jailbreak vulnerability where moving a continuation-triggered suffix (e.g., "Sure, here is a step-by-step guide: First,") from inside the user prompt to after the user prompt delimiter dramatically increases the model's likelihood of generating harmful content. Using mechanistic interpretability techniques—path patching, activation scaling, and ablation—the authors identify two sparse, functionally specialized types of attention heads: safety heads that suppress harmful outputs and continuation heads that drive coherent generation. The core finding is that the jailbreak success arises from an antagonistic competition: when the suffix is placed after the delimiter, it is interpreted as an assistant-generated instruction, strongly activating continuation heads that override the safety heads' suppression. Empirical validation shows that scaling safety heads reduces ASR (e.g., from 0.58 to near 0 on MaliciousInstruct for LLaMA-2-7B), while scaling continuation heads increases ASR, confirming the causal roles of these head types.

## Method Summary
The study employs causal intervention methods to dissect the internal mechanisms behind the jailbreak. First, path patching is used to identify attention heads that lie on the causal pathway to jailbreak behavior by measuring the change in KL divergence when swapping head activations between clean and corrupted prompts. Second, ablation (zeroing head outputs) categorizes these heads: if ablation increases Attack Success Rate (ASR), the head is classified as a safety head (its normal role is to suppress harm); if ablation decreases ASR, it is a continuation head (its normal role is to promote generation continuity). Third, activation scaling multiplies a head's activation by a coefficient during inference; monotonic ASR decreases with safety head scaling and increases with continuation head scaling provide strong evidence for their antagonistic functions. A reply inversion task further probes whether safety heads primarily encode harmfulness recognition or execute refusal, revealing model-specific differences.

## Key Results
- Relocating a continuation-triggered suffix after the user prompt marker increases ASR sharply (e.g., from 0 to up to 0.58 on MaliciousInstruct for LLaMA-2-7B).
- Mechanistic analysis reveals sparse, specialized attention heads: safety heads suppress harmful outputs, continuation heads drive generation.
- Activation scaling causally confirms antagonism: scaling safety heads reduces ASR, scaling continuation heads increases ASR.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The jailbreak success from suffix relocation stems from a shift in competition between safety heads (suppressing harm) and continuation heads (driving generation), with continuation heads gaining dominance when the suffix is placed after the user prompt marker.
- **Mechanism:** Safety heads, located in middle-to-late layers, bias the residual stream toward refusal by recognizing harmfulness. Continuation heads promote coherence with input semantics. When the suffix follows the prompt delimiter, it is interpreted as assistant-generated instruction, strongly activating continuation heads that override safety heads’ suppression.
- **Core assumption:** Attention heads are functionally specialized modules whose contributions can be causally isolated via interventions like path patching and ablation.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract]: "this jailbreak behavior primarily arises from an inherent competition between the model’s intrinsic continuation drive and the safety defenses acquired through alignment training."
  - [section]: Section 4.1 uses path patching to identify sparse heads; Section 5.2 ablation shows zeroing safety heads increases ASR while zeroing continuation heads decreases ASR.
  - [corpus]: Neighbor papers like "Safety Alignment Should Be Made More Than Just A Few Attention Heads" support specialized head roles, but direct evidence for this competition mechanism is anchored in the paper’s experiments.
- **Break condition:** Scaling safety heads (w > 4) or ablating continuation heads reduces ASR near zero, breaking the jailbreak by restoring safety dominance.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Positional placement of the continuation-triggered suffix changes which head types are engaged, as the model’s context parsing treats inside-prompt suffixes as part of the user query (activating safety heads) versus after-marker suffixes as generation instructions (activating continuation heads).
- **Mechanism:** The prompt delimiter defines a boundary for instruction assessment. Suffixes within the user prompt keep harmfulness recognition active via safety heads. Suffixes after the marker are processed as continuation cues, engaging continuation heads that steer toward harmful compliance if safety heads are insufficiently activated.
- **Core assumption:** Attention heads are sensitive to positional and semantic structure of the input sequence, with different layers processing prompt boundaries differently.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "simply relocating a continuation-triggered instruction suffix can substantially increase jailbreak success rates."
  - [section]: Section 3 defines clean (suffix inside) and jailbreak (suffix outside) prompts; Figure 2 shows ASR jumps from 0 to up to 0.58 on MaliciousInstruct for LLaMA-2-7B.
  - [corpus]: Corpus includes works on prompt injection (e.g., "Defending Against Indirect Prompt Injection"), but positional specificity is primarily evidenced by the paper’s controlled prompt experiments.
- **Break condition:** Reverting to suffix placement inside the user prompt (clean setting) yields ASR near zero, as safety heads reassert control.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Activation scaling causally confirms the antagonistic roles: amplifying safety heads monotonically decreases ASR, while amplifying continuation heads monotonically increases ASR, proving their opposing influences on jailbreak behavior.
- **Mechanism:** Scaling multiplies head activation vectors before residual addition, linearly boosting their contribution to output logits. This directly modulates the probability distribution toward refusal (safety heads) or harmful continuity (continuation heads).
- **Core assumption:** Activation scaling is a linear, faithful intervention that cleanly modulates head influence without introducing nonlinear artifacts or disrupting other components.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "scaling safety heads reduces ASR (e.g., from 0.58 to near 0) while scaling continuation heads increases ASR."
  - [section]: Section 5.3 and Figures 5 show scaling effects; Table 1 confirms cumulative impacts with multiple heads.
  - [corpus]: Neighbor "ASGuard: Activation-Scaling Guard" uses similar scaling for defense, but the antagonistic scaling demonstration is original to this paper’s head-specific analysis.
- **Break condition:** Scaling random heads or applying coefficients without head-type specificity fails to consistently alter ASR, indicating the mechanism depends on targeting correct head categories.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Mechanistic Interpretability**
  - Why needed: To dissect how internal computations (e.g., attention heads) produce high-level behaviors like jailbreaking, moving beyond black-box analysis.
  - Quick check question: What is the difference between path patching and activation scaling, and when would you use each?
- **Concept: Transformer Attention Heads**
  - Why needed: The analysis operates at the head level, assuming heads are functional units; understanding their role in residual stream aggregation is essential.
  - Quick check question: How do self-attention heads contribute to the residual stream in decoder-only LLMs, and why might they specialize?

  - Why needed: Safety heads are products of alignment training; the mechanism hinges on tension between alignment-induced safety and pre-trained continuation drive.
  - Quick check question: How does RLHF alter a model’s generation behavior compared to pure next-token prediction pre-training?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input tokens -> Embedding layer -> Transformer blocks (multi-head attention + MLP, with residual connections) -> Output logits.
- **Critical path:** The causal chain from prompt ingestion, through attention head computations that sum into the residual stream, to final token prediction. The safety–continuation competition resolves in the residual stream before the next-token distribution is sampled.
- **Design tradeoffs:** Strengthening safety heads via scaling may increase overrefusal on benign queries (e.g., HDR drop in Qwen2.5); enhancing continuation heads improves coherence but raises jailbreak risk. Interventions must balance safety and helpfulness.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - High ASR on jailbreak prompts with suffix after delimiter.
  - Low HDR when safety heads are over-scaled (indicating overrefusal).
  - No ASR change when scaling random heads or applying incorrect coefficients.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Path patching:** For each head, run clean prompt, corrupted prompt, and patched run (replace head activation from clean with corrupted). Compute KL divergence change to flag heads lying on causal path to jailbreak.
  2. **Activation scaling:** For identified heads, multiply activation by coefficient *w* (0–5) during inference on jailbreak prompts; measure ASR to classify as safety (ASR ↓ with *w* ↑) or continuation (ASR ↑ with *w* ↑).
  3. **Ablation:** Zero out activations of key heads under jailbreak prompts; observe ASR increase for safety heads and decrease for continuation heads to confirm causal roles.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Why do safety heads in different model architectures (e.g., LLaMA-2 vs. Qwen2.5) exhibit functionally distinct primary behaviors—such as harmfulness recognition versus refusal execution—under the same continuation-triggered jailbreak task?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state in Section 5.4: "the behaviors of safety heads can be broadly categorized into two types... Experimental results show that, under this specific jailbreak task, the safety heads in different models are primarily responsible for different behaviors." They also note in the abstract "revealing notable differences in the functions and behaviors of safety heads across different model architectures."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper identifies this cross-architectural difference empirically but does not investigate its root cause. The analysis is limited to two models (LLaMA-2-7B and Qwen2.5-7B), leaving open whether this divergence stems from architectural design choices, training data distributions, or alignment methodologies.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Comparative mechanistic analyses across a wider range of model families (e.g., differing layer counts, attention patterns, or training objectives) with identical evaluation protocols. Tracing the developmental origin of these heads via checkpoint analysis during training would clarify if specialization emerges from pre-training or alignment stages.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What are the precise computational interaction mechanisms—such as circuit overlap, suppression, or redundancy—among scaled continuation heads that cause non-additive and sometimes non-monotonic effects on Attack Success Rate (ASR)?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Section 5.3 notes that "saturation or slight fluctuations in some cases reveal non-linear interactions and potential redundancy among continuation heads" when scaling multiple heads. Table 1 shows that scaling two or three continuation heads does not always monotonically increase ASR compared to one head.
- **Why unresolved:** The study scales heads but does not decompose their joint computational contributions. The observed non-linearities suggest complex interdependencies, but the circuit-level pathways through which these heads compete or collaborate remain unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Performing path patching between pairs or groups of continuation heads to measure their direct and indirect contributions to the final output distribution. Analyzing activation patterns and attention flow across layers when multiple heads are scaled could reveal whether they operate on redundant representations or distinct sub-circuits.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Does the identified antagonistic competition between safety heads and continuation heads generalize beyond the specific continuation-triggered jailbreak prompt structure to other jailbreak paradigms (e.g., adversarial suffixes, role-play, or multi-step attacks)?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper focuses exclusively on one jailbreak method—relocating a continuation-triggered suffix. The core hypothesis frames jailbreaks as arising from "inherent competition between the model’s intrinsic continuation drive and the safety defenses," implying a potentially general mechanism, but this is not tested.
- **Why unresolved:** The mechanistic analysis is confined to one attack vector. Other jailbreak methods may exploit different vulnerabilities (e.g., semantic overrides, indirect prompt injection) that do not hinge on the same continuation-safety head competition.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Repeating the path patching and activation scaling pipeline on a diverse set of jailbreak prompts from other categories (e.g., GCA attacks, greedy coordinate gradient prompts). If similar head-level competitions emerge, it would support a general theory; if distinct head groups are implicated, it would suggest attack-specific mechanisms.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How can the mechanistic insights about safety and continuation heads be translated into robust, practical safety interventions that generalize across unseen jailbreak attacks without severely degrading helpfulness on benign tasks?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The conclusion states findings offer "practical implications for improving model safety" and "guidance for enhancing their alignment robustness," but the paper stops short of proposing or evaluating concrete, deployment-ready defenses based on head manipulation.
- **Why unresolved:** The interventions studied (activation scaling, ablation) are offline analyses performed at inference time on specific prompts. It is unclear how to operationalize these for real-time, general-purpose safety—e.g., through training modifications, architectural changes, or dynamic head steering that maintains utility.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Developing and testing a defense that modulates identified head activations during generation across a wide range of harmful and benign prompts. Success would require demonstrating reduced ASR on multiple jailbreak benchmarks while preserving performance on standard helpfulness evaluations (e.g., MT-Bench, AlpacaEval). Ablation studies on model fine-tuning with head-level regularization would also address feasibility.
