# Approximation of Log-Partition Function in Policy Mirror Descent Induces Implicit Regularization for LLM Post-Training

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.05933
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05933
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** PMD-MEAN outperforms GRPO by 2.6–14.3% on AIME math benchmarks and achieves a 4.6× training speedup over on-policy methods.

## Executive Summary
Policy Mirror Descent (PMD) is a principled framework for policy optimization but requires computing a log-partition function, which becomes intractable for large language models due to their vast action spaces and limited rollouts. This paper introduces PMD-MEAN, an approximation that replaces the log-partition term with the mean reward under the sampling policy and performs regression in log-policy space. Theoretically, this induces an adaptive mixed KL–Chi-squared regularizer that constrains large probability changes, improving robustness to finite-sample errors. Empirically, PMD-MEAN achieves significant gains on AIME math reasoning benchmarks (+2.6% to +14.3% over GRPO) and a 4.6× training speedup compared to on-policy methods, demonstrating its scalability and effectiveness for LLM post-training.

## Method Summary
PMD-MEAN addresses the intractability of the log-partition function Z(θ) = log Σ_a π(a) exp(r(a)/τ) by approximating it with the sample average of rewards under the current policy. This avoids the expensive summation over all actions. Instead of directly solving the PMD update, the method performs a regression where the target for the log-policy is the old log-policy plus the reward scaled by temperature. The resulting update implicitly includes a regularizer that is a data-dependent mixture of KL and χ² divergences, which adapts to the per-prompt pass rate. This adaptive regularization prevents overly large policy updates, making the algorithm more robust to noise from limited rollouts and enabling the use of larger batch sizes.

## Key Results
- Outperforms GRPO with absolute gains of +2.6% to +14.3% on AIME benchmarks across model scales.
- Provides 4.6× training speedup versus on-policy methods by leveraging larger rollout batches while maintaining stability.
- Theoretically induces an adaptive mixed KL–Chi-squared regularizer that constrains large probability changes, enhancing robustness to finite-sample errors.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core insight is that approximating the log-partition function with the mean reward introduces a specific form of bias that translates into an implicit regularizer. In exact PMD, the update minimizes the KL divergence to the old policy while maximizing expected reward. With the mean approximation, the optimization problem becomes equivalent to minimizing a combination of KL and χ² divergences, weighted by the estimated reward variance and the temperature. The χ² component grows with the size of policy changes, providing a stronger penalty for large deviations than KL alone. Moreover, the weights adapt based on the per-prompt pass rate (i.e., the fraction of correct responses for a given prompt), which automatically reduces regularization for prompts that are already well-learned and increases it for difficult prompts. This adaptive mechanism helps maintain stability in the face of finite-sample noise and varying reward sparsity, which are typical in LLM post-training with sparse rewards (e.g., binary correctness).

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  `Current Policy (π_old) -> Sample Rollouts -> Compute Rewards -> Mean Reward Estimation -> Log-Policy Regression -> New Policy (π_new)`
- **Critical path:**  
  1. Sample actions from π_old for each prompt.  
  2. Evaluate rewards (e.g., via verifier).  
  3. Compute the mean reward per prompt (or overall) as an approximation to the log-partition term.  
  4. Perform regression to update the log-policy parameters, effectively solving min_θ E[ (log π_θ(a) - (log π_old(a) + r(a)/τ))^2 ].  
  5. Normalize to obtain π_new.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Approximation bias vs. tractability:* The mean reward approximation is unbiased but introduces variance; it trades off the exactness of PMD for computational feasibility.
  - *Regularization strength:* The adaptive mixture depends on temperature τ and the estimated pass rates; tuning τ is crucial for balancing progress and stability.
  - *Batch size:* Larger rollouts improve the mean reward estimate and enable speedups but increase memory usage; the method leverages this for efficiency.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Exploding policy updates (e.g., large KL between successive policies) indicating insufficient regularization.
  - Performance collapse on prompts with low pass rates, suggesting the adaptive weighting may over-regularize easy prompts and neglect hard ones.
  - Divergence when rollout batch size is too small, leading to high variance in mean reward estimates.
- **First three experiments:**
  1. *Exact vs. Approximate PMD:* On a small synthetic problem (e.g., a bandit with 10 actions), compare PMD-MEAN to exact PMD (computable via enumeration) in terms of policy convergence and final reward.
  2. *Mean approximation ablation:* Replace the mean reward with a more accurate estimator of the log-partition (e.g., using importance sampling) and measure impact on stability and performance on a medium-scale LLM (e.g., 7B model on AIME).
  3. *Batch size scalability:* Vary the number of rollouts per prompt (e.g., 1, 4, 16, 64) and plot wall-clock time to reach a target accuracy on AIME, comparing to PPO and DPO.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What does PMD-MEAN optimize exactly, and what are the algorithmic consequences of that objective?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] - directly stated in Section 1: "What does PMD-MEAN optimize exactly, and what are the algorithmic consequences of that objective?"
- **Why unresolved:** The paper shows PMD-MEAN induces an adaptive mixed KL–χ² regularizer, but deeper consequences for diverse reward distributions, action space structures, and generalization properties remain unexplored.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Theoretical analysis of fixed points and convergence properties across varied problem settings; empirical comparisons of PMD-MEAN's behavior on tasks with different reward distributions and action space topologies.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How can oversampling strategies and importance sampling corrections be integrated into PMD-MEAN while preserving its implicit regularization guarantees?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] - Conclusion mentions these practical enhancements as future work: "advanced techniques such as oversampling strategies and importance sampling corrections ... could potentially enhance PMD-MEAN's performance further."
- **Why unresolved:** The analysis focuses on the principled form without these engineering tricks; combining them while maintaining theoretical understanding of the induced χ² regularization is challenging.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Derive modified PMD-MEAN algorithms that incorporate IS/oversampling with provable regularization guarantees; validate empirically whether theoretical benefits persist or whether new instabilities emerge.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Should the temperature parameter τ be adapted dynamically based on per-prompt pass-rate pt to improve stability in early training?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] - Section C.5 notes error bounds depend on pt and states: "This suggests an adaptive regularization scheme of PMD-MEAN that scales τ with per-prompt pass rate pt, which we leave for future investigation."
- **Why unresolved:** No principled scheduling rule is derived; early-training stability issues reported for PMD-PART might be mitigated by such adaptation in PMD-MEAN.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Theoretical derivation of optimal τ(pt) schedule minimizing finite-sample error; empirical validation comparing adaptive τ schedules against fixed τ, especially in low-p t regimes.
