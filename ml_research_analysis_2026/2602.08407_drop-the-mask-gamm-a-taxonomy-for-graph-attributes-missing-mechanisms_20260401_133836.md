# Drop the mask! GAMM-A Taxonomy for Graph Attributes Missing Mechanisms

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.08407
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08407
- **Reference count:** 27
- **Primary result:** The paper introduces GAMM, a taxonomy extending missing data mechanisms to graph structure, and finds that neighbor-dependent MNAR (N-MNAR) causes up to ~70% MAE increase in heterophilic graphs, with 46.6% of comparisons showing worse performance when transitioning from attribute-only to structurally-informed masks.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses a critical gap in handling missing data on attributed graphs: traditional missingness mechanisms (MCAR, MAR, MNAR) ignore dependencies on graph structure. The authors propose GAMM, a taxonomy that extends these mechanisms to capture four graph-specific sources—node Attributes (A), structural Properties (S), neighbors’ Attributes (N), and their combinations (G). Through systematic experiments across 12 datasets and 2,304 configurations, they demonstrate that state-of-the-art imputation methods (both tabular and graph-based) suffer significant performance degradation under graph-aware missingness. The most severe failure mode is neighbor-dependent MNAR (N-MNAR), especially in heterophilic graphs, where errors increase by up to ~70%. Moreover, these mechanisms induce distributional biases that flatten feature distributions, a problem not revealed by standard pointwise error metrics.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates imputation methods under a novel, controllable masking framework. Inputs are 12 real-world attributed graphs with fully observed features and topology. The core is a mask generator `g(·)` that produces binary missingness matrices Ω based on one of the GAMM mechanisms (e.g., N-MNAR, where missingness depends on neighbors’ unobserved features). The generator is calibrated to hit target missing rates (20%, 50%, 80%). For each masked dataset, several imputation engines—ranging from simple tabular averages to advanced graph methods like FP and GRIOT—are trained to reconstruct the withheld features. Performance is measured via MAE/RMSE on held-out entries, and secondary analyses examine imputed feature distributions.

## Key Results
- GAMM mechanisms expose a major vulnerability: 46.6% of comparisons show worse MAE/RMSE when moving from attribute-only (A-MAR) to structure-informed masks (S-/N-MAR).
- Neighbor-dependent MNAR (N-MNAR) is the most severe failure mode, with up to ~70% MAE increase on heterophilic graphs (e.g., GRIOT on Cornell).
- Graph-aware missingness flattens feature distributions (e.g., binary features lose bimodality), a bias undetected by pointwise errors.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Neighbor-dependent MNAR (N-MNAR) is the most severe failure mode for graph imputation, especially in heterophilic graphs.
- **Mechanism:** Missingness probability depends on the *unobserved* attributes of a node's neighbors `N(MIS)_i(h)`. This creates a doubly hard problem: the imputer must reconstruct values while the missingness pattern itself is determined by the very values it is trying to predict, and these cues come from potentially dissimilar neighbors in heterophilic graphs.
- **Core assumption:** The graph topology is fully observed, but feature missingness is generated according to a function `g(·)` that conditions on neighbors' unobserved attributes.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 3.1/Table 1] Formal definition: `P(Ωij = 0 | G)` includes `N(MIS)_i(h)` as a dependency for N-MNAR. [section 4.2/RQ3] Empirical finding: "N-MNAR is consistently the hardest regime, especially on heterophilic graphs." Quantified degradation: e.g., GRIOT MAE increase ~77% on Cornell.
- **Break condition:** Identifiability of MNAR vs. MAR is fundamentally unidentifiable without external data. The mechanism's severity hinges on the homophily level; in highly homophilic graphs, neighbor attributes are similar to self, partially mitigating the feedback loop.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The gap between attribute-based (A-MAR) and neighborhood-based (N-MAR) mechanisms is primarily driven by graph homophily.
- **Mechanism:** Under MAR, missingness depends only on *observed* data. For A-MAR, this is the node's own observed attributes. For N-MAR, it is the *observed* attributes of neighbors `N(OBS)_i(h)`. In homophilic graphs, neighbors' observed attributes are good proxies for the node's own attributes, so both mechanisms behave similarly. In heterophilic graphs, neighbors are dissimilar, so conditioning on their observed attributes injects misleading signals.
- **Core assumption:** Missingness generation uses only observed information for MAR variants.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 3.1] Formal distinction: A-MAR uses `F(OBS)_i·`, N-MAR uses `N(OBS)_i(h)`. [section 4.2/RQ2] Empirical finding: "In heterophilic graphs, switching from A-MAR to N-MAR leads to a significant degradation for most approaches." No significant gap in homophilic/neutral graphs.
- **Break condition:** The analysis assumes a single, static graph structure and that node attributes have some predictive power for missingness. The conclusion is specific to the MAR mechanism; the homophily effect may reverse or become irrelevant under MNAR or different mask generation functions.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Graph-aware missingness mechanisms induce distributional biases that are not captured by pointwise error metrics (MAE/RMSE).
- **Mechanism:** When missingness depends on structure or neighbors (especially N-MNAR), the imputation model is trained on a biased view of the data. The observed subset is not a random sample of the true feature distribution. This leads to systematic flattening of binary feature distributions (e.g., underestimating mass at 0 or 1), as the model learns to impute towards a mean that is itself a distorted representation.
- **Core assumption:** The true underlying feature distribution has structure (e.g., bimodality). The mask generation process `g(·)` distorts the empirical distribution of observed values.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "Furthermore, these mechanisms induce distributional biases, flattening feature distributions beyond what pointwise errors reveal." [section 4.2/RQ4] Empirical finding: Figure 2 shows KDE plots where imputed densities under N-MNAR flatten the bimodal structure on Tolokers. [section 3] Example: "That would be a MAR scenario based on structure, and imputers that ignore this structural pattern may systematically overestimate their roles, yielding distributional distortions."
- **Break condition:** This effect is most pronounced for discrete/binary features with clear modes. For continuous, normally distributed features, the "flattening" bias may be less visually obvious but could still alter moments (variance). The severity depends on the strength of the structural dependency in `g(·)`.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Standard Missing Data Mechanisms (MCAR, MAR, MNAR)**  
  *Why needed here:* GAMM is an *extension* of this classic taxonomy. Understanding the baseline definitions (missingness independent of data, dependent on observed data, dependent on unobserved data) is prerequisite to grasping what "graph-aware" adds.  
  *Quick check question:* In a tabular dataset, if younger employees (observed age) are less likely to report their salary, is this MAR or MNAR? What if high-earners (unobserved salary) hide it?

- **Concept: Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) & Message Passing**  
  *Why needed here:* The paper evaluates GNN-based imputers (FP, GRIOT, PCFI). These methods rely on the principle that a node's features can be aggregated from its neighbors. Understanding this core architecture is key to seeing why homophily/heterophily and neighbor-dependent missingness break them.  
  *Quick check question:* Why would a GNN-based imputer fail spectacularly if the graph is heterophilic and missingness depends on neighbors' unobserved features (N-MNAR)?

- **Concept: Graph Homophily vs. Heterophily**  
  *Why needed here:* RQ2 and RQ3 results are fundamentally partitioned by this property. Homophily (connected nodes are similar) validates the "smoothness" assumption of GNNs. Heterophily violates it, turning neighbor aggregation into a source of noise, especially when missingness is neighbor-informed.  
  *Quick check question:* How does the adjusted homophily metric (`H_adj`) from Pei et al. [13] differ from simple label-based homophily, and why is it more relevant for attributed graph imputation?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Mask Generator (g(·)) → Imputation Engine → Evaluator  

**Critical path:**  
Mask Specification → Mask Application → Imputation → Evaluation  

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Mask Generator Complexity:* Simpler `g(·)` (e.g., linear logistic) is transparent and tunable (calibrate `ω_k, b_k` for target `p_miss`). More complex `g(·)` may be more realistic but less interpretable and harder to control for ablation studies.  
- *Imputation Method Choice:* Tabular methods are fast but ignore structure. Graph methods model dependencies but risk compounding errors under graph-aware missingness (especially N-MNAR). GRIOT combines OT with GNNs for robustness but is computationally heavier.  
- *Evaluation Focus:* Reconstruction error (MAE/RMSE) directly measures fidelity to `F`. Downstream task performance (node classification) may be resilient to some imputation errors, potentially hiding distributional biases.  

**Failure signatures:**  
1. **Catastrophic MAE/RMSE spike** when transitioning from A-MNAR to N-MNAR, particularly on heterophilic datasets (Cornell, Texas, Tolokers).  
2. **No significant change** in MAE/RMSE from A-MAR to N-MAR on homophilic graphs, but **distributional plots show bias** (flattened KDE).  
3. **Tabular imputers** show less degradation than graph imputers under some N-MAR conditions (e.g., OT-tab vs. FP on Tolokers in Table 3c), suggesting graph structure is being exploited *incorrectly*.  

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline Sanity Check:** Run all imputers on a homophilic dataset (e.g., Cora) with `p_miss=20%` under pure MCAR. All methods should show reasonable MAE/RMSE (~0.1-0.3 for normalized features). This validates implementation.  
2. **Test RQ1 & RQ2:** On the same Cora dataset, compare A-MAR vs. N-MAR (`p_miss=50%`). Expect minimal degradation for N-MAR due to homophily. Then repeat on a heterophilic dataset (e.g., Texas). Expect significant degradation (e.g., >20% MAE increase) for most graph imputers under N-MAR.  
3. **Test RQ3 Severity:** On a heterophilic dataset (Texas) with `p_miss=20%`, compare A-MNAR vs. N-MNAR. Expect a massive, statistically significant MAE increase for methods like FP and GRIOT (as per Table 3b: ~70% increase). Also plot KDEs for a binary feature to visualize RQ4 distributional flattening.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What specific properties of graph-aware missingness mechanisms (e.g., S-, N-) causally drive the observed degradation in imputation performance compared to attribute-only masks?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper identifies significant performance drops (46.6% of configurations) when transitioning from A- to S-/N-based masks (RQ1, Table 4), but does not isolate the underlying reasons.
- **Why unresolved:** The analysis shows degradation occurs but does not decompose whether it stems from the masking function's dependence on structure, the interaction with graph topology, or method-specific inductive biases.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments where mask generation is decoupled—e.g., fixing the missing rate and dependency strength while varying only the source (A vs. S vs. N)—and measuring performance changes across imputation architectures (tabular vs. graph-based) to isolate mechanism-specific failure modes.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Under MAR conditions, why does neighborhood-dependent missingness (N-MAR) cause severe performance drops in heterophilic graphs but not in homophilic ones?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] RQ2 reports that switching from A-MAR to N-MAR degrades results mostly in heterophilic graphs, attributed to "misleading signals" from dissimilar neighbors.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper observes the correlation between homophily and N-MAR sensitivity but lacks a theoretical or empirical dissection of *why* heterophily amplifies the problem—e.g., whether it's due to feature aggregation, spectral properties, or missingness bias propagation.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic experiments varying homophily continuously (e.g., via edge rewiring) while measuring imputation error and analyzing neighborhood attribute distributions. Complementary theoretical analysis of message-passing under N-MAR in heterophilic regimes.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What makes neighbor-dependent MNAR (N-MNAR) the most severe failure mode, and why is its impact particularly magnified in heterophilic graphs?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] RQ3 and Table 3 show N-MNAR causes up to ~70% MAE increase, especially in heterophilic datasets (e.g., GRIOT on Cornell: -77.1%).
- **Why unresolved:** The paper identifies N-MNAR as a "universal Achilles’ heel" but does not explain the mechanistic interaction between (a) missingness depending on neighbors' unobserved values and (b) heterophilic topology. Is it due to biased supervision, corrupting signals, or both?
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablation studies where N-MNAR is simulated with controlled levels of neighbor attribute correlation and homophily. Diagnostic metrics tracking the propagation of missingness-induced bias through graph convolutions or diffusion processes (e.g., in FP, GRIOT).

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Why do imputation methods fail to preserve feature distributions under graph-aware missingness, and can we design evaluation metrics that capture these biases beyond pointwise errors?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] RQ4 and Figure 2 reveal systematic distributional flattening (e.g., underestimation of binary feature extremes) under N-MNAR/N-MAR, even when MAE appears moderate.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper highlights this as a critical issue but does not investigate *why* current imputers produce biased distributions—e.g., whether loss functions (MSE/MAE) implicitly encourage central tendency, or if graph aggregation smears distributions. No alternative evaluation framework is proposed.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Comparative analysis of training objectives (MSE vs. distribution-matching losses) and their effect on distributional fidelity. Development of new metrics (e.g., Wasserstein distance, KS-test on imputed vs. original feature marginals) and testing them across GAMM mechanisms to correlate distributional bias with downstream task performance.

## Limitations
- The exact parameter calibration procedure for `ωₖ, bₖ` to achieve precise target missing rates (e.g., 20%/50%/80%) is not fully detailed in the main paper, risking mismatched missingness strength across mechanisms.
- The precise definition of neighborhood aggregation for `N(MIS)_i(h)` in N-MNAR/N-MAR (e.g., mean/sum of h-hop neighbors' features) is not specified, critically shaping the missingness pattern's difficulty.
- The exclusion of S-MNAR (due to fully observed edges) limits full replication of the taxonomy; simulating missing structural properties (e.g., degree) is not implemented.

## Confidence
- **Claim:** Neighbor-dependent MNAR (N-MNAR) is the most severe failure mode for graph imputation, especially in heterophilic graphs. **Confidence:** High.
- **Claim:** The gap between attribute-based (A-MAR) and neighborhood-based (N-MAR) mechanisms is primarily driven by graph homophily. **Confidence:** Medium.
- **Claim:** Graph-aware missingness mechanisms induce distributional biases that are not captured by pointwise error metrics. **Confidence:** Medium.
