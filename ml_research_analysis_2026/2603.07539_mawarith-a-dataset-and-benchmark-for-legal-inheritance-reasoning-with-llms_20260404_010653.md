# MAWARITH: A Dataset and Benchmark for Legal Inheritance Reasoning with LLMs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.07539
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07539
- **Reference count:** 22
- **Primary result:** Introduces a 12,500-case Arabic dataset and a weighted metric (MIR-E) revealing a large performance gap in zero-shot Islamic inheritance reasoning, with Gemini-2.5-flash achieving ~90% and five other models scoring below 50%.

## Executive Summary
MAWARITH is a novel Arabic dataset and benchmark designed to evaluate large language models on end-to-end Islamic inheritance reasoning—a complex task requiring multi-step legal and numerical computation. The paper proposes MIR-E, a weighted metric that decomposes the reasoning pipeline into four verifiable stages: heir/blocking identification, share assignment, adjustment detection (ʿawl/radd), and final distribution. In zero-shot evaluations, only Gemini-2.5-flash approaches human-level performance (~90% MIR-E), while models like Fanar, LLaMA 3, Qwen 3, and GPT-OSS fall below 50%. Error analysis shows failures primarily stem from heir identification (false eligibility/blocking) and share miscalculation, with early errors propagating downstream. The dataset and metric enable fine-grained diagnosis of structured legal reasoning capabilities in LLMs.

## Method Summary
The authors constructed MAWARITH by generating 12,500 Arabic inheritance cases using a specialized calculator (Almawarith), then converting them into natural-language questions with expert-verified, step-by-step legal rationales. Models are prompted to reproduce this reasoning pipeline and output a predefined structured JSON (`answer_structured`) containing fields for identified heirs, blocked heirs, assigned shares, adjustment type, and final distribution. Evaluation uses MIR-E, a weighted sum of four component scores: heir identification (`Sh`, an F1×count accuracy hybrid), share assignment (`Ss`, fraction tolerance), adjustment (`Sa`, conditional on prior perfection), and final distribution (`Sf`, tolerance-based). Weights are αh=αs=αf=0.3 and αa=0.1. The test set (500 cases) is evaluated zero-shot across six LLMs, with results showing a stark performance divide.

## Key Results
- **Dataset:** 12,500 Arabic inheritance cases (12k training, 500 test) covering 36 heir categories, with explicit intermediate reasoning traces.
- **Metric:** MIR-E decomposes the task and conditions later-stage scores on earlier perfection, capturing error propagation.
- **Performance:** Gemini-2.5-flash achieves ~90% MIR-E in zero-shot; Fanar-C/Sadiq, LLaMA 3, Qwen 3, and GPT-OSS score below 50%.
- **Error analysis:** Primary failures are false eligibility (including ineligible heirs) and false blocking (excluding eligible heirs); share miscalculations and missed adjustment steps follow. Early errors cascade, invalidating downstream results.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Decomposing inheritance reasoning into discrete, verifiable stages enables LLMs to handle complex legal‑numerical tasks and allows fine‑grained diagnosis.  
**Mechanism:** The dataset provides explicit intermediate reasoning traces (`<thinking>`) covering heir extraction, blocking (ḥajb), share assignment, adjustment (ʿawl/radd), and final distribution. Models are prompted to reproduce this pipeline step by step.  
**Core assumption:** LLMs can generate coherent stepwise outputs when given clear stage boundaries and exemplars.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions "step‑by‑step solutions, including intermediate legal decisions"; Section 4.2 describes a step‑by‑step procedure.  
**Break condition:** If models ignore stage boundaries and produce monolithic outputs, the diagnostic benefits of decomposition vanish.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Conditional evaluation of later stages based on earlier correctness captures error propagation and yields interpretable failure modes.  
**Mechanism:** MIR‑E scores adjustment (`Sa`) and final distribution (`Sf`) only if heir identification (`Sh`) and share assignment (`Ss`) are perfect (Section 5), mirroring the deterministic cascade in legal reasoning.  
**Core assumption:** Inheritance rules are sequential; an error in an upstream stage invalidates downstream results.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract states the metric "captures error propagation"; Section 5 defines `Sa = I(αgold=αpred) if Sh=1 and Ss=1 else 0`.  
**Break condition:** If the domain had non‑linear dependencies (e.g., later steps could correct earlier errors), conditional scoring would misrepresent capability.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Enforcing a structured output schema transforms ambiguous free‑text into machine‑readable traces, enabling reliable automated evaluation.  
**Mechanism:** Models must output `answer_structured` (JSON with predefined fields: `heirs`, `blocked`, `shares`, `awl_or_radd`, `awl_stage`, `post_tasil`). This standardizes representation and allows exact comparison.  
**Core assumption:** LLMs can follow complex formatting instructions when explicitly guided.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract notes "models are required to produce outputs in a predefined structured format"; Section 4.2 details the schema.  
**Break condition:** If models fail to adhere to the schema due to formatting constraints, evaluation becomes noisy and unreproducible.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Data generation pipeline (Almawarith calculator → natural‑language conversion → expert enrichment → standardization → validation) → Dataset (12.5k Arabic cases with `<think>` and `answer_structured`) → Evaluation engine (prompting LLM with Arabic scenario + structured output requirement → parse `answer_structured` → compute MIR‑E components → aggregate weighted score).

**Critical path:**  
1. Load MAWARITH test split.  
2. Format prompt with inheritance scenario and required structured output (Appendix C).  
3. Query LLM (zero‑shot) and extract `answer_structured` JSON.  
4. Compare each field against gold reference to compute stage scores.  
5. Combine with weights (αh=αs=αf=0.3, αa=0.1) to obtain MIR‑E.  
*Key dependencies:* correct parsing of model output, faithful replication of expert heuristics in gold data.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Weight distribution:* Equal weights on identification, share, and final reflect their importance; adjustment gets 0.1 due to rarity (7.7% of cases). Overweighting adjustment would bias toward complex cases.  
- *Scope:* Dataset covers 36 heir categories but excludes rarest scenarios (pregnancy, multiple deaths) – a trade‑off for scalability.  
- *Language:* Arabic‑only; cross‑lingual transfer would require translation but risks losing juristic nuance.  
- *Zero‑shot vs. fine‑tuning:* Evaluation is zero‑shot to test inherent reasoning; fine‑tuning could close gaps but needs more data.

**Failure signatures:**  
- *Heir identification:* false blocking (excludes eligible heirs) and false eligibility (includes ineligible heirs) dominate (Table 3). Count mismatches are rare.  
- *Share assignment:* incorrect fractions even when heirs are correct (Table 4), indicating rule‑knowledge gaps.  
- *Adjustment:* confusion between radd and residual absorption by ʿaṣabah; skipped adjustment steps.  
- *Final allocation:* arithmetic errors in converting fractions to per‑head shares or percentages.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline replication:** Run the six models (Gemini‑2.5‑flash, Qwen3‑32B, GPT‑OSS‑120B, LLaMA‑3.3‑70B, Fanar‑Sadiq, Fanar‑C‑2‑27B) with the provided prompt on the test set and compute MIR‑E. Verify Gemini ~90% and others <50% (Table 2).  
2. **Stage ablation:** Compute unweighted accuracy for each stage (`Sh`, `Ss`, `Sa`, `Sf`) and plot cumulative pipeline success (Figure 2). Identify at which stage each model degrades most.  
3. **Error type quantification:** For a sample of failures, classify into FB/FE/ME (Table 3) and share errors (Table 4). Compare distributions across models to confirm false eligibility as the most common error.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1:** How effective are process reward models in improving stepwise correctness on MAWARITH when training LLMs for Islamic inheritance reasoning?  
  *Basis:* Future work will explore reinforcement learning approaches, including Process Reward Models, to better align model reasoning with legal constraints.  
- **Question 2:** How can the MAWARITH dataset be extended to cover complex inheritance scenarios (e.g., pregnancy, multiple deaths, missing persons, intersex heirs), and what additional reasoning challenges do these extensions introduce?  
  *Basis:* The authors plan to extend the corpus to cover more complex remaining inheritance cases, including pregnancy-related cases, multiple deaths, the missing person (mafqūd), and the intersex heir (khunthā).  
- **Question 3:** To what extent does fine-tuning open-source LLMs on the MAWARITH training set improve their MIR-E scores and reduce error propagation across the reasoning pipeline?  
  *Basis:* The paper provides 12,000 training instances but reports only zero-shot evaluation, leaving the practical utility of fine-tuning unexplored.

## Limitations
- Arabic‑only dataset limits cross‑lingual generalizability; transfer to other legal systems or languages is untested.  
- MIR‑E’s strict conditional scoring assumes irreversible error propagation; if models could sometimes self‑correct, the metric may undervalue downstream reasoning.  
- Zero‑shot evaluation may not reflect fine‑tuned performance; the gap between Gemini and others could narrow with domain adaptation.  
- Dataset excludes rare cases (e.g., pregnancy, multiple deaths), so coverage of the full juristic space is incomplete.  
- Exact tolerance thresholds (`εshare`, `εfinal`) and count‑mismatch penalties are not publicly documented, hindering exact replication.

## Confidence
- **Decomposition enables diagnosis:** High – robustly supported by stage‑wise scores and error analysis.  
- **Conditional scoring captures propagation:** High – aligns with deterministic nature of inheritance law; break condition unlikely in this domain.  
- **Structured output ensures reliable evaluation:** Medium – depends on model adherence; formatting failures could introduce noise.  
- **Performance gap reflects inherent reasoning limits:** Medium – zero‑shot only; fine‑tuning could narrow gap.  
- **Error analysis generalizes to all models:** High – consistent patterns across multiple models.

## Next Checks
1. **Exact replication:** Run the provided evaluation script (or re‑implement it) on the official test split, using the same models, prompts, and temperature. Verify that Gemini‑2.5‑flash’s MIR‑E ≈ 90% and that others remain below 50%.  
2. **Fine‑tuning ablation:** Fine‑tune each model on the 12k training cases and re‑evaluate. If scores improve substantially, the primary limitation is domain knowledge rather than reasoning; if not, the reasoning gap is inherent.  
3. **Conditional vs. unconditional metric:** Compute an unconditional version of MIR‑E (no stage gating) and compare model rankings. Manually inspect 50 cases where downstream scores were zeroed due to upstream errors to check whether downstream errors were truly dependent or could be correct independently.
