# Detecting LLM Hallucinations via Embedding Cluster Geometry: A Three-Type Taxonomy with Measurable Signatures

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.14259
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14259
- **Reference count**: 5
- **Primary result**: Polarity coupling α > 0.5 and cluster cohesion β > 0 are universal across all 11 transformer models; radial gradient λ_r is statistically significant in 9 of 11 models, with ALBERT and MiniLM exceptions explained by embedding compression and distillation-induced isotropy.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a method for detecting hallucinations in large language models by analyzing geometric signatures in static token embedding spaces. The authors cluster embeddings from 11 transformer models and define three statistics: α (polarity coupling within clusters), β (cluster cohesion), and λ_r (radial information gradient). They find that polarity structure and cluster cohesion are universal, while the radial gradient is significant in most models, with two architectural exceptions. These findings establish geometric prerequisites for a taxonomy of three hallucination types—center‑drift, wrong‑well convergence, and coverage gaps—and predict architecture‑specific vulnerability profiles. A tiered classifier using these signatures is proposed, with model‑specific thresholds derived from the static analysis.

## Method Summary
The analysis begins by extracting the input embedding matrix from each transformer model. Tokens are filtered to whole‑word units with non‑zero English frequency via the `wordfreq` library. The retained embeddings are clustered using MiniBatchKMeans (k=40) to obtain centroids. Three statistics are then computed:

- **α (polarity coupling)**: For clusters containing ≥2 antonym pairs, PCA is applied to the difference vectors; the ratio of the polarity span to the cluster radius is averaged across qualifying clusters.
- **β (cluster cohesion)**: For each cluster, the mean cosine similarity between members and their own centroid minus the mean cosine to other centroids is calculated; a positive value indicates tighter cohesion than the background.
- **λ_r (radial information gradient)**: Tokens are binned by embedding norm (B=40, min 10 tokens/bin). The mean self‑information (−log frequency) per bin is fit with linear and quadratic models. The quadratic coefficient λ_r and its significance via an F‑test are reported.

Results show α > 0.5 and β > 0 in all 11 models, confirming universal polarity structure and cohesion. λ_r is significant (p < 0.05) in 9 models; ALBERT (factorized embeddings) and MiniLM (distillation‑induced isotropy) are non‑significant, linking radial degradation to architectural choices. These properties underpin a tiered hallucination detector that classifies tokens into three types based on membership, norm, and density thresholds calibrated per model.

## Key Results
- Polarity coupling α exceeds 0.5 in all 11 models; cluster cohesion β is significantly positive in all.
- Radial gradient λ_r is statistically significant (p < 0.05) in 9/11 models; ALBERT and MiniLM are the exceptions.
- The three statistics define geometric prerequisites for a three‑type hallucination taxonomy: Type 1 (center‑drift) relates to λ_r, Type 2 (wrong‑well) to α/β, and Type 3 (coverage gaps) to absence of nearby centroids.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Degradation of the radial information gradient (λ_r) increases the model's susceptibility to Type 1 (center‑drift) hallucinations under weak context.
- **Mechanism**: The radial structure organizes tokens by information content (norm) — rare, informative tokens occupy outer regions, common tokens near the centroid. When this gradient is weakened (e.g., by embedding compression or distillation‑induced isotropy), the geometric signal distinguishing "generic center" from "specific periphery" blurs. Under ambiguous context, the model's hidden state more easily drifts into the high‑frequency centroid zone, producing vague but plausible outputs.
- **Core assumption**: The radial gradient is a necessary discriminant for center‑drift; its absence elevates Type 1 risk regardless of angular cluster structure.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - [abstract] "The radial gradient λ_r is statistically significant in 9 of 11 models... The two exceptions—ALBERT... and MiniLM... fail due to explicable architectural effects that degrade radial structure..."  
  - [section 5.4] "ALBERT... tokens are compressed into a narrow radial band... MiniLM... distillation‑preserved angular structure... while flattening radial structure... These anomalies... predict that such models should be more susceptible to Type 1 hallucinations."  
  - [corpus] Weak: The neighboring paper "Whitening Reveals Cluster Commitment..." studies Type 1/2 distinguishability but does not directly test radial‑gradient vulnerability predictions.
- **Break condition**: If compressed/isotropic models (ALBERT, MiniLM) show *lower* Type 1 rates in controlled hallucination induction, the radial‑gradient hypothesis is falsified.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Strong polarity coupling (α > 0.5) and cluster cohesion (β > 0) enable the existence of locally coherent but contextually inappropriate "wrong‑well" attractors (Type 2 hallucinations).
- **Mechanism**: Polarity axes within clusters create semantic contrast (e.g., hot vs. cold within a "temperature" cluster). Cohesion ensures tokens reliably map to their cluster centroid. Together, they form well‑defined semantic basins. When context provides insufficient disambiguation, the model can confidently converge to the wrong basin — generating detailed, specific text that is locally coherent (matches the cluster's internal polarity structure) but globally incorrect.
- **Core assumption**: Type 2 hallucinations require pre‑existing, tight cluster structure; without α and β, confident misclustering cannot occur.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - [abstract] "polarity structure (α > 0.5) is universal (11/11), cluster cohesion (β > 0) is universal (11/11)... These findings establish the geometric prerequisites for type‑specific hallucination detection."  
  - [section 6] "α exceeds 0.5 in all 11 models... β is significantly positive in all 11 models... clusters are centroid‑organized but diffuse... This is the expected geometry for clusters containing polarity structure."  
  - [section 4.2] "The signature is high local cluster membership... This produces hallucinations that are detailed, specific, and convincingly wrong."
- **Break condition**: If models with artificially weakened α/β (e.g., via adversarial training) show reduced Type 2 hallucination rates in targeted prompts, the cluster‑prerequisite claim is supported.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Sparse or absent cluster coverage in relevant embedding regions leads to Type 3 (coverage gap) hallucinations when queries require novel semantic compositions.
- **Mechanism**: The embedding space's cluster structure is derived from training data distribution. If a query's required concept combination lies in a region with no nearby centroids (low max centroid similarity) and high local variance, the model has no coherent semantic attractor to target. The generation becomes erratic or nonsensical due to lack of geometric guidance.
- **Core assumption**: Type 3 hallucinations arise from fundamental gaps in the embedding space's representational capacity, not from ambiguous context or wrong attractors.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - [abstract] "Type 3 (coverage gaps) where no cluster structure exists."  
  - [section 4.3] "The signature is weak membership across all clusters: max centroid similarity is low. (ii) High variance in local similarity."  
  - [section 3.1] "Tokens with related meanings occupy nearby regions, forming groups identifiable through standard clustering algorithms." (Implies: ungrouped regions lack structure.)
- **Break condition**: If coverage‑gap hallucinations persist even when query concepts *are* represented in the training data (verified via dataset inspection), the cause must be retrieval/activation failure, not embedding‑space absence.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept**: Static vs. contextual embedding geometry.  
  *Why needed here*: The paper analyzes **static** (input‑layer) embeddings to identify *geometric prerequisites*. Hallucination detection in practice uses **contextual** hidden states. Understanding this distinction is critical: cluster structure in static space enables, but does not guarantee, similar structure in dynamic contexts.  
  *Quick check*: If a model's static embeddings show strong clusters, under what conditions might its contextual representations during generation lose that structure?

- **Concept**: Cluster validation beyond inertia.  
  *Why needed here*: The paper uses **centroid‑based β** (own‑centroid minus other‑centroid similarity) rather than pairwise within‑cluster similarity. This accounts for "diffuse" clusters where members share a centroid but not direct similarity (common with polarity pairs). Misapplying standard silhouette scores could miss this geometry.  
  *Quick check*: Why would antonyms (opposites) cluster together, and how does that affect choice of cohesion metric?

- **Concept**: Information‑theoretic interpretation of embedding norms.  
  *Why needed here*: λ_r tests whether norm correlates with self‑information (I(τ) = −log f(τ)). A significant nonlinear relationship means the radial dimension encodes frequency‑based informativeness. This is key to interpreting Type 1 (low‑norm = generic) signatures.  
  *Quick check*: If norm and frequency are uncorrelated in a model, what would that imply for its Type 1 detection strategy?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Embedding Extractor -> Vocabulary Filter -> Clustering Engine -> Statistic Calculators -> Calibration Module -> Tiered Classifier

**Critical path**:
1. Extract and filter embeddings  
2. Cluster to get centroids  
3. Compute α, β, λ_r to validate geometric prerequisites  
4. For new token embedding *v*: compute H(*v*), ‖*v*‖, max centroid similarity  
5. Apply tiered rules with calibrated thresholds

**Design tradeoffs**:
- **k (cluster count)**: Larger k increases resolution but risks fragmentation; paper uses k=40 as fixed hyperparameter. Sensitivity untested.
- **Norm binning**: B=40 bins for λ_r fit; too coarse hides nonlinearity, too fine increases variance. Minimum token/bin (10) mitigates.
- **Thresholding**: Absolute thresholds (e.g., ‖*v*‖ < r_drift) fail across models; percentile‑based calibration (e.g., p_40 of norms) is essential.
- **Contextual extension**: Static analysis is prerequisite; moving to contextual hidden states requires tracking cluster membership across generation steps (Tier 2).

**Failure signatures**:
- **λ_r non‑significant** (p≥0.05): Model likely has compressed embeddings (ALBERT) or extreme isotropy (MiniLM) → Type 1 detection may fail.
- **α ≈ 0 in all clusters**: Polarity structure missing → Type 2 detection via polarity‑based confidence may be impaired.
- **β ≤ 0**: Clusters not tighter than background → k‑means artifacts; taxonomy invalid for this model.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Replicate Table 1**: For a new model (e.g., Llama‑2‑7B), extract embeddings, compute α, β, λ_r. Verify λ_r significance (p<0.05) and sign. Expectation: decoder model may show weaker |λ_r| (like GPT‑2).
2. **Controlled Type 1 induction**: Generate outputs from ALBERT and BERT‑base on low‑context prompts. Compare rates of tokens with H(*v*) < p_15 and ‖*v*‖ < p_40. Prediction: ALBERT shows higher Type 1 rate due to degraded λ_r.
3. **Trajectory discontinuity test**: For Type 2 detection, generate multi‑sentence outputs from a model with high β (e.g., DistilBERT). Compute cluster assignment jumps between sentences. Validate that high‑jump sequences correlate with known wrong‑well hallucination benchmarks (e.g., HaluEval).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question**: Do compressed‑embedding (ALBERT) and distilled‑isotropic (MiniLM) models exhibit higher rates of Type 1 (center‑drift) hallucinations as predicted?
- **Basis in paper**: Section 8.3 explicitly predicts elevated Type 1 vulnerability for these architectures due to degraded radial structure, though this remains unvalidated.
- **Why unresolved**: The prediction is derived from geometric diagnostics but has not been tested via controlled hallucination induction or comparative generation experiments.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Measure hallucination type frequencies under weak‑context prompts across ALBERT, MiniLM, and control models, correlating with radial gradient (λ_r) significance.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Do the observed geometric prerequisites (α, β, λ_r) and the three‑type taxonomy generalize to LLMs with >1.5 B parameters?
- **Basis in paper**: The conclusion lists extending to larger models as an immediate next step; the study only includes models up to 1.5 B parameters.
- **Why unresolved**: Scaling may alter embedding geometry; larger models could have different cluster cohesion, polarity coupling, or radial profiles.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Replicate the entire analysis (clustering, statistics) on models like Llama‑7B, GPT‑3, or PaLM, comparing the universality of α > 0.5, β > 0, and λ_r significance.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: How effective is the proposed tiered detection architecture at identifying hallucination types in practice?
- **Basis in paper**: Section 7 introduces the detection system, but Limitation 3 explicitly states it has not been benchmarked against datasets like HaluEval.
- **Why unresolved**: Thresholds are model‑specific and proposed heuristically; performance on real hallucination benchmarks is unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Implement the tiered system, evaluate on HaluEval or similar benchmarks, and report type‑specific precision/recall, adjusting thresholds via calibration.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Do contextual hidden‑state embeddings during generation exhibit the same cluster geometry as static input embeddings?
- **Basis in paper**: Limitation 1 notes all analysis used static embeddings, while hallucinations arise from contextualized states—a potential disconnect.
- **Why unresolved**: Static embeddings represent the model's "knowledge base," but generation involves dynamic contexts that may reorganize cluster structure.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Extract hidden states at multiple generation steps for diverse prompts, compute α, β, λ_r on these contextual embeddings, and compare distributions to static embedding results.

## Limitations
- Vocabulary filtering relies on `wordfreq` and token‑decoding heuristics, potentially excluding meaningful subwords and introducing selection bias.
- Clustering hyperparameters (k=40) are fixed without sensitivity analysis; models with small vocabularies or different embedding scales may produce different cluster structures.
- Validation of hallucination predictions is indirect; the paper does not measure actual hallucination rates against the proposed geometric signatures.
