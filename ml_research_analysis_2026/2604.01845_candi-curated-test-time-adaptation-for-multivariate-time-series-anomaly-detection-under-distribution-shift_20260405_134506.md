# CANDI: Curated Test-Time Adaptation for Multivariate Time-Series Anomaly Detection Under Distribution Shift

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.01845
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01845
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A test-time adaptation framework that improves MTSAD performance under distribution shift by up to 14% AUROC using less than 2% of test data, achieving state-of-the-art results on the 200-dataset TSB-AD benchmark.

## Executive Summary
CANDI is a test-time adaptation (TTA) framework designed to combat performance degradation in multivariate time-series anomaly detection (MTSAD) caused by real-world distribution shifts. It combines a False Positive Mining (FPM) strategy to selectively identify informative, ambiguous normal samples from the test stream with a lightweight, plug-and-play Spatiotemporally-Aware Normality Adaptation (SANA) module. SANA makes structured, residual updates while the pre-trained detector backbone remains frozen, thereby avoiding catastrophic forgetting. Evaluations across multiple benchmarks show significant gains in detection accuracy, parameter efficiency, and stability compared to prior TTA methods.

## Method Summary
CANDI operates in two phases. First, a reconstruction-based autoencoder is pre-trained exclusively on normal training data. Second, during test-time, it adapts to a shifted test distribution. The adaptation process begins offline by analyzing a normal validation set to identify "hard" normal samples—those with high anomaly scores—using the top α-percentile. Their latent vectors form a reference set (R_fp). Online, for each incoming test window, if its anomaly score exceeds a threshold, its latent vector is compared to R_fp via Mahalanobis distance. Samples with small distance are considered potential shifted normals (false positives) and accumulated into an adaptation buffer. Once the buffer holds enough samples (e.g., 16), only the parameters of the lightweight SANA module—attached to the input and output of the frozen backbone—are updated using a reconstruction loss. SANA uses temporal convolutions and inter-variable attention to model shift patterns, with its residual output (`X + tanh(g)*A`) ensuring stable, knowledge-preserving updates.

## Key Results
- Improves AUROC by up to **14%** compared to prior TTA methods on standard MTSAD benchmarks while using less than **2%** of test data for adaptation.
- Ranks **first** on the TSB-AD benchmark (200 datasets) across AUPRC, AUROC, VUS-PR, VUS-ROC, and F1 metrics.
- Ablation studies confirm both components are necessary: FPM without SANA causes forgetting, SANA without FPM is less effective.
- Introduces minimal overhead (~**+2.03MB**) and demonstrates low variance across random seeds and hyperparameter choices.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Selective Adaptation via False Positive Mining (FPM)
- **Claim:** FPM provides informative learning signals while reducing catastrophic forgetting risk by focusing adaptation on boundary-case normal samples.
- **Mechanism:** FPM identifies test samples that score as anomalous (high reconstruction error) but are near high-scoring normal validation samples in the latent space (via Mahalanobis distance). These "hard" normals represent underrepresented patterns that have shifted. Adapting only to these curated samples avoids updating on clear anomalies or trivial normals.
- **Core assumption:** The pre-trained encoder's latent space structures normal patterns such that false positives cluster near the decision boundary.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract/section] FPM selects samples based on score and latent proximity.
  - [ablation] Using FPM without SANA (full updates) harms performance; SANA without FPM still helps but is less effective.
  - [corpus] Prior work (M2N2) adapts to *all* predicted normals, missing informative boundary cases.
- **Break condition:** If the latent space is poorly structured (collapsed/entangled), Mahalanobis proximity may not reflect semantic similarity. Non-representative validation data also impairs building `R_fp`.

### Mechanism 2: Stable Updates via Spatiotemporally-Aware Normality Adaptation (SANA)
- **Claim:** A frozen backbone with a lightweight, structured adapter enables stable, knowledge-preserving updates.
- **Mechanism:** SANA adds small residual pathways (temporal convolutions + inter-variable attention) at input/output. Only these parameters are updated. The convolutional layers capture temporal shifts per variable; attention captures cross-variable shifts. The residual design (`X + tanh(g)*A`) and frozen backbone preserve core representations.
- **Core assumption:** Shifts manifest as changes in temporal dynamics and inter-variable dependencies; a shallow adapter can correct them without retraining the entire model.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract/section] SANA is a plug-and-play module capturing temporal and relational shifts.
  - [section] Only SANA parameters are updated; backbone θ remains frozen.
  - [ablation] SANA-only outperforms M2N2; updating a linear head instead of SANA performs poorly.
- **Break condition:** Extremely large or non-linear shifts may exceed a shallow adapter's capacity. Poor gating initialization could limit adaptation.

### Mechanism 3: Synergistic Curation and Safe Adaptation
- **Claim:** Combining curated sample selection (FPM) with a frozen backbone and structured adapter (SANA) yields robust, efficient TTA.
- **Mechanism:** In a loop: (1) Pre-compute `R_fp` from validation hard normals; (2) For test windows, score → if high, check latent distance to `R_fp`; (3) If close, add to adaptation set; (4) Periodically update SANA on accumulated set. The loop refines the adapter using reliable signals while the backbone's latent space (used for FPM) remains stable.
- **Core assumption:** A stable latent space is maintained by freezing the backbone, and FPM's criterion remains valid if adapter updates don't drastically alter encoder outputs. The small, curated adaptation set suffices for common shifts.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract/section] CANDI combines selective signals with a safe mechanism for robustness.
  - [results] CANDI uses fewer samples than M2N2 but achieves higher AUROC. "CANDI Hard" (only hard samples) outperforms M2N2.
  - [stability] Low variance across seeds indicates robust dynamics.
- **Break condition:** Abrupt/catastrophic shifts (e.g., sensor failure) may contaminate the adaptation set with true anomalies (observed 10-25%) or exceed adapter capability. A fixed `R_fp` may fail if shift evolves beyond initial reference patterns.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:**
  Pre-trained Backbone (frozen autoencoder) -> Encoder (produces latent `z`) -> Decoder (produces reconstruction)
  FPM module (offline: builds `R_fp` from validation; online: computes Mahalanobis distance to `R_fp`) -> Adaptation Buffer `A`
  SANA Modules (lightweight adapters at input and output) -> Adjusted input/output to backbone

- **Critical path:**
  1. **Offline:** Train autoencoder on normal training data. Validate on normal validation set. Run FPM: compute scores on validation → select top α% "hard normals" → store their latents in `R_fp`. Compute μ, Σ from all validation latents.
  2. **Online per test batch:** For each window, compute anomaly score and latent `z`. If score > τ, compute min Mahalanobis distance to `R_fp`. If distance < δ, add window to adaptation buffer `A`.
  3. **Adaptation trigger:** When |A| ≥ batch_size (e.g., 16), run forward pass through frozen backbone with SANA-adjusted inputs/outputs. Compute reconstruction loss. Backpropagate **only** through SANA parameters. Update via SGD. Clear `A`.
  4. Continue streaming test windows, repeating steps 2-3.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **FPM α (percentile) vs. δ (distance threshold):** Lower α (e.g., 0.5%) selects very hard normals (fewer, more informative samples but riskier if validation set small). Higher α (5%) includes more, possibly noisier samples. δ (e.g., χ² 5th percentile) controls strictness of latent proximity.
  - **SANA hidden dimension `h`:** Larger `h` (e.g., 512) increases adaptation capacity but slightly higher forgetting risk (though backbone frozen). Paper shows even h=32 (0.09MB) helps for many shifts.
  - **Adaptation batch size:** Accumulating samples (min 16) trades off update latency vs. gradient stability. Too small → unstable/inefficient; too large → slower reaction to non-stationarity.
  - **Frozen backbone:** Preserves latent space for FPM but cannot adapt core features. Assumes shifts are "correctable" via input/output residual adjustments.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - **No adaptation (performance equals pre-trained):** Adaptation buffer `A` remains empty. Diagnose: Verify τ (α-percentile) computation, Mahalanobis distance calculation, and δ threshold. Ensure latents are L2-normalized as required.
  - **Performance collapse after adaptation:** Likely due to FPM contamination (too many true anomalies in `A`) or overly aggressive SANA updates. Diagnose: Monitor anomaly ratio in `A`; reduce learning rate or increase buffer accumulation threshold.
  - **High variance across random seeds:** Suggests unstable adaptation dynamics. Diagnose: Check learning rate, gating initialization (`tanh(g)`), and buffer size. Paper reports low std; high variance indicates mismatch.
  - **SANA overfitting to small buffer:** If buffer size is too small (e.g., consistently 16 samples) or samples are temporally autocorrelated, SANA may overfit. Ensure buffer accumulates a mixed sample set before update.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Contaminated training data:** How can CANDI be extended to handle training data that contains anomalies, violating the standard normality assumption?
- **Dynamic threshold adjustment:** How should the detection threshold be adjusted during test-time adaptation to maintain a desired false positive rate as the anomaly score distribution shifts?
- **Extreme shift failure recovery:** What mechanisms can ensure resilience when the test-time distribution shift is abrupt or catastrophic?
- **Improved false positive mining:** Can FPM be refined to more accurately distinguish true false positives (normal but high-scoring) from actual anomalies? The paper observes 10%-25% of selected adaptation samples are true anomalies.

## Limitations
- **Architecture specifics omitted:** Exact MLP layer sizes, bottleneck dimension, and SANA internal parameters (conv kernel, attention heads) are not detailed, forcing assumptions for reproduction.
- **Hyperparameter reporting gaps:** Optimal learning rates, gating initializations, and adaptation steps per dataset are not provided, only search ranges.
- **Sensitivity and contamination:** Fixed FPM thresholds (α=5%, δ from χ²) lack sensitivity analysis; observed contamination (10-25% anomalies in adaptation set) may destabilize learning under larger shifts.
- **Robustness to abrupt shifts:** The frozen backbone + residual adapter assumption may fail under severe distribution changes; the failure boundary is unexplored.
