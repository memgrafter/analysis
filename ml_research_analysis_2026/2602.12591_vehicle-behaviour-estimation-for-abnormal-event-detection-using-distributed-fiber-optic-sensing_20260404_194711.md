# Vehicle behaviour estimation for abnormal event detection using distributed fiber optic sensing

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.12591
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12591
- **Reference count**: 15
- **Primary result**: The proposed method achieved 87.9% tracking accuracy on 124 vehicles and 80% lane change detection accuracy, enabling early congestion detection via identification of lane-change clusters.

## Executive Summary
Distributed fiber optic sensing (DFOS) provides continuous vibration monitoring along highways, but conventional aggregation techniques lack lane-level discrimination and thus cannot detect single-lane abnormalities such as accidents. The authors introduce a method that clusters DFOS-derived hit points to reconstruct individual vehicle trajectories and identifies lane changes through shifts in the vibration spectral centroid. Validation on Japan’s Shin‑Tomei Expressway using a test vehicle demonstrated 87.9% tracking accuracy across 124 vehicles over 8.87 km and 80% lane‑change detection accuracy from 30 events. Aggregating these detections allows early recognition of congestion‑forming clusters.

## Method Summary
Raw DFOS data undergoes hit extraction to obtain discrete spatial points where vehicle‑induced vibrations exceed a threshold. These points are clustered with a density‑based algorithm (e.g., DBSCAN) to separate trajectories of distinct vehicles based on temporal and spatial proximity. For each trajectory, spectral analysis computes the centroid of the vibration frequency content. A lane change induces a consistent shift in the centroid due to altered wheel‑fiber coupling and vehicle orientation; thresholding this shift yields binary lane‑change events. Finally, the spatiotemporal density of lane‑change detections is examined to identify clusters that often precede congestion, serving as an early warning system.

## Key Results
- 87.9% tracking accuracy on 124 vehicles across 8.87 km of highway.
- 80% lane change detection accuracy from 30 labeled lane‑change events.
- Demonstrated that clusters of detected lane changes can serve as an early indicator of developing congestion.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Clustering effectively separates overlapping vehicle signals in the continuous DFOS data, enabling reconstruction of individual paths. The spectral centroid provides a compact representation of the vibration’s frequency distribution. When a vehicle changes lanes, the relative geometry between its tires and the fiber changes, altering the vibration spectrum in a predictable manner and shifting the centroid. This shift remains detectable amid background noise, and repeated localized occurrences signal abnormal lane‑changing behavior typical of incidents or emerging congestion.

## Foundational Learning
- **Distributed Fiber Optic Sensing (DFOS)**: *Why needed*: To capture dense, continuous vibration measurements along the entire fiber length. *Quick check*: Explain the principle of phase‑sensitive OTDR and how Rayleigh backscatter localizes disturbances.
- **Hit Point Extraction**: *Why needed*: Converts raw vibrations into discrete spatial points indicative of vehicle presence. *Quick check*: Implement a simple threshold‑based detector on a sample DFOS trace to isolate candidate vehicle positions.
- **Clustering (e.g., DBSCAN)**: *Why needed*: Groups hit points belonging to the same vehicle while separating different vehicles. *Quick check*: Apply DBSCAN to a small set of hit points and verify that distinct clusters correspond to separate trajectories.
- **Spectral Centroid**: *Why needed*: Characterizes the frequency distribution of a vibration signal; lane changes cause a measurable shift. *Quick check*: Compute FFTs of vibration snippets from straight driving and lane‑change segments and compare centroids.
- **Lane Change Detection via Thresholding**: *Why needed*: Transforms continuous centroid shifts into discrete events for aggregation. *Quick check*: Select a threshold that maximizes F1 score on a labeled validation set of lane changes.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**:  
DFOS Data -> Preprocessing -> Clustering -> Trajectory Estimation -> Spectral Analysis -> Lane Change Detection -> Congestion Event Aggregation

**Critical Path**: The pipeline is sequential; errors in early stages (e.g., missed hits, poor clustering) propagate to trajectory quality and ultimately to spectral features and lane‑change decisions.

**Design Tradeoffs**:
- Clustering density parameters: Tighter settings reduce noise but may split vehicles in dense traffic; looser settings merge distinct vehicles.
- Spectral window length: Longer windows improve frequency resolution but sacrifice temporal precision for short lane changes.
- Detection threshold: Higher thresholds avoid false positives from road roughness but risk missing subtle lane changes.

**Failure Signatures**:
- Fragmented or missing trajectories indicating clustering failures.
- Spurious centroid shifts caused by road surface anomalies rather than lane changes.
- Elevated false alarm rates in heavily congested traffic due to overlapping vehicle signals.

**First Experiments**:
1. Parameter tuning for DBSCAN (eps, min_samples) on a ground‑truth subset to maximize tracking F1‑score.
2. Calibration of the spectral centroid shift threshold using a labeled set of lane‑change events.
3. Correlation of aggregated lane‑change clusters with independent traffic speed measurements (e.g., loop detectors) to validate early congestion detection.

## Limitations
- Evaluation restricted to a single expressway (Shin‑Tomei, Japan); generalizability to other regions, road types, and traffic densities is unproven.
- Lane change detection relies on a small sample (30 events); no confidence intervals, false‑alarm rates, or error breakdowns are provided.
- Spectral centroid shifts may be confounded by non‑vehicle vibrations (e.g., road roughness, weather) without sensitivity analysis.
