# Active Inference Agency Formalization, Metrics, and Convergence Assessments

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.21319
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21319
- Reference count: 14
- Primary result: Formalizes agency via active inference as a smooth convex agentic function balancing curiosity and empowerment, proves logarithmic convergence in sparse environments, and introduces the STARC metric for detecting mesa-optimizers.

## Executive Summary
The paper proposes a formal definition of agency within the active inference framework, where an agent maintains autopoiesis by balancing curiosity (prediction error minimization) and empowerment (control channel maximization). The resulting agentic loss function is shown to be smooth and convex, and under sparsity assumptions it converges logarithmically during optimization, making such agentic functions likely to emerge spontaneously during training. To detect mesa-optimizers, the authors introduce the STARC metric, which measures the distance between a system's behavioral reward and the ideal agentic objective in a canonicalized reward space. This work provides both theoretical foundations and a practical tool for AI safety research on emergent agency.

## Method Summary
The core method defines an agentic function A = α·L_Curiosity + β·L_Empowerment + γ·L_Mesa, where L_Curiosity is a KL divergence between predicted and actual state distributions, and L_Empowerment is mutual information between action sequences and future states. The authors prove A is C∞ smooth and convex after regularization. They analyze the measure of agentic functions in function space and show that, despite being a vanishingly small subset, they enjoy logarithmic convergence rates O(s log d / T) in sparse reinforcement learning problems. For detection, the STARC metric canonicalizes any reward function to a standardized form and computes the L1/L2 distance between a system's induced reward and the ideal agentic reward derived from A. The paper thus bridges formal agency theory with a concrete metric for mesa-optimizer identification.

## Key Results
- Agency emerges as a continuous representation that achieves autopoiesis through a dynamic balance between curiosity and empowerment.
- The agentic function A is smooth (C∞) and convex, ensuring favorable optimization landscapes without pathological local minima.
- Although agentic functions occupy a vanishingly small fraction of all functions, they exhibit logarithmic convergence in sparse environments, implying a non-negligible probability of spontaneous emergence during training.
- The STARC metric provides a quantitative tool to classify and detect mesa-optimizers by measuring distance to the ideal agentic objective in canonicalized reward space.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Agency emerges as a continuous representation that maintains autopoiesis through a dynamic balance between curiosity (prediction error minimization) and empowerment (control channel maximization).
- **Mechanism:** The agent optimizes a composite loss function `A = α·L_Curiosity + β·L_Empowerment + γ·L_Mesa`. Curiosity (`D_KL(p||q)`) drives the agent toward novelty, increasing its historical Kolmogorov complexity. Empowerment (`I(A_n; S_{t+n})`) structures this exploration by maximizing reliable control over future states. Their tension produces a self-sustaining, non-computable behavioral trajectory.
- **Core assumption:** Autopoiesis (self-maintenance) is the fundamental goal of living systems, mathematically formalized as active inference.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Agency is conceptualized as a Continuous Representation of accumulated experience that achieves autopoiesis through a dynamic balance between curiosity... and empowerment."
  - [page 3] Defines `L_Curiosity` and `L_Empowerment` and states: "Thus, when speaking of living beings as agents... agency arises naturally in a system that minimizes the following loss function: A=α·L_Curiosity +β·L_Empowerment +γ·L_Mesa."
  - [corpus] Weak direct evidence; neighbor paper "Active Inference as a Model of Agency" (FMR 0.66) is conceptually adjacent but not cited in the target paper.
- **Break condition:** If either `L_Curiosity` or `L_Empowerment` dominates unconditionally, the system becomes either entirely chaotic (failing empowerment's goal-directedness) or entirely predictable/compressible (failing curiosity's novelty requirement), losing the autopoietic balance.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The proposed agentic function `A` is smooth (`C^∞`) and convex, granting it favorable optimization landscapes (e.g., no pathological local minima) that facilitate convergence during training.
- **Mechanism:** The KL divergence and Shannon entropy components are smooth almost everywhere after standard regularization (e.g., `log(q(x)+ε)`). The `max` operator in empowerment's mutual information formulation inherits smoothness from the underlying function family (citing DeVore & Sharpley, 1984). Convexity arises from the linear combination of convex components (`L_Curiosity` is convex in the model's predictive distribution; `L_Empowerment`'s mutual information is convex in the action distribution).
- **Core assumption:** Smoothness and convexity are preserved through the specific construction of `A` and are meaningful properties for the high-dimensional, non-linear function spaces typical of neural network loss landscapes.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [page 5] "The KL divergence is smooth everywhere, except at points where... With this practical addition, the function becomes smooth `C^∞`... Since MI is a simple algebraic combination of entropies, it inherits the same smoothness characteristics."
  - [page 5] Cites Theorem 7.1 from DeVore & Sharpley to argue smoothness of the maximal function.
  - [corpus] No direct evidence; this is a formal analysis within the paper.
- **Break condition:** If the regularization (e.g., `ε`) is removed or if the functional form of `L_Curiosity` or `L_Empowerment` changes (e.g., using a non-convex divergence), the global smoothness/convexity guarantee fails, potentially introducing problematic optimization dynamics.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Although agentic functions form a vanishingly small subset (`~ (2ε/M)^n`) of all possible functions, they exhibit logarithmic convergence rates (`O(log(1/ε))`) in sparse environments, making their spontaneous emergence during model training probable.
- **Mechanism:** The low dimensionality (≤3) of the agentic subspace (linear combination of fixed `C0`, `E0`, `A0` vectors) gives it negligible volume in high-dimensional function space. However, the combination of smoothness, convexity, and inherent sparsity in reinforcement learning problems (few non-zero rewards) allows optimization algorithms (like gradient descent with L1 regularization) to converge to such functions at a rate where effective error `ε` decreases exponentially with iterations `T` (i.e., `O(s log d / T)`), decoupling the probability of finding an agentic function from the tiny initial measure.
- **Core assumption:** Real-world RL environments and training objectives produce sparse optimal policies, and standard optimizers implicitly or explicitly exploit this sparsity.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [page 6] Derives measure: `P(function is agentic) = (2ε/M)^n` and states: "agentic functions occupy a very small fraction in the space of all functions."
  - [page 7] "For smooth convex functions, standard gradient descent has a rate of O(d/T)... if the optimal solution is s-sparse... specialized algorithms... can achieve a rate... O(s log d / T)... significantly faster... We have already shown that A is convex and smooth."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence; neighbor "LIMI: Less is More for Agency" (FMR 0.58) discusses emergent agency but not convergence rates.
- **Break condition:** In dense, non-sparse reward environments where `s ≈ d`, the logarithmic acceleration disappears, and convergence reverts to `O(d/T)`. This drastically reduces the probability of stumbling upon the narrow agentic subspace within typical training budgets.

## Foundational Learning
### Active Inference (Free Energy Principle)
- **Why needed here:** It is the overarching theoretical framework defining agency as autopoiesis via minimizing a variational free energy that decomposes into `L_Curiosity` (complexity/accuracy) and `L_Empowerment` (expected free energy for action). Understanding this is core to the definition.
- **Quick check question:** In active inference, what two paths does an agent have to minimize "surprise," and how do they map to curiosity and empowerment?

### KL Divergence & Mutual Information
- **Why needed here:** These are the exact mathematical forms for `L_Curiosity` (`D_KL(p||q)`) and `L_Empowerment` (`I(A_n; S_{t+n})`). Their properties (smoothness, convexity, information-theoretic meaning) are central to the paper's proofs and metric.
- **Quick check question:** Why does adding a small constant `ε` inside the logarithm make the KL divergence `C^∞` smooth on the probability simplex?

### Convex Optimization & Convergence Rates
- **Why needed here:** The paper's claims about favorable optimization and logarithmic convergence rely on establishing the agentic function's convexity and applying standard results from convex analysis (e.g., Agarwal et al., 2012) to sparse problems.
- **Quick check question:** What is the key difference in convergence rate between a general smooth convex problem and one where the optimal solution is `s`-sparse?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Experience Buffer -> World Model; Experience Buffer -> Policy/Planner; World Model -> L_Curiosity; World Model + Policy -> L_Empowerment; Mesa Signal -> Loss Aggregator; Loss Aggregator (A = α·L_Curiosity + β·L_Empowerment + γ·L_Mesa) -> Total Loss -> Backpropagation. Separately: Trained Policy -> Behavioral Equivalent Reward -> STARC Canonicalization -> Distance to Ideal Agentic Reward.

### Critical path
Implement world model → compute curiosity loss → compute empowerment loss (requires planning/simulation) → obtain mesa signal → aggregate → backpropagate total loss A. Separately, after training, run STARC evaluation pipeline.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Horizon `n` in Empowerment:** Longer `n` increases computational cost for planning/simulation but better captures long-term control.
- **Weights `α, β, γ`:** Balance determines agent type. `β=0` yields a pure curiosity-driven explorer; `α=0` yields a pure empowerment-driven controller. `γ` tunes susceptibility to mesa-objectives.
- **`ε` in Smoothness:** Smaller `ε` is more accurate but numerically less stable; must be tuned for the specific model's output scale.

### Failure signatures
- **Collapse to `L_Mesa`-dominated:** `A` gradient updates are overridden by a strong, internal mesa-objective. Monitor: `|γ·L_Mesa| >> |α·L_Curiosity + β·L_Empowerment|`.
- **Non-smooth optimization:** Loss spikes or NaNs near decision boundaries. Sign of `q(x)` approaching zero without `ε` safeguard.
- **High STARC distance:** `M(F, A) > threshold` indicates the learned behavior is not agentic per definition; likely a mesa-optimizer or a brittle policy.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Smoothness Sanity Check:** On a simple tabular MDP, compute `A` analytically. Verify gradient norms are bounded and finite across all state-action pairs, even with tiny `q`.
2. **Convergence Rate Test:** Train a small neural network on a synthetic sparse RL task (e.g., chain domain). Plot `ε` (error to ideal agentic policy) vs. training steps on log-log scale. Fit slope; expect ~`O(log T)` improvement, not `O(1/sqrt(T))`.
3. **STARC Validation:** Train an agent with known `α,β,γ` on a simple environment. Compute its `R_F` via behavioral cloning. Compute `R_A` from the known ground-truth `A`. Verify `M(F, A)` is near zero. Then, inject a conflicting mesa-objective (`γ` negative/large) and verify `M(F, A)` increases significantly.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Does the active inference-based definition of agency fully account for all possible instrumental goals pursued by advanced AI agents, or are there goals that inherently escape this framework?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] On page 4, the authors state: "This does not mean that there are no more instrumental goals or that unmentioned behaviors will be described via active inference."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper only empirically maps a few classical instrumental goals (e.g., self-preservation, resource acquisition) to curiosity and empowerment components. There is no systematic evaluation against a comprehensive taxonomy of instrumental goals derived from AI safety literature, leaving open whether the definition is exhaustive.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A rigorous analysis applying the active inference formalism to a broad, formally defined set of instrumental goals (e.g., from meta-learning, gaming, or real-world deployment scenarios) to test for coverage or counterexamples.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Does the logarithmic convergence property of agentic functions hold in non-sparse (dense-reward) environments, or does the convergence rate degrade significantly?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The convergence analysis (pages 5–7) focuses on sparse tasks and leverages sparsity assumptions (e.g., L1 regularization benefits). The authors note that "functions in RL are typically sparse" but acknowledge real environments may vary, without extending the theoretical guarantee.
- **Why unresolved:** The logarithmic convergence is proven under sparsity assumptions, but many practical RL settings have dense or shaped rewards. It is unclear if the favorable convergence persists, which affects the claim of "high probability for spontaneous emergence" in diverse training scenarios.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Theoretical extension of the convergence analysis to general (non-sparse) reward structures, complemented by empirical measurements of emergence rates in dense-reward environments compared to sparse ones.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How accurate and robust is the proposed STARC-based metric for detecting mesa-optimizers in practice, especially when applied to complex, trained neural networks?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The metric is introduced on pages 8–9 as a tool for "classifying and detecting mesa-optimizers," but the paper provides no empirical validation or case studies on actual AI systems.
- **Why unresolved:** The formalism is derived from behavioral equivalence ideals, but its sensitivity to implementation details, function approximations, and nested optimization structures in modern models remains untested. Practical challenges like partial observability or non-stationarity could distort the metric.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Benchmarking the metric on synthetic environments with known mesa-optimizer instances (e.g., from gradient hacking or hidden objective studies) and comparing its detection performance against existing interpretability or anomaly detection methods.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How does the non-additivity of the agency metric M affect the analysis of hierarchical or composite agents with nested mesa-optimizers?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] On page 8, the authors note that M may violate additivity: "M(Lc + Le + A mesa) might equal M(Lc + Le) − M(A mesa)." This suggests metric values for a system may not decompose predictably across its subsystems, but implications are unexplored.
- **Why unresolved:** Mesa-optimization often involves inner optimizers within outer ones. If the metric is non-additive, measuring "agency distance" for a whole system may not reflect the contributions of its parts, complicating detection and attribution of inner optimization.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Analytical study of the metric's behavior on multi-agent or modular architectures with known inner/outer objectives, investigating whether non-additivity correlates with specific structural properties or failure modes.

## Limitations
- `L_Mesa` is left undefined, making the agentic loss incomplete and its practical implementation ambiguous.
- The ideal agentic reward `R_A` is a theoretical construct with no constructive derivation provided for arbitrary environments.
- The STARC metric requires a ground-truth reward `R` for canonicalization; for mesa-optimizer detection, the appropriate `R` is often unknown, limiting applicability.
- Theoretical guarantees (smoothness, convexity, logarithmic convergence) rely on sparsity assumptions that may not hold in dense-reward environments.
- No empirical validation on real RL benchmarks; emergence probability remains a theoretical speculation.
