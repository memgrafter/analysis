# Synthetic Craquelure Generation for Unsupervised Painting Restoration

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.12742  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12742  
- **Reference Count**: 38  
- **Primary Result**: Full pipeline achieves F1-score 61.36 and SSIM 64.87 on zero‑shot real painting restoration, outperforming state‑of‑the‑art photographic restoration models.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenging problem of unsupervised detection and restoration of fine craquelure patterns in paintings, where manual annotations are scarce and cracks closely resemble brushstrokes. The core method couples a classical morphological detector (black/white top‑hat transforms) with a detector‑guided SegFormer model fine‑tuned via LoRA on synthetic Bézier‑crack data. The refinement uses a masked hybrid loss and logit adjustment to focus learning on ambiguous regions. Crack masks refined by the model then guide anisotropic diffusion inpainting, which preserves original brushwork by diffusing along isophotes. In zero‑shot evaluation on real paintings, the pipeline significantly outperforms prior restoration approaches (e.g., SSIM 64.87 vs. Pik‑Fix 55.71) and a traditional Grassfire baseline, while maintaining artistic detail. Ablation studies confirm the critical contributions of LoRA adaptation and the masked loss.

## Method Summary
The proposed pipeline consists of four stages. First, a synthetic craquelure generator creates realistic crack masks by rendering tapered, branched Bézier curves onto clean paintings; parameters include 80–150 curves per image, control points perturbed with σ≈82, radius function *r(t)∼𝒩(2·(1−|t−0.5|), 0.5²)*, branching probability *p_br∈[0.3,0.5]*, followed by erosion, Gaussian blur, and thresholding. Second, a morphological detector employs black and white top‑hat transforms (disk SE radius 2) on the input painting, thresholds at 180, and retains connected components ≥5 px to produce a coarse mask *m*. Third, a SegFormer MiT‑B0 model is adapted to 4‑channel input (RGB concatenated with *m*) and fine‑tuned via LoRA (rank 8, α=16, dropout 0.1) on the synthetic paired data. Training uses a masked hybrid loss: weighted cross‑entropy with weights *w = m + 0.01·(1−m)* plus Dice loss (λ=2), and logit adjustment *p̃ = p + γ·m* (γ=1) applied only during training. Optimization uses AdamW (lr=2e‑4, batch=8) with spatial augmentations and early stopping on IoU. Fourth, restoration is performed by applying anisotropic diffusion (λ=0.25, *K*=127, 20 iterations) exclusively within the refined crack mask, with an alternative Modified Trimmed Mean (MTM) baseline. The pipeline operates zero‑shot on real paintings, requiring no real annotations.

## Key Results
- Zero‑shot detection on real paintings: **F1‑score 61.36**.  
- Restoration quality: **SSIM 64.87**, surpassing Pik‑Fix (55.71) and Grassfire baseline.  
- Ablation: Removing LoRA or the masked loss drops F1 to 27.64 or 49.45 respectively, confirming their necessity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Synthetic Bézier‑crack data enables unsupervised crack segmentation by providing a scalable, morphology‑accurate prior that generalizes to real paintings.  
- **Mechanism**: The generator models craquelure as networks of tapered, branched Bézier curves with perturbed control points and radius modulation. This creates diverse, realistic crack masks over clean paintings, forming a paired dataset (image, mask) for training.  
- **Core assumption**: The geometric and statistical properties (thinness, branching, tapering) of Bézier‑simulated cracks sufficiently match real craquelure patterns, allowing a model trained solely on synthetic data to transfer to real artworks.  
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - [abstract] “driven by a domain‑specific synthetic craquelure generator, which simulates realistic branching and tapered fissure geometry using Bézier trajectories.”  
  - [section IV] Details the Bézier curve definition, branching logic, and tapered radius function *r(t)∼𝒩(α(1−|t−0.5|), σ²_r)*.  
  - [corpus] Weak direct evidence; neighboring papers use synthetic data for heritage tasks but not specifically for craquelure. The claim’s validity rests on the paper’s own evaluation.  
- **Break condition**: If synthetic crack topology (e.g., branching frequency, curvature distribution) diverges significantly from real craquelure in a target painting style, the SegFormer model may fail to suppress false positives from brushstrokes, degrading F1‑score.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Injecting the coarse morphological detector’s output as a spatial prior into a SegFormer model fine‑tuned via LoRA effectively refines crack masks by focusing learning on ambiguous regions and mitigating class imbalance.  
- **Mechanism**: The detector (top‑hat + filtering) provides an initial binary map *m*. This map is (a) concatenated as a fourth input channel to SegFormer, and (b) added to the model’s logits (*p̃ = p + γm*) during training. A masked hybrid loss (*L_CE* weighted by *w = m + α(1−m)* + *λL_Dice*) ensures gradient focus on detector‑activated areas while maintaining global crack structure.  
- **Core assumption**: The morphological detector’s false positives (brushstrokes) and false negatives (missed cracks) are spatially consistent enough that a learned refinement can correct them using the detector map as a reliable, if noisy, guide.  
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - [section III‑B] “injecting the morphological map as an input spatial prior” and logit adjustment Eq. (4).  
  - [Table III] Ablation shows removing the masked loss or LoRA causes major performance drops (F1 from 61.36 to 49.45 or 27.64).  
  - [corpus] Neighbor “Allure of Craquelure” uses variational generation, not detector‑guided refinement, suggesting novelty in this hybrid approach.  
- **Break condition**: If the initial detector map is extremely sparse (missing >50% of true cracks) or overly dense (mostly brushstrokes), the spatial prior may bias SegFormer toward incorrect regions, leading to over‑ or under‑segmentation that the masked loss cannot correct.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Anisotropic Diffusion (AD) inpainting, constrained to refined crack masks, reconstructs missing paint by diffusing along isophotes, preserving original brushwork texture and edges better than simpler filters.  
- **Mechanism**: AD iteratively updates crack pixel intensities *I* using directional differences *D_k* weighted by conductivity *c_k = 1/(1 + (|D_k|/K)²)*. This diffuses values parallel to edges while preserving perpendicular intensity discontinuities. The process is applied only within the crack mask, using surrounding non‑crack pixels as Dirichlet boundaries.  
- **Core assumption**: The underlying paint texture and brushstroke gradients vary smoothly *within* crack neighborhoods, and the crack boundaries align with high‑contrast edges that AD’s edge‑stopping function can preserve.  
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - [section III‑C] Equation (5) and description: “diffuses intensity along homogeneous directions while preserving edges.”  
  - [Table II] AD consistently yields higher SSIM than MTM (e.g., 64.87 vs. 55.71 for MTM on Image 4).  
  - [Figure 6] Qualitative shows AD maintains sharper structures under mask dilation compared to MTM blurring.  
- **Break condition**: For very wide cracks (>5px) or cracks crossing highly textured, non‑stationary brushstroke regions, AD’s linear diffusion may propagate inconsistent textures, causing blurring or ghosting if the boundary conditions are not locally representative.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Morphological Top‑Hat Transform**  
  - **Why needed here**: Provides the initial, over‑inclusive crack candidate mask by isolating low‑intensity, small‑scale structures (dark cracks) from brighter textured backgrounds. It is the anchor for the detector‑guided learning.  
  - **Quick check question**: What is the difference between black and white top‑hat, and why is black top‑hat primarily used for dark cracks on light backgrounds?  

- **Concept: Low‑Rank Adaptation (LoRA)**  
  - **Why needed here**: Enables efficient fine‑tuning of a large transformer (SegFormer) on a small synthetic dataset without full fine‑tuning, preventing overfitting and catastrophic forgetting of pretrained features.  
  - **Quick check question**: How does LoRA’s decomposition *W' = W + BA* with rank *r << d* reduce trainable parameters compared to updating *W* directly?  

- **Concept: Anisotropic Diffusion for Inpainting**  
  - **Why needed here**: Performs the final restoration by filling crack regions in a way that respects image edges, crucial for preserving fine brushwork adjacent to cracks.  
  - **Quick check question**: What does the conductivity term *c_k* in Perona‑Malik diffusion control, and how does it preserve edges?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:  
  1. Input: RGB painting.  
  2. Morphological Detector: Applies black/white top‑hat, thresholds, size‑filters → coarse crack mask *m*.  
  3. Refinement Network: SegFormer‑B0 with LoRA (in Q, K, V, FFN projections). Input: [RGB, *m*] (4 channels). Output: refined crack probability map.  
  4. Training Pipeline: Synthetic data generator (Bézier cracks) → augmentations → masked hybrid loss + logit adjustment.  
  5. Inpainting: Apply AD (or MTM) only to pixels where refined mask > threshold.  

- **Critical path**:  
  Data generation → Detector (fixed, no learning) → SegFormer fine‑tuning (LoRA + detector‑guided) → AD inpainting. The detector output *m* is a persistent spatial prior through both refinement and training guidance.

- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - Synthetic realism vs. scalability: Bézier model captures key geometry but may miss subtle pigment‑loss texturing vs. real cracks; however, it enables unlimited data.  
  - Parameter efficiency (LoRA) vs. capacity: LoRA prevents overfitting on synthetic data but may limit adaptation if synthetic‑real domain gap is large.  
  - AD vs. MTM: AD better preserves edges but has more hyperparameters (λ, K, iterations); MTM is simpler but risks blurring.

- **Failure signatures**:  
  - Detector stage: Excessive false positives if top‑hat SE size/dilation is wrong; failure on low‑contrast cracks if threshold `180` is inappropriate.  
  - Refinement stage: LoRA rank `r=8` too low → underfitting; masked loss weight `α=0.01` mis‑set → model ignores detector prior.  
  - Inpainting stage: AD `K` too high → over‑diffusion across edges; crack mask not dilated enough → unfilled gaps.

- **First 3 experiments**:  
  1. Validate synthetic generator: Render Bézier cracks on a clean painting, compare crack width distribution, curvature, and branching statistics against manually annotated real cracks (if available) or visual realism.  
  2. Test detector‑guided signal: Ablate the four‑channel input (use only RGB) and compare validation IoU/F1 to the full model. Expect significant drop (~15‑20% F1 per Table III).  
  3. Compare inpainting quality on a held‑out synthetic pair: Apply AD vs. MTM to the same crack mask; compute SSIM/LPIPS against the original *undamaged* clean painting. AD should yield higher SSIM and lower LPIPS (closer to original texture).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1  
- **Question**: To what extent does the synthetic craquelure generator capture the morphological diversity of real cracks across different painting styles and aging conditions?  
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The paper relies on a synthetic dataset generated via Bézier curves and assumes it suffices for generalization, but evaluation is performed on only four paintings, which may not represent the full variability of real craquelure.  
- **Why unresolved**: Real craquelure exhibits complex branching patterns, varying widths, and interactions with underlying brushstrokes that may not be fully replicated by the current generative model; without a larger, diverse test set, it is unclear if the synthetic data covers the necessary distribution.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A systematic quantitative comparison between synthetic and real crack morphology using metrics such as fractal dimension, branch length distribution, and taper profiles across a broad dataset of annotated real cracks; plus a large‑scale evaluation on many unseen paintings.

### Open Question 2  
- **Question**: Would alternative parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning strategies or full fine‑tuning of the SegFormer backbone improve segmentation performance on real paintings, and what is the risk of overfitting to synthetic patterns?  
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The authors use LoRA to limit trainable parameters citing stability on synthetic data, but they do not compare against full fine‑tuning or other PEFT methods, leaving a gap in understanding the trade‑off between adaptation capacity and generalization.  
- **Why unresolved**: Full fine‑tuning might allow the model to better capture subtle differences between synthetic and real cracks, but could also cause overfitting to the synthetic domain; the optimal adaptation strategy remains unknown.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: An ablation study comparing LoRA with full fine‑tuning and other PEFT approaches (e.g., adapters, prefix‑tuning) on a held‑out set of real paintings, measuring both performance and overfitting indicators (e.g., discrepancy between synthetic training and real test accuracy).

### Open Question 3  
- **Question**: How sensitive are the segmentation results to the hyperparameters of the masked hybrid loss (weight factor α and Dice coefficient λ), and are the chosen defaults robust across diverse painting textures?  
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The masked hybrid loss uses fixed values α=0.01 and λ=2, motivated by preliminary experiments, but no systematic sensitivity analysis is provided, raising concerns about general applicability to varied artistic styles.  
- **Why unresolved**: Different paintings have varying brushstroke density, crack contrast, and background complexity, which could shift the optimal loss weighting; without robustness validation, the method’s reliability is uncertain.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A comprehensive sensitivity analysis across a range of α and λ values on multiple painting types, reporting performance stability metrics; ideally, automated hyperparameter selection per input based on texture analysis could be explored.

### Open Question 4  
- **Question**: Can modern inpainting techniques (e.g., diffusion models) further improve restoration quality while preserving original brushwork, compared to anisotropic diffusion?  
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The authors evaluate only Modified Trimmed Mean and Anisotropic Diffusion, noting AD’s superiority, but they do not consider recent data‑driven inpainting methods that might produce more coherent textures while maintaining fidelity to the surrounding brushwork.  
- **Why unresolved**: Diffusion‑based inpainting can generate high‑frequency details but risks hallucinating content that contradicts the artist’s style; it is unknown whether such methods can be constrained to preserve brushstroke integrity in the context of fine cracks.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Benchmarking state‑of‑the‑art diffusion inpainting models (e.g., Stable Diffusion Inpainting) on the same crack masks, with quantitative metrics (SSIM, LPIPS) and qualitative evaluation by art experts to assess brushwork preservation.

## Limitations
- **Synthetic‑real gap**: Bézier cracks lack realistic texture and pigment‑loss details; model may overfit and fail on diverse artistic styles.  
- **Morphological detector sensitivity**: Coarse mask *m* relies on fixed top‑hat parameters (SE radius 2, threshold 180); errors propagate, especially for low‑contrast cracks.  
- **LoRA specification missing**: Unclear which linear layers in SegFormer are adapted, risking inconsistent reproduction and under‑adaptation.
