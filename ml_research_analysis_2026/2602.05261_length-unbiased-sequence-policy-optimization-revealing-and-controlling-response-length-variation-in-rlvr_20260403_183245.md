# Length-Unbiased Sequence Policy Optimization: Revealing and Controlling Response Length Variation in RLVR

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.05261
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05261
- Reference count: 4
- Primary result: LUSPO consistently outperforms GRPO and GSPO across dense, MoE, and vision-language models, with gains up to 6.9% on AIME24 for Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct.

## Executive Summary
The paper uncovers a fundamental length bias in existing RLVR algorithms (GRPO, GSPO) that causes shorter responses for correct answers and longer ones for incorrect answers, leading to length collapse and degraded performance. To remedy this, the authors introduce Length-Unbiased Sequence Policy Optimization (LUSPO), which simply scales each sequence’s loss by its length, neutralising the bias. Experiments on dense (Qwen2.5-7B), mixture-of-experts (Qwen3-30B-A3B), and vision-language (Qwen2.5-VL-7B) models show that LUSPO not only improves accuracy on reasoning benchmarks (e.g., +2.9% AIME24 over GSPO for Qwen2.5-7B, +6.9% for Qwen3-30B) but also preserves longer response lengths, demonstrating superior stability and generalisation.

## Method Summary
LUSPO modifies the standard sequence-level RLVR loss to eliminate length bias. Given a group of G sampled responses per query, the algorithm computes importance ratios \(s_i(\theta) = (\pi_\theta(y_i|x)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x))^{1/|y_i|}\) and group-relative advantages \(A_i\). The GSPO objective averages the token-level term \(\min(s_i A_i, \text{clip}(s_i,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon) A_i)\) over tokens, implicitly dividing by response length. LUSPO multiplies this term by \(|y_i|\), yielding \(\min(s_i A_i, \text{clip}(s_i) A_i) \cdot |y_i|\) and thus an unbiased gradient. Training uses the verl framework with a reward function combining binary accuracy, a format compliance bonus (0.5), and an overlong penalty for responses exceeding a length buffer. Hyperparameters include group size 8, AdamW (lr=1e-6), and generation limits up to 32768 tokens (text) or 4096 (VL). The method is evaluated on DAPO-MATH-17K (math Q&A) and ViRL39K (multimodal reasoning).

## Key Results
- On Qwen2.5-7B-Base, LUSPO improves AIME24 accuracy by 2.9% over GSPO.
- On Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct (MoE), LUSPO achieves a 6.9% gain on AIME24.
- For Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct on ViRL39K, LUSPO yields +0.5% on MathVista-mini over GSPO and +1.6% over GRPO, with significantly longer responses.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: GRPO and GSPO inherently bias optimization toward shorter responses for correct samples due to token-level averaging in the loss function.
- **Mechanism**: The loss averages contributions over tokens within each trajectory (dividing by |y|), so shorter responses have higher per-token weight. For positive advantages (correct responses), this yields larger gradient updates per token, encouraging brevity.
- **Core assumption**: Response length correlates with token count, and averaging over tokens implicitly weights sequences inversely with length.
- **Evidence anchors**: 
  - [abstract] "GRPO’s improper utilization of importance sampling... averaging the contribution of all tokens within each trajectory in the GRPO objective leads to a length bias."
  - [section 4.2] "For long responses... each token in a long response contributes less to the loss, while for short responses, each token contributes more."
  - [corpus] Weak: Neighbor papers discuss length-aware policy optimization but do not directly address this token-averaging bias mechanism.
- **Break condition**: If rewards are explicitly normalized by response length or if the loss does not average over tokens (e.g., sequence-level only without per-token division).

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: GSPO exacerbates length bias via sequence-level clipping and the Clip-Higher mechanism, which truncates more tokens and removes disproportionately more negative samples.
- **Mechanism**: Sequence-level clipping applies the same threshold to entire sequences, causing longer sequences to have more tokens clipped. Clip-Higher sets a higher clip bound for negative advantages, leading to more negative tokens being truncated, which imbalances gradient contributions toward positive samples and reinforces shorter responses.
- **Core assumption**: Clipping at sequence level affects token retention unevenly based on sequence length and advantage sign.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "GSPO’s objective function further exacerbates this bias. Sequence-level clipping leads to a substantially greater number of tokens being truncated... clip-higher mechanism disproportionately removes negative sample tokens."
  - [section 4.2] "This disparity amplifies the response-level length bias... where positive samples incentivize the model to generate shorter outputs."
  - [corpus] Weak: Corpus includes "VL Norm: Rethink Loss Aggregation in RLVR" which addresses length-dependent normalization but not the clip-higher amplification.
- **Break condition**: If sequence clipping is replaced with token-level clipping or Clip-Higher is disabled, reducing token truncation asymmetry.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: LUSPO neutralizes length bias by scaling each sequence’s loss by its length, equalizing per-token gradient contributions across different response lengths.
- **Mechanism**: Multiply the GSPO loss by |y_i|, canceling the implicit division by length in the token averaging. This ensures that the gradient for each token is independent of total sequence length.
- **Core assumption**: The bias stems from the 1/|y| factor in the token-averaged loss; scaling by |y| counteracts this.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "LUSPO rectifies this by scaling each sequence’s loss by its length, neutralizing the bias."
  - [section 4.3] Defines \(J_{\text{LUSPO}}\) with factor \(|y_i|\): \(\min(...) \cdot |y_i|\).
  - [section 4.4] Gradient derivation shows LUSPO eliminates the length-dependent term present in GSPO’s gradient.
  - [corpus] Weak: "LSPO: Length-aware Dynamic Sampling" uses length-aware sampling but differs in method; no direct citation.
- **Break condition**: If length scaling is misapplied (e.g., not per-sequence) or if sequence length is miscalculated (e.g., including padding tokens).

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Query -> Old policy (LLM/VLM) -> Sampled responses (G per query) -> Reward verifier -> Rewards -> Group advantage normalization -> Loss calculator (LUSPO scaling) -> Optimizer -> Updated policy.
- **Critical path**: For each iteration: (1) Sample G responses per query from the old policy; (2) Compute rewards via the verifier; (3) Normalize advantages within the group; (4) Compute the sequence-level loss \(\min(s_i A_i, \text{clip}(s_i) A_i) \cdot |y_i|\); (5) Backpropagate the average loss across the group to update the policy.
- **Design tradeoffs**: Sequence-level importance ratios reduce variance in MoE models but require full sequence likelihood; length scaling is simple but assumes bias solely from the \(1/|y|\) factor, ignoring other reward dynamics. The overlong penalty helps control length but may interact with LUSPO.
- **Failure signatures**: (1) Response length collapse (monitor average length per step); (2) Diverging validation scores vs. training rewards; (3) Extreme variance in gradient norms due to unbalanced clipping.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Train Qwen2.5-7B-Base on DAPO-MATH-17K with GSPO and LUSPO; compare response length curves and AIME24 accuracy.
  2. Train Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct (MoE) on same data; evaluate on AIME24 and AIME25 to test MoE stability.
  3. Train Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct on ViRL39K; measure response length and multimodal benchmarks (MathVista-mini, LogicVista) to assess VL generalization.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Uniform length scaling optimality**: Is the fixed factor \(|y_i|\) universally optimal, or could adaptive scaling (e.g., \(|y_i|^\alpha\)) yield further improvements? *Basis*: Paper uses simple scaling without ablation. *Unresolved*: No experiments on alternative scaling functions. *Resolution*: Systematic ablation across benchmarks and analysis of length/gradient distributions.
2. **Interaction with overlong reward**: How does the overlong penalty \(R_{\text{overlong}}\) interact with LUSPO’s correction? *Basis*: Both components affect length incentives but their joint dynamics are not studied. *Unresolved*: No experiments ablating \(R_{\text{overlong}}\) under LUSPO. *Resolution*: Controlled experiments removing the overlong term and theoretical analysis of combined objective.
3. **Other implicit biases**: What biases remain beyond length (e.g., token position, group statistics)? *Basis*: Paper focuses solely on length bias; other components like group advantage estimation or clipping may introduce distortions. *Unresolved*: No variance or token-wise gradient analysis. *Resolution*: Token-level gradient measurements, synthetic reward experiments.
4. **Dependence on reward design**: Does LUSPO hold under different reward structures (dense process rewards, learned models)? *Basis*: All experiments use sparse accuracy+format+overlong rewards. *Unresolved*: Generalization to denser/noisier rewards not tested. *Resolution*: Re-run with alternative rewards (PRM, reward models) and assess performance sensitivity.
5. **Efficiency and verbosity consequences**: Does scaling cause unnecessary verbosity or hurt inference efficiency? *Basis*: Only accuracy and length measured; no efficiency metrics or human evaluation. *Unresolved*: Unclear if longer responses improve accuracy/token or just add latency. *Resolution*: Report accuracy per token, human studies on conciseness, and fixed-budged inference tests.
