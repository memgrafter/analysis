# Assessing the Ability of Neural TTS Systems to Model Consonant-Induced F0 Perturbation

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.21078
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21078
*   **Reference count:** 7
*   **Primary result:** Neural TTS systems (Tacotron 2, FastSpeech 2) accurately model consonant-induced f0 perturbation only for high-frequency words present in their training data, failing to generalize this fine-grained phonetic rule to low-frequency or unseen words, indicating reliance on lexical memorization over abstract segmental-prosodic encoding.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates whether state-of-the-art neural text-to-speech (TTS) systems can reproduce a subtle, physiologically grounded phonetic effect: consonant-induced fundamental frequency (f0) perturbation, where the voicing of a preceding consonant systematically affects the onset f0 of the following vowel. Using a segmental prosodic probing framework, the authors compared synthetic speech from Tacotron 2 and FastSpeech 2 against natural LJ Speech recordings for thousands of words, stratified by lexical frequency. Generalized Additive Mixed Models (GAMMs) revealed that both models replicate the expected pattern (higher f0 after voiceless obstruents) for high-frequency words, which largely overlap with their training corpora. However, performance degrades sharply for low-frequency words: Tacotron 2 shows incomplete or unstable patterns, while FastSpeech 2 eliminates onset-based differences entirely. Natural speech maintains consistent perturbation across all frequencies. A large-scale multi-speaker evaluation with deepfake datasets corroborates this generalization gap. The findings suggest current architectures primarily learn word-specific f0 associations rather than generalizable segmental-prosodic rules.

## Method Summary
The study uses a "segmental prosodic probing" framework. Text prompts were randomly sampled from the COCA corpus and synthesized using pre-trained Tacotron 2 and FastSpeech 2 models (trained on LJ Speech). Audio was force-aligned to text via Montreal Forced Aligner (MFA) to extract vowel-interval boundaries. f0 was extracted using Praat (single-speaker) or a speaker-adapted Polyglot pipeline (multi-speaker), then time-normalized to 21 points per vowel. f0 values were z-scored for multi-speaker pooling. Words were stratified by SUBTLEX-US frequency (high vs. low) and, in a direct analysis, by explicit presence in the LJ Speech training transcripts (seen vs. unseen). f0 trajectories for vowels following three onset types (voiceless obstruent, voiced obstruent, sonorant) were modeled using AR1 Generalized Additive Mixed Models (GAMMs) with smooth terms for time, onset type, vowel height, and random effects for word and speaker. Tokens were balanced (~1,000 per onset type × frequency band) to ensure robust statistical estimation. The primary metric was the significance of pairwise difference smooths between onset types across normalized vowel duration.

## Key Results
*   Both Tacotron 2 and FastSpeech 2 accurately reproduce the expected consonant-induced f0 perturbation pattern for high-frequency words, closely matching natural LJ Speech.
*   For low-frequency words, both models fail to maintain systematic onset-based differences: FastSpeech 2 produces nearly flat f0 contours across all onset types, while Tacotron 2 shows unstable or incomplete perturbation patterns.
*   A direct seen/unseen split confirms the generalization failure: models perform well on words seen during training but collapse to global prosodic templates (e.g., stress-based contours) for novel lexical items, unlike natural speech which remains consistent.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
*   **Claim:** Neural TTS systems rely on lexical memorization rather than abstract segmental-prosodic rules, leading to degraded performance on low-frequency or unseen words.
*   **Mechanism:** The models encode f0 perturbation patterns as word-specific associations learned from repeated exposure in the training corpus. For high-frequency words, these associations are robust; for low-frequency words, the absence of memorized representations forces the model to fall back on global prosodic patterns (e.g., stress assignment) without fine-grained segmental conditioning.
*   **Core assumption:** Lexical frequency is a valid proxy for training corpus exposure and that abstract segmental-prosodic rules would manifest consistently across frequency bands if encoded.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   "Results show accurate reproduction for high-frequency words but poor generalization to low-frequency items, suggesting that the examined TTS architectures rely more on lexical-level memorization rather than abstract segmental-prosodic encoding."
    *   "In the high-frequency condition... both the natural recordings and the synthetic outputs... exhibit similar f0 perturbation patterns... In contrast, for low-frequency words, the synthetic speech diverges considerably."
    *   The study uses lexical frequency (from SUBTLEX-US) as a stand-in for training exposure and validates it with a corpus overlap check (Section 3: 70.69% overlap for high-frequency vs. 31.74% for low-frequency). However, frequency is an indirect proxy, and the exact training data for commercial systems in Experiment 2 is unknown.
*   **Break condition:** If models were to show consistent f0 perturbation patterns across both high- and low-frequency words, or if performance did not correlate with frequency/seen-status, the memorization hypothesis would be contradicted.

### Mechanism 2
*   **Claim:** Architectural differences (autoregressive vs. non-autoregressive) lead to distinct failure modes in modeling segmental-prosodic effects, though neither inherently supports robust generalization.
*   **Mechanism:** Tacotron 2’s autoregressive frame-level recursion may allow some temporary propagation of segmental cues but lacks explicit supervision for segmental-prosodic mappings, resulting in unstable patterns for low-frequency words. FastSpeech 2’s parallel generation and explicit pitch predictor tend to smooth over local variation, eliminating distinctions entirely. Both fail to induce a compositional, word-independent rule.
*   **Core assumption:** The presence or absence of sequential dependencies and explicit acoustic predictors directly affects the model’s capacity to retain and apply segmental-level constraints during generation.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   "Autoregressive models... generate acoustic frames sequentially... This frame-level temporal recursion allows acoustic dependencies to be implicitly propagated... non-autoregressive models... generate... in parallel... temporal structure is imposed through predicted durations... f0 contour is modeled explicitly via a dedicated pitch predictor."
    *   "FastSpeech 2 fails to produce any systematic difference... Tacotron 2 shows partial success... its f0 output for vowels following sonorants and voiced obstruents is inaccurate in either overall contour shape or pitch range."
    *   Direct model comparison under identical training data (LJ Speech) and test sets.
*   **Break condition:** If either architecture, without architectural changes, were to show stable and accurate f0 perturbation across low-frequency words, the claim that current implementations lack necessary mechanisms would be falsified.

### Mechanism 3
*   **Claim:** The absence of explicit articulatory and cognitive constraints in TTS models prevents productive generalization of fine-grained phonetic effects to novel lexical items.
*   **Mechanism:** Human speakers apply learned phonetic rules grounded in biomechanics (e.g., glottal tension changes after voiceless obstruents) to any well-formed segment sequence. Neural TTS models, trained on acoustic-text pairs without explicit articulation or rule representations, cannot “reason” about segmental interactions and instead interpolate from memorized surface patterns. On nonce/low-frequency words, this results in a collapse to global prosodic templates (stress-based pitch contours) without local perturbation.
*   **Core assumption:** Generalization of fine-grained phonetic effects requires an explicit, compositional representation of segmental features and their articulatory-acoustic consequences, which current end-to-end TTS architectures do not induce from data alone.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   "Human speakers generate novel words by deploying abstract phonetic knowledge grounded in physiological and cognitive constraints, whereas current TTS models rely on lexicalized and distributional patterns... When a TTS model encounters a nonce word... the primary decision concerns where stress should be placed... the model might choose to neglect some explicit segmental-level interaction mechanisms."
    *   The empirical failure on low-frequency/unseen items (Figures 2, 3, 5) is interpreted as evidence of missing explicit mechanisms, but the paper does not directly probe model internals for rule-like representations.
*   **Break condition:** If a TTS model were modified to include an explicit segmental-prosodic interaction module (e.g., articulatory-informed loss) and subsequently showed generalized f0 perturbation, the necessity of explicit constraints would be supported. Conversely, if a purely data-driven model achieved generalization without such modules, the claim would be weakened.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Lexical frequency as a proxy for training exposure**
    *   **Why needed here:** The study stratifies words by frequency (high vs. low) to test whether models generalize phonetic detail beyond memorized items, since exact training set membership is often unknown for many TTS systems.
    *   **Quick check question:** Why might a high-frequency word be more likely to be “seen” during TTS training than a low-frequency word?  
        *Answer: Because training corpora typically follow Zipf’s law; high-frequency words occur more often, increasing their probability of inclusion and multiple exposures.*
*   **Concept: Generalized Additive Mixed Models (GAMMs) for time-series phonetic analysis**
    *   **Why needed here:** GAMMs model non-linear f0 trajectories over normalized vowel duration while accounting for random effects (word, speaker) and autocorrelation, allowing detection of subtle, time-varying perturbation effects.
    *   **Quick check question:** What is the key advantage of a GAMM over a traditional repeated-measures ANOVA for analyzing f0 contours?  
        *Answer: GAMMs can model smooth, non-linear changes over time (the f0 trajectory) rather than treating time points as independent categorical levels.*
*   **Concept: Segmental-prosodic interaction (consonant-induced f0 perturbation)**
    *   **Why needed here:** The paper probes whether TTS models capture a specific, physiologically grounded phonetic effect where the preceding consonant’s voicing systematically alters the onset f0 of the following vowel.
    *   **Quick check question:** Why do voiceless obstruents (like [p], [t], [k]) typically raise f0 on the following vowel in English?  
        *Answer: Increased vocal fold tension during aspiration suppresses voicing and raises fundamental frequency.*

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:** COCA Text Corpus -> TTS Model (Tacotron 2 / FastSpeech 2) -> Audio -> MFA Alignment -> Vowel Segmentation -> f0 Extraction (Praat/Polyglot) -> Time-Normalization & Z-scoring -> GAMM Statistical Modeling -> f0 Trajectory Analysis

**Critical path:**
1.  **Data preparation:** Select target words, ensure phoneme coverage (Table 1), stratify by frequency/seen-status.
2.  **Synthesis:** Generate audio with TTS models using identical text prompts.
3.  **Alignment & extraction:** Align synthetic/natural audio to text, extract vowel-interval f0, time-normalize.
4.  **Modeling:** Fit GAMMs with onset type, vowel height, word, speaker as smooth/random effects; include AR1 correlation.
5.  **Validation:** Check smoothing parameters (gam.check), compare difference smooths for significance.

**Design tradeoffs:**
*   **Token balancing vs. ecological validity:** Forcing 1,000 tokens per onset type (Table 2) ensures balanced GAMM estimation but may oversample rare consonant-vowel combinations.
*   **Single- vs. multi-speaker normalization:** Single-speaker (LJ) uses raw Hz; multi-speaker requires z-scoring to pool data but obscures absolute f0 ranges.
*   **Smoothing flexibility (k=5):** Sufficient for typical f0 contours but may underfit highly variable tokens; increased k raises computational cost and overfitting risk.
*   **Frequency proxy:** Using frequency is practical but noisy; direct seen/unseen comparison (Figure 3) is cleaner but requires training transcript access.

**Failure signatures:**
*   **Statistical:** Difference smooths overlapping zero across the vowel duration for low-frequency/unseen words.
*   **Visual:** f0 trajectories for different onset types (voiceless obstruent, voiced obstruent, sonorant) are overlapping or non-monotonic in unexpected ways.
*   **Systematic:** Tacotron 2 showing extreme pitch range distortion or FastSpeech 2 producing flat f0 contours across all conditions.
*   **Alignment:** Sudden f0 jumps or misaligned vowel boundaries in TextGrids, requiring manual QC.

**First 3 experiments:**
1.  **Replicate high-frequency pattern:** For LJ Speech, Tacotron 2, and FastSpeech 2 on high-frequency words, verify that (a) voiceless obstruents yield higher f0 than sonorants/voiced, and (b) difference smooths show significant separation (Fig. 1).
2.  **Test low-frequency generalization:** Repeat analysis on low-frequency words; expect natural speech to maintain patterns while synthetic speech shows degraded separation (Fig. 2). Directly compare seen vs. unseen tokens (Fig. 3) to confirm.
3.  **Scale to multi-speaker:** Apply probe to In-the-Wild dataset, using speaker-level z-scoring and factor smooths; check if synthetic speech still shows reduced f0 separation in low-frequency group (Figs. 4–5).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1.  **What are the perceptual consequences of failing to reproduce segmental f0 perturbation in synthetic speech?** The study only provides acoustic analysis; no listening tests were conducted to assess whether these deviations affect human perception of naturalness or authenticity. Establishing a direct causal link would require targeted perceptual experiments.
2.  **Do the observed limitations in modeling f0 perturbation generalize to other TTS architectures, such as diffusion-based or flow-based models?** The study only evaluated Tacotron 2 and FastSpeech 2; it is unclear if the reliance on lexical memorization is universal across different generation paradigms.
3.  **Is the failure to model consonant-induced f0 perturbation in low-frequency items a result of deliberate architectural trade-offs favoring global prosodic coherence over fine-grained segmental effects?** The authors speculate that when encountering nonce words, TTS models might prioritize stress assignment and thus neglect explicit segmental-level interactions, potentially as a modeling decision to avoid unstable pitch.
4.  **What architectural modifications or training strategies can enhance the encoding of segmental-prosodic interactions in neural TTS systems?** The study identifies the limitation but does not propose or evaluate specific interventions to improve segmental-prosodic generalization.

## Limitations
*   Lexical frequency is an indirect proxy for actual training data exposure; the corpus overlap check strengthens but does not eliminate ambiguity about what models have literally "seen."
*   Exact model checkpoints, vocoders, and inference settings for Tacotron 2 and FastSpeech 2 are unspecified, introducing unknown variance into synthetic f0 trajectories.
*   Forced alignment and f0 extraction settings are not fully detailed, and alignment on synthetic speech may be less reliable, potentially affecting vowel-interval f0 measurements.
*   The probe targets a single phonetic effect in English; findings may not extend to other prosodic phenomena, languages, or multi-speaker scenarios with larger acoustic variability.

## Confidence
*   **Claim: TTS systems rely on lexical memorization rather than abstract segmental-prosodic rules** → **High**
    *   Direct empirical evidence: consistent high-frequency success vs. low-frequency failure across two architectures, plus seen/unseen split analysis.
*   **Claim: Autoregressive vs. non-autoregressive architectures exhibit distinct but equally insufficient failure modes** → **Medium-High**
    *   Strong comparative evidence from controlled LJ Speech experiments, but mechanisms are inferred from architecture descriptions, not internal probing.
*   **Claim: Absence of explicit articulatory/cognitive constraints prevents productive generalization** → **Medium**
    *   Interpretation aligned with human vs. TTS contrast in discussion, but no direct test of what explicit mechanisms would suffice; could also be a data or optimization limitation.

## Next Checks
1.  **Replicate with canonical seen/unseen split**: Using the exact LJ Speech training transcripts (14,127 unique words), directly compare f0 perturbation on held-out versus in-vocabulary words, bypassing frequency as a proxy.
2.  **Test newer architectures**: Evaluate state-of-the-art expressive TTS models (e.g., VITS, Diffusion-based) with the same probing framework to determine if architectural advances close the generalization gap.
3.  **Internal representation probing**: Extract attention weights or intermediate linguistic/Prosodic embeddings from Tacotron 2 and FastSpeech 2 to test whether segmental features (e.g., consonant voicing) are disentangled from lexical identity and correlated with f0 prediction heads.
