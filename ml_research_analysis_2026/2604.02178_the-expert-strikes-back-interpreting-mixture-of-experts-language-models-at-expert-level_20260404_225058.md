# The Expert Strikes Back: Interpreting Mixture-of-Experts Language Models at Expert Level

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.02178
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02178
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** Sparse routing in MoE architectures reduces neuron-level polysemanticity and yields modular, fine-grained task-specialized experts, offering a clearer path to large-scale model interpretability compared to dense networks.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models are inherently more interpretable than dense feed-forward networks (FFNs). Using k-sparse probing across 12 model pairs, the authors find that MoE expert neurons are consistently less polysemantic, with the gap widening as routing becomes sparser. This monosemanticity at the neuron level makes entire experts interpretable functional units. The authors develop an automatic labeling pipeline that generates and validates high-quality functional descriptions for hundreds of experts, revealing that MoE experts specialize in fine-grained tasks (e.g., syntactic operations like closing LaTeX brackets) rather than broad domains. Their analysis shows that expert specialization intensifies in late layers at high granularity, suggesting MoE architectures naturally facilitate interpretable modularity.

## Method Summary
The study employs a multi-method approach. First, *k-sparse probing* compares neuron polysemanticity between matched MoE and dense models, using logistic regression on top-*k* neuron activations to predict binary concepts. Second, an *automatic labeling pipeline* identifies high-activating examples per expert, uses a large language model (LLM) explainer to generate a one-sentence functional label, and employs a separate LLM scorer to compute validation F1 scores on held-out examples. Third, *specialization analysis* clusters the output embedding matrix and measures Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD) between an expert’s promoted or routed token distribution and the layer base rate. Finally, *causal attribution* via Direct Logit Attribution (DLA) validates labels by testing whether an expert ranks highly for target tokens in synthetic trigger-target sentences.

## Key Results
- MoE expert neurons exhibit significantly lower polysemanticity than dense FFN neurons, with the monosemanticity gap increasing as the ratio of active experts to total experts (NA/N) decreases.
- The automatic labeling pipeline achieves high interpretability scores (F1 > 0.8) for most experts, and causal attribution experiments confirm that experts act as functionally coherent modules.
- Experts specialize in fine-grained, task-oriented operations (e.g., “closing brackets in LaTeX”), particularly in late layers, with specialization scores rising at high cluster granularity (large *k*).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Architectural sparsity (few experts active per token) reduces neuron-level polysemanticity in MoE experts compared to dense feed-forward networks.
**Mechanism:** Sparse routing limits the data distribution each expert sees, decreasing pressure for superposition. Neurons within an expert become dedicated to narrower concepts, as measured by higher k-sparse probe accuracy at k=1.
**Core assumption:** The router’s selection creates a coherent, narrow data distribution per expert, encouraging monosemantic representations.
**Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "We find that expert neurons are consistently less polysemantic, with the gap widening as routing becomes sparser."
  - [section 4] "This suggests that sparsity pressures both individual neurons and entire experts toward monosemanticity."
**Break condition:** If routing is not sufficiently sparse (e.g., high NA/N) or the router fails to filter inputs coherently, polysemanticity may remain high and the interpretability advantage diminishes.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Router-induced input filtering, combined with monosemantic neurons, makes entire experts modular and interpretable units.
**Mechanism:** The router activates experts only for semantically/syntactically related tokens. Since each expert’s neurons are already monosemantic, the expert’s aggregate output performs a coherent function, allowing the expert to be treated as a functional module rather than a superposition of concepts.
**Core assumption:** The router learns to group tokens by shared features, ensuring each expert processes a restricted subset of the data distribution.
**Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "experts do not see the entire data distribution; the router acts as a filter, ensuring an expert is only activated for a restricted, semantically or syntactically related subset of tokens."
  - [section 5.1] "we must measure the magnitude of its contribution to the residual stream" to find high-activating examples, confirming experts are only meaningfully active on specific inputs.
  - [section 6.2] Routing Specialization scores show experts consistently deviate from layer base rates, indicating selective input filtering.
**Break condition:** If routing becomes random or the router fails to specialize, experts receive diverse inputs and their outputs remain polysemantic, breaking modularity.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** In late layers, MoE experts specialize in fine-grained tasks (e.g., syntactic operations) rather than broad domains, and this is discoverable via automatic labeling and causal attribution.
**Mechanism:** As representations align with output vocabulary, experts promote highly specific tokens (e.g., closing LaTeX braces). Their functional specialization is quantified by Jensen-Shannon Divergence at high granularity (large k in cluster space), and labels generated by an LLM explainer achieve high F1 scores. Causal attribution (DLA) confirms experts causally influence predicted tokens in task-specific ways.
**Core assumption:** The residual stream’s additive structure allows attributing output changes to expert updates, and high granularity in promoted tokens reflects task-level function.
**Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "experts function as fine-grained task experts, specializing in linguistic operations or semantic tasks (e.g., closing brackets in LaTeX)."
  - [section 6.1] Taxonomy of expert roles (morphological, syntactic, operational) and case studies (e.g., OLMoE-L15-E17 for LaTeX closing).
  - [section 6.2] Functional Specialization scores spike at high k in late layers, confirming granular task specialization.
**Break condition:** In early layers, representations are not aligned with output vocabulary, so functional specialization is less detectable. Also, if an expert’s promoted tokens are too diverse or the label F1 is low, the specialization may be ambiguous.

## Architecture Onboarding
Router -> Experts -> Residual Stream

**Critical path:**
1. Forward pass: token -> router -> select experts -> expert computations -> add to residual stream.
2. For expert analysis: identify high-activating examples (max gi·||Ei(x)||2) -> provide snippets with top activations and Logit Lens promotions to explainer -> generate hypothesis -> use scorer to compute F1 -> conduct trigger-target DLA experiment to validate.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Sparsity vs. capacity**: More experts (larger N) with fewer active (smaller NA) increases interpretability (as shown) but may require more memory and complex routing.
- **Automatic labeling cost**: Using LLMs as explainers/scorers is expensive but scalable; manual validation is limited in scale.
- **Granularity**: Fine-grained task labels are more informative than broad domain labels but may capture very narrow functions that are hard to generalize.

**Failure signatures:**
- Low best-layer F1 at k=1 in probing indicates persistent polysemanticity, suggesting the MoE is not more interpretable.
- Automatic labeling F1 < 0.8 for many experts indicates labeling failure (e.g., overfitting, domain bias).
- In DLA trigger-target, the expert is not highly ranked (or not routed) on matched prompts, or ranks high on control prompts, indicating label inaccuracy.
- Routing Specialization scores near 0 across layers suggest router is not filtering inputs meaningfully.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Probing comparison**: On a matched MoE/dense pair (e.g., OLMoE-1B-7B vs. OLMo-1B), train k-sparse probes for a set of concepts and plot best-layer F1 vs. k. Expect MoE curves to be higher, especially at k=1.
2. **Automatic labeling pilot**: For one layer of a sparse MoE (e.g., Qwen3-30B-A3B), run the labeling pipeline on all experts. Compute average F1 and compare across NA/N ratios. Expect sparse models to have higher F1.
3. **Causal attribution check**: Select an expert with a high automatic labeling F1. Generate 20 trigger-target test cases from its label. Run DLA and compute rank statistics. Expect the expert to be top-ranked on matched prompts and not routed on control prompts.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Does the relationship between routing sparsity (NA/N) and monosemanticity continue to hold at extreme sparsity levels (e.g., NA/N < 0.01) in production-scale models, potentially approaching fully monosemantic experts?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state, "the interpretability of MoEs scales with the degree of sparse routing: as routing sparsity increases, the internal units become increasingly monosemantic" and speculate about "the next generation of models may be inherently transparent by design." However, their empirical study tops out at NA/N ≈ 0.06.
- **Why unresolved:** The study only observes the trend up to NA/N ≈ 0.06 (Qwen3-30B-A3B). The functional form of the scaling law at higher sparsity—whether it asymptotes, continues linearly, or exhibits phase changes—is unknown. This is critical for predicting the interpretability of future MoE architectures.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic probing and automatic labeling experiments on models with NA/N < 0.01 (e.g., models with 128+ total experts and 1-2 active per token) to measure if the polysemanticity gap with dense models continues to widen, plateaus, or reverses due to under-fitting or routing degradation.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** To what extent are the observed monosemantic properties and specialized task-expert behaviors preserved in the largest current MoE models (e.g., DeepSeek-V3, Claude 3.7 Sonnet), which may use different routing mechanisms, shared experts, or vastly larger parameter counts?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] In the Limitations section, the authors state: "Due to GPU memory and compute constraints, we were unable to include the largest current MoE models, such as DeepSeek-V3... however, given that these models use similar degrees of sparse routing, we expect our findings to hold." This is an untested assumption.
- **Why unresolved:** The largest models often incorporate additional complexity (e.g., extensive shared experts, different routing losses, or secondary token dropping) that could alter the pressure toward monosemanticity. Their results are based on models up to ~30B active parameters; scaling to 100B+ active parameters with different architectural choices is an open empirical question.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicating the k-sparse probing and automatic labeling pipeline on the largest available open-weight or accessible MoE models to directly measure neuron-level polysemanticity and expert functional coherence.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Are the fine-grained task specializations observed in mid-to-late layer experts (e.g., "closing LaTeX brackets", "RPG game mechanics") primarily emergent from the data distribution and training objective, or are they a direct, deterministic consequence of the sparse routing algorithm itself?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors conclude experts are "fine-grained task experts" but do not establish causality. They show specialization correlates with layer depth and routing sparsity but cannot disentangle whether the router discovers these functions or the training dynamics force them.
- **Why unresolved:** The work is observational. It is unclear if changing the routing algorithm (e.g., from Top-K to learned gating with entropy regularization) or the training data distribution would produce different specializations (e.g., more broad domain experts) or if the task-expert phenomenon is inevitable under sparse routing.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablation studies training identical MoE architectures with different routing mechanisms (e.g., varying `k`, adding load balancing losses, or using deterministic vs. stochastic routing) on controlled datasets (e.g., synthetic tasks with known ground-truth expert utilities) to see if the fine-grained task specialization pattern persists or changes systematically.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Does the "modular monosemanticity" property—where entire experts act as coherent, interpretable units—imply that expert computations are largely independent and additive in the residual stream, or are there significant nonlinear interactions and context-dependent handoffs between experts that undermine the expert-level unit of analysis?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper assumes the expert is a valid "causal unit" based on DLA experiments and high F1 scores. However, DLA is a first-order linearization and the scorer F1 measures descriptive fit on held-out examples, not functional independence. The possibility of superposition *between* experts or complex router logic that dynamically composes experts is not fully ruled out.
- **Why unresolved:** The causal attribution experiment (Trigger-Target) shows the expert is *necessary* for certain targets but does not prove *sufficiency* or independence. An expert's contribution might only make sense in combination with others, or the router might activate a group of experts whose collective output cannot be decomposed into individual roles. This is a key assumption for their proposed "toolbox" view of LLMs.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Circuit-level analysis using methods like path patching or activation addition to isolate an expert's *total* effect on the final output, comparing cases where only that expert is activated vs. its normal routing context. Quantifying the variance in model behavior explained by individual experts versus expert interactions would test the modularity claim.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** How stable and generalizable are the automatically generated expert labels across different random seeds, model initializations, or slight changes in the explainer/scorer LLM prompts? Do they represent discoverable, intrinsic functions or are they post-hoc rationalizations of noisy patterns?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The automatic labeling pipeline relies on an LLM explainer and scorer. While they report high F1 scores (>0.8), the paper does not analyze label consistency. Given known LLM biases (e.g., towards over-generalizing, favoring certain phrasings), it is uncertain if the labels are stable properties of the expert or artifacts of the annotation process.
- **Why unresolved:** The failure case analysis (Appendix E) shows the explainer can overfit to spurious correlations (e.g., domain overfitting, syntactic overfitting). This raises concerns about the reliability of the entire labeling procedure for hundreds of experts. If labels are not consistent across minor perturbations, the taxonomy of "fine-grained task experts" may be less robust than presented.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Running the full automatic labeling pipeline multiple times with different random seeds for example selection, different explainer/scorer models (e.g., GPT-4 vs. Claude vs. Gemini), and slightly varied prompt formulations. Measuring label agreement (e.g., using a semantic similarity metric) would assess reproducibility. Human expert validation on a subset would also provide a ground truth benchmark.

## Limitations
- Proprietary Gemini LLM used for labeling/scoring; replication hindered and potential biases exist.
- Routing token filter details are vague; exact reconstruction could alter probe sample sizes and results.
- Only two model families tested (OLMoE, Qwen3); generalizability to other MoE implementations is uncertain.
- Probing assumes linear separability of concepts, which may not always hold.
- Causal attribution uses synthetic triggers, which may not fully capture natural expert behavior.

## Confidence
- **Claim 1** (sparsity → less polysemanticity): **High** (consistent across 12 pairs, strong quantitative evidence).
- **Claim 2** (modular, interpretable experts): **Medium** (LLM-based labeling lacks human validation; F1 > 0.8 may be inflated).
- **Claim 3** (fine-grained task specialization): **High** for JSD trend; **Medium** for interpretive labels.
