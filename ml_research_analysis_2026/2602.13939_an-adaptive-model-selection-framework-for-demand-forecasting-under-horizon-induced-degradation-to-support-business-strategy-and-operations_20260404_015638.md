# An Adaptive Model Selection Framework for Demand Forecasting under Horizon-Induced Degradation to Support Business Strategy and Operations
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.13939
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13939
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: AHSIV achieves statistical equivalence with the top monometric baseline (RMSSE_h) in aggregated Global Relative Accuracy while increasing the frequency of selecting the true best model at specific horizons.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces AHSIV, an adaptive model selection framework to address horizon-induced instability in demand forecasting. AHSIV employs the Metric Degradation by Forecast Horizon (MDFH) to project out-of-sample errors to longer planning horizons under structural stability conditions. It classifies demand into regular-stable and intermittent/variable regimes, applying Pareto dominance on MDFH-adjusted RMSSE and MAE for the former (followed by bias minimization) and scaled error minimization for the latter. Evaluations across Walmart, M3, M4, and M5 datasets with 12-step horizons show that AHSIV is statistically equivalent to RMSSE_h in aggregated Global Relative Accuracy but more frequently selects the true best model at specific horizons, demonstrating improved decision coherence for multi-SKU forecasting.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates model selectors using Global Relative Accuracy (GRA), which measures the alignment between total forecasted and actual demand over a horizon. Training and testing follow a fixed-origin backtesting scheme with the last 12 periods as the test set. Candidate models (statistical and machine learning) are hyperparameter-optimized via Grid Search or Optuna using a Hierarchical Evaluation Function (HEF). For each series, AHSIV first computes structural diagnostics (CV of absolute differences and mean absolute second differences) to assess stability. If stable, MDFH adjusts MAE, RMSE, and RMSSE to the planning horizon via a power-law degradation exponent α (estimated from error blocks or defaulted to 0.5). The regime is then classified based on demand frequency (p) and variability (c). For regular-stable series (p ≥ 0.5, c < 0.7), the Pareto front on adjusted RMSSE and MAE is identified; the model with smallest |BIAS| (then sMAPE) is selected. For intermittent/variable series, the model with smallest adjusted RMSSE is chosen. Two baselines are compared: RMSSE_h (minimum adjusted RMSSE) and ERA (ranking aggregation of multiple metrics). The selected models’ forecasts are aggregated to compute horizon-specific GRA.

## Key Results
- AHSIV achieves no significant difference in aggregated GRA compared to RMSSE_h across datasets.
- At specific horizons, AHSIV selects the true best model (by GRA) more frequently than RMSSE_h and ERA.
- The framework’s regime-aware approach yields improved decision coherence, particularly for multi-SKU forecasting.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Forecast error grows predictably with horizon under structural stability, enabling projection from test to future planning horizons.
- **Mechanism:** MDFH adjusts metrics (MAE, RMSE, RMSSE) via a power law: \( E_m(h_{\text{future}}) = E_m(h_{\text{test}}) \cdot (h_{\text{future}} / h_{\text{test}})^{\alpha} \). The degradation exponent \(\alpha\) is estimated from error blocks or defaulted to 0.5. Adjustment occurs only if the series meets strict stability criteria: coefficient of variation of absolute differences < 0.2 and mean absolute second differences < 0.1 × mean absolute first differences.
- **Core assumption:** The demand trajectory is structurally invariant; error accumulation follows a consistent nonlinear pattern; the stability thresholds reliably identify non‑explosive series.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Abstract: “incorporates the Metric Degradation by Forecast Horizon (MDFH) to project out-of-sample errors to longer planning horizons under structural stability conditions.”  
  - Section 3.2: Detailed algorithm and regime definitions.  
  - Corpus: neighboring papers discuss horizon‑dependent error growth but MDFH’s conditional adjustment is novel; specific thresholds lack extensive corpus support.
- **Break condition:** If the series exhibits bias or explosive behavior (\(\gamma = 0\)), no adjustment is applied, potentially leaving short‑term errors unrepresentative for long horizons.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Model selection criteria must adapt to demand regime because rankings are unstable for intermittent or highly variable series.
- **Mechanism:** Series are classified by demand frequency \(p\) (proportion of positive demand) and variability \(c\) (CV of demand). Regular‑stable (\(p \ge 0.5, c < 0.7\)) triggers a multi‑objective Pareto filter on MDFH‑adjusted RMSSE and MAE, followed by lexicographic minimization of \( | \text{BIAS} | \) and sMAPE. Intermittent/highly variable series bypass Pareto and directly minimize RMSSE (adjusted).
- **Core assumption:** The thresholds \(p^* = 0.5, c^* = 0.7\) separate regimes where multi‑objective comparison is meaningful; bias and percentage error provide orthogonal, decision‑relevant information.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Abstract: “classifies demand as regular‑stable or intermittent/variable: for regular‑stable, it uses Pareto dominance on MDFH‑adjusted RMSSE and MAE, then bias minimization; for intermittent, it minimizes scaled error.”  
  - Section 3.3.2: AHSIV algorithm and thresholds.  
  - Corpus: Syntetos & Boylan (2005) and others advocate regime‑aware forecasting; AHSIV operationalizes this with specific numeric cutoffs.
- **Break condition:** Misclassification (e.g., a low‑frequency but smooth series) leads to overly simplistic RMSSE minimization, possibly ignoring bias that matters operationally.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Horizon‑aware metric adjustment combined with regime‑sensitive selection yields consistent model rankings across forecasting horizons, improving decision coherence.
- **Mechanism:** All error metrics used for comparison are first projected to the operational horizon via MDFH (when applicable). This ensures that models are evaluated on their expected long‑term performance rather than short‑term test metrics. The final ranking (AHSIV score) normalizes the ordinal position, enabling comparison across datasets and model sets.
- **Core assumption:** The planning horizon is known and fixed; MDFH provides a reliable estimate of future error; the normalization does not obscure meaningful differences.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Abstract: “horizon‑aware and regime‑conditioned model selection framework… increases the frequency of selecting the true best model at specific horizons.”  
  - Section 3.3.2: AHSIV computes normalized AHSIV score; Section 4 shows improved horizon‑specific selection frequency.  
  - Corpus: Literature documents horizon‑dependent ranking instability; AHSIV explicitly counters this.
- **Break condition:** If the future horizon deviates substantially from the projected horizon or if structural breaks occur, the adjusted metrics become misleading.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Metric Degradation by Forecast Horizon (MDFH)**  
  - *Why needed:* To compare models on equal footing across different planning horizons without requiring future data.  
  - *Quick check question:* Given a stable series with MAE=10 at test horizon 4, and planning horizon 12 with alpha=0.5, what is the adjusted MAE?

- **Concept: Structural Demand Classification**  
  - *Why needed:* To determine which selection logic (Pareto vs. simple minimization) is appropriate for the series’ statistical properties.  
  - *Quick check question:* Compute CV of absolute differences for a series and decide if it is “stable” based on the paper’s thresholds.

- **Concept: Pareto Dominance and Lexicographic Refinement**  
  - *Why needed:* To combine multiple accuracy objectives (scaled and absolute error) without arbitrary weighting, while still breaking ties with bias and percentage error.  
  - *Quick check question:* If two models have identical RMSSE_adj but different MAE_adj, which one wins in the Pareto front? What if they also have same MAE_adj?

## Architecture Onboarding
Input: Historical demand, candidate model forecasts, planning horizon H

Component map:
Historical demand -> Preprocessing (compute test-set metrics: MAE, RMSE, RMSSE, BIAS, sMAPE on last 12 periods) -> Structural diagnostics (CV of absolute differences, mean absolute second differences) -> Stability check -> if stable: MDFH module (estimate α, adjust metrics to horizon H) -> Regime classification (demand frequency p, coefficient of variation c) -> Selection logic: if regular-stable (p≥0.5, c<0.7): Pareto front on (RMSSE_adj, MAE_adj), then bias minimization and sMAPE; else intermittent: minimize RMSSE_adj -> Output: selected model, full ranking, normalized AHSIV score

Critical path: Data → metric computation → regime classification → conditional MDFH adjustment → selection logic → ranking

Design tradeoffs:
- Conservatism: Stability thresholds are strict to avoid unjustified extrapolation; this may reject adjustment for some series that could be stable.
- Simplicity vs. information: For intermittent series, using only RMSSE reduces to a monometric selector; loss of bias information is intentional to avoid unstable rankings.
- Tie-breaking: Lexicographic order (bias → sMAPE) is simple but may not reflect cost asymmetry; could be extended with economic weights.

Failure signatures:
- MDFH returns unchanged metrics for many series due to strict stability → selection relies on test‑horizon performance only, which may not generalize.
- Regime misclassification (e.g., a low‑frequency but smooth series labeled “intermittent”) forces RMSSE‑only selection, potentially ignoring bias that would matter for inventory.
- Pareto front contains multiple models and tie‑breakers (bias, sMAPE) produce inconsistent selections across similar series.
- GRA evaluation (used in paper) is post‑hoc; AHSIV does not directly optimize volume coherence, so selected models may still misalign aggregate supply‑demand.

First 3 experiments:
1. MDFH stability check: Run MDFH on a known stable synthetic series (e.g., AR(1) with small noise) and on a series with a structural break. Verify adjustment occurs only for the stable one and that alpha estimation converges with sufficient blocks.
2. Regime threshold sensitivity: Vary p* (0.4, 0.5, 0.6) and c* (0.6, 0.7, 0.8) on a validation set. Observe how often the selected model matches the one chosen by a full Pareto approach (theoretically optimal for regular series). Report sensitivity of selection frequency.
3. Horizon‑specific best model selection: For each dataset, train candidate models and identify the true best model per horizon (lowest GRA). Compare how often AHSIV picks that true best vs. RMSSE_h across horizons 1–12. Replicates Table D3/E3/F3 patterns.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can probabilistic forecasting metrics (e.g., prediction intervals) be integrated into the MDFH and AHSIV framework to enable uncertainty-aware model selection?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state in the conclusions: "incorporating probabilistic forecasting metrics and predictive‑interval evaluation may enable the selector to incorporate uncertainty‑aware decision criteria."
- **Why unresolved:** Current point‑error metrics do not capture forecast uncertainty, which is critical for risk management in inventory decisions.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Extend MDFH to probabilistic metrics (e.g., interval coverage, sharpness) and evaluate on datasets with prediction intervals; compare inventory risk outcomes like service levels and holding costs.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How can cost‑sensitive evaluation functions that reflect asymmetric costs of overstocking and stockouts be incorporated into model selection to improve operational economic outcomes?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors propose in the conclusions: "the inclusion of cost‑sensitive evaluation functions could explicitly reflect the asymmetric economic consequences of overstocking and stockouts in inventory systems."
- **Why unresolved:** Existing accuracy metrics are symmetric and ignore the different financial impacts of overestimation versus underestimation.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Develop a cost‑based metric combining overage and underage costs, integrate it into MDFH/AHSIV, and test via simulation whether cost‑sensitive selection reduces total inventory costs compared to accuracy‑based selection.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Are power‑law degradation functions optimal for projecting horizon‑induced error growth, or do alternative functional forms (e.g., exponential, regime‑dependent) better model error propagation across diverse demand structures?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors suggest in the conclusions: "further work may also explore nonlinear or regime‑dependent degradation functions that capture heterogeneous error propagation patterns across demand structures."
- **Why unresolved:** MDFH assumes a power‑law with a single bounded alpha parameter, which may not generalize to all demand patterns, especially those with structural breaks or non‑stationarity.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Compare projection accuracy of different degradation functions (power‑law, exponential, piecewise) on long‑horizon test sets across datasets with varying intermittency and variability; assess impact on model selection accuracy.

## Limitations
- The Hierarchical Evaluation Function (HEF) used for model optimization is not defined, preventing exact replication of trained forecasts.
- Train‑test split ratios (91:9, 80:20, 70:30) are conceptual; the exact partitioning logic is unclear.
- MDFH parameters (error blocks for α estimation) and strict stability thresholds (CV_Δ < 0.2, mean|Δ²| < 0.1·mean|Δ|) may misclassify series, limiting adjustment.
- Fixed regime thresholds (p≥0.5, c<0.7) lack sensitivity analysis, raising generalizability concerns.
- Evaluation relies on Global Relative Accuracy (GRA), which only measures aggregate bias and may not reflect operational costs.
