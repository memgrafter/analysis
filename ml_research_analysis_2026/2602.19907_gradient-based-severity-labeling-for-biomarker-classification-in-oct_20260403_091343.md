# Gradient based Severity Labeling for Biomarker Classification in OCT

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.19907
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19907
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Supervised contrastive learning with GradCON-derived severity pseudo-labels achieves up to 6% higher accuracy than self-supervised baselines for Diabetic Retinopathy biomarkers and a multi-label AUC of 0.774.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the challenge of biomarker classification in Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) scans, where labeled data is scarce and traditional augmentations in contrastive learning can distort small biomarker regions. Their core contribution is a method that generates severity pseudo-labels for unlabeled OCT scans using gradient responses from a GradCON anomaly detector. These pseudo-labels are then used to train a supervised contrastive learning framework, which better captures disease-related variations.

Experiments on diabetic retinopathy biomarkers show consistent improvements over self-supervised baselines. The proposed approach yields up to 6% higher accuracy and achieves a multi-label AUC of 0.774, outperforming SimCLR (0.754), PCL (0.767), and MoCo v2 (0.769). For individual biomarkers, DME accuracy increases from 80.61% to 84.52%, and IRHRF from 59.03% to 62.93%. These results suggest that severity-guided contrastive learning can effectively leverage unlabeled data to enhance biomarker classification.

## Method Summary
The method first trains an autoencoder on a dataset of healthy OCT scans. For any given OCT image, the GradCON detector computes two quantities: the normalized reconstruction error \(E_{\text{recon}}/E_0\) and the cosine similarity between the gradient of the reconstruction loss with respect to the input and that of a healthy reference. These are combined into a severity score \(S = \alpha \cdot (E_{\text{recon}}/E_0) + (1-\alpha) \cdot \cos(\nabla x, \nabla x_0)\). Discretizing these scores into \(N\) bins yields pseudo-labels that approximate disease severity. A contrastive encoder (e.g., ResNet) with a projection head is then trained using a supervised contrastive loss: images within the same bin form positive pairs, while images from different bins form negatives. The trained encoder can subsequently be used for downstream biomarker classification, typically with a linear probe. This approach avoids augmentations that might distort small lesions and provides a fine-grained supervisory signal from unlabeled data.

## Key Results
- Up to 6% higher accuracy than self-supervised baselines for Diabetic Retinopathy biomarkers.
- Multi-label AUC of 0.774, surpassing SimCLR (0.754), PCL (0.767), and MoCo v2 (0.769).
- DME accuracy improves from 80.61% to 84.52% and IRHRF from 59.03% to 62.93%.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Severity pseudo-labels provide a continuous and nuanced supervision signal that captures subtle differences in disease manifestation. The gradient alignment component of GradCON emphasizes the edges and boundaries of biomarkers, making the severity scores less sensitive to noise and more aligned with clinically relevant features. By grouping images of similar severity during contrastive training, the encoder learns representations that are sensitive to the morphological variations tied to disease progression. Consequently, the resulting features are more discriminative for biomarker classification compared to representations learned from generic augmentations or purely unsupervised objectives.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Raw OCT → Preprocessing → GradCON Autoencoder (trained on healthy) → Reconstruction error & Gradient alignment → Severity score (S = α·E_recon/E0 + (1-α)·cos(∇x,∇x0)) → Discretization into N bins (pseudo-label) → Contrastive Encoder (ResNet) + Projection head → Supervised Contrastive Loss → Encoder update.

### Critical Path
1. Train autoencoder on a set of healthy OCT scans.
2. For each unlabeled OCT image, compute severity score using the fixed autoencoder.
3. Discretize scores into N bins to assign a pseudo-label.
4. Train the contrastive encoder with the supervised contrastive loss using these pseudo-labels.
5. Freeze the encoder and train a linear classifier on top for final biomarker classification.

### Design Tradeoffs
- α weighting: Balances reconstruction error vs. gradient alignment; requires tuning.
- Bin count N: Controls granularity of pseudo-labels; trade-off between too noisy (large N) and too coarse (small N).
- Healthy dataset dependency: Requires a clean set of healthy scans; contamination degrades severity scoring.
- Computational cost: Gradient computation for GradCON is expensive; may limit scalability.

### Failure Signatures
- Severity scores uncorrelated with actual disease severity (low cluster purity) → poor downstream performance.
- Extreme α values (near 0 or 1) cause one component to dominate, losing the benefit of combined signal.
- N too large leads to bins with few positives, making contrastive learning unstable; N too small merges distinct severities.
- Autoencoder trained on insufficient or non-representative healthy data yields poor anomaly detection.

### First Experiments
1. **Ablation on α**: Vary α (e.g., 0.01, 0.03, 0.05, 0.1) and measure multi-label AUC and per-biomarker accuracy.
2. **Adaptive binning**: Develop a binning strategy based on initial severity distribution (e.g., equal-frequency bins) and compare to uniform discretization.
3. **Anomaly detector comparison**: Replace GradCON with MSP, ODIN, or simple reconstruction error, keeping all else constant, and evaluate downstream classification performance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: How does the choice of the weighting factor α in the severity score equation affect the discriminative quality of pseudo-labels and downstream biomarker classification performance?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The severity score combines reconstruction error and gradient alignment (Eq. 1), with α fixed at 0.03 without analysis of its sensitivity.
- Why unresolved: The trade-off between these components is not studied; α may need dataset-specific tuning, impacting robustness.
- What evidence would resolve it: Ablation experiments varying α, evaluating pseudo-label coherence (e.g., cluster purity) and final accuracy/F1 across biomarkers.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can an adaptive binning strategy for severity scores, tailored to individual biomarker characteristics, outperform the fixed bin count (N) used in the paper?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] Table 1 shows different optimal N for different biomarkers (e.g., DME best at N=15000, IRHRF at N=20000), suggesting fixed binning may not suit all.
- Why unresolved: The paper uses uniform discretization, ignoring that biomarker distinctiveness and prevalence might require different granularity to balance positive pair diversity and similarity.
- What evidence would resolve it: Develop a metric (e.g., based on initial severity distribution or biomarker prevalence) to set N per biomarker, then compare multi-label AUC and per-biomarker accuracy against fixed N.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Does reliance on a separate healthy dataset limit clinical applicability, and can the healthy manifold be effectively learned from unlabeled data containing both healthy and diseased samples?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The method requires a dedicated healthy dataset to train the autoencoder for severity scoring (Sec. 3.2), which may not be available in practice.
- Why unresolved: Real-world unlabeled pools often mix disease states; learning a pure healthy distribution without contamination is challenging but unexplored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Train the autoencoder on progressively contaminated "healthy" sets (adding diseased samples) and measure severity label quality (e.g., correlation with true severity) and downstream performance.

### Open Question 4
- Question: Why do gradient-based severity scores from GradCON outperform other anomaly detection methods (e.g., MSP, ODIN) in generating pseudo-labels for contrastive learning in this context?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] Table 2 shows GradCON-based labeling (SL) achieves higher multi-label AUC than other detectors with identical binning, but the reason is not analyzed.
- Why unresolved: The paper compares results but does not explain whether gradient representations capture more semantically relevant features for biomarker similarity than output-based scores.
- What evidence would resolve it: Analyze feature embeddings from each scoring method (e.g., via t-SNE) to assess cluster separation of similar-severity images; correlate severity scores with expert-graded biomarker severity if available.

## Limitations
- Lack of dataset details, code, and statistical tests hinders reproducibility and confidence in reported gains.
- Dependence on a separate healthy dataset may limit applicability in settings where such data is unavailable.
- Improvements for certain biomarkers (e.g., IRHRF at ~63% accuracy) are modest and may not be clinically sufficient.
