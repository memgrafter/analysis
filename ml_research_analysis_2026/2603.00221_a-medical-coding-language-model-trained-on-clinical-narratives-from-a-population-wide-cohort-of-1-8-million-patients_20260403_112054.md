# A medical coding language model trained on clinical narratives from a population-wide cohort of 1.8 million patients

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.00221
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00221
- Reference count: 17
- Primary result: Achieved micro F1 71.8% and top-10 recall 95.5% on a population-wide test set, enabling full automation for 54.6% of cases.

## Executive Summary
The study developed a language model (PLM-CA) using Danish BERT to automatically assign ICD-10 codes to electronic health records from 1.8 million patients across Denmark. Trained on 5.8 million clinical documents, the model predicts codes from notes, medications, and lab results. On a held-out test set it reached a micro F1 of 71.8% and exact match of 54.6%, while top‑10 recall hit 95.5%. Performance varied widely by medical specialty (53–91%). Notably, manual review confirmed that 76–86% of model‑predicted but previously uncoded secondary diagnoses (suicide‑related behaviors, weight disorders, hypertension) were clinically valid, revealing systematic under‑coding in human annotations. The work demonstrates that large‑scale data dramatically improves coding accuracy, especially for rare conditions, and highlights the need to rethink coding incentives and documentation practices.

## Method Summary
The authors used the Danish national EHR cohort (2006–2016) comprising 5.8 million records for 1.8 million patients. Preprocessing kept only cases with discharge summaries, removed codes occurring fewer than 10 times, excluded Z‑codes only, removed undefined codes, dropped texts >10 000 characters, and excluded adult psychiatry. Each patient’s concatenated clinical notes, medications, and lab results were tokenized and split into non‑overlapping 128‑token windows (up to 10 000 tokens total). The PLM‑CA architecture processed each window with a Danish BERT base encoder, then applied label‑wise attention: a trainable query per ICD‑10 level‑3 code attended over all token embeddings to produce a confidence score. The model was trained with binary cross‑entropy loss, AdamW (learning rate 5×10⁻⁵, batch size 128) for up to 10 epochs, early stopping on validation mean average precision. A threshold maximizing micro F1 on the validation set was selected for test evaluation. The code and data split (1.8 M training, 5 000 validation, 271 172 test) were provided.

## Key Results
- Micro F1: 71.8% on held‑out test set.
- Top‑10 recall: 95.5%.
- Exact match ratio: 54.6% (cases where all predicted codes matched human annotations).
- Specialty performance ranged from 53% (child psychiatry) to 91% (clinical neurophysiology).
- Manual validation of model‑identified uncoded secondary diagnoses: 76%–86% confirmed as clinically valid for suicide, weight disorders, and hypertension.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Larger training data (1.8M patients) significantly improves coding accuracy, especially for rare conditions.
- **Mechanism**: Increased data volume enhances representation of low‑frequency diseases and complex multimorbidity patterns, enabling the language model to learn more robust feature representations.
- **Core assumption**: Model capacity (BERT) is not the bottleneck; data quantity drives performance.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - Abstract: "larger training datasets... notably enhanced the model’s ability to predict rare diseases."
  - Section 2.1: Micro F1 rises from 59.9% to 71.8% and macro F1 from 25.6% to 47.3% as training patients increase from 50K to 1.8M.
  - Corpus weak; neighboring works focus on LLMs for EHR but not scale effects.
- **Break condition**: If label noise (e.g., under‑coding) dominates rare‑class learning, further scaling yields diminishing returns.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Low F1 scores for secondary diagnoses are primarily due to systematic under‑coding in human annotations, not model inability.
- **Mechanism**: The model detects relevant clinical features but is supervised by incomplete labels; it learns to down‑weight secondary diagnoses because they are often missing in training data, resulting in low confidence predictions.
- **Core assumption**: Model feature extraction is reliable; coding incentives or guidelines cause humans to omit secondary codes.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - Abstract: "manual review confirmed 76–86% of model‑identified, uncoded cases... valid, indicating systematic under‑coding."
  - Section 2.4: Lowering threshold raised recall to 64.5–86.0% and manual validation confirmed 76–86% of added cases.
  - Section 3.1: Attributes under‑coding to reimbursement structures rewarding primary diagnoses.
- **Break condition**: If coding practices change to capture all secondary diagnoses, this mechanism reverses; if confidence is not recalibrated, it may produce many false positives.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Specialty performance differences are driven by diagnostic workflow standardization and patient multimorbidity.
- **Mechanism**: Specialties with high protocol adherence (e.g., clinical neurophysiology) produce structured notes and clear code assignments, while specialties with high uncertainty and many comorbidities (e.g., geriatrics, child psychiatry) yield ambiguous documentation and incomplete secondary coding, posing greater learning challenges.
- **Core assumption**: Documentation quality and coding consistency vary by specialty and directly affect learnability.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - Abstract: "Performance varied by specialty (F1: 53–91%)."
  - Section 2.2: Highest scores in clinical neurophysiology (91%), lowest in child psychiatry (53%); links to standardized workflows and multimorbidity.
  - Section 2.5: Imprecise diabetes coding (type buried) and missing heart failure documentation show how note quality causes errors.
- **Break condition**: If documentation guidelines are uniformly enforced, variation may shrink; inherent clinical complexity may still cause differences.

## Foundational Learning
### Multilabel classification
- **Why needed**: Each patient can have multiple ICD‑10 codes; predicting each code independently as a binary task is a multilabel problem.
- **Quick check**: "What loss function is typically used for multilabel classification, and how does it treat each label?" (Binary cross‑entropy applied independently per label.)

### Class imbalance
- **Why needed**: ICD codes are heavily long‑tailed; macro‑F1 improvement with data shows rare codes benefit more. Without handling imbalance, the model ignores rare but important conditions.
- **Quick check**: "Why might simple oversampling of rare‑label examples fail in multilabel medical coding, and what alternative loss weighting strategy could help?" (Oversampling can duplicate noisy patterns and ignores label co‑occurrence; per‑label weighting or focal loss is more robust.)

### Label noise from under‑coding
- **Why needed**: Many true secondary diagnoses are missing from the training labels; this label noise depresses supervised learning performance if uncorrected.
- **Quick check**: "How did the authors estimate the true recall for secondary diagnoses despite incomplete ground truth?" (By manually reviewing a random sample of model‑predicted but uncoded cases and computing the proportion confirmed as clinically valid.)

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Input (concatenated clinical notes, medications, lab results) → Tokenization and splitting into non‑overlapping 128‑token windows → Danish BERT encoder → Label-wise attention (one attention head per ICD‑10 code) → Confidence score per code. Training uses binary cross‑entropy; inference applies a threshold to produce final code predictions.

### Critical path
1. **Preprocess**: Filter records (keep discharge summaries, remove rare codes, Z‑codes only, undefined codes, texts >10k chars, exclude adult psychiatry). Split patients into training (1.8M), validation (5k), test (271k).
2. **Tokenize and window**: Convert text to tokens, create non‑overlapping 128‑token chunks.
3. **Forward pass**: Process each window through BERT; label‑wise attention aggregates token embeddings across all windows into a score per code.
4. **Loss**: Binary cross‑entropy between scores and multi‑hot label vector.
5. **Validate**: Compute micro/macro F1, recall@K, MAP on validation set.
6. **Threshold tuning**: Grid search threshold on validation to maximize micro F1.
7. **Manual analysis**: Use AttInGrad (attention × gradients) to interpret predictions and investigate errors, especially for secondary diagnoses.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Windowing**: Balances memory use but loses cross‑window longitudinal context, potentially harming diagnoses that span the entire patient history.
- **Universal threshold**: Simplifies deployment but ignores per‑code calibration; per‑code thresholds could improve recall‑precision trade‑off but require more validation.
- **Code frequency cutoff**: Reduces output space and eases training but discards clinically relevant rare codes.
- **Recall vs precision**: Suggestion tools benefit from lower thresholds (high recall), but too low increases noise and user cognitive load.

### Failure signatures
- Macro‑F1 much lower than micro‑F1 → poor rare‑code performance.
- Specialty scores <60% (e.g., child psychiatry) → documentation ambiguity or coding guideline issues.
- High model confidence in secondary diagnoses suppressed during training → classic sign of under‑coding; lowering threshold recovers them.
- Explanations highlight administrative cues (e.g., department codes) instead of clinical findings → missing documentation.
- Generic terms (e.g., "diabetes") dominate specific details → imprecise coding.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Scale ablation**: Train on increasing patient counts (50K to 1.8M) and compare F1 metrics to quantify data impact.
2. **Specialty analysis**: Compute per‑department F1 and correlate with workflow characteristics (standardization, multimorbidity) to identify challenging areas.
3. **Threshold calibration for secondary codes**: Lower thresholds for selected secondary‑heavy codes, manually validate newly predicted cases, and estimate true positive rate to adjust calibration.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Real‑world deployment effectiveness** – How do automated coding systems perform when integrated as decision support in clinical workflows? The study notes that user studies are needed; current evaluations are retrospective.
2. **Joint handling of class imbalance and label noise** – What ML approaches can simultaneously address extreme class imbalance and systematic under‑coding? Standard techniques like up‑sampling fail due to co‑occurrence and noise.
3. **Threshold determination at scale** – How to set optimal decision thresholds for thousands of codes when ground truth for secondary diagnoses is incomplete? Authors propose synthetic data or expert‑curated subsets.
4. **Root causes of specialty variation** – What specific workflow and diagnostic characteristics drive the observed F1 range (53–91%)? The analysis is correlational; no interventions tested.
5. **Impact of documentation quality** – Can improving documentation practices (e.g., structured templates) reduce under‑coding and boost model accuracy? The authors link under‑coding to reimbursement incentives but lack intervention data.

## Limitations
- Generalizability limited to Danish EHRs (2006–2016); coding practices and documentation styles may differ elsewhere.
- Systematic label noise from under‑coding may be more widespread than the three conditions manually validated.
- Non‑overlapping 128‑token windowing may discard longitudinal context crucial for multimorbidity.

## Confidence
- **High**: Test‑set metrics (micro F1, top‑10 recall, exact match).
- **Medium**: Data scaling improves rare‑disease performance (macro F1 increase).
- **Medium‑High**: Under‑coding hypothesis (high manual validation rates for predicted uncoded cases).
- **Medium**: Specialty performance variation attributed to workflow standardization and multimorbidity.
- **Low**: Extrinsic validity to other healthcare systems or modern EHR architectures.

## Next Checks
1. **External validation** – Evaluate the model on a non‑Danish cohort (e.g., MIMIC‑IV) to assess portability across coding systems and documentation norms.
2. **Windowing ablation** – Compare non‑overlapping windows to overlapping or hierarchical approaches to quantify context loss and its effect on rare‑code recall.
3. **Per‑code threshold optimization** – Train code‑specific thresholds on validation data and evaluate precision/recall for low‑frequency codes to refine deployment trade‑offs.
