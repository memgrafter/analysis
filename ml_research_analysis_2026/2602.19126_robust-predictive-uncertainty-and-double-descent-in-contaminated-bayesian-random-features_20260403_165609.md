# Robust Predictive Uncertainty and Double Descent in Contaminated Bayesian Random Features

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.19126
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19126
- **Reference count:** 32
- **Primary result:** The study derives explicit, tractable bounds for lower and upper posterior predictive densities and variances under ε- and η-contaminated Bayesian random features, preserving double descent in high-dimensional asymptotics, and introduces an Imprecise Highest Density Region (IHDR) for robust uncertainty quantification.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles robust predictive uncertainty quantification in Bayesian random feature (RF) regression when both the prior and likelihood are misspecified. The core innovation is the Contamination RF model, which replaces the standard Gaussian prior and likelihood with ε-contaminated prior credal sets and η-contaminated likelihood credal sets. Inference proceeds via pessimistic generalized Bayesian updating, yielding worst-case posterior conclusions. The authors derive explicit and tractable bounds for the resulting lower and upper posterior predictive densities and variances. These bounds are affine transformations of the classical Gaussian predictive quantities, and crucially, they retain the high-dimensional asymptotics and double-descent behavior (a variance peak at the interpolation threshold) known for RF models.

For practical application, the paper defines an Imprecise Highest Density Region (IHDR) as the smallest set attaining a target lower predictive probability. It is shown that the IHDR can be efficiently outer-approximated by a scaled Gaussian credible interval, where the scaling depends on the contamination parameter η. This provides a computationally simple way to report robust predictive intervals. Theoretical results are supported by analysis of the double-descent phenomenon under contamination, proving that the peak location remains unchanged. The work establishes a bridge between imprecise probability theory and modern high-dimensional learning, offering principled worst-case guarantees without sacrificing tractability.

## Method Summary
The authors consider Bayesian linear regression with random features, where ridge-regularized training corresponds to a Gaussian prior on coefficients and a Gaussian likelihood. To guard against misspecification, they replace these point-valued distributions with credal sets: an ε-contaminated prior P_ε consisting of all mixtures (1-ε)P_N + εQ with finitely additive Q, and similarly an η-contaminated likelihood L_η. Inference uses the pessimistic generalized Bayesian update, which normalizes the product of prior density and likelihood via the Choquet upper integral over the credal set. This yields lower and upper posterior densities. Integrating these posteriors against the likelihood for a new input gives predictive densities. The authors prove that the lower predictive density is at most (1-η) times the classical Gaussian predictive density, while the upper predictive density is at least (1-η) times the classical density plus an arbitrary contamination term u(ỹ). Predictive variances satisfy analogous affine inequalities: Ṽ ≤ (1-η)V_classical and Ṽ ≳ (1-η)V_truncated + η(b-a)²/12, where the truncation approximates the upper bound.

These envelopes maintain the leading-order proportional-growth asymptotics and the double-descent peak location of the classical RF variance. Moreover, the Imprecise Highest Density Region (IHDR)—the minimal set with lower predictive probability at least 1-α—can be outer-approximated by the min{1, 1-α/(1-η)} credible interval of the classical Gaussian predictive, providing an easy-to-compute robust interval.

## Key Results
- **Affine predictive envelopes:** The lower posterior predictive density satisfies ℓ̃_pred(ỹ) ≤ (1–η) ℓ_pred(ỹ), and the upper satisfies ℓ̃_pred(ỹ) ≥ (1–η) ℓ_pred(ỹ) + η u(ỹ) for some contamination density u. Corresponding variance bounds are Ṽ ≤ (1–η) V_classical and Ṽ ≳ (1–η) V_truncated + η(b–a)²/12.
- **Preservation of double descent:** Under proportional-growth asymptotics (n, d, N → ∞ with n/d, N/d fixed), the contaminated predictive variance bounds retain the same leading-order behavior as the classical variance, and the double-descent peak at the interpolation threshold (N ≈ n) remains at the same location.
- **Efficient robust intervals:** The Imprecise Highest Density Region (IHDR) for a confidence level α can be outer-approximated by the Gaussian credible interval with coverage min{1, 1–α/(1–η)}, yielding a practical tool for robust uncertainty quantification.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Contamination of the prior and likelihood credal sets induces affine transformations of the classical posterior predictive density, yielding explicit worst-case predictive envelopes.
- **Mechanism:** Pessimistic generalized Bayesian updating with ε-contaminated prior and η-contaminated likelihood credal sets. The lower posterior predictive density ℓ̃_pred satisfies ℓ̃_pred ≤ (1–η) ℓ_pred, and the upper posterior predictive density ℓ̃_pred satisfies a complementary inequality. This arises because the Choquet integral with respect to lower/upper probabilities simplifies under contaminations, and the likelihood contamination acts as an effective adversarial dilution.
- **Core assumption:** The contamination parameters ε and η are moderate (not extreme), and the contaminating densities are chosen as bounded spikes (Dirac-like) to ensure well-defined envelope densities.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "We derive explicit and tractable bounds for the resulting lower and upper posterior predictive densities... the lower posterior predictive density ℓ̃_pred satisfies ℓ̃_pred ≤ (1–η) ℓ_pred."
  - [section 3] "We prove that the lower posterior predictive density ℓ̃_pred satisfies ℓ̃_pred ≤ (1–η) ℓ_pred, and that the upper posterior predictive density ℓ̃_pred satisfies a complementary inequality."
  - [corpus] Neighbor papers on "Bayesian predictive coding" and "Credal Bayesian Deep Learning" explore related robustness via imprecise probabilities, suggesting a growing interest in contamination-style robustness beyond exact models.
- **Break condition:** If ε or η approach 1, the bounds become vacuous (e.g., lower density → 0 or upper density → arbitrary) and the approximation "contamination ≈ direct predictive contamination" fails because prior/likelihood ambiguity no longer translates simply to posterior predictive envelopes.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The Imprecise Highest Density Region (IHDR), defined as the smallest set attaining target lower predictive probability, can be outer-approximated by an adjusted Gaussian credible interval, enabling computationally tractable robust predictive intervals.
- **Mechanism:** The lower posterior predictive density bound ℓ̃_pred ≤ (1–η) ℓ_pred implies that any region with classical Gaussian probability at least 1–α/(1–η) guarantees lower probability ≥ 1–α under the contaminated model. The IHDR minimizes region measure subject to this lower probability constraint; due to the unimodal Normal structure, the minimum-measure region is a central interval, and the adjusted coverage accounts for the (1–η) scaling.
- **Core assumption:** The classical posterior predictive ℓ_pred is a unimodal, symmetric density (Normal), so its highest density region coincides with a symmetric credible interval.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "We introduce an Imprecise Highest Density Region (IHDR) for robust predictive uncertainty quantification and show that it admits an efficient outer approximation via an adjusted Gaussian credible interval."
  - [section 3.1] Proposition 5 formally states the outer approximation: the α-level IHDR is contained in the min{1, 1–α/(1–η)}-level credible interval of ℓ_pred.
  - [corpus] Neighbor work on "Bayesian implementation of Targeted Maximum Likelihood Estimation" discusses robust uncertainty regions, indicating a parallel interest in practical robust interval construction.
- **Break condition:** If ℓ_pred is not unimodal (e.g., multimodal posterior from complex features), the highest density region may not be a single interval, and the adjusted credible interval may not outer-approximate the IHDR tightly.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Predictive variance bounds under contamination are affine transformations of the classical variance, preserving the double-descent peak location in proportional-growth high-dimensional asymptotics.
- **Mechanism:** The lower predictive variance is bounded above by (1–η)V_classical; the upper variance (under truncation) is bounded below by (1–η)V_truncated + constant η(b–a)²/12. Because these are monotone affine transformations of V_classical(ψ₁; λ), which exhibits double descent as a function of ψ₁ = N/d, the maximizer (interpolation peak) is invariant under such transformations by Lemma 9. The leading-order asymptotics of V_classical are inherited due to constant contamination parameters.
- **Core assumption:** The truncation interval [a, b] for the upper bound is chosen such that the truncated variance V_truncated approximates V_classical closely (e.g., a = μ – kσ, b = μ + kσ with k∈[2,3]).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "We further obtain predictive variance bounds... and prove that they preserve the leading-order proportional-growth asymptotics known for RF models... the contaminated predictive variances form an envelope around the same double-descent peak."
  - [section 4] Lemma 8 states the chain of inequalities Ṽ ≤ (1–η)V ≤ V ≤ (1–η)V + η(b–a)²/12 ≈ (1–η)V′ + η(b–a)²/12 ≤ Ṽ, and Section 5 proves Corollary 9.1 that the peak location ψ₁* is preserved.
  - [corpus] Neighbor papers "High-dimensional analysis of double descent" and "Incorporating priors in learning" study double descent in random matrix settings, supporting the mechanism that affine scaling does not shift the peak.
- **Break condition:** If the contamination level η is data-dependent and grows with dimension, the affine transformation assumption fails; the peak location could shift if η(ψ₁) is non-constant. Also, the truncation approximation V′ ≈ V may break if [a, b] does not capture most Gaussian mass (e.g., narrow interval).

## Foundational Learning
- **Credal sets and imprecise probability:** Understanding credal sets (convex, weak*-closed sets of probabilities) and lower/upper probabilities is essential, as the contamination sets P_ε and L_η are defined as mixtures of a baseline measure with arbitrary alternatives. The pessimistic Bayesian update uses lower/upper posteriors.
  - *Why needed here:* The entire method replaces point-valued priors/likelihoods with sets and performs set-valued updating.
  - Quick check question: How does the lower probability P(A) for a contaminated prior differ from the classical prior probability P_N(A) when ε>0?
- **Pessimistic generalized Bayesian updating:** The update rule P(a|data) ∝ p(a)ℓ_a(y) with denominator being a Choquet integral (upper expectation) under the credal set. This yields conservative posterior conclusions.
  - *Why needed here:* The paper explicitly adopts the "pessimistic" update (Walley's Theorem 6.4.6) to derive worst-case predictive bounds.
  - Quick check question: Why is the denominator in the update a Choquet integral rather than an ordinary expected likelihood?
- **Random features and Bayesian equivalence:** Classical ridge-regularized RF training is equivalent to Bayesian inference with a Gaussian prior on coefficients and Gaussian likelihood. The posterior predictive is Normal with mean = ridge predictor and variance = Bayesian predictive variance.
  - *Why needed here:* The contamination analysis builds on the classical RF predictive as a baseline; the asymptotic results (double descent) depend on the known behavior of this baseline.
  - Quick check question: In the classical RF Bayesian model, what is the form of the posterior predictive distribution for a new input, and how does it relate to the ridge solution?
- **Double descent in high dimensions:** In proportional-growth regimes (n, d, N → ∞ with n/d, N/d fixed), the predictive variance of ridgeless least squares/RF exhibits a peak at N ≈ n (interpolation threshold) when λ=0.
  - *Why needed here:* The paper's Corollary 9.1 states that contamination does not shift the peak location; this hinges on understanding the baseline double descent curve V_base(ψ₁; λ).
  - Quick check question: What does "double descent" refer to in the context of random feature regression, and what causes the variance peak near N=n?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  1. **Contamination parameters:** ε (prior contamination) and η (likelihood contamination) ∈ [0,1]. Control ambiguity budget.
  2. **Credal sets:** P_ε = {(1–ε)P_N + εQ : Q finitely additive probability} and similarly L_η.
  3. **Pessimistic update:** Lower posterior p(a|y) ∝ p(a)ℓ_a(y) with denominator = Choquet integral ∫ ℓ_a(y)P(da) over P∈P_ε.
  4. **Predictive density bounds:** Derived lower ℓ̃_pred(ỹ) ≤ (1–η)ℓ_pred(ỹ); upper ℓ̃_pred(ỹ) ≥ (1–η)ℓ_pred(ỹ) + η u(ỹ).
  5. **IHDR:** For target α, find smallest set with P_pred(Set) ≥ 1–α; outer-approximate by Gaussian credible interval with level min{1, 1–α/(1–η)}.
  6. **Variance bounds:** Lower Ṽ ≤ (1–η)V_classical; Upper Ṽ ≳ (1–η)V_truncated + η(b–a)²/12.
- **Critical path:**
  1. Define contamination credal sets for prior and likelihood.
  2. Perform pessimistic updating to obtain lower/upper posterior densities.
  3. Derive predictive densities via integration over a, yield affine bounds in terms of ℓ_pred.
  4. For IHDR: use bound to map desired lower probability to classical interval coverage.
  5. For variance: compute classical variance, apply affine bounds; confirm double descent peak preservation via Lemma 9.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Tractability vs. conservatism:* The bounds are explicit and computationally cheap but may be loose; the upper bound includes an arbitrary contamination density u(ỹ), requiring a choice (e.g., uniform on [a,b] for truncation).
  - *Truncation interval [a,b] in upper variance:* Must balance accuracy (wide interval to capture Gaussian mass) and tightness (narrow interval to minimize η(b–a)²/12 term). Heuristic: use μ ± 3σ.
  - *Finitely additive measures:* The credal sets require finitely additive probabilities to be weak*-closed; this is non-standard but allows natural spike contaminations. Implementation may approximate with countably additive alternatives.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Bounds become non-informative: if η=1, lower density bound → 0 and upper density bound → arbitrary; variance bounds degenerate.
  - IHDR outer approximation fails: if ℓ_pred is not unimodal (e.g., due to complex features), the highest density region is not a single interval, so the adjusted Gaussian interval may not outer-approximate the IHDR tightly.
  - Truncation error dominates: if [a,b] is too narrow, V_truncated deviates significantly from V_classical, making the upper variance bound overly conservative.
  - Asymptotic mismatch: if contamination levels ε, η depend on dimension and do not remain constant, the leading-order asymptotics of V_classical may not be preserved in the bounds.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Tightness of predictive density bounds:** Sample from contaminated prior/likelihood (e.g., spike-and-slab) and compute empirical lower/upper predictive densities via Monte Carlo; compare to theoretical bounds (1–η)ℓ_pred and (1–η)ℓ_pred + η u.
  2. **IHDR coverage validation:** For a given α, construct the adjusted Gaussian interval (level min{1, 1–α/(1–η}}) and check that for all P∈M(P_pred), P(Interval) ≥ 1–α holds empirically under various contamination scenarios.
  3. **Double descent peak preservation:** Vary ψ₁ = N/d in high-dimensional simulations (large d, n fixed ratio ψ₂) with label outliers (Huber contamination, ρ≈η). Plot test variance or MSE vs. ψ₁; verify that the peak location remains near ψ₁ ≈ ψ₂ (interpolation) across increasing ρ, consistent with Corollary 9.1.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can the contamination parameters ε and η be chosen adaptively from data to balance robustness and efficiency?
- **Basis:** Future work in the conclusion proposes "data‑adaptive choices of contamination levels."
- **Why unresolved:** The current analysis treats ε and η as fixed constants; a principled adaptive method is required for practical deployment and optimal trade-offs.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A cross‑validation or empirical Bayes procedure for selecting ε and η, validated by improved predictive coverage and preserved double descent in experiments.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Does the upper predictive variance bound strictly require the truncation approximation, and can an exact, non‑approximate bound be derived?
- **Basis:** Proposition 7 relies on a truncation interval to obtain a tractable envelope; the authors note this is a mild assumption.
- **Why unresolved:** Truncation may lead to conservative constant offsets; an exact bound would provide tighter uncertainty quantification without sacrificing analytic simplicity.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A rigorous calculation of the upper Choquet integral for squared loss under the contaminated model, yielding a closed‑form or a more refined bound that matches the truncated one only if necessary.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Are the derived robustness properties, including the double‑descent envelope, extendable to the kernel limit (N→∞) or to deep random‑feature networks?
- **Basis:** The conclusion explicitly suggests investigating "extensions to kernel limits and deep feature models."
- **Why unresolved:** The finite‑N analysis leverages specific Gaussian priors and ridge regularization; the infinite‑width or multi‑layer regimes involve different asymptotics and may break tractability.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Derivation of analogous contamination sets for the neural tangent kernel or deep random‑feature architectures, showing via random matrix theory that double descent and affine variance envelopes persist.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How do the proposed Imprecise Highest Density Regions (IHDRs) behave under structured corruptions beyond simple label outliers, such as covariate shift or adversarial noise?
- **Basis:** The contamination model focuses on Huber‑type label contamination; it does not address more complex, structured forms of misspecification.
- **Why unresolved:** Robustness guarantees are worst‑case over the specified credal sets, but structured corruptions may lead to different coverage properties; IHDR outer approximation could be overly conservative or under‑confident in such settings.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Empirical evaluations comparing IHDR coverage under a variety of corruption types (e.g., covariate shift, targeted attacks) and theoretical development of ambiguity sets that incorporate such structure.

## Limitations
- **Assumptions:** The analysis assumes constant ε and η, finitely additive priors, Gaussian posterior predictive, and a fixed truncation interval; violations may invalidate bounds or increase conservatism.
- **Tightness:** The affine envelope can be loose, particularly the upper bound which includes an arbitrary contaminating density and an additive η(b-a)²/12 term.
- **Asymptotics:** The preservation of double descent holds only in proportional-growth limits; in finite samples the peak may shift or flatten.
