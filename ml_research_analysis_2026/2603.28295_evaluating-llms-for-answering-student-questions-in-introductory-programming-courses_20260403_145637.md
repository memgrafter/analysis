# Evaluating LLMs for Answering Student Questions in Introductory Programming Courses

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.28295
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.28295
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Gemini 3 flash achieved a score of 3.34/5, surpassing the human educator baseline of 2.93/5, at a cost of ~$0.50 per 1,140 questions.

## Executive Summary
Educators in introductory programming courses struggle to provide timely, personalized feedback on student questions. This paper addresses that challenge by curating a benchmark of 170 authentic student questions from the Dodona LMS, each paired with an expert-authored answer that avoids giving away complete solutions. To evaluate LLM-generated answers, the authors developed a custom LLM-as-a-Judge metric calibrated against subject matter expert (SME) scores, measuring pedagogical accuracy on a 0–5 scale. Key results show that Gemini 3 flash outperformed the Best Available Human baseline (3.34 vs. 2.93) while incurring minimal cost. The study advocates a “teacher-in-the-loop” deployment, where educators review LLM drafts before they reach students, and proposes a task-agnostic evaluation framework to standardize pre-deployment validation of educational LLM tools.

## Method Summary
The research introduced a reproducible four-stage evaluation framework. First, a dataset of 170 real student questions (with associated code, assignment descriptions, failing tests, and lint errors) was collected and filtered for clarity and GDPR compliance. Second, a single SME authored ground truth answers focused on pedagogical hints rather than full solutions. Third, the team engineered an LLM-as-a-Judge metric: a rubric‑based prompt instructing an LLM to score generated answers by alignment with the ground truth’s “core teaching points.” Calibration on 100 samples yielded substantial agreement with SME scores (Cohen’s weighted κ = 0.655, 91.66% within ±1 point). Fourth, multiple LLMs (Gemini 3 flash, GPT‑5 mini, Claude Opus 4.5) were prompted consistently and evaluated on the benchmark; their outputs were scored by the calibrated judge, and cost‑efficiency trade‑offs were analyzed. The framework is designed to be reused for future models without new data collection.

## Key Results
- Gemini 3 flash achieved the highest pedagogical accuracy score (3.34/5), exceeding the Best Available Human educator baseline (2.93/5).
- Most flagship models outperformed humans at very low cost (≈ $0.50 per 1,140 questions), demonstrating economic viability.
- The findings support a teacher‑in‑the‑loop deployment as a practical balance between scalability and accuracy, mitigating risks like hallucination while reducing educator workload.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** A custom LLM-as-a-Judge metric can scalably and reliably evaluate pedagogical accuracy of LLM-generated answers against expert ground truth.
- **Mechanism:** The metric uses a carefully engineered prompt (with a scoring rubric and YAML output format) to instruct an LLM to compare a generated answer to an expert answer, scoring 0–5 based on coverage of "core teaching points" rather than lexical similarity. Its alignment with human SME scores is iteratively improved using a calibration dataset of 100 scored examples.
- **Core assumption:** Pedagogical quality (correctness, completeness, non-misleading) can be operationalized as "alignment with core teaching points" of an expert answer and be judged consistently by an LLM with proper prompt engineering and calibration.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Because traditional text-matching metrics are insufficient... we developed and validated a custom LLM-as-a-Judge metric optimized for assessing pedagogical accuracy."
  - [section 3.3] Details the development: rubric creation, calibration dataset (100 samples of verbose/incomplete answers), and alignment process using Cohen’s κ and heatmaps. Shows 91.66% of scores within ±1 point and κw = 0.655.
  - [corpus] Weak signal: neighboring papers (e.g., [24] "Can LLMs Replace Human Evaluators?") explore LLM-as-a-Judge but not for pedagogy-specific metrics. This paper’s contribution is the domain-specific calibration.
- **Break condition:** If the judge’s prompts fail to achieve substantial agreement (κ < 0.6) with multiple SMEs across diverse question types, the metric is unreliable for high‑stakes decisions.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** A "teacher-in-the-loop" deployment mitigates LLM risks (hallucination, complete solutions) while leveraging their scalability, leading to higher quality and trust than pure LLM or pure human baselines.
- **Mechanism:** The LLM generates a draft answer (using the optimized prompt/model), which an educator reviews and edits before student delivery. This hybrid workflow combines LLM efficiency with human pedagogical oversight and context awareness.
- **Core assumption:** Educator time is the bottleneck; LLM drafts reduce total workload per question while human review ensures correctness and pedagogical soundness, maintaining student trust in institutionally provided tools.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "To mitigate persistent risks like hallucination and ensure alignment with course‑specific context, we advocate for a 'teacher‑in‑the‑loop' implementation."
  - [section 6.1] Discusses implementation in Dodona: "An LLM generates a draft answer that is then reviewed and edited by an educator... reduces risk of misinformation... increases answer quality and reduces educator workload."
  - [corpus] Strong support: neighbors [12] (CodeAid) and [14] (CodeHelp) also explore guardrailed LLM assistants, indicating community consensus on hybrid models for programming education.
- **Break condition:** If educator review time remains high (e.g., >80% of original answer time) or if students perceive reviewed answers as inauthentic/low‑quality, the efficiency/trust benefits fail.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A task‑agnostic, pre‑deployment evaluation framework (benchmark + automated metrics) enables reproducible, future‑proof assessment of educational LLM tools, shifting development from ad‑hoc to quantifiable validation.
- **Mechanism:** The framework structures development into sequential stages: (1) Feasibility study, (2) Data Preparation (curate input data + SME ground truth), (3) Metrics Selection (define criteria, create/align LLM‑as‑a‑Judge if needed), (4) Actor Evaluation (test prompts/models on benchmark, rank by score/cost). The benchmark is reused for all future evaluations.
- **Core assumption:** Educational LLM tool quality can be measured pre‑deployment via a representative benchmark and automated metrics, allowing systematic comparison of actors (prompt/model combos, humans) and tracking progress over time.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "We abstract our methodology into a task‑agnostic evaluation framework... advocating for a shift... from ad‑hoc, post‑deployment testing to a quantifiable, pre‑deployment validation process."
  - [section 5] Explicitly defines the 4‑stage framework (Fig. 9) and principles: gather insights, automate evaluation, future‑proof evaluation. Emphasizes reusability: "no need for new datasets, manual annotation or new metrics" for future models.
  - [corpus] Moderate support: neighbors [55751] and [140017] discuss LLM course assistants but focus on deployment/usage; this paper provides a specific pre‑deployment validation methodology not explicitly detailed in neighbors.
- **Break condition:** If the benchmark does not generalize to new course contexts (e.g., different programming languages, advanced topics) or if metrics fail to predict real‑world educational impact (e.g., student learning gains), the framework is not truly task‑agnostic or predictive.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: LLM-as-a-Judge Evaluation**
  - **Why needed here:** The entire study hinges on automating open-ended pedagogical assessment. Understanding why traditional metrics (BLEU, ROUGE) fail for educational answers—and how an LLM can be prompted to judge "core teaching points"—is essential to grasp the methodological innovation.
  - **Quick check question:** Why would exact match or ROUGE scores be misleading for evaluating a hint‑based programming answer versus a complete solution?

- **Concept: Pedagogical Accuracy vs. Solution Correctness**
  - **Why needed here:** The benchmark distinguishes *pedagogical* quality (guiding without giving away solution) from mere functional correctness. An LLM might write code that works but violates course pedagogy (e.g., using advanced concepts not yet taught). The ground truth and judge metric explicitly capture this.
  - **Quick check question:** How could an LLM‑generated answer be functionally correct but still score low on the 0–5 pedagogical scale?

- **Concept: Teacher‑in‑the‑Loop Human‑AI Collaboration**
  - **Why needed here:** The proposed deployment model is not full automation but a hybrid. Understanding the rationale—balancing scalability, accuracy, trust, and educator workload—is key to applying this research.
  - **Quick check question:** What are the two primary risks of *not* having educator review of LLM‑generated answers in a CS1 course, as identified in the paper?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  1. **Input Data Pipeline:** Fetches student questions + context (code, assignment, test failures) from LMS (Dodona). Includes GDPR filtering.
  2. **Ground Truth Repository:** SME‑authored answers with "identified issue" and "answer for student" (hint‑based, non‑complete). Single source of truth.
  3. **LLM‑as‑a‑Judge Service:** Prompted LLM (e.g., Gemini) that takes (ground truth, actor output) → returns score (0‑5) + YAML breakdown (core/missing/extra points). Calibrated against SME scores.
  4. **Actor Registry:** Collection of prompt/model combinations to evaluate (e.g., `prompt_v3 + Gemini‑3‑Flash`). Also includes "Best Available Human" (BAH) baseline.
  5. **Evaluation Engine:** Iterates over Input Data → gets Actor Output → calls Judge Service → records score + cost/token stats. Generates rankings, cost/accuracy plots (Fig. 8), distribution histograms (Fig. 6).
  6. **Framework Orchestrator:** Task‑agnostic pipeline (Fig. 9) that sequences Data Preparation → Metrics Selection → Actor Evaluation. Manages benchmark versioning.

- **Critical path:** For a new task (e.g., math Q&A), the critical path is: (a) Curate 50–100 representative input samples → (b) Have SMEs author ground truth → (c) Define success criteria (e.g., "no advanced concepts") → (d) If no existing metric, develop custom LLM‑as‑a‑Judge (calibrate on 50–100 samples) → (e) Run Actor Evaluation on benchmark → (f) Select optimal actor based on score/cost trade‑off.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Judge complexity vs. cost: A more elaborate judge prompt (e.g., with chain‑of‑thought) may improve alignment but increases judge inference cost. The paper found zero‑shot with subtle CoT optimal.
  - Benchmark size vs. coverage: 170 questions is manageable but may not capture all student question types. Adding diverse questions improves generalizability but requires more SME annotation time.
  - Thinking‑enabled models vs. cost: Enabling thinking (reasoning) improves scores (e.g., Claude Opus –6.03% score drop when disabled) but increases token usage and cost. Trade‑off depends on budget.
  - Automation vs. human review: Fully automated judging is scalable but may miss subtle pedagogy. The solution: use judge for *actor selection* (pre‑deployment), but still deploy teacher‑in‑the‑loop for *student‑facing* answers.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - Judge misalignment: Heatmap shows scores concentrated off‑diagonal; SME‑judge κ < 0.5. → Judge prompt needs recalibration; benchmark may be ambiguous.
  - Actor cost explosion: Token usage per request spikes (>2x baseline) for a model with marginal score gain. → Prompt is inefficient; need prompt compression.
  - Benchmark overfitting: An actor performs exceptionally on benchmark but poorly in live pilot. → Benchmark lacks real‑world diversity (e.g., missing convoluted student code, ambiguous questions).
  - SME consensus absent: If multiple SMEs score ground truth inconsistently (low inter‑rater reliability), the "single source of truth" is questionable. → Need SME consensus process before benchmark finalization.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Judge Validation Experiment:** Run calibrated LLM‑as‑a‑Judge on the 100‑sample calibration set. Compute κ, % within ±1, and generate heatmap. Goal: κw ≥ 0.65, >90% within ±1. If not, iterate judge prompt.
  2. **Intra‑Family Prompt Optimization:** Use cheapest model (e.g., Gemini Flash) to test 20–50 prompt variations on 10% of benchmark. Select top prompt by average judge score. Validate: Does score improve vs. baseline prompt? Identify which input data fields matter (Fig. 4).
  3. **Inter‑Family Cost‑Accuracy Plot:** Run final prompt on 5 flagship models (with/without thinking). Plot score vs. USD cents/request (like Fig. 8). Identify Pareto front (e.g., Gemini 3 Flash). Validate: Does selected model surpass BAH baseline (2.93) by meaningful margin (≥0.4)?

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
  - **Question:** How does the teacher‑in‑the‑loop deployment affect the accuracy, educational impact, and responsiveness of LLM‑generated answers compared to fully automated or no‑LLM scenarios?
  - **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state in Section 6.1 that future research will investigate the "teacher‑in‑the‑loop" method’s effects on accuracy, educational impact, and responsiveness, as their current implementation in Dodona lacks formal evaluation.
  - **Why unresolved:** The paper describes the implementation but provides no empirical data on how educator review changes answer quality, student learning outcomes, or response times.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** A controlled study comparing student performance, satisfaction, and response latency across three conditions: (1) LLM drafts reviewed by educators (teacher‑in‑the‑loop), (2) fully automated LLM answers, and (3) educator‑only answers, using the same benchmark and metrics.

### Open Question 2
  - **Question:** Can the proposed task‑agnostic evaluation framework be reliably applied to other educational tasks (e.g., generating personalized assignments, enhancing error messages) and domains (e.g., mathematics, language learning)?
  - **Basis in paper:** [explicit] RQ2 and RQ3 explicitly ask for a reproducible process and reusable workflow, and Section 5 abstracts a task‑agnostic framework, but its validation is limited to the Q&A task in a CS1 Python course.
  - **Why unresolved:** The framework’s generalizability is assumed but not tested; different tasks may require different success criteria, metrics, or data preparation steps.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply the complete framework (feasibility study, data preparation, metrics selection, actor evaluation) to at least two distinct educational tasks (e.g., generating hints for Parsons problems, creating multilingual explanations) and document adaptations, performance rankings, and reproducibility across tasks.

### Open Question 3
  - **Question:** How does the reliance on a single subject matter expert for ground truth annotation affect the benchmark’s validity and the LLM‑as‑a‑Judge’s alignment with broader pedagogical standards?
  - **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The limitations note that ground truth was annotated by one expert, which may not capture solution variations or teaching style diversity; the judge was aligned to this single expert’s scores.
  - **Why unresolved:** Single‑expert ground truth risks bias and may not represent a consensus; the judge’s alignment might not hold with multiple experts, affecting metric reliability.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Have 3–5 SMEs independently annotate a subset of the benchmark, compute inter‑rater reliability (e.g., Cohen’s κ), and recalibrate the LLM‑as‑a‑Judge against the consensus; compare score distributions and model rankings before and after multi‑expert alignment.

### Open Question 4
  - **Question:** To what extent do the findings on LLM performance generalize to other programming languages (e.g., Java, C++) and advanced courses (CS2+), where conceptual complexity and idioms differ?
  - **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The study uses data from one CS1 Python course; limitations acknowledge this restricts generalizability to other languages or higher‑level courses.
  - **Why unresolved:** LLM capabilities may vary with language‑specific paradigms (e.g., object‑oriented vs. functional) and course‑level concepts (e.g., recursion, data structures), affecting pedagogical accuracy.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicate the study using curated benchmarks from CS2 courses in at least two other languages, applying the same evaluation framework and prompt; compare model performance metrics (scores, cost, token efficiency) and identify language‑specific failure modes.

## Limitations
- **Benchmark & judge secrecy:** The core 170‑question benchmark and calibrated judge prompt are unreleased, preventing independent replication or external validation of scores and the judge’s alignment (κ = 0.655).
- **Model availability issues:** Key tested models (Gemini 3 Flash preview, GPT‑5 mini, Claude Opus 4.5) are not publicly released or have changed, making exact cost/accuracy reproduction impossible.
- **Generalizability & impact:** The framework and benchmark are validated only for CS1 Python; real‑world educational impact (student learning gains, engagement) remains unmeasured.

## Confidence
- **LLM‑as‑a‑Judge Metric:** Medium
- **Teacher‑in‑the‑Loop Efficacy:** High
- **Pre‑Deployment Framework Utility:** Medium
