# The Stationarity Bias: Stratified Stress-Testing for Time-Series Imputation in Regulated Dynamical Systems

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.15637
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15637
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Uniform random masking in time-series imputation benchmarks introduces a stationarity bias that inflates simple methods' performance by over-sampling stationary regimes while obscuring catastrophic failures during clinically critical transients, necessitating regime-stratified evaluation.

## Executive Summary
This paper reveals a fundamental flaw in standard time-series imputation benchmarks: the ubiquitous practice of uniform random masking systematically over-represents long, stable (stationary) periods while under-sampling short, high-stakes transient events (e.g., post-meal glucose spikes). Using Continuous Glucose Monitoring (CGM) data, the authors demonstrate that this "Stationarity Bias" makes linear interpolation appear superior overall, even though it fails to preserve the morphological shape of transients—a risk masked by pointwise metrics like RMSE (the "RMSE Mirage"). They propose a Stratified Stress-Test that partitions evaluation into Stationary and Transient regimes, establishing that optimal model selection is regime-dependent: use computationally cheap linear methods for stable intervals and deep learning models (e.g., SAITS) for transients to ensure clinical safety.

## Method Summary
The authors conduct a large-scale empirical study on real-world CGM data from diabetic patients. They first characterize the signal's regime dynamics, identifying stable homeostatic intervals and critical transients (post-prandial peaks, hypoglycemia) triggered by exogenous events. They then design three stratified masking protocols: Protocol A targets stationary windows; Protocol B targets post-prandial peaks; Protocol C targets hypoglycemic events. For each protocol, masks are applied to fully observed sequences, and multiple imputation models (linear interpolation, LOCF, and several deep learning architectures) generate predictions. Performance is evaluated using pointwise metrics (RMSE, MARD) and shape-sensitive metrics (Dynamic Time Warping, DTW), with results stratified by regime. The core methodological contribution is replacing uniform random masking with regime-targeted masking to expose evaluation biases.

## Key Results
- Linear interpolation achieves state-of-the-art performance (lowest RMSE, MARD, DTW) during stationary, homeostatic intervals, confirming its efficiency for low-entropy regimes.
- During transients (post-prandial peaks, hypoglycemia), linear methods exhibit drastically degraded morphological fidelity (high DTW) disproportionate to their RMSE—the "RMSE Mirage"—where moderate pointwise error masks complete destruction of signal shape.
- Deep learning models (SAITS) maintain both pointwise accuracy and morphological integrity during transients, yielding superior clinical safety metrics (lower MARD, reduced bias) compared to linear interpolation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Uniform random masking inflates simple methods' performance by over-representing stationary regimes where they excel and under-sampling critical transients.
- **Mechanism:** In regulated dynamical systems (e.g., glucose homeostasis), the signal spends most of its time in a low-entropy stationary attractor. Uniform random sampling draws missing intervals proportionally to time spent in each regime, so the vast majority of masks fall on stationary segments. Simple methods achieve low error there, dominating aggregate metrics and hiding catastrophic transient failures.
- **Core assumption:** The system has a dominant stationary regime separated by infrequent, high-impact transients triggered by exogenous events.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "uniform random masking over-represents dominant stationary regimes, inflating performance of simple methods like linear interpolation while obscuring their failure during critical transients." [section 1] "When time-series from such systems contain missing data, imputation benchmarks that employ uniform random masking will, by construction, sample the dominant stationary regime far more often than the rare but critical transients."
- **Break condition:** Evaluation stratifies missing intervals by regime and weights transients according to clinical importance.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Pointwise metrics like RMSE can be deceptively low during transients while imputation destroys morphological shape—the "RMSE Mirage"—hiding safety risks.
- **Mechanism:** Linear interpolation draws a straight chord through a missing interval. If the true transient is symmetric or the chord passes near the mean, pointwise squared error may be moderate. However, the chord flattens peaks and troughs, erasing the exact trajectory and derivatives that downstream controllers rely on. RMSE averages these deviations and fails to penalize lost temporal structure.
- **Core assumption:** Downstream tasks depend on accurate signal morphology (peak height, timing, derivatives), not just pointwise values.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "During post-prandial peaks and hypoglycemia, linear methods exhibit drastically degraded morphological fidelity (DTW) disproportionate to their RMSE—a phenomenon termed the RMSE Mirage." [section 4.3.2] "RMSE serves as a poor proxy for the true divergence between an imputation and the ground truth, largely because it ignores the morphological relationship between time sequences."
- **Break condition:** Incorporate shape-sensitive metrics (e.g., DTW) or domain-specific morphological checks into evaluation.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Regime-conditional model selection is optimal: linear interpolation for stationary intervals (efficient, accurate), deep learning for transients (preserves morphology, safety).
- **Mechanism:** Stationary intervals are low-dimensional and smooth; linear interpolation approximates them perfectly without overfitting. Transients involve nonlinear, rapid changes driven by exogenous inputs; deep models (e.g., SAITS) learn these complex dynamics and reconstruct the true trajectory. The stress-test protocols isolate these regimes, enabling clear selection boundaries.
- **Core assumption:** Regimes can be reliably identified at inference time (e.g., via gradient stability thresholds) and training data includes realistic transients.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "Linear interpolation achieves state-of-the-art reconstruction during stable intervals... Deep learning models preserve both pointwise accuracy and morphological integrity during transients." [section 5] "Our findings suggest a heterogeneous deployment strategy."
- **Break condition:** If regime detection is too error-prone or transients are too rare to learn, a single robust model may be preferred despite computational cost.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Time-series stationarity and regime dynamics**
  - *Why needed here:* The analysis hinges on distinguishing stationary (homeostatic) from transient (excursion) regimes in physiological signals.
  - *Quick check question:* What physiological events trigger transient regimes in CGM data, and why are they clinically critical?
- **Concept: Imputation evaluation metrics (pointwise vs morphological)**
  - *Why needed here:* The paper critiques RMSE for masking morphological errors and introduces DTW as a remedy.
  - *Quick check question:* Why might a model with lower RMSE be clinically less safe than one with higher RMSE during a hypoglycemic event?
- **Concept: Realistic missingness modeling**
  - *Why needed here:* The authors argue uniform random masking is ecologically invalid; they derive missingness distributions from clinical trials.
  - *Quick check question:* What are the two main components (onset probability and duration model) of the empirical missingness process estimated from DCLP3/DCLP5?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Regime Identifier -> Masking Generator -> Model Library -> Metric Engine
- **Critical path:**
  1. Preprocess CGM data: resample to 5-minute grid, filter valid days (≥50% coverage).
  2. For a given protocol, identify target intervals (e.g., euglycemic stable windows for A, post-prandial peaks for B, hypoglycemic events for C).
  3. Apply the corresponding mask to the fully observed signal.
  4. Feed the masked sequence (with clinical inputs and time encoding) to each imputation model.
  5. Extract imputed values only at the masked positions.
  6. Compute metrics against ground truth, stratify by regime, and aggregate.
  7. Compare model performance across regimes to derive selection rules.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Simplicity vs safety:* Linear interpolation is trivial but fails catastrophically in transients; deep models require resources but preserve morphology.
  - *Metric cost vs fidelity:* RMSE is fast but blind to shape; DTW captures morphology but has O(N²) complexity—use it selectively.
  - *Realism vs controllability:* Empirical missingness distributions reflect real dropout patterns but add complexity; uniform random masking is simpler but introduces bias.
  - *Regime detection latency:* The gradient-stability check requires a sliding window; too large delays transient detection, too small increases false positives.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Low overall RMSE but high DTW on transient protocols indicates the RMSE Mirage—accuracy looks good but shape is destroyed.
  - Linear interpolation shows extreme positive bias during hypoglycemia (overestimates glucose), potentially masking dangerous lows.
  - Deep models may exhibit high variance (Emp-SE) if not trained with realistic missingness.
  - If the regime classifier mislabels transient windows as stationary, evaluation unfairly penalizes deep models and over-praises linear methods.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Replicate Scenario A (homeostatic): Mask 30-minute stable windows with ratios 10–30%. Verify linear interpolation achieves lowest RMSE, MARD, and DTW.
  2. Replicate Scenario B (post-prandial peaks): Mask 3.5–4 hour windows centered on meal peaks (1–3 peaks). Compare RMSE and DTW for Lerp vs SAITS; expect similar RMSE but much lower DTW for SAITS.
  3. Run distributional calibration (Figure 3): For Scenario B and C, plot the conditional density of imputed values vs ground truth. Confirm linear interpolation shifts the distribution (attenuating peaks, elevating hypoglycemia) while deep models partially recover it.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** What quantitative threshold (e.g., gradient magnitude) optimally distinguishes stationary vs. transient regimes for adaptive model switching across diverse systems? The authors propose |∇g_t| < 0.6 mg/dL/min for CGM but do not analyze threshold sensitivity, robustness, or transferability.
- **Open Question 2:** Does the Stationarity Bias universally inflate simple-method performance in time-series imputation benchmarks beyond glucose monitoring? The authors claim it applies to "any regulated system where routine stationarity dominates critical transients" but provide empirical validation only for CGM.
- **Open Question 3:** How can deep imputation models achieve full distributional alignment with ground-truth transients, eliminating the persistent miscalibration (mean/variance shifts) observed in post-prandial and hypoglycemic events? The paper diagnoses the problem but offers no architectural modifications to rectify it.
- **Open Question 4:** What is the quantitative impact of imputation-induced morphological distortion on safety-critical downstream tasks (e.g., closed-loop insulin delivery)? The authors discuss hazards qualitatively but lack empirical control validation.

## Limitations
- **Domain Generalizability:** The core analysis is specific to CGM data; it is uncertain whether the Stationarity Bias and Stationary/Transient dichotomy hold universally for all regulated dynamical systems (e.g., finance, industrial control).
- **Regime Detection Robustness:** The deployment strategy depends on accurate, real-time identification of transient vs. stationary regimes. The paper uses a gradient stability heuristic, but its performance on noisy data or with long-term missingness patterns is not thoroughly stress-tested.
- **Operational & Computational Trade-offs:** The work advocates a heterogeneous deployment (linear + deep models) but does not quantify the operational complexity, latency, or computational cost of dynamically switching models in a live, safety-critical system.

## Confidence
- **Stationarity Bias Mechanism:** High. The logic is sound, CGM data exhibits the described dynamics, and the experimental design cleanly isolates the effect within this domain.
- **RMSE Mirage Phenomenon:** Medium-High. Empirical evidence (disconnect between RMSE and DTW in transients) is strong for CGM. Confidence is slightly reduced for universal claims, as mirage severity likely depends on transient shape relative to pointwise metrics.
- **Regime-Conditional Selection as Optimal Strategy:** Medium. The conclusion is logical and supported by comparative results. Confidence is medium because the strategy's global optimality rests on two unproven assumptions: (a) perfect regime detection at inference time, and (b) that the cost/complexity of a hybrid system is always justified over a single robust model.

## Next Checks

Check: Does uniform random masking still over-represent a dominant "stationary" regime and inflate simple imputation performance? Can clear, operationally critical "transient" regimes be identified, and do deep models retain their relative advantage there?
2. **Stress-Test the Regime Detector:** Apply the paper's regime identification logic (gradient-based stability) to CGM data with simulated realistic, long-term missingness and to data from different CGM device generations with higher noise. Check: How sensitive is regime classification to sensor noise and extended missing data? What are the false positive/negative rates in classifying transient events, and how does misclassification propagate into reported model performance gaps?
3. **End-to-End Safety & Cost Benchmark:** Implement the proposed hybrid system (regime detector + linear/SAITS) in a simulated closed-loop insulin delivery environment (e.g., UVA/Padova simulator). Compare against: (a) always using linear interpolation, and (b) always using a deep model. Check: What are the comparative rates of hypoglycemic/hyperglycemic events? What is the total computational latency and energy cost of the hybrid approach versus single-model baselines in real-time?
