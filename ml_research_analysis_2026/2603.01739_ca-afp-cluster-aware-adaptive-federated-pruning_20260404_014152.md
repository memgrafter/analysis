# CA-AFP: Cluster-Aware Adaptive Federated Pruning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.01739
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01739
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: CA-AFP combines client clustering with adaptive pruning to tackle statistical and system heterogeneity, achieving higher accuracy and fairness with ~70% sparsity and significantly lower communication than dense clustering methods.

## Executive Summary
CA-AFP addresses the joint challenges of statistical and system heterogeneity in federated learning. It first clusters clients based on the similarity of their local updates, then performs cluster-specific adaptive pruning guided by a hybrid importance score that integrates weight magnitude, intra-cluster coherence, and gradient consistency. An iterative prune-and-heal schedule enables dynamic sparsity and weight regrowth. Evaluated on UCI HAR and WISDM, CA-AFP reaches 96.73% average accuracy on WISDM with 8.35% fairness at 375 MB communication, approaching dense baseline accuracy at a fraction of the cost, and remains robust under extreme non‑IID conditions.

## Method Summary
CA‑AFP operates in four main phases. After an optional warm‑up of dense FedAvg rounds (phase 1), the server clusters clients: each client computes a one‑epoch local update Δwₖ; the server measures pairwise cosine distances and applies agglomerative hierarchical clustering to assign clients to K=3 clusters. Phase 2 stabilizes cluster models via intra‑cluster FedAvg with a regularization term on personal models. Phase 3 introduces iterative prune‑and‑heal training: a binary mask per cluster is maintained; importance scores (α·Mag+β·Coh+γ·Con) guide pruning of low‑score active weights and regrowth of high‑gradient inactive weights; a healing period follows each pruning step. Finally, phase 4 fine‑tunes each client’s sparse cluster model locally with the mask fixed.

## Key Results
- On WISDM with 25 fine‑tuning epochs, CA‑AFP achieves 96.73% average accuracy and 8.35% fairness (standard deviation) at 375 MB communication.
- Outperforms pruning baselines (EfficientFL, FedSNIP) in accuracy and fairness, and uses substantially less communication than dense clustering methods (FedCHAR, ClusterFL).
- Remains robust under extreme non‑IID conditions (e.g., one class per client), maintaining ~80% accuracy while pruning baselines collapse to ~50%.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Clustering to Mitigate Statistical Heterogeneity
Grouping clients into clusters aggregates similar updates, reducing the negative impact of non‑IID data. After an optional warm‑up, each client computes a one‑epoch local update; the server measures pairwise cosine distance and performs agglomerative hierarchical clustering to assign clients to clusters. Each cluster then maintains and aggregates its own model. *Break condition*: insufficient warm‑up leads to noisy updates and unreliable clusters; under extreme heterogeneity clusters may degenerate to singletons, offering no benefit.

### Mechanism 2: Cluster‑Aware Importance Scoring
The hybrid importance score (α·Mag + β·Coh + γ·Con) identifies weights important at the cluster level, avoiding premature pruning of collectively relevant parameters. Mag is normalized absolute weight value; Coh is inverse of variance across clients in the cluster; Con is average sign of gradients for that weight. These collectively favor large, stable, and consistently supported weights. *Break condition*: with few clients in a cluster, Coh and Con become noisy; short local epochs make gradient signs unreliable.

### Mechanism 3: Iterative Prune‑and‑Heal Schedule
Periodic pruning combined with gradient‑guided regrowth and a healing phase enables dynamic sparsity while preserving capacity. At intervals, importance scores are re‑evaluated; low‑score active weights are pruned; an equal number of pruned weights with highest gradient magnitudes are regrown; then training continues for a healing period before the next pruning step. *Break condition*: excessive prune rates or regrowth based solely on gradients can permanently discard critical weights or reintroduce noise.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Server -> Cluster Model Manager: clustering results  
Server -> Importance Scorer: aggregated client updates  
Importance Scorer -> Mask Manager: importance scores  
Mask Manager -> Server: pruning masks  
Server -> Clients (per cluster): masked model broadcast  
Clients -> Fine‑tuning Module: local training  
Clients -> Server: aggregated updates  

**Critical path**  
1. (Optional) Warm‑up: run T₁ rounds of dense FedAvg to stabilize the global model.  
2. Clustering: clients compute one‑epoch updates; server computes pairwise cosine distances and performs agglomerative hierarchical clustering; assign K=3 clusters.  
3. Cluster stabilization: T₂ rounds of intra‑cluster FedAvg with regularization on personal models.  
4. Prune‑and‑heal training: T₃ rounds maintaining a binary mask per cluster; compute importance scores (α·Mag+β·Coh+γ·Con); prune low‑score active weights; regrow equal number of pruned weights with highest gradient magnitudes; follow with a healing phase.  
5. Fine‑tuning: each client locally fine‑tunes its sparse cluster model for P₄ epochs with the mask fixed.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Number of clusters K: more clusters improve personalization but increase management overhead and reduce per‑cluster data.  
- Target sparsity: higher sparsity lowers communication but risks accuracy; adaptive schedule helps but still requires tuning.  
- Warm‑up rounds T₁/T₂: longer improves clustering/stability but adds upfront communication cost.  
- Importance weights (α,β,γ): their balance is dataset‑dependent; no universal optimum.

**Failure signatures**  
- Sharp accuracy drop after a pruning step → over‑pruning or poor importance ranking.  
- Rising fairness (σ) → cluster assignments not grouping similar clients or uneven pruning impact.  
- Communication reduction plateaus below target → mask updates not synchronized or regrowth offsets pruning gains.  
- Frequent cluster membership changes across rounds → instability harming convergence.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. Main comparison (Table 2): CA‑AFP matches or exceeds dense baselines (FedCHAR, ClusterFL) in accuracy while using ~70% sparsity and far less communication; it also outperforms pruning baselines (EfficientFL, FedSNIP) in accuracy and fairness.  
2. Importance score ablation (Table 4): Seven weight combinations tested under standard, drift, and noisy scenarios show the hybrid score adapts to conditions, with consistency‑weighted variants often best for standard data.  
3. Non‑IID robustness (Figure 4): Under extreme label skew (1 class per client), CA‑AFP maintains ~80% accuracy while pruning baselines collapse to ~50%, demonstrating the crucial role of clustering.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1: Clustering Algorithm and Schedule
- **Question**: How do different clustering algorithms (e.g., k‑means, varying hierarchical linkages) and the timing of clustering relative to pruning affect CA‑AFP's accuracy, fairness, and communication efficiency?  
- **Basis**: The paper uses a fixed agglomerative hierarchical clustering with average linkage after initial training; no systematic comparison is performed.  
- **Why unresolved**: The optimal clustering strategy and its interaction with adaptive pruning remain unknown.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Comprehensive experiments varying clustering method, reclustering intervals, and integration points across multiple datasets and non‑IID settings.

### Open Question 2: Automatic Hyperparameter Tuning
- **Question**: Can adaptive mechanisms automatically set the importance score weights (α, β, γ) and target sparsity based on data distribution characteristics?  
- **Basis**: Hyperparameters require scenario‑specific tuning; ablations show dataset‑dependent optimal values.  
- **Why unresolved**: Manual tuning hinders deployment in diverse or unknown environments.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Algorithms that infer heterogeneity metrics from client updates and dynamically adjust parameters, validated against manual tuning on varied benchmarks.

### Open Question 3: Dynamic Cluster Reassignment
- **Question**: Does allowing cluster reassignment during training improve robustness under temporal data drift or dynamic client participation?  
- **Basis**: CA‑AFP performs clustering only once after Phase 1; no reclustering is considered.  
- **Why unresolved**: Real‑world FL may involve concept drift or changing client populations; static clusters could misalign over time.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Extend CA‑AFP to support periodic reclustering based on update similarity and evaluate under synthetic temporal heterogeneity or streaming data.

### Open Question 4: Generalization Beyond HAR
- **Question**: How well does CA‑AFP generalize to non‑HAR domains (e.g., image or language tasks) and different non‑IID partitioning schemes?  
- **Basis**: Experiments are limited to WISDM and UCI‑HAR with natural user splits; no results on standard FL benchmarks like CIFAR‑10 or Shakespeare.  
- **Why unresolved**: Domain‑specific properties and partitioning types may affect clustering and pruning dynamics differently.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Replicate CA‑AFP on diverse benchmarks with varied non‑IID settings (label skew, feature skew, quantity skew) and compare to baselines.

## Limitations
- Missing hyperparameters (regularization λ, pruning frequency f, initial sparsity, churn rate ρ interpretation) hinder exact reproduction.
- Fixed number of clusters (K=3) is not justified; its sensitivity and optimality across datasets are unclear.
- Evaluation is restricted to two user‑partitioned HAR datasets, limiting claims about generalizability to other domains and non‑IID types.
- Communication cost calculation likely underestimates overhead by ignoring mask transmission and synchronization costs.
