# Improving LLM-based Recommendation with Self-Hard Negatives from Intermediate Layers

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.17410  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17410  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** ILRec achieves Hit@10 of 0.1143 on the Instrument dataset, outperforming LC-Rec (0.1062) and SPRec (0.1041).

## Executive Summary
Existing LLM-based recommendation methods rely on offline-generated, sequence-level negative samples, which are coarse and uninformative for large item spaces. This paper introduces ILRec, a framework that extracts dynamic, token-level self-hard negatives from intermediate LLM layers during training. These negatives are integrated via cross-layer preference optimization (penalizing negatives in final logits) and cross-layer preference distillation (improving intermediate layer quality), with a collaborative filtering model providing token-level rewards to mitigate false negatives. ILRec consistently outperforms state-of-the-art baselines across three Amazon datasets while avoiding the computational overhead of additional preference alignment stages and rollout computations.

## Method Summary
ILRec is a single-stage fine-tuning framework that enhances LLM-based sequential recommendation by generating token-level negative samples on the fly. During each training step, for a given token position, it ensembles logits from *k* consecutive intermediate layers preceding the final layer. Tokens (excluding the ground-truth) whose ensemble probability exceeds a dynamic threshold τ = α · p_ground_truth are selected as self-hard negatives. These negatives are then incorporated through three loss components: (1) Cross-Layer Preference Optimization (CPO) modifies the standard cross-entropy loss by adding a token-specific penalty weight derived from the ensemble logits to the final layer’s logits; (2) Cross-Layer Preference Distillation (CPD) aligns each intermediate layer’s probability distribution with the final layer’s distribution via KL divergence; (3) Collaborative Reward Regularization (CRR) uses a pre-trained SASRec model to assign token-level rewards, softening the penalty for tokens predicted to lead to relevant items by CF. The total loss L = L_CPO + λ·L_CPD + μ·L_CRR updates only the LLM parameters while the CF model remains fixed.

## Key Results
- On the Instrument dataset (LC-Rec paradigm), ILRec achieves Hit@10 of 0.1143, surpassing LC-Rec (0.1062) and the best DPO-based SPRec variant (0.1041).
- Consistent improvements across all evaluated metrics (Hit@5, Hit@10, NDCG@5, NDCG@10) on three Amazon datasets (Instrument, Art, Game).
- Ablation studies confirm the individual contribution of CPO, CPD, and CRR components.
- Superior training efficiency: no separate DPO alignment stage or expensive rollout computations are required.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Token-Level Self-Hard Negatives from Intermediate Layers
- **Claim:** Extracts token-level, dynamic negative signals from intermediate LLM layers during training, providing fine-grained supervision for large item spaces.
- **Mechanism:** For each token step, logits from *k* consecutive intermediate layers (e.g., layers L−1 to L−k) are ensembled via averaging. Tokens with probability exceeding a dynamic threshold τ = α · p_ground_truth (excluding ground truth) are selected as self-hard negatives. This expands the negative space from sequence-level to token-level.
- **Core assumption:** Intermediate layers possess sufficient predictive capability to generate challenging negatives while being weaker than the final layer, creating a "difficult but learnable" gap. The dynamic threshold adapts to model confidence during training.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states ILRec identifies "self-hard negative tokens from intermediate layers as fine-grained negative supervision that dynamically reflects the model's preference learning process." Section 2.2 describes extracting "tokens with high generated probabilities from the intermediate layers’ outputs as negative signals."
- **Break condition:** If intermediate layers are too shallow (early layers), their predictions become noise rather than meaningful negatives, degrading performance (e.g., using layers 21–19 in Appendix A.5).

### Mechanism 2: Cross-Layer Preference Optimization and Distillation
- **Claim:** Integrates token-level negatives into the final layer's output via penalization (CPO) and simultaneously improves negative quality via distillation from the final layer to intermediates (CPD).
- **Mechanism:**  
  - **CPO:** Each negative token *v* in *V_N* is weighted *w_v* = exp(p_ensemble(*v*)) / Σ exp(p_ensemble(*v_n*)). The cross-entropy loss becomes *L_CPO* = −log( exp(*p_L*(*y_t*)) / Σ_v exp(*p_L*(*v*)·(1 + β·*w_v*)) ), increasing penalty for tokens highly confident in intermediate layers.  
  - **CPD:** KL divergence between each intermediate layer’s probability distribution and the final layer’s distribution is minimized: *L_CPD* = Σ_{l=L−k}^{L−1} KL(softmax(*P_l*) || softmax(*P_L*)). This forces intermediates to mimic the final layer’s discriminative patterns.
- **Core assumption:** The final layer is the most accurate predictor (teacher). Penalizing intermediate-layer confident tokens in the final layer (CPO) and aligning intermediates to the final layer (CPD) create a synergy: negatives become harder as intermediates improve, and the final layer learns to reject them.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions "two-stage framework comprising cross-layer preference optimization and cross-layer preference distillation." Section 2.3 details CPO integrating negatives as "fine-grained penalty coefficients" and CPD using the output layer as a teacher.
- **Break condition:** If intermediate layers match final layer capability prematurely (e.g., in very small models), distillation provides no gradient and may destabilize training; performance drops when *k* is too large (Figure 5).

### Mechanism 3: Collaborative Reward Regularization for False Negatives
- **Claim:** Mitigates over-penalization of false negatives by incorporating collaborative filtering (CF) rewards as soft regularization, blending sequential LLM signals with CF item correlations.
- **Mechanism:** A pre-trained CF model (e.g., SASRec) scores all items. For token *v* at step *t*, reward *r_v* = (Σ_{i∈I_≤v} R(*i*)) / (Σ_{i∈I_<v} R(*i*)) approximates the CF model’s confidence that *v* leads to a relevant item. This token-level reward is used as a soft label in *L_CRR*, which applies a cross-entropy-like loss over the set of penalized tokens *H* = *V_N* ∪ {*y_t*}. Tokens with higher CF reward are less penalized.
- **Core assumption:** CF models capture complementary collaborative signals (user-item co-occurrence) that are robust to false negatives in LLM token predictions. The token-level reward approximates CF’s preference for prefix sequences.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states ILRec introduces "a lightweight collaborative filtering (CF) model to assign token-level rewards for negative signals, mitigating the risk of over-penalizing false negatives." Section 2.4 describes using a CF model to "assign a reward score to each penalized token and reduce the penalty for those with higher rewards."
- **Break condition:** If the CF model is poorly trained or domain-mismatched, its token rewards become noisy and misguide the LLM. Table 4 shows weaker CF models (Caser, BPR) yield smaller gains.

## Foundational Learning
**Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)**  
- *Why needed here:* Current LLMRec methods often use DPO with sequence-level negatives, which are coarse. ILRec improves upon DPO by moving to token-level, dynamic negatives from intermediate layers, avoiding offline negative sampling issues.

**Knowledge Distillation**  
- *Why needed here:* Cross-layer preference distillation (CPD) uses the final layer as a teacher to improve intermediate layers’ negative-generating ability. This is a form of distillation where the student (intermediate) learns from the teacher (final).

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** LLM backbone (e.g., Llama3.1-8B) with layers *L_1*...*L_L* → intermediate layer logits extraction and ensemble (for last *k* layers) → negative token extractor (threshold τ = α·p_gt) → CPO loss module (final layer penalization) + CPD loss module (intermediate-to-final KL) + CRR loss module (CF reward-weighted) → total loss *L* = *L_CPO* + λ·*L_CPD* + μ·*L_CRR*.
- **Critical path:**  
  1. Forward pass through LLM to obtain hidden states of selected intermediate layers and final layer.  
  2. Ensemble intermediate logits and extract token-level negatives *V_N*.  
  3. Compute *L_CPO* with penalization weights derived from ensemble logits.  
  4. Compute *L_CPD* by KL between each intermediate and final layer distribution.  
  5. Compute token rewards *r_v* using pre-trained CF model.  
  6. Compute *L_CRR* using *r_v* as soft labels.  
  7. Backpropagate total loss to update LLM parameters (CF fixed).
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *k* (number of intermediate layers): More layers increase negative diversity but add noise from weaker shallower layers; optimal *k* is small (≤5) and near final layers.  
  - α (threshold multiplier): Controls negative quantity; lower α yields more negatives (risk of false negatives), higher α yields fewer but harder negatives.  
  - β (penalization factor): Scales penalty weight; too high distorts distribution, too low under-utilizes negatives.  
  - λ (distillation weight): Balances CPD vs CPO; high λ may force intermediates to mimic final layer too strictly, reducing negative diversity.  
  - μ (CF reward weight): Controls correction for false negatives; over-reliance on CF may dilute LLM’s own learning.  
  - CF model choice: Stronger CF (SASRec) yields larger gains; CF is pre-trained and frozen, so no joint training cost.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Degraded Hit@10 when *k* is too large (e.g., including layers 21–19) due to noisy negatives.  
  - Performance drop if α is extreme (Table 6: α=0.1 or 1.2 sometimes underperform).  
  - No improvement if CF model is weak (Table 4: Caser, BPR yield minimal gains).  
  - Training instability if β or λ are too high, causing gradient explosion in *L_CPO* or *L_CPD*.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Ablation study:** Remove *L_CPO*, *L_CPD*, and *L_CRR* individually (Table 3) to validate each component’s contribution.  
  2. **Hyperparameter sensitivity:** Vary α and β (Table 6) to find robust ranges; expect peak performance around α ∈ [0.8, 1.0], β ∈ [0.05, 0.1].  
  3. **Layer selection analysis:** Compare performance with different *k* and layer depths (Figure 5, Appendix A.5). Expect optimal *k* to be 1–3 for most models; deeper consecutive layers (near final) outperform shallow or random layers.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Question:** How can ILRec’s fine-tuning approach be effectively extended to diverse personalized recommendation tasks (e.g., multi-behavior, context-aware) beyond sequential recommendation, and can more computationally lightweight fine-tuning methods (e.g., parameter-efficient adaptation) maintain performance while reducing training costs?  
   **Basis in paper:** The conclusion explicitly states future work aims to "extend this fine-tuning approach to adapt LLMs to more diverse personalized tasks, while exploring more lightweight fine-tuning methods for efficient training."  
   **Why unresolved:** The paper’s experiments are limited to sequential recommendation on three Amazon datasets using full fine-tuning of Llama3.1-8B. No evaluation on other task formulations (e.g., candidate ranking with fixed candidate pools, interactive recommendation) or lightweight methods (e.g., LoRA, prefix-tuning) is provided. The efficiency gains reported are relative to DPO-based baselines but do not explore parameter-efficient alternatives.  
   **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic experiments across varied recommendation paradigms (e.g., next-item prediction with side information, conversational recommendation) and model scales, comparing ILRec’s full fine-tuning against parameter-efficient variants. Metrics should include task-specific accuracy (e.g., NDCG, recall) and training resource usage (GPU hours, memory footprint). Ablation studies isolating the contribution of cross-layer signals in non-sequential settings would clarify generalizability.

2. **Question:** What is the optimal strategy for selecting intermediate layers across different LLM architectures (e.g., decoder-only vs. encoder-decoder, varying depths), and does the current approach of using consecutive deep layers generalize to models with different internal representations or training objectives?  
   **Basis in paper:** Appendix A.5 shows that layer selection critically impacts performance, with consecutive deep layers (e.g., L-1 to L-3) outperforming shallow or random layers on Llama models. The method is justified based on the assumption that deeper intermediate layers have stronger predictive capability.  
   **Why unresolved:** Layer selection experiments are confined to Llama3.2-1B/3B and Llama3.1-8B. No analysis is provided for other architectures (e.g., GPT, Mistral, T5) or models with non-standard layer numbering (e.g., models with tied embeddings, different normalization placements). The rationale for consecutive vs. non-consecutive selection is based on empirical trends but lacks theoretical grounding or cross-architecture validation.  
   **What evidence would resolve it:** Comprehensive layer-probing studies across a diverse set of LLMs (e.g., varying sizes, architectures, pretraining data). Metrics should include the informativeness of extracted negatives (e.g., hardness measured by final layer accuracy) and final recommendation performance. Analysis could correlate layer-wise capabilities (e.g., via probing tasks) with negative quality to derive architecture-agnostic selection principles.

3. **Question:** How robust is ILRec’s collaborative reward regularization (CRR) to the choice of collaborative filtering (CF) model, particularly in cold-start scenarios (new items/users) or highly dynamic item spaces where CF signals are sparse or unreliable?  
   **Basis in paper:** Section 2.4 introduces CRR to mitigate false negatives using token-level rewards from a CF model. Appendix A.4 tests different CF models (SASRec, GRU4Rec, etc.) on a static dataset, showing consistent but small gains, and notes that stronger CF models yield larger improvements.  
   **Why unresolved:** Experiments use pre-trained CF models on datasets with stable item popularity. No evaluation under cold-start conditions (e.g., new items with no interactions) or temporal shifts (e.g., streaming data). The CRR design assumes CF model outputs are reliable per-token scores, but this may break down when CF models are uncertain or when item tokenizations are inconsistent (e.g., rare items). The paper does not analyze failure cases where CRR might introduce bias.  
   **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments simulating cold-start (e.g., masking percentages of items/users) and dynamic settings (e.g., time-split evaluation). Compare ILRec with variants using alternative reward sources (e.g., item popularity, semantic similarity) or uncertainty-aware CF models. Measure not only overall accuracy but also fairness metrics (e.g., performance on tail items) and robustness to CF model degradation.

4. **Question:** Can the hyperparameter thresholds for negative extraction (α) and penalization (β) be dynamically adapted during training based on model calibration or data characteristics to reduce manual tuning and improve stability across datasets?  
   **Basis in paper:** ILRec uses fixed hyperparameters α (threshold for negative selection) and β (penalization strength). Appendix A.2 presents sensitivity analysis showing performance varies across values, with optimal ranges differing between datasets (e.g., α=0.8 vs. 1.0).  
   **Why unresolved:** The paper acknowledges the need for hyperparameter tuning per dataset but does not propose or evaluate adaptive strategies. The static thresholds may not account for varying model confidence during training (e.g., early vs. late epochs) or dataset-specific properties (e.g., sparsity, token vocabulary size). This limits practical deployment where data distributions shift.  
   **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement scheduling schemes (e.g., decay α over epochs) or data-driven approaches (e.g., set α based on ensemble probability entropy per batch). Evaluate on multiple datasets with automatic tuning (e.g., Bayesian optimization) vs. manual tuning. Report performance variance when transferring hyperparameters from one dataset to another without retuning, and compare against adaptive methods in terms of both accuracy and tuning efficiency.

## Limitations
- **Layer selection sensitivity:** Optimal *k* (number of intermediate layers) depends on dataset and model architecture; not fully generalized beyond Llama models.
- **CF reward underspecification:** The token-level reward computation (*r_v* formula) is underspecified, risking implementation errors and limiting reproducibility.
- **Hyperparameter tuning burden:** Static thresholds α and β require per-dataset tuning; no adaptive mechanisms are proposed, hindering deployment in varying conditions.

## Confidence
- **Token-level self-hard negatives:** Medium (ablation shows benefit, but sensitivity to layer depth suggests fragility)
- **Cross-layer preference optimization & distillation:** Medium (gains from each component confirmed, but underlying assumption of final layer as teacher may not hold for very small models)
- **CF-based false negative mitigation:** Low (limited evidence; only SASRec works reliably; token-level reward formula lacks thorough validation)
- **Overall performance superiority:** High (consistent improvements across three datasets with statistical significance reported)

## Next Checks
1. **Systematic layer selection study:** Train ILRec with varying *k* (1–10) on different model sizes (e.g., 1B, 8B, 70B) to establish robust heuristics for intermediate layer choice.
2. **Reward construction reproducibility:** Re-implement the CF token reward exactly as described (using prefix matching) and compare against a variant that uses whole-item scores; measure impact on performance to validate the design.
3. **Ablation of teacher-student alignment:** Replace final layer teacher with an auxiliary classifier or use earlier layers as teachers to test if final layer distillation is truly necessary; observe any degradation.
