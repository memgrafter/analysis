# Quantization-Aware Collaborative Inference for Large Embodied AI Models
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.13052
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13052
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: The paper introduces a quantization‑aware co‑inference framework for large embodied AI models, providing tractable distortion approximations, analytical rate‑distortion bounds, and a joint optimization of quantization bit‑width and computation frequencies under latency and energy constraints; it achieves state‑of‑the‑art CIDEr (up to ~132) on MS‑COCO and VaTeX while meeting resource budgets.

## Executive Summary
Large embodied AI models (LAIMs) often rely on collaborative inference between a resource‑constrained agent and a powerful server. Quantization, while reducing agent compute, introduces distortion that degrades task performance. This work addresses the resulting co‑inference design challenge by developing a tractable analytical link between parameter‑level quantization errors and final output distortion. Building on this, the authors derive closed‑form rate‑distortion bounds for exponentially distributed weights under ℓ₁ distortion, enabling precise characterization of the bit‑rate vs. quality trade‑off. They then formulate a joint optimization problem that selects the agent’s quantization bit‑width and the computation frequencies of both agent and server to minimize an estimated distortion gap while respecting strict latency and energy budgets. A successive convex approximation (SCA) algorithm solves the non‑convex mixed‑integer program. Evaluations on vision‑language models BLIP‑2 and GIT demonstrate that the distortion bounds are tight for moderate bit‑widths, and the proposed joint design consistently outperforms PPO‑based, fixed‑frequency, and random baselines, delivering significant CIDEr improvements on MS‑COCO and VaTeX under both simulated and real‑world edge hardware constraints.

## Method Summary
The paper’s method consists of three intertwined parts. First, it establishes a theoretical upper bound on the inference output distortion caused by quantizing model parameters. Under assumptions of 1‑Lipschitz activations, normalized inputs, and bounded per‑layer quantization errors, the bound is a weighted sum of parameter deviations ∥W−∇W∥₁. Second, it characterizes the fundamental quantization rate‑distortion relationship for LAIM weights assumed to follow an exponential distribution, deriving closed‑form lower and upper bounds D_L(R) and D_U(R) that are validated against Blahut‑Arimoto computation. Third, it formulates a joint resource‑quality optimization: choose agent bit‑width ˜b and agent/server frequencies (f, ∇f) to minimize the gap D_U(˜b−1)−D_L(˜b−1) subject to total delay T(˜b,f,∇f) ≤ T₀ and energy E(˜b,f,∇f) ≤ E₀. The non‑convex mixed‑integer problem is relaxed to a continuous version and tackled by an iterative SCA scheme. The framework supports both uniform and non‑uniform (PoT‑log) quantization and is evaluated on image captioning and video understanding tasks using pre‑trained BLIP‑2 and GIT models.

## Key Results
- The proposed output distortion upper bound is tight for moderate to high bit‑widths (≥2 bits) across multiple large models, confirming the theoretical approximation.
- Analytical rate‑distortion brackets closely match numerically computed D(R) for exponential weights; the gap shrinks at higher rates, giving confidence in the design.
- Joint optimization of bit‑width and frequencies yields up to ~132 CIDEr on MS‑COCO and consistent gains over PPO, fixed‑frequency, and random baselines under identical latency and energy budgets on both simulated and Jetson AGX Orin testbeds.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Distortion Approximation via Parameter Perturbations
**Claim:** Parameter‑level distortion (e.g., ∥W−∇W∥₁) upper‑bounds the inference output distortion for quantized LAIMs in co‑inference.  
**Mechanism:** Quantization introduces bounded per‑layer perturbations ∥W^(l)−∇W^(l)∥₁ ≤ τ^(l). Under Lipschitz activations and normalized inputs, these errors propagate multiplicatively. Proposition 3.1 formalizes that ∥f(x,W)−f(x,∇W)∥₁ ≤ Σₗ A^(l)∥W^(l)−∇W^(l)∥₁, where coefficients A^(l) depend only on full‑precision weights and quantization errors.  
**Core assumptions:** Activations are 1‑Lipschitz (e.g., ReLU) and satisfy σ(0)=0; input data is normalized (∥x∥₁ ≤ 1); per‑layer quantization error is bounded.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions “tractable approximation for quantization‑induced inference distortion… linking parameter quantization to output distortion”; Section III Proposition 3.1 with proof in Appendix A; neighboring papers (e.g., SQAP‑VLA, L4Q) use a similar surrogate, but the multiplicative propagation bound is unique.  
**Break condition:** The bound may become loose if activations violate Lipschitzness (e.g., unbounded functions like softmax without clipping) or if weight distributions have heavy tails that cause τ^(l) underestimation.

### Mechanism 2: Rate‑Distortion Bounds for Exponentially Distributed Weights
**Claim:** The rate‑distortion function D(R) for quantizing exponentially distributed LAIM weights under ℓ₁ distortion is tightly bracketed by D_L(R)=1/(λ 2^{R+1}) and D_U(R)=(1/(2λ))·(√(1+4/2^{R})−1), enabling analytical system design.  
**Mechanism:** Using information theory, the minimum distortion for a given bit‑rate R is characterized. The lower bound follows from entropy maximization under a distortion constraint (Laplacian reconstruction noise is optimal); the upper bound is obtained by constructing a specific test channel with Laplacian quantization noise. The exponential weight distribution P_Θ(θ)=λ e^{−λθ} yields closed‑form expressions.  
**Core assumptions:** Model weight magnitudes are i.i.d. exponential; the distortion measure is mean absolute error; quantization is memoryless (no inter‑parameter dependencies).  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract states “derive analytical rate‑distortion bounds”; Section IV Propositions 4.1 and 4.2 present the bounds; Lemma 4.2 shows the Laplacian distribution maximizes entropy under a mean‑absolute constraint; Figure 4 validates the bounds against Blahut‑Arimoto numerical results.  
**Break condition:** If weight magnitudes follow a non‑exponential distribution (e.g., Gaussian with significant mass away from zero), the derived bounds may not accurately predict D(R), requiring re‑derivation for the actual distribution.

### Mechanism 3: Joint Optimization of Bit‑Width and Frequencies
**Claim:** Jointly optimizing quantization bit‑width ˜b and computation frequencies (f, ∇f) minimizes estimated distortion while meeting delay/energy constraints, outperforming separate optimization.  
**Mechanism:** The optimization problem (P1) minimizes the distortion upper‑bound gap D_U(˜b−1)−D_L(˜b−1) subject to T(˜b,f,∇f) ≤ T₀ and E(˜b,f,∇f) ≤ E₀. The coupling arises because ˜b affects both distortion (via the rate‑distortion bounds) and computational workload (t, e ∝ ˜b). The proposed successive convex approximation (SCA) algorithm solves the relaxed continuous problem by iteratively linearizing non‑convex terms in the objective and constraints.  
**Core assumptions:** The distortion gap is a valid surrogate for task performance; SCA converges to a high‑quality local optimum; frequency variables can be treated continuously or coarse‑grained hardware profiles suffice.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract reports “formulate a joint optimization problem… proposed joint design consistently achieves higher CIDEr scores… compared to PPO‑based, fixed‑frequency, and random baselines”; Section V details the problem formulation and Algorithm 1; Table I and Figures 5‑8 document empirical gains across quantization schemes and quality‑of‑service thresholds.  
**Break condition:** If the distortion approximation (Mechanism 1) is poor for a specific model or low bit‑width range, the optimized ˜b may not translate to actual quality improvements. SCA may also stagnate if initialized poorly or the feasible set is highly non‑convex and disconnected. Real‑world frequency granularity on the Jetson AGX Orin (coarse DVFS profiles) may cause slight deviations from the continuous solution.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map (plain text):  
Quantized Agent Model (bit‑width ˜b, frequency f) → Embedding Transmission → Full‑Precision Server Model (frequency ∇f) → Final Output. Supporting components: Distortion Approximator (computes ∥W−∇W∥₁ or D_U(˜b)); Resource Calculator (computes T(˜b,f,∇f) and E(˜b,f,∇f) from FLOPs, hardware parameters); Optimizer (Algorithm 1 iteratively updates ˜b, f, ∇f); Constraints Handler (enforces discrete bit‑width set B, frequency limits, and QoS thresholds T₀, E₀).

Critical path: The agent receives input x and processes it with its quantized model (determined by ˜b and f) to produce embedding o. o is transmitted to the server, which runs the full‑precision model at frequency ∇f to produce the final output. The distortion approximator estimates expected output quality; the optimizer adjusts the configuration to minimize the distortion gap while the resource calculator checks that the combined delay and energy stay within budgets. The chosen (˜b, f, ∇f) are then deployed for inference.

Design tradeoffs:
- **Bit‑width vs. quality vs. compute:** Lower ˜b reduces execution time and energy (since FLOPs scale linearly with ˜b) but increases distortion D_U; higher ˜b improves quality but may violate constraints.
- **Frequency vs. latency/energy:** Increasing f or ∇f reduces latency but energy grows quadratically (e ∝ f²) and may exceed hardware limits.
- **Joint vs. independent optimization:** Optimizing bit‑width and frequencies separately ignores their coupling and yields suboptimal solutions under tight constraints; the joint approach explicitly models both effects.

Failure signatures:
- **Distortion bound violation:** For very low bit‑widths or certain layers (e.g., with softmax), the actual output distortion may exceed the theoretical bound → indicates violation of Lipschitz or bounded‑error assumptions.
- **Constraint violation:** The SCA solution may propose frequencies beyond the maximum supported by hardware; needs mapping to discrete DVFS profiles and rechecking.
- **SCA stagnation:** Objective value plateaus after few iterations → try different initial points or increase SCA iteration threshold.
- **Real‑world performance drop:** Testbed on Jetson AGX Orin may show lower CIDEr or constraint breaches due to coarse frequency steps → refine profile selection or adjust relaxation.

First 3 experiments:
1. **Distortion approximation validation:** Quantize a fully‑connected 16‑layer ReLU network (FCDNN‑16), BLIP‑2, and GIT across bit‑widths 2‑7 (uniform and PoT‑log). For each, compute parameter distortion ∥W−∇W∥₁ and actual output distortion on a validation set. Verify that Proposition 3.1’s bound is close to the measured distortion for moderate/high bit‑widths.
2. **Rate‑distortion bound validation:** For an exponential source with λ=1, compute D(R) numerically using Blahut‑Arimoto for rates R=1,…,8 bits. Compare these simulated points with the analytical lower bound D_L(R) and upper bound D_U(R). Expect the bounds to enclose the numerical results, with the gap narrowing as R increases.
3. **Joint design vs. baselines:** On BLIP‑2 (MS‑COCO) and GIT (VaTeX), run the SCA optimizer under various latency (T₀) and energy (E₀) budgets. Evaluate CIDEr scores on the test set and compare against PPO‑based, fixed‑frequency (baseline from [12]), and random configuration baselines. The proposed method should dominate, especially when constraints are tight.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Tightness of rate‑distortion bounds at very low bit‑widths**  
   *Question:* How tight are the derived D_L(R) and D_U(R) for practical LAIMs when bit‑widths are ≤3, where the interval remains significant?  
   *Basis:* Remark 4.2 and Figure 4 show the bounds gap is wider in the very low‑rate regime.  
   *Why unresolved:* The optimization objective relies on the bound gap; a larger gap may lead to suboptimal quantization choices, yet such low bit‑widths are common on edge devices.  
   *Resolving evidence:* Empirically measure actual distortion vs. bit‑width for a diverse set of LAIMs (LLMs, diffusion, speech) and develop tighter analytical approximations for low rates.

2. **Optimality of the SCA‑based joint optimization**  
   *Question:* How close is the SCA solution to the true optimum of the original mixed‑integer non‑linear problem (P1)?  
   *Basis:* The authors admit “problem (P1) is highly non‑convex and non‑trivial to be optimally solved in general” and provide no optimality gap.  
   *Why unresolved:* Although the method beats PPO and random baselines, these are heuristics; without a ground‑truth optimum, the quality of the SCA solution is unknown.  
   *Resolving evidence:* Solve small‑scale instances (e.g., 2‑3 discrete bit‑width options, simplified model) with exhaustive search or global solvers (e.g., BARON) and compare the SCA result to the optimal value across constraint settings.

3. **Joint optimization of partition point and bit‑width/frequencies**  
   *Question:* Can jointly selecting the model partition (which layers run on the agent) together with bit‑width and frequencies yield further gains over fixing the partition?  
   *Basis:* The system model in Section II assumes a fixed two‑stage split; only ˜b, f, ∇f are optimized.  
   *Why unresolved:* The partition critically influences the compute‑communication trade‑off; ignoring it may miss significant optimization opportunities.  
   *Resolving evidence:* Extend the formulation to include a discrete partition variable (number of agent layers), solve via iterative search with inner optimization as in the paper, and evaluate on split‑capable models (e.g., BLIP‑2, GPT).

4. **Generality of distortion and rate‑distortion analyses**  
   *Question:* Do the distortion approximation (Prop. 3.1) and rate‑distortion bounds hold for a broader spectrum of LAIM architectures (decoder‑only LLMs, diffusion models) and for non‑uniform quantization schemes beyond uniform and PoT‑log?  
   *Basis:* The paper validates on ResNet‑152, VideoMAE, BERT, BLIP‑2, GIT, GPT‑3 and reports that weight magnitudes roughly follow an exponential distribution; but it does not test generative models or other transformer variants.  
   *Why unresolved:* The exponential assumption is central to the rate‑distortion derivation; generative models may have heavier‑tailed weight distributions (e.g., power‑law), potentially invalidating the bounds. Moreover, ℓ₁ distortion may not align with perceptual metrics for image/video generation.  
   *Resolving evidence:* Perform comprehensive weight distribution analysis across a wide range of LAIMs; compute empirical rate‑distortion curves for various quantization methods (including learned quantization) and compare with analytical bounds; if needed, propose refined distribution models or distortion metrics.

## Limitations
- The distortion upper bound’s validity is limited to models with Lipschitz activations and normalized inputs; its behavior for very low bit‑widths (<2) or for activations like softmax is not verified.
- The rate‑distortion bounds rely on an exponential weight distribution assumption, which may not hold for all transformer layers; the paper does not provide per‑layer weight histograms beyond a few examples.
- Quantization implementation details (layer‑wise scheme, exact PoT‑log parameters) are underspecified, hampering exact reproducibility; the mapping between continuous frequencies and the Jetson AGX Orin’s discrete DVFS profiles is coarse.
