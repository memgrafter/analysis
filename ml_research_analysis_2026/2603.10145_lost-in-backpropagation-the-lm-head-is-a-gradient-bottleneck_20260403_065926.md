# Lost in Backpropagation: The LM Head is a Gradient Bottleneck

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.10145  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10145  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** The LM head in language models discards 95–99% of the logit gradient norm during backpropagation due to a rank limitation (max rank = hidden dimension D), dramatically slowing convergence and degrading downstream performance.

## Executive Summary
This paper identifies the output layer (LM head) in autoregressive language models as a fundamental gradient bottleneck. Because the hidden dimension \(D\) is vastly smaller than the vocabulary size \(V\), the Jacobian of the mapping from hidden states to logits has rank at most \(D\), forcing the compression of a \(V\)-dimensional gradient signal into \(\leq D\) dimensions. Theoretical analysis shows logit gradients are typically high-rank, meaning the projected gradient diverges significantly from the optimal update direction. Empirical measurements across multiple model families confirm that 95–99% of the logit gradient norm is lost. Controlled pretraining experiments with 2B-parameter models demonstrate that stronger bottlenecks (smaller \(D/V\)) slow convergence by up to a factor of 16 and harm downstream task accuracy. A synthetic language experiment further shows that even with sufficient model capacity, the bottleneck makes learning basic patterns harder as \(V\) grows. The work reveals a critical, previously overlooked optimization limitation in standard LM design and motivates new head architectures that better preserve gradient information.

## Method Summary
The paper combines theoretical analysis with empirical measurements and controlled pretraining experiments. Theoretically, it derives the rank limitation of the LM head Jacobian and analyzes the expected alignment between the raw and projected gradients under i.i.d. assumptions. Empirically, it measures the fraction of logit gradient norm suppressed by the LM head across various model architectures (GPT-2, Pythia, LLaMA) at different scales. For controlled validation, it pretrains 2B-parameter decoder-only models with systematic variations in hidden dimension \(D\) (and thus \(D/V\)), monitoring convergence speed and downstream performance on a suite of benchmarks. A synthetic experiment on a simple closed-loop language further isolates the bottleneck effect by varying \(V\) while keeping other capacities fixed.

## Key Results
- The LM head suppresses 95–99% of the logit gradient norm across all tested model families and scales, with the projected gradient having very low cosine similarity (0.1–0.2) to the raw gradient.
- Smaller \(D/V\) ratios slow convergence dramatically; a model with \(D=1024\) required ~16× more training tokens to reach the same loss as one with \(D=4096\).
- Downstream task performance (e.g., MMLU, HellaSwag) degrades as the bottleneck strengthens, indicating the gradient loss harms not just optimization speed but final capability.
- In a synthetic language task, increasing \(V\) while keeping \(D\) fixed makes learning increasingly difficult, even when the model has enough parameters to represent the target function.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The mechanism is rooted in linear algebra: any mapping \(f_\theta(H)\) from hidden states \(H \in \mathbb{R}^{D}\) to logits \(Z \in \mathbb{R}^{V}\) has a Jacobian \(J_f\) with rank at most \(D\). During backpropagation, the gradient w.r.t. \(H\) is \(J_f^\top \cdot \nabla_Z \mathcal{L}\). If \(\nabla_Z \mathcal{L}\) lies largely outside the row space of \(J_f\) (i.e., has high rank \(> D\)), the projection \(J_f^\top \nabla_Z \mathcal{L}\) loses most of the gradient's norm and directionality. The paper argues that \(\nabla_Z \mathcal{L}\) is typically high-rank because the loss depends on many output dimensions simultaneously, so the compression is inherently lossy. This is not a numerical issue but a structural constraint of any feedforward head with \(V \gg D\).

## Foundational Learning
- **Gradient bottleneck:** The phenomenon where a layer's Jacobian rank restricts the gradient information that can flow backward. *Why needed:* It explains why optimization fails despite sufficient model capacity. *Quick check:* Compute the rank of the LM head's weight matrix; it is \(\leq D\) by definition.
- **LM head:** The final linear layer (often followed by softmax) that maps hidden states to vocabulary logits. *Why needed:* It is the site of the gradient compression. *Quick check:* Confirm \(V \gg D\) in any standard LM (e.g., GPT-3: \(D=12,288\), \(V=50,257\)).
- **Jacobian rank limitation:** The mathematical property that \(\text{rank}(J_f) \leq \min(\text{input dim}, \text{output dim})\). *Why needed:* It sets the maximum dimensionality of the gradient signal. *Quick check:* For a linear map \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times D}\), \(\text{rank}(W) \leq D\).
- **Logit gradient:** \(\nabla_Z \mathcal{L}\), the gradient of the loss with respect to the unnormalized outputs. *Why needed:* Its rank and distribution determine how much information is lost. *Quick check:* Estimate the effective rank of \(\nabla_Z \mathcal{L}\) snapshots during training (e.g., via nuclear norm).
- **Scaling laws:** Empirical relationships predicting model performance from size and training compute. *Why needed:* The paper suggests \(D/V\) is a missing variable in current scaling laws. *Quick check:* Re-plot existing scaling law data with \(D/V\) as an additional axis to see residual trends.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input Tokens -> Transformer Layers (hidden dim \(D\)) -> LM Head (linear weight \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times D}\)) -> Logits (\(V\)) -> Softmax -> Loss

**Critical path:** The LM head is the sole point where gradients from the \(V\)-dimensional logit space must be compressed to \(D\) dimensions before propagating into the transformer backbone. This makes it the universal bottleneck in standard decoder-only LMs.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Expressivity vs. compute:* Increasing the head's effective rank (e.g., via low-rank ensembles or nonlinear projections) could preserve gradients but adds parameters and FLOPs, potentially offsetting the savings from a small \(D\).  
- *Tying:* Tying input and output embeddings does not alleviate the bottleneck because the gradient still passes through the same low-rank mapping.  
- *Softmax alternatives:* Temperature scaling or adaptive softmax change the loss landscape but do not increase the Jacobian's rank, so they cannot fix the fundamental compression.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Measured gradient suppression >90% (norm of \(J_f^\top \nabla_Z \mathcal{L}\) vs. \(\|\nabla_Z \mathcal{L}\|\)).  
- Low cosine similarity (<0.3) between raw and projected gradients.  
- Convergence slowing disproportionately as \(V\) grows for fixed \(D\).

**First three experiments:**  
1. Measure gradient suppression across checkpoints of a pretrained model (e.g., Pythia-1.4B) to confirm it persists throughout training.  
2. Replace the standard LM head with an ensemble of \(k\) independent linear projections (each \(D \times V/k\)) and sum their outputs; test if \(k>1\) reduces suppression and speeds up convergence.  
3. Train small models (e.g., 100M params) with varying \(D\) but same \(V\) and total parameter count (adjust depth accordingly) to isolate the effect of \(D/V\) on final accuracy.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can LM head architectures be redesigned to preserve gradient information during backpropagation while maintaining computational feasibility?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors show that existing softmax alternatives (e.g., Yang et al., 2018; Ganea et al., 2019) fail to address the optimization bottleneck because any mapping \(f_\theta(H_\theta)\) has a Jacobian of rank at most \(D\). They conclude that "the exploration of such methods [that] better preserve gradient flow" is needed (Section 2.3.1).
- **Why unresolved:** The paper demonstrates the problem but provides no architectural solutions beyond noting that some functions \(f\) may offer "less lossy compression through \(J_f\)." It remains unknown which specific structural changes (e.g., nonlinear projections, multi-path heads, or adaptive dimensionality) can break the rank-\(D\) limitation without prohibitive compute or expressivity trade-offs.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement and evaluate candidate LM head designs that increase the effective rank of the gradient mapping during backpropagation (e.g., via ensembles of low-rank projections or gradient preconditioning). Measure the fraction of logit gradient norm preserved, convergence speed, and downstream performance compared to standard LM heads in controlled pretraining setups.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Should the ratio \(D/V\) be incorporated into scaling laws to better predict pretraining compute requirements and model capabilities?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state in the Discussion: "We hypothesize that taking hidden dimensions into account when computing scaling laws ... could help refine their extrapolation quality." They show that convergence speed degrades dramatically as the bottleneck strengthens (Figure 1), implying that \(D/V\) is a critical, currently overlooked factor.
- **Why unresolved:** Scaling laws (e.g., Hoffmann et al., 2022) typically relate performance to model size (parameters) and training tokens, not explicitly to \(D/V\). The paper’s controlled experiments vary \(D\) while keeping the backbone fixed, but scaling laws are derived from varying total parameter counts, making it unclear how to integrate \(D/V\) without redefining the axes of scaling.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct large-scale pretraining experiments spanning diverse model architectures (e.g., varying depth vs. width) and tokenizers (varying \(V\)), while precisely controlling \(D/V\). Fit scaling law curves that include \(D/V\) as a variable and compare predictive accuracy against traditional parameter-token scaling laws.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Beyond gradient norm loss, which specific properties of the compressed gradient (e.g., directional alignment, signal-to-noise ratio in tail components) most strongly impact downstream task performance?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper measures that 95–99% of the logit gradient norm is suppressed (Figure 6) and that the projected gradient has low cosine similarity with the original (0.1–0.2, Figure 11). It also shows that compressed gradients are less efficient for loss reduction (Figure 8). However, it does not investigate whether metrics like gradient noise magnitude or alignment with high-curvature directions correlate with downstream task accuracy.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper establishes that gradient compression harms convergence speed, but the link between the *structure* of the compressed gradient (e.g., redistribution of energy to tail coefficients as noise) and final model capability is not quantified. It is unknown whether certain compression patterns are more detrimental to specific skills (e.g., reasoning vs. factual recall).
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Across pretraining checkpoints with varying \(D/V\), compute detailed statistics of the projected gradient (e.g., spectral distribution, alignment with gradient covariance eigenvectors, gradient signal-to-noise ratio). Correlate these with downstream benchmark scores to identify which aspects of compression most strongly predict performance gaps.

## Limitations
- The study focuses exclusively on decoder-only transformer LMs; results may not generalize to encoder-decoder models, state-space models, or architectures with tied input/output embeddings.
- Gradient suppression metrics are measured as single snapshots; the longitudinal evolution of the bottleneck effect over the full training trajectory is not characterized.
- The theoretical analysis assumes i.i.d. gradient components, which may not hold in realistic training where gradients exhibit strong correlations and non-stationarity.
- Controlled pretraining experiments are limited to 2B-parameter models; interactions with larger scales, different data mixtures, or optimizer choices (e.g., AdamW vs. Sophia) remain uncertain.

## Confidence
- **Severe gradient suppression (95–99%)**: High – robust empirical evidence across multiple model families and ablations.
- **High‑rank logit gradients cause irrecoverable loss**: Medium – sound theoretical derivation but relies on distributional assumptions; direct rank measurements are absent.
- **Bottleneck slows convergence and degrades downstream performance**: Medium‑High – controlled experiments show clear effects, yet confined to a narrow set of model sizes and datasets.
- **Synthetic experiment isolates bottleneck effect**: Medium – proof‑of‑concept with a simple task; generalizability to natural language complexity is unclear.

## Next Checks
1. Measure gradient suppression across a wider range of architectures (encoder-decoder, state-space models, tied/untied LM heads) to test universality.
2. Directly compute singular value spectra of logit gradients throughout training to validate the high‑rank claim and monitor rank dynamics.
3. Scale controlled pretraining to >10B parameters and varied training setups (different optimizers, data distributions) to assess whether the bottleneck effect intensifies, diminishes, or remains constant.
