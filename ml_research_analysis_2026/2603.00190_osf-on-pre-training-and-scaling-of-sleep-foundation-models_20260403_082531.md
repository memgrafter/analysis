# OSF: On Pre-training and Scaling of Sleep Foundation Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.00190
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00190
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** OSF achieves state-of-the-art performance across nine datasets, e.g., sleep staging 97.3 AUC / 90.4 AUPRC, and shows significantly better robustness under missing-channel inference.

## Executive Summary
Polysomnography (PSG) data exhibit substantial heterogeneity in channel configurations across devices and clinical settings, causing existing sleep foundation models to fail when channels are missing at inference. This paper introduces OSF, a sleep foundation model pre-trained on the large multi-source SleepBench corpus (~166,500 hours from nine datasets) with channel-invariant self-supervised learning. By augmenting the SSL objective (DINO) with random channel masking (50%) and temporal block masking, OSF learns representations robust to channel subset variations. Combined with scaling in data, model capacity (ViT-85M), and multi-source mixing, OSF consistently outperforms prior work (SleepFM) on diverse downstream tasks—epoch-level sleep staging, apnea/hypopnea detection, and patient-level disease prediction—while maintaining strong performance under simulated missing-channel scenarios.

## Method Summary
The authors build the SleepBench corpus by harmonizing nine public PSG datasets (NSRR) into a standardized 12-channel montage resampled at 64 Hz and normalized per night. For pre-training, they adopt DINO with a ViT-85M backbone and introduce **channel masking**: randomly dropping 50% of channels per augmented view, in addition to temporal block masking (ratio 0.3–0.6). The model is trained on the full multi-source corpus using AdamW with a large batch (1024). Downstream evaluation uses frozen encoders and linear probing for epoch-level tasks (sleep staging, event detection). For patient-level disease prediction, epoch embeddings are aggregated (top-𝑘 selection) before classification. This design yields consistent gains across data scales and source diversity, and critical robustness when channels are absent at inference.

## Key Results
- Sleep staging linear probing: OSF reaches **97.3 AUC / 90.4 AUPRC**, improving over SleepFM (96.4 / 87.7).
- Hypopnea detection: **77.7 AUC / 62.2 AUPRC** vs SleepFM (75.5 / 60.6).
- Patient-level hypertension prediction: **66.3 AUC / 66.1 AUPRC** vs SleepFM (62.5 / 61.4).
- Under missing-channel inference (e.g., brain-only input), OSF shows dramatically smaller performance drops compared to SleepFM across all tasks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Overfitting to full channel set
- **Claim:** Existing sleep foundation models degrade significantly under inference with missing channels because they overfit to the full channel set seen during pre‑training.
- **Mechanism:** The model learns representations that implicitly encode correlations specific to the exact channel configuration used in pre‑training. When channels are absent at inference, the input distribution shifts and the learned feature space lacks invariance to such omissions.
- **Core assumption:** Real‑world PSG recordings inherently suffer from channel heterogeneity due to varying devices, protocols, and dropouts (e.g., home studies lack EEG/EOG).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "existing FMs fail to generalize to missing channels at inference"
  - [section 3.1] Simulated missing‑channel scenarios (brain‑only, respiration‑only) show large AUC drops on sleep staging and hypopnea detection for SleepFM.
- **Break condition:** If pre‑training includes diverse channel dropout patterns, the model may learn channel‑agnostic features instead.

### Mechanism 2: Channel‑invariant pre‑training via masking
- **Claim:** Explicit channel‑invariant pre‑training via channel masking augmentation forces the model to learn features robust to channel subset variations, improving downstream performance across tasks.
- **Mechanism:** During SSL, randomly dropping a subset of channels (e.g., 50%) for each augmented view forces the encoder to extract information from the remaining channels and align representations across different channel subsets. This encourages disentanglement of channel‑specific noise from task‑relevant physiological patterns.
- **Core assumption:** Invariance‑based SSL objectives (contrastive/distillation) are highly sensitive to augmentation design; augmentations that simulate deployment shifts (missing channels) improve robustness.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "channel‑invariant feature learning is essential for pre‑training"
  - [section 3.2] Controlled study: SimCLR/DINO with channel masking outperforms time‑only masking on sleep staging and hypopnea detection (Table 1). Channel‑only masking also improves over baseline.
- **Break condition:** Excessive channel masking (e.g., >80%) may remove too many predictive cues, degrading performance.

### Mechanism 3: Scaling only unlocks with channel invariance
- **Claim:** Scaling pre‑training sample size, model capacity, and multi‑source data mixture jointly yields consistent downstream gains, but only when combined with channel‑invariant pre‑training.
- **Mechanism:** Larger and more diverse pre‑training data exposes the model to broader PSG heterogeneity, while increased model capacity allows memorizing complex cross‑channel patterns. Multi‑source mixing prevents overfitting to any single cohort’s device/protocol biases. Channel invariance ensures this scaling benefits transfer to unseen channel configurations.
- **Core assumption:** Sleep FM performance follows scaling laws similar to vision/language, but only with appropriate pre‑training design (channel invariance).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "scaling sample size, model capacity, and multi‑source data mixture consistently improves downstream performance"
  - [section 3.3 & 5] Baseline SleepFM saturates with more data; OSF (with channel masking) scales consistently. Multi‑source pre‑training outperforms single‑source (Fig. 8). Model scaling (1M→85M) improves linear probing (Fig. 9, Table 30).
- **Break condition:** If channel invariance is not enforced, scaling may lead to overfitting on source‑specific channel patterns, limiting out‑of‑distribution generalization.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Input layer (12‑channel PSG epochs, resampled to 64 Hz, z‑normalized per night) → Patchification (1D convolution, window length 64) → Encoder (Vision Transformer backbone, ViT‑85M) → Pre‑training heads (projections for SSL objectives, e.g., DINO student/teacher) / Downstream heads (linear classifier or full fine‑tuning; for patient‑level, embedding aggregation via top‑𝑘, MIL, LSTM, or mean pool then classifier).

**Critical path:**
1. Pre‑process raw PSG: standardized 12‑channel, per‑night z‑score, trim wake, segment 30 s epochs at 64 Hz.
2. Pre‑train with channel masking (drop 50% channels per view) and temporal block masking (ratio 0.3–0.6) using DINO on full multi‑source SleepBench.
3. For downstream: freeze encoder, extract epoch embeddings. Train linear classifier for epoch‑level tasks; for patient‑level, aggregate embeddings (top‑𝑘) then train classifier.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Channel masking ratio: higher increases invariance but may discard task‑critical channels.
- Model size vs. compute/data: ViT‑85M requires substantial GPU memory; smaller models scale poorly.
- Aggregation complexity: top‑𝑘 selection requires training a small selection module but outperforms mean/LSTM; parameter‑free methods are suboptimal.

**Failure signatures:**
- Large performance gap between full‑channel and missing‑channel inference.
- Early saturation of performance with increased pre‑training data when channel invariance is not enforced.
- Single‑source pre‑training underperforms multi‑source mixing on out‑of‑distribution datasets.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Replicate channel masking ablation: Pre‑train SimCLR/DINO on SHHS (10% data) with time‑only vs. channel+time masking. Validate ≥2% AUPRC gain on hypopnea detection with channel masking.
2. Test scaling sample size: Pre‑train OSF (ViT‑85M) on 1%, 10%, 100% of SleepBench. Linear probe on MROS sleep staging; expect monotonic AUC increase.
3. Evaluate missing‑channel inference: Using OSF pre‑trained model, zero‑mask brain channels on MROS sleep staging. Compare AUC drop to SleepFM; expect drop ≤1%.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Question 1: Robustness to missing task‑critical channels
- **Question:** How can sleep foundation models be made more robust to missing channels during inference, especially when task‑critical channels (e.g., brain activity for sleep staging) are absent?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "However, a meaningful performance gap remains when task‑critical channels are absent. We encourage future work to further improve robustness under channel missingness." (Section 6.1)
- **Why unresolved:** OSF improves upon prior work but still shows significant performance drops in key missing‑channel scenarios, limiting real‑world deployment where channel availability is unpredictable.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Development of pre‑training objectives or architectural changes that explicitly handle variable channel sets, evaluated on a broader set of missing‑channel simulations and real‑world datasets with diverse protocols.

### Question 2: Theoretical mechanism behind scaling + invariance
- **Question:** What theoretical mechanisms enable channel‑invariant self‑supervised pre‑training to consistently unlock scaling benefits (in data size, model capacity, and multi‑source mixing) for sleep foundation models?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Finding 3 empirically shows that channel‑invariant SSL leads to scaling, but the paper does not explain why this occurs or if it generalizes beyond sleep data.
- **Why unresolved:** Without theoretical insight, scaling strategies may be ad hoc and not transferable to other physiological domains or pre‑training methods.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Analytical studies on representation dynamics under channel masking, such as measuring invariance metrics or loss curvature, combined with controlled scaling experiments on diverse objectives and architectures.

### Question 3: Optimal patient‑level aggregation
- **Question:** What is the optimal method for aggregating epoch‑level embeddings into patient‑level representations for disease prediction, and how does this interact with pre‑training objectives and task characteristics?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Section 6.2 compares aggregation methods (e.g., top‑𝑘 selection, MIL, LSTM) and finds top‑𝑘 superior for three diseases, but the scope is limited and not explored in depth.
- **Why unresolved:** The study uses a narrow set of aggregators and tasks; different diseases may require different temporal integration strategies, and pre‑training might influence optimal aggregation.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic evaluation of a wider range of aggregation techniques (e.g., attention, transformers) across more patient‑level tasks, with analyses linking aggregation performance to disease‑specific sleep patterns.

### Question 4: Fairness in multi‑source mixing
- **Question:** How does multi‑source data mixing during pre‑training affect fairness and bias in sleep foundation models across demographic groups, and can data blending be optimized to balance generalization and equity?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Takeaway 1 in Section 5 shows multi‑source pre‑training improves generalization, but the paper does not consider potential biases from mixed cohorts (e.g., age, ethnicity, socioeconomic factors).
- **Why unresolved:** Mixing datasets with varying demographics may amplify or reduce biases, impacting clinical utility; this is critical for deployable models but unaddressed.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Auditing model performance across demographic subgroups from each source, developing bias‑mitigation techniques (e.g., reweighting, adversarial debiasing), and evaluating trade‑offs between accuracy and fairness in multi‑source vs. single‑source training.

## Limitations
- DINO hyperparameters (teacher‑student configs, temperature, centering momentum, multi‑crop settings) are not fully specified, hindering precise replication.
- Preprocessing details (wake‑trimming thresholds, z‑score clipping range, handling of missing channels before zero‑padding) are ambiguous.
- The scaling claim may be confounded: the comparison with SleepFM changes both SSL objective and adds channel masking; no ablation isolates the effect of invariance alone.

## Confidence
- Existing FMs degrade with missing channels: **High**
- Channel‑invariant pre‑training improves robustness: **Medium**
- Scaling gains require channel invariance: **Low**
