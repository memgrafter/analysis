# Temporal Straightening for Latent Planning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.12231
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12231
- Reference count: 28
- Primary result: Temporal straightening via a curvature regularizer improves latent planning success rates by 20-60% on goal-reaching tasks, making Euclidean distance a better proxy for geodesic distance and enabling simple gradient descent to match costly search methods like CEM.

## Executive Summary
Latent planning with world models depends on learned representations that support effective gradient-based optimization. Pretrained visual encoders often produce curved latent trajectories, making planning difficult. This paper introduces temporal straightening, a curvature regularizer that maximizes cosine similarity between consecutive latent velocities, effectively straightening trajectories. The regularizer is combined with a prediction loss to jointly train the encoder and predictor. The authors show theoretically that reduced curvature improves the conditioning of the planning objective. Empirically, across multiple goal-reaching tasks (Wall, PointMaze, PushT), the method yields substantial improvements: open-loop planning gains of 20-60% (e.g., UMaze success from 44% to 94%) and MPC gains of 20-30% (e.g., Wall MPC from 90.7% to 100%). The straightened embeddings make Euclidean distance a more reliable proxy for geodesic distance, stabilize optimization, and allow simple gradient descent to compete with expensive search methods like CEM.

## Method Summary
The paper tackles the problem of improving latent planning for goal-reaching tasks by learning a representation that yields straight trajectories, facilitating gradient-based optimization. The method uses offline datasets of RGB observations from simulated environments (Wall, PointMaze, PushT). Training data consists of trajectories (e.g., 1,920 trajectories for Wall, each 50 steps). The world model comprises a visual encoder (either a frozen DINOv2 backbone with a trainable CNN projector or a ResNet from scratch), an action encoder (MLP), and a ViT-based predictor that forecasts the next latent state given a history of latents and actions. The loss combines a prediction loss (MSE with stop-gradient) and a straightening loss: negative cosine similarity between consecutive latent velocities, applied via a learnable pooling head for spatial features. Training uses Adam, batch size 32, history length K=3, and frameskip 5. After training, planning is performed by gradient descent (Adam, learning rate 0.01, 100 steps, horizon 25) to minimize the terminal MSE between predicted and goal latent state. This approach is evaluated in both open-loop and model predictive control (MPC) settings, showing significant success rate improvements.

## Key Results
- Open-loop planning gains of 20-60% (UMaze: 44%→94%) and MPC gains of 20-30% (Wall MPC: 90.7%→100%).
- Straightened embeddings align Euclidean distance with geodesic distance, stabilizing gradient-based planning.
- Simple gradient descent with straightened models matches the performance of expensive search methods like CEM.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Curvature Regularization Straightens Latent Trajectories
- **Claim:** Maximizing cosine similarity between consecutive latent velocities reduces trajectory curvature in the learned representation space, making dynamics more predictable.
- **Mechanism:** The curvature regularizer (L_curv = 1 − cosine similarity) directly penalizes directional changes between successive latent states (z_t, z_{t+1}, z_{t+2}). Combined with a stop‑gradient prediction loss (L_pred), this encourages locally linear transitions while preserving enough information for accurate future state prediction.
- **Core assumption:** The environment’s feasible state transitions are approximately locally linear in some representation; forcing higher cosine similarity aligns the learned latent dynamics with this structure.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: “…encourages locally straightened latent trajectories… makes the Euclidean distance in latent space a better proxy for the geodesic distance.”
  - [Section 3.2]: Defines the velocity vectors and cosine similarity objective as the “straightening loss.”
  - [corpus]: Neighbors discuss “perceptual straightening” in vision and language, but do not directly support the planning-specific mechanism; evidence here is weak and only conceptually parallel.
- **Break condition:** The regularizer strength λ is either too low (insufficient straightening) or too high (collapses dynamics or over‑smooths task‑relevant variations, e.g., in contact‑rich PushT). Success also depends on the encoder’s capacity and the predictor’s ability to remain accurate under the constraint.

### Mechanism 2: Straightening Aligns Euclidean with Geodesic Distance
- **Claim:** Reduced curvature makes Euclidean distance in latent space a more reliable approximation of the true geodesic distance along feasible transitions, improving goal‑cost semantics for planning.
- **Mechanism:** In a curved space, the straight‑line (Euclidean) distance between two states may far underestimate the minimum path length along valid dynamics. Straightening locally linearizes trajectories, so the chord length (Euclidean) better approximates the arc length (geodesic).
- **Core assumption:** The planning cost (e.g., MSE to goal) is computed in the latent space and relies on Euclidean distance; this cost is meaningful only if Euclidean distance correlates with reachability or progress.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: “…straightening latent trajectories… makes the Euclidean distance in latent space a better proxy for the geodesic distance.”
  - [Section 5.2]: Figure 6 shows heatmaps of Euclidean distance vs. ground‑truth A* geodesic; straightened models produce maps that closely match geodesic structure.
  - [corpus]: No direct evidence; neighboring papers on straightening focus on detection or video models, not planning cost alignment.
- **Break condition:** The environment contains “teleportation” or discontinuous transitions where a straight line in latent space crosses an infeasible region, even after straightening. The method may still fail if the representation does not capture the necessary transition constraints (see Teleported‑PointMaze success only with explicit straightening).

### Mechanism 3: Straightening Improves Conditioning of the Planning Optimization
- **Claim:** Reduced curvature (equivalently, dynamics matrices A closer to identity) improves the conditioning of the planning objective’s Hessian, leading to faster and more stable gradient-based optimization.
- **Mechanism:** For linear latent dynamics z_{t+1} = A z_t + B a_t, the planning Hessian depends on the controllability Gramian W_K. Theorem 4.4 shows that if ∥A−I∥_2 is small (ε‑straight), the effective condition number κ_eff(H) grows slowly with horizon K, guaranteeing better convergence rates for gradient descent on the quadratic terminal loss.
- **Core assumption:** Planning is performed via gradient descent on a differentiable model; the local linearization argument extends to nonlinear predictors if Jacobians along the trajectory are well‑conditioned.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: “…reducing curvature this way… improves the conditioning of the planning objective.”
  - [Section 4]: Theorem 4.4 provides the bound κ_eff(H) ≤ κ(B)^2 e^{6εK} for ε≤½.
  - [corpus]: No direct evidence; neighboring work on curvature in optimization (e.g., ISAAC Newton) addresses second‑order methods, not trajectory straightening.
- **Break condition:** The dynamics are highly nonlinear or the ε‑straight assumption fails (∥A−I∥ large). The bound also degrades if the action matrix B is ill‑conditioned (κ(B) large) or the horizon K is extremely long, though the method still helps empirically.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Joint‑Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA)**
  - *Why needed here:* The world model uses a JEPA‑style predictor: given past latent states and actions, predict a future latent state. This avoids reconstruction and focuses on predictable features, which the straightening regularizer then shapes geometrically.
  - *Quick check question:* Why does stop‑gradient in L_pred help prevent collapse while still allowing useful learning?
- **Concept: Latent Dynamics and Controllability Gramian**
  - *Why needed here:* Planning success depends on how actions influence the latent state over a horizon. The Gramian W_K quantifies reachability; straightening improves its conditioning by making A ≈ I.
  - *Quick check question:* What does it mean for a linear system to be “controllable” and how does that relate to the matrix B?
- **Concept: Geodesic vs. Euclidean Distance in Metric Spaces**
  - *Why needed here:* In a curved latent space, the shortest path (geodesic) between two states may be much longer than the straight line. Planning with a Euclidean cost can be misleading unless the space is flattened.
  - *Quick check question:* If two states are close in Euclidean distance but separated by an obstacle, what does that imply about the geodesic distance?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  1. **Sensory encoder** E_ϕ: Maps observations o_t → z_t (can be frozen pretrained backbone + trainable projector or ResNet from scratch).
  2. **Action encoder** E_ψ: Maps a_t → action embedding.
  3. **Predictor** f_θ: Given K past (z, a) pairs, predicts next ẑ_{t+1}.
  4. **Straightening module**: Computes cosine similarity between consecutive latent velocities; may use a learnable pooling head for spatial features.
  5. **Training loop**: Minimizes L_total = L_pred(ẑ_{t+1}, sg(z_{t+1})) + λ L_curv.
  6. **Planning module**: Gradient‑based optimizer (Adam) that minimizes ∥z_K − z_g∥_2 by differentiating through f_θ.
- **Critical path:** Encoder training (with predictor and regularizer) → trained world model → planner initialization → iterative gradient steps on actions → execute first action → re‑plan (MPC).
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Spatial vs. global features:* Spatial (patch) tokens preserve local geometry but require a pooling head for meaningful straightening; global (CLS) tokens simplify but may lose detail.
  - *λ strength:* Too low → insufficient straightening; too high → collapse or over‑regularization that harms prediction.
  - *Encoder trainability:* Frozen pretrained backbone + small projector vs. training full ResNet; the latter often yields lower curvature but may need more data.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - High curvature persists (low average cosine similarity in Eq. 4).
  - Distance heatmaps remain unrelated to geodesic structure (e.g., Figure 6a vs. 6b).
  - Planner loss landscape remains non‑convex (Figure 4a) and gradient descent converges slowly or to poor minima.
  - Long‑horizon rollouts diverge from simulator (Figure 9 failure).
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Curvature measurement:** Compute average cosine similarity of test trajectories; expect >0.95 for straightened models vs. ~0.8 for DINO baseline.
  2. **Distance alignment:** Generate latent space heatmaps vs. A* geodesic; compute correlation coefficient. Straightening should yield >0.9 correlation vs. <0.5 for unstraightened.
  3. **Planning success:** Run open‑loop GD planner on 50 test goals; measure success rate improvement over baseline (e.g., UMaze 44% → 94% as reported). Also compare loss landscape smoothness via grid search over first action.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can the theoretical analysis of curvature's effect on planning conditioning be extended from linear latent dynamics to nonlinear predictors, where the Hessian involves state-dependent Jacobians and higher-order terms?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] In Section 4 (Planning with Straightened Dynamics), the authors state: "For nonlinear predictors \(z_{t+1} = f_\theta(z_t, a_t)\), analogous guarantees require controlling products of state-dependent Jacobians and higher-order terms, which can be a promising direction for future work."
- **Why unresolved:** The current proof relies on linear assumptions (Assumption 4.1) to derive closed-form bounds on the Hessian's condition number. Nonlinear dynamics break this structure, making the analysis intractable with current tools.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A theoretical framework bounding the planning Hessian for smooth nonlinear dynamics, perhaps using norm-based Jacobian product bounds or mean-value expansions, validated on small nonlinear systems.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Why does aggressively compressing feature dimensions (e.g., reducing DINOv2 channels from 384 to 8) improve planning performance, while higher dimensions (e.g., 128) degrade it, and how does this interact with curvature regularization?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] In Section B.4 (Effect of Feature Dimensions), they report: "Very small dimensions (e.g., \(d_v = 2\)) result in poor performance... Increasing to a moderate dimension (\(d_v = \{8, 32\}\)) yields the best results, while too large dimensions (\(d_v = 128\)) consistently reduce success rates." They also note in Section 5.1 that lower dimensions simplify dynamics prediction and optimization.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper shows the empirical effect but does not explain the underlying mechanism. Possibilities include: high dimensions introducing irrelevant variations that increase curvature, optimization difficulty in larger latent spaces, or overfitting to dataset noise.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablations measuring curvature vs. dimension, experiments with regularized high-dimensional models (e.g., adding a bottleneck or sparsity), and optimization diagnostics (e.g., gradient norms, loss landscape smoothness) across dimensions.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How does temporal straightening interact with partial observability and stochastic dynamics, where the true underlying state manifold may not be well-represented by a single deterministic latent trajectory?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper assumes deterministic, fully observable dynamics (e.g., in the Wall and PointMaze environments, state is fully captured by agent position). The curvature regularization is defined on deterministic consecutive embeddings \(z_t, z_{t+1}, z_{t+2}\). However, real-world environments often have stochastic transitions or partial observability (e.g., PushT involves contact-rich dynamics with possible occlusions). The paper does not address whether straightening remains beneficial or needs modification under such conditions.
- **Why unresolved:** The theoretical analysis (Section 4) and experiments assume deterministic rollouts. The effect of straightening on expected curvature under stochastic policies or on latent spaces encoding uncertainty is unexplored.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Extending the method to latent stochastic models (e.g., with variational inference) and evaluating on environments with stochastic transitions or partial observability (e.g., visual occlusions). Measuring success rates and curvature statistics under policy distribution shifts.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What is the precise relationship between the proposed local curvature regularization and alternative representation learning objectives (e.g., temporal contrastive learning or JEPA alone) in terms of shaping the latent geometry for planning?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] In Section 3.3, they combine prediction loss (\(L_{\text{pred}}\)) and straightening loss (\(L_{\text{curv}}\)). In Section 5.2, they observe "implicit straightening" when training with the predictor loss alone. In Section 2, they contrast with temporal contrastive learning, noting it requires careful negative sampling and may push away geodesically close states.
- **Why unresolved:** While they show explicit straightening improves over implicit (from JEPA alone), the paper does not disentangle *how* the two losses interact geometrically. Does straightening merely amplify an emergent property of JEPA, or does it induce a fundamentally different latent manifold structure? The trade-off between prediction accuracy and straightness is not theoretically characterized.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Comparative studies ablating prediction vs. straightening losses with fixed architectures, measuring geodesic vs. Euclidean alignment (e.g., via trustworthiness of neighbor graphs), and analyzing the loss surfaces or Jacobian spectra of the combined objective. A geometric theory linking prediction error bounds to curvature constraints could clarify the relationship.

## Limitations
- Architectural specifics: Exact design of the CNN projector for DINOv2 and the proprioceptive state encoding scheme are underspecified, blocking exact replication.
- Generalization scope: Success is demonstrated on simulated 2D/contact tasks; real-world applicability, distribution shifts, and long-horizon robustness remain open questions.
- Theoretical assumptions: Theorem 4.4’s ε‑straight condition relies on local linearity; performance under highly nonlinear dynamics or discontinuous transitions is not fully bounded.
