# DRACO: a Cross-Domain Benchmark for Deep Research Accuracy, Completeness, and Objectivity

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2602.11685
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11685
*   **Reference count:** 40
*   **Primary result:** Perplexity Deep Research (Claude Opus 4.6) achieves the highest normalized score of 70.5% (72.8% pass rate) on a 100-task benchmark spanning 10 domains and 40 countries.

## Executive Summary
DRACO is a new benchmark for evaluating deep research agents—systems that perform complex, multi-step information gathering and synthesis. It is built from 100 anonymized, real-world user queries that were systematically augmented with specifications (e.g., persona, geographic constraints) and filtered for objectivity and difficulty. Each task is evaluated with an expert-designed rubric of approximately 39 weighted criteria covering factual accuracy, breadth/depth, presentation, and citation quality. Using LLM-as-a-judge grading, the paper evaluates five commercial deep research systems. Perplexity Deep Research performs best overall and in every domain, with particular strength in breadth/depth and factual accuracy. All systems score highest on presentation but struggle with factual accuracy, highlighting a key area for improvement.

## Method Summary
The DRACO benchmark was constructed in stages. First, high-difficulty queries were sourced from anonymized logs of the Perplexity Deep Research system to ensure real-world relevance. These queries were then systematically augmented across six dimensions (persona, output format, source specificity, temporal scope, cross-entity, and geographic constraints) to create well-defined, challenging tasks. Augmented tasks were filtered by an LLM classifier to retain only those that are objective, tractable, and sufficiently difficult. For each of the 100 final tasks, domain experts created detailed rubrics through a multi-stage process (drafting, peer review, saturation testing, QA). To evaluate a system, its response to each task is graded against the task's rubric by an LLM judge (e.g., `gemini-3-pro`), which provides a binary verdict (`MET`/`UNMET`) for each criterion. These verdicts are aggregated using the rubric's weights to compute a normalized score (0-100%) and an unweighted pass rate.

## Key Results
*   **System rankings:** Perplexity Deep Research (70.5%) > Gemini Deep Research (59.0%) > OpenAI Deep Research o3 (52.1%) > Claude Opus 4.6 (59.8%) > o4-mini (41.9%).
*   **Domain leadership:** Perplexity leads in all 10 domains (e.g., Law 90.2%, Academic 82.8%) and across all rubric axes (factual accuracy, breadth/depth, presentation, citation).
*   **Common weaknesses:** All systems score highest on presentation quality (~90%) and lowest on factual accuracy (50-68%), indicating synthesis and citation are relative strengths while factual grounding is a systemic challenge.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Real-world task sourcing
*   **Claim:** Benchmark validity is anchored in tasks from authentic user intents.
*   **Mechanism:** Sampling from production Perplexity Deep Research queries captures genuine information-seeking behaviors across diverse domains and geographies, avoiding synthetic or academically contrived scenarios.
*   **Core assumption:** The distribution of real-world queries is representative of deep research needs and will remain relevant for evaluation.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [abstract] "These tasks ... originate from anonymized real-world usage patterns within a large-scale deep research system."
    *   [section 3] "We source tasks from production Perplexity Deep Research queries ... to ensure they are ... representative of actual user use cases."
*   **Break condition:** If production queries reflect a biased user base (e.g., tech-savvy, English-speaking), the benchmark may not generalize to other populations or emerging use cases.

### Mechanism 2: Systematic query augmentation
*   **Claim:** Augmentation converts vague queries into bounded, challenging tasks suitable for benchmarking.
*   **Mechanism:** Adding persona (role context), output format, source specificity, temporal scope, cross-entity comparisons, and geographic constraints increases specificity and depth requirements while preserving core intent.
*   **Core assumption:** Augmentation faithfully reconstructs the user’s implicit research needs without distorting them.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [abstract] "Tasks are ... augmented with persona, output, source, temporal, cross-entity, and geographic specifications"
    *   [section 3] Details augmentation dimensions and their purpose: "turns ambiguous queries into well-defined and challenging research tasks ... ensuring consistent evaluation criteria."
    *   [section 6.1] Acknowledges risk: "Systematic augmentation reduces ambiguity and improves reproducibility, but it also risks over-specifying tasks and dampening the natural variability of user queries."
*   **Break condition:** Over-augmentation may introduce artificial constraints that do not reflect actual research workflows, possibly favoring systems that overfit to the augmentation patterns.

### Mechanism 3: Expert-designed rubrics with iterative validation
*   **Claim:** Rubrics created and vetted by domain experts yield nuanced, human-aligned evaluation.
*   **Mechanism:** Multi-stage process (initial drafting, peer review, saturation testing, final QA) ensures criteria are objective, comprehensive, and discriminative.
*   **Core assumption:** Experts can reliably articulate quality dimensions for complex open-ended research tasks.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [abstract] "Expert-designed rubrics contain ~39 weighted criteria across factual accuracy, breadth/depth, presentation, and citation quality."
    *   [section 4] Describes 4-stage rubric development, including saturation test: if Perplexity Deep Research scores >90% on a task, it is flagged as too simple and revised.
    *   [section 4] Reports rubric statistics: average 39.3 criteria per task, with negative criteria concentrated in high-stakes domains (Law, Medicine).
*   **Break condition:** Rubric creation is resource-intensive (45–60 minutes per task) and may not scale; inter-rater reliability across experts is not quantified, leaving potential inconsistency.

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:**
    Task source -> Augmentation pipeline -> Filtering module -> Rubric engine -> Grading service -> Reporting
*   **Critical path:**
    1. Sample high-difficulty production queries (proxy: negative user feedback).
    2. Pre-process: remove PII, resolve ambiguity.
    3. Augment sequentially across six dimensions.
    4. Filter with LLM to retain objective, bounded, challenging tasks.
    5. Sample 100 tasks maintaining domain distribution from Figure 2.
    6. Domain experts create and review rubrics (4 stages).
    7. Dispatch tasks to target deep research systems.
    8. Grade outputs per criterion using chosen LLM judge.
    9. Compute normalized score and pass rate per task and system.
*   **Design tradeoffs:**
    *   Objectivity vs. nuance: Rubrics focus on verifiable claims, but open-ended research often requires interpretive synthesis that is hard to criterion-ize.
    *   Coverage vs. depth: 10 domains and 40 countries provide breadth, but each task is evaluated on ~39 criteria, risking shallow coverage per domain.
    *   Automation vs. expertise: Task construction can be automated, but rubric design remains heavily human-dependent.
*   **Failure signatures:**
    *   Task-level: Pass rates near 0% or 100% across systems indicate tasks are either impossibly hard or too trivial.
    *   Rubric-level: High proportion of negative criteria in Presentation (32.1%) may flag stylistic over factual issues; check if failure modes are domain-aligned.
    *   System-level: Perplexity’s lead is largest in Breadth/Depth and Factual Accuracy—if a system underperforms specifically in these axes, its synthesis or retrieval may be weak.
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1. **Baseline sanity check**: Run a non-agentic RAG system (e.g., single-search GPT-4) on a 10-task subset to confirm DRACO discriminates between simple and deep research.
    2. **Judge consistency**: Grade a fixed set of 5 outputs with all three judge models (Gemini-3-Pro, GPT-5.2, Sonnet-4.5) to quantify score variance; expect stable rankings but absolute differences.
    3. **Augmentation ablation**: Compare unaugmented vs. augmented versions of 20 tasks to measure impact of added constraints on system scores; anticipate augmented tasks yield lower scores and higher differentiation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Question:** How can deep research benchmarks be extended to multi-turn interactions to evaluate systems' ability to ask clarifying questions?
    *   **Basis:** [explicit] Section 6.1 identifies "single-turn to multi-turn evaluation" as a key limitation.
    *   **Why unresolved:** DRACO only evaluates single-turn responses, omitting a core aspect of iterative human-like research.
    *   **Evidence:** Develop a multi-turn version of DRACO with tasks requiring clarifications; measure impact on final answer quality.
*   **Question:** Does systematic query augmentation (persona, output, source, temporal, cross-entity, geographic) compromise benchmark generalizability by over-specifying tasks?
    *   **Basis:** [explicit] Section 6.1 notes augmentation "risks over-specifying tasks and dampening the natural variability of user queries."
    *   **Why unresolved:** Augmentation ensures evaluability but may create artificial tasks not reflective of real-world queries.
    *   **Evidence:** Compare model performance on raw vs. augmented queries from the same distribution; correlate with human evaluations of naturalness.
*   **Question:** Can expert-designed rubric creation be automated at scale while maintaining alignment with human expert judgments?
    *   **Basis:** [explicit] Section 6.2 states "Expert-designed rubrics align more closely with human preferences... but they are costly... explore scalable variants."
    *   **Why unresolved:** The current hybrid human-LLM process is resource-intensive; fully autonomous methods may reduce rubric quality.
    *   **Evidence:** Generate rubrics using LLMs, validate via expert review and human preference comparisons on a task subset.
*   **Question:** How can overall deep research performance be decomposed into contributions from individual components (retrieval, planning, synthesis) to diagnose failure modes?
    *   **Basis:** [explicit] Section 6.3 calls for "finer decomposition" and "component-level evaluation" beyond system-level black-box assessment.
    *   **Why unresolved:** Holistic scores hide which sub-capabilities are weak, hindering targeted improvements.
    *   **Evidence:** Build modular research agents, design targeted sub-benchmarks for each component, and perform controlled ablation studies.

## Limitations
*   **Judge model fidelity:** Unclear if `gemini-3-pro`/`gpt-5.2`/`sonnet-4.5` are available with identical settings; model drift may affect scores.
*   **System opacity:** Internal prompts/tools for Perplexity, Gemini, and OpenAI agents are undisclosed, hindering exact replication.
*   **Dataset versioning:** Hugging Face release may differ from paper’s version (rubric weights, task filtering).

## Confidence
*   **Real-world sourcing:** Medium (strong evidence, but user-base bias threatens generalizability).
*   **Augmentation utility:** Medium-High (mechanistically sound, but over-specification may distort query distribution).
*   **Rubric rigor:** Medium (multi-stage process detailed, but no inter-rater reliability metrics).
*   **LLM-as-judge validity:** Low (core scoring relies on uncalibrated judge models; absolute scores vulnerable to bias/drift).
