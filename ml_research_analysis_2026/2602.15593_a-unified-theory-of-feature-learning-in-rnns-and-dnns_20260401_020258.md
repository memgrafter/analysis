# A unified theory of feature learning in RNNs and DNNs

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: `2602.15593`
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15593
- Reference count: 40
- One-line primary result: A unified mean-field theory explains how weight sharing in RNNs yields distinct inductive biases compared to DNNs, characterized by a phase transition in the learning signal strength λ.

## Executive Summary
This paper develops a unified mean-field theory to explain how weight sharing in recurrent neural networks (RNNs) leads to different functional properties than in deep neural networks (DNNs). The theory describes fully trained networks in the feature-learning regime using representational kernels, casting training as Bayesian inference. For endpoint-supervised tasks (typical of DNNs), a phase transition is identified: when the learning signal strength λ is below a critical value, RNNs and DNNs behave identically; above it, only RNNs develop temporally coherent (off-diagonal) representations. For sequential tasks (typical of RNNs), weight sharing induces an inductive bias that allows RNNs to generalize from fewer samples by interpolating over unsupervised time steps, whereas DNNs revert to a white prior. The theory is validated analytically and via SGLD experiments, showing that the representational kernel of trained RNNs converges to theoretical predictions as width increases. Primary outcomes include the identification of a second-order phase transition with critical exponent ½ in linear RNNs, and demonstration that architectural inductive bias can outweigh greater expressivity in DNNs for temporally structured tasks.

## Method Summary
The authors employ a mean-field approach to analyze wide neural networks, treating weight statistics in the infinite-width limit. They derive evolution equations for the representational kernel under Bayesian inference, considering both endpoint-supervised and sequential tasks. The analysis focuses on linear RNNs with orthogonal inputs to isolate the effect of weight sharing. For validation, they use stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics (SGLD) to sample from the posterior and compare empirical kernel matrices against theoretical predictions across varying network widths and learning signal strengths.

## Key Results
- A phase transition for endpoint-supervised tasks: when λ exceeds a critical threshold, only RNNs develop off-diagonal kernel entries; below it, RNNs and DNNs behave similarly.
- Weight sharing gives RNNs an inductive bias enabling generalization from limited sequential data, while DNNs require more samples and fail to exploit temporal structure.
- In linear RNNs, the transition is second-order with critical exponent ½; SGLD simulations confirm kernel convergence to theory in the infinite-width limit.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Weight sharing in RNNs couples the learning signals across time, which through the Bayesian inference framework leads to a structured prior favoring temporally coherent representations when the learning signal is strong enough (λ > λ_c). This coupling manifests as off-diagonal terms in the representational kernel. In contrast, DNNs treat each time step independently, resulting in a diagonal (white noise) kernel. The phase transition arises from the interplay between data fitting and the prior: above λ_c, the posterior favors correlated representations that explain sequential structure; below, the prior dominates and decorrelates. The mean-field equations precisely capture this balance.

## Foundational Learning
- **Representational Kernel** – Captures correlation between network representations at different inputs/time steps. *Why needed*: Provides a compact description of feature learning in infinite-width networks. *Quick check*: Does the kernel evolve with training? Yes.
- **Mean-Field Theory** – Replaces interactions in high-dimensional systems with average effects. *Why needed*: Enables tractable analysis of wide neural networks via weight statistics. *Quick check*: Does it assume infinite width? Yes.
- **Weight Sharing** – Reuse of parameters across input parts (e.g., time steps in RNNs). *Why needed*: Reduces parameters and imposes inductive biases for temporal coherence. *Quick check*: Present in RNNs? Yes.
- **Phase Transition** – Sharp change in behavior as a parameter crosses a critical value. *Why needed*: Explains qualitative shift in RNN representation coherence. *Quick check*: Is there a critical λ? Yes.

  Theory: Weight prior + network architecture (RNN/DNN) → mean‑field equations → representational kernel stationary solution.  
  Experiment: Initialize wide network → SGLD sampling → compute activations → empirical kernel → compare with theory.
- **Critical Path**: Infinite width → kernel well‑defined → Bayesian posterior → solve for kernel → analyze eigenvalues → identify λ_c → predict off‑diagonal emergence. Weight coupling via sharing is the key driver.
- **Design Tradeoffs**: RNNs have fewer parameters and strong temporal inductive bias but limited expressivity; DNNs have more parameters and flexibility but lack built‑in temporal biases, requiring more data. The theory shows RNNs can generalize better on sequential tasks despite lower capacity.
- **Failure Signatures**: (1) Finite‑width effects break mean‑field predictions; (2) Nonlinear activations or non‑orthogonal inputs may violate assumptions; (3) SGLD mixing issues near criticality; (4) Empirical kernel deviates from diagonal/DNN predictions in sequential settings.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: How does using correlated mini‑batch noise in SGD (instead of independent SGLD) affect the phase transition in RNN kernels?  
  *Basis*: They approximated SGD's correlated noise by independent SGLD noise.  
  *Why unresolved*: The theoretical derivation assumes independent noise; the impact of correlation on the transition λ is not analyzed.  
  *What evidence would resolve it*: Simulations comparing SGD and SGLD across λ; theoretical extension incorporating correlated noise.
- **Open Question 2**: Do the results (phase transition, generalization advantage) hold for non‑linear activations and non‑orthogonal input correlations?  
  *Basis*: They considered only linear networks and orthogonal inputs as a minimal setting.  
  *Why unresolved*: Non‑linearities and realistic inputs may alter kernel evolution; not studied.  
  *What evidence would resolve it*: Apply the kernel theory to non‑linear activations (e.g., ReLU) and structured data; verify phase behavior.
- **Open Question 3**: How do pattern structure and scaling limits (T, P ∝ N) affect the representational kernels and learning dynamics?  
  *Basis*: They noted that important phenomena depend on pattern structure and proportional limits, beyond the fixed T, P regime.  
  *Why unresolved*: The current framework assumes fixed T and P independent of N; scaling could induce new phases.  
  *What evidence would resolve it*: Solve mean‑field equations in the proportional limit numerically; simulations with varying T, P, N scaling exponents.
- **Open Question 4**: Since the theory marginalizes over weights, abstracting away dynamics, how do specific optimization algorithms influence the final kernel solutions?  
  *Basis*: They deliberately chose a functional perspective that abstracts away learning dynamics.  
  *Why unresolved*: The stationary distribution may depend on the underlying dynamics; optimizers like Adam could yield different kernels.  
  *What evidence would resolve it*: Empirically compare kernels from different optimizers; attempt to incorporate dynamics into the framework.

## Limitations
- Infinite‑width assumption: theory applies only asymptotically; finite‑width effects in practical models are uncharacterized.
- Analysis restricted to linear RNNs and orthogonal inputs; extension to nonlinear activations and realistic data lacking.
- Validation limited to synthetic sequential tasks; generalizability to complex real‑world temporal data is uncertain.

## Confidence
- Unified mean‑field theory and representational kernel formalism: **High**
- Phase transition at critical λ for endpoint‑supervised tasks: **Medium** (proven for linear RNNs; empirical confirmation for nonlinear cases is pending)
- Inductive bias enabling RNNs to generalize from fewer samples in sequential tasks: **Medium** (theory‑supported but experimental evidence restricted to limited settings)
- Second‑order phase transition with critical exponent ½ in linear RNNs: **High**
- Architectural bias outweighing DNN expressivity for temporal tasks: **Low to Medium** (highly task‑dependent; not broadly demonstrated)
