# Effective QA-driven Annotation of Predicate-Argument Relations Across Languages

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.22865
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22865
- **Reference count:** 33
- **Primary result:** Fine-tuned, language-specific parsers (7B parameters) substantially outperformed strong multilingual LLM baselines (GPT-4o, LLaMA-4-Maverick) on question generation, achieving Semantic Match F1 gains of 10–20 points.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of extending QA-SRL (Question-Answer driven Semantic Role Labeling) annotation to multiple languages without costly manual effort. It introduces a cross-lingual projection pipeline that uses English as a pivot: target-language sentences are translated to English, parsed with a high-quality QA-SRL parser, and annotations are projected back via word alignment and constrained translation to preserve predicates. Evaluated on Hebrew, Russian, and French, the projected data yields strong argument coverage and acceptable labeling quality. When used to fine-tune language-specific models, the resulting parsers significantly outperform large multilingual LLMs, demonstrating that targeted supervision can produce efficient, high-quality multilingual predicate-argument parsing.

## Method Summary
The projection pipeline operates in four stages: (1) translate the target-language sentence to English using a Helsinki-NLP model; (2) apply a pre-trained English QA-SRL parser (T5-XL) to generate question-answer pairs for each predicate; (3) use SimAlign for unsupervised word alignment between the English and target sentence, then project answer spans and predicates to the target language; (4) translate English questions back to the target language with a constrained LLM prompt that forces inclusion of the aligned predicate, preventing predicate drift. The resulting parallel QA pairs (target sentence, predicate, question, answer span) form a training set. A monolingual decoder-only model (7B parameters) is fine-tuned via LoRA on this data to produce a specialized QA-SRL parser for the target language. No manual annotation or role schema mapping is required.

## Key Results
- Manual evaluation of projected data shows strong argument coverage (Unlabeled F1: 78–87%) and acceptable role labeling quality (Labeled F1: 60–73%) across Hebrew, Russian, and French.
- Fine-tuned, language-specific parsers consistently outperform strong multilingual LLM baselines (GPT-4o, LLaMA-4-Maverick) on question generation, with Semantic Match F1 gains of 10–20 points.
- The constrained translation step effectively reduces predicate drift, ensuring the question's predicate matches the source sentence.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Cross-lingual projection via English as a pivot can generate structurally rich QA-SRL annotations for new languages with minimal human effort.
- **Mechanism:** The pipeline translates a target-language (L) sentence to English, applies a high-quality English QA-SRL parser to generate question-answer pairs anchored to predicates, then projects these annotations back to L using word alignment. The projected QA pairs inherit the semantic structures from English while being lexically anchored in L.
- **Core assumption:** The English QA-SRL parser provides a reliable, high-level semantic scaffold that can be mapped onto L sentences via word alignment, assuming sufficient lexical and structural overlap.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states reuse of English parser; Section 3.1 describes the four-stage pipeline; neighbor papers on similar projection indicate active research with known challenges.
- **Break condition:** If English-to-L translation is low quality or L structure diverges significantly from English, word alignment fails, corrupting the projected structure.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Constrained translation, where the target-language predicate is force-included in the translated question, prevents predicate drift and maintains alignment between the question and the source sentence.
- **Mechanism:** After projecting English answer spans to L, the English questions are translated back into L using an LLM prompt that explicitly includes the aligned L predicate as a lexical constraint.
- **Core assumption:** An LLM can reliably perform controlled translation while adhering to the specified predicate constraint, and the predicate is correctly identified and aligned.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions "constrained translation to preserve the predicate"; Section 3.3 describes the LLM prompt; Appendix B shows a failure case of naive translation that constrained translation fixes.
- **Break condition:** If the aligned predicate tokens are incorrect, constrained translation preserves the wrong predicate; LLM may ignore constraint for complex predicates.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Fine-tuning a moderately sized (~7B), language-specific model on the projected dataset yields a specialized QA-SRL parser that substantially outperforms much larger, general-purpose few-shot LLMs on question generation quality.
- **Mechanism:** The projected QA pairs serve as supervision to fine-tune a decoder-only language model via LoRA, adapting it to the QA-SRL task format and linguistic patterns of L.
- **Core assumption:** The projected training data, despite automatic generation, has a high enough signal-to-noise ratio for question generation; the language-specific pre-training provides necessary syntactic/lexical prior.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract reports 10-20 point gains; Section 5.3 & Table 5 show fine-tuned models dominate on Semantic Match; Section 4.3 & Table 4 provide manual evaluation of data quality (Labeled F1 60-73%).
- **Break condition:** If projected data has high error rates in question semantics, fine-tuned model will learn and amplify errors; advantage diminishes if base model lacks monolingual competence or language has divergent phenomena.

  - Why needed here: The paper's methodological innovation is a specific projection pipeline using parallel texts and word alignment to transfer annotations from resource-rich to low-resource languages.

  - Why needed here: The experiments fine-tune base models with LoRA adapters, updating a small subset of parameters. This allows efficient specialization without full fine-tuning.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input Corpus (L sentences with POS) -> Translation Module (Helsinki-NLP L→EN) -> English QA-SRL Parser (T5-XL) -> Word Alignment (SimAlign) -> Predicate Alignment & Span Projection -> Constrained Translation Module (LLM with predicate constraint) -> Projected Dataset (L sentence, L predicate, L question, L answer) -> Target-Language Parser (monolingual 7B LLM + LoRA) -> Evaluation Module (Argument Match, Semantic Match).
- **Critical path:** Sentence in L → Translate to EN → Run English QA-SRL parser → Word alignment (EN↔L) → Predicate/Answer projection → Constrained question translation (EN→L) → Projected QA pair → LoRA fine-tuning → Specialized L parser.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Modularity vs. Error Propagation: Modular design allows independent component improvement but errors cascade (e.g., poor translation corrupts downstream).
  - Resource Requirements vs. Quality: Requires only POS-tagged L text, L→EN MT, and an L LLM, which is low-cost but assumes these resources exist and are adequate.
  - Recall vs. Precision in Data: Projection favors high recall (many QAs) at the cost of labeled precision, a trade-off for training: noisy positives are better than missing valid arguments.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Predicate Drift: Question uses a different L predicate than the source sentence.
  - Alignment Mismatch: Projected answer span is incoherent or includes the predicate.
  - Nominalization Misclassification: Static noun wrongly treated as deverbal predicate.
  - Semantic Match Underperformance: Large gap between Unlabeled and Labeled F1, or fine-tuned model not beating few-shot LLMs on Semantic Match.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Projection Quality Sanity Check: Sample 100 projected QA pairs per language; manually verify question contains predicate, answer span is contiguous and correct, and QA is a valid role.
  2. Component Isolation Test: Run pipeline with gold English QA-SRL output instead of automatic parser to measure ceiling of projection (alignment + constrained translation) quality; compare to full-automatic pipeline to quantify parser's error contribution.
  3. Constrained Translation Ablation: For a sample, run constrained translation without predicate constraint; measure predicate drift rate vs. constrained version to validate necessity of constraint.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Extension to other QA-based frameworks:** Can the projection pipeline be extended to QA-Adj or QADiscourse? The paper leaves this for future work because only verbal and nominal predicates were demonstrated; other frameworks may require adaptations.
2. **Robustness for low-resource languages:** How does the pipeline perform for languages without UD treebanks or high-quality MT? The paper notes language requirements but only evaluates on Hebrew, Russian, French; performance for truly low-resource languages is unknown.
3. **Constrained translation for typologically diverse languages:** Does predicate-preserving constrained translation work well for languages with free word order or rich morphology? Error analysis focused on English parser errors, not systematic study of structural divergences in translation.
4. **Semantic Match threshold calibration:** Is the cosine similarity threshold (≥0.78) equally applicable across languages? It was calibrated on Hebrew but applied uniformly; the paraphrase model's performance might vary, requiring per-language calibration.

## Limitations
- Component opacity: Exact checkpoints for English QA-SRL parser and constrained translation LLM are unspecified, hindering replication.
- Data quality ceiling: Projected Labeled F1 (60–73%) indicates substantial noise in question semantics, risking error propagation during fine-tuning.
- Language scope: Evaluation limited to three European languages; performance for typologically divergent or low-resource languages remains unknown.

## Confidence
- Projection viability: Medium (manual evaluation supports coverage, but divergence risks remain).
- Constrained translation efficacy: High (directly tested in appendix, predicate drift reduced).
- Specialized fine-tuning advantage: High (consistent 10-20 point gains across languages vs. strong few-shot LLMs).

## Next Checks
- Gold parser isolation: Re-run projection using gold English QA-SRL outputs to measure ceiling of alignment + constrained translation quality, quantifying English parser’s error contribution.
- Predicate constraint fidelity: Compute exact-match rate of aligned predicate in constrained back-translated questions vs. source predicates; ablate constraint to measure drift difference.
- Error amplification analysis: Train parsers on oracle-corrected projected questions (only erroneous questions fixed) and compare performance to original projected model to determine if question noise limits fine-tuning gains.
