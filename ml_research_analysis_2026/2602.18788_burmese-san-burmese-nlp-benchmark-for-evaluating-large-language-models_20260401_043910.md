# BURMESE-SAN: Burmese NLP Benchmark for Evaluating Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2602.18788
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18788
*   **Reference Count:** 0
*   **Primary Result:** The first comprehensive Burmese NLP benchmark reveals commercial LLMs (e.g., Gemini 2.5 Pro, 72.35%) significantly outperform the best open-weight models (~54%), with performance tied more to architecture, instruction tuning, and regional fine-tuning than to scale alone.

## Executive Summary
BURMESE-SAN introduces a holistic benchmark for assessing Large Language Models across seven Natural Language Processing tasks—spanning Natural Language Understanding (NLU), Natural Language Generation (NLG), and Natural Language Reasoning (NLR)—for Burmese. The benchmark was constructed through a rigorous, native-speaker-driven curation process from diverse source materials to ensure cultural and linguistic authenticity. Evaluation of over 50 models uncovers a substantial performance gap favoring commercial proprietary models. The study further demonstrates that regional Southeast Asian fine-tuning, particularly for Llama-based models, yields significant gains in tasks like machine translation. The benchmark suite and a public leaderboard are released to catalyze progress in this low-resource language.

## Method Summary
The benchmark comprises seven tasks: Sentiment Analysis (SA), Topic Detection (TD), Causal Reasoning (CR), Question Answering (QA), Abstractive Summarization (AS), Machine Translation (MT), and Natural Language Inference (NLI). Data was sourced from native Burmese materials (SA, TD) and adapted from English datasets (others). A multi-step curation pipeline ensured quality: initial sampling, re-translation by native speakers, binary agreement rating, text normalization, label verification, and native revision. Evaluation employed zero-shot inference using formal, consistent Burmese prompt templates. Model outputs were scored with task-appropriate metrics (e.g., accuracy, MetricX-24, ROUGE-L), all normalized to a 0–100 scale for aggregation.

## Key Results
*   Commercial LLMs achieve a large performance advantage, with Gemini 2.5 Pro reaching 72.35% versus the top open-weight model at approximately 54%.
*   Model capability on Burmese depends more critically on architectural design, instruction tuning quality, and Southeast Asian fine-tuning than on raw parameter count.
*   Regional (Southeast Asian) fine-tuning provides a dramatic boost for Llama-based models, especially in machine translation (+19.29%).
*   Careful quantization (e.g., FP8) largely preserves performance, while aggressive 4-bit quantization (NVFP4) can degrade reasoning capabilities.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Proprietary Advantages for Low-Resource Languages
The claim that commercial LLMs significantly outperform open-weight models on Burmese is attributed to their proprietary training regimens, which likely involve larger and more curated pretraining corpora, specialized architectural optimizations, and targeted instruction tuning for underrepresented languages. Burmese's complex morphology and diglossia make such curated, high-quality data and iterative refinement particularly impactful. The internal evidence from evaluating 50+ models strongly supports this gap. The gap could close if open-weight models achieve parity through architectural innovation (e.g., Mixture of Experts) or access to massive, high-quality Burmese-specific training data.

### Mechanism 2: Primacy of Adaptation Over Scale
Performance on Burmese is argued to depend more on architecture, instruction tuning, and regional fine-tuning than on sheer model scale. The underlying assumption is that scale alone cannot resolve fundamental representational gaps if the model's tokenizer, pretraining data distribution, or tuning procedures do not adequately cover Burmese's tonal, SOV, and honorific features. Evidence includes the non-linear gains with scale and the documented impact of SEA fine-tuning. A pure scale-driven approach (e.g., a massive randomly initialized model) reaching parity would disprove this mechanism.

### Mechanism 3: Cross-Lingual Transfer via Regional Fine-Tuning
Substantial benefits from Southeast Asian regional fine-tuning, particularly for machine translation, are explained by cross-lingual transfer from linguistically related languages. This fine-tuning adapts the model to a "SEA language space" characterized by shared typological features (e.g., analytic structure), creating a better initialization for Burmese than a globally pretrained model. The large, measured MT gain (+19.29%) for Llama-based SEA-LION models provides clear evidence. If SEA fine-tuning yielded zero or negative returns across all base architectures, it would invalidate the assumed transferable benefit.

## Architecture Onboarding

Data Sources -> Curation Pipeline -> Evaluation Engine -> Leaderboard

**Critical Path:** Task Selection -> Native-Annotated Data Curation -> Formal Burmese Prompt Engineering -> Zero-Shot Evaluation -> Normalized Scoring. A failure in native annotation quality or prompt formalization directly compromises benchmark validity.

**Design Tradeoffs:**
*   **Formal vs. Colloquial Prompts:** Using formal written Burmese increases reliability and clarity but lowers ecological validity for informal social media text.
*   **Native-Sourced vs. Adapted Data:** Native-sourced data (SA, TD) ensures authenticity but may be smaller and less consistent; adapted data ensures broad task coverage but risks translation artifacts.
*   **Metric Normalization:** Rescaling diverse metrics (e.g., MetricX [0,25] → [0,100]) enables aggregation but can obscure absolute task difficulty differences.

**Failure Signatures:**
*   **Low Inter-Annotator Agreement:** Agreement below 95% on translation completeness/fluency during curation indicates potential data quality issues.
*   **Task-Specific Collapse:** Models scoring near 0% on Causal Reasoning or NLI suggest fundamental architectural/tuning failures in handling logical relationships in Burmese.
*   **Quantization-Induced Degradation:** A performance drop exceeding 5% on core NLU/NLR tasks after moving from full precision to NVFP4 quantization signals critical information loss.

**First 3 Experiments:**
1.  **Replicate Main Evaluation:** Load the `BURMESE-SAN` test sets and run zero-shot inference on a baseline model (e.g., `Llama-3.1-8B`) using the provided Burmese prompt templates. Verify your aggregate scores match the paper's reported ranges (±1%) to confirm pipeline integrity.
2.  **Prompt Variation Stress Test:** Modify a subset of formal prompt templates to use colloquial Burmese particles (e.g., `ပဲ` instead of formal `၏`). Evaluate this variant on a sample (n=100) per task and measure the performance delta. Expect a >5% drop on models trained on formal data, validating the design choice.
3.  **SEA Fine-Tuning Isolation Test:** Compare a base model (e.g., `Qwen-3-VL-8B`) with its SEA-LION v4 variant. Evaluate both *only* on Machine Translation (MY↔EN) and Causal Reasoning. Quantify the MT gain and check for any CR improvement to isolate the regional tuning effect.

## Limitations
*   **Cultural/Linguistic Authenticity:** The native-driven curation process may still reflect annotator demographics (e.g., urban, formal Burmese) and does not cover significant dialectal variation.
*   **Prompt Engineering Bias:** The exclusive use of formal written Burmese prompts may overstate model capability on informal, real-world Burmese text, a known use case.
*   **External Validity:** With zero references and a recent release, the benchmark has limited independent validation and few directly comparable Burmese benchmarks for cross-checking results.

## Confidence
*   **Commercial > Open-Weight Gap:** **Medium**. Strong internal evidence from 50+ models, but the gap could narrow rapidly with new open-weight SEA-tuned variants.
*   **Regional Fine-Tuning Benefits:** **High**. The specific, large machine translation gain (+19.29%) is precisely measured and architecture interaction is well-documented.
*   **Scale vs. Adaptation Primacy:** **Low**. The paper finds scale insufficient, but "adaptation" is a broad category; a future scale-driven success with improved data/architecture remains a plausible break condition.
*   **Quantization (FP8) Safety:** **Medium**. Task-dependent degradation (e.g., NVFP4 harming reasoning) is clear but tested on a limited set of model families.

## Next Checks
1.  **Cross-Benchmark Concordance:** Evaluate the top 5 BURMESE-SAN models on an independent Burmese NLP dataset (e.g., myPOS for part-of-speech tagging). A high correlation (ρ > 0.8) in rankings would confirm benchmark robustness.
2.  **Dialectal Stress Test:** Have a native speaker manually rephrase a 100-sample subset of BURMESE-SAN prompts into a major colloquial dialect (e.g., Mandalay variant). Measure the average performance drop; a >10% decrease on core NLU tasks would quantify the formal-bias risk.
3.  **SEA Transfer Ablation:** Fine-tune a non-Llama model (e.g., `Qwen-3-VL-8B`) on a matched SEA multilingual corpus (Thai, Vietnamese, Indonesian) **excluding Burmese**. If the resulting machine translation gain is significantly lower than for Llama-based models (<5%), it confirms the claimed architecture-dependent transfer mechanism.
