# WearVox: An Egocentric Multichannel Voice Assistant Benchmark for Wearables

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.02391
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.02391
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Multi-channel egocentric audio benchmark reveals poor baseline SLLM performance (29-59%) and demonstrates that custom multi-channel models improve noise robustness and side-talk rejection by up to 8.3 percentage points.

## Executive Summary
WearVox introduces the first benchmark for evaluating voice assistants on wearables, comprising 3,842 multi-channel egocentric audio recordings across five tasks (Search-Grounded QA, Closed-Book QA, Side-Talk Rejection, Tool Calling, Speech Translation) in diverse indoor/outdoor environments. Benchmarking existing speech-enabled large language models (SLLMs) yields low accuracies (29-59%) with substantial performance drops in noisy outdoor settings. A case study with custom SLLMs shows that multi-channel audio inputs significantly enhance noise robustness and side-talk rejection, achieving an 8.3 percentage point increase in side-talk rejection accuracy and a 4.5 point improvement in overall turn-based accuracy. This work highlights the need for robust multi-channel processing in wearable voice assistants and provides a standardized testbed for future research.

## Method Summary
The paper provides limited explicit details on reproduction. It describes the WearVox dataset collection: 3,842 multi-channel egocentric recordings captured in real-world indoor and outdoor environments, covering five distinct voice assistant tasks. Evaluation uses task-specific metrics and turn-based accuracy. The benchmark is publicly released to facilitate reproducible research.

## Key Results
- WearVox benchmark released with 3,842 multi-channel recordings across five tasks in varied environments.
- Existing SLLMs achieve only 29-59% accuracy, with notable degradation in outdoor noisy conditions.
- Custom SLLMs leveraging multi-channel audio improve side-talk rejection by 8.3 percentage points (85.6% → 93.9%) and overall accuracy by 4.5 points (61.9% → 66.4%).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Multi-channel audio leverages spatial diversity from microphone arrays to isolate the wearer's speech from ambient noise and other speakers. Beamforming and speech separation techniques applied to the multiple channels enhance the target signal, as evidenced by the reported gains in noise robustness and side-talk rejection. The benchmark results confirm that spatial filtering is crucial for wearable voice assistants operating in realistic, cluttered acoustic scenes.

## Foundational Learning
- **Multi-channel audio processing**  
  *Why needed*: Wearables have spatially distributed microphones; combining signals improves directional sensitivity and noise suppression.  
  *Quick check*: Measure beamforming gain by computing signal-to-noise ratio improvement on a synthetic mixture.
- **Speech separation and diarization**  
  *Why needed*: In social settings, side-talk must be filtered out; separation isolates the user's voice.  
  *Quick check*: Evaluate separation quality on mixtures with known sources using SDR improvement.
- **Egocentric audio sensing**  
  *Why needed*: Body-borne microphones produce a distinct acoustic perspective that affects channel responses.  
  *Quick check*: Assess recording consistency across different wearable placements (e.g., ear, wrist).
- **Speech-enabled large language models (SLLMs)**  
  *Why needed*: Voice assistants require both transcription and reasoning; SLLMs integrate these steps to reduce error propagation.  
  *Quick check*: Test model on diverse spoken queries and measure semantic accuracy against text-based references.
- **Benchmark design for wearables**  
  *Why needed*: Progress requires standardized evaluation under realistic conditions.  
  *Quick check*: Verify dataset diversity across environments, tasks, and noise conditions.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Multi-channel microphone array -> Beamforming module -> Feature extractor (e.g., mel-spectrogram) -> Speech encoder (e.g., wav2vec 2.0) -> Language model (e.g., LLaMA) -> Response generator.

**Critical path**: Beamforming and speech encoder stages dominate latency due to real-time signal processing; language model inference adds further delay, especially for large models.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Beamforming complexity vs. noise reduction: Adaptive beamformers (e.g., MVDR) offer better suppression but increase compute and latency.
- Model size vs. accuracy/power: Larger SLLMs improve performance but may exceed wearable compute budgets, possibly requiring cloud offloading.
- On-device vs. cloud: On-device ensures privacy and low latency; cloud provides more resources but introduces network dependency and privacy risks.

**Failure signatures**:
- Severe performance drop in high ambient noise or wind, indicating insufficient beamforming.
- Confusion when multiple speakers talk simultaneously, reflecting poor side-talk rejection.
- High response latency leading to user frustration.
- Overfitting to indoor conditions causing poor outdoor generalization.

**First three experiments**:
1. **Single-channel vs. multi-channel comparison**: Apply the same SLLM to beamformed multi-channel inputs and raw single-channel inputs on the WearVox test set; measure accuracy delta.
2. **Beamforming algorithm ablation**: Replace the default beamformer with alternatives (e.g., MVDR, GCC-PHAT) and evaluate their impact on noise robustness and side-talk rejection.
3. **Model scaling study**: Train SLLMs of varying sizes (e.g., 1B, 7B, 13B parameters) on the WearVox training data and analyze the accuracy-latency tradeoff.

## Limitations
- **Real-world generalizability**: The 3,842 recordings may not capture the full spectrum of wearable hardware, speaker accents, or extreme noise (e.g., high wind, machinery).
- **Task scope**: Only five tasks are included, potentially missing many voice assistant interactions; the definition of Side-Talk Rejection requires scrutiny.
- **Baseline evaluation**: Low SLLM accuracies are reported without details on tuning; the multi-channel benefit claim relies on a single custom model, limiting causal inference strength.

## Confidence
- Benchmark creation & dataset release: **High Confidence**.
- Poor baseline SLLM performance on wearable data: **Medium Confidence**.
- Multi-channel audio improves noise robustness & side-talk rejection: **Low Confidence**.

## Next Checks
1. **Replication with heterogeneous hardware**: Re-run the benchmark on at least three distinct commercial wearable devices (e.g., different earphones, smartwatches) to quantify hardware-induced performance variance.
2. **Cross-model validation of multi-channel benefit**: Evaluate the performance gain of multi-channel processing on at least two other publicly available SLLMs (e.g., Whisper, SpeechGPT) using the WearVox test set, controlling for model size and training data.
3. **Noise-type ablation study**: Segment outdoor recordings by specific noise sources (traffic, wind, crowd) and measure the performance delta between single-channel and multi-channel inputs for each category.
