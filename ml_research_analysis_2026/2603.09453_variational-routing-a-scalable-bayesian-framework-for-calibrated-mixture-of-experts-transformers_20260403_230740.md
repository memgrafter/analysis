# Variational Routing: A Scalable Bayesian Framework for Calibrated Mixture-of-Experts Transformers

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.09453
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09453
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** VMoER improves routing stability under noise by 38%, reduces calibration error by up to 94%, and increases out-of-distribution AUROC by 12%, while incurring less than 1% additional FLOPs.

## Executive Summary
Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) layers enable scaling of transformer models but suffer from deterministic routing brittleness and poor uncertainty calibration. This paper introduces Variational Mixture-of-Experts Routing (VMoER), a Bayesian framework that confines inference to the expert-selection stage via two complementary methods: (1) Logit-Space Variational Inference (VGLR) with full covariance modeling, and (2) Selection-Space Variational Inference (VTSR) learning input-dependent temperature. Applied to Granite-MoE, Qwen-MoE, and DeepSeek-MoE, VMoER yields substantial gains in routing stability, calibration, and out-of-distribution detection with negligible computational overhead, demonstrating that scalable Bayesian uncertainty is viable in large foundation models.

## Method Summary
VMoER employs a two-stage training procedure. First, a LoRA fine-tuning stage adapts the experts and attention modules on in-distribution data. Second, the backbone is frozen and a lightweight variational head is added to each targeted MoE router; only these heads are trained via amortized variational inference. VGLR treats routing logits as stochastic latent variables with an approximate posterior that predicts a residual mean and full covariance (Cholesky) relative to frozen deterministic logits, while VTSR learns a per-token temperature to scale the logits before sampling. Both methods use KL regularization against simple priors (Gaussian for VGLR, uniform categorical for VTSR) and maintain the original router weights. The Bayesian inference is restricted to a small subset of the most brittle MoE layers (typically 10), identified via a sensitivity analysis that measures expert assignment Jaccard similarity under input noise. This confinement limits additional FLOPs to less than 1%, making the approach practical for large models.

## Key Results
- Routing stability (Jaccard similarity under noise) improved by 38% on average.
- Calibration error (Expected Calibration Error) reduced by up to 94%.
- Out-of-distribution detection AUROC increased by 12%.
- Additional computational overhead remains below 1% FLOPs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Logit-Space Variational Inference (VGLR) captures expert correlations.
VGLR models the routing logits *l* as stochastic latent variables with an amortized approximate posterior *q<sub>ϕ</sub>(l|u)* parameterized by an inference network. The network predicts a residual mean Δµ and a Cholesky factor *L* (yielding full covariance Σ = LLᵀ) added to the frozen deterministic logits. A Gaussian prior centered on the deterministic logits regularizes the posterior. At inference, multiple logit samples are drawn and their softmax outputs averaged. This captures dependencies among expert suitabilities, improving calibration and robustness. Break condition: the O(N²) covariance cost becomes significant for very large expert counts *N*; also, if expert correlations are negligible, full covariance adds unnecessary complexity and sampling variance.

### Mechanism 2: Selection-Space Variational Inference (VTSR) learns input-dependent temperature.
VTSR defines a variational posterior over selection probabilities as Softmax(l<sub>det</sub>/T<sub>ϕ</sub>(u)) with a uniform categorical prior. The temperature T<sub>ϕ</sub>(u) > 0 is predicted by a small network and regularized by −log(T) (maximizing entropy). Training uses Gumbel-Softmax for differentiability; inference uses exact sampling. This allows the model to adjust routing sharpness dynamically per token, handling ambiguous inputs better than a fixed global temperature. Break condition: temperature collapse (T→0) can occur if initialization or regularization is poor; limited network capacity may also prevent meaningful input-dependent variation.

### Mechanism 3: Confinement of Bayesian inference to the router’s decisions enables scalability.
By limiting Bayesian inference to the expert-selection stage—a low-dimensional component—VMoER avoids the prohibitive cost of posterior inference over massive expert parameters. The base router weights remain frozen; only lightweight inference heads are trained. This decoupling means overhead grows with number of routers modified (e.g., 10 layers) rather than with model size, yielding <1% FLOPs increase. Assumption: dominant uncertainty affecting calibration and OoD detection resides in expert selection, not weight values. Break condition: if epistemic uncertainty is primarily weight-driven (e.g., due to dataset shift affecting expert computations), routing-only uncertainty may be insufficient; also, if the base router is already highly stochastic, marginal gains may be small.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Base MoE router (frozen) -> Variational head (trainable, VGLR: mean + covariance; VTSR: temperature) -> Sampling layer (reparameterization for VGLR; Gumbel-Softmax or exact scaling for VTSR) -> (VGLR only: averaging of multiple softmax samples) -> Expert selection (Top-K or Sample-K).

**Critical path (training):**  
1. Compute frozen deterministic logits from the base router.  
2. Forward through the variational head to obtain posterior parameters.  
3. Sample stochastic logits (VGLR) or scale logits (VTSR).  
4. Select experts (Top-K or Sample-K).  
5. Compute task loss (e.g., cross-entropy) on model output.  
6. Compute KL divergence between variational posterior and prior.  
7. Backpropagate loss to update only the variational head parameters (ϕ) using AdamW, with KL weight β.  
During inference, VGLR draws S=35 samples and averages softmax outputs; VTSR uses single-sample with predicted temperature.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- VGLR-FC (full covariance) captures expert correlations at O(N²) cost; VGLR-MF (mean-field) is cheaper but may miss dependencies.  
- VTSR is extremely lightweight (no MC sampling) but can be unstable (temperature collapse).  
- Applying VMoER to all layers maximizes benefit but increases overhead; the paper finds modifying only the 10 most brittle layers offers the best trade-off.

**Failure signatures:**  
- KL divergence dominates loss (posterior collapse) if β is too high or inference network too weak.  
- VTSR temperature approaches zero (collapse) without careful initialization or if −log(T) regularization is overwhelmed.  
- Poor calibration if MC samples S are too few (paper uses S=35).  
- Accuracy degradation if applied to early layers; a warm‑up period is recommended.

**First three experiments:**  
1. **Motivational replication:** Inject Gaussian noise into input embeddings, measure Jaccard similarity of expert selections to confirm brittleness; also test fixed-temperature sampling to identify layers where stochasticity helps.  
2. **Single‑layer sanity check:** Implement VGLR-MF on one MoE layer of a small model (e.g., Granite‑3B) on a toy dataset; verify KL loss decreases and ECE improves on validation.  
3. **Layer ablation:** Vary the number of modified layers L (e.g., 1,2,4,8,10,12,15) and placement strategy (first/middle/last vs. susceptible) to locate optimal configuration before full-scale training. Monitor ECE and AUROC to ensure diminishing returns beyond L=10.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How can VTSR's training instability (temperature collapse) be prevented without manual tuning? The paper acknowledges this limitation but offers no solution within the method; careful initialization is a workaround.
- **Open Question 2:** Does VMoER's <1% FLOP overhead claim hold when scaling to 70B+ parameter MoE models? Efficiency analysis only demonstrates overhead on Granite-3B-MoE; characteristics may change with larger expert counts and depths.
- **Open Question 3:** Can VMoER's uncertainty quantification improve sequence-level (not just token-level) calibration in generative tasks? Evaluation was restricted to MCQA; sequence generation involves sequential dependencies that could invalidate token-level assumptions.
- **Open Question 4:** Is modifying only the 10 most brittle layers optimal, or would different layer selection yield better performance-overhead trade-offs? The strategy is presented as practical, but its necessity relative to other choices remains unclear.
- **Open Question 5:** Do VMoER's internal uncertainty signals (e.g., Inf-Logit-Var) truly capture epistemic uncertainty, or are they conflated with aleatoric/noise effects? Without disentanglement experiments, the claim remains an assumption.

## Limitations
- Exact indices of the 10 most sensitive MoE layers are not provided; optimal placement is ambiguous.
- Hyperparameter settings (learning rate, β, batch size) for Qwen-MoE and DeepSeek-MoE are unreported; only Granite grid search is detailed.
- VTSR training instability (temperature collapse) is acknowledged but not systematically addressed.
