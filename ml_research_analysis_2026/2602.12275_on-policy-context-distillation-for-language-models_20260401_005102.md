# On-Policy Context Distillation for Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2602.12275  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12275  
- **Reference count**: 24  
- **Primary result**: OPCD improves task accuracy (e.g., math from 78.5% to 79.7%, Sokoban from 51.6% to 53.9%) and better preserves out-of-distribution performance compared to off-policy context distillation.

## Executive Summary
Existing context distillation relies on off-policy forward KL minimization, which suffers from exposure bias and mode-covering hallucinations. On-Policy Context Distillation (OPCD) addresses these issues by having the student generate its own trajectories without the target context and training with reverse KL divergence against a context‑conditioned teacher. This on‑policy setup aligns the student’s training distribution with its inference distribution, reducing train‑test mismatch. Across experiential knowledge distillation (math, text games) and system prompt distillation (medical, safety), OPCD yields consistent accuracy gains (≈1–2% absolute) and superior out‑of‑distribution (OOD) generalization (IF‑Eval ~2% higher). Moreover, OPCD enables effective cross‑size distillation—smaller students trained with OPCD outperform those simply given teacher‑generated context—and mitigates catastrophic forgetting relative to off‑policy methods.

## Method Summary
OPCD modifies standard context distillation in two key ways. First, the student model generates full response trajectories autoregressively without access to the expert context \(c\); these on‑policy samples reflect the student’s current behavior. Second, the loss uses reverse KL divergence: for each token in the student’s trajectory, the teacher (which receives the context \(c\) together with the partial trajectory) provides a token distribution, and the student’s distribution is penalized with \(D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\text{teacher}})\). This encourages the student to match the teacher’s high‑confidence modes along its own generated paths, rather than covering the full teacher distribution. The teacher remains frozen to provide a stable supervision signal. To reduce computational cost, the KL sum is approximated using only the student’s top‑k tokens. Training proceeds by repeatedly sampling context‑query pairs, generating student trajectories, evaluating the teacher, computing the reverse KL loss, and updating the student with a small learning rate (e.g., 1e–6 to 5e–6).

## Key Results
- OPCD yields modest but consistent accuracy improvements on experiential tasks: math accuracy rises from 78.5% (off‑policy) to 79.7%; Sokoban success rate improves from 51.6% to 53.9%.
- OOD performance is better preserved: IF‑Eval scores are ~2% higher than off-policy baselines, and catastrophic forgetting is reduced in system‑prompt distillation (medical/safety domains).
- Cross‑size distillation is enabled: smaller students (e.g., Qwen3 1.7B, 4B) trained via OPCD from an 8B teacher outperform models that simply receive the teacher’s context at inference time.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Reducing exposure bias via on‑policy reverse KL
- **Claim**: On‑policy sampling combined with reverse KL divergence reduces exposure bias and mitigates mode‑covering hallucinations.
- **Mechanism**: The student generates its own token trajectory without the context \(c\). At each step, the student’s token distribution is compared to the teacher’s distribution (which conditions on \(c\)) using reverse KL. This forces the student to focus on high‑probability teacher tokens along its actual generation path, rather than trying to cover all possibilities present in a fixed teacher dataset.
- **Core assumption**: The student’s self‑generated trajectories, though initially weaker, provide a training distribution closer to its inference‑time distribution, reducing train‑test mismatch.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract and Section 3 (Eq. 1, Alg. 1) explicitly describe student sampling \(y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)\) and reverse KL computation. Related work on self‑distilled reasoners and entropy‑aware on‑policy distillation supports the approach.
- **Break condition**: If the student’s trajectories diverge catastrophically from the teacher’s distribution early in training, the reverse KL signal may become uninformative or destabilizing, especially without a frozen teacher.

### Mechanism 2: Preserving out‑of‑distribution capabilities
- **Claim**: On‑policy context distillation better preserves OOD capabilities compared to off‑policy forward KL methods.
- **Mechanism**: Because the student learns from its own policy samples, it encounters a more diverse and self‑consistent token distribution. This prevents overfitting to the teacher’s narrow distribution in the fixed dataset, which can erode general abilities not represented in the context \(c\).
- **Core assumption**: The student’s own samples retain a broader base of distributional knowledge; aligning only on high‑likelihood teacher regions (via reverse KL) avoids forcing the student to assign probability mass to irrelevant low‑likelihood teacher tokens.
- **Evidence anchors**: Section 4.5 (Figure 3) shows OPCD retains medical performance when distilling on safety data; Section 4.3 (Tables 1–4) reports higher IF‑Eval scores for OPCD versus off‑policy distillation.
- **Break condition**: If the context \(c\) is extremely narrow or misaligned with OOD tasks, even on‑policy refinement may overspecialize; the mechanism assumes a sufficiently diverse pre‑training base.

### Mechanism 3: Effective cross‑size knowledge transfer
- **Claim**: Teacher‑student distillation (frozen teacher) enables effective cross‑size knowledge transfer, where smaller students internalize knowledge from larger teachers better than direct in‑context injection.
- **Mechanism**: The frozen teacher provides a stable, high‑capacity distribution conditioned on \(c\). The smaller student, via on‑policy reverse KL, learns to match the teacher’s high‑confidence regions on its own generated paths, effectively distilling the teacher’s context‑aware behavior into its smaller parameter space.
- **Core assumption**: The student’s capacity is sufficient to represent the teacher’s relevant mode‑seeking distribution; the teacher’s context \(c\) encodes knowledge compressible into the student’s weights.
- **Evidence anchors**: Section 4.4 (Figure 2) shows smaller Qwen3 models improve when distilling from an 8B teacher, while direct context injection harms performance. Section 4.6 (Table 5) shows teacher‑student OPCD outperforms self‑distillation.
- **Break condition**: If the capacity gap is too large (student too small to capture teacher’s distribution even in modes), the student may fail to match the teacher’s behavior, leading to collapse.

## Foundational Learning
- **Autoregressive generation & on‑policy sampling**  
  *Why needed*: OPCD requires the student to generate complete trajectories \(y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)\) without context \(c\). Understanding token‑by‑token sampling is essential to grasp the on‑policy rollout mechanism.  
  *Quick check*: What is the difference between sampling from the *current* student policy versus using a fixed dataset of teacher‑generated sequences?

- **Kullback‑Leibler divergence (forward vs. reverse)**  
  *Why needed*: The core innovation is switching from forward KL (off‑policy) to reverse KL (on‑policy) to change optimization behavior from mode‑covering to mode‑seeking.  
  *Quick check*: How does minimizing \(D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\text{teacher}})\) (reverse KL) differ from minimizing \(D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\text{teacher}} \| \pi_\theta)\) (forward KL) when \(\pi_\theta\) is being optimized?

- **In‑context learning (ICL) and context distillation**  
  *Why needed*: The paper aims to internalize transient in‑context knowledge \(c\) that would otherwise be lost. ICL as a non‑parametric, prompt‑based adaptation is prerequisite.  
  *Quick check*: What problem does context distillation solve that standard fine‑tuning does not, in terms of behavior preservation?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map** (plain text flow):  
Dataset \((x,c)\) → Student model \(\pi_\theta\) generates \(y\) (stored temporarily in a rollout buffer) → Teacher model \(\pi_{\text{teacher}}\) processes concatenated \([c; x; y]\) to produce token‑wise distributions → Loss calculator computes average reverse KL over \(y\) (using top‑k approximation) → Optimizer updates \(\theta\).

**Critical path**:
1. Sample batch of \((x,c)\) from dataset.
2. Student autoregressively generates \(y\) without \(c\).
3. Teacher receives \([c; x; y]\) and yields \(\pi_{\text{teacher}}(\cdot \mid c,x,y_{<t})\) for each position \(t\).
4. Compute token‑level reverse KL for each \(y_t\).
5. Average over sequence length to obtain final loss.
6. Back‑propagate and update the student parameters \(\theta\).

**Design tradeoffs**:
- *Teacher freezing vs. updating*: Frozen teacher gives stable supervision but cannot improve; updating risks instability.
- *Top‑k approximation*: Restricts KL sum to student’s top‑k tokens for efficiency but may omit important teacher modes.
- *On‑policy batch size*: Larger batches reduce variance but increase memory for trajectory storage.

**Failure signatures**:
- Training loss spikes or becomes NaN, indicating student divergence from teacher modes.
- Validation accuracy plateaus or drops while OOD performance degrades rapidly (overspecialization).
- Generated outputs become repetitive, short, or degenerate relative to the base model.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Math experiential distillation (DAPO‑Math‑17K)** – validates core claim; expect ~1–2% accuracy gain over off‑policy baseline.
2. **Text game distillation (Frozen Lake/Sokoban)** – tests sequential decision‑making; monitor if student learns to avoid holes/push boxes correctly.
3. **System prompt distillation (Medical/Safety)** – checks internalization of prompt‑based behaviors; compare against in‑context baselines and off‑policy distillation.

## Limitations
- **Teacher dependence**: Requires a frozen context‑aware teacher; performance may degrade if the teacher’s context distribution is narrow or misaligned.
- **Top‑k approximation bias**: Gradients based only on the student’s top‑k tokens might miss important teacher modes, limiting fidelity.
- **Capacity gap risk**: Extreme size differences between teacher and student can prevent the smaller model from accurately matching the teacher’s distribution, leading to collapse.

## Confidence
- **Exposure bias reduction**: High  
- **Out‑of‑distribution preservation**: Medium  
- **Cross‑size transfer**: Medium  
- **Overall accuracy gains**: High  

## Next Checks
- **Scale stress test**: Systematically vary teacher‑student size ratios (e.g., 70B→1B, 70B→8B) to map effective distillation limits and failure modes.
- **Policy tracking**: During training, compute forward KL from student to teacher on held‑out prompts; correlate with final accuracy to validate the mode‑seeking hypothesis.
- **Broad OOD sweep**: Evaluate distilled students on a diverse benchmark suite (e.g., MMLU, coding, multilingual) to ensure OOD preservation is not task‑specific.
