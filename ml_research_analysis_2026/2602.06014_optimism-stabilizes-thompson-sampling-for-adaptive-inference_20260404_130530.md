# Optimism Stabilizes Thompson Sampling for Adaptive Inference

## Quick Facts
- ArXiv ID: 2602.06014
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06014
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Injecting optimism (via variance inflation or mean bonus) into Gaussian Thompson sampling stabilizes arm-pull counts and enables valid asymptotic inference with only a mild regret overhead.

## Executive Summary
Thompson sampling (TS) is a widely used algorithm for adaptive decision-making, but under adaptive sampling it can suffer from unstable arm-pull counts and invalid statistical inference. This paper shows that adding optimism—through either variance inflation or a mean bonus—to Gaussian TS restores stability for any multi-armed bandit with at least two arms, even when multiple arms are optimal. The authors prove that under these modifications, arm-pull counts converge almost surely to deterministic target values. This stability in turn implies asymptotic normality of studentized sample means, allowing for valid Wald confidence intervals. The regret cost is mild: only an extra multiplicative factor that can be as low as log log T. The results highlight optimism as a unifying principle for stabilizing TS and enabling reliable inference.

## Method Summary
Two modifications to Gaussian Thompson sampling are proposed to inject optimism. The first inflates the posterior variance by a factor σ(A) > 1, making sampled indices more dispersed. The second adds a time- and count-dependent bonus to the posterior mean: bₐ,ₜ = √(2β(A) log T / Nₐ,ₜ). Both ensure that Thompson-sampled indices remain optimistic with sufficient probability, counteracting the negative feedback that causes instability. The algorithms otherwise follow standard Bayesian updates for Gaussian rewards and maintain pull counts.

## Key Results
- **Stability**: For both modifications, arm-pull counts satisfy Nₐ,ₜ / Nₐ,ₜ* → 1 almost surely, where Nₐ,ₜ* is the deterministic target (T/m for optimal arms, (2c log T)/Δₐ² for suboptimal arms, with c = σ(A) or β(A)).
- **Valid inference**: Stability implies asymptotic normality of studentized sample means, enabling Wald confidence intervals with correct coverage.
- **Mild regret overhead**: The optimism adds at most a multiplicative factor c to regret; c can be as low as log log T.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Standard TS suffers from a negative feedback loop: arms that appear better get pulled more often, but their empirical means then regress toward the true means, causing them to be pulled less, leading to oscillations that prevent pull counts from settling. Optimism—via inflated variance or a mean bonus—introduces a bias that decays slowly (∼1/√Nₐ,ₜ). This bias ensures under-sampled arms retain a higher selection probability, thus maintaining steady pull rates. Mathematically, with optimism parameters growing appropriately (e.g., σ(A)/log log T → ∞), martingale and concentration arguments show that Nₐ,ₜ/Nₐ,ₜ* → 1 almost surely. Once pull counts are stable, the sample means behave like those from a fixed allocation, and classical limit theorems give asymptotic normality, which justifies Wald intervals.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
GaussianPosterior -> OptimismInjection (VarianceInflation / MeanBonus) -> ThompsonSample -> ArmPull -> PosteriorUpdate & CountUpdate -> StabilityMonitor -> Inference

**Critical path:**  
1. Maintain separate Gaussian posterior (mean, variance) for each arm.  
2. At each time t, compute an optimistic index: either sample from N(μₐ,ₜ, σ²ₐ,ₜ·σ(A)²) or from N(μₐ,ₜ + bₐ,ₜ, σ²ₐ,ₜ) with bₐ,ₜ = √(2β(A) log T / Nₐ,ₜ).  
3. Pull the arm with the highest index and observe its reward.  
4. Update the posterior parameters (Bayesian update for Gaussian) and increment the pull count Nₐ,ₜ.  
5. Once stability (Nₐ,ₜ/Nₐ,ₜ* → 1) is established, compute studentized sample means to form Wald confidence intervals.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Variance inflation* uses a constant σ(A) > 1, while the *mean bonus* adapts to pull counts via √(log T / Nₐ,ₜ). Both require choosing a growth rate (σ(A) or β(A)) that grows slowly enough to keep regret mild but fast enough to ensure stability. The mean bonus automatically vanishes as Nₐ,ₜ increases, potentially offering a more refined optimism.
- Implementation: variance inflation is simpler (just scale variance), whereas mean bonus requires per-arm bonus computation each round.
- Regret overhead: both introduce a multiplicative factor c (σ(A) or β(A)); the paper shows c can be as low as log log T if parameters grow appropriately.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Pull counts for arms deviate significantly from their target proportions (e.g., optimal arms not pulled ∼ T/m times).  
- Cumulative regret grows super-logarithmically, indicating optimism parameters are too large or instability persists.  
- Wald intervals have coverage far below the nominal level, violating asymptotic normality assumptions (e.g., due to non-Gaussian rewards or insufficient T).

**First experiments (3):**  
1. **Convergence of pull counts**: Run both variants on a K-armed Gaussian bandit (K≥2) with multiple optimal arms; plot Nₐ,ₜ/Nₐ,ₜ* over time; verify convergence to 1 for optimal and suboptimal arms.  
2. **Wald interval coverage**: For a fixed horizon T, compute 95% Wald intervals for each arm’s mean; repeat over many simulations; measure coverage probability; it should approach 95% as T increases.  
3. **Regret overhead comparison**: Compare cumulative regret of the stabilized TS variants against standard TS and UCB; compute the ratio of regrets; confirm only mild multiplicative overhead that diminishes with T.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: Can optimistic Thompson sampling stability be extended to Markov decision processes?
- Basis: [explicit] The conclusion explicitly states "extending our results beyond multi-armed bandits to more general settings such as Markov decision processes" as an open direction.
- Why: MDPs involve state transitions and correlated observations; the stability concept must adapt to state-action visitation counts and the interaction between exploration and temporal credit assignment is complex.
- Evidence: A proof that an optimistic TS variant (e.g., variance inflation or mean bonus in posterior sampling for RL) satisfies a stability condition (e.g., visitation counts concentrate around deterministic sequences), thereby enabling asymptotically valid inference for RL quantities.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Are the growth conditions on optimism parameters (σ(A) ≫ log log T, β(A) → ∞, etc.) necessary?
- Basis: [inferred] The analysis imposes σ(A)/log log T → ∞ and σ(A)(log T)²/T → 0 (Eq 3.2), and β(A) → ∞ with (β(A) log T)/T → 0. These ensure stability but the paper does not discuss minimality.
- Why: If parameters grow too slowly, the algorithm might become unstable; however, no lower bounds or impossibility results are known.
- Evidence: Either a lower bound showing instability when σ(A) = o(log log T) (or similarly for β(A)), or a refined analysis establishing stability with weaker parameter growth, thereby demonstrating the current conditions are not tight.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can stability be achieved without prior knowledge of the horizon T?
- Basis: [inferred] Both variants explicitly use T in the bonus B_{a,t} = √(2β(A) log T / N_{a,t}) and in the variance inflation constraints. A horizon‑agnostic algorithm is not considered.
- Why: In practice T is often unknown; designing an anytime‑valid stable TS would increase applicability.
- Evidence: Replace log T with a time‑dependent quantity (e.g., log t) or a data‑dependent confidence sequence, and prove that the resulting pull counts N_{a,t} still concentrate around deterministic sequences (involving log t) while maintaining asymptotic normality.

### Open Question 4
- Question: Do stability results extend beyond Gaussian rewards to heavy‑tailed or bounded distributions?
- Basis: [inferred] The entire analysis relies on Gaussian assumptions (explicit posterior and Gaussian tail bounds). For other reward families, the tail behavior of the TS indices may differ.
- Why: Non‑Gaussian noise changes concentration properties; the negative‑feedback mechanism that yields uniformity might still hold but requires different tools.
- Evidence: Generalize the analysis to sub‑Gaussian or sub‑exponential bandits, using appropriate posterior concentration and bonus design, and prove the same stability property (e.g., N_{a,T}/N_{a,T}* → 1) holds.

## Limitations
- Stability proofs assume Gaussian rewards; extension to general bandits (e.g., Bernoulli) is not justified.
- Results are asymptotic (T → ∞); finite‑sample convergence rates, required sample sizes, and practical stability thresholds are missing.
- The optimism parameters (σ(A)>1, β(A)) need selection; no guidance is provided, risking mis‑specification and potential instability if chosen poorly.
