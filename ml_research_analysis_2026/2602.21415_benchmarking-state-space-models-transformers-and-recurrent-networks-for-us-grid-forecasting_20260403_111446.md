# Benchmarking State Space Models, Transformers, and Recurrent Networks for US Grid Forecasting

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.21415
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21415
- Reference count: 39
- Primary result: No universal winner; PatchTST leads in load-only scenarios, iTransformer gains most from weather features; task-specific strengths (PatchTST for solar, SSMs for wind/price).

## Executive Summary
This paper benchmarks five deep learning architectures—two state space models (PowerMamba, S-Mamba), two Transformers (iTransformer, PatchTST), and an LSTM—for hourly load forecasting across six U.S. independent system operators. With a controlled experimental setup featuring temporal embeddings and modular weather fusion, the study finds that model performance is highly contingent on data availability. Using only historical load, PatchTST and SSMs achieve the lowest errors. Adding weather covariates flips the ranking, with iTransformer improving average MAPE by 1.62 percentage points—three times more than PatchTST—an effect traced to architectural design rather than parameter count. The results also reveal task-specific patterns: PatchTST excels on rhythmic solar forecasts, while SSMs handle chaotic wind and price signals better. The benchmark provides actionable guidance for operators: use PatchTST or SSMs for load-only tasks, and switch to iTransformer or weather-aware SSMs when meteorological inputs are available.

## Method Summary
The authors conduct a rigorous comparison by training each architecture on identical preprocessed data from six ISOs (CAISO, ISO-NE, MISO, PJM, ERCOT, NYISO). Inputs include hourly load, optional weather covariates (temperature, humidity, etc.) aligned with thermal lags, and learnable temporal embeddings for hour-of-day and day-of-week. All models share a common input layer and output head but differ in core encoder and strategy for integrating weather: iTransformer tokenizes each variable for cross-variate attention; PatchTST uses channel-independent patching with interleaved cross-attention; SSMs employ early summation or pre-decomposition; LSTM concatenates weather early. Training uses AdamW with OneCycleLR, a fixed lookback of 240 hours, and forecast horizons from 24 to 168 hours. Evaluation metrics include normalized MSE, MAPE, and tail error percentiles, with results aggregated across grids and horizons. Capacity-controlled experiments hold the hidden dimension constant to isolate architectural effects.

## Key Results
- No single model dominates all scenarios; rankings depend on whether weather features are included.
- With load-only inputs, PatchTST and SSMs achieve the lowest errors, with PatchTST leading in 15 of 30 grid-horizon evaluations.
- Adding weather features benefits iTransformer the most (average MAPE reduction of 1.62 percentage points), three times greater than PatchTST’s gain, due to its cross-variate attention design.
- Task-specific trends emerge: PatchTST excels on solar and ancillary services (highly rhythmic), while SSMs outperform on wind and price (more chaotic).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Architectural Determination of Weather Benefit
*Claim*: The magnitude of improvement from adding weather covariates is architecturally determined, not solely a function of parameter count.
*Mechanism*: Architectures with explicit cross-variate interaction (iTransformer’s tokenization) directly model time-varying correlations between load and weather within each step. Channel-independent models (PatchTST, SSMs) rely on implicit mixing, providing a less direct pathway.
*Core assumption*: Weather’s primary value lies in explaining variance at specific hours through contemporaneous relationships, requiring cross-variable mixing at the same time step.
*Evidence anchors*: [section V-C] iTransformer’s cross-variate attention achieves 3× greater weather benefit than PatchTST’s channel-independent design (−1.62 vs. −0.52 percentage points average ΔMAPE); capacity control confirms the advantage stems from architecture.

### Mechanism 2: PatchTST’s Strength in Rhythmic Signals
*Claim*: PatchTST excels on grid load and solar because fixed-length patching suits signals with strong, regular multi-periodicities.
*Mechanism*: Patching compresses a long series into a shorter sequence of patch embeddings, each representing a fixed window (e.g., 16 hours). This acts like a wavelet decomposition, efficiently encoding repeating patterns (e.g., solar’s bell curve) into a small number of tokens for the Transformer.
*Core assumption*: The signal’s local structure within the patch length is stationary and repetitive; the patch length aligns with dominant seasonality (e.g., 24 hours).
*Evidence anchors*: [abstract] PatchTST excels on highly rhythmic solar; [section VI-B] patches align with grids dominated by strong diurnal patterns; [section V-D] PatchTST dominates solar (nMAE 23.1) and ancillary services (79.3).

### Mechanism 3: SSMs for Aperiodic Dynamics
*Claim*: State Space Models (PowerMamba, S-Mamba) outperform on wind and price because their selective gating adapts to irregular, non-stationary dynamics.
*Mechanism*: SSMs maintain a compressed, evolving state with linear complexity and input-dependent transition dynamics, enabling dynamic memory adjustment. This flexibility handles irregular, bursty, or chaotic characteristics better than fixed patches or rigid attention.
*Core assumption*: Forecasting requires modeling long-range, context-sensitive dependencies that are not neatly periodic; the state provides adaptive memory.
*Evidence anchors*: [abstract] SSMs outperform on chaotic wind and price; [section V-D] S-Mamba leads on wind (nMAE 35.4) and price (27.5); [section IV-A] PowerMamba’s decomposition and bidirectionality address disparate frequencies.

## Foundational Learning
### Temporal Embeddings (Hour-of-Day, Day-of-Week)
- *Why needed*: Grid load exhibits extremely strong 24h and 168h cycles. Explicit, learnable embeddings inject this prior, letting models focus on residuals like weather effects.
- *Quick check*: Removing day-of-week embedding would likely degrade CAISO more than ISO-NE because CAISO’s summer peak aligns strongly with weekly work/cycle patterns.

### Bidirectional Encoding within Lookback Window
- *Why needed*: The historical lookback (L=240h) is a completed sequence; future information within it (e.g., yesterday’s afternoon peak) informs current state (e.g., today’s morning ramp). Bidirectional processing captures these relationships.
- *Quick check*: Bidirectional encoding is applied only to the fixed historical lookback because the forecast horizon is future; applying it there would cause leakage.

### Thermal-Lag-Aware Weather Fusion
- *Why needed*: Weather’s effect on load is delayed (2–6 hours) due to building thermal inertia. Fusion must align weather features with causal impact time, not observation time.
- *Quick check*: Naively concatenating current temperature with current load without lag adjustment would likely cause the model to overfit to spurious instantaneous correlations or fail to learn the true delayed effect.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Input Layer (Load history + optional lagged weather + temporal embeddings) -> Core Encoder (Architecture-specific: SSM stack / Transformer on patches / LSTM) -> Architectural Adapter (Attention-weighted pooling or inherent patch structure) -> Output Head (Linear projection to W-step forecast)

### Critical Path
For weather-augmented forecasting: Raw Load + Lagged Weather + Temporal Embeddings -> Architecture-Specific Fusion (early summation, pre-decomposition, interleaved cross-attention, or token concatenation) -> Core Encoder -> Context Summarization (Pooling/CLS) -> Forecast Head. The fusion point is the key differentiator.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Channel-Independence vs. Cross-Variate Attention**: PatchTST’s channel independence provides robustness without weather but weaker integration; iTransformer’s cross-variate attention leverages multiple variables effectively but fails with single-variate input.
- **Linear vs. Quadratic Complexity**: SSMs offer O(n) scalability; Transformers (PatchTST) may capture finer local patterns at higher computational cost.
- **Fixed Patching vs. Continuous State**: Patching aligns with periodic cycles; SSMs’ adaptive state handles irregular events but may add overhead for rhythmic signals.

### Failure Signatures
- iTransformer: Catastrophic performance (high MAPE) in load-only mode; sudden drop when weather removed.
- PatchTST: Degradation on grids with irregular, non-repeating local patterns (e.g., PJM/NYISO load, price spikes) where patch boundaries disrupt transient dynamics.
- SSMs (PowerMamba): Underperformance on extremely rhythmic, predictable signals where pattern-matching suffices.
- LSTM: Consistently higher error, especially at longer horizons (W>=96), indicating difficulty with long-term dependencies.

### First 3 Experiments
1. Load-Only Baseline: Train all five models with only historical load and temporal embeddings for a target ISO (e.g., CAISO). Verify PatchTST and SSMs lead; iTransformer is weakest.
2. Add Temporal Tokens Control: For iTransformer and LSTM, add dummy “temporal-only” tokens to control for token quantity. Verify they improve, supporting that iTransformer benefits from tokenization generally.
3. Weather Integration with Lag: Implement architecture-specific weather fusion with empirical thermal lags (e.g., 4h for temperature). Train weather-augmented models, measure ΔMAPE vs. load-only. Confirm iTransformer’s gap over PatchTST widens on weather-sensitive grids (e.g., ERCOT).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: How do model rankings vary across different random seeds, and are the performance differences statistically significant? The paper uses a fixed seed (42) and notes multi-seed evaluation would quantify per-grid variance.
- **Open Question 2**: Do the weather integration benefits persist for longer forecast horizons (72–168 hours), and does the architecture-dependent gap change? The weather benchmark is limited to W=24; extension to longer horizons is planned.
- **Open Question 3**: How do zero-shot foundation models (e.g., Chronos, TimesFM) perform relative to the benchmarked TFS architectures, especially with weather covariates? The paper excludes foundation models to focus on TFS architectures that natively integrate weather.
- **Open Question 4**: How do forecast errors differ between system peak and off-peak periods for each architecture, and does this influence model selection? The paper reports aggregate metrics but does not stratify by peak/off-peak.

## Limitations
- Exact hyperparameter configurations (layers, heads, patch length, state size) are not fully disclosed; reproduction relies on repository defaults or inference.
- Thermal lag values for weather alignment are unspecified, potentially affecting weather-augmented results.
- Benchmark limited to six U.S. ISOs; findings may not generalize to other regions or data regimes.

## Confidence
- *No universal winner; performance depends on data availability*: High – consistent rankings across 30 horizon–grid evaluations.
- *Architectural advantage in weather integration*: Medium – supported by capacity-controlled experiments, but thermal lag uncertainty remains.
- *Task-specific architecture strengths*: Medium – observed on solar, wind, price; needs validation on broader tasks.

## Next Checks
1. **Hyperparameter fidelity**: Extract exact model settings from the official repository; if missing, perform a grid search per architecture to confirm reported performance hierarchy under optimal tuning.
2. **Lag sensitivity ablation**: Vary thermal lag (2–6 h) for key weather variables, recompute ΔMAPE for iTransformer and PatchTST, and test robustness of the threefold benefit gap.
3. **Cross-regional test**: Apply the benchmark to an additional ISO (e.g., Spain’s Red Eléctrica) with hourly load and weather data to evaluate generalizability of architecture recommendations.
