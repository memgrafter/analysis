# Regularized $f$-Divergence Kernel Tests

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.19755
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.19755
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Kernel-based f-divergence tests via regularized likelihood ratio estimation enable adaptive permutation testing; DrMMD excels on outlier detection, Hockey-Stick on privacy auditing, and a relative three-sample MMD for unlearning.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a flexible framework for two-sample testing based on $f$-divergences. The core method estimates likelihood ratios in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space with regularization and plugs them into the variational form of any $f$-divergence. An adaptive permutation test over hyperparameters (kernel bandwidth, regularization) yields a test that controls type I error and approaches the power of the best configuration without manual tuning. Experiments demonstrate that different $f$-divergences are complementary: DrMMD (regularized chi-square) achieves state-of-the-art performance on outlier detection, while the Hockey-Stick divergence is effective for privacy auditing. Additionally, for machine unlearning evaluation, the paper identifies a flaw in standard two-sample comparisons and proposes a relative three-sample MMD test that successfully distinguishes effective from ineffective unlearning methods.

## Method Summary
The method consists of two main components: (1) A kernel-based estimator for the likelihood ratio $r = dP/dQ$ obtained by solving a regularized least-squares problem in an RKHS, which is then substituted into the variational representation of an $f$-divergence to produce an estimate $ \hat{D}_{f,\lambda} $. (2) An adaptive permutation test that aggregates statistics over a grid of hyperparameters (choice of $f$, regularization $\lambda$, kernel bandwidth) using a softmax "Fuse" operator. The aggregated statistic is evaluated under permutations to obtain a $p$-value, ensuring exact type I error control. For machine unlearning, the paper critiques the absolute two-sample test (comparing unlearned model to a retrained model) due to inherent stochasticity and instead proposes a relative three-sample MMD test: compare the unlearned model to both the original and retrained models, testing if it is closer to the retrained one. The test statistic is the difference of two MMD estimates, and its asymptotic normality under permutation yields a $p$-value.

## Key Results
- DrMMD (regularized chi-square) achieves state-of-the-art power on the Expo-1D outlier detection benchmark, reaching 0.96 at $k=80$ with $n=1000$ samples.
- The Hockey-Stick test detects privacy violations more effectively than prior work, with a rejection rate of 0.785 for SVT5 using only $n=500$ samples.
- The relative three-sample MMD test for unlearning perfectly separates effective unlearning (rejection rate 0.0 for full retraining) from ineffective methods (rejection rate 1.0 for finetuning, pruning, SSD).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Consistent $f$-divergence estimation via kernel-regularized likelihood ratio
**Claim**: Any $f$-divergence can be estimated consistently by plugging a kernel-regularized likelihood ratio into the variational formula.
**Mechanism**: Given i.i.d. samples from $P$ and $Q$, estimate the likelihood ratio $r = dP/dQ$ using the closed-form kernel estimator $\hat{r}_\lambda$ (Equation 4) that solves a regularized least-squares problem in an RKHS. Then compute $\hat{D}_{f,\lambda} = \frac{1}{\tilde{m}}\sum \tilde{X}_i f'(\hat{r}_\lambda) - \frac{1}{\tilde{n}}\sum \tilde{Y}_j f^*(f'(\hat{r}_\lambda))$ (Equation 3). The choice of $\lambda$ trades off bias and variance.
**Core assumptions**: Kernel bounded by $K$; $\mu_P - \mu_Q \in \operatorname{Ran}(\Sigma_Q^\theta)$ for some $\theta\in(1,2]$ (smoothness condition); sample sizes balanced; for non-smooth $f$ (Total Variation, Hockey-Stick) the estimated ratio density is bounded near the threshold.
**Evidence**: [abstract] "kernel-based likelihood ratio estimation (regularized least-squares) in the variational form of any f-divergence"; [section] Equation (4) and Theorem 2.1 provide the ratio estimator and its RKHS convergence rate; Theorem 2.2 proves convergence of the $f$-divergence estimator.
**Break condition**: If $\mu_P - \mu_Q$ is not in the range of $\Sigma_Q^\theta$ for any $\theta\le 2$, the rate deteriorates and consistency may fail. For TV/Hockey-Stick, if the estimated ratio density is not bounded near the threshold, the finite-sample bound does not hold.
**Corpus**: The corpus contains related kernel-based $f$-divergence estimators, but none use the same adaptive permutation testing combination.

### Mechanism 2: Adaptive permutation test with hyperparameter fusion
**Claim**: An adaptive permutation test that fuses statistics over a grid of hyperparameters yields a test that controls type I error and approaches the power of the best single configuration without manual tuning.
**Mechanism**: For each hyperparameter configuration $c$ in a set $C$, compute an $f$-divergence statistic $\tau(Z,c)$ on the combined sample $Z$. Combine these with a softmax "Fuse" operator into a single $T_\text{fuse}(Z)$. Generate $B$ random permutations of the labels; for each permutation $\sigma$, recompute $T_\text{fuse}^\sigma$. The $p$-value is $(1+\#\{T_\text{fuse}^\sigma \ge T_\text{fuse}\})/(B+1)$. Reject $H_0$ if $p\le\alpha$.
**Core assumptions**: Under $H_0$, the combined sample labels are exchangeable; the Fuse aggregation preserves exchangeability (it is permutation-invariant); the grid $C$ contains at least one hyperparameter that yields non-trivial power against the considered alternative.
**Evidence**: [abstract] "adaptive permutation test over hyperparameters"; [section] Algorithm 1 and Section A.5 define the adaptive permutation procedure including Fuse; Theorem 3.2 provides a non-asymptotic power guarantee for the adaptive test.
**Break condition**: If the optimal hyperparameter lies outside $C$, the adaptive power may be lower than that of the best fixed configuration. Fuse can be dominated by one configuration if statistics are on very different scales and not properly normalized; the paper uses same $f$-divergence type and normalizes by sample size to mitigate.
**Corpus**: Neighbors like "Minimax Optimal Kernel Two-Sample Tests with Random Features" study kernel selection for MMD, but not the same Fuse aggregation for $f$-divergences.

### Mechanism 3: Relative three-sample MMD for machine unlearning evaluation
**Claim**: For machine unlearning evaluation, an absolute two-sample test is too strict because even valid retrained models differ due to stochasticity; a relative three-sample MMD test instead checks whether $M_u$ is closer to $M_r$ than to the original model $M$, providing a meaningful evaluation.
**Mechanism**: Obtain sample sets from $M$, $M_u$, and $M_r$. Compute unbiased MMD estimates $\widehat{\text{MMD}}_2(M_u, M)$ and $\widehat{\text{MMD}}_2(M_u, M_r)$. Under asymptotic normality of the joint distribution (Equation 13), the $p$-value is $\Phi\big(-(\widehat{\text{MMD}}_2(M_u, M_r)-\widehat{\text{MMD}}_2(M_u, M))/\sqrt{\sigma_{uM}^2+\sigma_{uM_r}^2-2\sigma_{uM,uM_r}}\big)$ (Equation 14). Reject $H_0$ (ineffective unlearning) for small $p$.
**Core assumptions**: The three distributions are pairwise distinct; the kernel used in MMD is characteristic; the asymptotic Gaussian approximation is accurate for the sample size; the $H_0$ composite hypothesis is appropriate.
**Evidence**: [abstract] "propose a relative three-sample MMD test that successfully distinguishes effective unlearning..."; [section] Section 6.4 explains the flaw of two-sample tests and introduces the three-sample method; Table 3 shows results; Section C.3 details the experimental implementation.
**Break condition**: If unlearning is nearly perfect (distributions extremely close), the test may lack power to detect subtle failures. Conversely, if all three models are very similar (e.g., large oversampling in retraining), the test may always pass even with poor unlearning. The outcome depends on kernel choice and number of model samples.
**Corpus**: No neighbor specifically addresses unlearning, but the statistical foundation follows Bounliphone et al. (2016) on relative similarity tests, referenced in the paper.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept 1: $f$-divergences and their variational representation
**Why needed**: The entire test framework derives from substituting a likelihood ratio estimate into the variational formula for an arbitrary $f$-divergence. This provides flexibility to target specific alternatives (e.g., outliers via $\chi^2$, privacy via Hockey-Stick).
**Quick check**: For a given convex $f$, what is the optimal witness function $g^\star$ in terms of the likelihood ratio, and why does it involve $f'$?

### Concept 2: Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS) and kernel mean embeddings
**Why needed**: The likelihood ratio estimator $\hat{r}_\lambda$ lives in an RKHS; its convergence relies on $\mu_P-\mu_Q$ belonging to the range of $\Sigma_Q^\theta$. The embeddings $\mu_P = \mathbb{E}_{X\sim P} k(X,\cdot)$ turn distribution comparison into inner products.
**Quick check**: How is the population kernel mean embedding $\mu_P$ estimated from finite samples, and what operator does $\Sigma_Q$ represent?

### Concept 3: Permutation-based hypothesis testing with adaptive aggregation
**Why needed**: Permutations give exact type I error control without distributional approximations. Adaptive aggregation (Fuse) over hyperparameters removes sensitivity to bandwidth/regularization choices, yielding a nearly “universally consistent” two-sample test in practice.
**Quick check**: Why does the permutation test guarantee type I error at level $\alpha$ for any exchangeable statistic, and how does the Fuse operator preserve exchangeability?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Data loader supplies $X$ and $Y$ samples → Ratio estimator module computes $\hat{r}_\lambda$ for each hyperparameter using precomputed kernel matrices → Divergence modules compute statistics → Hyperparameter grid generator creates configurations → Fusion engine aggregates statistics via softmax → Permutation engine draws label permutations, re-runs ratio and divergence for each config, and recomputes aggregated statistic → Quantile estimator computes empirical $(1-\alpha)$ quantile from permuted statistics → Decision module compares observed aggregated statistic to quantile and outputs boolean.

### Critical Path
1. Precompute all pairwise kernel entries for combined sample (for each bandwidth in grid).
2. For each hyperparameter config $c$, compute $\hat{r}_\lambda^c$ and $\tau(Z,c)$. Keep values for all permutations.
3. Compute observed $T_\text{fuse} = \text{FUSE}(\{\tau(Z,c)\})$.
4. For each permutation $\sigma$: re-split data; for each config $c$, recompute $\tau(\sigma Z,c)$; compute $T_\text{fuse}^\sigma$.
5. Compute $p$-value and threshold.
6. Make decision.
Note: Step 4 is computationally dominant; reusing precomputed kernel matrices can avoid recomputing $k(x,y)$ but the regularized system must be assembled for each split.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Accuracy vs. compute**: More hyperparameters and permutations improve power and $p$-value accuracy but increase computational cost $O(B \cdot |C| \cdot N^3)$.
- **Bias-variance in $\lambda$**: Small $\lambda$ yields accurate ratio for smooth alternatives but can overfit; large $\lambda$ stabilizes but may miss subtle signals. Adaptation mitigates but requires sensible range.
- **Kernel bandwidth**: Narrow widths detect local differences (good for outlier detection); wide widths detect global shifts (good for smooth perturbations). Fuse effectively averages across scales.
- **Sample splitting**: The paper splits $N$ vs $\tilde{N}$ samples for ratio vs divergence estimation; reduces bias but wastes data. In practice the code uses the same samples, so theoretical bound is slightly loose.

### Failure Signatures
- **Type I error inflation**: Too few permutations ($B<500$) gives noisy quantiles; also if hyperparameters are data-dependent in a way that breaks exchangeability.
- **Numerical instability**: $\lambda$ below machine precision leads to singular matrix; increase $\lambda$ minimum and add jitter.
- **Memory blowout**: Kernel matrices $O(N^2)$ limit $N$ to ~5000–10000 on a single GPU. Larger datasets require approximations not implemented.
- **Zero power**: For a given $f$-divergence, the test may be blind to certain alternatives; if none of the included divergences match the alternative, power remains low.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question**: For which alternative distributions does the KALE divergence achieve optimal test power?
**Basis**: "Characterizing the set of alternative distributions for which KALE is optimal is an important direction for future work." (Section 6.4)
**Why unresolved**: Experimental results show KALE excels for small density ratios but degrades in high dimensions; theoretical conditions under which it outperforms other $f$-divergences remain unknown.
**What evidence would resolve it**: A non-asymptotic power analysis identifying precise density-ratio or smoothness conditions favoring KALE, linked to the geometry of the likelihood ratio and regularization effects.

### Open Question 2
**Question**: How can kernel-based two-sample $f$-divergence tests be generalized to the three-sample setting, with provable asymptotic guarantees?
**Basis**: "While it will be of interest to generalize the two-sample $f$-divergence tests to the three-sample setting, this requires establishing asymptotics of the variational $f$-divergence form in Equation (2) for divergences of interest (such as the KL and $\chi^2$)." (Section 6.4)
**Why unresolved**: The paper only treats two-sample tests; three-sample tests require the joint asymptotic distribution of two $f$-divergence statistics under permutation, which is non-trivial for non-smooth divergences.
**What evidence would resolve it**: Prove a multivariate central limit theorem for the vector of estimated $f$-divergences (e.g., $D(P||Q), D(P||R)$) under permutation, enabling relative tests as in the three-sample MMD.

### Open Question 3
**Question**: Can we construct a practical hypothesis test for the composite null hypothesis that an unlearned model distribution matches any model retrained from scratch on the retain set?
**Basis**: "A potentially better-suited null hypothesis ... could be the composite goodness-of-fit null hypothesis ... However, constructing a practical test for this composite null is challenging and remains an open problem." (Section 6.4)
**Why unresolved**: Standard two-sample tests compare to a single retrained model, leading to false rejections due to benign variation. The composite null involves infinitely many retrained distributions, making test construction difficult.
**What evidence would resolve it**: A kernel-based test that uniformly controls type I error over a whole family of retrained models, using concentration inequalities for supremum-type statistics over the function class.

### Open Question 4
**Question**: What function classes beyond traditional RKHS can accurately represent discontinuous witness functions (e.g., the step function in the Hockey-Stick divergence) for regularized $f$-divergence estimation?
**Basis**: "We nonetheless present this direct approximation as it will be of interest to revisit with more suitable function classes in future work." (Section 5)
**Why unresolved**: The direct RKHS-based optimization of the regularized Hockey-Stick divergence yields overly smooth approximations, failing to capture the true discontinuity of the witness. The paper thus resorted to a thresholded likelihood ratio estimator instead.
**What evidence would resolve it**: Investigate alternative variational formulations using non-smooth function classes (e.g., binary, piecewise constant) with appropriate regularizers (e.g., total variation) that allow efficient optimization while representing discontinuities.

## Limitations
- **DP audit reproducibility**: The differential privacy experiments lack explicit ε values and details on constructing adjacent datasets, making exact replication difficult.
- **Unlearning pipeline ambiguity**: Experimental setup for unlearning (training hyperparameters, forget set sampling, model output extraction) is not fully documented, hindering faithful reproduction.
- **Theoretical assumptions may not hold in practice**: The power guarantees rely on smoothness conditions (e.g., $\mu_P-\mu_Q \in \operatorname{Ran}(\Sigma_Q^\theta)$) and bounded likelihood ratios, which may be violated in real-world high-dimensional data, potentially reducing power.
