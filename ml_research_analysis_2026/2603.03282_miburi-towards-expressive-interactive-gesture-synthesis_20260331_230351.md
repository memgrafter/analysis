# MIBURI: Towards Expressive Interactive Gesture Synthesis
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.03282
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03282
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Real‑time causal gesture synthesis achieving SOTA Fréchet Gesture Distance (0.480) on BEAT2 with 36 ms per‑frame latency.

## Executive Summary
MIBURI introduces a real‑time, causal framework for generating expressive co‑speech gestures in embodied conversational agents. Existing methods require full future speech context and cannot operate online. MIBURI overcomes this by encoding motion into a hierarchical discrete token space via body‑part aware codecs and autoregressively generating these tokens with a two‑dimensional causal transformer. The model is conditioned directly on the internal speech‑text token stream of the real‑time dialogue model Moshi, augmented with contrastive and voice‑activation losses to ensure semantic alignment and diversity. Evaluations on the BEAT2 benchmark show state‑of‑the‑art Fréchet Gesture Distance (0.480), competitive beat alignment (0.461), and high motion diversity (L1‑Div 10.44), while user studies confirm a significant preference over non‑causal baselines. This paves the way for truly interactive, low‑latency agents with natural gestures.

## Method Summary
MIBURI first trains a gesture codec to compress 3D body motion into discrete tokens per body part, capturing a hierarchical representation. During inference, the real‑time dialogue model Moshi processes incoming audio and emits causally available speech‑text tokens. These tokens condition a two‑dimensional causal transformer: one branch models temporal dynamics across frames, and the other captures kinematic relationships across body parts at each frame. The transformer autoregressively predicts the next token in both dimensions, yielding a multi‑level motion token sequence. The codec decoder then transforms tokens back into 3D poses. Auxiliary losses – a contrastive loss aligning gestures with speech semantics and a voice‑activation loss synchronizing gesture intensity with speech energy – are applied to enhance expressiveness and diversity.

## Key Results
- Achieves SOTA Fréchet Gesture Distance (0.480) on BEAT2, competitive Beat Alignment (0.461), and high motion diversity (L1‑Div = 10.44).
- Runs in real‑time with a latency of 36 ms per frame, suitable for online deployment.
- User studies (n = 30) show statistically significant preference over non‑causal baselines EMAGE and GestureLSM for both naturalness and contextual appropriateness.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
MIBURI’s success rests on three pillars. (1) The body‑part aware codec produces a compact yet expressive discrete representation that respects the kinematic hierarchy. (2) The two‑dimensional causal transformer separately captures temporal coherence and cross‑body‑part coordination, preserving causality without sacrificing quality. (3) Conditioning on Moshi’s internal token stream offers rich, high‑level semantic context without needing future audio, while the auxiliary contrastive and voice‑activation losses explicitly align gestures with speech content and energy, further boosting appropriateness and diversity.

## Foundational Learning
**Gesture Codec**  
*Why needed:* Compresses continuous motion into discrete tokens for efficient autoregressive generation while preserving essential kinematics.  
*Quick check:* Can the codec reconstruct motion with low error (< 5% MPJPE) from its discrete tokens?

**Discrete Token Autoregression**  
*Why needed:* Enables sequence modeling of motion using transformer architectures, facilitating high‑quality generation.  
*Quick check:* Does increasing token vocabulary size improve reconstruction/generation quality up to a plateau?

**Two‑Dimensional Causal Transformer**  
*Why needed:* Separates temporal and kinematic dependencies, ensuring causality while capturing cross‑body‑part relationships.  
*Quick check:* Does ablating either the temporal or kinematic branch significantly degrade FGD or Beat Alignment?

**Contrastive Learning for Gesture‑Speech Alignment**  
*Why needed:* Encourages generated gestures to be semantically consistent with speech content, improving contextual appropriateness.  
*Quick check:* Does removing the contrastive loss reduce user‑rated appropriateness by a noticeable margin?

**Voice Activation Loss**  
*Why needed:* Synchronizes gesture intensity with speech energy, making gestures more expressive and natural.  
*Quick check:* Does gesture velocity correlate with speech volume when the loss is active?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Audio Input → Moshi (real‑time dialogue model) → Speech‑Text Token Stream → Two‑Dimensional Causal Transformer (Temporal branch + Kinematic branch) → Integrated Multi‑level Token Sequence → Gesture Codec Decoder → 3D Motion Frames.

**Critical Path**  
The generation loop must process each audio chunk in real‑time: Moshi emits tokens → Transformer generates motion tokens autoregressively (bounded by short context) → Codec decodes tokens to pose. The total time per output frame must stay below the frame budget (e.g., 33 ms for 30 Hz).

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *Causality vs. Future Context*: Causality enables online deployment but restricts coherence; future context could improve long‑range smoothness but breaks real‑time operation.  
- *Multi‑level Tokenization vs. Complexity*: Finer granularity increases expressiveness but enlarges the model and slows inference.  
- *Dependency on Moshi*: Leveraging Moshi’s tokens yields strong semantic alignment but ties the system to a specific dialogue model, potentially limiting generalizability.  
- *Two‑Branch Transformer vs. Full 2D Attention*: Splitting dimensions reduces compute but may omit interactions between time and body‑part axes.  
- *Auxiliary Losses vs. Training Stability*: Contrastive and voice‑activation losses boost expressiveness but require careful balancing to avoid instability.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Latency spikes causing stutter or dropped frames.  
- Repetitive or mode‑collapsed gestures (lack of diversity).  
- Poor temporal alignment (gestures out of sync with speech beats or semantics).  
- Reconstruction artifacts (e.g., foot sliding, unnaturally bent joints).  
- Degradation on noisy or out‑of‑distribution speech inputs.

**First Experiments**  
1. *Latency and throughput profiling*: Measure average and 95th‑percentile per‑frame latency on target hardware (CPU/GPU) with varying context lengths. Expectation: average ≤ 36 ms as reported.  
2. *Ablation of auxiliary losses*: Train without contrastive loss and without voice‑activation loss separately; evaluate FGD and L1‑Div. Expectation: each removal degrades expressiveness/diversity metrics by > 5%.  
3. *Cross‑dataset generalization*: Test BEAT2‑trained MIBURI on an out‑of‑domain corpus (e.g., TED talks) and compute FGD plus human naturalness ratings. Expectation: moderate degradation (FGD increase < 20%) indicating robustness.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can MIBURI be extended to incorporate the user's body dynamics and full dyadic context for truly interactive, multi-party gestures?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "Our current framework models only the agent’s motion and does not incorporate the user’s body dynamics or full dyadic context, limiting its ability to handle interactive, multi-party gestures. Extending MIBURI to perceive and respond to a partner’s gestures is an important direction for future work."
- **Why unresolved:** The current model generates gestures solely from the agent's speech-text tokens, ignoring visual cues from the user's gestures, which are essential for natural turn-taking and mutual interaction in conversations.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Develop a perception module (e.g., real‑time pose estimation) to capture user gestures and integrate it into MIBURI's framework; evaluate through user studies in dyadic settings to measure improvements in interactivity and naturalness.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How can we model the shared intent behind speech and gestures in a causal, real‑time framework, either by training LLMs to generate intent before output or by disentangling intent from intermediate features?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "We believe that a common modeling of the intent before generating the speech and the gestures is a goal worth pursuing... For truly interactive ECAs, we either need a real‑time LLM that is trained to generate the intent before the final output, or, we need an approach to disentangle the LLM’s intent from the intermediate features of the model."
- **Why unresolved:** Causal models like MIBURI rely only on past speech‑text tokens, which may not capture the underlying communicative intent that simultaneously drives speech and gestures; modeling intent explicitly could improve semantic alignment but under real‑time constraints remains unsolved.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Create a method to predict communicative intent from LLM internal states in real‑time, condition gesture generation on this intent, and assess whether it yields more semantically appropriate gestures without increasing latency.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** To what extent does the choice of speech‑text foundation model impact the quality, expressiveness, and latency of causally generated gestures?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper exclusively uses Moshi's internal token stream, claiming benefits in semantic context and speed; they ablate wav2vec and find worse performance, but do not explore other LLM‑based audio‑language models. This leaves open whether alternative models could further improve gesture synthesis while maintaining causality.
- **Why unresolved:** Different foundation models have varying architectures, tokenization, and latency profiles; assuming Moshi is optimal may limit potential gains in expressiveness or alignment that other models could provide.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct a systematic evaluation of MIBURI conditioned on multiple speech‑text foundation models (e.g., GPT‑4o, Whisper‑based LLMs), comparing metrics (FGD, BeatAlign, diversity) and generation times to identify optimal conditioning.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Can alternative efficient architectures (e.g., state‑space models, linear attention) be adapted for fully causal, low‑latency gesture generation without compromising expressiveness?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] MIBURI employs a two‑dimensional causal transformer with short context to meet real‑time needs; they note MambaTalk uses Mamba but with non‑causal cross‑attention. The paper does not investigate whether purely causal variants of newer architectures could achieve better quality‑latency trade‑offs.
- **Why unresolved:** The two‑transformer design is a practical solution but may not be the most expressive or scalable; architectures like Mamba or linear transformers could offer better efficiency but their causal adaptation for multi‑level token generation is unexplored.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement causal versions of state‑space models or linear attention for the temporal and kinematic transformers, then benchmark against MIBURI on gesture quality, diversity, and per‑step latency to determine feasibility and advantages.

## Limitations
- **Generalizability**: MIBURI’s conditioning on Moshi’s internal token stream may hinder portability to other real‑time dialogue systems.
- **Evaluation scope**: Validation primarily on BEAT2 (scripted monologues) and limited conversational scenarios; real‑world multi‑party, emotionally diverse, or culturally variable interactions remain untested.
- **Expressiveness metrics**: Operationalization via losses and automated metrics may miss nuanced cultural or emotional alignment; user studies had limited participants (n=30) and short stimuli, risking sampling bias.

## Next Checks

Expectation: performance should not degrade > 15% if conditioning is robust.
2. **Out‑of‑distribution conversational evaluation**: Conduct user studies with 50+ participants rating gestures in multi‑speaker, emotionally charged (angry/joyful), and turn‑taking dialogues from outside BEAT2 (e.g., Speed Dating corpus). Metric: appropriateness ratings should remain > 4.0/5.0 to confirm contextual generalization.
3. **Long‑horizon coherence analysis**: Generate 2‑minute continuous dialogues with MIBURI and analyze motion variance (L1‑Div) and repetitive gesture frequency per minute. Check against human motion entropy baselines; expect < 10% repetition increase over 30‑second clips to rule out degeneracy in extended generation.
