# Neural Diffusion Intensity Models for Point Process Data

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.24083
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.24083
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Neural Diffusion Intensity Models enable fast amortized variational inference for Cox processes via drift-corrected neural SDEs, achieving MCMC-level accuracy with orders-of-magnitude speedups.

## Executive Summary
Cox processes—point processes with latent stochastic intensities—are well-suited for overdispersed event data but typically require expensive MCMC for posterior inference due to intractable path integrals. This paper introduces Neural Diffusion Intensity Models (NDIM), a variational framework that employs neural SDEs as priors over intensity paths. The key theoretical contribution uses enlargement of filtrations to derive an exact posterior SDE with an explicit drift correction. This ensures the variational family contains the true posterior, so maximizing the ELBO yields maximum likelihood given sufficient model capacity. An amortized encoder based on DeepSets maps variable-length observation sequences to the posterior correction uβ in a single forward pass. Experiments on synthetic data and real bank call arrival times show that NDIM accurately recovers latent intensity dynamics and matches MCMC predictive log-likelihoods while being substantially faster (e.g., 39.9 seconds vs. 17 minutes 38 seconds). The amortized inference generalizes reliably even when test sequences are longer than those seen during training.

## Method Summary
The prior over intensity paths Z_t is a neural SDE: dZ_t = b_θ(Z_t, t) dt + σ(Z_t, t) dB_t, where b_θ is a neural network. Observations are event times τ_i within [0, T]. The posterior is approximated by another diffusion with the same σ but an added drift σ u_β(z, t), where u_β is learned by an encoder that conditiones on all observed events up to time T' (≤ T). The encoder uses a DeepSets architecture: each event is embedded via a network ψ using features (Z, Δ, T'−τ), embeddings are summed, and a network ρ aggregates this sum with (t, T') to produce the scalar u_β. This construction ensures permutation invariance and handles variable sequence lengths. Enlargement of filtrations guarantees that the posterior SDE with correction u_β exactly matches the true posterior when u_β is unrestricted. The Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) is:
L(θ,β; X) = E_{Z∼q_β}[ Σ_{τ_i≤T'} log Z_{τ_i} − ∫_0^{T'} Z_t dt − ½ ∫_0^{T'} u_β^2(Z_t, t) dt ] + const.
To optimize, the authors simulate paths of Z and simultaneously track the Jacobians J_θ = ∂Z/∂θ and J_β = ∂Z/∂β by augmenting the state and using Euler–Maruyama integration on a uniform grid. This enables pathwise gradient estimates for both θ and β via automatic differentiation. The loss is averaged over m Monte Carlo Brownian paths per sequence and over minibatches. Training uses Adam with gradient clipping.

## Key Results
- The variational posterior recovers true latent intensity dynamics on synthetic data and matches MCMC accuracy.
- Amortized inference achieves comparable predictive log-likelihoods to MCMC but with orders-of-magnitude speedups (e.g., 39.9 seconds vs. 17 minutes 38 seconds on a synthetic task).
- Generalization: The amortized encoder performs reliably even when training sequence lengths are smaller than those at test time.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The theoretical foundation is the enlargement-of-filtrations theorem: conditioning a diffusion (the prior neural SDE) on the occurrence of an inhomogeneous Poisson process with intensity Z_t yields a posterior diffusion with the same diffusion coefficient but an additive drift correction. This correction, u_β(z,t), is an explicit functional of the entire observed event history (up to time T') and depends on the residual intensity and the observed events. The theorem guarantees that when u_β is unrestricted, the posterior SDE exactly matches the true posterior. By parameterizing u_β with a neural network, the variational family becomes dense in the set of all posteriors given sufficient model capacity, making ELBO maximization equivalent to maximum likelihood. Amortization via the DeepSets encoder allows u_β to be computed in a single forward pass for new sequences, avoiding expensive per-sequence optimization.

## Foundational Learning
- **Cox process**: A point process where the intensity is itself a random process. *Why needed*: Cox processes are essential for modeling event data with overdispersion and complex latent intensity dynamics, which cannot be captured by standard Poisson processes. *Quick check*: Simulate a stochastic intensity process (e.g., using a Cox-Ingersoll-Ross (CIR) model) and sample event times by thinning a homogeneous Poisson process with rate equal to the intensity. Then, for a fixed interval, compute the variance and mean of the event counts over multiple simulations. The variance should exceed the mean, confirming overdispersion.
- **Neural SDE**: An SDE with drift and diffusion parameterized by neural networks. *Why needed*: Neural SDEs allow for flexible, non-linear dynamics in continuous time and are differentiable, making them suitable for end-to-end variational inference. *Quick check*: Fit a neural SDE to a known linear SDE (e.g., Ornstein-Uhlenbeck) and compare the learned drift to the ground truth parameters after training.
- **Enlargement of filtrations**: A stochastic calculus result describing how conditioning on future information changes the drift of a diffusion. *Why needed*: This theorem provides the exact form of the posterior drift correction, which is crucial for the variational family to contain the true posterior. *Quick check*: Apply the formula to a Brownian motion conditioned on its endpoint and verify the posterior mean matches the known Brownian bridge.
- **Variational inference (VI)**: Approximates a posterior by optimizing a tractable lower bound. *Why needed*: VI provides a tractable lower bound (ELBO) that can be optimized with gradient-based methods, avoiding the computational cost of MCMC for each new dataset. *Quick check*: Implement a simple Gaussian VI for a Bayesian linear regression; confirm ELBO increases during optimization and the approximate posterior mean is close to the ground truth.
- **Amortized inference**: Learning an encoder that directly produces posterior approximations for new data. *Why needed*: Amortization shares computation across datasets, enabling fast inference on new sequences by encoding them with a neural network, rather than optimizing per-sequence parameters. *Quick check*: Train an amortized VI on a synthetic Gaussian mixture model; check that the encoder outputs sensible means for held-out data points and that the ELBO on test data is comparable to that of per-sequence VI.
- **DeepSets**: A neural architecture that is permutation-invariant for set-structured inputs. *Why needed*: DeepSets ensures permutation invariance and handles variable-length sequences, which is necessary because event times are unordered and the number of events varies across sequences. *Quick check*: Feed two different permutations of the same set of event times to a trained DeepSets encoder and confirm identical outputs.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:
Event times -> DeepSets encoder (ψ + ρ) -> u_β(z,t) function -> Posterior SDE simulator (Euler-Maruyama, state augmented with J_θ, J_β) -> Z path, log-intensity at events, integral of Z and u_β^2 -> ELBO mean over MC samples -> Loss -> Backpropagation -> Adam updates for θ and β.

Critical path:
1. Sample minibatch of sequences.
2. For each sequence, run encoder to obtain parameters for u_β (or the u_β function).
3. Simulate augmented SDE on time grid (shared Brownian increments across J_θ and J_β).
4. Compute ELBO components: Σ log Z at event times, ∫ Z dt, ½ ∫ u_β^2 dt.
5. Average over Monte Carlo paths and minibatch to form loss.
6. Backpropagate and update θ, β with Adam (gradient clipping).

Design tradeoffs:
- Discretization steps K: more steps reduce discretization bias but increase compute and memory for Jacobian tracking.
- Neural capacity: wider/deeper networks increase expressivity of b_θ and u_β but risk overfitting and slower training.
- Monte Carlo samples m: higher m lowers variance of ELBO estimate at the cost of simulation time.
- Diffusion coefficient σ: often chosen as √Z to guarantee non-negative intensity; this may limit flexibility compared to a fully parameterized σ.

Failure signatures:
- Z_t ≤ 0 during simulation, causing log-Z = -Inf. Fix: use positivity-preserving SDE (e.g., CIR) or include a softplus transform.
- Gradient norms explode (NaNs). Fix: stronger gradient clipping, lower learning rate, increase m.
- ELBO stagnates or diverges. Checks: verify correct implementation of ELBO terms, proper scaling of time, and that u_β is not zero from the start (initialize with small random values).
- Encoder fails on long sequences (out-of-memory). Mitigation: bucket sequences by length or use a maximum length with masking.

First three experiments:
1. **SDE integrator sanity**: Simulate a known SDE (e.g., Ornstein-Uhlenbeck) with analytical solution. Run the same Euler-Maruyama discretization as in the model (with the same step size and number of steps) and compare the empirical mean and variance of the simulated paths at multiple time points to the analytical values. Additionally, pick a drift parameter (e.g., the mean reversion rate in OU) and compute the gradient of the final state Z_T with respect to that parameter using the tracked Jacobian J_θ. Compare this gradient to a finite difference approximation (with a small perturbation) to ensure the gradient computation is correct.
2. **Single-sequence overfitting**: Generate a synthetic sequence from a known intensity process (e.g., a sinusoidal intensity with added noise). Train the NDIM model on this single sequence with a small network (e.g., one hidden layer in the drift network). Monitor the ELBO to ensure it increases. Then, simulate posterior intensity paths from the learned posterior SDE and compare the posterior mean intensity to the true intensity. The two should closely align, confirming that the model can fit the intensity dynamics on a single sequence.
3. **Amortization generalization**: Generate multiple synthetic sequences with varying lengths (by controlling the number of events, e.g., by thinning a Poisson process with a fixed rate and then truncating to a maximum length). Train the model on sequences with at most 10 events. Then, evaluate the trained model on held-out sequences of length 10 (within training distribution), 15, and 20 (outside training distribution). Compute the predictive log-likelihood (using the same Monte Carlo approximation as in training) for each test sequence and average by length. The performance should not drop significantly as the sequence length increases beyond the training maximum, indicating that the encoder generalizes to longer sequences.

## Limitations
- Neural network architectures (layer sizes, activation functions) for b_θ, ψ, and ρ are unspecified, requiring assumptions that may affect reproducibility.
- Critical hyperparameters (Monte Carlo sample count m, time discretization steps K, Adam betas) are not reported; the reconstruction relies on educated guesses.
- Data preprocessing details (conversion of minute counts to event times, train/test split, thinning probability) are ambiguous and could lead to mismatches with the original experiments.

## Confidence
- Theoretical guarantee: **High** – the proof via filtration enlargement appears rigorous.
- Predictive performance: **Medium** – results on synthetic and one real dataset are promising but lack extensive benchmarking.
- Amortization generalization: **Medium** – claims are supported by limited experiments; broader validation needed.
- Speedup: **High** – explicit timings confirm orders-of-magnitude improvement.
