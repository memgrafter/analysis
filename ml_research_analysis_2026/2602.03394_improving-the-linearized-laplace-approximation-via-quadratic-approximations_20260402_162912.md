# Improving the Linearized Laplace Approximation via Quadratic Approximations
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.03394
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03394
- **Reference count**: 10
- **Primary result**: QLA reduces overconfident out-of-distribution predictions by incorporating second-order curvature via rank‑one approximations, yielding consistent NLL and CRPS improvements over LLA with negligible extra cost.

## Executive Summary
We introduce the Quadratic Laplace Approximation (QLA) to improve uncertainty calibration in deep neural networks. QLA extends the Linearized Laplace Approximation (LLA) by incorporating second‑order curvature information through rank‑one approximations of per‑datum Hessian contributions, obtained efficiently via power iterations. Prediction retains the linearized model, preserving stable uncertainty behavior. On five UCI regression datasets with out‑of‑distribution “in‑between” splits, QLA achieves small but consistent reductions in Negative Log Likelihood and Continuous Ranked Probability Score compared to LLA, demonstrating refined uncertainty estimation at negligible additional computational cost.

## Method Summary
QLA constructs a Gaussian posterior approximation using a quadratic expansion of the log‑posterior. For each training point, it computes the residual and uses power iteration (starting from the Jacobian) to find the leading eigenvector of the per‑datum curvature matrix \(A = r_n H_n - J_n \Lambda J_n^\top\). The rank‑one outer product of this eigenvector is added to the posterior precision, which is summed over all data plus a diagonal prior. The linearized network output is used for prediction, as in LLA. Hyperparameters (network size, weight decay) are selected via inner cross‑validation on the training split; the prior variance is estimated by maximizing the linearized marginal likelihood. The approach adds only a few Hessian‑vector product iterations per datapoint, so computational overhead remains negligible.

## Key Results
- QLA achieves lower or equal Negative Log Likelihood (NLL) compared to LLA on all five UCI datasets with out‑of‑distribution splits.
- QLA achieves lower or equal Continuous Ranked Probability Score (CRPS) on all five datasets.
- The improvements are consistent but modest; computational overhead relative to LLA is negligible.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** QLA yields a posterior precision matrix that better approximates the full Laplace (including second‑order effects) than LLA, without explicitly computing the full Hessian.
- **Mechanism:** The method replaces the Generalized Gauss‑Newton (GGN) matrix \(J\Lambda J^\top\) used in LLA with a rank‑one factor derived from the dominant eigenvector of the per‑datum curvature matrix (which includes the \(rH\) term). This rank‑one approximation is obtained via power iteration starting from the Jacobian, thereby incorporating both first‑order and the most significant second‑order curvature information.
- **Core assumption:** The dominant eigenvector of the per‑datum curvature matrix captures enough of the second‑order information to improve the Laplace posterior; a rank‑one approximation is sufficient for this purpose.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “QLA approximates each second order factor in the approximate Laplace log‑posterior using a rank‑one factor obtained via efficient power iterations.”
  - [section 3] Detailed derivation of the quadratic expansion, definition of matrix \(A\), and power iteration procedure (Eqs. 13‑15).
  - [corpus] Corpus shows related works on scalable LLA but no prior method using power‑iteration‑based rank‑one updates for per‑datum curvature; QLA appears novel in this respect.
- **Break condition:** If the curvature matrix has several eigenvalues of similar magnitude, a single rank‑one factor may be insufficient, causing the posterior precision to deviate from the full Laplace and potentially harm uncertainty estimates.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Using the linearized model for prediction (instead of the full quadratic model) stabilizes uncertainty estimation and prevents over‑confident out‑of‑distribution forecasts.
- **Mechanism:** Even though the posterior is built from a quadratic expansion, the predictive distribution is obtained by marginalizing under the Gaussian posterior while evaluating the likelihood with the first‑order linearized network output. This design, inherited from LLA, avoids the tendency of full LA (which uses the nonlinear network) to allocate excessive probability mass to regions of parameter space unsupported by data.
- **Core assumption:** The linearized predictive model sufficiently approximates the true predictive distribution near the MAP while providing well‑calibrated uncertainty; the second‑order posterior can be retained for improved precision without compromising predictive reliability.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “For prediction, QLA also uses the linearized model.”
  - [section 2] Discusses the bias of full LA and how linearized prediction mitigates it; also presents the GLM predictive.
  - [section 5] “retains stable predictive behavior of LLA through the linearized predictive model, which mitigates the tendency to allocate excessive probability mass.”
  - [corpus] Corpus indicates that linearized Laplace is a well‑studied remedy for over‑confidence; QLA adopts this design, aligning with established practice.
- **Break condition:** If the network exhibits strong nonlinearities that cannot be captured even by a quadratic posterior, linearizing at prediction time may still lead to model misspecification and degraded calibration in regions far from the training data.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The power‑iteration approach avoids explicit construction of the full per‑datum Hessian, keeping computational overhead negligible compared to LLA.
- **Mechanism:** Each iteration of power iteration requires only Hessian‑vector products \(\mathbf{H}_{\theta^*}(x)\mathbf{z}\), which can be computed efficiently via automatic differentiation. Since only a few iterations (e.g., 10) are needed, the extra cost per data point is small relative to the Jacobian computation already performed in LLA.
- **Core assumption:** The dominant eigenvector converges within a small number of iterations and the resulting rank‑one approximation is informative; Hessian‑vector products are not prohibitively expensive relative to Jacobian computation.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “QLA incorporates second‑order terms by approximating per-datum Hessian contributions with rank‑one factors obtained via efficient power iterations, avoiding explicit full Hessian computation.”
  - [section 3] Describes the initialization with Jacobian, the recursive update, and the use of Hessian‑vector products; mentions “a few iterations” and “negligible additional cost.”
  - [section 4] Experimental results report “negligible additional computational cost” relative to LLA.
  - [corpus] Corpus includes papers on efficient second‑order methods (e.g., “Debiasing Mini‑Batch Quadratics”) confirming that Hessian‑vector products are a standard tool; however, the specific power‑iteration‑based rank‑one Laplace update is not present in the neighboring set.
- **Break condition:** If the Hessian‑vector product is significantly more expensive than Jacobian computation (e.g., due to large network size), or if the power iteration requires many iterations to converge (e.g., due to slowly decaying spectral gaps), the computational advantage may diminish.

## Architecture Onboarding
#### Component map
MAP Trainer → Jacobian Extractor → Hessian‑Vector Product → Power‑Iteration Module → Precision Assembler → Predictor

#### Critical path
1. Train network → \(\theta^*\).
2. For each training sample \((x_n, y_n)\):
   a. Compute \(J_n\) and residual \(r_n\).
   b. Run power iteration to obtain \(z_n\) (requires repeated HVP).
3. Store all \(z_n\) (or accumulate into \(\Sigma^{-1}\) if memory allows).
4. For each test point:
   a. Compute \(J^*\).
   b. Solve linear system \(\Sigma v = J^*\) using CG with \(\Sigma^{-1}\) matrix‑vector product.
   c. Form predictive distribution.

#### Design tradeoffs
- Power iterations vs. accuracy: More iterations yield a better approximation to the dominant eigenvector but increase HVP cost; typical values (5‑20) offer a good trade‑off.
- Prior variance selection: Estimating \(S_0\) via marginal likelihood adds an outer optimization loop; using a fixed diagonal prior simplifies but may degrade uncertainty.
- Storage vs. recomputation: Storing all \(z_n\) enables fast \(\Sigma^{-1}\) application but costs \(O(NP)\) memory. For large \(N\), one may compute \(z_n\) on‑the‑fly during each CG iteration (costly) or use subsampling of \(z_n\).
- HVP implementation: Choosing between exact but expensive second‑order autograd vs. approximations (e.g., Pearlmutter) affects speed and memory.

#### Failure signatures
- Power iteration fails to converge (residual norm plateaus) → \(z_n\) unreliable. Check convergence criterion.
- \(z_n\) vectors become near zero or exhibit large norms, indicating that the curvature matrix is ill‑conditioned or that the dominant eigenvalue is close to zero. This can lead to numerical instability when solving for \(v\).
- Memory overflow when storing all \(z_n\) for large datasets; resorting to on‑the‑fly recomputation may make prediction prohibitively slow.
- CG solver does not converge within reasonable iterations → weak preconditioning; consider using LLA’s \(\Sigma\) as preconditioner.

#### First 3 experiments
1. **UCI regression replication**: Train on Boston, Energy, Yacht, Concrete, Wine with in‑between splits; compute NLL and CRPS for LLA and QLA; verify consistent modest improvements of QLA.
2. **Power iteration sensitivity**: Run QLA with varying numbers of iterations (e.g., 1, 5, 10, 20) on a selected dataset; plot NLL/CRPS vs. iterations to confirm diminishing returns and typical optimal range.
3. **Full Laplace baseline (small dataset)**: On a tiny synthetic dataset (e.g., <100 points, small network), compute the exact Laplace posterior (full Hessian) and compare QLA’s \(\Sigma^{-1}\) to the true precision (e.g., in Frobenius norm) to validate that QLA is closer than LLA.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Classification extension**  
   - **Question:** Can QLA be effectively extended to classification tasks, and how does its uncertainty quantification compare to existing Bayesian deep learning methods for classification?  
   - **Basis in paper:** The authors state in the conclusion: "Future work will address extending QLA to classification and multivariate regression..."  
   - **Why unresolved:** The paper only evaluates QLA on regression problems; classification requires a different likelihood (e.g., softmax) and the behavior of the quadratic approximation with discrete outputs is uncertain.  
   - **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply QLA to standard classification benchmarks (e.g., MNIST, CIFAR-10), compute classification-specific uncertainty metrics (e.g., negative log‑likelihood, expected calibration error), and compare against baselines like LLA, full Laplace, and variational inference methods.

2. **Multivariate regression**  
   - **Question:** How does QLA perform on multivariate regression problems where the target has multiple correlated dimensions?  
   - **Basis in paper:** The same future work statement mentions "multivariate regression".  
   - **Why unresolved:** The current derivation assumes a univariate output; the per‑datum precision term is scalar. For multivariate outputs, the Hessian structure becomes more complex, possibly affecting the rank‑one approximation.  
   - **What evidence would resolve it:** Test QLA on multivariate regression datasets (e.g., predicting multiple physical quantities), evaluate using multivariate proper scoring rules (e.g., energy score, multivariate CRPS), and compare to LLA and other Bayesian regression methods.

3. **Scalability via low‑rank/inducing‑point methods**  
   - **Question:** Can the computational cost of QLA be reduced by incorporating low‑rank approximations or inducing‑point methods, enabling its application to very large neural networks and datasets?  
   - **Basis in paper:** The authors state: "improving scalability through low‑rank or inducing‑point based strategies for large neural networks [2]."  
   - **Why unresolved:** QLA currently requires a power iteration per datapoint, which may be expensive for massive datasets; low‑rank or inducing‑point approximations could alleviate this but have not been explored.  
   - **What evidence would resolve it:** Develop a variant of QLA that uses a low‑rank representation of the summed Hessian contributions or an inducing set of datapoints, then evaluate on large‑scale regression tasks (e.g., using a subset of UCI or larger datasets), measure speed, memory usage, and uncertainty quality relative to standard QLA and LLA.

## Limitations
- The computational efficiency claim (“negligible additional cost”) lacks detailed wall‑time benchmarks and scaling analysis, leading to medium confidence.
- Empirical improvements are based on five UCI splits without statistical testing; hyperparameter details (inner‑CV folds, early stopping, Adam settings) are underspecified, hindering replication.
- The prior variance estimation procedure and the exact marginal likelihood optimization are not fully described, blocking faithful reproduction.

## Confidence
- QLA improves uncertainty estimates via rank‑one curvature: **High**
- Linearized prediction stabilizes uncertainty: **High**
- Computational cost is negligible: **Medium**
- Empirical NLL/CRPS improvements are reliable: **Medium**
