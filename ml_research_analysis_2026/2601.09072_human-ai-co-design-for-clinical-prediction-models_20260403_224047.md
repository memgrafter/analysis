# Human-AI Co-design for Clinical Prediction Models
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.09072  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.09072  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** HACHI accelerated development of high-performance, clinically credible CPMs, achieving AUC 0.91 for traumatic brain injury (TBI) and 0.73–0.77 for acute kidney injury (AKI) while identifying novel, interpretable concepts.

## Executive Summary
Traditional clinical prediction model (CPM) development from unstructured clinical notes is slow and resource-intensive. HACHI is an iterative human-AI co-design framework where an AI agent rapidly explores interpretable yes/no concept questions for linear models, and clinical experts provide feedback to refine prompts, data, and objectives. In case studies for traumatic brain injury (TBI) and acute kidney injury (AKI), HACHI produced models with higher AUC than standard baselines (e.g., PECARN) and expert brainstorming, improved site-specific generalizability, and uncovered novel clinically relevant concepts. The process required only 3–4 rounds of ~1–2 hours expert time per round, demonstrating a path to efficient, transparent, and high-performance CPM development.

## Method Summary
HACHI operationalizes human-AI co-design for CPMs via a greedy hill-climbing loop. An AI agent (an LLM) generates candidate yes/no concept questions from clinical notes, extracts binary answers for each patient encounter, and fits a lasso-penalized logistic regression. Clinical experts review the resulting model's performance, concept definitions, and generalizability through a PHI-compliant web interface and provide targeted feedback. This feedback directly modifies the AI's search space by adjusting prompts (e.g., adding prefix constraints), data filters (e.g., excluding transferred patients), or regression objectives (e.g., sample weighting). The cycle repeats until performance plateaus and concepts achieve clinical credibility. Four core prompts manage keyphrase extraction, concept proposal, concept replacement, and annotation.

## Key Results
- For traumatic brain injury (TBI), HACHI achieved an AUC of 0.91, outperforming PECARN (0.75) and expert brainstorming (0.88), with improved site-specific generalizability (Oakland 0.93, Mission Bay 0.80).
- For acute kidney injury (AKI), HACHI reached 0.73 AUC on internal validation (vs. 0.65–0.70 for comparators) and 0.77 on a temporally held-out set.
- The process discovered novel, clinically plausible concepts (e.g., "normal gait" for TBI; "minimally invasive surgery" and "sleep apnea" for AKI) within 3–4 iterative rounds.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Iterative Feedback Loops Align Goals
**Claim:** Iterative human-AI feedback loops close the "gulf of envisionment" by progressively aligning the AI agent's concept exploration with the clinical AI team's implicit goals and clinical realities.
**Mechanism:** In each round, the AI agent generates and evaluates candidate concepts. The clinical team then reviews performance, concept definitions, and generalizability, providing feedback that directly modifies the AI's search space and evaluation criteria for the next iteration. This systematically corrects misalignments like data leakage, spurious correlations, or unrealistic concepts.
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] Describes the alternating loop; [section 2.1-2.2] Shows specific feedback (e.g., on "brain bleed" leakage, wrong coefficient signs, campus disparity) triggering prompt and procedure changes.
**Break condition:** Loop stalls if experts cannot identify actionable deficiencies or the AI cannot incorporate feedback meaningfully.

### Mechanism 2: Binary Concept Bottleneck Ensures Transparency
**Claim:** Representing clinical knowledge as binary yes/no concept questions enables transparent, auditable, and clinically actionable model development.
**Mechanism:** The LLM converts unstructured notes into structured, clinician-interpretable questions (e.g., "Does the note mention the patient having normal gait?"). These serve as atomic features in a sparse linear model. Clinicians can verify both the logical validity of concept extraction and the statistical reasonableness of coefficients.
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] Highlights transparent review via yes/no concepts; [section 4.1] Details the `ConceptAnnotationPrompt`; [section 2.2] Final models list explicit questions and coefficients.
**Break condition:** If the LLM fails to extract consistent, accurate answers due to ambiguous phrasing or insufficient context, the bottleneck fails.

### Mechanism 3: High-Level Human Steering vs. Low-Level AI Autonomy
**Claim:** The clinical AI team's primary value lies in high-level direction-setting—steering exploration, adjusting metrics, diagnosing issues—rather than low-level feature engineering.
**Mechanism:** The AI automates brute-force search over concept space (proposing candidates, extracting values, fitting regressions). Humans operate at a meta-level: analyzing performance disparities, identifying local optima (e.g., spurious note-style features), and reconfiguring the search (e.g., changing objectives, enforcing sign priors).
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "The role... is to analyze the results and repeatedly refine the instructions"; [section 3] Describes modifications like constraining definitions and reweighting data; [section 2.1-2.2] Examples: detecting transferred-patient leakage, normal GCS sign error, campus imbalance.
**Break condition:** If the team fails to recognize critical flaws or provides contradictory signals, the process may converge to an invalid or biased model.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Data Pipeline (EHR notes → temporal/data-quality filtering → train/validation split) → AI Agent Core (LLM with four prompts: KeyphrasePrompt, ConceptInitializationProposalPrompt, ConceptReplacementProposalPrompt, ConceptAnnotationPrompt) → Statistical Backend (scikit-learn pipeline: keyword selection → lasso logistic regression) → Human Steering Interface (PHI-compliant web app showing concepts, annotations, errors, stratified performance) → Feedback Implementation Loop (data scientists translate clinical feedback into prompt edits, data filters, or code changes like sample weights).

### Critical Path
1. **Setup:** Assemble dataset, define outcome, split data (optionally by site/time).  
2. **Round Initialization:** Run AI agent with default prompts. Review results in web interface.  
3. **Diagnose & Feedback:** Clinical AI team identifies issues (e.g., leakage, sign errors, site gaps).  
4. **Implement Feedback:** Modify prompts (most common), dataset filters, or regression objective (e.g., add sample_weight).  
5. **Rerun & Iterate:** Repeat steps 2–4 until performance plateaus and concepts are credible (typically 3–4 rounds).  
6. **Finalize:** Lock prompts, run final model on full training data, validate on held-out sets.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Prompt Flexibility vs. Reproducibility:** Free-text prompts allow rich steering but introduce stochasticity; replication becomes sensitive to wording.
- **AI Autonomy vs. Human Effort:** More autonomous AI saves time but risks missing flaws; more rounds increase clinical burden.
- **Generalizability vs. Local Customization:** Framework is designed for local training, improving site-specific performance but requiring per-site co-design.
- **Concept Breadth vs. Precision:** Early rounds propose broad concepts; feedback narrows them. Too broad → noisy extractions; too narrow → loss of signal.

### Failure Signatures
- **Leakage:** Concepts with high coefficients that require future information (e.g., "brain bleed" before CT). *Detection:* Clinical review flags temporally impossible concepts.
- **Spurious Correlation:** Concepts tied to note style/provider/hospital (e.g., "note mentions GCS"). *Detection:* Concepts change radically when restricting note types or sites without clinical reason.
- **Sign Inversion:** Coefficients with signs contrary to pathophysiology (e.g., "normal GCS" increases risk). *Detection:* Clinical team reviews coefficient signs vs. prior knowledge.
- **Poor Cross-Site/Temporal Performance:** Large AUC gaps between campuses or time periods. *Detection:* Stratified evaluation in web interface.
- **Vague/Inconsistent Concepts:** Low inter-annotator agreement if humans extracted (e.g., "risk factor"). *Detection:* Clinical team judges definition clarity.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Reproduce TBI Case Study:** Follow Round 1–4 exactly. Verify AUC progression (0.92→0.90→0.90→0.91) and final inclusion of "normal gait." Confirm that removing transferred patients and adding site weights improves campus-specific AUCs.
2. **Prompt Ablation - Concept Prefix:** Re-run TBI Round 1 without the "Does the note mention the patient having..." prefix. Expect emergence of note-style concepts (e.g., "GCS mentioned") and inflated AUC due to leakage.
3. **Stability Check:** For AKI Round 3, run the AI agent loop with 5 different random seeds. Examine variability in final concept sets and validation AUCs. Expect core concepts (CKD, major surgery) to be stable, with variance in lower-coefficient concepts.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Optimal number of rounds:** How many HACHI rounds are necessary for conceptually stable and credible CPMs across diverse tasks? The paper used 3–4 rounds but does not explore the trade-off between expert time and marginal gains.
2. **Critical clinical expertise for steering:** What specific aspects of clinical expertise are most critical for effective "prompt steering," and can this be formalized to reduce reliance on scarce clinician time? The paper does not decompose which reasoning skills (causal, workflow, data quality) matter most at which stage.
3. **Generalizability across institutions:** How does HACHI's performance and concept novelty generalize to other institutions with different EHRs, documentation, and populations? Testing was at a single academic center; multi-center validation is needed.
4. **Bias mitigation via human-in-the-loop:** To what extent can the human-in-the-loop systematically mitigate algorithmic bias (e.g., across race, sex) compared to automated discovery? The paper addressed site disparity but did not analyze bias across protected attributes.
5. **Impact of note type and quality:** How do note type (e.g., triage vs. discharge summary) and quality affect HACHI's ability to discover valid concepts? The paper does not analyze which note types contributed most or if certain concepts required specific sections.

## Limitations
- **Unknown hyperparameters:** Exact LLM configuration, lasso λ range, and train/validation split seeds are unreported, preventing exact replication.
- **Incomplete prompt specification:** Full prompt templates for later rounds are only in supplementary materials, obscuring how feedback materially changed agent behavior.
- **Limited external validation:** Generalizability tested only on two sites and one temporal hold-out; performance on other institutions remains unknown.

## Confidence
- **Performance outcomes (AUC 0.91 TBI, 0.73–0.77 AKI):** High – Results are explicitly reported and comparators are clearly inferior.
- **Mechanism 1 (iterative feedback alignment):** Medium – Supported by case studies but relies on retrospective review; no independent replication of the feedback loop.
- **Mechanism 2 (binary concept bottleneck):** Medium – Evidence from final model tables, but LLM extraction reliability per concept is not quantified.
- **Generalizability across sites/time:** Medium-Low – Oakland vs. Mission Bay AUC gap (0.93 vs. 0.80) persists despite weighting; only two sites limit claims.
