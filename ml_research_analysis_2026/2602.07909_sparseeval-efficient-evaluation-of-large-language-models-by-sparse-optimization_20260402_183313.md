# SparseEval: Efficient Evaluation of Large Language Models by Sparse Optimization

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.07909
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07909
- Reference count: 8
- Primary result: With only 100 anchor items, SparseEval achieves mean absolute error below 1% and Kendall’s τ above 0.90 across six LLM benchmarks, significantly outperforming state‑of‑the‑art baselines.

## Executive Summary
Evaluating large language models on comprehensive benchmark suites is computationally expensive. SparseEval tackles this by reformulating the problem as a sparse optimization task: it selects a small set of representative “anchor” items per benchmark and learns to predict full‑benchmark scores from a model’s performance on these anchors. The method uses a multi‑layer perceptron (MLP) trained on a reconstruction loss, and iteratively refines the anchor set via gradient‑based importance scores.

Across six widely used LLM benchmarks (ARC, GSM8K, HellaSwag, MMLU, TruthfulQA, Winogrande) and over 5,000 models, SparseEval with only 100 anchors attains mean absolute error (MAE) below 1% and Kendall’s τ above 0.90. It outperforms three strong baselines, requires less training time than IRT‑based methods, maintains robustness with limited training data, generalizes to unseen model families, and excels when the anchor count is very small.

## Method Summary
Given a matrix of model‑item binary accuracies \( S \in \{-1,1\}^{m \times n} \), SparseEval selects a small anchor set \( A \subseteq \{1,\dots,n\} \) of size \( k \). The anchor set is initialized either by \( k \)-means clustering on item cosine similarity or randomly, with the choice determined by validation performance. An MLP \( f_\theta \) maps a model’s scores on the anchor subset to its full benchmark average \( S W_a \) (where \( W_a = (1/n) \mathbf{1}_n \)). The MLP is trained to minimize the reconstruction loss \( \| f_\theta(S[:,A]) - S W_a \|_2^2 \), implicitly learning a weighted combination of anchors via its first‑layer weights.

After training, residuals \( e = f_\theta(S[:,A]) - S W_a \) are computed. Two scores drive iterative refinement: Anchor Importance Score (AIS) = \( \|\partial L / \partial S[:,i]\|_1 \) for each anchor \( i \) (gradient magnitude), and Candidate Importance Score (CIS) = \( |S[:,j]^\top e| \) for each non‑anchor \( j \) (alignment with residuals). Over \( R \) steps (e.g., 10), the anchor with smallest AIS is replaced by the candidate with largest CIS. After refinement, a final MLP is trained on the selected anchors. For a new model, its scores on the anchors are fed to the final MLP to predict full‑benchmark performance.

## Key Results
- With only 100 anchors, SparseEval achieves MAE < 1% and Kendall’s τ > 0.90 on six LLM benchmarks, significantly beating three state‑of‑the‑art baselines.
- The method requires less training time than IRT‑based approaches, is robust when training data is limited, generalizes to unseen model families, and shows particular strength at very low anchor counts.
- SparseEval’s anchor weights can become negative, capturing non‑linear relationships between item performances and overall scores.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The model‑item performance matrix exhibits sparsity (redundancy) such that a few anchor items can reconstruct full‑benchmark performance.
- **Mechanism:** Item‑item cosine similarity reveals clustering: high intra‑cluster similarity (0.72–0.89) and detectable inter‑cluster structure (0.48–0.67) indicate that items with similar model response patterns group together. Selecting one representative per cluster captures cluster characteristics, enabling weighted reconstruction of overall scores.
- **Core assumption:** Model responses to items are structured—items that models find similarly hard/easy cluster together, and this clustering is stable across diverse model architectures and scales.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "show that it exhibits sparsity, that representative items can be selected as anchors"
  - [section 3.1]: Figure 1 visualizes clustered similarity matrix with diagonal blocks; quantitative intra/inter‑cluster similarity values reported.
  - [corpus]: Corpus neighbors discuss "anchor" concepts (e.g., anchor attention, web anchoring) but do not address evaluation sparsity; evidence for this specific mechanism is weak and primarily paper‑internal.
- **Break condition:** If item response vectors become randomly uncorrelated (intra‑cluster similarity near random baseline), no small set can represent the whole; anchor‑based estimation fails with MAE rising sharply.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Gradient‑based weight optimization with an MLP predictor outperforms linear or clustering‑based weight assignments.
- **Mechanism:** An MLP takes anchor subset predictions as input and predicts the full‑benchmark score. Training minimizes reconstruction loss via gradient descent; the MLP’s first‑layer weights implicitly learn non‑linear combinations of anchor performances, including negative weights (Fig. 8), which expand the representational space beyond uniform averaging or k‑means centroids.
- **Core assumption:** The mapping from anchor subset performance to full‑benchmark average is smoothly learnable by an MLP given sufficient training models; gradient descent finds a good solution without overfitting.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "adopts gradient descent to optimize anchor weights and employs an iterative refinement strategy"
  - [section 4.1]: Explicit form of loss \( L = \| f(S \odot (1_M W^\top)) - S W_a \|_2^2 \) using MLP \( f(\cdot) \).
  - [section 5.3]: Figure 4 shows MLP significantly reduces MAE compared to linear weights on GSM8K.
- **Break condition:** With very few training models (e.g., <30), the MLP may overfit or fail to converge; also if the true mapping is highly discontinuous or non‑smooth, a small MLP may not approximate it well.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Task‑aware iterative anchor refinement using Anchor Importance Score (AIS) and Candidate Importance Score (CIS) improves anchor quality beyond initialization.
- **Mechanism:** After training a proxy MLP on current anchors, compute:
  - Residual vector \( e = f(S_A) - S W_a \) (per‑model estimation error).
  - \( \text{AIS}_i = \|\partial L / \partial S_{:,i}\|_1 \) for anchor i: gradient magnitude indicating how much that anchor contributes to loss reduction.
  - \( \text{CIS}_j = |S_{:,j}^\top e| \) for candidate j: alignment between candidate’s prediction pattern and residuals; high absolute dot product means candidate strongly correlates with systematic over/under‑estimation patterns.
  - Remove anchor with min(AIS), add candidate with max(CIS). Repeat.
- **Core assumption:** AIS reliably identifies anchors whose removal most increases reconstruction error (i.e., least useful), and CIS identifies candidates that most decrease error when added; the greedy refinement improves the set because the residual structure is informative and stable across proxy models.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4.3]: Equations (5) and (6) define CIS and AIS; describes iterative swap.
  - [section 5.3]: Table 2 shows refinement reduces MAE versus random/k‑means initialization, especially at low anchor counts (e.g., ARC: 3.131% → 1.778% at k=20).
  - [section 5.4]: Figure 6 shows AIS/CIS distributions shift right after refinement, indicating improved anchor quality.
- **Break condition:** If the proxy MLP is too shallow, gradients may be uninformative; if residuals are noisy (e.g., due to insufficient training epochs), CIS may select misleading candidates; if the anchor set is already near‑optimal, refinement may introduce noise.

## Foundational Learning

### Sparse signal reconstruction
Why needed here: SparseEval’s goal is to estimate a dense statistic (full‑benchmark average) from a sparse subset. Understanding that a few weighted samples can reconstruct a signal if the signal is compressible (sparse or low‑rank) is foundational.
Quick check question: "Given a matrix S of model×item scores, if you want to pick k columns such that their weighted sum closely matches the average of all n columns, what mathematical property of S makes this possible?"

### Gradient‑based feature importance
Why needed here: AIS and CIS derive from backpropagation gradients and residual alignment. Without understanding how gradients in a simple MLP can indicate input feature relevance, the refinement mechanism is opaque.
Quick check question: "In training the proxy MLP, why does a larger gradient magnitude \( \partial L / \partial S_{:,i} \) suggest that item i is more important for accurate reconstruction?"

### Model‑item matrix structure
Why needed here: The entire method hinges on the assumption that model responses to items are structured (clusterable). Recognizing this structure as distinct from item text similarity is key—SparseEval uses model predictions, not item embeddings, to define similarity.
Quick check question: "How is the item‑item similarity matrix constructed in SparseEval, and why does high intra‑cluster similarity in this matrix support the use of anchors?"

## Architecture Onboarding

Component map:
Score matrix \( S \) (models × items) → anchor set \( A \) (selected items) → MLP \( f_\theta \) predicts full‑benchmark average \( S W_a \). Training loss \( \| f_\theta(S[:,A]) - S W_a \|_2^2 \). Residual \( e = f_\theta(S[:,A]) - S W_a \) computes Anchor Importance Score \( \text{AIS}_i = \|\partial L / \partial S[:,i]\|_1 \) and Candidate Importance Score \( \text{CIS}_j = |S[:,j]^\top e| \). Iterative refinement swaps min‑AIS anchor with max‑CIS candidate.

Critical path:
1. Split model scores into train/val/test.
2. Initialize \( A \) via \( k \)-means (or random) based on val performance.
3. For refinement step \( r = 1 \) to \( R \) (e.g., 10):
   - Train proxy MLP \( f_r \) for \( E \) epochs on \( S_{\text{train}}[:,A] \rightarrow S_{\text{train}} W_a \).
   - Compute residuals \( e \) on training models.
   - Compute AIS for anchors, CIS for non‑anchors.
   - Replace anchor with smallest AIS by candidate with largest CIS.
4. Train final MLP \( f_{\text{final}} \) for \( F \) epochs on refined \( A \).
5. For a new model: obtain its scores on anchor items \( s_A \), pass through \( f_{\text{final}} \) to predict full benchmark accuracy.

Design tradeoffs:
- Anchor count \( k \): smaller \( k \) reduces inference cost but increases approximation error; \( k=100 \) yields MAE <1% and τ >0.90.
- MLP depth vs. training time: 4 layers used; deeper may improve non‑linear capture but increases time/overfitting.
- Refinement steps \( R \): each requires training a proxy MLP; \( R=10 \) used, with diminishing returns beyond.
- Initialization: \( k \)-means exploits clustering but fails on weakly clustered data; random sometimes better; validation‑based selection is essential.
- Loss choice: L2 used for stability; L1 more robust to outliers but less smooth.

Failure signatures:
- High val MAE (>3% at k=100) → anchors not representative; check intra‑cluster similarity, try random init or increase \( k \).
- Kendall’s τ < 0.8 on val → ranking unreliable; may indicate underfitting (increase MLP capacity) or biased anchors (increase \( R \)).
- Refinement increases MAE → proxy MLP too small; increase \( E \) or capacity.
- Extreme negative weights in final MLP (magnitudes >10× typical) → optimization instability; reduce learning rate or add L1 regularization.
- High variance across random seeds → anchor selection unstable; increase \( k \) or average predictions from multiple anchor sets.

First 3 experiments:
1. Reconstruction sanity check: With fixed random anchors (k=100), train final MLP on 80% of models, evaluate MAE on held‑out 20%. Expect MAE <2% on training benchmarks; if not, adjust architecture or learning rate.
2. Refinement ablation: Compare MAE with and without the 10‑step refinement using same initialization (e.g., k‑means). Expect clear improvement, especially at k=20,40.
3. Cross‑family generalization: Train on models from Llama/Mistral families, test on DeepSeek family. SparseEval should show lower MAE than baselines; if MAE spikes >5%, anchors may be overfitted to training family—collect more diverse models.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
Question: What is the minimal amount of training data (model‑item pairs) required for SparseEval’s gradient‑based weight optimization to maintain target performance levels (e.g., MAE < 1%, τ > 0.90), and can alternative optimization strategies (e.g., linear programming, Bayesian optimization) achieve comparable performance with less data?
Basis: The conclusion states "gradient descent requires sufficient samples to guarantee performance" as a limitation, but does not quantify this threshold or explore alternatives.
Why unresolved: The paper ablates training data proportion (Fig. 5) but only at fixed ratios (20%, etc.), not pinpointing a critical minimum. No comparison to non‑gradient methods is provided.
What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments training SparseEval on递减 subsets of models (e.g., 50, 100, 500 models) across benchmarks, measuring performance drop. Concurrently, replace gradient‑based weight learning with a mixed‑integer linear solver or a Gaussian process latent model and compare sample efficiency.

### Open Question 2
Question: Can the observed sparsity in the model‑item matrix be explicitly leveraged to improve anchor selection, such as by incorporating model‑model similarity structures alongside item‑item similarity in the refinement process?
Basis: Section 5.4 notes "we also observe sparsity in the model‑model similarity matrix" and states "We leave incorporating this sparsity structure into anchor weight optimization and anchor selection in the future work."
Why unresolved: SparseEval’s refinement currently only uses item‑level signals (AIS/CIS based on item vectors). The potential of model similarity (e.g., grouping similar models to share anchor contributions) is unexplored.
What evidence would resolve it: Extend the Anchor/Candidate Importance Scores to incorporate model clustering. For example, weight anchors not just by their item gradients but also by their ability to predict performance for model clusters. Evaluate on benchmarks with held‑out model families to see if joint item‑model sparsity improves generalization.

### Open Question 3
Question: How robust is SparseEval to distribution shifts in the model‑item matrix sparsity pattern, such as when applied to fundamentally different model architectures (e.g., sparse MoE models vs. dense transformers) or novel task types (e.g., code generation vs. multiple‑choice QA) not seen during training?
Basis: The paper tests on diverse benchmarks (ARC, GSM8K, etc.) and a hold‑out model family (DeepSeek), but assumes the sparsity structure (high intra‑cluster similarity) is stable. No explicit stress test on architecture or task distribution shifts.
Why unresolved: The "task‑aware" refinement is trained per benchmark. It is unclear if anchor sets and weights learned on one task transfer to another, or if sparsity patterns break for models with very different internal representations (e.g., non‑transformer architectures).
What evidence would resolve it: Train SparseEval on one benchmark (e.g., MMLU) and directly apply its anchors to a distant benchmark (e.g., code generation tasks) without retraining. Similarly, test on a set of models with radically different architectures (e.g., RWKV, Mamba) if available. Measure degradation in MAE/τ against training on in‑distribution models.

### Open Question 4
Question: Is the use of a multi‑layer perceptron (MLP) for the aggregation function *f* necessary, or could a simpler linear model achieve similar performance given sufficiently many anchors or different weight regularization?
Basis: The paper contrasts "Linear Formulation" (Equation 2) with the MLP approach and shows in Fig. 4 that MLP outperforms linear weights. However, the linear model was likely trained without the iterative refinement (AIS/CIS) and with fixed anchors. It is unclear if refinement + linear weights could close the gap.
Why unresolved: The ablation in Fig. 4 compares "Linear Weight" (presumably without refinement) vs. "MLP" (with refinement). This confounds model capacity with refinement strategy. The independent effect of using a linear *f* with the full SparseEval pipeline (including AIS/CIS) is not tested.
What evidence would resolve it: Run SparseEval’s complete pipeline (initialization + iterative refinement) but with a linear predictor (*f* = dot product) instead of an MLP. Compare MAE/τ across anchor counts. If performance gap narrows with more anchors, it suggests MLP’s benefit may be partly from better optimization with refinement, not just capacity.

### Open Question 5
Question: To what extent are the selected anchors benchmark‑specific versus model‑specific? Would a universal anchor set exist that works reasonably well across a wide range of LLMs and evaluation tasks?
Basis: The paper treats each benchmark separately, selecting anchors per dataset. The hold‑out model family experiment (Table 3) tests model generalization but anchors are still selected on the same benchmark. There is no cross‑benchmark anchor evaluation.
Why unresolved: If anchor selection is highly task‑dependent, SparseEval requires retraining for every new benchmark. A universal or transferable anchor set would greatly increase practicality. The paper does not address whether anchors from one task can predict performance on another.
What evidence would resolve it: Select anchors on one benchmark (e.g., ARC) using SparseEval, then use these fixed anchors (with weights re‑optimized or just averaged) to predict model performance on a different benchmark (e.g., GSM8K). Compare to anchors selected natively on GSM8K. A small performance drop would indicate transferability; a large drop suggests benchmark‑specific tuning is essential.

## Limitations
- Missing hyperparameters (MLP dimensions, training epochs) and the exact criterion for choosing k‑means vs random initialization block full reproducibility.
- The fundamental sparsity assumption—that item‑response clusters are stable across model families—lacks cross‑family evidence.
- Refinement incurs significant extra compute, and the binary accuracy formulation may not extend to other metrics (e.g., log‑likelihood).

- Hyperparameter recovery: Obtain the official implementation or conduct a hyperparameter search to pinpoint MLP architecture and training schedules that reproduce reported metrics on at least one benchmark.
