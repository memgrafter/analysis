# Diverging Flows: Detecting Extrapolations in Conditional Generation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.13061
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13061
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Enables reliable detection of off-manifold inputs in conditional generation by inducing trajectory divergence, achieving AUROC >0.95 across multiple domains while preserving generation fidelity and inference speed.

## Executive Summary
Flow Matching (FM) models generate high-quality conditional samples via deterministic ODEs but silently extrapolate efficiently when presented with out-of-manifold conditions, posing risks in safety-critical applications. Diverging Flows (DiFlo) addresses this hazard by structurally *enforcing inefficient transport* for invalid conditions through contrastive vector‑field regularization. During training, adversarially sampled hard negatives (via PGD) are pushed away from the optimal‑transport geodesic while valid conditions are pulled toward it. This creates a geometric phase transition: off‑manifold conditions produce curved, divergent trajectories measured by the simple DOT (Divergence from Optimal Trajectory) score. Without additional network branches, the calibrated DOT threshold yields strong detection (AUROC up to 0.998) and matches or improves predictive fidelity on synthetic, weather, and image‑to‑image tasks.

## Method Summary
DiFlo builds on top of standard conditional Flow Matching. The core model is a time‑conditioned network \(v_\theta_t(x,c)\) trained with the usual FM loss \(L_{\text{FM}}\). The key additions are (1) an adversarial negative mining step that, for each valid condition \(c\), finds a perturbed \(\tilde{c}\) within an \(\epsilon\)-ball that maximizes deviation from the OT velocity field \(u_{\text{OT}_t} = x_1 - x_0\); (2) contrastive losses that *pull* the vector field for \(c\) toward \(u_{\text{OT}_t}\) and *push* that for \(\tilde{c}\) away, using an L2‑based margin \(L_{\text{repel}}\) and a cosine‑based margin \(L_{\text{curve}}\). The total loss is \(L_{\text{DiFlo}} = L_{\text{FM}} + \lambda L_{\text{repel}} + \beta L_{\text{curve}}\).

At inference, given a test condition \(c_{\text{test}}\) and a noise sample \(x_0\), the ODE \(dx/dt = v_\theta_t(x,c_{\text{test}})\) is integrated to obtain a trajectory \(\{\hat{x}_t\}\) and final prediction \(\hat{x}_1\). The ideal straight‑line OT path is \(x^{\text{opt}}_t = (1-t)x_0 + t\hat{x}_1\). The DOT score is the integrated L1 deviation: \(\text{SDOT} = \int_0^1 \|\hat{x}_t - x^{\text{opt}}_t\|_1 dt\). Using a held‑out set of valid samples, split conformal prediction computes acceptance bounds \([q_{\text{lo}}, q_{\text{hi}}]\) for a chosen coverage level (e.g., 95%). If SDOT lies within this interval, the condition is accepted; otherwise it is flagged as off‑manifold.

## Key Results
- **Synthetic 2D spiral:** AUROC 0.998, FPR 3.45%, MSE 3.5e‑4.
- **ERA5 weather forecasting (with artificial thermal hotspots):** AUROC 0.980, FPR 5.20%, MSE 0.0034 (vs. FM 0.0038).
- **MNIST → SVHN style transfer:** AUROC 0.955, FID unchanged (~4.1) compared to baseline FM.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The method succeeds through three interconnected mechanisms:

1. **Contrastive regularization induces a geometric phase transition.** By pulling valid vector fields toward the optimal transport geodesic and pushing off‑manifold fields away, the training creates a clear separation: on‑manifold inputs follow near‑straight, energy‑minimizing paths; off‑manifold inputs are forced into inefficient, curved trajectories. This transition is the core signal for detection.
2. **Adversarial PGD discovers the manifold boundary.** Random negatives are too easy and fail to shape a tight decision boundary. PGD iteratively maximizes \(\|v_\theta_t(x_t,\tilde{c}) - u_{\text{OT}_t}\|^2\) within an \(\epsilon\)-ball, locating the most challenging local perturbations that sit just beyond the valid support. These hard negatives ensure the contrastive loss focuses on the true boundary.
3. **DOT provides a simple, discriminant score.** The L1 deviation from the straight‑line OT path cleanly separates the two regimes: valid trajectories yield near‑zero DOT, while extrapolation yields large DOT. No extra parameters are needed; the score emerges from the trained dynamics.

## Foundational Learning
- **Optimal Transport Flow Matching (OT‑FM):** The base method defines a velocity field that generates samples by solving an ODE whose marginal paths are optimal‑transport interpolants. Its inherent smoothness bias (Lipschitz continuity) is what allows silent extrapolation for off‑manifold inputs. *Quick check:* Why does OT‑FM produce straight‑line trajectories in data space, and how does this "straightness" contribute to the silent hazard?
- **Optimal Transport Theory:** The Benamou‑Brenier formulation shows that the optimal dynamic transport plan has a constant velocity field along straight‑line interpolants. This provides the geometric reference (the geodesic) against which divergence is measured. *Quick check:* What is the Benamou‑Brenier formulation, and why does it imply constant velocity?
- **Contrastive Learning (Triplet Loss):** DiFlo adapts triplet‑style constraints to the vector‑field space: \(L_{\text{repel}}\) enforces a margin on L2 distances to the anchor, while \(L_{\text{curve}}\) adds a directional margin (cosine similarity). Together they shape both magnitude and orientation of the field. *Quick check:* How do these two margins geometrically complement each other?
- **Manifold Learning & Lipschitz Continuity:** The assumption is that valid conditions lie on a locally connected manifold. Lipschitz continuity of the neural‑network vector field implies that for a perturbed condition \(\tilde{c}\) near a valid \(c\), the predicted fields are close, leading to similar trajectories and thus failing to detect extrapolation. *Quick check:* How does the combination of Lipschitz continuity and spectral bias cause \(v_\theta_t(x_t,\tilde{c}) \approx v_\theta_t(x_t,c)\) for near‑boundary samples?
- **Conformal Prediction:** This statistical tool turns the DOT score into a calibrated decision rule with a guaranteed false‑positive rate. Split conformal prediction uses a held‑out set to compute quantiles of the SDOT distribution under the valid condition, yielding acceptance intervals \([q_{\text{lo}},q_{\text{hi}}]\). *Quick check:* How does the choice of \(\alpha\) (e.g., 0.05) and equal‑tail splitting determine the interval and the resulting coverage guarantee?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Vector Field Network \(v_\theta_t\) → PGD Negative Miner → Contrastive Loss Module → ODE Integrator → DOT Scorer → Conformal Calibrator

**Critical path**  
*Training:* For a batch of \((x_1,c)\): sample \(t\in[0,1]\), draw \(x_0\), compute \(x_t=(1-t)x_0 + t x_1\) and OT velocity \(u_{\text{OT}}=x_1-x_0\). Compute FM loss \(\|v_\theta_t(x_t,c)-u_{\text{OT}}\|^2\). Run PGD for \(K\) steps on \(\tilde{c}\) (initialized as \(c\)) to maximize the same loss within \(\epsilon\)-ball. Then compute \(v_\theta_t(x_t,\tilde{c})\) and the contrastive losses \(L_{\text{repel}}, L_{\text{curve}}\). Total loss \(L_{\text{DiFlo}}\) backpropagated.  
*Inference:* Given \(c_{\text{test}}\), fix a noise \(x_0\). Integrate ODE \(dx/dt=v_\theta_t(x,c_{\text{test}})\) to obtain \(\{\hat{x}_t\},\hat{x}_1\). Compute ideal path \(x^{\text{opt}}_t\) and DOT = \(\int \|\hat{x}_t - x^{\text{opt}}_t\|_1 dt\). Compare to pre‑calibrated \([q_{\text{lo}},q_{\text{hi}}]\).

**Design tradeoffs**  
- **PGD steps \(K\):** More steps yield harder negatives but increase training cost (paper uses \(K=3\) for synthetic, \(K=5\) for others).  
- **Perturbation step \(\eta\) and radius \(\epsilon\):** Must be large enough to cross the manifold but small enough to stay near the boundary; mis‑specification leads to trivial or invalid negatives.  
- **Regularization weights \(\lambda,\beta\):** Control the trade‑off between detection strength and fidelity; excessive values harm in‑distribution MSE.  
- **Integrator:** Euler is fast but less accurate; RK4 is more precise; choice depends on task.  
- **Conformal \(\alpha\):** Lower \(\alpha\) gives stricter detection (lower FPR) but reduces valid‑sample coverage.

**Failure signatures**  
- **Detection collapse:** Overlapping SDOT distributions for valid and off‑manifold samples (AUROC ≈0.5). Check score histograms; likely due to too small \(\epsilon\), low \(\lambda,\beta\), or insufficient PGD steps.  
- **Fidelity drop:** In‑distribution MSE rises significantly vs. FM; indicates over‑regularization (reduce \(\lambda,\beta\)).  
- **PGD divergence:** PGD steps cause \(\tilde{c}\) to exit the \(\epsilon\)-ball or produce NaNs; apply gradient clipping, smaller \(\eta\), ensure projection is correct.  
- **Calibration drift:** If calibration set is not exchangeable with test data, conformal coverage fails; monitor empirical FPR.  
- **Integrator instability:** Off‑manifold trajectories may explode; use adaptive step sizes or clip states.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. **Synthetic 2D spiral manifold:** Verify that DiFlo achieves AUROC >0.98 while baseline FM yields ~0.5.  
2. **Weather temperature forecasting with artificial hotspots:** Test physical consistency; inject thermal anomalies; expect AUROC ~0.98 and competitive MSE.  
3. **Cross‑domain MNIST → SVHN style transfer:** Use FMNIST and KMNIST as OOD; expect AUROC >0.86 with unchanged FID.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Replacing PGD with efficient negatives:** Can simpler or learned perturbations (e.g., one‑step PGD, generative negatives) match detection performance? The paper acknowledges training overhead as a limitation and shows random negatives fail, but the space between remains unexplored.
2. **Extension to Riemannian manifolds:** The current method uses Euclidean OT; can it be reformulated for non‑Euclidean geometries (e.g., spheres, hyperbolic) to support geometric deep learning tasks such as molecular generation? The authors suggest it is straightforward but provide no concrete derivation or experiments.
3. **Scaling DOT to high dimensions:** For high‑resolution data (video, 3D volumes), computing the L1 deviation over all pixels may dominate inference. No analysis or approximations (e.g., subsampling, learned bottlenecks) are given.
4. **Theoretical guarantees of the phase transition:** Under what conditions on the data manifold and model architecture does the contrastive loss actually produce the desired separation in transport energy? The paper lacks a formal proof linking the triplet constraints to a Lipschitz bound.
5. **Robustness to subtle OOD types:** How does DiFlo perform when OOD inputs are semantically anomalous but geometrically close (e.g., physically impossible weather patterns) or when covariate shift changes the conditional distribution while the condition remains valid? The method targets condition OOD; joint distribution shifts may not be detected.

## Limitations
- **PGD negative mining ambiguity:** It is unclear whether gradients are recomputed with fresh \((x_0,x_1,t)\) at each PGD step or reused; this affects hardness and correctness.  
- **DOT score sensitivity:** The score depends on the random noise \(x_0\) used at inference; the paper does not specify a fixed vs. randomized policy, potentially destabilizing thresholds.  
- **Manifold assumption risk:** The method assumes valid conditions form a locally connected manifold. For highly disconnected submanifolds (e.g., disjoint digit classes), a small \(\epsilon\)-ball may never contain off‑manifold examples, causing the contrastive loss to miss global boundaries.

## Confidence
- **High:** Contrastive regularization consistently yields high detection (AUROC >0.95) without compromising fidelity across three diverse tasks.  
- **Medium:** Ablations confirm that adversarial PGD negatives are necessary; simpler strategies (random noise, outlier exposure) underperform.  
- **Low:** The claimed geometric phase‑transition mechanism (efficient vs. inefficient transport) as a universal extrapolation signal lacks theoretical justification and may fail on non‑Euclidean or highly complex manifolds.

## Next Checks
1. **Resolve PGD implementation:** Compare two variants—(a) recompute \(L_{\text{FM}}\) with fresh \((x_0,x_1,t)\) at each step, (b) reuse gradients from the main batch—to measure impact on detection and training stability on the synthetic spiral benchmark.
2. **DOT robustness to \(x_0\):** For a fixed test set, sample multiple \(x_0\) per condition and evaluate variance in SDOT and resulting AUROC/FPR. If variance is high, a deterministic \(x_0\) policy is required.
3. **Disconnected manifold stress test:** Train on a dataset with two well‑separated valid condition clusters (e.g., only MNIST digits ‘0’ and ‘1’) and test OOD from a third digit (‘2’). A significant drop in AUROC would indicate failure of the local \(\epsilon\)-ball assumption and the need for larger‑scale negative mining.
