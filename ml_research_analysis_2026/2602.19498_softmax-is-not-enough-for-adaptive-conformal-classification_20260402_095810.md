# Softmax is not Enough (for Adaptive Conformal Classification)

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.19498  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19498  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: Energy-based reweighting of nonconformity scores using Helmholtz free energy reduces average prediction set size by 15–25% while maintaining target coverage and improving adaptiveness across multiple datasets and architectures.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the inefficiency of softmax-based nonconformity scores in adaptive conformal classification. The authors propose to reweight these scores using a sample-specific uncertainty measure derived from the Helmholtz free energy, computed from pre-softmax logits. The monotonic transformation shrinks prediction sets for easy inputs and expands them for hard or out-of-distribution (OOD) inputs, all while preserving the coverage guarantee. Experiments on CIFAR-100, ImageNet, and Places365 with ResNet, VGG, and transformer architectures show consistent reductions in average set size (up to 15–25%) without sacrificing coverage. The method also demonstrates robustness under class imbalance and distributional shift and adds negligible computational overhead.

## Method Summary
The method builds on split conformal prediction. For a given input \(x\) and class label \(y\), a base nonconformity score \(S(x,y)\) (e.g., from APS, RAPS, SAPS, or LAC) is first computed from the model’s softmax probabilities. Then the Helmholtz free energy \(F(x) = -\tau \log \sum_{k=1}^K \exp(f_k(x)/\tau)\) is derived from the pre-softmax logits \(f(x)\). The free energy is transformed via a softplus: \(g(x) = \frac{1}{\beta}\log(1+e^{-\beta F(x)})\), and the scaled score is \(S_{\text{energy}}(x,y) = S(x,y)\cdot g(x)\). During calibration, the quantile \(\hat{q}_{1-\alpha}\) of the scaled scores on a held-out calibration set is estimated. At test time, for a new input \(x\), all labels \(y\) with \(S_{\text{energy}}(x,y) \leq \hat{q}_{1-\alpha}\) are included in the prediction set. Hyperparameters—softmax temperature \(T\) for calibrating probabilities and free-energy temperature \(\tau\)—are tuned on a nested calibration split to optimize efficiency while ensuring coverage. The approach is post-hoc, requires only logit access, and adds no extra training.

## Key Results
- Energy-enhanced variants of APS, RAPS, and SAPS reduce average prediction set size by up to **15–25%** compared to their standard versions.
- Target coverage \((1-\alpha)\) is maintained across all evaluated datasets (CIFAR-100, ImageNet, Places365) and base scores.
- The method improves adaptiveness: prediction sets shrink for low‑difficulty samples and expand for high‑difficulty or OOD inputs.
- It shows robustness under class imbalance (long‑tailed CIFAR‑100‑LT) and distributional shift (CIFAR‑100 → Places365) without increasing computational complexity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Free energy as a reliable uncertainty measure
For a well‑trained classifier, the negative Helmholtz free energy \(-\!F(x)\) tightly approximates the maximum logit and thus reflects the model’s confidence. Theoretically (Proposition 2.1), it linearly relates to the negative log‑density of the model‑implied distribution, making it a proxy for epistemic uncertainty. Empirically, the distribution of \(-F(x)\) shifts monotonically with sample difficulty: easy inputs have high \(-F\) (low free energy), while hard inputs have low \(-F\).

### Adaptive reweighting of nonconformity scores
The scaling factor \(g(x) = \frac{1}{\beta}\log(1+e^{-\beta F(x)})\) increases with \(-F(x)\). Therefore, for easy inputs (high \(-F\)), \(g(x)\) is larger, amplifying the base nonconformity score \(S(x,y)\). This pushes incorrect labels above the fixed calibration quantile, resulting in smaller prediction sets. Conversely, for hard inputs (low \(-F\)), \(g(x)\) is smaller, dampening scores and yielding larger sets. This reweighting preserves exchangeability (Theorem G.1), so the coverage guarantee holds.

### Sample‑dependent threshold equivalence
Multiplying the base score by \(g(x)\) is mathematically equivalent to comparing \(S(x,y)\) against a sample‑dependent threshold \(\theta(x) = \hat{q}_{1-\alpha}/g(x)\) (Proposition 2.3). Since \(g(x)\) varies with difficulty, \(\theta(x)\) automatically adjusts: higher for easy inputs (fewer labels pass), lower for hard inputs (more labels pass). This explains the improved adaptiveness.

  *Why needed*: Acts as a pre‑softmax uncertainty measure that does not saturate, unlike softmax confidence.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:  
  pretrained backbone → free energy module → base nonconformity module → energy scaler → calibration quantile estimator → prediction set builder
- **Critical path**:  
  – *Calibration*: Pass calibration samples through the backbone to obtain logits; compute \(F(x)\) and base scores \(S(x,y)\); scale each calibration score; compute empirical \((1-\alpha)\)-quantile of scaled scores.  
  – *Test*: For a new input \(x\), compute logits, \(F(x)\), base scores, scale, compare to the quantile, and form the prediction set.
- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  – The \(\beta\) parameter in the softplus controls scaling aggressiveness; many values work, but extreme \(\beta\) may cause numerical instability.  
  – Calibration set size: small sets yield noisy quantile estimates; the method still relies on exchangeability.  
  – Model dependence: requires direct logit access, which may be unavailable for black‑box APIs.
- **Failure signatures**:  
  – *Poor adaptiveness*: Energy values show no separation across difficulty bins (e.g., overlapping distributions in Figure 3).  
  – *Coverage violations*: If the scaling factor \(g(x)\) is zero for many calibration points, the effective calibration sample size shrinks, risking under‑coverage.  
  – *Empty sets*: For extreme OOD, very small \(g(x)\) can push all scores below the quantile, yielding empty sets; this is desirable only if the energy detector correctly identifies OOD.
- **First three experiments**:  
  1. *Balanced datasets*: Evaluate on CIFAR‑100, ImageNet, Places365 with ResNet/VGG/transformer backbones; compare energy‑enhanced APS/RAPS/SAPS to baselines in terms of coverage and average set size.  
  2. *Imbalanced training*: Train on long‑tailed CIFAR‑100‑LT, calibrate on a balanced set, and measure minority‑class coverage and MacroCov.  
  3. *OOD shift*: Calibrate on CIFAR‑100 and test on Places365; analyze the increase in average set size as a sign of adaptive uncertainty.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Does energy‑based modulation generalize to non‑image data?  
- Can energy integration during training improve conformal adaptiveness?  
- What are coverage guarantees under distribution shift for energy‑based methods?  
- Are there better monotonic transformations of free energy than softplus?

## Limitations
- Exact train/cal/test splits and their balance are not specified; reproduction relies on the defaults in the provided code.  
- Optimal hyperparameter values (softmax temperature \(T\) and free‑energy scale \(\tau\)) after grid search are not reported.  
- The method assumes a well‑trained and reasonably calibrated classifier; its behavior on under‑fitted or miscalibrated models is unexplored.  
- Access to pre‑softmax logits is required, which may not be available for black‑box models.

## Next Checks
1. **Hyperparameter sensitivity analysis**: For each dataset and model, run a dense grid over \(T\) and \(\tau\) to assess performance stability and identify robust default ranges.
2. **Evaluation on poorly calibrated models**: Intentionally degrade calibration (e.g., via label noise or reduced model capacity) and verify whether energy scaling still maintains adaptiveness and coverage.
3. **Black‑box adaptation test**: Approximate logits from softmax outputs using a tuned inverse softmax and compare the resulting set efficiency against the true‑logit baseline.
