# Spatial Calibration of Diffuse LiDARs
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.06531
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06531
- **Reference count:** 9
- **Primary result:** Per-pixel spatial response maps for diffuse LiDAR (TMF8828) show high cross-range consistency (IoU 0.915±0.029, centroid displacement 2.94±0.67 pixels, cosine similarity 0.984±0.008).

## Executive Summary
Diffuse LiDARs, such as the ams OSRAM TMF8828, employ flood illumination and wide per-pixel fields-of-view, causing each pixel's histogram to aggregate photon returns over a broad area of the co-located RGB image. This violates the single-ray assumption underlying standard LiDAR-to-RGB calibration, making it impossible to assign a unique 3D ray to a LiDAR pixel. This paper introduces a method to calibrate such sensors by scanning a high-reflectance retroreflective patch across the shared field of view while capturing synchronized LiDAR histograms and RGB images. Background subtraction isolates the patch-specific signal, yielding a per-pixel spatial response map that characterizes the LiDAR pixel's sensitivity distribution over the RGB image plane. Validation across two ranging modes demonstrates repeatable, robust maps with high agreement (IoU 0.915±0.029, centroid shift 2.94±0.67 px, cosine similarity 0.984±0.008), enabling explicit correspondence between diffuse LiDAR measurements and RGB imagery.

## Method Summary
The calibration procedure uses a robotic arm to move a small retroreflective patch across a dense 2D grid positioned at a fixed distance from the sensors. For each grid location, two synchronized captures are performed: one with the patch present and one without, serving as background. From each capture, raw per-pixel time-of-flight histograms (128 bins) are recorded. For a given LiDAR pixel, the expected patch return time bin is estimated from the mean depth across the grid. A temporal window around this bin is selected; the maximum count within that window, after background subtraction and non-negativity enforcement, provides a scalar response proportional to the spatial sensitivity of that LiDAR pixel at the current RGB pixel location where the patch is detected (via Hough transform). Repeating this over all grid points yields a sparse set of response samples, which are interpolated onto a regular grid to form the complete spatial response map per LiDAR pixel. Normalization (dividing by the maximum response) renders comparable maps. The method is validated by comparing maps obtained in different ranging modes (e.g., short vs long range) using intersection-over-union, centroid displacement, and cosine similarity, under the hypothesis that the spatial kernel should be depth-invariant within a mode.

## Key Results
- Spatial response maps for all 64 pixels (3×3 Wide mode) were reconstructed, revealing varied effective supports and sensitivity patterns.
- Cross-range mode consistency yielded IoU of 0.915±0.029, centroid displacement of 2.94±0.67 pixels, and cosine similarity of 0.984±0.008.
- The method successfully captures per-pixel spatial mixing kernels, enabling explicit LiDAR-to-RGB correspondence despite diffuse IFOV.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The wide instantaneous field-of-view (IFOV) of diffuse LiDARs spatially mixes photon returns from multiple scene points into a single per-pixel histogram, violating the single-ray assumption required for standard LiDAR-RGB calibration.
- **Mechanism:** Flood illumination and large per-pixel angular acceptance cause each histogram to aggregate time-of-flight measurements over a broad region of the RGB image plane, rather than from a single direction. This creates a spatially low-passed, ambiguous measurement that cannot be trivially mapped to a camera pixel.
- **Core assumption:** Standard calibration methods assume each LiDAR pixel corresponds to a well-defined 3D ray projectable into the RGB image. Diffuse LiDARs invalidate this by design.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 1 describe the aggregation over a wide IFOV and mixing of contributions. Corpus signals on "blurred LiDAR" and iToF histogram utilization indicate recognition of the problem class.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Scanning a high-reflectance retroreflective target across the shared field of view allows sampling the spatial sensitivity kernel of each diffuse LiDAR pixel in the RGB coordinate system.
- **Mechanism:** At each robot arm position *k*, the retroreflective patch dominates the returned photon counts within a predictable time-bin window *G* in the LiDAR histogram. The measured response `Rp(uk)` (Eq. 2) is proportional to the unknown spatial weighting `wp(uk)` at that specific RGB pixel location `uk`.
- **Core assumption:** The retroreflective patch provides a high signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) point source in time-of-flight, and its position in the RGB image can be accurately detected. The sensor operates in a linear (non-saturated) regime where response scales with incident photon flux.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 2.4 states that in the linear regime, the retroreflective patch dominates returns within *G*, so `Rp(uk)` is proportional to `wp(uk)`. Section 2.2 describes the scan grid and synchronized capture. Corpus lacks direct citations for this exact technique.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Background subtraction using an identical scan without the patch isolates the patch-specific signal from ambient and structural returns, enabling extraction of the per-pixel spatial response map.
- **Mechanism:** For each scan location *k*, the patch-removed histogram `τ_bg_p,k(t)` estimates ambient and static scene contributions. Subtracting this from the patch-present histogram `τ_p,k(t)` within the window *G* (Eq. 2) yields a scalar response attributable solely to the retroreflector. Aggregating these responses over the scan grid reconstructs `wp(u)` up to scale.
- **Core assumption:** The background (robot arm, room, static objects) is identical between the two scans, and only the retroreflector differs. The patch's temporal response is confined to the selected histogram bins *G*.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 2.2 describes two scans (patch present and patch removed). Section 2.4 defines the scalar response `Rp(uk)` as the max of the background-subtracted, non-negative response within *G*. No direct corpus evidence.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Diffuse dToF LiDAR physics**
  - Why needed: Understanding flood illumination, wide IFOV, and per-pixel histogram aggregation is essential to grasp why standard calibration fails and what the calibration estimates (a spatial mixing kernel, not a ray).
  - Quick check: If a diffuse LiDAR pixel integrates light over a 20° cone, what does a single histogram value represent about the scene?

- **Concept: Photon-arrival histogram processing**
  - Why needed: The method operates on raw histograms (not precomputed depths), selecting a temporal window *G* around the expected patch depth and using peak counts within it. This requires knowing how histograms are built and how to extract a scalar response.
  - Quick check: Why is the `max` over bins *G* used instead of summing all bins in *G* for the response `Rp(uk)`?

- **Concept: Cross-modal spatial correspondence**
  - Why needed: The output is a per-pixel response map in RGB coordinates, which is a form of soft, spatially-varying extrinsic calibration. This differs from traditional point-cloud-to-image projection and requires thinking in terms of kernels and supports.
  - Quick check: If a diffuse LiDAR pixel's response map shows two disjoint hot spots in the RGB image, what does that imply about the pixel's sensitivity?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Hardware Rig (custom 3D-printed mount) -> Scan Apparatus (robotic arm with retroreflective patch) -> Capture Pipeline (synchronized LiDAR histogram and RGB frame acquisition) -> Processing Core (RGB patch detection, histogram background subtraction, windowing, scalar response extraction, grid interpolation, normalization) -> Validation Module (compute IoU, centroid displacement, cosine similarity across modes)
- **Critical path:**
  1. Mount stability: ensure no relative motion between sensors during scans.
  2. Patch detection accuracy: Hough circle detection must reliably locate the retroreflector in RGB images.
  3. Histogram window *G* selection: must align with the estimated patch depth to capture the peak.
  4. Background subtraction quality: requires identical static scene between two scans.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Scan grid density vs. capture time: finer grids improve sampling but increase robot motion time and data volume.
  - Patch size vs. spatial resolution: larger patch blurs the response map; smaller patch improves resolution but may reduce SNR.
  - Histogram window *G* width: wider window tolerates depth uncertainty but includes more background noise; narrower window is cleaner but risks missing signal.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - Low SNR in response maps: sparse/noisy `Rp(uk)` values, indicating patch too small, range too far, or sensor saturation.
  - Asymmetric or irregular support regions: jagged boundaries or unexpected shapes suggest patch detection errors or background mismatch.
  - Inconsistent cross-mode maps: low IoU (<0.8) or high centroid displacement (>5 pixels) between short/long-range calibrations indicates non-repeatable or depth-dependent kernels.
  - Systematic offset in patch detection: uniform shift in all `uk` implies poor rough alignment before calibration.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Verify patch-only response:** In a dark, static environment with only the retroreflector, confirm that `Rp(uk)` forms a coherent single-lobe map per LiDAR pixel centered near the expected optical axis.
  2. **Cross-range consistency test:** Repeat full calibration in both short-range (1.5 m) and long-range (5 m) modes; compute IoU, centroid displacement, and cosine similarity; expect values circa (0.915, 2.94 px, 0.984).
  3. **Background robustness check:** Introduce a controlled, mild change in the background (e.g., move a non-reflective object slightly) between patch-present and patch-removed scans; quantify degradation in response map quality metrics compared to baseline.

## Limitations
- Real-world scene generalizability: method assumes a static, controlled lab environment; performance with moving objects, varying albedo, or multipath interference is unvalidated.
- Depth-dependence of response maps: validity only tested across two ranging modes, not across scene depths within a single mode; kernel might vary with range.
- Corpus evidence gap: limited direct citations validate the retroreflector-scanning technique; its novelty relative to prior work on blurred LiDAR or iToF calibration is not strongly evidenced.

## Confidence
- Problem definition (diffuse LiDAR mixing violates single-ray assumption): **High**
- Method (retroreflector scanning + background subtraction estimates spatial kernels): **Medium**
- Validation metrics (IoU, centroid, cosine similarity): **High**

## Next Checks
1. **Depth-varying kernel test:** Perform calibration at multiple scene depths (e.g., 1 m, 2 m, 3 m) in a fixed mode; measure intra-mode kernel variation via centroid shift and support area change.
2. **Natural scene robustness:** Introduce realistic distractors (e.g., moving foliage, specular reflections) during the two scans; assess degradation in response map SNR and support shape consistency.
3. **Direct correspondence ground truth:** Use a programmable spatial light modulator (e.g., DMD) as a known pattern target; compare estimated `wp(u)` against the ground truth pattern to measure absolute spatial accuracy.
