# Estimating Individual Tree Height and Species from UAV Imagery

## Quick Facts

- arXiv ID: 2603.23669
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23669
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: DINOvTree achieves state-of-the-art height estimation (δ1.25 up to 94.14% on BCI, 89.28% on Quebec Trees) and competitive species classification (macro F1 up to 88.38%) with only 54–58% of the parameters of the second-best approach.

## Executive Summary

Accurate estimation of individual tree height and species from UAV imagery is essential for large-scale forest biomass assessment. This paper introduces BIRCH-Trees, the first benchmark dedicated to these tasks, comprising over 40,000 tree-centered RGB images from temperate, tropical, and boreal forests. The authors also propose DINOvTree, a multi-task model that leverages a Vision Foundation Model (DINOv3) as a backbone and employs separate heads for height regression and species classification. Experiments show that DINOvTree outperforms allometric equations, Mask R-CNN, and other CNN/ViT baselines, achieving high accuracy while using significantly fewer parameters. The work highlights the potential of foundation models for scalable forest monitoring.

## Method Summary

The paper addresses two tasks: height regression and species classification from single 512×512 RGB tiles centered on tree crowns. The BIRCH-Trees benchmark aggregates three datasets—Quebec Trees (22.3K tiles, 14 species), BCI (2.0K, 21 families), and Quebec Plantations (17.7K, 8 species)—with ground truth heights derived from LiDAR canopy height models (CHMs) using a buffered 99th percentile extraction and a linear correction for one dataset. DINOvTree uses a fine-tuned DINOv3 ViT backbone. Two task-specific heads process the patch tokens: each applies an MLP, LayerNorm, and cross-attention with a learned query and 2D sine positional encoding. The height head outputs a scalar via a linear layer and ReLU; the classification head concatenates the query output with the [CLS] token and applies a linear layer with softmax. Training employs a weighted combination of smooth L1 loss (height) and cross-entropy loss (species) with Dynamic Weight Averaging (DWA). Optimization uses AdamW with a cosine learning rate schedule, D4 augmentations, and a backbone learning rate multiplier of 0.5. Model selection averages macro F1 and δ1.25 on a validation set.

## Key Results

- Height estimation: DINOvTree achieves δ1.25 up to 94.14% (BCI) and 89.28% (Quebec Trees), with MAE/RMSE comparable to or better than prior methods.
- Species classification: macro F1 scores reach up to 88.38% on BCI, competitive with state-of-the-art.
- Efficiency: DINOvTree uses only 54–58% of the parameters of the second-best model, demonstrating strong parameter efficiency.
- The benchmark includes diverse forest types and provides a testbed for future research on individual tree trait prediction.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

DINOvTree’s effectiveness stems from three key design choices. First, starting from a powerful vision foundation model pre-trained on large-scale data provides rich visual representations that generalize to UAV imagery, especially when fine-tuned on the target domain. Second, the multi-task architecture with separate heads allows each task to specialize while sharing common features, and the cross-attention mechanism with learned queries focuses on relevant parts of the tree crown, improving both height localization and species discrimination. Third, the Dynamic Weight Averaging loss automatically balances the two tasks during training, preventing one task from dominating and ensuring stable optimization. Together, these elements yield high accuracy with a compact model.

## Architecture Onboarding

Component map: Data (RGB tile) -> DINOv3 backbone (patch tokens + [CLS]) -> (1) Height head: MLP -> LayerNorm -> CrossAttention (query) -> Linear -> ReLU -> Height; (2) Species head: MLP -> LayerNorm -> CrossAttention (query) -> concat([CLS], query output) -> Linear -> Softmax -> Species. Loss: SmoothL1 + CE with DWA -> Backward.

Critical path: The input tile is split into patches and fed through the backbone. The resulting patch tokens are processed independently by the two heads. For height, the head produces a scalar; for species, it produces a distribution over classes. The combined loss updates all trainable parameters.

Design tradeoffs: Fine-tuning the entire backbone vs. freezing it (frozen backbones performed poorly). Using a larger backbone (ViT-L) improves accuracy but increases parameters. DWA simplifies loss weighting but requires a warm-up period. Cross-attention adds computational overhead but allows task-specific token aggregation.

Failure signatures: 
- Height bias: predictions systematically off (check mean signed error). 
- Classification imbalance: rare species have near-zero F1; confusion matrix shows dominance of majority classes. 
- Training instability: one loss plateaus while the other decreases (inspect separate loss curves). 
- Backbone not adapting: gradient norms near zero or backbone LR too high causing oscillation.

First experiments:
1. Validate preprocessing: extract a small set of tiles and compute heights from CHMs; compare with reported distributions; ensure buffering and correction steps are implemented correctly.
2. Train single-task baselines: train height-only and classification-only models (with frozen backbone) to establish task-specific performance ceilings and the benefit of multi-task learning.
3. Ablate cross-attention: replace cross-attention with simple average pooling of patch tokens in both heads and measure impact on δ1.25 and macro F1.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- Rare species identification: How to improve accuracy for underrepresented species given limited labeled data? The authors note high standard errors for rare classes and call for large-scale data collection or advanced few-shot techniques.
- End-to-end biomass estimation: Can we directly predict biomass from UAV imagery, bypassing intermediate height and species steps? This requires new datasets with direct biomass ground truth.
- VFM generalization: How well do fine-tuned VFMs perform on out-of-distribution forest types (e.g., African, Asian forests)? The community is encouraged to use BIRCH-Trees as a testbed for cross-region generalization.
- Semi-supervised learning: Can leveraging abundant unlabeled UAV imagery reduce dependence on limited labeled data? Future work could explore pseudo-labeling, mean teachers, or contrastive learning.
- Multi-modal fusion: Would incorporating DSMs or photogrammetric point clouds with RGB imagery improve predictions, and what fusion strategies are optimal? The paper currently uses only RGB.

## Limitations

- Exact DINOv3 model identifiers (e.g., “ViT-L Sat”) are ambiguous and standard checkpoints may not match the paper’s variants.
- Cross-attention implementation details (query initialization, positional encoding frequencies) are unspecified.
- Spatial train/val/test splits from prior work [72] are not provided; reconstructing them may introduce variance.
- The benchmark covers only temperate, tropical, and boreal forests; generalizability to other biomes is uncertain.
- Height ground truth extraction (buffer size, 99th percentile, linear correction) may be sensitive to parameter choices.
