# KazakhOCR: A Synthetic Benchmark for Evaluating Multimodal Models in Low-Resource Kazakh Script OCR

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.13238
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13238
- Reference count: 5
- Primary result: Synthetic benchmark reveals MLLMs underperform Tesseract on low-resource Kazakh scripts, especially Arabic script (CER 35.5%–72.5% vs. 15.0%) and fail to identify Arabic script as Kazakh (0.0%–1.1% accuracy).

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces KazakhOCR, the first synthetic OCR benchmark for Kazakh in its three scripts (Arabic, Latin, Cyrillic) to evaluate multimodal language models on low-resource script recognition. By generating 7,219 images with controlled variations from authentic corpora, the study assesses three open-source MLLMs (Gemma-3-12B-it, Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct, Llama-3.2-11B-Vision-Instruct) on a 600-image subset and compares them to a Tesseract baseline. Results show MLLMs struggle with Arabic and Latin scripts, achieving high character error rates (up to 72.5%) and nearly zero accuracy in identifying the Arabic script as Kazakh, while Tesseract attains significantly lower error rates across all scripts. The findings highlight a substantial performance gap for current MLLMs on low-resource Abjad-based scripts and suggest that classical OCR remains more effective for such scenarios.

## Method Summary
The authors construct a synthetic dataset by sourcing authentic Kazakh texts in Arabic, Latin, and Cyrillic scripts. These texts are rendered into images using a diverse set of fonts (135 Arabic, 431 Cyrillic, 1,376 Latin) and augmented with color variations (ensuring minimum contrast ratio 4.5), noise levels (4–18), blur (0–0.8), and rotation (−3° to 3°). The dataset comprises 7,219 images (approximately 2,400 per script). For evaluation, a stratified sample of 200 images per script (600 total) is used. Three multimodal LLMs are prompted in a zero‑shot setting to return JSON with language code and transcription. Tesseract OCR is run with pretrained models (ara, eng, rus). Performance is measured via Character Error Rate (CER), Word Error Rate (WER), and, for MLLMs, language identification accuracy against the true ISO code "kk". The study reveals that MLLMs, despite their general multimodal capabilities, lag behind the specialized Tesseract engine on these low‑resource scripts.

## Key Results
- MLLMs exhibit high CER on Arabic script (35.5%–72.5%) and Latin script (21.6%–31.0%).
- Language identification for Arabic script is nearly zero (0.0%–1.1% accuracy), with models misclassifying Kazakh Arabic text as Arabic, Farsi, or Kurdish.
- Tesseract achieves lower CERs across all scripts: Arabic 15.0%, Latin 11.4%, Cyrillic 4.3%, outperforming the best MLLM in each category.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Synthetic data generation as a scalable substitute for real scanned documents.**  
The claim is that a synthetic OCR benchmark can mitigate data scarcity by generating controllable, diverse images. The mechanism: authentic Kazakh text corpora are rendered with varied fonts, colors, noise, and rotations to simulate scanning conditions. Core assumption: these synthetic variations approximate real document degradation and typographic diversity. Evidence: the abstract mentions constructing 7,219 images with variations; Section 2.2 details font filtering (135 Arabic, 431 Cyrillic, 1,376 Latin) and augmentation parameters (noise 4–18, blur 0–0.8, rotation −3° to 3°); similar approaches exist for Pashto and Kashmiri. Break condition: if synthetic images diverge from real document characteristics (e.g., specific paper textures, complex backgrounds), model performance may not transfer.

**Mechanism 2: MLLM script misidentification cascades into transcription errors.**  
The claim is that MLLMs fail on low-resource Abjad scripts partly due to misclassifying the script's linguistic identity. Mechanism: MLLMs, pretrained mainly on high-resource languages, first implicitly identify the script/language; misidentifying Arabic‑script Kazakh as Arabic/Farsi/Kurdish leads to applying inappropriate character sets and segmentation strategies. Core assumption: language identification is a precursor to accurate transcription in MLLMs, and failure cascades. Evidence: abstract states models fail to recognize Arabic script as Kazakh; Table 1 shows Arabic ID accuracy 0.0%–1.1%; Section 4 discusses hallucinations leading to low OCR accuracy. Break condition: if prompting or fine‑tuning separates language identification from transcription and improves CER, the hypothesis gains support.

**Mechanism 3: Classical OCR's script‑specific design yields superior performance.**  
The claim is that script‑specific OCR engines like Tesseract outperform generalist MLLMs on low‑resource scripts. Mechanism: Tesseract uses dedicated LSTM recognizers trained on large script‑specific datasets with explicit modeling of connected glyphs, diacritics, etc., while MLLMs treat text as dense visual patterns without such inductive biases. Core assumption: modular design with script‑tuned components is more robust than end‑to‑end models without script‑aware priors. Evidence: abstract states Tesseract has lower CER; Table 1 shows Tesseract's CERs (Arabic 15.0%, Latin 11.4%, Cyrillic 4.3%) are significantly lower than MLLMs; related work on Arabic‑specialized MLLMs shows they need adaptation. Break condition: if an MLLM architecture modified with script‑specific modules (e.g., separate attention heads for connected glyphs) achieves parity with Tesseract without script‑specific fine‑tuning, the classical advantage would be contingent on training data rather than architecture.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Synthetic Data Generation for Low‑Resource NLP**  
  *Why needed here:* The paper relies entirely on synthetic images due to the absence of real scanned Kazakh Arabic/Latin documents. Understanding the pipeline (corpus → font/noise injection) is crucial to assess benchmark validity and limitations.  
  *Quick check:* What are two ways the synthetic process might fail to capture real‑world document artifacts (e.g., stains, warped pages), and how could those gaps affect model evaluation?

- **Concept: Multiscript and Multilingual OCR Challenges**  
  *Why needed here:* Kazakh uses three distinct scripts with different orthographic rules. A model must handle script‑specific character sets, directionality, and grapheme boundaries. The paper's results show scripts are not equidistant from model capabilities.  
  *Quick check:* Why might a model that performs well on Cyrillic Kazakh still fail on Arabic Kazakh beyond mere character set differences? (Consider connectedness, diacritics, language identification coupling.)

- **Concept: OCR Evaluation Metrics (CER, WER) and Language Identification**  
  *Why needed here:* CER/WER measure transcription accuracy, but the paper also evaluates script‑language identification. Disentangling these is key: a model could have moderate CER but zero correct identification (e.g., transcribing Arabic‑script Kazakh as standard Arabic).  
  *Quick check:* If a model transcribes Kazakh Arabic text perfectly but labels the language as "ar" instead of "kk", is that an OCR failure, a language ID failure, or both? How would you design an evaluation to separate these?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Corpus ingestion -> Text balancing -> Synthetic image rendering -> Model inference -> Metrics computation.

**Critical Path:**  
The most failure‑prone segment is synthetic image rendering; if variations do not match real scans, all downstream model evaluations become questionable.

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- *Synthetic vs. real data:* Synthetic allows scale and control but may miss subtle real‑world artifacts; chosen due to lack of real Kazakh Arabic scans.  
- *Model selection:* Used 7B–12B parameter MLLMs for cost accessibility, sacrificing potential performance of larger proprietary models.  
- *Script coverage:* Balanced sample sizes (200 per script) but original Arabic corpus text length was longer; subsampling may discard rare glyphs.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- CER spikes on Arabic script (>35% for MLLMs) and near‑zero language ID accuracy for Arabic (0–1.1%).  
- Tesseract outperforms all MLLMs on every script, indicating a capability gap for generalist models.  
- High WER for Arabic (>90% for some models) suggests segmentation failures beyond character errors.

**First 3 Experiments:**  
1. **Synthetic robustness test:** Replicate the benchmark with increased noise/rotation bounds to probe whether MLLMs degrade more gracefully than Tesseract under harsher conditions.  
2. **Prompt sensitivity ablation:** Modify the prompt to explicitly request "Kazakh language in Arabic script" vs. generic OCR; measure impact on language ID and CER for Arabic script.  
3. **Real-image pilot:** Collect a small set (~50) of authentic Kazakh Arabic scan images (e.g., from Chinese regional documents) and evaluate models to quantify synthetic–real generalizability gap.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Why do MLLMs fail to correctly identify the Kazakh Arabic script, consistently misclassifying it as Arabic, Farsi, or Kurdish?**  
   *Basis in paper:* The paper reports language identification accuracies of 0.0%–1.1% for the Arabic script and states that models misclassify it as Arabic, Farsi, or Kurdish.  
   *Why unresolved:* The paper does not analyze whether misclassification stems from shared graphemes, insufficient training on Kazakh orthography, or a broader limitation in handling low‑resource Abjad‑based languages.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* An error analysis identifying which specific characters, diacritics, or n‑gram patterns cause misclassification, and testing models with controlled variations that isolate Kazakh‑specific features from common Arabic‑script traits.

2. **To what extent does the synthetic nature of the KazakhOCR benchmark limit the validity of performance comparisons between MLLMs and classical OCR like Tesseract?**  
   *Basis in paper:* The Limitations section notes the benchmark is synthetic and may not capture all characteristics of real‑world documents. The paper compares MLLMs to Tesseract but does not evaluate whether synthetic variations favor traditional OCR.  
   *Why unresolved:* It is unclear if Tesseract's superior CERs generalize to authentic, non‑synthetic Kazakh script images or if synthetic artifacts advantage its segmentation‑based approach.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Evaluating both MLLMs and Tesseract on a small set of authentic Kazakh script images or a separate real‑world benchmark to see if the performance gap persists.

3. **What specific architectural or training data characteristics enable Tesseract to achieve lower CERs than MLLMs on low‑resource Kazakh scripts, despite MLLMs' general multimodal capabilities?**  
   *Basis in paper:* Results show Tesseract outperforms all MLLMs in CER across scripts; the paper hypothesizes MLLMs may "hallucinate" but does not dissect why Tesseract succeeds.  
   *Why unresolved:* The paper contrasts the systems but does not investigate whether Tesseract's advantage comes from script‑specific pretraining data, its segmentation‑first pipeline, or other factors like handling of connected scripts.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* An ablation study comparing MLLM performance when using Tesseract's segmentation masks as input vs. raw images; analysis of Tesseract's training corpus composition for Kazakh‑related scripts.

4. **Can prompt engineering or structured output constraints significantly reduce MLLM OCR errors and improve language identification for low‑resource Kazakh scripts?**  
   *Basis in paper:* The Discussion proposes future work on prompt sensitivity and engineering for optimal OCR results.  
   *Why unresolved:* The study used a single fixed prompt; it is unknown if tailored prompts (e.g., explicitly listing Kazakh‑specific characters or script rules) could close the performance gap with Tesseract or improve language ID.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Systematically testing a range of prompts with varying specificity (e.g., including ISO language codes, script names, or character set hints) and measuring changes in CER and identification accuracy.

5. **How do cultural and orthographic differences between transliterated Latin script Kazakh and authentic Latin‑script Kazakh usage affect OCR performance and language identification?**  
   *Basis in paper:* The paper admits using transliterated Latin texts and notes they may have different uses and cultural differences from the Cyrillic script, though this has not been studied. Models showed mixed Latin script results (CER 21.6%–31.0%, ID accuracy 13.5%–99.4%).  
   *Why unresolved:* It is unclear whether transliteration artifacts (e.g., unnatural grapheme choices) confuse models or if authentic Latin‑script Kazakh would be easier or harder to recognize.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Collecting a small corpus of authentic Latin‑script Kazakh text (e.g., from diaspora communities or official Latin‑alphabet publications) and comparing MLLM performance against the transliterated subset.

## Limitations
- Synthetic image distribution may not capture real‑world artifacts (e.g., paper textures, complex backgrounds), threatening external validity.
- Unevaluated 200‑image subset introduces sampling variability; exact indices are unpublished.
- MLLM selection (7B–12B) may understate capabilities of larger proprietary models.

## Confidence
- Synthetic benchmark efficacy: Medium
- MLLM failure on Arabic script: Low
- Tesseract superiority: Medium

## Next Checks
- **Real‑world pilot:** Collect ~50 authentic Kazakh Arabic/Latin scan images (e.g., historical documents, regional publications) and evaluate top MLLMs vs. Tesseract. Quantify performance delta vs. synthetic results.
- **Prompt ablation:** Re‑run Arabic‑script evaluation with explicit prompts (e.g., “Transcribe Kazakh text in Arabic script”) vs. generic OCR prompt. Measure change in language ID accuracy and CER.
- **Model scaling:** Test a larger MLLM (e.g., GPT‑4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet) on the 600‑image subset. If CER approaches Tesseract’s, the gap may be capacity‑related rather than architectural.
