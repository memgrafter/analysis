# First-Mover Bias in Gradient Boosting Explanations: Mechanism, Detection, and Resolution

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.22346  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22346  
- **Reference count:** 39  
- **Primary result:** DASH stabilizes SHAP explanations under multicollinearity, achieving stability improvements up to +0.61 on real data.

## Executive Summary
Gradient boosting models are prone to a subtle form of instability in SHAP feature importance known as first‑mover bias. When features are highly correlated, the sequential nature of gradient boosting causes the importance to concentrate on whichever feature is selected first in early splits, arbitrarily giving it higher attribution while its correlates are downplayed. This results in unstable rankings across retrained models. The paper introduces DASH (Diverse Aggregated SHAP) as a remedy: it trains a large population of independent gradient boosted models with restricted column sampling, filters to retain only those near the best validation performance, selects a diverse subset of models, and then averages their SHAP values on a held‑out explain set. By breaking the sequential dependency chain, DASH cancels out arbitrary first‑mover selections. On synthetic data with ρ=0.9, DASH reaches a stability of 0.977, outperforming both a single best model (0.958) and a large sequential ensemble (0.938). On the Breast Cancer dataset, stability jumps from 0.32 with a standard model to 0.93 with DASH, without harming predictive accuracy. The work also proposes two diagnostic tools—the Feature Stability Index (FSI) and the Importance‑Stability (IS) Plot—to detect collinearity‑driven bias without ground truth.

## Method Summary
DASH operates in five stages. First, it generates a population of M=200 XGBoost models, each trained with randomly sampled hyperparameters (notably low `colsample_bytree` values between 0.1 and 0.5) and independent random seeds, using early stopping. Second, it performance‑filters this population, keeping only models whose validation score is within a tolerance ε of the best (ε=0.08 absolute for synthetic, 0.05 relative for real data). Third, it selects up to K=30 models from the filtered set via a greedy max‑min diversity criterion on the L2‑normalized feature gain vectors (cosine distance δ≥0.05). Fourth, for each selected model, it computes interventional TreeSHAP values on a held‑out explain set (background size B=100) and averages the resulting SHAP matrices element‑wise to obtain a consensus explanation. Finally, it diagnoses the result using the Feature Stability Index (cross‑model SHAP standard deviation divided by mean absolute SHAP) and the IS Plot, which visualizes importance versus FSI to highlight collinear clusters. Baselines include a single best model, a large sequential model, stochastic retrain (averaging over many random seeds without diversity selection), and random selection.

## Key Results
- On synthetic data with within‑group correlation ρ=0.9, DASH achieves a mean stability (pairwise Spearman of global importance across 20 repetitions) of **0.977**, compared to **0.958** for a single best model and **0.938** for a large sequential model with ∼15 000 trees.
- On the Breast Cancer dataset, DASH raises SHAP stability from **0.32** (single best) to **0.93**, a gain of **+0.61**, while maintaining comparable predictive AUC.
- DASH and Stochastic Retrain (seed averaging) yield statistically equivalent stability (~0.977), confirming that model independence, not any specific design choice of DASH, resolves first‑mover bias.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Sequential Residual Dependency Creates First‑Mover Bias
**Claim:** In gradient boosting, the sequential fitting of trees to residuals creates a path‑dependent concentration of SHAP importance on an arbitrary feature within correlated groups whenever that feature is selected first in early splits.  
**Core assumption:** Multicollinearity and sequential residual fitting cause the effect; the magnitude grows with tree count T and correlation strength ρ.  
**Mechanism:** When a tree splits on feature *j* from a correlated set, it partially removes *j*’s signal from the residuals. Subsequent trees find both *j* and its correlates less useful, but *j* retains a slight residual advantage from its own partial fit. Over many trees, this “first‑mover advantage” accumulates, causing SHAP values to concentrate on whichever feature was arbitrarily chosen first.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] “First‑mover bias in gradient boosting causes SHAP feature importance to concentrate on an arbitrary member of correlated features due to sequential residual fitting.”  
- [section 3.3] Formal hypothesis for a correlated pair with ρ→1: E[|ϕj|] > E[|ϕk|] if *j* is first‑split, with the gap increasing in tree count T.  
- [section 6.1] The Large Single Model (∼15 000 sequential trees) achieves the poorest stability (0.938 at ρ=0.9) despite matching DASH’s total tree count, confirming that sequential dependency amplifies instability.  
**Break condition:** The bias is neutralized when explained models are trained independently; DASH and Stochastic Retrain both achieve high stability by averaging across independent models.

### Mechanism 2: Model Independence Resolves First‑Mover Bias via Averaging
**Claim:** Training multiple independent gradient‑boosted models and averaging their SHAP values cancels arbitrary first‑mover selections, distributing importance proportionally across correlated features.  
**Core assumption:** Independence between explained models is sufficient; the specific aggregation strategy (e.g., diversity selection) is secondary.  
**Mechanism:** Independent models randomize which feature from a correlated group is selected first (via hyperparameter variation, random seeds, or column sampling). The ensemble’s average SHAP matrix therefore attributes importance roughly equally to all group members, provided each model’s arbitrary choice is independent.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] “DASH addresses this by training many independent models with restricted column sampling, selecting a diverse subset, and averaging their SHAP values, thereby breaking the sequential dependency.”  
- [section 6.2] Stochastic Retrain and DASH both achieve stability 0.977 at ρ=0.9, with no significant difference in accuracy or equity (Wilcoxon p>0.6). Independent methods consistently outperform dependent ones (Single Best, LSM).  
- [corpus] Neighboring papers focus on gradient boosting stability or feature selection but do not address first‑mover bias; evidence here is drawn from controlled experiments.  
**Break condition:** If models are not independent (e.g., one large sequential ensemble), averaging does not resolve the bias; LSM performs worst.

### Mechanism 3: Diagnostic Tools Detect Collinearity‑Driven Instability Without Ground Truth
**Claim:** The Feature Stability Index (FSI) and Importance‑Stability (IS) Plot can identify features affected by first‑mover bias using only the ensemble of SHAP values, without requiring true feature importance.  
**Core assumption:** First‑mover bias causes high variance in SHAP values for features that compete in early splits; FSI captures this variance relative to importance.  
**Mechanism:**  
- **FSI** = (mean cross‑model SHAP standard deviation) / (mean absolute SHAP value). High FSI indicates a feature’s attributions vary widely across models—typical of collinear group members that are arbitrarily the “first mover.”  
- **IS Plot** scatters features by mean importance (x‑axis) vs. FSI (y‑axis), partitioning into four quadrants:  
  - Quadrant I (high importance, low FSI): robust drivers.  
  - Quadrant II (high importance, high FSI): collinear cluster members—important but unstable.  
  - Quadrant III (low importance, low FSI): irrelevant features.  
  - Quadrant IV (low importance, high FSI): fragile or noisy features.  
Quadrant II flags features whose individual rankings are unreliable due to first‑mover bias.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [section 4.5] Definition of FSI and IS Plot quadrants.  
- [section 6.4] On Breast Cancer, radius/perimeter/area show high FSI (>1.2) while robust drivers like mean concave points have FSI ~0.9, matching known collinearity.  
**Break condition:** If all features have low FSI, first‑mover bias is likely absent; high FSI on low‑importance features (Quadrant IV) suggests other instability sources.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**:  
Population Generation → Performance Filtering → Diversity Selection → Consensus SHAP → Stability Diagnostics

**Critical Path**: The pipeline’s effectiveness hinges on two factors: (1) model independence in the initial population and (2) performance filtering to exclude poor models that would add noise. Diversity selection (via max‑min) improves equity (more balanced importance within collinear groups) but is not strictly necessary for stability; random selection from the filtered set yields comparable stability. The consensus aggregation (averaging SHAP matrices) is the core step that cancels arbitrary first‑mover choices.

**Design Tradeoffs**:  
- **Cost vs. Stability**: DASH trains M=200 models and computes SHAP for up to K=30, making it ∼3× more expensive than a Single Best baseline. Stochastic Retrain (averaging over all filtered models without diversity selection) can be ∼5.4× more expensive. The trade‑off is higher stability, especially under high ρ.  
- **Diversity Selection**: Max‑min selection adds a modest stability gain over random selection but significantly improves equity (lower within‑group coefficient of variation). If only stability matters, random selection from the filtered population suffices.  
- **ε Threshold**: The performance tolerance ε determines how many models survive filtering. Larger ε retains more models but may include lower‑quality ones; the paper reports stability robust across ε∈[0.03,0.10] (synthetic absolute) and ε=0.05 relative (real).  

**Failure Signatures**:  
- **Low stability improvement despite DASH**: Check correlation level (ρ<0.7 may not need DASH) and nonlinearity (nonlinear DGPs reduce overall stability). Also verify ensemble diversity: if all models have nearly identical feature utilization vectors, the population may be too narrow (increase M or lower `colsample_bytree`).  
- **High FSI on important features**: Indicates persistent first‑mover bias; consider increasing M or lowering `colsample_bytree` to force more diversity.  
- **Quadrant II features in IS Plot**: These are collinear cluster members; their individual rankings are unreliable. Treat them as a group rather than ordering them.  
- **Predictive performance drop**: If filtered models are too numerous (high ε) or too diverse (very low `colsample_bytree`), validation scores may suffer. Monitor validation scores during filtering and adjust ε or hyperparameter ranges.  

**First 3 Experiments**:  
1. **Mechanism validation (Section 6.1)**: Compare DASH, Single Best, and Large Single Model across increasing correlation levels ρ∈{0,0.5,0.7,0.9,0.95}. Expect LSM stability to decrease monotonically with ρ, while DASH remains flat—isolating sequential dependency as the driver.  
2. **Independence principle (Section 6.2)**: At ρ=0.9, compare dependent (Single Best, LSM) vs. independent (DASH, Stochastic Retrain, Random Selection) methods. DASH and Stochastic Retrain should achieve statistically equivalent stability (~0.977), significantly higher than dependent baselines.  
3. **Real‑world validation (Section 6.5)**: On the Breast Cancer dataset, run Single Best (M=200) vs. DASH. Expect stability to jump from ~0.32 to ~0.93. Verify that DASH does not degrade predictive RMSE. Use the IS Plot to identify which features fall into Quadrant II (collinear cluster).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Question 1:** Does model independence alone (as exhibited by random forests) resolve first‑mover bias without the need for DASH’s aggregation across models?  
- **Basis in paper:** “Random forests train trees independently, predicting higher baseline explanation stability without any DASH‑like pipeline. Including an RF baseline would provide a direct test of the independence principle on a model family that is independent by construction. This comparison is planned for the journal version.”  
- **Why unresolved:** The paper evaluates only gradient boosting methods; random forests (independent trees by design) are not included. Hence it is unknown whether independence at the model‑training level (without post‑hoc aggregation) suffices.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Train random forests on the same synthetic and real‑world datasets, compute TreeSHAP values for each forest, and compare stability against DASH and single‑model GBMs. If random forests achieve comparable stability, independence alone is key; otherwise DASH’s additional design choices contribute.

**Question 2:** Can DASH be efficiently extended to produce stable SHAP interaction values for high‑dimensional datasets?  
- **Basis in paper:** “The current pipeline averages SHAP value matrices Φ(i)∈RN′×P, which preserves main effects but not pairwise interaction structure. However, TreeSHAP supports exact interaction values via tensors Φ(i)_int∈RN′×P×P … Averaging these tensors element‑wise across the ensemble would yield stable interaction estimates by the same independence argument. The computational cost is O(TLD^2) per model … making this practical for moderate P but expensive for large feature spaces.”  
- **Why unresolved:** The extension is identified as “concrete near‑term” but not implemented due to computational concerns; feasibility and stability benefits remain untested.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement tensor‑averaging within DASH and evaluate on synthetic data with known pairwise interactions, varying P. Measure stability of interaction rankings across repeated DASH runs and assess computational cost and scalability (e.g., with sampling approximations).

**Question 3:** How sensitive is DASH’s stability and diagnostic reliability to the size of the background dataset used in interventional TreeSHAP?  
- **Basis in paper:** “All experiments use B = 100 background samples for interventional TreeSHAP. For high‑dimensional datasets with strong correlation structure, this may be insufficient to capture the joint distribution faithfully. A sensitivity analysis over B is deferred to the journal version.”  
- **Why unresolved:** The background size B may impact SHAP quality under multicollinearity; no analysis exists to confirm that B=100 is sufficient.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Run DASH with varying B (e.g., 50, 100, 200, 500) on highly collinear datasets (e.g., Breast Cancer, ρ=0.9 synthetic) and assess changes in (i) stability of global rankings, (ii) Feature Stability Index values, and (iii) IS Plot quadrant assignments. Determine whether B=100 lies on a plateau or if larger B significantly alters conclusions.

## Limitations
- **Generalizability across implementations:** The study tests only XGBoost; potential differences in LightGBM or CatBoost (e.g., handling of categorical features, leaf‑wise growth) may affect first‑mover bias and DASH’s efficacy.  
- **Real‑world validation scope:** Only three datasets (Breast Cancer, Superconductor, California Housing) are used; broader evaluation across more diverse high‑collinearity domains is needed to confirm robustness.  
- **Heuristic diagnostic thresholds:** The quadrants of the IS Plot and the FSI thresholds are chosen heuristically; their false positive/negative rates for detecting collinearity‑driven instability are not quantified.
