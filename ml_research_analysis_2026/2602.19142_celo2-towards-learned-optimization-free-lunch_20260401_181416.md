# Celo2: Towards Learned Optimization Free Lunch

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2602.19142
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19142
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Meta-trained for only 4.5 GPU hours on small image tasks, Celo2 generalizes zero‑shot to billion‑scale language models (GPT‑3 XL 1.3B), outperforming AdamW and VeLO in validation loss.

## Executive Summary
Celo2 introduces a learned optimizer that achieves strong generalization to large‑scale tasks with minimal meta‑training compute. Unlike prior learned optimizers (e.g., VeLO) that require thousands of TPU‑months and fail on billion‑parameter models, Celo2 uses a simple normalized MLP architecture, Persistent Evolution Strategies (PES) for efficient meta‑gradient estimation, and task augmentation to expose the optimizer to diverse parameter scales. Meta‑trained on 8×8 image classification tasks for just 4.5 GPU hours, Celo2 matches or exceeds AdamW and VeLO on a range of targets: GPT‑3 XL (1.3B) language modeling, ImageNet training with Vision Transformers, and Atari PPO. The design is also compatible with orthogonalization and decoupled weight decay, and ablations confirm the necessity of RMS normalization, orthogonalization, and task augmentation.

## Method Summary
Celo2 is an Optax‑compatible transformation that replaces the hand‑designed update rule with a small learned MLP. For each parameter tensor, the optimizer constructs a feature vector consisting of the current gradient, parameter values, and several running statistics: three momentum accumulators (β=0.9,0.99,0.999), a RMS accumulator (β=0.95), and Adafactor‑style row/column second‑moment estimates. All features are normalized by their per‑tensor RMS. The MLP (2 layers, 8 hidden units, ReLU) processes these normalized features and outputs a raw update, which is then scaled by its own RMS norm to produce a unit‑norm direction. An optional Newton‑Schulz orthogonalization is applied to 2‑D+ parameters. The update is multiplied by a user‑tuned global learning rate and combined with decoupled weight decay.

Meta‑training is performed via Persistent Evolution Strategies (PES) on a suite of small image datasets (MNIST, Fashion‑MNIST, CIFAR‑10, SVHN) all resized to 8×8. The inner loop trains a small MLP classifier for up to 2000 steps, with the unroll broken into segments of 50 updates. At each segment boundary, an unbiased meta‑gradient is estimated using ES, and the learned MLP is updated without resetting the optimizer state, thus accumulating perturbations across segments. Cru­ially, task augmentation multiplies all initial parameters by a log‑uniform scalar α∈[0.001,1000], forcing the optimizer to handle a wide range of scales. Meta‑training runs for 100K iterations on 8 parallel tasks, taking only 4.5 GPU hours.

## Key Results
- **Compute‑efficient meta‑training**: 4.5 GPU hours vs. VeLO’s 4000 TPU‑months; PES enables truncated unrolls with unbiased gradients.
- **Zero‑shot generalization**: On GPT‑3 XL (1.3B) language modeling, Celo2 achieves lower validation loss than tuned AdamW and VeLO; also scales to 50K‑step ImageNet ViT training and Atari PPO.
- **Architecture benefits**: RMS normalization stabilizes meta‑training; task augmentation is essential for scale robustness; orthogonalization (Newton‑Schulz) provides additional gains and is compatible with decoupled weight decay.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: RMS Normalization
- **Claim**: RMS normalization of the learned MLP’s output produces a stable, scale‑invariant update rule that generalizes to large models and long unrolls.
- **Mechanism**: The MLP outputs a raw parameter update; dividing by its per‑tensor RMS magnitude yields a unit‑norm direction. This decouples step size from direction, letting a global learning rate control magnitude. The MLP is forced to learn only the direction, promoting task‑invariant behavior and dynamics similar to AdamW.
- **Core assumption**: The optimal update direction is independent of parameter scale; magnitude can be handled separately.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  [abstract] “crafting a simple normalized optimizer architecture”  
  [section 3] “simply normalizing the outputs of the MLP update… results in a similar optimization dynamics as AdamW”  
  [section A, Table 2] Ablation: “norm” (per‑step RMS) achieves lowest validation loss (3.812) vs. raw output (3.962)  
  [corpus] Corpus shows related normalization in optimizers (e.g., Muon), but direct evidence for this specific learned‑optimizer normalization is weak.
- **Break condition**: Removing RMS normalization leads to unstable meta‑training and poor generalization, as seen in ablations without normalization.

### Mechanism 2: Persistent Evolution Strategies (PES)
- **Claim**: PES reduces meta‑training compute by orders of magnitude by enabling frequent meta‑updates on truncated unrolls while keeping gradient estimates unbiased.
- **Mechanism**: PES splits the inner unroll (length T) into short segments (length K). After each segment, meta‑gradients are estimated via ES, and the learned optimizer is updated. Perturbations to the optimizer are accumulated over time and inner states persist across segments, ensuring unbiasedness despite truncation.
- **Core assumption**: The meta‑gradient of a long unroll is the sum of contributions from shorter segments if perturbations accumulate and states carry over.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  [abstract] “using persistent evolution strategies”  
  [section 3] “PES yields unbiased gradients with truncated training… This is much more computationally efficient than ES (used in VeLO)”  
  [section D] Formal PES description  
  [corpus] Corpus mentions ES‑based meta‑learning (e.g., μLO) but does not directly index PES; evidence is indirect.
- **Break condition**: Using full‑unroll ES (as in VeLO) incurs prohibitive compute (~4000 TPU‑months), making large‑scale meta‑training infeasible.

### Mechanism 3: Task Augmentation
- **Claim**: Task‑augmentation by random parameter scaling during meta‑training exposes the optimizer to diverse magnitudes, improving generalization across model scales.
- **Mechanism**: At the start of each inner task, initial parameters θ₀ are multiplied by a log‑uniform scalar α ∈ [0.001, 1000]. This rescales the loss landscape, forcing the learned optimizer to handle a wide range of parameter magnitudes.
- **Core assumption**: The update rule can be made scale‑robust through exposure to varied scales during meta‑training.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  [abstract] “augmenting meta‑training”  
  [section 3] “task augmentation… to simulate a diverse range of tasks… enhances generalization”  
  [section 5, Table 1c] Validation loss drops from 4.417 → 3.812 with task augmentation  
  [corpus] Neighbor paper “Celo: Training Versatile Learned Optimizers on a Compute Diet” also uses task augmentation, supporting its role.
- **Break condition**: Without task augmentation, the learned optimizer overfits to the narrow scale distribution of meta‑training tasks (8×8 images) and fails on models billions of times larger.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Celo2 → holds learned MLP (`theta`) and decay constants. CeloOptaxState → stores rolling accumulators: `mom_rolling` (3× momentum), `rms_rolling` (RMS), `fac_rolling` (Adafactor row/col features). The MLP (`mlp_apply`) consumes per‑tensor features (param, grad, mom, rms, fac outputs) and returns a raw update. Steps: 1. Clip gradients ([-1000, 1000]). 2. Update rolling accumulators with current gradient. 3. For each tensor, build feature vector using `params`, `grads`, updated accumulators. 4. Apply MLP → raw update. 5. Normalize update by its RMS norm. 6. Orthogonalize if enabled. 7. Apply LR and weight decay. 8. Update parameters, increment step counter.

### Critical Path
The update proceeds through feature normalization, MLP inference, output normalization, optional orthogonalization, and finally parameter application. The global learning rate controls step size, while decoupled weight decay is applied separately.

### Design Tradeoffs
- Normalization: Per‑step RMS (default) works best; rolling RMS slightly worse.
- Orthogonalization: Adds ~30% compute overhead but improves performance; may be disabled for speed.
- MLP hidden size: 8 units optimal (vs. 4 or 16) for performance‑efficiency trade‑off.
- RMS decay β: 0.95 best for LMs; Adam uses 0.999, but shorter memory helps.
- Memory: ~5× overhead due to multiple accumulators (vs. Adam’s 3×); mitigable with FSDP.

### Failure Signatures
- NaNs during meta‑training: unstable MLP outputs; check normalization/clipping.
- Poor generalization on large models: missing task augmentation or RMS norm; verify meta‑training recipe.
- Excess memory: incorrect accumulator usage (should have 3 momentum + 1 RMS + Adafactor features).
- Slow wall‑time: orthogonalization overhead; consider disabling if not needed.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Can we meta‑learn a general learning rate scheduler that eliminates user‑tuned step sizes across tasks?**  
   *Basis*: [explicit] The authors state: "We keep the step size tunable as per the user and leave the learning of a general scheduler for all tasks to future work."  
   *Why unresolved*: Current Celo2 requires per‑task learning rate tuning; a learned scheduler could further reduce manual intervention and improve robustness.  
   *What evidence would resolve it*: A Celo2 variant that includes a meta‑learned scheduler trained jointly, evaluated zero‑shot on diverse large‑scale tasks without any learning rate tuning, matching or exceeding the best tuned results.

2. **Does meta‑training Celo2 on a more diverse set of architectures (e.g., CNNs, RNNs, SSMs) improve its generalization to unseen model families beyond Transformers?**  
   *Basis*: [inferred] The meta‑training tasks consist solely of small multilayer perceptrons (MLP) on 8x8 image data, and evaluation is limited to Transformer‑based language models, ViT, and MLP policies. The paper does not explore whether the learned optimizer transfers to non‑Transformer architectures.  
   *Why unresolved*: Meta‑training on only MLPs may bias the learned optimizer toward specific activation patterns and gradient statistics, potentially limiting cross‑architecture generalization. Understanding this boundary is crucial for a truly general‑purpose optimizer.  
   *What evidence would resolve it*: A thorough empirical study measuring Celo2’s performance when meta‑trained on a distribution including CNNs (e.g., ResNet on CIFAR‑10), RNNs (e.g., LSTM on sequential data), and SSMs (e.g., Mamba on audio), then testing on held‑out architectures like Vision Transformers and large language models. Success would be demonstrated by comparable performance to AdamW or Muon on both seen and unseen architectures.

3. **What is the theoretical mechanism by which output normalization (RMS) in Celo2’s learned MLP stabilizes training and enables scale‑free generalization?**  
   *Basis*: [explicit] The ablations in Table 1(d) show that RMS normalization during meta‑training significantly improves validation loss, and the paper emphasizes that this normalization is key to Celo2’s performance. However, no theoretical explanation is provided.  
   *Why unresolved*: While empirical results confirm the benefit, the underlying reason—whether it relates to gradient scale invariance, matching the scale of AdamW updates, or preventing explosion/vanishing in the MLP—remains unknown. A theory would inform design of future learned optimizers and help diagnose failures.  
   *What evidence would resolve it*: A formal analysis linking the normalized learned update to properties like Lipschitz continuity, scale invariance, or correspondence with preconditioned gradient descent. Alternatively, controlled experiments isolating the effect of normalization on update magnitude distributions across layers would shed light.

4. **Can Celo2’s learned update rule be effectively combined with second‑order preconditioners like Shampoo or SOAP while maintaining computational efficiency?**  
   *Basis*: [inferred] The paper demonstrates compatibility with orthogonalization and distinct update rules (e.g., Adam for 1D parameters), and mentions recent advances like Muon. However, it does not test combinations with full second‑order optimizers such as Shampoo or SOAP. The claim “compatible with modern optimization harness” is broad but empirical evidence is limited.  
   *Why unresolved*: Integrating learned updates with more sophisticated preconditioning could further improve per‑parameter adaptation, but the interaction might break generalization or increase memory overhead. This could limit the practical utility of Celo2 in settings where Shampoo‑like methods are already state‑of‑the‑art.  
   *What evidence would resolve it*: Implement a hybrid optimizer that applies Celo2’s learned MLP as the core update, then wraps it with Shampoo’s Kronecker product preconditioning or SOAP’s modular norm adaptation. Compare large‑scale training (e.g., GPT‑3 scale) of such hybrids against pure Celo2, pure Shampoo, and Muon. Demonstration of consistent gains would resolve the question.
