# KNIGHT: Knowledge Graph-Driven Multiple-Choice Question Generation with Adaptive Hardness Calibration

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.20135  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20135  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: KNIGHT generates high-quality, difficulty‑controlled MCQs from a reusable knowledge graph, achieving <5% flag rates and calibrated difficulty with ~600 tokens per question; model rankings on KNIGHT correlate with MMLU.

## Executive Summary
KNIGHT addresses the bottleneck of creating evaluation datasets for LLMs and RAG systems by constructing a reusable, topic‑specific knowledge graph (KG) from external sources like Wikipedia. This KG avoids repeated ingestion of source documents for each question. The framework generates multiple‑choice questions (MCQs) by traversing multi‑hop paths in the KG, using path depth as a knob to control difficulty. Questions are validated on five criteria (grammar, unambiguity, option uniqueness, answerability, topic relevance) using an LLM critic.  

Empirical results show that KNIGHT produces high‑quality MCQs with low human flag rates (<5%), and difficulty calibration is confirmed by rising predictive entropy and decreasing probe accuracy across depth levels. The approach is token‑efficient (~600 tokens per question) and model rankings on KNIGHT correlate strongly with MMLU. Generation completes in minutes on a T4 GPU, enabling rapid creation of refreshable, topic‑specific benchmarks.

## Method Summary
KNIGHT operates as a four‑stage pipeline.  
(i) **KG Construction**: Given a topic, the system retrieves relevant documents (e.g., Wikipedia), synthesizes parsimonious entity descriptions, induces triples (subject‑relation‑object), and applies curation (type checks, NLI entailment, policy rules) to produce a clean, depth‑bounded KG via BFS.  
(ii) **MCQ Generation**: For each sampled multi‑hop path in the KG (forward or reverse), an LLM synthesizes a question, correct answer, and plausible distractors. The path length directly controls the declared difficulty level.  
(iii) **Difficulty Calibration**: Path depth and abstraction level serve as an explicit hardness knob; predictive entropy of a fixed probe model and probe accuracy are used to verify that deeper paths yield harder questions.  
(iv) **Validation**: An LLM validator enforces five quality criteria (grammatical fluency, single‑correct‑key unambiguity, option uniqueness, answerability from the provided evidence, and topic relevance). Only items passing all checks are retained. All LLM calls use GPT‑4o‑mini.

## Key Results
- **Token efficiency**: ~600 tokens per question vs. naive RAG’s repeated context loading, enabling cost‑effective benchmark creation.  
- **High quality**: Human audits show <5% flag rates for grammar, ambiguity, off‑topic, and unanswerable items.  
- **Calibrated difficulty**: Predictive entropy increases from Level 1 to 3 (e.g., Math: 0.023→0.083) and probe accuracy drops, confirming that depth controls hardness.  
- **Valid model rankings**: KNIGHT’s model ordering (GPT‑4o > Mistral Large > Llama3‑70B) correlates with MMLU scores.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
KNIGHT compresses topical knowledge into a structured graph, which serves as a compact, reusable source of facts. Multi‑hop path traversal naturally yields reasoning chains of varying lengths, providing a direct handle on question difficulty. The LLM validator ensures that generated questions are well‑formed, grounded in the graph, and free of common flaws. By leveraging a single, capable model (GPT‑4o‑mini) for all stages, the pipeline remains simple yet effective. The combination of a grounded KG, difficulty‑controlling traversal, and rigorous validation yields high‑quality MCQs with minimal token overhead.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Input Topic → Build Knowledge Graph → Generate MCQs (via Path Traversal) → Calibrate Difficulty → Validate (LLM Critic) → Output Dataset  

**Critical path**:  
1. KG construction must produce a connected graph with at least one multi‑hop path of the desired depth.  
2. MCQ generation must produce a question answerable from that path.  
3. Validation must pass all five criteria.  

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- *KG depth vs. coherence*: deeper paths increase difficulty but risk convoluted or nonsensical questions.  
- *Single LLM (GPT‑4o‑mini) vs. specialized models*: cost‑effective but may not optimize each stage.  
- *In‑memory vs. Neo4j storage*: in‑memory is simpler for small topics; Neo4j adds scalability but infrastructure overhead.  
- *Reusability vs. freshness*: a static KG reduces tokens and ensures consistency but may become outdated for dynamic domains.  

**Failure signatures**:  
- Sparse KG (few nodes/edges) → limited or unanswerable questions.  
- High validation failure rate (e.g., many ANSWERABLE flags) → weak grounding or poor generation.  
- Flat entropy across difficulty levels → path sampling or calibration broken.  
- Specific flag clusters (e.g., OPTION_UNIQUENESS) → distractor generation issues.  

**First three experiments**:  
1. Install KNIGHT and verify connectivity (set `NEO4J_URI`, `NEO4J_USER`, `NEO4J_PASS`, `OPENAI_API_KEY`; test with `backend="memory"`).  
2. Build a KG for a test topic (e.g., “Biology”) with shallow depth (e.g., `depth=2`); inspect graph completeness.  
3. Generate a small dataset (e.g., 10 MCQs) and run validation; examine pass rates and entropy values.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How does KNIGHT's performance generalize when instantiated on ontologies and domains beyond Wikipedia/Wikidata, particularly in fields with low relational density (e.g., Physics or numerical computation)?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state in the Limitations (Sec. A) that their evaluation is restricted to Wikipedia/Wikidata and three domains, and they note that "our findings may not fully generalize to domains with low relational density, such as Physics... where knowledge is often encoded in numerical constants and first‑principle equations rather than explicit entity‑relation triples."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper’s framework claims to be domain‑ and ontology‑agnostic, but this is not empirically validated. The KG‑driven approach might be less effective or require significant adaptation for domains where knowledge is not primarily structured as interconnected entities and relations.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A systematic evaluation of KNIGHT on multiple non‑Wikipedia corpora (e.g., scientific textbooks, legal documents, code repositories) and alternative structured ontologies (e.g., domain‑specific schemas in medicine or engineering), measuring MCQ quality, validity, and difficulty calibration against baselines.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can the residual hallucination rate (unanswerable items) be reduced below the current non‑zero baseline, and what is the most effective combination of grounding, validation, and KG techniques for minimizing it?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The Limitations (Sec. A) acknowledge that "Residual hallucination... remains non‑zero" despite grounding and validation. Table 2 shows KNIGHT’s ANSWERABLE rate is low but not zero across all topics and levels.
- **Why unresolved:** While KNIGHT reduces hallucinations compared to baselines (Plain/RAG), the exact cause of remaining unanswerable items is not isolated. It could stem from imperfect KG construction, relation extraction errors, or validator limitations. The paper does not perform an error analysis to pinpoint the primary failure mode.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A detailed error analysis of all flagged ANSWERABLE items, categorizing failures (e.g., incorrect KG triple, faulty description synthesis, validator oversight). Then, targeted ablations of specific curation steps (e.g., stricter NLI thresholds, alternative LLM critics) to measure impact on hallucination rate.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What are the specific linguistic or cognitive factors (beyond KG multi‑hop depth) that contribute to perceived difficulty in KNIGHT‑generated questions, and can they be modeled for finer‑grained hardness control?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper uses KG path length/depth as the primary "hardness knob" and validates it with entropy and probe accuracy. However, the Limitations (Sec. A) note that "Difficulty is multi‑factorial. Our primary hardness control relies on KG‑based signals... but linguistic complexity and domain prerequisites can also affect hardness." This indicates an unstated assumption that KG depth is the dominant factor.
- **Why unresolved:** The correlation between depth and difficulty is shown empirically, but the paper does not decompose difficulty into its constituents (e.g., vocabulary rarity, syntactic complexity, required prior knowledge). This limits the precision of difficulty calibration and may explain variance within the same depth level.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A study where human experts rate generated questions on multiple difficulty dimensions (e.g., linguistic complexity, prerequisite knowledge, reasoning steps). Use regression analysis to identify which factors best predict human‑rated difficulty and model entropy/probe accuracy, then incorporate these signals into the generation process.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How robust is KNIGHT's difficulty calibration and quality to variations in the base LLM (e.g., using different models for extraction, generation, or validation), and does model choice interact with the KG's benefits?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper consistently uses GPT‑4o‑mini for all stages due to cost and reproducibility, explicitly stating in the Limitations (Sec. A): "Model choice. KNIGHT is modular... we use a single model (GPT‑4o‑mini) for all LLM calls; we do not perform an exhaustive, task‑wise model selection study."
- **Why unresolved:** The framework’s modularity suggests different models could be used, but the interplay between model capability and KG‑guided generation is unknown. Weaker models might produce lower‑quality KGs or fail to leverage paths effectively, while stronger models might reduce the relative benefit of the KG structure. This affects the framework’s portability and cost‑quality trade‑offs.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An ablation study varying the LLM for each stage (description synthesis, relation extraction, MCQ generation, validation) across models of different scales (e.g., GPT‑4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Llama 3.1 70B, smaller 7B models). Measure impacts on KG density/validity, MCQ quality metrics, and difficulty calibration stability.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Does the use of a compressed, reusable KG representation (vs. naive RAG) provide a statistically significant advantage in preserving model‑ranking validity when evaluating on highly dynamic or rapidly evolved topics?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper champions KG reusability for "token‑ and cost‑efficient generation" and producing "refreshable" benchmarks. It shows model rankings on KNIGHT correlate with MMLU (Table 5). However, it does not test whether the KG’s static snapshot (built once) becomes stale for fast‑moving domains, potentially degrading benchmark validity compared to frequently re‑ingested RAG contexts.
- **Why unresolved:** The case studies are on Wikipedia topics (History, Biology, Math), which are relatively stable. For topics like "current AI research" or "recent geopolitical events," a KG built today may quickly become outdated. The paper assumes KG reusability is always beneficial, but this might not hold for time‑sensitive domains where fresh evidence is critical.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Build comparative benchmarks for a dynamic topic (e.g., "2024 U.S. Presidential Election" or "latest LLM architectures") using three methods: (1) KNIGHT with a static KG built from sources at time T₀, (2) naive RAG that re‑retrieves fresh documents for each question, (3) a "refreshed KG" that periodically updates. Compare model ranking stability, human‑assessed answerability, and correlation with a manually curated, time‑aware gold standard over time.

## Limitations
- **Exact prompting**: The precise prompt templates and few‑shot examples are not version‑controlled, risking deviation from paper results.  
- **Curation specifics**: The exact Wikidata type mapping, NLI model, and entailment threshold for the curation function ϕ are not fully specified, potentially affecting KG precision.  
- **Implementation parity**: The public PyPI/GitHub release may differ from the paper’s implementation, which could alter metrics like validation pass rates.
