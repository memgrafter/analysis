# MIDST Challenge at SaTML 2025: Membership Inference over Diffusion-models-based Synthetic Tabular data

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.19185
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19185
- Reference count: 31
- Primary result: Membership inference attacks achieved up to 46% TPR at 10% FPR on diffusion-model synthetic tabular data, revealing significant privacy leakage.

## Executive Summary
The MIDST challenge at SaTML 2025 evaluated privacy risks of synthetic tabular data generated by diffusion models. Participants performed membership inference attacks (MIAs) across four tracks: black/white-box and single/multi-table settings. Using the Berka financial dataset and models like TabDDPM, TabSyn, and ClavaDDPM, the competition provided 30 shadow models to aid attack development. The results showed non-negligible attack success, with the best white-box single-table attack reaching 46% TPR at 10% FPR, challenging the common assumption that synthetic data automatically anonymizes individuals.

The challenge highlighted that diffusion models can leak membership information through various signals such as loss trajectories, gradients, and shadow-model ensembles. It also revealed gaps in understanding how multi-table relational generation affects privacy and whether latent-space diffusion models offer inherent resilience. These findings underscore the need for rigorous privacy auditing before deploying synthetic data in sensitive domains.

## Method Summary
The task was to distinguish whether a given record from the Transaction table was used to train a diffusion-based synthetic generator. The Berka dataset (8 tables) was split into training and holdout sets. Three generator architectures were used: TabDDPM and TabSyn for single-table generation, and ClavaDDPM for multi-table generation with auxiliary tables as conditioning. For each generator, 30 shadow models were pre-trained on different data splits, providing known membership labels for attack development. Attackers were given either white-box access (model parameters, synthetic samples) or black-box access (query interface only) to the target model. Their submitted confidence scores for 20 target models were evaluated by the primary metric: true positive rate at 10% false positive rate (TPR@10%FPR).

## Key Results
- White-box single-table: 46% TPR@10%FPR
- White-box multi-table: 35% TPR@10%FPR
- Black-box single-table: 25% TPR@10%FPR
- Black-box multi-table: 23% TPR@10%FPR

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
#### Mechanism 1: Loss-trajectory based white-box attack
White-box attacks succeed by analyzing the step-wise loss of the diffusion model when reversing the noising process for a challenge point. The attacker runs the reverse diffusion from multiple random noise seeds and records the loss at each timestep. Member samples exhibit a distinct loss pattern (e.g., faster convergence) compared to non-members because the model has memorized nuances of the training data. The top submission (Tartan Federer) adapted the SecMI technique from computer vision, training a classifier on these loss features to achieve 46% TPR@10%FPR. Break condition: strong regularization or excessive randomization can obscure the loss trajectory differences.

#### Mechanism 2: Gradient-based white-box attack
Alternatively, white-box attacks can measure the gradient of the model's loss with respect to the input. The hypothesis is that member records have smaller gradient norms or different directions due to prior fitting. The runner-up (Yan Pang) applied the Gradient Saliency Attack (GSA) to tabular diffusion models, exploiting this signal. This mechanism is less proven (medium confidence) and may fail if gradient clipping or per-example noise is used.

#### Mechanism 3: Shadow-model ensemble for black-box attacks
In black-box settings, the attacker lacks internal model access and instead trains multiple shadow generative models on auxiliary data mimicking the target. For each challenge point, query outputs (e.g., synthetic samples, likelihood estimates) from target and shadow models are used to construct feature vectors (e.g., distances, ensemble predictions). A binary classifier trained on these features distinguishes members from non-members. Top black-box submissions used ensembles (e.g., RMIA, DOMIAS) combined with Gower distance, achieving up to 25% TPR@10%FPR. This approach depends on shadow model fidelity; mismatches cause failure.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Diffusion Model Training & Sampling (e.g., TabDDPM)**
  - *Why needed:* To understand which internal states (loss, gradients, intermediate samples) are exposed in white-box attacks and what query outputs are available in black-box attacks.
  - *Quick check:* How does the forward noising process and reverse denoising network in TabDDPM create a differentiable pathway for exploitation?

- **Concept: Membership Inference Attack (MIA) Fundamentals**
  - *Why needed:* Framing the problem as binary classification of membership is fundamental; knowing standard strategies (shadow modeling, metric-based, loss-based) is prerequisite for adaptation.
  - *Quick check:* What distinguishes a white-box MIA from a black-box MIA, and what are canonical techniques for each?

- **Concept: Tabular Data Synthesis Challenges (Mixed Types, Relationships)**
  - *Why needed:* Heterogeneous data types and multi-table relationships (as in ClavaDDPM) affect attack design; ignoring them can lead to suboptimal performance.
  - *Quick check:* Why might an attack designed for a single homogeneous table underperform on a multi-table generator like ClavaDDPM, even when challenge points are from one table?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**
- Target Layer: Diffusion model implementations (TabDDPM, TabSyn, ClavaDDPM) with training checkpoints and sampling code.
- Attack Input Layer: Challenge point records, access mode (black/white-box), and provided shadow model zoo (30 models per type).
- Core Attack Engine:
  1. Loss-trajectory analyzer (for white-box).
  2. Gradient calculator (for white-box).
  3. Shadow model orchestrator & query executor (for black-box).
  4. Feature engineering module (computes Gower distance, synthetic sample proximities).
- Classifier/Scorer Layer: Binary classifier (e.g., logistic regression, MLP) or thresholding module trained on shadow model data to output membership confidence [0,1].
- Evaluation Harness: Provided scoring program that computes TPR@10%FPR across concatenated model predictions.

**Critical path:**
1. Data & Model Acquisition: Load Berka dataset splits, target model checkpoints, and shadow model zoo.
2. Attack Feature Extraction: For each challenge point and relevant models, compute the chosen signal (e.g., step-wise losses for 10 noise seeds, gradient norm, likelihood, nearest synthetic neighbor distance).
3. Attack Model Training: Using the 30 shadow models (membership known), assemble feature matrix and train a binary classifier.
4. Inference: Apply the trained classifier to features from the 20 dev/final target models to produce confidence scores.
5. Submission: Format scores for the live scoreboard or final evaluation.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- White-box vs. Black-box: White-box offers direct probing (loss/gradients) but is often unrealistic; black-box is more general but requires faithful shadow models and robust feature engineering.
- Single-table vs. Multi-table: Single-table attacks ignore cross-table relationships; multi-table attacks must decide whether to incorporate auxiliary table information, which may improve signals but adds complexity.
- Signal Diversity vs. Complexity: Ensembling diverse signals (loss, gradient, distance, likelihood) likely improves robustness but increases computational cost and shadow model queries.

**Failure signatures:**
- Tuning failure: Attack classifier AUC-ROC on shadow validation set ~0.5 (random).
- Generalization failure: High performance on shadow models but near-random TPR on final target models, indicating overfitting.
- Structural mismatch: Attack works on TabDDPM but fails on TabSyn, suggesting latent-space models need different features.
- Multi-table anomaly: Single-table attack performs *worse* on multi-table models than on single-table models, contrary to expectation, indicating relational constraints obscure signals.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Baseline Shadow Attack: Implement a basic shadow-model attack using only synthetic sample likelihood or minimum distance (DCR) as a single feature. Train logistic regression on the 30 shadow models to establish a performance floor.
2. Adapt SecMI to Tabular: Implement the SecMI mechanism: for a challenge point, run reverse diffusion from K different noise seeds, record the MSE loss at each timestep, and use loss curve statistics as features. Test separately on TabDDPM and TabSyn.
3. Ensemble & Distance Features: Replicate the CITADEL approach: compute multiple MIA scores (e.g., from RMIA, DOMIAS) using shadow models, augment with continuous feature statistics and Gower distance between the challenge point and the synthetic dataset. Train a meta-classifier (e.g., XGBoost) and evaluate if the ensemble beats single-signal attacks.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Is the lower success of membership inference attacks on TabSYN compared to TabDDPM due to latent space diffusion models being under-explored in attack design, or because TabSYN's architecture inherently provides better privacy resilience? The paper states: "It remains an open question whether the preference comes from the fact that latent space diffusion models like TabSYN are less explored, or that the structure makes these models more resilient against membership inference attacks."
2. Why do white-box membership inference attacks on multi-table diffusion models fail to significantly outperform single-table attacks despite access to additional relational tables? The paper notes: "However, the submitted results – particularly on white-box track, do not follow this intuition" that multi-table attacks should be more effective given extra tables.
3. Is the privacy vulnerability exposed by membership inference attacks in diffusion-model synthetic tabular data specific to diffusion models, or a general flaw across synthetic data generation techniques? The paper says: "without further investigation and comparison with other GenAI approaches, it remains unclear whether this privacy leakage is specific to diffusion models."

## Limitations
- Unspecified Berka preprocessing (encoding, normalization) may alter feature distributions and attack signals.
- Challenge model hyperparameters (diffusion steps, learning rate, etc.) may differ from defaults, hindering exact replication.
- ClavaDDPM's relational schema and join logic are undocumented, obscuring how auxiliary tables are used in multi-table attacks.

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (loss-trajectory, white-box): High
- Mechanism 2 (gradient-based, white-box): Medium
- Mechanism 3 (shadow-model ensemble, black-box): High
