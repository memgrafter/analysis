# Breaking the Pre-Sampling Barrier: Activation-Informed Difficulty-Aware Self-Consistency

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.09438
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09438
- **Reference count**: 1
- **Primary result**: ACTSC reduces average sampling cost by up to 87.1% vs. standard Self-Consistency and up to 40% vs. Difficulty-Adaptive Self-Consistency, while maintaining comparable accuracy, by estimating difficulty from a single forward pass using a lightweight probe on FFN activations.

## Executive Summary
Self-Consistency (SC)Large Language Models improves reasoning through majority voting over multiple sampled chains of thought, but this comes with high inference costs. Difficulty-Adaptive Self-Consistency (DSC) reduces cost by allocating more samples to harder problems, yet requires a costly pre-sampling phase for each new dataset. This paper introduces Activation-Informed Difficulty-Aware Self-Consistency (ACTSC), which bypasses pre-sampling entirely. ACTSC uses a lightweight linear probe trained on the feed-forward network activations of the last token toestimate problem difficulty in a single forward pass. Based on this estimate, it routes problems: easy ones are answered with a single sample, while hard ones use an adaptive, window-based sampling loop that stops when answer confidence stabilizes. Across five reasoning benchmarks and three model families, ACTSC achieves substantial sample reduction (up to 87.1% vs. SC) with equal or slightly better accuracy, eliminates DSC’s preparation overhead, and adds negligible extra inference time (~0.59s per problem).

## Method Summary
ACTSC comprises three core components: a difficulty estimation probe, a routing controller, and a dynamic window sampler. First, a set of *difficulty-sensitive neurons* (DSNs) is identified in the model's FFN layers by measuring activation gaps between manually labeled easy and hard problems. A linear probe is then trained on the activations of these DSNs to predict a continuous difficulty score \( P_{\text{hard}} \). During inference, a single forward pass extracts the last-token activations, the probe outputs \( P_{\text{hard}} \), and a threshold \( \tau \) (typically the dataset mean) routes the problem: if \( P_{\text{hard}} < \tau \), the model produces one chain of thought and returns the answer. Otherwise, it enters a loop that generates samples in small batches (window size \( w \)), tracks the frequency of the most common answer in the most recent window, and stops once that confidence exceeds a threshold \( \gamma \). The method is training-free for the base LLM, requires only a small labeled subset for probe training, and introduces no extra model calls beyond the initial forward pass used for routing.

## Key Results
- **Sample Reduction**: ACTSC uses 40–87.1% fewer samples than standard SC and up to 40% fewer than DSC, while matching or slightly exceeding accuracy.
- **Accuracy Maintenance**: Comparable or improved performance across MATH-500, AIME, GPQA-Diamond, and other benchmarks for Gemma and Qwen models.
- **Efficiency Gains**: Eliminates DSC’s pre-sampling preparation cost; token consumption is significantly lower than DSC (e.g., 6.2k vs. 12.2k tokens on GPQA-Diamond).
- **Generalization**: The probe adds minimal overhead (~0.59s) and shows promise beyond mathematical reasoning, though evaluation was primarily on math benchmarks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Linear Decodability of Difficulty from FFN Activations
- **Claim**: Problem difficulty is encoded in the feed-forward network activations of the last token and can be extracted via a linear probe.
- **Mechanism**: Identify a sparse set of *difficulty-sensitive neurons* (DSNs) by computing the activation gap between easy and hard examples. Train a lightweight linear classifier on these neurons' activations to predict a binary or continuous difficulty label.
- **Evidence Anchors**:
  - Abstract and Section 3.1 define the DSN selection via activation gap and linear probe training.
  - Figure 4 shows probe logits increase monotonically with true difficulty levels.
  - The neighbor DSC paper requires pre-sampling; ACTSC avoids this by using internal activations.

### Mechanism 2: Dynamic Window with Confidence-Based Stopping
- **Claim**: For hard problems, generating samples in small batches and stopping when the most frequent answer in the recent window reaches a confidence threshold stabilizes majority voting with fewer samples than fixed-budget SC.
- **Mechanism**: After routing to the "hard" path, iteratively generate \( w \) new samples. Compute the relative frequency of the modal answer in the last \( w \) samples. Terminate when this confidence \( \geq \gamma \).
- **Evidence Anchors**:
  - Section 3.2 and Algorithm 2 formalize the loop.
  - Table 1 shows ACTSC uses far fewer samples than SC on AIME (e.g., 28.17 vs. 40 for Qwen2.5-3B) with similar accuracy.
  - ESC uses fixed windows unconditionally; ACTSC applies it only to hard problems for larger savings.

### Mechanism 3: Single-Forward-Pass Routing Without Prep Cost
- **Claim**: Difficulty-based routing from a single forward pass separates problems that need multiple samples from those that do not, eliminating DSC's pre-sampling overhead.
- **Mechanism**: Compute \( P_{\text{hard}} \) via the probe. If \( P_{\text{hard}} < \tau \) (dataset-specific threshold), answer with one sample; else invoke the dynamic window sampler.
- **Evidence Anchors**:
  - Abstract states ACTSC "eliminates DSC’s preparation overhead."
  - Section 3.2 describes routing logic (Algorithm 2 lines 3–4 vs. 5–12).
  - Table 2 shows ACTSC reduces token cost vs. DSC while maintaining accuracy.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Frozen LLM -> DSN Selector -> Difficulty Probe -> Router -> (Single-Sample Path OR Dynamic Window Sampler)

### Critical Path
1. Input problem text enters the frozen LLM.
2. A single forward pass generates the initial hidden states.
3. Extract FFN activations of the last token for the pre-identified DSNs.
4. Linear probe computes \( P_{\text{hard}} \).
5. Router compares \( P_{\text{hard}} \) to threshold \( \tau \).
6. If \( P_{\text{hard}} < \tau \): generate one CoT and output answer.
7. Else: initialise empty sample buffer.
8. Loop: generate up to \( w \) new samples, add to buffer.
9. Compute confidence of the most frequent answer in the last \( w \) samples.
10. If confidence \( \geq \gamma \): return that majority answer; else repeat from step 8.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Probe Sparsity**: More DSNs capture nuance but increase probe size; paper uses high-gap neurons for sparsity.
- **Threshold \( \tau \)**: Set to dataset mean vs. median or percentile affects routing balance for skewed difficulty distributions.
- **Window Size \( w \)**: Larger \( w \) stabilizes confidence but slows adaptation; paper uses \( w = 5 \).
- **Confidence \( \gamma \)**: Lower \( \gamma \) speeds up but risks premature stop; paper uses \( \gamma = 0.50 \).
- **Single-Forward Estimation**: Zero prep cost but probe accuracy depends on training data distribution and model architecture.

### Failure Signatures
- **Probe Mis-routing**: Overall accuracy drop, especially on the hard subset; check per-difficulty accuracy.
- **Non-convergent Windows**: Many hard problems hit max samples (e.g., 40) without reaching \( \gamma \); indicates \( \gamma \) too high or \( w \) too small.
- **Dataset Shift**: Probe trained on MATH underperforms on non-math benchmarks (GPQA, CommonsenseQA); may need domain adaptation.
- **Overhead Dominance**: On easy-dominant datasets, the probe's ~0.59s overhead may outweigh sample savings; monitor latency distribution.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Reproduce Table 1**: Run ACTSC, SC, AC, ESC, DSC on MATH-500 with Qwen2.5-3B. Confirm ACTSC achieves ≥75% sample reduction vs. SC with comparable accuracy.
2. **Probe Calibration**: Train probe, plot histogram/logits of \( P_{\text{hard}} \) across MATH difficulty levels (as in Figure 4). Verify monotonic increase and separation; compute Pearson correlation between probe score and true difficulty.
3. **Routing Accuracy Analysis**: Using true binary labels (easy/hard via \( \theta_{\text{easy}}, \theta_{\text{hard}} \)), compute precision/recall of probe predictions. Report average samples used per true difficulty level to confirm easy problems mostly take the single-sample path.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Generalization to Non-Autoregressive Models**: Does the activation-based difficulty probe generalize to encoder-decoder or non-autoregressive architectures without retraining? The paper only evaluates decoder-only models (Gemma, Qwen).
- **Threshold Selection**: How does the choice of difficulty threshold \( \tau \) (mean vs. median vs. percentile) affect ACTSC's cost-accuracy trade-off on datasets with skewed difficulty distributions? The paper uses the dataset mean without comparison.
- **Intermediate-Difficulty Samples**: Does excluding intermediate-difficulty samples (between \( \theta_{\text{easy}} \) and \( \theta_{\text{hard}} \)) during probe training harm calibration for medium-difficulty problems and overall ACTSC performance? The paper excludes them to reduce label uncertainty but does not evaluate this choice.
- **Alternative Activation Sources**: Do activation signals from layers other than the last token or from attention mechanisms contain additional difficulty information that could improve probe accuracy? The method uses only last-token FFN activations.

## Limitations
- Probe generalizability is unproven beyond math benchmarks; performance on diverse domains (e.g., commonsense, creative writing) remains unknown.
- DSN selection and probe training are likely model-specific; stability across different LLM families or sizes is not established.
- Routing threshold \( \tau \) is heuristic (dataset mean); misclassification could degrade accuracy, especially on datasets with unusual difficulty distributions.
- Dynamic window parameters (\( w=5, \gamma=0.5 \)) may fail to converge on noisy answer distributions, leading to max-sample fallbacks.
- Probe overhead (~0.59s per problem) may erode efficiency gains on datasets where most problems are easy and take the single-sample path.

## Confidence
- **Claim 1 (Probe captures difficulty signal)**: *Medium*. Monotonic probe logits (Fig 4) show decodable signal, but evidence is limited to math benchmarks and the linear probe assumption may not hold universally.
- **Claim 2 (Dynamic window reduces samples)**: *Medium*. Sample reductions on AIME/GPQA demonstrate utility, but convergence behavior of the window and sensitivity to \( w, \gamma \) are not deeply analyzed.
- **Claim 3 (Overall efficiency without prep cost)**: *High*. Consistent 40–87% reduction vs. SC/DSC with maintained accuracy across 5 benchmarks and 3 models; elimination of pre-sampling is clearly demonstrated.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-Domain Probe Robustness**: Evaluate a probe trained on MATH on GPQA and CommonsenseQA. Report difficulty prediction accuracy and correlation, and measure ACTSC's sample reduction and accuracy on these out-of-domain benchmarks.
2. **Parameter Sensitivity Sweep**: Grid-search window size \( w \in \{3,5,7\} \) and confidence threshold \( \gamma \in \{0.5,0.6,0.75\} \). Measure rates of convergence failure (hitting max samples), average sample count, and accuracy.
3. **Latency Profiling Across Difficulty Spectrums**: Compare wall-clock time of ACTSC vs. SC vs. DSC on datasets with varying easy/hard ratios. Identify the breakeven point where probe overhead outweighs sample savings, and characterize the latency distribution per difficulty class.
