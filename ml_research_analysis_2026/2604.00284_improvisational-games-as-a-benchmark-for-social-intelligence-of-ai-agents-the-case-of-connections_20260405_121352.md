# Improvisational Games as a Benchmark for Social Intelligence of AI Agents: The Case of Connections

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2604.00284
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00284
- **Reference count**: 9
- **Primary result**: Contextual priming increases clue diversity and reduces setter blocking, but LLMs fail to autonomously learn knowledge differences from partners.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces *Connections*, an improvisational wordplay game designed to benchmark social intelligence in AI agents. In the game, a Setter chooses a hidden word, and Guessers collaboratively generate clues that must start with the progressively revealed prefix, while the Setter attempts to block clues by guessing the intended word. The authors formalize the game using semantic embeddings and derive an optimal clue probability that balances guesser success against setter blocking. Experiments with GPT‑4o agents show that without explicit priming, identical agents suffer high blocking due to shared semantic networks; with role‑based priming (e.g., profession, age), agents produce more diverse clues and achieve better outcomes, yet they cannot spontaneously adapt to unseen knowledge differences. The work highlights a significant gap in LLMs’ ability to reason about and learn from other agents’ cognitive states.

## Method Summary
The study models *Connections* with three LLM‑based agents: one Setter and two Guessers. The Setter selects a target word and reveals its first letter; each turn, a Guesser creates a clue phrase and secretly selects a word starting with the current prefix. The Setter and the other Guesser then try to guess that word. A clue “succeeds” if the other Guesser guesses correctly while the Setter does not, leading to the next letter’s reveal. The probability of success is tied to the semantic similarity (dot product) between the clue and the target word in an embedding space. An analytical optimum \(p^*\) for the per‑guesser success probability is derived. Agents maintain perceived “discourse vectors” for each other, updated based on guessing outcomes to gradually adapt to partners’ knowledge. Two main experimental conditions are compared: unprimed agents with identical prompts, and agents given contextual primes (e.g., “you are a marine biologist”) to induce heterogeneous knowledge. Gameplay metrics (iterations, reveals, guesser wrongs, setter blocks) are recorded and analyzed.

## Key Results
- Metrics vary widely across target words (e.g., iterations from 7 to 173), reflecting lexical difficulty and semantic overlap.
- Without priming, the identical LLM frequently blocks clues because Guessers and Setter share semantic networks.
- With contextual priming, agents produce more diverse clues and reduce setter blocking, indicating some social awareness; however, they do not autonomously discover and exploit knowledge differences without explicit cues.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Optimal Clue Probability as Social Trade‑off**  
- *Claim*: Social intelligence in *Connections* is reduced to selecting a clue whose semantic similarity to the target optimally balances the chance that at least one Guesser succeeds while the Setter fails.  
- *Mechanism*: Given a monotonic mapping from dot product \(\Phi(w)\cdot\Phi(p)\) to per‑guesser success probability \(p\), the overall success probability is \((1-p)(1-(1-p)^{n-1})\). The optimal \(p^* = 1-(1/n)^{1/(n-1)}\) maximizes reveal likelihood.  
- *Evidence*: Derivation in section 2; section 4.1 shows rare‑starting‑letter words yield fewer iterations, consistent with constrained search affecting \(p\).  

**Mechanism 2: Incremental Learning via Discourse Vectors**  
- *Claim*: Agents can learn other agents’ knowledge profiles by maintaining and updating a “discourse vector” \(d_{i\leftarrow j}\): after a turn, add \(\eta v_w\) if agent \(j\) guessed \(w\) correctly, subtract if incorrect.  
- *Mechanism*: This gradient‑like adjustment shifts future word/clue generation toward vectors aligned with successful guessers and away from the setter, assuming linear, stable knowledge representations in the embedding space.  
- *Evidence*: Update rule defined in section 2; section 4.2 notes that without priming agents fail to autonomously exploit differences, implying the mechanism is theoretically sound but not spontaneously implemented by LLMs.  

**Mechanism 3: Contextual Priming Alters Semantic Networks**  
- *Claim*: System prompts that assign roles (e.g., “you are a botanist”) bias the LLM’s token predictions, creating distinct effective embedding functions \(\Phi\) across agents.  
- *Mechanism*: Divergent semantic networks make certain clues obscure to the setter but clear to a primed guesser, increasing clue diversity and reducing blocking.  
- *Evidence*: Direct comparison in section 4.2: priming yields fewer setter blocks and higher lexical diversity; the abstract notes this “indicates some social awareness.”

## Foundational Learning
- **Semantic Embedding Spaces**: Words are mapped to vectors; dot products encode relatedness, directly determining clue validity and success probabilities. *Quick check*: If “catalyst”, “catamaran”, and “cantaloupe” have dot products 0.7, 0.4, and 0.3 with “cat”, which would be easier for a setter to block? Higher similarity means the clue is more guessable, thus harder to block.  
- **Probabilistic Game Modeling**: The optimal clue probability \(p^*\) is derived from a binomial success model; knowing how \(p\) changes with the number of guessers is essential. *Quick check*: With 3 players (1 setter, 2 guessers), \(p^* \approx 0.63\). Why does \(p^*\) decrease as guessers increase? More guessers raise the chance that at least one succeeds, allowing clues to be slightly harder (lower \(p\)).  
- **In‑Context Learning for Agent Differentiation**: Priming via role prompts aims to produce heterogeneous knowledge without fine‑tuning. *Quick check*: What distinguishes exogenous priming (“You are a marine biologist”) from endogenous learning from gameplay? Priming is an external instruction; endogenous learning arises from observed interaction outcomes.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Embedding Encoder (Φ) → Game State Manager (tracks prefix, blocked words, turn) → Agent Policy Module (generates clues and guesses) → Success Evaluator (computes dot products, determines outcomes) → Discourse Vector Store (holds and updates \(d_{i\leftarrow j}\)).

**Critical Path**  
1. Setter picks word \(W\); state initialized with first letter.  
2. Guesser turn: produces clue phrase and intended word \(w\) (starts with prefix).  
3. Setter and other Guesser produce guesses.  
4. Evaluate: if setter guesses \(w\) → block; else if other guesser guesses \(w\) → reveal next letter; else wrong.  
5. Update discourse vectors based on correctness.  
6. Alternate clue giver; repeat until \(W\) fully revealed or max iterations reached.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- Embedding choice: Static embeddings (e.g., all‑MiniLM‑L6‑v2) provide a fixed semantic map but may misalign with LLM’s internal semantics; dynamic embeddings (using the LLM itself) match generation but introduce cost and noise.  
- Update step size \(\eta\): Too large causes oscillation; too small slows learning; requires empirical tuning.  
- Clue validity thresholds (\(\lambda_L\), \(\lambda_U\)) for dot products are heuristic; must be calibrated per embedding space.

**Failure Signatures**  
- High “Setter Blocked” (>70%) → clues too obvious or agents too similar.  
- Zero reveals after many iterations → guessers choose words outside setter’s vocabulary or clues too vague.  
- No reduction in blocking after priming → priming failed to alter \(\Phi\).  

**First 3 Experiments**  
1. **Baseline replication**: Run 100 unprimed games with GPT‑4o; verify that iteration counts decrease for rarer starting letters, confirming the difficulty‑success relationship.  
2. **Priming efficacy test**: Compare identical prompts vs. distinct profession primes; measure changes in “Setter Blocked” rate and clue lexical diversity (type‑token ratio).  
3. **Vector tracking diagnostic**: Implement discourse vector updates; after each successful reveal, compute \(||d_{i\leftarrow\text{setter}} - d_{i\leftarrow\text{guesser}}||\) and test correlation with subsequent clue success.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Autonomous exploitation of knowledge heterogeneity**: Can LLMs learn to leverage partners’ differing knowledge without explicit cues? Current experiments show failure.  
2. **Dynamic setter behavior**: Can the game be extended so the setter adaptively chooses the target word during play? LLMs struggle with long‑term recall and improvisation.  
3. **Discourse vectors with genuine experience**: Does the update mechanism work when agents have varied pretraining/fine‑tuning histories, beyond superficial priming?  
4. **Generalization of \(p^*\) to heterogeneous populations**: The uniform‑agent derivation may not hold when agents have distinct vocabularies and embeddings; a more general model is needed.

## Limitations
- The formal model assumes uniform guessing probabilities and a monotonic semantic mapping, which real agents violate due to knowledge heterogeneity.  
- It is unclear whether the discourse vector updates were actually implemented in the reported results; Table 1 may reflect simple prompting only.  
- The word set used for evaluation is small and not randomly sampled, limiting generalizability across lexical domains.

## Confidence
- Optimal clue probability derivation: **Medium** (mathematically sound under strong assumptions).  
- Discourse vector learning: **Low** (no practical convergence evidence; agents fail to exploit differences spontaneously).  
- Contextual priming creates social awareness: **Medium** (observed effects but scale and persistence uncertain).
