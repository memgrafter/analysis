# Discourse-Aware Dual-Track Streaming Response for Low-Latency Spoken Dialogue Systems
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.23266
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23266
- **Reference count:** 28
- **Primary result:** Reduces waiting latency by 19%-51% (e.g., 548ms vs. 1003ms) while preserving discourse quality and speech naturalness, achieving sub-second response onset.

## Executive Summary
Spoken dialogue systems often suffer from high latency due to sequential ASR-LLM-TTS processing. This paper introduces DDTSR, a framework that decouples response generation into dual tracks: a small model produces minimal-committal discourse connectives for immediate output, while a large model performs deep reasoning in parallel. By overlapping ASR, LLM, and TTS via streaming cross-modal collaboration, DDTSR advances the earliest speakable moment. Evaluations on SD-Eval and SpokenNativQA show waiting latency reductions of 19%-51%, with comparable discourse quality and speech naturalness to baselines. The plug-and-play module is robust across utterance lengths and LLM backbones.

## Method Summary
DDTSR addresses cascaded latency by splitting response generation. A lightweight model (`f_s`) generates short, semantically safe discourse connectives (e.g., "well", "I see") that can be spoken immediately. Concurrently, a large model (`f_l`) handles knowledge-intensive reasoning to produce the full response. The system operates on streaming ASR hypotheses: the small model processes partial transcripts and signals when a safe connective is ready, triggering TTS without waiting for ASR finalization. The large model runs on the final transcript and its output is spoken after the connective. To ensure early connectives remain coherent with the eventual response, the small model is trained with a curriculum that gradually introduces truncation, and with losses that align its output with the large model's style and content. The framework uses Qwen3-0.6B as `f_s` (LoRA-finetuned) and Qwen3-8B/32B as `f_l`, with sherpa-onnx for streaming ASR and CosyVoice2 for TTS.

## Key Results
- DDTSR reduces waiting latency by 19%-51% (e.g., 548ms vs. 1003ms on SD-Eval with 8B model), achieving sub-second response onset.
- Discourse quality (coherence, consistency) and speech naturalness are preserved comparably to cascaded baselines.
- The module is plug-and-play and robust across utterance lengths and LLM backbones.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Decoupling response generation into a fast, low-risk connective prefix and a slower, knowledge-intensive main response reduces perceived latency without semantic conflict.
- **Mechanism:** A lightweight model (`f_s`) generates short, minimal-committal discourse connectives (e.g., "well," "I see") in parallel with a large model (`f_l`) performing full reasoning. The connectives are designed to be semantically safe (non-truth-conditional) so they can be spoken immediately, even before the large model finishes.
- **Core assumption:** Turn-initial discourse connectives can be generated reliably from partial or complete user input and do not introduce propositional content that would later contradict the main response.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract] Explicitly states: "an auxiliary small model generates minimal-committal discourse connectives for immediate speech output, while a large model performs knowledge-intensive reasoning in parallel."
  - [section 3.1] Details the decomposition: `y = TTS(f_s(u)) ⊕ TTS(f_l(u))` and defines connectives as "short, discourse-appropriate connective prefixes that are semantically safe."
  - [corpus] Related work on incremental generation supports the concept of early partial responses, but does not validate the *semantic safety* of discourse-connectives specifically for this architecture. The corpus evidence for the assumed safety property is indirect and relies on linguistic theory rather than system-level validation.
- **Break condition:** If the connective generator produces a phrase that introduces truth-conditional content or a discourse relation incompatible with the subsequent main response, coherence will be broken.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Streaming-based cross-modal collaboration overlaps ASR, LLM, and TTS stages, advancing the earliest speakable moment by starting processing on partial ASR hypotheses.
- **Mechanism:** The system consumes streaming ASR output (`ˆu(k)`). The small model reasons on these partial transcripts and signals (`sig(k)`) when a safe connective is ready. TTS for the connective is triggered before ASR finalization, while the large model continues reasoning on the final transcript. This interleaving masks reasoning latency.
- **Core assumption:** Partial ASR hypotheses are sufficiently accurate and stable to allow the small model to generate a discourse-appropriate connective with low risk of error.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "streaming-based cross-modal collaboration, which dynamically overlaps ASR, LLM inference, and TTS to advance the earliest speakable moment."
  - [section 3.2] Formally defines the process: `ˆu(k) = ASR(x1:k)`, `(c(k), sig(k)) = f_s(ˆu(k))`, and `k⋆ = min{k | sig(k) = 1}`. It states this enables "rapid audible feedback while higher-level reasoning continues."
  - [corpus] The corpus contains work on incremental ASR and TTS, which demonstrates technical feasibility of streaming. However, it does not provide evidence that the *specific* decision threshold (`sig(k)`) based on partial ASR is reliably safe for the *purpose* of emitting discourse connectives. This is an assumption linking incremental accuracy to discourse safety.
- **Break condition:** High early ASR error rates cause the small model to generate an incoherent or inappropriate connective, leading to a jarring early response.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Curriculum-learning-based discourse continuity enhancement trains the small model to generate connectives that are coherent and consistent with the eventual large-model response, despite being produced under partial-input conditions.
- **Mechanism:** The small model is trained with three objectives: (1) a style consistency loss (`L_con`) that encourages connectives leading to semantically compatible large-model continuations; (2) a coherence loss (`L_coh`) that transfers discourse coherence from the pre-trained small model; (3) a prior regularization (`L_prior`). Training proceeds from full-context to truncated inputs (curriculum). At inference, a confidence-based selection mechanism (`Conf(k)`) filters low-confidence connectives.
- **Core assumption:** A connective's discourse function can be aligned with the main response's content and style via auxiliary training losses, and this alignment persists when the connective is generated from a partial transcript.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] Lists "curriculum-learning-based discourse continuity enhancement" as the third mechanism to "maintains coherence and logical consistency between early responses and subsequent reasoning outputs."
  - [section 3.3] Details the losses and curriculum strategy. It states the goal is to ensure "early-emitted discourse connectives remain aligned with the final discourse trajectory."
  - [corpus] The corpus includes papers on discourse markers for coherence modeling, but these focus on offline text generation or analysis. There is no direct corpus evidence that a *curriculum* of truncation during training successfully teaches a model to emit *safe* connectives under *real-time streaming* conditions. This is an inferred mechanism supported by general curriculum learning principles.
- **Break condition:** The training fails to produce a small model that reliably predicts the discourse trajectory of the large model. Early connectives frequently mismatch the main response's relation, or confidence scoring fails to filter poor connectives, resulting in discourse incoherence.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Discourse Connectives as Low-Risk Pragmatic Markers**
  - **Why needed here:** The dual-track design relies on the linguistic property that certain turn-initial words manage interaction without adding truth-conditional content. Engineers must know which phrases are safe to emit early.
  - **Quick check:** If a user says "I think the project deadline is next Friday," is the connective "Actually," safe to emit immediately after ASR begins? (Answer: No; "Actually" often signals a correction and is truth-conditional. A safer choice is "Okay," which is purely an acknowledgment.)
- **Concept: Streaming Incremental Processing**
  - **Why needed here:** The system operates on a sequence of partial ASR hypotheses and must decide when to act. Understanding streaming semantics is crucial for implementing overlap.
  - **Quick check:** If the ASR updates every 200ms with a new word, what is `ˆu(k)` when the small model first generates a candidate? (Answer: It is the most recent complete-but-still-partial transcript up to that chunk, possibly missing final words.)
- **Concept: Curriculum Learning for Temporal Robustness**
  - **Why needed here:** The small model must perform well on partial, evolving transcripts seen during real-time streaming. Curriculum learning (training on easier, longer contexts first, then harder, shorter ones) builds this robustness.
  - **Quick check:** Why train first on complete utterances then gradually on truncated versions? (Answer: The model first learns the ideal mapping on clean data, then adapts to noise and incompleteness of partial inputs, preventing catastrophic forgetting.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Streaming ASR → (1) Small Connective Model → Confidence Selector → Streaming TTS (connective); (2) Large Reasoning Model (triggered on ASR final) → Streaming TTS (main response).
- **Critical path:** User audio → ASR streaming updates (k=1,2,...) → f_s(ˆu(k)) for each k → if sig(k)=1 → TTS(connective) starts → (in parallel) f_l(ˆu(∞)) → TTS(main response) starts after connective finishes.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Latency vs. Coherence: Lower confidence threshold τ yields earlier emission but increases incoherence risk; higher τ improves safety but may waste latency gains.
  - Small Model Capacity vs. Speed: Larger small model may generate better connectives but increases its inference time, reducing latency budget.
  - Curriculum Complexity vs. Training Cost: The 4-stage curriculum adds training time but is claimed necessary for robustness; training only on partial inputs could be a simpler ablation.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - High connective conflict rate: Human/LLM judges find logical mismatch between connective and main response (e.g., "Furthermore" followed by negation).
  - Early ASR error propagation: System emits a connective based on a partial transcript that later changes significantly, making it contextually inappropriate.
  - Confidence mis-calibration: Confidence score consistently exceeds threshold for poor connectives or falls below for good ones.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Ablate streaming collaboration:** Disable partial-input processing for f_s (trigger only on final ASR). Measure perception latency; expect a rise to baseline (~388ms) confirming the contribution of streaming overlap.
  2. **Ablate curriculum learning:** Train f_s without curriculum (only full-context data). Evaluate textual coherence (G-Eval) and connective conflict rate on streaming test set; expect significant degradation.
  3. **Vary confidence threshold τ:** Sweep τ from 0.0 to 0.95. Plot waiting latency vs. coherence score to characterize the trade-off and identify Pareto-optimal operating points.

## Limitations
- Semantic safety of early-emitted connectives not validated under streaming ASR errors.
- Decision threshold `sig(k)` tolerance to ASR mistakes unquantified.
- Curriculum training effectiveness for streaming robustness lacks direct evaluation; reproducibility hindered by vague implementation details.

## Confidence
- Decoupling via early connectives: **Medium**
- Streaming cross-modal overlap: **Medium**
- Curriculum for continuity: **Low**
