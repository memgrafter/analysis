# Dual Optimal: Make Your LLM Peer-like with Dignity

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2604.00979  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00979  
- **Reference Count**: 40  
- **Primary Result**: Lag-DPO on Qwen3-4B achieves balanced improvements across four persona dimensions (Anti‑sycophancy = 14.54, Trustworthiness = 13.05, Empathy = 4.89, Creativity = 3.97 logits), preserves general reasoning (MMLU/BBH), halves sycophancy rate (to 0.542), and raises unsafe refusal to 64.3% on OOD benchmarks, breaking the evasive‑servant trade‑off.

## Executive Summary
Aligned language models often exhibit the “Evasive Servant” pattern: they sycophantically agree with users while evading responsibility for unsafe requests. This paper introduces the **Dignified Peer** framework, which defines desirable agent behavior along four dimensions: **Anti‑sycophancy**, **Trustworthiness**, **Empathy**, and **Creativity**. To train models in these dimensions, the authors construct **PersonaKnob**, a dataset of partially ordered preferences where a reference response dominates exactly one negative per active dimension. They propose **Lag‑DPO**, a Lagrangian‑constrained extension of Direct Preference Optimization that uses dynamic multipliers and tolerance margins to balance competing objectives without collapse. Evaluation employs an IRT‑based Many‑Facet Rasch Model to produce calibrated scores, isolating model capability from judge bias. On Qwen3‑4B, Lag‑DPO yields uniformly better results across all four dimensions compared to baselines, retains performance on general‑purpose benchmarks (MMLU, BBH), and simultaneously reduces sycophancy and increases safe refusal on out‑of‑distribution tests, demonstrating that the “evasive‑servant” trade‑off is not inherent.

## Method Summary
The method comprises three main components.  
1. **Dataset construction**: PersonaKnob generates scenarios requiring multiple behavioral dimensions. For each scenario, a reference response `y*` is produced alongside negative responses `y-d`, each failing **only** dimension `d`. This partial‑order structure isolates per‑dimension failure modes.  
2. **Training with Lag‑DPO**: Given the dataset, the standard DPO loss is computed per active dimension `d` as `L_d(θ)`. Rather than averaging losses, Lag‑DPO solves a constrained optimization: minimize `∑_d (1+λ_d)L_d(θ)` subject to `L_d(θ) ≤ ε_d`. Multipliers `λ_d` are updated online via dual ascent: `λ_d ← min(λ_max, max(0, λ_d + η_λ(L_d(θ) - ε_d)))`. This amplifies gradients for dimensions whose loss exceeds tolerance, preventing collapse caused by conflicting gradients.  
3. **Calibrated evaluation**: Model responses are scored by multiple LLM judges using binary rubrics for each dimension. The scores are fed into a Many‑Facet Rasch Model (MFRM) to estimate latent abilities `θ_mq` per model `m` and question `q`, separating judge severity, rubric difficulty, and question difficulty. Peer (Empathy + Creativity) and Dignity (Anti‑sycophancy + Trustworthiness) scores are derived from calibrated dimension abilities.

## Key Results
- Lag‑DPO yields dimension‑wise logit scores on Qwen3‑4B: **Anti‑sycophancy = 14.54**, **Trustworthiness = 13.05**, **Empathy = 4.89**, **Creativity = 3.97**, uniformly outperforming Multi‑Neg DPO, SafeRLHF, and PCGrad baselines.
- General reasoning (MMLU and BBH) is preserved, showing no degradation from persona specialization.
- On OOD benchmarks (SycophancyEval, XSTest), Lag‑DPO simultaneously **halves the sycophancy rate** (from 0.87 to 0.542) and **raises unsafe refusal** to **64.3%** (from 38%), breaking the traditional trade‑off.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

The authors identify three key mechanisms that enable the Dignified Peer outcome.

**Mechanism 1: Inherent trade‑off in H&H alignment**  
The “Evasive Servant” pattern arises from the fundamental tension between safety and helpfulness objectives. Safety optimization tends to induce over‑refusal, while preference‑based helpfulness training encourages sycophancy (deference to user opinions even when incorrect). These competing gradients pull in opposite directions, making it impossible to satisfy both with a single scalar objective. The four dimensions (A, T, E, C) cannot be captured by one H&H objective and require separate treatment with explicit trade‑off management. Evidence: The abstract notes the tension inherited from Helpful and Harmless alignment; Section 1 explains how safety and helpfulness optimizations degrade into the dual failure. If the dimensions proved to have a truly Pareto‑optimal frontier where some combinations are impossible, the Dignified Peer ideal would be unattainable for some base models.

**Mechanism 2: Partial‑order dataset prevents collapse**  
PersonaKnob’s structure—one reference dominating exactly one negative per active dimension—provides isolated per‑dimension contrastive signals. Each negative fails only its target dimension, so the model can distinguish “bad on dimension X but good on others” from “bad on dimension Y”. This prevents gradient averaging that would collapse one dimension when failures are entangled in a total order. Evidence: Section 3 discusses the need for a partially ordered dataset; Section 6 shows that SFT‑Anchor degrades while PersonaKnob preserves representations. The dataset pipeline explicitly generates negatives with single‑dimension failures. If references themselves were low‑quality, the partial order `y* ≻_d y-d` would be violated, contaminating the signal.

**Mechanism 3: Dynamic Lagrangian reweighting**  
Lag‑DPO prevents dimension collapse by updating multipliers online. When a dimension’s loss exceeds its tolerance `ε_d`, its multiplier `λ_d` increases, amplifying that dimension’s gradient contribution until the constraint is satisfied; satisfied dimensions fade to near‑zero weight. This contrasts with simple averaging (Multi‑Neg DPO) where conflicting gradients cancel. Evidence: Section 6.1 describes the mechanism; Table 5 shows that `ε=0` (no tolerance) causes collapse, `ε=0.8` reverts to averaging, and the chosen `ε=0.5` balances; Figure 10 shows faster convergence; Appendix F provides theoretical analysis of gradient scaling. The mechanism only helps when dimensions exhibit meaningful conflict; if they were perfectly aligned, Lag‑DPO would reduce to uniform averaging.

## Foundational Learning

**Concept: Partial Orders in Preference Learning**  
*Why needed*: Standard DPO assumes a total order (`y ≻ y'`), but PersonaKnob uses a partial order where `y*` dominates `y-d` only on dimension `d`. This requires constructing and optimizing over datasets that capture multi‑dimensional trade‑offs without entangling failures.  
*Quick check*: If `A >_A B`, `B >_T C`, and `C >_E A`, can a single reference dominate all three? The answer depends on whether there exists a response that is simultaneously better than `B` on A, better than `C` on T, and better than `A` on E. This illustrates that not all triples are comparable, limiting active dimensions per instance.

**Concept: Lagrangian Duality for Constrained Multi‑Objective Optimization**  
*Why needed*: Lag‑DPO formulates the problem as minimizing a weighted sum subject to per‑dimension loss constraints. The Lagrange multipliers `λ_d` are updated online (dual ascent) rather than solving the dual to convergence each step, allowing adaptive reweighting during training.  
*Quick check*: Why not pre‑compute fixed weights from validation losses? Online updates respond to the current state of optimization, adjusting quickly when a dimension falls behind without permanently over‑penalizing once it recovers.

**Concept: Item Response Theory (IRT) for Bias‑Agnostic Evaluation**  
*Why needed*: Raw LLM‑as‑judge scores conflate model quality with judge severity, rubric difficulty, and question difficulty. The Many‑Facet Rasch Model (MFRM) disentangles these factors to estimate true latent model capability `θ_mq`.  
*Quick check*: If Judge A is consistently stricter than Judge B, should Model X’s score be penalized? MFRM accounts for judge severity, so the calibrated ability `θ` remains unbiased by such judge effects.

**Concept: Gradient Surgery vs. Constrained Optimization**  
*Why needed*: Baselines include PCGrad (gradient surgery) which removes conflicting gradient components, and SafeRLHF (Lagrangian). Understanding the difference clarifies why constrained optimization with dynamic reweighting is necessary.  
*Quick check*: PCGrad projects gradients to eliminate conflict; Lag‑DPO amplifies underperforming gradients. PCGrad assumes conflict should be avoided, while Lag‑DPO assumes that some conflict is inevitable and seeks to satisfy constraints despite it.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**

PersonaKnob Construction Pipeline:  
SeedSampler → QuestionSynthesizer → AttributionVerifier → ReferenceProposer → LLMVerifier → HumanReview

Lag‑DPO Trainer:  
DualState (maintains λ_d) → PerDimLossCalculator → DualUpdater → PrimalUpdater  
(per batch)

IRT‑MFRM Evaluation:  
DataCollector → MFRMFitter → Aggregator

**Critical path**  
1. Build PersonaKnob while ensuring partial‑order property holds.  
2. Extract per‑dimension DPO losses from dataset (requires JSON with `{reference, negatives_by_dimension}`).  
3. Implement dual variable updates *before* the primal step each batch.  
4. Collect model responses on test sets, score with multiple judges, run MFRM calibration.  
5. Validate OOD behavior on SycophancyEval and XSTest.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- *Dataset diversity vs. control*: PersonaKnob anchors on four seed datasets; expanding by synthesizing new seeds may break partial‑order guarantees.  
- *Tolerance margin ε*: Fixed ε balances stability and flexibility; too tight (ε=0) stalls, too loose (ε→∞) reverts to averaging. Adaptive scheduling is an open question.  
- *Dual learning rate η_λ*: Must be smaller than primal LR; too large causes oscillation, too small yields no adaptation.  
- *Judge count*: Two judges suffice theoretically; additional judges reduce variance but increase cost 2–3×.

**Failure signatures**  
*Dataset construction*:  
- Type 1 hallucination rate >5% → unrealistic scenarios.  
- Type 2 rate >5% → reference unfaithful, breaking `y* ≻_d y-d`.  
- Type 3 rate >10% → negatives fail multiple dimensions, losing contrast.

*Training*:  
- All λ_d → 0 quickly → η_λ too small or ε too loose → collapse to Multi‑Neg DPO.  
- One λ_d dominates (e.g., λ_A = 10) → ε too tight for that dimension or its loss consistently high.  
- All L_d negative → preference violations (y* not preferred), likely data error.

*Evaluation*:  
- Low inter‑judge agreement (Kendall τ < 0.7) → rubric misunderstanding; retrain annotators.  
- All θ_mq near 0 → questions too hard or models too weak; need calibration.  
- Peer and Dignity scores perfectly correlated (r > 0.9) → judge bias not removed; check MFRM convergence.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. *Partial‑order verification audit*: Sample 200 instances; have 3 blind raters judge if (a) `y*` satisfies all active dimensions and (b) each `y-d` fails only its target dimension. Expect >90% agreement with the paper’s 91.2% pass rate.  
2. *Gradient cancellation quantification*: On a held‑out batch, compute per‑dimension loss gradients and pairwise cosine similarities. Expect strong negative correlations (~‑0.5) before Lag‑DPO; after updates, λ_d should increase for underperforming dimensions, amplifying their gradient norms relative to others.  
3. *OOD robustness test*: Train Lag‑DPO on Qwen3‑4B; evaluate on SycophancyEval and XSTest. Expect sycophancy rate ≈ 0.54 (down from 0.87) and unsafe refusal ≈ 64% (up from 38%). If sycophancy drops but refusal also drops, ε may be too loose or training too short.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Adaptive ε scheduling**: How to schedule tolerance margins during Lag‑DPO training to maintain balanced persona performance without manual tuning? (Section N, Conclusion).  
- **Scaling to larger models**: Does Lag‑DPO’s balance benefit extend beyond 4B–8B parameter models? May larger models exhibit different gradient interference dynamics.  
- **Completeness of dimensions**: Are the four chosen traits sufficient to characterize a Dignified Peer? Other traits (e.g., humility, context retention) might be needed.  
- **Dataset quality limits**: Residual hallucinations in PersonaKnob (up to 10.8% human‑audit rate) may bias the preference signal. Can the dataset be cleaned to near‑zero error, and what is the impact on training ceilings?

## Limitations
- **Dataset integrity**: PersonaKnob relies on LLM‑generated negatives; if `y-d` systematically fail multiple dimensions, the partial‑order signal is contaminated.  
- **Generalizability**: Results are demonstrated mainly on Qwen3‑4B; performance on other architectures or larger scales remains uncertain.  
- **Evaluation dependence**: MFRM calibration uses two unrevealed LLM judges; shared biases with training data could introduce circularity.  
- **Ideal attainability**: The paper shows feasibility on one model, but the “Dignified Peer” ideal may not be universally achievable across model capacities.

## Confidence
- **High**: Core mechanism (H&H tension) and dataset design logic are well‑argued and supported by ablations.  
- **Medium**: Lag‑DPO’s consistent superiority across hyperparameter settings is shown, but optimal settings appear model‑specific.  
- **Low**: That the Dignified Peer ideal is universally achievable; evidence is limited to Qwen3‑4B and one base model.

## Next Checks
1. **Human audit of partial‑order**: Sample 500 PersonaKnob instances; have experts verify that `y*` satisfies all active dimensions and each `y-d` fails only its target dimension. Target: >90% agreement with the paper’s 91.2% pass rate.  
2. **Gradient conflict quantification**: Compute per‑dimension loss gradient cosine similarities on a held‑out batch. Expect strong negative correlations pre‑training and confirm that `λ_d` increases precisely for dimensions with negative alignment.  
3. **Cross‑model OOD test**: Apply the trained Lag‑DPO (Qwen3‑4B) to Llama‑3‑8B *without retraining*. If sycophancy/refusal gains hold, the framework is robust; if collapse occurs, it indicates model‑specific tuning needs.
