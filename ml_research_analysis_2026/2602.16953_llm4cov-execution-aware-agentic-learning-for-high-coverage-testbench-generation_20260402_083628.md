# LLM4Cov: Execution-Aware Agentic Learning for High-coverage Testbench Generation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.16953
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16953
- **Reference count:** 30
- **Primary result:** A 4B-parameter model achieves 69.2% coverage pass rate, improving its base model by 40.8 percentage points and outperforming its 30B teacher by 5.3%, while matching or exceeding models 50–100× larger.

## Executive Summary
Hardware verification via testbench generation suffers from prohibitive costs of iterative simulator feedback, limiting the scalability of agentic learning. LLM4Cov introduces an offline agent-learning framework that avoids online interaction by modeling verification as deterministic, memoryless state transitions and curating training data from the student’s own failures. On a benchmark derived from CVDP, a 4B-parameter model achieves a 69.2% coverage pass rate, demonstrating substantial gains over its base model and teacher, and competitive performance against much larger models. The approach validates that execution-aware supervision and distribution-aligned training can yield efficient verification agents under tight simulator budgets.

## Method Summary
LLM4Cov is an offline framework for training LLM-based testbench generators without online simulator interaction. It defines a memoryless MDP where state `st = (R, xt, ot)` includes the design, current testbench, and latest execution feedback. Training proceeds in stages: at each stage `k`, the current student model samples intermediate states, the worst-coverage state is selected, candidate next testbenches are generated and filtered by coverage improvement, and the resulting transition pairs `(st, xt+1)` are used for supervised fine-tuning. This worst-state-prioritized, verification-conditioned progressive learning aligns supervision with the evolving student distribution, maximizing the learning signal per expensive simulator call.

## Key Results
- 4B model achieves 69.2% coverage pass rate on CVDP-ECov benchmark.
- Improves base model by 40.8 percentage points and beats 30B teacher by 5.3%.
- Matches or exceeds performance of models 50–100× larger (e.g., GPT-4, Claude 3).
- Reduces reliance on online execution via offline agent learning with curated data.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Memoryless State Transitions
Models verification as deterministic, memoryless transitions: `st = (R, xt, ot)` and `Mθ(xt+1 | s0:t) = Mθ(xt+1 | st)`. This discards full history, forcing relevant context (e.g., failure causes) into the testbench and feedback, reducing prompt redundancy while preserving necessary information. Evidence shows memoryless agents outperform history-conditioned ones (e.g., Qwen3-Coder-30B: 63.9% vs 59.5%). Break condition: designs requiring explicit tracking of multi-round sequential semantics may lose critical patterns.

### Mechanism 2: Worst-State-Prioritized Sampling
From student-sampled intermediate states, selects the worst-coverage state `sworst`. Generates candidate `xt+1` and retains only those with coverage improvement `≥ τ∆`, keeping the maximal improvement. This concentrates supervision on recovering from failures rather than easy successes. Evidence: worst-state selection yields 66% Cov Pass vs. uniform (61.7%) in ablation. Break condition: noisy or coarse coverage metrics may cause single worst-state selection to miss nuanced failures.

### Mechanism 3: Verification-Conditioned Progressive Learning
Training stages `k` synthesize dataset `D(k)` using current student `M(k)` via the worst-state procedure, then fine-tune to `M(k+1)`. This aligns supervision with the student’s evolving state distribution. Contrasted with naive mixing of all stages’ data, progressive learning avoids diluting recovery signals. Evidence: staged training (69.2%) beats naive augmentation (~65%). Break condition: early student plateau may make staged overhead unrewarding.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Imitation Learning & Covariate Shift (DAgger)**
  - **Why needed:** Addresses mismatch between teacher-generated training states and student’s visited states during deployment. LLM4Cov uses progressive alignment instead of aggregating all teacher data.
  - **Quick check:** Why would training only on teacher trajectories fail for a student agent, even if the teacher is optimal?

- **Concept: Coverage Metrics in Hardware Verification**
  - **Why needed:** Coverage is the primary dense reward; it measures exercised design logic (branches, lines, FSMs). Understanding its non-trivial improvement is key to interpreting `Cov(·)`.
  - **Quick check:** If a testbench achieves 100% coverage, what does that imply about the design's correctness? (Coverage ≠ bug absence.)

- **Concept: Deterministic, Memoryless MDP Formulation**
  - **Why needed:** The framework rests on `st → xt+1 → st+1` transitions with policy depending only on `st`. This simplifies offline learning by removing historical dependencies.
  - **Quick check:** In `st = (R, xt, ot)`, what information from round `t−1` is captured, and what might be lost vs. full history?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:**  
  Simulator Interface -> State Constructor -> Trajectory Synthesizer -> Stage Manager -> SFT Trainer -> Updated Student

- **Critical path:**  
  Data generation (sample states via current student) -> worst-state selection -> candidate generation (teacher/student) -> coverage-filtered transition -> stage-aligned SFT -> updated student -> repeat.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  Memoryless vs. history: reduces prompt length but may lose implicit debugging context.  
  Worst-state vs. diversity: maximizes recovery signal per simulator call but risks overfitting to one failure mode.  
  Teacher vs. self-sampling transitions: early stages use teacher for stability; later self-sampling captures student-specific strategies but may yield lower-quality repairs if student is weak.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  High syntax error rate in Stage 0: insufficient execution-validated curation.  
  No coverage gain from Stage 1→2: student plateau; progressive learning yields diminishing returns.  
  Agentic performance drop vs. direct inference: training data does not match interaction distribution; revisit worst-state selection or stage alignment.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Memoryless ablation: train agents with full history vs. memoryless state; expect memoryless ≥ vanilla.  
  2. State selection test: compare worst-state, uniform, median, best-state in Stage 1; expect worst-state highest Cov Pass.  
  3. Progressive vs. naive augmentation: train on `D(0)+D(1)+D(2)` sequentially vs. mixed; expect progressive higher final coverage.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
Does the memoryless state transition model universally hold for hardware verification tasks beyond the CVDP-ECov benchmark, particularly for designs requiring multi-step reasoning or long-range dependency tracking?

### Open Question 2
Can LLM4Cov's execution-aware agentic learning be extended to jointly optimize coverage and bug detection, and what architectural modifications are needed to incorporate assertion-based feedback?

### Open Question 3
Is the fixed three-stage progressive learning schedule optimal, or can an adaptive curriculum that dynamically adjusts stage transitions based on student model performance or state distribution characteristics improve sample efficiency?

### Open Question 4
How well does LLM4Cov transfer to hardware verification tasks involving different HDLs (e.g., VHDL, Chisel) or industry-standard methodologies like UVM, which have distinct syntax and verification patterns?

## Limitations
- Memoryless assumption may discard critical sequential context for complex designs.
- Coverage feedback could be noisy or coarsely binned, undermining worst-state selection.
- Evaluation confined to CVDP-ECov with SystemVerilog; generalizability to other HDLs and benchmarks is untested.
- Claims about matching 50–100× larger models are unsupported by direct comparison data.

## Confidence
- **High:** Memoryless benefit (Table 1), worst-state sampling (Fig 5), progressive learning (Fig 6).
- **Medium:** 4B vs. teacher/base improvement (no raw data disclosed).
- **Low:** Performance vs. 50–100× larger models (no substantiated baseline evaluations).

## Next Checks
