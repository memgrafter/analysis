# ADRD-Bench: A Preliminary LLM Benchmark for Alzheimer's Disease and Related Dementias

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.11460  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11460  
- **Reference Count:** 40  
- **Primary Result:** Evaluation of 33 LLMs on ADRD-Bench showed open-weight general models accuracy 0.63–0.93 (mean 0.78), open-weight medical models 0.48–0.93 (mean 0.82), and closed-source general models 0.83–0.91 (mean 0.89). Top models exceeded 0.9 accuracy, but case studies revealed reasoning inconsistencies and stability issues.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces ADRD-Bench, a novel benchmark designed to address the severe lack of Alzheimer’s Disease and Related Dementias (ADRD)-specific evaluation resources for Large Language Models (LLMs). The benchmark integrates two components: (1) ADRD Unified QA, aggregating 1,352 ADRD-relevant questions from seven existing medical benchmarks, and (2) ADRD Caregiving QA, comprising 149 new, practical caregiving questions derived from the evidence-based Aging Brain Care program. Evaluation of 33 state-of-the-art LLMs reveals high accuracy (up to mean 0.89 for closed-source models), yet qualitative case studies highlight brittle reasoning and instability, underscoring the need for domain-specific safety improvements. The dataset is publicly available.

## Method Summary
ADRD-Bench consists of two curated question sets: ADRD Unified QA (1,352 multiple-choice or true/false items extracted from seven public medical QA datasets) and ADRD Caregiving QA (149 novel items based on the Aging Brain Care program’s caregiver education materials). Models are assessed in a zero-shot setting with strictly formatted prompts that force a single-letter or numeric output. Inference is run for each question, and responses are scored via exact match against gold labels. Accuracy is reported per subset and overall, and correlation analyses plus qualitative case studies (using chain-of-thought prompting) are conducted to diagnose reasoning failures.

## Key Results
- Evaluated 33 LLMs; open-weight general models accuracy 0.63–0.93 (mean 0.78).  
- Open-weight medical models accuracy 0.48–0.93 (mean 0.82).  
- Closed-source general models accuracy 0.83–0.91 (mean 0.89); several models surpassed 0.9.  
- Case studies uncovered inconsistent reasoning and stability limitations despite high aggregate scores.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Domain-Specific Benchmark Construction
- **Claim:** ADRD-Bench improves the validity of LLM safety assessment by addressing the severe under-coverage of ADRD in existing medical benchmarks.  
- **Mechanism:** It aggregates scattered ADRD-relevant questions from seven established medical QA datasets into a unified set (ADRD Unified QA), concentrating evaluation on a previously fragmented domain and raising its signal-to-noise ratio.  
- **Core Assumption:** A domain-specific evaluation scaffold is necessary because general medical benchmarks do not adequately stress-test models on the unique knowledge and reasoning required for ADRD care.  
- **Evidence Anchors:**  
  - Abstract: existing benchmarks include ADRD items “mostly under 1%.”  
  - Section 2.1, Table 1 quantifies <1% ADRD representation.  
  - Neighboring papers similarly note ADRD NLP resource gaps.  
- **Break Condition:** If a new, widely adopted general medical benchmark unexpectedly achieves >5% ADRD content coverage with high‑quality, care‑focused items, the primary rationale weakens.

### Mechanism 2: Practical Caregiving Context Integration
- **Claim:** Including a practical caregiving component (ADRD Caregiving QA) mitigates the “exam‑style” bias of existing benchmarks and introduces evaluation of real‑world, human‑centered ADRD support tasks.  
- **Mechanism:** The benchmark derives 149 novel True/False and multiple‑choice questions from the evidence‑based Aging Brain Care (ABC) program’s caregiver education materials, focusing on behavioral management, communication, and daily living support.  
- **Core Assumption:** LLM deployment safety for ADRD requires competence beyond clinical knowledge recall; it necessitates reasoning about context‑sensitive, non‑pharmacological caregiving scenarios.  
- **Evidence Anchors:**  
  - Abstract: ADRD Caregiving QA is “derived from the Aging Brain Care (ABC) program… designed to mitigate the lack of practical caregiving context.”  
  - Section 3.2 describes ABC as a mature, rigorously evaluated standard for high‑quality ADRD care.  
  - Neighboring paper highlights distinct mental health and practical needs of ADRD caregivers.  
- **Break Condition:** If subsequent large‑scale caregiver interaction studies show that performance on this multiple‑choice/True‑False format is uncorrelated (r < 0.1) with real‑world caregiver satisfaction or patient outcomes, the component’s ecological validity is questioned.

### Mechanism 3: Reasoning Diagnosis via Subset Split
- **Claim:** High aggregate accuracy is a necessary but insufficient indicator of reliable, safe model behavior; explicit reasoning analysis is required to uncover stability and generalization flaws.  
- **Mechanism:** The benchmark facilitates error pattern diagnosis by providing a clean split between clinical knowledge (Unified QA) and practical reasoning (Caregiving QA). Case studies on the latter reveal systematic failures like overgeneralization and goal misalignment, which are masked by overall >0.9 accuracy scores.  
- **Core Assumption:** Safety‑critical deployment demands not just correct answers but robust, context‑aware reasoning processes; benchmark design must enable the exposure of brittle reasoning.  
- **Evidence Anchors:**  
  - Abstract: “While top‑tier models achieved high accuracies (>0.9), case studies revealed that inconsistent reasoning quality and stability limit their reliability.”  
  - Section 4.4 details case analyses where models with >90% overall accuracy failed due to overgeneralizing disease progression and misinterpreting disengagement cues.  
  - Neighboring work discusses need for reliable reasoning in agentic ADRD systems.  
- **Break Condition:** If follow‑up work with chain‑of‑thought prompting and human expert evaluation finds that the identified error patterns (overgeneralization, goal misalignment) are non‑replicable across other ADRD caregiving benchmarks or resolved by standard fine‑tuning, the diagnostic power diminishes.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Domain-Specific Benchmark Construction**  
  - *Why needed here:* Understanding how to construct a valid evaluation from heterogeneous sources (aggregating existing datasets + generating novel items from evidence‑based programs) is core to trusting the benchmark’s results. One must grasp the trade‑offs between reusing established data versus creating new, context‑rich tasks.  
  - *Quick Check:* What is a key methodological risk when aggregating questions from multiple existing benchmarks into a unified set, and how did the authors attempt to mitigate it?  
- **Concept: Caregiving Context vs. Clinical Knowledge**  
  - *Why needed here:* The paper’s core argument is that ADRD care involves a significant, distinct “caregiving” dimension missing from exam‑style benchmarks. Learners must differentiate between assessing recall of medical facts (e.g., drug mechanisms) and assessing practical, situation‑based care decisions (e.g., responding to agitation).  
  - *Quick Check:* Why might a model with strong performance on MedQA or PubMedQA still fail on a question derived from the ABC program about managing patient behavior at home?  
- **Concept: Reasoning Quality vs. Accuracy Metrics**  
  - *Why needed here:* The paper emphasizes that exact‑match accuracy can hide critical flaws. Foundational learning requires understanding that safety evaluation must probe *how* an answer is reached (e.g., via case study analysis of chain‑of‑thought outputs), not just *what* answer is given.  
  - *Quick Check:* According to the case studies, what is a specific example of a reasoning error (not a knowledge gap) that led a high‑accuracy model to an incorrect answer on the Caregiving QA?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Data Curation Layer -> Evaluation Engine -> Analysis & Diagnostic Layer  

**Critical Path:**  
Raw Data Sources -> ADRD Relevance Filtering -> Question Format Standardization -> Prompt Template Application -> Model Inference -> Exact‑Match Scoring -> Correlation & Case Analysis  

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- Coverage vs. Depth: Aggregating from existing datasets provides breadth but inherits limitations (e.g., multiple‑choice format bias). Creating novel ABC questions adds critical depth but limits scale (149 items).  
- Controlled Evaluation vs. Ecological Validity: Using rigid single‑letter output prompts ensures reliable, automated scoring but eliminates assessment of open‑ended communication, empathy, or multi‑turn dialogue.  
- Generalizability vs. Specificity: Rooting Caregiving QA in the U.S.-centric ABC model ensures expert‑validated, high‑quality content but limits direct cross‑cultural applicability.  

**Failure Signatures:**  
- High overall accuracy (>0.9) paired with a significant performance drop (>0.15) on the Caregiving QA subset compared to Unified QA.  
- Weak or non‑significant correlation (r < 0.3, p > 0.05) between a model’s Unified QA and Caregiving QA scores, indicating disjoint competency profiles.  
- Reproducible case study patterns: models consistently reasoning from a salient but non‑universal feature (e.g., late‑stage symptoms) to overgeneralized conclusions.  

**First Three Experiments:**  
1. **Replication & Baseline:** Run the complete pipeline (using provided prompts and dataset) on a small, managed set of 3‑4 models (e.g., Llama‑3.1‑8B‑Instruct, Meditron3‑70B). Verify that reported baseline numbers in the paper for these models are reproducible within ±0.02.  
2. **Subset Stress Test:** Isolate the 149 Caregiving QA questions. Focus on the 5 models with the largest performance gap between Unified and Caregiving QA (from Table 2). Confirm if the gap persists and qualitatively inspect CoT reasoning for those specific failures.  
3. **Prompt Sensitivity Check:** For a single high‑performing model (e.g., GPT‑5.2), re‑run the Caregiving QA set while slightly modifying the instruction prompt (e.g., adding “Consider the caregiver’s goal to reduce patient distress”). Quantify if strict adherence to the provided prompt format is critical to the reported scores, assessing robustness to minor deployment variations.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** What specific reasoning failure modes (e.g., overgeneralization, goal misalignment) characterize LLM errors in ADRD caregiving scenarios?  
  *Basis:* Case studies in Section 4.4 identified overgeneralization across disease stages and misalignment with caregiving goals as common errors. Why unresolved: analysis was qualitative and limited to high‑error items; no systematic taxonomy exists. *Evidence:* Large‑scale annotation of model reasoning traces across both Unified QA and Caregiving QA sets, categorized by failure type, clinical severity, and model characteristics.  
- **Open Question 2:** Why do domain‑specific medical LLMs fail to consistently outperform general‑purpose LLMs on ADRD tasks despite targeted training?  
  *Basis:* Section 4.2 states medical models showed no clear advantage and sometimes underperformed general models of similar parameter counts. Why unresolved: the paper speculates caregiving relies on commonsense but does not empirically investigate why medical fine‑tuning does not reliably transfer to practical caregiving contexts. *Evidence:* Controlled experiments comparing training data distributions, fine‑tuning objectives, and performance decomposition on knowledge‑intensive vs. reasoning‑intensive question subsets.  
- **Open Question 3:** How can ADRD‑Bench be systematically expanded to better represent the diversity of real‑world ADRD caregiving scenarios while maintaining clinical validity?  
  *Basis:* Limitations acknowledge the caregiving set is “modest in size” (149 questions) and derived solely from the ABC program, limiting scope and generalizability. Why unresolved: No methodology exists for scaling ADRD benchmarks across cultural contexts, care settings, disease stages, or multiple evidence‑based care models. *Evidence:* Development of a multi‑source curation framework incorporating additional validated programs, caregiver narratives, and clinical case repositories, with diversity metrics and expert validation.  
- **Open Question 4:** What evaluation paradigms beyond single‑turn forced‑choice accuracy are needed to assess LLM performance on multi‑turn, empathetic ADRD caregiving dialogues?  
  *Basis:* Limitations state the benchmark “does not measure a model's ability in conversational interactions, such as proactively demonstrating empathy” and uses only single‑turn Q&A. Why unresolved: The field lacks validated metrics for conversational quality, emotional attunement, consistency, and safety in extended ADRD care interactions. *Evidence:* Creation of multi‑turn dialogue benchmarks with human evaluation rubrics for empathy, harm reduction, caregiver support quality, and longitudinal coherence, correlated with real‑world outcomes.

## Limitations
- The ADRD Caregiving QA subset is small (149 questions) and rooted in a U.S.-centric program, limiting generalizability.  
- The rigid multiple‑choice/True‑False format cannot evaluate open‑ended caregiving dialogue or empathy.  
- Reproducibility is hindered by unspecified model checkpoints, inference parameters, and potential data leakage.
