# TMS: Trajectory-Mixed Supervision for Reward-Free, On-Policy SFT

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.03073  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03073  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: Trajectory-Mixed Supervision (TMS) matches or exceeds standard SFT on target-task accuracy while drastically reducing catastrophic forgetting on held-out benchmarks, shifting the accuracy-retention Pareto frontier without reward models or RL.

## Executive Summary
Standard supervised fine-tuning (SFT) improves performance on a target task but often causes catastrophic forgetting on other capabilities. This stems from a temporal supervision mismatch: the model’s evolving policy is trained against static reference labels, leading to mode collapse and brittleness. Trajectory-Mixed Supervision (TMS) addresses this by replacing fixed supervision with a mixture of outputs from the model’s own intermediate checkpoints. This creates a near-policy curriculum that stabilizes learning. Across multiple model scales and reasoning benchmarks, TMS achieves target accuracy comparable to SFT while drastically improving retention of base-model capabilities (e.g., ARC-C: -1.4 vs. -37.8 points drop). TMS attains retention similar to on-policy RL methods like GRPO but with a simpler, reward-free procedure.

## Method Summary
TMS requires a baseline SFT run to harvest trajectories. First, train a model on the target task for a fixed budget, saving *T* checkpoints at uniform intervals. For each checkpoint and training input, generate one response using a fixed decoding policy. These are stored in a trajectory buffer. Then, train a fresh student model from the same initialization. For each minibatch, with probability α (default 0.25) use the original reference completion; otherwise sample a checkpoint uniformly and use its harvested trajectory as the target. The student is trained with standard token-level negative log-likelihood for the same compute budget as the baseline SFT. The mixture distribution effectively shifts supervision toward the student’s evolving policy, avoiding large distributional shifts that cause forgetting.

## Key Results
- TMS matches SFT on target-task accuracy (e.g., MATH) while reducing catastrophic forgetting on held-out benchmarks (e.g., ARC-C drop: -1.4 vs. -37.8 for Qwen-2.5-3B).
- On average cross-task delta, TMS incurs only -2.3 points vs. SFT’s -39.2 (Qwen-2.5-3B).
- TMS achieves retention comparable to on-policy RL (GRPO) but without reward modeling or reinforcement learning loops.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1 – Temporal Supervision Mismatch
**Claim**: When a policy (π_θ) evolves under SFT while the supervision distribution q(y|x) remains static, a drift occurs where held-out generalization degrades even as training loss improves.  
**Mechanism**: Cross-entropy loss supplies large corrective gradients when π_θ(y*|x) shrinks, pulling the model toward rigid templates that overfit surface forms rather than generalizable features.  
**Break condition**: If the policy remains closely aligned with the reference distribution on held-out inputs (PLD stays low), the brittleness cascade is avoided.

### Mechanism 2 – Mode Collapse under Single-Reference CE
**Claim**: For tasks with multiple valid solution paths, token-level CE on a single reference suppresses alternative reasoning modes, reducing diversity and robustness.  
**Mechanism**: The training signal only reinforces the specific labeled trajectory y*; no gradient encourages probability mass on other correct but different outputs y'.  
**Break condition**: If the task has a nearly unique solution format (e.g., multiple-choice QA), mode collapse is less severe; TMS benefits are smaller on such tasks.

### Mechanism 3 – PLD-Controlled Forgetting
**Claim**: Drift in Policy-Label Divergence (PLD) on held-out data is a mechanistic predictor of catastrophic forgetting, and TMS mitigates forgetting by minimizing this drift.  
**Mechanism**: PLD(θ_t; q) ≈ held-out validation NLL measures the gap between current policy and supervision distribution. Increasing PLD (“mismatch drift”) correlates with degradation on unseen tasks. TMS replaces q with a trajectory mixture m(·|x) that keeps supervision near the evolving policy, constraining PLD growth.  
**Break condition**: If PLD on held-out data does not exhibit a rising phase, catastrophic forgetting is less likely; however, the paper shows a drift phase is common in standard SFT on non-unique tasks.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)**  
*Why needed*: SFT is the baseline whose failure modes (mismatch, mode collapse) TMS fixes. Understanding its objective L_SFT(θ) = -E[(x,y*)][log π_θ(y*|x)] is essential.  
*Quick check*: If you minimize cross-entropy against a fixed y* during continued training, what happens when π_θ starts generating valid but different outputs for the same x?

**Concept: Policy-Label Divergence (PLD)**  
*Why needed*: PLD is the core diagnostic metric introduced to quantify temporal supervision mismatch: D_KL(q(·|x) || π_θ(·|x)).  
*Quick check*: How is held-out PLD approximated in practice for single-reference SFT, and why does it initially decrease but later increase during training?

**Concept: KL Divergence as a Capability Bound**  
*Why needed*: Theorem 6.1 proves that the change in any bounded performance metric is bounded by the expected KL divergence from the base model, justifying KL-to-base as a predictor of forgetting.  
*Quick check*: Given the theorem, if your post-trained model has a KL-to-base of 2.0 nats on evaluation prompts, what can you say about the maximum possible absolute drop in any 0-1 scored task?

**Concept: On-Policy vs. Off-Policy Learning**  
*Why needed*: The key distinction is that RL updates use targets from the current policy (on-policy in y|x), while SFT uses static targets (off-policy). TMS approximates on-policy stability without RL.  
*Quick check*: Why does generating supervision targets from the model’s own historical checkpoints help emulate on-policy benefits?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:
Trajectory Buffer (H) -> Checkpoint Sampler -> Mixture Distribution q_α(·|x) -> Student Model (π_ϕ) -> Evaluation Monitor

**Critical path**:
1. Harvest: Run baseline post-training (e.g., SFT) for a fixed budget; save T checkpoints at uniform intervals; generate one sample per (checkpoint, training example) using fixed decoding.
2. Mix: For each minibatch, for each example x: with prob α sample reference y*; else sample t~p(t) and use b^(t)(x).
3. Train: Update student π_ϕ by minimizing NLL on the mixed minibatch.
4. Validate: Periodically compute PLD on a held-out split of the training task and accuracy on retention benchmarks to monitor drift/forgetting.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- T (number of checkpoints): Larger T captures a longer, more diverse portion of the optimization path, potentially improving mode preservation. However, very large T adds noise from poor early checkpoints and may re-introduce late-stage over-specialization. Default: T=10.
- α (oracle weight): Higher α anchors to high-quality references, improving target accuracy but reducing trajectory influence. Lower α boosts retention but risks target accuracy if early checkpoints are noisy. Default: α=0.25.
- p(t) (checkpoint sampling distribution): Uniform is robust. Early-heavy increases diversity but injects low-quality supervision; late-heavy preserves correctness but may revive mismatch. Use uniform unless a specific bias is desired.

**Failure signatures**:
- High held-out PLD / validation NLL increase during student training (mismatch drift not contained).
- Sharp drop in answer entropy or Pass@100 on the target task, indicating mode collapse despite target accuracy gains.
- Large cross-task delta and retention collapse, especially if KL-to-base is high.
- Target performance degradation if T is too small (insufficient diversity) or α is too low (too much early noise).

**First 3 experiments**:
1. Baseline Forgetting Replication: Run standard SFT on a held-out task (e.g., Qwen-2.5-3B on MATH). Verify: training NLL ↓, validation NLL ↑ after knee point, ARC-C retention drop >30 points.
2. TMS vs. SFT Head-to-Head: With identical T, α=0.25, and total compute, compare TMS Student vs. Final SFT checkpoint. Expected: similar target accuracy on MATH, but ARC-C retention within 2-3 points of base (vs. ~-35 for SFT).
3. PLD Trajectory Visualization: For both SFT and TMS (student), compute and plot held-out PLD (validation NLL) over the student’s training steps. TMS should show a flat or slowly rising PLD, while SFT (student trained on static y*) should exhibit the knee-and-rise pattern.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: Does TMS's retention advantage hold for models beyond 8B parameters?  
  *Basis*: The paper only tests up to 8B; larger models may differ.
- **Open Question 2**: Can adaptive checkpoint selection improve TMS over uniform mixing?  
  *Basis*: The paper ablates fixed distributions but uses uniform; adaptivity unexplored.
- **Open Question 3**: Does TMS generalize to multimodal models and non-reasoning tasks like code generation?  
  *Basis*: Evaluation mostly on text reasoning; only a tiny multimodal pilot.
- **Open Question 4**: Can theory guarantee that TMS's trajectory mixture yields lower forgetting than SFT?  
  *Basis*: Theorem 6.1 bounds forgetting via KL but does not connect mixture to that bound.

## Limitations
- Decoding parameters (temperature, top_p, max_new_tokens) for trajectory harvesting and evaluation are not reported.
- Prompt formatting and chat templates per model-task pair are unspecified, hindering exact replication.
- Held-out splits for PLD and safety benchmarks, and any judge models used for evaluation, remain unclear.

## Confidence
- Catastrophic forgetting mitigation: **High** – robust empirical results across three model scales and diverse benchmarks.
- PLD as forgetting predictor: **Medium** – strong correlation and theoretical bound, but causal isolation unverified.
- Generalizability beyond 3B models: **Medium** – scaling studies limited; conclusions largely drawn from Qwen-2.5-3B and small LLaMA variants.

## Next Checks
1. **Scale stress test**: Replicate TMS on 7B/13B models; verify that forgetting reduction and target accuracy gains persist at larger scales.
2. **Hyperparameter ablation**: Systematically vary T (5–20) and α (0.0–0.5); confirm reported defaults (T=10, α=0.25) are near-optimal and the accuracy-retention Pareto holds.
3. **Task uniqueness analysis**: Compare SFT vs. TMS on low-entropy (multiple-choice QA) vs. high-entropy (open-ended reasoning) tasks; validate that TMS benefits diminish for near-unique solution spaces.
