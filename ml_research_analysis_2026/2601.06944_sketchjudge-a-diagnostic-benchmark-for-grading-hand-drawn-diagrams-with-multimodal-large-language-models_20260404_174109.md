# SketchJudge: A Diagnostic Benchmark for Grading Hand-drawn Diagrams with Multimodal Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.06944
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06944
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** State-of-the-art MLLMs significantly underperform humans in grading hand-drawn STEM diagrams (e.g., GPT-5: 78.42% vs. human: 83.33% accuracy) and struggle with robustness, reference-free grading, and fine-grained error diagnosis.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces SketchJudge, a novel diagnostic benchmark designed to evaluate the capability of Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) to grade hand-drawn student diagrams in STEM domains. The benchmark comprises 1,015 annotated student responses across geometry, physics, charts, and flowcharts, each labeled for binary correctness and a fine-grained error taxonomy. Systematic experiments reveal that current MLLMs, despite advances, exhibit substantial performance gaps compared to human graders, particularly in handling sketch noise, performing reference-free evaluation, and consistently recognizing nuanced error types. The work exposes critical limitations in the vision-language alignment of MLLMs when applied to noisy, symbolic visual contexts.

## Method Summary
The evaluation is conducted in a strict zero-shot setting using a dataset of 300 problems and 1,015 student-drawn answers. For each instance, the model receives a triple `{Question, Reference Diagram?, Student Answer}` and must output a structured JSON object containing a binary correctness judgment (`is_correct`) and a list of fine-grained error types from a domain-specific taxonomy (23 categories total). Performance is measured using binary metrics (Accuracy, FNR, FPR) and a multi-label example-based F1 (`ebF1`). The study manipulates input conditions (presence of question diagram and reference diagram) and prompting strategies (Baseline, Rubric, Chain-of-Thought) to diagnose model capabilities and failure modes.

## Key Results
- **Accuracy Gap:** The best model (GPT-5) achieves 78.42% accuracy, trailing human inter-annotator agreement (83.33%).
- **Error Diagnosis:** Models attain a maximum `ebF1` of 61.13 on error-type recognition, substantially below the human upper bound of 66.42.
- **Critical Weaknesses:** MLLMs show brittleness to sketch variations, poor performance in reference-free settings, and inconsistent bias patterns (over-strict or over-lenient grading), with chain-of-thought prompting often degrading performance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Hand-drawn noise disrupts vision-language alignment.
- **Claim:** The perceptual noise and stylistic variation in student sketches cause MLLMs' visual encoders to produce out-of-distribution embeddings that fail to map reliably to the semantic symbols required for grading.
- **Mechanism:** MLLMs pre-trained on clean, standardized images develop brittle feature extractors. Ambiguous strokes, construction lines, and skew impede accurate parsing, leading the language component to rationalize based on shallow perceptual matches rather than robust structural understanding.
- **Core Assumption:** Models rely on surface-level pattern matching instead of abstraction-level diagram parsing.
- **Evidence Anchors:** Abstract states it "exposes the fragility of current vision-language alignment"; Section 3.4 discusses complications from perceptual noise; neighboring work on "structural blindness" in schematics supports the claim.

### Mechanism 2: Diagnostic grading requires multi-layered reasoning absent in MLLMs.
- **Claim:** Grading necessitates sequential perceptual parsing, structural/topological analysis, application of domain rules, and metacognitive plausibility checks—a pipeline MLLMs do not execute consistently.
- **Mechanism:** Models falter at the structural and semantic layers, especially with subtle errors (e.g., missing labels altering flowchart logic) or valid but unconventional layouts. Their reasoning appears as post-hoc rationalization of an initial perceptual judgment.
- **Core Assumption:** The model's "reasoning" is not a true multi-stage diagnostic process.
- **Evidence Anchors:** Abstract notes dependence on "complex structural, semantic, and metacognitive reasoning"; Section 4.3.4 shows high recall for salient local cues but low for global/strategy errors.

### Mechanism 3: Over-reliance on reference diagrams masks weak internal solvers.
- **Claim:** Performance drops severely in NoRef settings because models cannot internally generate a canonical solution structure for comparison.
- **Mechanism:** With a reference, the task reduces to visual-semantic matching. Without it, the model must first solve the problem (construct the correct diagram) and then compare. The large WithRef-NoRef delta indicates a lack of a robust internal diagrammatic reasoning model.
- **Core Assumption:** Models default to a "compare to reference" heuristic rather than independent verification.
- **Evidence Anchors:** Abstract frames NoRef as a stress-test for direct reasoning; Section 4.3.2 shows reference primarily aids correctness verification but not deep error typing.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Vision-Language Alignment in Noisy Domains
- **Why needed here:** The core failure is misalignment between messy sketch features and the semantic symbols the LLM expects. Understanding this gap is prerequisite to diagnosing *why* a model fails (e.g., encoder vs. world model).
- **Quick check:** If you perturb a clean reference diagram with random ink blots, at what level (pixel, object, scene) would visual features diverge most, and why would that matter for grading?

### Concept: Multi-Label Error Taxonomy Construction
- **Why needed here:** SketchJudge’s innovation is a structured, domain-specific error taxonomy (23 types). Its construction (bottom-up clustering + expert refinement) is a curriculum defining the "skills" to measure.
- **Quick check:** For two flowchart errors—(A) wrong connection branch, (B) wrong shape type—what are the pros and cons of grouping them under one "Symbol Error" versus keeping them separate for model diagnosis?

## Architecture Onboarding

Input Interface -> MLLM Backbone -> Prompt Template Engine -> Post-Processor -> Scoring Module

The evaluation pipeline flows: Problem Instance -> Prompt Template (with Q, R?, A, Taxonomy) -> MLLM Forward Pass -> Text Output -> JSON Extraction -> Metric Calculation. The **MLLM Forward Pass** is the critical and variable node determining all downstream metrics.

**Design Tradeoffs:**
- *Reference Availability:* WithRef boosts accuracy but encourages lazy matching; NoRef isolates internal solver capability.
- *Prompt Complexity:* Simple Baseline prompts are most robust; adding Rubric/CoT often hurts by introducing distraction.
- *Taxonomy Granularity:* Fine-grained labels enable diagnosis but increase multi-label difficulty; coarser labels yield higher but less actionable scores.

**Failure Signatures:**
- High FNR (over-strict): Intolerance of visual variations or missing expected elements.
- High FPR (over-lenient): Missing subtle semantic errors (e.g., reversed logic).
- Large WithRef→NoRef drop: Indicates weak internal solution construction.
- Poor *Alt* subset performance: Brittleness to valid alternative correct layouts.
- Inconsistent per-class recall: Unstable diagnostic capability across error types.

**First 3 Experiments:**
1. **Baseline Establishment:** Run all models on WithRef + Baseline prompt; record overall Acc and ebF1.
2. **Reference Reliance Isolation:** For top 5 models, run NoRef; compute accuracy delta (WithRef - NoRef).
3. **Reasoning Strategy Probe:** For a strong (GPT-5) and weak (Gemma-3-4B-it) model, run Rubric and CoT prompts; measure changes in FNR/FPR.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What causes systematic grading biases (over-strictness vs. over-leniency) in MLLMs?
- **Basis:** Inferred from Section 4.3.1 and Figure 4 showing diverse FNR/FPR patterns.
- **Why Unresolved:** Paper identifies pattern but not causes (e.g., calibration, training mismatch).
- **Resolution Evidence:** Controlled experiments on model confidence, pretraining data analysis, fine-tuning on balanced grading data.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Why does chain-of-thought prompting degrade performance, and how can reasoning be effectively elicited for noisy visuals?
- **Basis:** Explicit from Section 4.3.3; CoT lowers accuracy and increases FNR.
- **Why Unresolved:** Hypothesis that CoT amplifies early visual errors is unvalidated; noise-reasoning interaction unclear.
- **Resolution Evidence:** Ablations separating perception from reasoning, CoT templates designed for ambiguity, measuring error propagation.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How can MLLMs improve fine-grained error-type recognition for globally structured errors (e.g., strategy, logic reversal)?
- **Basis:** Explicit from Section 4.3.4 and Figure 6; such errors have low recall and high variability.
- **Why Unresolved:** Benchmark diagnoses gap but tests no architectural/training solutions; lack of explicit structural representations.
- **Resolution Evidence:** Structured supervision (diagram-to-graph parsing), integration with symbolic verifiers/rule engines, evaluating these additions.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Can grading be reliable without reference diagrams, and what capabilities are needed?
- **Basis:** Explicit from Section 4.3.2; NoRef causes substantial drops, exposing limited problem-solving.
- **Why Unresolved:** Challenge is highlighted but no proposed/tested solutions; requires internal canonical solution construction.
- **Resolution Evidence:** Models that first generate internal solution from question, then compare; measuring if self-consistency improves NoRef accuracy.

## Limitations
- Proprietary model API specifics (endpoints, costs, rate limits) are undisclosed, hindering reproducibility logistics.
- Exact image preprocessing pipeline (resolution, normalization) is only in code, not described in the paper.
- Prompt templates are truncated in the paper; exact strings must be sourced from the repository, risking deviation.

## Confidence
- **Hand-drawn noise disrupts vision-language alignment:** High (direct experiments + supporting corpus).
- **Diagnostic reasoning gaps (perceptual → structural → semantic):** Medium (inferred from error-type analysis).
- **MLLMs over-rely on reference diagrams:** High (consistent WithRef vs. NoRef performance drop).

## Next Checks

2. **Reference reliance test:** Recompute accuracy delta between WithRef and NoRef for top-5 models; expect a ~10–15% drop, confirming weak internal solver capability (Table 23).
