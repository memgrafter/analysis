# MixSarc: A Bangla-English Code-Mixed Corpus for Implicit Meaning Identification
## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2602.21608
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21608
*   **Reference Count:** 22
*   **Primary Result:** A manually annotated corpus (MixSarc) reveals that fine-tuned transformers detect humor well (F1 ~0.71) but fail on sarcasm/offense/vulgarity (F1 ~0.0) due to extreme class imbalance, while zero-shot LLMs achieve competitive micro-F1 (~0.56) but poor holistic exact-match accuracy.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces MixSarc, the first large-scale manually annotated corpus for identifying four types of implicit meaning—humor, sarcasm, offensiveness, and vulgarity—in Bangla-English (Banglish) code-mixed social media text. The core contribution is a dataset of 9,087 sentences collected from social media and annotated by a diverse group of native speakers using majority voting to handle the inherent subjectivity of pragmatic tasks. Benchmarking demonstrates that state-of-the-art fine-tuned transformer models, while effective for the frequent humor class, catastrophically fail on minority classes (offense, vulgarity) because of severe data imbalance. Zero-shot large language models achieve reasonable performance on a per-label micro-averaged basis but struggle with the holistic, simultaneous prediction of all four labels, highlighting the difficulty of multi-label classification for nuanced, culturally embedded phenomena.

## Method Summary
The authors constructed the MixSarc corpus by scraping social media (Facebook, YouTube, e-commerce) and applying a two-stage filtering pipeline: first, heuristic rules removed emojis and normalized text; second, a mBERT-based classifier validated code-mixed sentences. Three independent native annotators labeled each sentence for the four binary categories, with final labels determined by majority voting. A balanced training subset was created by capping the primary classes (humor, sarcasm, offense, vulgarity) at 750 samples each, supplemented by 750 neutral samples. For modeling, they fine-tuned Banglish-BERT and Gemma-2B with a multi-label classification head using binary cross-entropy loss. Zero-shot evaluation used structured JSON prompts with LLaMA and Gemini models. Performance was assessed via per-label precision/recall/F1 (micro-averaged) and holistic exact-match accuracy.

## Key Results
*   Fine-tuned Banglish-BERT achieved strong humor detection (F1=0.708) but very low F1 for sarcasm (0.3998), offensive (0.0563), and vulgar (0.1928) categories.
*   Zero-shot LLMs (e.g., LLaMA-3.1-8B) yielded competitive micro-F1 scores (~0.56) but low exact-match accuracy (~0.26), indicating poor holistic multi-label prediction.
*   Analysis of an external sentiment dataset showed 42.13% of negative samples were also sarcastic, demonstrating sarcasm's confounding effect on standard sentiment analysis tasks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
*   **Claim:** A manually annotated, code-mixed corpus with majority voting yields reliable ground truth for culturally nuanced implicit tasks despite inherent subjectivity.
*   **Mechanism:** Social media collection captures authentic usage; systematic filtering removes noise; three independent native annotators per sentence with majority voting resolve disagreements, distributing the cognitive load of interpreting pragmatics across a demographically diverse group.
*   **Core assumption:** Collective human judgment, even with moderate Fleiss' κ (~0.26), better captures the natural variation in perceiving humor/sarcasm/offense than a single gold standard.
*   **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "...manually annotated sentences... multi-annotator majority voting." [section 3.4] Details annotation protocol, 18 annotators, majority voting rule. [corpus] Table 4 shows full vs. balanced label distribution; [section 3.6] reports Fleiss' κ = 0.26 and attributes it to task subjectivity and annotator diversity.
*   **Break condition:** If Fleiss' κ falls below 0.15 in a replication study, indicating either annotation guidelines are insufficient or the sample is exceptionally ambiguous, invalidating the "fair agreement" as a reliable proxy for label quality.

### Mechanism 2
*   **Claim:** Fine-tuned transformers (e.g., Banglish-BERT) effectively model code-mixed humorous and sarcastic cues via contextual embeddings but fail on offensive/vulgar minority classes due to overwhelming class imbalance.
*   **Mechanism:** Pretrained on similar mixed text, the model learns patterns for frequent classes (humor). For rare classes, the binary cross-entropy loss is dominated by majority-class gradients, so the model learns a "predict all zeros" heuristic that maximizes accuracy but yields F1 ~0.00.
*   **Core assumption:** The model architecture has sufficient capacity; the primary bottleneck is the training data distribution, not model expressiveness.
*   **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "Fine-tuned transformer models... achieve strong humor detection (F1 ~0.71) but suffer severe performance drops on sarcasm, offense, and vulgarity (F1 often near zero) due to extreme class imbalance." [section 5.1.1] Banglish-BERT humor F1=0.708, vulgar F1=0.1928, offensive F1=0.0563. Gemma-2B offensive F1=0.0000. [corpus] Table 4: Humorous (4,041) vs. Offensive (207) vs. Vulgar (290) in full dataset; balanced subset caps minority classes at 750/750.
*   **Break condition:** If applying class-weighted loss or aggressive oversampling (e.g., SMOTE for text) does not improve minority-class recall by at least 0.2 absolute F1, the deficit may stem from the subtle, context-dependent nature of the minority classes rather than mere frequency.

### Mechanism 3
*   **Claim:** Zero-shot large language models (LLMs) achieve competitive micro-F1 scores by generating high-recall, low-precision predictions across labels, but their holistic multi-label exact-match accuracy remains low due to inability to calibrate all four binary decisions per sentence simultaneously.
*   **Mechanism:** LLMs trained on diverse corpora recognize superficial lexical and pragmatic cues linked to each category (e.g., slurs for vulgarity), prompting them to label many positive instances across the board. However, without fine-tuning, they lack the specialized decision thresholds and interplay modeling needed for precise co-occurrence prediction (e.g., distinguishing sarcastic humor from pure humor).
*   **Core assumption:** The structured JSON prompt successfully elicits independent binary judgments, but LLMs do not internally model label dependencies or the dataset's specific prevalence rates.
*   **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "Zero-shot large language models yield competitive micro-F1 scores (~0.56) but low holistic exact-match accuracy, indicating multi-label prediction difficulty." [section 5.3] LLaMA-3.1-8B micro-F1=0.5615, exact-match=0.2616; Gemini-3-Flash micro-F1=0.5610, exact-match=0.2530. [corpus] The prompt in Figure 3 enforces four independent binary outputs with no joint reasoning constraint.
*   **Break condition:** If few-shot prompting with explicit examples of multi-label combinations (e.g., "This sentence is Humorous=1, Sarcastic=1, Offensive=0, Vulgar=0") improves exact-match accuracy by >0.15 absolute, the primary issue is prompt design rather than inherent LLM capability.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept:** Code-mixed linguistic variation (transliteration, intra-sentential switching)
    *   *Why needed here:* Models must handle non-standard spellings (e.g., "Ki boro genius") and script mixing (Romanized Bangla + English) to recognize semantic and pragmatic cues. Failure to normalize or identify language at token level propagates errors.
    *   *Quick check question:* How might a tokenizer that always assigns words to a single language (English or Bangla) fail on a sentence like "Ami.e tumi.je shobai.chilam?"?

*   **Concept:** Multi-label classification with co-occurring pragmatic categories
    *   *Why needed here:* A single utterance can be both humorous and sarcastic (or offensive and vulgar). Treating tasks as independent binary classifiers ignores label correlations that could improve precision (e.g., sarcasm strongly co-occurs with humor in this corpus).
    *   *Quick check question:* Why would a model that predicts each label independently with a sigmoid output likely produce more false positives for "Offensive" when "Sarcastic" is also true?

*   **Concept:** Class imbalance and its impact on evaluation metrics
    *   *Why needed here:* With vastly fewer offensive/vulgar samples, accuracy is misleading (model can get >95% by always predicting "Not Offensive"). Micro-F1 accounts for per-label imbalance, but exact-match is a stringent holistic measure.
    *   *Quick check question:* If a classifier predicts "Vulgar=1" for only 2 out of 290 vulgar test sentences (recall=0.007) but is correct on all 9,000+ non-vulgar predictions, what will its accuracy and micro-F1 be approximately, and why is that score deceptive?

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:** 
    Scraper -> Emoji/Cleaning Filter (Algorithm 1) -> mBERT Code-Mix Validator (Algorithm 2) -> Annotation Interface -> Majority Voting -> Balanced Subset -> Transformer Encoder (Banglish-BERT/Gemma-2B) + Multi-Label Head -> Evaluation Metrics (Per-label F1, Exact-Match) OR Zero-Shot LLM + Structured JSON Prompt -> Metrics
*   **Critical path:** Raw social posts → Filtered code-mixed sentences → Annotated corpus (with agreement metrics) → Balanced subset for training → Supervised fine-tuning OR zero-shot inference → Metrics calculation → Error analysis (e.g., sarcasm confusion in sentiment).
*   **Design tradeoffs:** 
    *   *mBERT token-level classifier* for code-mix validation is simple but may miss nuanced Banglish; a dedicated transliteration model would be more precise but requires more resources.
    *   *Balanced subset* ensures minority-class learning but distorts natural prevalence; full-dataset training with class weights preserves distribution but risks vanishing gradients.
    *   *Exact-match accuracy* is a strict holistic measure but can be too harsh for multi-label tasks; micro-F1 is more forgiving but may mask pairwise label errors.
*   **Failure signatures:** 
    *   Annotation: Fleiss' κ < 0.2 across a random 10% subset after training.
    *   Modeling: On the balanced test set, any minority class (offensive/vulgar) F1 < 0.1 after fine-tuning.
    *   Zero-shot: Exact-match accuracy < 0.2 *and* micro-recall > 0.7, indicating high false positive rate across labels.
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1.  Reproduce corpus statistics & agreement: Parse the provided dataset, verify label distributions match Table 4, compute Fleiss' κ on a held-out annotator subset to confirm ~0.26.
    2.  Benchmark fine-tuning on balanced subset: Fine-tune Banglish-BERT with BCE loss, track per-label F1 on validation set; expect humor ~0.7, sarcasm ~0.4, offensive/vulgar ~0.0 without class weighting.
    3.  Zero-shot LLM prompt engineering: Run the provided JSON prompt on 100 test samples with LLaMA-3.1-8B; manually inspect false positives/negatives to understand whether errors stem from cultural context (e.g., Banglish idioms) or prompt format parsing failures.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Open Question 1:** How can the severe class imbalance in offensive and vulgarity detection be addressed to improve recall for minority classes?
    *   *Basis in paper:* [explicit] The authors explicitly identify class imbalance as the root cause of near-zero recall for offensive and vulgar categories in transformer models and propose dataset expansion and targeted augmentation as solutions (Section 7, Limitations and Future Directions).
    *   *Why unresolved:* Offensive and vulgar samples are rare and contextually nuanced, making them prone to being ignored by models without specialized imbalance-handling techniques.
    *   *What evidence would resolve it:* Systematic experiments applying oversampling, class-weighted loss functions, or culturally specific data augmentation on the balanced subset, with evaluation focused on recall improvements for these minority classes.

*   **Open Question 2:** Can few-shot prompting with instruction-tuned LLMs (e.g., GPT-4, Claude) significantly improve exact-match accuracy for multi-label implicit meaning detection compared to zero-shot baselines?
    *   *Basis in paper:* [explicit] The future work section explicitly recommends evaluating instruction-tuned LLMs under few-shot prompting to address the low exact-match accuracy observed in zero-shot evaluations (Section 7).
    *   *Why unresolved:* Zero-shot LLMs achieve competitive micro-F1 but fail at holistic multi-label prediction, suggesting a need for better task adaptation through in-context examples.
    *   *What evidence would resolve it:* Few-shot experiments with varying numbers and types of exemplars on the MixSarc test set, comparing exact-match accuracy and per-label F1-scores against the reported zero-shot results.

*   **Open Question 3:** How can annotator agreement be improved for highly subjective, culturally embedded categories like sarcasm and humor in code-mixed corpora?
    *   *Basis in paper:* [inferred] The paper reports fair Fleiss' Kappa (0.26) and attributes it to the implicit nature of the tasks, annotator diversity, and inherent subjectivity (Section 3.6). However, it does not propose concrete methods to enhance consistency, representing a methodological limitation.
    *   *Why unresolved:* Low agreement threatens label reliability for nuanced categories, which directly impacts model training and evaluation validity.
    *   *What evidence would resolve it:* Conduct iterative annotation cycles with adjudication, refine guidelines with more culture-specific examples, or implement LLM-assisted pre-annotation to standardize interpretations, then measure resulting Kappa improvements.

*   **Open Question 4:** What hybrid architectures combining supervised fine-tuning with LLM-guided label refinement can best mitigate the performance gap on sarcasm and minority classes?
    *   *Basis in paper:* [explicit] The authors explicitly suggest hybrid frameworks that merge supervised fine-tuning with LLM-guided label refinement as a key future direction (Section 7).
    *   *Why unresolved:* Current approaches show stark contrasts: fine-tuned transformers fail on minority classes while zero-shot LLMs have poor exact-match, indicating neither is sufficient alone.
    *   *What evidence would resolve it:* Implement and evaluate hybrid models where LLMs provide confidence scores or soft label augmentations during training, measuring gains in F1 for sarcasm/offense/vulgarity and overall exact-match accuracy versus standalone models.

## Limitations
*   Annotation agreement (Fleiss' κ = 0.26) is only fair, reflecting the high subjectivity of pragmatic tasks and potentially limiting label reliability for borderline cases.
*   The severe performance drop for offensive and vulgarity detection is attributed to class imbalance, but it remains unclear if minority samples are inherently more subtle or if model capacity/feature representation is a contributing bottleneck.
*   Zero-shot LLM evaluation's low exact-match accuracy may be partly due to JSON parsing errors or API inconsistencies, not solely the models' holistic reasoning difficulty.

## Confidence
*   **Manual annotation with majority voting yields usable labels:** High (Evidence: Standard annotation protocol, κ reported, dataset released).
*   **Class imbalance is primary cause of minority class failure in fine-tuned models:** Medium (Evidence: Performance correlates with frequency, but feature subtlety not fully ruled out).
*   **Zero-shot LLMs struggle with holistic multi-label accuracy:** High (Evidence: Consistent large gap between micro-F1 and exact-match across models).
*   **Sarcasm confounds standard sentiment analysis:** High (Evidence: Direct analysis of external BnSentMix dataset provided).
