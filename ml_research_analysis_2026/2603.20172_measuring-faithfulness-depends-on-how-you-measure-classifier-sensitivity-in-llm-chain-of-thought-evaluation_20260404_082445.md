# Measuring Faithfulness Depends on How You Measure: Classifier Sensitivity in LLM Chain-of-Thought Evaluation
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.20172
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20172
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Faithfulness rates for identical CoT traces vary by 12.9 percentage points (69.7%–82.6%) depending on the classifier used, with per‑model differences up to 30.6 pp and ranking reversals observed.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates whether chain-of‑thought (CoT) faithfulness is an intrinsic model property or a measurement artifact. By applying three distinct classifiers—regex‑only, a regex‑plus‑LLM pipeline, and Claude Sonnet 4—to 10,276 identical influenced reasoning traces from 12 open‑weight LLMs, the authors find substantial divergence in faithfulness measurements. The spread in overall rates (12.9 pp) and per‑model gaps (up to 30.6 pp) demonstrate that the choice of operational definition—lexical mention versus epistemic dependence—drives results. For sycophancy hints, the gap reaches 43.4 pp with near‑zero agreement (Cohen’s κ ≈ 0.06). Model rankings also reverse (e.g., Qwen3.5‑27B from rank 1 to 7), showing that conclusions about which models are “more faithful” depend critically on the measurement instrument.

## Method Summary
The analysis uses a dataset of 10,276 influenced CoT traces where 12 models (7B–1T parameters) changed their answer to match an injected hint across five hint types (sycophancy, consistency, metadata, unethical, grader). Three classifiers are applied in parallel: (1) a regex‑only detector looking for explicit lexical mentions of the hint; (2) a pipeline that first uses regex, then a majority vote among three Ollama LLM judges (GLM‑5, Kimi K2, Gemini 3 Flash) for non‑matches; (3) Claude Sonnet 4 prompted to judge epistemic dependence (whether the hint was a load‑bearing reason). Metrics include per‑classifier faithfulness rates, confusion matrices, Cohen’s κ for inter‑rater reliability, McNemar’s test for paired disagreement, and Spearman ρ for ranking stability.

## Key Results
- Faithfulness rates vary significantly by classifier: overall range 69.7%–82.6% (12.9 pp gap); per‑model differences up to 30.6 pp.
- Model rankings are unstable: Spearman ρ = 0.67 between pipeline and Sonnet rankings, with Qwen3.5‑27B dropping from rank 1 to 7 and OLMo‑3.1‑32B rising from 9 to 3.
- Disagreement is systematic and hint‑type dependent: Cohen’s κ ranges from 0.06 (slight) for sycophancy to 0.42 (moderate) for grader hints; McNemar tests are significant (p<0.001) for all types.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1 – Construct divergence:** Classifiers implement different operational definitions of faithfulness. Regex captures surface‑level lexical mention of the hint, while Claude Sonnet 4 judges epistemic dependence (whether the hint was causally load‑bearing). The same CoT can satisfy one criterion and fail the other because “faithfulness” is not a single binary property but a spectrum from incidental acknowledgment to causal influence. Evidence: regex catches phrases like “the professor suggested” while Sonnet rejects them if followed by independent reasoning; weak direct support in neighboring literature but no quantification of divergence on identical data.
**Break condition:** If all classifiers converged on a single decision boundary for all hint types, this mechanism would be falsified.

**Mechanism 2 – Ambiguity concentration:** Disagreement is asymmetric and concentrates on hint types where the “mention” vs. “dependence” distinction is most ambiguous. Social‑pressure hints (sycophancy, consistency) often include a mention but then present seemingly independent reasoning, causing regex to flag them and Sonnet to reject them. This yields extreme asymmetric confusion (e.g., 883 vs 2 cases for sycophancy). For rule‑based hints (grader, unethical), the hint is either fully integrated or ignored, leaving little ambiguity. Evidence: Table 3 shows asymmetry; κ lowest for sycophancy (0.06).
**Break condition:** Uniformly high κ (>0.8) across hint types would falsify this.

**Mechanism 3 – Ranking reversal interaction:** Models have idiosyncratic “reasoning signatures” that align differently with the “mention” vs. “dependence” constructs. A model that frequently mentions hints (hitting regex) but often follows with independent analysis (missing Sonnet) will rank higher under the pipeline than under Sonnet. Evidence: Qwen3.5‑27B rank 1 (pipeline) → 7 (Sonnet); OLMo‑3.1‑32B rank 9 → 3; ρ=0.67 with wide CI [0.16, 0.90].
**Break condition:** Preservation of all ordinal relationships (ρ=1) across classifiers would falsify this.

## Foundational Learning
- **Cohen’s κ**  
  *Why needed:* Quantifies agreement between classifiers while correcting for chance, proving disagreements are substantial beyond base‑rate effects.  
  *Quick check:* Why is raw percentage agreement insufficient for imbalanced datasets where most cases are faithful?

- **Construct validity**  
  *Why needed:* The core argument is that “faithfulness” is a family of related but distinct constructs; different operationalizations (mention vs. dependence) violate the assumption that a single number captures an objective property.  
  *Quick check:* If two measurement tools disagree systematically, does that mean one is wrong, or that they measure different (but related) constructs?

- **McNemar’s test**  
  *Why needed:* Appropriately tests whether two classifiers disagree more than chance on the *same* cases; used to confirm all hint‑type disagreements are significant (p<0.001).  
  *Quick check:* Why is a paired test like McNemar necessary instead of comparing two independent proportions?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Dataset -> Classifier A (Regex‑only) -> Classifier B (Regex + Ollama pipeline) -> Classifier C (Claude Sonnet 4) -> Metrics  
**Critical path:** Filter full dataset to influenced cases -> apply all three classifiers in parallel -> aggregate by model and hint type -> compute disagreement metrics (κ, McNemar) -> rank models -> qualitative error analysis.  
**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Regex:* Ultra‑low cost, instant, but shallow (captures only explicit lexical mention); high precision, low recall for dependence.  
- *Pipeline:* Zero marginal GPU cost, higher recall than regex, but introduces local LLM biases and inconsistency across judge models.  
- *Sonnet:* Highest cost per case (~$0.005), applies a stricter “dependence” standard consistently, but susceptible to LLM‑as‑judge biases.  
*Tradeoff:* Cost vs. construct depth; regex and pipeline measure a different construct than Sonnet.  
**Failure signatures:**  
- High per‑model gap (>20 pp) between pipeline and Sonnet indicates model‑specific sensitivity.  
- κ < 0.2 for a hint type signals unreliable measurement for that construct.  
- Ranking reversal (>5 position change) means conclusions about model superiority are artifacts.  
- Asymmetric confusion (e.g., 883 vs 2) shows one classifier’s construct is a strict subset of the other’s.  
**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Replicate aggregate faithfulness rates on a random 1k‑subset to confirm the 69.7%–82.6% spread.  
2. For sycophancy hints, compute the confusion matrix and κ; expect κ≈0.06 and a ~43 pp asymmetric gap.  
3. Compute Spearman ρ between pipeline and Sonnet rankings; expect ρ≈0.67 with wide CI; identify extreme models (Qwen3.5‑27B, OLMo‑3.1‑32B).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Operational definition:** What definition of “faithfulness” (lexical mention vs. epistemic dependence) should become standard, given current measurements are classifier‑dependent and not directly comparable?
- **LLM‑judge bias:** To what extent do current LLM‑based judges (e.g., Claude Sonnet) exhibit biases that distort faithfulness measurements compared to human judgment or non‑LLM methods?
- **Model mechanisms:** What internal factors (architecture, training, scale) predict construct‑divergent behavior where a model’s CoT is “faithful” by mention but “unfaithful” by dependence?
- **Classifier‑agnostic signals:** Can alternative signals (thinking tokens, internal representations, causal interventions) yield more stable and valid faithfulness measurements than any single text‑based metric?

## Limitations
- Dataset limited to 12 open‑weight models on MMLU/GPQA; may not generalize to other models or domains.
- No human‑annotated gold standard for “faithfulness”; cannot validate which classifier captures the true construct.
- Input preprocessing mismatch: pipeline truncates CoTs to 4,000 chars for LLM judges, Sonnet likely sees full text → potential confound.
- Pipeline uses three unspecified Ollama models; exact versions/prompts missing, risking replication and bias.
- Binary classification ignores possible gradations of dependence.

## Confidence
- **Measurement sensitivity (high spread, rank flips):** High – empirical divergence is directly observable and robust.
- **Construct divergence (lexical mention vs epistemic dependence):** Medium – plausible but not exclusive; alternative factors (truncation, judge design) could contribute.
- **Systematic hint‑type disagreement (κ, asymmetry):** High – patterns (e.g., sycophancy κ≈0.06, 883 vs 2 asymmetry) are clear and unlikely random.

## Next Checks
1. **Human annotation benchmark:** Recruit experts to label a stratified sample for epistemic dependence; compute each classifier’s agreement with human consensus to identify the most valid operationalization.
2. **Truncation control:** Rerun Claude Sonnet 4 on identically truncated CoTs (4,000 chars) to isolate input length effects from definition differences.
3. **Judge variation test:** Rebuild the pipeline with alternative LLM judges (e.g., GPT‑4o mini, Claude Haiku, Llama‑3.1‑70B) and re‑evaluate divergence and ranking stability to check robustness to judge selection.
