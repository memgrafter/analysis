# Streaming Translation and Transcription Through Speech-to-Text Causal Alignment

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.11578
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11578
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Hikari achieves new state-of-the-art BLEU scores for simultaneous speech-to-text translation on English-to-Japanese, German, and Russian, e.g., 42.17 BLEU at 2.96s StreamLAAL on IWSLT25Instruct.

## Executive Summary
Simultaneous machine translation traditionally relies on offline models with engineered read/write policies, which struggle with syntactically divergent language pairs. This work introduces Hikari, a policy-free, fully end-to-end model that performs speech-to-text translation and streaming transcription by encoding read/write decisions into a probabilistic WAIT token mechanism. Key innovations include Decoder Time Dilation, which balances the training distribution by reducing WAIT token dominance, and a supervised fine-tuning strategy that trains the model to recover from delays. Evaluated on English-to-Japanese, German, and Russian, Hikari achieves new state-of-the-art BLEU scores in both low- and high-latency regimes, significantly outperforming IWSLT 2025 baselines.

## Method Summary
Hikari is an encoder-decoder Transformer based on Whisper-medium, adapted for simultaneous streaming. The audio encoder uses causal self-attention to process audio frames in a lower-triangular mask. The decoder generates tokens autoregressively, with cross-attention to the encoder using a dilated causal mask: at decoder step *m*, it can only attend to encoder outputs up to index *D*(*m*−1), where *D* is the dilation factor (set to 4). A special WAIT token (ID 93) is added to the vocabulary; emitting WAIT means reading more audio, while emitting any text token means writing a translation. Training data is generated by a causal alignment pipeline: source audio is processed by Faster-Whisper to obtain word timestamps, translated by an LLM with 4-sentence context, and aligned via SimAlign. The target sequence interleaves tokenized translations with the minimum number of WAIT tokens required to ensure each output token’s logical time meets or exceeds the aligned source word’s timestamp plus a stochastic delay (δ ~ U(0,200ms)). Pre-training mixes translation (en-ja, en-de, en-ru) and transcription (ASR) tasks for 35K steps. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) then artificially inserts extra WAIT tokens to simulate delays, masking the loss for those tokens and preceding history so the model learns to “catch up.” This two-stage training yields a model that implicitly learns a read/write policy without an external controller.

## Key Results
- Hikari achieves SOTA BLEU on English-to-Japanese, German, and Russian in both low- and high-latency regimes.
- On IWSLT25Instruct (en-ja), it reaches 42.17 BLEU at 2.96s StreamLAAL (low latency) and 41.35 BLEU at 4.85s StreamLAAL (high latency), beating all IWSLT 2025 baselines.
- A supervised fine-tuning variant improves the quality-latency trade-off, reducing latency by approximately 1 second across tasks with minimal BLEU degradation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Implicit Policy via WAIT Token**  
Hikari unifies translation quality and emission timing by encoding the READ/WRITE policy as a probabilistic WAIT token in the output distribution. The decoder interleaves text tokens with WAIT; emitting WAIT corresponds to a READ action (buffering more audio), while any text token is a WRITE action (committing translation). This removes the need for a separate policy controller. The model learns to associate acoustic uncertainty or insufficient source context with higher WAIT probability, and when semantic boundaries are resolved, it emits text rapidly. Evidence: abstract and Section 3.1 describe the unification. A break condition would be if WAIT emission does not correlate with source ambiguity, causing either excessive latency or premature writes.

**Mechanism 2: Decoder Time Dilation for Balanced Training**  
Decoder Time Dilation (D) solves the extreme class imbalance caused by a dominant WAIT token distribution during training. Instead of generating one token per audio embedding (D=1), the model emits one token per *D* audio embeddings (D=4). This reduces the proportion of WAIT tokens, preventing a degenerate “always WAIT” policy, while maintaining sufficient temporal granularity. The core assumption is that *D* balances alignment precision, training stability, and decoder context capacity. Evidence: Section 3.1 and Figure 3 show the trade-off. If *D* is too high, alignment precision degrades; if too low, training fails due to WAIT domination.

**Mechanism 3: Supervised Fine-Tuning for Latency Recovery**  
Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on artificially delayed samples trains the model to recover from latency drift, improving the quality-latency Pareto frontier. During SFT, a pivot index in the target sequence is selected, and subsequent content tokens are forcibly delayed by inserting extra WAIT tokens. The loss is masked for these artificial WAIT tokens and all preceding history, so the model is only penalized on correctly generating the delayed content tokens, teaching it to “catch up.” Evidence: abstract, Section 3.3.2, and Figure 6 show the leftward shift in the quality-latency curve. If the forced delay exceeds the decoder’s context capacity, catch-up generation becomes incoherent.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept: Causal Alignment**  
- Why needed: It creates the training signal linking source audio timestamps to target token release times. Without it, there is no ground-truth for the READ/WRITE policy.  
- Quick check: If a target word has no direct lexical alignment to a source word, how does the causal alignment algorithm decide its release time to maintain causality?

**Concept: Decoder Time Dilation vs. Audio Frame Rate**  
- Why needed: The model must reconcile a high-rate audio encoder (e.g., 50 Hz) with a lower-rate text generation rate. Dilation sets this rate ratio explicitly, defining the temporal resolution of the policy.  
- Quick check: What happens to alignment precision and maximum achievable speech rate if *D* increases from 4 to 10?

**Concept: Implicit vs. Explicit Policy Learning**  
- Why needed: Hikari’s central innovation is moving from a modular explicit policy (separate controller) to an implicit one (token distribution). Understanding this shift is key to the architecture.  
- Quick check: In an explicit policy model, where does the decision signal originate? Where does it originate in Hikari’s policy-free model?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Audio → Faster-Whisper (timestamps) → Sentence Segmentation → MT via LLM → Causal Alignment → Interleaved (WAIT + text) sequence → Audio Encoder (causal self-attention) → Dilated Cross-Attention → Text Decoder (causal self-attention) → Output Tokens (text or WAIT)

**Critical Path**  
Audio frames (1500 embeddings for 30s) → Causal Encoder → Dilated Cross-Attention → Autoregressive Decoder → (WAIT or Text Token). The cross-attention mask is the crucial constraint linking decoder step to permissible encoder context.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *D* (Dilation Factor): Low *D* (precision) vs. High *D* (token density/RTF). Paper chooses *D*=4 empirically.
- Task Mixing: Including ASR as auxiliary improves translation robustness but competes for training capacity.
- Fixed vs. Adaptive Latency: Latency is a function of the internal text/WAIT distribution. Inference-time WAIT logit biasing can trade quality for latency, but SFT is the primary training-time control.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Excessive WAIT Sequences: Model generates long runs of WAIT tokens, StreamLAAL significantly exceeds theoretical minimum.
- Context Debt: Decoder’s accumulated lag consistently exceeds the theoretical minimum.
- Catastrophic Forgetting (from SFT): BLEU drops >5 points with only minor latency gain.
- Out-of-Domain Collapse: High WER on non-podcast data (Earnings21).

**First 3 Experiments**  
1. **WAIT Token Distribution Analysis**: With *D*=1,2,4,6, plot histogram of consecutive WAIT counts and total WAIT% on a held-out validation set. Expect *D*=4 to yield ~10-15% WAIT tokens without excessively long runs.
2. **Dilated Mask Sanity Check**: Generate the cross-attention mask matrix for decoder context 375 tokens and encoder frames 1500. Confirm it is strictly lower-triangular with step size *D*=4.
3. **SFT Impact Probe**: Take the pre-trained model and compare two strategies: (a) applying a fixed WAIT logit penalty (e.g., –1.0) at inference, and (b) using the SFT checkpoint. Measure the quality-latency curves; expect SFT to show a superior Pareto improvement.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Scaling to larger architectures**: How does training Hikari with Whisper-large or larger affect quality and latency? The paper only evaluated medium and small variants.
- **Dynamic decoder time dilation**: Can adjusting *D* based on real-time speech density improve the quality-latency trade-off? Hikari uses a fixed *D*=4.
- **Alternative inference-time strategies**: Do speculative decoding or special token conditioning match or surpass logit biasing for latency control? Only logit biasing was evaluated.
- **Speech-to-speech extension**: Can Hikari’s policy-free approach be extended to direct speech-to-speech translation? This remains unexplored.
- **Training data diversity**: How does including more noisy and domain-specific corpora improve generalization to out-of-distribution test sets like Earnings21? Training data is currently podcast-dominated.

## Limitations
- **Data pipeline fragility**: Causal alignment relies on Faster-Whisper timestamps and SimAlign word alignments; errors propagate to WAIT placement and corrupt training signal.
- **Hyperparameter opacity**: Missing training details (gradient accumulation, dropout, LR schedule) hinder faithful reproduction and may affect convergence.
- **Domain overfitting**: Training dominated by podcasts leads to high WER on non-podcast Earnings21 data, indicating limited robustness.

## Confidence
- WAIT token unifies policy & translation – **High confidence** (clearly defined architecture, ablations confirm necessity).
- Decoder Time Dilation (*D*=4) balances training – **Medium confidence** (empirically justified but optimal *D* may vary with data and model size).
- SFT improves quality-latency trade-off – **Medium confidence** (validated by experiments but depends on correct loss masking and delay patterns).
- SOTA BLEU on en-ja – **Low-to-Medium confidence** (results await broader validation; reproduction blocked by pipeline unknowns).
