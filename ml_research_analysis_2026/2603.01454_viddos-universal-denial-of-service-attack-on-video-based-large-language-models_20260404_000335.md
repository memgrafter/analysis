# VidDoS: Universal Denial-of-Service Attack on Video-based Large Language Models
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.01454
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.01454
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** VidDoS achieves up to 205× token expansion and 15× latency increase on Video-LLMs.

## Executive Summary
VidDoS introduces the first effective Energy-Latency Attack (ELA) specifically designed for Video-based Large Language Models (Video-LLMs). Existing image-centric attacks fail against video due to temporal aggregation and real-time constraints. VidDoS overcomes these by using a spatially concentrated universal patch optimized offline, combined with masked teacher forcing and refusal penalties to force the model into unbounded token generation. Evaluated on three Video-LLMs across three datasets, the attack induces severe degradation, with up to 205× more generated tokens and 15× higher latency. It also exhibits strong cross-dataset transferability and leads to critical safety violations in autonomous driving simulations. These results highlight a significant vulnerability in deploying Video-LLMs for safety-critical applications.

## Method Summary
VidDoS employs a universal adversarial patch that is optimized once on a surrogate video dataset and then deployed on any incoming video stream without further computation. The patch is a small, learnable region (e.g., 96×96 pixels) that replaces pixel values at a fixed location (e.g., bottom-right corner) in every frame. Offline optimization uses a joint loss function: Masked Teacher Forcing (LTF) steers the model’s decoder toward a pre-defined long “sponge” target sequence; a Refusal Penalty (Lban) suppresses the probability of short answer tokens (e.g., “Yes”, “No”, EOS) at the first generation step; and an Early-Termination Suppression (Lstop) discourages EOS emission over an initial horizon K. The patch is updated using sign-PGD under ℓ∞ constraints. At inference, the fixed patch is simply overlaid on the video, causing the Video-LLM to generate excessively long outputs and exhaust computational resources.

## Key Results
- **Token expansion:** Up to 205× increase in generated tokens compared to clean inputs.
- **Latency inflation:** Up to 15× increase in inference latency.
- **Real-world impact:** Cross-dataset transferability demonstrated; induced latency causes critical safety violations in autonomous driving simulations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Spatially concentrated patch survives temporal aggregation
- **Claim:** A spatially concentrated universal patch anchors cross‑modal attention, surviving temporal aggregation that would otherwise dilute diffuse frame‑wise noise.
- **Mechanism:** The patch is injected at a fixed peripheral location (e.g., bottom‑right corner) across all frames, creating a high‑magnitude semantic anomaly in the visual token space. Video encoders typically employ spatio‑temporal pooling or subsampling; because the patch replaces pixels rather than adding small noise, its feature representation remains strong after compression, hijacking the decoder’s attention.
- **Core assumption:** Temporal aggregation acts as a low‑pass filter that attenuates incoherent, frame‑specific perturbations but preserves a consistent, localized pattern.
- **Evidence:** [abstract] “spatially concentrated patch optimized offline… bypasses the structural ‘low‑pass filter’ effect of video encoders by anchoring cross‑modal attention.” [section 3.2] “a localized replacement patch creates a concentrated, high‑magnitude semantic anomaly… rendering it significantly more resilient to the model’s internal feature compression.”
- **Break condition:** If the model uses extremely aggressive spatial downsampling (e.g., reducing frames to a single token very early) or if the patch is placed on an object that is heavily occluded or out‑of‑view.

### Mechanism 2: Masked teacher forcing steers toward expensive target sequences
- **Claim:** Masked teacher forcing explicitly steers the decoder toward a computationally expensive “sponge” target sequence, overriding the model’s native distribution.
- **Mechanism:** During offline optimization, the loss is computed only on the target assistant tokens (the sponge sequence) with higher weights for the first K tokens. This weighted cross‑entropy forces the model’s early predictions to align with the expensive trajectory, stabilizing entry into a long‑generation regime.
- **Core assumption:** The autoregressive decoder can be coerced into following a predefined token sequence if the visual context is sufficiently perturbed to bias its conditional probability.
- **Evidence:** [abstract] “masked teacher forcing to steer models toward expensive target sequences.” [section 3.2] Equation (1) and description: “we compute a weighted token‑level cross‑entropy loss exclusively on the target assistant tokens… wi > 1 for the first K tokens.”
- **Break condition:** If the sponge sequence is not sufficiently “expensive” (e.g., short or with low activation norms) or if the model has strong fine‑tuning that resists being forced into repetitive patterns.

### Mechanism 3: Refusal penalty and early‑termination suppression dismantle the conciseness prior
- **Claim:** Refusal penalty and early‑termination suppression dismantle the model’s conciseness prior, preventing early halts or short task‑compliant answers.
- **Mechanism:** Two auxiliary penalties operate within a prefix horizon K: (1) Lban penalizes the probability mass of banned tokens (e.g., “Yes”, “No”, EOS) at the first generation step; (2) Lstop applies a negative log loss to 1 minus EOS probability over K steps, aggressively suppressing EOS emission.
- **Core assumption:** Video‑LLMs fine‑tuned for discriminative tasks (e.g., binary QA) have a strong bias toward short, deterministic answers; these penalties explicitly counteract that bias.
- **Evidence:** [abstract] “refusal penalty and early‑termination suppression to override the model’s conciseness prior.” [section 3.2] Equations (2) and (3) and surrounding text.
- **Break condition:** If the victim model is not biased toward conciseness (e.g., generative chat models), these penalties may be redundant or could even destabilize the attack.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Adversarial patch (δ) → Injected video frames → Video encoder (temporal aggregation) → Visual tokens → LLM decoder (with prompt) → Generated tokens (long). The attack loss (LTF, Lban, Lstop) guides δ optimization via sign‑PGD on a surrogate dataset.

### Critical Path
1. Patch injection (fixed location) into each frame.
2. Visual encoding with temporal aggregation.
3. Visual tokens combined with textual prompt.
4. Decoder distribution biased by adversarial visual tokens.
5. Long generation regime triggered.
6. Inflated inference latency and resource exhaustion.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Patch size vs. stealth:** Larger patches increase effectiveness but are more visible; ablations show even 48×48 patches work.
- **Patch location:** Fixed corner placement avoids occluding salient objects (stealth), but random placement retains strong transferability.
- **Training domain:** Patch transferability drops significantly when source and target domains differ semantically (e.g., driving vs. general QA).

### Failure Signatures
- Model produces normal‑length outputs despite patch (e.g., if temporal pooling is too aggressive).
- Attack fails on non‑Video‑LLMs (e.g., image‑only VLMs) because the temporal dimension is missing.
- Full‑frame additive ℓ∞ noise fails, indicating the need for discrete replacement patches.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Baseline comparison:** Evaluate VidDoS against Random Noise, Verbose Images, and NICGSlowDown on three Video‑LLMs across three datasets; measure token expansion and latency overhead.
2. **Cross‑dataset transfer:** Train the patch on BDDX (driving) and test on D2‑City and VideoSimpleQA to assess generalization across domains.
3. **Ablation study:** Systematically vary patch size, number of temporal frames, perturbation mode (additive vs. replacement), and loss components to quantify each contribution.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Why does reducing temporal sampling density amplify attack effectiveness?**  
   The paper observed that fewer frames (8 vs. 16) lead to more generated tokens, but the mechanism is unclear. This is critical for edge devices with low frame rates. Resolution would require analyzing internal attention maps under varying frame rates.

2. **Which architectural components determine a Video‑LLM’s susceptibility?**  
   Different models show varying vulnerability, but the paper does not isolate the responsible factors (e.g., temporal pooling, projector). Identifying these would guide robust design. Evidence could come from systematic component swapping between vulnerable and robust models.

3. **Can the universal trigger generalize across drastically different video domains?**  
   Transfer from driving to general QA datasets fails, limiting universality. Overcoming this would show a more widespread vulnerability. A multi‑source domain training strategy could improve cross‑domain performance.

4. **Does VidDoS maintain effectiveness in a black‑box setting?**  
   The threat model assumes white‑box access. Real‑world attacks are often black‑box, so transferability to unseen model architectures is unknown. Testing on different model families (e.g., from open‑source to proprietary) would clarify.

## Limitations
- **Unspecified training details:** The exact source dataset (*D_src*) and its preprocessing (frame rate, resolution, clip sampling) are not disclosed, hindering faithful reproduction.
- **Hyperparameter gaps:** Critical values (λ_ban, λ_stop, intervention horizon K, sponge sequence) are absent from the main text, requiring guesswork or source code access.
- **Limited external validity:** Evaluation is restricted to three Video‑LLMs and three datasets; broader model families and real‑world streaming scenarios remain untested.
