# Which Feedback Works for Whom? Differential Effects of LLM-Generated Feedback Elements Across Learner Profiles

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.11650
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11650
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** Learning gains were highest from Baseline (clear correction + guidance) and Keywords feedback, while subjective preferences varied by Big Five personality cluster, with Keywords, Baseline, and Actionability consistently favored.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates how six specific elements of LLM-generated feedback (Baseline, Keywords, Actionability, Novelty, Coverage, Positivity) influence learning outcomes and subjective evaluations among 321 Japanese high school students. Learning effectiveness was measured via accuracy on the first revised answer and an efficiency metric (Effectiveness). Results indicate that feedback providing clear correction and focused guidance (Baseline) or highlighted key terms (Keywords) most effectively improves answer accuracy. In contrast, learners subjectively prefer feedback that is practical and easy to apply (Keywords, Baseline, Actionability). Critically, feedback preferences cluster significantly based on personalities derived from the Big Five traits: learners high in Neuroticism and low in Extraversion strongly prefer Keywords; those high in Agreeableness/Extraversion value trustworthiness; and learners high in Openness/Conscientiousness/Agreeableness accept a wider range, including Actionability and Coverage. The findings suggest that effective feedback design must account for learner personality to optimize both learning gains and acceptance.

## Method Summary
In a controlled experiment, 321 first-year high school students in Japan completed an online learning task using a curated set of 7 basic biology multiple-choice questions. After an initial answer, each participant received feedback generated by GPT-5 that manipulated one of six predefined elements (e.g., keyword highlighting, comprehensive coverage). They could revise their answer up to a fixed maximum number of attempts. After each feedback instance, they rated six subjective criteria (trustworthiness, ease of understanding, etc.) on a three-point scale. Participants first completed a pre-questionnaire measuring Big Five personality traits. The primary outcomes were (1) first-revision accuracy for initially incorrect answers and (2) an Effectiveness score (1 - attempts/N). Learners were clustered using k-means on their Big Five scores to identify distinct personality profiles. Differences in learning outcomes and subjective ratings across feedback elements and between personality clusters were analyzed using non-parametric tests (Kruskal-Wallis, Dunn post-hoc).

## Key Results
- **Learning Gains:** Baseline and Keywords feedback produced the highest first-revision accuracy; Coverage ranked second for learning gains but was less efficient.
- **Subjective Preferences:** Keywords was consistently highest-rated across multiple criteria; Baseline and Actionability also ranked highly; Positivity was consistently lowest.
- **Personality Clusters:** Three distinct clusters emerged showing significant differences in feedback preferences—Cluster 1 (high Neuroticism/low Extraversion) strongly favored Keywords; Cluster 2 (high Openness/Conscientiousness/Agreeableness) valued Actionability and Coverage; Cluster 3 (high Agreeableness/Extraversion) prioritized trustworthiness.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Clarity and Conciseness Drive Learning Gains
- **Claim:** Feedback that clearly indicates the mistake and provides focused, correct guidance (Baseline) most effectively moves learners toward correct answers.
- **Mechanism:** Explicit error highlighting reduces cognitive load in identifying the gap, while concise, correct reasoning directly bridges the knowledge gap without overwhelming extraneous information.
- **Core assumption:** Learners can act on feedback if they can easily parse what is wrong and what the correct reasoning is.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract notes Baseline yields highest learning gains by showing both what to correct and how. Section 5 compares Baseline to Coverage and Keywords, confirming its primacy. Neighboring literature supports directive feedback for gains.
- **Break condition:** If the provided "correct reasoning" does not address the learner's specific misconception, the guidance becomes irrelevant.

### Mechanism 2: Practicality and Ease Drive Subjective Preference
- **Claim:** Learners subjectively prefer feedback that is practical, focused, and easy to apply (Keywords, Baseline, Actionability) over expansive (Coverage) or affectively positive (Positivity) feedback.
- **Mechanism:** Practical, keyword-highlighted, or step-oriented feedback lowers perceived effort required to use it, increasing subjective utility and trust. Overly comprehensive or praise-heavy feedback is seen as less efficient or credible.
- **Core assumption:** Learners optimize for cognitive ease and perceived utility when evaluating feedback.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 5.1 show Keywords, Baseline, and Actionability are consistently favored; Positivity is lowest. Related work links subjective preference to feedback usage.
- **Break condition:** For learners high in Neuroticism, affective support (Positivity) might be subjectively valuable despite overall low ratings.

### Mechanism 3: Personality Shapes Feedback Processing
- **Claim:** Feedback effectiveness and acceptance are not uniform but systematically vary with learner personality profiles derived from the Big Five traits.
- **Mechanism:** Personality traits influence cognitive and emotional processing styles. For example, high Neuroticism/low Extraversion learners prefer low-cognitive-load, clear cues (Keywords), while high Openness/Conscientiousness learners accept and value comprehensive, actionable guidance.
- **Core assumption:** Stable personality traits correlate with consistent information processing and feedback-seeking preferences.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 5.2 detail cluster profiles showing divergent preferences. Section 6.2 links clusters to prior findings on Neuroticism (anxiety) and Openness/Conscientiousness (intellectual curiosity).
- **Break condition:** If personality measures are unreliable in this context or the biology task does not activate personality-based processing differences, the pattern may not hold.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept: Big Five Personality Traits (OCEAN)**
- **Why needed:** The study's core finding is that feedback preferences cluster based on Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, and Neuroticism. Understanding these dimensions is essential to interpret *for whom* feedback works.
- **Quick check:** Which Big Five trait is most associated with anxiety and a preference for clear, low-cognitive-load feedback, according to the study's Cluster 1?

**Concept: Feedback Elements (Baseline, Keywords, etc.)**
- **Why needed:** The study manipulates specific, operationalized elements (e.g., keyword highlighting, coverage breadth). Knowing their definitions is necessary to understand *what* about feedback is being evaluated.
- **Quick check:** Which feedback element is defined by providing a checklist of steps like "(1) the detecting organ, (2) the involved nervous system..."?

**Concept: Learning Outcome Metrics (Accuracy, Effectiveness)**
- **Why needed:** The study uses two complementary measures: correctness of the first revised answer (Accuracy) and efficiency (Effectiveness = 1 - attempts/N). These define *how* learning success is measured.
- **Quick check:** If a learner reaches the correct answer on the 2nd attempt out of a maximum of 5, what is their Effectiveness score?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**
Pre-questionnaire system (Big Five inventory, background) -> Task interface (displays question, collects answer, presents pre-generated feedback) -> Feedback generation pipeline (GPT-5 prompt engineering based on selected element) -> Evaluation collection system (3-point scale for 6 criteria per feedback instance) -> Learning log database (stores responses, feedback shown, evaluations, personality scores) -> Analysis module (clustering by personality, element-effect statistical tests)

**Critical path:**
1. Learner completes pre-quiz → personality vector computed.
2. Learner selects question → system retrieves pre-generated feedback strings for all 6 elements for that specific (question, wrong answer) pair.
3. System randomly selects one feedback element variant to show → learner revises answer → cycle repeats until correct or max attempts.
4. After each feedback, learner submits 6 criterion ratings.
5. Post-study: Cluster learners via k-means on personality vectors → compare average element ratings per cluster → test significance.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Pre-generation vs. On-demand:* Pre-generation (used) ensures consistency but limits adaptivity to real-time response. Tradeoff: scalability & experimental control vs. dynamic personalization.
- *Element Isolation:* Using a single APPLY element per feedback controls for confounding but doesn't test synergies (e.g., Keywords + Actionability). Tradeoff: internal validity of element effects vs. ecological validity of combined feedback.
- *Fixed Max Attempts (N):* Setting N=5 in Effectiveness metric introduces an arbitrary ceiling. Tradeoff: bounded experiment vs. potentially misrepresenting efficiency for learners needing >5 attempts.

**Failure signatures:**
- Low first-revision accuracy (<40%) across all elements suggests the biology questions are too difficult or feedback is universally ineffective.
- Uniformly high ratings (ceiling effect) on all 6 criteria across all elements would invalidate the subjective evaluation metric's discriminative power.
- No significant Kruskal-Wallis results between clusters for any criterion would contradict RQ3's premise of personality-based differences.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Element Efficacy Replication:** Run the same within-subject design with a new cohort, using only the top 3 performing elements (Baseline, Coverage, Keywords) on learning outcomes to confirm their ranking.
2. **Element-by-Cluster Interaction:** Conduct a mixed ANOVA with Feedback Element (6 levels) and Big Five Cluster (3 levels) as factors, testing for significant interaction effects on subjective ratings beyond main effects.
3. **Cross-Model Generalization:** Regenerate all feedback using a different LLM (e.g., Claude 3.5 Sonnet) and run a between-subject study to test if the element effect hierarchy (RQ1, RQ2) and cluster patterns (RQ3) hold across model architectures.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question:** Do the effects of feedback elements on learning outcomes and subjective evaluations generalize to academic domains beyond high school biology?
  - **Basis:** The study focuses exclusively on biology tasks with a limited number of questions.
  - **Why unresolved:** The experimental design was confined to a single subject and specific question types, so cross-domain validity has not been tested.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct identical experiments across multiple subjects (e.g., mathematics, language arts) and question formats using the same six feedback elements.

- **Question:** To what extent are the observed feedback element effects dependent on the specific LLM (GPT-5) used for generation?
  - **Basis:** The study uses only GPT-5 to generate feedback.
  - **Why unresolved:** Different LLMs may produce variations in feedback style, factual accuracy, or pedagogical alignment that could alter learner responses.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Generate feedback for the same set of questions using multiple LLMs (e.g., GPT-4, Claude, open-source models) and compare learning outcomes and subjective evaluations.

- **Question:** How do additional learner characteristics (e.g., prior knowledge, academic achievement, learning motivations) interact with personality traits to influence feedback preferences and effectiveness?
  - **Basis:** The analysis focuses only on personality traits, not other learner characteristics.
  - **Why unresolved:** Other individual differences could moderate or mediate how feedback is perceived and utilized, potentially explaining additional variance.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Collect data on prior knowledge (pre-tests), academic achievement, and learning motivations, then include these as covariates or interaction terms in the analysis.

## Limitations
- The exact value of *N* (maximum attempts) for the Effectiveness metric is unspecified, introducing uncertainty in the efficiency measurement.
- The precise wording and directional anchors of the three-point subjective evaluation scale are not detailed, potentially affecting rating interpretation.
- Generalizability is limited: the study uses a single subject (biology), a Japanese high-school sample, and GPT-5–specific prompts, so findings may not apply to other domains, cultures, or LLMs.

## Next Checks
1. **Robustness of Effectiveness metric:** Re-run primary analyses with *N* = 3 and *N* = 5 to confirm element ranking stability.
2. **Cross-model generalization:** Regenerate all feedback using Claude 3.5 Sonnet and replicate the within-subject design to test if element hierarchies and cluster patterns persist.
3. **Cluster validation:** Apply consensus clustering (e.g., Clue) or hierarchical methods to verify that *k* = 3 is optimal and that cluster profiles (high Neuroticism/low Extraversion, etc.) are reproducible.
