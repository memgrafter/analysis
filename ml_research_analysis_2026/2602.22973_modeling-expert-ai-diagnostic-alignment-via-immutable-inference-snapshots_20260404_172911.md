# Modeling Expert AI Diagnostic Alignment via Immutable Inference Snapshots

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.22973  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22973  
- Reference count: 32  
- Primary result: Achieving 71.4% exact primary match and 100% comprehensive concordance on 21 dermatological cases via immutable inference snapshots and a four-level concordance analysis.

## Executive Summary
Human-in-the-loop clinical AI validation often overwrites the AI’s initial output, losing the opportunity to analyze the transformation from AI inference to expert correction. This study addresses that gap by preserving the AI-generated diagnostic report as an immutable snapshot (R0) and systematically comparing it with the physician-validated report (R1). The pipeline integrates a vision-enabled large language model, BERT-based medical entity extraction, and Sequential Language Model Inference (SLMI) to refine the AI output for domain consistency before freezing R0. A four-level concordance framework—Primary Match Rate (PMR), Adjusted Match Rate (AMR, τ=0.60), cross-category alignment, and Comprehensive Concordance Rate (CCR)—reveals that binary lexical metrics substantially underestimate clinically meaningful alignment. On 21 dermatological cases, PMR and AMR were both 71.4%, CCR reached 100% with zero complete divergence, cross-category reprioritization occurred in 23.8% of cases, and the average differential overlap was 1.76. These results underscore the value of preserving inference states and multi-level evaluation for robust human–AI alignment assessment.

## Method Summary
The system processes a dermatological image through a vision-enabled LLM (e.g., GPT-4V) to generate an initial text report containing a primary diagnosis and up to three differentials. A BERT-based medical NER extracts diagnosis entities from this output. The extracted entities then undergo Sequential Language Model Inference (SLMI), an iterative refinement that enforces domain-consistent constraints (valid medical terms, plausible relationships), yielding a structured report R0 that is frozen as an immutable snapshot. A physician subsequently reviews and may edit R0 to produce a validated report R1. The concordance engine compares R0 and R1 using four metrics: (1) PMR – exact string match of primary diagnoses; (2) AMR – semantic similarity of primary diagnoses with a threshold τ=0.60; (3) cross-category alignment – whether one’s primary matches the other’s differential; and (4) CCR – existence of any diagnosis pair (primary or differential) with similarity ≥ τ. Differential overlap (count of shared diagnoses) is also computed. Evaluation was performed on 21 dermatological image cases from a public dataset.

## Key Results
- Exact primary match: 71.4%; semantic adjusted (τ=0.60): 71.4%.  
- Comprehensive concordance: 100% (95% CI: [83.9%, 100%]); zero cases with complete diagnostic divergence.  
- Cross-category reprioritization in 23.8% (5/21) of cases; mean differential overlap = 1.76 shared diagnoses per case.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Preserving the AI-generated diagnostic report as an immutable inference snapshot (R0) enables structured signal analysis of expert corrections.
- **Mechanism:** Freezing R0 before physician intervention treats AI output and physician output as two observable states in a transformation process, allowing measurement of changes (additions, removals, reprioritizations) and constants, thus quantifying alignment as a transformation rather than a binary match.
- **Core assumption:** The initial AI inference provides a meaningful signal; overwriting it loses the opportunity to analyze correction dynamics.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states the snapshot is preserved and systematically compared. Section 1 (p.2) emphasizes traceability; Section 3 (p.4) defines R0 and R1 as paired reports. Corpus support is weak (peer review immutability literature).
- **Break condition:** If R0 is overwritten during validation, the transformation cannot be observed and the framework collapses.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** A hierarchical concordance framework captures clinically meaningful alignment at multiple semantic levels, revealing that binary lexical metrics substantially underestimate true alignment.
- **Mechanism:** The four-level framework (PMR → AMR → cross-category → CCR) progressively relaxes constraints from exact string match to set overlap. This monotonic progression shows many apparent disagreements are actually alignments when considering broader semantics (synonyms, shared differentials).
- **Core assumption:** Clinical diagnostic reasoning encompasses the differential set and priorities; agreement can exist even if primary diagnoses differ lexically.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract highlights binary lexical underestimation. Section 3 (pp.5-7) defines metrics and proves monotonicity (Eq.18). Discussion (p.11) notes exact lexical 71.4% vs comprehensive 100%. Corpus includes CRIMSON metric paper supporting multi-faceted evaluation.
- **Break condition:** If physician reports lack structured differentials, cross-category and set overlap metrics become invalid.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Sequential Language Model Inference (SLMI) enforces domain-consistent refinement of AI outputs, increasing initial alignment with expert reasoning.
- **Mechanism:** SLMI processes the initial LLM output through entity extraction and sequential refinement steps to ensure adherence to medical domain constraints (valid diagnosis terms, plausible relationships). This yields an R0 semantically close to expert reports, reducing the need for major corrections.
- **Core assumption:** Domain-consistent refinement can be automated without expert input and aligns AI outputs with the diagnostic space used by physicians.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions SLMI integration. Section 4.1 (p.4) describes the inference engine. The low complete disagreement rate (0%) indicates R0 lies within a bounded neighborhood of R1. Corpus (MIRNet, CRIMSON) provides indirect support.
- **Break condition:** If SLMI is disabled or flawed, R0 may deviate from domain constraints, leading to lower alignment (reduced CCR, increased complete divergence).

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Multi-level diagnostic concordance analysis**  
  - *Why needed:* The evaluation hinges on understanding how different concordance levels reveal alignment missed by binary metrics; without this, the significance of CCR=100% vs PMR=71.4% is lost.  
  - *Quick check:* What does CCR measure that PMR does not?

- **Concept: Immutable inference snapshots in human-in-the-loop AI**  
  - *Why needed:* The core innovation is preserving R0; without it, transformation analysis is impossible and overwriting AI outputs during validation is the norm.  
  - *Quick check:* Why is it important to keep the AI-generated report unchanged before physician review?

- **Concept: Sequential Language Model Inference (SLMI)**  
  - *Why needed:* SLMI is the refinement mechanism that yields a more expert-aligned R0, supporting the observed high concordance metrics.  
  - *Quick check:* What are the potential consequences if SLMI introduces errors or fails to enforce domain constraints?

- **Concept: Cross-category reprioritization**  
  - *Why needed:* This phenomenon shows alignment can exist across different roles (primary vs differential), which binary metrics miss.  
  - *Quick check:* How does cross-category reprioritization affect the interpretation of PMR vs AMR?

- **Concept: Differential overlap**  
  - *Why needed:* It quantifies shared diagnostic hypotheses beyond the primary, informing the comprehensiveness of alignment.  
  - *Quick check:* Why does an average differential overlap of 1.76 matter clinically?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Input image -> Vision-enabled LLM -> BERT-based medical entity extractor -> Sequential Language Model Inference (SLMI) -> Structured R0 (immutable) -> Physician review interface -> Physician R1 -> Concordance engine

### Critical path
Image → vision-LLM → entity extraction → SLMI → R0 → physician review → R1 → concordance computation.

### Design tradeoffs
- **String similarity (τ=0.6) vs ontology-based metrics**: Lightweight but misses hierarchical relationships (e.g., subtype vs superclass).
- **Small dataset (21 cases)**: Enables detailed analysis but limits statistical generalizability.
- **Single-physician validation**: Reduces variability but may not capture inter-rater differences.
- **Fixed differential limit (3)**: Ensures structured output but may omit relevant alternatives.

### Failure signatures
- CCR < 80% or any case with complete diagnostic divergence (ACM_i=0) indicates poor alignment.
- High variance in differential overlap across cases suggests inconsistent SLMI performance.
- Mean inference latency >30s could hinder real-time use.
- Physician interface not capturing all edits would produce R1 that does not reflect true corrections.

### First 3 experiments
1. **End-to-end reproduction**: Load a sample image, run the pipeline, and verify R0 contains a primary and up to three differentials in the expected format. Compare with provided example outputs.
2. **Metric validation**: Using the 21 case pairs, compute PMR, AMR, and CCR manually (or via provided code) to replicate the reported 71.4% and 100%. Check monotonicity (PMR ≤ AMR ≤ CCR).
3. **SLMI ablation**: Disable SLMI and reprocess the dataset; compare alignment metrics to the full pipeline. Expect lower CCR and/or higher complete disagreement.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Does the four-level concordance framework produce reliable metrics when applied to larger, multi-center datasets and other medical specialties?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The study acknowledges limitations due to small sample size (n=21) and single-physician validation.
- **Why unresolved:** The current evaluation may not reflect broader clinical diversity, so the framework’s robustness and thresholds are uncertain.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Multi-center trials involving hundreds of cases across multiple specialties and several experts to validate the consistency and normative ranges of PMR, AMR, and CCR.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can embedding-based or ontology-aware similarity measures improve semantic alignment detection compared to string-based similarity in the concordance analysis?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors suggest future work may incorporate embedding-based similarity or ontology-aware distance measures.
- **Why unresolved:** String similarity cannot capture hierarchical relationships (e.g., subtype vs superclass) or nuanced medical equivalence.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replace or augment the current string similarity with metrics like BERT embeddings or UMLS-based distances on the same R0‑R1 pairs and compare resulting AMR and CCR values to assess whether they better match clinical judgment.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How does inter-physician variability affect the Comprehensive Concordance Rate and other alignment metrics?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper uses single-physician validation per case, which is noted as a limitation.
- **Why unresolved:** With only one expert’s report as ground truth, the stability and generalizability of the metrics across different expert opinions remain unclear.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Obtain R1 from multiple independent dermatologists for the same set of R0 cases; analyze the distribution of concordance metrics using consensus or majority ground truth to quantify variability.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Can the expert correction operator Φ(R0) be formally modeled to iteratively improve AI diagnostic outputs in a human‑in‑the‑loop system?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors interpret R1 = Φ(R0) as a structured transformation but do not develop a computational model for Φ.
- **Why unresolved:** The framework currently only evaluates alignment, not leveraging the transformation for active learning or model refinement; the properties of Φ remain abstract.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Learn a predictive model of Φ from the preserved snapshots (e.g., using sequence‑to‑sequence learning), integrate it into the AI pipeline, and test whether subsequent R0′ outputs achieve higher concordance with physicians without additional expert input.

## Limitations
- **Dataset ambiguity**: The specific public dataset for the 21 dermatological cases is not named, risking input variance.
- **SLMI opaqueness**: The algorithmic details of Sequential Language Model Inference (prompt sequences, knowledge base, refinement loops) are unspecified.
- **Limited generalizability**: Single-physician validation and a small sample (n=21) restrict statistical power and the ability to assess inter-rater reliability.
- **Model black-box nature**: The reliance on a proprietary vision-enabled LLM (e.g., GPT-4V) limits reproducibility and may obscure biases inherent to the model.
- **Threshold selection**: The fixed similarity threshold τ=0.60 for AMR and CCR lacks clinical justification and may not generalize across different medical domains.
