# SiPaKosa: A Comprehensive Corpus of Canonical and Classical Buddhist Texts in Sinhala and Pali

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.29221
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.29221
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: A bilingual corpus of 786,839 sentences (9.25M words) enables low-perplexity language modeling; proprietary models achieve 1.09–1.77 perplexity, outperforming open-source models by 3–6×.

## Executive Summary
SiPaKosa is a large-scale corpus of Sinhala and Pali Buddhist texts designed to address the scarcity of structured NLP resources for these low-resource languages. The corpus combines two sources: the complete Tripitaka canon (web-scraped from tripitaka.online) and 16 copyright-cleared historical books from the IFBCnet Library. Text extraction employs a hybrid pipeline—direct parsing for digital PDFs and Google Document AI OCR for scanned images, filtered by character confidence (≥0.85). A lexicon-based classifier, leveraging Sinhala and custom Pali lexicons with morphological cues, separates Sinhala-only and Mixed Sinhala-Pali content at the sentence level. The final corpus comprises 786,839 sentences (9.25 million words) split into 465,539 Sinhala-only and 320,805 Mixed segments, with a negligible pure Pali subset (495 sentences).

Evaluation using nine language models on held-out test sets reveals a stark performance divide: proprietary models (GPT-3.5-Turbo, GPT-4o-mini) achieve very low perplexity (1.09–1.77) on Buddhist texts, while the best open-source model (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct) performs substantially worse (perplexity 3.29–189.67). All models suffer increased perplexity on the Mixed subcorpus, highlighting the challenge of intra-sentential code-mixing. SiPaKosa provides a valuable resource for advancing NLP on Buddhist literature and underscores the need for specialized models for mixed-language Indic texts.

## Method Summary
The construction of SiPaKosa follows a multi-stage pipeline. Data collection sources are (1) web-scraping of the five Nikayas (Dīgha, Majjhima, Saṃyutta, Aṅguttara, Khuddaka) from tripitaka.online, yielding canonical Sinhala translations and Pali texts, and (2) acquisition of 16 copyright-cleared historical Buddhist books (pre-1954 authors) from the IFBCnet Library. PDFs are processed through a hybrid extraction: `pdfplumber` extracts embedded text directly, while scanned pages undergo OCR via Google Document AI; pages are classified as content or non-content automatically, and only pages with mean confidence ≥0.85 are retained.

Language classification operates at the sentence level. A Sinhala lexicon (41,617 words from Google) and a custom Pali lexicon (13,776 words derived from a Sinhala-Pali dictionary, with overlaps removed) are used. Each sentence receives a Sinhala and a Pali confidence score computed as a weighted sum of lexicon coverage (70%) and morphological pattern matches (30%). Sentences scoring ≥70% for one language and dominant over the other are labeled accordingly; remaining sentences are marked Mixed. The subcorpora are merged from both sources and split into 80% training, 10% validation, and 10% test sets, fixed across sources to avoid leakage. The corpus is annotated with bibliographic, technical, and copyright metadata per book. Finally, nine language models (four proprietary GPT variants, five open-source) are evaluated via perplexity on three 1,024-sentence test sets: Buddhist Sinhala, Mixed Sinhala-Pali, and a general Sinhala sample from CulturaX.

## Key Results
- **Corpus composition:** 465,539 Sinhala-only sentences (5.4M words) and 320,805 Mixed Sinhala-Pali sentences (3.8M words); pure Pali is nearly absent (495 sentences).
- **Model performance gap:** Proprietary models achieve perplexity 1.09–1.77 on Buddhist test sets; the best open-source model (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct) ranges from 3.29 to 189.67—a 3–6× worse performance.
- **Mixed-text difficulty:** All models exhibit higher perplexity on the Mixed subcorpus than on Sinhala-only; the Mixed-to-General (M/G) domain gap ratio is substantially larger than the Buddhist-Sinhala-to-General (BS/G) ratio.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Dual-Source Coverage
**Claim:** Combining web-scraped canonical texts with copyright-cleared historical PDFs enables comprehensive coverage of Sinhala and Pali Buddhist literature.  
**Mechanism:** The web source provides a complete, structured canonical corpus (Tripitaka Nikayas), while the PDFs contribute classical commentaries and treatises otherwise unavailable digitally.  
**Core assumption:** The two sources are complementary and non-redundant in content and genre.  
**Evidence:** Corpus statistics show distinct contributions: PDFs contributed ~111k Sinhala and ~135k Mixed sentences; web-scraped contributed ~354k Sinhala and ~185k Mixed sentences. The paper documents sourcing from IFBCnet Library (PDFs) and tripitaka.online (scraping) with copyright filtering.  
**Break condition:** If copyright restrictions block a major textual genre or if a primary source (e.g., tripitaka.online) becomes inaccessible, coverage gaps emerge.

### Mechanism 2: High-Quality OCR with Confidence Filtering
**Claim:** Google Document AI enables accurate text extraction from degraded historical Sinhala script documents.  
**Mechanism:** A two-path pipeline (direct `pdfplumber` for digital-born PDFs; Document AI for scanned images) with confidence-based quality control (threshold 0.85) and automated filtering of non-content pages.  
**Core assumption:** Document AI’s pre-trained models generalize to Sinhala historical prints, and character-level confidence scores reliably indicate extraction quality.  
**Evidence:** The pipeline achieved an average character-level confidence of 99.8%; automated page classification retained 7,064 content pages (99.8% retention).  
**Break condition:** If source scans are too degraded or Document AI’s Sinhala model lacks coverage for historical orthographic variants, confidence drops below threshold, causing data loss or noisy text.

### Mechanism 3: Lexicon-Based Language Classification
**Claim:** A lexicon-based classifier with morphological feature weighting can separate Sinhala-only, Pali-only, and Mixed Sinhala-Pali text at the sentence/page level.  
**Mechanism:** For each segment, compute a confidence score for Sinhala (41,617-word lexicon) and Pali (13,776-word custom lexicon, filtered to remove Sinhala overlaps). Scores combine lexicon coverage (70% weight) and morphological pattern detection (30% weight). A ≥70% threshold and dominance determine the label.  
**Core assumption:** Diagnostic vocabulary exists in each lexicon after filtering, and morphological cues (case markers, verb endings) are reliably detectable and language-specific.  
**Evidence:** The classifier produced 465,539 Sinhala, 320,805 Mixed, and only 495 Pali sentences—demonstrating success in identifying mixed content but difficulty isolating pure Pali.  
**Break condition:** High lexical overlap or ambiguous morphological cues in rapid code-switching would degrade accuracy, inflating the Mixed category or mislabeling pure text.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Source Ingestion (Web Scraper / PDF Processor) → Text Extraction (pdfplumber or Document AI) → Quality Filtering (page classification & confidence gate) → Language Classification (lexicon + morphology) → Corpus Integration (subcorpora & splits) → Metadata & Provenance.

- **Critical path:** PDF acquisition → OCR/Extraction (Document AI for scanned pages) → Confidence filter → Language classification → Subcorpus integration. The web-scraped path runs in parallel and is simpler (structured HTML extraction).

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  *Web Scraping:* Speed (5 parallel workers, 72h) vs. server stability (2 req/sec rate limiting).  
  *OCR:* Cost/API dependency (Google Document AI) vs. quality (99.8% average confidence; no manual post-processing).  
  *Classification:* Precision (70% threshold) vs. recall of genuine mixed text; custom Pali lexicon construction required manual filtering.  
  *Coverage vs. Legality:* Conservative 70-year copyright rule excludes potentially valuable modern scholarship.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Persistent low OCR confidence (<0.85) on many pages from a specific book.  
  - Imbalanced language classification (e.g., >99% Sinhala, <1% Mixed) for a historically mixed-genre text.  
  - Metadata gaps: missing author death dates preventing copyright assessment.  
  - Web scraper breakage if `tripitaka.online` changes its data source or HTML structure.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. OCR Validation Sample: Manually inspect 100 random low-confidence OCR pages vs. source scans to quantify character error rate and identify degradation patterns.  
  2. Lexicon Classifier Stress Test: Apply the classifier to a known balanced Sinhala-Pali parallel text to measure precision/recall against a gold-labeled subset.  
  3. Domain Gap Replication: Re-run the perplexity evaluation on the first 512 sentences of each test set to confirm the proprietary/open-source performance gap and Mixed text difficulty.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Impact of copyright restrictions** – Excluding post-1950s works may limit the corpus’s representativeness of modern Buddhist scholarship; unresolved because no comparative analysis is provided; evidence: compare linguistic/thematic features with post-1950s texts.
2. **Effect of OCR errors and orthographic variation** – Residual errors may impair downstream tasks; unresolved because error rate and impact are unquantified; evidence: manually correct a sample and compare model performance.
3. **Optimality of language classification threshold** – The 70% threshold is heuristic; unresolved because no gold-standard validation exists; evidence: create an annotated set and find the threshold that maximizes F1.
4. **Correlation between perplexity and downstream task performance** – Low perplexity does not guarantee practical utility; unresolved because no task-specific benchmarks; evidence: evaluate models on QA/generation with human assessment.
5. **Influence of Sanskrit loanwords** – Lack of a Sanskrit lexicon may cause misclassification; unresolved because extent is unknown; evidence: incorporate a Sanskrit lexicon and reclassify to measure discrepancies.

## Limitations
- OCR residual errors persist despite high average confidence (99.8%); no manual post-processing was performed.
- Lexicon-based classification uses a heuristic threshold, yielding near-zero pure-Pali sentences and potential mislabeling.
- Critical resources (custom Pali lexicon, specific CulturaX test subset) are unreleased, hindering exact replication.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (dual-source coverage):** High  
- **Mechanism 2 (OCR quality):** Medium
