# CORE: Robust Out-of-Distribution Detection via Confidence and Orthogonal Residual Scoring

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.18290
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18290
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: CORE achieves highest grand average AUROC (84.9%) across five model-dataset settings, ranks first in three of five benchmarks, and maintains robust performance where other methods fail (e.g., on ViT/Swin).

## Executive Summary
CORE is a post-hoc OOD detection method that disentangles two orthogonal signals from classifier penultimate features: a confidence signal derived from the classifier-aligned component and a membership signal from the orthogonal residual. By combining these via z-score normalized summation, it achieves state-of-the-art robustness across diverse architectures (CNNs and Transformers) with minimal calibration overhead. The method requires only ~1% of in-distribution data for estimating class-specific residual directions and adds negligible computational cost.

## Method Summary
CORE operates on the penultimate feature vector z of a pretrained classifier. It first decomposes z orthogonally into z∥, the projection onto the predicted class weight vector w_ŷ, and the residual z⊥ = z - z∥. The confidence score uses the Energy of logits (or equivalently the norm of z∥), while the membership score computes the cosine similarity between z⊥ and the precomputed mean residual direction µ(ŷ)⊥ for the predicted class. Both scores are normalized using statistics from an in-distribution calibration set and summed to produce the final OOD score. This design leverages the insight that the classifier relies solely on z∥ for decisions, while z⊥ carries class-specific structure for in-distribution data that is absent for out-of-distribution samples.

## Key Results
- Grand average AUROC of 84.9% across five model-dataset pairs, ranking first in three benchmarks.
- Robust performance on vision transformers (ViT, Swin) where activation-shaping methods fail catastrophically.
- Consistent gains over both confidence-based (e.g., Energy) and feature-based (e.g., Mahalanobis) methods, with only ~1% calibration data and O(d) overhead.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The penultimate feature z can be orthogonally decomposed into a classifier-aligned component z∥ (which determines the logit magnitude) and an orthogonal residual z⊥ (which the classifier discards).
- **Mechanism:** For predicted class ŷ with weight w_ŷ, z∥ = (z·w_ŷ/||w_ŷ||²) w_ŷ (or normalized version), and z⊥ = z - z∥. By construction, z∥ ⊥ z⊥.
- **Core assumption:** The classifier's decision depends only on z∥. The discarded z⊥ may contain structured information irrelevant to classification but relevant to data membership.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract, section 3.2]; weak direct corpus evidence.
- **Break condition:** If classifier weights W are unstable or not well-separated, the projection basis w_ŷ may not meaningfully separate confidence from other information.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The orthogonal residual z⊥ carries a class-specific directional signature for in-distribution (ID) data, serving as a membership signal largely absent for OOD samples.
- **Mechanism:** For each class c, ID training samples' residuals z⊥ cluster around a mean direction µ(c)⊥. An ID test sample's residual (for its predicted class ŷ) aligns with µ(ŷ)⊥, yielding high cosine similarity; OOD samples' residuals are arbitrarily oriented, yielding low similarity.
- **Core assumption:** ID data from the same class share a consistent "leftover" structure in the orthogonal complement beyond what the classifier needs. This structure is disrupted for OOD data.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 3.4, A.2] show statistically significant positive gaps Δ in cosine similarity across architectures.
- **Break condition:** For semantically extremely adjacent OOD data (e.g., CIFAR-10 vs. CIFAR-100), z⊥ may share the same directional structure as ID, causing degradation.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Combining independently computed z∥-based confidence (Energy) and z⊥-based membership (cosine) scores via z-score normalized summation yields robust OOD detection because their failure modes are approximately independent.
- **Mechanism:** The two scores operate on orthogonal subspaces, so an OOD sample that evades one is likely caught by the other. The sum provides OR semantics. Normalization makes scores comparable.
- **Core assumption:** Because z∥ ⊥ z⊥ by construction, events causing one score to fail are not strongly correlated with events causing the other to fail.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 3.5, 4.5] show weak correlations (Pearson r ~0.3-0.4) between normalized components, contrasting with entangled components of other methods.
- **Break condition:** If both signals fail simultaneously for a particular OOD type (e.g., near-OOD that achieves high z∥ projection and whose z⊥ accidentally aligns with µ⊥), the sum may not detect it.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Orthogonal Projection**
  - *Why needed here:* To separate the classifier-relevant (z∥) and classifier-irrelevant (z⊥) parts of the feature vector. This is the core geometric operation enabling the dual-signal design.
  - *Quick check question:* Given feature vector z and classifier weight w_ŷ for the predicted class, how would you compute z∥ and z⊥ to ensure they are orthogonal and sum to z?

- **Concept: Cosine Similarity as Directional Match**
  - *Why needed here:* The membership score cos(z⊥, µ(ŷ)⊥) measures whether the residual points in the characteristic ID direction for that class. It is scale-invariant, focusing purely on direction.
  - *Quick check question:* Why is cosine similarity appropriate for comparing residual directions z⊥ and µ⊥, and what would a high vs. low value indicate about sample membership?

- **Concept: Z-Score Normalization**
  - *Why needed here:* The raw Energy and cosine similarity scores have vastly different scales. Z-score normalization using ID training statistics puts both on a common, zero-centered scale so their sum is meaningful.
  - *Quick check question:* What statistics (μ, σ) are computed from the ID calibration set for each score, and why is using the same ID data for both normalizations important for fair combination?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Calibration Data -> Offline Estimator -> (µ⊥, μ_E, σ_E, μ_R, σ_R); Test Input -> Feature Extractor (ϕ) & Classifier (W) -> (z, logits, ŷ); (z, ŷ, stats) -> Online Scorer -> OOD Score.

- **Critical path:**
  1. Run forward pass to obtain penultimate feature z and logits ℓ.
  2. Compute z∥ as projection of z onto weight w_ŷ of predicted class ŷ.
  3. Compute residual z⊥ = z - z∥.
  4. Retrieve precomputed µ(ŷ)⊥ and compute membership score S_mem = cos(z⊥, µ(ŷ)⊥).
  5. Compute confidence score S_conf = Energy(ℓ) (or Energy from z∥).
  6. Normalize both scores using precomputed means and standard deviations from ID calibration set.
  7. Sum normalized scores to produce final OOD score.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Calibration budget:** Minimal (~1% of ID data) suffices for µ⊥ estimation vs. ~100% for KNN/Mahalanobis. Tradeoff: very few samples may yield noisy µ⊥.
  - **Confidence score choice:** Energy is default and best; Max Logit is nearly as good; MSP weaker. Tradeoff: simplicity vs. marginal performance.
  - **Combination weight:** Equal weighting (α=0.5) is robust; no tuning needed for new architectures.
  - **Storage:** Stores C×d matrix of µ⊥ (~8MB for ImageNet). Tradeoff: negligible vs. memory cost of KNN indices (~100MB+).

- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Architecture-specific collapse:** Activation shaping (ASH, ReAct) fails catastrophically on ViT/Swin due to activation distribution differences.
  - **Hubness / distance concentration:** KNN, Mahalanobis fail on high-d transformers (ViT) due to hubness; CORE uses cosine in z⊥, less susceptible.
  - **Near-OOD limitation:** When OOD data is semantically adjacent (e.g., CIFAR-10 vs. CIFAR-100), z⊥ may retain class-specific structure, causing failure.
  - **Correlated features:** On WRN, S_conf and S_mem correlation is higher (r≈0.5 ID), reducing complementary gain, though CORE still works.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Ablation (components):** Run with S_conf only, S_mem only, and full CORE. Expect: S_mem alone > Energy on ImageNet transformers; full CORE > best single on all settings.
  2. **Calibration sensitivity:** Vary calibration set size (1%, 10%, 100%). Expect: CORE stable across budgets; KNN/Mahalanobis highly volatile, especially on transformers.
  3. **Component correlation:** Compute Pearson r between normalized S'_conf and S'_mem on ID and OOD sets. Expect: low correlation (|r|<0.4) for most settings, higher for WRN; scatter plots show ID cluster in top-right, OOD spread along axes.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Why do all existing OOD detection method categories exhibit systematic blind spots across different architectures?
- **Basis:** The authors state that no single post-hoc method is consistently best across architectures and datasets, attributing this to a shared structural limitation: logit-based methods see only confidence, while feature-based methods measure membership in the entangled full feature space.
- **Why unresolved:** While CORE decouples these signals, it is not proven that entanglement is the sole or primary cause of inconsistencies; other architectural factors may contribute.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments where confidence and membership signals are artificially separated or mixed in feature spaces across architectures, measuring resulting OOD performance changes.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How can CORE's residual membership score be improved for near-OOD detection where ID and OOD data share similar residual directional structures (e.g., CIFAR-10 vs. CIFAR-100)?
- **Basis:** The authors acknowledge that CORE’s membership signal degrades on semantically extremely adjacent OOD data, which is inherent to any class-conditional membership method.
- **Why unresolved:** The method relies on class-specific residual mean directions, which become nearly identical for closely related datasets, reducing separability.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Development of a higher-order membership statistic (e.g., per-class residual covariance or higher-moment directions) to capture subtle distributional differences beyond mean alignment.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Is the class-specific directional signature in the orthogonal residual causally linked to Neural Collapse, or does it arise independently from other training dynamics?
- **Basis:** The paper mentions Neural Collapse as a possible explanation and verifies statistical alignment gaps, but does not establish causality or isolate it from other factors like data augmentation or optimization.
- **Why unresolved:** The observed correlation could be coincidental or driven by alternative mechanisms; Neural Collapse is not manipulated experimentally.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Train classifiers under conditions that suppress Neural Collapse (e.g., with explicit regularization or alternative loss functions) and measure whether the residual discriminativity persists.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Does CORE's orthogonal decomposition approach generalize to non-vision domains (e.g., NLP or time-series) where feature space geometries and classifier structures differ significantly?
- **Basis:** Experiments are limited to image classification models (CNNs and vision transformers); applicability to other modalities is unstated and untested, assuming feature properties generalize.
- **Why unresolved:** Theoretical justification relies on geometric properties of penultimate features that may not hold in domains like language or sequential data.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply CORE to pretrained language models (e.g., BERT) on text OOD benchmarks and to time-series classifiers, comparing against domain-specific baselines and analyzing residual structures.

## Limitations
- Near-OOD degradation: When ID and OOD data share similar residual directional structures (e.g., CIFAR-10 vs. CIFAR-100), the membership signal weakens.
- Calibration sensitivity: With very small calibration sets (<1%), noise in estimated µ⊥ may hurt performance; method assumes stable class-specific residual means.
- Signal correlation: On some architectures (e.g., WRN), confidence and membership scores exhibit higher correlation (r≈0.5), reducing the benefit of combination.

## Confidence
- Decomposition validity: High
- Residual membership signal: Medium
- Score independence: Medium
