# Interpreting Negation in GPT-2: Layer- and Head-Level Causal Analysis
## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.12423  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12423  
- **Reference count:** 16  
- **Primary result:** Negation processing in GPT-2 Small is localized to a small set of mid-layer attention heads (layers 4–6, notably L5H11), as shown by activation patching and ablation studies using the Negation Effect Score (NES).

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how GPT-2 Small handles negation, a linguistic phenomenon where models often misinterpret or produce factual errors. The authors introduce the Negation Effect Score (NES) to quantify a model's sensitivity to negation by comparing token probabilities under affirmative and negated contexts. Using a curated dataset of 12,000 sentence pairs, they conduct causal interventions—activation patching and ablation—across layers and attention heads. The main finding is that negation processing is highly localized: a compact subnetwork of mid-layer attention heads (primarily layers 4–6, with L5H11 being particularly influential) is causally responsible. Ablating these heads increases NES (indicating weakened negation sensitivity) on in-domain synthetic data, but on the natural xNot360 benchmark, ablation slightly decreases NES, and rescue restores performance above baseline. This reveals a consistent yet context-dependent circuit for negation across different morphological forms. The work provides mechanistic evidence for sparse, interpretable subcircuits implementing logical polarity in transformers.

## Method Summary
The study employs a frozen GPT-2 Small model and a self-curated dataset of 12,000 matched affirmative/negated sentence pairs covering eight semantic templates and seven negation forms. The core metric, NES, is defined as the difference in log-probability for the same target token under affirmative and negated prefixes; a negative NES indicates correct negation sensitivity. Causal localization proceeds in three stages: first, layer-wise activation patching on the *can ability* development set identifies layers 4–6 as having the greatest influence on NES; second, head-level patching within these layers ranks individual attention heads by their impact, revealing a small set (e.g., L5H11) that dominates; third, ablation (zeroing out top-k head outputs) and rescue (patching in affirmative activations) on held-out test sets test necessity and sufficiency. The same head set is then evaluated on the external xNot360 benchmark to assess cross-distribution generalization.

## Key Results
- NES effectively captures negation sensitivity, showing systematic failures (e.g., strong affirmative bias) on factual templates like "capital of".
- A compact mid-layer attention subnetwork (layers 4–6, notably L5H11) is necessary and sufficient for negation processing, as disrupting it significantly alters NES.
- The circuit's effect is consistent across negation forms but context-dependent: ablation increases NES on in-domain data (weaker sensitivity) yet slightly decreases NES on xNot360 (improved sensitivity), with rescue restoring above-baseline performance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Localized Negation Circuit
- **Claim:** Negation processing is handled by a compact, localized subnetwork of mid-layer attention heads (primarily layers 4–6, e.g., L5H11).
- **Mechanism:** Activation patching and ablation results show that replacing or removing these specific heads disrupts the model's negation sensitivity (as measured by NES), indicating they are causally necessary and sufficient for encoding polarity.
- **Core assumption:** Linguistic features like negation are implemented by sparse, functionally distinct circuits rather than being distributed throughout the model.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Abstract: "...negation processing is highly localized: a small set of mid-layer attention heads (layers 4–6, notably L5H11) is causally responsible."
  - Section IV.B: "Head-level analysis confirms that only a handful of units dominate negation processing. Heads like L5H11/L4H4 show large positive ΔNES..."
  - Corpus: Neighbors like "Mechanistic Interpretability of GPT-2..." and "How does GPT-2 compute greater-than?..." support the finding that mid-layer heads implement specific algorithmic functions.
- **Break condition:** If the identified heads are ablated but NES does not change significantly (ΔNES ≈ 0), the claim of localized causality would be falsified.

### Mechanism 2: Negation Effect Score (NES) as a Valid Probe
- **Claim:** The Negation Effect Score (NES) validly captures a model's sensitivity to logical polarity reversal by contrasting token probabilities under affirmative vs. negated contexts.
- **Mechanism:** NES = logP(t|affirmative) − logP(t|negated). A negative score indicates the model assigns higher probability to the token expected under negation (correct sensitivity); positive indicates failure (affirmative bias).
- **Core assumption:** The difference in next-token log-probability for the same target token, given minimally different contexts (only a negation cue), isolates the model's internal representation of logical polarity.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Section III.B: Definition and computation of NES.
  - Section IV.A: Behavioral baseline table showing NES varies across templates, confirming it measures a meaningful gradient of negation handling.
  - Corpus: Weak direct evidence; the metric is novel to this paper. Corpus neighbors focus on negation benchmarks, not this specific scoring method.
- **Break condition:** If NES shows no correlation with human judgment of negation understanding or with other established negation evaluation metrics, its validity as a behavioral probe would be compromised.

### Mechanism 3: Context-Dependent Consistency Across Forms
- **Claim:** The causal role of the identified mid-layer circuit is consistent across different morphological forms of negation (e.g., "never", "does not", "can't") but its directional effect on NES (improving vs. harming polarity) depends on the distributional context (synthetic templates vs. natural text).
- **Mechanism:** Ablating the same head set increases NES (weaker sensitivity) across all *can ability* forms in-domain, but on the natural xNot360 benchmark, ablation slightly *decreases* NES. This suggests the circuit's output interacts with local lexical cues and factual priors differently in each data distribution.
- **Core assumption:** A single functional subcircuit can be modulated by context to have opposing effects on a downstream behavioral metric.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Section IV.D: "Using the held-out test split... all five negation variants... ablating these heads consistently increased NES... On the xNot360 dataset... ablating... decreased the mean NES... rescue restored it above baseline."
  - Abstract: "...these patterns are consistent across various negation forms... though with smaller magnitude."
  - Corpus: Titles like "When Prohibitions Become Permissions..." and "Paraphrasing in Affirmative Terms..." confirm negation behavior is context-sensitive and form-dependent.
- **Break condition:** If the direction of ΔNES upon ablation were reversed for some morphological forms within the same in-domain test set, the claim of consistent circuit function across forms would be weakened.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Activation Patching & Causal Tracing**
  - **Why needed here:** These are the core diagnostic tools used to localize where negation information flows. Without understanding how to intervene on activations and measure ΔNES, the structural localization claims cannot be made.
  - **Quick check question:** What is the key difference between measuring ΔNES from activation patching (substituting a clean run's activation) versus measuring ΔNES from ablation (zeroing out a component)?
- **Concept: Negation Effect Score (NES)**
  - **Why needed here:** NES is the primary behavioral metric that quantifies "negation sensitivity." All causal claims (localization, necessity, sufficiency) are anchored to changes in this score.
  - **Quick check question:** If NES = 0.5 for an example, what does that say about the model's prediction for the target token under the negated prefix vs. the affirmative prefix?
- **Concept: Localized Circuits vs. Distributed Representations**
  - **Why needed here:** The paper's central thesis challenges a possible naive view that language features are broadly represented. Understanding the concept of a "circuit" (a sparse, functionally connected set of components) is key to interpreting the findings.
  - **Quick check question:** If a behavior is localized to a circuit, what would you expect to see when you ablate *only* the components in that circuit versus randomly chosen components?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** residual_in -> attention heads -> (patch/ablate specific head outputs) -> sum with MLP output and residual_in -> residual_out -> next layer
- **Critical path:**
  1. Define behavioral probe (NES) on curated sentence pairs.
  2. Perform full-layer activation patching to identify peak influence layers.
  3. Perform head-level patching within peak layers to rank influential heads.
  4. Validate necessity/sufficiency via ablation/rescue on the top-ranked head set.
  5. Test generalization across forms (in-domain) and distribution (xNot360).
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Focusing on the *can ability* template yields a clean signal but risks overfitting; addressed by testing across other templates and on xNot360.
  - Intervening at the final prefix token assumes negation consolidation there; earlier interventions might reveal different dynamics.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Behavioral: High failure rate (Pr[NES>0] ≈ 100%) on factual templates like "capital of" indicates overwhelming affirmative bias.
  - Causal: Ablation producing a flat ΔNES curve (no change) vs. random-head controls would falsify the localized circuit hypothesis.
  - Distribution shift: Reversal of ablation effect direction between in-domain and xNot360 signals context-dependence rather than invariance.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Dataset & Baseline Construction:** Build the 12k-pair dataset with stratified negation forms. Compute baseline NES for all templates to identify where the model struggles (e.g., "capital of" vs. "drives vehicle").
  2. **Layer-Wise Activation Patching:** For the *can ability* dev set, run affirmative sentences, cache post-attention activations per layer. For each negated sentence, patch in the cached affirmative activation layer-by-layer, recompute NES, calculate ΔNES(L). Plot to locate peak influence layers (expected: ~L4-L6).
  3. **Head Ranking & Ablation/Rescue:** Within peak layers, perform head-level patching to rank heads by |ΔNES(L,H)|. Select top-k (e.g., k=8). On the *can ability* test set, run ablation (zero top-k head outputs) and rescue (patch in top-k head outputs from affirmative run). Plot mean NES vs. k to verify necessity (ablation increases in-domain NES) and sufficiency (rescue increases NES further). Run same controls on xNot360.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Do larger transformer models use deeper layers for negation processing?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors state in the future work: "we plan to conduct scale studies across model sizes and families (GPT–2 Medium/XL, LLaMA) to test whether polarity circuits shift deeper with depth."
- **Why unresolved:** The study was limited to GPT-2 Small; it is unknown whether the mid-layer localization pattern holds for larger models or different architectures.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicate the activation patching and ablation–rescue pipeline on larger GPT-2 variants and LLaMA, then compare the layer distributions of influential heads to see if they shift deeper.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What roles do MLP layers and residual streams play in the negation circuit?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors note they "did not exhaustively trace MLP paths or quantify interactions with factual priors" and plan to "extend tracing to MLP blocks and residual streams with causal mediation to map full pathways, not just heads."
- **Why unresolved:** Causal analysis was performed only on attention head outputs; the contributions of feedforward networks and residual connections remain unexplored.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply activation patching and causal mediation analysis to MLP outputs and residual stream dimensions across layers, measuring their effects on the Negation Effect Score to determine whether they carry complementary or redundant negation signals.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Why does ablating the negation heads increase NES in-domain but decrease NES on xNot360?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors observe the reversal and state: "A full causal decomposition of this distributional effect is an important direction for future work."
- **Why unresolved:** The interaction between the identified circuit, factual priors, and local negation cues differs between synthetic templates and natural text, but the precise mechanism causing the polarity flip is unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct a comparative analysis of activations and intervene on pathways related to factual priors in both synthetic and natural examples, isolating variables such as factuality strength and negation marker type to explain the distribution-dependent behavior.

## Limitations
- Dataset generation code and full template list not released, hindering exact replication.
- Head selection threshold and final head composition are underspecified.
- NES lacks direct validation against human judgments or external negation benchmarks.
