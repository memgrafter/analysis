# Resources for Automated Evaluation of Assistive RAG Systems that Help Readers with News Trustworthiness Assessment
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.24277
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.24277
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: AutoJudge preserves human rankings with Kendall’s τ = 0.678 (Task 1) and 0.872 (Task 2).

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the scarcity of reusable benchmarks for evaluating assistive RAG systems that support readers in assessing news trustworthiness. It introduces the TREC 2025 DRAGUN Track resources: 30 news articles accompanied by investigator-authored, importance-weighted rubrics derived from open-web lateral reading, along with human judgments and participant system runs. To enable automated evaluation, the authors develop an LLM-based AutoJudge using few-shot prompting to score outputs against the rubrics. Validation shows that AutoJudge rankings correlate strongly with human rankings, offering a reproducible foundation for future research.

## Method Summary
The authors assembled a benchmark comprising 30 news articles. Human assessors performed open-web lateral reading to formulate investigative questions and corresponding expected answers, assigning importance weights to reflect priority. These rubrics capture the essential information needed for trustworthiness assessment. Participant systems produced outputs for two tasks: question generation and report generation. An AutoJudge was built using large language models with few-shot prompting to compare system outputs against the rubric, assigning coverage scores. The entire package—including topics, rubrics, human judgments, system runs, AutoJudge, and scoring scripts—was released to support reproducible research.

## Key Results
- AutoJudge achieves Kendall’s τ = 0.678 for Task 1 (Question Generation) and τ = 0.872 for Task 2 (Report Generation) when compared to human rankings.
- The released benchmark resources enable fully automated evaluation of assistive RAG systems.
- Human-AutoJudge label-level agreement is moderate (Cohen’s κ ≈ 0.47–0.50), indicating room for improvement.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The mechanism hinges on a well-designed rubric that distills human lateral-reading processes into concrete, weighted questions and answers. The AutoJudge leverages few-shot learning to teach an LLM to apply this rubric consistently, effectively simulating human evaluators. The importance weighting ensures that evaluation emphasizes the most critical trustworthiness signals. By automating the scoring with an LLM, the approach scales while preserving the nuance of human judgment, as evidenced by strong rank correlation.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Topics -> Rubric Creation (Human Assessors) -> System Runs (Participant Models) -> AutoJudge (LLM) -> Scores -> Evaluation Metrics -> Rankings

**Critical path:**  
The shortest path from rubric definition to final rankings: (1) Rubric creation, (2) System output generation, (3) AutoJudge scoring, (4) Metric computation. All subsequent analyses depend on this sequence.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Fixed corpus (MS MARCO V2.1) ensures reproducibility but may miss information retrievable from the live web.  
- Single LLM for AutoJudge reduces complexity and cost but may lack robustness across model variations.  
- Importance weighting adds evaluation granularity but increases assessor burden and potential inconsistency.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Low human-AutoJudge rank correlation (τ < 0.6) or label agreement (κ < 0.5).  
- High proportion (>20%) of rubric answers absent from the fixed corpus, indicating coverage gaps.  
- Significant variance in system scores across different random seeds or prompt formulations.

**First three experiments:**  
1. Cross-dataset generalization: Apply AutoJudge to a new set of news articles with fresh human evaluation to measure correlation decay.  
2. Prompt/model sensitivity: Vary few-shot examples (0,1,3,5) and test multiple LLMs (e.g., GPT-4, Claude, Llama) to assess stability.  
3. Corpus augmentation: Integrate live web retrieval to supplement MS MARCO, measure change in rubric answer coverage and AutoJudge-human correlation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: How can citation faithfulness (whether cited evidence actually supports the associated claim) and readability be integrated into the rubric-based evaluation framework for assistive RAG systems?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper states that future work should extend the framework with complementary dimensions like citation faithfulness and readability, beyond current supportive/contradictory scoring.
- Why unresolved: The current AutoJudge only assesses rubric-answer coverage; these additional dimensions are recognized as important for RAG but lack operational definitions and validation within the rubric paradigm.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing prompt templates or modular judges for citation faithfulness and readability, then correlating these scores with human assessments and measuring impact on system rankings.

### Open Question 2
- Question: To what extent does the fixed MS MARCO V2.1 Segmented Corpus constraint limit the completeness of rubric coverage, and what methods can mitigate missing information?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper notes that rubric answers may be absent from the fixed corpus, a trade-off for avoiding pool-nugget bias, but headroom analysis suggests gaps even for discoverable information.
- Why unresolved: The paper does not quantify how often rubric answers are unretrievable in the corpus nor explore retrieval expansion techniques to address this.
- What evidence would resolve it: Measuring the proportion of rubric answers unanswerable from the corpus and evaluating whether augmenting with external search (e.g., live web) improves system scores and human-AutoJudge correlation.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Why is label-level agreement between AutoJudge and humans only moderate (Cohen’s κ ≈ 0.47–0.50), and how can prompt engineering or model choice improve fine-grained accuracy?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper reports label-level agreement metrics (κ and AC1) and acknowledges κ is deflated by class imbalance, but AC1 remains moderate; it suggests future work on stronger judges.
- Why unresolved: The root causes of disagreement (e.g., subtle distinctions between “partial” and “supports”) are not analyzed, and the prompt design or LLM limitations are not systematically probed.
- What evidence would resolve it: Error analysis of AutoJudge failures, ablation studies on prompt variations (e.g., more examples, chain-of-thought), and testing newer LLMs to isolate whether better models or better prompts drive gains.

### Open Question 4
- Question: Can the assessor-authored rubric creation pipeline be scaled to hundreds of topics while maintaining quality and adherence to lateral-reading principles?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper concludes that the pipeline demonstrates feasibility and suggests scaling to generate larger benchmarks and training datasets.
- Why unresolved: Scaling introduces challenges: cost of expert assessors, consistency across many assessors, and ensuring rubrics remain unbiased and reader-oriented without dilution.
- What evidence would resolve it: A pilot study creating rubrics for, e.g., 200 topics with multiple assessors, measuring inter-assessor rubric overlap, importance-weight consistency, and downstream impact on system evaluations (e.g., rank stability).

## Limitations
- Generalizability beyond the 30 curated articles is uncertain; performance may degrade on other news topics or styles.
- Reliance on a single LLM and prompt design lacks robustness validation across models and prompt variations.
- Rubric creation suffers from potential subjectivity due to insufficient documentation of assessor training and inter-annotator agreement.

## Confidence

- Resources enable reproducible benchmarking: Medium-High
- Rubric-based evaluation mirrors human trustworthiness assessment: Medium
