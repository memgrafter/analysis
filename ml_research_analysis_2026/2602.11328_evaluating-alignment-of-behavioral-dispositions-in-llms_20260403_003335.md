# Evaluating Alignment of Behavioral Dispositions in LLMs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.11328
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11328
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** LLMs exhibit severe distributional misalignment with human behavioral dispositions, showing systematic overconfidence and a gap between stated values and actual behavior.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates how well large language models (LLMs) align with human behavioral dispositions across empathy, emotion regulation, assertiveness, and impulsiveness. The authors convert established psychological self-report statements into Situational Judgment Tests (SJTs) that present realistic user-assistant dilemmas with two opposing actions. Human preferences are collected from 550 participants (10 annotations per SJT) to form ground truth, while 25 LLMs generate free-form responses evaluated via an LLM-as-a-judge system. Results reveal that LLMs suffer from severe distributional misalignment, especially in low-consensus scenarios, due to systematic overconfidence—models maintain >90% consistency even when human agreement is ~50%. In high-consensus cases, frontier models still deviate from human preferences in 15–20% of instances. Critically, self-reported trait values show poor predictive validity for revealed behavior, indicating a fundamental gap between claimed and actual behavioral alignment. These findings underscore the need for more nuanced alignment evaluation and highlight the challenge of ensuring that LLM behavior genuinely reflects human values.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates 25 LLMs by converting psychometric self-report items (e.g., from Trait EI) into actionable advice statements via a three-step pipeline: filtering for behavioral volatility, reframing into prescriptive statements, and embedding in user-assistant dilemmas with two clear opposing actions. Human annotators first validate each SJT (three independent judgments, ~8% rejection rate). Then, 10 annotators per SJT select the preferred action, yielding a Trait-Positive Rate (TPR) as ground truth. LLMs generate open-ended responses to the same dilemmas, which are classified by a separate LLM judge (Gemini 1.5 Flash) as supporting one of the two actions. The judge's accuracy is verified on a random sample of 100 cases. Model alignment is quantified by Trait Misalignment (difference between model and human TPR) and Directional Alignment (accuracy against high-consensus items, defined by a human consensus threshold τ = 0.8). This approach combines the ecological validity of open-ended generation with the statistical rigor of binary choice analysis.

## Key Results
- LLMs exhibit systematic overconfidence, maintaining >90% consistency even when human agreement is ~50%.
- In high-consensus scenarios (τ ≥ 0.8), frontier models still deviate from human preferences in 15–20% of cases.
- Self-reported trait values poorly predict actual behavioral choices in SJTs, indicating a disconnect between claimed and revealed dispositions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The evaluation framework relies on three interconnected mechanisms:

1. **SJT Conversion Enhances Behavioral Validity**  
   Abstract self-report claims (e.g., "I am empathetic") are reframed into prescriptive advice (e.g., "I recommend that a person should consider others' feelings") and embedded in realistic dilemmas. The model's free-text response is mapped to one of two opposing actions, shifting focus from stated agreement to actionable choice, which better reveals latent behavioral tendencies. Human validation ensures the SJTs are valid (8% rejection rate). *Break condition:* Invalid SJTs (non-dilemmas, unclear actions) collapse the mechanism.

2. **Consensus Thresholding Isolates Clear Norms**  
   A high consensus threshold (τ = 0.8) identifies scenarios with strong human agreement, treating these as reflecting robust social norms. Alignment against these items provides a clean test of whether the model's disposition matches established preferences. Empirical results show that model alignment improves with human consensus (Figure 2), supporting the mechanism. *Break condition:* Non-representative human annotator pool could produce spurious consensus; demographic analysis (Table 2) shows minimal variance but not full resolution.

3. **LLM-as-a-Judge Enables Scalable, Flexible Evaluation**  
   A separate LLM (Gemini 1.5 Flash) maps free-form model responses to the predefined actions, avoiding multiple-choice priming while retaining measurable outcomes. Human validation (100% accuracy on sample) confirms the judge's reliability. *Break condition:* Judge-specific biases or drift could systematically misclassify responses; the single-judge design is a vulnerability.

Together, these mechanisms produce a robust, replicable measure of behavioral alignment across models and traits.

## Foundational Learning
### Psychometric Questionnaire Adaptation
- **Why needed:** The pipeline starts from established psychological instruments; understanding filtering, reframing, and validation criteria is critical for generating valid SJTs.
- **Quick check:** What are the key criteria (Appendix A) for disqualifying a self-report statement from SJT transformation? (Answer: Must describe a voluntary behavior/strategy, not a fixed biological state, passive outcome, or innate capability.)

### Human Consensus as a Ground Truth Operationalization
- **Why needed:** Core metrics rely on aggregating human preferences; consensus thresholds (τ) define how we segment ambiguous vs. clear-cut scenarios.
- **Quick check:** Why is τ=0.8 chosen, and how does it affect Directional Alignment interpretation? (Answer: High threshold ensures robust majority signal; results show frontier models still fail 15-20% of clear cases.)

### LLM-as-a-Judge with Human Validation Loop
- **Why needed:** This is the core evaluation engine; understanding open-ended generation, judge prompting, and human validation is essential for claim validity.
- **Quick check:** What is the purpose of human validation for SJTs vs. for the judge? (Answer: SJT validation ensures the test item itself is valid; judge validation ensures the scoring mechanism is accurate.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
QuestionnaireAdapter -> SJTGenerator -> HumanValidationModule -> HumanAnnotationModule -> ModelEvaluationOrchestrator -> LLMJudge -> MetricsEngine

**Critical path:**  
The sequence above must succeed; a failure in human validation (steps 3–4) corrupts all downstream metrics.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Binary action constraint vs. ecological validity: forces binary choice, sacrificing nuance for clear alignment metrics.
- Single LLM judge vs. robustness: efficient but vulnerable to judge-specific biases.
- Ground truth from 550 US/UK annotators vs. cultural universality: provides statistical power but limits generalizability.

**Failure signatures:**
1. High SJT rejection rate (>15%): generator or prompt producing incoherent/non-dilemma items.
2. Low inter-annotator agreement within high-consensus bucket: indicates SJT ambiguity or rater pool issues.
3. Judge "neither" rate spikes (>10%): model responses too evasive or judge prompt ineffective.
4. Negative self-report vs. behavior correlation across dataset: possible flaw in mapping or fundamental model inconsistency.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Judge Accuracy Check: Verify judge performance with human review of 100 examples; ensure >95% agreement.
2. Pipeline Stability Check: Analyze the 8% rejected SJTs to refine generator prompt.
3. Baseline Replication: Replicate Figure 3 (overconfidence pattern) on a subset of models.

## Limitations
- Cultural generalizability: Human annotators limited to US/UK; consensus thresholds may not generalize across cultures.
- Judge monoculture: Evaluation relies on a single LLM judge (Gemini 1.5 Flash); its unmeasured biases could skew model TPRs.
- Self-report disconnect: The poor predictive validity of self-reports is correlational; flaws in the SJT mapping cannot be ruled out as an alternative explanation.

## Confidence
- Systematic Overconfidence: High
- High-Consensus Misalignment: Medium
- Self-Report Invalidity: Medium

## Next Checks
- Judge Robustness Check: Re-run evaluation with a diverse ensemble of LLM judges (GPT-4, Claude 3, Llama 3) to measure TPR variance.
- Cross-Cultural Consensus: Recruit demographically diverse annotators (e.g., 5 regions) for a stratified subset of high-consensus SJTs; assess consensus stability.
- Mapping Sanity Check: For a trait subset, generate SJTs from alternative questionnaire items; if self-report/behavior correlation persists, it supports fundamental model inconsistency; if it vanishes, it points to item-specific mapping flaws.
