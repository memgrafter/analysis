# AG-REPA: Causal Layer Selection for Representation Alignment in Audio Flow Matching

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.01006  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01006  
- **Reference count:** 16  
- **Primary result:** AG-REPA reduces Fréchet Audio Distance (FAD) by 18% on speech (LibriSpeech) and 16% on general audio (AudioSet) vs the best fixed-layer REPA baseline, while improving Word Error Rate (3.45 vs 4.21) and Mean Opinion Score (4.12 vs 3.92).

## Executive Summary
Token-conditioned audio Flow Matching often uses Representation Alignment (REPA) to transfer knowledge from frozen teacher encoders (e.g., Whisper, BEATs) into the student diffusion model. However, standard REPA relies on heuristic layer selection (e.g., all layers or top by representational similarity), ignoring that layers storing rich semantic or acoustic features do not necessarily contribute most to the velocity field that drives generation—a phenomenon the authors call Store-Contribute Dissociation (SCD). To address this, they propose Attribution-Guided REPA (AG-REPA), which introduces Forward-only Gate Ablation (FoG-A) to measure each layer’s causal impact on the predicted velocity field. By ablating a layer’s output in a forward pass and observing the induced change, FoG-A identifies functionally critical layers (often very early ones) and assigns them adaptive alignment weights. This sparse, causally guided alignment yields substantial gains across both speech (LibriSpeech) and general audio (AudioSet) generation, with significant reductions in FAD and improvements in intelligibility and perceptual quality.

## Method Summary
AG-REPA operates in two phases. **Phase I (Probe):** With the student DiT backbone frozen and teacher encoders (Whisper for semantics, BEATs for acoustics) also frozen, three layer-wise metrics are computed for every DiT block: BiT-C (input interface alignment), LASP (layerwise similarity to teacher representations), and FoG-A (causal attribution via forward gate ablation). FoG-A measures the normalized L₂ difference between the velocity field predicted with a layer ablated (gate set to 0) and the standard prediction (gate = 1), averaged over a validation set. From the FoG-A scores, the top-K layers \(S\) are selected and per-layer alignment weights \(\lambda_k\) are derived. **Phase II (Intervene):** Starting from the probe checkpoint, the model is trained with the standard Flow Matching loss \(L_{FM}\), an interface-level BiT-C loss \(L_{BiT}\), and an additional sparse alignment loss applied only to the selected layers \(S\): \(\sum_{k \in S} \lambda_k \cdot (1 - \cos(h_{\phi_k}(\bar{h}_k), T(x)))\), where \(h_{\phi_k}\) are lightweight MLP projection heads. This targeted alignment directs training toward layers that truly influence the generation dynamics, avoiding interference from less critical layers.

## Key Results
- **FAD reduction:** 18% on speech (LibriSpeech) and 16% on general audio (AudioSet) compared to the best fixed-layer REPA baseline.
- **Intelligibility & quality:** Word Error Rate (WER) improved to 3.45 (vs 4.21) and Mean Opinion Score (MOS) increased to 4.12 (vs 3.92).
- **Efficiency:** Using FoG-A for layer selection yields 3.4× larger FAD reduction and 3.3× faster convergence than selecting layers based on representational similarity (LASP).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
AG-REPA succeeds because it directly quantifies each layer’s causal contribution to the velocity field, bypassing the misleading correlation between stored features (high teacher similarity) and actual impact on generation. Forward-only Gate Ablation (FoG-A) isolates the effect of a layer by intervention (zeroing its output) and measuring the resulting change in the model’s core prediction. This reveals that early layers, despite having lower semantic similarity to teachers, often exert the strongest influence on the flow trajectory. By focusing alignment on these top-contributing layers and weighting them proportionally to their FoG-A scores, AG-REPA ensures that training effort is spent where it matters most, reducing interference and accelerating convergence. The sparse alignment also preserves the student’s capacity to learn generation dynamics not captured by the teachers, mitigating over‑constraining.

- **Flow Matching:** Defines a stochastic path between noise and data and trains a neural network to predict the conditional velocity field. *Why needed:* Provides the generative framework for audio synthesis. *Quick check:* Explain how the instantaneous velocity field \(v_t(x)\) is parameterized and trained via a regression loss.
- **Representation Alignment (REPA):** Aligns hidden representations of a student model with those of a frozen teacher via cosine or MSE loss. *Why needed:* Transfers rich semantic/acoustic knowledge from pre‑trained encoders. *Quick check:* Identify typical alignment targets (e.g., layer activations) and common projection heads.
- **Store‑Contribute Dissociation (SCD):** The mismatch between layers that store high‑level features (measured by teacher similarity) and layers that most affect the generative output (measured by causal impact). *Why needed:* Exposes the flaw in heuristic REPA selection. *Quick check:* Describe why LASP rankings may differ from FoG‑A rankings.
- **Forward‑only Gate Ablation (FoG‑A):** Attribution method that sets a layer’s gate to 0, runs a forward pass to recompute the velocity field, and measures the normalized L₂ difference from the original prediction. *Why needed:* Provides a scalable, gradient‑free estimate of causal contribution. *Quick check:* Write the FoG‑A score formula (Eq. 9) and explain the role of the stability term ϵ.
- **Probe‑then‑Intervene:** Two‑stage protocol where diagnostics are run on a fixed checkpoint before training modifications begin. *Why needed:* Ensures layer selection is deterministic and not confounded by simultaneous weight updates. *Quick check:* Outline the two phases and their respective objectives.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Text prompt (+ optional reference audio) → LLM (Qwen3) → acoustic tokens (S3/AudioSet) → DiT backbone (with AG‑REPA alignment) → predicted mel‑spectrogram → vocoder → audio.  
Frozen teacher encoders (Whisper, BEATs) process target mel‑spectrograms to produce alignment targets for selected DiT layers.

**Critical path:**  
During training, the LLM generates a token sequence conditioned on the input. This sequence is fed to the DiT, which, at a random diffusion timestep \(t\), predicts a velocity field. The FM loss compares this prediction to the target velocity. In AG‑REPA, selected DiT layers extract intermediate representations, project them, and compute a cosine similarity loss against the corresponding teacher features. The total loss combines FM, BiT‑C (input interface), and sparse alignment; gradients flow only through the student DiT and projection heads, never through teachers.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Sparse alignment** (top‑K) reduces training interference and speeds convergence but risks omitting useful layers if K is too small.
- **Adaptive weighting** via FoG‑A focuses loss on highly influential layers but may amplify noise if FoG‑A estimates are unstable.
- **Probe‑then‑intervene** avoids non‑stationarity but requires an extra warm‑up epoch and may become outdated if training shifts distribution.
- **Unified speech/audio tokenization** shares a single DiT vocabulary but must handle interleaved BEATs tokens and differing token sets (S3 vs AudioSet), adding tokenization complexity.

**Failure signatures:**
- **FoG‑A mismatch:** If the top‑ablation layers are not predominantly early (e.g., layers 1‑3), the FoG‑A implementation is likely incorrect (confusion with gradient norms).
- **Protocol violation:** Recomputing FoG‑A scores during Phase II or allowing the selected set \(S\) to change leads to unstable training and degraded metrics.
- **Tokenization crash:** Mixed token types without proper vocabulary mapping or sequence length batching cause runtime errors or degenerate outputs.
- **Over‑alignment:** Aggressive alignment weights (λ) or including too many layers can suppress the student’s generative freedom and harm FAD.

**First three experiments:**
1. **FoG‑A sanity check:** For a trained DiT, manually ablate each layer and record the velocity change; verify that early layers yield the largest FoG‑A scores.
2. **SCD demonstration:** On a small validation set, compute both LASP and FoG‑A rankings; plot their Spearman correlation. A low correlation (<0.5) confirms Store‑Contribute Dissociation.
3. **Mini‑training validation:** Train two models for 1k steps on a subset (e.g., 1 k LibriSpeech samples): baseline (no alignment) vs AG‑REPA (FoG‑A top‑2). Compare FAD and convergence speed; AG‑REPA should show lower FAD and faster loss decrease.

## Limitations
- **Hyperparameter gaps:** Exact LLM variant, style injection strength, dropout, DiT depth/width, and adaLN details are not disclosed, hindering exact replication.
- **FoG‑A protocol incompleteness:** The probe phase lacks specifics on batch size, number of samples for the expectation, stability constant ϵ, and whether velocity predictions are averaged over diffusion timesteps.
- **Generalization concerns:** Results may be tied to Whisper/BEATs as frozen teachers; other encoder choices or end‑to‑end training could behave differently. Tokenization ambiguities (interleaving ratio, shared vocabulary) also limit broader applicability.

## Confidence
- Store‑Contribute Dissociation (SCD): Medium  
- FoG‑A as causal attribution: Medium  
- AG‑REPA performance gains: High  

## Next Checks
1. **Stability of FoG‑A rankings:** Compute FoG‑A scores on multiple random validation subsets (e.g., 5 seeds) and report mean Spearman rank correlation; high correlation (>0.9) indicates robust attribution.
2. **Ablation over top‑K and weighting:** Train AG‑REPA with varying K (1,2,4,8,all) and with uniform vs FoG‑A‑derived λ; plot FAD, WER, and convergence steps to identify optimal K and quantify adaptive weighting benefits.
3. **Alternative attribution comparison:** Replace FoG‑A with gradient‑based selection (\(\|\partial v/\partial h_k\|\)) or LASP‑based selection, train corresponding AG‑REPA branches, and compare performance to the FoG‑A version to validate the forward ablation design.
