# Fair Decisions from Calibrated Scores: Achieving Optimal Classification While Satisfying Sufficiency

## Quick Facts
* **arXiv ID**: 2602.07285
* **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07285
* **Reference count**: 35
* **Primary result**: The paper provides a geometric characterization of the set of (positive predictive value, false omission rate) pairs achievable by classifiers based on finite-valued, group-calibrated scores. This yields a post-processing algorithm that computes the sufficiency-optimal classifier for any loss function. On COMPAS, the fair optimal classifier achieves 0.677 accuracy (vs. unconstrained 0.681) using group-specific thresholds.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles optimal binary classification under the fairness constraint of sufficiency (predictive parity) when predictions rely on finite-valued, group-calibrated scores. It introduces a geometric characterization of the feasible (PPV, FOR) region for each group, proving it is star-convex and its boundary can be traced via a soft thresholding rule. Under sufficiency, the overall feasible set is the intersection of the two group-wise regions. Because many losses (e.g., 0‑1) are monotonic in PPV for fixed selection rate, the optimal classifier lies on the boundary of this intersection. The authors present a simple post‑processing algorithm that computes this optimum without retraining models. Additionally, they consider the problem of minimizing deviation from separation (equalized odds) while meeting sufficiency, showing that the solution often coincides with the loss‑optimal fair classifier. Empirical results on COMPAS demonstrate almost no accuracy loss: the fair optimal classifier attains 0.677 vs. an unconstrained 0.681, using thresholds S≥7 for Caucasians and S≥6 for non‑Caucasians.

## Method Summary
The method operates in a post‑processing setting: given a finite‑valued score S and a binary protected attribute A, the classifier R is a randomized function of (S, A) only (i.e., R ⊥ Y | S, A). The score is assumed group‑calibrated: P(Y=1 | S=s, A=a)=s. For each group a, the classifier’s behavior is determined by selection probabilities µ_i^a = P(R=1 | S=s_i, A=a). The authors show that the set of achievable (PPV, FOR) pairs for group a is star‑convex and parameterized by the overall selection rate µ via a greedy soft‑threshold rule: sorting scores descending, for a given µ the rule selects a prefix with probability 1 and randomizes on the next score bin to exactly achieve µ. This yields a piecewise hyperbolic boundary q_a(p). Under sufficiency (equal PPV and FOR across groups), the feasible region is the intersection C = C_0 ∩ C_1. For losses of the form L = πℓ₀₁ + µℓ₁₀ – µP(ℓ₀₁+ℓ₁₀) (covering 0‑1 loss), fixing µ makes L decrease linearly with P, so the minimum over C occurs at the maximal feasible P for that µ – i.e., on the upper boundary of C. The core algorithm (Algorithm 1) constructs this boundary by merging the per‑group boundaries, partitions the P‑axis into intervals where a particular group’s boundary is active, and evaluates the objective (loss or separation deviation) on each piece. The global optimum is found by checking interval endpoints and interior critical points. Finally, the optimal (P, Q) pair maps back to group‑specific selection rates via µ_a = (π_a – Q)/(P – Q), and the soft‑threshold rule reconstructs the classifier.

## Key Results
* Feasible (PPV, FOR) pairs for a single group form a star‑convex set; its nontrivial boundary is a piecewise curve obtainable via a soft threshold rule.
* The sufficiency‑optimal classifier for any loss with ℓ₀₁+ℓ₁₀ > 0 lies on the boundary of the intersection of the two group feasible sets; a post‑processing algorithm computes it exactly.
* On the COMPAS dataset, the optimal fair classifier yields 0.677 accuracy (vs. 0.681 unconstrained) with thresholds S≥7 (Caucasian) and S≥6 (non‑Caucasian).
* The classifier minimizing deviation from separation while satisfying sufficiency often coincides with or has very similar performance to the loss‑optimal fair classifier.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Star‑Convex Feasible Region per Group
**Claim**: The set of achievable (PPV, FOR) pairs for a single group is star‑convex with center (π, π), where π is the group’s base rate. Its nontrivial boundary is a piecewise curve parameterized by selection rate µ, traceable via a soft threshold rule.  
**Mechanism**: For a fixed selection rate µ, maximizing PPV subject to calibration is a fractional knapsack problem: the greedy solution selects scores in descending order until the cumulative probability reaches µ; the highest PPV is achieved by taking all scores above a threshold s_k with probability 1 and randomizing on s_k to exactly achieve µ. This yields a hyperbolic segment between (p_{k‑1}, q_{k‑1}) and (p_k, q_k), where p_k and q_k are the average scores of the selected and rejected populations respectively. Eliminating µ using the base‑rate constraint π = µP + (1‑µ)Q gives the boundary curve Q = q(p). Star‑convexity follows because any convex combination of a feasible (P, Q) with (π, π) is feasible.  
**Core assumption**: Scores are finite‑valued and group‑calibrated; the classifier is non‑constant (0 < µ < 1); feasible pairs satisfy Q ≤ π ≤ P.  
**Evidence anchors**: The abstract states the geometric characterization; Section 3.1 derives the convexity at each µ and the soft threshold rule (Theorem 3.3); the fractional knapsack argument appears in Section 3.1 and proofs.  
**Break condition**: If scores are not finite or not group‑calibrated, the knapsack reduction fails. The star‑convex shape relies on linearity of selection probabilities (Eq. 4), which may not hold for continuous full‑support scores without discretization.

### Mechanism 2: Sufficiency‑Optimal Classifier Lies on Intersection Boundary
**Claim**: The loss‑minimizing classifier under sufficiency (predictive parity) lies on the boundary of the intersection C = C_0 ∩ C_1 of the two group feasible regions. For losses with ℓ₀₁+ℓ₁₀ > 0 (e.g., 0‑1 loss), minimization reduces to maximizing PPV for fixed µ, hence the optimum is on the upper boundary.  
**Mechanism**: The expected loss for a (P, Q) representation is L = πℓ₀₁ + µℓ₁₀ – µP(ℓ₀₁+ℓ₁₀), where µ = (π – Q)/(P – Q). For fixed µ, L is linear decreasing in P; thus for that µ the minimal L is attained at the maximal feasible P. The intersection C is star‑convex, and its upper boundary is piecewise defined by the active group’s boundary q_a(p). Therefore, the global loss‑optimal point must lie on this upper boundary. Algorithm 1 traces this boundary by considering all candidate segments where either C_0 or C_1 dictates the constraint, and optionally a vertical segment at the maximal P.  
**Core assumption**: Sufficiency imposes equal PPV and FOR across groups; loss function has ℓ₀₀=ℓ₁₁=0 and ℓ₀₁+ℓ₁₀ > 0; the group feasible regions C_0, C_1 are known and intersect non‑trivially.  
**Evidence anchors**: The abstract mentions the post‑processing algorithm; Section 5 derives equation (12) and argues that for fixed µ, minimizing loss is equivalent to maximizing P; Appendix F details the boundary construction.  
**Break condition**: If ℓ₀₁+ℓ₁₀ ≤ 0, the loss direction flips and the optimum may lie on the lower boundary. If the intersection is empty, no non‑constant classifier satisfies sufficiency.

### Mechanism 3: Minimizing Deviation from Separation
**Claim**: Among classifiers satisfying sufficiency, the deviation from separation ∆sep(R) is minimized on the boundary of C_0 ∩ C_1, and this minimization often yields a classifier with performance close to the loss‑optimal one.  
**Mechanism**: The separation deviation ∆sep can be expressed as K·( (1‑µ)/(p‑π) – µ(p‑π)/(π(1‑π)) ), where K depends on the group base rates. For fixed µ, the derivative w.r.t. p is ≤ 0, so ∆sep decreases as p increases. Hence the minimum for a given µ occurs at the maximal feasible p, i.e., on the upper boundary of the intersection. Algorithm 1 can evaluate ∆sep(p) along each boundary segment and locate the minimal point by solving a quadratic. Empirically, the optimal (p,q) for ∆sep often lies near the loss‑optimal point because both objectives increase as p decreases.  
**Core assumption**: Sufficiency holds; separation measured by total variation of TPR/FPR across groups; group base rates are unequal (π₀ ≠ π₁).  
**Evidence anchors**: The abstract states that the method identifies the classifier minimizing deviation from separation; Section 6 provides equation (14) and the derivative sign; the COMPAS example shows the two classifiers coincide.  
**Break condition**: If π₀ = π₁, then K=0 and ∆sep ≡ 0 for all sufficient classifiers, making the problem trivial. The derivation assumes unequal base rates.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept: Finite‑valued group‑calibrated scores**  
*Why needed here*: The entire geometric construction assumes scores take finitely many values and satisfy P(Y=1 | S=s, A=a)=s. This enables partitioning score space into bins and applying a fractional knapsack argument.  
*Quick check*: If scores are continuous with full support, does the paper's piecewise boundary construction still apply directly? (Answer: No; the paper notes that continuous full support is considered in other works like Baumann et al., 2022; here they require finite values.)

**Concept: Markov decision process and post‑processing**  
*Why needed*: The classifier R is restricted to depend only on S and A (i.e., R ⊥ Y | S, A). This structural constraint makes the group‑wise analysis separable and reduces the design problem to selecting P(R=1 | S=s, A=a) for each score bin.  
*Quick check*: What would break if R could also depend directly on Y during training? (Answer: This would be a different model; the post‑processing approach only observes S and A at decision time, so the Markov property is essential.)

**Concept: Sufficiency (predictive parity) vs. separation (equalized odds)**  
*Why needed*: The paper’s primary goal is optimization under sufficiency. The known incompatibility between sufficiency and separation motivates the secondary objective of minimizing deviation from separation while still meeting sufficiency.  
*Quick check*: Why does thresholding a group‑calibrated score generally violate sufficiency? (Answer: Because the resulting PPV becomes a weighted average of scores above the threshold, which differs across groups due to different score distributions even though each group’s score is calibrated.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Calibration data (group‑specific score distributions & calibration curves) → Per‑group boundary construction (sort scores, compute segment breakpoints) → Intersection boundary tracing (Algorithm 1, merging piecewise segments) → Objective evaluation along boundary (compute loss or ∆sep) → Optimal point selection (check endpoints & interior roots) → Mapping to selection probabilities → Final classifier with group‑specific (possibly randomized) thresholds.

**Critical path**: (1) verify group calibration and score support overlap; (2) compute per‑group feasible boundary curves; (3) trace the intersection boundary via Algorithm 1 (core step); (4) evaluate the chosen objective over the resulting pieces to find the minimum; (5) translate the optimal (PPV, FOR) back to group‑specific selection rates. Errors in step 3 directly corrupt the final classifier.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- *Deterministic vs. randomized thresholds*: The optimal solution may randomize over exactly one score bin per group. In practice, a deterministic approximation (e.g., rounding to the nearest breakpoint) may be preferred for interpretability.
- *Metric selection*: The choice between minimizing 0‑1 loss (accuracy) and minimizing separation deviation influences the selected boundary point; practitioners must align with application goals.
- *Estimation error*: The method assumes perfect knowledge of group‑calibrated score distributions. In realistic settings these are estimated from finite data; estimation errors can distort the feasible region, leading to suboptimal or even fairness‑violating classifiers.

**Failure signatures**:
- *Empty or degenerate intersection*: If group base rates are equal or score distributions do not overlap enough, C_0 ∩ C_1 may be empty (or only contain constant classifiers). The algorithm may then fail to produce a non‑trivial classifier.
- *Incorrect active group detection*: The active boundary is determined by the sign of the quadratic Φ_{k,l}(p). Numerical inaccuracies near its roots could cause the algorithm to switch groups erroneously, yielding an infeasible (PPV, FOR) pair.
- *Invalid p_max*: The maximal feasible P, p_max, bounds the effective domain. Miscomputing p_max (e.g., due to not resolving a vertical segment correctly) can cause the algorithm to miss the true optimum or generate out‑of‑range values.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. *Replicate COMPAS analysis*: Follow the paper’s data split (20% calibration, 80% evaluation) and recompute the optimal fair classifier. Verify reported accuracy 0.677 and thresholds S≥7 (Caucasian) vs. S≥6 (non‑Caucasian). Perform multiple random splits to assess stability.
2. *Synthetic calibration test*: Generate synthetic scores with known finite support and perfect group calibration (e.g., two groups with different score distributions but known conditional probabilities). Apply the algorithm and confirm that the returned classifier exactly satisfies sufficiency and achieves the theoretically optimal loss.
3. *Binning sensitivity*: For a continuous calibrated score (e.g., raw COMPAS scores), vary the number of bins (e.g., 5, 10, 20, 50). Run the algorithm for each binning and compare the resulting optimal metrics (accuracy, PPV, FOR) and thresholds. This tests whether the piecewise approximation converges and how sensitive the results are to discretization.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1: Extension to continuous score distributions**  
*Basis*: The paper assumes finite‑valued scores (abstract). The geometric construction relies on discrete ordering and piecewise hyperbolic segments.  
*Why unresolved*: Continuous distributions would require a functional characterization; the necessary integral equations and the shape of the feasible boundary are not derived.  
*Resolving evidence*: Develop a continuous analogue, perhaps using quantile functions, and prove that the boundary is described by a convex function of p.

**Open Question 2: Robustness to calibration violations**  
*Basis*: The method assumes perfect group calibration (Section 4). In practice, calibration is estimated from data.  
*Why unresolved*: No error analysis is provided; the impact of misestimated calibration curves on the fairness constraint and optimality is unknown.  
*Resolving evidence*: Derive sensitivity bounds: given an upper bound on calibration error (e.g., in total variation), bound the resulting violation of sufficiency and loss suboptimality. Possibly formulate a robust optimization variant.

**Open Question 3: Conditions for coincidence of loss‑optimal and separation‑minimizing classifiers**  
*Basis*: The paper observes that in the COMPAS example the two coincide, and more generally they “often” have similar performance (abstract, Section 7).  
*Why unresolved*: No theoretical characterization of when these two objectives align on the feasible boundary.  
*Resolving evidence*: Analyze the expressions for loss and ∆sep as functions of p on each boundary segment; derive conditions on group base rates and score distributions that force the minima to coincide. Complement with systematic simulations.

**Open Question 4: Sample complexity and statistical consistency**  
*Basis*: The algorithm operates on known conditional distributions (Section 4). The paper does not discuss estimation from finite data.  
*Why unresolved*: Without guarantees, practitioners cannot assess how much data is needed or how close the empirical optimum is to the true one.  
*Resolving evidence*: Prove uniform convergence of the empirical feasible region to the true one; derive finite‑sample bounds on the deviation of the estimated optimal (p,q); show that the induced classifier is consistent (satisfies sufficiency asymptotically and attains optimal loss).

## Limitations
- The method relies on accurate group calibration; finite‑sample estimation errors can compromise optimality and fairness guarantees.
- It requires scores to have finite support; applying it to continuous scores necessitates discretization, which may introduce approximation bias and affect the boundary.
- The intersection of group feasible sets may be empty under certain conditions (e.g., equal base rates), in which case no non‑constant classifier can satisfy sufficiency.
