# Measuring the Representational Alignment of Neural Systems in Superposition

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2604.00208  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00208  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: Standard neural alignment metrics (RSA, CKA, linear regression) conflate feature content with compression under superposition, leading to systematic deflation and potential inversion of alignment scores; raw activation-based alignment is thus unreliable.

## Executive Summary
Neural systems often represent high-dimensional features via superposition—compressed linear mixtures. This paper shows that standard representational alignment metrics (RSA, CKA, linear regression) are confounded by the degree of superposition, not just by the underlying features. The authors derive closed-form expressions demonstrating that alignment scores depend on the cross-similarity of projection matrices, causing systematic deflation even when systems encode identical features. Moreover, with partial feature overlap, alignment ordering can invert: systems with less feature overlap may appear more aligned due to differences in compression. These findings reveal that raw activation comparisons are misleading; extracting latent features (e.g., via sparse autoencoders) is necessary for true alignment measurement. The analysis is supported by theory and simulations, and connects to compressed sensing principles, showing that apparent misalignment does not necessarily imply information loss.

## Method Summary
The paper models neural representations as linear projections y = A z, where A is a projection matrix (m×n) with m < n, compressing latent features z. Alignment metrics such as RSA, CKA, and linear regression are applied to the resulting activations Y. The authors analytically derive that these metrics reduce to forms involving the Gram matrices G = AᵀA (e.g., RSA ~ cos(Gₐ, G_b)). For random independent projections, the expected alignment scales with m/n and vanishes as compression increases. Simulations with synthetic data (sparse z, random A) confirm monotonic decay of alignment with compression. For partial feature overlap, they construct block-structured projection matrices and show that alignment can invert due to compression differences. Additionally, they argue that compressed sensing guarantees recoverability of sparse features, decoupling coding from content.

## Key Results
- Standard alignment metrics conflate feature content with compression under superposition, causing systematic deflation: scores decrease monotonically with compression ratio, even for identical features.
- With partial feature overlap, alignment ordering can invert: a pair sharing fewer features may show higher alignment than a pair sharing more, due to relative compression differences.
- These findings imply that raw activation-based alignment is unreliable; extracting and comparing latent features (e.g., via sparse autoencoders) is essential for accurate representational comparison.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Superposition deflates raw alignment.** Standard metrics operate on neural activations y = A z. The representational similarity matrix (RSM) becomes M(Y) = Zᵀ G Z with G = AᵀA. Alignment scores essentially compare Gₐ and G_b. When projection matrices are independent random projections, their Gram matrices are typically dissimilar, causing low alignment even if Z is identical. The expected alignment scales as m/n for large n, vanishing as compression increases. A break condition occurs only if Aₐ = A_b (identical projections), which is unlikely across independently initialized systems.

**Mechanism 2: Inversion under partial overlap.** When systems share only a subset of features, their Gram matrices have block structures corresponding to feature support. The alignment numerator (Tr(GₐG_b)) counts overlap, but the denominator (geometric mean of Frobenius norms) grows with total compressed dimensionality and off-diagonal interference. A highly compressed system (small m) has larger off-diagonal entries, increasing Tr(GₐG_b) coincidentally even with less true overlap. Thus, a pair with less feature overlap but higher compression (smaller m) may appear more aligned than a pair with more overlap but lower compression. Inversion requires relative compression differences; if all systems share the same m/n, ordering preserves overlap.

**Mechanism 3: Misalignment does not imply lost information.** According to compressed sensing, if features z are sparse (∥z∥₀ ≤ k) and the projection A satisfies the restricted isometry property (RIP), then z can be perfectly reconstructed from y even when m ≪ n. Hence two systems may have highly dissimilar raw activations yet both perfectly encode the same sparse features. The alignment deflation reflects a coding difference, not a content difference. This decoupling breaks if sparsity is violated or m is too small relative to k and n (RIP fails), in which case genuine information loss occurs.

## Foundational Learning
- **Linear superposition (y = A z)**. This formalism defines neural representations as compressed mixtures of latent features. Without additional assumptions, z cannot be uniquely recovered from y.  
  *Quick check:* Given y = A z with m < n, can you uniquely determine z from a single y? What additional condition makes it possible? (Answer: No; sparsity and RIP enable recovery.)

- **Gram matrix (G = AᵀA) as induced metric**. The RSM M(Y) = Zᵀ G Z shows that neural similarity is measured under the inner product induced by G, not Euclidean. Non‑diagonal G indicates feature interference in neural space.  
  *Quick check:* If G is not diagonal, what does that imply about how features interfere in neural space? (Answer: Features are mixed; activations reflect correlated contributions.)

- **Compressed sensing theory**. Two conditions—sparsity of z and the restricted isometry property (RIP) of A—ensure that z can be recovered from y. The required number of measurements m scales as Ω(k log(n/k)), where k is sparsity. This theory separates coding (superposition) from information content.  
  *Quick check:* What two conditions ensure a sparse vector z can be recovered from y = A z? How does m relate to k and n? (Answer: Sparsity (∥z∥₀ ≤ k) and RIP; m must be at least on the order of k log(n/k).)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:  
  Latent feature space (z) → Projection matrices (Aₐ, A_b) → Neural activation extractor (computes Y = A Z) → Alignment metric module (RSA, CKA, linear regression) → (optional) Feature extractor (e.g., sparse autoencoder) → True feature-based alignment.

- **Critical path**: The chain Z → A → Y → alignment score. The projection A is the interference locus; different A transform the same Z into distinct Y, distorting raw alignment.

- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  *Metric choice*: Linear metrics (RSA, CKA, regression) all asymptotically reduce to ⟨Gₐ,G_b⟩_F; none is fundamentally superior under superposition.  
  *Feature extraction*: Necessary to disentangle superposition but introduces inductive biases (e.g., sparsity) and computational cost. Incomplete extraction may still confound.  
  *Dimensionality (m)*: Larger m reduces superposition deflation but increases memory/computation; the compressed sensing regime (m ≥ k log(n/k)) provides a useful bound.

- **Failure signatures**:  
  *Unexpected scaling*: Alignment decreases as model size (number of neurons) increases if larger models have less superposition—counter to intuition.  
  *Inversion*: A pair with less task similarity (e.g., different objectives) shows higher raw alignment than a more similar pair, due to compression differences.  
  *Post‑feature extraction correction*: Raw alignment is low, but alignment after sparse autoencoder disentanglement is high, indicating superposition confound.

- **First 3 experiments**:  
  1. **Controlled deflation test**: Fix latent features Z, generate independent random Aₐ, A_b, vary compression m/n, and measure RSA/CKA/regression. Expect monotonic decrease with m/n.  
  2. **Partial overlap inversion test**: Set feature supports with overlap ratio u; fix Aₐ (less compressed), vary A_b compression m_b. Find m_b where a partial‑overlap pair exceeds a full‑overlap pair in alignment.  
  3. **Feature‑recovery alignment comparison**: Train a sparse autoencoder (SAE) on concatenated activations Yₐ ∪ Y_b to extract shared feature directions. Compute alignment on SAE latents Ẑₐ, Ẑ_b; expect high alignment if underlying features are identical, contrasting with low raw alignment.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Generality of superposition**: To what extent does superposition occur across diverse neural systems, such as different architectures, brain regions, and task regimes? Empirical verification is needed.
2. **Feature extraction methods**: How can we develop reliable pipelines for extracting and aligning latent features from superposed representations? Validation against behavioral or task similarities is required.
3. **Local alignment metrics**: How does superposition affect metrics that focus on local neighborhood structure rather than global distances? A formal analysis is an open direction.
4. **Nonlinear extensions**: Do the findings extend to neural models with nonlinear response functions? The current analysis assumes linear projections; nonlinearities could alter the confound.

## Limitations
- Assumes linear projections, independent random weights, and sparse zero‑mean latents; real neural systems often violate these.
- Theoretical findings are asymptotic; finite‑sample/dimensionality effects can cause deviations from predictions.
- Inversion effect requires relative compression differences; if all systems have similar m/n, true feature overlap preserves alignment ordering.
- Compressed‑sensing recovery hinges on exact sparsity and RIP; approximate sparsity may limit decoupling of coding and content.

## Confidence
- **Conflation/deflation**: High – backed by rigorous closed‑form derivations and consistent simulations.
- **Inversion under partial overlap**: Medium‑High – supported by analytic block‑Gram matrices and empirical curves, but assumes disjoint feature supports and independent random projections.
- **Misalignment ≠ information loss**: Medium – grounded in compressed sensing theory, but neural data often violate exact sparsity and RIP, so the decoupling may be incomplete.

## Next Checks
1. **Nonlinear robustness test**: Apply kernel CKA (RBF kernel) and nonlinear RSA to the same synthetic linear‑superposition setups. If deflation persists, the confound extends beyond linear metrics; if it diminishes, linear metrics are uniquely vulnerable.
2. **Real neural data validation**: Record multi‑site neural activity (e.g., visual cortex) or compare layers of CNNs trained on identical tasks. Compute raw alignment and alignment after sparse autoencoder disentanglement. Verify whether raw alignment correlates with known functional hierarchy and whether disentanglement restores expected ordering.
3. **Sparsity‑compression sensitivity sweep**: In simulations, systematically vary sparsity k and compression ratio m/n across wide ranges. Map the parameter space where deflation saturates and where inversion occurs, identifying conditions under which raw alignment remains trustworthy.
