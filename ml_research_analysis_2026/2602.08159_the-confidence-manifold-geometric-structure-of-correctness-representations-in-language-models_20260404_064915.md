# The Confidence Manifold: Geometric Structure of Correctness Representations in Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.08159
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08159
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Language models encode factual correctness in a low-dimensional linear subspace, enabling precise internal detection and causal steering, while output confidence metrics fail to capture this signal.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether language models possess an internal awareness of factual correctness. Using a contrastive probing setup across nine models, the authors find that correctness information resides in a low-dimensional linear subspace (3–8 dimensions for discrimination, 8–12 for the overall manifold) within the residual stream. The decision boundary is essentially linear: centroid distance matches the performance of a trained probe (0.90 AUC), and adding dimensions harms accuracy. Activation steering along the learned direction changes error rates by 10.9 percentage points, confirming that the representation is causally involved in generation. In stark contrast, output‑based confidence measures such as P(True) and semantic entropy yield only 0.44–0.64 AUC. The findings demonstrate that models maintain a simple, geometric internal representation of correctness that is decoupled from their expressed confidence.

## Method Summary
The study employs a contrastive probing pipeline. For each question from TruthfulQA, paired correct and incorrect answers are fed to the model, and residual stream activations at the last token position are extracted across all layers. Partial Least Squares (PLS) regression reduces dimensionality to a subspace that maximizes covariance with the binary correctness label. In this subspace, L2‑regularized logistic regression is trained to classify correctness. Hyperparameters (optimal layer, PLS dimensions) are selected via GroupKFold cross‑validation with question IDs as groups to prevent leakage. The optimal subspaces are remarkably low‑dimensional (3–8 dimensions). To assess linear separability, centroid distance (using class means) is compared to probe performance. Causal relevance is established through activation steering: the normalized probe weight vector is added to activations at the optimal layer during generation, sweeping the coefficient α to measure effects on error rate. Baselines include output‑based methods (P(True), semantic entropy) and unsupervised features. The pipeline is validated on nine models (GPT‑2 family, LLaMA, Qwen, etc.) and cross‑dataset transfer tests are performed on SciQ, CSQA, HaluEval, and FEVER.

## Key Results
- Correctness is encoded in a low‑dimensional subspace (3–8 discriminative dimensions, 8–12 intrinsic) across all nine models.
- The optimal decision boundary is linear: centroid distance matches trained probe performance (~0.90 AUC), and adding dimensions harms accuracy.
- Activation steering along the learned direction changes error rates by **10.9 percentage points**, confirming causality.
- Internal probes achieve **0.80–0.97 AUC**, while output‑based confidence metrics yield only **0.44–0.64 AUC**, demonstrating decoupling.
- Cross‑architecture transfer retains only **54–58%** performance, indicating model‑specificity of the confidence manifold.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Correctness information is encoded in a low‑dimensional linear subspace (3–8 dimensions for discrimination) within the residual stream.
- **Mechanism:** Contrastive pairs (correct vs. incorrect answers to same question) are mapped to distinct clusters in a compressed subspace discovered via PLS regression. The separation is primarily a mean shift between class centroids.
- **Core assumption:** The model's internal representation of factual correctness forms a geometrically simple structure that can be isolated from other semantic content.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "The discriminative signal occupies 3–8 dimensions, performance degrades with additional dimensions, and no nonlinear classifier improves over linear separation."
  - [section 5.1] "Our central finding: the discriminative confidence signal occupies a low‑dimensional subspace (Table 1)... All 9 models peak at 3–8 dimensions, with performance decreasing at higher dimensions."
  - [corpus] Limited direct evidence; neighboring papers explore manifold learning generally but not this specific correctness encoding structure.
- **Break condition:** If intrinsic dimension cannot be compressed below 20D while maintaining AUC, the low‑dimensional structure claim fails.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Class separation is driven by mean shift rather than covariance differences, enabling simple centroid‑based detection without discriminative training.
- **Mechanism:** In the PLS subspace, correct and incorrect activations form approximately Gaussian clusters with similar covariance. The optimal decision boundary is the perpendicular bisector of centroids.
- **Core assumption:** The generative distribution of activations given correctness is well‑approximated by isotropic Gaussians with differing means.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Centroid distance matches trained probe performance (0.90 AUC), enabling few‑shot detection."
  - [section 5.3] "Centroid distance in PLS space matches linear probe performance... The theoretical implication: class separation is dominated by a mean shift between correct and incorrect distributions, not by covariance differences."
  - [corpus] Insufficient evidence; this geometric characterization is novel to this work.
- **Break condition:** If Mahalanobis distance or convex hull methods significantly outperform centroid distance, the mean‑shift hypothesis fails.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The learned confidence direction is causally relevant to factual accuracy, not merely correlational.
- **Mechanism:** Activation steering adds/subtracts the learned direction to residual activations at a specific layer during generation. Positive coefficients decrease error rates; negative coefficients increase them monotonically.
- **Core assumption:** Steering modifies the internal correctness representation, which then propagates to output generation through the transformer's computational graph.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Activation steering along the learned direction changes error rates by 10.9 percentage points, confirming causality."
  - [section 5.7] "Steering toward confidence (positive α) decreases [error rate] to 0.52 at α=+5... The learned direction produces a monotonic, symmetric effect."
  - [corpus] Related work on activation steering exists but this specific causal validation for correctness is novel.
- **Break condition:** If random or orthogonal directions produce comparable monotonic effects, the specificity claim fails.

## Foundational Learning
- **Linear Representation Hypothesis** – The assumption that semantic concepts can be extracted via linear operations on activations. *Why needed:* The entire approach probes for correctness using linear probes and steering directions; without linear representation, such methods would fail. *Quick check:* "What would it mean for a concept to be 'linearly represented' in neural activations, and why does this enable probing?"
- **Contrastive Learning Setup** – Using paired correct/incorrect answers to the same question to isolate the correctness signal from content variation. *Why needed:* Controls for topic and language confounds, ensuring the probe learns the correctness distinction rather than topic differences. *Quick check:* "Why can't we just mix random correct and incorrect answers from different questions when training a correctness probe?"
- **Activation Steering** – An intervention that adds a learned direction vector to activations at a specific layer during inference. *Why needed:* This provides causal evidence that the internal representation influences output correctness; correlation alone (from probe AUC) does not prove causality. *Quick check:* "What's the key difference between a probe showing high AUC and steering showing error rate changes, in terms of evidence for causality?"

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
1. **Data loader**: TruthfulQA paired dataset (question → {correct, incorrect} answers)  
2. **Feature extractor**: Forward hooks capturing residual stream at last‑token position across all layers  
3. **Dimensionality reducer**: PLS regression projecting to 3–8 dimensions maximizing label covariance  
4. **Classifier**: Logistic regression (L2, C=0.1) trained in PLS space  
5. **Evaluator**: GroupKFold CV (5 folds) grouped by question ID  
6. **Steering module**: Inference‑time intervention adding α·ŵ at optimal layer  
7. **Baseline comparators**: Output‑based methods (P(True), semantic entropy), unsupervised features  

**Critical path**  
1. Extract last‑token residual activations for all (question, answer) pairs.  
2. Perform nested CV to select optimal layer and PLS dimension (avoid leakage).  
3. Train final probe on full training set with selected hyperparameters.  
4. Evaluate test AUC; if >0.80, proceed to steering validation.  
5. Train steering direction on holdout set; sweep α ∈ [-5, +5] measuring error rate.  

**Design tradeoffs**  
- **Layer selection**: Automatic via CV, but note architecture differences (GPT‑2 peaks at final layers; instruction‑tuned at mid‑layers).  
- **Dimensionality**: Fixed 5D often works well; but sweep 1–32 to confirm low‑dimensional peak for new models.  
- **Centroid vs. probe**: Centroid needs fewer labels but requires labeled means; probe uses discriminative learning.  
- **Cross‑dataset transfer**: Full‑dimensional probes overfit; PLS projection (5D) improves generalization.  

**Failure signatures**  
- **AUC collapses (<0.60) after PLS**: Either wrong layer selected or model doesn't encode correctness in residual stream.  
- **Performance improves with >16D**: Signal not genuinely low‑dimensional; may be capturing dataset artifacts.  
- **Steering shows no monotonicity**: Wrong layer/direction, or correctness not causally represented at chosen intervention point.  
- **Cross‑dataset transfer <0.55**: Signal may be TruthfulQA‑specific rather than general correctness representation.  

**First 3 experiments**  
1. **Layer sweep**: Train probes on each layer separately (no PLS initially) to identify where correctness signal peaks. Expect: GPT‑2‑family at 100% depth, instruction‑tuned at 40–75% depth.  
2. **Dimensionality peak validation**: At optimal layer, sweep PLS dimensions 1–32. Expect clear peak at 3–8D with degradation beyond 16D.  
3. **Steering causality check**: Using holdout data, intervene with learned direction vs. 10 random directions. Expect: learned direction shows monotonic 5–15pp error rate swing; random directions show no systematic effect (p>0.5).  

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can online adaptation of steering vectors track real‑time shifts in confidence during generation?
- **Basis in paper:** The Discussion section poses: "Can online adaptation track generation‑time shifts? Characterizing these dynamics would extend our geometric framework to real‑time monitoring."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper uses fixed steering coefficients and assumes a static confidence direction; generation dynamics may involve time‑varying signals not captured by a single learned direction.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Experiments measuring steering effectiveness across multiple timesteps within a single generation, comparing fixed vs. adaptive steering vectors, and potentially probing intermediate states to see if confidence representation evolves during text production.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Does the low‑dimensional confidence manifold generalize to other types of errors beyond TruthfulQA's common misconceptions, such as long‑form hallucinations or RAG failures?
- **Basis in paper:** The Discussion states: "extending to naturalistic errors (long‑form generation, RAG failures) would broaden applicability."
- **Why unresolved:** The study focuses exclusively on TruthfulQA's paired QA format; real‑world hallucinations occur in diverse contexts (summarization, code, dialogue) where correctness signals may differ.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicating the manifold analysis on datasets with free‑form generation errors (HaluEval's summarization tasks, code hallucination benchmarks) and comparing dimensionality and separability; if the manifold differs, characterize the new structure.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Why do output‑based confidence metrics (entropy, P(True)) fail dramatically (0.44‑0.64 AUC) despite the strong internal correctness signal (0.80‑0.97 AUC)?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper empirically demonstrates this gap (Table 6) but does not explain the underlying mechanism: whether decoding discards the signal, whether output distributions are optimized for fluency over factuality, or whether the signal is only present in residual streams but not in logits.
- **Why unresolved:** Understanding this disconnect is crucial for building self‑aware models that can report accurate confidence; without knowing why, we cannot fix output calibration.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Mechanistic analysis comparing the evolution of the confidence signal from residual stream through attention blocks to unembedding; experiments measuring if adding a learned correctness direction to the output logits improves verbalized confidence; testing whether training objectives (RLHF) suppress the signal.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What explains the limited cross‑architecture transfer of confidence detection (54–58% retention) despite universal geometric patterns?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper shows cross‑architecture transfer results (e.g., GPT‑2 to Qwen2‑7B retains 54–58%) and notes universal compression patterns, but does not pinpoint whether the gap is due to parameter scale, training differences, or architectural specifics.
- **Why unresolved:** Cross‑model generalization is essential for deploying confidence detectors without model‑specific training; understanding the bottleneck could inform model design or transfer learning.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic analysis of architectural factors (depth, width, training data) on transfer; probing whether scaling laws might close the gap; investigating if fine‑tuning on a few examples can adapt confidence representations across architectures.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Is the equivalence between centroid distance and linear probes purely coincidental, or does it reflect specific statistical properties (Gaussianity, equal covariance) of correct/incorrect distributions?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper empirically finds that centroid distance matches probe performance (0.90 vs. 0.89 AUC) and claims "class separation is dominated by a mean shift," but does not verify the generative assumptions (e.g., equal covariance) that would make centroids optimal.
- **Why unresolved:** If the distributions truly are mean‑shifted Gaussians with equal covariance, then centroid‑based detection is theoretically optimal; if not, the empirical match might be approximate and fail under different conditions.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Statistical tests of distributional assumptions (multivariate normality, equality of covariance matrices) on the PLS‑reduced activations; simulations with shifted Gaussians to see if the match holds; testing centroid performance when covariances differ differentially across models.

## Limitations
- Key hyperparameters (PLS solver/tolerance, steering scaling norm basis, generation settings) not fully specified, blocking exact replication.
- Mean‑shift linear separation claim lacks comparison to Mahalanobis distance or convex hull classifiers; alternative geometries untested.
- Evaluation solely on TruthfulQA; cross‑dataset generalization unknown.

## Confidence
- **Low‑dimensional correctness subspace (3–8D):** High (robust across 9 models, clear dimensionality peak).
- **Mean‑shift linear separation:** Medium (centroid‑probe congruence shown, but alternative geometries untested).
- **Causal steering effect:** Medium (monotonic error change demonstrated, but scaling/layer specifics ambiguous).
- **Internal probes vs. output confidence superiority:** High (large, consistent AUC gap).
