# ReactMotion: Generating Reactive Listener Motions from Speaker Utterance

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.15083
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15083
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** ReactMotion achieves near-perfect appropriateness (Win(g>N)=1.000) and high generation quality (Gen@3=0.960, FID=4.760) on the new ReactMotionNet dataset, outperforming baselines.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the problem of generating reactive listener body motions in dyadic conversations. The authors introduce ReactMotionNet, a large-scale dataset with 151K (speaker, listener) pairs annotated with tiered appropriateness (Gold/Silver/Negative). They propose ReactMotion, a T5-based multimodal generative model that conditions on transcript, audio, and emotion to produce motion token sequences. Group-wise preference learning enforces a ranking of tier qualities. Evaluations using a multimodal judge network and a user study show that the model generates highly appropriate and diverse listener motions, significantly beating retrieval and LLM→T2M cascade baselines.

## Method Summary
ReactMotion uses a unified token vocabulary that extends T5’s text tokens with audio tokens (from the MiMi neural codec), motion tokens (from a T2M-GPT VQ-VAE), and special emotion tokens. The model is built on T5-base in an encoder-decoder architecture. Inputs may include any combination of modalities (text, audio, emotion) processed by the respective tokenizers and fed to the encoder. The decoder generates motion token sequences autoregressively.

Training employs group-wise preference learning: for each speaker utterance, multiple candidate motions are sampled from the Gold, Silver, and Negative tiers. Length-normalized log-likelihood scores for each tier are aggregated (log-mean-exp), and a ranking loss enforces ℓ_G > ℓ_S > ℓ_N with a margin. The overall loss combines the negative log-likelihood of a Gold motion and the ranking loss, weighted by an inverse-frequency term to counter motion distribution skew. Modality dropout (p=0.3) randomly masks one modality during training to improve robustness. Optimization uses AdamW (lr=2e-5, 100k steps, batch size 8 with gradient accumulation 2).

## Key Results
- Near-perfect appropriateness: Win(g>N)=1.000, Win(g>S)=0.797.
- High generation quality: Gen@3=0.960, FID=4.760, Diversity=4.804.
- User study: 67.8% preference over CE, 72.0% over LLM→MG-MotionLLM*.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The approach succeeds by explicitly modeling the ordinal nature of appropriateness through group-wise preference learning. The ranking loss directly optimizes the model to assign higher likelihood to more appropriate motions, bridging the gap between training objective and evaluation criterion. Modality dropout encourages the encoder to remain informative even when certain cues are missing, mirroring real-world variability. Inverse-frequency weighting prevents common motions from dominating, preserving diversity. The unified tokenization allows a single transformer to process heterogeneous inputs seamlessly while leveraging the strong language modeling capabilities of T5. Finally, the tiered dataset provides a dense supervisory signal that encourages nuanced, context-sensitive motion generation.

- **Tiered appropriateness annotations:** Labels (Gold/Silver/Negative) reflecting how suitable a motion is for a given utterance. *Why needed:* Provides an ordinal relevance signal for training. *Quick check:* Annotator agreement on tier labels; ensure Gold > Silver > Negative holds statistically.
- **VQ-VAE motion tokenization:** Discretizes continuous motion sequences into discrete tokens via a vector-quantized variational autoencoder. *Why needed:* Enables language-model-style generation and aligns with T5’s output space. *Quick check:* Reconstruct motion from tokens with low error; vocabulary coverage analysis.
- **Neural audio codec (MiMi):** Compresses audio into discrete tokens. *Why needed:* Allows audio to be fed as a sequence of tokens alongside text. *Quick check:* Audio reconstruction quality; tokenization stability across speech samples.
- **Group-wise preference learning:** A training objective that aggregates predictions over multiple candidates per group and enforces ranking. *Why needed:* Directly optimizes for the appropriateness ordering used in evaluation. *Quick check:* Monitor per-tier log-likelihoods during training; verify ℓ_G > ℓ_S > ℓ_N.
- **Multimodal transformer (T5):** Encoder-decoder transformer architecture handling multiple modalities via shared token space. *Why needed:* Offers a flexible and powerful sequence modeling backbone. *Quick check:* Modality dropout improves robustness; ablation studies of modality combinations.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Speaker utterance (transcript, audio, emotion) -> Modality tokenizers (text: T5 tokenizer, audio: MiMi codec, emotion: special tokens) -> Unified embedding layer -> T5 encoder -> T5 decoder -> Motion token sequence -> VQ-VAE decoder -> Listener motion

**Critical path:** The most sensitive pipeline is the tokenization and embedding of audio and motion tokens. Incompatibility between the MiMi codec version or VQ-VAE checkpoint will alter token sequences and degrade generation quality.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Using a single transformer simplifies training but may limit capacity for each modality.  
- Group-wise preference learning provides a stable ranking signal but introduces extra sampling overhead.  
- Modality dropout improves generalization but can slow convergence if dropout rate is too high.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Diversity near 0 indicates mode collapse; likely due to over-weighting of frequent motions or insufficient ranking pressure.  
- ℓ_S or ℓ_N exceeding ℓ_G suggests ranking loss dominance or label noise.  
- Poor judge scores (e.g., Win(G>S) < 0.85) imply a mis‑trained evaluation network or dataset mismatch.  
- High reconstruction error from motion token decoder hints at VQ‑VAE checkpoint issues.

**First three experiments:**  
1. Ablate modality dropout and observe changes in Win rates and diversity.  
2. Vary λ_rank and margin m to study their impact on the per‑tier log-likelihood ordering.  
3. Replace the T5 backbone with a larger transformer (e.g., T5‑large) and measure improvements in FID and Gen@3.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Advanced architectures:** Can diffusion models or larger transformers further improve reactive motion generation beyond the current T5 model?
- **Deeper conversational intent:** How can models incorporate multi‑turn dialogue context and explicit intent to produce more contextually precise reactions?
- **Better use of tiered annotations:** Can more sophisticated preference optimization (e.g., RLHF) close the gap to Silver/Gold references in naturalness and diversity?

## Limitations
- The ReactMotionNet dataset is not publicly released; its construction relies on proprietary tools (ChatGPT‑o1, Qwen3, GPT‑4o mini TTS, HumeAI) and undisclosed parameters, hindering exact replication.
- Critical tokenizer details (MiMi version, VQ‑VAE hyperparameters) are unspecified, leading to potential mismatches that affect token sequences and model performance.
- All reported metrics depend on the specific dataset and tokenization; without access to them, independent verification is impossible.

## Confidence
- Dataset construction quality (tiered annotations, scale): **Low**
- ReactMotion model efficacy (ranking loss, multimodal fusion): **Low**
- Generalizability to other conversational contexts: **Low**
