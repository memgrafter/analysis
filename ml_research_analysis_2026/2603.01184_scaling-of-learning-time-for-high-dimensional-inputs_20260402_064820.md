# Scaling of learning time for high dimensional inputs

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.01184
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01184
- Reference count: 17
- Primary result: Learning time in a Hebbian ICA model scales supralinearly with input dimension N: T ∝ N² / log K for asymmetric sources and T ∝ N³ / [log K]² for symmetric sources.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how the time required for a single-neuron Hebbian learning model to extract sparse independent features scales with the dimensionality of the input. By leveraging the geometry of high-dimensional spaces, the authors reduce the full N-dimensional weight dynamics to a single scalar representing the initial overlap between the random weight vector and the nearest true feature. They derive that this overlap scales as √(log K / N), where K is the number of hidden features. The resulting learning time—the number of samples needed for the overlap to reach a target threshold—scales supralinearly with N: T ∝ N² / log K for asymmetric source distributions and T ∝ N³ / [log K]² for symmetric ones. Empirical simulations confirm the asymmetric scaling. These findings highlight a fundamental limitation: as input dimension grows, learning becomes prohibitively slow due to vanishing initial gradients, with implications for both artificial and biological neural systems.

## Method Summary
The study considers an unsupervised linear generative model: an N‑dimensional whitened input x is a linear mixture of K independent sparse sources. A single neuron with weight vector w (‖w‖=1) learns a hidden feature by maximizing ⟨F(wᵀx)⟩ via a Hebbian update Δw ∝ x f(wᵀx), where f is a nonlinear function. After each sample, w is renormalized. Learning time T is defined as the number of samples required for the maximum absolute weight component (overlap with the closest true feature) to reach 0.7, averaged over 100 random initializations. The core theoretical insight reduces the high-dimensional stochastic differential equation for w to a one‑dimensional drift‑diffusion process for the overlap d. The initial distribution of d is shown to scale as √(log K / N), which heavily influences the time to reach threshold. The authors analyze the drift and diffusion coefficients for two classes of source distributions (symmetric vs. asymmetric) and obtain the scaling laws for T. Simulations for the asymmetric case (standardized χ² sources) and N ranging over decades confirm the predicted T ∝ N² / log N scaling.

## Key Results
- The initial overlap between random weights and the nearest feature scales as d₀ ∼ √(log K / N).
- For asymmetric source distributions, learning time T scales as N² / log K (empirically ∝ N² / log N).
- For symmetric source distributions, the predicted scaling is T ∝ N³ / [log K]².

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The reduction to a single variable relies on two geometric facts: (i) the weight vector’s direction in high dimensions is nearly uniformly distributed, so its inner product with any fixed feature concentrates around zero with variance 1/N; (ii) among K features, the maximum absolute inner product (the overlap) scales as √(log K / N). The Hebbian update only affects the component along the matching feature; all other components remain approximately Gaussian noise because of the central limit theorem. Consequently, the evolution of the overlap d decouples from the rest and follows a one‑dimensional stochastic process with drift μ(d) and diffusion σ(d) that depend on the source distribution through moments of f and its derivative. For asymmetric sources (non‑zero third cumulant), the drift behaves as μ ∝ d² leading to an escape time from the small‑d region that scales as 1/d₀², i.e., ∝ N / log K. Since the later deterministic growth phase adds a factor of N, the total T becomes ∝ N² / log K. For symmetric sources, the leading drift term vanishes (μ ∝ d⁴), making the noise-dominated regime longer and yielding an extra factor N / log K, thus T ∝ N³ / [log K]². The small initial d₀ therefore causes a dramatic slowdown as N grows.

## Foundational Learning
**High‑dimensional geometry**: Concentration of measure, random projection norms.  
*Why needed*: To justify that the weight vector’s components behave like independent Gaussians and to derive the √(log K / N) initial overlap scaling.  
*Quick check*: Compute max_i |w_i| for random normal w; verify its typical value scales as √(log K / N).

**Independent Component Analysis (ICA)**: Sparse linear generative model.  
*Why needed*: Defines the hidden features that the neuron aims to recover.  
*Quick check*: Generate whitened mixtures of Laplacian sources; verify statistical independence.

**Hebbian learning & nonlinearity**: Local plasticity rule Δw ∝ x f(wᵀx).  
*Why needed*: Provides the update mechanism; nonlinearity f selects for specific source distributions.  
*Quick check*: Simulate with f(u)=u² on asymmetric data and observe weight alignment.

**Stochastic differential equations (SDEs)**: Modeling weight dynamics in continuous time.  
*Why needed*: Enables analytic derivation of drift/diffusion and escape times.  
*Quick check*: Derive μ(d) and σ(d) from the update rule by averaging over data distribution.

**Renormalization**: Constraining ‖w‖=1 after each step.  
*Why needed*: Prevents trivial divergence and keeps dynamics on a sphere.  
*Quick check*: Track ‖w‖; ensure it stays at 1.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**:  
Data Generation (sparse sources -> whitened mixture x) -> Sample x -> Compute projection u = w·x -> Apply f(u) -> Compute gradient increment Δw ∝ x f(u) -> Renormalize w -> repeat. The overlap d = max_i |w_i| is monitored.

**Critical Path**:  
The overlap d is the key state variable. Its initial value d₀ (set by random projection geometry) determines the time to reach the target d_th=0.7. The drift μ(d) is small for early d, causing a bottleneck; as d grows, growth accelerates.

**Design Tradeoffs**:  
- Learning rate η: Too large induces instability; too small prolongs convergence. Authors use η = α/N to keep stepsize consistent across N.  
- Nonlinearity f: Must match source distribution to guarantee positive drift for all d; otherwise learning can stall.  
- Dimensionality N vs. number of features K: Scaling exponents depend on K via log terms; increasing K modestly slows learning but less than N.

**Failure Signatures**:  
- Overlap d never increases from its initial value (or decreases), indicating drift non‑positive at small d.  
- Convergence time vastly exceeds predicted scaling, often due to inappropriate η or f.  
- Numerical overflow if updates become too large; manifests as sudden explosion of weights.

**First Experiments**:  
1. Measure the distribution of initial max |w_i| for varying N and K; confirm √(log K / N) scaling.  
2. For the asymmetric case (χ² sources), compute average learning time T over 100 seeds for N = [50, 100, 200, 500, 1000, 2000, 5000] with η=α/N (α tuned); fit log‑log slope of T vs N—should be ≈2.  
3. Test robustness: vary α by factors of 2 up/down and observe changes in T; ensure exponent remains stable while prefactor changes.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Can the unidimensional reduction and the derived supralinear scaling laws (T ∝ N³/log²K for symmetric, T ∝ N²/logK for asymmetric) be generalized to deep neural networks with multiple layers?
2. Do the scaling laws derived for a fully connected neuron also apply to convolutional neural networks, and can they explain the practical benefits of limiting receptive field size?
3. Can the unidimensional reduction and high-dimensional scaling analysis be extended to supervised learning and reinforcement learning settings where gradients depend on target outputs or rewards?
4. Is the predicted supralinear scaling of learning time with input dimensionality a limiting factor for synaptic connectivity in biological neural networks, and can it explain the number of synapses per neuron observed in the cortex?

## Limitations
- The exact form of the nonlinearity f(u) is ambiguous (“(u−2.) +”), which may alter the drift scaling and thus the exponents.
- The learning rate schedule is unspecified; using η = α/N with constant α could affect prefactors or potentially the exponent if α is not sufficiently small.
- The analysis assumes an idealized whitened data model and infinite dimension; for moderate N the reduction to one dimension might be less accurate.

## Confidence
- Initial overlap scales as √(log K / N): **High**
- Asymmetric case scaling T ∝ N² / log K: **Medium**
- Symmetric case scaling T ∝ N³ / [log K]²: **Low**
- Reduction to single‑variable dynamics: **Medium**
