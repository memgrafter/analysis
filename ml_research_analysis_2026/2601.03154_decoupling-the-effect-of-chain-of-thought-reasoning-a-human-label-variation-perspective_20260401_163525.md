# Decoupling the Effect of Chain-of-Thought Reasoning: A Human Label Variation Perspective

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.03154
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03154
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: CoT reasoning improves distributional alignment (lower JSD) but has mixed effects on accuracy; Cross‑CoT reveals that final accuracy is overwhelmingly determined by CoT content (99% variance) while distributional ranking is governed by model priors (>80% variance).

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether chain‑of‑thought (CoT) reasoning in large language models (LLMs) enhances their ability to model human label variation (HLV)—i.e., predicting full answer distributions rather than single labels. Using the ChaosNLI dataset and a suite of reasoning‑tuned LLMs, the authors compare accuracy and Jensen‑Shannon Divergence (JSD) before and after CoT generation. Through Cross‑CoT experiments (injecting one model’s reasoning into another) and step‑wise truncation analyses, they find that CoT consistently improves distributional alignment but has mixed effects on accuracy. Critically, the content of the CoT almost entirely dictates the final top‑1 answer (99% of variance explained), whereas models’ intrinsic priors control the ranking and probability allocation among non‑top options (over 80% variance in JSD and Spearman’s ρ). Step‑wise results show CoT’s influence on accuracy builds monotonically toward the final reasoning step, while distributional structure remains stable and prior‑driven. The study concludes that long CoT effectively determines the top choice but fails to calibrate fine‑grained uncertainty for alternative options.

## Method Summary
The evaluation pipeline leverages the ChaosNLI dataset (subsets of MNLI, SNLI, αNLI) which includes human label distributions from 100 annotators. Instances are formatted as multiple‑choice QA with options A, B, C (binary for αNLI). Models are prompted to answer either directly (no reasoning) or with chain‑of‑thought; for CoT, the model first generates an intermediate reasoning chain. Output probability distributions are derived from the logits of the first token following a forced answer suffix (e.g., "\n\nBased on the reasoning so far, the Answer is: "), using the first‑token probability method (Santurkar et al., 2023). Key experiments include: (i) comparing direct vs. full CoT performance on accuracy (top answer matching human majority) and JSD against human distributions; (ii) Cross‑CoT, where CoT generated by a source model is injected into a target model to isolate reasoning effects; (iii) step‑wise analysis, where CoT is split into 10 accumulative segments and evaluated after each segment with the same suffix. Variance contributions of model identity and CoT content are quantified by two‑way ANOVA, all under greedy decoding.

## Key Results
- CoT consistently improves distributional alignment (lower JSD) but yields mixed changes in accuracy.
- Cross‑CoT shows that final accuracy is overwhelmingly driven by CoT content (99% variance contribution), while distributional ranking (JSD, Spearman’s ρ) remains governed by model priors (>80% variance).
- Step‑wise truncation reveals that CoT’s impact on accuracy grows monotonically toward the final step, whereas distributional structure is stable and prior‑driven throughout.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: CoT as Decision‑Maker
- **Claim**: The content of the generated Chain‑of‑Thought (CoT) reasoning chain overwhelmingly determines the final top‑1 answer (accuracy) in Human Label Variation (HLV) tasks.
- **Mechanism**: The reasoning steps culminate in an explicit conclusion that shifts the model’s output probability mass toward the most plausible option, overriding the model’s intrinsic prior. This is manifested by a column‑dominant pattern in Cross‑CoT heatmaps: all target models conditioned on the same CoT produce nearly identical accuracy.
- **Core assumption**: The model can condition on the provided CoT text and use it to bias the answer distribution; the CoT contains sufficient information to identify the argmax.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] “CoT serves as a decisive LLM decision‑maker for the top option”
  - [section] Section 3.3 (Cross‑CoT Evaluation) and Section 4.1 (ANOVA: CoT explains ≈99% of accuracy variance on MNLI)
  - [corpus] Neighboring work “Can Reasoning Help Large Language Models Capture Human Annotator Disagreement?” (arXiv:2506.19467) shows reasoning improves accuracy on objective questions but not distributional modeling, consistent with a split role.
- **Break condition**: Truncating the CoT before its concluding statement prevents the accuracy gain; step‑wise analysis shows a sharp inflection only at the final step.

### Mechanism 2: Prior‑Dominated Distributional Structure
- **Claim**: The model’s intrinsic parametric knowledge (prior) governs the distributional structure (ranking and probability allocation among non‑top options) and is largely unaffected by CoT.
- **Mechanism**: The base model’s logit distribution for alternative options remains stable even when the CoT is manipulated; only the top option moves. Consequently, metrics like Jensen‑Shannon Divergence (JSD) and Spearman’s ρ are determined by the model identity (row‑dominant pattern), not the CoT content.
- **Core assumption**: The model’s pretrained weights encode a relatively fixed prior distribution over answer choices that resists perturbation from surface‑level reasoning for non‑argmax options.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] “distributional ranking is governed by model priors (over 80%)”
  - [section] Section 3.3 (Cross‑CoT JSD/Spearman row‑dominance) and Section 4.1 (ANOVA: model factor >80% variance in JSD/ρ)
  - [corpus] “Understanding and Predicting Human Label Variation in Natural Language Inference through Explanation” (arXiv:2304.12443) notes that explanations help but fine‑grained distributional alignment remains challenging, aligning with limited CoT effect.
- **Break condition**: Explicit distribution‑oriented fine‑tuning could weaken this anchoring.

### Mechanism 3: Temporal Decoupling of CoT Influence
- **Claim**: CoT’s influence on accuracy increases monotonically and peaks only at the final reasoning step, whereas its influence on distributional metrics (JSD, Spearman) stays low and constant throughout.
- **Mechanism**: CoT chains typically end with a decisive conclusion (e.g., “Therefore, the answer is A”), which strongly signals the final choice. The ranking and probability mass among other options are set early and remain stable, reflecting the model’s prior. Thus, CoT acts as a late‑stage decision catalyst, not a continuous distribution calibrator.
- **Core assumption**: Step‑wise truncation reveals the temporal dynamics; the 10‑segment accumulation approximates the reasoning progression.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] “step‑wise analysis confirms CoT’s impact on accuracy grows monotonically toward the final step, whereas distributional structure is stable and prior‑driven.”
  - [section] Section 4.2, Table 3 (step‑wise ANOVA), Figure 3 (metric curves across steps)
  - [corpus] “Unveiling and Causalizing CoT” (arXiv:2502.18239) suggests CoT may be a tight constraint to imitate rather than true reasoning, explaining its limited distributional effect.
- **Break condition**: If the CoT lacks an explicit concluding statement, the accuracy inflection may disappear.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Human Label Variation (HLV)
- **Why needed here**: HLV redefines the evaluation objective from single‑label accuracy to predicting full answer distributions, which is the core setting of this work. Understanding HLV is necessary to interpret metrics like JSD and why accuracy alone is insufficient.
- **Quick check question**: In an NLI task with high human disagreement, what does a model’s probability distribution represent, and how is it evaluated against human judgments?

### Concept: Chain‑of‑Thought (CoT) Reasoning
- **Why needed here**: CoT is the primary independent variable; the paper investigates its effect on HLV modeling. One must grasp how CoT generation (reason‑then‑answer) works and how it can be manipulated (injected, truncated).
- **Quick check question**: How does the explicit generation of intermediate reasoning steps potentially influence the final answer distribution of a language model?

### Concept: Jensen‑Shannon Divergence (JSD)
- **Why needed here**: JSD is the key metric for distributional alignment between model predictions and human label distributions. It quantifies the difference between two probability distributions, essential for evaluating HLV performance.
- **Quick check question**: What property of JSD makes it suitable for comparing two discrete probability distributions, and how does it differ from KL divergence?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:
  - Dataset loader: Loads ChaosNLI subsets (MNLI, SNLI, αNLI) and human judgment distributions.
  - Prompt formatter: Converts NLI instances into multiple‑choice QA format with options A, B, C.
  - Model interface: Loads reasoning‑tuned LLMs (e.g., Qwen, R1‑Llama) and either generates CoT internally or accepts externally injected CoT.
  - Logit extractor: Captures pre‑softmax logits for option tokens at the first token position after the answer prompt.
  - Probability normalizer: Applies sum‑normalization (not softmax) to preserve entropy and reflect intrinsic model preferences.
  - Metric calculator: Computes accuracy (argmax match), JSD, and Spearman’s ρ.
  - Cross‑CoT manager: Swaps CoT texts between source and target models; ensures the target model conditions on foreign reasoning.
  - Step‑wise segmenter: Splits CoT into 10 cumulative segments (using sentence‑aware heuristics), appends a stopping token (“...Answer is: ”) to extract intermediate distributions.
  - Variance decomposition module: Performs two‑way ANOVA (no interaction) to quantify contributions of model, CoT, and residual factors for each metric and step.

- **Critical path**:  
  Load instance → format prompt → (generate or inject) CoT → (optional) segment & stop → extract first‑token logits → normalize → compute metrics → aggregate → (for Cross‑CoT) repeat across model pairs → (for step‑wise) iterate over segments → ANOVA.

- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - First‑token probability is efficient but may miss late‑token effects; chosen for consistency with prior work.
  - Sum‑normalization preserves native entropy, avoiding softmax temperature bias, but assumes logits are comparable.
  - Early‑stopping prompt must be carefully crafted; if ignored, intermediate distributions become unreliable.
  - CoT segmentation balances natural boundaries (sentence ends) with equal‑length splits; imperfect alignment may blur step boundaries.
  - Computational cost: step‑wise evaluation requires 11 forward passes per instance (~20 h on two A100 GPUs per experiment).

- **Failure signatures**:
  - CoT injection fails if model continues past the stop token, breaking first‑token extraction.
  - Segmentation cuts through a logical step, producing unnatural intermediate answers.
  - High variance in JSD across models may indicate dataset imbalance or annotation noise.
  - Missing clear concluding statement in CoT weakens the accuracy spike at final step.
  - ANOVA assumptions (additivity, normality) might be violated; interpret contributions cautiously.

- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Baseline CoT effect**: For each model, compare accuracy and JSD before reasoning (direct answer) vs. after full CoT reasoning.
  2. **Cross‑CoT disentanglement**: For every source‑target model pair, inject source CoT into target model and measure final accuracy and JSD; observe column‑dominant accuracy vs. row‑dominant JSD patterns.
  3. **Step‑wise analysis**: Truncate CoT at 10 incremental segments, compute metrics at each step, and run ANOVA to quantify how CoT influence evolves temporally for each metric.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How can we annotate intermediate reasoning steps to provide gold‑standard distributions for HLV evaluation?
- **Basis in paper**: The authors note that ChaosNLI lacks annotated intermediate reasoning steps and suggest that future work should collect human annotations of reasoning chains and intermediate answers.
- **Why unresolved**: Without step‑wise gold distributions, we cannot directly measure how well the evolution of a model’s reasoning aligns with the dynamic human uncertainty.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Creation of a benchmark with human‑written CoT and corresponding per‑step answer distributions; or development of validated automatic metrics that reliably proxy step‑wise HLV alignment.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: What textual features of CoT explanations correlate with improved distributional alignment (e.g., lower JSD)?
- **Basis in paper**: The paper acknowledges the absence of direct human evaluation of CoT textual content and highlights the need for such evaluation to connect reasoning metrics with human judgment.
- **Why unresolved**: It remains unclear whether reductions in JSD stem from higher‑quality reasoning or simply from better output manipulation; linking CoT quality to HLV metrics requires human assessment.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Human studies rating CoT texts for coherence, relevance, and explicit uncertainty expression, with correlations computed against HLV metrics across diverse models and datasets.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Why do model priors dominate the non‑argmax probability structure and how can we design training or inference methods to reduce this reliance?
- **Basis in paper**: The study finds that over 80% of variance in JSD and Spearman’s ρ is attributable to model identity rather than CoT content, indicating a strong prior effect on distributional ranking.
- **Why unresolved**: Understanding the mechanisms (e.g., logit space initialization, softmax biases) that lock non‑argmax probabilities to priors is essential for building models that truly calibrate uncertainty via reasoning.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Controlled experiments altering model architecture (e.g., explicit probability heads), incorporating distributional fine‑tuning objectives, or using prompting strategies that enforce probability re‑allocation, followed by variance decomposition showing increased CoT contribution.

## Limitations
- Reliance on first‑token logits and a forced answer suffix introduces artificial constraints that may affect distribution extraction.
- Experiments exclusively use reasoning‑tuned LLMs, limiting generalizability to other model families.
- Scope is confined to NLI tasks; the decoupling pattern may not hold for other HLV domains (e.g., subjective QA, content moderation).

## Confidence
- **Claim 1**: CoT content determines final accuracy – *High confidence* (supported by Cross‑CoT column dominance and monotonic step‑wise inflection, >99% variance explained).
- **Claim 2**: Model priors govern distributional alignment (JSD, Spearman’s ρ) – *High confidence* (ANOVA attributes >80% variance to model identity; consistent row‑dominant patterns).
- **Claim 3**: CoT’s impact diverges temporally – *High confidence* (step‑wise ANOVA shows accuracy gains increase while distributional metrics remain stable).

## Next Checks
1. **Broaden model coverage**: Replicate the study with a diverse set of LLMs (e.g., GPT‑4, Llama variants, non‑reasoning‑tuned models) to test the robustness of the CoT/prior split across architectures and training regimes.
2. **Robustify answer extraction**: Replace the first‑token method with alternatives such as multi‑sample aggregation or full logit sequence analysis to rule out artefacts from the current extraction protocol.
3. **Cross‑task validation**: Apply the same Cross‑CoT and step‑wise design to other HLV tasks (e.g., subjective QA, content moderation, political stance prediction) to determine whether the observed decoupling is task‑general.
