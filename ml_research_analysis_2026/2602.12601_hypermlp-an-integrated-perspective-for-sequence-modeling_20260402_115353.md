# HyperMLP: An Integrated Perspective for Sequence Modeling
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.12601  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12601  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Recasts autoregressive attention as a dynamic two-layer MLP; HyperMLP/HyperGLU match or exceed softmax-attention Transformers under equal parameter budgets, with +14 MAD avg points from sequence mixing and top Open LLM leaderboard ranks at 1.3B scale.

## Executive Summary
This paper challenges the necessity of softmax normalization in self-attention by reformulating autoregressive attention as a dynamic two-layer MLP whose weights are instantiated from context history. The proposed HyperMLP and HyperGLU architectures integrate learnable feature and sequence mixing using a reverse-offset (lag) layout and low-rank factorization. Theoretical analysis shows HyperMLP’s function class strictly contains token-wise ReLU attention. Under matched parameter budgets, HyperMLP/HyperGLP consistently outperform softmax-attention Transformers across language modeling and mechanistic tasks. Controlled studies attribute most gains to sequence mixing (+14 MAD avg points) and earlier low-loss convergence. Scale-up evaluations on 1.3B models show HyperGLU achieving the best Open LLM Leaderboard rank and top scores on 9/18 benchmarks, including MMLU-P and MATH.

## Method Summary
HyperMLP recasts attention as a dynamic two-layer MLP: for a context \(X = [x_t; \dots; x_1]\), the first layer computes a length-\(t\) pre-activation vector \(h_t = x_t L^{(1)}(x_t) X^\top R^{(1)}(x_t)\), where \(L\) mixes features and \(R\) mixes the sequence dimension. The second layer applies an affine-free normalization \(L2Norm_t\) followed by ReLU to gate active slots, then reads out \(o_t = \sigma_t(h_t) R^{(2)\top}(x_t) X L^{(2)\top}(x_t)\). The sequence mixing matrices \(R\) are parameterized as diagonal-plus-low-rank (DPLR) with a reverse-offset (lag) layout to ensure autoregressive truncation consistency. HyperGLU decouples routing from magnitude modulation by splitting the first-layer rank into two branches: one for gating (ReLU after \(L2Norm\)) and one for scaling (Softplus), yielding consistent gains. Parameter budgets are rebalanced by compressing the first-layer (QK) rank to afford second-layer (VO) and sequence mixing.

## Key Results
- Under equal parameter budgets, HyperMLP/HyperGLU outperform softmax-attention Transformers on all evaluated tasks, including NanoGPT (loss 2.9865 vs 3.0466) and MAD average (81.12 vs 80.50).
- Sequence mixing alone contributes +14 MAD average points and accelerates convergence.
- At 1.3B scale, HyperGLU achieves the best overall rank on the Open LLM Leaderboard and tops 9/18 benchmarks (e.g., MMLU-P 0.120, MATH 0.012).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Autoregressive attention can be viewed as a dynamic two-layer MLP whose weights are instantiated from the context history; the “attention scores” form an ever‑growing hidden representation, and standard MLP activations naturally implement input‑conditioned selection over a context‑dependent memory pool.
- **Mechanism:** For a context \(X = [x_t; \dots; x_1]\), define \(h_t = x_t L^{(1)}(x_t) X^\top R^{(1)}(x_t)\) (length‑\(t\) pre‑activation) and \(o_t = \mathrm{ReLU}(L2Norm_t(h_t)) R^{(2)\top}(x_t) X L^{(2)\top}(x_t)\). The \(R\) matrices mix the sequence dimension, while \(L\) mixes features; \(h_t\) has width \(t\) and is a vector of “scores” that gate which memory slots are active.
- **Core assumption:** \(L2Norm_t\) is an affine‑free positive scalar rescaling across length \(t\) so that \(\mathrm{ReLU}(L2Norm_t(z))\) preserves sign patterns (gating) and only rescales magnitudes.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] explicitly recasts attention as a dynamic two‑layer MLP with context‑instantiated weights.  
  [section 2.2] derives the effective matrices \(W_{1t} = L^{(1)}(x_t) X^\top R^{(1)}(x_t)\) and \(W_{2t} = R^{(2)\top}(x_t) X L^{(2)\top}(x_t)\) and shows \(o_t = \sigma(x_t W_{1t}) W_{2t}\).  
  [corpus] Only distant analogies (e.g., Mamba as fast‑weight programmer); no direct evidence for this exact formulation.
- **Break condition:** If normalization introduces elementwise gain/bias or otherwise changes sign patterns, the gate set \(\{ h_t > 0 \}\) can change arbitrarily and the selection mechanism breaks.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Learning sequence‑space mixing (\(R^{(1)}(x), R^{(2)}(x)\)) constructs context‑wide slots as linear combinations of all tokens, and using a reverse‑offset (lag) layout aligns the canonical prefix‑extension with autoregressive truncation semantics.
- **Mechanism:** Instead of tying each slot to a fixed token row \(X[i,:]\), the \(R\) matrices mix the context: slot \(u_i = R^{(1)\top}(X)[i,:] X\) (a weighted sum over all tokens). The lag layout means extending the history appends rows at the *end* of \(X_{t:1}\); under extension consistency \(R_T(X) = P_{t\to T} R_t(X) P^\top_{t\to T}\), adding past tokens does not affect recent computations.
- **Core assumption:** The temporal mixing operators are parameterized in a prefix‑extension‑consistent way (e.g., DPLR with fixed \(A, B\) matrices sliced in lag order) and normalization satisfies padding invariance \(\rho_T([z,0]) = \rho_t(z)\).
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [section 2.4] introduces HyperMLP with DPLR \(R\) and lag layout.  
  [Theorem 2.3] proves that under extension consistency and padding invariance, \(o_T(X_{T:1}) = o_t(X_{t:1})\) for the lag order (autoregressive truncation), while forward order would keep the *oldest* \(t\) tokens instead.  
  [section 3.a] ablations show that removing lag (non‑offset) collapses performance on MAD and NanoGPT (e.g., `R-12!` vs `R-12o!` gap huge), confirming the necessity of the offset layout.
- **Break condition:** If sequence mixing is not extension‑consistent (e.g., different parameterization for different lengths) or the layout is not lag‑ordered, longer contexts can change gate boundaries for recent tokens and break AR invariance.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** HyperGLU decouples routing (which slots are active) from magnitude modulation (how strongly they contribute) by using two independent first‑layer score vectors, yielding consistent gains over plain ReLU‑based mixing.
- **Mechanism:** Split the first‑layer rank into two halves: compute \(h_{\text{gate}}\) and \(h_{\text{scale}}\). Activation is \(a_t = \mathrm{Softplus}(h_{\text{scale},t}) \odot \mathrm{ReLU}(L2Norm_t(h_{\text{gate},t}))\). Routing depends only on \(\{ h_{\text{gate},t} > 0 \}\) (L2Norm preserves sign), while \(\mathrm{Softplus}(h_{\text{scale},t}) > 0\) provides a separate positive multiplier for each active slot. In measure terms, the output becomes \(o_t \propto \sum \alpha^+ \beta \cdot \mathrm{Softplus}(h_{\text{scale}})\).
- **Core assumption:** The L2Norm does not change sign patterns and the two branches share the same feature/sequence mixers but have separate linear projections (split rank), so the gate branch only determines active set and the scale branch only modulates magnitudes.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] states “HyperGLU decoupling routing and magnitude modulation for further gains.”  
  [Proposition 2.4] formally shows the gate branch sets the active set while the scale branch modulates slot strength.  
  [section 3.a] Table 1 shows HyperGLU (e.g., `G-cg-q-12o`) beats HyperMLP (`R-cg-q-12o`) on both MAD avg (81.12 vs 81.01) and NanoGPT loss (2.9865 vs 2.9956), confirming the benefit of decoupling.
- **Break condition:** If the scale branch influences the sign of the pre‑activation (e.g., through nonlinear interactions or bias), the active set may change; if the gate branch is altered by the scale branch’s output, decoupling is lost.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Dynamic two‑layer MLP abstraction**  
  - **Why needed here:** It is the core reframing that unifies attention variants and enables the parameter analysis (Theorem 2.5) and measure view. Without viewing attention as a dynamic MLP, one cannot see the input‑conditioned sub‑network selection.  
  - **Quick check question:** In the dynamic‑MLP view, what is the width of the hidden layer (score vector) at context length \(t\)?  
    **Answer:** \(t\), exactly the sequence length.

- **Concept: Three‑stage memory (Global → Pool → Activated)**  
  - **Why needed here:** It explains how the block uses context: parameters define an addressing/readout dictionary (Global), the context instantiates a finite atomic pool (Pool), and ReLU gates to an active subpool (Activated). This clarifies why longer contexts enlarge candidate slots and how routing works.  
  - **Quick check question:** What are the three stages and what does each represent?  
    **Answer:** Global (parameter‑defined dictionary over \(\Omega\)), Context‑instantiated Pool (atoms from current history), Current‑step Activated Memory (pool restricted to \(\alpha(x_t;u)>0\)).

- **Concept: NTK/lazy regime and gate stability**  
  - **Why needed here:** It predicts when routing (active set) remains stable during training. Under a margin \(\min_j |(h_t)_j| \geq \delta\) and small parameter changes, the gate pattern is unchanged (Lemma D.11). This helps interpret why HyperMLP can converge to better minima without catastrophic routing changes.  
  - **Quick check question:** Under what condition does the activated set stay fixed despite parameter updates?  
    **Answer:** If the pre‑activation vector \(h_t\) changes by less than its minimum margin \(\delta\) at all coordinates.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  \([x_t, X_{t:1}] \rightarrow L^{(1)}(x_t) \rightarrow z = x_t L^{(1)} X^\top \rightarrow R^{(1)}(x_t) \rightarrow h \rightarrow \sigma = \mathrm{ReLU}(L2Norm(h)) \rightarrow w \rightarrow R^{(2)\top}(x_t) \rightarrow w \rightarrow X L^{(2)\top} \rightarrow o_t\). State: KV cache stores \(X\); per‑head gate vectors \(s^{(1)}, s^{(2)} \in \mathbb{R}^{rs}\).

- **Critical path:**  
  1. Compute \(z = x_t L^{(1)} X^\top\) (QK projections + context dot product).  
  2. Apply \(R^{(1)}\): \(h = z R^{(1)}(x_t)\) using the DPLR contraction identity (no \(t\times t\) materialization).  
  3. Activate: \(w = \mathrm{ReLU}(L2Norm_t(h))\).  
  4. Apply \(R^{(2)}\): \(w = w R^{(2)\top}(x_t)\) via second DPLR contraction.  
  5. Read out: \(o = w X L^{(2)\top}\).  
  Fusion: Fuse the \(R\) contractions and elementwise ops into a single kernel per sequence‑mixing stage.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - **Parameter budget:** Shrink QK rank (\(d_{qk}\)) to pay for sequence mixing while keeping VO rank (\(d_{vo}\)) large (Theorem 2.5). Compressing \(d_{vo}\) directly restricts the update subspace.  
  - **Sequence rank \(rs\):** Larger \(rs\) increases context‑wide slot flexibility (warped routing) but adds \(O(T^2 rs)\) train overhead and \(O(rs)\) state. \(rs=16\) default.  
  - **Number of heads \(nhead\):** Higher \(nhead\) increases parameter count and complexity; fix \(nhead=2\) to contain overhead while keeping sufficient capacity.  
  - **Lag layout:** Mandatory for AR truncation invariance; forward order breaks it (Theorem 2.3).

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - **Non‑offset temporal mixing:** Performance collapses on long‑context tasks (Table 1: `R-12!` vs `R-12o!` huge losses).  
  - **Omitting sequence mixing (\(rs=0\)):** Gains disappear; MAD avg drops ~14 points.  
  - **Aggressive QK compression or \(rs\) too large:** Either routing capacity vanishes or overhead dominates; both degrade efficiency and accuracy.  
  - **L2Norm with per‑coordinate gain/bias:** Gate pattern changes, breaking the selection mechanism.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Controlled ablation on MAD & NanoGPT:** Vary one factor at a time (activation, feature mods, compression side, temporal mixing, lag layout) to isolate each mechanism’s contribution. Check that (i) sequence mixing (+14 MAD avg), (ii) lag layout (restores performance), and (iii) HyperGLU (best loss) hold.  
  2. **Extension consistency check:** Train with variable context lengths and verify that extending history with past tokens (lag order) does not change recent token outputs (Theorem F.1). Compare against forward‑order extension; the latter should show drift.  
  3. **Parameter budget compliance:** Ensure matched‑budget HyperMLP/HyperGLU have the same total parameter count as their softmax/ReLU baselines; otherwise differences may be due to scale.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1:** Does HyperGLU retain its advantage at frontier model scales (>10B)?  
  Basis: Authors cite resource constraints and defer scaling validation.

- **Question 2:** Can HyperMLP achieve FlashAttention-level efficiency with kernel optimization?  
  Basis: Current implementation explicitly cannot match FlashAttention.

- **Question 3:** How does sequence mixing rank \(rs\) affect the expressivity–compute trade-off?  
  Basis: \(rs=16\) fixed; no systematic study of its effect.

## Limitations
- Exact data preprocessing and train/validation splits for OpenWebText2/FineWeb-Edu are unspecified.  
- DPLR matrix initialization details (e.g., \(d\) near zero?) are lacking, affecting early training stability.  
- Fused kernel implementation specifics (offset layout, chunk size) are not described, hindering efficiency replication.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (dynamic two‑layer MLP view):** High – supported by formal derivation (Theorem 2.2) and algebraic equivalence.  
- **Mechanism 2 (lag layout & extension consistency):** High – backed by Theorem 2.3 and strong ablative evidence (Table 1).  
- **Mechanism 3 (HyperGLU decoupling):** Medium – Proposition 2.4 and Table 1 gains are suggestive but rely on precise rank splitting; further validation needed.  
- **Large‑scale benchmark results:** Medium – promising but untested under exact resource and data conditions.
