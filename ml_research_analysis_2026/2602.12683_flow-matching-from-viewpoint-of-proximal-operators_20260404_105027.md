# Flow Matching from Viewpoint of Proximal Operators

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.12683
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12683
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: OT-CFM admits exact formulation via extended Aleksandrov–Brenier potential and proximal operators, yielding explicit vector field and terminal normal hyperbolicity on low-dimensional manifolds.

## Executive Summary
This paper delivers a rigorous theoretical analysis of Optimal Transport Conditional Flow Matching (OT-CFM). The central insight is that OT-CFM can be expressed exactly using convex analysis tools—specifically an extended Aleksandrov–Brenier potential—without requiring absolute continuity of the target distribution. The resulting mapping from intermediate noisy samples to target points is a Euclidean proximal operator, providing an explicit, non‑parametric form of the learned vector field. For minibatch implementations, the authors prove that the empirical vector fields converge (along subsequences) to the population OT-CFM as the batch size grows, bridging theory and practice. Moreover, assuming the data lies on a smooth low‑dimensional manifold, they prove that the flow is *terminally normally hyperbolic*: after time rescaling, trajectories contract exponentially in directions normal to the manifold while remaining neutral (zero Lyapunov exponent) tangentially. Experimental validation on toy manifolds (circle, two moons) confirms the predicted contraction rate and zero tangential exponent, and MNIST experiments reveal an eigenvalue gap suggestive of a low‑dimensional structure.

## Method Summary
The methodology reinterprets OT-CFM through convex duality. By constructing an extended Aleksandrov–Brenier potential for the optimal transport map between a Gaussian source and the target distribution, the authors show that the vector field at any time can be written as a proximal operator of a convex function. This yields an exact, density‑free representation. To connect theory with stochastic training, they analyze the empirical potentials computed from minibatches and prove epi‑convergence of their minimizers (the vector fields) along subsequences as the batch size increases. To study dynamics near a manifold, they introduce assumptions (A1–A3) guaranteeing that the second epi‑derivative of the potential captures curvature orthogonal to the manifold. Using this, they derive Lyapunov exponents that characterize terminal normal hyperbolicity. The theoretical predictions are verified by computing Lyapunov spectra on toy manifolds and by observing an eigenvalue gap in a neural network trained on MNIST.

## Key Results
- OT-CFM admits an exact representation via extended Aleksandrov–Brenier potential and proximal operators, without requiring density assumptions on the target.
- Minibatch OT‑CFM vector fields converge along subsequences to the population field as batch size grows.
- Under the manifold hypothesis, the flow is terminally normally hyperbolic: exponential contraction normal to the manifold, zero Lyapunov exponent along it.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The proximal operator formulation emerges from Brenier’s theorem: the optimal transport map between a Gaussian source and any target is the gradient of a convex potential. The extended Aleksandrov–Brenier potential generalizes this to possibly singular targets, and its gradient can be expressed as the proximal operator of a convex function derived from the potential. Consequently, the learned vector field in OT‑CFM is exactly this proximal map.

Terminal normal hyperbolicity arises from the geometry of the convex potential along the data manifold. Under Assumptions A1–A3, the effective domain of the second epi‑derivative of the potential coincides with the tangent space of the manifold. As a result, the linearization of the flow has zero eigenvalues in tangent directions and strictly positive eigenvalues in normal directions. After rescaling time, the normal eigenvalues become negative, leading to exponential contraction off the manifold, while tangential directions remain neutral (zero Lyapunov exponent). This matches the intuitive behavior of flow matching models that rapidly align with the data manifold.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Data manifold → Extended Aleksandrov–Brenier potential → Proximal operator representation → Neural network parameterization → Minibatch training → Converged vector field.

**Critical Path**  
1. Formulate the OT‑CFM objective and derive the proximal operator representation.  
2. Parameterize the vector field with a neural network.  
3. Train using a minibatch version of the CFM loss.  
4. As batch size increases, the empirical potentials epi‑converge, and the learned vector field approaches the population OT‑CFM solution.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- Exact OT solution is computationally heavy; minibatch approximations reduce cost but introduce bias.  
- Theoretical guarantees rely on smooth manifolds and assumptions A1–A3; violating them may break hyperbolicity.  
- The proximal representation is explicit but ties the model to convex geometry, potentially limiting flexibility compared to unconstrained neural flows.

**Failure Signatures**  
- If the manifold assumption fails, Lyapunov exponents may not show the predicted zero/negative split, indicating poor contraction.  
- Small batch sizes can cause divergence or high variance in the learned vector field, deviating from the population limit.  
- Non‑smooth or non‑unique potentials could make the second epi‑derivative ill‑defined, leading to unstable training dynamics.

**First Experiments**  
1. Toy manifold validation: generate data on a circle or two moons, compute the analytical OT potential and proximal vector field, then compare to a learned neural net; verify Lyapunov exponents.  
2. Minibatch convergence study: vary batch size on a simple manifold, measure distance between empirical and population vector fields (e.g., via potential values), and observe subsequence convergence trends.  
3. Robustness to manifold violation: add increasing levels of high‑dimensional Gaussian noise to manifold data, train OT‑CFM, and quantify degradation of normal contraction rates.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: Can the proximal operator framework for OT-CFM be extended to rectified flow models?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors state: "Our framework does not apply directly to rectified flow Liu et al. [2023], and this direction will be in our future work." (Introduction and Conclusion)
- Why unresolved: Rectified flow uses different path constructions (e.g., linear interpolation) and couplings that differ from the OT-based potentials. The convex-analytic structure that yields the proximal representation may not carry over.
- What evidence would resolve it: Derive an analogous proximal representation for rectified flow, perhaps by identifying a suitable potential or proving that such a representation is impossible under certain conditions. Alternatively, modify the framework to accommodate rectified flow's stochastic interpolants.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Does the minibatch OT-CFM vector field converge almost surely (or in probability) to the population OT-CFM vector field as batch size increases, without requiring subsequence extraction?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper proves: "the sequence (ϕn) has a subsequence (ϕnk) epi-converging to some ϕ" (Sec. 3.1), yielding convergence of the vector field along that subsequence. No claim about full convergence.
- Why unresolved: Epi-convergence guarantees only a subsequence limit; the entire sequence might not converge, which leaves a gap between theory and practice where a single sequence of increasing batch sizes is used.
- What evidence would resolve it: Show that under additional assumptions (e.g., uniqueness of the limit, convexity of the OT potentials, or specific sampling schemes), the potentials ϕn converge uniformly (or in a stronger sense) along the full sequence as n→∞. Alternatively, provide counterexamples where full convergence fails.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can the terminal normally hyperbolic manifold theory (Sec. 4) be extended to target distributions supported on manifolds with boundaries or singularities?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors note: "the 'Two Moons' example constitutes a manifold with boundary, at which the theoretical analysis in Section 4 does not necessarily apply." (Sec. 6) This indicates the current theory requires smooth manifolds without boundary.
- Why unresolved: The Lyapunov analysis relies on smoothness of the potential along the manifold (Assumption A1) and the full-dimensional variability of subgradients (A2, A3). At boundaries, these may break down due to edge effects or missing normal directions.
- What evidence would resolve it: Develop a version of the Lyapunov theorem for manifolds with boundary, perhaps using one-sided derivatives or boundary-adapted coordinate systems. Empirically verify whether the exponential contraction/neutral behavior persists near boundaries; then adapt the convex-analytic assumptions accordingly.

### Open Question 4
- Question: Can the assumptions (A1)–(A3) (especially the relative interior condition (A3)) be relaxed to cover more general target distributions while preserving the Lyapunov exponent characterization?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The analysis uses (A1)–(A3) to equate the effective domain of the second epi-derivative with the tangent space (Prop. 4.1). The relative interior condition (A3) rules out cases where 0 lies on the relative boundary of ∂f(x1). The paper does not discuss necessity or alternatives.
- Why unresolved: If 0 is on the relative boundary, the identification of tangent directions may fail, and the Lyapunov spectrum could change. Many natural data manifolds might exhibit such degeneracies (e.g., corners or lower-dimensional faces in the subdifferential).
- What evidence would resolve it: Investigate the geometry of the second epi-derivative without (A3). Prove that the Lyapunov exponents remain unchanged under weaker conditions (e.g., 0 ∈ cl(∂f(x1)) with additional structure). Provide counterexamples where (A3) fails and the spectrum differs, clarifying its essential role.

## Limitations
- Theoretical results depend on the manifold hypothesis and smoothness assumptions, which may not hold for real‑world high‑dimensional data.
- Minibatch convergence is only guaranteed along subsequences, not full convergence; practical training behavior and rates remain unclear.
- Empirical validation on MNIST is indirect; eigenvalue gaps could arise from other structural factors besides low‑dimensional manifolds.

## Confidence
- Exact proximal formulation: **High** (rigorous derivation under stated assumptions).
- Minibatch convergence: **Medium** (subsequence convergence is weaker; finite‑sample behavior uncertain).
- Manifold normal hyperbolicity: **Medium** (assumes ideal manifold; limited empirical tests).
- Empirical results (toy manifolds, MNIST): **Low** (toy cases trivial; MNIST analysis correlational).
