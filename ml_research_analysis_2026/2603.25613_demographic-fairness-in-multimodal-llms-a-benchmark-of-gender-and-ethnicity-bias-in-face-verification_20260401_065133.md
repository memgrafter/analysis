# Demographic Fairness in Multimodal LLMs: A Benchmark of Gender and Ethnicity Bias in Face Verification

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.25613
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25613
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** Zero-shot multimodal LLMs show distinct demographic bias patterns in face verification, with the face-specialized FaceLLM-8B achieving superior accuracy, while general-purpose models exhibit trade-offs between accuracy and fairness.

## Executive Summary
This study provides the first systematic benchmark of demographic fairness for multimodal large language models (MLLMs) performing zero-shot face verification. Evaluating nine MLLMs on the IJB-C and RFW datasets across four ethnicities and two genders, the work measures both accuracy (EER, TMR) and fairness via four FMR-based metrics. Key findings reveal that the sole face-specialized model, FaceLLM-8B, substantially outperforms general-purpose MLLMs in accuracy. Bias patterns differ from traditional systems: on IJB-C, Caucasians are most disadvantaged in most models, while on RFW, Africans face the largest disparities. Gender gaps are smaller. A critical trade-off is observed where the most accurate models are not always the fairest, and models with poor overall accuracy can deceptively appear fair due to uniformly high error rates.

## Method Summary
The study establishes a zero-shot visual prompting pipeline where MLLMs receive two face images and a fixed text prompt asking for a similarity score from 0 to 100. The numeric output is normalized to [0,1]. To handle biometric templates (sets of images per identity), all pairwise scores between two templates are computed and averaged to produce a single template-level similarity score. This score is then thresholded to compute global and per-demographic group error rates (FMR, FNMR). Fairness is quantified by measuring the dispersion of per-group FMRs at fixed operating points using four metrics: maximum differential (Δ), maximum ratio (R), ratio-to-geometric-mean (M), and Gini coefficient (G). The pipeline is evaluated on a 10,000-pair subset of IJB-C (with ~20 images/template) and the full RFW dataset (~4 images/template) across nine off-the-shelf MLLMs.

## Key Results
- FaceLLM-8B, the only face-specialized model, dramatically outperforms general-purpose MLLMs in accuracy (lower EER, higher TMR).
- On IJB-C, five of eight general MLLMs show the highest FMR disparities against Caucasians; on RFW, Africans face the largest disparities in most models.
- Gender-based fairness disparities are consistently smaller than ethnic disparities.
- A trade-off exists: the most accurate models are not always the fairest, and low fairness metric values can coincide with poor global accuracy, creating an illusion of fairness.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Zero-Shot Scoring via Visual Prompting
The pipeline repurposes an MLLM's general vision-language capabilities by presenting two face images with a fixed prompt requesting a numeric similarity score. This score, interpreted as `s_ij ∈ [0,1]`, abstracts face verification from specialized embedding extraction to a free-form textual judgment. The core assumption is that pretraining has endowed the model with sufficient latent visual identity discrimination and numerical calibration to produce meaningful, thresholdable scores without task-specific fine-tuning. Break condition: if minor prompt rephrasing causes large score distribution shifts or models frequently fail to output parseable numbers, the mechanism is unreliable.

### Mechanism 2: Template-Level Score Aggregation
For templates containing multiple images (`m × n` pairs), the final template similarity score is the arithmetic mean of all pairwise MLLM scores (`s(T_p, T_g) = mean(s_ij)`). This stabilizes verification scores by averaging out noise from individual image conditions (occlusions, pose, lighting) under an i.i.d. error assumption. However, this creates a confound: groups with systematically larger template sizes (e.g., IJB-C's ~20 vs. RFW's ~4) gain a built-in performance advantage from greater averaging. Break condition: if per-image errors are correlated across a demographic (e.g., shared artifacts), averaging may amplify bias instead.

### Mechanism 3: FMR-Disaggregation as Fairness Metric
Fairness is quantified by computing the false match rate (FMR) per demographic group at a common decision threshold, then summarizing the dispersion across groups using four complementary scalar metrics (Δ, R, M, G). This operationalizes fairness as equality of false match risks. The choice of FMR (over FNMR) reflects a prioritization of preventing false positives (e.g., wrongful identification). Break condition: ratio-based metrics (R, M) become numerically unstable (→∞) when any group's FMR approaches zero at strict thresholds, rendering comparisons meaningless.

## Foundational Learning
- **Zero-Shot Task Transfer:** MLLMs apply general knowledge to specialized tasks without examples. *Why needed:* The entire evaluation assumes viability of zero-shot face verification via prompting. *Quick check:* If a model fails, is it a vision-language alignment failure or a prompt mismatch?
- **Template-Based Biometric Scoring:** Biometric systems aggregate multiple samples per identity into a template. *Why needed:* The benchmark uses template pairs; the mean aggregation is a specific, non-standard choice for MLLMs. *Quick check:* How would median or max aggregation change observed fairness patterns?
- **FMR-Centric Fairness:** Fairness in verification often focuses on false match rate equality. *Why needed:* The study's conclusions hinge on FMR-disaggregation. *Quick check:* When would FNMR disparity be a more critical fairness failure than FMR?
- **Metric Dispersion Summarization:** Converting multi-group comparisons into scalar fairness indices (Δ, R, M, G). *Why needed:* The four metrics provide absolute and relative perspectives on group disparity. *Quick check:* What are the statistical properties (robustness, sensitivity) of each metric?
- **Vision-Language Pretraining Biases:** Encoded societal biases from pretraining corpora may affect visual reasoning. *Why needed:* Explains why general MLLMs inherit bias patterns differing from traditional face recognition systems. *Quick check:* Are the observed biases correlating with dataset representational imbalances or model architecture?
- **DET Curve Analysis:** Detection error trade-off curves plot FNMR vs. FMR across thresholds. *Why needed:* Standard for biometric evaluation; used to derive EER and TMR. *Quick check:* How does the shape of a group's DET curve relative to the global curve indicate disadvantage?

## Architecture Onboarding
Data Ingestion -> Prompt Engine -> MLLM Inference -> Template Aggregator -> Metric Engine

**Critical Path:** Data Ingestion -> Prompt Engine -> **MLLM Inference** (primary bottleneck) -> Template Aggregator -> Metric Engine.

**Design Tradeoffs:**
- **Accuracy vs. Efficiency:** Template mean aggregation requires `O(mn)` inferences per pair, improving reliability but costing ~20 days on an H100. A single-image-pair approach would be faster but noisier and potentially more biased.
- **Metric Stability:** Ratio-based metrics (R, M) provide relative measures but explode numerically at low FMR thresholds. The paper reports `∞` entries, suggesting Δ and G are more robust for comparison.
- **Prompt Sensitivity:** The entire pipeline's validity depends on one untested prompt. Different phrasing could yield different absolute scores and fairness rankings.

**Failure Signatures:**
- **Inference Failure:** Models like LLaVA-NeXT-Mistral-7B produce degenerate outputs (only two unique scores), failing at the MLLM Inference stage.
- **Metric Breakdown:** At strict thresholds (FMR=0.1%), most models have `✗` or `∞` for ratio-based metrics because one or more groups achieve near-zero FMR, making comparison impractical.
- **Fairness Illusion:** Models with poor global accuracy (high EER) can have low fairness metric values (e.g., Ovis1.5 on RFW, `G_F=0.020`) because all groups have similarly high error rates. A low fairness metric does **not** imply good fairness if overall performance is unacceptable.

**First 3 Experiments:**
1. **Prompt Sensitivity Replication:** For a high-performing (FaceLLM-8B) and low-performing (Qwen2-VL-2B) model, run the full pipeline with 3–5 semantically equivalent prompt variations. Quantify the shift in global EER and ethnic ΔF to establish conclusion sensitivity.
2. **Template Size Sensitivity:** On IJB-C, artificially downsample all templates to a fixed small size (n=3 images). Recompute global EER and ethnic disparity (ΔF). Compare changes to original results to isolate the contribution of template size disparity.
3. **Metric Stability Threshold Sweep:** For FaceLLM-8B on IJB-C, compute all four fairness metrics at 20 equally spaced FMR thresholds from 10% down to 0.01%. Plot trajectories, mark where metrics become undefined/∞, and identify a robust operating point for fair comparison.

## Limitations
- The zero-shot pipeline's validity is highly sensitive to the single fixed prompt used; prompt robustness is unassessed.
- Template averaging assumes i.i.d. per-image errors; correlated demographic bias could be amplified.
- FMR-based fairness metrics are unstable at strict thresholds (→∞), and the choice of operating point is arbitrary.
- The IJB-C subset of 10,000 pairs is undisclosed, preventing exact replication; template size disparity between datasets (IJB-C ~20 vs. RFW ~4) confounds fairness analysis.
- Missing inference specifications (generation parameters, image preprocessing, handling of non-numeric outputs) hinder faithful reproduction.

## Confidence
- **Zero-shot scoring viable:** Medium (assumption of latent discrimination; prompt sensitivity untested)
- **Template mean aggregation beneficial:** Low (i.i.d. assumption not validated; template size confound)
- **FMR-disaggregation adequate:** Medium (standard in biometrics, but FNMR ignored)
- **Reported bias patterns (Caucasian disadvantaged on IJB-C, African on RFW):** Medium (patterns consistent across models but confounded by dataset differences)
- **Accuracy-fairness trade-off (best models not always fairest):** High (clear in results; illusion risk noted)

## Next Checks
1. **Prompt robustness validation:** Evaluate top/bottom models with multiple prompt variants; quantify changes in EER and per-group fairness metrics.
2. **Template size bias isolation:** Uniformly downsample IJB-C templates to a small fixed size (e.g., n=3) and recompute metrics to decouple template size effects from model bias.
3. **Metric stability sweep:** For the best model, compute all fairness metrics across a wide FMR threshold range (10% to 0.01%) to visualize breakdown points and identify a robust, practically meaningful operating threshold.
