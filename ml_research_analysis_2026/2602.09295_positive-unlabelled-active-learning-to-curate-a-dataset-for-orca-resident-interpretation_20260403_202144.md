# Positive-Unlabelled Active Learning to Curate a Dataset for Orca Resident Interpretation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.09295
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09295
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Curated 919-hour SRKW acoustic dataset via weakly-supervised PU active learning with Whisper-tiny; detector achieved 28.8% specificity at 95% sensitivity on DCLDE-2026; multiclass classifiers achieved 42.1% (species) and 43.0% (ecotype) top-1 accuracy, outperforming prior detectors in speed and energy efficiency.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a weakly-supervised pipeline to curate a large-scale acoustic dataset for Southern Resident Killer Whales (SRKW) from over 30 years of hydrophone archives. Leveraging positive-unlabelled active learning with noisy labels from citizen scientists and a frozen Whisper-tiny transformer encoder, the authors curated 919 hours of SRKW audio—the largest such dataset to date. The resulting detector achieves 28.8% specificity at 95% sensitivity on the DCLDE-2026 benchmark, while multiclass species and ecotype classifiers reach 42.1% and 43.0% top-1 accuracy, respectively. The pipeline outperforms existing detectors (PAMGuard, ROCCA, Animal-Spot) in accuracy, speed, and energy efficiency, processing a year of data in under one day on a consumer GPU.

## Method Summary
The authors address the challenge of curating a large-scale acoustic dataset for Southern Resident Killer Whales from decades of hydrophone recordings. Their weakly-supervised pipeline uses positive-unlabelled active learning with noisy citizen scientist labels. Audio from multiple public archives (ONC, OOI, SanctSound, OrcaSound) is resampled to 32 kHz, high-pass filtered at 1 kHz, and segmented into 5-minute clips. Each clip is processed by a frozen Whisper-tiny encoder to extract a 384-dimensional embedding via layer normalization and mean-pooling. A logistic regression classifier is trained on these embeddings for binary detection of orca calls; positives come from seed labels and citizen-labelled instances, while negatives are randomly sampled unlabelled clips. The active learning loop iteratively selects batches of unlabelled samples (e.g., by entropy or loss-estimate) for citizen labelling, retraining the classifier after each batch. For multiclass species and ecotype classification, separate linear classifiers are trained atop the same embeddings. The system is evaluated on held-out test sets (DCLDE-2026, FGPD, PSGCH) with metrics including specificity at fixed sensitivity, top-1 accuracy, and computational efficiency.

## Key Results
- Curated 919 hours of SRKW audio from 30+ years of archives using PU active learning with citizen scientist labels.
- Binary detector reaches 28.8% specificity at 95% sensitivity; multiclass species/ecotype classifiers achieve 42.1%/43.0% top-1 accuracy, surpassing prior detectors.
- Achieves high computational efficiency: processes a year of data in under one day on a consumer GPU, with 406.20 day audio/kWh on RTX 2060.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Positive-unlabelled active learning for noisy labels
- **Claim:** Positive-unlabelled active learning enables effective dataset curation from noisy citizen scientist labels by balancing label noise tolerance and informative sample selection.
- **Mechanism:** Active learning iteratively selects samples (e.g., by entropy or loss-estimate) for labelling, while positive-unlabelled (PU) learning trains classifiers using only reliably labelled positives and an unlabelled set that may contain positives, under the selected completely-at-random (SCAR) assumption. This reduces sensitivity to false negatives in the unlabelled set.
- **Core assumption:** Citizen scientists can identify high signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) positive instances, and the SCAR assumption approximately holds, meaning the propensity of a positive sample to be labelled is independent of its features.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract]: Explicitly mentions "weakly-supervised, positive-unlabelled, active learning strategy" and "Citizen scientists provided noisy labels."  
  [section]: Section 1.2 derives PU learning convergence bounds and discusses citizen science labelling capabilities; Section 2.4 details active learning strategies (positive only, entropy, loss-estimate, mixed, alternating).  
  [corpus]: Neighbor papers provide contextual support but no direct evidence for this specific application; corpus evidence is weak.
- **Break condition:** If citizen scientists systematically miss low-SNR positives or if labelling propensity varies with sample characteristics (violating SCAR), PU learning may yield biased models, reducing specificity at high sensitivity.

### Mechanism 2: Frozen Whisper-tiny embeddings for efficient detection
- **Claim:** Frozen Whisper-tiny embeddings with a simple logistic regression classifier achieve state-of-the-art detection with high computational efficiency by leveraging transfer learning.
- **Mechanism:** A pre-trained Whisper-tiny encoder (39M parameters) extracts hierarchical audio features; layer normalization and mean-pooling produce fixed-length file-level embeddings; logistic regression trained on these embeddings for binary detection, avoiding costly fine-tuning.
- **Core assumption:** Whisper's representations, trained on speech, generalize to marine mammal sounds without adaptation, and the task concerns discriminative features rather than fine-grained temporal patterns.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract]: States "transformer-based detectors outperform state-of-the-art detectors" and "processing a year of data in under one day on a consumer GPU."  
  [section]: Section 2.2 specifies preprocessing: Whisper-tiny encoder with frozen weights, layer norm, mean-pooling. Section 3.2 shows benchmark results (e.g., 28.9% specificity at 95% sensitivity on DCLDE-2026) and Table 2 compares efficiency.  
  [corpus]: Neighbors include deep learning bioacoustics models but none use Whisper; indirect support only.
- **Break condition:** If marine mammal acoustics diverge significantly from Whisper's speech-trained distribution (e.g., non-harmonic calls), embeddings may lack discriminative power, necessitating fine-tuning or alternative architectures.

## Foundational Learning
- **Positive-Unlabelled (PU) Learning**  
  Why needed: Addresses the core challenge of noisy labels where only positives are reliably identified by citizen scientists; negatives are unlabelled and may include missed positives.  
  Quick check: What is the selected completely-at-random (SCAR) assumption in PU learning, and how does its violation affect risk bounds?

- **Active Learning**  
  Why needed: Reduces labelling effort for massive archives by intelligently selecting samples that maximally improve model performance, balancing uncertainty and diversity.  
  Quick check: How does entropy-based sampling differ from loss-estimate sampling in active learning, and when might one prefer the other?

- **Transfer Learning with Frozen Encoders**  
  Why needed: Leverages large pre-trained models (Whisper) for feature extraction without expensive fine-tuning, enabling efficient scaling to 260,000 hours of audio.  
  Quick check: Why might freezing pre-trained weights be advantageous in low-data regimes compared to full fine-tuning?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Data Ingestion (hydrophone archives) -> Preprocessing (resampling, filtering, Whisper embedding extraction) -> Storage (disk) -> Logistic Regression Classifier Training -> Active Learning Loop (sample selection, citizen labeling, retraining) -> Post-processing (label propagation, multiclass classification)

**Critical path:** Precompute all embeddings → Train initial model on seed labels → Iterate active learning cycles (sample selection → citizen labelling → retraining) until convergence → Curate final dataset with species/ecotype labels → Benchmark on held-out sets.

**Design tradeoffs:** Frozen vs fine-tuned Whisper (speed vs accuracy); active learning strategy (entropy for discovery vs positive-only for precision); embedding storage vs recomputation; citizen labelling throughput vs expert accuracy.

**Failure signatures:** Specificity at 95% sensitivity below 28.8% on DCLDE-2026; ecotype top-1 accuracy below 43.0%; high variance in active learning curves; citizen-expert Cohen's kappa below 0.5; excessive false positives in curated dataset.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Compare active learning strategies (positive only, entropy, mixed, alternating) by plotting specificity at 95% sensitivity vs. labelling iterations on a held-out test set (e.g., 2022 data).  
2. Benchmark detector against PAMGuard, ROCCA, and Animal-Spot on DCLDE-2026, DEEPAL, and FGPD/PSGCH test sets, reporting specificity at fixed sensitivity and processing time.  
3. Evaluate citizen scientist labelling quality by having them label a subset of expert-annotated files and compute Cohen's kappa; also assess impact of simulated label noise (e.g., 30% positive flips) on model performance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** How does the violation of the Selected Completely At Random (SCAR) assumption—due to citizen scientists preferentially labeling high-SNR samples—affect the convergence bounds and final performance of the positive-unlabelled active learning model in this bioacoustic context?  
**Basis:** The paper states: "The caveat is that the SCAR assumption used to derive the bounds may be broken by selectively identifying high SNR samples." They hypothesize citizen scientists identify high-SNR instances but do not quantify the impact on their theoretical bounds.  
**Why unresolved:** The theoretical convergence rate relies on the SCAR assumption, but the labeling strategy inherently biases the positive set. The empirical results show success, but the theoretical penalty for this bias in large-scale, imbalanced PAM data is not quantified.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments where the propensity score of positive sample labeling is measured and explicitly varied (e.g., by having experts and citizens label the same set with known ground truth), followed by an analysis of how the empirical risk and the derived VC-dimension bound change with the measured propensity.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** Would fine-tuning the entire Whisper architecture (beyond just the classifier head on frozen embeddings) or using a larger Whisper model (e.g., `whisper-small`) provide a statistically significant improvement in detection specificity at 95% sensitivity for SRKWs, justifying the increased computational cost?  
**Basis:** The discussion says: "In all of our experiments, we only use the whisper-tiny model with frozen weights... Using a large whisper architecture or fine-tuning the entire model would likely improve performance."  
**Why unresolved:** The claim is speculative. The trade-off between accuracy gain and the 20-100x increase in compute/memory for full fine-tuning or larger models is not evaluated for their specific detection task on long-term PAM archives.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** A rigorous ablation study comparing `whisper-tiny` (frozen embeddings), `whisper-tiny` (full fine-tune), `whisper-small` (frozen), and `whisper-small` (full fine-tune) on the DCLDE-2026 and FGPD/PSGCH test sets, measuring changes in the specificity-sensitivity curve, top-1 accuracy, and wall-clock time/energy per hour of audio.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** What are the primary acoustic or contextual factors causing the ~42% top-1 accuracy for the multiclass species and ecotype classifiers, particularly for the 4 "test classes" in DCLDE-2026 that were not present in the training data?  
**Basis:** Results state: "Our multiclass species classifier obtains a top-1 classification accuracy of 42.1% (11 train classes, 4 test classes)" and the ecotype classifier "43.0% (4 train classes, 5 test classes)." The paper does not provide a per-class error analysis or confusion matrix for these out-of-training-distribution classes.  
**Why unresolved:** Low accuracy on unseen classes could stem from covariate shift (different acoustic environments), concept shift (novel call types), or insufficient representation in the pseudo-labeled neighboring files. The paper lists classes but does not diagnose failure modes.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Providing a detailed confusion matrix for the DCLDE-2026 test set, including precision/recall per class (train and test). Coupling this with qualitative analysis (e.g., listening to/failing examples for the test classes) to determine if errors are due to acoustic similarity (e.g., mistaking a humpback for a dolphin), poor label propagation from neighbors, or unique noise conditions in the test set locations.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** Can the DORI dataset (919 hours of SRKW audio) be effectively used for unsupervised or self-supervised pre-training of a whale-specific acoustic model that generalizes better to unseen ecotypes/species than a model pre-trained on general audio (like Whisper)?  
**Basis:** The conclusion and discussion state: "The openly licensed dataset we curated should serve as a useful tool for investigating animal linguistics" and is "suitable for unsupervised machine translation." However, no such model is trained or evaluated.  
**Why unresolved:** Whisper was pre-trained on 680,000 hours of human speech. The domain shift to whale vocalizations is substantial. The value of a large, curated, but still relatively small (919h) whale-specific dataset for pre-training, versus fine-tuning Whisper, is unknown. A whale-specific pre-trained model could capture domain-specific invariances (e.g., variable SNR, ship noise, specific harmonic structures).  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Training a self-supervised model (e.g., contrastive predictive coding, masked autoencoder) on the unlabeled portions of the DORI archive (all ~30 years, not just the curated 929h SRKW subset). Then, freezing its encoder and training a linear probe for SRKW detection/classification on a small labeled subset, comparing performance to a Whisper-tiny encoder frozen and fine-tuned on the same labeled subset.

### Open Question 5
**Question:** What is the quantitative impact of the high-pass filter at 1 kHz and the 32 kHz sampling rate on the detection recall of non-orca marine mammals (e.g., humpbacks, dolphins, sea lions) whose key vocalizations may have significant energy below 1 kHz or above 16 kHz?  
**Basis:** The discussion explicitly states: "By having a sampling rate of only 32 kHz, we exclude some dolphin species, while having a high-pass filter at 1 kHz we lose sensitivity for blue, sei, fin, humpback, gray, and north pacific right whales." This is acknowledged as a design choice for orca-focused detection but not evaluated.  
**Why unresolved:** The paper reports multi-species classification results (42.1% accuracy) but does not measure the drop in performance if these filter settings were changed. The curated dataset includes humpbacks and dolphins, but the detector's efficacy for them under this filtering is not quantified, limiting the dataset's utility for broader PAM.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Re-running the detection pipeline on a held-out subset (e.g., the DCLDE-2026 or FGPD test sets) with variations: (a) no high-pass filter, (b) lower cutoff (e.g., 300 Hz), and (c) higher sampling rate (48 kHz). Measure the change in sensitivity/specificity for non-orca species calls (e.g., humpback songs, dolphin whistles) to provide a concrete performance trade-off curve.

## Limitations
- SCAR assumption for PU learning not empirically validated; potential bias if citizen scientists systematically miss low-SNR positives.
- Whisper-tiny encoder's generalization to non-resident orca ecotypes and other species remains untested.
- Scalability claims lack independent replication; energy and storage costs for massive archives not fully analyzed.
