# TherapyGym: Evaluating and Aligning Clinical Fidelity and Safety in Therapy Chatbots

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.18008
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18008
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Training with THERAPYGYM’s rewards increased expert-rated CTRS scores from 0.10 to 0.60 and reduced safety violations from 0.38 to 0.20.

## Executive Summary
THERAPYGYM is a framework for evaluating and improving therapy chatbots using clinically validated standards. It addresses the limitation of generic dialogue metrics by anchoring evaluation in the Cognitive Therapy Rating Scale (CTRS) for fidelity and a multi-label safety scheme. The framework provides THERAPYJUDGEBENCH—a benchmark of 116 expert-annotated dialogues—to audit an LLM judge, and a reinforcement learning harness with configurable patient simulations derived from CBT models.  
In experiments, reinforcement learning with composite rewards derived from CTRS skills and safety penalties led to substantial improvements: expert-rated fidelity scores more than sextupled (0.10 → 0.60) while safety violations nearly halved (0.38 → 0.20). This demonstrates that clinically grounded rewards can simultaneously enhance skillfulness and safety in automated therapy agents.

## Method Summary
THERAPYGYM integrates four components: (1) THERAPYJUDGEBENCH, a curated set of 116 ten-turn therapy dialogues with 1,270 expert ratings across CTRS dimensions and safety flags, used to validate an LLM-based evaluator; (2) THERAPYJUDGE, an LLM prompted with rubrics and examples to score dialogues on CTRS and safety; (3) Patient–Ψ–CM-Augmented, a synthetic patient generator that produces 13,000 diverse CBT profiles via augmentation of a cognitive model, each driving a reactive turn-by-turn simulator; and (4) an RL harness that fine-tunes a therapist model using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with rewards computed as a weighted sum of normalized CTRS scores minus safety penalties. The workflow validates the judge, defines the reward, augments profiles, runs RL, and evaluates on held-out simulations.

## Key Results
- Expert-rated CTRS fidelity scores rose from 0.10 to 0.60 after RL training.
- Safety violations decreased from 0.38 to 0.20.
- THERAPYJUDGE achieves Spearman ρ≈0.56 with human experts on CTRS and 99% accuracy on safety flags.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** THERAPYGYM enables reliable, scalable evaluation of therapy chatbots by anchoring automated judging in clinician-validated standards.  
**Mechanism:** A validated benchmark (THERAPYJUDGEBENCH) with 116 expert-annotated dialogues is used to audit and calibrate an LLM judge (THERAPYJUDGE). The judge evaluates full 10-turn sessions against the Cognitive Therapy Rating Scale (CTRS) and safety flags, producing rewards for RL.  
**Core assumption:** LLM judges can approximate expert therapist ratings on clinically validated constructs (CTRS skills, safety) when systematically validated against human annotations.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] introduces THERAPYJUDGEBENCH for auditing LLM judges.  
- [section 2.1.3] reports THERAPYJUDGE achieves Spearman ρ≈0.56 with human raters on CTRS and 99% accuracy on safety.  
- [corpus] neighboring work (e.g., *Fine-Tuning Open-Weight Language Models to Deliver CBT*) also seeks clinically grounded evaluation, supporting the need for such benchmarks.  
**Break condition:** If human–LLM agreement on key CTRS dimensions falls below a moderate threshold (e.g., ρ<0.4) for critical skills like *Agenda* or *Guided Discovery*, the judge’s feedback may misguide RL optimization.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Reinforcement learning with composite rewards (CTRS skill + safety penalties) steers therapist models toward clinically faithful and safer behavior.  
**Mechanism:** THERAPYJUDGE outputs session-level scores for 9 reliable CTRS dimensions and 4 safety flags. These are normalized and combined into a reward: `R(d) = Σ wi·CTRSi(d) − Σ λj·Safetyj(d)`. GRPO optimizes the therapist policy using these rewards across 13k simulated patient profiles.  
**Core assumption:** Differentiable, multi-dimensional rewards derived from clinical rubrics can shape LLM generation to improve both skillfulness and safety in a trade-off aware manner.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] states RL training raised expert-rated CTRS from 0.10→0.60 and reduced safety violations from 0.38→0.20.  
- [section 2.2.2] defines the composite reward formulation.  
- [section 3.3] confirms gains without increased risk; ablation [section B.5] shows dropping safety penalty increases violations (0.43 vs. 0.13).  
**Break condition:** If reward scales are mis calibrated (e.g., safety penalty λj too low), RL may overfit to skill rewards and increase unsafe behaviors; if CTRS weights wi ignore low-reliability skills, training may neglect foundational competencies.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Diverse, clinically plausible patient simulations are essential for robust multi-turn evaluation and RL training.  
**Mechanism:** Patient profiles are derived from CBT cognitive models (Patient–Ψ–CM) and augmented via TreeSynth to 13k profiles. Each profile grounds a turn-by-turn patient simulator that reacts to therapist moves, creating varied therapeutic trajectories.  
**Core assumption:** Simulated patients that preserve cognitive-behavioral coherence (beliefs, emotions, behaviors) can generate training dialogues that transfer to real-world therapeutic alignment.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [section 2.1.1] describes patient simulation using clinically validated Patient–Ψ.  
- [section 2.2.1] and [appendix B.4] show augmented profiles match/exceed originals in clinical plausibility (mean Likert 4.7–4.95/5) and 100% profile-matching accuracy in conversations.  
- [corpus] *MAGneT* and *Synthetic Therapy Conversations* papers also explore synthetic data for mental health, indicating community focus on this challenge.  
**Break condition:** If profile augmentation distorts cognitive structures (e.g., incoherent belief–behavior links), simulated dialogues may teach maladaptive patterns; if simulator lacks reactivity, dialogues become stereotyped and RL overfits to narrow scenarios.

## Foundational Learning
- **Cognitive Therapy Rating Scale (CTRS)**: Why needed: Primary fidelity metric; defines 11 skill dimensions (e.g., Agenda, Guided Discovery) scored 0–6 per session. Understanding its rubric is necessary to interpret rewards and judge outputs. Quick check: What distinguishes a "4" from a "6" on the *Collaboration* dimension?
- **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**: Why needed: The RL algorithm used; normalizes advantages within groups of rollouts per patient profile to stabilize multi-turn dialogue training. Quick check: How does GRPO differ from standard PPO in the context of variable-length therapy dialogues?
- **Patient Simulation vs. Real Patients**: Why needed: Enables scalable, ethical interaction for evaluation/RL. Must understand its limits (synthetic fidelity) versus real human dynamics. Quick check: What core CBT constructs from Patient–Ψ–CM must a simulator preserve to be clinically valid?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: THERAPYJUDGEBENCH → THERAPYJUDGE (validation); Patient–Ψ–CM-Augmented → Patient Simulator → Dialogues → THERAPYJUDGE → Composite Reward → GRPO → Therapist LLM
- **Critical path**: Validate THERAPYJUDGE → Fix reward formulation (select reliable CTRS skills, set λj) → Augment patient profiles → Run GRPO with group-normalized rewards → Evaluate on held-out profiles with human/LLM judges.
- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - LLM judge vs. human experts: Speed/scalability vs. nuanced clinical judgment (inter-rater ρ≈0.56).  
  - Synthetic patients vs. real: Privacy/scale vs. genuine human complexity (profile-matching accuracy=100% but may miss idiosyncrasies).  
  - Dialogue-level vs. turn-level rewards: Captures therapeutic process vs. credit assignment difficulty.
- **Failure signatures**:  
  - Human–LLM agreement on critical CTRS skills like *Guided Discovery* (ρ=0.23) or *Feedback* (ρ=0.45) remains low → reward signal noisy.  
  - Safety violations increase after RL → λj penalties too weak or judge missing subtle risks.  
  - Trained model’s CTRS gains vanish on new symptom profiles → overfitting to simulated patient distribution.
- **First 3 experiments**:  
  1. Reproduce THERAPYJUDGE validation: Run THERAPYJUDGE on THERAPYJUDGEBENCH, compute Spearman ρ with expert CTRS scores; expect ρ≈0.56 overall.  
  2. Reward ablation: Train with/without safety penalty on a small profile subset (e.g., 100 profiles), measure safety violation delta; expect increase without penalty.  
  3. Patient simulation sanity check: Generate 40 dialogues from 10 distinct profiles, have a human rater match each dialogue to one of the 10 profiles; expect >90% accuracy as in [appendix B.6].

## Limitations
- LLM judge reliability is moderate (ρ≈0.56) and notably low on key skills like *Guided Discovery* (ρ=0.23), risking noisy reward signals.
- Reward calibration is sensitive; inappropriate weighting of CTRS skills vs. safety penalties could lead to trade-off failures (e.g., improved skill but increased harm).
- Simulation fidelity transfer to real-world patient interactions remains uncertain despite high in-simulation plausibility and profile-matching accuracy.

## Next Checks
1. Judge Skill-Specific Agreement Audit: Re-evaluate THERAPYJUDGE on THERAPYJUDGEBENCH, computing separate Spearman correlations for each of the 9 CTRS dimensions. Flag any dimension with ρ<0.4 (e.g., *Guided Discovery*, *Feedback*) for reward exclusion or recalibration.
2. Reward Sensitivity Sweep: Run RL training with varied safety penalty weights (λj) on a fixed profile subset. Monitor for any condition where safety violations increase >10% relative to baseline, identifying unstable regions of the reward trade-off space.
3. Simulation Generalization Probe: Train a therapist model on the 13k synthetic profiles, then test it on a small hold-out set of real human therapy dialogues (if ethically accessible). Compare CTRS and safety scores against a model trained only on synthetic data to quantify generalization gap.
