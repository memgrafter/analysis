# Learning to Reason with Curriculum I: Provable Benefits of Autocurriculum

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.18325
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18325
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Autocurriculum exponentially reduces teacher CoT demonstrations for supervised fine‑tuning (from Õ(d/ε) to Õ(d)) and decouples computational cost from reference model coverage for RL with verifiable rewards (from Õ(dC_seq/ε) to Õ(dC_seq + d/ε)).

## Executive Summary
Training reasoning models with chain‑of‑thought (CoT) is computationally expensive because it requires many teacher demonstrations or large volumes of rollouts. This paper introduces **autocurriculum**, a framework where the learner adaptively selects which prompts to train on based on its own errors, using a cheap outcome verifier. In the supervised fine‑tuning (SFT) setting, autocurriculum provably reduces the number of required teacher CoT demonstrations from Õ(d/ε) to Õ(d)—an exponential improvement in the target error ε. For reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR), it decouples the computational cost from the reference model’s coverage: total rollouts drop from Õ(dC_seq/ε) to Õ(dC_seq + d/ε). These gains hold without distributional assumptions, thanks to a boosting‑style curriculum that focuses supervision on the model’s weak regions.

## Method Summary
The paper studies two learning problems: (1) supervised fine‑tuning (SFT) with a teacher CoT oracle, and (2) reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) that starts from a reference model. The core algorithm **AutoTune** (for SFT) and its RL counterpart **AutoTune.RL** iteratively construct an ensemble of models. At each round, the current ensemble is evaluated on a fixed prompt pool using a binary outcome verifier. Prompts where the ensemble is wrong receive higher sampling probability via a reweighting scheme reminiscent of boosting. In SFT, only the selected prompts query the teacher for full CoT traces; a base learner (next‑token prediction) then trains a new model on these traces. In RLVR, the base learner (RLFineTune) generates multiple traces from the reference model, filters for correct traces with sufficient probability (governed by a coverage parameter C_seq), and performs empirical risk minimization. The final predictor is the plurality vote (or a mixture) of the ensemble. Theoretical analysis shows that, under realizability and a weak‑learning guarantee, the number of teacher CoTs (or total generated traces) is dramatically smaller than with non‑adaptive training.

## Key Results
- **Exponential reduction in SFT:** Teacher CoT demonstrations drop from Õ(d/ε) to Õ(d).
- **Decoupling in RLVR:** Total rollouts decrease from Õ(dC_seq/ε) to Õ(dC_seq + d/ε).
- **Adaptive efficiency:** Gains come from focusing supervision on errors, with no distributional assumptions beyond realizability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Adaptive selection of training prompts via a verifier reduces the number of expensive teacher CoT demonstrations by orders of magnitude.
- **Mechanism:** The algorithm iteratively evaluates the current model on the unlabeled prompt set using the cheap outcome verifier. Prompts where the model is wrong are sampled with higher probability, and only those prompts query the teacher for full reasoning traces. This shifts supervision toward the model’s weak regions.
- **Core assumption:** A perfect outcome verifier exists (oracle V) and the optimal model π⋆ is realizable.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “autocurriculum: models adaptively select training queries based on their own errors, guided by a verifier.”
  - [Section 3] detailed in AutoTune (Algorithm 1) and Theorem 3.3.
  - [corpus] shows multiple contemporary systems (e.g., DAPO, PCL, REST) that use similar adaptive filtering heuristics, supporting the empirical relevance of this mechanistic idea.
- **Break condition:** If the verifier is noisy or incomplete, the selective sampling may misidentify errors and could even degrade performance.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** A boosting‑style iterative training process yields an exponential improvement in sample efficiency over non‑adaptive supervised fine‑tuning.
- **Mechanism:** Each “weak learner” is trained to constant accuracy (err⋆≈¼) on a reweighted distribution ρ⋆ that concentrates on prompts misclassified by the current ensemble. The reweighting function α_j,k(r) (Eq. 5) ensures the ensemble’s plurality vote’s error decreases geometrically.
- **Core assumption:** The base learner can achieve the weak‑learning guarantee under any reweighted distribution (realizability and sufficient model capacity).
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “drawing on classical techniques from boosting and learning from counterexamples”.
  - [Section 3.3] the proof sketch and Lemma B.1–B.5.
  - [corpus] the conceptual parallel to boosting‑by‑filtering (Freund, 1995) provides theoretical grounding.
- **Break condition:** If the weak learner fails to reach err⋆ on a distribution (e.g., due to distribution shift or insufficient capacity), the geometric decay does not hold and the final guarantee may not be met.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** In RL with verifiable rewards, autocurriculum decouples the computational cost of rejection sampling from the reference model’s coverage, reducing total rollouts from Ξ(d·C_seq/ε) to Ξ(d·C_seq + d/ε).
- **Mechanism:** Using the verifier, prompts where the current ensemble errs are selected. Each weak learner then employs rejection sampling on its selected prompts via the reference model, requiring only O(C_seq) rollouts per prompt in that iteration rather than across all ε‑scale iterations. This caps the coverage‑dependent cost to a one‑time “burn‑in”.
- **Core assumption:** The reference model π_ref satisfies sequence‑level coverage (Definition 4.2) and the model class Π is deterministic.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] “For RL fine‑tuning, autocurriculum decouples the computational cost from the quality of the reference model….”
  - [Section 4] formalized in Theorem 4.5 and Algorithm 5 (AutoTune.RL).
  - [corpus] works like RLFineTune (rejection sampling) and REST use similar generate‑filter‑retrain loops; our result quantifies the benefit of adding an outer curriculum.
- **Break condition:** If coverage is extremely poor (C_seq large) or the prompt set includes many uncovered prompts, the burn‑in cost may dominate and the exponential gain over the baseline diminishes.

## Foundational Learning
- **Chain‑of‑Thought (CoT) reasoning and outcome verifiers.**  
  *Why needed:* The framework models training and inference as autoregressive generation of reasoning traces ending with an answer evaluated by a verifier; accuracy is defined with respect to that verifier.  
  *Quick check:* What property must the outcome verifier satisfy for adaptive sampling to be valid?
- **Natarajan dimension d as model complexity measure.**  
  *Why needed:* Sample‑complexity bounds scale linearly with d (e.g., Θ(d/ε) for prompts).  
  *Quick check:* How does increasing the model class size (larger d) affect the number of CoT demonstrations required?
- **Sequence‑level coverage C_seq.**  
  *Why needed:* In RLVR, coverage quantifies how often the reference model produces a high‑probability correct CoT; it directly appears in the computational cost.  
  *Quick check:* What happens to the cost bound if C_seq is very small (good coverage) versus very large (poor coverage)?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Prompt pool (unlabeled dataset D_prompt) → Verifier V → Supervision source (teacher CoT oracle or reference model π_ref) → Base learner (NTP or RLFineTune) → Autocurriculum controller (AutoTune wrapper) → Ensemble → Final model (plurality vote).
- **Critical path:**  
  1. Evaluate current ensemble on D_prompt using V → compute error ranks.  
  2. Sample new subset D_out via rejection sampling with weights based on ranks.  
  3. Invoke base learner on D_out to produce next model bπ_j.  
  4. Add model to ensemble, repeat until desired accuracy.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  *Deterministic vs stochastic Π:* deterministic analysis yields exponential improvement; stochastic case relaxes to moderate accuracy unless consensus voting is used.  
  *Verifier quality:* perfect verifier assumed; noisy verification likely weakens guarantees.  
  *Coverage reliance:* RLVR cost hinges on C_seq; if reference model has gaps, autocurriculum cannot recover those prompts.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - **Empty D_out** after a few rounds → no informative prompts left; may need to re‑initialize or broaden prompt pool.  
  - **Weak learner fails** to reach err⋆ on its reweighted distribution → consider increasing prompt budget or expanding model capacity.  
  - **Ensemble degeneration** where plurality vote stalls > ε error → check that ranks are computed correctly and that coverage assumptions hold.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Deterministic SFT:** Unique‑answer setting (Assumption 3.1), compare AutoTune vs non‑adaptive NTP; expect CoT query count drop from O(d/ε) to O(d).  
  2. **Stochastic SFT:** General Π, test AutoTune_stoch; verify that mixture outcome achieves moderate accuracy (≈3/5) as predicted.  
  3. **RLVR with coverage:** Apply AutoTune.RL on a reference model with known C_seq; measure total rollouts vs RLFineTune baseline; observe decoupling effect.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:** How does autocurriculum interact with improving coverage across rounds when the reference model is iteratively updated (self‑play), and can the O(dC_seq) burn‑in cost be further reduced?  
*Basis:* The current analysis assumes a fixed reference model with a given coverage coefficient C_seq. In practice, self‑play iteratively updates the reference model using the current model, potentially increasing C_seq over time. The interplay between adaptive curriculum and coverage expansion is not analyzed, and the bound on computational cost may not directly apply. *What evidence would resolve it:* A theoretical analysis showing that the burn‑in cost can be reduced when the reference model is updated based on the current policy, perhaps by proving a bound that depends on the initial C_seq only as a low‑order term or that decreases over rounds. Alternatively, empirical demonstrations of coverage expansion via autocurriculum in self‑play settings, with corresponding complexity reductions.

**Open Question 2:** Can the autocurriculum framework be extended to online reinforcement learning settings where the model is updated continuously (e.g., via policy gradients like PPO or GRPO) and the prompt distribution co‑evolves with the model?  
*Basis:* Our algorithms operate in a batch setting: train a model on a fixed dataset, then re‑evaluate. Modern RL pipelines use online policy gradient updates (PPO, GRPO) where the model is updated continuously. Extending the autocurriculum framework to this online setting, where the prompt distribution and the model co‑evolve, would bring the theory closer to practice. *What evidence would resolve it:* Development of an online autocurriculum algorithm with provable convergence guarantees under policy gradient updates, possibly by maintaining a curriculum distribution that mixes exploration and exploitation, and analysis showing that it improves sample efficiency compared to uniform sampling.

**Open Question 3:** Can autocurriculum enable coverage expansion, i.e., improve the sequence‑level coverage property on prompts that initially have low coverage by training on easier problems, and how does this interact with length‑based curricula?  
*Basis:* Whether autocurriculum can enable coverage expansion, where training on easier problems builds coverage on harder ones (Lee et al., 2025; Motwani et al., 2025), is a natural question, closely related to explicit length‑based curricula (Setlur et al., 2025). *What evidence would resolve it:* An algorithm that adaptively selects prompts based on current coverage estimates (instead of just verifier feedback) and theoretical guarantee that such an approach expands coverage to all prompts, lowering the effective C_seq over time. Alternatively, empirical results showing successful coverage expansion.

**Open Question 4:** How can the autocurriculum theory be extended to settings with imperfect verification, such as noisy verifiers or learned reward models?  
*Basis:* Our framework assumes access to a perfect outcome verifier, which is natural for domains with verifiable rewards (math, code), but extending the theory to noisy or learned reward models is an important open problem. *What evidence would resolve it:* Derive robust variants of the autocurriculum algorithm that tolerate a certain level of verifier noise, perhaps by smoothing the curriculum distribution or using majority voting to estimate errors, and prove that the exponential gains are preserved up to a factor depending on noise level. Or empirical validation that the algorithm works with imperfect synthetic verifiers.

## Limitations
- **Perfect verifier assumption:** Requires an oracle outcome verifier; noisy or incomplete verifiers may degrade or invert the adaptive selection benefit.
- **Realizability & weak learning:** Assumes optimal model is in the class and base learner can achieve constant error on any reweighted distribution; may fail with limited capacity or hard distributions.
- **Coverage idealization in RLVR:** Assumes reference model has nontrivial correct‑trace probability for every prompt; in practice many prompts may be uncovered, breaking the decoupling guarantee.

## Confidence
- Theoretical sample‑complexity gains (SFT exponential improvement): **High**
- Theoretical decoupling in RLVR (cost from C_seq/ε to d·C_seq + d/ε): **High**
- Practical benefit with neural LMs & noisy verifiers: **Low to Medium**
- Boosting convergence with real weak learners: **Medium**

## Next Checks
1. **Noisy verifier robustness test:** Introduce controlled noise into V (random flips with probability ρ). Measure how SFT sample complexity scales with ρ and whether exponential improvement persists or degrades gracefully.
2. **Coverage gap sensitivity analysis:** In RLVR, construct π_ref with varying C_seq and with a fraction η of completely uncovered prompts. Empirically quantify the breakdown point where AutoTune.RL fails to achieve target accuracy and compare to the partial‑coverage theory.
3. **Neural weak‑learner feasibility:** Use a small neural language model as the base learner in SFT. Measure the actual error err_j on the reweighted distribution ρ*_j at each boosting round. Check if err_j ≤ err* (e.g., 0.25) holds consistently; if not, identify distributional shifts or capacity limits that violate the weak‑learning assumption.
