# Cross-Embodiment Offline Reinforcement Learning for Heterogeneous Robot Datasets

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.18025
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18025
- **Reference count:** 30
- **Primary result:** Embodiment Grouping improves cross-embodiment offline RL performance by up to 39.8% on high-suboptimal datasets compared to standard offline RL baselines.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles cross-embodiment offline reinforcement learning, where a single policy must learn from heterogeneous robot datasets. The authors create a benchmark with 16 robot platforms and pooled offline data (expert, replay, suboptimal). They find that offline RL (e.g., IQL) outperforms pure behavior cloning when suboptimal data dominates, because it reweights actions by estimated advantage, effectively stitching together recoverable segments. However, as suboptimal data and robot diversity increase, conflicting gradients across morphologies cause negative transfer. To address this, they propose Embodiment Grouping (EG): robots are clustered by morphology graph similarity (Fused Gromov–Wasserstein), and the actor is updated sequentially per group. This static grouping reduces gradient interference, leading to substantial gains—up to 39.8% on high-suboptimal datasets—over standard offline RL and outperforming methods like PCGrad and SEL. The method is effective with both IQL and TD3+BC backbones, enabling robust learning from heterogeneous data without per-robot expert demonstrations.

## Method Summary
The approach combines a cross-embodiment representation with offline RL and a novel grouping strategy. The URMA encoder processes variable-length observations (general state, joint sets, foot sets) using descriptor-conditioned attention, producing a fixed-size latent conditioned on robot morphology. A pooled dataset from 16 robots—each providing expert, replay, and suboptimal trajectories—is used to train a single actor-critic policy. Standard offline RL algorithms (IQL or TD3+BC) are applied: offline RL leverages advantage reweighting to learn from suboptimal data, while the shared policy struggles with gradient conflicts as morphological diversity increases. Embodiment Grouping (EG) mitigates this by first clustering robots based on Fused Gromov–Wasserstein distances between their morphology graphs. During training, after a global critic update, the actor is updated sequentially on minibatches filtered by each precomputed robot group. This ensures that each gradient step aggregates updates only from morphologically similar robots, aligning gradient directions and reducing destructive interference. The method requires no additional learned parameters and is compatible with any offline RL backbone.

## Key Results
- Offline RL (IQL) outperforms pure behavior cloning on datasets with abundant suboptimal trajectories.
- Gradient conflicts between morphologies intensify as suboptimal data proportion and robot diversity increase, leading to negative transfer.
- Embodiment Grouping (with FGW clustering) alleviates these conflicts, achieving up to a 39.8% return improvement on high-suboptimal datasets and surpassing gradient surgery methods (PCGrad, SEL).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Offline RL Advantage Reweighting
- **Claim:** Offline RL algorithms like IQL outperform pure behavior cloning on datasets with abundant suboptimal trajectories because they reweight actions by estimated advantages, filtering low-quality behaviors and stitching together recoverable segments.
- **Mechanism:** IQL fits a state value function via expectile regression to capture an upper expectile of the return distribution. The policy is updated using advantage-weighted behavioral cloning: actions are weighted by exp(β(Q(s,a)−V(s))). This emphasizes actions that are better than the estimated state value, allowing the policy to learn from suboptimal data.
- **Core assumption:** The dataset contains a mixture of expert and suboptimal trajectories such that some actions have positive advantage, and the Q-value estimation is sufficiently accurate to avoid severe extrapolation errors.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract] "Our experiments confirm that this combined approach excels at pre-training with datasets rich in suboptimal trajectories, outperforming pure behavior cloning."
  - [section 4.1] Table 1 shows IQL achieving higher returns than BC on Expert Replay and 70% Suboptimal datasets, which contain more suboptimal data.
  - [corpus] The corpus includes work on offline RL with imbalanced data, supporting the general idea, but direct evidence for cross-embodiment requires the paper’s own experiments.
- **Break condition:** If the dataset lacks any positive-advantage actions (e.g., entirely random), offline RL may not surpass BC; also, if the Q-function overestimates out-of-distribution values, performance can degrade.

### Mechanism 2: Cross-Embodiment Gradient Conflict
- **Claim:** When training a single policy on heterogeneous robot data, gradient updates from different morphologies conflict, leading to negative transfer; this worsens as suboptimal data proportion and robot diversity increase.
- **Mechanism:** Each robot’s data generates a per-embodiment actor gradient g_τ. The sum of these gradients across robots is used for the policy update. However, due to morphological differences, the optimal update direction for one robot may oppose that for another. These conflicts are measured by pairwise cosine similarity C[τ_i,τ_j] = ⟨g_i, g_j⟩/(‖g_i‖‖g_j‖). Negative C indicates misalignment. More suboptimal data and more diverse morphologies increase the frequency and severity of negative C, causing some robots’ learning signals to cancel and overall performance to drop.
- **Core assumption:** Shared policy parameters force a single update to satisfy multiple objectives, and gradient alignment is not guaranteed across morphologies.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "However, as the proportion of suboptimal data and the number of robot types increase, we observe that conflicting gradients across morphologies begin to impede learning."
  - [section 5.1] Figure 2 shows the fraction of negative pairwise gradient cosines rising with suboptimal data and with added robot diversity. Table 2 correlates transfer gain (cross-embodiment vs single-robot performance) with average gradient cosine (r=0.815 for IQL, r=0.766 for TD3+BC), confirming that negative transfer coincides with gradient conflict.
  - [corpus] Related multi-task learning work (e.g., gradient surgery) addresses similar issues, but the specific link to cross-embodiment suboptimal data is unique to this paper.
- **Break condition:** If all robots are morphologically nearly identical, gradients align and conflicts are minimal; also, if dataset is entirely expert, conflicts may be less pronounced (though not absent).

### Mechanism 3: Embodiment Grouping Mitigates Conflict
- **Claim:** Grouping robots by morphological similarity and updating the policy sequentially per group reduces gradient conflicts and improves performance, especially in high-suboptimal settings.
- **Mechanism:** Robots are clustered based on a morphology graph distance (Fused Gromov–Wasserstein). During training, after a global critic update, the actor is updated group-by-group: for each group, the batch is filtered to that group’s data and the actor loss computed and minimized. This ensures that within each update, the gradient aggregates only morphologically similar robots, which have more aligned gradients (positive correlation between embodiment similarity and gradient cosine). By avoiding simultaneous updates from dissimilar robots, destructive interference is prevented.
- **Core assumption:** Morphological similarity (captured by graph distance) correlates with gradient alignment; grouping based on this similarity yields within-group gradients that are mostly positively aligned.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "To mitigate this, we introduce an embodiment-based grouping strategy... This simple, static grouping substantially reduces inter-robot conflicts and outperforms existing conflict-resolution methods."
  - [section 5.3] Algorithm 1 details the procedure. Section 6.1 shows IQL+EG achieving the best average performance, with large gains on 70% Suboptimal datasets (e.g., 51.19 vs 36.62 on Forward). Ablations in Table 4 show EG outperforms random and heuristic grouping.
  - [section 5.2] Figure 3c shows Pearson r=0.63 between embodiment similarity and mean gradient cosine for IQL (and r=0.711 for TD3+BC), supporting that grouping by FGW aligns gradients.
  - [corpus] Compared methods: PCGrad and SEL; EG outperforms them on these benchmarks.
- **Break condition:** If the morphology clustering fails to capture gradient-relevant features (e.g., coarse leg count split performs poorly as shown), or if groups are too small (few data per group) leading to overfitting/instability, benefits diminish. Also, if the number of groups M is too high, computational cost increases with limited gain (Figure 5).

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Offline Reinforcement Learning
- **Why needed here:** The paper uses offline RL as the core learning paradigm to exploit both expert and suboptimal data across robots. Understanding how IQL or TD3+BC works is essential to grasp why they succeed over BC and how grouping interacts with the learning objective.
- **Quick check question:** In IQL, what is the role of the expectile regression on the value function, and how does it avoid evaluating out-of-distribution actions?

### Concept: Cross-Embodiment Representation (URMA)
- **Why needed here:** The URMA encoder handles heterogeneous observations (different joint counts, action dimensions) by factorizing into general and robot-specific streams using descriptor-conditioned attention. This architecture enables a single policy to condition on morphology descriptors, which is fundamental to the cross-embodiment setup.
- **Quick check question:** How does URMA process variable-length sets of joint and foot observations into a fixed-size latent representation?

### Concept: Gradient Conflict in Multi-Task Learning
- **Why needed here:** The paper frames the cross-embodiment challenge as gradient conflicts between robots. This concept explains why naive simultaneous updates degrade performance and motivates grouping. It connects to broader multi-task optimization literature.
- **Quick check question:** What does a negative pairwise cosine similarity between per-robot gradients imply about their update directions, and why might this become more common with suboptimal data?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Data pool (heterogeneous robot trajectories) -> URMA encoder (state encoding) -> Actor (policy) -> actions; Data pool -> Critic (Q, V) -> value estimates; Robot descriptors -> Graph builder -> FGW calculator -> Clustering -> Groups; Training loop: global batch -> Critic update; Groups (shuffled) -> Actor update per group.

### Critical path
1. Preprocessing: Build robot graphs, compute pairwise FGW distances, cluster to get M groups.
2. During each training iteration:
   a. Sample a minibatch from the entire pooled dataset.
   b. Update critic(s) using all samples (standard offline RL critic loss).
   c. Shuffle group order.
   d. For each group: filter the minibatch to transitions from robots in that group; compute actor loss on that subset; perform one or more actor gradient steps.
3. Repeat until convergence.

### Design tradeoffs
- Static vs dynamic grouping: Static grouping based on morphology is simple and computationally cheap; dynamic grouping could adapt but adds overhead and may be unstable offline.
- Actor-only vs actor+critic grouping: Actor-only grouping sufficient; grouping the critic provides no clear benefit and increases compute/time.
- Number of groups M: Small M (2–4) yields strong gains; larger M increases compute and may fragment data, leading to diminishing/negative returns.
- Grouping criterion: FGW distance on morphology graphs outperforms heuristics (e.g., leg count) because it captures richer structural and actuation similarities.

### Failure signatures
- No improvement with EG: Check within-group pairwise gradient cosines; if they are not higher than cross-group, clustering may misrepresent gradient alignment.
- Performance collapse when increasing M: May indicate too few samples per group; reduce M or increase batch size.
- Slow convergence/high variance: Could be due to imbalanced group sizes; ensure each group has sufficient samples.
- Transfer to new robots fails: Might indicate morphology similarity does not predict policy similarity for the target; consider whether the new robot falls near an existing group.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Baseline comparison:** Train IQL and IQL+EG on the 70% Suboptimal Forward dataset with M=4. Expect IQL+EG to achieve significantly higher final return (~51 vs ~36).
2. **Ablation on grouping strategy:** Compare EG (FGW) against random grouping and a heuristic split (bipeds/quadrupeds/hexapods/torso-less) on the same dataset. Expect EG to outperform both.
3. **Gradient alignment validation:** During training, compute average pairwise cosine similarity within groups and across groups for IQL+EG. Within-group cosines should be substantially higher than cross-group, confirming grouping aligns updates.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** Does Embodiment Grouping transfer successfully to real-world robotic systems despite sim-to-real gaps?  
  *Basis in paper:* The authors state that evaluation is restricted to MuJoCo in simulation, leaving sim-to-real transfer untested.  
  *Why unresolved:* Real-world complexities (friction, sensor noise, hardware imperfections) may degrade EG’s effectiveness.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Benchmark EG-trained policies on real robots across various terrains and compare against baselines.

- **Open Question 2:** Can EG be effectively applied to manipulation tasks beyond locomotion?  
  *Basis in paper:* The conclusion notes that broader domains such as manipulation remain untested.  
  *Why unresolved:* Manipulation involves different contact dynamics, action spaces, and possibly heterogeneous end-effectors; gradient conflicts might manifest differently.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Construct a cross-embodiment manipulation dataset with diverse grippers/arms, apply EG, and measure success rates relative to baselines.

- **Open Question 3:** Does a dynamic grouping strategy—where robot groups are updated during training based on learning progress—outperform static FGW-based grouping?  
  *Basis in paper:* Future work: improve EG with dynamic grouping that uses embodiment information together with learning progress and data characteristics.  
  *Why unresolved:* Static grouping based solely on initial morphology may not adapt to evolving policy or data distribution.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Implement an algorithm that periodically recomputes groups using current gradient similarities or performance metrics; evaluate on high-suboptimal datasets and compare returns and training stability.

- **Open Question 4:** Does combining EG with group-specific contrastive representation learning enhance within-group transfer and overall performance?  
  *Basis in paper:* Future work: learning group-specific embodiment or task representations within each group, for example using contrastive objectives, could further strengthen within-group sharing.  
  *Why unresolved:* EG groups robots but does not explicitly align latent representations; contrastive losses might encourage additional structure.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Add an auxiliary contrastive loss that pulls together embeddings of data from the same group while pushing apart others; train on challenging datasets and assess performance improvement.

- **Open Question 5:** Is the Fused Gromov–Wasserstein (FGW) distance the optimal metric for grouping robots, or could alternative similarity measures yield better performance?  
  *Basis in paper:* Inferred: The authors use FGW to compute embodiment similarities and show a correlation with gradient alignment, but they do not compare to other metrics.  
  *Why unresolved:* FGW balances graph structure and node features, but other factors like dynamics parameters or kinematic edit distances might better capture gradient-relevant similarities.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Compute robot similarities using alternative metrics (e.g., dynamics-based, kinematic distance, learned embeddings), apply EG with these groupings, and compare final returns and training stability on diverse datasets.

## Limitations
- **URMA architecture details unspecified:** Exact hidden layer sizes and network dimensions for URMA modules are not provided, requiring inference or guesswork to reproduce.
- **PPO dataset generation thresholds unclear:** The precise reward thresholds defining "90% performance" and early stopping criteria are partially underspecified.
- **FGW distance assumption may not generalize:** The grouping relies on the assumption that morphology similarity (via FGW) aligns with gradient alignment; this correlation might not hold for robot families outside the studied locomotion benchmark.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (Offline RL advantage reweighting):** High – Strong empirical support across datasets and algorithms (Table 1, Figure 2).
- **Mechanism 2 (Cross-embodiment gradient conflict):** Medium – Correlation evidence robust (Table 2, Figure 2), but causal attribution solely to morphology is not fully isolated.
- **Mechanism 3 (Embodiment Grouping mitigation):** Medium-High – Clear empirical gains (Table 3, Figure 5) and alignment correlation (Figure 3c), but depends on correct FGW implementation and may not scale to very large numbers of groups.
