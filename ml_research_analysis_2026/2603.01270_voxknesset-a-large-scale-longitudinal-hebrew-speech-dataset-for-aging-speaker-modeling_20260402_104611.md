# VoxKnesset: A Large-Scale Longitudinal Hebrew Speech Dataset for Aging Speaker Modeling

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.01270
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01270
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Introduces a 2,300-hour Hebrew parliamentary speech dataset with verified longitudinal spans up to 15 years, enabling the first large-scale evaluation of speaker verification and age prediction degradation under natural vocal aging.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents VoxKnesset, a unique large-scale Hebrew speech corpus derived from Israeli parliamentary proceedings (2009–2025) with meticulously verified speaker identities. The dataset's core value is its longitudinal structure: it contains 393 speakers with multiple recordings across years, some spanning 15 years. Benchmarks reveal that state-of-the-art speaker verification systems experience a significant performance drop (EER rising from 2.15% to 4.58%) as the temporal gap between enrollment and test audio increases. Furthermore, models trained to predict age in a standard cross-sectional manner fail to capture within-speaker aging, predicting deltas of only 1–2 years regardless of true elapsed time, while models trained on longitudinal pairs successfully recover a meaningful temporal signal. The publicly released dataset and benchmarks address a critical gap in aging-robust speech research.

## Method Summary
The authors curated VoxKnesset by ingesting parliamentary audio/video and transcripts, extracting audio segments via forced alignment with Whisper, and rigorously matching speech segments to speaker identities using official Knesset metadata. This produced a longitudinal subset of speaker-attributed, time-stamped segments. For benchmarking, they extracted embeddings from models like WavLM and ECAPA-TDNN. They evaluated (a) cross-sectional age regression, (b) longitudinal age delta prediction, and (c) speaker verification performance as a function of year gap between enrollment and test sessions, comparing models trained on different data regimes.

## Key Results
- Speaker verification EER more than doubles from ~2.15% to ~4.58% over a 15-year temporal gap for top models (WavLM-based).
- Age regressors trained on standard cross-sectional data (e.g., TIMIT) fail on within-speaker aging prediction, with estimated deltas plateauing at 1–2 years.
- Models trained directly on longitudinal VoxKnesset pairs recover a monotonic aging signal, predicting up to ~10 years of elapsed time (Wav2Vec2-XLSR-1B).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Longitudinal Data Isolates Vocal Drift
- **Claim:** Verified longitudinal data with repeated speaker appearances enables measurement of within-speaker vocal drift over time.
- **Mechanism:** The dataset's structure provides multiple recordings per speaker across years. By comparing embeddings from the same speaker at different ages, models can isolate age-related changes from static speaker identity.
- **Core assumption:** Vocal aging is a continuous, measurable process that manifests consistently within a speaker over multi-year spans.
- **Evidence anchors:** 393 speakers with spans up to 15 years; median span 3.4 years. The longitudinal subset is explicitly constructed from speaker-attributed segments.
- **Break condition:** Speaker matching errors in the curation pipeline would conflate different individuals, corrupting within-speaker signals.

### Mechanism 2: Cross-Sectional Training Lacks Temporal Signal
- **Claim:** Models trained on cross-sectional age data fail to generalize to within-speaker aging prediction because they primarily learn between-speaker static features rather than time-varying traits.
- **Mechanism:** Cross-sectional training optimizes for absolute age from a single snapshot, which is confounded by permanent inter-speaker differences. When applied to longitudinal deltas, the model's output plateaus because it has not learned to isolate the incremental aging signal.
- **Core assumption:** Age-related vocal change is a subtle, temporal shift superimposed on a larger static speaker-specific vocal signature.
- **Evidence anchors:** Cross-sectionally trained regressors show predicted deltas plateauing at 1–2 years regardless of true elapsed time.
- **Break condition:** If the aging signal were not distinct from speaker identity, cross-sectional models might show some delta sensitivity. The plateau indicates a misalignment between training objective (absolute age) and test condition (relative change).

### Mechanism 3: Aging Induces Embedding Divergence in Verification
- **Claim:** Speaker verification systems degrade as the temporal gap increases because aging-induced vocal drift causes embeddings of the same speaker to diverge.
- **Mechanism:** Verification systems compute embedding similarity. Aging alters vocal characteristics (e.g., fundamental frequency, formants), moving the embedding away from the enrolled representation, increasing false rejections.
- **Core assumption:** Modern speaker embeddings are not fully invariant to age progression.
- **Evidence anchors:** EER curves show a clear rise from 2.15% to 4.58% over 15 years across multiple models.
- **Break condition:** If embeddings were perfectly age-invariant, EER would remain constant. The observed increase confirms aging as a factor, but channel drift remains a potential confound.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Longitudinal vs. Cross-Sectional Data**
  - **Why needed here:** The dataset's value lies in its longitudinal nature, allowing analysis of *within-speaker change*, which cross-sectional snapshots cannot provide.
  - **Quick check question:** If you have only one recording per speaker from 100 different people at different ages, can you measure how a *single person's* voice changes from age 40 to 55? Why or why not?

- **Concept: Speaker Embedding Invariance**
  - **Why needed here:** Speaker verification relies on embeddings being consistent for the same speaker. This paper investigates a violation of that assumption—aging—and quantifies its impact.
  - **Quick check question:** What does an Equal Error Rate (EER) of 5% mean in a speaker verification system? How would you expect EER to change if all test recordings were 10 years older than the enrollment sample, assuming aging affects embeddings?

- **Concept: Domain Generalization in Age Prediction**
  - **Why needed here:** The paper tests if an age predictor trained on one corpus works on another. The finding that VoxKnesset is the most transferable target suggests the age signal may be more language-agnostic than other data-specific artifacts.
  - **Quick check question:** What does a small performance drop (∆R²) in the leave-one-dataset-out (LODO) experiment imply about the underlying signal (age) versus dataset-specific noise?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Raw A/V & transcript ingestion -> Audio extraction & timestamp correction -> Word-level forced alignment (Whisper/Stable-Whisper) -> Confidence filtering & segment extraction -> Speaker matching (Knesset metadata) -> Longitudinal subset validation -> Embedding extraction (WavLM, ECAPA, etc.) -> Task-specific heads (Ridge regressor, MLP for delta, verification scoring).
- **Critical path:** The most sequence-sensitive steps are forced alignment (step 3) and speaker matching (step 5). Errors here propagate to all downstream analyses. Longitudinal analysis (step 8) depends entirely on the integrity of speaker-time pairs from step 5.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Alignment confidence threshold:** Higher thresholds increase segment quality but reduce total hours and speaker coverage.
  - **Segment duration:** A 30-second minimum ensures robust embedding extraction but may exclude short parliamentary interruptions.
  - **Train/test split:** Splitting by speaker (not session) prevents data leakage for verification but reduces the longitudinal speaker pool if many have only one session.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Poor alignment:** Segments with low Whisper confidence or timestamp drift will contain misaligned text/audio. Check confidence score distributions.
  - **Speaker matching error:** Incorrectly attributed segments create false longitudinal pairs. Validate via spot-checks against known MK speech patterns.
  - **Channel confounding:** Systematic degradation over time might reflect equipment changes, not biology. Compare degradation curves across models; parallel declines suggest a channel effect.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Reproduce dataset statistics: Verify speaker count, total hours, and longitudinal span distribution using released metadata.
  2. Replicate cross-sectional age prediction baseline: Train a Ridge regressor on VoxKnesset WavLM embeddings; target MAE ~6.3 years.
  3. Replicate longitudinal verification degradation: For a fixed model (e.g., WavLM-Large), compute EER for test sessions at increasing year gaps from enrollment; aim for EER rising from ~2% to ~4.5% over 15 years.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Channel vs. Biological Aging:** To what extent does equipment/channel drift contribute to the observed EER increase versus true physiological vocal aging? The paper notes recording conditions evolved over 16 years but cannot separate these factors.
2. **Model-Specific Longitudinal Capacity:** Why does Wav2Vec2-XLSR-1B recover a stronger longitudinal signal than WavLM-Large or ECAPA-TDNN, despite WavLM's superior cross-sectional performance? The reason for this variance is not analyzed.
3. **Cross-Register Generalization:** Does the longitudinal aging signal generalize across speech registers (e.g., parliamentary debate vs. casual conversation), or is it specific to the formal parliamentary domain? All data is from a single register.
4. **Aging-Aware Verification:** Can an "aging-aware" speaker verification system, explicitly trained to compensate for vocal drift, reduce the observed EER degradation? The paper documents the problem but does not evaluate mitigation.
5. **Representational vs. Data Limitation:** Does the cross-sectional age prediction failure reflect a fundamental limitation of speech representations, or solely a lack of longitudinal data in training corpora? Success of longitudinal training suggests the information exists but is unused by cross-sectional objectives.

## Limitations
- **Channel confounding:** Performance degradation over time may reflect evolving broadcast equipment/encoding, not just biology—this is not fully disentangled.
- **Hebrew-specificity:** Aging patterns may be language-dependent; findings may not generalize to non-Semitic languages.
- **Speaker matching errors:** Despite pipeline validation, residual misattributions could corrupt longitudinal pairs, especially for less prominent speakers.

## Confidence
- **Dataset creation & scale:** High — well-documented curation, verified demographics, public release.
- **Cross-sectional failure on longitudinal deltas:** Medium — plateau effect is clear, but could partially stem from model architecture limitations.
- **Verification degradation over time:** Medium — robust trend across models, but channel drift remains a plausible alternative explanation.

## Next Checks
1. **Speaker matching audit:** Randomly sample 100 longitudinal speaker pairs; verify via external Knesset records (video archives, transcripts) that segments belong to the same individual across years.
2. **Channel invariance test:** Re-run verification degradation analysis after normalizing audio to a fixed standard (resampling, spectral flattening). Persistence of degradation strengthens biological aging claim.
3. **Hebrew aging pattern replication:** Train age delta predictor on VoxAging (English/Mandarin) embeddings; test on VoxKnesset longitudinal pairs. Poor transfer would indicate language-specific aging signals.
