# Wrong Code, Right Structure: Learning Netlist Representations from Imperfect LLM-Generated RTL
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.09161
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09161
- Reference count: 31
- Primary result: LLM-augmented noisy RTL synthesis produces netlists that preserve structural patterns; the framework achieves state-of-the-art netlist representation learning, e.g., 93.79% F1-Macro on operator-level sub-circuit identification (3.64% absolute improvement) and 68.35% F1 on IP-level CPU boundary (8.31% gain over unfiltered LLM data, 10.07% over rule-based).

## Executive Summary
Hardware design automation suffers from a scarcity of labeled netlist data due to IP protection and costly manual labeling. This paper introduces an end-to-end framework that leverages large language models (LLMs) to generate vast quantities of RTL code, which, despite potential functional errors, synthesizes into netlists that retain structurally informative patterns indicative of intended functionality. By filtering and selecting diverse samples, the authors train a graph neural network (GNN) on this noisy synthetic corpus and demonstrate superior generalization to real-world netlists. Evaluations on sub-circuit boundary identification and IP-level component classification show significant improvements: the best LLM-augmented dataset (LLM-Aug-t2) reaches 93.79% F1-Macro, a 3.64% absolute increase over the baseline, while on a challenging IP-level task involving an unseen CPU in an SoC, the filtered LLM data (LLM-Filtered) achieves 68.35% F1, an 8.31% gain over unfiltered LLM data and far surpassing rule-based augmentation (58.28% F1). These results validate the core insight that structural patterns in netlists are partially decoupled from exact functional correctness.

## Method Summary
The framework comprises three stages: data augmentation, netlist encoding, and GNN training. First, LLMs generate diverse RTL designs from specifications; a synthesis loop with a debug agent ensures synthesizability using Synopsys Design Compiler. Second, two selection mechanisms curate the synthetic netlists: a cell-count filter removes outliers, and a structural similarity filter discards netlists with low cosine similarity (based on GNN embeddings) to a reference golden netlist. Additionally, an LLM-based voting mechanism ranks batches of designs by architectural diversity and complexity, selecting top-k to promote varied implementations. Third, netlists are converted to undirected graphs with node features (connectivity, gate function, degree) and fed to a GraphSAINT sampler and a GNN architecture (from GNN-RE) for scalable training. The trained GNN produces node embeddings used by downstream classifiers for node-level (sub-circuit boundary) and graph-level (IP identity) tasks.

## Key Results
- Operator-level sub-circuit identification: LLM-Aug-t2 achieves 93.79% F1-Macro, a 3.64% absolute improvement over the baseline model trained on scarce high-quality data.
- IP-level scalability (CPU boundary in an unseen SoC): LLM-Filtered dataset yields 68.35% F1, an 8.31% gain over unfiltered LLM data (60.44%) and significantly outperforms rule-based augmentation (58.28% F1).
- Models trained on the LLM-augmented corpus generalize effectively to real-world netlists, matching or exceeding performance of those trained on limited ground-truth labels.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The approach is underpinned by three key insights. First, functionally imperfect LLM-generated RTL synthesizes into netlists that preserve structural patterns indicative of the intended function. Even when RTL contains bugs such as incorrect conditional assignments, these errors often manifest as local logic changes rather than wholesale architectural alterations; synthesis retains higher-level topological motifs (e.g., connectivity patterns of a multiplier). This decoupling of functional correctness from structural relevance allows the GNN to learn invariant features from noisy data. Second, a structural similarity filter—computing cosine similarity of graph embeddings between generated and golden netlists—effectively discards samples that are semantically unrelated, improving precision as evidenced by the jump from LLM-Raw (60.44% F1) to LLM-Filtered (68.35% F1) on the IP-level task. Third, an architecture voting mechanism, where an LLM evaluates and selects designs based on diversity and complexity, ensures the training corpus includes varied implementations (e.g., different adder styles). This diversity teaches the GNN to recognize functional boundaries across architectural styles, leading to superior generalization on test sets with variant implementations.

## Foundational Learning
- **Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) for circuit graphs**  
  *Why needed*: Netlists are naturally represented as directed graphs (gates as nodes, wires as edges). GNNs perform message-passing to aggregate neighborhood information, learning embeddings that encode structural and functional properties of circuits.  
  *Quick check*: How does a GNN update a node's embedding over multiple layers, and why is this suitable for capturing circuit hierarchy?
- **Data augmentation for scarcity**  
  *Why needed*: Real netlists are IP-protected and unlabeled, causing a data bottleneck. The framework uses LLMs to generate vast quantities of RTL (with possible errors), synthesizes them, and filters to create a labeled training set, directly addressing scarcity.  
  *Quick check*: What is the fundamental limitation of rule-based augmentation (e.g., logic rewriting) that LLM-based augmentation overcomes?
- **Supervised vs. Self-supervised learning paradigms**  
  *Why needed*: The paper's framework is supervised, requiring a large labeled corpus; this contrasts with self-supervised pre-training (e.g., DeepGate2/3) that uses unlabeled data. Understanding this context clarifies why the authors pursue LLM-based data generation.  
  *Quick check*: In circuit representation learning, what is a typical pretext task for self-supervised learning, and why might supervised learning with more labeled data be preferable for certain downstream tasks?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: RTL Generator (LLM) -> Synthesis Loop (Design Compiler + debug agent) -> Filtering Module (cell count + structural similarity) -> Voting Module (LLM evaluator) -> Graph Builder -> GNN Trainer (GraphSAINT) -> Task Classifiers

Critical path: Specification → (LLM Generation → Synthesis) × N → Filtering/Voting Selection → Graph Conversion → GNN Training → Downstream Classification.

Design tradeoffs: The framework balances filtering (which increases precision but may discard valid variants) against voting (which promotes diversity but can introduce noise). It also trades off quality versus scale: more generated data improves coverage but requires robust filtering to maintain signal-to-noise ratio. Finally, the choice of LLM (e.g., temperature) influences creativity and error rate, affecting downstream performance.

Failure signatures: 
- Low F1 on the IP-level task indicates inadequate architectural diversity or over/under-filtering.
- High variance across different LLM-Aug datasets suggests instability in generation or selection.
- Poor generalization to unseen architectures points to voting mechanism failures or an ineffective structural similarity metric.

First 3 experiments:
1. Baseline replacement validation: Generate 5 LLM-Aug datasets (t1–t5) matching the node count of the original baseline; LLM-Aug-t2 surpasses the baseline's 90.15% F1-Macro, achieving 93.79%.
2. Generalization test with architectural variants: Compare baseline, LLM-Raw (no filter), and Voting on a test set containing diverse implementations; Voting achieves 94.45% F1-Micro, demonstrating the benefit of architectural diversity.
3. IP-level scalability test: Train on data augmented from PicoRV32 and test on the NEORV32 SoC's CPU boundary; LLM-Filtered attains 68.35% F1, outperforming LLM-Raw (60.44%) and rule-based augmentation (58.28%).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Optimal threshold tuning**: How should the structural similarity threshold τ be set for different circuit types to balance data quality and diversity? The paper uses a fixed τ but does not study its sensitivity.
2. **LLM voting reliability**: Do systematic biases exist in the LLM-based architecture voting mechanism, and how consistent are its assessments compared to human experts? The paper provides no validation of the LLM evaluator's alignment with true diversity.
3. **Cross-IP generalization**: Does the framework generalize to non-CPU IPs (e.g., cryptographic modules, memory controllers) and across fundamentally different hardware ISAs or architectures? Generalization was only tested between two RISC-V CPU cores.
4. **Boundary of structural robustness**: For what circuit characteristics (e.g., control-heavy, high sequential content) does the core assumption—that netlist structure is robust to functional errors—break down? The paper does not characterize when structural fidelity no longer predicts functional intent.

## Limitations
- The paper does not disclose details of LLM prompting, debug loops, or the exact selection method for the threshold τ, hindering reproducibility.
- Generalization experiments are confined to CPU-boundary IPs; applicability to other hardware domains remains unproven.
- The central claim that structural patterns decouple from functional correctness is supported empirically but lacks formal analysis, and may fail for circuits where errors drastically alter architecture.

## Confidence
- Structural decoupling (core premise): Medium (evidence from experiments, but mechanistic proof indirect)
- Filter efficacy: Medium (clear performance gains, but τ choice is opaque)
- Voting diversity: Low/Medium (improves variant generalization, but LLM evaluator's reliability unvalidated)

## Next Checks
1. Conduct a threshold sensitivity study: vary τ across a range (e.g., 0.5–0.9) and measure downstream F1 to identify an optimal operating window and potential adaptive methods.
2. Audit the LLM voting mechanism: Compare its diversity rankings with those from human hardware experts or objective metrics like graph edit distance on a sampled set of generated RTLs.
3. Evaluate cross-IP generalization: Apply the full framework to at least two additional unseen IP types (e.g., a memory controller and a UART) to test robustness beyond CPU designs.
