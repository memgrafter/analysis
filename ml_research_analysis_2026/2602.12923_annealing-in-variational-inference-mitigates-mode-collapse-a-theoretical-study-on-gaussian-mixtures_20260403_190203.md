# Annealing in variational inference mitigates mode collapse: A theoretical study on Gaussian mixtures

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.12923  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12923  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: Annealing schedules with a slow enough rate (β_i⁻¹/t₀) prevent mode collapse in variational inference by enabling explosive separation of means in the high-temperature regime.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses mode collapse in variational inference (VI), where minimizing the reverse KL divergence causes the variational distribution to miss modes of a multimodal target. The authors analyze annealed VI—tempering the target with inverse temperature β(t) during optimization—in a tractable setting: a bimodal Gaussian mixture target with a symmetric Gaussian mixture student. By assuming student variances track β(t)⁻¹ quasi-statically, they reduce the high-dimensional gradient flow to a three-dimensional ODE for summary statistics (mean alignments). The analysis of high- and low-temperature regimes yields a sharp formula for the probability of mode collapse, showing it depends on an integral I(β) that captures cumulative separation work during the high-temperature phase. For exponential annealing, they derive a trade-off: a lower initial temperature (higher temperature) only helps if the annealing time is increased proportionally. In the limit of very high initial temperature, the collapse probability depends solely on the annealing rate β_i⁻¹/t₀, not on β_i alone. Numerical experiments on Gaussian mixtures and RealNVP normalizing flows confirm the theory, establishing annealing rate as the key parameter for robustly mitigating mode collapse.

## Method Summary
The study formulates annealed VI as minimizing D_KL(q_θ || π^β) with β(t) increasing from a small β_i to 1. For a symmetric Gaussian mixture student (two isotropic Gaussians), the loss is analytic, and gradient updates can be expressed via a JKO scheme. The dynamics of mean overlaps m₁ = μ₁·μ*/R², m₂ = μ₂·μ*/R², and dot product s = m₁m₂ are studied. Under the quasi-static assumption σ₁² = σ₂² = β(t)⁻¹, the system reduces to a three-dimensional ODE. This ODE reveals a saddle point at (0,0) in the high-temperature regime (β ≪ R⁻²) with an unstable eigenvalue ~√{βR²}, causing exponential separation of means. At a critical temperature β ≈ α/R² (α≈0.608), the saddle becomes stable and the dynamics switch to low-temperature attraction to the two modes. The probability that the means have separated enough by that critical time determines collapse. This leads to a closed-form collapse probability p(β)=∫_{−2εe^{-I(t₁)}}^{2εe^{-I(t₁)}} N(0,1/d) dx, where I(t₁)=∫₀^{t₁} √{β(s)R²/(2π)} ds and t₁ is the transition time. For exponential schedules β(t)=β_i^{-t/t₀}, I(t₁) simplifies and shows that the annealing rate β_i⁻¹/t₀ governs collapse. Experiments on Gaussian mixtures (100 random restarts per schedule) and RealNVP flows (8-layer affine coupling) confirm the theoretical predictions.

## Key Results
- A precise collapse probability formula p(β)=∫_{−2εe^{-I(t₁)}}^{2εe^{-I(t₁)}} N(0,1/d) dx is derived, linking collapse to the integral I(β) of √{β(s)} during the high-temperature phase.
- For exponential annealing in the high-temperature limit (β_i ≪ R⁻²), p(β) depends only on the annealing rate β_i⁻¹/t₀; lowering β_i requires a proportional increase in t₀ to maintain the same collapse probability.
- Numerical results on both Gaussian mixtures and RealNVP flows match the theory, demonstrating that annealing rate is the key design parameter for avoiding mode collapse.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Entropic separation before cross-entropy locking**  
The reverse KL loss balances two forces: entropy of q (which repels the means) and cross-entropy (which attracts each mean to a target mode). At high temperatures (β ≪ R⁻²), entropy dominates, making the symmetric configuration an unstable saddle; small perturbations cause explosive separation. At low temperatures (β ≫ R⁻²), cross-entropy dominates and the two modes become separate attractors. Annealing that lingers in the high-temperature regime long enough allows the unstable separation to complete before the system gets trapped. If annealing ascends too quickly (high β_i, short t₀), the system passes the critical temperature β ≈ α/R² before separation is sufficient, after which collapse becomes irreversible. Core assumption: variances quasi-statically follow σ² = β⁻¹. Evidence: ODE analysis shows an unstable eigenvalue scaling as √{βR²} and exponential divergence |μ₁-μ₂| ∝ exp(∫ √{βR²/(2π)} dt).

**Mechanism 2: Collapse probability as a threshold crossing**  
The instability drives mean difference growth, but random initialization (μ₁,μ₂ on sphere with Gaussian-projected overlaps of variance 1/d) yields tiny initial separation. The means must achieve a minimum separation 2ε at transition time t₁ (β = α/R²) to avoid collapse, where ε = (1/(2R²))log(w*/(1-w*)) accounts for weight imbalance. The mean difference evolves approximately as |μ₁-μ₂| ∝ exp(I(t₁)), with I(t₁)=∫₀^{t₁} √{β(s)R²/(2π)} ds. Escape condition: I(t₁) > log(2ε) + (1/2)log(d). Under Gaussian approximation of initial overlaps, this yields p(β)=∫_{−2εe^{-I(t₁)}}^{2εe^{-I(t₁)}} N(0,1/d) dx. Core assumptions: Linearization around saddle holds until t₁; initial overlaps are i.i.d. Gaussian with variance 1/d. Evidence: Figures 2,5,7 show excellent agreement between empirical collapse rates and the theoretical formula.

**Mechanism 3: Annealing rate supremacy**  
For exponential schedules β(t)=β_i^{-t/t₀} with very high initial temperature (β_i → 0), I(t₁) ∼ (√2/π)(t₀⁻¹ log(1/β_i))⁻¹, depending only on the ratio β_i⁻¹/t₀. Thus, in the high-temperature limit, the collapse probability is a function of the annealing rate alone. Lowering β_i does not inherently help; it requires a proportional increase in t₀ to maintain the same annealing rate. Core assumption: β_i ≪ R⁻² and exponential schedule. Evidence: Experimentally, collapse curves for different β_i collapse onto a single curve when plotted against β_i⁻¹/t₀ (Figures 5,7).

## Foundational Learning

**Variational Inference with reverse KL**  
- *Why needed*: The analysis operates within VI where the objective is D_KL(q||π) (ELBO). This mode-seeking behavior causes collapse.  
- *Quick check*: Why does minimizing D_KL(q||π) tend to miss modes compared to minimizing D_KL(π||q)? (Reverse KL penalizes q where π is low, so q avoids under-covering; forward KL penalizes q where π is high, encouraging coverage of all modes.)

**Annealing / Simulated annealing**  
- *Why needed*: The core intervention is temperature scheduling; β controls the balance between entropy (repulsion) and cross-entropy (attraction).  
- *Quick check*: If β is too large initially, what dynamical regime is missed? (The high-temperature regime where entropy drives explosive separation; skipping it leads to early collapse.)

**High-dimensional random geometry**  
- *Why needed*: The reduction to low-dimensional ODEs relies on random vectors in high dimensions concentrating dot products around zero with variance 1/d, making the symmetric saddle almost always the starting point.  
- *Quick check*: Why does increasing dimension d make mode collapse more likely even with an expressive student? (Initial fluctuations become of order 1/√d, which vanish as d→∞, so the system starts arbitrarily close to the saddle and requires more separation work to escape.)

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**:  
Target π → Bimodal isotropic Gaussian mixture (μ*, σ²=1, weights w*,1−w*), separation R=||μ*||  
Student q_θ → Gaussian mixture (μ₁,μ₂,σ₁,σ₂) or RealNVP flow with Gaussian base  
Loss: L(θ,β)=D_KL(q_θ || π^β)  
Annealing: β(t)=min(β_i^{-t/t₀},1)  
Optimizer: Gradient flow (theoretical) or SGD with JKO for Gaussians  

**Critical path**:  
1. Initialize μ₁,μ₂ uniformly on sphere S^{d-1}(R); set σ₁,σ₂ = 1/β_i  
2. Set β = β_i ≪ 1/R² (high temp)  
3. Run optimization while annealing β→1  
4. Monitor m₁=μ₁·μ*/R², m₂, s=μ₁·μ₂/R²; or proxy V=Var[x·μ*/R]  
5. Check final V > 1.6 (for R=3) indicates multimodality captured  

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- Lower β_i (higher temp) needs longer t₀; otherwise ineffective.  
- Higher β_i reduces required t₀ but increases collapse risk.  
- t₀ too short → insufficient separation.  
- t₀ too long → wasted compute; benefit saturates after I(β) passes threshold.  
- Robust: Scale β_i and t₀ together to keep annealing rate β_i⁻¹/t₀ constant.  

**Failure signatures**:  
- Early: σ²β(t) deviates from 1 (quasi-static breakdown).  
- Mid: m₁,m₂ remain near 0 and symmetric.  
- Late: |μ₁-μ₂| → 0; final V ≈ 1 (single mode).  
- Numerical: Loss decreases but samples all in one lobe.  

**First 3 experiments**:  
1. *Vanilla VI baseline*: β=1 fixed, σ₁,₂ initialized to 1 → rapid collapse.  
2. *Covering initialization*: σ₁,₂ initialized large (∼R) → still collapses, σ shrinks to 1.  
3. *Anneal with exponential schedule*: β(t)=β_i^{-t/t₀} with β_i⁻¹=10R², t₀=500 → avoids collapse, σ tracks 1/β. Then sweep β_i, t₀ to map tradeoff.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Does annealing mitigate mode collapse for targets with more than two modes, and how does the temperature-annealing trade-off generalize?
2. How does mode collapse depend on mismatch between student and target mode counts?
3. Are these annealing insights applicable to VI with diffusion models?
4. Does the quasi-static variance approximation hold for RealNVP-like families, and if not, what is the correct reduction?

## Limitations
- The quasi-static variance assumption (σ² = β⁻¹) is only validated for exponential schedules; its validity for other schedules or normalizing flows is unclear.
- The theoretical reduction to a 3D ODE assumes a symmetric Gaussian mixture student and high-dimensional limit; finite-dimensional and asymmetric cases are not analyzed.
- The constant α ≈ 0.608 in the collapse formula is fitted; its dependence on target parameters (R, w*) and dimension d lacks theoretical derivation.
