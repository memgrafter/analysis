# A Computationally Efficient Learning of Artificial Intelligence System Reliability Considering Error Propagation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.18201
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18201
- **Reference count**: 14
- **Primary result**: Proposes a composite likelihood expectation-maximization (CLEM) algorithm for efficient parameter estimation in AI reliability modeling with error propagation, achieving near-EM accuracy at significantly reduced computational cost.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of modeling error propagation (EP) in AI systems, particularly in autonomous vehicle perception pipelines. The authors develop a physics-based simulation framework with error injection to generate reliability data that captures cross-stage error interactions. They introduce an intensity-decomposition model that distinguishes primary errors from propagated errors, enabling explicit EP modeling. To estimate the model parameters efficiently, they propose the composite likelihood expectation-maximization (CLEM) algorithm, which partitions time into sub-windows to reduce computational complexity from quadratic to near-linear while maintaining estimation accuracy. The method is validated on synthetic and simulated autonomous driving scenarios, showing superior prediction accuracy compared to benchmarks and significant speedups over standard EM. Theoretical properties such as ascent and consistency are established, and optimal partition selection is guided by a stepwise Friedman test.

## Method Summary
The approach comprises three main components: (1) An error injection (EI) mechanism integrated with a physics-based AV simulator (e.g., CARLA) via ROS to intercept and inject realistic errors between AI modules, generating event logs with timestamps and types. (2) A point process intensity model where the total error intensity at a module is decomposed into a constant primary intensity and propagated contributions from the immediate upstream module, each following an exponential decay. (3) The CLEM algorithm for parameter estimation, which approximates the full likelihood by summing block-wise likelihoods over K non-overlapping sub-windows; within each sub-window, only upstream errors from the same sub-window are considered as triggers. Expectation–maximization is then performed on this composite likelihood, with an E-step computing posterior probabilities of error types and an M-step updating parameters. Optimal K is selected via a stepwise Friedman test on relative root mean square error (RRMSE) across replications. The method is evaluated on synthetic data and an AV perception case study, measuring estimation accuracy (MRRMSE), prediction accuracy (MAE), and computational time.

## Key Results
- CLEM achieves estimation accuracy (MRRMSE) comparable to the standard EM algorithm when the sub-window count K is chosen optimally, while reducing computational time by an order of magnitude for large datasets.
- In the autonomous vehicle perception case study, the proposed model yields lower mean absolute error (MAE) in predicting localization errors compared to benchmark models (NHPP, Gompertz, HPP), particularly under intermittent error injection scenarios.
- Theoretical guarantees include the ascent property of the composite likelihood and statistical consistency as K → ∞ and sub-window length → ∞, and the stepwise Friedman test consistently selects an effective sub-window length of approximately 50.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: The error injection (EI) framework within a physics-based simulation platform generates high-quality reliability data that captures cross-stage error propagation (EP), addressing the data scarcity challenge.
- **Mechanism**: EI uses ROS publisher-subscriber to intercept data between upstream and downstream modules. Realistic errors (e.g., Gaussian noise, point cloud removal) are injected at user-defined timestamps and probabilities, causing upstream failures that can propagate. This creates partially labeled events for logging. The physics-based environment (e.g., CARLA) adds realistic driving dynamics.
- **Core assumption**: The simulation and error models accurately represent real AI module failures and their propagation patterns; injection probabilities based on empirical data bridge the simulation-reality gap.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "leverages a physics-based autonomous vehicle simulation platform with a justifiable error injector to generate high-quality data for AI system reliability analysis."
  - [section 2.1]: Details the three-component framework: simulation platform, ROS-based EI, and data logging; explains how EI targets modules with defined probabilities and times, and that probabilities can be set from real-world findings.
  - [corpus]: The corpus mentions disjoint real-world datasets (KITTI, AV disengagement) lacking EP information, highlighting the need for simulated data; no corpus paper directly validates the specific EI approach.
- **Break condition**: The simulation fails to capture key real-world error modes or environmental factors; the ROS injection distorts natural data flow relative to actual AI system behavior.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: The intensity-decomposition framework explicitly models latent and probabilistic EP by distinguishing primary and propagated error intensities, enabling accurate multi-stage AI reliability modeling.
- **Mechanism**: The total intensity λ_ms(t) = λ^0_ms + Σ_{m_{s-1}} α_{ms,m_{s-1}} exp[−β_{ms,m_{s-1}}(t − t_{upstream})]. Primary intensity λ^0_ms is constant, representing module-inherent errors. Propagated terms add a temporary elevation after each upstream error, with α controlling strength and β the decay rate.
- **Core assumption**: (1) EP originates only from the immediate preceding stage (no multi-stage jumps). (2) Parallel modules in the same stage are independent given external covariates. (3) Exponential decay correctly describes EP attenuation in AV perception.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "proposes intensity-decomposition framework distinguishing primary and propagated errors for explicit EP modeling."
  - [section 2.2]: Formalizes the decomposition (Eqs. 2–4), states Assumptions 1–2, and motivates constant primary intensity (AV safety standards) and exponential decay (new data overrides old errors).
  - [corpus]: Builds on NHPP and multi-stage Hawkes processes (e.g., Pan et al. 2022), but the hybrid constant+exponential structure is novel; corpus does not provide direct empirical evidence for this decomposition.
- **Break condition**: Violation of immediate upstream propagation (e.g., significant direct effect from two stages up), correlated within-stage errors, or non-exponential decay introduces misspecification bias.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: The composite likelihood expectation–maximization (CLEM) algorithm provides scalable parameter estimation for the EP model by partitioning time into sub-windows, reducing computational complexity from quadratic to near-linear while maintaining accuracy for appropriately chosen partition sizes.
- **Mechanism**: CLEM approximates the full log-likelihood by summing block-wise likelihoods over K non-overlapping sub-windows. Within each sub-window, only upstream errors from the same sub-window are considered as triggers (cross-sub-window EP ignored). This reduces the number of pairwise interactions from O(N²) to Σ_k O(N_k²). EM iterations (E-step: posterior of latent error type; M-step: parameter update via stochastic gradient) then operate on the composite likelihood.
- **Core assumption**: EP effects are negligible across sub-window boundaries if sub-window length d is sufficiently large; the paper finds optimal d ≈ 50 in experiments. This reflects the limited temporal influence of past errors in autoregressive perception.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "Develops composite likelihood expectation–maximization (CLEM) algorithm for efficient parameter estimation. ... CLEM significantly reduces computational time versus standard EM while maintaining comparable estimation accuracy (similar MRRMSE for optimal sub-window count)."
  - [section 2.3]: Defines CLEM (Eqs. 8–9), analyzes computational complexity, proves ascent property and statistical consistency as K→∞, d→∞; introduces stepwise Friedman test for K selection.
  - [section 3.1]: Numerical results show MRRMSE comparable to EM for moderate K (e.g., K=100 at T=5000) but degrades for very large K; computational time savings increase with K and T; Friedman test consistently chooses sub-window length ≈50.
  - [corpus]: Composite likelihood is established in statistics (e.g., Varin et al., 2005) but its application to point process EP models is novel; corpus contains general methods but not this specific combination.
- **Break condition**: Sub-window length too small (K too large) loses statistical efficiency by omitting cross-sub-window EP; too large (K too small) forfeits computational gains. Slow EP decay (small β) makes cross-window triggers significant even for moderate d, causing bias. Theoretical consistency requires K→∞ and d→∞, which may conflict in finite samples.

## Foundational Learning
### Point process intensity modeling
- **Why needed**: The error events are treated as a point process; the intensity function λ(t) determines the instantaneous rate of occurrence. The EP model specifies λ(t) as a sum of primary constant and propagated exponential terms, which underlies the likelihood.
- **Quick check**: In a point process, what does the intensity function λ(t) represent mathematically and how is it used to compute the probability of events in an interval?
### Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm for latent variables
- **Why needed**: The indicators of whether an error is primary or propagated are missing data. EM iteratively estimates their posterior expectations (E-step) and maximizes the expected likelihood (M-step). CLEM modifies EM to use composite likelihood.
- **Quick check**: What are the two steps of the EM algorithm and what does each achieve in the context of incomplete data?
### Composite likelihood
- **Why needed**: The full likelihood for large recurrent event datasets is computationally prohibitive due to quadratic terms. Composite likelihood approximates it by summing simpler block likelihoods (e.g., within sub-windows), trading some efficiency for scalability.
- **Quick check**: How does composite likelihood differ from the standard full likelihood, and what is the typical trade-off regarding statistical efficiency?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**
- Simulation & EI: physics-based AV simulator (e.g., CARLA) with ROS error injector
- Data logger: collects error timestamps, module, stage, type
- EP model: parameter set Θ = {λ^0_ms, α_{ms,m_{s-1}}, β_{ms,m_{s-1}}}
- CLEM estimator: partition into K sub-windows, construct composite likelihood, run EM iterations
- Evaluator: computes RRMSE, MAE, runs Friedman test for K

**Critical path**
1. Set up simulation and implement EI to inject errors with controllable probabilities.
2. Log error events across modules/stages during simulation runs.
3. Implement CLEM algorithm (including K selection via Friedman).
4. Validate on simulated data with known parameters.
5. Apply to scenarios and compare with benchmarks.

**Design tradeoffs**
- Partition size K: Small sub-windows (large K) → faster but risk bias; large K → slower but accurate. Friedman test automates selection.
- Model assumptions: immediate upstream propagation only, exponential decay, constant primary intensity. These simplify computation and match some AV contexts but may not generalize.
- Simulation realism vs control: EI enables precise experiments but may oversimplify real error modes; calibrating injection probabilities with field data improves fidelity.
- M-step optimization: stochastic gradient descent requires tuning step size; poor choices may cause non-convergence.

**Failure signatures**
- CLEM log-likelihood decreases in an iteration (violates ascent property) → implementation error or numerical instability.
- Parameter estimates become negative (especially β) or extreme → inappropriate K, poor initialization, or violation of assumptions.
- Prediction MAE worse than simple benchmarks (e.g., HPP) → model misspecification (e.g., long-range EP not captured).
- Friedman test fails to find any K with RRMSE not significantly worse than EM (all K rejected) → data too small or EP effects too weak; maybe K=1 (full EM) is only reliable option.

**First 3 experiments**
1. **Numerical simulation validation**: Simulate two-stage, three-module data with known parameters (λ₁₁=λ₂₁=0.2, λ₀₁₂=0.5, α=β=0.3) and varying window lengths T. Compare EM and CLEM across K and T. Expect CLEM with optimal K to have similar MRRMSE but much lower runtime than EM.
2. **Physics-based case study replication**: Use CARLA with EI for the seven scenarios in Table 5. Train on first 180 s, predict errors in last 20 s. Compute MAE for localization errors. Expect proposed model to outperform NHPP, Gompertz, HPP, especially in intermittent EI scenarios (Sces. 5–7).
3. **Sub-window sensitivity analysis**: For fixed T (e.g., 5000), vary K = {2,5,10,20,50,100,250,500}. Plot MRRMSE and runtime vs. K. Identify region where MRRMSE remains close to EM’s but runtime is reduced, confirming optimal d ≈ 50. Also examine bias in α and β estimates.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: Can the proposed framework be validated using real-world operational data from autonomous vehicles, and how does its performance compare when trained on simulation versus real data?
- **Basis**: [explicit] The authors state in the conclusion: "extending the proposed framework to real-world operational data beyond simulation will be critical for validating its practical applicability and robustness across diverse driving environments."
- **Why unresolved**: The study relies solely on simulation due to data scarcity; without real-world validation, the model's generalizability remains uncertain.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Collect cross-stage error event data from AV fleets (e.g., disengagement reports) and directly compare prediction accuracy of simulation-trained vs. real-trained models.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: How can uncertainty quantification be integrated into the CLEM algorithm to provide reliable confidence intervals for parameter estimates and predictions?
- **Basis**: [explicit] The authors note: "integrating uncertainty quantification and adaptive learning could enhance the model’s ability to update reliability assessments as new data are collected."
- **Why unresolved**: Current CLEM provides only point estimates; uncertainty is essential for risk-aware decision-making in safety-critical deployments.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Develop asymptotic theory for CLEM, implement bootstrap or Bayesian extensions, and evaluate coverage probabilities via extensive simulation.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: To what extent can the error propagation model be generalized to other safety-critical AI systems (e.g., healthcare diagnostics, energy grid management) and what domain-specific adaptations are required?
- **Basis**: [explicit] The conclusion suggests: "extending the methodology to other safety-critical domains, such as healthcare, energy systems, and industrial automation, offers the potential to generalize the framework."
- **Why unresolved**: The model is tailored to AV perception pipelines; other domains may have different architectures, error types, and propagation mechanisms.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Apply the framework to reliability data from target domains (with expert input on stages and errors) and measure prediction accuracy against domain-specific benchmarks.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: How realistic is the error injection mechanism in the simulation platform, and what metrics can quantify its fidelity relative to real-world error propagation?
- **Basis**: [inferred] The introduction acknowledges: "concerns regarding the fidelity, realism, and representativeness of simulated scenarios relative to real-world driving conditions continue to limit the reliability of simulation data."
- **Why unresolved**: Simulated error patterns may not match real errors; if the EI process is unrealistic, the learned model could be biased.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Compare statistical properties (e.g., error rates, temporal correlations, propagation delays) of errors in real AV logs versus simulated data; conduct sensitivity analysis to EI probability misspecification.

### Open Question 5
- **Question**: What are the impacts of modeling assumptions (e.g., propagation only from immediate preceding stage, independence among parallel modules) and how can they be relaxed to capture more complex dependencies?
- **Basis**: [inferred] Assumption 1 restricts propagation to the immediate prior stage; Assumption 2 assumes conditional independence of parallel modules. The paper does not test these assumptions.
- **Why unresolved**: Real systems may exhibit longer-range propagation (e.g., an error affecting several downstream stages) or common-cause failures among parallel modules, leading to misattribution of error sources if ignored.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Extend the intensity model to allow arbitrary upstream triggering (e.g., via a Hawkes process with longer memory) and incorporate random effects to model parallel module dependencies; validate using synthetic data with known cross-stage linkages or real data if available.

## Limitations
- Simulation-based error injection may not fully capture the diversity and complexity of real-world AI failure modes and propagation dynamics.
- Model assumptions (immediate upstream propagation, exponential decay, constant primary intensity) could be violated in complex AI systems, introducing estimation bias.
- CLEM's partition-based approximation discards cross-sub-window error propagation; the Friedman test for selecting the number of sub-windows may be unstable with short time series or weak propagation signals.

## Next Checks
1. **Fidelity test**: Compare propagation statistics (e.g., delay distributions) from EI-generated data to those extracted from real AV disengagement logs to assess realism.
2. **Assumption stress test**: Fit the model to synthetic data where one assumption is broken (e.g., two-stage jumps, non-exponential decay); measure bias in key parameters (α, β) and prediction MAE.
3. **CLEM robustness sweep**: Systematically vary K and true decay rate β across wide ranges; analyze stability of Friedman-chosen K and its correlation with MRRMSE relative to full EM, to define safe operating regimes.
