# Machine learning-enhanced non-amnestic Alzheimer's disease diagnosis from MRI and clinical features

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2601.15530  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.15530  
- **Reference count:** 38  
- **Primary result:** Combining clinical tests and whole‑brain MRI features increases recall for atypical Alzheimer’s disease from 52% to 69% (NACC) and from 34% to 77% (ADNI).

## Executive Summary
This study tackles the frequent misdiagnosis of atypical Alzheimer’s disease (atAD) by developing a machine learning model that integrates standard clinical assessments with comprehensive whole‑brain MRI features, moving beyond the traditional reliance on hippocampal volume alone. The model significantly improves sensitivity (recall) for atAD compared to clinician diagnoses on both the NACC and ADNI cohorts. An ablation study confirms that the inclusion of MRI measures distributed across the brain is essential for these gains, with the multimodal approach achieving high precision (0.89) while maintaining recall. The work demonstrates the potential of data‑driven methods to enhance detection of atAD, a condition with diverse clinical presentations.

## Method Summary
Data were drawn from the National Alzheimer’s Coordinating Center (NACC) and the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI). Participants received standard clinical evaluations (e.g., neuropsychological tests, clinical dementia ratings) and brain MRI scans. The authors extracted a wide array of MRI metrics—regional volumes, cortical thickness, and surface area—from the entire brain using automated segmentation pipelines. These imaging features were combined with clinical test scores to train a machine learning classifier (type not specified) to distinguish atAD from other non‑AD causes of cognitive impairment. Model performance was measured by recall and precision. An ablation analysis compared three feature configurations: (i) clinical features alone, (ii) clinical features plus hippocampal volume, and (iii) clinical features plus whole‑brain MRI features.

## Key Results
- Recall improved from 52% to 69% on NACC and from 34% to 77% on ADNI.  
- Precision reached 0.89 on ADNI while sustaining the 77% recall.  
- Ablation study showed that whole‑brain MRI features added to clinical data significantly boosted diagnostic accuracy over models using only clinical or hippocampal features.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The model succeeds because atypical AD variants exhibit characteristic atrophy patterns that extend beyond the hippocampus to diverse cortical and subcortical regions. Whole‑brain MRI metrics capture these distributed changes, providing the classifier with discriminative signals that are missed when focusing solely on the hippocampus. By fusing clinical and extensive imaging data, the approach leverages complementary information, leading to higher sensitivity without sacrificing precision.

## Foundational Learning
- **Non‑amnestic Alzheimer’s disease (atAD)** – Atypical AD presentations (language, visuospatial, behavioral, motor) that often escape detection by memory‑centric criteria. *Why needed*: Defines the target population where diagnostic improvement is most needed. *Quick check*: Does the patient show progressive deficits in non‑memory domains with supporting AD biomarkers?  
- **Whole‑brain MRI feature extraction** – Quantitative measures (volumes, cortical thickness, surface area) derived from automated segmentation of the entire brain. *Why needed*: AtAD can affect many brain areas; whole‑brain coverage ensures relevant pathology is captured. *Quick check*: Are features obtained from tools like FreeSurfer covering all lobes and subcortical structures?  
- **Machine learning classification** – Supervised algorithms that learn patterns from labeled examples to predict diagnoses. *Why needed*: High‑dimensional clinical and imaging data require flexible modeling to integrate information and improve accuracy. *Quick check*: Does the pipeline include proper cross‑validation and held‑out testing to avoid overfitting?  
- **Ablation study** – Systematic removal of feature groups to quantify their individual contribution. *Why needed*: Isolates the impact of whole‑brain MRI on performance, ruling out that gains come from other factors. *Quick check*: Are models trained with identical data splits and only feature sets varying (e.g., clinical vs. clinical+hippocampus vs. clinical+whole‑brain)?  
- **Clinician baseline diagnosis** – Diagnostic decision made by neurologists using standard criteria and available data. *Why needed*: Establishes a real‑world reference to assess the added value of the ML model. *Quick check*: Was the clinician’s diagnosis made independently of the ML system’s predictions?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Clinical Data -> Clinical Feature Extraction -> Clinical Feature Vector; MRI Scans -> MRI Feature Extraction -> MRI Feature Vector; Clinical Feature Vector + MRI Feature Vector -> Feature Concatenation -> Integrated Feature Vector -> Machine Learning Classifier -> Diagnosis Prediction.

**Critical path:** The sequence from raw data acquisition to feature extraction, feature integration, and final classification is crucial. Comprehensive MRI feature extraction and the fusion of clinical with imaging data are the most influential steps determining performance.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Whole‑brain features increase coverage but also dimensionality, raising risks of overfitting and computational cost; dimensionality reduction or regularization is needed.  
- Simple concatenation of feature vectors is interpretable but may ignore complex interactions; more sophisticated fusion (e.g., attention) could improve accuracy at the expense of transparency.  
- Optimizing for higher recall may lower precision, especially with class imbalance; the tradeoff should align with clinical priorities (e.g., minimizing missed atAD cases).

**Failure signatures:**  
- Performance drop on external sites indicates poor generalization due to scanner protocol or population differences.  
- Excessive influence of features susceptible to artifacts (e.g., motion) suggests preprocessing failures.  
- Ablation results showing no benefit or degradation when adding whole‑brain features might point to overfitting or uninformative feature selection.

**First experiments:**  
1. Train and evaluate a model using only clinical features.  
2. Add hippocampal volume to the clinical feature set and measure the incremental gain.  
3. Incorporate the full suite of whole‑brain MRI features (volumes, cortical thickness, surface area) together with clinical features, and compare performance to the previous models and to the clinician baseline.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Which MRI-based regional measures (volume, cortical thickness, surface area) most reliably distinguish each atAD variant (language, visuospatial, behavioral, motor) from common non-AD dementias (e.g., frontotemporal, Lewy body), and do these discriminative features replicate across public datasets?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state: “Future studies incorporating larger samples of non-AD patients with non-AD diagnostic information (e.g., frontotemporal dementia) may further investigate MRI features that reliably identify atAD variants from other diagnoses contributing to cognitive impairment.”
- **Why unresolved:** Current analysis pooled all atAD cases and used a broad non‑AD group, lacking power to detect variant‑specific patterns and to compare with specific alternative diagnoses.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A multi‑center cohort with detailed clinical phenotyping of atAD subtypes and non-AD etiologies, applying separate classification tasks per variant and performing reproducibility analysis across independent datasets.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How do co‑occurring neuropathologies (e.g., mixed AD with Lewy bodies or cerebrovascular disease) influence machine learning model performance and the importance of MRI features in atAD versus non‑AD discrimination?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors write: “dementia may be multi‑etiology, e.g., both AD and Lewy bodies or vascular, and various combinations of pathology may result in various patterns of atrophy… Further comprehensive studies are needed to disentangle the effects of disease heterogeneity and co‑occurring neurological conditions.”
- **Why unresolved:** The non‑AD group likely includes mixed pathologies, but the study lacked biomarker or autopsy data to characterize co‑pathology; thus, model results may be confounded.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Datasets with confirmed co‑pathology status (e.g., PET, CSF, autopsy) would allow stratification or inclusion of interaction terms to assess how each comorbidity alters MRI signatures and classification accuracy.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What factors account for the discrepancy between NACC and ADNI in the brain regions identified as significant for differentiating atAD from non‑AD, and can a harmonized model achieve consistent feature selection across sites?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors note variation in significant regions (Fig. 4) and speculate about possible causes but do not test them.
- **Why unresolved:** Differences may stem from cohort‑specific characteristics (e.g., disease severity distribution, scanner protocols, demographic differences) rather than true biological signals; unaddressed, this hampers generalizability.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Direct comparison controlling for scanner type, image preprocessing, and clinical covariates; use of harmonization techniques (e.g., ComBat) to remove site effects; or validation in a single‑site high‑quality dataset.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Does cortical thickness in atAD and non‑AD follow a non‑linear trajectory over time, with early‑stage thickening (possibly compensatory) followed by later thinning, and can this be detected using longitudinal MRI?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors cite Williams et al. reporting such a pattern and observe increased thickness in some groups, but their cross‑sectional design precludes temporal conclusions.
- **Why unresolved:** Cross‑sectional snapshots cannot distinguish between cohort effects and true within‑subject change; the role of thickness alterations in disease progression remains speculative.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Longitudinal MRI data from the same individuals across prodromal and symptomatic stages, analyzed with mixed‑effects models to map thickness trajectories and relate them to clinical outcomes.

## Limitations
- Potential data leakage between NACC and ADNI may inflate reported performance.  
- Insufficient description of the clinician baseline diagnostic process hinders fair comparison.  
- Missing details on model architecture, hyperparameters, and MRI feature extraction impede reproducibility.  
- Lack of confidence intervals or statistical tests makes it uncertain whether improvements are reliable.  
- Evaluation restricted to research cohorts; real‑world generalizability is unclear.  
- Class imbalance not addressed could affect precision estimates.

## Confidence
- Improved recall versus clinician diagnosis: **Medium**  
- Multimodal (clinical + whole‑brain MRI) superiority: **Medium**  
- Added value of non‑hippocampal MRI features: **Medium**

## Next Checks
1. **External validation:** Test the model on an independent multi‑center cohort not derived from NACC or ADNI.  
2. **Prospective clinician comparison:** Conduct a head‑to‑head trial where the model and experts diagnose the same set of cases using identical data, with an expert panel as reference.  
3. **Leakage audit & disjoint retest:** Ensure no overlap between training and test sets across sites, re‑evaluate with proper cross‑validation, and report confidence intervals.
