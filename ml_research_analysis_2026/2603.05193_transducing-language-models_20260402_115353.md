# Transducing Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.05193
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05193
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Transduced language models compose a pretrained LM with a deterministic finite‑state transducer to compute exact or approximate target string probabilities and next‑token distributions without retraining, enabling inference‑time adaptation to new output formats.

## Executive Summary
Pretrained language models output token sequences (e.g., byte‑pairs), but many downstream tasks require different string formats (bytes, words, amino acids). Retraining or fine‑tuning for each format is costly and often impractical. This paper introduces a general framework called **transduced language models**, which treats deterministic string‑to‑string transformations as first‑class language models. By encoding the transformation as a finite‑state transducer (FST), the target distribution is obtained by marginalizing the source LM over all source strings that map to a given target string. The approach does not alter the source LM parameters or inference code; instead it modifies the interface to produce target‑side probabilities and next‑token distributions. The authors provide a formal definition, algorithms for exact or approximate computation, theoretical analysis of when the decomposition yields a finite quotient, and an efficient lazy implementation using frontiers. Empirical evaluation across three domains—token‑to‑byte conversion, token‑to‑PTB word segmentation, and DNA‑to‑amino acid translation—shows very low Jensen‑Shannon divergence and effective adaptation, with a controllable trade‑off between accuracy and speed via a pruning threshold. The work opens a path to repurpose large, existing LMs for arbitrary deterministic output formats at inference time.

## Method Summary
Given a source autoregressive LM \(p_X\) over strings \(x\) and a deterministic functional FST \(f\) mapping \(x\) to \(y\), the transduced LM defines \(p_Y(y) = \sum_{x:f(x)=y} p_X(x)\). Directly summing over the preimage is infeasible. The key insight is to work with the **precover** \(P(y) = \{x : y \preceq f(x)\}\), which contains all source strings that have \(y\) as a prefix of their output. \(P(y)\) is decomposed into a maximal cylindrical quotient \(Q(y)\) (all prefixes that belong to a cylinder fully contained in \(P(y)\)) and a remainder \(R(y)\). Then the target prefix probability is
\[
\overrightarrow{p}_Y(y) = \sum_{x\in Q(y)} \overrightarrow{p}_X(x) + \sum_{x\in R(y)} p_X(x).
\]
This decomposition reduces an infinite sum to a finite combination because \(Q(y)\) captures all prefixes that guarantee the existence of a full extension with high probability, while \(R(y)\) requires full‑string scoring. The algorithm discovers \(Q(y)\) and \(R(y)\) via a breadth‑first search that tests candidate prefixes for cylinder containment and membership, using a **frontier** representation that tracks reachable transducer states and partial output buffers. The frontier avoids materializing the potentially large composed automaton \(\mathrm{proj}_X(f \circ yY^*)\); instead transitions are computed on demand from the original FST. To manage computational cost, a pruning threshold \(\tau\) discards low‑probability candidates; a backtracking mechanism recovers from dead ends. For next‑token distributions, the BFS is extended to simultaneously explore all children \(y'\) by reusing the parent frontier (target recursion) and skipping expansions for \(R(y)\) strings (non‑cylinder monotonicity). The source LM wrapper batches queries to amortize GPU overhead. Thus the system yields exact or approximate transduced LMs with a tunable accuracy‑efficiency trade‑off.

## Key Results
- Token‑to‑byte conversion with GPT‑2 Large achieves extremely low Jensen‑Shannon divergence (< 3.7e‑5 bits/byte) at tight pruning thresholds (\(\tau \le 3\!\times\!10^{-4}\)), while throughput ranges from ~80 B/s (\(\tau=10^{-1}\)) to ~0.2 B/s (\(\tau=10^{-5}\)).
- The framework adapts successfully to PTB word segmentation and DNA‑to‑amino acid conversion, demonstrating robustness across different transducer structures and source models (LLaMA, Phi‑4, custom DNA GPT‑2). Approximate results converge with moderate pruning (e.g., \(\tau \approx 3\!\times\!10^{-4}\) for PTB).
- Accuracy and speed depend on transducer properties such as the number of input‑projection‑universal states; for instance, the PTB transducer (56/479 IP‑universal) requires more careful \(\tau\) tuning than the fully IP‑universal token‑to‑byte transducer.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: FST‑based marginalization
The transformation \(f\) is encoded as a deterministic finite‑state transducer. The target probability \(p_Y(y)\) equals \(\sum_{x:f(x)=y} p_X(x)\). Instead of enumerating the preimage, the algorithms compute prefix probabilities \(\overrightarrow{p}_Y(y)\) by examining the precover \(P(y) = \{x : y \preceq f(x)\}\). Cylinder and membership checks on \(P(y)\) are performed efficiently using the FST’s structure, avoiding construction of the full composed machine. This yields a well‑defined transduced LM provided \(f\) is deterministic and finite‑state. If the transducer contains \(\varepsilon\)-output cycles or unsafe states (Lemma 6.1), the quotient may be infinite and termination relies on pruning.

### Mechanism 2: Prefix decomposition into quotient and remainder
The intractable sum over \(f^{-1}(y)\) is reduced by splitting the precover into a maximal cylindrical quotient \(Q(y)\) (all prefixes whose cylinder lies inside \(P(y)\)) and a remainder \(R(y)\). Then
\[
\overrightarrow{p}_Y(y) = \sum_{x\in Q(y)} \overrightarrow{p}_X(x) + \sum_{x\in R(y)} p_X(x).
\]
Elements of \(Q(y)\) contribute their directed prefix probabilities, while \(R(y)\) requires full‑string probabilities. A breadth‑first search algorithm (Fig. 2) discovers \(Q(y)\) and \(R(y)\) by testing each candidate for cylinder containment (\(\langle x \rangle \subseteq P(y)\)) and membership (\(x \in P(y)\)), using the precover automaton. When the quotient is finite, the algorithm is exact; otherwise, pruning approximates by discarding low‑probability candidates, risking omitted mass if \(\tau\) is too large or the source mass is heavily dispersed.

### Mechanism 3: Frontier‑based lazy composition for efficient next‑token scoring
To avoid materializing \(\mathrm{proj}_X(f \circ yY^*)\), the implementation tracks **frontiers**: sets of pairs \((s,b)\) where \(s\) is a reachable transducer state and \(b\) is an output buffer compatible with the target prefix \(y\). For next‑token distribution, `decompose_next` seeds a BFS with \(Q(y)\cup R(y)\) and incrementally extends each source prefix \(x\) by one target symbol \(y'\). The frontier for \(yy'\) is derived by filtering buffers; cylinder checks reuse the parent frontier via target recursion (Prop C.1). Moreover, if \(x \in R(y)\) it cannot belong to any \(Q(yy')\) (non‑cylinder monotonicity, Prop C.3), so expansion for all children is skipped. The exact preimage \(f^{-1}(y)\) is collected as a byproduct. Frontier sizes remain tractable in practice because many transducer states are input‑projection‑universal (IP‑universal), enabling fast cylinder resolution. If frontier growth becomes excessive, pruning controls memory but may degrade accuracy when discarded paths hold significant probability mass.

Quick check: *Given two functional FSTs \(f_1\) and \(f_2\), what is the relation \(f_1 \circ f_2\) (composition) and how does its state space grow relative to \(f_1\) and \(f_2\)?*

The decomposition formulas and next‑token scoring depend on this factorization and the handling of the end‑of‑sequence (EOS) token. Quick check: *In an autoregressive LM, how can you compute the probability of a full string from the next‑symbol distributions? What role does the EOS token play?*

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Source LM wrapper → Transducer library → Frontier engine → Decompose module → Decompose_next → Pruning scheduler → Next‑symbol scorer.  
(The pruning scheduler intercepts during decomposition and next‑token computation; the frontier engine consults the transducer library for transitions.)

**Critical path** (computing \(\overrightarrow{p}_Y(y\!\mid\!y)\)):
1. `next_dist(y)` calls `decompose_next(y)`.
2. If `decompose(y)` is not cached, the BFS (Decompose module) with the frontier engine classifies source prefixes into \(Q(y)\) and \(R(y)\); the result is cached.
3. `decompose_next` seeds a queue with \(Q(y)\cup R(y)\) and iterates: for each \(x\), if `is_exact_member(x,y)` adds \(x\) to exact preimage \(p_s\); determines reachable output symbols \(\mathcal{Y}\); for each \(y'\in\mathcal{Y}\), checks `is_cylinder(x,yy')` (using frontier) to assign \(x\) to \(Q_{y'}\), else if `is_member(x,yy')` assigns to \(R_{y'}\). After BFS, caches \((Q_{y'},R_{y'})\) for all observed \(y'\).
4. The next‑symbol scorer sums contributions: \(\sum_{x\in Q_{y'}} \overrightarrow{p}_X(x) + \sum_{x\in R_{y'}} p_X(x)\) for each \(y'\), adds EOS mass from \(p_s\), and normalizes by \(\overrightarrow{p}_Y(y)\).
5. All \(\overrightarrow{p}_X(\cdot)\) and \(p_X(\cdot)\) are fetched from the Source LM wrapper, which batches queries to amortize GPU overhead.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- **Pruning vs. exactness**: Lower \(\tau\) retains more mass, improving accuracy but increasing BFS size and lowering throughput; higher \(\tau\) risks dead ends and larger backtracking overhead.
- **Frontier representation vs. precision**: Storing full output buffers preserves correctness but may cause state explosion; truncation to \(|y|+1\) keeps the state space finite and correct (Prop C.1) but must be applied consistently.
- **Caching strategy vs. memory**: Caching `decompose(y)` for every visited prefix amortizes work but grows linearly with sequence length; an LRU cache trades memory for recomputation.
- **LM batching vs. latency**: Grouping many LM calls by shared parent context amortizes GPU launch costs but introduces CPU‑GPU synchronization; low‑latency settings may prefer finer‑grained calls.
- **All‑universal fast path vs. generality**: When all transducer states are IP‑universal and non‑\(\varepsilon\)‑input arcs produce output, the next‑token distribution collapses to a single LM call per quotient element, drastically speeding inference. This holds for token‑to‑byte but not for PTB.

**Failure signatures**:
- **Dead end during scoring**: Returned probability for some \(y'\) is zero because contributing \(x\) was pruned; triggers backtracking which repeatedly lowers \(\tau\) and evicts cache, incurring large overhead.
- **Out‑of‑memory explosion**: The candidate queue or frontier grows without bound, typically when the transducer lacks IP‑universal states and the quotient is large; mitigation: increase \(\tau\) or \(n_{\text{max}}\), or switch to state‑based DFA decomposition.
- **Infinite BFS without progress**: With \(\tau=0\) and an unbounded quotient (e.g., Example 5), the queue never empties; guard by enforcing a minimal \(\tau\) or maximum depth.
- **Poor accuracy (high JSD)**: Cross‑entropy spikes or JSD relative to a tight reference is high, indicating aggressive pruning discarded cumulatively significant mass; remedy: lower \(\tau\) or increase \(n_{\text{max}}\).

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Token‑to‑byte (fα) with GPT‑2 Large**: Compose \(p_{\text{gpt2}}\) with \(fα\). Evaluate on the first 10 paragraphs of WikiText‑2‑raw. Vary \(\tau\) and measure JSD against a reference (\(\tau=10^{-5}\)). Expected JSD < \(1.5\!\times\!10^{-4}\) for \(\tau \le 3\!\times\!10^{-4}\); throughput declines from ~80 B/s at \(\tau=10^{-1}\) to ~0.2 B/s at \(\tau=10^{-5}\).
2. **Tokens → Bytes → PTB words (fα ∘ fptb)**: First transduce tokens to bytes using \(fα\), then compose with PTB word‑segmentation transducer \(fptb\). Compute JSD for various \(\tau\) (reference \(\tau=10^{-4}\) or \(3\!\times\!10^{-4}\) for Phi‑4). Converges at \(\tau \le 3\!\times\!10^{-4}\) but slower than plain token‑to‑byte because \(fptb\) has only 56 IP‑universal states out of 479.
3. **DNA‑to‑amino acids (fdna2aa)**: Train a small GPT‑2 variant on human DNA to obtain \(p_{\text{dna}}\). Compose with \(fdna2aa\) and test on 65 sampled human proteins (4–12 amino acids each). Vary \(\tau\) and candidate cap \(n_{\text{max}}\); report JSD and throughput. With \(n_{\text{max}}=5000\), JSD can reach ~\(10^{-4}\) at \(\tau=3\!\times\!10^{-6}\), but throughput scales with the cap.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Non‑deterministic transducers**: Can the framework handle dynamic or stochastic finite‑state transducers? Determinism is a core assumption; stochastic maps would require redefining universality and adapting decomposition.
2. **Importance sampling**: When source LM mass is highly dispersed, pruning degrades approximation. Could importance sampling provide a less biased alternative? Its effectiveness and variance remain untested.
3. **Broader model coverage**: Experiments covered GPT‑2, LLaMA, and Phi‑4, and three transduction tasks. It is unclear whether results generalize to other architectures (e.g., encoder‑decoder) or more complex transformations (e.g., code generation, multilingual tokenization).
4. **Systems optimization**: Current throughput (~10–20 bytes/sec) is far from practical. What algorithmic or hardware‑accelerated optimizations (parallel composition, GPU‑native FSTs) could yield orders‑of‑magnitude speed‑ups?

## Limitations
- The method is restricted to deterministic, finite‑state transformations; non‑FST or nondeterministic mappings are not supported.
- Decomposition may not terminate for transducers with \(\varepsilon\)-output cycles or unsafe states; practical use relies on pruning heuristics, which can introduce errors.
- No theoretical runtime bounds are provided; worst‑case queue growth forces aggressive pruning and can hurt accuracy, as observed with the PTB transducer.
- Empirical validation is limited to three domains; generalizability to other tasks remains an open question.

## Confidence
- FST composition defines transduced LMs: **High** (formal foundation, proven in §2–§3).
- Prefix decomposition reduces marginalization to finite sums: **Medium** (depends on safe FSTs and pruning; theory solid but practical termination requires heuristics).
- Frontier‑based lazy evaluation enables efficient next‑token scoring: **Medium** (empirically shown to scale, but worst‑case behavior is not fully understood).

## Next Checks
1. **Benchmark decomposition robustness**: Generate random FSTs with varying density and \(\varepsilon\)-cycles; measure failure rate, queue size, and minimum \(\tau\) to keep the candidate set below \(10^4\) across 1 000 runs.
2. **Sensitivity sweep**: For each benchmark, vary \(\tau\) from \(10^{-1}\) to \(10^{-6}\) and \(n_{\text{max}}\) (DNA). Map \(\tau\)–JSD curves and correlate \(\tau\) with transducer properties (IP‑universal state count, average cylinder size) to derive heuristic selection rules.
3. **Non‑deterministic extension**: Replace deterministic FSTs with weighted nondeterministic transducers (e.g., ambiguous segmentation). Measure transduced LM perplexity and JSD against a reference, and compare computational cost to the deterministic baseline to quantify the impact of nondeterminism.
