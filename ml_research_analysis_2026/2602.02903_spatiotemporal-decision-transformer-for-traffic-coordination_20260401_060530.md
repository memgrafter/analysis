# Spatiotemporal Decision Transformer for Traffic Coordination

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.02903
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02903
- **Reference count**: 19
- **Primary result**: Reduces average travel time by 5–6% over state-of-the-art baselines using fully offline training on historical traffic data.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses multi-agent traffic signal control, aiming to coordinate numerous intersections to optimize network-wide traffic flow. Traditional reinforcement learning approaches require extensive online interaction, which is sample-inefficient and impractical for real-world deployment. The proposed Spatiotemporal Decision Transformer (MADT) reformulates the problem as sequence modeling, leveraging graph attention to capture spatial dependencies between intersections, a temporal transformer to model traffic dynamics, and return-to-go conditioning to specify desired performance. Trained entirely offline on historical trajectories, MADT achieves state-of-the-art results on synthetic and real-world networks, significantly improving travel time, throughput, and coordination while maintaining low inference latency.

## Method Summary
The task is multi-agent traffic signal control where each intersection is an agent observing local traffic states (queues, waiting times, current phase, etc.) and neighboring information via a road network graph. The objective is to minimize average travel time (reward = negative average waiting time). MADT combines three components: a graph attention network (GAT) to encode spatial relationships among intersections, a temporal transformer encoder to process sequential observations, and a return-to-go conditioning mechanism that injects desired cumulative reward at inference. The model is trained via supervised learning (cross-entropy loss) on offline trajectories collected by a behavior policy (e.g., MaxPressure). At inference, a target return is specified to generate control actions without further environment interaction.

## Key Results
- Average travel time reduced by 5–6% compared to the strongest baseline (CoLight).
- Throughput increased by up to 6.2%.
- Coordination index among adjacent intersections improved by 50%.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
MADT succeeds by explicitly modeling both spatial and temporal structure in traffic networks. Graph attention allows each intersection to aggregate relevant neighbor states, capturing coordination needs. The temporal transformer encodes long-range dependencies in traffic flow evolution, while return-to-go conditioning provides a flexible way to specify performance levels at deployment without retraining. This combination enables effective learning from purely offline data, avoiding the sample complexity and safety issues of online RL. The sequence modeling framework also naturally handles variable-horizon decision making.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Graph Attention Layers -> Temporal Transformer Encoder -> Return-to-Go Conditioning -> Action Prediction

### Critical Path
Spatial features via GAT are extracted first to encode neighbor influences; these are concatenated with observations and fed into the temporal transformer to model dynamics over the input sequence; finally, the return-to-go token conditions the decoder to output actions matching a specified performance level.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Offline vs Online**: Offline training avoids exploration risks but depends on data quality; online fine-tuning could improve adaptation but reintroduces sample inefficiency.
- **Attention Complexity**: Full self-attention scales quadratically with sequence length and intersections; the design uses fixed sequence length and per-intersection processing but may not scale beyond ~36 intersections.
- **Fixed Horizon**: Input sequence length is fixed (20 steps), limiting ability to capture very long-term patterns without truncation.

### Failure Signatures
- Performance degrades on networks with heterogeneous signal phase configurations (~1.7% ATT increase).
- Sensitivity to poor-quality offline data; random demonstrations lead to failure.
- Inference latency grows quadratically; networks >36 intersections may exceed real-time requirements.
- Distribution shift (e.g., incidents) causes 12–18% ATT degradation.

### First Three Experiments
1. **Replication**: Follow the minimum viable reproduction plan—collect 1,000 episodes per environment (Grid 3x3, Grid 4x4, Atlanta, Boston) using MaxPressure, preprocess with sequence length 20, train MADT (hidden dim 128, 4 heads, 3 encoder layers, 2 GAT layers) for 100 epochs, evaluate on 50 test episodes, and report travel time, throughput, coordination index against baselines.
2. **Scalability Stress Test**: Deploy MADT on a synthetic 5×10 grid (50 intersections), measure end-to-end inference latency and memory usage; verify latency stays below 1 second and scaling remains near-linear.
3. **Robustness Under Shift**: Introduce random incidents and 30% demand spikes in 20% of test episodes for Atlanta/Boston; compare ATT degradation against baselines and test fallback to MaxPressure.

## Limitations
- Baseline hyperparameters (CoLight, MAPPO) rely on external defaults, risking performance deviations.
- Graph adjacency matrices for real-world networks (Atlanta, Boston) are not provided; manual construction may introduce errors.
- Scalability to large networks (>36 intersections) is unclear due to quadratic attention complexity.
- Robustness to non-recurrent events (incidents, spikes) is not evaluated; expected degradation is significant.

## Confidence
- Core performance (5–6% travel time reduction): **Medium** – depends on exact data preprocessing and baseline implementations.
- Inference latency: **High** – measured on A100 with moderate model size; likely generalizes.
- Offline training overcoming sample inefficiency: **Medium** – no direct sample complexity comparison.
- Generalization to heterogeneous phase configurations or large networks: **Low** – only homogeneous grids up to 4×4 tested.
