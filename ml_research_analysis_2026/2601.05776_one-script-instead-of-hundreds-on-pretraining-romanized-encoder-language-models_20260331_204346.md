# One Script Instead of Hundreds? On Pretraining Romanized Encoder Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.05776
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05776
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Romanization yields negligible performance loss for segmental scripts (Arabic, Hindi, Russian, Vietnamese) but a small residual gap for morphosyllabic scripts (Chinese, Japanese), while improving tokenization efficiency and not causing negative cross-lingual interference in multilingual models.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates whether script romanization can serve as a viable pretraining strategy for high-resource languages in multilingual encoder language models, addressing a gap in prior work that focused on low-resource or closely-related language pairs. Through systematic pretraining of monolingual and multilingual encoders from scratch on both native-script and romanized text (using URoman and UConv) for six typologically diverse high-resource languages, the study finds that for segmental scripts (alphabets/abjads/abugidas) performance loss is negligible. For morphosyllabic scripts (Chinese, Japanese), higher-fidelity romanization mitigates but does not fully eliminate the gap. Critically, increased subword overlap from romanization does not induce harmful cross-lingual interference, and tokenization efficiency improves substantially for segmental scripts at minimal cost.

## Method Summary
The authors pretrained ModernBERT Base (149M parameters) from scratch on the Fineweb-2 corpus for six high-resource languages: Arabic, Chinese, Hindi, Japanese, Russian, and Vietnamese. For each language, they created native-script (`MonoNat`) and romanized variants (`MonoUroman`, `MonoUconv`) using two romanization tools: URoman (universal, ASCII-focused) and UConv (standardized, higher fidelity with diacritics). Multilingual models were trained on concatenated native corpora (`MultiNat`) versus mixed romanized corpora (`MultiRom`, combining URoman for segmental languages and UConv for morphosyllabic ones). All models saw identical document sets with fixed compute. Tokenizers used SentencePiece BPE (50k vocabulary) trained on 100k documents per language. Downstream evaluation spanned five NLU tasks (XNLI, SIB200, MASSIVE, WikiAnn) with 5 random seeds per fine-tuning run, reporting mean accuracy/F1 ± standard deviation.

## Key Results
- For segmental scripts (Arabic, Hindi, Russian, Vietnamese), romanization causes negligible performance loss (within one standard deviation of native-script models).
- For morphosyllabic scripts (Chinese, Japanese), higher-fidelity romanization (UConv) reduces but does not eliminate the performance gap (e.g., -2.1% for Chinese vs. native).
- Romanization substantially improves tokenization efficiency (lower fertility) for segmental scripts at minimal performance cost, and increased subword overlap does not induce negative cross-lingual interference in multilingual models.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1:** Romanization preserves sufficient linguistic information for downstream NLU in segmental scripts because semantic and syntactic cues are primarily encoded at the morpheme/word level, not the glyph level. Converting characters to Latin script retains core phonological and morphological structure; the loss of diacritics (e.g., Vietnamese tones via URoman) is tolerable for high-resource languages with abundant training data, as context resolves ambiguity. Evidence: MonoUroman and MonoUconv perform within ±1 std dev of MonoNat for Arabic, Hindi, Russian, Vietnamese.

**Mechanism 2:** Romanization improves tokenization efficiency (lower fertility) for segmental scripts by reducing character set diversity, allowing BPE to form longer, more frequent subword units. This shortens sequences, reducing computational cost without proportional performance loss. The smaller romanized character set (e.g., <100 unique tokens vs. hundreds of native glyphs) increases merging of common stems. Evidence: Figure 1a shows ~5–7% fertility reduction for Arabic/Russian with URoman; Figure 2 indicates robustness to vocabulary reduction.

**Mechanism 3:** Increased subword overlap from romanization does not cause negative cross-lingual interference in multilingual encoder LMs because models contextualize ambiguous shared tokens using surrounding words, leveraging high-resource data to learn language-specific representation modes. Since all languages are high-resource, sufficient data exists to distinguish shared forms without harmful competition. Evidence: MultiRom performs statistically on par with MultiNat for all segmental script languages (differences <1 std dev); corpus neighbors (e.g., CORI, TransliCo) also support transliteration aiding alignment without harming in-language performance.

## Foundational Learning
- **Tokenization Fertility:** Measures tokens per word/character. Needed to evaluate the trade-off between information loss and computational gains from romanization. If romanized tokenization has 20% lower fertility on Arabic, it implies longer average token length and potential reductions in memory/compute.
- **Script Typology (Segmental vs. Morphosyllabic):** Needed to explain differential results. Segmental scripts represent phonemes (letters), so romanization preserves core structure. Morphosyllabic scripts represent morphemes/syllables (characters), so romanization loses logographic cues. Why might losing tonal marks be less damaging for Vietnamese (segmental) than losing character identities for Chinese?
- **Romanization Fidelity vs. Coverage:** Needed to interpret URoman vs. UConv differences. URoman prioritizes ASCII coverage (e.g., stripping tones), while UConv preserves diacritics and uses language-specific standards. Higher fidelity might improve morphosyllabic performance but could reduce tokenization gains. How would a Vietnamese romanizer that preserves tones change results?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**
Fineweb-2 corpus -> language filter -> romanization (URoman/UConv) -> document sharding -> SentencePiece BPE training (100k docs per language) -> tokenizer -> ModernBERT Base pretraining (MLM objective, 1 epoch) -> downstream fine-tuning on 5 NLU tasks (5 seeds).

**Critical path:** Romanization must occur before tokenizer training to ensure subword units are learned on the final input script. Changing romanization after tokenization invalidates comparisons.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Romanizer choice: URoman (ASCII, fast) vs. UConv (diacritics, standard) affects information retention and whitespace handling.
- Vocabulary size: Larger (50k) reduces fertility loss but increases model size; smaller (10k) maximizes efficiency but increases token collapse ambiguity.
- Whitespace splitting: Essential for UConv (inserts spaces) but not for URoman; incorrect flag breaks token merging for CJK.
- Pre-segmentation: For Chinese/Japanese, pre-segmenting (pkuseg/Sudachi) before tokenization may create meaningful subwords beyond single characters but is not always used.

**Failure signatures:**
- Tokenization inconsistency: Check if `split_by_whitespace` matches romanizer (UConv needs `true`). Compare tokenized sentence lengths; UConv Chinese should produce whitespace-separated tokens.
- Pretraining data misalignment: Verify identical document sets across variants (same MD5 checksums, token counts within 1% of Table 2).
- Multilingual regression: If MultiRom underperforms MultiNat by >2–3% on segmental scripts, indicates negative interference.
- Tokenizer train/test mismatch: Using `split_by_whitespace=false` for UConv yields unusable CJK tokens; using `true` for URoman wastes capacity.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Monolingual Baseline:** Train MonoNat, MonoUroman, MonoUconv for Russian. Expected: UConv ~Nat, URoman slightly lower but within noise. Confirms script loss mechanism.
2. **Fertility-Accuracy Sweep:** Train monolingual models with vocab sizes {10k,25k,50k} for Arabic (segmental) and Chinese (morphosyllabic). Expected: Arabic fertility drops steadily with minimal accuracy loss; Chinese accuracy drops significantly at 10k. Confirms trade-off.
3. **Multilingual Interference Check:** Train MultiNat and MultiRom on all six languages. Expected: No gap for Arabic/Hindi/Russian/Vietnamese; gap for Chinese/Japanese. Confirms interference hypothesis.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- For morphosyllabic scripts like Chinese and Japanese, what specific aspects of romanization cause residual performance degradation despite higher-fidelity methods, and how can romanization be tailored to preserve critical script-specific information? The paper identifies the gap but does not diagnose underlying reasons or offer solutions.
- How do the effects of romanization generalize across the full diversity of world writing systems (e.g., abugidas, abjads, logosyllabic scripts), and can script typology predict effectiveness? Only six scripts were tested; other types may behave differently.
- Does the lack of negative cross-lingual interference persist when scaling up model size, pretraining data, and number of languages? The study used Base-scale models with limited languages; larger settings might reveal interference.

## Limitations
- Language selection limited to six high-resource languages; findings may not generalize to other scripts or lower-resource contexts.
- Romanizer specificity: only URoman and UConv tested; other schemes (e.g., tone-preserving for Vietnamese) could yield different trade-offs.
- Task scope confined to five NLU tasks; performance on generation, speech, or multimodal tasks remains unknown.
- Reproduction gaps: exact document sampling strategy and complete tokenizer hyperparameters (e.g., pre-segmentation configs) are not fully specified.

## Confidence
- **Segmental scripts:** Romanization causes negligible performance loss. *Confidence: High* (consistent across four languages, two romanizers, within statistical noise).
- **Morphosyllabic scripts:** Residual performance gap (~-2% for Chinese) even with high-fidelity romanization. *Confidence: Medium* (only two languages; gap modest but may be sensitive to tokenization details).
- **Tokenization efficiency:** Romanization reduces fertility for segmental scripts. *Confidence: High* (direct measurements show 5–7% reduction).
- **No negative interference:** Increased subword overlap does not harm multilingual performance on segmental scripts. *Confidence: Medium* (based on six languages; larger multilingual sets could reveal interference).

## Next Checks
1. Broaden language coverage: Test romanization on additional script types (e.g., Ethiopic, Georgian) and low-resource languages to verify if high data volume is essential for the negligible loss effect.
2. Probe contextual disambiguation: Use diagnostic classifiers or attention analysis to confirm that multilingual models assign language-specific representations to shared romanized subwords.
3. Fidelity ablation for segmental scripts: Introduce controlled diacritic preservation (e.g., modified URoman) to measure whether higher fidelity yields measurable gains, testing the glyph-loss assumption.
