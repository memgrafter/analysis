# Many AI Analysts, One Dataset: Navigating the Agentic Data Science Multiverse
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.18710  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18710  
- **Reference count:** 28  
- **Primary result:** Autonomous AI analysts exhibit substantial dispersion in effect sizes and hypothesis support across fixed datasets; this dispersion is steerable by analyst persona and underlying LLM, and persists even after compliance auditing removes non-compliant runs.

## Executive Summary
This paper explores analytic variability by deploying fully autonomous AI analyst agents to conduct independent statistical analyses on shared datasets and hypotheses. Mirroring human many-analyst studies, these agents produce wide dispersion in results. The dispersion is systematically influenced by the analyst’s persona (e.g., skeptical vs. confirmation-seeking) and the choice of underlying LLM. After an AI auditor filters non-compliant runs, two-thirds of analyses remain, yet persona-driven differences in conclusion rates persist. The findings highlight that when generating defensible analyses is cheap, evidence becomes plentiful but vulnerable to selective reporting, and they argue for mandatory multiverse reporting and prompt disclosure for AI-generated analyses.

## Method Summary
The authors create autonomous AI analyst agents that iteratively plan, write code, execute, and revise using a ReAct loop with Python and shell tools. Each agent receives a fixed dataset, hypothesis, and estimand, plus a persona prompt framing its prior stance. A separate AI auditor reviews the full transcript to flag methodological failures (e.g., hallucination, estimand misalignment). The system is run across three datasets with five personas and four LLMs, generating thousands of analysis sessions. Primary outcomes—effect size, p-value, and a binary support flag—are extracted, along with a codebook of analytic decisions (e.g., model type, covariate handling). Dispersion is measured pre- and post-audit, and the influence of persona and LLM is quantified.

## Key Results
- AI analysts show substantial dispersion in effect sizes and hypothesis support across all datasets and LLMs, replicating the pattern seen in costly human many-analyst studies.
- Both analyst persona and underlying LLM steer outcomes; support-rate gaps reach 34–66 percentage points between the most skeptical and most confirmation-seeking personas.
- After compliance auditing, 67% of runs are retained, but persona-driven variation in support rates remains statistically significant, indicating that methodological pluralism—not just outright errors—drives dispersion.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Persona Prompting Biases Analytic Choices**  
The persona prompt (e.g., “skeptical” vs. “confirmation-seeking”) frames the agent’s stance and influences its chain-of-thought and tool-use decisions. This biases the distribution of defensible analytic choices (e.g., covariate selection, model specification) toward confirmation or skepticism. The effect persists after auditing because multiple specifications are methodologically sound. Evidence: Support-rate gaps of 34–66 pp between personas; persona effects vary by dataset but remain post-audit. Break condition: If persona variations produce no significant difference in support rates or effect size distributions among compliant runs, steering fails.

**Mechanism 2: LLM Inherent Biases Generate Divergent Conclusions**  
Different foundation models have distinct pretraining, parameterizations, and decoding behaviors that shape how an analyst interprets instructions, generates code, handles edge cases, and recovers from errors. These model-level “analytic styles” cause outcome dispersion even under identical persona prompts. Evidence: Cross-model variation in support rates and effect sizes within a given persona; testing four contemporary LLMs. Break condition: If controlling for extracted analytic decisions eliminates cross-model differences, LLM effects are mediated by choice variability.

**Mechanism 3: Auditing Filters Failures but Not Pluralism**  
The AI auditor removes runs with clear flaws (e.g., hallucination, incorrect estimand) but accepts a wide zone of “reasonable multifariousness” where analysts legitimately differ on specification. Persona-driven steering operates within this zone, so dispersion persists post-audit. Evidence: 67% retention rate; persona-driven variation remains; auditor uses full transcript review to catch coherent but flawed reasoning. Break condition: If all compliant runs converge to the same conclusion, either the task had one defensible specification or auditor scope was too narrow.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Task Configurator -> AI Analyst Agent (ReAct loop with tools) -> AI Auditor -> Decision Extractor -> Run Database  
Critical path: Task definition (dataset, hypothesis, estimand, persona) → Analyst spawn → Code generation/execution loop → Report submission → Auditor evaluation → Verdict/Outcome extraction → Storage.  
Design tradeoffs: Audit thoroughness vs. scale (full transcript review limits runs); Persona design vs. realism (explicit prompts like “Strong CS” stress-test but may not reflect typical behavior); Fixing estimand vs. specification freedom (pre-specifying estimand ensures comparability but leaves implementation choices open).  
Failure signatures: Analyst: hallucinated results, infinite loops, missing uncertainty quantification, estimand misalignment. Auditor: false negatives (passing hidden p-hacking), false positives (rejecting valid robust methods), inconsistency across re-judges. System: correlation between persona and exclusion rates (auditor capturing steering as non-compliance).  
First 3 experiments:  
1. Baseline dispersion: Spawn 30 Standard-persona analysts per LLM on one dataset; quantify raw dispersion in effect sizes and support rates pre-audit.  
2. Persona steering test: Fix LLM (Claude Sonnet), compare all 5 personas; measure support-rate gap between Negative and Strong CS, stratified by compliance.  
3. LLM identity test: Fix persona (Standard), compare all 4 LLMs; quantify model-driven variation in effect size distributions and key decision frequencies (e.g., logistic vs. linear models).

## Limitations
- Generalizability is uncertain; the study uses 3 specific datasets, 5 crafted personas, and 4 LLMs—effects may differ across domains or with subtler persona variations.  
- The auditor’s criteria are a black box; we lack ground-truth validation for its 33% exclusion rate or whether its own LLM choice biases decisions.  
- The causal mechanism linking persona to analytic choices is inferred but not proven; steering could operate via direct prompt influence or indirect style differences (e.g., comment density).

## Confidence
- Existence of dispersion: High – Consistent across datasets/LLMs pre- and post-audit.  
- Persona steerability: Medium – Substantial gaps (34–66 pp) but vary by dataset; no mediation analysis identifying specific choice drivers.  
- LLM-driven variation: Medium – Cross-model differences reported, but confounding with random seed/temperature not fully ruled out.  
- Auditing efficacy: Low – Auditor is a single LLM without expert validation; false-negative/false-positive rates unknown.

## Next Checks
1. **Choice distribution ablation**: Extract discrete analytic decisions (model type, covariates, missing-data handling) from 100 compliant runs per persona/LLM on one dataset; test whether persona predicts decision distributions (chi-square). If not, steering may operate via subtler code differences.  
2. **LLM independence test**: Control random seeds/temperature; regress effect size on model identifiers while controlling for extracted decision indicators. If model coefficients vanish, LLM differences are mediated by choice variability.  
3. **Auditor sensitivity analysis**: Re-audit a stratified sample of 100 rejected runs using a different LLM (e.g., GPT-4) and human experts (n=50); compute agreement (Kappa) and identify systematic disagreements (e.g., rejection of robust SEs).
