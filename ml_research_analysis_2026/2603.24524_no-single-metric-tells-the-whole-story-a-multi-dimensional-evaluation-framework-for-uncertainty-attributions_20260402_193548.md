# No Single Metric Tells the Whole Story: A Multi-Dimensional Evaluation Framework for Uncertainty Attributions
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.24524
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24524
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: The proposed multi-dimensional evaluation framework reveals that no single metric suffices to assess uncertainty attribution methods; gradient-based and LRP methods outperform SHAP and LIME on most metrics for Wine Quality, and Monte-Carlo dropconnect improves correctness and conveyance over dropout.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the lack of a standardized, comprehensive evaluation paradigm for uncertainty attribution methods, which aim to explain model uncertainty in terms of input features. The authors adapt the Co-12 XAI evaluation framework to the uncertainty attribution context, introducing a set of metrics covering correctness (via feature flipping), consistency (repeatability), continuity (Relative Input Stability), compactness (complexity), and a new dimension—conveyance—with Relative Rank Improvement (RRI) and Uncertainty Conveyance Similarity (UCS). They evaluate combinations of Monte-Carlo dropout/connect with several feature attribution techniques (LRP, Integrated Gradients, Input×Gradient, SHAP, LIME) on the Wine Quality (tabular) and MNIST datasets. Results show that gradient-based and LRP methods consistently outperform SHAP and LIME across most metrics on Wine Quality, and Monte-Carlo dropconnect yields better correctness and conveyance than dropout. Importantly, the metrics exhibit low inter-method agreement, underscoring that no single metric can capture all aspects of a good uncertainty attribution. The study thus provides a multi-faceted evaluation toolkit and highlights the need for diverse criteria when selecting or comparing uncertainty attribution methods.

## Method Summary
The authors propose a multi-dimensional evaluation framework by extending the Co-12 XAI taxonomy to uncertainty attribution. They define five properties:
- **Correctness**: whether flipping the most-attributed features changes the model’s uncertainty as expected, measured by a correctness score.
- **Consistency**: stability of attributions under minor input perturbations or across repeated runs (repeatability).
- **Continuity**: smoothness of attributions when the input changes continuously, quantified by Relative Input Stability.
- **Compactness**: simplicity of explanations, operationalized via sparsity or complexity measures.
- **Conveyance**: a novel property capturing how well the attribution reflects the underlying uncertainty mechanism, measured by two new metrics—Relative Rank Improvement (RRI) and Uncertainty Conveyance Similarity (UCS).
The framework is instantiated by applying Monte-Carlo dropout and Monte-Carlo dropconnect to obtain uncertainty estimates, then generating attributions via LRP, Integrated Gradients, Input×Gradient, SHAP, and LIME. These are evaluated on the Wine Quality regression dataset and the MNIST classification dataset, with metrics computed across multiple samples. The resulting scores are compared to identify strengths and weaknesses of each method combination.

## Key Results
- Gradient-based (IG, IxG) and LRP methods achieve higher correctness, consistency, continuity, and compactness compared to SHAP and LIME on the Wine Quality dataset.
- Monte‑Carlo dropconnect yields better correctness and conveyance scores than Monte-Carlo dropout when paired with the same attribution method.
- Inter‑method correlation across metrics is generally low, indicating that different metrics measure largely independent aspects and that no single metric is sufficient for comprehensive evaluation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The effectiveness of the framework stems from its multi-dimensional design, which acknowledges that uncertainty attribution quality cannot be captured by a single scalar. Each metric corresponds to a distinct theoretical desirable property of explanations: correctness validates causal influence, consistency ensures reliability, continuity reflects sensitivity to input changes, compactness promotes interpretability, and conveyance quantifies alignment with the uncertainty model’s mechanics. By combining them, the framework provides a balanced scorecard that exposes trade‑offs. The empirical results confirm that methods excelling in one dimension may falter in another (e.g., SHAP and LIME often score lower on correctness), thereby guiding users toward method selection aligned with their priorities. Moreover, the introduction of RRI and UCS offers quantifiable ways to assess whether an attribution truly represents uncertainty, a gap in prior work.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Uncertainty Attribution**  
   *Why needed*: To explain which input features contribute to a model’s predictive uncertainty, increasing trust and enabling debugging.  
   *Quick check*: Given a model and an input with high uncertainty, can you identify features that, if altered, would most reduce that uncertainty?

2. **Monte‑Carlo Dropout / Dropconnect**  
   *Why needed*: Practical Bayesian approximation techniques that provide tractable estimates of epistemic uncertainty for neural networks.  
   *Quick check*: Does performing multiple forward passes with dropout enabled yield a variance that correlates with model uncertainty?

3. **Feature Attribution Methods (LRP, IG, SHAP, LIME)**  
   *Why needed*: To assign importance scores to input features, forming the basis of uncertainty attributions.  
   *Quick check*: For a given input, can each method produce a vector of feature scores that sum to a meaningful baseline (e.g., zero for SHAP)?

4. **Evaluation Principles (Co‑12 XAI Framework)**  
   *Why needed*: Offers a taxonomy of properties (correctness, robustness, etc.) that guide the design of metrics for any XAI method.  
   *Quick check*: Can you map a proposed metric to one of the Co‑12 properties (e.g., correctness, continuity)?

5. **Relative Input Stability (RIS)**  
   *Why needed*: Measures how gradually attributions change with small input perturbations, a continuity proxy.  
   *Quick check*: Compute attributions for two similar inputs; does a small L2 distance between inputs lead to a small change in attribution scores?

6. **Conveyance Metrics (RRI, UCS)**  
   *Why needed*: Specifically assess whether an attribution captures the mechanisms driving uncertainty, a gap in standard XAI metrics.  
   *Quick check*: Does an attribution’s ranking of features correlate with the model’s internal uncertainty signals (e.g., dropout mask effects)?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:  
Input Data -> Trained Model (with MC dropout/connect) -> Attribution Generation (LRP/IG/IxG/SHAP/LIME) -> Metric Computation (Correctness, Consistency, Continuity, Compactness, Conveyance) -> Aggregated Scores -> Comparative Analysis

Critical path:  
1. Train or obtain a neural network model equipped with Monte‑Carlo dropout or dropconnect.  
2. For a set of test inputs, run the attribution method multiple times (if stochastic) to obtain feature importance scores that explain the model’s uncertainty.  
3. Feed the attributions and the model into each metric function to produce scalar scores.  
4. Normalize and compare metrics across methods; examine correlations.

Design tradeoffs:  
- **Feature flipping (Correctness)** is intuitive and easy to implement but may not capture non‑linear interactions or joint effects.  
- **SHAP and LIME** provide model‑agnostic explanations but are computationally heavy and sensitive to sampling; gradient‑based methods are faster but assume differentiability.  
- **Conveyance metrics (RRI, UCS)** introduce a novel way to link attributions to uncertainty mechanisms but lack user studies to confirm their pedagogical value.

Failure signatures:  
- **Correctness** near zero for all methods suggests the model’s uncertainty is insensitive to feature changes.  
- **Consistency** (repeatability) low variance fails if attributions fluctuate wildly across runs with the same input.  
- **Compactness** extremely low scores indicate overly dense, uninterpretable attributions.  
- **Conveyance** metrics yield near‑random rankings, implying attributions do not align with uncertainty signals.  
- **High inter‑metric correlation** would undermine the claim of multi‑dimensionality and could indicate metric redundancy.

First three experiments to run:  
1. Compute correctness via feature flipping on the Wine Quality dataset for all attribution methods using MC dropout; verify that flipping the top‑k features reduces uncertainty significantly.  
2. Measure consistency by generating 30 attributions per input for each stochastic method; calculate intra‑method correlation of scores.  
3. Evaluate conveyance by computing RRI and UCS for each method and checking whether gradient‑based methods outperform SHAP/LIME, as hypothesized.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper does not explicitly list open questions beyond the future work suggested in the limitations section.

## Limitations
- **Dataset scope**: Evaluation restricted to Wine Quality (tabular) and MNIST (image); generalizability to other domains such as text or time series is uncertain.
- **Method coverage**: Only MC dropout/connect combined with LRP, IG, IxG, SHAP, and LIME are examined; newer uncertainty attribution techniques (e.g., deep ensembles, Bayesian neural network gradients) remain untested.
- **Metric validity**: Correctness via feature flipping may oversimplify complex, non‑linear models; the newly proposed conveyance metrics lack external validation against human interpretability or user trust.

## Confidence
- **No single metric suffices**: High
- **Gradient‑based/LRP superiority on Wine Quality**: Medium
- **Monte‑Carlo dropconnect improvements**: Medium
- **RRI vs. UCS distinctiveness**: Medium

## Next Checks
1. **Cross‑dataset replication**: Apply the framework to at least three diverse datasets (e.g., UCI tabular collection, CIFAR‑10, text classification tasks) with varied model architectures to test robustness.
2. **Method expansion**: Integrate recent uncertainty attribution approaches such as deep ensembles and Bayesian neural network gradients, comparing them against the baseline set.
3. **Human study correlation**: Conduct user experiments measuring perceived clarity and trust of attributions, then correlate scores with RRI and UCS to externally validate the conveyance metrics.
