# The Alignment Curse: Cross-Modality Jailbreak Transfer in Omni-Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.02557  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02557  
- **Reference count:** 23  
- **Primary result:** Text-transferred audio jailbreaks (e.g., AutoDAN-Turbo) achieve comparable or superior success rates to native audio attacks, with success linked to low KL divergence (<2.0) between text and audio representations in aligned omni-models.

## Executive Summary
This paper identifies the “alignment curse,” where strong cross-modality alignment in end-to-end omni-models inadvertently propagates textual jailbreak vulnerabilities to the audio modality. The core method converts textual jailbreak prompts into audio via text-to-speech (TTS) and evaluates them as “text-transferred audio attacks” against recent omni-models under a black-box threat model. Results on JailbreakBench show that advanced textual jailbreak methods (AutoDAN-Turbo, PAP) transferred to audio often outperform specialized audio-only attacks (Speech Editing, VoiceJailbreak). The phenomenon is theoretically grounded in a continuity bound (Theorem 3.2) linking output probability differences to the KL divergence between text- and audio-induced representations. Empirical analysis confirms that successful transfer occurs when this divergence is low (below a threshold near 2.0) in mid-to-late model layers, while failures (e.g., for ReNeLLM or InteractiveOmni) correspond to higher divergence.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates cross-modality jailbreak transfer by first generating adversarial textual prompts using established jailbreak methods (PAP, ReNeLLM, AutoDAN-Turbo) on a set of harmful queries from JailbreakBench. These textual prompts are then converted to audio via OpenAI’s gpt-4o-mini-tts with default settings. Both the original textual prompts and the text-transferred audio inputs are fed to target omni-models (Qwen2.5-Omni-3B/7B, Qwen3-Omni, InteractiveOmni, GPT-4o-audio-preview) under black-box or audio-only access conditions. Attack success is measured via keyword (KW) matching against a refusal dictionary and the StrongReject (SR) semantic harmfulness score (0–1). For analysis, layer-wise representations for text and audio inputs are extracted, reduced via PCA to 15 dimensions, and a classifier-based Monte Carlo estimator computes the KL divergence between their distributions, following a cross-fitting procedure.

## Key Results
- AutoDAN-Turbo (A) attains an average SR of 0.61, substantially higher than audio-only methods like Speech Editing (avg SR 0.23) and VoiceJailbreak (avg SR 0.24) across multiple omni-models.
- Text-transferred attacks remain effective under cross-model transfer (using Qwen3-Omni as surrogate) and a stricter audio-only access model, often matching or exceeding native audio attacks.
- Success correlates with low KL divergence (δ < 2.0) between text and audio representations in mid-to-late layers; failures (e.g., ReNeLLM (A), InteractiveOmni) show δ > 2.0, confirming the theoretical continuity condition (Corollary 3.4).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Strong cross-modality alignment in omni-models causes textual jailbreak vulnerabilities to transfer to the audio modality via shared representation space continuity. End-to-end training aligns audio and text into a unified embedding space. Theorem 3.2 proves that if the KL divergence between text- and audio-induced representation distributions (P_text, P_audio) is ≤ δ, then the model’s output probabilities for any response set U differ by ≤ √(δ/2). Consequently, if a textual jailbreak yields high unsafe output probability (P_text(Y∈Û) ≥ τ), its audio counterpart achieves similar probability when δ is small (P_audio(Y∈Û) ≥ τ − √(δ/2)). The core assumption is that omni-models are trained to minimize representation-level divergence between semantically equivalent text and audio inputs, especially in mid-to-late layers where high-level semantics are encoded. Evidence: [abstract] states alignment propagates vulnerabilities; [section 3.2] formalizes the continuity argument; neighbor “Jailbreak Transferability Emerges from Shared Representations” (arxiv:2506.12913) supports representation-sharing as a transfer enabler. Transfer fails when KL divergence exceeds the non-vacuous threshold (δ > 2.0 in experiments), breaking the continuity guarantee.

### Mechanism 2
Text-transferred audio attacks (via TTS) exploit alignment to achieve success rates equal or superior to native audio attacks. Mature textual jailbreak methods (AutoDAN-Turbo, PAP) generate semantically adversarial prompts. When converted to audio via TTS, these prompts produce representations that remain close to the original text representations (KL < 2.0), inheriting the jailbreak effectiveness. Audio-only attacks (e.g., Speech Editing) rely on signal manipulations that do not leverage the aligned semantic space and thus underperform. The core assumption is that TTS preserves the semantic content of the jailbreak prompt sufficiently to maintain low KL divergence with the original text representation. Evidence: [abstract] notes text-transferred attacks often perform better; [section 4.2, Table 1] shows AutoDAN-Turbo (A) avg SR 0.61 vs. Speech Editing 0.23; [section 4.4, Figure 2] shows PAP and AutoDAN-Turbo KL < 2.0 on Qwen2.5-Omni-7B; neighbor “JALMBench” provides benchmarks where text-transferred attacks surpass audio-only. Break condition: TTS introduces semantic distortion (e.g., loss of formatting in ReNeLLM prompts), increasing KL above 2.0 and reducing transfer success.

### Mechanism 3
Transfer failure for specific methods (ReNeLLM) or models (InteractiveOmni) occurs when text-audio representation alignment is weak (high KL divergence). ReNeLLM’s jailbreak prompts rely on fine-grained structural cues (code-like formatting, punctuation). TTS linearizes these cues, altering semantics and causing audio representations to drift from text ones (KL > 3.0). Similarly, InteractiveOmni exhibits inherent alignment weakness (higher KL across methods). The continuity bound becomes vacuous, decoupling audio from text attack success. Core assumption: Prompt structures depending on non-semantic formatting are poorly preserved by TTS; some models achieve weaker multimodal alignment during training. Evidence: [section 4.2, Observation 2] ReNeLLM (A) underperforms except on newest models; t-SNE shows separated representations; [section 4.2, Observation 3] InteractiveOmni shows large text/audio gap; Table 3 reports KL > 3.0; [section 4.4, Figure 2] ReNeLLM KL > 2.0 across layers. Neighbor “Failures to Find Transferable Image Jailbreaks Between Vision-Language Models” documents analogous modality-specific failures. Break condition: KL divergence > 2.0 in mid-to-late layers, or TTS-induced semantic drift exceeding the model’s alignment robustness.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Text tokens -> embedding lookup; Audio waveform -> feature extractor (log-mel) -> audio encoder -> projector -> audio embeddings; Fusion: concatenate text and audio embeddings into unified sequence; Backbone: shared LLM processes multimodal sequence autoregressively; Training: two-stage (freeze LLM, train audio encoder/projector; then joint fine-tuning).
- **Critical path:** 1. Audio feature extraction (preserves temporal structure). 2. Audio encoder + projector mapping to text-embedding space (alignment bottleneck). 3. LLM attention over multimodal sequence (where representation closeness determines behavior consistency). 4. Output generation conditioned on fused representations.
- **Design tradeoffs:** Alignment vs. modality-specific preservation (strong alignment improves cross-modal utility but risks propagating adversarial directions); Cascaded vs. end-to-end (cascaded has perfect alignment but loses acoustic cues and adds latency); Security-utility Pareto (enhancing alignment for better audio understanding may lower audio-specific safety barriers).
- **Failure signatures:** High KL divergence (>2.0) between text/audio representations in mid-to-late layers; Large performance gap between textual and text-transferred audio attacks on the same model; TTS conversion altering prompt semantics (e.g., formatting loss) without model’s alignment compensating.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **KL estimation replication:** For Qwen2.5-Omni-7B, compute layer-wise KL between text and audio representations for PAP prompts; verify KL < 2.0 in mid-to-late layers.  
  2. **Jailbreak transfer test:** Generate textual jailbreaks (AutoDAN-Turbo, PAP), convert to audio via TTS, evaluate SR on same model; compare against audio-only attacks (Speech Editing) and naive audio.  
  3. **Cross-model transfer under audio-only access:** Use Qwen3-Omni as surrogate to collect high-SR textual jailbreaks (SR ≥ 0.75), convert to audio, attack target model (e.g., GPT-4o-audio) with audio-only input; measure SR and compare to direct audio-only attacks.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question:** Are modality-aligned omni-models also adversarially aligned?  
  **Basis:** Introduction explicitly asks this.  
  **Why unresolved:** Transfer observed in some models but not others; boundary conditions (alignment strength, architecture, prompts) unknown.  
  **What evidence would resolve:** Systematic evaluation across many models, measuring KL and attack success to identify precise conditions for the alignment curse.

- **Question:** Which textual jailbreak prompt properties enable cross-modality transfer?  
  **Basis:** Authors attribute ReNeLLM failure to TTS distortion of formatting, implying prompt structure matters.  
  **Why unresolved:** No systematic study of prompt attributes; only a few methods compared.  
  **What evidence would resolve:** Controlled experiments varying attributes (punctuation, code, numbers) and measuring KL/success to derive transfer principles.

- **Question:** Can KL divergence between text and audio representations predict jailbreak transfer?  
  **Basis:** Theorem 3.2 bounds output difference by KL; empirical KL<2 for success, >2 for failure, but threshold not validated.  
  **Why unresolved:** Curse line (KL=2) based on limited data; unknown if generalizable across models, TTS, attacks.  
  **What evidence would resolve:** Large-scale measurement of KL and success for many (text,audio) pairs across diverse settings to establish a robust threshold.

## Limitations
- TTS configuration (voice, speed, engine) unspecified; could alter semantic fidelity and attack success rates.
- Proprietary model internals (preprocessing, tokenization) and API constraints limit precise replication and representation extraction.
- StrongReject evaluator’s model and prompts unclear, introducing potential scoring inconsistency across reproductions.

## Confidence
- **Alignment curse (text→audio transfer):** High – consistent pattern across models, supported by theory and empirical data.
- **KL divergence threshold (δ < 2.0) as condition:** Medium – empirical support but estimation noise and dataset specificity may affect generalizability.
- **Failure modes (ReNeLLM, InteractiveOmni):** Medium – clear pattern but based on limited model set; additional testing needed.

## Next Checks
1. **TTS robustness:** Run text-transferred attacks with multiple TTS engines/voices; compute SR and KL; expect stable high success if alignment holds.
2. **KL stability:** Re-estimate layer-wise KL with varied seeds and PCA dimensions (10–20); produce confidence intervals for the δ threshold.
3. **Break condition probe:** For high-KL cases (ReNeLLM), force TTS to preserve formatting (e.g., spell out symbols) and measure whether SR improves and KL drops below 2.0.
