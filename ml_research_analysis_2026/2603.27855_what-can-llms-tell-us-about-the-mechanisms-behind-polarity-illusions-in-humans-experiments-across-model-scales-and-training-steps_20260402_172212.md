# What can LLMs tell us about the mechanisms behind polarity illusions in humans? Experiments across model scales and training steps

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.27855
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27855
- **Reference count**: 7
- **Primary result**: LLMs exhibit opposite scaling trends for two polarity illusions (NPI illusion weakens, depth charge illusion strengthens) with model size, indicating that shallow processing may explain such illusions without requiring rational inference.

## Executive Summary
This paper explores whether large language models (LLMs) manifest human-like polarity illusions—specifically, the negative polarity item (NPI) illusion and the depth charge illusion—to shed light on mechanisms of human sentence processing. Using the Pythia scaling suite (70M to 12B parameters) and beam search to generate continuations, the authors classify semantic polarity via Δlogprob. They find that the NPI illusion (where embedded negation licenses NPIs more than matrix negation) weakens and eventually disappears in larger models, while the depth charge illusion (where sentences like "No X is too Y to Z" prompt negative completions) becomes stronger. Training dynamics of a 1.4B model show the NPI illusion emerging later, whereas the depth charge illusion appears early (~10k steps). These results argue against accounts that rely on rational inference about noisy communication, favoring instead explanations based on shallow processing or grammaticalization in LLMs, with potential implications for theories of human cognition.

## Method Summary
The study employs the Pythia suite of autoregressive language models spanning 70M to 12B parameters. Stimuli are crafted to elicit two polarity illusions: the NPI illusion (comparing embedded negation vs. matrix negation contexts) and the depth charge illusion (sentences of the form "No X is too Y to Z"). For each prompt, the model generates multiple continuations using beam search. The semantic polarity (positive or negative) of each continuation is quantified by computing the difference in log probability between positive and negative cue words (Δlogprob). This continuous measure is thresholded to assign a binary polarity label. The frequency of negative classifications per condition is then compared across model scales and, for a 1.4B model, across training checkpoints (from 0 to 143k steps). Statistical analyses (e.g., mixed-effects models) test for significant trends.

## Key Results
- **NPI illusion effect size decreases with model scale**, becoming non-significant in the largest models (12B).
- **Depth charge illusion effect size increases with model scale**, showing a positive correlation across the 8 model sizes.
- **Training dynamics differ**: The NPI illusion pattern emerges after ~20k steps, while the depth charge illusion is detectable as early as 10k steps and strengthens subsequently.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
LLMs provide a controlled testbed for isolating the contributions of statistical learning and architectural constraints on phenomena that in humans are tied to complex cognitive systems. By systematically varying model scale and training time, we can observe how illusion patterns change as linguistic representations become more refined. The observed inverse scaling for NPI and direct scaling for depth charge suggest that these illusions may arise from different underlying mechanisms: the NPI illusion may depend on surface-level negator detection that improves with scale, while the depth charge illusion may stem from deeper semantic compositionality that benefits from increased capacity. The absence of a monotonic trend also challenges theories that would predict both illusions to strengthen with more data or parameters, pointing instead to the role of grammaticalization processes that emerge only under certain resource constraints.

## Foundational Learning
- **Polarity Illusion**: A mismatch between literal meaning and expected interpretation due to negation scope. *Why needed*: Central phenomenon investigated. *Quick check*: Identify the illusion in “No student is too weak to fail” (expected negative outcome).
- **Negative Polarity Item (NPI)**: Words like “ever” that require a negative licensor in the same clause. *Why needed*: NPI licensing is used to probe the NPI illusion. *Quick check*: “She didn’t ever go” is acceptable; “She ever went” is not.
- **Depth Charge Illusion**: A specific polarity illusion where sentences like “No X is too Y to Z” evoke negative completions despite lacking explicit negation in the embedded clause. *Why needed*: Contrast with NPI to test if different illusions scale differently. *Quick check*: “No expert is too skilled to err” suggests a negative result (erring).
- **LLM Scaling**: The practice of increasing model size (parameters, data, compute) to improve performance. *Why needed*: The paper tests whether illusion strength changes with scale. *Quick check*: Larger models generally achieve higher zero-shot accuracy on NLP benchmarks.
- **Δlogprob (Delta log probability)**: The difference in log probability between a positive and a negative continuation. *Why needed*: Serves as a continuous measure of polarity bias. *Quick check*: If p(positive) > p(negative), Δlogprob > 0 indicates a positive bias.
- **Beam Search**: A decoding algorithm that explores the k most probable sequences at each step. *Why needed*: Used to generate multiple high-quality continuations for each prompt. *Quick check*: Beam search often yields more fluent but less diverse outputs than sampling.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Prompt → LLM (Pythia) → Beam Search → Continuations → Δlogprob calculation → Polarity classification → Aggregation → Scaling/Training analysis  

**Critical path**:  
1. Design stimulus sets for NPI and depth charge illusions.  
2. For each model checkpoint, run beam search over prompts to obtain continuations.  
3. Compute Δlogprob for each continuation using pre-defined positive/negative cue words.  
4. Apply threshold (e.g., Δlogprob > 0) to assign polarity label.  
5. Aggregate the proportion of negative classifications per condition.  
6. Fit statistical models (e.g., logistic regression) to assess scaling and training trends.  

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- *Beam search vs. sampling*: Beam search ensures high probability outputs but may reduce diversity; sampling captures broader distribution but introduces noise.  
- *Δlogprob thresholding*: A continuous measure is more nuanced than binary classification, but threshold choice affects results.  
- *Model selection*: Pythia offers tightly controlled training; however, findings may not generalize to other architectures (e.g., encoder-decoder models).  

**Failure signatures**:  
- Extremely low log probabilities for both polarities → unreliable Δlogprob.  
- High variance across random seeds or across different cue word choices.  
- Models generating irrelevant or nonsensical continuations (e.g., repetition, off-topic).  
- Discrepancy between Δlogprob and human acceptability judgments.  
- Non-monotonic scaling trends that could indicate overfitting or data artifacts.  

**Three first experiments**:  
1. **Scale sanity check**: Run a small (70M) and a large (12B) model on the full NPI stimulus set; confirm that the NPI illusion is strong in the small model but absent in the large one.  
2. **Training curve replication**: For the 1.4B model, extract checkpoints at 0, 10k, 20k, 50k, 100k, 143k steps; compute the NPI illusion metric; verify it emerges around step 20k and grows.  
3. **Method robustness**: Replace beam search with top-p (nucleus) sampling (p=0.9); re-run on a subset; check whether the scaling trends for both illusions remain stable.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper does not explicitly list open questions; however, the authors note that the contrasting scaling trends for the two illusions suggest the need for further investigation into why different polarity phenomena respond differently to increased model capacity. They also highlight the importance of testing whether these findings generalize to other model families and to human behavioral data.

## Limitations
- Polarity classification relies on beam search outputs and Δlogprob, which may not fully capture subtle semantic differences or human-like judgments.  
- Findings are based solely on Pythia models; generalizability to other architectures (e.g., decoder-only vs. encoder-decoder) is unknown.  
- The analogy to human cognition is indirect—no behavioral human data is contrasted with model outputs, weakening mechanistic claims.  

## Confidence
- **Empirical scaling trends** (NPI illusion weakening, depth charge illusion strengthening with scale): High  
- **Training dynamics** (NPI illusion emerging later, depth charge illusion appearing ~10k steps): Medium  
- **Interpretation regarding human cognition mechanisms** (shallow processing/grammaticalization vs. rational inference): Low  

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-architecture replication**: Test the same stimuli on non-Pythia models (e.g., LLaMA, GPT, T5) to confirm scaling trends are not Pythia-specific.  
2. **Human-model behavioral alignment**: Run a matched human experiment measuring illusion strength (e.g., acceptability judgments) and correlate with model Δlogprob patterns.  
3. **Methodological robustness check**: Replace beam search with diverse sampling (top-p, temperature) and re-analyze; also validate Δlogprob polarity categorization via human annotation of a subset of continuations.
