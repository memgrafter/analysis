# Interactive 3D visualization of surface roughness predictions in additive manufacturing: A data-driven framework
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.09353
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09353
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Conditional GAN augmentation reduces roughness prediction MAE by 20% (2.200→1.752 μm) and increases R² from 0.844 to 0.900 on hold-out test set.

## Executive Summary
Surface roughness in fused filament fabrication (FFF) is challenging to predict during process planning because it depends on complex interactions between printing parameters and local surface inclination. To address this, the authors generated a structured experimental dataset using a three-level Box–Behnken design, producing 87 specimens that yielded 1566 roughness measurements across a range of process conditions and surface angles.

They trained a multilayer perceptron (MLP) regressor on the 7 process parameters and surface angle. To overcome limited training data, a conditional generative adversarial network (CGAN) generated synthetic samples conditioned on the same inputs, enriching under-sampled regions of the parameter–inclination space. CGAN augmentation significantly improved predictive performance on a hold-out test set, reducing mean absolute error from 2.200 μm to 1.752 μm and increasing the coefficient of determination (R²) from 0.844 to 0.900. SHAP analysis identified surface angle and layer height as the primary roughness drivers. The authors also developed a web-based interface that visualizes predicted roughness as an interactive colormap on uploaded 3D models, enabling rapid exploration of parameter and orientation trade-offs.

## Method Summary
The study began with a designed experiment: a three-level Box–Behnken matrix across seven FFF process parameters (layer height, extrusion temperature, outer wall speed, infill density, wall thickness, bed temperature, fan speed). This yielded 87 printed specimens; each was measured with a profilometer at multiple surface inclinations, resulting in 1566 (Ra, parameters, angle) records, publicly available on Zenodo (doi:10.5281/zenodo.18827518). For prediction, the authors implemented a multilayer perceptron regressor with batch normalization, dropout, and ReLU activations. Hyperparameter optimization (layers, width, dropout rate, learning rate, L2 regularization) was performed via Optuna with 5‑fold cross‑validation, using min‑max normalization within each fold. To mitigate data scarcity, a conditional GAN was trained on the real training subset to generate synthetic (Ra, parameters, angle) tuples; the best augmentation ratio (3× the real training size) was selected based on validation performance. The final MLP, trained on the combined real and synthetic data, was exported to a web application that parses user‑uploaded meshes, computes per‑facet normals and inclination angles, queries the model for Ra, and renders a continuous colormap on the 3D surface. This pipeline enables practitioners to explore how build orientation and process parameters jointly affect roughness before printing.

## Key Results
- CGAN augmentation improved hold-out test performance: MAE decreased from 2.200 μm to 1.752 μm and R² increased from 0.844 to 0.900.
- SHAP analysis confirmed surface angle and layer height as the two most influential features governing roughness.
- A web-based decision‑support tool provides real‑time, geometry‑aware roughness visualization on arbitrary 3D models, allowing rapid identification of high‑roughness regions and comparison of build orientations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Conditional GAN (CGAN) augmentation improves the MLP's generalization by enriching sparsely sampled regions of the joint parameter‑inclination space.
- **Mechanism:** The CGAN learns the conditional distribution \(P(R_a | \mathbf{x})\) from the limited experimental data, where \(\mathbf{x}\) includes process parameters and surface angle. It generates synthetic \((\mathbf{x}, R_a)\) pairs that fill gaps in the training distribution, reducing model variance without introducing significant bias if augmentation is controlled.
- **Core assumption:** The experimental design, while structured, leaves some combinations of parameters and inclinations under‑sampled; synthetic samples that preserve conditional relationships can stabilize learning in these regions.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "...CGAN augmentation improved predictive performance on a hold‑out test set, reducing mean absolute error from 2.200 μm to 1.752 μm and increasing the coefficient of determination (R²) from 0.844 to 0.900."
  - [section 3.3] "To mitigate limited experimental data, a conditional generative adversarial network was used to generate additional condition‑specific tabular samples, thereby improving predictive performance."
  - [corpus] Weak direct support; neighboring papers discuss data augmentation generally but do not validate this specific CGAN‑inclination conditioning approach for AM roughness.
- **Break condition:** If the CGAN learns erroneous conditional modes or if augmentation ratio exceeds an optimal threshold, synthetic samples may dominate and degrade performance on real measurements.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The MLP regressor effectively captures the nonlinear, interactive effects of process parameters and surface inclination on roughness.
- **Mechanism:** The fully connected architecture with nonlinear activations approximates the complex mapping \(R_a = f(\text{params}, \theta)\), where interactions (e.g., layer height × inclination) are implicitly learned from the feature vector. Batch normalization and dropout help generalize despite small dataset size.
- **Core assumption:** The experimentally measured relationships contain learnable patterns beyond simple linear or quadratic responses; the BBD provides sufficient coverage of main effects and key interactions for the MLP to generalize.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "A multilayer perceptron regressor was trained to capture nonlinear relationships between manufacturing conditions, inclination, and \(R_a\)."
  - [section 4.1.3] "EDA indicates that \(R_a\) is governed by both direct process effects (e.g., layer height) and geometry‑driven effects (surface angle). The multimodal structure... supports the use of nonlinear learners."
  - [section 4.2] Scatter plots show the MLP tracks non‑monotonic roughness trends across angles and parameters.
- **Break condition:** If the true underlying relationships involve higher‑order interactions or unmeasured confounders (e.g., material thermal history) not represented in the 7 process parameters + angle, the MLP's predictions will be biased for out‑of‑support conditions.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Interactive 3D colormap visualization translates point‑wise roughness predictions into intuitive geometry‑aware trade‑off exploration.
- **Mechanism:** The web tool computes facet‑wise inclination from the uploaded mesh, combines with user‑specified parameters, queries the trained MLP/CGAN pipeline, and renders \(R_a\) as a continuous colormap. Real‑time updates allow users to see how orientation changes redistribute roughness across surfaces.
- **Core assumption:** Practitioners can interpret spatial color gradients on a 3D model more readily than scalar metrics or 2D plots, enabling faster identification of problematic regions and comparison of build orientations.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "A web‑based decision‑support interface... visualizes predicted \(R_a\) as an interactive colormap on the surface, enabling rapid identification of regions prone to high roughness and immediate comparison of parameter and orientation choices."
  - [section 3.4] "The system computes face‑wise inclination from the model geometry and visualizes predicted \(R_a\) as a color‑mapped overlay... Users can select any surface region to access the predicted roughness and its associated inclination angle."
- **Break condition:** If the mesh is too dense (performance lag) or if users misinterpret colormap continuous gradients for discrete categorical decisions, the tool may not reduce trial‑and‑error as intended.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Design of Experiments (DoE) with Box‑Behnken Design**
  - *Why needed here:* To efficiently explore the 7‑factor space (capturing curvature and interactions) with only 87 specimens instead of a full factorial (\(3^7\)), while ensuring each specimen provides 18 inclination‑specific measurements.
  - *Quick check question:* What specific advantage does a three‑level Box‑Behnken design offer over a full factorial for this study’s parameter set?
- **Concept: Conditional Generative Adversarial Networks (CGANs) for Tabular Data**
  - *Why needed here:* To synthetically augment the small experimental dataset in a way that respects the conditioning on process parameters and inclination, avoiding unrealistic samples that could harm learning.
  - *Quick check question:* How does conditioning the generator on \(\mathbf{x}\) (params + angle) differ from unconditional data augmentation, and why is it critical here?
- **Concept: SHAP (SHapley Additive Explanations) for Model Interpretability**
  - *Why needed here:* To validate that the MLP’s learned features align with domain knowledge (surface angle and layer height as primary drivers) and to understand non‑monotonic effects, building trust in the model’s predictions for decision support.
  - *Quick check question:* Why might SHAP show both positive and negative contributions for surface angle across different samples, and what does that imply about roughness formation?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Data Generation → Augmentation Pipeline → Predictive Model → Decision‑Support Interface
- **Critical path:** Experimental data collection → MLP/CGAN training (with validation‑based augmentation ratio selection) → model export → integration into web backend → frontend visualization layer.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *MLP architecture simplicity vs. expressiveness:* Deeper/wider networks risk overfitting on 1331 training samples; dropout and early stopping were used.
  - *Augmentation ratio:* Too little synthetic data yields minimal benefit; too much introduces generator bias (validated via hold‑out test degradation at high ratios).
  - *Web interface interactivity vs. precision:* Facet‑level inclination calculation is fast for moderate mesh complexity but may require decimation for large parts.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - *Predictive model:* High MAE on high‑\(R_a\) tail (Figure 12 dispersion) indicates under‑sampled roughness regimes; SHAP may reveal unmodeled interactions.
  - *CGAN:* Synthetic \(R_a\) distribution diverges from real (Figure 14); poor coverage of conditional manifolds → no validation improvement.
  - *Interface:* Laggy updates for complex meshes; user confusion if colormap scale isn’t normalized across parameter/orientation changes.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Replicate baseline MLP: Train MLP on real‑only data using the exact 85/15 split; verify MAE ≈ 2.2 μm, \(R^2\) ≈ 0.844 on hold‑out.
  2. CGAN augmentation sweep: Train CGAN on real training subset; generate synthetic sets at 1×, 3×, 5× real training size; retrain MLP on each augmented set; identify optimal ratio (here 3×) via validation MAE.
  3. Interface usability pilot: Have 5 novice users predict roughness for a sample part; measure time to identify "roughest face" before vs. after using the tool; collect qualitative feedback on orientation trade‑offs.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How well does the proposed roughness prediction model generalize to different filament materials (e.g., ABS, PETG, composites) and hardware configurations (e.g., varying nozzle diameters, printer models, slicer implementations) without retraining or recalibration?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper explicitly states the dataset was collected using a single printer (Bambu Lab A1) and a single material (PLA marble), and lists "generalizability across a broader range of materials and filament formulations" and "system‑specific variables, such as printer model, nozzle diameter" as future work. The limitation section notes the fixed machine and material may limit direct applicability.
- **Why unresolved:** The model's learned relationships are tied to the specific process‑structure‑property chain of the experimental setup. Transferring to new materials or machines requires understanding if the core mappings (e.g., layer height → roughness) hold or if new interactions dominate, which is unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A follow‑up study where the trained model is applied directly to print jobs using different materials/printers, measuring the degradation in predictive accuracy (e.g., increase in MAE, drop in R²). Systematic testing across a matrix of material‑printer combinations would quantify the scope of the generalization gap.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can the framework's geometry‑aware prediction be extended from planar facets to complex, non‑planar geometries (e.g., curved surfaces, freeform shapes) by developing robust local surface descriptors beyond simple inclination angle?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The "Future work" section explicitly calls for expanding "beyond planar facets to encompass more complex geometries by incorporating curved surfaces and more detailed local descriptors, such as curvature or neighborhood‑based orientation statistics."
- **Why unresolved:** The current method calculates roughness based on per‑facet normals relative to the build direction, which is well‑defined for planar triangles but becomes ambiguous for curved surfaces where "local inclination" is not a single value. The interaction between process parameters and continuous geometric features like curvature density is untested.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Creation of a new experimental dataset with specimens containing controlled curved regions (e.g., cylinders, spheres, blended fillets), measurement of roughness on those surfaces, and development/validation of new geometric features (e.g., mean curvature, Gaussian curvature, local cone angle) to replace or augment the single surface angle. Performance of the MLP with these new features would be compared against the planar‑only baseline.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Does integrating real‑time, in‑situ sensor data (e.g., thermal imaging, vibration, acoustic emission) with the pre‑print predictive model enable accurate *online* surface roughness estimation and adaptive process control to compensate for machine‑specific disturbances?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The final "Future work" point proposes "integrating in‑situ sensing and real‑time monitoring... could enable online roughness estimation and closed‑loop control," transitioning from pre‑print decision support to adaptive control.
- **Why unresolved:** The current framework is purely a feedforward predictor using planned process parameters and static geometry. It does not account for temporal process variations (e.g., thermal fluctuations, inconsistent filament flow, printer mechanical wear) that cause deviation from the predicted roughness. The feasibility of fusing a static data‑driven model with dynamic sensor streams for control is unexplored.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Development of a hybrid system where the pre‑print MLP provides a baseline prediction, and deviations in real‑time sensor data (e.g., temperature profiles, axis motor currents) are used by a secondary model (e.g., a time‑series predictor or reinforcement learning agent) to adjust subsequent layer parameters (e.g., flow rate, speed) in a closed loop. Validation would require experimental runs where the system actively controls printing based on sensor feedback, with post‑print roughness measurements used to quantify control efficacy versus the open‑loop (pre‑print only) baseline.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Does the interactive 3D visualization tool actually reduce costly trial‑and‑error in real‑world process planning compared to traditional slicing software, and what are its usability limitations for novice vs. expert users?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper claims the interface "facilitates rapid exploration of trade‑offs" and "reduces reliance on costly trial‑and‑error," but provides no empirical user evaluation. This is an assumed benefit based on the tool's design, not a measured outcome.
- **Why unresolved:** The tool's efficacy as a decision‑support system hinges on human factors: whether users can correctly interpret the colormap, efficiently explore the parameter‑orientation space, and make better choices than without it. Its complexity (managing multiple parameters, 3D orientation) might overwhelm novices, while experts might find it redundant. No user study validates these assumptions.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled user experiments with participants of varying expertise (novice, intermediate, expert) performing a defined process planning task (e.g., "minimize average roughness on critical faces" for a given part). Metrics would include number of physical test prints required, time to reach a decision, and quality of the final chosen parameters compared to a control group using standard slicing software. Qualitative feedback on interface usability and trust in predictions would also be collected.

## Limitations
- Dataset is small (87 specimens) and restricted to a single material (PLA) and one FFF printer; generalization to other materials, printers, or AM processes is untested.
- The MLP model remains a black box for specific interaction effects; out‑of‑distribution geometries (e.g., extreme overhangs) may perform poorly.
- The web tool's performance on complex meshes and its actual impact on user decision‑making have not been empirically validated.
