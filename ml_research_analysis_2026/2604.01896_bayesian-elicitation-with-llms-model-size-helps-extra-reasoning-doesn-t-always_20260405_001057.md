# Bayesian Elicitation with LLMs: Model Size Helps, Extra "Reasoning" Doesn't Always

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.01896  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01896  
- **Reference count:** 17  
- **Primary result:** Larger LLMs produce more accurate Bayesian elicitation estimates; increased reasoning effort does not consistently improve accuracy or calibration; all models are severely overconfident but normalized conformal prediction effectively recalibrates intervals.

## Executive Summary
This paper evaluates eleven large language models on Bayesian elicitation tasks—estimating population statistics with credible intervals—across health, personality, and labor datasets. The key findings are threefold: model scale strongly predicts accuracy, with larger-capability models performing better; extending reasoning effort via chain-of-thought or thinking budgets yields no consistent gains in accuracy or calibration; and all models exhibit severe overconfidence (empirical coverage of nominally 95% intervals ranging from 9% to 44%), which can be nearly entirely corrected by a simple post-hoc conformal prediction recalibration. Preliminary web-search tool use harms accurate models but modestly helps weaker ones.

## Method Summary
The study prompts each model (OpenAI GPT-5.2/mini/nano, Anthropic Claude Opus/Sonnet/Haiku, Google Gemini 3 Pro/Flash, DeepSeek-Reasoner/Chat) with 400 parameterized questions derived from four real-world datasets. Prompts request a point estimate and a 95% credible interval (lower, upper). Models are queried at varying reasoning effort levels (low/medium/high) via token budgets or vendor-specific parameters. Responses are parsed to extract numeric triplets; invalid responses are flagged. Performance is measured by negative log-likelihood (NLL), empirical coverage, and interval sharpness. For calibration, a split-conformal procedure uses normalized residuals \(|y - \hat{y}|/\sigma\) on a calibration set (30%) to compute a multiplicative factor \(q̂\), which is applied to expand test intervals. A preliminary tool-augmentation experiment enables native web search on a subset of questions.

## Key Results
- Larger, more capable models achieve lower NLL (higher accuracy) across datasets; within-family comparisons confirm scale as the primary driver.
- Across all thinking-capable models, pooled Spearman tests show no significant monotonic relationship between reasoning effort and NLL (\(\rho = -0.014, p=0.24\)) or absolute error (\(\rho = -0.013, p=0.32\)).
- Raw 95% interval coverage is severely low (9%–44%); after normalized conformal prediction, coverage substantially improves and approaches the nominal 95% target, with typical expansion factors \(q̂\) between 2 and 5.
- Preliminary web search degrades predictions for already-accurate models (e.g., Claude Sonnet) but yields modest improvements for weaker models (e.g., GPT-5 nano).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Model Scale Improves Accuracy
- **Claim:** Larger, more capable LLMs produce more accurate point estimates in Bayesian elicitation tasks.
- **Mechanism:** Increased model scale (parameters, training data, compute) enhances internalized knowledge about population statistics, enabling more accurate retrieval and combination of relevant training examples during the forward pass.
- **Core assumption:** Task-relevant statistical knowledge exists in the training distribution and scales with model capacity.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] "First, larger, more capable models produce more accurate estimates..."  
  [section IV.D] "Within-family comparisons confirm that model capability is the primary driver... Claude Opus vs. Haiku... larger 5.2 achieves better coverage."  
  [corpus] Neighbors confirm a broader theme of LLM overconfidence and calibration issues but do not directly contradict the size-accuracy link.
- **Break condition:** If future architectures or training methods enable smaller models to achieve comparable knowledge compression, or if tasks shift to domains entirely absent from training data, the scaling advantage may diminish.

### Mechanism 2: Reasoning Effort Does Not Consistently Help
- **Claim:** Increased reasoning effort (e.g., extended CoT) does not consistently improve accuracy or calibration for Bayesian elicitation.
- **Mechanism:** Extended "thinking" tokens primarily generate longer, more verbose reasoning traces that recombine or elaborate on the same underlying pattern-matched knowledge rather than performing genuine logical or mathematical revision. It can amplify existing biases or overconfidence without improving factual grounding.
- **Core assumption:** CoT is a conditional generation process over the model's distribution, not a discrete reasoning step that revises an initial estimate.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] "...increased reasoning effort showed no consistent benefit for accuracy or calibration."  
  [section IV.B] "Pooled Spearman trend tests across all thinking models show no significant monotonic relationship between effort and either NLL (ρ=−0.014, p=0.24) or absolute error (ρ=−0.013, p=0.32)."  
  [corpus] Neighbors [4,5] directly support the pattern-matching view of CoT, suggesting reasoning collapses under distribution shifts.
- **Break condition:** If a future model architecture explicitly separates knowledge retrieval from deliberative reasoning (e.g., with verifier modules or external tools for calculation), or if CoT is fine-tuned for uncertainty-aware revision, reasoning effort could become beneficial.

### Mechanism 3: Normalized Conformal Prediction Recalibrates Overconfidence
- **Claim:** Normalized conformal prediction effectively recalibrates severely overconfident LLM-generated intervals to near-nominal coverage.
- **Mechanism:** The LLM's raw intervals are systematically too narrow because its internal uncertainty representation (\(\sigma_i\)) underestimates true error. Conformal prediction uses a calibration set to compute a multiplicative quantile factor (\(\hat{q}\)) on normalized residuals \(|y_i - \hat{y}_i|/\sigma_i\). Scaling each interval by \(\hat{q}\) expands it to achieve marginal coverage guarantees under exchangeability.
- **Core assumption:** Overconfidence is a systematic, distribution-level bias (narrow intervals) rather than random noise, and the error distribution is sufficiently homogeneous across the task.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] "Third, a statistical recalibration technique called conformal prediction can correct this overconfidence..."  
  [section IV.C] "After applying normalized residual conformal prediction, coverage improves substantially and approaches the 95% target... The conformal quantiles \(\hat{q}\) typically range from 2 to 5..."  
  [section III.E] Details the normalization and quantile calculation.  
  [corpus] Neighbors [13,14] demonstrate CP for LLM calibration, providing external validation.
- **Break condition:** If the source of overconfidence varies dramatically across subpopulations (violating exchangeability) or if the model's interval width \(\sigma_i\) becomes a poor predictor of absolute error (non-monotonic relationship), normalized CP may fail to produce valid coverage for all groups.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Bayesian Elicitation  
  **Why needed here:** The paper evaluates LLMs on extracting a full probability distribution (point estimate + credible interval) from internal knowledge. Understanding elicitation clarifies the goal: it is not just point accuracy but calibrated uncertainty.  
  **Quick check question:** What is the key difference between a *credible interval* (Bayesian) and a *confidence interval* (frequentist) in the context of this paper's task?

- **Concept:** Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Reasoning & Extended Thinking  
  **Why needed here:** The paper manipulates "reasoning effort" via CoT/thinking budgets. Understanding that CoT is a decoding strategy (not necessarily logical reasoning) is crucial to interpret why effort might not help.  
  **Quick check question:** According to the cited work [4,5], why might increasing CoT length fail to improve performance on mathematical or distribution-shifted questions?

- **Concept:** Conformal Prediction (CP)  
  **Why needed here:** CP is the primary technical solution proposed to fix the overconfidence problem. Knowing it is a distribution-free, post-hoc calibration method that provides marginal coverage guarantees is essential.  
  **Quick check question:** Why does the paper use *normalized* residuals (\(|y-\hat{y}|/\sigma\)) instead of absolute residuals (\(|y-\hat{y}|\)) for the nonconformity score?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Model API Layer -> Prompt Engine -> Response Parser -> Metrics Calculator -> Conformal Calibrator -> Tool-Augmentation Module
- **Critical path:**  
  1. Model selection & configuration  
  2. Question generation & prompting  
  3. Response collection & validation  
  4. Metric computation (raw)  
  5. Conformal calibration (split data)  
  6. Metric recomputation (calibrated)  
  7. Comparative analysis (size vs. effort, pre/post CP)
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Reasoning effort vs. cost:* High effort tokens are expensive and often yield no accuracy gain; medium effort may be sufficient.  
  - *Calibration data requirement:* CP needs ~15+ labeled examples per (model, dataset) group; zero-shot elicitation cannot use CP without a pilot calibration set.  
  - *Domain-specific prompts:* Using generic prompts may perform worse; tailoring templates to dataset structure (percentages vs. means) is important.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Invalid response rate >25%: Model frequently refuses or asks for clarification, indicating topic is too unfamiliar or prompt is unclear.  
  - Raw coverage <<95% (e.g., <40%): Universal overconfidence; requires recalibration before any use.  
  - Post-CP coverage still <<95% for groups with <15 calibration points: Unreliable CP due to small calibration set.  
  - Web search degrades accurate models: Suggests tool use disrupts internal knowledge rather than augmenting it.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Size Baseline:** Run all models at a single, standard reasoning effort (e.g., "medium") across all 400 questions. Compute raw NLL and coverage. Confirm larger models (Gemini Pro, Claude Opus) significantly outperform smaller ones.  
  2. **Effort Sweep:** For each thinking-capable model, vary reasoning effort (low/medium/high). Plot NLL and coverage per model. Perform per-model Kruskal-Wallis and pooled Spearman tests to verify no consistent effort trend.  
  3. **Conformal Recalibration:** For each (model, effort, dataset) group with ≥15 calibration points, apply normalized CP. Compare pre/post coverage histograms. Report multiplicative factors \(q̂\) (typically 2–5).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:** Can fine-tuning LLMs directly improve calibration of elicited uncertainty intervals?  
**Basis:** [explicit] "Future work should explore calibration fine-tuning" (Conclusion).  
**Why unresolved:** Models are severely overconfident (coverage 9–44%); fine-tuning could embed calibration without post‑hoc corrections.  
**What evidence:** Train on elicitation tasks with a coverage‑oriented loss; evaluate raw interval coverage against the target without conformal prediction.

**Open Question 2:** Do ensemble methods reduce overconfidence and improve accuracy in LLM Bayesian elicitation?  
**Basis:** [explicit] "Future work should explore ensemble methods" (Conclusion).  
**Why unresolved:** Individual LLMs show universal under‑coverage; ensembles might aggregate knowledge for better calibration.  
**What evidence:** Combine predictions from multiple LLMs or samples; compare coverage, sharpness, and NLL against single‑model baselines.

**Open Question 3:** How can tool‑augmented LLM elicitation avoid degrading accurate models while assisting weaker ones?  
**Basis:** [explicit] "Future work should explore improved tool integration" (Conclusion).  
**Why unresolved:** Web search decreased performance for accurate models (e.g., Claude Sonnet) indicating interference with internal priors; optimal tool use unclear.  
**What evidence:** Conduct large‑scale experiments varying search policies, query count, and result integration; measure asymmetric effects across model capabilities.

## Limitations
- Exact model sizes/architectures are unknown; reproduction relies on vendor tier names.
- Prompt templates are described but not publicly released, risking reconstruction variance.
- Invalid/refusal responses introduce selection bias, especially at high token budgets.

## Confidence
- Larger models yield more accurate estimates: **High**
- Increased reasoning effort shows no consistent benefit: **Medium**
- Normalized CP effectively recalibrates overconfident intervals: **High**

## Next Checks
1. **Scale diversity replication:** Test additional open-weight models (e.g., Llama 3, Command R) across a wider size spectrum to confirm the monotonic size‑accuracy relationship and identify any deviations.
2. **Reasoning process analysis:** Perform a fine‑grained content analysis of chain‑of‑thought outputs to distinguish genuine self‑correction from mere verbosity; compare alternative decoding strategies like self‑consistency or verifier reranking.
3. **Conformal calibration under subgroup shift:** Apply normalized CP within demographic strata (e.g., age, gender) to probe exchangeability; report coverage variance and, if needed, evaluate stratified conformal methods.
