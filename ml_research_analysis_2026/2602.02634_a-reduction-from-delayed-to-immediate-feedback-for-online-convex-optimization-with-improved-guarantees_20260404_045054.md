# A Reduction from Delayed to Immediate Feedback for Online Convex Optimization with Improved Guarantees

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.02634
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02634
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Introduces a continuous-time model for delayed feedback, achieving regret $O(\sqrt{d_{\text{tot}}})$ for convex BCO and $O(\min\{\sigma_{\max}\ln T, \sqrt{d_{\text{tot}}}\})$ for strongly convex BCO, improving previous delay-dependent terms.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses online convex optimization (OCO) and bandit convex optimization (BCO) with round-dependent delays. The key innovation is a continuous-time model that reorders predictions and observations on a shared timeline, leading to a regret decomposition into a non‑delayed learning term and a delay‑induced drift term. This decomposition enables a black‑box reduction that transforms any algorithm for online linear optimization with drift penalization into one that handles delays. For convex BCO, the reduction improves the delay‑dependent regret term from $O(\min\{\sqrt{T d_{\max}},(T d_{\text{tot}})^{1/3}\})$ to $O(\sqrt{d_{\text{tot}}})$, and for strongly convex BCO, from $O(d_{\max}\ln T)$ to $O(\min\{\sigma_{\max}\ln T,\sqrt{d_{\text{tot}}}\})$. The reduction also recovers state‑of‑the‑art bounds for first‑order OCO with a simpler analysis. An adaptive skipping scheme further refines the delay term to $O(\min_{Q\subseteq[T]}|Q|+\sqrt{\sum_{t\notin Q}d_t})$. The method is delay‑adaptive, requiring no prior knowledge of delays or the horizon.

## Method Summary
The method centers on a continuous‑time model (CTM) that places each round’s prediction time and observation time on a timeline. The player’s predictions are assumed steady (constant between observations). By reordering rounds by observation times, the regret decomposes into a standard regret term (as if feedback were immediate) and a drift term that measures deviation due to delays. Two wrapper algorithms—WOCO for first‑order feedback and WBCO for bandit feedback—forward gradient estimates along with dual quantities (dual‑delay $d'_n$ and dual‑backlog $\sigma'_n$) to a base algorithm for online linear optimization with drift penalization. The base algorithm’s stability (small drift between consecutive predictions) controls the total drift term. For convex losses, proximal FTRL (P‑FTRL) yields the desired drift bound; for strongly convex losses, a constant step size suffices. The bandit case uses single‑point gradient estimation via $\delta$‑smoothing. An optional adaptive skipping wrapper (Sskip) discards feedback from rounds with excessively large delays, improving the delay term to a more flexible expression.

## Key Results
- For convex BCO, regret is $O(\sqrt{d_{\text{tot}}} + T^{3/4}\sqrt{k})$, improving the delay‑dependent term from $O(\min\{\sqrt{T d_{\max}},(T d_{\text{tot}})^{1/3}\})$ to $O(\sqrt{d_{\text{tot}}})$.
- For strongly convex BCO, regret is $O(\min\{\sigma_{\max}\ln T,\sqrt{d_{\text{tot}}}\} + (T^2\ln T)^{1/3}k^{2/3})$, improving from $O(d_{\max}\ln T)$.
- For first‑order OCO, the reduction recovers state‑of‑the‑art bounds with a simpler analysis.
- The adaptive skipping scheme refines the delay term to $O(\min_{Q\subseteq[T]}|Q|+\sqrt{\sum_{t\notin Q}d_t})$.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Continuous‑time model and decomposition
By modeling each round as an interval between prediction and observation, and reordering by observation time, the regret decomposes into a non‑delayed learning term and a drift term proportional to the dual‑delay. This separation holds for steady algorithms (predictions constant between observations). Lemma 3.6 shows that the actual prediction equals the base prediction minus a dual‑delay shift; Lipschitzness then yields the decomposition. *Evidence*: Definition 3.1, Lemma 3.6, Figure 4.

### Mechanism 2: Black‑box reduction
The wrapper (WOCO/WBCO) processes feedback in observation order, computes dual quantities, and forwards gradient estimates with lags $\lambda_n = d'_n$ to a base algorithm for drift‑penalized online linear optimization. The base algorithm’s small drift (controlled by its update rule) bounds the total drift term, and thus the delay‑induced error. The reduction is black‑box: any base algorithm with good drift‑penalized regret yields a delayed algorithm. *Evidence*: Algorithms 1 and 2, Theorems 5.2 and 5.6, Theorem 4.1.

### Mechanism 3: Adaptive skipping
The Sskip wrapper monitors pending feedback; if a round’s pending time exceeds $\sqrt{D_t}$, it is skipped (dummy zero loss sent). This caps effective delays and yields a regret bound $O(\min_{Q\subseteq[T]}(|Q|+\sqrt{\sum_{t\in Q}d_t}))$, which can be significantly smaller than $\sqrt{d_{\text{tot}}}$ for skewed delay distributions. The technique builds on Zimmert & Seldin (2020). *Evidence*: Algorithm 3, Theorem 6.1.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Base algorithm (P‑FTRL/OMD) <-> Wrapper (WOCO/WBCO) <-> Environment; optional Sskip wrapper can be inserted between Wrapper and Environment to preemptively skip feedback.

### Critical path
1. Initialize base algorithm $B$.
2. For each round $t$: if no new feedback, output current base prediction (WOCO: steady; WBCO: compute $x_t$ from base).
3. When feedback for round $s$ arrives (i.e., $s+d_s = t$):
   - Determine observation index $n$ (count of observations so far).
   - Compute $d'_s$ and $\sigma'_s$ (from stored timestamps or Theorem 3.4).
   - For bandit: compute gradient estimate using stored exploration direction.
   - Forward $(gradient\_estimate, d'_s, \sigma'_s)$ to $B$, get updated base prediction.
4. For skipping: before querying $B$ for a prediction, check pending rounds and forward dummy zeros if pending too long.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Base algorithm choice:** P‑FTRL gives tighter drift bound (Lemma 4.2) crucial for bandit improved rates; OMD is simpler but yields weaker strong convexity rates.
- **Learning rate schedules:** Must be non‑increasing. For convex OCO/BCO, $\eta_n$ depends on cumulative $\sum \sigma'_{\rho(m)}$ or $\sum d'_{\rho(m)}$. For strong convexity, constant rates $1/(n\lambda)$ work.
- **Smoothing parameters $\delta_t$:** For bandit, can be round‑dependent. Smaller $\delta_t$ reduces bias but increases variance.
- **Skipping:** Adds an external wrapper that modifies the perceived problem. Trade‑off between reducing delay‑dependent terms and discarding potentially useful information.

### Failure signatures
- **Excessive drift:** Base algorithm’s predictions change too much between updates (e.g., learning rate too large), causing drift term to blow up.
- **Incorrect dual computation:** Miscomputing $d'_n$ or $\sigma'_n$ (e.g., ignoring observation ordering) invalidates the reduction.
- **Bandit domain violation:** Forgetting $rB_k\subseteq K$; predictions may leave $K$, breaking smoothing guarantees.
- **Missing variance term in bandit learning rate:** Omitting $(\nu k r/\delta'_{1n})^2$ in the denominator leads to insufficient learning rate when $\delta_t$ is large.
- **Skipping over‑aggressiveness:** Threshold too low skips too many rounds, harming learning.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Base algorithm stability test:** Implement P‑FTRL and OMD for drift‑penalized OLO. Verify drift $\sum\|z_n - z_{n-\lambda_n}\|$ matches bounds from Theorem 4.1 for synthetic lag sequences.
2. **Wrapper correctness test:** On synthetic delayed OCO (linear losses, fixed delays), compare WOCO(P‑FTRL) against naive delayed gradient descent. Verify regret decomposition in Theorem 5.2 (plot non‑delayed vs drift terms).
3. **Skipping benefit test:** On heterogeneous delays (e.g., first $\sqrt{T}$ rounds have delay $T$, rest delay $1$), compare Sskip(WOCO) with/without skipping. Measure effective delay term and test under strong convexity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** Can the continuous‑time reduction framework be extended to handle adaptive adversaries where delays depend on the learner’s actions?  
**Basis in paper:** The authors state in Section 7: “Extending the framework to adaptive adversaries, where delays may depend on the learner’s actions, would be technically challenging and is relevant to congestion control and strategic environments.”  
**Why unresolved:** The analysis relies on fixed delay sequences known in advance for the drift term decomposition; adaptive delays would require adapting the reduction or bounding techniques.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** A new analysis showing regret bounds for adaptive delay setting, or a reduction that transforms adaptive delays into an effective oblivious problem with controlled regret overhead.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** How can the continuous‑time framework be applied to multi‑agent online learning settings with heterogeneous, time‑varying delays, such as federated learning?  
**Basis in paper:** The authors mention: “Our model may provide a useful abstraction for multi‑agent online learning with heterogeneous, time‑varying delays, including federated learning systems.”  
**Why unresolved:** The paper only considers single‑agent OCO/BCO; multi‑agent introduces game‑theoretic complications, communication constraints, and heterogeneous delays that aren’t addressed.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** A concrete multi‑agent protocol based on the continuous‑time model, with regret or convergence analysis for specific settings (e.g., distributed OCO with delayed gradients).

### Open Question 3
**Question:** Do the theoretical regret bounds improve in practice when using more sophisticated base algorithms for drift‑penalized OLO beyond P‑FTRL/OMD?  
**Basis in paper:** The paper uses P‑FTRL and OMD as base algorithms but doesn’t explore whether other algorithms (e.g., adaptive methods, mirror descent with different potentials) could yield tighter practical performance, especially for the drift term.  
**Why unresolved:** The analysis bounds drift in terms of learning rates and $\nu_n$; better stability properties or adaptive step sizes might reduce the drift coefficient.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Experimental comparison of the reduction with different base algorithms on standard delayed OCO/BCO benchmarks, or theoretical analysis showing improved constants or dependency on delay parameters.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** What are the practical implications of the improved delay‑dependent terms (e.g., $O(\sqrt{d_{\text{tot}}})$ vs $O(\sqrt{T} d_{\max})$) on real‑world systems with highly skewed delay distributions?  
**Basis in paper:** The paper focuses on asymptotic worst‑case regret bounds but doesn’t discuss practical delay distributions or the skipping scheme’s impact on real data.  
**Why unresolved:** The theoretical improvement might not manifest if $\sigma_{\max}$ is large or if the skipping scheme’s thresholding is hard to tune; empirical studies are needed to understand which delay metrics correlate with actual system performance.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Simulation studies or real‑world deployments measuring the effect of delay distributions on the algorithms’ performance, comparing the theoretical bounds to observed regret.

### Open Question 5
**Question:** Can the reduction framework be applied to non‑convex online optimization or other learning paradigms like reinforcement learning with delayed feedback?  
**Basis in paper:** The continuous‑time model is presented as general (Section 7: “Our continuous‑time model provides a general framework for delayed feedback that extends beyond the specific scope of online convex optimization”), but all results are for convex losses.  
**Why unresolved:** Non‑convex losses break the convexity needed for regret decompositions and drift control; the reduction’s guarantees may not hold or require different analysis.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Extending the reduction to non‑convex settings with appropriate notions of performance (e.g., stationarity measures) or showing limitations via lower bounds.

## Limitations
- Exact smoothing schedule for two‑point bandit (Appendix G) and constant factors in P‑FTRL drift bounds (Lemma 4.2, Theorem E.3) are unspecified.
- Interaction of the skipping wrapper (Sskip) with WOCO/WBCO is not formally derived; recomputing dual quantities after skips may be needed.
- Analysis assumes an oblivious adversary; adaptive adversaries are not covered.
