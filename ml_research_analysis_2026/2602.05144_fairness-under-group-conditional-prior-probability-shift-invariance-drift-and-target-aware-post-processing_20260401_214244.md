# Fairness Under Group-Conditional Prior Probability Shift: Invariance, Drift, and Target-Aware Post-Processing

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.05144
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05144
- **Reference count**: 29
- **Primary result**: TAP-GPPS reduces the demographic parity gap from 0.106 to 0.020 on synthetic data with about 2% accuracy loss and consistently achieves sub-0.02 DP gaps on real datasets (Adult, COMPAS) under simulated prevalence shifts.

## Executive Summary
Deploying machine learning models often involves changes in the prevalence of outcomes across demographic groups, a phenomenon called group‑conditional prior probability shift (GPPS). Under GPPS, the relationship between features and labels conditioned on group membership stays stable, but the group‑specific label distributions change. This shift breaks fairness guarantees for metrics that depend on group prevalences, such as demographic parity, while preserving those based on conditional error rates like equalized odds.

To combat this, the authors propose TAP‑GPPS, a post‑processing algorithm that requires only unlabeled target data. It estimates target group prevalences, adjusts the source model’s posterior probabilities via importance weighting, and selects group‑specific decision thresholds to equalize acceptance rates. Theoretically, they prove that separation‑based criteria are invariant under GPPS, whereas acceptance‑rate criteria drift generically. Empirically, TAP‑GPPS reduces the demographic parity gap from 0.106 to 0.020 on synthetic data with a mere 2% accuracy drop, and it consistently achieves sub‑0.02 DP gaps on real datasets under simulated shifts. Additionally, the method supports label‑free estimation of target risk with finite‑sample guarantees.

## Method Summary
TAP‑GPPS adapts a pretrained binary classifier to a target domain with shifted group‑conditional label prevalences without needing target labels. The pipeline consists of: (1) training and calibrating a source model \(P(Y=1|X,A)\) on labeled source data; (2) estimating target group prevalences \(\pi_{\text{tgt},a}\) from unlabeled target samples via EM or BBSE; (3) correcting source posteriors using importance weights \(w_{1,a}=\pi_{\text{tgt},a}/\pi_{\text{src},a}\) and \(w_{0,a}=(1-\pi_{\text{tgt},a})/(1-\pi_{\text{src},a})\) in a closed‑form transformation; (4) for a grid of desired acceptance rates \(\gamma\), finding group‑specific thresholds by bisecting so that empirical corrected acceptance rates equal \(\gamma\); and (5) selecting \(\gamma^*\) that minimizes a label‑free estimate of target risk (Theorem 6.1) subject to a demographic parity bound. The approach is model‑agnostic, works with common classifiers, and leverages unlabeled target data for both prevalence estimation and threshold selection.

## Key Results
- TAP-GPPS reduces DP gap from 0.106 to 0.020 on synthetic data with ~2% accuracy loss.
- Consistently achieves sub-0.02 DP gaps on real datasets (Adult, COMPAS, MEPS) under simulated prevalence shifts.
- Provides label-free target risk estimation with finite-sample guarantees.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Invariance of Group-Conditional ROC Quantities
- **Claim**: Under group‑conditional prior probability shift (GPPS), group‑specific true positive rates (TPR) and false positive rates (FPR) remain unchanged between source and target domains.
- **Mechanism**: GPPS assumes \(P_{\text{tgt}}(X \mid Y=y, A=a) = P_{\text{src}}(X \mid Y=y, A=a)\). Since TPR and FPR are defined as expectations conditional on \((Y,A)\), they inherit this invariance directly.
- **Core assumption**: GPPS holds exactly; no change in \(P(X \mid Y, A)\) across domains.
- **Evidence anchors**: 
  - Abstract states “separation‑based criteria like equalized odds are structurally invariant under GPPS.”
  - Section 4.1 (Lemma 4.1, Corollary 4.2) proves score distribution and ROC invariance.
  - Works on label shift (Saerens et al., 2002; Lipton et al., 2018) support conditional invariance; this paper extends to group‑wise settings.
- **Break condition**: If \(P_{\text{tgt}}(X \mid Y, A) \neq P_{\text{src}}(X \mid Y, A)\) (e.g., covariate shift), ROC invariance fails and separation‑based fairness may drift.

### Mechanism 2: Drift of Demographic Parity via Prevalence Dependence
- **Claim**: Demographic parity (equal acceptance rates) can violate under GPPS because acceptance rates depend affinely on group‑conditional prevalences.
- **Mechanism**: Acceptance rate for group \(a\) at threshold \(t\) is \(AR_{s,a}(t) = \pi_{s,a} \cdot \text{TPR}_a(t) + (1 - \pi_{s,a}) \cdot \text{FPR}_a(t)\). Under GPPS, \(\text{TPR}_a\) and \(\text{FPR}_a\) are invariant but \(\pi_{s,a}\) changes, causing \(AR_{s,a}\) to drift linearly with prevalence shifts.
- **Core assumption**: The classifier is informative (\(\text{TPR}_a \neq \text{FPR}_a\)) and prevalence shifts are heterogeneous across groups (\(\pi_{\text{tgt},a} / \pi_{\text{src},a}\) varies with \(a\)).
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - Abstract notes “acceptance‑rate criteria like demographic parity are not” invariant.
  - Section 4.3 (Proposition 4.6, Corollary 4.7) derives the affine decomposition and quantifies DP drift.
  - Related work on fairness under shift (e.g., Giguere et al., 2022) studies demographic shift but this paper isolates the prevalence mechanism.
- **Break condition**: If \(\text{TPR}_a = \text{FPR}_a\) for all groups (uninformative classifier) or prevalence ratios are identical across groups, DP might hold; otherwise drift is generic (Theorem 4.11).

### Mechanism 3: TAP‑GPPS for Target‑Aware Fairness Adaptation
- **Claim**: TAP‑GPPS is a label‑free post‑processing pipeline that corrects source posteriors for target prevalences and selects group‑specific thresholds to achieve demographic parity in the target domain with minimal utility loss.
- **Mechanism**: It estimates target prevalences \(\pi_{\text{tgt},a}\) from unlabeled target data (via EM or BBSE), applies groupwise posterior correction using importance weights \(w_{1,a} = \pi_{\text{tgt},a} / \pi_{\text{src},a}\), and then finds thresholds via bisection such that corrected acceptance rates are equal across groups.
- **Core assumption**: Unlabeled target data is available; the source model is well‑calibrated or recalibrated; GPPS approximately holds for posterior correction.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - Abstract summarizes TAP‑GPPS as “estimates target group prevalences from unlabeled data, corrects posterior probabilities… selects group‑specific thresholds.”
  - Section 5 details steps: prevalence estimation (5.2), posterior correction (5.1), threshold selection (5.3), and Algorithm 1.
  - Builds on label shift adjustment (Saerens et al., 2002; Lipton et al., 2018) and fair post‑processing (Hardt et al., 2016).
- **Break condition**: If unlabeled target data is too scarce for reliable prevalence estimation, or if source model miscalibration is severe, posterior correction may be inaccurate, leading to persistent DP gaps or excessive accuracy drop.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Group‑Conditional Prior Probability Shift (GPPS)
- **Why needed here**: It defines the shift model where label prevalences \(P(Y \mid A)\) change across domains but \(P(X \mid Y, A)\) stays fixed, explaining fairness drift patterns.
- **Quick check question**: How does GPPS differ from standard label shift, and why does conditioning on \(A\) make it more realistic for group fairness?

### Concept: Fairness Criteria Dependence on Prevalence
- **Why needed here**: To predict which fairness metrics (e.g., equalized odds vs. demographic parity) transfer under GPPS without adaptation.
- **Quick check question**: Derive why demographic parity depends on prevalence via the law of total probability, while equalized odds does not.

### Concept: Posterior Correction via Importance Weights
- **Why needed here**: It enables adapting a source‑calibrated model to target prevalences without retraining, central to TAP‑GPPS.
- **Quick check question**: Given \(p_{\text{src}}(1 \mid x, a)\) and prevalence ratios \(w_{1,a}, w_{0,a}\), write the formula for \(p_{\text{tgt}}(1 \mid x, a)\) and interpret it in log‑odds space.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Source model training -> Prevalence estimator (EM/BBSE) -> Posterior corrector -> Threshold optimizer -> Risk estimator (optional)

**Critical path**:
1. Train and calibrate source model on labeled source data.
2. Estimate target group prevalences \(\hat{\pi}_{\text{tgt},a}\) from unlabeled target samples using EM or BBSE.
3. Compute corrected posteriors \(\hat{p}_{\text{tgt}}(1 \mid x,a)\) via the importance‑weight formula.
4. Grid‑search over target acceptance level \(\gamma\); for each, use bisection to find thresholds \(t_a\) such that empirical corrected acceptance rates equal \(\gamma\).
5. Select \(\gamma^*\) that minimizes label‑free estimated risk (Theorem 6.1) while respecting a DP gap tolerance \(\delta\).
6. Deploy the classifier \(\hat{y} = \mathbb{1}\{p_{\text{tgt}}(1 \mid x,a) \ge t_{\gamma^*}(a)\}\).

**Design tradeoffs**:
- EM assumes a well‑calibrated source model; BBSE is more robust but requires a stable confusion matrix and may be unstable for rare groups.
- Bisection search for thresholds is efficient but relies on monotonicity of acceptance rate curves; insufficient unlabeled data can cause non‑monotonicity.
- The utility‑fairness trade‑off is controlled by \(\gamma\); lower \(\gamma\) improves accuracy but may enlarge the DP gap if not tuned properly.

**Failure signatures**:
- Residual DP gap > 0.05 after post‑processing suggests inaccurate prevalence estimates, source miscalibration, or violation of GPPS.
- Large discrepancy between estimated and true prevalences (when labels are available) indicates insufficient unlabeled target data or ineffective estimator.
- Accuracy drop > 5% relative to source‑only may indicate over‑correction, poor threshold alignment, or gross miscalibration.
- Non‑monotonic acceptance‑rate curves during bisection can be diagnosed with inadequate unlabeled sample size or extreme posterior clipping.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Synthetic invariance test**: Generate Gaussian data with fixed \(P(X|Y,A)\) and varying target prevalences. Measure DP and EO gaps to confirm EO remains constant while DP drifts, reproducing Figure 1.
2. **TAP‑GPPS validation on synthetic shift**: Apply the full pipeline to a predefined prevalence shift (e.g., \(\pi_{\text{src}}=(0.3,0.5)\) → \(\pi_{\text{tgt}}=(0.5,0.3)\)). Report DP gap reduction and accuracy loss relative to source‑only and unadapted baselines, replicating Table 1.
3. **Real‑data simulation**: Use Adult or COMPAS datasets, induce GPPS by stratified resampling to match target prevalences. Compare TAP‑GPPS against source‑only and naive baselines in terms of DP gap and accuracy under various shift magnitudes.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How can we develop methods and theoretical guarantees that are robust to violations of the GPPS assumption, i.e., when the conditional feature distribution \(P(X|Y,A)\) also shifts rather than remaining invariant?
- **Basis in paper**: The authors state: “Developing methods and guarantees under approximate GPPS … remains important future work.” (Discussion, Limitations and extensions)
- **Why unresolved**: The TAP‑GPPS algorithm and its guarantees rely critically on exact GPPS. Real‑world deployments may only approximate this assumption, and it is unclear how deviations affect posterior correction and threshold selection.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Deriving robustness bounds that quantify the impact of small departures from GPPS, combined with empirical validation on datasets with known approximate shifts, would demonstrate feasibility.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: How can we adapt fairness interventions when the sensitive attribute \(A\) is not available at deployment time for groupwise prevalence estimation and thresholding?
- **Basis in paper**: The authors note: “Developing methods … under restricted access to A, remains important future work.” (Discussion)
- **Why unresolved**: TAP‑GPPS requires knowing each instance’s group membership to apply group‑specific thresholds and estimate group‑specific prevalences. In many settings, \(A\) may be missing or privacy‑sensitive, necessitating alternative approaches.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: An algorithm that uses proxy variables or aggregated statistics to satisfy fairness constraints without direct access to \(A\), supported by theoretical guarantees under reasonable assumptions and empirical tests on data where \(A\) is masked.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: How can the TAP‑GPPS framework be generalized to multi‑class classification outcomes and to continuous or non‑binary sensitive attributes?
- **Basis in paper**: The authors write: “Extensions to multi‑class outcomes and continuous sensitive attributes are natural and can be handled by applying the same correction and thresholding ideas within each class/stratum.” (Discussion) This leaves the details open.
- **Why unresolved**: The current theory and algorithm are developed for binary labels and discrete groups. Multi‑class problems involve multiple label shifts and potentially more complex fairness criteria; continuous \(A\) may require new thresholding or stratification strategies.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Formal extension of the invariance/drift theorems to the multi‑class setting, derivation of appropriate multi‑group correction formulas, and a working multi‑class TAP‑GPPS implementation evaluated on multi‑class datasets.

## Limitations
- Exact hyperparameters (regularization strengths, learning rates, network size) for the models used in the main experiments are unreported.
- Detailed resampling procedure for real datasets (target prevalences, sample sizes) is not specified.
- BBSE implementation details (regularization, validation split usage) and EM convergence criteria are unspecified.
