# Investigating GNN Convergence on Large Randomly Generated Graphs with Realistic Node Feature Correlations

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.16145  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16145  
- **Reference count:** 34  
- **Primary result:** Realistic feature correlations can prevent GNN convergence on large sparse graphs, implying greater uniform expressiveness than previously thought.

## Executive Summary
Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) are known to converge to constant outputs on large random graphs when node features are independent, limiting their uniform expressiveness. This paper challenges that assumption by introducing homophilic correlations between node features that are proportional to preferential attachment during graph growth. Theoretical analysis predicts that for sparse graphs with a specific “rescaled” correlation scheme, the variance of aggregated features remains non‑vanishing as the graph grows, preventing convergence. Empirical tests with untrained GCN and GAT classifiers confirm that only under these conditions does the output distribution maintain high, size‑independent variance. Thus, realistic correlations can avert convergence, suggesting GNNs may be more expressive than prior work indicated.

## Method Summary
The authors extend the Barabási‑Albert (BA) model to generate random graphs with correlated node features. New nodes are added sequentially, each connecting to *m* existing nodes via preferential attachment. While sampling neighbors, a correlation coefficient is assigned to each neighbor based on its preferential‑attachment probability: either none, “simple” (ρᵢ = *p*ᵢ), or “rescaled” (ρᵢ = *p*ᵢ/Σ*p*ᵢ). Node features are 32‑dimensional continuous vectors initially drawn from Uniform[0,1], then transformed via a conditional multivariate normal to embed the correlations. Two densities are studied: sparse (*m* = 5) and dense (*m* = ⌊*n*/5⌋). For each graph size *n* (25–2000), 30 graphs are generated. An untrained 3‑layer GCN or GAT (with ReLU, weighted‑sum aggregation, mean pooling, and softmax readout) processes each graph. The mean and standard deviation of the three‑class probability output across the 30 graphs are computed and tracked across *n*. Convergence is identified when the standard deviation decays to zero.

## Key Results
- Theoretical analysis shows that for sparse graphs with rescaled correlations, the mean neighbor correlation stays constant (~1/*m*), preventing variance collapse in the aggregated features; in all other cases it decays to zero.
- Empirically, GNN outputs converge (variance → 0) for independent features, simple correlations, and all dense graphs. Only sparse graphs with rescaled correlations maintain a non‑zero, *n*-independent variance.
- This pattern holds for both GCN and GAT, confirming that realistic homophilic correlations can significantly enhance GNN uniform expressiveness on large graphs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1:**  
The correlation scheme ties feature correlation to preferential‑attachment probabilities, generating homophily that is stronger for high‑degree nodes. During BA growth, a new node’s feature vector is sampled from a conditional multivariate normal given its neighbors’ features, with correlation coefficients ρᵢ set either as *p*ᵢ (simple) or *p*ᵢ/Σ*p*ᵢ (rescaled). This produces positive correlation between connected nodes and ensures that nodes with higher degree exert greater influence. The core assumption is that real networks exhibit assortative mixing by degree and that node features correlate with degree. Evidence is found in the detailed sampling procedure of Section 2 and the contrast with prior work that assumes independent features.

**Mechanism 2:**  
Theoretical analysis predicts that for sparse graphs with rescaled correlations, the expected ratio of mean neighbor correlation to total correlation (E(C₁)/E(Q)) remains constant (~1/*m*) as *n* → ∞, whereas in all other cases it decays to zero. This is derived using approximations of the BA degree distribution and Wallenius’ noncentral hypergeometric distribution. Constant non‑zero correlations imply that the variance of aggregated node features does not vanish, preventing convergence. The core assumption is that the BA model’s asymptotic degree distribution follows a power law and that the Wallenius approximation accurately captures sampling without replacement. Evidence is provided in Section 3.1 and builds on the framework of prior work (e.g., “Almost Surely Asymptotically Constant Graph Neural Networks”).

**Mechanism 3:**  
Empirical tests with untrained GNNs confirm the theoretical predictions. For each condition (sparse/dense × no/simple/rescaled correlation × GCN/GAT), 30 graphs are generated per *n*, and the GNN output distribution’s standard deviation across graphs is measured. Convergence (decaying standard deviation) occurs for no correlation, simple correlation, and all dense graphs. Only sparse graphs with rescaled correlations exhibit a size‑independent, non‑zero standard deviation. This result demonstrates that realistic correlations can avert convergence, suggesting that GNN uniform expressiveness may be higher than previously believed. The experiment design follows Adam‑Day et al. but diverges when correlations are introduced.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Graph Generator (BA + correlation) → Graph instances of size *n* → Fixed‑random‑init GNN (GCN or GAT, 3 layers, mean pooling, softmax) → 3‑class probability output → Variance calculation across 30 graphs per *n*.

**Critical path:** To reproduce the main figure: (a) Implement BA generation with the three correlation schemes exactly as in Section 2. (b) For each *n* in {25, 50, 100, 200, 500, 1000, 2000} (or similar log‑spaced values), generate 30 graphs under each condition (sparse/dense × correlation). (c) Apply the same randomly initialized GNN (e.g., PyTorch Geometric defaults, hidden dim 32, GAT heads=1). (d) For each *n*, compute the average class probability vector and its standard deviation across the 30 graphs. (e) Plot mean probabilities vs. *n* with shaded bands representing ±1 std. (f) Compare across conditions and GNN types.

**Design tradeoffs:** Using untrained GNNs isolates the effect of graph structure from training. Mean pooling provides a simple readout. The correlation schemes are designed to be degree‑proportional, mimicking assortative mixing. The dense setting uses *m* = ⌊*n*/5⌋ to ensure quadratic edge growth. The study acknowledges limitations: BA’s unrealistic clustering, exclusive focus on continuous features, and the independence assumption between neighbor features during sampling.

**Failure signatures:**  
1. Misimplemented correlation sampling (e.g., using post‑sampling degrees, wrong ρᵢ). Diagnose: compute average ρᵢ; for simple on sparse, it should decay with *n*; for rescaled on sparse, it should be ~1/*m*.  
2. GNN architecture mismatch (e.g., sum instead of mean pooling, missing ReLU). Diagnose: first replicate no‑correlation case—standard deviation must decay with *n*.  
3. Insufficient number of graphs per *n* (less than 30) may yield noisy variance estimates.  
4. For dense graphs, *m* grows with *n*, so memory usage can spike; ensure efficient batching.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Correlation sanity:** For *n* = 100, sparse graphs, compute the average feature correlation between connected nodes. For simple correlation, expect ~ (log *n*)/(2*n*) ≈ 0.0023; for rescaled, expect ≈ 1/*m* = 0.2.  
2. **Convergence check:** For no‑correlation, run *n* = 100, 500, 1000 (30 graphs each) and verify that the standard deviation of the GNN’s class probabilities decreases monotonically with *n*, approaching zero.  
3. **Divergence verification:** For sparse + rescaled correlation, repeat the same size sweep and confirm that the standard deviation remains roughly constant (non‑zero) and does not decay with *n*.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How do variations in GNN architecture (non‑linearities, skip connections, width, aggregation functions) affect convergence behavior on large graphs with correlated features?  
2. Can the proposed correlation sampling scheme be adapted to discrete (one‑hot) node features, and would similar convergence avoidance occur?  
3. How robust are the findings when using other random graph models (e.g., Watts‑Strogatz, Bianconi‑Barabasi, GraphRNN/GRAN) that capture different network properties?  
4. What is the effect of negative or anti‑preferential correlations (heterophily) on GNN convergence, particularly for sparse graphs?  
5. To what extent do the theoretical approximations (independent feature dimensions, independence between neighbor features across iterations) affect the validity of the asymptotic predictions, and can a more rigorous analysis be developed?

## Limitations
- The study is confined to the Barabási‑Albert model, which has unrealistic clustering compared to many real networks.  
- Experiments use only untrained GCN and GAT with mean pooling; other architectures (e.g., with residual connections, different readouts) may behave differently.  
- Node features are continuous and uniformly distributed; discrete or categorical attributes are not considered.  
- Theoretical analysis relies on asymptotic approximations (e.g., Wallenius distribution) that may not hold for finite *n*.

## Confidence
- **Claim:** The correlation scheme creates realistic homophilic correlations tied to degree. **Confidence:** Medium – plausible but not directly validated on real networks.  
- **Claim:** For sparse rescaled correlations, theoretical analysis predicts constant E(C₁)/E(Q) ≈ 1/*m*. **Confidence:** Medium – derivation is sound under BA assumptions but depends on asymptotic approximations.  
- **Claim:** Empirically, only sparse rescaled graphs show non‑vanishing output variance. **Confidence:** Medium – consistent across sizes and GNN types, but limited to one graph model and untrained networks.
