# Locating Demographic Bias at the Attention-Head Level in CLIP's Vision Encoder

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.11793
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11793
- Reference count: 25
- Primary result: Mean ablation of four terminal attention heads reduces global gender bias (Cramér’s V: 0.381 → 0.362) while slightly improving accuracy (+0.42%); a single head (L23H4) accounts for most reduction, while age bias remains diffuse.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces a mechanistic audit to locate demographic bias within CLIP’s vision transformer at the attention-head level. By decomposing the model’s output into per-head contributions in the joint vision-language space, ranking heads via zero-shot Concept Activation Vectors (CAV) that measure alignment with demographic vs. occupational concepts, and corroborating with bias-augmented TextSpan, the authors identify a small set of terminal-layer heads driving gender bias. Mean ablation of these heads reduces a global gender bias metric (Cramér’s V) and even slightly improves accuracy, whereas age bias appears less localized and more diffusely encoded.

## Method Summary
The pipeline comprises five main steps. First, projected residual-stream decomposition extracts each attention head’s contribution to the final image embedding by projecting the head’s output via the final linear projection matrix into CLIP’s joint space. Second, zero-shot CAV ranking computes centroids of these projected outputs per profession and measures cosine similarity to text prototypes for occupations and demographic groups; heads with high relative demographic alignment (directional gap τ_gap) and sufficient occupational relevance (τ_occ) are flagged. Third, bias-augmented TextSpan extends the concept dictionary with demographic prototypes and uses SVD to identify top explanatory texts for head variance, providing qualitative corroboration. Fourth, a grid search over τ_gap and τ_occ selects head sets that minimize global Cramér’s V while ensuring accuracy does not drop. Fifth, mean ablation replaces the projected output of selected heads with their dataset mean, and the model’s zero‑shot predictions are re-evaluated. Results are compared against layer‑matched random ablations to test specificity.

## Key Results
- Four terminal-layer heads (L21H2, L21H10, L22H14, L23H4) drive gender bias; their mean ablation reduces Cramér’s V from 0.381 to 0.362 and improves accuracy by 0.42%.
- The final‑layer head L23H4 alone accounts for the majority of the bias reduction.
- For age bias, CAV identifies candidate heads but ablation yields weak and inconsistent effects, suggesting a more diffuse encoding.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Decomposing CLIP’s output into per-head contributions via residual-stream decomposition enables isolation of individual heads’ influence on biased predictions.
- **Mechanism:** The residual-stream hypothesis treats transformer outputs as a sum of additive components. For each attention head, its write to the residual stream is projected into CLIP’s joint vision-language space (Equation 2). This yields a human‑interpretable representation of what that head encodes, allowing targeted ablation.
- **Core assumption:** Head contributions are linearly additive and can be neutralized via mean replacement without corrupting the entire forward pass.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions “projected residual‑stream decomposition” as a core method; Section 3.1 formalizes decomposition; builds on prior work [11] for CLIP interpretability.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Zero‑shot CAV ranking identifies heads sensitive to demographic (vs. occupational) features by measuring alignment between head‑centric visual centroids and text prototypes.
- **Mechanism:** For each head and profession, a visual centroid is computed from projected head outputs. Cosine similarity to CLIP‑encoded text prototypes (occupation vs. demographic) yields alignment scores (Equations 3–4). Heads with high demographic alignment relative to occupation (directional gap τ_gap) and sufficient occupation relevance (τ_occ) are flagged.
- **Core assumption:** CLIP’s multimodal space linearly separates demographic and occupational concepts; a head’s centroid alignment reflects its role in routing predictions based on demographic attributes.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract cites “zero‑shot Concept Activation Vectors”; Section 3.2 defines prototypes and similarity metrics; adapts CAV [13] to zero‑shot setting.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Bias‑augmented TextSpan analysis provides semantic corroboration by checking whether demographic prototypes appear among the top explanatory texts for a head’s output variance.
- **Mechanism:** TextSpan projects head outputs into text space via SVD and identifies top‑K concepts explaining cross‑image variance. By augmenting the dictionary with demographic and occupation prototypes, if demographic terms surface in the top list, it independently suggests the head encodes bias‑related signal.
- **Core assumption:** Head variance is sufficiently captured by linear projection onto the extended text dictionary; demographic terms surfacing indicates the head is responsible for bias‑driven decisions.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions “bias‑augmented TextSpan analysis”; Section 3.3 details augmentation; based on Gandelsman et al. [11].

## Foundational Learning
- **Residual‑stream decomposition**  
  *Why needed here:* To isolate and manipulate individual attention heads’ contributions, which is essential for causal testing of bias sources.  
  *Quick check:* Why does the residual‑stream hypothesis allow attributing model output to specific heads or MLPs?

- **Zero‑shot Concept Activation Vectors (CAV)**  
  *Why needed here:* To rank heads by their alignment with demographic vs. occupational directions without training additional classifiers, leveraging CLIP’s pre‑aligned space.  
  *Quick check:* How does using text prototypes instead of labeled image sets change the CAV approach in multimodal models?

- **TextSpan algorithm**  
  *Why needed here:* To generate human‑readable labels for what each head encodes, providing qualitative validation that demographic concepts are among the top explanations.  
  *Quick check:* What is the effect of augmenting TextSpan’s concept dictionary with demographic prototypes, and why is this necessary for bias audits?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Image → Patch tokens + [cls] token → Patch embedding + Position embedding → Transformer blocks (L=24, each: MSA (H=16 heads) → LayerNorm → MLP → LayerNorm, with residual around MSA and MLP) → Final [cls] token → Projection to joint space. Per-head outputs: after MSA, each head’s output is projected via value‑output matrix and summed across tokens to yield a joint‑space vector.

**Critical path**  
1. Extract per‑head projected outputs for all images in FACET.  
2. Compute visual centroids per head and profession.  
3. Calculate cosine similarities to text prototypes (occupation, demographic groups).  
4. Apply τ_gap and τ_occ thresholds to select candidate bias heads.  
5. Perform mean ablation on selected heads (replace with dataset mean).  
6. Re‑run inference on evaluation set; compute Cramér’s V for gender/age and accuracy.  
7. Compare to layer‑matched random ablation (10 seeds) to confirm specificity.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Mean ablation gives diagnostic clarity (removes input‑specific signal) but may also remove other useful information and could redistribute predictions.  
- Grid search for thresholds uses the same evaluation metric, risking circularity; mitigated by independent random control, TextSpan corroboration, and class‑level analysis.  
- Focus on terminal layers (20–23) because earlier layers already near mean behavior; ablation effects are weaker there.  
- Attribute specificity: pipeline localised gender bias well but not age, suggesting different encoding strategies require tailored analysis.

**Failure signatures**  
- Selected heads’ ablation does not reduce target bias (ΔV ≈ 0) or increases it.  
- Random control shows similar ΔV → identified heads not specific.  
- CAV‑ranked heads lack demographic terms in TextSpan top‑K → misalignment between quantitative and qualitative evidence.  
- Age bias: CAV identifies heads but ablation effects inconsistent → bias may be diffuse or not head‑localisable.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. **Baseline fairness audit:** Compute Cramér’s V per profession for gender/age; identify significantly biased classes (BH‑corrected p<0.05). Establish baseline accuracy (64.30%).  
2. **CAV‑based head ranking:** For all heads, compute alignment scores (Equations 3–4). Sweep τ_gap/τ_occ grid; select threshold pair that minimizes V with no accuracy drop. Extract heads passing thresholds for at least one profession.  
3. **Mean ablation validation:** Ablate selected heads; measure global ΔV and accuracy. Run layer‑matched random ablation (same number of heads from same layers, 10 seeds). If suspected ΔV is outside random distribution’s mean ±2σ, effect is specific.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Why does gender bias concentrate in a small number of terminal attention heads, whereas age bias appears more diffusely encoded and less amenable to head‑level localisation in CLIP?
2. Does the head‑level bias localisation approach generalise to other vision‑transformer architectures (e.g., different sizes, training objectives) and other protected attributes (e.g., race, intersectional identities)?
3. How can we detect and mitigate bias affecting severely underrepresented demographic groups (e.g., non‑binary individuals) when evaluation datasets lack sufficient samples for reliable statistical analysis?
4. Is the cross‑attribute entanglement observed in head L23H4 (affecting both gender and age bias) indicative of a broader pattern where single attention heads encode multiple demographic signals, and how does this impact debiasing strategies?

## Limitations
- Grid search thresholds (τ_gap, τ_occ) and accuracy tolerance are unspecified, risking selection bias and overfitting.
- The projection matrix `P` definition (bias term, inclusion of layer normalization) is unclear, affecting head representations and CAV scores.
- TextSpan augmentation procedure (averaging order of 80 templates and 5 synonyms) is ambiguous, potentially altering qualitative corroboration.
- Demographic group sparsity (e.g., only 55 Non‑Binary images) limits statistical power and generalizability.

## Next Checks
1. **Hold‑out threshold selection:** Choose τ_gap/τ_occ on an independent validation split, then evaluate bias reduction on a held‑out test set to avoid circularity.
2. **Projection matrix verification:** Reconstruct baseline CLIP image embeddings by summing all per‑head projected outputs (plus MLP) and confirm near‑identical reconstruction (e.g., cosine similarity > 0.999) across a diverse image sample.
3. **Robustness to alternative metrics:** Repeat mean ablation using fairness metrics beyond Cramér’s V (e.g., demographic parity difference, equalized odds) and with ≥ 10 random seeds for control; check consistency of identified heads and effect sizes.
