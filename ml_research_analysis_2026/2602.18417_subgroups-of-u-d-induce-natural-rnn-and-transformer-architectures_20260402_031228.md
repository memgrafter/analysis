# Subgroups of $U(d)$ Induce Natural RNN and Transformer Architectures

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.18417
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18417
- **Reference count**: 7
- **Primary result**: OSMFormer with linear mixing achieves lower test BPC than standard Transformer on Tiny Shakespeare (2.464 vs 2.583) and Penn Treebank (1.614 vs 1.664) at ~100K parameters.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces a unified framework for sequence models where hidden states reside on closed Lie subgroups of the unitary group $U(d)$. By imposing three minimal axioms—causality, group closure, and local tangent updates—the authors derive general RNN and Transformer templates. States $H_t$ and token embeddings $M_x$ are elements of a subgroup $G$, and updates follow $H_{t+1}=H_t \exp(\Phi_\theta(H_t, M_x))$ with $\Phi_\theta$ mapping to the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$. For Transformers, attention scores use the invariant similarity $\Re\operatorname{tr}(H_i^* H_j)$, and a linear mixing component in tangent space reweights updates based on the readout’s predictive and memory channels. Instantiating the framework with $G=O(d)$ yields OSM-RNN and OSMFormer, which outperform Euclidean baselines on character-level language modeling and demonstrate improved optimizer robustness.

## Method Summary
The method generalizes sequence modeling to matrix groups. A closed Lie subgroup $G\le U(d)$ with Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ is chosen. Token embeddings are formed as $M_x=\exp(\Pi_\mathfrak{g}(B_x))$ with $B_x$ learned. For RNNs, the update is $H_{t+1}=H_t \exp(\Phi_\theta(H_t,M_x))$, where $\Phi_\theta$ outputs a tangent matrix in $\mathfrak{g}$. For Transformers, states are stacked across layers; attention scores are $s_{ij}=\tau\Re\operatorname{tr}(H_i^{(\ell)\,*} H_j^{(\ell)})$ (causal mask). The attended aggregate $V=\sum \alpha_{ij} H_j$ and the current embedding $M_{x_i}$ are processed by separate tangent generators $\Psi_{\theta,\text{attn}}$ and $\Psi_{\theta,\text{ground}}$ whose outputs are summed and projected to $\mathfrak{g}$. A tangent map $\Gamma_\theta$ (identity or linear mixing) reweights this sum before exponentiation: $H^{(\ell+1)}_i = H^{(\ell)}_i \exp(\Gamma_\theta(\cdot))$. The readout uses an invariant linear form $\ell_v(H)=\tau\Re\operatorname{tr}(H^*P_v)+b_v$ with $P_v=\exp(\Pi_\mathfrak{g}(C_v))$. The $O(d)$ instantiation uses $\Pi_\mathfrak{g}(Y)=\operatorname{skew}(Y)$ and $\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{o}(d)$. Linear mixing applies a learned linear layer to tangent coordinates. This yields the OSM-RNN and OSMFormer models.

## Key Results
- OSMFormer with linear mixing achieves lower test BPC than standard Transformer on Tiny Shakespeare (2.464 vs 2.583) and Penn Treebank (1.614 vs 1.664) at ~100K parameters.
- OSM-RNN surpasses LSTM baseline on both datasets.
- Ablation studies confirm linear mixing’s critical role and demonstrate improved robustness across optimizer settings.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Bounded geometric updates
Choosing a closed Lie subgroup $G\le U(d)$ as the state space replaces Euclidean hidden states with bounded, geometrically constrained dynamics. Hidden states $H_t$ and token embeddings $M_x$ lie in $G$, and the update $H_{t+1}=H_t\exp(\Phi_\theta(H_t,M_x))$ ensures $H_{t+1}\in G$ by group closure under multiplication and exponentiation. This construction embeds causality (dependence on $H_t$ and current token) and local updates (tangent-space innovation) directly into the transition. The compactness of $G$ and the exponential map’s local diffeomorphism property guarantee well-defined local-to-global moves.

### Mechanism 2: Tangent-space mixing for channel reallocation
The tangent map $\Gamma_\theta: \mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}$ (e.g., linear mixing) reallocates update capacity between the readout-visible (predictive) and readout-null (memory) channels defined by the differential of the logits. For the USIM readout, $D_H$ splits $\mathfrak{g}=\ker(D_H)^\perp\oplus\ker(D_H)$. The identity $\Gamma=\operatorname{Id}$ preserves raw tangent geometry; a learned linear $\Gamma$ can reweight updates across these subspaces, enhancing parameter efficiency under fixed budgets.

### Mechanism 3: Invariant similarity enables subgroup-native attention
The attention score $\Re\operatorname{tr}(H_i^* H_j)$ is invariant under global conjugation $H\mapsto G H G^{-1}$ (with $G\in G$). This eliminates learned query/key/value projections while preserving equivariance, making attention “subgroup-native.” The attention distribution and subsequent grounding steps produce tangent updates derived from relative terms like $H_i^*V$ before projection to $\mathfrak{g}$.

## Foundational Learning
- **Lie groups and Lie algebras** (e.g., $U(d)$, $O(d)$, $\mathfrak{u}(d)$, $\mathfrak{o}(d)$): Needed because the entire framework rests on states lying in a Lie group $G$ and updates in its Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ via the exponential map.  
  *Quick check*: Why must the tangent update $\Phi_\theta(H_t, M_x)$ lie in $\mathfrak{g}$ rather than an arbitrary matrix space to guarantee $H_{t+1}\in G$?
- **Matrix exponential** ($\exp: \mathfrak{g}\to G$): Needed to map tangent elements back to the group manifold; its local diffeomorphism property underlies the “local update” axiom.  
  *Quick check*: For $G=O(d)$, what is the form of elements in $\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{o}(d)$, and why does $\exp(A)$ yield an orthogonal matrix for any $A\in\mathfrak{o}(d)$?
- **Invariant bilinear forms** (e.g., $\Re\operatorname{tr}(A^*B)$ on matrix groups): Needed for attention scores and the USIM readout; conjugation-invariance ensures equivariance.  
  *Quick check*: Show that $\Re\operatorname{tr}((GAG^{-1})^*(GBG^{-1})) = \Re\operatorname{tr}(A^*B)$ for $G\in U(d)$; why is this important for the transformer’s attention mechanism?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map** (plain text flow):  
Token embedding $M_x$ and current state $H_t$ → tangent generator $\Phi_\theta$ (RNN) or $\Psi_\theta$ (Transformer) → tangent map $\Gamma_\theta$ → exponential $\exp(\cdot)$ → updated state $H_{t+1}$ → readout (invariant bilinear) → logits.

**Critical path**:
1. Pick a closed Lie subgroup $G$ (e.g., $O(d)$) and define its Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$, projection $\Pi_\mathfrak{g}$ (skew-symmetrization for $O(d)$), and exponential.
2. Design $\Phi_\theta$ or $\Psi_\theta$ to output matrices that, after $\Pi_\mathfrak{g}$, lie in $\mathfrak{g}$.
3. Choose $\Gamma_\theta$: start with identity; optionally implement linear mixing as a linear layer in tangent coordinates.
4. Implement token embeddings and readout prototypes via $\exp(\Pi_\mathfrak{g}(B_v))$ to stay in $G$.
5. For Transformers, compute attention scores as $\Re\operatorname{tr}(H_i^* H_j)$, form context $V$, and apply separate attention and grounding sub-networks before $\Gamma_\theta$.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- *Subgroup choice*: $U(d)$/$SU(d)$ offer complex, non-abelian representations; $O(d)$ uses real matrices (computationally cheaper); $\mathbb{T}^k$ is abelian. Trade-off between representational capacity and complexity.
- *Linear mixing*: Adds $\approx\dim(\mathfrak{g})^2$ parameters but can reallocate capacity beneficially. Risk of overfitting on small datasets.
- *On-group parameterization*: Using $\exp(\Pi_\mathfrak{g}(B))$ ensures validity but may cause gradient issues if $B$ grows; careful initialization needed.

**Failure signatures**:
- Training instability (diverging BPC) may indicate poor $\Gamma_\theta$ initialization or too large learning rates for exponential parameters.
- No improvement over Euclidean baselines could stem from insufficient subgroup capacity (e.g., $O(d)$ lacks phase) or ineffective linear mixing.
- Parameter budget mismatches: ensure $d$ and mixing sizes are adjusted to keep total parameters comparable.

**First three experiments**:
1. **Group closure sanity check**: For random $H_t$, $M_x$, compute $H_{t+1}=H_t\exp(\Gamma_\theta(\Phi_\theta(H_t,M_x)))$ and verify group constraint (e.g., $\|H^\top H-I\|_F\approx0$ for $O(d)$).
2. **Ablation of linear mixing**: Train OSMFormer with $\Gamma=$ identity vs. linear mixing at fixed parameter budget (~100K). Expect mixing to lower validation BPC.
3. **Optimizer robustness sweep**: Train with varied learning rates, clipping, weight decay; compare BPC ranges. OSMFormer should remain trainable across a broader range than the baseline.

## Limitations
- Evaluation is limited to two small character-level datasets (Tiny Shakespeare, Penn Treebank) and only the $O(d)$ subgroup; results may not generalize to larger or multimodal tasks.
- The linear mixing and invariant attention components are coupled in OSMFormer; their separate contributions are not isolated.
- Theoretical assumptions (closed subgroup, exponential map coverage) might be violated in practice with large updates, potentially causing instability—this was not extensively studied.

## Confidence
- Subgroup framework yields competitive models: **Medium** (evidence only on small datasets and one subgroup).
- Linear mixing improves performance: **Medium** (strong ablation at 100K parameters but scalability unverified).
- Invariant attention is natural/effective: **Medium** (works for $O(d)$; no comparison to learned QKV or other subgroups).
- Improved optimizer robustness: **Low** (limited optimizer sweep; needs systematic validation).

## Next Checks
1. **Scale evaluation**: Train OSMFormer and baseline Transformer on WikiText-103 (or larger) with model sizes from 100K to 10M parameters; report BPC/perplexity and stability.
2. **Subgroup diversity**: Implement at least two other closed Lie subgroups (e.g., $SU(3)$, $\mathbb{T}^4$) and compare to $O(d)$ on identical tasks, with and without linear mixing.
3. **Invariant attention ablation**: Replace the invariant similarity score $\Re\operatorname{tr}(H_i^* H_j)$ with learned query/key/value projections while keeping group-valued states and tangent updates; compare performance to the invariant version and Euclidean baselines.
