# MM-NeuroOnco: A Multimodal Benchmark and Instruction Dataset for MRI-Based Brain Tumor Diagnosis

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.22955  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22955  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: Introduces a multimodal brain tumor MRI dataset with 200k semantically enriched instructions and shows a 27% absolute accuracy improvement from fine-tuning, while a rejection mechanism reduces benchmark scores by ~10% for more reliable evaluation.

## Executive Summary
MM-NeuroOnco addresses the scarcity of diagnostic reasoning data for brain tumor MRI by introducing a multimodal benchmark comprising 24,726 MRI slices and 200,000 instruction samples. A multi-model pipeline automatically generates dense radiological attributes from sparse segmentation masks, enabling evidence-based chain-of-thought supervision. On the rejection-aware MM-NeuroOnco-Bench, ten state-of-the-art vision-language models (VLMs), including Gemini 1.5 Flash, achieve only 41.88% accuracy on diagnosis questions, highlighting significant challenges in clinical reasoning. Fine-tuning a model (NeuroOnco-GPT) on the proposed instruction set yields a 27% absolute accuracy improvement, demonstrating the dataset's effectiveness. The integrated rejection mechanism reduces scores by approximately 10%, mitigating forced-choice bias and offering a more reliable assessment of model reasoning.

## Method Summary
The dataset construction leverages existing brain tumor MRI with sparse segmentation masks. A multi-model pipeline processes these masks to auto-generate dense radiological attributes (e.g., tumor location, morphology, signal characteristics). These attributes are used to create semantically enriched instruction-response pairs that mimic radiographic reporting and diagnostic reasoning. The resulting MM-NeuroOnco-Bench incorporates a rejection-aware evaluation protocol, allowing models to abstain from answering when uncertain. Models are assessed on their ability to answer diagnosis questions, with the rejection option intended to reduce inflation of error rates from forced guesses. Fine-tuning experiments are conducted on a variant of a VLM (NeuroOnco-GPT) using the instruction dataset.

## Key Results
- MM-NeuroOnco provides 24,726 MRI slices and 200,000 instruction samples with auto-generated radiological attributes for chain-of-thought supervision.
- Top VLMs achieve a low 41.88% accuracy on the rejection-aware MM-NeuroOnco-Bench, indicating substantial difficulty.
- Fine-tuning NeuroOnco-GPT on the dataset produces a 27% absolute gain in diagnostic accuracy.
- The rejection mechanism lowers benchmark scores by ~10%, suggesting it successfully mitigates forced-choice bias.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The approach works by transforming sparse segmentation masks into rich, radiology-like attribute descriptions through an automated pipeline. This creates a large-scale, multimodal training corpus that teaches models to associate imaging patterns with structured clinical findings. The instruction format explicitly encourages chain-of-thought reasoning, moving beyond simple classification. The rejection-aware evaluation further anchors assessment in clinical realism, penalizing guesses and rewarding calibrated uncertainty. Together, the dataset's scale, semantic enrichment, and evaluation design address key gaps in medical VLM training and benchmarking.

## Foundational Learning
- **Multimodal MRI Analysis**: Integrating visual MRI slices with textual radiological attributes. *Why needed*: Brain tumor diagnosis requires correlating spatial imaging features with clinical descriptors. *Quick check*: Does the model correctly link tumor location on MRI to described neurological deficits?
- **Radiological Attribute Extraction**: Converting sparse segmentation masks into dense, descriptive attributes. *Why needed*: Sparse masks lack the nuanced descriptors radiologists use for reporting. *Quick check*: Are generated attributes (e.g., "ring-enhancing lesion") consistent with standard radiological terminology?
- **Chain-of-Thought Supervision**: Training models to produce step-by-step diagnostic reasoning. *Why needed*: Diagnostic accuracy depends on logical inference, not just endpoint prediction. *Quick check*: Does the model's reasoning trace logically connect imaging findings to a diagnosis?
- **Instruction Tuning**: Adapting vision-language models via task-specific instructions. *Why needed*: General VLMs lack proficiency in specialized medical reasoning. *Quick check*: Does fine-tuning improve performance on out-of-distribution but related medical imaging queries?
- **Rejection-Aware Evaluation**: Allowing models to abstain from answering uncertain questions. *Why needed*: Forced choices inflate error rates and misrepresent clinical utility. *Quick check*: Does abstention correlate with lower confidence and higher actual error rates?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: MRI slices with sparse segmentation masks -> Attribute extraction module -> Semantic instruction generator -> Instruction dataset -> NeuroOnco-GPT (fine-tuned VLM) -> Rejection-aware evaluator

**Critical path**: Sparse masks are processed by the attribute extraction module to generate radiological attributes; these attributes inform the creation of semantically enriched instructions; the instruction dataset is used to fine-tune a base VLM into NeuroOnco-GPT; the fine-tuned model is evaluated on the rejection-aware benchmark.

**Design tradeoffs**: Auto-generating attributes enables scalability but introduces noise from synthetic supervision; the rejection mechanism improves evaluation reliability but may reduce answer rate and complicate metric interpretation.

**Failure signatures**: Low baseline VLM accuracy indicates poor generalization to medical reasoning; high rejection rates could signal overcaution or mis-calibrated uncertainty; inconsistent attribute extraction may degrade instruction quality.

**First experiments**:
1. Evaluate top VLMs on MM-NeuroOnco-Bench with and without the rejection option to isolate the mechanism's impact.
2. Fine-tune a base VLM (e.g., LLaVA) on a stratified subset of instructions and measure accuracy changes across tumor subtypes.
3. Ablate the attribute generation step (use only raw masks) to quantify the contribution of semantic enrichment to model performance.

## Limitations
- Auto-generated radiological attributes lack direct human validation; errors in this synthetic supervision could propagate to model training.
- The MM-NeuroOnco-Bench is derived from a single institution's data, limiting generalization to diverse MRI protocols, scanner vendors, and global populations.
- The clinical utility of the rejection mechanism (e.g., reducing false positives in practice) remains unevaluated.

## Confidence
- **Dataset scale & enrichment** (24,726 slices, 200k instructions): High  
- **27% absolute gain from fine-tuning**: Medium  
- **Rejection mechanism mitigates forced-choice bias**: Low  

## Next Checks
1. **Human-in-the-loop attribute verification**: Manually audit a stratified sample of auto-generated radiological attributes against radiologist annotations to quantify error rates and systematic biases.
2. **External validation run**: Evaluate NeuroOnco-GPT and top baseline VLMs on a completely independent, publicly available brain tumor MRI dataset (e.g., BraTS) to test out-of-distribution robustness.
