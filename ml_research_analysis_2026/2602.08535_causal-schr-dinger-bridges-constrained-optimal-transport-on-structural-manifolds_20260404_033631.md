# Causal Schrödinger Bridges: Constrained Optimal Transport on Structural Manifolds

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv:** 2602.08535  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08535  
- **Reference Count:** 16  
- **Primary Result:** CSB reformulates counterfactual inference as entropic optimal transport, using diffusion processes to robustly "tunnel" through support mismatches while enforcing structural admissibility via a causal graph, achieving high-fidelity transport in high-dimensional causal systems where structure-blind methods fail.

## Executive Summary
Deterministic generative flows collapse under causal interventions because they must transport probability across low-density "off-manifold" regions, leading to numerical instability and anticipatory control. The Causal Schrödinger Bridge (CSB) addresses this by reformulating counterfactual inference as an entropic optimal transport problem constrained by a known causal graph. It employs diffusion processes (SDEs) to stochastically tunnel through support mismatches and introduces a Structural Decomposition Theorem that factorizes the high-dimensional transport into local, parallelizable transitions. Empirical results on a 100,000-dimensional causal chain show CSB achieving high fidelity (MSE ≈ 0.06) in minutes, while structure-blind MLPs fail (MSE ≈ 0.31) and cubic-complexity baselines become infeasible. On Morpho-MNIST, CSB reduces transport cost by 72% and improves structural consistency by 3.2×, demonstrating robustness to off-manifold challenges.

## Method Summary
CSB frames counterfactual inference as finding a stochastic process (the Schrödinger bridge) that transports mass from an observational source distribution to an interventional target distribution while minimizing the KL divergence from a reference dynamic and respecting causal constraints. The core innovation is the Structural Decomposition Theorem, which proves that under a known acyclic causal graph, the global high-dimensional bridge factorizes into a product of independent local bridges between each node and its parents. This factorization reduces the problem to solving many small entropic optimal transport tasks—each amenable to diffusion-based SDE solvers—enabling linear scalability in the number of nodes. The resulting global process is formed by composing these local stochastic transitions in topological order, ensuring that the final transport respects the graph's conditional independencies.

## Key Results
- **High-dimensional scalability:** On a full-rank 100,000-dimensional causal chain, CSB achieves MSE ≈ 0.06 in 73.73 seconds, while structure-blind MLPs fail (MSE ≈ 0.31) and O(d³) baselines are estimated to take >6 years.
- **Improved transport quality:** On Morpho-MNIST, CSB reduces transport cost by 72% and improves structural similarity (SSIM) by 3.2× compared to baselines.
- **Causal adherence:** In a confounder isolation test, CSB maintains causal independence (ΔZ=0.01), whereas a standard Optimal Transport baseline incurs a large error (ΔZ=+10.47), demonstrating robustness to off-manifold distortions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
CSB succeeds because it replaces deterministic, global mappings with stochastic local transitions guided by causal structure. The entropic optimal transport formulation yields a well-posed problem even when source and target supports mismatch, as the entropy regularization encourages mass to spread through low-density regions rather than collapse. Diffusion processes (SDEs) implement this transport as continuous-time stochastic flows that can "tunnel" through off-manifold space. The Structural Decomposition Theorem ensures these local stochastic flows combine to form a globally valid bridge without information bottlenecks: each node's evolution depends only on its parents, respecting the causal graph's Markov property. This avoids the curse of dimensionality that plagues direct high-dimensional OT and prevents the anticipatory artifacts of deterministic flows.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Causal Graph -> Factorized Local Bridges (each node conditioned on its parents) -> SDE Solvers (per local bridge) -> Composed Global Stochastic Process

**Critical Path:**  
1. Input: source distribution *μ₀*, target distribution *μ₁*, and known DAG *G*.  
2. Topologically sort nodes of *G*.  
3. For each node *X* in order, define its local transition *P(X_t | Pa_G(X))* as an entropic OT problem between marginal distributions of *X* given its parents.  
4. Solve each local transition via an SDE (e.g., via iterative proportional fitting or neural Schrödinger bridge solvers).  
5. Compose local SDEs by performing ancestral sampling: simulate parent nodes first, then conditionally sample children.

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- *Graph correctness vs. robustness:* CSB strictly enforces the provided DAG; errors in the graph directly invalidate counterfactuals.  
- *Factorization strength vs. dependencies:* The decomposition assumes local Markovianity; unmodeled long-range interactions may be lost.  
- *Entropy regularization vs. fidelity:* Higher entropy improves tunneling but may oversmooth the transport, reducing precision.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- Large counterfactual error when the input DAG is misspecified (e.g., reversed or missing edges).  
- Failure to converge or extreme transport cost when source and target have near-disjoint support and entropy is too low.  
- Superlinear runtime scaling if per-node cost grows with indegree (dense graphs).

**First Experiments:**  
1. Replicate the 100k-dimensional synthetic chain experiment: measure MSE and runtime versus structure-blind MLP and theoretical O(d³) baselines.  
2. Reproduce Morpho-MNIST results: compute transport cost and SSIM against ground-truth morphological factors.  
3. Perform graph robustness test: systematically introduce edge errors (flips, deletions) into a known DAG and measure degradation in counterfactual error (ΔZ).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can CSB be extended to jointly learn the causal graph structure and the optimal transport plan, enabling fully autonomous causal discovery from high-dimensional data?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state in the Discussion (Section 5): "Future work will leverage this speed to jointly optimize the graph structure and the transport plan (min_{G,P} L_{CSB}), turning the 'weakness' of structural dependence into a powerful capability for autonomous causal discovery from high-dimensional raw data."
- **Why unresolved:** Current CSB requires a known causal graph a priori; joint graph learning introduces combinatorial complexity and may invalidate the factorization property that ensures linear scalability.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Introduce a differentiable relaxation of graph adjacency (e.g., continuous matrix with sparsity priors) within the CSB loss; evaluate on synthetic datasets with known ground-truth graphs, measuring both graph recovery accuracy and counterfactual transport fidelity.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How can CSB be made robust to errors in the provided causal graph (e.g., missing edges, spurious edges, latent confounders) without degrading counterfactual validity?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors intentionally use a misspecified graph (Y→X instead of Y←X) in the confounder isolation test and observe a large counterfactual error (ΔZ≈4.04). They also treat an unobserved confounder X but do not detail its handling, implying an assumption of full node observability.
- **Why unresolved:** Real-world causal knowledge is often incomplete or incorrect; CSB’s strict adherence to structural constraints means small specification errors can lead to invalid counterfactuals, limiting practical applicability.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Develop methods that marginalize over graph uncertainty (e.g., Bayesian model averaging) or augment the graph with latent variables via variational inference; test on datasets with injected graph noise (flipped edges, omitted nodes) and quantify robustness via counterfactual error metrics.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Does the claimed O(d) computational scalability of CSB hold for arbitrary graph topologies, especially dense graphs with high indegree? If not, how can efficiency be maintained?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The Structural Decomposition Theorem and implementation (e.g., 1D convolutions for chains) assume local processing with few parents. Experiments only cover sparse graphs; no analysis is provided for dense graphs where a node may have up to O(d) parents, potentially increasing per-node cost to O(d²) or more.
- **Why unresolved:** Many scientific domains (e.g., gene regulatory networks) exhibit dense connectivity patterns. If per-node cost grows with parent set size, overall complexity may become superlinear, undermining the claimed ability to handle 10^5-node systems.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Provide a theoretical runtime analysis that explicitly bounds complexity by the number of edges rather than nodes; run empirical scaling tests on synthetic dense graphs (e.g., Erdős–Rényi with varying edge probability) to measure runtime versus node/edge counts.

## Limitations
- The theoretical decomposition assumes acyclic and fully known causal graphs, limiting application to cyclic or latent-variable settings.
- Empirical validation relies on synthetic high-dimensional chains and a single image dataset (Morpho-MNIST), raising generalizability concerns.
- The claimed O(d) scalability for arbitrary high-dim causal systems is unverified for dense graphs; per-node cost may grow with indegree.

## Confidence
- **Structural Decomposition Theorem:** High (mathematical statement)
- **CSB efficacy for off-manifold transport:** Medium (empirical but limited benchmarks)
- **Scalability claims to arbitrary high-dim causal systems:** Low (lack of real-world high-dim validation and dense-graph analysis)
