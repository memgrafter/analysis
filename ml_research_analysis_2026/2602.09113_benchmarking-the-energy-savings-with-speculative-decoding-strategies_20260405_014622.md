# Benchmarking the Energy Savings with Speculative Decoding Strategies
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.09113
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09113
- Reference count: 10
- Primary result: Energy outcomes of speculative decoding are highly variable; while state-of-the-art methods like EAGLE-3 can achieve up to 2.51× total energy savings, simpler strategies often consume more energy than vanilla autoregressive decoding despite lower latency.

## Executive Summary
This paper challenges the assumption that speculative decoding (SD) automatically yields energy savings by providing a comprehensive benchmark of SD strategies across multiple model families (VICUNA, LLAMA, FLAN-T5, QWEN3), datasets (HUMAN-EVAL, GSM-8K, CNN-DM), and SD methods (COGA, DYGA, EAGLE-2/3, MEDUSA). The authors measure total energy (GPU, CPU, RAM) and latency relative to vanilla autoregressive decoding. Results show that SD is not inherently energy‑efficient: EAGLE-3 achieves up to 2.51× total energy savings on VICUNA‑13B for code generation, while COGA and DYGA often increase energy consumption despite reducing latency. Key determinants include the target‑assistant model size gap (larger gaps favor savings), dataset characteristics (code generation yields best savings, summarization worst), and model architecture (LLAMA and FLAN‑T5 generally more efficient than VICUNA). Implementation overheads (6–10% of runtime) and backend quantization (NF4优于 GPTQ) also critically shape the energy‑time trade‑off, highlighting the need for careful configuration when deploying SD for sustainable inference.

## Method Summary
The study conducts inference‑only benchmarking on fixed hardware (NVIDIA A5000/A6000 GPU, Intel Xeon Silver 4210R, 128 GB RAM) with PyTorch 2.6. Target models are quantized to 4‑bit NF4 via bitsandbytes; assistant models are smaller variants. Three datasets are used: HUMAN‑EVAL (164 code generation samples), GSM‑8K (256 math reasoning samples), and CNN‑DM (256 summarization samples). Model‑specific chat templates ensure consistent prompt formatting. For each combination of target model, assistant model, SD strategy (including COGA‑x, DYGA‑20, EAGLE‑2, EAGLE‑3, MEDUSA) and dataset, the authors run vanilla decoding and SD, measuring total time and energy with CodeCarbon (1‑second sampling). Energy is split into GPU (pynvml), CPU (RAPL), and RAM. Metrics computed per 1 K tokens are speedup γt, GPU energy saving γGPU_e, and total energy saving γTotal_e relative to vanilla. All experiments use batch size 1. The paper further varies draft lengths and compares quantization backends (HuggingFace NF4 vs. vLLM GPTQ) to quantify overhead and quantization impacts.

## Key Results
- Energy savings from speculative decoding are highly variable; EAGLE‑3 reaches up to 2.51× total energy savings on VICUNA‑13B for code generation, while COGA/DYGA often consume more energy than vanilla decoding despite lower latency.
- Determinants of efficiency include the target‑assistant model size gap (larger gaps favor savings), dataset characteristics (HUMAN‑EVAL best, CNN‑DM worst), and model architecture (LLAMA and FLAN‑T5 generally more energy‑efficient than VICUNA).
- Implementation overheads (6–10% of runtime) and backend quantization (NF4优于 GPTQ) significantly impact the energy‑time trade‑off, demonstrating that speculative decoding is not inherently energy‑efficient.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Runtime ≠ Energy Proportionality**  
Lower inference latency from speculative decoding does not guarantee proportional energy savings because SD introduces orchestration overheads (CPU/memory management, token verification) that consume non‑GPU energy. When speedup is modest (γt ≈ 1–1.5×), these overheads can outweigh GPU energy reductions from fewer target model passes, leading to higher total energy (γTotal_e < 1). This occurs for COGA/DYGA on VICUNA and CNN‑DM, where the paper reports cases like COGA‑20 on VICUNA‑13B (GSM‑8K) with γt = 1.08× but γTotal_e = 0.88×.

**Mechanism 2: Target‑Assistant Model Size Gap Drives Efficiency**  
A larger size gap between target and assistant models generally increases energy savings because the assistant’s cheaper computation replaces more expensive target steps. The assistant’s token acceptance rate correlates with its capability relative to the target; a larger gap means the assistant is sufficiently capable while remaining far cheaper. The paper shows that LLAMA and FLAN‑T5 families (with larger gaps) achieve better savings than VICUNA (smaller gaps). Figure 2 illustrates that the energy optimisation slope is steeper for pairs with greater size differences.

**Mechanism 3: Dataset Characteristics Modulate Acceptance and Overhead**  
Task token predictability critically influences net energy savings. Datasets like HUMAN‑EVAL (code generation) yield high acceptance rates, enabling longer draft sequences and fewer target verifications, which amplifies savings. Unpredictable tasks like CNN‑DM (summarization) give low acceptance, causing frequent draft‑verify cycles where overhead dominates. The paper reports the highest γTotal_e on HUMAN‑EVAL and the lowest on CNN‑DM across most models (e.g., VICUNA‑13B: 2.51× vs 2.10×). Overheads contribute approximately 6–10% of total runtime, so low acceptance multiplies their impact, making SD less efficient.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:  
Assistant Model -> Draft Strategy Module -> Verification Engine -> Target Model -> Orchestrator

Critical path: Assistant generates draft tokens → Target verifies all draft tokens in a single parallel pass → Orchestrator determines acceptance → If rejection, rollback to the first rejected token and run target for one token → repeat.

Design tradeoffs:
- Speedup vs Energy: Longer drafts increase speedup but also raise assistant overhead and risk lower acceptance; energy optimum may be at shorter drafts.
- Model Choice: Larger target‑assistant gap favours energy savings but a too‑weak assistant reduces acceptance; architecture (decoder‑only vs encoder‑decoder) also plays a role.
- Quantization: NF4 reduces target memory/compute, lowering verification cost and improving SD efficiency compared to GPTQ.

Failure signatures:
- γTotal_e < 1 despite γt > 1 (energy increase with speedup).
- High variance in γTotal_e across datasets for the same model pair.
- COGA/DYGA failing on decoder‑only models (VICUNA) but working on encoder‑decoder (FLAN‑T5).

First 3 experiments:
1. Replicate counterexample: Run COGA‑20 on VICUNA‑13B (target) vs VICUNA‑68M (assistant) for GSM‑8K. Expect γt > 1 but γTotal_e < 1.
2. Dataset sensitivity test: For LLAMA‑8B vs LLAMA‑1B, run EAGLE‑3 on HUMAN‑EVAL and CNN‑DM. Expect γTotal_e ≈ 2.09× vs 1.52×. Compute acceptance rate and correlate.
3. Quantisation impact: On VICUNA‑13B with EAGLE‑3 for HUMAN‑EVAL, compare HuggingFace (NF4) baseline vs vLLM (GPTQ) backend. Expect significant drop in γTotal_e.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Hardware generalisability:** All experiments used NVIDIA A5000/A6000 GPUs; how do results translate to edge GPUs, multi‑GPU systems, or power‑optimised cloud instances?
- **Batch size effects:** Evaluation was at batch size 1; how do larger batch sizes (common in production) affect energy savings?
- **Root causes of variability:** Why do model family and dataset characteristics cause such dramatic differences? A mechanistic, predictive understanding is missing.
- **Quantisation interaction:** How do different quantisation methods (NF4, GPTQ, AWQ) and bit‑widths interact with SD? Can we design quantisation‑aware SD techniques?

## Limitations
- Generalisability: Findings are limited to specific model families and hardware; may not extend to MoE, multimodal, or newer architectures.
- Measurement dependency: Energy metrics depend on hardware (A5000/A6000), software stack (PyTorch 2.6, CodeCarbon), and quantisation (NF4); other environments could alter overhead magnitudes.
- Overhead attribution: Reported 6–10% orchestration overhead is aggregate; finer decomposition (CPU vs memory vs synchronisation) is lacking, limiting optimisation insights.

## Confidence
- Runtime ≠ Energy Proportionality: High
- Target‑Assistant Size Gap Drives Efficiency: Medium
- Dataset Characteristics Modulate Savings: High

## Next Checks
1. **Controlled size‑gap isolation:** For a single target (e.g., LLAMA‑7B), use assistant models of multiple scales (0.5B, 1B, 2B) with identical training data and architecture; measure γTotal_e across datasets.
2. **Acceptance‑rate correlation:** Log per‑token acceptance rates during SD for all model‑dataset pairs; compute Pearson correlation between average acceptance and γTotal_e.
3. **Hardware/quantisation sweep:** Replicate EAGLE‑3 on VICUNA‑13B/HUMAN‑EVAL on newer GPUs (H100, RTX 4090) and with alternative quantisation (GPTQ, AWQ); assess robustness of overhead and trade‑offs.
