# Time Series, Vision, and Language: Exploring the Limits of Alignment in Contrastive Representation Spaces
## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2602.19367  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19367  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: Alignment of time series with vision and language in contrastive spaces is asymmetric, with images acting as a semantic bridge, and depends on representational format and semantic explicitness rather than scale alone.

## Executive Summary
<PRESIDIO_ANONYMIZED_EMAIL> investigates whether time series signals can be aligned with vision and language in a shared contrastive representation space, probing the Platonic Representation Hypothesis for non‑conventional modalities. The authors employ frozen pretrained encoders for each modality and train only lightweight projection heads using a symmetric contrastive loss over aligned triplets. Experiments span four medical datasets—CaTS, TRUCE, MIMIC, and PTB‑XL—and cover 34 distinct encoder combinations of varying sizes.

The study reveals several key phenomena: alignment improves with encoder scale but displays pronounced asymmetry—time‑series–image alignment is consistently stronger than time‑series–text alignment; introducing an image modality significantly boosts time‑series–text alignment, indicating that images can serve as semantic bridges; and factors beyond scale, such as textual information density, visual richness, and language choice, critically affect alignment. These findings underscore that representational format and semantic explicitness are fundamental to multimodal convergence, challenging a simplistic “bigger models align better” narrative.

## Method Summary
The core methodology uses three frozen pretrained encoders—one each for time series, images, and text—to extract representations that are subsequently projected into a shared space via learnable linear heads. The training objective is a symmetric contrastive loss (similar to InfoNCE) applied to triplets of matching samples across modalities: given a batch of aligned triplets, the loss encourages the embeddings of each modality to be close for the same sample while being far from other samples’ embeddings. This design isolates the intrinsic compatibility of the representations by preventing any change to the encoders themselves.

Evaluation is conducted on four medical datasets that provide aligned time series, images (e.g., ECG plots or chest X‑rays), and text (clinical notes or radiology reports). The authors systematically vary the encoder architectures (e.g., ResNet for images, Transformer for text, TCN for time series) and sizes, yielding 34 combinations. Alignment strength is measured by retrieval accuracy (e.g., top‑1, top‑5) within the shared space. Additionally, controlled ablations manipulate textual information density (e.g., raw notes vs. summaries), visual richness (e.g., grayscale vs. color), and language (English vs. German) to probe the factors influencing multimodal convergence.

## Key Results
- Alignment improves with model size but is highly asymmetric: time‑series–image alignment consistently outperforms time‑series–text alignment across all tested encoder combinations.  
- The image modality acts as a semantic bridge; adding an image encoder between time series and text markedly enhances time‑series–text alignment.  
- Representational format and semantic explicitness—operationalized through textual density, visual richness, and language—are critical determinants of alignment, with diminishing returns beyond a saturation point for density and degradation for indirect descriptions or multilingual text.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The contrastive loss aligns representations by minimizing distance between positive pairs and maximizing distance to negatives. The consistent outperformance of time‑series–image alignment likely stems from the similarity in data structure: both time series and images are dense, spatially aligned signals that capture continuous measurements over a grid (temporal vs. spatial). In contrast, text is discrete, symbolic, and often abstracts away from the raw signal, making direct mapping to time‑series features more challenging. The bridging effect of images can be understood as images providing an intermediate, concrete medium that shares visual patterns with both modalities—images often depict the underlying physiological phenomena (e.g., ECG waveforms or organ states) that also generate time‑series data, while text describes these images. Thus, aligning all three modalities together allows the image representation to mediate and regularize the alignment between time‑series and text. Moreover, semantic explicitness matters because richer visual details and denser textual descriptions supply more features that can be matched across modalities, up to a point where additional tokens do not introduce new information.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**:  
TS_encoder (frozen) → TS_proj (MLP) → norm → TS_emb  
IMG_encoder (frozen) → IMG_proj (MLP) → norm → IMG_emb  
TXT_encoder (frozen) → TXT_proj (MLP) → norm → TXT_emb  
All embeddings share a common contrastive space where pairwise similarities are computed; loss = symmetric InfoNCE over all positive pairs among the three modalities.

**Critical Path**:
1. Input triplet (time series sample, corresponding image, corresponding text) is fed to respective frozen encoders.  
2. Encoder outputs are passed through trainable projection heads to obtain embeddings of fixed dimensionality.  
3. Embeddings are L2‑normalized.  
4. For each modality pair (TS‑IMG, TS‑TXT, IMG‑TXT), a similarity matrix is computed.  
5. The symmetric contrastive loss aggregates the cross‑entropy losses for each direction (e.g., predicting the correct image from a time series, and vice versa) and averages across all three pairs.  
6. Gradients flow only through the projection heads; encoders remain fixed.  
7. Optimization updates the projection heads to maximize alignment.

**Design Tradeoffs**:
- **Frozen vs. fine‑tuned encoders**: Frozen encoders ensure a clean comparison of representational compatibility but limit adaptability; fine‑tuning could improve alignment at the cost of confounding factors.  
- **Symmetric loss**: Treats all modalities equally and is straightforward to implement, but may obscure modality‑specific performance differences if one modality is inherently harder to align.  
- **Projection head capacity**: Simple linear heads are computationally cheap and reduce overfitting, but may be insufficient to bridge large representational gaps; deeper MLPs could help but increase risk of collapse.  
- **Batch size**: Contrastive losses require large batches for sufficient negatives; small batches degrade performance but reduce memory constraints.  
- **Encoder selection**: Using a diverse set of pretrained encoders provides breadth but introduces variability that may be hard to disentangle.

**Failure Signatures**:
- **Representation collapse**: All embeddings become indistinguishable, leading to random retrieval performance.  
- **Modality dominance**: One modality’s encoder produces embeddings that cluster tightly, causing the contrastive loss to ignore others.  
- **Poor convergence**: Loss plateaus at a high value, indicating that the projection heads cannot bridge the representational gap.  
- **Overfitting to dataset bias**: The model learns shortcuts (e.g., patient ID) rather than semantic alignment, leading to high training but low validation retrieval.  
- **Temperature mis‑tuning**: An inappropriate temperature value can make the loss too stringent or too lenient, harming alignment.

**First Experiments**:
1. **Baseline**: Train projection heads with random initialization while keeping encoders frozen. Measure top‑1 and top‑5 retrieval accuracies for all three pairwise alignments to establish a non‑trivial alignment floor.  
2. **Projection dimension sweep**: Vary the hidden dimension of the projection MLP (e.g., 128, 256, 512, 1024) and evaluate impact on alignment metrics to find the optimal capacity/complexity trade‑off.  
3. **Loss function variation**: Replace the symmetric contrastive loss with a CLIP‑style pairwise loss (only one direction per pair) and compare alignment strengths to understand the benefit of symmetry.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: Do the observed alignment behaviors generalize to multivariate time series signals?  
- **Basis in paper**: "[explicit] Our analysis focuses on univariate time series, and it remains an open question whether the alignment behaviors observed here extend to multivariate signals."  
- **Why unresolved**: The study only considered univariate time series; multivariate signals introduce cross‑channel dependencies and richer temporal structures that may affect alignment differently across modalities.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Systematic experiments on multivariate time series datasets using the same trimodal contrastive framework, measuring alignment metrics while varying the number of channels and dependency patterns.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: How do the identified alignment phenomena correlate with downstream task performance in multimodal time series applications?  
- **Basis in paper**: "[explicit] An important direction for future work is to investigate how the alignment phenomena that we identify correlate with downstream task performance in multimodal time series applications."  
- **Why unresolved**: The paper focuses on representation alignment metrics, not on how these metrics translate to practical gains; large‑scale datasets with aligned vision/language annotations are scarce.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Creation or use of comprehensive multimodal time series benchmarks with paired plots and text, followed by evaluation of alignment scores against performance on tasks like zero‑shot retrieval, forecasting, or diagnostic classification.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: What impact would end‑to‑end fine‑tuning or modality‑specific adaptation have on trimodal alignment compared to frozen encoders?  
- **Basis in paper**: "[explicit] This design isolates intrinsic representational compatibility across modalities, but precludes studying how end‑to‑end fine‑tuning or modality‑specific adaptation might further shape alignment. Exploring such training regimes would be a natural extension..."  
- **Why unresolved**: The use of frozen encoders was necessary for controlled comparison, but fine‑tuning could alter representations in ways that improve or degrade alignment, and this remains unexplored.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Comparative experiments where projection heads and encoders are jointly fine‑tuned on the contrastive objective, measuring changes in alignment metrics and computational efficiency relative to the frozen‑encoder baseline.

## Limitations
- **Domain specificity**: All datasets (CaTS, TRUCE, MIMIC, PTB‑XL) are medical/time‑series; findings may not generalize to other domains (e.g., finance, climate).  
- **Frozen encoders**: Using fixed pretrained encoders avoids confounding from fine‑tuning but may mask alignment dynamics under adaptivity.  
- **Contrastive loss only**: Other alignment objectives (e.g., generative, diffusion) remain unexplored.
