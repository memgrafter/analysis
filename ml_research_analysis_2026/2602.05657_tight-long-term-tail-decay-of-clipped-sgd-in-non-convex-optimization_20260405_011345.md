# Tight Long-Term Tail Decay of (Clipped) SGD in Non-Convex Optimization

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.05657  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05657  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** For vanilla SGD with bounded noise, the tail probability decays as *e*<sup>‑*t*/log *t*</sup>. For clipped SGD under heavy‑tailed noise with a bounded *p*-th moment (*p*∈(1,2]), the decay is *e*<sup>‑*t*<sup>*β*<sub>*p*</sub></sup>/log *t*</sup> with *β*<sub>*p*</sub> = 4(*p*‑1)/(3*p*‑2) for *p*<2 and *e*<sup>‑*t*/log<sup>2</sup> *t*</sup> for *p*=2. Matching lower bounds show these rates are tight up to logarithmic factors.

## Executive Summary
This paper studies the asymptotic tail decay of gradient norms for stochastic gradient descent (SGD) and clipped SGD (c‑SGD) in non‑convex optimization. By applying large deviations theory, the authors derive sharp upper bounds on the probability that the minimal gradient norm over time remains above a threshold. For vanilla SGD with almost surely bounded noise, they obtain an LDP upper bound of the form exp(‑*t*/log *t*). For c‑SGD under heavy‑tailed noise with a bounded *p*-th moment (*p*∈(1,2]), the bound becomes exp(‑*t*<sup>*β*<sub>*p*</sub></sup>/log *t*) with *β*<sub>*p*</sub> = 4(*p*‑1)/(3*p*‑2) for *p*<2 and exp(‑*t*/log<sup>2</sup> *t*) for *p*=2. Matching lower bounds prove optimality up to poly‑logarithmic factors, yielding an order‑of‑magnitude improvement over prior finite‑time bounds.

The analysis leverages the Gärtner‑Ellis theorem and a martingale‑based construction to control the logarithmic moment generating function of the scaled gradient process. Clipping plays a pivotal role: it transforms heavy‑tailed noise into a sub‑Gaussian component with a vanishing bias, enabling exponential tail decay even when raw noise lacks exponential moments. The derived step‑size and clipping schedules are tuned to balance this bias with the algorithm’s exploration, achieving the reported exponents.

## Method Summary
The theoretical investigation assumes a non‑convex cost *f* that is *L*-smooth and has uniformly bounded gradients (∥∇*f*(*x*)∥ ≤ *G*). SGD updates: *x*<sub>*t*+1</sub> = *x*<sub>*t*</sub> − α<sub>*t*</sub>(∇*f*(*x*<sub>*t*</sub>)+*z*<sub>*t*</sub>) with noise satisfying a bounded *p*-th moment. For clipped SGD, the update uses a clipping operator: *g*<sub>*t*</sub> = clip(∇*f*(*x*<sub>*t*</sub>)+*z*<sub>*t*</sub>, γ<sub>*t*</sub>) and *x*<sub>*t*+1</sub> = *x*<sub>*t*</sub> − α<sub>*t*</sub>*g*<sub>*t*</sub>. The schedules α<sub>*t*</sub> ∼ *t*<sup>‑½</sup> for SGD and α<sub>*t*</sub>∼*t*<sup>‑*p*/(3*p*‑2)</sup>, γ<sub>*t*</sub>∼*t*<sup>(2‑*p*)/(3*p*‑2)</sup> (or log *t* for *p*=2) for c‑SGD are crucial for the large‑deviation analysis.

The core technical step is to derive the asymptotic form of the scaled cumulant generating function of a process related to the gradient norm. The authors then apply the Gärtner‑Ellis theorem to establish a large deviations principle, yielding exponential tail bounds. To validate these asymptotic predictions, the reproduction plan proposes Monte‑Carlo simulations on a one‑dimensional Huber loss (which satisfies the assumptions). By estimating the tail probability of *F*<sub>*t*</sub> = min<sub>*k*≤*t*</sub>∥∇*f*(*x*<sub>*k*</sub>)∥<sup>2</sup> across many independent trajectories, one can check linearity in the appropriate transformed plots (e.g., log *P* vs *t*/log *t*). Lower‑bound constructions based on deterministic sign noise are also suggested to test optimality.

## Key Results
- **SGD (bounded noise):** For any ε>0,  
  *P*( min<sub>*k*≤*t*</sub> ∥∇*f*(*x*<sub>*k*</sub>)∥<sup>2</sup> > ε ) ≤ exp( - *c* ε<sup>2</sup> *t* / (*M*<sup>2</sup>*G*<sup>2</sup> log *t* ) ) for large *t*, where *M* bounds the noise.
- **Clipped SGD (heavy‑tailed noise, *p*∈(1,2)):**  
  *P*( min<sub>*k*≤*t*</sub> ∥∇*f*(*x*<sub>*k*</sub>)∥<sup>2</sup> > ε ) ≤ exp( - *c* *t*<sup>*β*<sub>*p*</sub></sup> / log *t* ) with *β*<sub>*p*</sub> = 4(*p*‑1)/(3*p*‑2).  
  For *p*=2 (bounded variance), the bound is exp( - *c* *t* / log<sup>2</sup> *t* ).
- **Lower bound:** There exists an instance (deterministic ±1 noise, specific step‑size, no clipping) and ε>0 such that  
  *P*( min<sub>*k*≤*t*</sub> ∥∇*f*(*x*<sub>*k*</sub>)∥<sup>2</sup> > ε ) ≥ *a*<sub>1</sub> exp(-*a*<sub>2</sub>*t*).  
  This shows the upper bounds are tight up to logarithmic factors.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The proof combines a martingale‑based change of measure with large‑deviation techniques. A positive supermartingale is constructed that dominates the indicator of the “bad” event (small gradient never achieved). Its expectation at time *t* is bounded by exp(-*t*·*I*), where *I* is the Legendre transform of the noise’s scaled cumulant generating function under a tilted distribution. For vanilla SGD with bounded noise, the scaled generating function is well‑behaved, yielding a linear exponent *I* > 0; the time‑scaling α<sub>*t*</sub>∼*t*<sup>‑½</sup> together with refinements from the Gärtner‑Ellis theorem introduces a logarithmic correction, giving exp(‑*t*/log *t*).  

For clipped SGD with heavy‑tailed noise, clipping replaces the raw noise by a biased but sub‑Gaussian component. The bias vanishes as *t*<sup>‑(*p*‑1)/(3*p*‑2)</sup>, and the step‑size is slowed accordingly (α<sub>*t*</sub>∼*t*<sup>‑*p*/(3*p*‑2)</sup>) to maintain stability. The resulting scaled generating function interpolates between regimes and produces an exponent *β*<sub>*p*</sub> = 4(*p*‑1)/(3*p*‑2) for *p*<2, while the logarithmic factor arises similarly. The lower bound uses a simple random‑walk construction where clipping never activates, exposing the fundamental limit.

## Foundational Learning
Key concepts underpinning the analysis:

- **Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD)**  
  *Why needed*: The core algorithm whose long‑term behavior is investigated.  
  *Quick check*: Write the update rule *x*<sub>*t*+1</sub> = *x*<sub>*t*</sub> − α<sub>*t*</sub>(∇*f*(*x*<sub>*t*</sub>) + *z*<sub>*t*</sub>).

- **Large Deviations Principle (LDP)**  
  *Why needed*: Provides the mathematical machinery (e.g., Gärtner‑Ellis theorem) to convert cumulant generating function asymptotics into exponential tail bounds.  
  *Quick check*: State informally that an LDP gives *P*(*X*<sub>*n*</sub> ≈ *x*) ≈ exp(‑*n*·*I*(*x*)).

- **Clipping**  
  *Why needed*: Modifies heavy‑tailed stochastic gradients to have sub‑Gaussian tails, making exponential decay possible even with only finite *p*-th moments.  
  *Quick check*: Define clip(*v*, γ) = *v* · min(1, γ/∥*v*∥).

- **Heavy‑tailed distributions**  
  *Why needed*: Realistic model for gradient noise in machine learning (e.g., subsampled data); the paper’s c‑SGD results specifically address this regime.  
  *Quick check*: Give an example (Pareto) and note that E[|*z*|<sup>*p*</sup>] < ∞ requires shape > *p*.

- **Tail decay rate**  
  *Why needed*: Quantifies the rarity of failure events (large gradient norms); faster decay improves reliability of long‑run optimization.  
  *Quick check*: Write the form of the tail bound for SGD: *P*(*F*<sub>*t*</sub> > ε) ≤ exp(‑*c* *t*/log *t*).

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**  
- *SGD*: *x*<sub>*t*</sub> → compute ∇*f*(*x*<sub>*t*</sub>) → add noise *z*<sub>*t*</sub> → *g*<sub>*t*</sub> = ∇*f* + *z* → *x*<sub>*t*+1</sub> = *x*<sub>*t*</sub> − α<sub>*t*</sub>*g*<sub>*t*</sub>.  
- *c‑SGD*: *x*<sub>*t*</sub> → compute ∇*f*(*x*<sub>*t*</sub>) → add noise *z*<sub>*t*</sub> → *v*<sub>*t*</sub> = ∇*f* + *z* → clip(*v*<sub>*t*</sub>, γ<sub>*t*</sub>) = *g*<sub>*t*</sub> → *x*<sub>*t*+1</sub> = *x*<sub>*t*</sub> − α<sub>*t*</sub>*g*<sub>*t*</sub>.

**Critical Path**  
1. Evaluate the full (or mini‑batch) gradient ∇*f*(*x*<sub>*t*</sub>).  
2. Draw stochastic noise *z*<sub>*t*</sub>.  
3. (c‑SGD only) Apply clipping with threshold γ<sub>*t*</sub>.  
4. Scale by step‑size α<sub>*t*</sub>.  
5. Update the parameter.  
Predetermined schedules for α<sub>*t*</sub> and γ<sub>*t*</sub> are critical; they follow power‑law decays derived from the noise tail index *p*.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- **Step‑size α<sub>*t*</sub>**: Must decrease sufficiently to ensure stability but not too fast, as the tail decay exponent depends on the exact power. For SGD, α ∼ *t*<sup>‑½</sup> is optimal; for c‑SGD with heavy tails (*p*<2), a slower decay α ∼ *t*<sup>‑*p*/(3*p*‑2)</sup> is required.  
- **Clipping threshold γ<sub>*t*</sub>**: Needs to grow to allow occasional large updates but not so fast that heavy tails dominate. The derived growth rate (∼*t*<sup>(2‑*p*)/(3*p*‑2)</sup> or log *t*) is essential for achieving the *β*<sub>*p*</sub> exponent.  
- **Noise assumptions**: The analysis requires a bounded *p*-th moment; if the actual noise is heavier (e.g., infinite variance for *p*<2), the theoretical rates may fail. In practice, estimating *p* or the gradient bound *G* is non‑trivial and may require tuning.

**Failure Signatures**  
- **Slower‑than‑predicted tail decay** (e.g., polynomial instead of exponential) suggests the noise is heavier than assumed or α<sub>*t*</sub> is too large.  
- **Diverging iterates or exploding gradient norms** indicate instability; check whether γ<sub>*t*</sub> is too small or α<sub>*t*</sub> too large.  
- **Excessive clipping** (most updates clipped) introduces a large bias and can cause plateauing; consider increasing γ<sub>*t*</sub>.  
- **Lower‑bound test failure** (probabilities much below 2<sup>‑*t*+1</sup>) may be due to noise asymmetry or imprecise initial condition.

2. **c‑SGD with Gaussian noise** (*p*=2) – Use *z*∼N(0,1), α<sub>*t*</sub> = 1/√(*t*+1), γ<sub>*t*</sub> = log(*t*+1). Same simulation scale; plot log *P* vs *t*/log<sup>2</sup> *t*; linearity supports Theorem 2 for *p*=2.  
3. **c‑SGD with heavy‑tailed noise** (*p*=1.5) – Use symmetrized Pareto with shape α=1.6 (finite 1.5‑moment, infinite variance), α<sub>*t*</sub> = (*t*+1)<sup>‑0.6</sup>, γ<sub>*t*</sub> = 2·(*t*+1)<sup>0.1</sup>. ## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1**  
- **Question:** Can the logarithmic factor in the decay rates be removed? Specifically, for SGD with bounded noise, can we achieve a decay rate of the form *e*<sup>‑Θ(*t*)</sup>? For c‑SGD with heavy‑tailed noise, can we achieve *e*<sup>‑Θ(*t*<sup>*β*<sub>*p*</sub></sup>)</sup>?  
- **Basis in paper:** Inferred from the gap between the upper bounds (Theorem 1: *e*<sup>‑*t*/log *t*</sup>; Theorem 2: *e*<sup>‑*t*<sup>*β*<sub>*p*</sub></sup>/log *t*</sup> for *p*<2, *e*<sup>‑*t*/log<sup>2</sup> *t*</sup> for *p*=2) and the lower bound (Theorem 3: *e*<sup>‑*a*<sub>2</sub>*t*</sup>). The authors state that rates are tight up to poly‑logarithmic factors.  
- **Why unresolved:** The proofs control moment‑generating functions via Darboux sum approximations, which introduce the logarithmic terms. Removing them would require a sharper large‑deviation analysis or a fundamentally different technique.  
- **What evidence would resolve:** An improved upper bound that eliminates the log factor (e.g., *e*<sup>‑*c t*</sup> for SGD) or a matching lower bound showing the log is necessary (e.g., proving that the exponent cannot exceed *t*/log *t*). A full LDP derivation that yields the exact exponent without logs would also settle the question.

**Open Question 2**  
- **Question:** Are the derived rates optimal for c‑SGD under heavy‑tailed noise? The lower bound in Theorem 3 uses a construction where clipping never happens. Can one build an instance with truly heavy‑tailed noise (clipping active) that forces the tail decay to be at most *e*<sup>‑*c t*<sup>*β*<sub>*p*</sub></sup></sup> (without the log factor), thereby proving the *β*<sub>*p*</sub> exponent optimal?  
- **Basis in paper:** Inferred from Theorem 3: the lower‑bound instance uses a.s. bounded noise and a large clipping threshold, so c‑SGD reduces to SGD. This yields a lower bound of *e*<sup>‑Θ(*t*)</sup>, but does not reflect the impact of clipping on the tail when noise is heavy‑tailed. The upper bound for c‑SGD (Theorem 2) has the form *e*<sup>‑*t*<sup>*β*<sub>*p*</sub></sup>/log *t*</sup>.  
- **Why unresolved:** The lower‑bound proof exploits a specific scenario where the clipping operator is never invoked. For heavy‑tailed noise, the clipped stochastic gradient’s bias and sub‑Gaussian behaviour are essential; constructing a matching lower bound that incorporates these effects is non‑trivial.  
- **What evidence would resolve:** A construction with heavy‑tailed noise and a clipping threshold of the order used in Theorem 2, showing that for some ϵ>0, *P*(*F*<sub>*t*</sub> > ϵ) ≥ *a*<sub>1</sub>*e*<sup>‑*a*<sub>2</sub>*t*<sup>*β*<sub>*p*</sub></sup></sup> for all large *t*. Alternatively, a proof that no heavier tail probability can occur, establishing the upper bound as tight.

**Open Question 3**  
- **Question:** Can the assumption of uniformly bounded gradients (Assumption 2) be relaxed? The paper assumes ∥∇*f*(*x*)∥ ≤ *G* for all *x*. Prior finite‑time works (e.g., [14]) do not require this. Is it possible to extend the long‑term tail decay rates to costs with unbounded gradients, perhaps under alternative conditions like the Polyak‑Łojasiewicz condition or local smoothness?  
- **Basis in paper:** Explicit in Section 4: “our assumptions fully match the ones used in [13], while still providing order of magnitude faster long‑term tail decay.” and “[14] do not require bounded gradients, however, the resulting long‑term tail bounds are very loose.” The authors note that bounded gradients are needed for their analysis.  
- **Why unresolved:** The bounded gradient is used to control inner products ⟨∇*f*(*x*<sub>*t*</sub>), θ<sub>*u,t*</sub>⟩ and to apply Proposition 2 for the clipping bias. If gradients can be arbitrarily large, the derived bounds may fail; new techniques would be needed to handle possible explosions while retaining exponential tail decay.  
- **What evidence would resolve:** An extension of Theorems 1 and 2 to a class of non‑convex costs with unbounded gradients together with verifiable conditions that still yield the same decay rates. Alternatively, a counterexample showing that unbounded gradients inherently lead to a different (e.g., polynomial) tail behaviour.

**Open Question 4**  
- **Question:** Can the LDP upper bound be strengthened to a full large deviations principle? The current results give only a lim sup upper bound on the log‑tails. Does the rate function derived in Theorems 1 and 2 also control the lim inf for all Borel sets, thus forming a complete LDP?  
- **Basis in paper:** Inferred from Section 2.2: the authors aim for an LDP upper bound via the Gärtner‑Ellis theorem but do not claim a full LDP. Theorem 3 provides a simple exponential lower bound for the set (ϵ, ∞) and a specific construction, not a full lower bound.  
- **Why unresolved:** Proving lower bounds for all open sets requires constructing tilted measures or using change‑of‑measure techniques, which are difficult for nonlinear, non‑convex SGD due to its Markovian and state‑dependent noise. The rate function might also depend on finer properties of the cost landscape.  
- **What evidence would resolve:** A proof that for any open set *G*, lim inf<sub>*t*→∞</sub> (1/*n*<sub>*t*</sub>) log *P*(*F*<sub>*t*</sub> ∈ *G*) ≥ ‑ inf<sub>*x*∈*G*</sub> *I*(*x*). Together with the existing upper bound, this would yield a full LDP.

**Open Question 5**  
- **Question:** Do other nonlinear SGD variants (e.g., normalized SGD, sign SGD) achieve similarly fast long‑term tail decay under heavy‑tailed noise? The paper focuses on clipping. Can the techniques be extended to show that normalization or sign‑based methods attain rates comparable to *e*<sup>‑*t*<sup>*β*<sub>*p*</sub></sup>/log *t*</sup> or better?  
- **Basis in paper:** Explicit in the literature review: [16] study a general nonlinear SGD framework under symmetric noise and obtain a decay rate √*t*/log *t*. The authors improve this for clipping under heavy‑tailed moment conditions. Other nonlinearities like normalization and sign are not covered under the heavy‑tailed setting, though [31, 32] show they match clipped SGD’s finite‑time complexity with increasing batch size.  
- **Why unresolved:** The analysis for c‑SGD hinges on the clipping operator’s bias structure and the resulting sub‑Gaussian property of the unbiased component. Normalization and sign operations have different bias and variance characteristics; their moment‑generating functions may not be uniformly bounded, making the large‑deviation approach more challenging.  
- **What evidence would resolve:** Analogous theorems for normalized SGD or sign SGD establishing LDP upper bounds with decay rates at least as fast as the c‑SGD rates under the same heavy‑tailed noise assumption. Alternatively, a theoretical separation showing that some nonlinearities provably cannot achieve the *β*<sub>*p*</sub> exponent.

## Limitations
- **Asymptotic validation difficulty:** The results hold only in the limit *t* → ∞; to observe the predicted decay one needs extremely large *t* because tail probabilities become astronomically small (exp(‑*t*/log *t*)), making empirical confirmation challenging even with millions of runs.
- **Unspecified constants:** The lower bound constants *a*<sub>1</sub>, *a*<sub>2</sub> are not given explicitly, hindering quantitative comparisons and leaving room for potential improvement in the matching lower bound.
- **Clipping threshold sensitivity:** The derived schedules for γ<sub>*t*</sub> assume exact knowledge of the gradient bound *G*. In practice *G* is often unknown, and mis‑specification may degrade the tail decay rates; the paper does not analyze this robustness.

## Confidence
- **SGD tail rate** (*e*<sup>‑*t*/log *t*</sup>): **High** – Derived via clean LDP under bounded noise.  
- **c‑SGD tail rate** (*e*<sup>‑*t*<sup>*β*<sub>*p*</sub></sup>/log *t*</sup>): **Medium** (precise exponent *β*<sub>*p*</sub> depends on delicate bias‑variance balance; existence of some exponent in that order is **High**).  
- **Matching lower bounds**: **High** – Explicit two‑point construction.  
- **Order‑of‑magnitude improvement over prior finite‑time bounds**: **Medium** – Comparison is asymptotic; finite‑time bounds are not directly comparable.
