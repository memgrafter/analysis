# Latent Generative Models with Tunable Complexity for Compressed Sensing and other Inverse Problems

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.07357
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07357
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Tunable-complexity generative priors via nested dropout yield lower reconstruction errors (higher PSNR, lower LPIPS) than fixed-complexity baselines across a range of inverse problems, with an intermediate latent dimensionality consistently optimal.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces tunable-complexity generative priors using nested dropout, enabling a single model to operate at multiple latent dimensionalities. Unlike traditional generative priors with fixed latent size, which force a trade-off between representation error and noise overfitting, the proposed approach allows practitioners to tailor the prior complexity to the specific inverse problem at hand. The method applies to various generative families (VAEs, diffusion models, normalizing flows) via a shared training scheme with parameter sharing. Empirical evaluations on compressed sensing, denoising, inpainting, phase retrieval, deblurring, and super-resolution demonstrate a characteristic inverted-U relationship between reconstruction error and latent dimension, with an intermediate optimal k. Theoretically, for linear generative models in denoising, closed-form MSE expressions reveal that the optimal k depends on the noise variance and the singular values of the generator. The tunable prior also enhances contemporary inversion algorithms and maintains high generative quality (FID) across dimensions, establishing tunability as a valuable and orthogonal axis for improvement.

## Method Summary
The core technique is nested dropout, which during training randomly truncates the latent vector to a prefix of length k (sampled from a geometric distribution) and reconstructs from that truncated latent. This encourages the earliest latent coordinates to capture the most salient and noise-robust features of the data distribution. A VAE is first trained with a loss that includes a perceptual penalty on the truncated reconstruction. Then, a latent diffusion model (LDM) is trained on the VAE’s latent space using a mixed objective that alternately denoises full and truncated latents. At inference time for a given inverse problem, the practitioner selects an appropriate k (e.g., via validation) and solves the inverse problem using an iterative data-consistency loop (e.g., Algorithm 2) that at each step predicts a clean latent, performs a few gradient steps to enforce measurement consistency, and then truncates the latent to the chosen k. This yields reconstructions that balance fidelity to the measurements with the inductive bias of the prior at the selected complexity.

## Key Results
- Reconstruction error as a function of latent dimensionality k follows an inverted-U curve for all tested inverse problems; an intermediate k (e.g., 150–300 for 4096-dimensional latents) consistently minimizes PSNR and LPIPS.
- Using the optimal k, tunable priors achieve statistically significant improvements in PSNR and LPIPS over fixed-complexity baselines that always use the full latent dimension.
- The tunable approach integrates seamlessly with existing inversion algorithms (e.g., PSLD) and preserves generative quality (FID) across the full range of k, even when operating at suboptimal dimensions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Nested dropout creates a hierarchical latent structure where lower dimensions capture essential signal features, enabling adaptive complexity.
- **Mechanism**: During training, the model is regularized to perform reconstruction using only a prefix of latent coordinates (`z↓k`). This forces the first few dimensions to encode the most critical, noise-robust information about the signal class, with subsequent dimensions adding increasingly finer details.
- **Core assumption**: The natural signal manifold can be meaningfully captured by a nested sequence of subspaces of increasing dimensionality.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "leveraging nested dropout... enable practitioners to tune its complexity to the inverse problem at hand."
  - [section 3.1]: "reconstruction is performed from `z↓k`, which encourages earlier coordinates to carry more information about the signal class."
  - [corpus]: Corpus evidence is weak; the concept of hierarchical representations in dropout is referenced but not deeply analyzed in provided neighbors.
- **Break condition**: If the signal class requires highly entangled, non-hierarchical features (e.g., textures that depend on all coordinates equally), truncation may discard critical information, degrading performance.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Inverse problems exhibit an inverted-U relationship between reconstruction error and latent dimensionality `k`, with an intermediate `k` optimal due to a bias-variance trade-off.
- **Mechanism**: Low `k` leads to high representation error (bias) due to insufficient capacity. High `k` leads to overfitting to measurement noise (variance). An intermediate `k` balances fidelity to the prior with adaptability to the specific measurements.
- **Core assumption**: The forward operator `A` and noise level `σ` define an effective signal complexity that may differ from the full training distribution complexity.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "with too small [complexity]... high representation error, or overfitting to noise if too large."
  - [section 1/Figure 2]: "reconstruction error follows an upside-down U-shaped curve... intermediate latent dimensions (150≤k≤300) yield the lowest reconstruction error."
  - [corpus]: Neighbors like "Chasing Better Deep Image Priors" discuss over/under-parameterization trade-offs, supporting this mechanism.
- **Break condition**: When the measurement count `m` is very high relative to true signal complexity, the optimal `k` may approach full dimensionality, reducing the benefit of tuning. Conversely, with extremely noisy or few measurements, even the best `k` may yield poor results.

### Mechanism 3
- **Assumption**: In the linear denoising setting, the optimal `k` can be derived analytically from noise variance `σ²` and the singular values `s_i` of the generator `G`.
- **Mechanism**: The closed-form MSE (Theorem 5.1) shows error as a sum over singular values. The first `k` terms decrease with `k` but with diminishing returns due to the `(s_i²+γ)²` denominator, while the tail sum `∑_{j>k} s_j²` increases. The optimal `k` truncates where `s_k` drops below `√(σ² - 2γ)` (Corollary 5.2).
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 5]: Provides Theorem 5.1 and Corollary 5.2 with explicit formulas.
  - [corpus]: "How many measurements are enough?" discusses sample complexity with priors, conceptually related.
- **Break condition**: The theoretical guarantee holds only for linear invertible generators and MAP/MLE estimators. For nonlinear models (like real VAEs/LDMs), the singular value spectrum analogy is indirect and the optimal `k` may not follow the same rule.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:
  1. **Pretrained Autoencoder (E, D)**: Encodes images to latent `z` and decodes back. Must be trained with nested dropout (Eq. 6).
  2. **Diffusion U-Net (`ϵ_θ`)**: Trained in latent space with mixed objective (Eq. 7) that blends full-`k` and truncated-`k` denoising.
  3. **Inversion Engine**: Implements a data-consistency loop (e.g., Algorithm 1/2) that applies the truncation operator `(·)↓k` at each step.
  4. **Tuning Parameter `k`**: Inference-time hyperparameter chosen per problem (e.g., via validation LPIPS).
- **Critical path**:
  1. Train VAE with nested dropout loss `L_drop` over `k∼pk`.
  2. Freeze VAE; train diffusion `ϵ_θ` with loss `LLDM` that alternates between full and truncated latents.
  3. For a new inverse problem, select `k` on a held-out set.
  4. Run inversion (e.g., Algorithm 2) with fixed `k`.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - *λ in Eq. 7*: Higher `λ` emphasizes truncated-`k` performance, making the model more robust at low `k` but potentially harming full-`k` generation quality (see Figure 8).
  - *Dropout distribution `pk`*: Geometric truncation is used; success parameter `p` controls how quickly mass concentrates on small `k`.
  - *Inversion algorithm*: Simpler (Algorithm 2) is broadly applicable but may be outperformed by problem-specific tunable versions of PSLD/DPS.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - *Degenerated generation at low `k`*: If `k` is too small, images are blurry or lack content (FID spikes below a threshold in Figure 3).
  - *Noise amplification at high `k`*: If `k` is too large for the measurement budget, reconstructions fit noise (LPIPS plateaus or worsens at high `k` in Figure 4).
  - *Inversion instability*: Using fully deterministic DDIM (`σ_t=0`) with large latent space can cause divergence; small stochasticity stabilizes.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Hierarchical validation**: Train a tunable LDM, sample from varying `k`, compute FID on held-out data. Confirm FID degrades gracefully as `k` decreases (Figure 3).
  2. **Inverse problem sweep**: On a validation set, solve a representative inverse problem (e.g., 10% compressed sensing) for a range of `k`. Plot LPIPS vs. `k` to verify the inverted-U curve and select optimal `k`.
  3. **Baseline comparison**: With optimal `k` selected, run the full test set and compare PSNR/LPIPS against a fixed-complexity baseline operating at full `k` (Table 1). Also check that generation quality at full `k` remains competitive (FID close to vanilla LDM).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** Can the nested dropout training approach for tunable complexity be effectively adapted to pixel-space generative models (e.g., U-Net based diffusion models), and does such adaptation yield similar reconstruction benefits across inverse problems?
**Basis in paper:** The conclusion states: "We have focused primarily on latent generative models, but an important direction for future work is to extend tunability to broader model families, including those that operate directly in pixel space."
**Why unresolved:** Pixel-space models lack an explicit low-dimensional latent hierarchy; it is unclear how to impose an ordered structure akin to nested dropout without a latent bottleneck. New architectural or training modifications may be needed, and the impact on generation quality and inversion stability is unknown.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Develop a tunable pixel-space diffusion model (e.g., via spatial or channel-wise dropout with a prescribed ordering), train it on standard datasets, and evaluate reconstruction metrics (PSNR, LPIPS) on inverse problems (compressed sensing, inpainting, etc.) against both fixed-complexity pixel-space baselines and latent-space tunable priors.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** How can the optimal latent dimensionality k for a tunable generative prior be automatically determined from the measurement y and forward operator A, without relying on a held-out validation set?
**Basis in paper:** The conclusion notes: "A natural next step is to design inversion methods that are specifically tailored to tunable models, including strategies for selecting the tuning parameter automatically at inference time based on measurement conditions or noise statistics."
**Why unresolved:** Current practice requires picking k via validation on a small set, which is inefficient and may not generalize to varying measurement conditions (e.g., noise level, undersampling ratio). An automatic method would need to infer the appropriate complexity directly from y and A, balancing representation error and noise overfitting without additional data.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Propose a mechanism (e.g., a small neural network that ingests A and y, or an analytical rule derived from problem statistics) to predict k at test time. Validate by comparing reconstruction quality against validation-selected k across diverse inverse problems and noise levels, showing comparable or better performance without access to validation data.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** Can the theoretical characterization of optimal k for linear generative models in denoising (Theorem 5.1, Corollary 5.2) be generalized to nonlinear generators (e.g., deep neural networks) and to other inverse problems (e.g., compressed sensing, phase retrieval), yielding closed-form or asymptotic insights?
**Basis in paper:** The conclusion states: "Finally, on the theoretical side, our current analysis is restricted to linear denoising. Extending this theory to nonlinear generative models or other inverse problems, would be highly valuable."
**Why unresolved:** The linear analysis exploits closed-form solutions and singular value decomposition; nonlinear models break this structure. Extending theory may require tools from random matrix theory, manifold inference, or high-dimensional statistics, and may only yield probabilistic or asymptotic results.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Develop a theoretical framework (e.g., based on local linear approximations, neural tangent kernel, or population risk analysis) that predicts the optimal k for a class of nonlinear generators under a specific inverse problem. Empirically verify by showing the predicted k aligns with observed optima across synthetic and real models.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** Do softer or continuous forms of tunability (e.g., stochastic masks with tunable retain probability, learned attention weights over latent dimensions) provide improved reconstruction stability and quality compared to hard truncation?
**Basis in paper:** The conclusion mentions: "It is also worth exploring more flexible forms of tunability: while our approach has relied on 'hard' restrictions on latent dimensions, softer or continuous forms of complexity control may provide additional benefits."
**Why unresolved:** Hard truncation is simple but discontinuous; it forces a sharp boundary between retained and zeroed dimensions. Softer approaches might allow the model to gradually reduce complexity, potentially yielding smoother performance curves and better optimization during inversion.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Implement alternative tunable mechanisms (e.g., applying a binary mask with retention probability p that is learnable or scheduled, or weighting latent coordinates with a continuous coefficient vector) within the same training framework. Compare reconstruction metrics (PSNR, LPIPS) as a function of effective dimensionality and assess sensitivity to the tuning parameter across multiple inverse problems.

## Limitations
- **Hyperparameter gaps:** Exact values for key training losses (λ_reg, λ_adv, L_drop weight), LDM mixing λ, dropout distribution p, and optimizer schedules are unspecified, hindering exact replication. Assumed defaults may diverge from the authors' optimal settings.
- **Theory-experiment gap:** The closed-form optimal k analysis is restricted to linear, invertible generators and MAP estimation; its applicability to nonlinear VAEs/diffusion models remains an unverified analogy.
- **Scalability & cost:** Training a tunable LDM requires significant compute (large UNet, long schedule) plus an additional VAE stage; the benefit must outweigh this overhead for practical adoption.

## Confidence
- **High Confidence:** Empirical claim that reconstruction error vs. k exhibits an inverted-U curve with an intermediate optimum across diverse inverse problems (PSNR/LPIPS improvements over fixed-k baselines).
- **Medium Confidence:** Theoretical analysis for linear denoising is sound within its narrow assumptions; the analogy to nonlinear models is plausible but unverified.
- **Low Confidence:** Broad claim that this approach "improves contemporary inversion algorithms (e.g., PSLD)"—the paper likely shows a tunable version of PSLD, but the orthogonality and generality of the benefit need more rigorous comparison.

## Next Checks
1. **Validation-Tuning Robustness:** Replicate the k selection protocol on a held-out validation set. Does the chosen k generalize to a different held-out test set? Compare against an "oracle" k chosen per-sample (impractical but upper-bounding) to quantify selection overhead.
2. **Linear Theory Proxy for Nonlinear Models:** For a trained VAE, compute the Jacobian of the decoder at the latent origin and estimate its singular values. Does the empirical optimal k from inverse problems correlate with the theoretical prediction `k_opt ≈ argmax_k (sum_{i≤k} s_i²/(s_i²+σ²)² - (1/n)*∑_{j>k} s_j²)`?
3. **Nested Dropout Ablation:** Systematically vary the geometric dropout parameter p and the LDM mixing λ. Does a steeper p (more mass on small k) sharpen the U-curve? Does higher λ (more truncated training) shift the optimal k leftward and degrade full-k FID?
