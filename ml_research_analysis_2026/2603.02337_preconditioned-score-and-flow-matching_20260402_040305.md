# Preconditioned Score and Flow Matching
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.02337
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02337
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Reversible preconditioning maps reduce condition numbers of intermediate distributions, yielding large FID improvements (e.g., MNIST from 13.83 to 2.62) across multiple datasets.

## Executive Summary
Flow matching and score-based diffusion training are hampered by slow convergence due to ill-conditioned intermediate distributions \(p_t\), where the covariance \(\Sigma_t\) exhibits a high condition number. This causes gradient-based optimization to rapidly fit high-variance directions while neglecting low-variance modes, leading to suboptimal plateaus. The authors propose reversible, label‑conditional preconditioning maps that reshape the geometry of \(p_t\) without altering the underlying generative model. The preconditioner, implemented as either a normalizing flow or a low‑capacity flow‑matching model, transforms the data into a more Gaussian‑like representation. Evaluations on MNIST, LSUN Churches, Oxford Flowers‑102, and AFHQ Cats show consistent FID improvements and substantial reductions in the condition number \(\kappa(\Sigma_t)\), confirming enhanced optimization stability.

## Method Summary
The approach follows a two‑stage pipeline. First, a **preconditioner** is trained to map the original data distribution (e.g., VAE latents) to an isotropic, approximately Gaussian distribution. For MNIST, a normalizing flow with affine coupling layers (6 layers, MLPs with 2×256 hidden units, ReLU, alternating partitions and permutations) is trained via maximum likelihood. For high‑resolution datasets, a flow‑matching‑based preconditioner (lower‑capacity MLP/UNet, trained with ≤ 1/3 of the main model’s epoch budget) is used instead, due to invertibility constraints. After training, the preconditioner is applied to all training samples to obtain conditioned latents \(\tilde{x}_1\). In the second stage, a standard flow‑matching model (conditional MLP for MNIST, UNet for high‑res) is trained on the preconditioned data using the same sine‑cosine interpolation and loss. Sampling proceeds by integrating the learned ODE backward from a Gaussian noise sample and then applying the inverse preconditioner. The transformation is fully reversible, preserving the original data manifold.

## Key Results
- **MNIST:** FID improved from 13.83 (baseline) to 2.62 (normalizing flow) and 6.95 (flow‑based preconditioner).
- **LSUN Churches:** FID reduced from 19.53 to 14.47.
- **Oxford Flowers‑102:** FID reduced from 23.82 to 22.64.
- **AFHQ Cats:** FID reduced from 8.41 to 7.75.
- Condition numbers \(\kappa(\Sigma_t)\) across time \(t\) were significantly lowered for all datasets, confirming better conditioning and continued progress along previously suppressed directions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Flow‑matching optimization suffers from an intrinsic bias: gradient‑based methods rapidly adapt to high‑variance directions of the intermediate distribution \(p_t\) while making little progress along low‑variance modes. This imbalance creates suboptimal plateaus where the model fails to improve certain characteristics of the data. Preconditioning with a reversible map modifies the geometry of \(p_t\) by applying a deterministic transformation that reduces the condition number of \(\Sigma_t\). The transformed distribution becomes more isotropic and Gaussian‑like, ensuring that all directions are learned at comparable rates. The reversibility guarantees that the final generative model (after applying the inverse map) remains unchanged, but the intermediate training process becomes far more stable and efficient. The reduction in \(\kappa(\Sigma_t)\) directly addresses the root cause of slow convergence.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Raw images → VAE encoder → Latent codes \(x_1\) → Preconditioner (Normalizing Flow **or** Flow‑Matching Model) → Conditioned latents \(\tilde{x}_1\) → Main Flow Matching Model (MLP/UNet) → Learned vector field → ODE integration (backward from noise) → Sampled conditioned latents → Inverse Preconditioner → Final latent samples → VAE decoder → Generated images.

**Critical Path**
1. Train or load a VAE (for MNIST) or use a pretrained Stable Diffusion VAE (for high‑res).
2. Extract latent codes for the entire training set.
3. Train the preconditioner on the raw latents (MLE for NF; flow‑matching loss for flow‑based, limited epochs).
4. Apply the trained preconditioner to all training latents.
5. Train the main flow‑matching model on the preconditioned data using the same architecture and hyperparameters as the baseline (no‑preconditioning) model.
6. Generate samples via backward ODE integration and apply the inverse preconditioner.
7. Decode with the VAE and compute FID against real test images.

**Design Tradeoffs**
- *Preconditioner choice*: Normalizing flows give exact likelihood and invertibility but become computationally heavy or unstable in high dimensions; flow‑based preconditioners are more flexible and faster to train but require a matching‑point operator (details missing) to align intermediate distributions.
- *Capacity*: NF uses large MLPs (2×256 units) for expressiveness; flow preconditioner is deliberately low‑capacity to save compute, yet must still sufficiently Gaussianize the data.
- *Overhead*: Added preconditioner training increases total computation, but the main flow often converges faster and to a better optimum, potentially offsetting the cost.

**Failure Signatures**
- *Insufficient Gaussianization*: κ(Σₜ) remains high; FID improvement absent. Diagnose by plotting κ vs. t or using MMD/Sliced‑Schaefer distance.
- *Composition instability*: Training loss of the main flow diverges or plateaus abruptly after starting preconditioned training. Check gradient norms and preconditioner reconstruction error.
- *Invertibility breakdown*: For NF, high reconstruction error on a held‑out latent set indicates the flow is not properly learned.

**First Three Experiments**
1. Train a baseline flow‑matching model (no preconditioning) on all datasets; record FID and κ(Σₜ) curves.
2. Train each preconditioner (NF for MNIST, flow‑based for high‑res) and measure its effect on conditioning (κ reduction) and distributional similarity to Gaussian (MMD/Sliced‑Schaefer).
3. Train the preconditioned flow‑matching model; compare final FID with baseline and verify that κ(Σₜ) improvement persists throughout training.

## Limitations
- The **matching‑point operator** used with the flow‑based preconditioner is not described in detail, making exact replication difficult.
- The claim that normalizing flows are incompatible with high‑resolution latent spaces is stated without quantitative evidence or failed‑attempt results.
- FID gains vary widely across datasets (large on MNIST, marginal on Flowers), suggesting sensitivity to baseline training quality and hyperparameter choices that are not fully disclosed.

## Confidence
- *Ill‑conditioned intermediate distributions cause slow convergence*: **Medium** – κ(Σₜ) analysis supports the claim but causality is not rigorously proven.
- *Reversible preconditioning reshapes geometry and improves conditioning*: **Medium** – κ reduction is shown, but the alignment mechanism (matching‑point operator) remains unspecified.
- *Consistent FID improvements across datasets*: **Medium** – Reported numbers are promising, but reproducibility is uncertain due to omitted details.
- *Significant reduction of condition numbers κ(Σₜ)*: **High** – Direct measurement; straightforward to verify independently.

## Next Checks
1. **Reproduce κ(Σₜ) curves** for baseline and preconditioned models at multiple time steps (e.g., t = 0.1, 0.5, 0.9) to confirm conditioning improvement.
2. **Ablate the matching‑point operator** by substituting a simple affine alignment (e.g., linear regression between flow outputs) and measure its impact on FID and training stability.
3. **Test NF preconditioning on high‑resolution latents** using a flexible invertible architecture (e.g., Glow with actnorm and 1×1 convolutions); evaluate feasibility and FID change to clarify the invertibility constraint limits.
