# TrustMH-Bench: A Comprehensive Benchmark for Evaluating the Trustworthiness of Large Language Models in Mental Health

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.03047  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03047  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: All evaluated LLMs exhibit significant trustworthiness deficiencies across multiple dimensions, indicating they are not yet reliably deployable in mental health settings.

## Executive Summary
TrustMH-Bench introduces a comprehensive, multi-dimensional evaluation framework to assess the trustworthiness of large language models (LLMs) in mental health applications. Grounded in clinical norms and professional guidelines, the benchmark operationalizes eight pillars—Reliability, Crisis Handling, Safety, Fairness, Privacy, Robustness, Anti-sycophancy, and Ethics—into quantitative metrics and test protocols. Evaluation of twelve models (six general-purpose and six specialized) reveals systematic failures: every model shows significant gaps, with high privacy leakage rates (>50%), pronounced fairness instability (performance range up to 0.289 across demographics), and inadequate refusal of harmful requests (as low as 35.4%). Even top models like GPT-5.1 exhibit weaknesses in robustness and ethics, underscoring that current LLMs are not yet suitable for high‑stakes mental health deployment.

## Method Summary
The TrustMH-Bench framework defines eight trustworthiness pillars, each mapped to specific tasks and metrics using a mix of existing datasets (e.g., USMLE-Mental for reliability, EMOBENCH for emotion recognition) and newly constructed ones (e.g., PsyLeak for privacy, JailbreakMH for safety, EthicMH for ethics). Evaluation automates scoring through a combination of GPT‑4.1 as an LLM‑judge for subjective dimensions (e.g., emotional support quality, ethical reasoning) and specialized classifiers for risk detection (e.g., toxicity, refusal). The methodology includes zero‑shot/chain‑of‑thought prompting, perturbation analysis for robustness, counterfactual fairness tests, and Theory‑of‑Mind probes for privacy. A provided codebase orchestrates data preparation, model inference, metric computation, and aggregation, supporting scalable assessment of multiple models. Limitations include reliance on unreleased judge models and potential dataset contamination.

## Key Results
- All 12 evaluated LLMs show significant trustworthiness gaps; no model achieves consistently high performance across the eight pillars.  
- Critical deficiencies include privacy leakage (>50% in generative responses), fairness instability (performance range up to 0.289 across demographics), and low anti‑sycophancy refusal rates (as low as 35.4%).  
- Even state‑of‑the‑art general models (e.g., GPT‑5.1) exhibit weaknesses in robustness and ethics, underscoring that current LLMs are unsuitable for high‑stakes mental health deployment.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Comprehensive Pillar Coverage**  
Evaluating across eight distinct trustworthiness pillars increases the likelihood of detecting critical failures that narrower benchmarks would miss. Each pillar is operationalized into specific test protocols and metrics (e.g., suicide risk assessment via C‑SSRS for Crisis Handling, privacy leakage via Theory‑of‑Mind tasks for Privacy). This forces a broad assessment that mimics real‑world clinical risk management. The core assumption is that trustworthiness in mental health is inherently multi‑faceted; failure in any single dimension can compromise safety and render an LLM unsuitable. Evidence: The benchmark’s design directly addresses the previously narrow focus of LLM evaluation in mental health.

**Mechanism 2: Norm‑to‑Metric Translation**  
The benchmark translates established mental health guidelines (ACA Code of Ethics, WHO guidance, NIST AI RMF, clinical standards like C‑SSRS and APA Ethics) into measurable criteria and concrete tasks (e.g., PsyHarm dataset for safety, EthicMH for ethics). This grounding in professional norms provides an objective, reproducible foundation. The core assumption is that ethical and clinical principles can be systematically converted into testable scenarios for LLMs. This addresses the prior lack of unified taxonomies and domain‑specific benchmarks in NLP.

**Mechanism 3: Automated Scalable Evaluation**  
Using GPT‑4.1 as an LLM‑judge and pre‑trained classifiers (e.g., LibrAI/longformer‑harmful‑ro) enables comprehensive assessment of many models across many tasks without prohibitive human expert cost. The judge evaluates subjective generative tasks (emotional support, intervention, crisis escalation, ethics) while classifiers automate risk detection. The core assumption is that a powerful judge LLM can approximate human clinical judgment when carefully prompted. This automation made it feasible to evaluate 12 models across all eight pillars.

## Foundational Learning
- **Multi‑dimensional trustworthiness**: Why needed: Trustworthiness in mental health cannot be reduced to a single score; failure in any dimension can be catastrophic. Quick check: Why might a model that scores highly on emotional empathy still be unsafe for mental health deployment?  
- **Clinical risk management**: Why needed: The eight pillars derive from clinical risk management (e.g., crisis escalation protocols, privacy norms) to ensure evaluation reflects real‑world stakes. Quick check: How does “crisis identification” differ from ordinary safety filtering in general‑purpose LLMs?  
- **Operationalization of ethical principles**: Why needed: Abstract ethical codes (e.g., APA, ACA) must be turned into concrete scenarios (e.g., EthicMH dilemmas) to measure model behavior. Quick check: What challenges arise when converting a principle like “maintain professional boundaries” into a testable LLM prompt?  
- **LLM‑as‑judge evaluation**: Why needed: Many trustworthiness dimensions (e.g., empathy, ethical reasoning) are subjective; using a powerful LLM as a proxy for human experts allows scalable scoring. Quick check: What are the risks of relying on an LLM (e.g., GPT‑4.1) to judge another LLM’s psychological support responses?  
- **Robustness under perturbation**: Why needed: Real‑world mental health dialogues contain noise (typos, colloquialisms); robustness ensures consistent performance despite input variability. Quick check: Why might character‑level perturbations (e.g., swapped letters) be particularly harmful to mental health classification tasks?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**  
Datasets (USMLE‑Mental, EMOBENCH, D4, SWMH, ESConv, CPsyCounE, LLMs‑Mental‑Health‑Crisis, C‑SSRS, PsyLeak, JailbreakMH, EthicMH) → Evaluation modules (reliability.py, crisis.py, safety.py, fairness.py, privacy.py, robustness.py, sycophancy.py, ethics.py) → Judge infrastructure (llm_judge.py using GPT‑4.1; classifiers/ with pre‑trained models for toxicity and refusal) → Aggregation (metrics.py computing pillar scores and rankings) → Reporting. Utilities (prompt templates, perturbation functions, logging) support all stages.

**Critical Path**  
1. Data preparation: Download and preprocess datasets into standardized JSONL (dialogue, label, demographics).  
2. Model inference: For each model, generate responses on all prompts; store raw outputs.  
3. Metric computation: Run evaluation scripts per pillar—classification tasks yield accuracy/F1 directly; generative tasks are fed to LLM‑judge or classifiers; privacy/robustness apply perturbations and compare to baselines.  
4. Aggregation: Combine pillar scores into normalized composites and model rankings.  
5. Reporting: Generate summary tables, figures (radar charts, confusion matrices).

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- Cost vs. coverage: GPT‑4.1 judge is expensive; mitigated by sampling or caching responses.  
- Generalizability vs. domain specificity: Mix of general and mental health‑specific datasets; the latter may be smaller and noisier.  
- Automation vs. nuance: LLM‑as‑judge may miss therapeutic subtleties but is necessary for scale.  
- Perturbation realism: Character/word/sentence perturbations are systematic but may not capture all real‑world noise (e.g., speech errors).  
- Metric validity: Some metrics oversimplify complex constructs (e.g., binary refusal for sycophancy ignores ethical nuance).

**Failure Signatures**  
- Judge model instability: Inconsistent scores due to temperature/prompt variance.  
- Classifier domain mismatch: Toxicity classifier may mislabel clinically inappropriate language.  
- Dataset contamination: Fine‑tuned mental health models may have seen test data, inflating scores.  
- Resource bottlenecks: API rate limits, GPU OOM for large models.  
- Missing edge cases: Benchmark may not cover rare severe failures (e.g., subtle grooming, long‑term dependency).

**First 3 Experiments**  
1. Reproduce reliability baselines: Run GPT‑5.1 and Meditron3‑70B on USMLE and emotion recognition tasks; verify scores match Tables 12 and 13.  
2. Safety jailbreak stress test: Execute JailbreakMH attacks on all models; compute refusal rates; compare to Table 21.  
3. Privacy leakage audit: Run PsyLeak protocol on GPT‑5.1 and Simpsybot; manually inspect a response sample to validate leakage metrics.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **Constructing expert‑annotated datasets for subtle ethical boundary violations**  
   *Basis*: Appendix B notes that constructing such datasets remains an open problem.  
   *Why unresolved*: Existing benchmarks focus on explicit safety risks; ethical violations in mental health are often subtle, contextual, longitudinal, requiring domain expertise.  
   *What evidence would resolve*: A dataset annotated by certified mental health professionals covering multi‑turn conversations with varied boundary scenarios, demonstrating reliable inter‑annotator agreement and effectiveness in model evaluation.

2. **Operationalizing abstract clinical ethics principles into observable dialogue behaviors**  
   *Basis*: Appendix B states that principles like role boundaries, competence, non‑maleficence are conceptual and hard to translate into measurable behaviors.  
   *Why unresolved*: Lack of standardized metrics and protocols for converting principles into operational definitions suitable for automated, scalable evaluation.  
   *What evidence would resolve*: A validated coding scheme or rubric with clear behavioral indicators for each principle, expert consensus, and empirical correlation with real‑world ethical outcomes.

3. **Why specialized mental health LLMs underperform general‑purpose models on trustworthiness dimensions**  
   *Basis*: Results show general models consistently outperform specialized ones; the paper notes that domain‑specific fine‑tuning may not improve performance and could introduce higher variance.  
   *Why unresolved*: The paper identifies the pattern but does not investigate underlying causes, which may include data quality, training objectives overfitting to conversational skills, or misalignment between fine‑tuning datasets and trustworthiness criteria.  
   *What evidence would resolve*: Controlled experiments comparing training data distributions, model architectures, fine‑tuning objectives; error analysis to pinpoint whether underperformance stems from lack of foundational knowledge, over‑specialization, or evaluation mismatch.

4. **Systemic failures in privacy, fairness, and anti‑sycophancy behavior: root causes and improvements**  
   *Basis*: Benchmark reveals pervasive deficits: privacy leakage >50%, fairness range up to 0.289, low refusal rates (35.4%).  
   *Why unresolved*: Evaluation identifies symptoms but not root causes; possible explanations include insufficient training on constraints, lack of diverse demographic data, or fundamental limitations in model reasoning about ethics and boundaries.  
   *What evidence would resolve*: Ablation studies isolating impact of training data, ethical alignment techniques (RLHF, adversarial training), prompting strategies; targeted diagnostics to pinpoint failure modes; empirical validation of mitigation approaches across TrustMH‑Bench dimensions.

## Limitations
- Model and judge ambiguity: Unreleased models (GPT‑5.1, Claude‑Sonnet‑4.5) and vague judge specification (“GPT‑4.1”) prevent exact replication.  
- Dataset integrity concerns: Potential contamination in specialized models and incomplete public release of novel datasets (PsyLeak, JailbreakMH, EthicMH) may bias results.  
- Evaluation validity: LLM‑as‑judge may not capture clinical nuance; classifiers could miss mental‑health‑specific safety violations (e.g., subtle boundary breaches).

## Confidence
- Eight‑pillar coverage: Medium  
- Norm‑to‑metric translation: Medium  
- Automated evaluation (LLM‑as‑judge): Low  
- Generalized failure claims (systemic deficits across models): Medium
