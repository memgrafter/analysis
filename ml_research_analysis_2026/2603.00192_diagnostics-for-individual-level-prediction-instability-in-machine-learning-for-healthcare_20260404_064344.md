# Diagnostics for Individual-Level Prediction Instability in Machine Learning for Healthcare

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.00192
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00192
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Overparameterized ML models (e.g., neural networks) exhibit substantial instability in individual risk estimates under fixed data due to optimization randomness; standard aggregate metrics miss this; proposed diagnostics ePIW and eDFR detect it.

## Executive Summary
This paper reveals that individual patient risk predictions from overparameterized machine learning models can vary materially across training runs with identical data and architecture, purely due to randomness in optimization and initialization. Such instability threatens reliable clinical decision-making, particularly near treatment thresholds, yet standard aggregate metrics (e.g., AUC, log-loss) remain unaffected. To expose this hidden variability, the authors define two diagnostics: empirical prediction interval width (ePIW) for continuous risk dispersion and empirical decision flip rate (eDFR) for binary decision disagreement at a chosen threshold. In simulations and the GUSTO-I myocardial infarction dataset, neural networks show dramatically higher ePIW and eDFR than logistic regression, even under fixed data. The findings argue for integrating stability checks into model validation and preferring simpler models when accuracy is comparable, thereby ensuring trustworthy deployment in healthcare.

## Method Summary
The authors repeatedly train the same model architecture under two conditions: (1) resampling the training set and (2) fixing the training set while varying random seeds (initialization and SGD minibatch order). For each test sample, they gather B=100 predictions and compute ePIW (width of the central 95% interval) and eDFR (pairwise disagreement rate in binary decisions at a clinical threshold τ). Models are filtered to ensure competitiveness—all must achieve test loss within a small ε of the best—so that differences in instability are not confounded by large gaps in aggregate performance. The framework is applied to logistic regression (L-BFGS, SGD, polynomial features) and neural networks (1–2 hidden layers) on a simulated dataset and the GUSTO-I dataset. This design isolates the contribution of model architecture and optimization randomness to per-individual variability.

## Key Results
- Neural networks exhibit substantially higher ePIW and eDFR than logistic regression, even with fixed training data, confirming that optimization randomness alone drives instability in overparameterized models.
- Instability concentrates near clinical decision thresholds in simulation and in the high-risk tail in GUSTO-I, highlighting region-specific reliability concerns.
- Aggregate metrics (AUC, log-loss, accuracy) are nearly identical across models, demonstrating that they fail to detect large differences in individual-level stability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Randomness in optimization and initialization can cause materially different individual risk estimates from the same model architecture trained on fixed data.  
**Mechanism:** For overparameterized, non-convex models (e.g., neural networks), the loss landscape contains many near-optimal solutions. Different random seeds lead to different trajectories through stochastic optimization (e.g., SGD), converging to distinct local minima that yield divergent predictions for the same input.  
**Core assumption:** The hypothesis space is sufficiently complex that multiple distinct parameterizations achieve nearly identical aggregate performance (the Rashomon set).  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract statement; Section 3.1 defines the competitive set and prediction matrix; Log‑LBFGS (convex, deterministic) shows zero ePIW/eDFR under fixed data, confirming the role of non‑convexity/stochasticity.  
**Break condition:** If the optimization problem is convex and solved to global optimality with deterministic solvers, this source of instability vanishes.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Model capacity amplifies sensitivity to algorithmic randomness; higher expressive power correlates with greater individual-level prediction dispersion.  
**Mechanism:** Flexible architectures (e.g., deep neural networks) have a larger Rashomon set—more parameter configurations satisfy the competitiveness criterion. This expands the range of plausible decision boundaries, making predictions for a given xᵢ more sensitive to which specific solution is reached.  
**Core assumption:** There is a monotonic relationship between hypothesis space complexity (e.g., parameter count) and the volume of the Rashomon set in function space.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract notes neural networks exhibit greater instability; Section 4.2 & 4.3 show consistently higher ePIW and eDFR for neural networks vs. logistic regression across both datasets; Section 2 discusses overparameterization expanding the Rashomon set.  
**Break condition:** If model capacity is constrained (e.g., logistic regression), the Rashomon set shrinks and predictions become invariant to initialization.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Aggregate metrics (e.g., AUC, log-loss) are insufficient to detect individual-level instability because they average away per‑observation variance.  
**Mechanism:** Aggregate metrics evaluate expected performance over the test distribution. They are insensitive to the variance of f̂(xᵢ) across fits for a fixed xᵢ, as long as mean performance is similar. Models can thus be “indistinguishable” by AUC yet differ vastly in ePIW/eDFR.  
**Core assumption:** Clinical utility depends on the reliability of predictions for specific patients, not just population-level averages.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract explicitly states standard metrics are agnostic to individual stability; Section 1 criticizes averaging away instability; Table 2/3 show comparable BCE/accuracy while Figures 1/4 reveal stark dispersion differences.  
**Break condition:** If evaluation explicitly incorporates per‑individual variability measures (ePIW, eDFR), the disconnect is resolved.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Rashomon Effect**  
  Why needed: The paper’s framework assumes the existence of many equally‑performing models (the Rashomon set), explaining why optimization randomness yields different fits that are all “competitive”.  
  Quick check: Can two models with almost identical test AUC produce different predictions for the same patient? If yes, they likely belong to the same Rashomon set.

- **Concept: Overparameterization**  
  Why needed: Modern ML models have many more parameters than data points, creating a vast Rashomon set and making solutions non‑unique. This is the condition that enables optimization‑induced instability.  
  Quick check: If you double the number of parameters in a neural network while keeping data fixed, does individual prediction stability (ePIW) generally increase, decrease, or stay the same? (Expected: increase or stay similar, not decrease.)

- **Concept: Stochastic Optimization**  
  Why needed: Methods like SGD introduce randomness via mini‑batch sampling and weight initialization. This is the direct engine that navigates the Rashomon set to different local minima.  
  Quick check: Would using a deterministic optimizer (like full‑batch L‑BFGS) for a neural network eliminate eDFR? Not necessarily—it may get stuck in a single local minimum but the landscape may still have many minima; however, with fixed seed it would produce one deterministic path.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Fixed test set → multiple training runs (varying seeds or resampled data) → per‑sample prediction matrix B → compute ePIW/eDFR → filter by aggregate performance (competitiveness) → compare across architectures.
- **Critical path:** Start with a model that meets target aggregate performance → enforce competitiveness across B runs → compute per‑observation predictions → calculate ePIW/eDFR → stratify by risk/feature subgroups → compare architectures.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Computational cost vs. insight: B=100 retrains is expensive; reducing B speeds up but may increase noise.  
  - Model complexity vs. reliability: Higher capacity may improve aggregate metrics slightly but sacrifices individual stability; trade‑off should favor simpler models when ΔAUC < ε but ΔePIW/eDFR large.  
  - Threshold selection for eDFR: Choice of τ is clinical, not technical; must be defined a priori based on actionable decision boundaries.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - High ePIW/eDFR in regions far from the decision boundary → undermines trust in continuous risk estimates even if binary decisions appear stable.  
  - Instability concentrated near τ → high risk of “decision flips” for borderline patients.  
  - Log‑SGD shows non‑zero ePIW/eDFR under fixed data → confirms SGD introduces variance even for convex models.  
  - Neural network instability does not vanish with large n_train under fixed data → optimization randomness independent of sample size.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Replicate Figure 1 (Simulation): Train Log‑LBFGS, Log‑SGD, NN‑2L with n_train=500, B=100. Compute ePIW/eDFR under (a) resampled D_train and (b) fixed D_train + seed variation. Verify NN‑2L has >> ePIW/eDFR than Log‑LBFGS, especially near τ≈0.53.  
  2. GUSTO‑I Threshold Analysis: For NN‑G and Log‑G with n_train=5000, plot eDFR vs. predicted risk for τ=0.07. Confirm neural network’s eDFR peak aligns with the threshold region.  
  3. Optimizer ablation: For NN‑1L, compare ePIW under (a) SGD (stochastic) vs. (b) L‑BFGS (deterministic) with fixed D_train. Expect SGD to show non‑zero ePIW; L‑BFGS should approach zero (if converges to same point).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can the computational burden of repeated retraining for stability diagnostics be reduced without sacrificing accuracy in estimating ePIW and eDFR?  
2. What factors determine whether prediction instability concentrates near decision thresholds (as in simulation) or in extreme risk regions (as in GUSTO-I)?  
3. When aggregate accuracy differs between models, how should prediction stability be quantitatively balanced against performance in clinical model selection?  
4. Are optimization‑induced instability patterns generalizable beyond logistic regression and fully connected neural networks to other common healthcare ML architectures (e.g., gradient boosting, transformers, graph networks)?

## Limitations
- Hyperparameter details (learning rates, L2 strengths, epoch counts) are incompletely specified, risking non‑reproducible instability magnitudes.  
- Simulation design (true coefficients, threshold derivation) is ambiguous; small changes could alter observed ePIW/eDFR patterns.  
- The study’s model scope is limited to logistic regression and fully connected neural networks; instability in other architectures (e.g., XGBoost, clinical BERT) remains unexplored.

## Confidence
- **Optimization‑driven instability under fixed data:** High (direct evidence: deterministic models show zero ePIW/eDFR, stochastic models show non‑zero).  
- **Capacity amplifies instability:** Medium (consistent empirical pattern but monotonicity not proven).  
- **Aggregate metrics mask individual instability:** High (tables show similar AUC/log‑loss while ePIW/eDFR diverge widely).

## Next Checks
1. Replicate simulation with grid search over capacities (hidden units) and regularization strengths; confirm ePIW/eDFR increase monotonically with parameter count when aggregate performance is held constant.  
2. Deterministic optimizer ablation: For neural networks, replace SGD with full‑batch L‑BFGS (fixed seed); if ePIW/eDFR approach zero, it confirms stochasticity—not just non‑convexity—as the primary driver.  
3. Stability–accuracy Pareto analysis: Vary ε in the competitiveness filter (0.0001–0.01) and quantify how often simpler models (logistic regression) dominate in ePIW/eDFR at marginal AUC sacrifices (<0.005). If simpler models win >80% of ε settings, the “prefer simpler” recommendation is robust.
