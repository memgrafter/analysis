# A Geometry-Aware Efficient Algorithm for Compositional Entropic Risk Minimization

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.02877  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02877  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: SCENT, a stochastic algorithm using a geometry-aware proximal mirror descent for the dual variable, achieves O(1/√T) convergence for convex compositional entropic risk minimization (CERM) and empirically outperforms BSGD, ASGD, and SOX on extreme classification (360k classes), partial AUC maximization, CLIP training, and distributionally robust optimization.

## Executive Summary
Compositional entropic risk minimization (CERM) with Log-E-Exp losses is ubiquitous in extreme classification, contrastive learning, and distributionally robust optimization, but the inner expectations are costly to compute and existing methods often suffer from non-convergence, instability, and slow rates. This paper introduces SCENT, a stochastic algorithm that solves a min‑min dual reformulation using a proximal mirror descent update tailored to the exponential geometry of the problem. The dual update leverages a Bregman divergence induced by φ(ν)=e^{-ν}, which admits a closed form and prevents explosive steps, while the primal update uses an unbiased gradient estimator. Theoretically, SCENT achieves an O(1/√T) convergence rate for convex objectives and provably mitigates the exponentially large smoothness constants that hinder standard SGD. Extensive experiments on extreme classification (360k classes), partial AUC maximization, CLIP training, and DRO demonstrate consistent improvements in robustness and speed over baselines like BSGD, ASGD, and SOX.

## Method Summary
SCENT operates on the min‑min dual formulation \(\min_{w,\nu} \frac{1}{n} \sum_i \mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim P_i}[e^{s_i(w;\zeta)-\nu_i} + \nu_i]\). Its core innovation is the use of a geometry‑aware stochastic proximal mirror descent (SPMD) for the dual variable, where the Bregman divergence is derived from φ(ν)=e^{-ν}. This choice ensures a closed‑form update that implicitly normalizes gradients by an exponential scale, preventing the dual variable from exploding and avoiding numerical overflow.

At each iteration \(t\), a random minibatch \(B_t\) is drawn. For every \(i \in B_t\):
- A fresh sample \(\zeta_{i,t}\) is used to compute \(s_i(w^t;\zeta_{i,t})\) and to update the dual variable via
\[
\nu_{i,t} = \nu_{i,t-1} + \log(1+\alpha_t e^{s_i}) - \log(1+\alpha_t e^{\nu_{i,t-1}}).
\]
- Then an independent set of samples \(\zeta'_{i,t}\) yields the primal gradient estimator
\[
z_t = \frac{1}{|B_t|} \sum_{i\in B_t} e^{s_i(w^t;\zeta'_{i,t}) - \nu_{i,t}} \nabla s_i(w^t;\zeta'_{i,t}).
\]
Finally, the primal parameter \(w^t\) is updated via projected stochastic gradient descent:
\(w^{t+1} = \Pi_W[w^t - \eta_t z_t]\) (or via AdamW, etc.). The step sizes \(\alpha_t\) and \(\eta_t\) are typically decayed (e.g., \(\alpha_t \propto 1/\sqrt{T}\), \(\eta_t = \eta \alpha_t\)). This block‑coordinate scheme produces an unbiased estimate of the primal gradient under convexity, and the geometry‑aware dual step yields theoretical bounds that avoid the exponential dependence that plagues standard approaches.

## Key Results
- SCENT attains an O(1/√T) convergence rate for convex CERM, theoretically beating standard SGD on the dual whose smoothness constant is \(L = e^{\nu^* - c_0}\) (exponential in problem scale).
- The SPMD update mitigates the exponentially large smoothness constant, leading to stable optimization and superior empirical robustness.
- Across four challenging tasks—extreme classification with 360k classes, partial AUC maximization, CLIP training, and distributionally robust optimization—SCENT consistently outperforms BSGD, ASGD, and SOX in both speed and final objective values.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
SCENT's effectiveness arises from three tightly coupled mechanisms that collectively address the challenges of CERM.

### Mechanism 1: Geometry-matching Bregman divergence
The Bregman divergence induced by \(\phi(\nu)=e^{-\nu}\) aligns exactly with the exponential structure of the entropic risk objective. In the SPMD update, this divergence defines a proximal term that normalizes the gradient by the exponential scale of the current iterate, preventing the dual variable from exploding and avoiding numerical overflow. The closed‑form derivation (Lemma 3.1) makes the update efficient. The mechanism assumes that loss values \(s_i\) are bounded within \([c_0, c_1]\), ensuring \(\phi\) remains smooth and the projection is well‑defined. Evidence: the abstract and Section 3 explicitly construct the tailored Bregman divergence and prove the closed‑form update.

### Mechanism 2: Mitigation of exponential smoothness
Standard projected SGD for the dual minimization suffers from a Lipschitz constant \(L = e^{\nu^* - c_0}\), which can be astronomically large. The SPMD update’s geometry‑aware step implicitly rescales the gradient using the Bregman divergence, effectively reducing the dependence on \(L\). The analysis (Section 4.3, Theorem 4.5) shows SPMD’s bound depends on the second‑order moment ratio \(\kappa\) rather than explicitly on \(L\). Under the condition \(\alpha_t \le \rho e^{-\nu_{i,t-1}}\) for some constant \(\rho>0\), the variance of the gradient estimator is controlled. Evidence: The abstract claims provable outperformance; Section 4.3 compares the bounds of SPMD and SGD, showing the ratio can be exponentially small.

### Mechanism 3: Unbiased block‑coordinate updates
By reformulating the Log‑E‑Exp as a min‑min problem, SCENT decouples dual and primal steps. It updates a random block of dual variables \(\nu_i\) using a fresh sample, then computes the primal gradient using independent samples and the current \(\nu_i\). This yields an unbiased estimator of \(\nabla_w F(w,\nu)\) conditional on the dual variables, even when only a subset of \(\nu_i\) is updated per iteration. The convexity of each \(s_i(\cdot;\zeta)\) and a proper step‑size schedule (\(\alpha_t \propto 1/\sqrt{T}\)) then guarantee an O(1/√T) convergence rate. Evidence: The paper contrasts BSGD’s bias with SCENT’s unbiasedness in Section 2 and provides Theorem 3.6.

### Overall perspective
These mechanisms operate under a common set of assumptions: convexity of each loss function, boundedness of \(s_i\), step‑size conditions (\(\alpha_t\) decaying as \(1/\sqrt{T}\) and \(\eta_t = \eta \alpha_t\)), and the use of independent samples for dual and primal updates. Together they enable SCENT to achieve a theoretically optimal rate while taming the exponential factors that cripple naive dual approaches.

## Foundational Learning

- **Convex optimization**  
  *Why needed*: The convergence analysis assumes convexity of each \(s_i(\cdot;\zeta)\) and joint convexity of the primal‑dual objective; these properties are essential for deriving linearized regret bounds and telescoping sums to obtain the final suboptimality guarantee. In the proof of Theorem 3.6, joint convexity allows the transformation of the primal‑dual gap into a sum of one‑step residuals, which are then bounded using convexity and the unbiased gradient estimator.  
  *Quick check*: Why does the analysis require *joint* convexity of \(F(w,\nu)\) and not just separate convexity? (Because the linearized bounds for both variables must sum to a lower bound on the primal‑dual gap.)

- **Bregman divergence**  
  *Why needed*: SCENT employs a specific Bregman divergence \(D_\phi(\nu,\nu')\) with \(\phi(\nu)=e^{-\nu}\) in the proximal step. This mirror map captures the exponential geometry of the dual variable and yields a closed‑form update (Lemma 3.1). The divergence measures discrepancy in the exponential scale, which matches the entropic risk structure and prevents numerical overflow.  
  *Quick check*: If we replaced the Bregman divergence with the Euclidean one (\(\phi(\nu)=\frac{1}{2}\nu^2\)), what key property would be lost? (Adaptation to the exponential scale; steps would no longer be implicitly normalized, potentially requiring much smaller step sizes and losing numerical stability.)

- **Stochastic gradient descent (SGD)**  
  *Why needed*: The primal update in SCENT is a projected SGD step; understanding its variance and step‑size trade‑offs is necessary to interpret the convergence bound and to implement the algorithm with optimizers like Adam or cosine decay. The unbiased estimator of the primal gradient (see Mechanism 3) ensures that the SGD iterates converge at the optimal O(1/√T) rate under diminishing step sizes.  
  *Quick check*: What makes \(\nabla_w \Phi(w_t,\nu_t;\zeta')\) unbiased in SCENT and how does it differ from the biased estimator in BSGD? (BSGD approximates the inner expectation with a mini‑batch directly; SCENT’s dual variable adjusts the scale so that \(e^{s-\nu}\) has conditional mean 1, making the estimator unbiased given \(\nu\).)

- **Dual decomposition of entropic risk**  
  *Why needed*: The identity \(\log(\mathbb{E}[e^s]) = \min_\nu \mathbb{E}[e^{s-\nu}+\nu]\) is the foundation of SCENT. Without this reformulation, the algorithm would not have the dual variables to which the SPMD update is applied. This decomposition converts the original expectation of a log‑sum‑exp into an equivalent min‑min problem, enabling block‑coordinate updates and an unbiased gradient construction.  
  *Quick check*: Why can’t we directly apply SGD to the primal Log‑E‑Exp objective? (The gradient involves a ratio of expectations, which is hard to compute exactly; approximations lead to bias or large variance.)

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**:  
Mini‑batch sampler → Dual read‑update‑write → Primal gradient estimator → Primal updater.  
Variables: primal parameters \(w\), dual variables \(\nu_i\) (one per data point or block), step sizes \(\alpha_t\) (dual) and \(\eta_t\) (primal).

**Critical path**:
1. Initialize \(w^1\) and \(\nu^0\) (e.g., \(\nu_i = 0\) or a small constant).
2. For each iteration \(t = 1,\dots,T\):
   a. Sample a minibatch \(B_t \subset [n]\) of size \(B\).
   b. For each \(i \in B_t\):
      - Draw \(\zeta_{i,t} \sim P_i\).
      - Compute \(s_i(w^t;\zeta_{i,t})\).
      - Update \(\nu_i\) via closed‑form:
        \[
        \nu_{i,t} = \nu_{i,t-1} + \log(1+\alpha_t e^{s_i}) - \log(1+\alpha_t e^{\nu_{i,t-1}}).
        \]
   c. For each \(i \in B_t\), draw an independent \(\zeta'_{i,t} \sim P_i\) and compute:
      - weight \(= e^{s_i(w^t;\zeta'_{i,t}) - \nu_{i,t}}\)
      - gradient contribution \(= \text{weight} \cdot \nabla s_i(w^t;\zeta'_{i,t})\)
      Accumulate \(z_t = \frac{1}{|B_t|} \sum\) contributions.
   d. Update \(w\):
      \[
      w^{t+1} = \Pi_W[w^t - \eta_t z_t] \quad (\text{or use AdamW, etc.})
      \]
3. Return \(w_T\) (or an average of past \(w\)’s).

**Design tradeoffs**:
- \(\alpha_t\) vs. \(\eta_t\): The theory suggests \(\eta_t = \eta \alpha_t\); decoupling them can deteriorate the O(1/√T) guarantee.
- Step‑size scaling: \(\alpha_t\) must satisfy \(\alpha_t \le \rho e^{-\nu_{i,t-1}}\) for some \(\rho>0\) to control variance; starting too large can cause dual blow‑up. Typically \(\alpha_t\) starts small and decays.
- Mini‑batch size \(B\): Larger \(B\) reduces variance of the primal gradient estimate but increases memory/computation; very small \(B\) may require smaller \(\alpha_t\) for stability.
- Dual storage: \(\nu_i\) must be stored for all data points, which can be huge (e.g., millions). In extreme classification, memory can become a bottleneck; solutions include CPU offloading or approximations.
- Numerical stability: The updates involve exponentials; log‑sum‑exp tricks are used, but extreme \(s_i\) values still risk overflow. Monitoring and optional clipping may be needed.
- Sampling overhead: SCENT requires two independent samples per selected data point per iteration (one for the dual update and one for the primal gradient), effectively doubling the number of stochastic oracle calls compared to single‑sample methods. This may increase the computational cost per iteration.

**Failure signatures**:
- Dual variables \(\nu_i\) diverge or become NaN if \(\alpha_t\) is too large or if \(s_i\) grows too quickly early in training.
- Stagnation or very slow convergence if \(\alpha_t\) is too small or step sizes are not properly decayed.
- Out‑of‑memory errors for massive datasets or models due to storing \(\nu_i\) and parameters.
- High sample variance when the second‑order moment ratio \(\kappa\) is large, leading to poor empirical performance.
- Implementation bugs (e.g., reusing the same sample for dual and primal updates) break the unbiasedness guarantee.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. *Synthetic verification of SPMD vs SGD*: Fix \(w\) and generate synthetic \(s_i\) with varying noise levels; compare dual update error of SPMD and projected SGD. Expect SPMD’s error ratio to improve as the variance of \(s_i\) increases (replicates Fig. 1).
2. *Mini‑benchmark on extreme classification*: Use a 10k‑class subset of Glint360K with minibatch size 128. Tune \(\alpha,\eta\) for SCENT; compare against BSGD, ASGD, and SOX. SCENT should achieve lower training and validation cross‑entropy loss (as in Fig. 2).
3. *Stability test with large \(\alpha_t\)*: Set a constant \(\alpha_t = 10\) while keeping \(\eta_t\) reasonable; monitor \(\nu_i\) over iterations. Divergence or explosion of \(\nu_i\) confirms the necessity of the condition \(\alpha_t \le \rho e^{-\nu_{i,t-1}}\).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Can SCENT be extended to non‑convex objectives while maintaining theoretical convergence guarantees?  
2. Is the O(1/√T) rate in Theorem 3.6 tight for convex CERM, or can SCENT achieve O(1/T) under strong convexity?  
3. Does the convergence bound of SCENT necessarily depend on the second‑order moment ratio \(\kappa\), and can that dependence be reduced or eliminated?  
4. Can the proximal step size \(\alpha_t\) be chosen adaptively (e.g., via Adam‑like rules) while preserving the O(1/√T) guarantee?

## Limitations
- Exact hyperparameter values (\(\eta\), \(\alpha\), \(\gamma\) for SOX) for each dataset are not provided; only search ranges are given, hindering exact replication.
- Initialization of the dual variables \(\nu_i\) is not described; poor initialization may cause early instability.
- Potential numerical overflow from exponentials is acknowledged; the paper does not detail clipping or scaling strategies for extreme cases.
- Memory footprint of storing \(\nu_i\) for all \(n\) samples may be prohibitive at extreme scale (e.g., millions of classes); distributed implementations are not discussed.
- Theoretical analysis assumes convexity, yet the method is applied to non‑convex deep networks without guarantees.
- The requirement for independent samples for dual and primal updates increases the number of stochastic gradient evaluations per iteration, which may affect wall‑clock time compared to methods that reuse samples.
- The closed‑form dual update involves exponentials that can still overflow for extreme \(s_i\) values; practical implementations need additional stabilization (e.g., log‑sum‑exp tricks or gradient clipping), which are not covered in the paper.

## Confidence
- **Convex convergence rate O(1/√T)**: *High* – The proof is complete and assumptions are clearly stated.
- **Superiority on extreme classification (360k classes)**: *Medium* – Strong empirical results but hyperparameter details are missing, limiting reproducibility.
- **Scalability to 360k classes**: *High* – Experiments convincingly demonstrate large‑scale operation.
- **Effectiveness in non‑convex settings (e.g., CLIP)**: *Low–Medium* – Only one empirical case and no theoretical backing.
- **Robustness to dual step size \(\alpha_t\)**: *Medium* – Analysis indicates a condition, but practical tuning guidelines are sparse.
