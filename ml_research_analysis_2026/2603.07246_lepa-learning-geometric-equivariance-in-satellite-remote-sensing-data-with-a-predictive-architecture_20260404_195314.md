# LEPA: Learning Geometric Equivariance in Satellite Remote Sensing Data with a Predictive Architecture

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.07246
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07246
- Reference count: 39
- Primary result: LEPA achieves high geometric equivariance (MRR >0.8) vs. interpolation failure (<0.2) by predicting transformed embeddings with a conditioned predictor.

## Executive Summary
Geometric mismatches between user-defined areas and fixed precomputed embedding grids in satellite remote sensing require transforming embeddings. Standard latent-space interpolation fails because the embedding manifold is highly non-convex, yielding unrealistic representations. LEPA extends I-JEPA with a predictor conditioned on transformation parameters (translation, rotation, scaling) to directly predict transformed embeddings. Training uses combined inpainting and augmentation prediction objectives, followed by augmentation-only finetuning. On the PANGAEA benchmark, LEPA embeddings yield competitive segmentation performance, and for geometric equivariance measured by mean reciprocal rank (MRR), LEPA surpasses 0.8 while interpolation remains below 0.2.

## Method Summary
LEPA builds on the Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) by introducing a predictor that receives both a context set of patch embeddings from an input image and explicit geometric transformation parameters. The predictor, implemented as a cross-attention network, outputs a full grid of embeddings corresponding to the transformed image. A teacher encoder (exponential moving average of the student) provides target embeddings for the transformed image. Training proceeds in two stages: first, the predictor learns to simultaneously inpaint masked patches and predict augmented views, forcing the encoder to preserve fine-grained structure; second, the encoder is frozen and the predictor is finetuned solely on augmentation prediction to sharpen equivariance without collapse. Conditioning is achieved either by appending transformation tokens to mask tokens or by modifying positional encodings to be centered and transformation-aware.

## Key Results
- Geometric equivariance (MRR) improves from <0.2 with interpolation to >0.8 (e.g., 0.8062 on HLS with conditioned positional encodings after finetuning).
- Segmentation performance on the PANGAEA benchmark remains competitive with baseline foundation models.
- Qualitative results show predicted embeddings closely match target embeddings, while interpolation exhibits clear artifacts.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The method succeeds because the embedding manifold of a standard foundation model is non-convex, making linear interpolation unrealistic. By training a predictor conditioned on transformation parameters, LEPA learns to map an original embedding to the embedding of a geometrically transformed image directly, bypassing the need for interpolation. The two-stage training regime (combined inpainting + augmentation prediction, then augmentation-only finetuning) prevents predictor collapse, which would occur if augmentation prediction were used alone, by maintaining a rich, detailed representation from the inpainting objective.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Student Encoder (ViT-Base) -> Context Embeddings; Teacher Encoder (EMA of Student) -> Target Embeddings; Predictor takes Context Embeddings + Transformation Parameters -> Predicted Full-Grid Embeddings.

Critical path: Input image -> Student Encoder -> Context Embeddings -> Predictor (conditioned on transformation parameters) -> Predicted Target Embeddings; loss computed against Teacher Encoder(Transformed_Input).

Design tradeoffs: Conditioning method (appending parameters to tokens vs. conditioned positional encodings); predictor scope (full grid vs. only masked patches); training objective (joint inpainting + augmentation prediction vs. augmentation-only).

Failure signatures: Low MRR (<0.3) indicating predictor collapse or invariance; class-specific noise in PCA plots; collapse when finetuning with augmentation-only without prior joint training.

First 3 experiments:
1. Reproduce interpolation failure by transforming patch embedding grids and decoding, observing artifacts.
2. Train a baseline I-JEPA model and measure its MRR (expected ~0.18).
3. Implement LEPA, train with combined objective, then finetune predictor; evaluate MRR (>0.8) and compare qualitative predictions.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How can LEPA’s conditioning on transformation parameters be improved, especially during augmentation-only finetuning? The paper notes that conditioned positional encodings underperform in this stage and suggests exploring relative positional encodings like ALiBi or RoPE.
- Does the fixed number of classes in benchmark datasets (e.g., ImageNet) cause the observed class-specific noise? The hypothesis is that datasets with many fixed classes induce background artifacts; testing across datasets with varying class cardinality could resolve this.
- What is the optimal balance between inpainting and augmentation prediction objectives? The paper uses a combined approach but does not systematically vary loss weighting or masking ratios.
- Can more compact predictor architectures achieve comparable equivariance? The authors mention the potential for smaller predictors but do not evaluate efficiency trade-offs.

## Limitations
- Hyperparameters (learning rate, batch size, mask ratio, EMA decay) are not fully specified, hindering exact replication.
- Transformation sampling distributions (ranges for rotation, scaling, translation) are unclear, which could affect the generalizability of results.
- Predictor conditioning details (MLP architecture, parameter normalization) are missing, leaving implementation ambiguities.

## Confidence
- Non-convex manifold claim: Medium
- Predictor conditioning efficacy: High
- Two-stage training necessity: High

## Next Checks
1. Ablate conditioning methods: Compare appended parameters vs. conditioned positional encodings across training stages to understand performance differences.
2. Test transformation generalization: Evaluate the predictor on out-of-distribution rotations and scales (e.g., ±180°, 2× scale) to quantify equivariance limits.
3. Vary inpainting mask ratio: Systematically adjust the mask ratio during joint training to determine its impact on equivariance and downstream segmentation performance.
