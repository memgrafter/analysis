# When Should an AI Act? A Human-Centered Model of Scene, Context, and Behavior for Agentic AI Design

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.22814
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22814
- **Reference count:** 28
- **Primary result:** A foundational framework for designing agentic AI systems that act with contextual sensitivity and judgment, based on a human-centered model of Scene, Context, and Behavior.

## Executive Summary
Agentic AI systems often intervene at inappropriate times or in intrusive ways because they lack a principled understanding of when action is warranted. This paper addresses that gap by proposing a human-centered conceptual model that distinguishes the objectively observable *Scene* (Actor, Objects, Background, Activity) from the subjectively meaningful *Context* (Spatial, Temporal, Interoceptive, Individual, Social/Cultural). The model integrates established behavioral theories—Human Behavior Factors (Attitude, Perceived Opportunity, Perceived Capability, Motivation, Trigger)—to explain behavior as an interpretive outcome rather than a direct stimulus-response. From this synthesis, five agent design principles are derived: behavioral alignment, contextual sensitivity, temporal appropriateness, motivational calibration, and agency preservation. Together, these principles guide an agent’s intervention depth, timing, intensity, and restraint. The work is purely conceptual and has not been empirically validated, but offers a rigorous foundation for future research and system development.

## Method Summary
The paper employs a conceptual synthesis methodology. It combines prior work on situational assessment (the Scene–Context distinction) with behavioral determinant theories (Theory of Planned Behavior, COM-B, Fogg’s model) to construct a unified framework for interpreting human behavior. This framework then informs the derivation of five design principles via logical deduction. No empirical data, algorithmic implementation, or quantitative evaluation is presented; the output is a theoretical model and associated heuristics for designing agentic systems. Reproduction requires manually applying the model and principles to concrete use cases through scenario analysis, as the paper provides no operationalization details or experimental protocol.

## Key Results
- A human-centered model unifying *Scene* (observable), *Context* (subjective meaning), and *Human Behavior Factors* (likelihood determinants) to explain behavior as an interpretive outcome.
- Five agent design principles: Behavioral Alignment, Contextual Sensitivity, Temporal Appropriateness, Motivational Calibration, Agency Preservation.
- These principles collectively guide the depth, timing, intensity, and restraint of interventions, aiming to mitigate intrusive or mistimed actions in agentic AI.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Scene–Context Separation Enables Meaning Inference
- **Claim:** Separating the objectively observable *Scene* from the subjectively meaningful *Context* enables an agent to infer *why* a user is acting, not just *what* they are doing—the prerequisite for judging intervention appropriateness.
- **Mechanism:** The agent first constructs a structured *Scene* representation from sensor/stated data. It then maps contextual dimensions (Spatial, Temporal, Interoceptive, Individual, Social/Cultural) onto this scene to generate a *Contextual Interpretation* (e.g., “this living room is ‘my home’ vs. ‘an Airbnb’”). Intervention decisions rely on this combined meaning, not raw cues.
- **Core assumption:** Different contextual interpretations of the same scene lead to different behavioral predictions and thus different intervention needs.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract] “...separates what is observable from what is meaningful to the user and explains how the same scene can yield different behavioral meanings and outcomes.”
  - [section 2.1.2] “Context provides subjective meaning to a situation... Context determines whether a given space is experienced as one’s own home or a friend’s home.”
  - [corpus] Related work on contextual inference (e.g., paper 270372 on VLMs) and proactive behavior (papers 22, 23, 24) indicates an active research gap but does not directly validate this mechanism.
- **Break condition:** If key contextual dimensions (e.g., Individual or Social/Cultural) cannot be reliably accessed or inferred, the mechanism collapses, forcing guesses based on *Scene* alone—a scenario the model warns against.

### Mechanism 2: Human Behavior Factors Calibrate Intervention Intensity
- **Claim:** The *Internal Behavior Generation Loop*—where Human Behavior Factors shape the *likelihood* of a behavior within a given Scene/Context—provides a meta-cognitive framework for estimating a user’s *behavioral readiness* and thus intervention intensity.
- **Mechanism:** Given a Scene and its Contextual Interpretation, the agent uses proxy signals (logs, sensors, dialogue) to assess the state of key factors (Attitude, Opportunity, Capability, Motivation, Trigger). For instance, low Perceived Capability (e.g., user confusion) may predict a need for assistance, while high Automatic Motivation (e.g., a strong habit) may predict resistance. The agent’s intervention strength is calibrated to these assessed likelihoods.
- **Core assumption:** Human Behavior Factors are not directly observable but their proxies can be sensed or inferred, and their configuration reliably influences the probability of a target behavior.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 2.1.3] Synthesizes TPB, COM-B, and Fogg’s model to define these factors as “conceptual criteria for judging which behaviors are more likely to occur.”
  - [section 3] “Human Behavior Factors... shape the direction of behavioral likelihood in a given situation.”
  - [section 4.4] The Motivational Calibration Principle operationalizes: “agents should vary how strongly and in what way they intervene” based on these factors.
- **Break condition:** Noisy, culturally biased, or incomplete proxies for Human Behavior Factors cause miscalibration (e.g., misinterpreting cultural restraint as low capability, leading to overbearing help).

### Mechanism 3: Five Principles as Concurrent Constraints on Intervention Policy
- **Claim:** The five derived design principles act as *concurrent constraints* on an intervention policy, forcing the system to balance depth, timing, intensity, and restraint.
- **Mechanism:** A candidate intervention (e.g., “offer alternative route”) is evaluated against each principle: Does it match the user’s current Activity level (Alignment)? Fit the interpreted Context (Sensitivity)? Occur at an appropriate moment (Temporal)? Match inferred readiness (Calibration)? Preserve user control (Agency)? An intervention passes only if it satisfies a threshold across all active constraints.
- **Core assumption:** These five principles are sufficient and necessary heuristics for “principled judgment” in agentic intervention.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “we derive five agent design principles... that guide the depth, timing, intensity, and restraint of interventions.”
  - [section 4] Each principle is explicitly defined and linked back to the model’s components (e.g., Agency Preservation links to the human-centered perspective).
  - [section 5] The discussion states the principles “translate the model into actionable guidance.”
- **Break condition:** Principles may conflict (e.g., Temporal Appropriateness demands waiting, but high Motivation suggests immediate support). The model lacks a conflict-resolution hierarchy, forcing arbitrary or learned prioritization that risks inconsistent behavior.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Scene vs. Context Distinction
- **Why needed here:** This is the foundational lens. Without reliably parsing the objective *Scene* and generating a plausible *Contextual Interpretation*, all downstream reasoning about behavior and intervention is grounded in observable data alone, replicating the very failure the paper identifies.
- **Quick check:** Given a raw data stream (e.g., GPS, calendar, device usage), can you generate two different plausible Contextual Interpretations for the same Scene (e.g., “user at cafe at 3pm” → “working remotely” vs. “waiting for a friend”) that would lead to different intervention decisions?

### Concept: Human Behavior Factors as Likelihood Modifiers
- **Why needed here:** These factors (Attitude, Opportunity, Capability, Motivation, Trigger) are the dynamic levers that determine if/when a behavior occurs within a Scene/Context. The agent must map its available signals to these factors to judge *readiness* and calibrate intervention strength, not just detect a state.
- **Quick check:** For a target behavior (e.g., “user takes medication”), list at least two distinct proxy signals from a smartwatch or phone that could inform assessments of *Perceived Capability* (e.g., sleep data, step count) versus *Automatic Motivation* (e.g., routine adherence history).

### Concept: The Internal Behavior Generation Loop
- **Why needed here:** This loop (Scene → Context → Factors → Intention/Choice → Behavior) is the process model the agent is trying to *mirror in reverse*. The agent doesn’t execute behavior but must hypothesize where a user is in this loop to decide if and how to intervene. It explains *why* an intervention must be interpretive.
- **Quick check:** If an agent infers a user is in the “Motivation” stage but lacks a “Trigger” for a desired behavior, what intervention type does the model suggest (per the principles) and why? (Hint: consider Motivational Calibration and Temporal Appropriateness.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Perception & Scene Constructor -> Context Interpreter -> Behavior Readiness Assessor -> Intervention Policy Engine -> Agency & Feedback Module

**Critical path:** Raw Data -> Scene Constructor -> Context Interpreter -> Readiness Assessor -> Policy Engine -> Intervention. A failure at any point (e.g., poor Scene parsing) corrupts the entire chain.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Context Precision vs. Privacy:* High-fidelity Context Interpretation requires sensitive data (location, biometrics, social graphs). Tradeoff between intervention relevance and user privacy/acceptance.
- *Model Complexity vs. Interpretability:* A deep learning model for Readiness Assessment may be more accurate but is a “black box,” violating the paper’s human-centered “judgment” ethos. A rule-based system is transparent but brittle.
- *Intervention Aggressiveness vs. Annoyance:* High Motivational Calibration (strong intervention when readiness is high) risks violating Agency Preservation if the user’s *actual* intent differs from the inferred state. Requires very low-latency feedback handling.

**Failure signatures:**
- **Scene-Only Interventions:** Actions triggered solely by objective scene cues (e.g., “user is in car → suggest podcast”) without Context check.
- **Context Blindness:** Ignoring key contextual dimensions (e.g., suggesting a social activity when Social Context indicates user is in a “focus” or “mourning” state).
- **Timing Mismatch:** Interventions delivered during high cognitive load periods (inferred from Interoceptive/Activity contexts) despite Temporal Appropriateness principle.
- **Agency Violation:** An intervention that executes an action without an easy “undo” or “defer” option, or that repeatedly overrides explicit user choices.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Component Validation via Synthetic Scenes:** Create paired Scene-Context examples (e.g., “kitchen, 7am” → “morning routine” vs. “hosting breakfast party”). Test if the Context Interpreter outputs distinct, plausible interpretations. *Metric:* Human evaluator rating (1–5) of interpretation plausibility and differentiation.
2. **Policy Principle Stress Test:** Simulate a daily activity (e.g., “prepare for work”) and inject scenarios where principles conflict (e.g., high Motivation to leave but poor Temporal Appropriateness due to an ongoing meeting). Observe if the Policy Engine produces a coherent choice. *Metric:* Qualitative analysis of failure cases; count of principle violation instances.
3. **Agency Preservation in-the-Wild:** Deploy a prototype with a single intervention (e.g., “offer to reschedule a meeting when calendar looks full”). Vary the deferral mechanism (one-click vs. multi-step vs. voice command). Measure user deferral/override rate and correlate with user-reported sense of control. *Metric:* Deferral rate, SUS items on control, post-hoc interview on perceived agency.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can the conceptual model’s components (Scene, Context, Human Behavior Factors) be operationalized into measurable features from real-world sensor data and interaction logs to guide real-time intervention?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state the work is conceptual and “future research should examine how the model can be operationalized using sensor data and interaction traces” (Discussion).
- **Why unresolved:** The model provides a theoretical framework but no methodology for extracting its abstract constructs (e.g., subjective Context) from concrete data streams, hindering implementation.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Empirical studies defining and validating feature extraction pipelines (e.g., computer vision for Scene elements, NLP for contextual meaning, behavioral traces for Factors) across diverse domains.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** When should an agent prioritize Scene-based (objective, observable) cues over Contextual (subjective, user-assigned) meaning in intervention decisions, and how should conflicts between them be resolved?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The model explicitly separates Scene and Context but does not specify a decision heuristic for integrating them during intervention timing (“the model separates what is observable from what is meaningful”).
- **Why unresolved:** Without integration rules, agents may default to objective cues, risking misinterpretation (e.g., seeing “user resting” but missing “resting due to stress versus relaxation”).
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments manipulating Scene-Context alignments and measuring intervention appropriateness via user feedback to derive prioritization thresholds.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Do the five derived design principles (e.g., contextual sensitivity, agency preservation) measurably improve intervention outcomes compared to baseline or heuristic-driven agent behaviors?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The principles are proposed but the paper acknowledges its conceptual nature and lack of empirical validation (“This work is primarily conceptual and has not yet been empirically validated”).
- **Why unresolved:** The principles are theoretically grounded but their impact on reducing disruptive interventions or enhancing user trust is untested.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Between-subjects user studies comparing agents guided by the principles against control agents on metrics like intervention acceptance, user autonomy, and task success.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Can Human Behavior Factors (Attitude, Opportunity, Capability, Motivation, Triggers) be reliably inferred from passive sensor data (e.g., wearables, app usage) without explicit user input, and what are the accuracy limits?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The model treats these factors as determinants of behavioral likelihood but assumes they are accessible to the agent; the paper does not address inference feasibility.
- **Why unresolved:** These factors are internal cognitive/perceptual states. Passive data may misrepresent them (e.g., inferring low capability from slow movements when the user is simply cautious), leading to flawed interventions.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Correlation studies between passive sensor streams and ground-truth self-reports of each factor, establishing predictive models and their error bounds across populations.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** How should agentic AI balance temporal appropriateness (acting at the right moment) with agency preservation (deferring to user control) in high-stakes scenarios where delay could cause harm?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The principles of “temporal appropriateness” and “agency preservation” are presented as co-equal but the model offers no guidance for when they conflict (e.g., urgent health risk vs. user refusal).
- **Why unresolved:** Without conflict-resolution rules, designers must make ad hoc trade-offs, potentially compromising safety or autonomy inconsistently.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Mixed-methods research (e.g., Delphi studies, scenario-based ethics workshops) establishing context-specific override thresholds acceptable to users and stakeholders.

## Limitations
- **Operationalization gap:** The model provides no specification for detecting or inferring Scene elements, Context dimensions, or Human Behavior Factors from raw data (sensors, logs, dialogue), blocking implementation.
- **No evaluation framework:** Success metrics for principles like “contextual sensitivity” or “agency preservation” are undefined; no benchmark or quantitative assessment method exists.
- **Principle conflict resolution:** The five principles are treated as concurrent constraints but no hierarchy or arbitration mechanism is provided for inevitable conflicts (e.g., temporal appropriateness vs. motivational calibration).

## Confidence
- The logical separation of Scene and Context is a compelling foundation: **Medium** confidence (valuable conceptually but depends on unsolved inference challenges).
- The five design principles are sufficient and necessary heuristics for principled judgment: **Low** confidence (plausible but lack empirical grounding or validation).
- Human Behavior Factors can be reliably inferred from passive sensor data without explicit user input: **Low** confidence (highly speculative and prone to error due to internal state opacity).

Administer to human evaluators to rate plausibility and distinctness; success requires robust, meaningful variation from identical Scenes.
2. **Policy Engine Conflict Stress Test:** Simulate a domain (e.g., smart home) with scenarios forcing principle conflicts (e.g., high perceived motivation to adjust temperature during a sensitive meeting). Log all decisions; analyze whether trade-offs are coherent and defensibly aligned with the model’s human-centered ethos.
3. **Agency Preservation Field Deployment:** Deploy a prototype intervention (e.g., calendar rescheduling suggestion) with randomized deferral mechanisms (one-tap vs. multi-step voice). Measure deferral/override rates and correlate with standardized usability (SUS) and autonomy-specific survey items to validate the principle’s operational impact on perceived control.
