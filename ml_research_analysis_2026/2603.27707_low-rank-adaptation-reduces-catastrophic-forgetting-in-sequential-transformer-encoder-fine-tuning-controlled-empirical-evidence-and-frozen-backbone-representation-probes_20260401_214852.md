# Low-Rank Adaptation Reduces Catastrophic Forgetting in Sequential Transformer Encoder Fine-Tuning: Controlled Empirical Evidence and Frozen-Backbone Representation Probes

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.27707  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27707  
- **Reference count:** 37  
- **Primary result:** LoRA reduces catastrophic forgetting by ~97% via strong backbone freezing, preserving a stable shared feature scaffold.

## Executive Summary
Sequential fine‑tuning of large pretrained language models suffers from catastrophic forgetting: performance on earlier tasks degrades after training on later ones. This paper investigates whether Low‑Rank Adaptation (LoRA)—a parameter‑efficient method that freezes the pretrained backbone and learns low‑rank updates—can mitigate forgetting. Using a controlled protocol on BERT‑base with four GLUE tasks, the authors compare full fine‑tuning (all parameters trainable) against standard LoRA (rank 8 on attention query/value projections). Over five fully validated random seeds, LoRA reduces average forgetting from 19.9% to 0.6% (97.2% relative reduction), with high statistical significance (paired t‑test p=0.002, Cohen’s dz=3.12). A replication with RoBERTa‑base confirms the pattern. Fine‑grained freezing ablations reveal a sharp forgetting drop once >95% of backbone parameters are frozen. Representation probes in GPT‑2 and RoBERTa show that frozen backbones preserve much higher inter‑task similarity, especially in the final transformer layer. The evidence supports that LoRA’s robustness stems primarily from strong backbone freezing, which maintains a stable shared feature extractor.

## Method Summary
The evaluation uses a sequential fine‑tuning regime on the GLUE benchmark (RTE → MRPC → CoLA → SST‑2). Starting from a pretrained encoder (BERT‑base or RoBERTa‑base), a fresh linear classification head is initialized for each task. For full fine‑tuning, all parameters (backbone + head) are updated; for LoRA, the backbone is frozen and low‑rank adapters (rank 8 on query/value projections) are trained alongside the head. After each task, the model is evaluated on all previously seen tasks; forgetting for a prior task is defined as the drop in its task‑specific score (accuracy or MCC multiplied by 100) from immediately after its own training to the end of the sequence. The main comparison runs five independent seeds (45–49) with full validation to ensure reliable statistics. Additional experiments systematically vary the proportion of frozen parameters and include cross‑model representation probes to analyze inter‑task similarity across transformer layers.

## Key Results
- LoRA reduces average forgetting from 19.9% ± 4.8% (full FT) to 0.6% ± 1.4% (97.2% relative reduction) on a four‑task GLUE sequence.
- The reduction is highly significant (paired t‑test p=0.002, Cohen’s dz=3.12) and replicates across BERT‑base and RoBERTa‑base.
- Freezing >95% of backbone parameters yields a dramatic forgetting drop; models with ~86–93% frozen suffer forgetting comparable to full fine‑tuning.
- Frozen‑backbone regimes preserve substantially higher inter‑task cosine similarity of [CLS] activations; full fine‑tuning shows the largest divergence at the final transformer layer.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Strong backbone freezing as an architectural bottleneck
Freezing >99% of backbone parameters restricts the optimizer’s degrees of freedom, preventing overwriting of weights critical to earlier tasks. Adaptation is forced into the small low‑rank LoRA subspace, preserving the stability of the shared pretrained feature extractor. Evidence: the freezing ablation shows a sharp forgetting reduction once frozen parameters exceed ~95%; the threshold aligns with LoRA’s near‑complete backbone freezing. Break condition: forgetting rises sharply when frozen % drops below ~95%.

### Mechanism 2: Preservation of a task‑invariant representation space
A frozen backbone provides a consistent feature scaffold; subsequent tasks adapt via a tiny additive pathway rather than reshaping the encoder. This limits representational drift in shared layers. Evidence: companion probes (GPT‑2, RoBERTa) show frozen‑backbone regimes maintain much higher cosine similarity between task activation centroids, with the largest gap in the final layer for full fine‑tuning. Break condition: task‑similarity degrades monotonically with greater unfreezing, collapsing under full fine‑tuning.

### Mechanism 3: Forgetting reduction is an emergent property of freezing + low‑rank, not low‑rank alone
Classifier‑only and shallow‑adapter baselines (both with >99% frozen) achieve forgetting rates similar to standard LoRA, despite having similarly small trainable subspaces. This indicates that the combination of a tiny trainable subspace with a strongly frozen backbone is key; generic low‑rank parameterization without strong freezing (e.g., partially unfrozen) fails to reduce forgetting. Evidence: freezing ablation shows classifier‑only (99.5% frozen) and LoRA‑on‑layer‑11‑only (100% frozen) achieve forgetting ~3%, while partially unfrozen models with comparable or smaller trainable counts (e.g., 7.7M at 93% frozen) suffer ~13% forgetting.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Pretrained backbone (frozen) → LoRA adapters (trainable) on attention Q/V → Encoder output → [CLS] token → Task‑specific classifier head (trainable) → Predictions.

**Critical path**:  
1. Load pretrained model and freeze all backbone parameters.  
2. For each task in sequence (RTE → MRPC → CoLA → SST‑2): initialize a fresh linear head; train head and LoRA adapters (or all parameters for full FT) on that task for three epochs.  
3. After training, evaluate the current model on all prior tasks; record the peak score for the current task immediately after its training.  
4. Proceed to the next task without resetting the model (carry over adapted weights and heads).  
5. After the final task, compute forgetting for each prior task as (peak – final) score; average across prior tasks.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- **Rank vs. capacity/forgetting**: Higher LoRA rank improves single‑task performance but increases trainable parameters; forgetting reduction appears tied to >95% frozen backbone, so rank increases may be acceptable if the freezing threshold holds.
- **Adapter location vs. accuracy/forgetting**: Injecting LoRA into all linear layers (vs. only Q/V) yields higher screening accuracy but more trainable parameters; forgetting reduction still depends on overall frozen percentage.
- **Accuracy‑forgetting trade‑off**: Full fine‑tuning yields higher final average accuracy (64.5% vs. 59.5% on BERT) but with severe forgetting; LoRA inverts this trade‑off.

**Failure signatures**:
- Forgetting spikes when frozen percentage falls below ~95%.
- Heterogeneous forgetting across tasks (e.g., MRPC may be almost completely forgotten); always inspect task‑level forgetting matrices.
- In representation probes, full fine‑tuning shows a sharp drop in inter‑task cosine similarity at the final transformer layer.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Replicate confirmatory BERT comparison**: Run seeds 45‑49 with full validation for full FT and LoRA (r=8, Q/V). Verify average forgetting ~20% vs. ~0% and statistical significance (p=0.002, dz=3.12).
2. **Fine‑grained freezing ablation**: Systematically vary which layers are frozen (e.g., train only last layer, last two layers, etc.) and plot average forgetting vs. % frozen parameters. Expect a sharp drop around 95% freezing; include classifier‑only and shallow‑adapter points.
3. **Companion task‑similarity probe on same architecture**: Though the paper used GPT‑2/RoBERTa for probes, repeat the representation analysis on the BERT‑base model from Experiment 1: extract [CLS] activations for each task, compute pairwise cosine similarity centroids, and compare layer‑wise trends between full FT and LoRA.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Does LoRA’s forgetting reduction generalize to decoder‑only transformers and generative tasks, and to longer task sequences (≥10 tasks)? The current evidence is limited to encoder‑only BERT/RoBERTa over four GLUE tasks.
2. How do LoRA hyperparameters (rank, target modules, variants like AdaLoRA/DoRA) influence the trade‑off between forgetting reduction and final accuracy in sequential fine‑tuning?
3. How does standard LoRA compare to modern continual learning methods (e.g., O‑LoRA, InfLoRA, replay‑augmented PEFT) under a matched‑seed protocol? The paper only compares against EWC and full fine‑tuning.
4. Do the representation‑level findings (frozen backbone preserves task similarity) hold when using the same BERT‑base architecture as the main forgetting experiments? The companion probes used GPT‑2 and RoBERTa, creating an architecture mismatch.

## Limitations
- Evaluation restricted to four GLUE tasks on BERT‑base/RoBERTa‑base; unknown for longer sequences, other domains, or newer architectures (DeBERTa, T5).
- Representation probes were conducted on different models (GPT‑2, RoBERTa) than the BERT used in main experiments, weakening direct mechanistic linkage.
- The critical ~95% freezing threshold derives from a limited ablation; its generalizability across ranks, adapter scopes, and task orders is uncertain.
- Seed count (n=5) is modest for robust statistical inference despite the large effect size.

3. **Cross‑architecture test**: Apply the same four‑task sequence to DeBERTa‑v3 and T5‑base. Compare forgetting of full FT vs. LoRA to assess generalizability beyond BERT/RoBERTa encoders.
