# Marginals Before Conditionals

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.10074  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10074  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Neural networks exhibit a directional asymmetry: they first learn the marginal distribution \(P(A|B)\) and later the conditional \(P(A|B,z)\), producing a plateau at loss = \(\log K\) and a sharp collective transition.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates how neural networks learn conditional relationships, using a minimal synthetic task where a base string \(B\) maps ambiguously to \(K\) possible targets \(A\) and a selector token \(z\) disambiguates. Training a transformer reveals a consistent two-phase dynamics: the model first converges to a marginal solution that ignores \(z\), yielding a loss plateau exactly at \(\log K\) nats; after a waiting time \(\tau\) it undergoes a sharp collective transition to the full conditional solution, with loss dropping to near zero. Key findings show that the plateau height is independent of \(K\), the duration \(\tau\) scales super‑linearly with dataset size \(D\) (\(\tau \propto D^{1.19}\)), gradient noise (from learning rate or batch size) stabilizes the marginal saddle and prolongs \(\tau\), the transition is synchronous across all ambiguous groups, and a specific attention head (L0H3) activates early and drives the change.

## Method Summary
The authors construct a “wind tunnel” task: each unique base string \(B\) (6 characters) is associated with \(K\) distinct target strings \(A\) (4 characters). A selector token \(z\) (2 characters) indexes the correct \(A\) for a given \((B, z)\) pair. The dataset size is \(D = n_b \times K\), where \(n_b\) is the number of base strings. Input sequences are \([BOS], B, [SEP], z, [SEP]\); the model must generate \(A\) autoregressively. A 4‑layer transformer (embedding dimension 128, 4 attention heads, MLP dimension 512) is trained with AdamW (batch size 128, learning rate \(10^{-3}\), cosine warm‑up 500 steps). Training loss \(L(t)\) is monitored. The plateau height is expected to be \(\log K\); the waiting time \(\tau\) is defined as the first step where \(L(t) < 0.5 \log K\) for three consecutive evaluations. To detect whether the model uses \(z\), the *z‑shuffle gap* \(\Delta_z = L_{\text{z‑shuffle}} - L_{\text{clean}}\) is computed: shuffling \(z\) tokens within a batch destroys the conditional mapping while preserving marginal statistics.

## Key Results
- The plateau loss equals \(\log K\) with high precision (mean ratio \(1.01 \pm 0.02\) across \(K = 3 \dots 36\)).
- The waiting time \(\tau\) scales super‑linearly with total dataset size \(D\): \(\tau \propto D^{1.19}\) (\(R^2 = 0.98\)), and is independent of the ambiguity \(K\).
- Gradient noise stabilizes the marginal solution: increasing learning rate \(7\times\) prolongs \(\tau\) by \(3.6\times\) in tokens; decreasing batch size (after throughput normalization) delays escape by \(1.8\times\).
- The collective transition is extremely sharp: at \(\tau/2\) none of 200 sampled \(B\)-groups achieve \(>80\%\) accuracy, while by \(1.5\tau\) \(99\%\) have snapped.
- A specific attention head (L0H3 in the first layer) begins to route \(z\) information around halfway through \(\tau\); ablating this head at the mid‑transition increases loss by 1.72 nats, confirming its leading role.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Marginal Plateau at \(\log K\)
The task is a surjective map: each base \(B\) has exactly \(K\) candidate targets \(A\). Without using the selector \(z\), the optimal strategy is to predict uniformly over those \(K\) candidates, giving cross‑entropy loss \(\log K\) nats. The model quickly converges to this marginal solution because gradient contributions from different \((B, A)\) pairs cancel when predictions are uniform. The resulting plateau is a high‑anisotropy saddle where the gradient norm is very low; the dominant curvature is \(500–1000\times\) larger than the escape direction. The plateau ends only when the model develops a mechanism to incorporate \(z\) and break the symmetry.

### Mechanism 2: Collective Transition via a Routing Head
During the plateau, the model ignores \(z\) (\(\Delta_z \approx 0\)). A specific attention head (L0H3) gradually specializes to extract and route information from \(z\) to the correct candidate within each \(B\)-group. Once this head’s output becomes predictive, the change propagates through the network, causing nearly all ambiguous groups to snap to the correct conditional mapping within a short window (\(\approx 0.5\tau\)). This collective behavior is evidenced by the z‑shuffle gap \(\Delta_z\) rising about \(0.52\tau\) before the loss drop, and by per‑group accuracy: \(0\%\) of 200 sampled groups exceed \(80\%\) at \(\tau/2\), while \(99\%\) have snapped by \(1.5\tau\). Ablating L0H3 at mid‑transition increases loss by 1.72 nats, confirming its leading role.

### Mechanism 3: Gradient Noise Stabilizes the Marginal Saddle
The marginal solution sits on an extremely anisotropic saddle (\(|\lambda_{\text{max}}/\lambda_{\text{min}}| \approx 10^4–10^5\)). Stochastic gradient noise does not project uniformly; theory suggests the noise covariance aligns with high‑curvature eigenvectors, predominantly exciting directions that are *not* the shallow escape direction. This acts as an “entropic force” that restores the model to the marginal saddle. Empirically, increasing noise (via higher learning rate or smaller batch size) monotonically increases \(\tau\): a \(7\times\) learning‑rate increase leads to a \(3.6\times\) longer plateau in tokens; smaller batches (after throughput normalization) delay escape by \(1.8\times\). At escape, weight‑update coherence (cosine similarity) jumps from \(\sim 0.04\) to \(\sim 0.8\), indicating alignment with the shallow direction that overcomes the stabilization.

## Foundational Learning
- **Conditional entropy and information decomposition**  
  The task relies on the exact decomposition: \(H(A|B) = \log K\) (marginal) and \(H(A|B,z) = 0\) (conditional). Without \(z\), the optimal prediction is uniform over the \(K\) candidates, yielding loss \(\log K\).  
  *Quick check:* If each \(B\) maps uniformly to \(K=4\) possible \(A\), what is the optimal cross‑entropy loss given only \(B\)?  
- **High‑anisotropy saddles in optimization landscapes**  
  The plateau is a saddle with extreme eigenvalue ratio (\(|\lambda_{\text{max}}/\lambda_{\text{min}}| \sim 10^4\)). This explains the very low gradient norm (slow escape) and why noise projects onto the wrong (high‑curvature) directions.  
  *Quick check:* What does an eigenvalue ratio of 1000:1 imply about the loss surface shape near the marginal solution?  
- **Stochastic gradient noise and entropic forces**  
  The counterintuitive result that more noise *slows* escape challenges the usual view of noise as a barrier‑crossing aid. Here, noise alignment with high curvature stabilizes the saddle.  
  *Quick check:* Why would increasing the learning rate (which increases gradient noise) lead to a longer plateau if noise usually helps escape saddles?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Input: `[BOS]` + base string \(B\) (6 chars) + `[SEP]` + selector \(z\) (2 chars) + `[SEP]` → token embeddings + positional encodings → transformer encoder (4 layers, 4 attention heads per layer, embedding dimension 128, MLP dimension 512) → final token residual stream → linear output head → autoregressive prediction of target \(A\) (4 chars). The critical component is **layer‑0 head 3 (L0H3)**, which develops a routing function from \(z\) to the correct \(A\) within each \(B\)-group.

### Critical path
1. During initial training, \(B\) tokens are processed in early layers while \(z\) is largely ignored; the model quickly learns the marginal distribution \(P(A|B)\).
2. While the loss plateaus near \(\log K\), L0H3 gradually tunes its attention to extract information from \(z\) that correlates with the correct \(A\) within each \(B\)-group.
3. Once L0H3’s output becomes predictive, it feeds into subsequent layers, triggering a cascade where the entire network starts using \(z\) correctly.
4. The collective snap occurs: within roughly \(0.5\tau\), almost all \(B\)-groups transition from uniform predictions to correct delta distributions, and loss drops sharply.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Learning rate:** Higher \(\eta\) increases gradient noise and step size; the noise effect dominates, slowing escape (up to \(3.6\times\) longer in tokens). Excessively high \(\eta\) (e.g., \(5\times10^{-3}\)) may prevent convergence.
- **Batch size:** Smaller batches increase per‑step noise; after normalizing for throughput (tokens processed), they still delay escape by \(\sim1.8\times\).
- **Model capacity:** The 600K‑parameter transformer is sufficient; linear baselines fail to escape the plateau, confirming the need for nonlinear \(B\)-\(z\) interaction.

### Failure signatures
- **Stuck at \(\log K\) plateau indefinitely:** Indicates failure to develop selector routing (e.g., linear model, insufficient capacity, or inadequate training).
- **No \(\Delta_z\) onset:** The z‑shuffle gap never becomes positive, meaning \(z\) never influences predictions.
- **Incremental group solving:** If some \(B\)-groups solve early while others remain unsolved at \(\tau\), it contradicts the collective transition; the paper observed \(0\%\) groups \(\ge 80\%\) accuracy at \(\tau/2\).

### First 3 experiments
1. **Dataset size sweep:** Vary \(D = n_b \times K\) at fixed \(K\) (or vary \(K\) at fixed \(n_b\)) to confirm \(\tau \propto D^{1.19}\). Plot \(\tau\) vs. \(D\) on log‑log axes and check \(R^2\).
2. **Noise manipulation:** At fixed \(K, D\), sweep batch size (e.g., 32, 64, 128, 256, 512) and learning rate (e.g., \(3\!\times\!10^{-4}\), \(1\!\times\!10^{-3}\), \(2\!\times\!10^{-3}\)). Measure \(\tau\) in *tokens processed* to decouple throughput effects; expect monotonic increase in \(\tau\) with noise.
3. **Head ablation:** At a mid‑transition checkpoint (around \(\tau/2\)), zero‑ablate each attention head (16 total) and measure the resulting loss increase. Expect L0H3 to show the largest impact, confirming its leading role. Use a library like TransformerLens for easy activation access.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Why does \(\tau\) scale super‑linearly with dataset size \(D\) (\(\tau \propto D^{1.2}\)) rather than linearly?** The exponent is fit over a single decade of \(D\) with limited fixed‑\(D\) controls; no theory yet explains this scaling.
2. **Can the entropic stabilization of the marginal solution be formalized?** The evidence is correlational (noise manipulations delay escape). A rigorous link between SGD noise covariance, Hessian anisotropy, and escape dynamics is missing.
3. **Does the “marginals before conditionals” staging generalize to natural‑structure tasks?** The synthetic task has exact entropy bounds and a surjective map; natural tasks may involve distributed representations and lack clean marginals. Applying identical diagnostics (z‑shuffle gap, plateau at \(\log K\), collective transition) to LLMs trained on controlled ambiguous fact triples could test this.

## Limitations
- **D‑scaling exponent:** Primarily based on a single seed; multi‑seed variability is high (coefficient of variation 22–71%), weakening confidence in the precise power law.
- **Hessian anisotropy estimates:** The reported extreme ratios (\(10^4–10^5\)) rely on power iteration with unspecified convergence; implementation details could affect robustness.
- **Generalizability:** The wind‑tunnel task is highly simplified; real‑world conditional learning may not exhibit the same clear staging, and the identified router head may not generalize across architectures or seeds.

## Confidence
- **Plateau height = \(\log K\):** High (consistent across \(K = 3–36\), mean ratio \(1.01 \pm 0.02\)).
- **Collective transition (all groups snap simultaneously):** Medium (based on 200‑group sampling; finer‑grained validation needed).
- **L0H3 as the routing driver:** Medium (ablation supports, but single‑seed; requires cross‑seed validation).
- **Gradient noise stabilizes the marginal saddle:** Medium (LR/batch sweeps consistent, but confounded by step‑size effects; direct noise‑covariance measurements lacking).
- **\(\tau \propto D^{1.19}\):** Low (single‑seed, high multi‑seed variability; exponent may be overfitted).

## Next Checks
1. **Multi‑seed scaling replication:** Train ≥5 random seeds for the \(D\)-sweep (fixed \(K\)) to obtain a confidence interval for the exponent \(\alpha\). Also track which head emerges as the primary router across seeds.
2. **Hessian and noise alignment verification:** At the marginal solution, compute Hessian eigenvalues via two independent methods (e.g., Lanczos, finite differences) to confirm extreme anisotropy. Estimate minibatch gradient covariance and test whether noise projects predominantly onto high‑curvature eigenvectors.
3. **Fine‑grained transition dynamics:** Sample loss and per‑group accuracy every ~50 steps around \(\tau\) to measure the true duration of the collective snap. Introduce dataset variants with heterogeneous group difficulties (e.g., some \(B\) groups have \(z\) strongly correlated with the correct \(A\) even marginally) to test whether the transition remains collective.
