# Locally Private Parametric Methods for Change-Point Detection
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.13619
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13619
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Locally private parametric change-point detection attains an error exponent attenuated by a factor tanh²(ε/2) relative to the non-private case; binary mechanism excels at high privacy, randomized response at low privacy.

## Executive Summary
We consider parametric change-point detection in time series under local differential privacy (LDP). Each data owner privatizes observations locally before sending them to a central analyst. The authors propose two LDP mechanisms—randomized response (RR) and binary mechanism (BM)—and analyze them using strong data processing inequalities (SDPI) for Jeffreys-Rényi divergences. Even in the (non-private) standard setting, the paper delivers improved finite-sample bounds for the generalized likelihood ratio (GLR) test via martingale arguments. Under LDP, the SDPI-based analysis yields explicit finite-sample error bounds and reveals that the worst-case SDPI coefficient is attained by binary input distributions, which greatly simplifies calculations. The privacy cost is quantified by an attenuation factor of tanh²(ε/2) in the error exponent compared to the non-private optimum. Empirical results show that BM outperforms RR at high privacy (small ε), while RR is superior when ε is large (low privacy).

## Method Summary
The core contribution is two LDP mechanisms for change-point detection: randomized response (RR) and binary mechanism (BM). Each local agent applies one of these mechanisms to its observation X_t, producing a privatized value Y_t that is sent to a central server. The server computes the generalized likelihood ratio (GLR) statistic across all candidate change-point locations and declares a change when the statistic exceeds a threshold. Theoretical analysis builds on strong data processing inequalities (SDPI) tailored to Jeffreys-Rényi divergences. The SDPI coefficients, which govern the contraction of divergence under the privatization channel, are shown to be maximized by binary input distributions; this binary optimality streamlines the derivation of finite-sample error bounds. Moreover, the classical non-private GLR test is refined using martingale techniques to obtain sharper finite-sample guarantees. For the LDP case, the error exponent—governing asymptotic error decay—is attenuated by a factor tanh²(ε/2) relative to the non-private benchmark, precisely capturing the privacy-accuracy trade-off.

## Key Results
- Tight finite-sample error bounds for the GLR test; the non-private bounds are improved via martingale techniques, and under LDP the SDPI-based analysis shows that binary input distributions are worst-case for Jeffreys-Rényi divergences.
- The LDP error exponent is attenuated by a factor of tanh²(ε/2) relative to the non-private optimum.
- Binary mechanism (BM) yields better detection accuracy for small ε (high privacy), while randomized response (RR) is preferable for larger ε (low privacy).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core of the analysis is the strong data processing inequality (SDPI) for Jeffreys-Rényi divergences, which contracts the divergence between the distribution of the data before and after privatization. Because the divergence controls the error exponent in hypothesis testing, applying SDPI yields a direct trade-off between the privacy budget ε and the detection performance. The factor tanh²(ε/2) emerges from the specific contraction incurred by the RR and BM channels when measured in the chosen divergence. Moreover, the authors show that for Jeffreys-Rényi divergences the worst-case contraction over input distributions is achieved by binary inputs; this binary optimality reduces the analysis to a one-dimensional optimization and extends the results to any parametric family whose sufficient statistic is binary or can be reduced to binary without loss of power. The GLR test, which is known to be asymptotically optimal for change-point detection, inherits these bounds and remains feasible under LDP because the privatization mechanisms preserve the sufficiency structure needed for likelihood ratio computation.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Raw Observations (X_t) -> Local Privatizer (RR or BM, ε) -> Noisy Observations (Y_t) -> Central Accumulator -> GLR Statistic Computation -> Change‑Point Decision

**Critical path**: 1. Each data owner locally applies the chosen privatization mechanism to their observation. 2. The perturbed data are transmitted to a central server. 3. The server computes the GLR statistic for all possible change‑point locations, assuming a pre‑change and post‑change parametric model. 4. The statistic is compared to a threshold derived from the desired false positive rate (or via a stopping rule). 5. The location with the largest GLR value above threshold is reported as a change‑point.

**Design tradeoffs**: 
- RR vs BM: BM offers better accuracy for small ε at the cost of slightly higher implementation complexity; RR is simpler but degrades faster as ε increases. 
- Finite‑sample vs asymptotic: The SDPI‑based bounds hold for finite samples but may be conservative; asymptotic regimes yield simpler expressions (error exponent). 
- Privacy budget ε: Smaller ε strengthens privacy but increases detection delay and missed‑detection rates, captured by tanh²(ε/2).

**Failure signatures**: 
- High false negative rate when ε is too small or when the change magnitude is small. 
- Elevated false positive rate if the threshold is not properly calibrated to the privacy‑distorted distribution. 
- Complete failure when the assumed parametric family is grossly miss‑matched; the SDPI bounds no longer reflect actual error. 
- Excessively long detection delays in streaming settings due to accumulated noise.

**Three first experiments**: 
1. Replicate the synthetic Gaussian shift experiment: compare detection power of RR and BM across ε ∈ [0.1, 2.0] and verify the crossover point. 
2. Validate the tanh²(ε/2) attenuation by estimating error exponents (slope of log‑type I vs type II error curves) and checking alignment with theory. 
3. Stress‑test robustness using a non‑Gaussian exponential family (e.g., Gamma distribution) to assess the sensitivity of the results to model misspecification.

## Limitations
- The optimality of binary input distributions for SDPI coefficients may limit the applicability to non‑binary or multi‑dimensional settings.
- The tanh²(ε/2) attenuation factor is derived under specific modeling assumptions; its robustness to alternative parametric families (e.g., non‑zero means, different exponential families) is uncertain.
- Empirical validation is based on limited synthetic scenarios; real‑world time series with noise, drift, or misspecification might shift the performance crossover between BM and RR.

## Confidence
- Finite‑sample bounds via SDPI analysis: **Medium**
- Binary optimality of SDPI coefficients: **Medium**
- Privacy cost quantified by tanh²(ε/2): **Medium‑High**
- BM outperforms RR at small ε, RR at large ε: **Low‑Medium**
