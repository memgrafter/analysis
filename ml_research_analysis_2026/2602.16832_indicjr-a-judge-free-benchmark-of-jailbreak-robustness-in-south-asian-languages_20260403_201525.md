# IndicJR: A Judge-Free Benchmark of Jailbreak Robustness in South Asian Languages

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.16832
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16832
- **Reference count:** 19
- **Primary result:** Contract-bound evaluation inflates refusals without reducing jailbreaks; JSON-track JSR exceeds 0.92 for some models, FREE-track JSR approaches 1.0; strong English-to-Indic adversarial transfer; romanization reduces JSON JSR by ~0.34 on average.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces Indic Jailbreak Robustness (IJR), a judge‑free benchmark for evaluating jailbreak robustness across 12 South Asian languages with 45,216 single‑turn prompts. It compares contract‑bound (JSON) and unconstrained (FREE) interaction tracks, revealing a “contract gap”: structured refusal prompts increase apparent safety through over‑refusal on benign inputs but fail to prevent harmful content generation. Key findings include strong cross‑lingual transfer of English adversarial prompts (especially format‑based attacks), orthography effects where romanization lowers success rates in the JSON track, and high reliability of the deterministic scoring method via human audit. These results underscore vulnerabilities hidden by English‑only, contract‑focused assessments and advocate for more robust multilingual safety evaluation.

## Method Summary
IJR is a comprehensive, judge‑free benchmark comprising 45,216 prompts in 12 South Asian languages (bn, gu, hi, kn, ml, mr, ne, or, pa, ta, te, ur). Prompts are split into a JSON track (requiring a structured refusal schema: REFUSE/COMPLY/ABSTAIN + justification) and a FREE track (raw text). Adversarial prompts are created by applying wrappers (instruction overrides, role‑play, etc.) to benign or harmful cores, with orthography variants (native, romanized, mixed) generated via standardized transliteration. Evaluation uses deterministic, language‑aware heuristics: JSON outputs are parsed for schema validity and then checked with a multilingual leakage detector; FREE outputs are scanned for refusal phrases. Metrics include Jailbreak Success Rate (JSR), Schema Validity (SV), Leakage Rate (LR), over/under‑refusal, abstain rate, and Refusal Robustness Index (RRI). Inference is fixed (max_tokens=256, temperature=0.3, top_p=0.9, seed=13). The study covers multiple open‑weight and API models, correlates orthographic changes with tokenization metrics, and validates scoring via human audits (κ≈0.68–0.74).

## Key Results
- Contracts inflate over‑refusal on benign prompts (0.5–0.7) but do not reduce jailbreak success: JSON‑track JSR >0.92 for LLaMA and Sarvam; FREE‑track JSR ≈1.0 for all models.
- English‑to‑Indic adversarial transfer is strong, with format‑based attacks achieving mean JSR 0.68–0.77, outperforming instruction‑based attacks across all 12 languages.
- Romanization lowers JSON‑track JSR by an average of 0.34, with moderate negative correlation to bytes/char ratio (ρ≈−0.29 to −0.32) and positive correlation to romanization share (ρ≈0.28–0.32).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Contract‑Bound Evaluation Inflates Refusals but Does Not Prevent Jailbreaks**  
- **Claim:** JSON‑track prompts cause models to over‑refuse on benign inputs due to schema pressure, yet they remain vulnerable to adversarial prompts that achieve high JSR.  
- **Mechanism:** Models are incentivized (via fine‑tuning or prompting) to produce structured refusal outputs satisfying a machine‑checkable schema. This leads to conservative output (e.g., `ABSTAIN`) when uncertain, but the underlying safety capability remains weak; adversarial prompts that bypass or overload the contract still elicit compliance.  
- **Evidence:** High over‑refusal on clean‑benign in JSON (0.5–0.7) vs near‑zero in FREE; both tracks show high JSR on attacked‑benign (>0.92 JSON, ≈1.0 FREE).

**Mechanism 2: Cross‑lingual Transfer of English Adversarial Prompts**  
- **Claim:** Adversarial wrappers in English effectively transfer to South Asian language cores, indicating shared vulnerabilities.  
- **Mechanism:** Adversarial cues (e.g., “ignore previous instructions”) are language‑agnostic and leverage model representations that override the semantic content of the Indic core, leading to compliance even when the core language differs.  
- **Evidence:** Format attacks yield transfer JSR means 0.68–0.77 versus instruction attacks 0.46–0.61 across all Indic languages; some models show >0.96 JSR on transferred prompts.

**Mechanism 3: Orthographic Stress via Romanization**  
- **Claim:** Romanized or mixed‑script inputs reduce JSR in the JSON track, primarily due to tokenization fragmentation.  
- **Mechanism:** Romanization increases subword token count, making coherent schema adherence harder; this leads to more `ABSTAIN` outputs (not counted as jailbreaks) or malformed refusals, lowering JSR.  
- **Evidence:** Across models/languages, JSON JSR drops from 0.755 (native) to 0.416 (romanized); negative correlations between JSR reduction and bytes/char (ρ≈−0.29 to −0.32) and positive correlation with romanization share (ρ≈0.28–0.32).

## Foundational Learning
- **Multilingual Tokenization Fragmentation**: *Why needed:* Interpreting orthography effects requires understanding how subword tokenizers (BPE, SentencePiece) split text in different scripts; romanization creates out‑of‑vocabulary sequences, increasing token count. *Quick check:* If a Hindi sentence is romanized, what happens to its token count and how might that impact a model’s ability to follow a structured JSON output instruction?
- **Judge‑Free Evaluation Design**: *Why needed:* The benchmark relies on deterministic rules instead of LLM judges; this choice impacts what is measured and what is missed. *Quick check:* What are the main failure modes of a keyword/pattern‑based “leakage detector” compared to a human or LLM judge?
- **Contract‑Gap Phenomenon**: *Why needed:* Recognizing the gap between contract adherence and genuine safety is essential to avoid false conclusions from contract‑bound evaluations. *Quick check:* Why does a high “Schema Validity” score in the JSON track not guarantee the model is safe?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
1) Prompt Generation Pipeline – takes benign/harmful cores, applies adversarial wrappers, generates orthography variants (native, romanized, mixed), formats for JSON/FREE.  
2) Scoring Engine – for JSON: parses output, validates schema, extracts `decision` field; also runs multilingual leakage detector on `justification`. For FREE: applies language‑aware refusal phrase detector on raw text.  
3) Metrics Aggregator – computes JSR, SV, LR, over/under‑refusal, abstain rate, RRI per language/model/subset; handles cross‑lingual transfer slicing.  
4) Validation & Audit Module – manages human audit sampling, calculates agreement (κ), and performs error analysis.

**Critical path:** Prompt Generation → Model Inference (fixed params) → JSON Parsing & Scoring → FREE Text Scoring → Metrics Aggregation → Statistical Analysis (correlations, slices). Any parsing or detector failure can break downstream conclusions.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Judge‑free vs semantic accuracy: Heuristics scale and are reproducible but may miss nuanced leakage or misclassify hedged refusals.  
- Contract‑bound vs naturalistic: JSON tests schema compliance under pressure; FREE tests raw safety; reporting both reveals the contract gap.  
- Coverage vs depth: 45k prompts across 12 languages is broad, but limited to 3 harm categories and single‑turn; depth per language/scenario is sacrificed.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High Abstain Rate (>0.7) in JSON: indicates model avoids schema commitment, possibly due to confusion; metric inflation risk.  
- Low Schema Validity (<0.8): many outputs malformed; JSR may be unreliable due to ABSTAIN defaults.  
- Leakage Rate >0.05 in clean‑harmful (CH): detector under‑sensitive or model catastrophically unsafe.  
- Lite↔Full correlation (E7) <0.7: evaluation size too small for that model/language; conclusions unstable.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. E1 (Contract Gap): Run JSON and FREE tracks on a subset; expect high JSON JSR + high CB over‑refusal; FREE JSR≈1.0 + near‑zero over‑refusal. Confirms contract gap.  
2. E2 (Cross‑lingual Transfer): Use English‑wrapped prompts on Indic cores; expect high JSR (>0.6) for format attacks; checks language family similarity vulnerability.  
3. E3 (Orthography Stress): Compare native vs. romanized JSR in JSON track; expect significant JSR reduction (negative ∆JSR) with romanization, and correlation to romanization share and token fragmentation metrics.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:**  
*Question:* To what extent do high JSON JSR values reflect genuine safety vulnerability vs. artifact of contract parsing failure in Indic orthographies?  
*Basis:* High JSON JSR but low schema validity for some models; contract gap suggests refusals are contract‑driven.  
*Unresolved:* Disentangling semantic compliance from syntactic failure requires fine‑grained error analysis of JSON outputs.  
*Resolution evidence:* Categorize JSON failures (COMPLY with harm, ABSTAIN due to malformed schema) and correlate with orthographic features and model architecture.

**Open Question 2:**  
*Question:* How do authentic, noisy code‑switching patterns affect jailbreak robustness compared to controlled native/romanized variants?  
*Basis:* IJR uses standardized transliteration; real‑world romanization is noisy.  
*Unresolved:* Unclear if chaotic code‑switching amplifies fragmentation effect or introduces new failure modes.  
*Resolution evidence:* Evaluate models on naturalistic code‑switched data from social media; compare JSR patterns and token fragmentation correlations.

**Open Question 3:**  
*Question:* What mechanisms link tokenization fragmentation to orthography‑dependent JSR variations, and can this inform robust tokenizers?  
*Basis:* Paper reports correlations (ρ≈0.28–0.32) but no causal analysis.  
*Unresolved:* Causal pathway unclear—does fragmentation impair adversarial intent representation or affect safety classifier?  
*Resolution evidence:* Mechanistic analyses: compare embeddings of native/romanized prompts; fine‑tune with romanized adversarial examples; test alternative tokenizers (byte‑level).

**Open Question 4:**  
*Question:* To what degree do English→Indic transfer results imply shared language‑agnostic vulnerabilities, and can including Indic adversarial examples mitigate?  
*Basis:* Strong transfer observed; suggests safety fine‑tuning may be English‑centric.  
*Unresolved:* Not known whether vulnerability stems from model representations, training data, or objective inductive bias.  
*Resolution evidence:* Analyze safety training corpora for non‑English examples; conduct intervention fine‑tuning with Indic adversarial prompts; test correlation with pretraining coverage.

**Open Question 5:**  
*Question:* How do safety behaviors generalize to multi‑turn dialogue jailbreak scenarios?  
*Basis:* IJR is single‑turn only; multi‑turn interactions common in real‑world.  
*Unresolved:* Unclear if contract gap and orthography effects persist or worsen in multi‑turn settings.  
*Resolution evidence:* Extend IJR to multi‑turn protocol; track refusal sustainability across turns; compare to single‑turn baseline.

## Limitations
- Unknown components: exact lexicons, transfer pairings, FREE sampling strategies are not specified; reproduction may deviate from original.
- Design trade‑offs: judge‑free heuristics risk missing nuanced leakage; single‑turn and limited harm categories reduce generalizability to broader safety scenarios.
- Inference assumptions: tokenization metric computation (model‑specific?) and contract handling of `ABSTAIN` may introduce bias; schema validity thresholds affect JSR reliability.

## Confidence
- **Contract Gap (Mechanism 1):** High – Supported by consistent over‑refusal gaps and FREE track collapse.
- **Cross‑lingual Transfer (Mechanism 2):** Medium – Strong empirical evidence, but mechanistic link to shared representations remains inferential.
- **Orthography Effect (Mechanism 3):** Medium‑Low – Correlations presented, but no ablation with tokenization‑free models; driver remains speculative.
