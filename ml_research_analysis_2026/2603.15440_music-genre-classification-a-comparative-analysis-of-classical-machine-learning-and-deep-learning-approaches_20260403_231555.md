# Music Genre Classification: A Comparative Analysis of Classical Machine Learning and Deep Learning Approaches

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.15440
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15440
- Reference count: 12
- Primary result: A sequential CRNN (CNN→LSTM) achieves 84% accuracy on a novel Nepali music dataset, substantially outperforming classical machine learning (71%) and other deep architectures.

## Executive Summary
This study addresses the absence of automatic genre classification for culturally diverse Nepali music by constructing a novel dataset of approximately 8,000 labeled 30-second audio clips across eight genres. It systematically compares five classical machine learning models trained on 51 hand-crafted Librosa features against four deep learning architectures operating on Mel spectrograms. The sequential Convolutional Recurrent Neural Network (CRNN), which processes spectrograms through convolutional layers followed by an LSTM, achieves a classification accuracy of 84%. This performance significantly exceeds that of the best classical models (Logistic Regression and XGBoost at 71%) and other deep configurations. The analysis reveals that misclassifications often occur between genres with genuine cultural and acoustic similarities, such as Purbeli Bhaka and Deuda, or Pop and Aadhunik Sangeet, highlighting the inherent complexity of musical genre boundaries.

## Method Summary
The researchers built a dataset of ~8,000 30-second clips from eight Nepali genres, split into 7,200 training and 800 test samples. For classical machine learning, they extracted 51 hand-crafted audio features (e.g., MFCCs, chroma, spectral contrast) using Librosa and averaged them over time to produce a static 51-dimensional vector per clip. Five models were trained: Logistic Regression, SVM, KNN, Random Forest, and XGBoost. For deep learning, they converted audio to Mel spectrograms (640×128, log-scaled) and implemented four TensorFlow/Keras architectures: a CNN, an LSTM, a parallel CNN-RNN, and a sequential CRNN (CNN→LSTM). All deep models used Adam optimization, categorical cross-entropy, batch size 32, dropout, and L2 regularization, with early stopping on a validation split. The dataset is not publicly available, requiring manual collection and preprocessing for reproduction.

## Key Results
- The sequential CRNN achieves 84% classification accuracy, outperforming the best classical models (Logistic Regression and XGBoost at 71%) and standalone CNN (71%) by a substantial margin.
- Per-genre F1-scores exceed 0.90 for acoustically distinctive genres (Rap, Rock, Lok Dohori), while folk genres like Purbeli Bhaka and Deuda show lower scores.
- The confusion matrix shows systematic misclassifications between Purbeli Bhaka↔Deuda and Pop↔Aadhunik Sangeet, pairs that share documented cultural origins or modern production influences.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The sequential CRNN outperforms other architectures because it creates a hierarchical representation where spectral features are extracted before temporal modeling.
- **Mechanism:** Convolutional layers first learn local, translation-invariant spectro-temporal patterns (timbre, harmonic structure) from Mel spectrograms. Their output, a compressed sequence of abstract feature vectors, is then fed to an LSTM. This pre-processing disentangles spectral from temporal structure, allowing the LSTM to model how these learned features evolve over time (rhythm, song structure) more effectively than if it processed raw spectral frames.
- **Core assumption:** Musical genre discrimination requires both spectral detail and temporal dynamics, and modeling them in a sequential hierarchy is more effective than parallel or isolated processing.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] States the CRNN "convolutions feed into an LSTM" and achieves 84% accuracy, "substantially outperforming" parallel CNN-RNN (76%) and standalone CNN (71%).
  - [section IV.B & V.A] Provides the 8-point accuracy gap between sequential and parallel CRNN and explains that the RNN branch in the parallel model "must learn temporal patterns directly from 128-dimensional raw Mel frames—a task it demonstrably struggles with."
  - [corpus] Corpus shows related work on CRNNs (e.g., paper 80281 on Bangla music uses Bidirectional LSTMs; paper 116261 compares CNN/XGBoost), but no direct anchors to the sequential hierarchy mechanism. *Assumption: The sequential advantage generalizes beyond this paper's specific Nepali dataset.*
- **Break condition:** If the performance gap between sequential and parallel CRNN closes significantly on a larger, more diverse dataset where the LSTM branch can learn robust temporal patterns from raw spectrograms, the hierarchical preprocessing benefit may diminish.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Deep learning (specifically the CRNN) outperforms classical machine learning primarily because it retains and models temporal structure, which hand-crafted feature vectors discard.
- **Mechanism:** Classical models use 51 features averaged over all frames of a 30-second clip, collapsing temporal evolution into static descriptors. The CRNN operates on the full spectrogram sequence, preserving the temporal dimension. The LSTM component explicitly models dependencies across time steps, capturing rhythmic patterns, structural transitions, and evolving instrumentation that are invisible to averaged features.
- **Core assumption:** Time-varying characteristics are crucial for distinguishing between Nepali genres with similar static spectral profiles (e.g., Purbeli Bhaka vs. Deuda).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section IV.C] Shows the best classical models (LR, XGBoost at 71%) match the standalone CNN (71%) but trail the CRNN (84%) by 13 points. The text states: "The 13-point accuracy gap... is largely attributable to temporal modeling."
  - [section V.B] Explicitly states: "Classical features are computed as frame-level averages, discarding all information about how features evolve over time. A song’s temporal structure... is invisible to a feature vector that collapses 30 seconds into a single summary."
  - [corpus] Related papers (e.g., 53674, 178428) compare classical ML on hand-crafted features, supporting the paradigm, but do not address temporal modeling gaps. *Assumption: The temporal modeling advantage is the primary driver of the 13-point gap, not solely the learned spectral features.*
- **Break condition:** If a classical model were engineered with dynamic, sequence-aware features (e.g., using temporal modeling on summary statistics) and matched the CRNN's performance, the core claim that deep learning's advantage is *specifically* temporal modeling would be weakened.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Misclassifications by the best model (CRNN) are not random errors but reflect genuine, culturally grounded acoustic and stylistic overlaps between certain Nepali genres.
- **Mechanism:** The model's confusion matrix shows systematic clusters: Purbeli Bhaka ↔ Deuda, and Pop ↔ Aadhunik Sangeet. These pairs share documented cultural origins (rural folk traditions) or modern production influences (Western pop techniques). The model is sensitive to these underlying acoustic similarities, causing it to confuse categories that may have subjective or evolving boundaries.
- **Core assumption:** A perfect, zero-error classifier is unrealistic for this taxonomy because some genre boundaries are inherently porous, reflecting a musical continuum rather than discrete clusters.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section IV.D.2] Presents the confusion matrix and states: "The most frequent confusions occur between Purbeli Bhaka and Deuda, and between Pop and Aadhunik Sangeet—pairs that share genuine cultural and acoustic overlap."
  - [section V.C] Provides the cultural interpretation: "Genres that share folk roots... are more likely to be confused," and links Pop/Aadhunik Sangeet confusion to "ongoing cultural evolution" and blurred lines.
  - [corpus] No direct corpus anchors; neighboring papers on non-Western music (80281 on Bangla, 30074 on Indian art music) acknowledge genre complexity but don't analyze this specific Nepali overlap. *Assumption: The authors' cultural interpretation is accurate and the primary driver of these specific confusion pairs.*
- **Break condition:** If expert ethnomusicologists re-label the confused samples and assign them to a single, unambiguous category, the perceived "overlap" would be an annotation artifact rather than a signal. Conversely, if the model also confuses acoustically dissimilar genres (e.g., Rap with Tamang Selo), the claim of culturally grounded errors fails.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Mel spectrogram as a time-frequency image representation  
  **Why needed here:** The deep learning models operate on 640x128 Mel spectrograms, treating time steps as sequence length and Mel bands as feature channels. This is the primary input representation that enables convolutional layers to learn local patterns.  
  **Quick check question:** What is the effect of the logarithmic (dB) scaling applied to Mel spectrogram magnitudes in this study, and why is it important for model input?
- **Concept:** Hand-crafted audio feature vector (MFCCs, chroma, spectral contrast, etc.)  
  **Why needed here:** These 51 features, extracted via Librosa, are the sole input for all classical machine learning models. They represent decades of domain knowledge about musically salient characteristics.  
  **Quick check question:** What key musical information is lost when these frame-level features are averaged across the entire 30-second clip to create a single vector?
- **Concept:** Sequential modeling with Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)  
  **Why needed here:** The CRNN and standalone RNN use an LSTM to model dependencies across the 640 time steps of the spectrogram (or CNN output). This is intended to capture rhythmic patterns, phrasing, and structural changes over time.  
  **Quick check question:** According to the paper's results, why does the standalone LSTM (68% accuracy) perform significantly worse than the sequential CRNN (84%) when both are trained on the same Mel spectrogram input?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Input (30s audio clip) → Classical Path: Librosa feature extraction (51 hand-crafted features, frame-averaged) → Classical Models (LR, SVM, KNN, RF, XGBoost)  
  OR  
  Input → Deep Path: Mel spectrogram (640×128, log-scaled) → CNN (three 1D conv blocks with ReLU, Batch Norm, Max Pooling) → LSTM(96) → Dense(64, ReLU) → Softmax(8)
- **Critical path:** For the CRNN, success hinges on the CNN output being a sufficiently compressed and abstract sequence for the LSTM to model temporally. The hierarchical extraction (spectral then temporal) is key.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Sequential vs. Parallel CRNN: Sequential forces the LSTM to work on learned features, parallel forces it to learn from raw spectrograms. The paper shows sequential is superior (84% vs 76%).
  - Spectrogram parameters: 640×128 resolution chosen; 128 Mel bands balances frequency resolution and model size.
  - Hand-crafted vs. Learned features: Tradeoff is interpretability/inductive bias vs. data-driven optimization. Here, hand-crafted features surprisingly match a standalone CNN.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Standalone RNN failure (68%): Model cannot learn meaningful temporal patterns from high-dimensional raw spectrogram frames.
  - Standalone CNN failure vs CRNN (71% vs 84%): Model lacks explicit mechanism to model long-range temporal dependencies.
  - Cultural confusion clusters: Model systematically confuses acoustically/culturally similar folk genres (Purbeli Bhaka/Deuda) or modern/contemporary categories (Pop/Aadhunik Sangeet). High error on these pairs is expected and may not indicate model "failure" but taxonomy limitation.
  - Overfitting: Training accuracy reaches near 100% while test plateaus at 84% (Fig 4), indicating model capacity exceeds the 7.2k training samples despite dropout/L2.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Replicate baseline: Train and evaluate the standalone CNN and standalone LSTM on the Mel spectrograms. Expect ~71% and ~68% accuracy respectively, confirming the paper's finding that neither alone is sufficient.
  2. Test parallel fusion: Implement and train the parallel CNN-RNN architecture. Expect ~76% accuracy, verifying that late fusion improves upon individual components but is still inferior to sequential.
  3. Validate sequential CRNN: Train the exact sequential CRNN (CNN→LSTM). Expect convergence to ~84% test accuracy. This is the primary benchmark. Also, inspect the first-layer CNN filters to see if they learn interpretable spectral patterns (e.g., harmonic, noise-like).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1:** Does incorporating attention mechanisms or transformer architectures improve temporal modeling for Nepali music genre classification compared to LSTMs in CRNNs?  
  **Basis in paper:** Future work includes "exploring attention mechanisms and transformer architectures that may capture long-range dependencies more effectively than LSTMs."  
  **Why unresolved:** The current CRNN uses LSTMs, but attention-based models might better capture long-range patterns in music sequences, potentially boosting accuracy for genres with complex temporal structures like Deuda or Lok Dohori.  
  **What evidence would resolve it:** Train and evaluate CRNN variants with attention layers (e.g., Transformer encoders) on the same dataset, comparing metrics like accuracy, per-genre F1-scores, and training efficiency against the baseline LSTM-based CRNN.
- **Question 2:** Can data augmentation techniques (time stretching, pitch shifting, noise injection) significantly improve robustness and generalization of deep learning models for Nepali music genres given the modest dataset size?  
  **Basis in paper:** Future work proposes "applying data augmentation (time stretching, pitch shifting, noise injection) to improve robustness."  
  **Why unresolved:** With only 7,200 training samples, models may overfit or fail to generalize. Augmentation could increase effective dataset variability, but its impact on Nepali music's acoustic diversity is untested.  
  **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply augmentation to training data, retrain deep models (especially CRNN), and measure performance gains on the test set, focusing on improvements for low-F1 genres like Purbeli Bhaka and robustness to noise.
- **Question 3:** How effective is multi-label or hierarchical classification in handling genuine genre overlaps in Nepali music, compared to the current single-label approach?  
  **Basis in paper:** Future work suggests "investigating multi-label or hierarchical classification to better handle genre overlap," and the Discussion notes some songs may belong to multiple genres.  
  **Why unresolved:** The single-label setup forces exclusive assignments, but cultural overlaps (e.g., between Purbeli Bhaka and Deuda) cause misclassifications. Multi-label or hierarchical methods might reflect musical reality better.  
  **What evidence would resolve it:** Reformulate the task as multi-label (e.g., using binary relevance) or hierarchical (e.g., grouping folk vs. modern genres), train models, and evaluate using multi-label metrics (e.g., Hamming loss) or hierarchical F1, comparing to the current 8-class setup.
- **Question 4:** To what extent does scaling the dataset size through external partnerships improve deep learning model performance for Nepali music genre classification?  
  **Basis in paper:** Future work aims to "expand the dataset through partnerships with streaming platforms and cultural archives," and limitations note the dataset is "modest by the standards of large-scale deep learning."  
  **Why unresolved:** The current ~8,000 clips may limit model capacity, especially for nuanced genres. More data could enhance feature learning and reduce overfitting, but the required scale and sources are unclear.  
  **What evidence would resolve it:** Collect additional labeled clips (e.g., doubling or tripling the dataset), retrain the CRNN and other models, and track changes in accuracy, per-genre F1-scores, and convergence to determine the returns on data investment.

## Limitations
- The Nepali music dataset is not public, blocking direct external validation and introducing potential dataset shift in any reproduction; cultural/genre boundary subjectivity also limits objective "ground truth."
- Critical CRNN hyperparameters (filters, kernel sizes, exact LSTM units) and the method to achieve 640×128 spectrograms from ~1289 raw frames are omitted, preventing exact replication.
- Generalizability of the sequential CRNN superiority and the temporal modeling advantage claim to other musical traditions or larger dataset scales remains unestablished.

## Confidence
- **High:** Reporting of internal results (accuracy numbers, dataset construction) and comparative performance gaps (e.g., CRNN vs. classical, sequential vs. parallel).
- **Medium:** Mechanistic interpretations (hierarchical representation, temporal modeling as primary gap driver, culturally grounded errors). These are plausible but lack ablative proof.
- **Low:** Claims about universal superiority of sequential CRNNs and that temporal modeling is the primary driver of the performance gap across all musical contexts.
