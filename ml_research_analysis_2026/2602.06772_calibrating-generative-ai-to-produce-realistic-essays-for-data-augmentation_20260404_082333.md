# Calibrating Generative AI to Produce Realistic Essays for Data Augmentation
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.06772
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06772
- Reference count: 12
- Primary result: Among three LLM prompting strategies (Predict Next, Sentence, 25 Examples), Predict Next produced simulated essays with rater agreement and realism comparable to real student essays; 25 Examples also showed high realism but lower agreement; Sentence best preserved original rubric scores.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates how to generate realistic AI-simulated essays using large language models (LLMs) for data augmentation in automated essay scoring (ASE). The authors compare three prompting strategies—Predict Next, Sentence, and 25 Examples—by having human raters score the essays and judge their realism. Results show that Predict Next yields the highest rater agreement (proportion = 0.72, QWK = 0.74) and 75% of its essays are misidentified as real, matching the agreement of authentic essays (0.71, 0.75). The 25 Examples method also produces highly realistic text (82% misidentified) but suffers from lower agreement, especially at higher score points. The Sentence method best preserves the average score distribution of the original essays but is easier for raters to detect as synthetic. These findings highlight trade-offs between score fidelity and realism when using LLMs for data augmentation in ASE.

## Method Summary
The experiment used a corpus of 96 authentic ninth-grade informative-explanatory essays, evenly distributed across four rubric score points (24 per point), originally scored by professional raters. For each seed essay, three simulated versions were generated via different LLM prompting techniques:

- **Predict Next**: The LLM first analyzes a reference essay (drawn from the same score point) to create a detailed note set, then uses those notes to generate a new essay mimicking the target’s style, errors, and length.
- **Sentence**: The target essay is split into sentences; the LLM rewrites each sentence individually while preserving the specified number and types of grammatical, spelling, and punctuation errors and the overall structure.
- **25 Examples**: The prompt interleaves 24 real essays (positive examples) with 25 low-quality “amateur” essays (negative examples) and instructs the LLM to produce an essay that matches the real examples in quality.

In total, 288 simulated essays were created (96 × 3). A set of expert raters, blind to condition, scored all essays using the same operational rubric and judged whether each text was real or simulated. Metrics included rater agreement (proportion of exact agreement, quadratic weighted kappa/QWK) relative to the original professional scores, as well as realism detection accuracy.

## Key Results
- Predict Next achieved the highest rater agreement among simulated methods (proportion=0.72, QWK=0.74), statistically similar to real essays (0.71, 0.75). Its essays were misidentified as real 75% of the time.
- 25 Examples yielded even higher realism (82% misidentified) but showed lower agreement, particularly at the top score point, where average score fidelity was poor.
- Sentence method preserved the original average score points across the rubric scale best, but its essays were more easily detected as synthetic, indicating a trade-off between score preservation and realism.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Predict Next
- **Claim**: The Predict Next prompting strategy produces simulated essays with high interrater agreement and realism, comparable to real essays.
- **Mechanism**: By generating detailed notes from a reference essay and then predicting a target essay, the LLM captures nuanced stylistic and structural elements, leading to text that mirrors authentic student writing in quality and authenticity.
- **Core assumption**: The note-taking process effectively transfers features such as argument flow, sentence complexity, and error patterns, maintaining score consistency.
- **Evidence**: Human rater agreement for Predict Next essays reached 0.72 (proportion) and 0.74 (QWK), comparable to real essays (0.71, 0.75), and 75% were misidentified as real.

### Mechanism 2: Sentence
- **Claim**: The Sentence prompting strategy best preserves the average score points of original essays across the rubric scale.
- **Mechanism**: Iterative sentence-level modification while replicating specified errors and structural constraints ensures the overall writing quality, as defined by the rubric, remains stable in the simulated version.
- **Core assumption**: Rubric criteria are distributed across sentences; constraining sentence alterations maintains holistic score.
- **Evidence**: The study found that Sentence essays had average scores closest to the original distribution across all score points.

### Mechanism 3: 25 Examples
- **Claim**: The 25 Examples prompting strategy generates essays with high realism, as judged by low detection rates by human raters.
- **Mechanism**: Presenting interleaved real (positive) and low-quality simulated (negative) examples enables the LLM to learn and emulate authentic writing patterns, avoiding artificial traits.
- **Core assumption**: Contrastive learning from paired examples effectively calibrates the model to the distribution of real student responses.
- **Evidence**: 82% of 25 Examples essays were incorrectly labeled as real by human raters, surpassing the realism of Predict Next.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**
Essay Prompt Administration → Real Essay Scoring (professional raters) → Simulated Essay Generation (LLM with Predict Next, Sentence, 25 Examples) → Scoring & Realism Rating (expert raters)

**Critical Path**
1. Collect real essays from an operational assessment.
2. Score real essays with professional raters to establish ground truth.
3. Select a stratified subset (96 essays) and generate simulated versions using each prompt strategy.
4. Have expert raters blind-score all essays and judge realism.
5. Analyze agreement and realism metrics across strategies.

**Design Tradeoffs**
- *Predict Next*: High agreement and realism but requires complex note-generation prompt; may be sensitive to reference essay selection.
- *Sentence*: Best score preservation but lower realism and agreement, especially at some score points; iterative process may be computationally expensive.
- *25 Examples*: Highest realism but poor score agreement, particularly at higher score points; uses many examples, increasing token cost.

**Failure Signatures**
- Rater agreement (QWK) below 0.70 for simulated essays.
- Realism detection accuracy above 60% (easily identified as fake).
- Simulated essay average scores deviate >0.5 points from original (on 4-point scale).
- Inconsistent performance across rubric score points (e.g., collapse at top score).

**First 3 Experiments**
1. **Generalizability test**: Replicate with a different essay genre (e.g., argumentative) to see if findings hold.
2. **Parameter sweep**: Vary the number of examples in the 25 Examples condition (e.g., 10, 50) to find optimal realism-agreement balance.
3. **Rubric sensitivity**: Test Sentence and Predict Next on a 5-point rubric to assess score preservation robustness to scale changes.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **What linguistic/structural features make Predict Next and 25 Examples seem more realistic than Sentence?** Understanding these could help design prompts balancing realism and score fidelity. (No fine-grained analysis was conducted.)
2. **Does training ASE models on synthetic essays from these methods improve performance on unseen authentic essays?** Realism does not guarantee beneficial data augmentation. (Impact on model accuracy and fairness remains untested.)
3. **How generalizable are the results across contexts?** The study used a specific US ninth-grade informative prompt and a 4-point rubric. Transfer to other populations, languages, genres, and rubrics is unknown.
4. **Can prompts be optimized per score point to minimize the trade-off between score fidelity and realism?** The study found interactions but used fixed prompts; tuning may close gaps.
5. **Do these DA methods introduce or exacerbate scoring bias against demographic subgroups?** Synthetic essays might under-represent linguistic features of minority dialects, potentially biasing ASE models.

## Limitations
- The specific LLM model (e.g., version) and generation parameters (temperature, max tokens) used are unreported; outputs are highly model-sensitive.
- Exact prompt templates and phrasing are not provided; minor wording changes could alter results.
- The original dataset of 96 essays is not publicly available, preventing exact replication and limiting generalizability to similar corpora.
- The rater protocol (training, blinding procedures, number of ratings per essay) is sparsely described, making human evaluation reliability difficult to assess.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (Predict Next)**: Medium (strong metrics but dependent on unknown note-generation quality).
- **Mechanism 2 (Sentence)**: Medium (score preservation claim relies on aggregate averages without score-point-level agreement).
- **Mechanism 3 (25 Examples)**: Low (high realism noted but agreement poor, especially at high scores; negative example generation undescribed).
