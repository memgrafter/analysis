# Feed m Birds with One Scone: Accelerating Multi-task Gradient Balancing via Bi-level Optimization

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.07389
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07389
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: MARIGOLD reduces per-iteration complexity of gradient balancing from \(O(md)\) to \(O(d)\) via bi-level optimization and zeroth-order hypergradient estimation, matching or outperforming baselines on multi-task benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
Multi-task learning (MTL) often suffers from gradient conflicts, where the descent directions for different tasks oppose each other, leading to negative transfer. Existing gradient-balancing methods (e.g., MGDA, CAGrad) mitigate this by computing all task gradients each iteration, incurring \(O(md)\) cost that scales poorly with the number of tasks \(m\). This paper introduces MARIGOLD, which reformulates gradient balancing as a bi-level optimization problem. The upper level optimizes task weights to minimize the worst-case loss decrement, while the lower level trains the model. The hypergradient is estimated using a single-point zeroth-order method, requiring only one forward pass of the combined loss and reducing per-iteration complexity to \(O(d)\). Experiments on NYU-v2 and Cityscapes show MARIGOLD achieves comparable or superior performance (e.g., higher mIoU, lower absolute error) to state-of-the-art baselines while being significantly faster. Additional experiments on an industrial ads ranking task confirm consistent improvement over linear scalarization.

## Method Summary
MARIGOLD frames gradient balancing as a bi-level optimization: the upper level adjusts task weights \(\lambda\) to minimize a worst-case loss decrement objective based on a surrogate of the lower-level model parameters, while the lower level updates the model \(\theta\) using the current weighted combination of task losses. The key innovation is the use of a zeroth-order hypergradient estimator that approximates \(\nabla_\lambda \Phi\) by perturbing \(\lambda\) with a random direction and evaluating the change in the combined loss, thus avoiding the need to compute and store individual task gradients. This estimator relies on the smoothness of the combined loss in \(\lambda\) and introduces a bias-variance trade-off controlled by the perturbation radius. The algorithm is model-agnostic, working with any lower-level optimizer (e.g., Adam), and uses a softmax parameterization for weights with tunable temperature. A surrogate step (using \(\theta_k\) instead of the exact lower-level solution) keeps the overall cost low while still yielding effective weight updates.

## Key Results
- On NYU-v2 (3 tasks) and Cityscapes (2 tasks), MARIGOLD matches or exceeds the performance of CAGrad and Nash-MTL while reducing per-iteration time by a factor of 2–3×.
- Wall‑clock measurements confirm that MARIGOLD is significantly faster than the previous fastest gradient balancer, FAMO.
- In a real‑world auxiliary task for ads ranking, MARIGOLD consistently improves normalized entropy over linear scalarization across all tasks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Decoupling via bi-level optimization
Reformulating gradient balancing as a bi-level problem separates the expensive full‑gradient computation from the weight‑update step. The upper level optimizes task weights \(\lambda\) based on a worst‑case loss decrement metric, while the lower level trains the model \(\theta\). This structure allows the hypergradient to be estimated without ever computing all \(m\) task gradients, directly reducing the computational bottleneck. The approach assumes the lower‑level problem is solved approximately (i.e., \(\theta \approx \theta^*(\lambda)\)) and that the combined loss is sufficiently smooth in \(\lambda\); otherwise the hypergradient estimate may become biased.

### Zeroth‑order hypergradient estimation
MARIGOLD replaces backpropagation through each task loss with a single‑point zeroth‑order estimator: a random perturbation \(\mathbf{u}\) yields an approximate gradient \((\frac{d}{r})[L(\lambda+ru,\theta)-L(\lambda,\theta)]\mathbf{u}\). This requires only one forward pass of the combined weighted loss, independent of \(m\). The estimator’s bias grows with the perturbation radius \(r\) while its variance shrinks; a well‑tuned \(r\) balances these errors. Theoretical analysis (Lemma 3.1) bounds the estimation error under smoothness assumptions.

### Model‑agnostic black‑box design
The algorithm treats the lower‑level optimizer \(A(\lambda,\theta)\) as a black box, making it compatible with any autograd‑compatible optimizer (SGD, Adam, etc.) and model architecture. This contrasts with many prior gradient‑balancing methods that assume specific gradient update rules. However, the assumption of a deterministic or low‑variance lower‑level optimizer is critical; non‑determinism (e.g., from data shuffling or dropout) could inflate hypergradient variance and destabilize training—a scenario not explored in the paper.

## Foundational Learning
### Multi‑task gradient conflict
**Why needed**: Gradient conflicts occur when task gradients point in divergent directions, causing one task’s performance to degrade when optimizing for another. Resolving these conflicts is essential for positive transfer in MTL.  
**Quick check**: If two task gradients are exactly opposite, MARIGOLD’s worst‑case decrement objective would seek weights that avoid moving in a direction that harms either task, effectively scaling one gradient to match the other’s magnitude or finding a compromise direction.

### Bi‑level optimization
**Why needed**: The gradient balancing problem has a nested structure: task weights \(\lambda\) influence the model \(\theta\) through the training process. Bi‑level optimization formally captures this hierarchy, defining an upper‑level problem that depends on the solution of a lower‑level training loop.  
**Quick check**: The exact lower‑level solution \(\theta^*(\lambda)\) would require fully training the model to convergence for every candidate \(\lambda\), which is computationally infeasible in deep MTL. MARIGOLD therefore uses a surrogate that approximates \(\theta^*(\lambda)\) with the current \(\theta\) after a few steps.

### Zeroth‑order gradient estimation
**Why needed**: To avoid the \(O(m)\) cost of computing all task gradients, MARIGOLD estimates the hypergradient w.r.t. \(\lambda\) using only evaluations of the combined loss, reducing per‑iteration complexity to \(O(d)\).  
**Quick check**: The estimator in Lemma 3.1 introduces two error sources: bias (from the linear approximation) and variance (from stochastic function evaluations). The perturbation radius \(r\) controls the trade‑off: larger \(r\) increases bias but reduces variance, and vice versa.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Lower‑level: any model with shared and task‑specific heads, optimizer `Optim_LL` (Adam). Upper‑level: weight parameters \(\lambda,\rho\) (via softmax), optimizer `Optim_UL` (Adam), and hypergradient estimator `Hypergrad`. Global loop alternates between \((\lambda,\rho) \leftarrow \text{Optim\_UL}(g^k)\) and \(\theta \leftarrow \text{Optim\_LL}(\lambda_{k+1},\theta_k)\).
- **Critical path**: `Hypergrad` → `Optim_UL` → `Optim_LL`. The hypergradient must be computed efficiently (\(O(d)\)) and remain compatible with the autograd graph of the lower‑level optimizer.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Perturbation radius \(r\): small \(r\) → low bias, high variance; large \(r\) → high bias, low variance. Tuned on validation.
  - Softmax temperature \(\beta\): controls sharpness of weight distribution; larger \(\beta\) yields more extreme weights, smaller \(\beta\) yields uniform weights. Tuned per dataset.
  - Surrogate choice: using \(\theta_k\) instead of \(\theta^*(\lambda_k)\) introduces approximation error; increasing inner‑loop steps reduces error but increases cost.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Weight collapse: \(\lambda\) concentrates on one task early. Remedy: reduce \(\beta\) or increase \(r\).
  - Instability: hypergradient variance high, causing \(\lambda\) oscillations. Remedy: lower `Optim_UL` learning rate or increase batch size for hypergradient estimation.
  - Divergence: model loss increases after weight update. Remedy: lower \(\beta\), verify `Optim_LL` learning rate unchanged.
- **First experiments**:
  1. NYU‑v2/Cityscapes vs. baselines: MARIGOLD matches or outperforms MGDA‑type methods on segmentation, depth, and normal tasks at lower cost.
  2. Wall‑clock efficiency: MARIGOLD is faster than FAMO, the previous fastest gradient balancer.
  3. Industrial auxiliary learning: MARIGOLD yields consistent normalized entropy gains over linear scalarization in an ads ranking scenario.

## Limitations
- **Scalability untested**: Experiments use at most three tasks; performance and efficiency for large \(m\) (e.g., 10+ tasks) remain unvalidated; zeroth‑order bias may compound.
- **Smoothness assumption**: The zeroth‑order estimator relies on \(\ell\)-smoothness of the combined loss in \(\lambda\); real‑world MTL losses (e.g., segmentation with class imbalance) may violate this, increasing hypergradient error.
- **Optimizer dependency**: The claimed model‑agnosticism assumes deterministic lower‑level optimizers; non‑determinism (data shuffling, dropout) likely inflates hypergradient variance and was not evaluated.

## Confidence
- **Computational efficiency (\(O(d)\) cost)**: Medium. Theoretical reduction is sound, but practical gains depend on perturbation tuning and stochastic variance.
- **Performance parity/superiority**: Medium. Strong results on limited 2–3‑task benchmarks, but generalization to many tasks or heterogeneous tasks is uncertain.
- **Model‑agnosticism**: Low. Evidence only tests Adam; break condition for stochastic optimizers is theoretically identified but not addressed empirically.

## Next Checks
1. **Scale to many tasks**: Replicate on a benchmark with \(m \geq 10\) (e.g., MultiMTD) to measure per‑iteration time vs. \(m\) and monitor \(\lambda\) sparsity/diversity.
2. **Stress smoothness**: Introduce non‑smooth losses (e.g., quantile regression, discontinuous metrics) into a controlled MTL setup; compare MARIGOLD’s hypergradient bias against analytical \(\nabla_\lambda \Phi\) via finite differences.
3. **Stochastic optimizer test**: Run with SGD + dropout or high‑variance data samplers; monitor hypergradient norm variance and weight stability across seeds; if variance >10% of mean, tune perturbation \(r\) or inner steps.
