# Cultural Perspectives and Expectations for Generative AI: A Global Survey Approach

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.05723
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05723
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A global survey of 5,629 people across 13 countries found Religion/Tradition was the top-ranked cultural dimension for importance (48%) and sensitivity, with over 20% (up to 42% in UAE) stating some cultural aspects should never be represented by GenAI, and inaccuracy cited as the top perceived harm.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the critical gap in empirical evidence regarding global public expectations for how Generative AI (GenAI) should represent culture. Through a large-scale survey (n=5,629) across 13 diverse countries, it investigates how individuals define culture, rank the importance of various social identities, and establish "redlines" for sensitive representation. The findings reveal a consistent global prioritization of Religion/Tradition as both the most important and sensitive cultural dimension. A significant minority of respondents mandated that certain cultural elements—particularly religious artifacts, prayers, and specific historical traumas—should never be depicted by AI. Inaccuracy was universally identified as the primary harm, regardless of user familiarity with GenAI. The study concludes that current development pipelines, which often extract cultural data without community input, lead to misrepresentation. It recommends moving beyond geographically-based safety to participatory, dimension-specific frameworks where communities help define "sensitivity" and inform model safeguards.

## Method Summary
The study employed a cross-national online survey with a mixed-methods design. Quantitative data was collected from 5,629 adults across Brazil, Cameroon, France, Germany, India, Indonesia, Italy, Japan, Mexico, Nigeria, South Korea, the UAE, and the USA. Participants ranked 19 pre-selected social identities (e.g., ethnicity, religion, nationality) in terms of personal importance and sensitivity for GenAI depiction. They also answered open-ended questions about cultural "redlines." The data was weighted via secondary post-stratification to align with World Bank population proportions. Quantitative analysis used Python (Pandas, Seaborn) for descriptive statistics, frequency co-occurrence heatmaps, and cross-tabulations. Qualitative analysis involved inductive thematic coding of translated open-ended responses to categorize prohibited content.

## Key Results
- Religion/Tradition was the highest-ranked dimension for both cultural importance (48% globally) and sensitivity, consistently across all surveyed countries.
- Over 20% of global respondents (rising to 42% in the UAE) asserted that some cultural aspects should never be represented by GenAI; primary redlines included religious artifacts, prayers, and specific historical traumas.
- Inaccuracy was the most frequently perceived harm from cultural misrepresentation, cited by a majority regardless of respondents' self-reported GenAI familiarity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** GenAI cultural misrepresentation stems from development pipelines that extract cultural data without direct input from the communities represented, leading to stereotyping and erasure.
- **Mechanism:** Models are trained on majority-English, WEIRD-biased internet datasets. Developers, often lacking anthropological or regional expertise, make implicit assumptions about cultural categories (e.g., prioritizing geography over religion/tradition). This creates a "latent space" where culturally sensitive symbols are misaligned with community values.
- **Core assumption:** Culture is not a monolith defined by geography; it is a contested, multi-faceted construct where certain dimensions (e.g., religious artifacts) hold universal sensitivity.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "models are built by a minority of researchers in the Global North and deployed globally without adequate cultural competency... resulting in biases and knowledge gaps."
  - [section 2.1] "'culture' is an essentially contested concept" and "individuals often maintain idiosyncratic interpretations."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence for mechanism-specific causation; neighbors discuss cultural distance in education and risks of erasure but do not directly test the extraction-to-harm pipeline.
- **Break condition:** If development includes continuous, structured feedback loops with diverse communities (as recommended), the model's latent space can be steered to align with pluralistic values, reducing misrepresentation.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Direct participation of cultural communities in development (e.g., as domain-specific raters) can operationalize "sensitivity" and redefine model safeguards beyond generic safety filters.
- **Mechanism:** Standard RLHF uses homogeneous rater pools. Substituting or augmenting these with "Dimension-Based Judge Models" (e.g., local religious scholars for UAE queries) injects emic perspectives. These raters provide fine-grained feedback on outputs related to salient identities (like Religion/Tradition), teaching the model to recognize prohibitive vs. high-fidelity representation contexts.
- **Core assumption:** Community-defined norms are more valid for cultural "redlines" than inferred norms from broad internet data. Accuracy on sensitive topics requires authoritative sourcing, not just data prevalence.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 6.2] "Standard Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) pipelines should be augmented with domain-specific raters that reflect a range of social and cultural identities."
  - [section 4.4] Open-ended responses show specific "redlines" (e.g., "prayers," "monuments," "historical traumas") that generic filters would miss.
  - [corpus] Neighbors like "Risks of Cultural Erasure in LLMs" and "The American Ghost in the Machine" support the claim that current models lack cultural reasoning and that prompting can surface misalignment.
- **Break condition:** If community raters are not genuinely representative (e.g., tokenized, unrepresentative of intra-group diversity) or their feedback is overridden by optimization for broader engagement metrics, the mechanism fails and may reinforce new stereotypes.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A tiered, dimension-specific sensitivity framework (Tier 1: Prohibitive Redline; Tier 2: High-Fidelity Representation) is more effective than binary safety filters at respecting cultural boundaries while preserving utility.
- **Mechanism:** The survey reveals users distinguish between aspects that should "NEVER be represented" (Tier 1, e.g., specific sacred rituals) and aspects where harm is in distortion (Tier 2, e.g., religious artifacts requiring theological accuracy). Models can use "cultural configuration files" (dynamic per-region rules) to adjust refusal thresholds or enforce RAG for Tier 2, balancing cultural protection with functional need.
- **Core assumption:** Cultural harm is not a single dimension; "inauthenticity" and "prohibition" require technically different responses.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4.4] "More than 20%... asserted some cultural aspects should never be represented... primarily religious artifacts, prayers, and specific historical traumas."
  - [section 6.4] Recommends a "tiered taxonomy" where Tier 1 elements are "never generated" and Tier 2 require "high degree of historical and theological accuracy, potentially utilizing RAG."
  - [corpus] Neighbors emphasize value conflict and bias, but do not directly evaluate tiered frameworks.
- **Break condition:** If the taxonomy is static or not updated with community feedback, it will ossify and fail to capture dynamic cultural imaginaries, leading to either over-censorship (stifling respectful expression) or under-protection (allowing harmful misrepresentation).

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Cultural Contingency & "Essentially Contested Concepts"**
  - **Why needed here:** The paper's foundation is that "culture" has no universal definition. Engineers must understand that any system encoding "cultural" rules is making contested choices. Assuming a single, correct interpretation guarantees misalignment.
  - **Quick check question:** If a model is designed to "respect Indian culture," whose definition of Indian culture is being encoded (e.g., regional, linguistic, religious, caste-based)? How would you decide?
- **Concept: Sociotechnical Imaginaries**
  - **Why needed here:** The paper frames GenAI as a "technological cultural artifact" shaped by and shaping collective visions of the future. Understanding this helps engineers see that model design choices are not neutral but enact specific cultural values (often Western, rationalist).
  - **Quick check question:** How might the goal of "helpful and harmless" AI conflict with a cultural imaginary where certain knowledge is sacred and not for computational processing?
- **Concept: Participatory Design & Community-Led Auditing**
  - **Why needed here:** The core recommendation is shifting from extractive to participatory development. This requires understanding methods like co-design workshops, community-based system dynamics, and audit processes where affected groups define harm and norms.
  - **Quick check question:** What are the risks of a "red team" exercise for cultural safety if the red team does not include members of the cultural groups whose symbols are being tested?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Survey & Data Intake Module -> Analysis Pipeline -> Cultural Configuration Engine -> Model Integration Layer -> Audit & Feedback Loop
- **Critical path:**
  1. Deploy foundational global survey (as in the paper) to establish baseline sensitivity heatmaps.
  2. Process results to create initial regional configuration files (e.g., `sensitivity_weights_UAE.json` prioritizing Religion/Tradition).
  3. Integrate configurations into serving stack: for queries detected as culturally sensitive (via classifier), apply tiered logic (Tier 1: refuse; Tier 2: query RAG).
  4. Augment RLHF with domain-specific rater pools identified from survey (e.g., scholars for religious domains).
  5. Launch community audit cycles to validate outputs and revise configurations.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Regional specificity vs. Scalability:** Highly tailored per-country configs increase engineering complexity but match survey findings. Coarser regional buckets (e.g., "Middle East") reduce overhead but may erase subnational nuance (cf. paper's note on minority erasure).
  - **Dynamic Configuration vs. Performance:** Real-time config lookup adds latency. Must balance sensitivity with user experience.
  - **Tiered Framework vs. Over-censorship:** Strict Tier 1 refusals protect sacred elements but may block legitimate educational or respectful artistic queries. Requires clear, auditable boundaries.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Erroneous generalization:** Applying a single global sensitivity threshold, ignoring the paper's finding that "identities...are perceived as sensitive in a GenAI context" varies (e.g., Health Status uniquely sensitive in South Korea).
  - **Static configuration:** Configs become outdated as cultural imaginaries evolve. No process for periodic re-survey or feedback integration.
  - **Tokenized participation:** "Dimension-Based Judge Models" use community members who are not empowered or representative, merely checking a box.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Sensitivity Threshold Calibration:** For a high-priority dimension (e.g., Religion/Tradition), A/B test different refusal thresholds from the UAE configuration file against a panel of local religious scholars to measure false positive/negative rates on Tier 1/2 boundaries.
  2. **Judge Model Efficacy:** Compare model outputs for culturally sensitive prompts (derived from survey "redlines") when RLHF uses standard crowdworkers vs. augmented with domain-specific raters from the UAE and Nigeria. Score for alignment with community-defined "redlines."
  3. **Configuration Granularity Test:** Implement configs at country-level vs. regional-level (e.g., "West Africa") for Nigeria and Cameroon. Audit outputs for loss of salient nuance (e.g., Cameroonian "masques" vs. Nigerian "Nok culture" treatment) and measure performance impact.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What underlying socio-historical or psychological factors explain the cross-cultural consistency in prioritizing Religion/Tradition as the most important and sensitive dimension for GenAI representation?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper identifies Religion/Tradition as top-ranked globally but does not investigate causal explanations for this pattern.
- **Why unresolved:** Without understanding drivers, developers cannot reliably anticipate similar priorities in unsurveyed cultures or adapt systems appropriately.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Mixed-methods research combining the survey data with qualitative ethnographic interviews across diverse cultures to identify common and culture-specific reasons for the primacy of religion/tradition in cultural identity and AI sensitivity.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Does GenAI familiarity moderate perceptions of specific harm types (e.g., stereotyping, erasure, inaccuracy), and do these relationships interact with cultural dimensions like religion or ethnicity?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] RQ2 addresses familiarity and harm perception, but the paper’s analysis aggregates harms and reports inaccuracy as the top concern regardless of familiarity, without testing for interactions between familiarity, harm type, and cultural dimension.
- **Why unresolved:** If familiarity influences specific harms differently (e.g., more aware users may worry more about stereotyping), mitigation strategies could be tailored; current analysis does not explore this nuance.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Statistical interaction models (e.g., multilevel regression) on the survey data with each harm type as a dependent variable, and GenAI familiarity, cultural dimension rankings, and their interactions as independent variables, controlling for country and demographics.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Which attributes of cultural elements (e.g., sacredness, association with historical trauma, communal ownership) predict their designation as "redlines" that should never be represented in GenAI?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper documents frequent redlines (e.g., religious artifacts, historical traumas) but does not systematically extract attributes from open-ended responses to build a predictive taxonomy.
- **Why unresolved:** A taxonomy of redline attributes would enable proactive identification of sensitive content in new cultural contexts, moving beyond reactive lists.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Thematic coding of open-ended redline responses to derive attribute categories (e.g., sacred, traumatic, communal), followed by statistical modeling (e.g., logistic regression) to predict redline status from these attributes, validated across the 13 surveyed countries.

## Limitations
- The exact survey instrument and secondary post-stratification weighting algorithm are not publicly available, hindering precise replication.
- Sampling relies on online recruitment, potentially underrepresenting offline populations and skewing familiarity metrics.
- Qualitative thematic coding of "redlines" is subject to researcher interpretation with limited reported inter-coder reliability.
- The cross-sectional design cannot establish causality between development practices and harm; proposed mechanisms remain inferential.
