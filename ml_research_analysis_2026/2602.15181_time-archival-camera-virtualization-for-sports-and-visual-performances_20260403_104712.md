# Time-Archival Camera Virtualization for Sports and Visual Performances

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.15181
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15181
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** The proposed method achieves state‑of‑the‑art novel view synthesis for time‑archival with PSNR up to 34.28 and LPIPS as low as 0.0275, using ~48.8 MB per time step, and supports parallel training.

## Executive Summary

Camera virtualization for sports and visual performances requires photorealistic novel view synthesis with efficient time‑archival. Existing 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) approaches rely on accurate per‑frame 3D point clouds and fail under large non‑rigid motions and multi‑subject interactions. This paper introduces an implicit neural volume rendering framework where each time step is represented by an independent MLP with spatial hash encoding, trained on synchronized multi‑view images without any 3D point initialization. The temporally indexed representation allows exact reconstruction of any past frame and compact storage. Evaluations on synthetic datasets (Dancing‑Walking‑Standing, Soccer Penalty Kick, Soccer Multiplayer) and the real‑world CMU Panoptic dataset show superior quality: PSNR up to 34.28 vs 28.17 (4DGS) and LPIPS as low as 0.0275 vs 0.08. Per‑time‑step memory footprint is ~48.8 MB, significantly lower than 77–91 MB for 3DGS and far below the 6.2 GB required by point‑cloud‑based methods. Training is fully parallelizable across time, making the approach scalable for long‑horizon archival and establishing a new benchmark for time‑archival camera virtualization.

## Method Summary

The core idea is to treat each discrete time instant as an independent static scene, modeled by a neural radiance field (NeRF). The representation uses a spatial hash grid to encode 3D positions into feature vectors. Two shallow MLPs then predict volume density (σ) from the encoded position, and RGB color (c) from the density feature and view direction (encoded via spherical harmonics). Volume rendering along camera rays accumulates color and density via the standard transmittance formulation. For a given time t, the network is trained on all multi‑view images captured at that instant by minimizing mean squared error between rendered and ground‑truth pixels. There is no temporal coupling; each time step has its own parameters Θₜ, enabling exact retrieval of any past state and drift‑free reconstruction.

Architecturally, the method employs fixed hyperparameters: hash grid with L=16 levels, feature dimension C=2, minimum resolution 16, maximum resolution 2048×B (B=2); density MLP with hidden sizes 32→64→ output size 1+15; color MLP with input size 16+15 → hidden 64→64 → output 3 with sigmoid. Each model contains ~12.7 million parameters (~48.8 MB on disk). After training, each Θₜ is serialized for archival. During inference, the appropriate model is loaded to render novel views. The independence of time steps allows training to be distributed across multiple GPUs, each optimizing a different t without communication overhead, thus scaling to long sequences.

## Key Results
- **Quality:** Achieves PSNR up to 34.28 and LPIPS as low as 0.0275, surpassing prior 4DGS (PSNR 28.17, LPIPS 0.08) and ST‑GS.
- **Memory:** Per‑time‑step footprint ~48.8 MB, significantly smaller than 77–91 MB for 3DGS and orders of magnitude below point‑cloud methods (6.2 GB).
- **Scalability:** Fully parallelizable training across time steps yields near‑linear speedup; suitable for long‑horizon archival.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

The method's effectiveness derives from three interconnected mechanisms.

### 1. Independent Per-Time-Step Neural Radiance Fields
Each time step t is represented by a separate MLP Θₜ, trained solely on the images at that instant. The MLP predicts density and color from a 3D point and view direction, with volume rendering producing the observed views. Independence ensures exact temporal indexing and drift‑free reconstruction of any past frame. The core assumption is that a dynamic scene at a single time behaves as a static, rigid scene; synchronized multi‑view images then provide sufficient photometric constraints to reconstruct it without any additional 3D data. Evidence: The abstract states "each time step is represented by an independent MLP... trained on synchronized multi‑view images without any 3D point initialization"; Section 3 formalizes the per‑time‑step parameter set. This design breaks down if the camera setup is too sparse (under‑constrained) or if significant motion blur occurs within an exposure, violating the static‑scene assumption.

### 2. Rigid Multi‑View Geometry Replaces 3D Point Initialization
Instead of relying on an SfM point cloud to initialize scene geometry, the method directly optimizes a continuous radiance field. Known camera poses and intrinsics define rays; volume rendering enforces that rays from different cameras intersecting the same 3D point should yield consistent color. Photometric consistency across all views drives the geometry learning from scratch. The core assumptions are Lambertian surfaces and accurate camera calibration. Evidence: The abstract notes the problem is "geometrically well‑constrained" under synchronized multiview setup; Section 1 explains that any common dynamic subject between viewpoints at a given time will be rigidly related. This approach fails with strong view‑dependent effects (specularities, transparency) or if camera calibration is inaccurate (e.g., due to poor synchronization), as those introduce ambiguities in the photometric constraints.

### 3. Compact Fixed‑Size Representation and Parallel Training Enable Scalable Archival
Each time step uses the same hash grid resolution and shallow MLP architecture, resulting in a fixed parameter count (~12.7 M, ~48.8 MB) regardless of scene complexity. This predictability facilitates storage and management for long sequences. Moreover, because time steps are independent, their training can be distributed across multiple GPU workers without any inter‑node communication, dramatically reducing wall‑clock time. The core assumption is that the hash encoding provides enough representational capacity to capture complex dynamics with a small model, and that temporal independence does not harm rendering quality. Evidence: The abstract reports per‑time‑step memory and parallelizability; Section 4.3 provides tables comparing memory and training scalability. Potential break conditions: if ultra‑high‑frequency details are required, the fixed capacity may be insufficient, necessitating larger models at higher memory cost; also, the parallelism requires sufficient hardware—with limited GPUs, training many time steps may still be slow.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Data loader (synchronized multi‑view images and camera parameters) → Ray sampler → Spatial hash encoder → Density MLP → Color MLP → Volume renderer → Loss module (MSE) → Trainer (optimizes Θₜ) → Storage manager (serializes Θₜ). The training of each time step is independent, enabling parallel distribution across GPUs.

### Critical Path
1. **Calibration**: Obtain accurate camera intrinsics and extrinsics (e.g., via SfM on a static sequence).
2. **Per‑time‑step training** (parallelizable for each time t):
   - Load multi‑view images Iₜ.
   - Sample rays from all views.
   - For each ray, sample points, query hash encoder and MLPs to obtain (c,σ).
   - Render pixel colors via volume rendering.
   - Compute photometric loss against ground truth.
   - Backpropagate and update Θₜ.
3. **Archival**: Save each Θₜ to storage, indexed by t.
4. **Inference**: For a query (t, virtual camera pose), load Θₜ and render the novel view.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Independence vs. temporal coherence**: Independent models guarantee exact retrieval and parallel training but may produce flickering; adding a temporal smoothness regularizer (e.g., κ‖Θₜ₊₁ − Θₜ‖²) could improve smoothness but risks breaking exact archival.
- **Model capacity vs. memory**: Larger hash tables or deeper MLPs increase quality but raise per‑step memory; the chosen ~12.7 M parameters balances quality (~48.8 MB) with feasibility for long sequences.
- **Parallelization vs. hardware cost**: Full parallelism requires many GPUs; with limited resources, sequential training increases wall‑clock time.

### Failure Signatures
- **Training loss plateaus high**: Model underfitting (increase capacity) or poor hyperparameters (learning rate, ray sampling).
- **Low PSNR on novel views**: Insufficient multi‑view coverage, inaccurate calibration, or violation of static‑per‑frame assumption (e.g., motion blur).
- **Memory overflow**: Reduce ray batch size or image resolution; monitor total storage for many time steps.
- **Inconsistent rendering across time**: Visible flickering between frames; if temporal smoothness is desired, add lightweight regularization between adjacent Θₜ.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Baseline replication**: On synthetic datasets (Dancing‑Walking‑Standing, Soccer Penalty Kick), train the model and compare PSNR/LPIPS against 4DGS and ST‑GS as in Table 1. Verify quality gains.
2. **Memory profiling**: Measure disk size of a single saved model (~48.8 MB) and compute total storage for a long sequence (e.g., 1000 frames) to confirm linear scalability.
3. **Parallel scaling test**: Train the same number of time steps on 1, 2, 4, and 8 GPUs. Plot wall‑clock time vs. GPU count; expect near‑linear speedup, confirming full parallelizability.

## Limitations
- The static‑per‑frame assumption fails under motion blur or strong non‑Lambertian effects (specularities, transparency).
- Requires highly accurate, synchronized multi‑view calibration; errors directly degrade reconstruction quality.
- Fixed model capacity (~12.7 M parameters) may limit capture of ultra‑fine details like hair strands or thin structures.

## Confidence
- **Superior quality (PSNR/LPIPS):** High – validated across multiple synthetic and real datasets.
- **Low per‑time memory (~48.8 MB):** High – measured and clearly compared to explicit point‑cloud methods.
- **Parallel training scalability:** High – experiments show near‑linear speedup with GPU count.
- **Exact archival via independent models:** Medium – depends on static scene per frame and accurate calibration; break conditions exist (e.g., exposure blur).
- **No 3D point initialization:** Medium – leverages rigid multi‑view geometry but assumes Lambertian surfaces and sufficient baseline coverage.
