# Revisiting Sharpness-Aware Minimization: A More Faithful and Effective Implementation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.10048
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10048
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: XSAM consistently outperforms SAM and multi-step variants, achieving higher test accuracy (e.g., up to +0.8% on CIFAR-100/ResNet-18) and demonstrating improved flatness via smaller Hessian eigenvalues.

## Executive Summary
Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM) improves generalization by minimizing the worst-case loss within a neighborhood around each parameter update. However, SAM's mechanism—applying the gradient from a single ascent step to the current parameters—remains poorly understood and can be inaccurate, especially with multiple ascent steps. This paper proposes eXplicit SAM (XSAM), which explicitly estimates the direction toward the worst-case loss by probing a 2D subspace spanned by the ascent displacement and the final gradient. Experiments across vision and translation tasks show XSAM consistently outperforms SAM and its multi-step variants, yielding higher test accuracy and flatter minima.

## Method Summary
XSAM modifies SAM by replacing the implicit use of the ascent point's gradient with an explicit estimation of the worst-case direction. After performing \(k\) gradient ascent steps from the current parameters \(\theta\) to obtain \(\vartheta_k\) and its gradient \(g_k\), XSAM constructs a 2D hyperplane spanned by the normalized displacement \(\mathbf{v}_0 = (\vartheta_k - \theta)/\|\vartheta_k - \theta\|\) and the normalized gradient \(\mathbf{v}_1 = g_k/\|g_k\|\). It then samples directions \(\mathbf{v}(\alpha)\) along the spherical arc between \(\mathbf{v}_0\) and \(\mathbf{v}_1\) (parameterized by \(\alpha\)), evaluates the loss at \(\theta + \rho_m \mathbf{v}(\alpha)\) within a probe radius \(\rho_m \ge \rho\), and selects \(\alpha^*\) that maximizes loss. The final update uses \(\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \cdot \mathbf{v}(\alpha^*) \cdot \|g_k\|\). This explicit search more faithfully captures the worst-case direction, especially when multiple ascent steps cause curvature, and maintains a computational cost comparable to SAM by amortizing the probe across epochs.

## Key Results
- XSAM consistently outperforms SAM and multi-step variants across a wide range of models (VGG, ResNet, DenseNet, ViT, Transformer) and datasets (CIFAR-10/100, Tiny-ImageNet, ImageNet, IWSLT14).
- Achieves up to +0.8% higher test accuracy on CIFAR-100 with ResNet-18 compared to SAM.
- Yields flatter minima as evidenced by smaller Hessian eigenvalues.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Better approximation of worst-case direction
The gradient computed at a single-step ascent point \(\vartheta_1\), when applied back to the original parameters \(\theta\), points more directly toward high-loss regions than the local gradient at \(\theta\). This is because the ascent step reveals curvature and orientation of the loss landscape. However, the approximation relies on smoothness and a properly chosen ascent radius \(\rho\).

### Mechanism 2: Degradation of SAM's approximation with multi-step ascent
With \(k > 1\) ascent steps, the trajectory \(\vartheta_k\) curves away from the ray defined by the initial gradient. Applying the final gradient \(g_k\) (evaluated at \(\vartheta_k\)) to \(\theta\) can yield a poor estimate of the direction to the local maximum because the displacement from \(\theta\) to \(\vartheta_k\) is not aligned with \(g_k\). The error grows with step size and number of steps.

### Mechanism 3: XSAM's explicit 2D search
XSAM explicitly estimates the worst-case direction by searching over a 2D hyperplane defined by the normalized displacement and gradient. By sampling along the spherical arc between these two vectors and probing the loss at radius \(\rho_m\), XSAM identifies a direction that better approximates the true maximum loss within the neighborhood. This approach both mitigates inaccuracy in the single-step gradient and handles curved multi-step trajectories.

## Foundational Learning
### Loss Landscape Geometry & Sharpness
**Why needed:** SAM and XSAM rely on the distinction between sharp (high curvature) and flat (low curvature) minima; flat minima are associated with better generalization. Understanding Hessian eigenvalues and the worst-case loss within a \(\rho\)-ball is crucial.  
**Quick check:** If a minimum has a large \(\lambda_1\) (largest Hessian eigenvalue), how does a small perturbation in the top eigenvector direction affect the loss value?  
*Answer:* The loss increases quadratically with the perturbation size, scaled by \(\lambda_1/2\).

### Gradient Descent and Step Direction
**Why needed:** The core innovation is choosing the update direction. Comparing the standard gradient \(g_0\), SAM's \(g_1@\theta_0\), and XSAM's \(\mathbf{v}(\alpha^*)\) requires understanding how gradients indicate steepest ascent and how shifting the evaluation point alters the local linear approximation.  
**Quick check:** Why is the gradient computed at a point different from \(\theta\) not generally a reliable direction for updating \(\theta\)?  
*Answer:* Because the gradient is the direction of steepest ascent **locally** at its evaluation point; using it at a different point ignores the curvature that connects the two points and may point away from the actual ascent direction from \(\theta\).

### Multi-step Optimization Trajectories
**Why needed:** SAM's multi-step variants and XSAM's use of \(\vartheta_k\) depend on understanding how successive gradient ascent steps curve through parameter space. The orientation of \(g_k\) relative to the start \(\theta\) determines the quality of the "worst-case direction" approximation.  
**Quick check:** If you take two small gradient ascent steps from \(\theta\) along \(g_0\), why might the final gradient \(g_2\), when applied to \(\theta\), point in a poorer direction toward the local maximum than \(g_1\) applied to \(\theta\)?  
*Answer:* After the first step, the gradient direction changes; a second step may turn the trajectory, so \(g_2\) is aligned with the landscape at \(\vartheta_2\) but the displacement from \(\theta\) to \(\vartheta_2\) is not collinear with \(g_2\), causing mismatch.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Ascent Engine -> Spanning Vector Generator -> Dynamic Probe -> Update Driver

**Critical path:** During each training iteration, the Ascent Engine runs \(k\) gradient ascent steps to produce \(\vartheta_k\) and \(g_k\). Periodically (e.g., at the start of every epoch), the Dynamic Probe samples the 2D hyperplane to find \(\alpha^*\) that maximizes loss; the Update Driver then applies the step \(\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \cdot \mathbf{v}(\alpha^*) \cdot \|g_k\|\). The probe is amortized, keeping overhead low.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Subspace dimension: 2D balances expressiveness and probe cost; higher dimensions increase samples needed, lower (1D) is too restrictive.  
- Update frequency \(T_\alpha\): More frequent updates adapt faster to changing landscapes but increase compute; epoch-wise is near-optimal.  
- Probe radius \(\rho_m\): Should be ≥ ascent radius \(\rho\) to explore beyond the estimated maximum; too large probes may venture into irrelevant high-loss regions.

**Failure signatures:**  
- \(\alpha^*\) fluctuates wildly across epochs → \(T_\alpha\) too large or \(\rho_m\) too small.  
- Test accuracy lower than SAM → 2D subspace insufficient, \(\rho_m\) mis-scaled, or incorrect interpolation method.  
- Training instability/NaNs → \(\rho_m\) or \(\rho\) too large for loss scale.  
- Accuracy below expected → data augmentation mismatch or forgetting to rescale by \(\|g_k\|\).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Synthetic 2D function: Visualize trajectories of SGD, SAM, XSAM; expect XSAM to converge to the flat basin.  
2. CIFAR-100/ResNet-18 single-step: Compare SAM, WSAM (fixed α), XSAM across \(\rho\); expect XSAM > WSAM > SAM and lower sensitivity to \(\rho\).  
3. Multi-step validation: Set \(k=3\), \(\rho = \rho^*/3\); compare SAM, LSAM, MSAM, XSAM; expect XSAM to consistently outperform and SAM to degrade relative to single-step.

## Limitations
- Mechanism evidence is primarily internal; lacks external corroboration.
- The 2D subspace assumption may fail under high orthogonal curvature.
- Hyperparameter sensitivity (\(\rho_m\), \(T_\alpha\)) not fully characterized.
- Implementation details (exact α sampling, update timing) unspecified, hindering reproducibility.

## Confidence
- The gradient at the single-step ascent point approximates the worst-case direction better than the local gradient: **Medium** (supported by internal visualizations and theory).
- SAM's approximation quality degrades with multiple ascent steps: **Medium** (internal experiments and analysis).
- XSAM's explicit 2D search improves accuracy and robustness: **High** for empirical gains, **Medium** for mechanistic justification.
