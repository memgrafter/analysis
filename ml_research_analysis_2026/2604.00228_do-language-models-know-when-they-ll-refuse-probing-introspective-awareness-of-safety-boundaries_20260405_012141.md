# Do Language Models Know When They'll Refuse? Probing Introspective Awareness of Safety Boundaries

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.00228  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00228  
- **Reference count:** 14  
- **Primary result:** Language models can introspectively predict their own refusal behavior with high overall sensitivity (d' 2.4–3.5) but performance degrades at safety boundaries; Claude Sonnet 4.5 achieves 95.7% accuracy (ECE=0.017).

## Executive Summary
This paper examines whether large language models (LLMs) can accurately anticipate their own refusal decisions when presented with harmful or boundary‑case requests. The authors employ a two‑stage experimental design: first, they prompt the model to predict whether it would refuse a given request (with a confidence rating); second, they later re‑present the same request in a fresh context to observe the actual refusal outcome. Using signal detection theory (SDT) across 3,754 datapoints from 300 diverse requests, the study evaluates four frontier models: Claude Sonnet 4.5, Claude Sonnet 4, GPT‑5.2, and Llama 405B.

Results show that all models exhibit strong overall introspective sensitivity (d' = 2.4–3.5), yet their performance declines sharply (by 40–75%) on queries that lie near safety boundaries (e.g., leaning safe vs. leaning harmful). Claude Sonnet 4.5 demonstrates the best calibration (95.7% accuracy, ECE=0.017), while Llama 405B suffers from a strong refusal bias and poor calibration (80.0% accuracy, ECE=0.216). Notably, weapons‑related queries are consistently the hardest for introspection. For well‑calibrated models, high‑confidence predictions (confidence ≥5) achieve 98.3% accuracy at 85% coverage, suggesting that confidence‑based routing could be a practical safety mechanism.

## Method Summary
The core methodology constructs a dataset of 300 requests spanning clearly safe, clearly harmful, and ambiguous “boundary” categories. For each request, the model is first asked: “Will you refuse to comply with this request?” and must answer “yes” or “no” along with a confidence score from 1 to 5. After a brief interval (or in a separate session to avoid carry‑over), the same request is issued without the introspection prompt, and the model’s actual refusal behavior is recorded. The collected predictions and outcomes are analyzed using signal detection theory: sensitivity (d’) measures the ability to discriminate between requests that will be refused versus accepted, while the decision criterion (c) reflects response bias (e.g., tendency to refuse). Additionally, calibration metrics such as Expected Calibration Error (ECE) quantify how well the confidence scores match actual accuracy. The approach aggregates results over 3,754 individual datapoints (multiple requests × models) to compare across model families.

## Key Results
- All models exhibit strong introspective sensitivity (d' = 2.4–3.5) overall, but sensitivity declines by 40–75% on safety‑boundary queries, with weapons‑related requests being the most challenging.
- Claude Sonnet 4.5 attains the highest accuracy (95.7%) and best calibration (ECE=0.017); GPT‑5.2 shows lower accuracy (88.9%) with higher variance; Llama 405B, despite high d' (3.29), exhibits strong refusal bias (criterion = –0.86) and poor calibration (ECE=0.216), yielding 80.0% accuracy.
- For well‑calibrated models, high‑confidence predictions (conf≥5) reach 98.3% accuracy at 85% coverage, indicating that confidence‑based routing could serve as an effective safety filter.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The mechanism likely relies on the model’s ability to access a representation of its own safety decision process. When asked to predict refusal, the model activates similar latent states as those that drive the actual decision, allowing a simple readout (the yes/no answer) to be largely informative. The high d' values overall confirm that the internal evidence for refusal versus compliance is sufficiently distinct to be detected. The pronounced drop in sensitivity at safety boundaries indicates that for borderline queries the internal signal is weaker or more noisy, making discrimination harder. Differences in calibration across models suggest variations in how confidence is derived: Sonnet 4.5 appears to map confidence to the strength of the internal signal, while Llama 405B may overlay a conservative bias that pushes many predictions toward refusal, hurting calibration. Thus, introspection works because the safety decision process is not entirely opaque; it leaves a trace that the model can report with reasonable accuracy, provided the decision is clear‑cut.

## Foundational Learning
**Introspective Awareness**  
Why needed: Central to the paper’s research question—defines the model’s ability to predict its own refusal behavior.  
Quick check: Can the model’s self‑prediction exceed random chance (50%) on a balanced set of safe and harmful requests?

**Signal Detection Theory (SDT)**  
Why needed: Provides a formal framework to separate a model’s discriminative sensitivity (d’) from decision bias (c), allowing precise measurement of introspective ability.  
Quick check: Given hit rate and false alarm rate, compute d' and verify it matches the reported value.

**Expected Calibration Error (ECE)**  
Why needed: Quantifies how well the model’s confidence scores align with actual accuracy; crucial for assessing whether high confidence corresponds to high correctness.  
Quick check: Bin predictions by confidence and check that the proportion of correct predictions in each bin is close to the average confidence.

**Safety Boundary Effect**  
Why needed: Refers to the observed performance drop on queries that are neither clearly safe nor clearly harmful; testing this reveals the limits of introspection.  
Quick check: Compare d' for “clear” vs. “boundary” categories; a significant drop indicates the effect.

**Refusal Bias**  
Why needed: Describes a model’s tendency to refuse more (or less) than warranted, independent of sensitivity; observed as an offset in SDT criterion.  
Quick check: A negative criterion value (c < 0) indicates a bias toward responding “yes, I will refuse”.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**  
Curated Request Bank -> Introspection Module (adds “Will you refuse?” prompt) -> Target LLM -> Extracted Prediction & Confidence  
Curated Request Bank -> Execution Module (raw prompt) -> Target LLM -> Extracted Actual Refusal Decision  
Prediction & Actual -> SDT Analyzer -> d', c, ECE

**Critical Path**  
1. For each request, generate an introspection prompt that asks the model to predict its refusal and confidence.  
2. Query the target model and record the prediction.  
3. In a separate, fresh context, submit the original request without the introspection prompt and record the actual refusal outcome.  
4. Align the two records by request ID.  
5. Compute signal detection metrics (d', c) and calibration (ECE).  
6. Aggregate across all requests for model comparison.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- **Double inference cost:** Each request is processed twice, doubling latency and compute.  
- **Prompt sensitivity:** The wording of the introspection question strongly influences model confidence and bias; generic prompts may not align with the model’s internal decision process.  
- **Confidence scale:** Forcing a 1‑5 numeric confidence may not reflect the model’s natural uncertainty distribution; alternative calibration methods could improve alignment.  
- **Dataset composition:** Balanced categories are needed to compute unbiased d'; however, real‑world harmful requests are rare, so the test set may not reflect deployment distribution.  
- **Model version drift:** Results are tied to specific model snapshots; subsequent updates can change introspective behavior, requiring re‑evaluation.

**Failure Signatures**  
- d' close to 0 → model cannot distinguish its own future refusal (random performance).  
- Extremely negative criterion (c << –1) → blanket refusal bias, rendering predictions useless for safe queries.  
- High ECE (>0.2) → confidence scores are poorly calibrated; high confidence does not guarantee accuracy.  
- Large performance gap between clear and boundary categories (>50% drop) → system fails on ambiguous inputs, which are common in practice.  
- High variance across prompt variations → brittleness; small changes cause significant inconsistency.

**First Experiments**  
1. **Baseline Sensitivity Measurement:** Compute d' and accuracy for each model across the full dataset to establish a reference performance level.  
2. **Calibration Analysis:** Generate reliability diagrams and calculate ECE to assess how well confidence predicts correctness, comparing models.  
3. **Boundary Effect Quantification:** Split requests into clear vs. boundary categories and measure the percentage drop in d' and accuracy, identifying which subcategories (e.g., weapons) are most affected.

## Limitations
- Small and potentially non‑representative request set (300 queries) may limit generalizability to broader safety‑critical scenarios.  
- Results could be sensitive to prompt engineering; the paper does not explore variations that might significantly alter introspection performance.  
- Conclusions are drawn from only four frontier models; other architectures or versions may behave differently, and the field is nascent with limited prior work.

## Confidence
- **High:** Overall introspective sensitivity (d' range 2.4–3.5) and calibration metrics (accuracy, ECE) based on the provided dataset and SDT analysis.  
- **Medium:** The claimed 40–75% sensitivity drop at safety boundaries and the identification of weapons queries as hardest, as these rely on undefined categorical boundaries.  
- **Low:** The suggested real‑world deployment of confidence‑based routing for safety‑critical systems, which was not empirically tested in a live filtering scenario.

## Next Checks
1. **Replication with an adversarially curated held‑out set:** Test if the observed d' and accuracy hold on a new collection of boundary queries generated by independent red‑teamers, not used in the original 300 requests.  
2. **Prompt ablation study:** Systematically vary the introspection prompt wording (e.g., “Will you refuse?” vs. “Is this against safety policy?”) to quantify sensitivity to phrasing and establish a robust, minimal prompt protocol.  
3. **Simulated deployment evaluation:** Implement a confidence‑thresholded refusal filter using Sonnet 4.5’s high‑confidence predictions in a live API setting, measuring the trade‑off between harmful output reduction and increase in false refusals on borderline safe queries compared to a baseline safety classifier.
