# Rubric-Guided Fine-tuning of SpeechLLMs for Multi-Aspect, Multi-Rater L2 Reading-Speech Assessment

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.16889  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16889  
- **Reference count:** 0  
- **Primary result:** The proposed MRR.GC configuration significantly outperforms baselines, achieving PCC ≈ 0.81, RMSE ≈ 0.83, and QWK ≈ 0.50 across rubrics, with calibrated intervals capturing human variability (e.g., 94.7% rater coverage for prosody).

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the misalignment between automated L2 speech assessment and human raters’ nuanced, variable judgments across accuracy, fluency, and prosody. It introduces a rubric-guided fine-tuning framework for the SpeechLLM Qwen2-Audio-7B-Instruct, integrating multi-rater supervision, Gaussian uncertainty modeling (GNLL), and conformal calibration. The resulting MRR.GC model yields state-of-the-art performance on the SpeechOcean762 corpus, providing both accurate point predictions and statistically reliable confidence intervals. While the approach shows strong results for fluency and prosody, accuracy remains challenging, and mid-range prediction bias persists.

## Method Summary
The method fine-tunes Qwen2-Audio-7B-Instruct using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) on 5000 read utterances from SpeechOcean762, each scored 1–10 by five experts on three rubric dimensions. Input prompts combine raw audio with textual rubric instructions (e.g., “Score sentence-level accuracy on a scale from 1 to 10”). The architecture adds parallel regression heads, each outputting a mean (µ) and variance (σ²) per aspect. Training uses a multi-rater GNLL loss that incorporates inter‑rater variance to model aleatoric uncertainty. For the MRR.GC variant, a post‑training conformal calibration step (5‑fold on the test set) scales the uncertainties to guarantee 90% coverage of human scores.

## Key Results
- **MRR.GC dominates baselines:** PCC ≈ 0.81, RMSE ≈ 0.83, QWK ≈ 0.50, with statistically calibrated intervals.
- **Confidence intervals capture rater spread:** Prosody achieves 94.7% coverage for all five raters, demonstrating effective uncertainty quantification.
- **Aspect-specific performance gap:** fluency and prosody are reliably assessed, but accuracy lags, suggesting rubric or acoustic feature limitations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Explicit rubric-guided prompting and multi-aspect output heads align the SpeechLLM’s internal reasoning with the dimensional structure of human assessment (accuracy, fluency, prosody).
- **Mechanism:** The input prompt concatenates raw audio with a textual description of the scoring rubric for a specific aspect. The model’s architecture is modified with parallel regression heads, each dedicated to one rubric dimension. This forces the shared encoder to produce representations that disentangle aspects, guiding the model to attend to different acoustic-linguistic cues (e.g., pronunciation errors for accuracy, temporal patterns for fluency) as specified by the rubric text.
- **Core assumption:** Human-defined rubrics effectively decompose speech proficiency into orthogonal, instruction-following dimensions that a multimodal model can learn to map onto.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “rubric-guided reasoning framework that explicitly encodes multi-aspect human assessment criteria: accuracy, fluency, and prosody”
  - [section 2.2] Detailed definitions of each rubric dimension and the instruction prompt format (“Score sentence-level accuracy on a scale from 1 to 10”)
  - [corpus] Neighbor papers like “Slot Filling as a Reasoning Task for SpeechLLMs” and “MMSU… Reasoning Benchmark” show a trend of using structured textual prompts to guide model reasoning.
- **Break condition:** If rubric definitions are ambiguous or overlapping, the model may fail to learn truly separate representations, causing cross-dimension interference.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Gaussian Negative Log-Likelihood (GNLL) loss enables the model to produce an uncertainty estimate (variance) per prediction, which adapts to the inherent difficulty or disagreement in the training data.
- **Mechanism:** Each regression head outputs two values: µ (predicted score) and σ² (predicted variance). The GNLL loss penalizes errors scaled by the inverse variance and regularizes the variance term. During training, for utterances with high inter‑rater disagreement or acoustic ambiguity, the model learns to increase σ², accepting a higher loss on the mean to avoid overconfidence.
- **Core assumption:** The aleatoric uncertainty (data noise) in human ratings for a given speech sample can be approximated and modeled as a unimodal Gaussian distribution centered on the mean rater score.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “Gaussian uncertainty modeling… to capture natural rating variability”
  - [section 2.4.4] Formal definition of L_MRR.G(i) = (ȳi − µi)²/(2σ²_i) + ½ log σ²_i and its role in joint mean‑variance prediction.
  - [section 3.3.3] Coverage analysis shows the model’s predicted intervals capture varying amounts of human rater spread, indicating the learned variance correlates with empirical disagreement.
- **Break condition:** If the true distribution of rater scores is multi‑modal, skewed, or non‑Gaussian, the single‑Gaussian assumption would be misspecified, leading to poorly calibrated uncertainties.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Conformal calibration transforms the model’s raw uncertainty estimates into prediction intervals with a statistically guaranteed coverage probability (e.g., 90%) for the true human score, making the model’s confidence interpretable and trustworthy.
- **Mechanism:** After fine‑tuning with GNLL, the model’s normalized residuals |y_i − µ_i|/σ_i are computed on a held‑out calibration set. The quantile of these residuals (q_aspect) is determined for each rubric. For any new input, the 90% confidence interval is [µ_i − q_aspect * σ_i, µ_i + q_aspect * σ_i]. This non‑parametric step corrects for any systematic mis‑calibration in the GNLL’s variance estimates.
- **Core assumption:** The calibration set is drawn from the same distribution as the test set, and the exchangeability assumption holds.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “conformal calibration for interpretable confidence intervals”
  - [section 2.4.5] Detailed procedure: “Normalized residuals |yi − µi|/σi were computed on the calibration folds to estimate aspect‑wise quantiles (qaspect)… Final prediction intervals were then obtained…”
  - [section 3.3.3] Table 4 shows coverage percentages (e.g., 94.7% for prosody with 5 raters), empirically validating the calibrated intervals.
- **Break condition:** If there is significant covariate shift between the calibration and test data, the conformal guarantee may fail in practice.

## Foundational Learning
- **Multi‑output (Multi‑task) Regression**  
  *Why needed:* Predict three correlated proficiency scores (accuracy, fluency, prosody) from a single speech input; shared encoder exploits correlations while allowing rubric‑specific specialization.  
  *Quick check:* What are the risks of training three separate models versus a single shared model with parallel heads?

- **Aleatoric Uncertainty Modeling (Gaussian NLL)**  
  *Why needed:* Human ratings are inherently variable; the model must express “I am unsure” rather than overconfident point estimates. GNLL directly models irreducible noise as a learned variance.  
  *Quick check:* How does aleatoric uncertainty (data noise) differ conceptually from epistemic uncertainty (model knowledge lack), and why is aleatoric uncertainty the primary target here?

- **Conformal Prediction for Distribution‑Free Calibration**  
  *Why needed:* The predicted variance from GNLL is a relative confidence measure but not guaranteed to align with actual error frequencies. Conformal prediction uses a simple, distribution‑free method to scale uncertainties into intervals with a user‑specified coverage guarantee.  
  *Quick check:* In the described conformal calibration (using normalized residuals), what specific property of the residuals’ distribution sets the interval width, and why does this guarantee coverage under exchangeability?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input Encoder -> Fusion & Decoder -> Scoring Heads -> Loss & Calibration Module

**Critical path:**  
1. Prepare dataset: Load SpeechOcean762, extract rater scores, format prompts with rubric text, resample audio.  
2. Select loss configuration: Choose among DiCl, SRR.M, MRR.M, MRR.G, MRR.GC based on need for uncertainty/calibration.  
3. Fine‑tune with LoRA: Apply rsLoRA to all linear layers of Qwen2‑Audio, using AdamW (lr=2e‑5, batch size 1).  
4. (For MRR.GC only) Run conformal calibration: Split test set into 5 folds, compute normalized residuals on calibration folds to find q_aspect for 90% coverage.  
5. Evaluate: Compute PCC, RMSE, QWK; for MRR.GC, also compute coverage (% of human raters within interval).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Shared Encoder vs. Separate Models:* Shared encoder (MRR) is efficient and exploits cross‑rubric signals but risks negative transfer if aspects are independent. Separate models avoid this but lose parameter efficiency and synergies.  
- *Loss Function Choice:* MSE is simplest but cannot express uncertainty. GNLL adds uncertainty capacity but requires careful training (variance collapse). Cross‑entropy (DiCl) is stable but discards ordinal information.  
- *Calibration Overhead:* Conformal prediction requires held‑out calibration data and an extra inference‑time scaling step (multiplying σ by q), adding complexity but yielding guaranteed coverage.

**Failure signatures:**  
- *Mid‑range bias:* Predictions cluster around scores 6–8, failing on extremes (1–2, 9–10). Check confusion matrices for empty corners.  
- *Poor accuracy performance:* Consistently lower PCC/RMSE for accuracy vs. fluency/prosody suggests rubric or acoustic feature bottlenecks.  
- *Overconfident, narrow intervals:* Low coverage percentages indicate under‑disconfidence or calibration failure due to distribution shift.  
- *Loss of rubric separation:* If heads for different rubrics produce nearly identical predictions, the multi‑aspect setup has failed; check correlation between µ_acc, µ_flu, µ_pro.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline replication:** Implement DiCl (5‑class classification) using exact prompt format and LoRA setup. Expect F1 ~0.55–0.67; verify training stability and discrete outputs.  
2. **Multi‑rubric regression comparison:** Implement MRR.M (multi‑head MSE). Compare PCC/RMSE against SRR.M (single‑head). Confirm shared representation learning provides a boost, especially under ±1 tolerance.  
3. **Uncertainty validation:** Implement MRR.G (GNLL). Inspect predicted σ² distributions: they should be higher for samples with higher inter‑rater variance. Check that σ² does not collapse to near‑zero, validating the uncertainty mechanism before adding conformal calibration.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Mid‑range bias:** The model clusters predictions around 6–8, reducing sensitivity to extreme proficiency levels. The paper identifies this limitation but does not evaluate solutions such as balanced sampling or adjusted loss functions.
- **Rubric definition ambiguity:** The specific aspects of the rubric definitions for accuracy, fluency, and prosody that cause poorer accuracy performance are unclear, and the paper does not test rubric variants or conduct causal analysis.
- **Diagnostic feedback extension:** The current framework only outputs scores; it lacks mechanisms for error localization or providing actionable guidance to learners (e.g., highlighting problematic segments, generating natural language feedback).
- **Calibration coverage discrepancy:** Conformal intervals for fluency and prosody show very low coverage (near 0% for all 5 raters), while accuracy coverage is higher. The paper does not explain why intervals are too narrow for these aspects or how to improve calibration.

## Limitations
- Rubric definitions may be ambiguous, potentially leading to entangled representations across accuracy, fluency, and prosody heads.
- The Gaussian assumption for rater score distributions might be violated (real data can be skewed or multi‑modal), affecting GNLL calibration.
- Evaluation solely on SpeechOcean762 (5000 read utterances) limits generalizability to spontaneous speech or other L2 contexts.
- Missing implementation specifics (number of epochs, exact prompt template, GNLL numerical details) raise reproducibility concerns.

## Confidence
- **Rubric‑guided architecture:** Medium – multi‑head ablation shows benefit, but rubric overlap and lack of representation analysis leave uncertainty.
- **Gaussian uncertainty modeling:** Medium – learned variance tracks rater disagreement, but Gaussianity not validated; non‑Gaussian rater noise could impair calibration.
- **Conformal calibration:** High for coverage under exchangeability, but Medium under distribution shift scenarios.
- **Overall MRR.GC performance:** Medium – strong metrics on one dataset, yet consistent accuracy struggles and mid‑range bias suggest limitations.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross‑dataset validation:** Test on an independent L2 speech corpus (e.g., ETLPCC) to evaluate robustness of metrics and calibration coverage under different speaking tasks and L1 backgrounds.
2. **Normality testing of residuals:** Perform Shapiro‑Wilk tests on normalized residuals; if Gaussianity is rejected, compare with alternative uncertainty models (e.g., quantile regression, mixture density networks).
3. **Prompt sensitivity analysis:** Systematically vary rubric phrasing (e.g., synonyms, example scores) and measure impact on aspect separation, prediction accuracy, and uncertainty calibration.
