# Characterizing MARL for Energy Control: A Multi-KPI Benchmark on the CityLearn Environment

## Quick Facts
* **arXiv ID**: 2602.19223
* **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19223
* **Reference count**: 40
* **Primary result**: DTDE (Independent PPO) consistently achieved the best and most robust average performance (lowest IQM) and outperformed CTDE (MAPPO) in worst-case metrics (CVaR); recurrent networks significantly improved memory-dependent KPIs like ramping reduction and battery Depth of Discharge.

## Executive Summary
This paper benchmarks six Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) algorithms on the CityLearn urban energy management environment, using a multi-KPI evaluation framework to compare Decentralized Training with Decentralized Execution (DTDE) against Centralized Training with Decentralized Execution (CTDE) and to assess feedforward versus recurrent architectures. The results show that DTDE (Independent PPO) delivers superior and robust average performance, while CTDE (MAPPO) suffers from high seed-to-seed variability. Incorporating recurrent networks (GRUs) yields significant gains on memory-dependent KPIs such as ramping reduction and battery Depth of Discharge. Furthermore, decentralized policies exhibit resilience to agent or resource removal and feature well-distributed agent contributions, underscoring their practical deployability.

## Method Summary
The authors evaluate six MARL algorithms—Independent PPO (IPPO), Recurrent IPPO (REC-IPPO), Multi-Agent PPO (MAPPO), Recurrent MAPPO (REC-MAPPO), SAC, and Recurrent SAC (REC-SAC)—on the CityLearn v2.2b environment, which simulates six buildings equipped with energy storage, cooling, and domestic hot water systems. Each algorithm is tested with both feedforward and recurrent (GRU) network configurations. A systematic hyperparameter sweep (21 configurations, 3 seeds) identifies the best hyperparameter set per algorithm. All algorithms are then trained for 6 million timesteps using JAX and the Sebulba actor-learner architecture. Final evaluation spans 640 episodes and computes a comprehensive set of Key Performance Indicators (KPIs), including the aggregate CityLearn score, ramping, carbon emissions, occupant discomfort, battery Depth of Discharge (DoD), and others. Robustness is quantified via Interquartile Mean (IQM) and Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR), and a novel Agent Importance metric (Shapley-inspired) measures individual agent contributions.

## Key Results
* DTDE (Independent PPO) achieved the lowest IQM (best average) and best CVaR (worst-case) among all algorithms, while CTDE (MAPPO) exhibited high seed-to-seed variability.
* Recurrent architectures (GRUs) significantly improved performance on memory-dependent KPIs, notably ramping reduction and battery Depth of Discharge, enabling longer and shallower discharge cycles.
* Agent importance scores were low and evenly distributed; removal of any single agent caused only marginal performance degradation, indicating robust decentralization.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Decentralized training (DTDE) yields more robust aggregate and worst-case performance than centralized training (CTDE) in the CityLearn environment.
- **Mechanism**: In DTDE, agents learn independently using only local observations, avoiding the non-stationarity and high variance introduced by CTDE's centralized critic that processes joint observations. This results in more stable learning and less sensitivity to random seeds.
- **Core assumption**: Each building's control problem has significant local structure; global coordination is not strictly necessary for good performance.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "DTDE (Independent PPO) consistently achieved the best and most robust average performance (lowest IQM) and outperformed CTDE (MAPPO) in worst-case metrics (CVaR)."
  - [section 3.2] "IPPO consistently achieves the lowest IQM confirming its robust and superior average-case performance... MAPPO displays high seed-to-seed variability, evidenced by visibly wider confidence intervals."
  - [corpus] Weak or missing: neighboring papers do not directly compare DTDE vs. CTDE in CityLearn.
- **Break condition**: If the environment requires tight global coordination (e.g., shared constraints that bind all agents), DTDE may fail because agents cannot learn to resolve conflicts.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Recurrent architectures (GRUs) improve performance on memory-dependent KPIs like ramping and battery Depth of Discharge.
- **Mechanism**: Recurrent networks retain a hidden state across timesteps, allowing agents to capture long-term temporal patterns (e.g., daily cycles) and make decisions that anticipate future conditions. This leads to smoother power transitions and more sustainable battery usage.
- **Core assumption**: The environment's dynamics contain slow-moving variables (e.g., stored energy, thermal inertia) that benefit from historical context not present in feedforward networks.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "Incorporating temporal dependencies via recurrent networks significantly improved performance on memory-dependent KPIs like ramping reduction and battery Depth of Discharge."
  - [section 3.4] "Recurrent IPPO... consistently improves performance on both metrics, enabling discharge cycles that are not only longer but also shallower."
  - [corpus] Weak: neighboring works use recurrence but not in this specific KPI context.
- **Break condition**: If the environment is Markovian or if recurrent training is unstable (e.g., vanishing gradients), the added complexity may degrade performance.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Decentralized policies in CityLearn are resilient to agent or resource removal because contributions are well distributed.
- **Mechanism**: Agent Importance scores (a Shapley-inspired measure) show that no single agent is critical; each contributes marginally. The system can therefore tolerate removal of individual agents without large drops in global reward.
- **Core assumption**: The reward structure encourages cooperative but loosely coupled behavior; each building can independently contribute to district goals.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "Results also reveal robustness to agent or resource removal, highlighting both the resilience and decentralizability of the learned policies."
  - [section 3.5] "Agent importance scores remain low (typically within ±0.05)... Removing any individual agent leads to only marginal performance degradation."
  - [corpus] Weak: no direct analysis of agent importance or removal in neighboring papers.
- **Break condition**: If the task has hierarchical dependencies (some agents must act as coordinators) or if the reward is highly competitive, removal of key agents could cause collapse.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:  
  CityLearn environment (v2.2b) → MARL algorithms (IPPO, REC-IPPO, MAPPO, REC-MAPPO, SAC, REC-SAC) with optional GRU → Training stack: JAX + Sebulba (actor-learner) with hyperparameter sweeps → Evaluation: IQM, CVaR, per-KPI breakdowns, Agent Importance.
- **Critical path**:  
  1. Load CityLearn dataset and schema; instantiate environment.  
  2. For each algorithm configuration, run hyperparameter tuning (3 seeds) to select best config.  
  3. With fixed best config, train each algorithm for 6M timesteps.  
  4. Evaluate on test set over many episodes; compute KPIs, IQM, CVaR, Agent Importance.  
  5. Generate comparative plots and statistical tests.
- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - DTDE vs. CTDE: DTDE is simpler and more robust but may underutilize global information; CTDE can learn better coordination but is unstable.  
  - Feedforward vs. recurrent: Recurrence adds memory but increases compute and may overfit; feedforward is faster but ignores temporal patterns.  
  - Evaluation depth: Using many evaluation episodes (e.g., 640) gives reliable IQM/CVaR but is costly; fewer episodes risk high variance.
- **Failure signatures**:  
  - MAPPO runs show large confidence intervals across seeds (high variance in average score).  
  - Recurrent models do not improve on ramping/DoD compared to feedforward (suggesting poor GRU training or irrelevant temporal patterns).  
  - Agent Importance plot shows one agent with large magnitude (lazy or over-specialized agent).  
  - Training curves for any algorithm do not converge (average score plateaus at high value).
- **First 3 experiments**:  
  1. **Sanity check**: Run IPPO (feedforward) for 100k timesteps on a single seed; verify average score decreases and basic KPIs (discomfort < 0.3) are within plausible range.  
  2. **Replicate key result**: Compare IPPO vs. MAPPO (feedforward) with 3 seeds each; compute IQM and CVaR on aggregate score. Expect IPPO to have lower IQM and narrower confidence interval.  
  3. **Validate recurrence benefit**: Compare feedforward vs. recurrent IPPO on ramping KPI with 3 seeds. Expect recurrent to have lower (better) ramping IQM.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: What specific modifications to the reward function (e.g., dynamic weighting, auxiliary tasks, shaped sub-rewards) or transition to multi-objective RL frameworks would most effectively resolve the observed trade-offs between conflicting KPIs like discomfort and cost/ramping, particularly for DTDE algorithms?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The paper states its reward weights were fixed equally and that "temporal dependency... improved control on memory dependent KPIs such as ramping and battery usage, contributing to more sustainable battery operation" while noting performance trade-offs (e.g., Rec-IPPO sacrificed comfort for gains in cost/ramping). The Future Work section explicitly calls for exploring "reward shaping" to improve robustness and scalability.
- **Why unresolved**: The study uses a static, equally-weighted linear reward. The resulting KPI trade-offs suggest this simple formulation may not optimally guide policies toward Pareto-efficient solutions across all objectives. Unresolved is the precise form of reward modification (e.g., learned weights, constrained optimization, multi-head critics) that would best balance these competing goals without destabilizing learning.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Systematic experiments comparing different reward engineering approaches (e.g., adaptive weighted sum, multi-objective policy gradient methods, constrained RL) on the same multi-KPI benchmark, measuring not just aggregate performance but the shape of the Pareto frontier and learning stability.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: For CTDE algorithms like MAPPO, what principled observation design (beyond simple concatenation) for the centralized critic—such as attention mechanisms, graph-based representations, or learned communication protocols—can reduce its high variance and sensitivity to seeds while retaining its potential for peak performance?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The paper identifies MAPPO's "high-dimensional, centralized critic" and "sensitivity to joint observations" as the likely cause of its "high seed-to-seed variability" and wide confidence intervals. The Appendix E "Modified Observation" experiment shows that removing certain predictive features (temperature/electricity forecasts) improved Rec-MAPPO's stability, implying observation design is critical. The Future Work section mentions "observation design" as an area for further gains.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper only tests one naive modification (removing forecasts). The fundamental question of *how* to best structure global information for the critic—balancing richness with noise and redundancy—remains open. The core challenge is designing a centralized value function that effectively models inter-agent dependencies without exacerbating variance.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A comprehensive ablation study on MAPPO's critic input representation, testing architectures like attentional pooling of agent observations, relational graph networks, or learned communication masks, all evaluated under the same rigorous multi-seed protocol to measure impacts on IQM, CVaR, and rank stability.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: To what extent are the observed benefits of recurrent architectures (GRUs) on temporally extended KPIs (ramping, battery DoD) attributable to learning *true temporal dependencies* versus simply providing a longer effective memory horizon that could be matched by larger feedforward networks with extended context windows?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The paper consistently attributes improved performance on "memory-dependent KPIs like ramping reduction and battery Depth of Discharge" to "temporal dependency learning" and the ability to "capture temporal dependencies." However, the comparison is strictly between feedforward and recurrent networks with otherwise similar architectures. The paper notes "temporal dependency... has a more pronounced positive effect on independent learners," but does not isolate the mechanism.
- **Why unresolved**: The advantage of RNNs could stem from their inherent statefulness (learning compressed representations of history) or simply from having a longer unrolled input sequence. The paper does not control for effective history length (e.g., by using truncated backpropagation longer horizons for feedforward nets) to disentangle architectural recurrence from context window effects.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: An experiment where feedforward baselines are given not just current observations but a window of past observations (e.g., last 24 hours) as a flat vector, comparing their performance on temporal KPIs against the recurrent models. If performance converges, the benefit is mainly from longer context; if recurrents still win, it points to learned state representation.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Does the well-distributed agent importance and resilience to agent removal observed in the 6-building CityLearn scenario generalize to larger, more heterogeneous districts (e.g., 50+ buildings with diverse loads, renewables, and storage), where credit assignment and coordination failure risks are theoretically higher?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The paper's "Agent Importance" analysis (Section 3.5, Fig 6) finds "no single agent plays a critical role" and "no evidence of 'lazy agents'," concluding policies are "decentralized robust." The "Scalability" test in Appendix E shows algorithms trained on 3 agents generalize to 6, but this is a modest scale increase. The implication is that decentralized policies are inherently robust.
- **Why unresolved**: The conclusion of inherent robustness is based on a very small, homogeneous system (6 identical building types). The paper itself notes CityLearn simulates a "single-family neighborhood," which has limited heterogeneity. In a large, diverse district with varying building types, sizes, and distributed resources, the potential for some agents to become critical or for lazy agent emergence increases dramatically. The scalability test only doubles agent count, not diversity.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Running the same MARL algorithms on a scaled-up, heterogeneous variant of CityLearn (or a new environment) with dozens of agents having differing dynamics and objectives. The key metrics would be the variance of the "Agent Importance" scores (Shapley-inspired) across the population and the performance drop when removing top-contributing vs. random agents.

### Open Question 5
- **Question**: What is the causal relationship between optimizing for the provided reward components (discomfort, energy use, ramping, solar penalty) and the emergent battery Depth of Discharge (DoD) behavior? Can we explicitly shape policies to optimize for battery longevity (e.g., by incorporating degradation models into the reward) without catastrophically degrading performance on the primary KPIs?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The paper introduces a novel "Battery Depth of Discharge (DoD)" KPI and finds recurrent independent learners (Rec-IPPO, Rec-SAC) achieve better (lower) DoD. It explicitly states: "DoD is not explicitly included in the reward function. The observed improvements... likely arise as a side effect of optimizing related objectives such as ramping minimization and the solar penalty." This implies an indirect, unlabeled relationship.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper demonstrates correlation (recurrent models better on ramping and DoD), but not causation. It is unknown if the observed DoD improvements are *sufficient* for meaningful battery life extension, or if they could be significantly better with direct optimization. Furthermore, directly optimizing for a degradation proxy could conflict sharply with the existing reward's economic and comfort goals.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: 1) Ablation experiments where a simplified, explicit degradation penalty (e.g., a function of cycle depth/frequency) is added to the reward, measuring the change in all KPIs, including operational cost and discomfort. 2) Using the rainflow-counted DoD data to train a battery life model, then evaluating policies on predicted lifetime extension versus baseline economic KPIs. This would test if the side-effect learning is optimal or if direct rewards could break the trade-off.

## Limitations
* Hyperparameter details for PPO and SAC are missing, hindering exact replication of training conditions.
* The custom agent importance metric lacks external validation; its ability to predict system robustness under larger perturbations or different removal patterns is uncertain.
* Evaluation is confined to a single CityLearn dataset (6 buildings); generalizability to other urban topologies or larger multi-agent systems remains untested.

## Next Checks
1. Hyperparameter independence across implementations: Replicate the DTDE vs. CTDE comparison using at least two independent MARL libraries (e.g., RLlib, PyMARL) with thorough hyperparameter sweeps to ensure IPPO’s advantage is not codebase‑specific.
2. Scalable agent removal and subset testing: Systematically remove 1–3 agents and evaluate on diverse building subsets (e.g., mixed‑use, all‑commercial) to verify graceful degradation and rule out hidden critical agents.
3. Matched‑parameter recurrent ablation: Train feedforward and recurrent networks with identical parameter counts on both the standard CityLearn task and synthetic memory‑augmented tasks. This isolates recurrence effects from model size and confirms gains stem from temporal modeling.
