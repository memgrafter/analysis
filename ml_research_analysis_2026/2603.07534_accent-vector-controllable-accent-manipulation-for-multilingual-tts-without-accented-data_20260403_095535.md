# Accent Vector: Controllable Accent Manipulation for Multilingual TTS Without Accented Data

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.07534
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07534
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Significant accent shift achieved across six target accents (e.g., British: 56.7% vs 23.3% pretrained; Hindi: 24.2% vs 2.2%), with linear control over strength and successful mixed-accent synthesis, though at the cost of reduced intelligibility.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces Accent Vector, a method to control accent in multilingual text-to-speech (TTS) without needing accented training data for the base language. By fine-tuning a pretrained multilingual TTS model (XTTS-v2) on native speech from a target language while conditioning on the base language ID, a parameter shift (task vector) is extracted. Scaling this vector linearly controls accent strength, and interpolating multiple vectors enables mixed-accent synthesis. Experiments across six accents show large increases in target accent probability and high human identification accuracy. However, accent enhancement trades off with speech intelligibility (higher WER) and effectiveness varies with linguistic distance between base and target languages.

## Method Summary
The method leverages LoRA-based fine-tuning on a pretrained multilingual TTS model (XTTS-v2). First, the model is fine-tuned on a high-quality dataset of native speech from a target language (e.g., Spanish Common Voice), but during training, the language ID token is set to the base language (e.g., `[en]`). This forces the model to produce target-language acoustic patterns for base-language content. The trained LoRA weights minus the initialized (zero) pretrained LoRA weights form the Accent Vector. At inference, the pretrained model’s weights are modified by adding a scaled version of this vector: `θ_accent = θ_pre + α * τ_accent`, where `α` controls strength. For mixed accents, multiple vectors are interpolated: `τ_mix = Σ α_i * τ_i`. Synthesis uses a base-language transcript and optionally a reference speech sample from the target language to guide speaker characteristics.

## Key Results
- Accent probability increases substantially: British English 56.7% (vs 23.3% pretrained), Hindi 24.2% (vs 2.2%).
- Human evaluation shows high accent identification accuracy (e.g., 78.46% for British and Hindi).
- Mixed-accent synthesis successfully blends characteristics (e.g., Spanish + British), though one accent may dominate.
- Linear scaling of `α` yields monotonic accent strength growth but degrades intelligibility (WER rises) and naturalness (UTMOS drops).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1:** Fine-tuning on native target-language speech while conditioning on the base language ID induces a parameter shift that encodes accent characteristics for the base language. The model must map base-language phonological input to target-language acoustic patterns, adjusting weights toward producing target-like realizations. This assumes the pretrained model’s parameter space is linearly navigable for such shifts. Evidence: The approach is defined in the abstract and implemented in section 3.1. Break condition: For typologically distant language pairs (e.g., Mandarin-English), the linear shift may not fully capture suprasegmental differences, leading to weaker accent transfer.

**Mechanism 2:** Scaling the extracted Accent Vector by `α` provides linear, fine-grained control over accent strength. The parameter shift `τ_accent = θ_ft - θ_pre` defines a direction; adding `α * τ_accent` to `θ_pre` interpolates between the original model (`α=0`) and the fine-tuned accent model (`α=1`). This assumes accent strength correlates monotonically with distance along this ray. Evidence: Section 3.3 and Figure 3 show monotonic increase in accent probability with `α`. Break condition: Non-linear weight interactions could cause saturation or quality degradation before `α=1`.

**Mechanism 3:** Interpolating multiple Accent Vectors via weighted sum enables linear composition of mixed accents. Combined shift `τ_interpolated = Σ α_i * τ_accent(i)` is added to `θ_pre`. This relies on approximate linearity and independence of accent directions. Evidence: Section 3.3 defines the interpolation; section 6.4 (Tables 5, Figure 4) shows changing accent probabilities with coefficients. Break condition: If accent directions share underlying components (e.g., vowel duration), interpolation may not blend evenly; one accent can dominate.

## Foundational Learning
- **Task Vectors (Ilharco et al.)**  
  *Why needed:* The Accent Vector is a specific task vector; understanding that fine-tuning on a task yields a parameter difference that can be manipulated independently is foundational.  
  *Quick check:* If you fine-tune a model on two datasets (A and B), what does `(θ_A - θ_pre) + (θ_B - θ_pre)` intuitively represent when applied to `θ_pre`?  
  *Answer:* It represents applying both task vectors sequentially, effectively combining the adaptations from both datasets; this may lead to additive or interfering effects depending on task similarity, consistent with the linear combination used for mixed-accent synthesis.

- **Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)**  
  *Why needed:* LoRA enables efficient fine-tuning and yields a compact, additive direction (the LoRA weights) that serves as the Accent Vector, avoiding full-model storage.  
  *Quick check:* Why is LoRA particularly suitable for extracting a compact, additive "direction" compared to full fine-tuning?  
  *Answer:* Because LoRA constrains weight updates to low-rank matrices, resulting in a small number of additional parameters that can be directly added to the base model, preserving the additive property needed for scaling and interpolation.

- **Multilingual Zero-Shot TTS (XTTS architecture)**  
  *Why needed:* The backbone must support language ID conditioning and zero-shot voice transfer, allowing decoupling of accent from speaker and content.  
  *Quick check:* In XTTS, what roles do the language ID token and the reference speech spectrogram play separately during inference?  
  *Answer:* The language ID token conditions the model on the linguistic content's language, while the reference speech spectrogram provides speaker characteristics (e.g., timbre, prosody) for zero-shot voice conversion, enabling accent manipulation without altering speaker identity.

## Architecture Onboarding
Pretrained backbone (XTTS-v2) -> LoRA modules (trained on target language) -> Accent Vector (stored LoRA weights) -> Inference pipeline (weight modification + generation)

**Critical path:**  
1. Data preparation: Gather native speech corpus for target language (e.g., filtered Common Voice Spanish), ensure high quality (DNSMOS > 3.4) and consistent dialect.  
2. Fine-tuning: Use LoRA (rank=16) on all encoder linear layers; train with base language ID token (e.g., `[en]`) on target-language audio/transcripts; save LoRA weights as `τ_accent`.  
3. Vector arithmetic: Load `θ_pre` and desired `τ_accent`(s); compute `θ_accent = θ_pre + Σ(α_i * τ_accent(i))`.  
4. Generation: Synthesize with base-language transcripts and a reference sample from the target language to guide speaker traits.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- LoRA rank: Higher rank increases accent capacity but risks overfitting and larger vectors (paper uses rank=16).  
- `α` vs. intelligibility: Higher `α` boosts accent but raises WER and lowers UTMOS; requires application-specific tuning.  
- Linguistic distance: Works better for similar pairs (e.g., Spanish-English); weaker for distant pairs (e.g., Mandarin-English) due to prosodic/phonological constraints.  
- Evaluation proxies: VoxProfile/LID may be biased and unreliable for cross-lingual transfer.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Weak accent shift: Accent probabilities remain near pretrained baseline. Diagnose: verify base language ID usage, check LoRA weights non-zero, ensure data quality/dialect consistency.  
- High WER degradation: Intelligibility drops sharply even at low `α`. Diagnose: inspect target data quality (UTMOS), try cleaner subsets, adjust `α` downward, verify reference speech quality.  
- Accent collapse in mixing: One accent dominates during interpolation (e.g., British overwhelms Spanish). Diagnose: VoxProfile bias; test with a balanced classifier; try different accent pairs.  
- Speaker identity loss: SSIM drops significantly. Diagnose: LoRA rank too high, insufficient speaker diversity in fine-tuning data.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Replicate single-accent shift: Fine-tune for Spanish-accented English (Spanish CV, English ID). Evaluate accent probability (VoxProfile) and WER. Expect `α=1` to yield >35% target accent probability (per Table 3).  
2. Controllability sweep: For a fixed accent (e.g., Hindi), vary `α` from 0 to 1 in 0.2 steps. Plot accent probability vs. `α`; expect monotonic increase. Identify `α` where WER rises sharply.  
3. Mixed-accent composition: Combine two vectors (e.g., Spanish + Hindi) at equal weight (`α=0.5`). Check if both accent probabilities increase relative to pretrained (Table 5). Adjust `α_i` to observe smooth trade-offs (per Figure 4).

## Limitations
- LoRA fine-tuning details (exact layer scope, merging procedure) are underspecified, hindering exact replication.
- Primary accent metric (VoxProfile) is likely biased toward native English accents and its cross-lingual reliability is unverified.
- Linearity assumptions may break for linguistically distant language pairs or at high scaling factors, causing intelligibility degradation and accent dominance in mixing.
- Training data quality (UTMOS scores) strongly influences results, but sensitivity is not fully explored.

## Confidence
- **Accent Vector extraction** (fine-tuning on target language with base ID yields accent shift): Medium  
- **Linear scaling control** (`α` provides monotonic accent strength): Medium  
- **Accent interpolation** (linear composition of multiple accents): Low  
- **Overall framework** (effective accent manipulation without accented data): Medium-High  

## Next Checks
1. **Linearity robustness test:** Randomize the sign or introduce orthogonal perturbations to the accent vector; measure accent shift deviations to confirm the extracted direction is specific and not incidental.
2. **Balanced multi-accent evaluation:** Train a classifier on a balanced dataset of diverse accents and conduct a human perceptual study to assess blending quality, reducing reliance on potentially biased automated metrics.
3. **Typological distance correlation:** Systematically vary base-target language pairs across language families, measure accent shift effectiveness, and correlate with linguistic distance metrics (e.g., phonetic inventory overlap) to validate the proposed break condition.
