# CAF-Score: Calibrating CLAP with LALMs for Reference-free Audio Captioning Evaluation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.19615
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19615
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: CAF-Score achieves 73.11% accuracy on BRACE-Main and 97.11% on the Hallucination subset, outperforming both reference-based and CLAP/LALM baselines.

## Executive Summary
Audio captioning evaluation traditionally relies on reference-based metrics that are costly to obtain and ignore acoustic fidelity. While CLAP-based metrics avoid reference needs, they fail to capture fine-grained semantic and syntactic errors. This paper introduces CAF-Score, a reference-free metric that combines the coarse-grained audio-text alignment of CLAP with the precise reasoning of large audio-language models (LALMs). CAF-Score uses sliding-window CLAP with max pooling (S‑CLAPScore) to handle variable-length audio, and a probabilistic FLEUR score derived from LALM token probabilities to assess caption quality. The two scores are fused with a fixed weight α=0.8. On the BRACE benchmark, CAF-Score achieves state-of-the-art correlation with human judgments (73.11% on BRACE-Main, 97.11% on the Hallucination subset). It also generalizes to text-to-audio evaluation, showing consistent improvements on RELATE and PAM.

## Method Summary
CAF-Score operates without any training; it fuses two frozen models at inference time. Given an audio clip and a candidate caption, the S‑CLAPScore branch first splits the audio into overlapping windows (length 7–10 s, hop 1 s). Each window is encoded by a CLAP audio encoder, while the caption is encoded by the CLAP text encoder. Cosine similarity between the two embeddings is computed per window, and the maximum score across windows is retained. The FLEUR branch feeds the audio (via the LALM’s built‑in audio encoder) and caption to a prompted LALM (e.g., Qwen3‑Omni‑Instruct). Instead of using the model’s discrete output, FLEUR aggregates the token‑level probabilities for the first two decimal digits of the score (e.g., “0.85”) to produce a continuous, confidence‑aware value. The final CAF‑Score is a weighted sum: S_CAF = α·S_S‑CLAP + (1−α)·FLEUR, with α fixed at 0.8 in all main experiments. The same framework, with a prompt inversion, applies to text‑to‑audio evaluation.

## Key Results
- CAF‑Score (M2D‑CLAP + Qwen3‑Omni‑Instruct) reaches 73.11% accuracy on BRACE‑Main, exceeding the best standalone S‑CLAP (70.48%) and FLEUR (69.52%) as well as reference‑based metrics.
- On the Hallucination subset, it achieves 97.11% accuracy, dramatically reducing false positive errors.
- Generalization to text‑to‑audio tasks on RELATE and PAM benchmarks yields consistent improvements over prior metrics.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Sliding‑window CLAP with max‑pooling (S‑CLAPScore) better captures audio‑caption alignment for variable‑length audio than truncation or average pooling.  
**Mechanism:** Audio is segmented into overlapping windows; each window is scored via CLAP cosine similarity with the caption; the maximum score across windows is taken. This selects the best‑matching temporally‑localized segment rather than diluting salient event scores with irrelevant audio.  
**Core assumption:** Relevant acoustic events for a caption are often brief and sparsely distributed within longer audio, so a max operator preserves their signal better than averaging.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
– [Section 3.1] Argues average pooling “attenuates the high alignment score” and motivates max pooling for “salient segment” selection.  
– [Section 5.3.1] Table 2 shows max pooling consistently outperforms avg and no‑pooling on Clotho‑Main for most CLAP backbones.  
**Break condition:** If captions describe persistent background sounds rather than discrete events, or if the optimal segment is ambiguous and max pooling selects a spurious high‑scoring but irrelevant window.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Using the probabilistic FLEUR score from LALMs (instead of raw discrete outputs) provides a continuous, discriminative measure of fine‑grained semantic and syntactic alignment.  
**Mechanism:** The LALM is prompted to output a score (e.g., “0.85”). Instead of using this value directly, FLEUR computes a weighted sum of probabilities for digit tokens at each decimal place from the model’s distribution, smoothing the output and capturing model uncertainty/confidence.  
**Core assumption:** The token probability distribution around the generated score contains richer information about the model’s fine‑grained judgment than the single decoded value, reducing ties and hypersensitivity.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
– [Section 3.2] Introduces FLEUR to “mitigate” the “high frequency of ties” in raw scores, providing an equation and example.  
– [Section 5.3.3] Figure 4 and text show raw scores have very high tie rates (>50% for AudioFlamingo), while FLEUR provides continuous variation; FLEUR outperforms raw scores in Table 1.  
**Break condition:** If the LALM’s token probability distributions are miscalibrated or highly uniform for relevant scores, the smoothing may not add discriminative power and could amplify noise.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Linearly combining S‑CLAPScore (coarse alignment) and FLEUR (fine‑grained assessment) with a fixed weight α=0.8 yields a metric (CAF‑Score) that correlates better with human judgment than either alone.  
**Mechanism:** The final score is S_CAF = α·S_S‑CLAP + (1−α)·FLEUR. CLAP provides stable acoustic grounding; LALM corrects superficial keyword matches and detects syntactic errors. The weight prioritizes CLAP stability while allowing LALM calibration.  
**Core assumption:** The errors of CLAP (ignoring fine details) and LALMs (hallucination, instability) are complementary and partially independent, so a weighted average can exploit their strengths.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
– [Abstract] States CAF‑Score “calibrates CLAP’s coarse‑grained semantic alignment with the fine‑grained comprehension and syntactic awareness of LALMs.”  
– [Section 5] Table 1 shows CAF‑Score (e.g., Qwen3+M2D‑CLAP: 73.11%) outperforms best standalone S‑CLAP (70.48%) and best FLEUR (69.52%) on BRACE‑Main Overall.  
– [Section 5.3.2] Figure 3 shows hybrid (0<α<1) consistently beats pure CLAP (α=1) or pure LALM (α=0) for several backbones.  
**Break condition:** If both components systematically fail together on certain audio‑caption pairs (“double failure”), or if the optimal α varies widely across data and the fixed α=0.8 is suboptimal for a new distribution.

## Foundational Learning

### Contrastive Language‑Audio Pretraining (CLAP)
- **Why needed here:** Provides the coarse‑grained, embedding‑based audio‑text similarity foundation. Understanding its modality gap and cosine similarity scoring is essential to grasp the S‑CLAPScore branch.
- **Quick check:** Why might standard CLAPScore fail on a caption that accurately describes a brief event within a longer audio clip?

### Generative Evaluation with LALMs & Token Probability Smoothing
- **Why needed here:** The FLEUR component relies on extracting a score from the LALM’s token probability distribution, not its string output. This requires understanding autoregressive decoding, logits, and how to aggregate digit token probabilities.
- **Quick check:** How does computing a score from the probability distribution over “0.8” and “0.9” tokens at the first decimal place reduce ties compared to using the model’s generated string “0.85”?

### Sliding‑Window Processing for Variable‑Length Audio
- **Why needed here:** CLAP models have maximum input duration limits. The paper’s sliding‑window + max‑pooling strategy is a key architectural choice to handle longer audios without truncation loss. Understanding temporal segmentation and pooling strategies is critical.
- **Quick check:** What is the conceptual difference between using average pooling vs. max pooling over the window‑level CLAP scores, and why might max be preferred for audio captioning?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Input (audio + caption) →  
  ├─ CLAP Branch: sliding‑window segmentation → CLAP audio encoder (per window) + CLAP text encoder → cosine similarity (per window) → max pooling → S_S‑CLAP  
  ├─ LALM Branch: audio encoder + caption → LALM (prompted) → token log‑probability extraction for first two decimal digits → weighted sum → FLEUR  
  └─ Fusion: α·S_S‑CLAP + (1−α)·FLEUR → CAF‑Score  

- **Critical path:** The two branches are parallel and independent; there is no cross‑attention or interaction between CLAP and LALM processing before the final linear fusion. Calibration occurs purely at the score level.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  – *Accuracy vs. Compute:* Adding the LALM branch introduces significant latency (~0.28 s/sample for Qwen3) versus lightweight CLAP‑only. The tradeoff is justified by improved human alignment, especially on fine‑grained/hallucination subsets.  
  – *Simplicity vs. Optimality:* Using a fixed α=0.8 (vs. adaptive) simplifies deployment but may not be globally optimal. The paper found adaptive α (based on FLEUR entropy) underperformed.  
  – *Generality vs. Specialization:* The framework uses a single prompt for both audio captioning and text‑to‑audio (just inverts perspective). This is simple but may not be prompt‑optimized for each task.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  – *Double Failure:* Both S‑CLAP and FLEUR prefer the wrong caption (Section 6.2, Fig 7). Indicates fundamental audio misalignment confusing both models.  
  – *Insufficient Calibration:* CLAP is wrong, LALM is correct but with nearly identical scores for both candidates, so CLAP’s weight dominates (Fig 8).  
  – *Over‑calibration:* CLAP is correct, but LALM hallucinates/extreme preference overrides it (Fig 9).

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **S‑CLAPScore vs. CLAPScore:** Run on Clotho‑Main (variable length). Confirm max‑pooled sliding‑window scores (S‑CLAP) beat truncated/averaged baselines (Table 2). This validates the audio processing design.  
  2. **FLEUR vs. Raw LALM Score:** For a chosen LALM (e.g., Qwen3‑Omni‑Instruct), compute both metrics across BRACE‑Main. Expect FLEUR to show higher accuracy and dramatically lower tie rates (Table 1, Section 5.3.3). This validates score smoothing.  
  3. **α Sensitivity Sweep:** For a strong CLAP+LALM pair (e.g., M2D‑CLAP + Qwen3), vary α from 0 to 1. Expect peak near α=0.8 (Fig 3) and that hybrid (0<α<1) beats both endpoints. This validates the fusion strategy.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
Can adaptive weighting strategies for α beyond entropy‑based methods improve CAF‑Score’s robustness across diverse audio‑caption pairs and CLAP backbones?  
- *Basis:* The paper finds that optimal fixed α varies by CLAP backbone (e.g., 0.2 for MS‑CLAP, 0.8 for M2D‑CLAP) and that a simple entropy‑based adaptive α underperformed fixed α (Section 5.3.2, Table 3).  
- *Why unresolved:* The failure of entropy‑based adaptation suggests unexplored adaptive mechanisms (e.g., based on LALM confidence or audio complexity) could better handle sample‑specific uncertainties, but no such methods were evaluated.  
- *What evidence would resolve it:* Systematically evaluate alternative adaptive weighting schemes (e.g., using LALM token probability variance or CLAP score distribution) on BRACE and additional benchmarks, reporting correlation with human judgments and tie reduction.

### Open Question 2
What architectural modifications can mitigate the three identified failure modes (double failure, insufficient calibration, over‑calibration) in CAF‑Score without sacrificing its hybrid advantages?  
- *Basis:* The paper categorizes failure cases where CLAP and LALM errors compound or one component overrides the other (Section 6.2, Figures 7‑9), attributing them to frozen backbones and static linear weighting.  
- *Why unresolved:* The current linear combination cannot dynamically resolve conflicting signals; failure modes stem from inherent model limitations that the fixed α=0.8 cannot address universally.  
- *What evidence would resolve it:* Introduce uncertainty‑aware gating (e.g., sample‑level confidence from LALM logits or CLAP score consistency) and evaluate on failure‑case subsets from BRACE, measuring error rate reduction while maintaining overall accuracy.

### Open Question 3
How does CAF‑Score generalize to other audio‑text alignment tasks (e.g., environmental sound tagging, music captioning, spoken audio summarization) beyond captioning and text‑to‑audio, and what task‑specific adaptations are necessary?  
- *Basis:* The paper extends CAF‑Score to text‑to‑audio (Section 10) but acknowledges limited benchmark diversity (Section 7) and reliance on BRACE, which focuses on caption‑like descriptions. Generalizability to non‑caption tasks remains untested.  
- *Why unresolved:* CAF‑Score’s prompts and CLAP alignment were designed for descriptive captions; tasks with shorter labels (tagging) or domain‑specific semantics (music) may require different prompting or CLAP fine‑tuning, but this is unexplored.  
- *What evidence would resolve it:* Evaluate CAF‑Score on diverse datasets (e.g., ESC‑50 for tagging, MusicCaps for music) with minimal prompt adaptations, analyzing performance drops and identifying necessary modifications (e.g., task‑specific LALM instructions or CLAP retraining).

## Limitations
- **Prompt and tokenization fragility in FLEUR:** The LALM prompt template and token mapping for decimal digits are not fully specified; different LALMs may tokenize scores differently, which could break the probability aggregation.
- **Fixed fusion weight (α=0.8) may not generalize:** The optimal weight was tuned on BRACE‑Main and may fail on out‑of‑distribution data; the paper’s adaptive α (based on FLEUR entropy) underperformed, but other adaptive schemes remain unexplored.
- **Sliding‑window parameters are heuristic:** Window length (7–10 s) and hop (1 s) are chosen based on CLAP training constraints, not systematically optimized; max‑pooling assumes brief salient events, which may degrade performance on persistent background sounds.

## Confidence
- **S‑CLAPScore (max‑pooled sliding windows):** Medium‑High confidence (consistent gains in Table 2, but break conditions under‑tested).
- **FLEUR (probabilistic smoothing):** Medium confidence (reduces ties but tokenization‑sensitive; Fig 4 and Table 1 support).
- **Fixed α=0.8 fusion:** Medium confidence (hybrid beats pure components in Fig 3, but fixed weight may not generalize).
- **Generalization to text‑to‑audio:** Low‑Medium confidence (improvements on RELATE/PAM are promising but limited to two benchmarks).

## Next Checks
- **Tokenization‑robust FLEUR:** Re‑implement FLEUR using a tokenization‑agnostic approach (e.g., parse LALM’s string output “0.85” and retrieve probabilities for the corresponding token sequence via logit processing). Validate that scores remain stable across tokenizers and that accuracy matches or exceeds the original method.
- **Adaptive per‑sample α:** Design an adaptive fusion weight based on audio features (e.g., event density from S‑CLAPScore variance) or caption complexity (e.g., length, syntactic depth). Benchmark against fixed α=0.8 on BRACE’s Hallucination subset and a held‑out domain (e.g., environmental sounds with long backgrounds).
- **Prompt optimization per task:** Systematically vary the LALM prompt for captioning and TTA tasks (e.g., add instructions to “ignore background noise” or “focus on temporal order”). Compare CAF‑Score correlations against the fixed prompt to quantify prompt sensitivity.
