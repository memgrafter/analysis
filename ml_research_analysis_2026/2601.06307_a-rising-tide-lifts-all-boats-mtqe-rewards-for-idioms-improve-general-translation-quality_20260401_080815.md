# A Rising Tide Lifts All Boats: MTQE Rewards for Idioms Improve General Translation Quality
## Quick Facts
* arXiv ID: 2601.06307
* Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06307
* Reference count: 13
* Primary result: GRPO fine-tuning with MTQE reward models improves idiom translation by ~14 points and general non-idiomatic translation by ~8 points across Chinese and Hindi, with positive cross-lingual transfer.

## Executive Summary
Neural machine translation systems struggle with non-compositional expressions like idioms, often producing literal, nonsensical outputs. This paper addresses this by using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with a Machine Translation Quality Estimation (MTQE) model as a reward function. Trained on Chinese and Hindi idiom datasets, the method yields significant absolute gains in idiom-specific translation quality (~14 points) and, as a side effect, improves general translation (~8 points) and cross-lingual transfer (~6 points). The gains hold across Qwen-2.5-3B and Llama-3.1-8B models, demonstrating that MTQE rewards effectively distill robust multilingual semantic representations.

## Method Summary
The core method applies GRPO reinforcement learning fine-tuning to a base LLM translator. An MTQE model (e.g., COMET) acts as a frozen reward function, scoring candidate translations (`mt`) against the source idiom (reference-free) or a reference translation (reference-based). Four reward variants are explored: positive semantic equivalence (`QE-Positive`), negative literal translation penalty (`QE-Negative`), their combination (`QE-Constrained`), and reference-based (`QE-DA`). Training data comprises idiom pairs: Chinese from PETCI (1k train) and Hindi from OPUS OpenSubtitles plus GPT-5 synthetic data (800 train). Baselines include supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and a training-free structured prompting baseline (LIA). Evaluation aggregates five metrics—Direct Assessment (DA), Quality Estimation (QE), ROUGE-L, embedding similarity, and LLM-as-judge (LAJ)—on held-out idiom tests, a general Opus-100 sample, and cross-lingual splits.

## Key Results
* Idiom translation quality improved by approximately 14 absolute points across aggregated metrics vs. base models.
* General non-idiomatic translation quality implicitly improved by about 8 points.
* Cross-lingual transfer ability (training on one language, evaluating on the other) improved by around 6 points.
* Gains were consistent for both Qwen-2.5-3B and Llama-3.1-8B models and across all four MTQE reward formulations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: MTQE Reward Distills Non-Compositional Understanding
Claim: MTQE reward models distill non-compositional semantic understanding into the LLM via GRPO fine-tuning.
Mechanism: The MTQE model, trained on human-parallel data, provides a differentiable reward signal `r = MTQE(src_idiom, mt)`. GRPO optimizes the LLM's policy to maximize expected reward over sampled completions. This "weak distillation" teaches the LLM to produce translations aligned with the MTQE's learned notion of semantic equivalence for idioms, bypassing the need for explicit gold references during training.
Core assumption: MTQE models have internalized patterns of correct idiom translation from their training on human-annotated quality scores and parallel corpora, making their scores a proxy for cultural/figurative accuracy.
Evidence anchors: Abstract states idiom translation abilities improve by ~14 points using MTQE rewards; Section 2.1 notes MTQE models are trained on human preference data containing implicit signals of non-compositional phrases.
Break condition: If the MTQE model's training data lacks coverage of non-compositional expressions or is biased toward literal text, the reward signal will fail to guide the LLM toward idiomatic translations.

### Mechanism 2: Implicit Generalization to Non-Idiomatic Text
Claim: Training on idiom translation with MTQE rewards implicitly improves general (non-idiomatic) translation quality.
Mechanism: The GRPO process, guided by semantic similarity rewards, encourages the LLM to refine its cross-lingual representation space. Optimization pressure to satisfy the MTQE reward for complex, non-compositional cases likely enhances the model's ability to align meanings more robustly across languages overall, benefiting simpler compositional sentences as a side effect.
Core assumption: The underlying cross-lingual semantic representation is shared; strengthening it for difficult cases (idioms) generalizes to easier cases.
Evidence anchors: Abstract reports general non-idiomatic translation implicitly improves by ~8 points; Section 4.2 states performance on non-idiomatic sentence translation does not deteriorate and improves with GRPO models.
Break condition: If optimization becomes too specialized (overfitted) to the idiom distribution, general translation performance would degrade rather than improve.

### Mechanism 3: Cross-Lingual Transfer via Abstract Representations
Claim: GRPO fine-tuning with MTQE rewards on one language's idioms improves cross-lingual transfer ability.
Mechanism: The RL fine-tuning, driven by a language-agnostic semantic reward (MTQE scores), forces the LLM to develop more abstract, language-independent representations. The model learns to map source idioms to target meanings in a way not tied to surface forms of the training language, allowing it to apply this improved mapping capability to a different source language during zero-shot evaluation.
Core assumption: The MTQE reward function operates on a shared multilingual semantic embedding space; optimizing for high reward in this space for Language A idioms improves alignment in that space for Language B idioms.
Evidence anchors: Abstract notes cross-lingual translation abilities improve by ~6 points; Section 4.2 (Figures 7 & 8) shows consistent gains for cross-lingual evaluation (e.g., Chinese-trained models translating Hindi idioms).
Break condition: If languages are typologically very distant and the base model's representations for the two languages are poorly aligned, the transfer benefit may not materialize. The reward signal must operate on a space where both languages' idioms are meaningfully comparable.

## Foundational Learning
* **MTQE/Quality Estimation Models**
  * Why needed: The entire training signal (reward) is derived from the MTQE model. It outputs a scalar score representing semantic equivalence (not grammatical correctness) and is reference-free or reference-based. Misinterpreting its output as a fluency score would break the mechanism.
  * Quick check: Given an idiom and a literal translation, would a reference-free MTQE (like COMET-Kiwi) rate the literal translation higher or lower than a culturally appropriate idiomatic translation? Why?
* **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**
  * Why needed: GRPO is the specific RL algorithm used. It optimizes the policy relative to a group of sampled completions per step, crucial for stable training with a sparse/delayed reward signal like MTQE. It is not standard PPO.
  * Quick check: How does GRPO's group-relative advantage calculation differ from standard PPO's, and why might this be more suitable when the reward model (MTQE) is noisy?
* **Non-Compositionality**
  * Why needed: This is the core problem class. An engineer must grasp that idioms violate the principle of meaning being a function of parts+structure. A model that only learns compositional patterns will fail. This justifies the need for a specialized reward.
  * Quick check: For the Hindi idiom "पानी-पानी होना" (literal: "to become water-water", idiomatic: "to feel embarrassed"), why would a model trained only on compositional text likely translate it incorrectly?

## Architecture Onboarding
LLM (Policy) -> MTQE (Reward) -> GRPO Module -> Updated LLM. Dataset provides (source idiom, reference, literal gloss) tuples.
Critical path: 1) Sample batch of source idioms. 2) For each idiom, have LLM generate *k* completions (e.g., k=4). 3) For each `(src, mt)` pair, query the fixed MTQE model to get reward `r`. 4) Compute group-relative advantages within each set of *k* completions for the same `src`. 5) Perform policy gradient update on LLM using these advantages and a KL penalty (vs. initial reference policy).
Design tradeoffs:
* MTQE Choice: Reference-free (QE-Positive) is cheaper (no gold ref needed) but noisier. Reference-based (QE-DA) is more accurate but requires expensive gold references for all training idioms.
* Reward Shaping: QE-Constrained (`QE-Pos - QE-Neg`) is clever but relies on a separate process to generate accurate literal translations (`literal`). Errors in `literal` generation can mislead the reward.
* Compute vs. Data: Training took 6-12 hrs on 4xH100s. Using smaller models (3B/8B) was a tradeoff to keep this feasible, but limits absolute performance ceiling.
Failure signatures:
* Reward Hacking: LLM learns to exploit MTQE quirks (e.g., produce high-frequency n-grams that score well semantically but are nonsensical idiom translations). Monitor by sampling outputs and checking for unnatural text.
* Degraded General Translation: If the KL penalty coefficient is too low, the policy overfits to idiom data and collapses on Opus-100 (non-idiom) evaluation. Check Fig 5/6 vs. Base model.
* Cross-lingual Failure: If training on Chinese yields no gain on Hindi (or vice versa), it suggests the learned representations are not abstract enough. Check Fig 7/8 for positive transfer.
First 3 experiments:
1. **MTQE Validation**: Before any RL, manually inspect a sample of idiom pairs. Score them with your chosen MTQE model (e.g., COMET). Do scores correlate with your judgment of a "good" idiomatic translation vs. a literal one? This validates the reward signal.
2. **Sanity Check GRPO Loop**: On a tiny subset (e.g., 10 idioms), run one full GRPO step. Verify that the reward scores for "good" completions are higher than for "bad" ones, and that the policy loss decreases. Ensures the pipeline is wired correctly.
3. **Ablation: Reward Type**: Train three tiny models (1 epoch) with QE-Positive, QE-Negative, and QE-Constrained on the same 100 examples. Evaluate on a held-out set. Does the constrained reward produce outputs that are (a) semantic close to the idiom, and (b) distant from the literal gloss? Confirms the reward shaping intuition.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
* Question: How can the computational cost of GRPO-based fine-tuning for multilingual translation be reduced?
* Basis in paper: [explicit] Limitations: "Future work involves understanding how to reduce the computational cost... training took around 6-12 hours on 4 NVIDIA H100s."
* Why unresolved: Reinforcement learning is sample-inefficient; current hardware demands hinder accessibility and scalability.
* What evidence would resolve it: Develop more efficient RL algorithms or non-RL alternatives that match performance gains with fewer resources; test on larger models and more languages.

### Open Question 2
* Question: How can the upper bound imposed by MTQE model performance be overcome?
* Basis in paper: [explicit] Limitations: "our method is upper-bounded by the performance of MTQE models – the GRPO-trained translation models can only be as good as the MTQE models are."
* Why unresolved: MTQE accuracy limits final translation quality; improving MTQE or finding alternative rewards could lift this ceiling.
* What evidence would resolve it: Train more accurate MTQE models on richer data or replace MTQE with other reward signals (e.g., human judgments) and demonstrate improved translation quality.

### Open Question 3
* Question: What mechanism explains the cross-lingual transfer of idiom translation abilities observed when training on a single language?
* Basis in paper: [explicit] Conclusion: "future work to understanding the complementary nature of mapping semantics in various languages..." and results show ~6 point cross-lingual improvement.
* Why unresolved: The paper demonstrates transfer but does not clarify whether it arises from shared semantic representations, enhanced general translation skills, or other factors.
* What evidence would resolve it: Probing analyses of multilingual representations; controlled experiments with multiple language pairs varying in semantic relatedness; isolating transfer for idiomatic vs. non-idiomatic text.

### Open Question 4
* Question: Why do different MTQE reward formulations (QE-Positive, QE-Negative, QE-Constrained, QE-DA) yield similar performance improvements despite distinct supervision signals?
* Basis in paper: [inferred] Results: "there are no noticeable effects of each reward" even though designs differ (e.g., QE-DA uses references, QE-Positive does not).
* Why unresolved: Similar outcomes suggest either redundancy in reward signals or that the mere presence of MTQE supervision is the key driver; the relative contribution of each component is unknown.
* What evidence would resolve it: Ablation studies removing positive/negative components; gradient analysis of policy updates; testing on idiom subsets where reward signals diverge significantly.

### Open Question 5
* Question: What factors determine the inconsistent effectiveness of structured prompting across base models (works for Qwen-2.5-3B but not Llama-3.1-8B)?
* Basis in paper: [inferred] Results: "Qwen performs better with LIA ... while Llama performs better with TrainingFree" indicating prompting reliability varies by model.
* Why unresolved: Prompting is training-free and cheap, but its unpredictability limits utility; understanding model-specific responses could identify when it is viable.
* What evidence would resolve it: Correlate performance with model properties (size, instruction-tuning, pretraining data); systematic prompt engineering studies; evaluating inherent idiom knowledge in each model.

## Limitations
* Missing critical reproducibility details: GRPO hyperparameters (learning rate, KL penalty coefficient, batch size), reward normalization, random seeds, and exact MTQE checkpoints used.
* Unspecified source of literal translations for the QE-Negative reward during training.
* Evaluation sampling (Opus-100 400 pairs) lacks seed, risking selection bias; potential metric miscalibration across languages.

## Confidence
* Idiom translation improvement: Medium (strong multi-metric gains but dependent on unvalidated reward signal and training details).
* General translation improvement: Medium (plausible yet could be driven by metric artifacts; needs human validation).
* Cross-lingual transfer: Medium (observed for Chinese↔Hindi but may not generalize to other language pairs).
