# Debiasing Central Fixation Confounds Reveals a Peripheral "Sweet Spot" for Human-like Scanpaths in Hard-Attention Vision

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.14834
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14834
- **Reference count:** 10
- **Primary result:** A center-debiased Gaze Consistency Score (GCS) identifies a peripheral sweet spot (intermediate foveal patch size with moderate peripheral context) in a hard-attention model where scanpaths exhibit both debiased human-like spatial alignment and human-like movement dynamics.

## Executive Summary
Standard scanpath similarity metrics (e.g., NSS, AUC) are inflated on object-centric gaze datasets due to strong center bias in human fixations, allowing trivial center-fixation policies to appear human-like. This paper introduces GCS, a composite metric that normalizes scanpath similarity against human–human and corner baselines and incorporates movement statistics to mitigate center bias. Applying GCS to a hard-attention classifier (MRAM) under varying sensory constraints reveals a "peripheral sweet spot": only an intermediate foveal patch size (e.g., 8 pixels) combined with moderate peripheral context yields human-like scanpaths after debiasing. Larger fields-of-view induce a shortcut regime with high accuracy but poor behavioral alignment, highlighting a trade-off between recognition performance and genuine human-like scanning.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates the Multi-Level Recurrent Attention Model (MRAM), a hard-attention classifier that actively selects 12 fixation locations to classify CIFAR-10 images using a foveated glimpse mechanism. The agent’s sensory input can be configured: a foveal high-resolution patch only (`fov_only`), foveal plus a low-resolution peripheral patch covering a wider area (`fov+per`), or a wide peripheral-dominated field (`big_per`). Across a sweep of foveal patch sizes (2–32 pixels), the authors compute standard scanpath metrics (DTW, ScanMatch, NSS, AUC) between model-generated fixation sequences and human data from Gaze-CIFAR-10. To address center bias, they derive a center-debiased normalized score for each metric by min-max scaling between corner (minimum) and identical-human (maximum) baselines, then subtracting the normalized score of a constant-center policy. A movement similarity term, based on run-level statistics such as total path length and saccade amplitude, is added to form the composite Gaze Consistency Score (GCS). The final GCS averages the debiased metric scores plus a weighted movement similarity, providing a more reliable measure of human-like scanning behavior.

## Key Results
- A constant-center fixation policy achieves deceptively high raw NSS (1.145) on Gaze-CIFAR-10, confirming strong center bias confound.
- After debiasing with GCS, most MRAM configurations fall near or below zero; only a narrow range of intermediate foveal patch sizes (e.g., 8 pixels) with moderate peripheral context (`fov+per`) yields a positive GCS (~0.0291), indicating genuine alignment with human scanpaths.
- The sweet-spot model also exhibits movement statistics (total path, saccade amplitude, entropy) that are intermediate between human observers and highly active or static policies, while achieving moderate classification accuracy; larger fields-of-view favor accuracy at the expense of human-like dynamics.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Center bias inflates standard metrics
Standard scanpath metrics primarily measure spatial overlap between predicted and human fixation distributions. Because human gaze on object-centric datasets is strongly centered, any policy that fixates centrally—even without task-driven sequential structure—achieves high scores. This confounds true behavioral alignment with exploitation of a dataset artifact. Evidence: the center baseline attains NSS 1.145, close to many learned models, and far above the corner lower bound.

### Mechanism 2: GCS removes center bias by normalization and subtraction
GCS normalizes each base metric between the corner baseline (minimum) and identical-human baseline (maximum), then subtracts the normalized center baseline score. This penalizes policies that rely on central tendency. Additionally, a movement similarity term (`Sim_move`) compares the model’s run-level movement statistics to humans’ using a relative-error distance, further discouraging static central fixation. The composite GCS thus captures alignment beyond mere spatial central bias.

### Mechanism 3: Sensory constraints shape a peripheral sweet spot
The MRAM’s foveal patch size and peripheral context width govern the exploration–exploitation trade-off. Very narrow foveal views force excessive, inefficient saccades (high path length, low evidence gain). Very wide views enable shortcut recognition with minimal movement (low path length). Only an intermediate constraint (moderate foveal resolution with some peripheral context) balances active sampling and efficient evidence accumulation, producing movement statistics and debiased scanpath similarity that match human behavior.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Center bias in gaze data
- **Why needed here:** The core problem is that human fixations are not uniformly distributed but concentrate near the image center. This bias makes constant-center policies appear human-like under standard metrics, so understanding it is prerequisite to grasping the need for debiasing.
- **Quick check question:** Why does a constant-center policy achieve high NSS on Gaze-CIFAR-10?

### Concept: Hard-attention models as active perceivers
- **Why needed here:** The studied agent (MRAM) is a recurrent model that selects fixations to accumulate evidence for classification. This defines the class of policies being evaluated, as opposed to passive, whole-image classifiers.
- **Quick check question:** How does a hard-attention model differ from a passive CNN in processing an image?

### Concept: Scanpath similarity metrics
- **Why needed here:** The paper critiques and builds upon DTW, ScanMatch, NSS, AUC. Knowing what each captures—temporal alignment, spatial overlap, or distributional similarity—is essential to understand the design of GCS.
- **Quick check question:** What does a high NSS score indicate about a model's fixation map compared to humans?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Glimpse module -> Two-level recurrent state (fast sensorimotor h^(1), slow evidence accumulator h^(2)) -> Location policy (samples next fixation) -> Classification head

**Critical path:** The agent initializes at a fixed location, then for 12 steps: extract foveal (and optional peripheral) glimpse, update fast recurrent state, sample next fixation, update slow recurrent state. After the loop, the classification head predicts the image label.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Foveal patch size*: smaller patches require more steps to cover an object, risking inefficiency, but promote active exploration; larger patches risk passive recognition (shortcut).  
- *Peripheral context*: adds low-resolution wide-field information to guide saccades without sacrificing foveal acuity; too much peripheral context may allow the model to solve the task without moving.  
- *Two-level recurrence*: separates fast sensorimotor control from slow evidence integration, mirroring a proposed cognitive architecture; collapsing these levels might reduce flexibility.

**Failure signatures:**  
- *Shortcut regime*: Large peripheral FOV (e.g., `big_per`) yields high accuracy but minimal movement and negative GCS, indicating reliance on center bias or static recognition.  
- *Over-exploration*: Very small foveal patches (e.g., ps=2) produce long paths with low evidence gain; GCS remains low due to poor alignment with human movement patterns.  
- *Center exploitation*: Raw metrics close to the center baseline but far from the human–human upper bound, leading to near-zero debiased scores.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Generalizability of the peripheral sweet spot**  
   Does the identified sweet spot generalize beyond the MRAM architecture and Gaze-CIFAR-10 to other hard-attention models and datasets with different center bias levels? The current conclusions are limited to a specific architecture, fixed action horizon, and an object-centric, low-resolution dataset. Testing across varied architectures and datasets is needed.

2. **Human viewing geometry manipulation**  
   If human viewing geometry (e.g., viewing distance, image scale) is systematically varied, do human scanpaths shift across regimes like the model’s patch-size manipulation? The authors propose this as a testable prediction but it remains empirically unverified.

3. **Expanding movement descriptors in GCS**  
   Can incorporating additional movement statistics (e.g., micro-saccade patterns, fixation durations) improve discrimination between genuine behavioral alignment and center bias? The current GCS uses a fixed set; future work should explore richer descriptors.

4. **Task complexity and sweet spot stability**  
   For tasks demanding active information gathering beyond static classification (e.g., visual search, interactive decision-making), does the peripheral sweet spot persist, or does task complexity shift the optimal sensory constraint regime?

## Limitations
- The peripheral sweet spot’s low confidence stems from its dependence on a single model architecture, dataset, and fixed hyperparameters; generalizability is uncertain.
- The GCS metric’s validity relative to alternative debiasing approaches (e.g., explicit center-bias modeling) is not established.
- Many movement statistics used in GCS (e.g., collapse rate, coverage) lack precise mathematical definitions in the paper, hindering replication.

## Confidence
- **Center bias confound:** High confidence (directly evidenced by strong center baseline scores and prior literature).
- **GCS metric design:** Medium confidence (the debiasing transform is sound but not compared to alternatives).
- **Peripheral sweet spot:** Low confidence (specific to the experimental setting; open questions about generality).

## Next Checks
1. Replicate the center confound: Implement a constant-center policy and compute raw NSS/AUC on Gaze-CIFAR-10 test set; confirm scores are within 5% of the reported 1.145 (NSS).
2. Stress-test GCS sensitivity: Ablate the `Sim_move` term and recompute GCS across patch sizes; the sweet spot should diminish or disappear if movement statistics are critical.
3. Cross-validate the sweet spot: Train a different hard-attention model (e.g., a simpler RNN with learned glimpse policy) under the same `fov_only`/`fov+per` sweep on Gaze-CIFAR-10; a replicated sweet spot would strengthen generality beyond MRAM.
