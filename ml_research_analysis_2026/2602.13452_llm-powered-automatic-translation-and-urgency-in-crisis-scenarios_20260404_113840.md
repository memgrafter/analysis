# LLM-Powered Automatic Translation and Urgency in Crisis Scenarios

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.13452
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13452
- **Reference count:** 3
- **Primary result:** Crisis translation quality is low (spBLEU as low as 16.76) and LLM urgency classification is unstable across prompt languages, with only 14/32 languages achieving >80% agreement and a systematic bias toward higher urgency; even linguistically adequate translations distort urgency, posing significant deployment risks.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates state-of-the-art machine translation (MT) and large language models (LLMs) on multilingual crisis communication, focusing on preserving urgency. Using the TICO-19 dataset (39 languages) and a new UrgencyScenarios dataset (32 languages), the authors test NLLB-200, Aya101, Llama3.2, and X-ALMA. Results show poor translation quality, especially for low-resource languages (e.g., Tigrinya spBLEU = 16.76). Moreover, even when translations appear linguistically adequate, human annotators' perception of urgency frequently shifts post-translation. LLM-based urgency classification varies dramatically with the language of the prompt, with only 14 of 32 languages achieving over 80% classification rates and a consistent overestimation of urgency. The findings highlight critical risks in deploying general-purpose LLMs for crisis translation and automated triage without domain-specific adaptation.

## Method Summary
The evaluation uses two multilingual crisis resources: (1) TICO-19, a test set of COVID‑19 related sentences in 39 languages, and (2) the newly constructed UrgencyScenarios, which comprises 100 English crisis scenarios translated into 32 medium/low‑resource languages using NLLB‑200. Four models are benchmarked: NLLB‑200 (1.2B), Aya101, Llama3.2‑1B, and X‑ALMA. Translation quality is measured with spBLEU and chrF++ (both English→target and target→English). For urgency, human annotations (two annotators per language) are collected for four languages (Spanish, Greek, Bengali, Hindi) and compared to LLM classifications obtained by prompting Llama3.2‑1B in each target language with a fixed 6‑class urgency prompt. The analysis correlates translation scores with changes in human urgency judgments and examines classification consistency across prompt languages.

## Key Results
- Translation quality is generally low; Aya101 achieves mean spBLEU of 22.4 (en→target) and 41.4 (target→en), while even the best model, NLLB‑200, yields spBLEU below 21 for Tigrinya (16.76) and Sorani Kurdish (20.82).
- Human annotators show stable urgency judgments across languages, but LLM classifications vary widely by prompt language; only 14 of 32 languages exceed an 80% classification rate, and the model exhibits a systematic bias toward higher urgency levels.
- Even translations that score reasonably well on automatic metrics (e.g., spBLEU > 20) often distort perceived urgency, with human assessments shifting by one or more urgency levels after translation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Crisis-domain translation quality degrades substantially for both dedicated MT models and LLMs, especially for low-resource languages, making linguistically adequate outputs unreliable for critical communication.
- **Mechanism:** Models trained on general-domain data fail to capture domain-specific terminology, syntax, and semantic nuances in crisis messages. This leads to loss or distortion of key information (e.g., severity markers), quantified by low spBLEU/chrF++ scores.
- **Core assumption:** General-purpose models lack crisis-specific fine-tuning or data, causing lexical and semantic divergence from reference translations in high-stakes contexts.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "translation quality is low, with mean spBLEU scores of 22.4... NLLB-200 performs best but still fails in some languages, e.g., Tigrinya (16.76 spBLEU)"
  - [section] Table 1 & 2 show spBLEU <20 for multiple languages (e.g., Sorani Kurdish 20.82, Tigrinya 16.76), indicating "significant information loss."
  - [corpus] Weak; corpus signals show related work on LLM-MT but no direct evidence for mechanism.
- **Break condition:** When spBLEU falls below 20 for a target language, translations risk losing essential crisis-related semantics (e.g., medical terms, action verbs), rendering them unsafe for dissemination.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Even linguistically adequate translations can distort perceived urgency, altering risk perception and triaging decisions.
- **Mechanism:** Translation errors or lexical choices (e.g., translating "immediately" weakly) shift semantic weight of urgency cues. Human annotators' assessments change post-translation despite overall consistency.
- **Core assumption:** Urgency is conveyed via specific lexical/syntactic patterns that are fragile to translation; adequacy metrics (BLEU) do not capture this pragmatic dimension.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Even linguistically adequate translations distort perceived urgency"
  - [section] Figure 1 (left) shows human annotation changes pre/post translation; e.g., 'Critical'→'Not Urgent' in some cases.
  - [section] "hypothesis that the translation quality of some relevant keyword associated with ‘urgency’ might have affected the assessment."
- **Break condition:** When translation alters or omits urgency-indicative tokens (e.g., "evacuate now"→"consider evacuation"), human/LLM classification shifts by ≥1 urgency level.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** LLM-based urgency classification is highly unstable across prompt languages, with systematic bias toward higher urgency levels and low consistency, making automated triage unreliable.
- **Mechanism:** LLMs' internal representations of urgency are language-dependent; prompting in different languages triggers different response strategies (e.g., overcaution), leading to divergent classifications for identical scenarios.
- **Core assumption:** LLMs do not have a language-agnostic urgency concept; their few-shot classification behavior is sensitive to prompt language phrasing and cultural biases in training data.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "LLM classifications vary widely by prompt language, with only 14 of 32 languages achieving over 80% classification rates and a systematic bias toward higher urgency levels."
  - [section] Figure 1 (right): Scenario 2 classified as 'Not Urgent' in Chinese, 'High' in English, 'Critical' in Spanish.
  - [section] "LLM occasionally refuses to classify... hallucinates... only 14 achieved classification rate ≥80%."
- **Break condition:** When classification agreement (same scenario, different prompt languages) falls below 70% or bias ratio (overestimation of Medium/Critical) exceeds 60%.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**  
- Datasets: TICO-19 (crisis MT), UrgencyScenarios (multilingual urgency labels).  
- Models: NLLB-200 (dedicated MT), Aya101/Llama3.2 (generic LLMs), X-ALMA (LLM-powered MT).  
- Evaluation: spBLEU/chrF++ for translation; human/LLM annotation for urgency classification; cross-lingual consistency checks.

**Critical path**  
1. Run MT models on TICO-19 test set (en→X, X→en).  
2. Generate translations of UrgencyScenarios for 32 languages.  
3. Collect human urgency annotations (4 languages) and LLM annotations (all 32) on translated scenarios.  
4. Analyze: (a) translation quality vs. urgency distortion correlation; (b) LLM classification agreement across prompt languages.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Model selection: NLLB-200 trades broader language coverage for sometimes lower peak performance vs. X-ALMA’s volatility.  
- Evaluation depth: Automated metrics (BLEU) are cheap but miss urgency; human annotation is accurate but limited to few languages.  
- Pipeline design: Direct LLM classification in target language vs. translate-to-English-then-classify— cascading errors vs. prompt-language instability.

**Failure signatures**  
- spBLEU < 20 in any target language → do not deploy for information dissemination.  
- LLM classification rate < 80% in a prompt language → avoid automated triage in that language.  
- Urgency label shift frequency > 30% between original and translated version → translation breaks pragmatic intent.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. Low-resource language MT stress test: Run all models on Tigrinya and Sorani Kurdish (from TICO-19); quantify token-level errors in crisis terms (e.g., "quarantine", "evacuate").  
2. Urgency keyword perturbation: In UrgencyScenarios, replace urgency markers (e.g., "immediately"→"soon") in source; measure classification shift in humans vs. LLM across languages.  
3. Prompt-language ablation: For 10 languages, prompt LLM with identical scenario but vary instruction language (e.g., classify in Spanish vs. English prompt); compute pairwise agreement and bias toward higher classes.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1:** What specific linguistic or structural features in crisis-domain text cause the most significant degradation in translation quality for low-resource languages, beyond what general multilingual models like NLLB-200 can handle?  
*Basis in paper:* The paper highlights catastrophic failures for languages like Tigrinya (16.76 spBLEU) and Sorani Kurdish (20.82 spBLEU) even with NLLB-200, and notes X-ALMA/Llama3.2 exhibit "high performance volatility" and "collapse" for certain regions. It states that "crisis messages are often brief, emotionally charged, and time-critical" and involve "underrepresented" languages.  
*Why unresolved:* The study measures overall quality (spBLEU/chrF++) but does not conduct fine-grained error analysis to isolate whether failures stem from lack of crisis-specific terminology, morphological complexity, script issues, or data scarcity during model training. Understanding the primary failure modes is critical for targeted model improvement.

**Open Question 2:** To what extent does the automatic translation process itself (using NLLB-200) alter the perceived urgency of crisis scenarios in the UrgencyScenarios dataset, and can this distortion be formally modeled or predicted from translation quality metrics?  
*Basis in paper:* The paper finds that "even linguistically adequate translations can distort perceived urgency" and shows human annotators' urgency assessments change after translation (Figure 1, left). The dataset was created by translating English scenarios with NLLB-200, but the translation quality of these specific sentences per language is not reported or correlated with the observed annotation shifts.  
*Why unresolved:* The study demonstrates the phenomenon but does not establish a causal or predictive link between translation quality (as measured by automatic metrics or human judgment) and the magnitude/direction of urgency distortion. It hypothesizes that "translation quality of some relevant keyword associated with ‘urgency’ might have affected the assessment" but does not test this.

**Open Question 3:** What are the underlying causes of the prompt-language-dependent instability in LLM-based urgency classification, and can this be mitigated through prompt design, model fine-tuning, or a translation-then-classify pipeline?  
*Basis in paper:* The paper shows Llama3.2's urgency classifications vary widely by prompt language, with only 14/32 languages achieving >80% classification rates and a "systematic bias toward higher urgency levels." It briefly mentions a "hypothetical solution could first translate into English, and then query the LLM in English" but states this suffers from "cascading errors."  
*Why unresolved:* The root cause of the instability (e.g., model's multilingual encoding biases, prompt template sensitivity, lack of crisis-domain fine-tuning, or inherent cultural variance in urgency expression) is not investigated. The viability of the translate-then-classify approach is not empirically tested against direct classification, nor are mitigation strategies explored.

**Open Question 4:** Does the observed "language-independent" human understanding of urgency hold across a larger, more culturally diverse set of annotators and crisis scenarios, and are there cultural frameworks that systematically modulate urgency perception?  
*Basis in paper:* The paper claims urgency assessment is "in general language-independent" based on high agreement among 2 annotators per language for only 4 languages (Spanish, Greek, Bengali, Hindi). This is a very limited sample for making a broad cross-linguistic/cultural claim. The dataset creation used ChatGPT to generate scenarios based on an English prompt, which may embed Anglo-Saxon crisis norms.  
*Why unresolved:* The sample size per language (n=2) and number of languages (n=4) is too small to generalize about universal urgency perception. The scenarios were generated from an English-centric seed, potentially lacking cultural diversity in crisis expression. The paper does not explore if "urgency" is conflated with concepts like "immediacy" or "severity" differently across cultures.

**Open Question 5:** Can dedicated, crisis-aware fine-tuning or architecture modifications significantly improve both translation quality and urgency preservation in multilingual crisis communication models, and what should the target evaluation framework be?  
*Basis in paper:* The conclusion strongly argues for "crisis-aware benchmarks, evaluation criteria, and model design practices." It shows current SOTA models (NLLB, Aya, Llama, X-ALMA) are insufficient. However, it does not propose or test any specific model adaptations or a comprehensive evaluation suite beyond standard MT metrics and a simplified urgency classification task.  
*Why unresolved:* The paper diagnoses the problem but does not explore solutions. It does not investigate whether fine-tuning on crisis data (like TICO-19) improves urgency preservation, or if model architectures need modifications (e.g., multi-task learning for translation+urgency tagging). The "crisis-aware evaluation framework" is not defined—should it include metrics for urgency preservation, safety, robustness to noise, etc.?

## Limitations
- The exact list of 32 languages in the UrgencyScenarios dataset is not disclosed, hindering full reproducibility and assessment of language coverage.
- Prompt language and formatting details for LLM urgency classification are underspecified; minor variations could shift results.
- Human urgency annotations were collected for only four languages, limiting the generalizability of the observed distortion effect.
- The specific NLLB‑200 variant used to generate UrgencyScenarios translations is unreported, creating uncertainty about baseline translation quality.
- Dependence on automatic metrics (spBLEU/chrF++) may not fully capture crisis‑specific semantic errors or urgency‑relevant nuances.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1** (Crisis‑domain translation quality degrades): High confidence.  
- **Mechanism 2** (Urgency distorts even with adequate translations): Medium confidence.  
- **Mechanism 3** (LLM urgency classification unstable across prompt languages): Medium confidence.

## Next Checks
1. **Full‑language human annotation:** Conduct human urgency labeling for all 32 UrgencyScenarios languages; compute pre/post‑translation agreement to measure distortion per language.  
2. **Prompt‑language ablation:** Systematically vary instruction language and class label translations for a single LLM to separate prompt‑phrasing effects from inherent model bias.  
3. **Multi‑model instability check:** Evaluate three additional multilingual LLMs (e.g., BLOOM, mT0, Gemini) on the same 32‑language set; test whether classification instability is model‑agnostic.
