# Introducing MELI: the Mandarin-English Language Interview Corpus

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.27043
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27043
*   **Reference Count:** 0
*   **Primary Result:** A new open-source bilingual corpus providing 29.8 hours of studio-quality, force-aligned speech from 51 Mandarin-English bilinguals, linked to detailed language attitude metadata to support within-speaker cross-language acoustic analysis.

## Executive Summary
The MELI (Mandarin-English Language Interview) Corpus is a novel, open-source resource designed to fill a critical gap in computational sociophonetics: high-quality bilingual speech data that tightly couples acoustic information with speakers' sociolinguistic context and attitudes. It comprises 29.8 hours of recordings from 51 Mandarin-English bilinguals, each completing parallel sessions in both languages. These sessions include both structured read sentences and spontaneous, interviewer-led discussions about language varieties and standardness. The audio is meticulously transcribed and force-aligned at the word and phone levels. A key feature is its parallel-mode design, where each speaker performs identical tasks in both languages, controlling for individual vocal characteristics. This structure, combined with rich metadata on language background and interview-derived attitude data, enables sophisticated analyses of how linguistic context and personal ideology intersect with phonetic realization, particularly in code-switching behavior.

## Method Summary
The corpus was constructed through a multi-stage hybrid annotation pipeline. First, stereo recordings were segmented to isolate the participant's audio channel and split sessions by language. An initial transcription was generated using the Whisper large-v3 ASR model. This output underwent rigorous manual correction by bilingual research assistants in ELAN, who standardized orthography, added tags for code-switches and non-speech events, and resolved errors. The corrected transcripts were then used as input for the Montreal Forced Aligner (MFA), which produced precise word- and phone-level timings using language-specific acoustic models and dictionaries. Custom dictionaries were manually updated to handle out-of-vocabulary items. Finally, an anonymization layer was applied, where participants could request specific redactions; corresponding audio segments were muted and replaced with a tone, and transcript intervals were marked as `[REDACTED]`. The final release package includes anonymized audio, final TextGrids, metadata, and interview materials per speaker.

## Key Results
*   **Corpus Size:** 29.8 total hours (14.7h Mandarin, 15.1h English), with a mean of ~17.5 minutes of speech per speaker per language.
*   **Linguistic Content:** Rich token/type statistics are reported for both languages. Code-switching is frequent in Mandarin interviews but markedly limited in English interviews.
*   **Analytic Potential:** The parallel design and multi-level alignments support within-speaker, cross-language acoustic comparisons and sociophonetic investigation linked to structured attitude data.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Controlled Cross-Language Comparison
The parallel bilingual design, where each speaker completes matched read and interview tasks in both languages, holds constant speaker-intrinsic factors (vocal tract, habitual rate) and recording conditions. This allows differences in acoustic measures (e.g., vowel formants) to be more confidently attributed to language-specific articulatory settings rather than individual speaker variance. A critical assumption is that the matched tasks elicit sufficiently analogous speaking styles across languages. A break condition would be if the interview prompts inadvertently induced systematically different discourse styles or emotional valence in the two language sessions, contaminating comparisons with style variance.

### Mechanism 2: Linking Acoustics to Attitude
The interviews explicitly query speakers on language varieties, standardness, and learning experiences. The resulting transcript, time-aligned to the audio, provides a contextual anchor. Researchers can locate moments where a speaker expresses a specific attitude (e.g., "Shanghai Mandarin is sloppy") and then analyze the phonetic tokens produced before, after, or within that same discourse turn. This enables testing hypotheses about whether stated ideologies correlate with real-time speech production. The core assumption is that expressed attitudes systematically covary with acoustic behavior. A break condition is if the correlation is spurious; the interview context may elicit socially desirable attitudes that do not align with habitual speech.

### Mechanism 3: Scalable Hybrid Annotation
The pipeline leverages Whisper for efficient initial transcription, human correction for accuracy on bilingual code-switching and disfluencies, and MFA for precise, phone-level timing. This hybrid approach balances feasibility (avoiding full manual transcription of ~30h) with the phonetic precision required for fine-grained acoustic analysis (e.g., VOT, vowel duration). The critical assumption is that human correction sufficiently sanitizes Whisper output, resulting in low-error alignments. A break condition is systematic alignment failures, particularly at code-switch boundaries or for items poorly represented in the MFA dictionaries.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Parallel-Mode Bilingual Corpus Design**
    *   **Why needed:** To study *within-speaker* cross-language phonetic influence (e.g., how a speaker's Mandarin vowel space relates to their English vowel space), one must control for speaker-intrinsic factors like vocal tract size. A corpus with matched tasks across both languages for the same speaker is essential.
    *   **Quick check:** Why is comparing a monolingual Mandarin corpus with a monolingual L2 English corpus insufficient for studying bilingual speech production?
*   **Concept: Forced Alignment & Pronunciation Dictionaries**
    *   **Why needed:** Acoustic analysis of specific phones requires exact start/end times in the audio. Forced alignment maps an orthographic transcript to audio using an acoustic model and a dictionary linking words to phones. The MELI process required custom dictionaries for both languages to handle out-of-vocabulary items.
    *   **Quick check:** What is the consequence for acoustic measurement if the pronunciation dictionary lacks a word a speaker actually produced?
*   **Concept: Sociolinguistic Metadata as Analytical Covariate**
    *   **Why needed:** The corpus links acoustic data to structured background (age of acquisition, self-rated proficiency) and attitudinal data (from interviews). This allows testing if, for example, earlier English AoA correlates with different English vowel formants, or if identification with a "standard" Mandarin variety correlates with less local phonetic variation.
    *   **Quick check:** Beyond speaker description, how does a language background questionnaire quantitatively inform an acoustic study of L2 English vowels?

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component Map:**
    Raw WAV (stereo) -> Segmentation (extract participant channel, split by language) -> Whisper-large-v3 (initial utterance-level transcript) -> Human Correction (ELAN: fix errors, add tags) -> Montreal Forced Aligner (word & phone boundaries) -> Anonymization (audio mute/tone replace, transcript redaction) -> Release Package (per-speaker dirs with audio, TextGrids, metadata).
*   **Critical Path:** Recording -> Segmentation -> Whisper -> **Human Correction** -> MFA -> Anonymization. The human correction step is the central bottleneck and primary quality control gate, as errors here propagate to alignment.
*   **Design Tradeoffs:**
    *   **Whisper vs. Manual Transcription:** Whisper provides speed and consistency but requires meticulous review, especially for code-switches and accented speech. Full manual transcription was infeasible.
    *   **Alignment Granularity:** Phone-level alignments are provided, but MFA uses different phonetic symbol sets for Mandarin (IPA) and English (ARPA), requiring careful feature-level mapping for cross-language comparison.
    *   **Anonymization Depth:** Audio redaction (tone replacement) preserves timing for alignment utility but may slightly alter adjacent acoustic context. Transcript redaction (`[REDACTED]`) removes text but leaves boundary marks.
*   **Failure Signatures:**
    *   **Alignment drift:** Systematic timing errors in phone boundaries, often at word boundaries or disfluencies. Detect by sampling high-variability segments (code-switches, fillers).
    *   **Metadata-attitude mismatch:** Incongruence between structured metadata (e.g., high proficiency claim) and interview content (e.g., negative attitudes), indicating poor question design or participant performance.
    *   **Redaction cascade:** Over-redaction of common terms (e.g., a university name) creates large `[REDACTED]` gaps, reducing usable data for surrounding conversational turns.
*   **First 3 Experiments:**
    1.  **Alignment Validation Study:** Manually measure 50 phone durations from a subset and compare to MFA outputs to quantify mean absolute error and bias.
    2.  **Code-Switching Boundary Analysis:** Using `@` tags, extract switch points and compare phonetic characteristics (duration, spectral properties) of pre-/post-switch boundaries to non-switch boundaries.
    3.  **Metadata-Acoustic Correlation:** For English vowels in read sentences, run a linear mixed model with F1/F2 as DVs, and L2 proficiency (self-rated), AoA, and Mandarin hometown region as fixed effects, speaker as random effect.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Question 1:** How do acoustic features of Mandarin (e.g., tone realization, vowel quality) vary with speakers' self-identified regional origins, and does this variation differ between read sentences and spontaneous interviews?
    *   *Basis:* Corpus includes diverse regional origins and aligned data for both styles.
    *   *Why Unresolved:* Only token/type counts and code-switching frequencies are reported; no acoustic analysis linking region to phonetic measures.
    *   *Resolution Evidence:* Measure acoustic variables (tone contours, vowel formants) and model their association with regional identifiers and speaking style.
*   **Question 2:** To what extent do English proficiency (self-rated and age of acquisition) and Mandarin regional background predict acoustic correlates of foreign accent in L2 English speech?
    *   *Basis:* Participants completed language background questionnaires; corpus includes parallel English recordings.
    *   *Why Unresolved:* Descriptive proficiency stats are provided, but no analysis connects them to accent-related acoustic measures.
    *   *Resolution Evidence:* Compute accent features from English alignments and regress them on proficiency, AoA, and Mandarin regional variables.
*   **Question 3:** What sociolinguistic factors (e.g., language dominance, interview topic, attitudes toward code-switching) explain the marked asymmetry in code-switching frequency between Mandarin and English interviews?
    *   *Basis:* Paper documents frequent Mandarin-switching but limited English-switching; interviews include attitude questions.
    *   *Why Unresolved:* Asymmetry is noted but not analyzed; corpus contains transcript tags and attitude data but no causal investigation.
    *   *Resolution Evidence:* Quantify code-switching rates from transcripts and correlate with dominance indicators, coded topics, and sentiment/mentions of code-switching in attitude responses.
*   **Question 4:** Do speakers' stated language attitudes toward Mandarin and English standardness predict phonetic alignment with those standards in their own speech production?
    *   *Basis:* Corpus design links acoustic data to interviews about standardness and varieties.
    *   *Why Unresolved:* Attitudes are collected but not tested for correlation with speakers' own acoustic adherence to standards.
    *   *Resolution Evidence:* Derive attitude scores from interview transcripts and examine their relationship with acoustic distance from standard reference models in both languages.

## Limitations
*   **Interview Protocol Opacity:** Exact wording, follow-up prompts, and randomization of interview questions are not publicly documented, limiting replication and potentially affecting cross-session comparability.
*   **Annotation Pipeline Specificity:** Critical hand-correction rules for dialectal pronunciations, rare code-switch tags, and disfluency normalization are not exhaustively specified, creating reproducibility risk for the annotation quality gate.
*   **Anonymization Scope Ambiguity:** Criteria for what constitutes "personal information" for redaction (e.g., city names vs. regions) are not formalized, risking inconsistency in the release package.

## Confidence
*   **Claim 1: Parallel design enables valid within-speaker cross-language acoustic comparison.**
    *   **Confidence: Medium.** Design rigorously controls speaker/equipment, but the break condition (unintended style/emotional differences between language interviews) is a significant, unaddressed threat.
*   **Claim 2: Corpus links acoustic data to stated language attitudes for sociophonetic analysis.**
    *   **Confidence: Low to Medium.** Infrastructure is in place, but the fundamental link between *stated* attitudes and *realized* speech is an empirical question the corpus merely enables, not validates.
*   **Claim 3: Hybrid annotation pipeline yields phone-level alignments suitable for fine-grained acoustic analysis.**
    *   **Confidence: Medium.** Pipeline is logical and state-of-the-art, but confidence is reduced by the central, unreported role of human correction and lack of presented validation data (e.g., alignment error rates).

## Next Checks
1.  **Alignment Validation at Code-Switch Boundaries:** Manually measure phone-boundary timing error for 100 instances of Mandarin-to-English and English-to-Mandarin switches (using `@` tags) in a stratified sample (read vs. spontaneous). Compare to MFA outputs to quantify systematic drift at vulnerable points.
2.  **Metadata-Acoustic Predictive Power Test:** For a controlled subset (e.g., read-sentence English vowels), run a linear mixed model with acoustic measures (F1, F2, duration) as DVs, and key metadata (English AoA, self-rated proficiency, Mandarin hometown) as fixed effects. This tests if collected sociolinguistic covariates have predicted relationships with speech production.
3.  **Cross-Language Interview Style Equivalence Audit:** Conduct a blinded qualitative analysis of the first 5 minutes of each participant's spontaneous interview in both languages. Code for discourse style markers (narrative vs. descriptive, emotional valence, topic depth, filler rate). This audits the core assumption that matched tasks produced comparable speaking contexts.
