# Limits of Imagery Reasoning in Frontier LLM Models
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.26779
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26779
- Reference count: 11
- Primary result: Even with an external 3D imagery module, frontier LLM accuracy on a spatial rotation benchmark peaked at 62.5%, far below human performance (79%), and consistently failed to infer rotation direction or predict rotated object states.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates why frontier large language models (LLMs) perform poorly on spatial tasks requiring mental simulation, such as 3D mental rotation. The authors equipped an LLM with an external ‘Imagery Module’ that renders and rotates 3D models, testing it on the SpatialViz benchmark. Three conditions were evaluated: resetting objects to canonical views (which reduced the task to 2D matching), incremental rotation with a 360° search hint, and incremental rotation only. Across all conditions, LLM accuracy never exceeded 62.5%, while a specialized vision model (VGGT) succeeded in pose recovery tasks. The findings conclude that LLMs lack fundamental visual-spatial primitives—depth/motion sensing and short-horizon dynamic prediction—and cannot engage in contemplative visual attention, making them unable to leverage external imagery tools for true spatial reasoning.

## Method Summary
The study implemented a dual-module agentic system. A frontier multimodal LLM (e.g., GPT-5.2) served as the reasoning module, issuing rotation commands (e.g., `left:30`) to an external imagery module built with PyVista. The imagery module maintained a stateful 3D renderer, applying rotations in camera space and returning 2D snapshots (before/after grids) after each command. The LLM iterated for at least five turns, analyzing returned images before providing a final answer. Evaluation occurred on the SpatialViz benchmark (40 problems), where the task was to identify which of three alternative 3D cube stacks could not be aligned with the original via rotation. Three experimental conditions modulated the imagery module’s capabilities: (1) enabling a reset to canonical views, (2) instructing a full 360° orbit, and (3) standard incremental rotation only.

## Key Results
- Accuracy with the imagery module peaked at 62.5% (Condition 3), compared to human performance of 79%.
- When a reset to canonical views was allowed (Condition 1), accuracy reached 97.5%, but this reduced the task to pure 2D image matching, bypassing spatial simulation.
- Across multiple frontier models, performance consistently remained below 63%. Isolation experiments showed LLMs failed to identify small rotation directions from sequential images (5/6 errors) and could not generate plausible after-rotation images, while the VGGT vision model succeeded.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- Claim: LLMs cannot infer rotation direction and angle from sequential images, treating each view as an unrelated static snapshot.
- Mechanism: Absence of low-level motion sensitivity prevents extracting temporal-spatial cues; the model relies on symbolic feature comparison instead of perceptual continuity.
- Core assumption: Human mental rotation depends on sensing incremental change; LLMs process images token-wise without inherent sequential integration.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] Lists "motion" as a missing primitive. [section 4.6.1] "Identify small rotation" experiment: GPT-5.2 mispredicted direction in 5/6 trials, conflating static visibility with movement.
- Break condition: If the model could correctly identify small rotations (>80% accuracy), it could iteratively correct its view; instead it fails systematically.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: LLMs lack the ability to predict the visual state after a rotation, preventing proactive planning.
- Mechanism: Without dynamic prediction, rotation commands are guesses rather than guided steps; the model cannot simulate outcomes to converge on a target view.
- Core assumption: Spatial reasoning requires forward modeling; LLMs only react to images after the fact.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] Cites missing "short-horizon dynamic prediction". [section 4.6.3] Four frontier image-generation models all failed to produce a rotated image from an input, outputting the original unchanged. [section 4.3] The model never confirmed a geometric match through rotation, instead relying on verbal deduction.
- Break condition: Accurate after-rotation generation would enable goal-directed rotation; the total failure here blocks such strategies.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: LLMs exhibit a strong bias toward symbolic-associative reasoning, overriding visual-spatial evidence even when explicitly prompted to use imagery.
- Mechanism: Autoregressive text training favors high-level linguistic abstractions; images are reduced to gist descriptions rather than engaged contemplatively.
- Core assumption: Human imagery reasoning involves dynamic visual attention balancing; LLMs default to verbal tracks.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] Points to missing "capacity to reason contemplatively over images". [section 4.4] Extensive prompt variations could not exceed 62.5% accuracy; models reverted to discussing object composition. [section 5.1] "The model does not attend to images in a focused, human-like manner; its reasoning is grounded in shallow perceptual observations."
- Break condition: If visual attention could be sustained, models might match views; the persistent drift to text indicates an irreconcilable bias.

## Foundational Learning
- Concept: 3D-to-2D projection and rotation  
  - Why needed here: The core task is to determine if two 3D objects can be aligned by rotation, requiring interpretation of how 2D silhouettes change under 3D transformations.  
  - Quick check question: When a cube is rotated 90° around the vertical axis, how does its front-facing square change?
- Concept: Dual-coding theory (verbal vs. perceptual systems)  
  - Why needed here: The paper frames LLMs as purely verbal reasoners lacking a perceptual imagery system; this dichotomy explains why attaching an imagery tool does not automatically yield spatial reasoning.  
  - Quick check question: What is the difference between describing a triangle's properties in words and visualizing it rotating in your mind?
- Concept: Iterative tool-augmented reasoning loops  
  - Why needed here: The architecture hinges on a turn-based exchange between LLM and imagery module; understanding this loop is essential to grasp the experimental design and failure modes.  
  - Quick check question: In a tool-use loop, what information flows from the tool back to the LLM after each command?

## Architecture Onboarding
- Component map: Reasoning Module (frontier MLLM) -> Controller (orchestrator) -> Imagery Module (stateful PyVista renderer). Memory and images flow back to the Reasoning Module each turn.
- Critical path: 1) Receive problem text and initial images. 2) LLM analyzes and outputs JSON rotation commands. 3) Controller sends commands to imagery module. 4) Imagery module updates poses, renders 2D snapshots (grid), returns images. 5) LLM receives new images plus memory, repeats until final answer.
- Design tradeoffs: Camera-space rotations (intuitive but gimbal lock risk) vs. world-space (consistent but less human-like); reset condition simplifies to 2D matching but forfeits spatial simulation; elaborate prompts required to enforce iteration yet couldn't overcome intrinsic limits; imagery module isolation isolates LLM limits but may miss benefits of tighter integration (e.g., latent 3D representations).
- Failure signatures: Immediate answer without rotations (ignores tool); repeated wrong-direction rotations despite feedback; rationales focus on static parts (e.g., "2x2 base") without referencing alignment; inability to recognize same object from different angles; malformed syntax or axis confusion (e.g., mixing cw/ccw with left/right).
- First 3 experiments: 1) Condition 1 (Reset enabled): accuracy reached 97.5%, showing reducibility to 2D matching with perfect alignment. 2) Condition 2 (Incremental with 360° hint): accuracy only 52.5%, indicating difficulty scanning and comparing many sequential views. 3) Condition 3 (Incremental only): standard setup; accuracy 50–62.5%, demonstrating limited gains from iterative imagery use.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: What architectural modifications can endow LLMs with intrinsic spatial primitives—depth extraction, motion perception, and short-horizon dynamic prediction—so they can interpret visual feedback without external scaffolding?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors state LLMs "lack the low-level sensitivity to extract spatial signals" and conclude future architectures must "intrinsically support spatial awareness."
- Why unresolved: The imagery module supplies rendered views, but the LLM cannot derive spatial relations from them, as shown by failures to detect rotation direction or predict rotated states.
- What evidence would resolve it: A modified multimodal architecture that, when trained on spatial tasks, demonstrates sensitivity to parallax/depth cues, infers motion between consecutive images, and predicts plausible post-rotation views, with ablation studies linking these capabilities to benchmark improvements.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How can we train LLMs to adopt a contemplative visual attention mode—dynamically shifting focus across image regions and balancing visual evidence with symbolic reasoning—rather than defaulting to gist-based, associative shortcuts?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors identify the lack of "the capacity to reason contemplatively over images" as a core barrier, noting models fail to deliberately inspect specific parts or track features across iterations.
- Why unresolved: Current training objectives (e.g., next-token prediction) do not incentivize targeted visual scrutiny; the model’s rationales reveal reliance on superficial structural guesses instead of geometric verification.
- What evidence would resolve it: Training regimes that enforce attention supervision (e.g., human-like fixation patterns) or require multi-step visual comparisons (e.g., "rotate until edge aligns"), yielding attention maps that show systematic shifts and improved accuracy on iterative spatial tasks.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can specialized vision modules (like VGGT) that recover camera extrinsics be seamlessly integrated into LLM reasoning loops to provide spatial grounding, and what fusion strategies best preserve reasoning fluency?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] VGGT succeeds at pose recovery, suggesting the transformer architecture itself is not the bottleneck; the paper speculates that generalist MLLMs lack supervised training on such geometric tasks.
- Why unresolved: The dual-module architecture outsourced state maintenance but still failed, indicating that simply providing imagery is insufficient; the LLM must reason over spatial metadata (e.g., pose estimates) rather than raw pixels alone.
- What evidence would resolve it: Hybrid systems where the LLM queries a vision module for explicit spatial cues (e.g., "current yaw angle," "depth map") during iteration, with evaluations showing that such metadata drastically reduces arbitrary rotation choices and boosts accuracy on SpatialViz.

## Limitations
- **Dataset & Calibration Replicability**: The SpatialViz 3D model files and exact camera-pose calibration workflow are not publicly released, blocking exact replication of the imagery module’s initial state.
- **Generalization Scope**: Findings are tied to a specific 3D rotation benchmark (cube stacks); it remains unclear if deficits extend to other spatial tasks or could be mitigated with different tool interfaces (e.g., continuous video streams).
- **Causal Attribution**: The paper isolates LLM deficits via the imagery module, but does not rule out that the turn-based prompt design itself may discourage iterative visual scrutiny; a tighter, latent-space integration might yield different results.

## Confidence
- **LLMs lack motion sensitivity & dynamic prediction**: High – Backed by controlled isolation experiments (4.6.1, 4.6.3) showing systematic failure on direction inference and after-rotation generation, consistent across frontier models.
- **Symbolic-associative bias overrides visual evidence**: Medium – Supported by prompt ablation studies (4.4) and qualitative analysis (5.1), but the degree to which this is inherent vs. training-data artifact is not fully disentangled.
- **External imagery tools cannot bridge the gap**: High – The core result (≤62.5% accuracy even with iterative tool use) directly demonstrates this, comparing favorably against the near-perfect “reset” condition that reduces the task to 2D matching.

## Next Checks
1. **Reproduce command parsing stability**: Run the agentic loop with GPT-5.2 on a 10-problem subset, logging every rotation command’s syntax and semantic validity to test whether parsing errors contribute to low accuracy.
2. **Test alternative tool integration**: Replace the turn-based snapshot exchange with a streaming video feed or a continuous state vector (e.g., camera pose parameters) to determine if the interface design itself impedes iterative reasoning.
3. **Benchmark against specialized spatial models**: Equip a vision-only model (e.g., VGGT) with the same rotation command interface and evaluate on SpatialViz. If it approaches human performance, this isolates the deficit to the LLM component rather than the overall architecture.
