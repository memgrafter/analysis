# JUBAKU: An Adversarial Benchmark for Exposing Culturally Grounded Stereotypes in Japanese LLMs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.20581  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20581  
- **Reference count**: 20  
- **Primary result**: All nine evaluated Japanese LLMs scored below random baseline (50%) on JUBAKU, with average accuracy 23% (range 13–33%), while human annotators achieved 91% accuracy.

## Executive Summary
JUBAKU is a novel adversarial benchmark designed to evaluate culturally specific social biases in Japanese large language models (LLMs). Existing bias benchmarks rely on Western-centric translations, which fail to capture nuanced Japanese societal stereotypes. To address this, native Japanese annotators hand-crafted 1,216 dialogue scenarios across ten cultural categories, iteratively refined using GPT-4o until the model selected the biased response. Evaluation of nine Japanese LLMs revealed severe latent biases: all models performed well below chance (average 23% accuracy), whereas humans identified unbiased responses with 91% accuracy. The results demonstrate JUBAKU’s effectiveness in exposing biases that other Japanese-adapted English benchmarks overlook.

## Method Summary
The JUBAKU dataset construction began with native Japanese annotators creating dialogue contexts and two candidate responses (one unbiased, one biased) across ten cultural categories. Instances were iteratively refined: GPT-4o was used to select the biased response, and only those where it succeeded were retained. This adversarial filtering ensured the biased option is subtly but clearly preferred by a powerful model. The final dataset contains 1,216 instances (152 base prompts × 4 instruction variants × 2 answer orders). Evaluation follows a binary-choice protocol: each instance is presented with a prompt template containing few-shot examples, the dialogue context, and the two responses. The model’s log-likelihood for each full response is computed, and the response with higher likelihood is selected. Accuracy is the proportion of instances where the unbiased response is chosen. For comparison, three existing Japanese-adapted English benchmarks (JBNLI, JBBQ, SSQA-JA) were similarly reformatted into binary choice.

## Key Results
- All nine Japanese LLMs performed below the random baseline of 50% on JUBAKU; average accuracy was 23% (range 13–33%).
- The same models achieved higher scores on the other Japanese-adapted English benchmarks, indicating JUBAKU’s unique sensitivity to culturally grounded biases.
- Human annotators reached 91% accuracy in selecting unbiased responses, confirming the dataset’s reliability and the adversarial challenge’s validity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
JUBAKU’s adversarial construction guarantees that biased responses reflect prevalent Japanese cultural stereotypes, making them internally consistent and tempting to models trained on such data. Iterative refinement with GPT-4o ensures each instance is challenging: the biased option must be preferred by a state-of-the-art model, ruling out trivial or ambiguous cases. The binary-choice format with log-likelihood selection directly measures the model’s latent preference without confounding factors from generative quality or instruction following. Cultural grounding by native annotators captures nuances that translated benchmarks miss, while the multiple instruction variants and answer order swaps reduce prompt sensitivity. This combination exposes deep-seated biases even in models that perform adequately on standard tasks.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**: JUBAKU Dataset → Prompt Template → Model + Tokenizer → Log-Likelihood Calculator → Selection Logic → Accuracy Evaluator

**Critical Path**: Prompt construction (inserting context and responses into the template) → computing token-by-token log-probabilities for each response → selecting the higher‑likelihood response → aggregating selections against ground‑truth unbiased labels. Errors in prompt formatting or likelihood calculation directly invalidate results.

**Design Tradeoffs**:  
- Log-likelihood vs. generation: Likelihood is fast and objective but may not reflect generative quality; generation is more natural but introduces variability.  
- Binary choice vs. multi‑class: Binary simplifies but cannot capture gradations of bias.  
- Adversarial filtering with GPT‑4o: Efficient but may import non‑Japanese biases from the filtering model.

**Failure Signatures**:  
- Model accuracy clusters near 50% (but not exactly) on JUBAKU while humans achieve 91%: likely prompt or implementation error.  
- Huge discrepancy between JUBAKU and adapted benchmark scores could stem from inconsistent prompt formatting across benchmarks.  
- Human accuracy significantly lower than 91% on a subset suggests ambiguous dataset instances.

**First Experiments**:  
1. Sanity check with human‑verified subset: Run evaluation on a small set where human accuracy is known to confirm prompt and implementation.  
2. Prompt variation test: Compare model performance across the four instruction variants to assess robustness to phrasing.  
3. Cross‑benchmark consistency: Adapt JBNLI, JBBQ, SSQA‑JA using the exact same binary‑choice and log‑likelihood protocol to ensure fair comparison with JUBAKU.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Question**: What additional cultural aspects (e.g., age, occupation, sexual orientation) are relevant for evaluating bias in Japanese LLMs, and how can JUBAKU be extended to cover them systematically?  
   **Basis**: The limitations state: "it does not explicitly address aspects such as age, occupation, or sexual orientation, which could be explored in future extensions."  
   **Why unresolved**: The current benchmark only covers ten categories; missing aspects might harbor biases not captured, limiting the comprehensiveness of cultural bias evaluation.  
   **What evidence would resolve it**: Create and validate new adversarial instances for these additional categories, measure model performance, and compare with existing categories to identify novel bias patterns and ensure broader coverage.

2. **Question**: How can we design evaluation metrics that jointly assess bias (safety) and response quality (utility) in culturally sensitive dialogues?  
   **Basis**: The limitations note: "Future work could explore richer evaluation metrics or tasks that explicitly measure or balance both the safety and utility aspects of LLM responses in culturally sensitive interactions."  
   **Why unresolved**: Accuracy alone does not capture whether unbiased responses are also helpful and contextually appropriate; real‑world deployment requires both safety and utility.  
   **What evidence would resolve it**: Develop a multi‑dimensional evaluation framework combining bias detection with utility scores derived from human or model‑based assessments, then apply it to JUBAKU instances to validate its discriminative power.

3. **Question**: How can we improve the reliability of culturally grounded bias benchmarks by incorporating multiple annotators and measuring inter‑annotator agreement?  
   **Basis**: The limitations state: "incorporating multi‑annotator agreement procedures could further enhance the dataset’s reliability and mitigate potential individual subjectivity."  
   **Why unresolved**: JUBAKU instances were crafted by six annotators without systematic agreement analysis; individual cultural interpretations may introduce noise and affect the clarity of unbiased answers.  
   **What evidence would resolve it**: Recruit a larger, diverse set of native annotators to independently label a subset, compute agreement metrics (e.g., Cohen’s kappa), refine guidelines based on disagreements, and potentially revise ambiguous instances.

4. **Question**: Why do LLMs show different levels of vulnerability to biases across cultural categories (e.g., Religion vs. Region) and what does this indicate about the distribution of stereotypes in training data or alignment processes?  
   **Basis**: The results show: "The required number of edits varied significantly... This implies that GPT‑4o may be more robust in sensitive domains such as religion or race, but remains vulnerable to region‑and ethnicity‑related stereotypes."  
   **Why unresolved**: Understanding the underlying causes (e.g., data prevalence, alignment priorities) could guide targeted bias mitigation and improve benchmark design.  
   **What evidence would resolve it**: Analyze model internal representations or training data statistics for each category; conduct ablation studies on alignment data; test whether the pattern holds across diverse model architectures and scales.

## Limitations
- **Prompt construction fidelity**: The exact `{EXAMPLES}` instruction text and few‑shot demonstrations are not provided, forcing reproduction based on inference and risking task misinterpretation.
- **Benchmark adaptation specifics**: The transformation rules for JBNLI, JBBQ, and SSQA‑JA into binary‑choice format are underspecified, potentially compromising comparability with JUBAKU.
- **Dataset validation**: Category distribution is not enumerated, and adversarial filtering with GPT‑4o may introduce non‑Japanese cultural biases.
