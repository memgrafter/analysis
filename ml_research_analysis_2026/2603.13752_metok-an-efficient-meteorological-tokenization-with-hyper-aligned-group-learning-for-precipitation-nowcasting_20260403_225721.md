# MeTok: An Efficient Meteorological Tokenization with Hyper-Aligned Group Learning for Precipitation Nowcasting

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2603.13752  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13752  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: MeTok achieves at least 8.2% IoU improvement for extreme precipitation prediction on ERA5, with overall gains of 4.57% and 2.37% on ERA5 and WeatherBench, respectively, and outperforms operational NWP models on WeatherBench2.

## Executive Summary
MeTok addresses the mismatch between position-centric tokenization in vision Transformers and the multi‑factor, distribution‑driven nature of meteorological systems, which causes poor performance on long‑tail extreme precipitation events. It introduces a distribution‑centric tokenization scheme that rearranges spatial meteorological patch embeddings into non‑overlapping groups based on similarity in historical precipitation patterns. This is integrated with the Hyper‑Aligned Grouping Transformer (HyAGTransformer), featuring a Grouping Attention mechanism for intra‑group feature learning and a Neighborhood Feed‑Forward Network to preserve local context. Evaluated on ERA5 and WeatherBench datasets, MeTok significantly improves extreme precipitation forecasting (≥8.2% IoU) while being more scalable and stable than operational NWP models.

## Method Summary
MeTok reimagines the tokenization step for spatiotemporal meteorological data. Instead of the fixed grid of Vision Transformers, it sorts input patches by historical precipitation intensity (the most recent frame for the encoder, the last input frame for the translator) and partitions the sorted sequence into non‑overlapping groups. Each group is aggregated via a 1D convolution to produce a group embedding. Within HyAGTransformer blocks, queries come from individual patch embeddings while keys and values come from the corresponding group embeddings, enabling Grouping Attention that reduces computational complexity from O(n²) to O(n·m). A Neighborhood Feed‑Forward Network then applies a 1D convolution along the sorted patch dimension, mixing adjacent group features to retain local spatial context lost by grouping. The overall architecture stacks these blocks in an encoder (4 blocks), a translator (2 blocks) that bridges temporal dynamics, and a decoder that upsamples to the forecast horizon. The model uses solar elevation as a positional encoding and is trained with AdamW.

## Key Results
- Extreme precipitation IoU improved by at least 8.2% on ERA5 compared to prior methods.  
- Overall mean IoU gains: 4.57% on ERA5, 2.37% on WeatherBench.  
- Outperforms operational NWP models on WeatherBench2 with superior scalability and stability as data and parameters increase.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Grouping patches by historical precipitation enhances learning for rare extreme events by isolating tail‑sample features from dominant head‑class noise.  
- **Mechanism**: Patches are sorted by past precipitation intensity and split into fixed‑size groups. Group embeddings aggregate intra‑group features, aligning representations of similar precipitation patterns.  
- **Core assumption**: Historical precipitation reliably proxies underlying dynamic‑thermodynamic similarity.  
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract and Sec 3.2 emphasize distribution‑centric grouping; prior works use position‑centric tokenization.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Grouping Attention (GA) reduces computational cost and enforces pattern‑specific learning by attending to group‑level summaries.  
- **Mechanism**: Queries derive from patches; keys/values from group embeddings. Complexity reduces from O(n²) to O(n·m).  
- **Core assumption**: Group embeddings are sufficient condensed representations; patches within a group share relevant structure.  
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract and Sec 4.2 describe GA’s efficiency and pattern discrimination.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Neighborhood Feed‑Forward Network (N‑FFN) restores local context lost by grouping via 1D convolutions along the sorted sequence.  
- **Mechanism**: Applies a 1D convolution to patch embeddings in the sorted order, mixing adjacent group features.  
- **Core assumption**: Sorted‑order neighbors carry meaningful contextual cues.  
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract and Sec 4.2 state that N‑FFN integrates adjacent group features to mitigate information loss.

- **Why needed here**: Precipitation follows a heavy‑tailed distribution; tail events are critical but under‑represented. This imbalance causes position‑centric tokenization to overfit head classes, motivating distribution‑centric grouping.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Patchify -> Encoder (stack of Ne HyAGTransformer blocks, each block: MeTok rearrangement & grouping -> Grouping Attention -> Neighborhood FFN -> Unshuffle) -> Translator (stack of Nt HyAGTransformer blocks with temporally shared grouping) -> Decoder (transposed convolutions + feature fusion) -> Output

### Critical path
Data flow: Input → Patchify → Encoder (MeTok + HyAGTransformer × Ne) → Translator (reshape + HyAGTransformer × Nt) → Decoder → Output. Key dependencies: MeTok rearrangement must align with the grouping strategy; GA and N‑FFN assume the sorted order is meaningful.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Group size (n/m)**: Larger groups improve tail aggregation but risk mixing distinct patterns; smaller preserve diversity but may lack robustness. Optimal around 8 patches/group.  
- **Neighborhood kernel in N‑FFN**: Fixed size (n/m−1) balances local context recovery vs. inclusion of irrelevant neighbors.  
- **Historical precipitation window**: Longer history stabilizes grouping but may lag fast changes; immediate past used.  
- **Parameter efficiency**: MeTok achieves strong results with only 1.42M parameters via inductive biases, not brute force.

### Failure signatures
1. **Ablation – Random ordering**: If random ordering drastically degrades IoU (Fig 4a), the distribution‑centric hypothesis holds.  
2. **Extreme‑event drop**: Insufficient improvement in heavy‑rain IoU suggests grouping misaligned tail features; check group size or historical metric.  
3. **Temporal degradation**: Performance decaying faster than baselines with lead time indicates translator insufficiency; consider increasing Nt.  
4. **Regional bias**: Large IoU variance across regions (Fig 7) hints at non‑stationary relationships; may need region‑adaptation.

### First 3 experiments
1. **End‑to‑end validation on ERA5**: Reproduce Table 1. Expect overall IoU ≈ 44.4, TS ≈ 22.3, and >4.5% IoU gain over SimVP/gSTA.  
2. **Ablation of MeTok components**: Build variants without MeTok (no rearrangement), without GA (full self‑attention), and without N‑FFN (standard FFN). Expect full model best; removal of any component hurts significantly (Table 8 pattern).  
3. **Group size sensitivity**: Train with patches‑per‑group ∈ {1, 8, 64}. Expected optimal at 8; extremes underperform (Fig 4b).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Could incorporating multi‑factor historical distributions (e.g., temperature, wind) instead of only historical precipitation improve grouping and extreme event prediction?  
2. What specific meteorological or topographic factors cause the observed regional performance imbalance, and how can the model be made region‑agnostic?  
3. Why did the learnable grouping strategy fail to match the performance of the precipitation‑based static ordering, and what architectural changes could enable effective learned grouping?  
4. How can MeTok be adapted to better capture discrete, convective precipitation events, given its current tendency to produce dense forecasts?
