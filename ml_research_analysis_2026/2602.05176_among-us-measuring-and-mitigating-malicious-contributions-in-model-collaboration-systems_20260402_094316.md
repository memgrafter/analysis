# Among Us: Measuring and Mitigating Malicious Contributions in Model Collaboration Systems
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.05176
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05176
- **Reference count**: 37
- **Primary result**: Malicious models in multi-LLM collaboration degrade performance by up to 34.99% on reasoning/safety tasks; supervisor-based mitigation recovers 96.8% on average.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the security risk of malicious or compromised language models in decentralized multi-LLM collaboration systems. Researchers engineered malicious models using four methods—prompting, activation steering, supervised fine-tuning, and reinforcement learning—and integrated them into eight collaboration algorithms spanning API, text, logit, and weight-level coordination. Evaluation on ten datasets revealed severe performance drops, averaging 7.12% in reasoning and 7.94% in safety, with a worst-case 34.99% decline for API-level routing. To mitigate, the authors proposed supervisor-free and supervisor-based strategies where models self-police or an external supervisor disables suspicious models. Supervisor-based methods, using either an LLM-as-a-judge or a reward model, recovered 96.8% of the original performance on average (95.31% overall), though full resilience, especially in safety-critical domains, remains an open challenge.

## Method Summary
The authors built a pool of five Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct models, each fine-tuned for a specific domain (reasoning, safety, knowledge, code, instruction-following). They engineered malicious variants by either non-parametric means (adversarial system prompts, activation steering vectors) or parametric means (fine-tuning on adversarial datasets, RL with inverted rewards). These malicious models replaced one benign model in the pool and were evaluated within eight collaboration frameworks: two API-level routers, two text-level debate/feedback systems, two logit-aggregation methods, and two weight-merging techniques. Performance was measured on ten benchmarks. Two mitigation approaches were tested: a supervisor-free variant that uses internal consensus (top-k selection, voting) and supervisor-based variants that employ an external LLM judge or a reward model to detect and disable suspicious models before or during collaboration.

## Key Results
- Malicious models cause average drops of 7.12% (reasoning) and 7.94% (safety), with worst-case 34.99% degradation for GRAPH ROUTER on safety tasks.
- Logit- and weight-level collaboration (e.g., LOGIT AVERAGE, GREEDY SOUP) show inherent robustness, suffering negligible average degradation due to aggregation dilution.
- Supervisor-based mitigation (using LLM judge or reward model) recovers 96.8% of the original performance on average (95.31% overall), though safety-critical domains remain challenging (≈89% recovery).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Aggregation Density Dilutes Malicious Influence
**Claim**: Logit- and weight-level aggregation dilute the impact of any single malicious model because each model contributes with a small coefficient, so skewed outputs are averaged out.  
**Mechanism**: Linear or sublinear aggregation functions assign small weights; greedy merging (GREEDY SOUP) filters out models that degrade validation performance.  
**Core assumption**: Sufficient benign models outweigh the malicious one.  
**Evidence**: Abstract notes logit- and weight-level robustness; Section 4.1 reports LOGIT AVERAGE unaffected and GREEDY SOUP filtering poorly performing models.  
**Break condition**: High steering magnitude or preferential weighting during merging can let the malicious model dominate.

### Mechanism 2: Output Divergence Enables Supervisory Detection
**Claim**: Malicious models often produce outputs that diverge from benign consensus, allowing an external supervisor to detect and disable them.  
**Mechanism**: Supervisor evaluates each model's response for correctness or safety; the lowest-scoring model is removed before final aggregation.  
**Core assumption**: Supervisor is more competent and reliably distinguishes malicious outputs.  
**Evidence**: Abstract and Section 4.2 show supervisor-based methods consistently outperform supervisor-free; Section 2.3 defines disabling based on supervisor scores.  
**Break condition**: Malicious model produces high-quality outputs for the supervisor's metric, or the supervisor itself is compromised.

### Mechanism 3: Maliciousness Generalization is Domain-Limited
**Claim**: Malicious behavior induced via domain-specific fine-tuning or steering is most effective in the domain it was trained on; cross-domain transfer is weaker.  
**Mechanism**: Fine-tuning alters parameters to favor adversarial patterns primarily in the target domain; in other domains these altered parameters do not consistently yield malicious outputs.  
**Core assumption**: Malicious capabilities are not fully generalizable without explicit cross-domain training.  
**Evidence**: Abstract mentions generalizable impact but limited transfer; Section 5 and Figure 4 heatmap show highest degradation on the diagonal (in-domain) and lower off-diagonal values.  
**Break condition**: Emergent misalignment—adversarial fine-tuning causing broadly misaligned behavior across tasks—could make transfer stronger.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept 1: Multi-LLM Collaboration Taxonomy**  
*Why needed*: The paper's analysis hinges on four coordination levels (API, text, logit, weight). Understanding their differences explains why some methods are robust (logit/weight) and others vulnerable (API/text).  
*Quick check*: For a router that selects one model per query (API-level) versus a method that averages model outputs (logit-level), which is more likely to suffer catastrophic failure if one model is malicious, and why?

**Concept 2: Maliciousness Engineering Spectrum (Non-parametric vs. Parametric)**  
*Why needed*: The four attack methods span this spectrum, informing threat severity and mitigation applicability (e.g., parametric attacks are harder to remove at inference time).  
*Quick check*: What is the fundamental difference between a prompting-based malicious model and an SFT-based malicious model in terms of when and how the malicious behavior is introduced?

**Concept 3: Supervisor-Based Mitigation Design**  
*Why needed*: The proposed defense relies on external supervision. Understanding the design space (supervisor-free vs. supervisor-based, LLM-judge vs. reward model) is essential to interpret recovery results and limitations.  
*Quick check*: Why might a supervisor-based system using a reward model (S2) perform differently than one using an LLM-as-a-judge (S1) against a sophisticated malicious model?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Benign Models -> Maliciousness Injectors (M1:prompting, M2:steering, M3:SFT, M4:RL) -> Malicious Models -> Model Pool = Benign ∪ Malicious -> [Mitigation Controller (supervisor-free/supervisor-based) optional filter] -> Collaboration Engine (8 algorithms across API/Text/Logit/Weight levels) -> System Outputs -> Evaluation Harness (10 datasets) -> Performance Metrics

**Critical Path**  
1. Construct malicious models by applying M1–M4 to benign base models.  
2. Form a mixed model pool by replacing one benign model with a malicious variant.  
3. Run the collaboration engine on evaluation datasets; record performance.  
4. Apply mitigation (supervisor-free or supervisor-based) and re‑run evaluation.  
5. Compute degradation relative to the benign baseline and recovery relative to mitigated performance.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *Supervision source*: LLM-as-a-judge (flexible but potentially jailbreakable) vs. reward model (task‑specific, less generalizable); both showed similar effectiveness in experiments.  
- *Collaboration level vs. feasibility*: Logit- and weight‑level methods are robust but require homogeneous architectures; API- and text‑level support heterogeneous models but are more vulnerable to malicious influence.  
- *Detection granularity*: Supervisor‑based approaches disable whole models; they do not address partial maliciousness or attacks that activate only on certain inputs.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Persistent domain‑specific performance drops, especially in safety (CocoNot) and reasoning (GSM8k), even after mitigation (≈89% recovery).  
- High activation steering magnitude (α) overwhelming aggregation and detection.  
- Homogeneous malicious patterns (all malicious models trained on the same domain) causing severe damage and potentially fooling the supervisor.  
- Lack of evaluation for mitigation on logit- and weight‑level collaboration, leaving a blind spot.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Full resistance across all collaboration levels and threat models**: The abstract and conclusion state this as an open research question. Current methods achieve ~95% average recovery but fall short in safety domains and have not been tested on logit/weight‑level collaboration.  
2. **Why safety‑critical domains are especially vulnerable**: Results show lower recovery for safety tasks (~89%) but the paper does not investigate underlying causes, leaving the gap unexplained.  
3. **Why activation steering and RL cause more severe and broader impact than prompting or SFT**: These attacks exhibited stronger adversarial effects, yet the paper offers no mechanistic explanation and the mitigations tested against them are less effective.  
4. **Extent and predictability of maliciousness cross‑domain transfer**: Cross‑domain SFT still harms performance, though less than in‑domain attacks; the paper does not elucidate the transfer mechanism or how to prevent it.  
5. **Effectiveness of supervisor‑based mitigation for logit‑ and weight‑level collaboration**: The limitations explicitly note that mitigation was evaluated only on API/Text levels; its applicability to aggregation‑based methods is unknown.

## Limitations
- Incomplete details on adversarial dataset generation and preprocessing for SFT/RL, preventing exact replication of malicious model construction.  
- Missing configuration specifications for several collaboration algorithms (e.g., router training details, debate prompts, merging thresholds) and M4‑RL hyperparameters (GRPO settings, reward model usage).  
- Mitigation strategies evaluated solely on API- and text-level methods; their effectiveness for logit- and weight-level collaboration remains an open question.  
- Experiments assume a single malicious model in the pool; multiple or stealthy attacks are not considered.
