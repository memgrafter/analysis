# Colon-Bench: An Agentic Workflow for Scalable Dense Lesion Annotation in Full-Procedure Colonoscopy Videos
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.25645
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25645
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Introduces a multi-stage agentic annotation pipeline producing 528 full-procedure colonoscopy videos with >300k boxes, >213k masks, and 133k words of clinical text; establishes a benchmark where Gemini 3 Flash achieves 76.6% VQA and 48.3% mIoU, and a colon-skill prompt improves zero-shot VQA by up to 9.7%.

## Executive Summary
Colon-Bench tackles the lack of densely annotated, long-sequence colonoscopy video datasets by introducing an agentic, multi-stage annotation workflow. This pipeline combines temporal lesion proposals from a vision-language model, AI verification, bounding-box tracking with EdgeTAM, cued AI confirmation, and final human review to scalably produce high-quality spatiotemporal and textual annotations. The resulting benchmark spans 528 full-procedure videos with 14 lesion categories, over 300,000 bounding boxes, 213,000 segmentation masks, and 133,000 words of clinical descriptions. The paper evaluates state-of-the-art multimodal large language models (MLLMs) on four tasks—binary classification, detection, open-vocabulary video object segmentation, and visual question answering—revealing significant performance gaps, especially for segmentation. A novel "colon-skill" prompting strategy, distilled from cross-model error patterns, improves zero-shot VQA accuracy by up to 9.7% across most models. Colon-Bench provides a comprehensive resource for developing and assessing AI in real-world colonoscopy.

## Method Summary
The annotation pipeline progressively filters candidate lesion windows. First, a VLM scans entire procedures to propose temporal windows likely containing lesions. A verification agent reviews these raw windows and rejects obvious false positives. Accepted windows are processed by EdgeTAM, which tracks bounding boxes across all frames and generates initial segmentation masks. A cued confirmation agent then re-examines the tracked video with box overlays as visual cues to validate the localized lesion and optionally refine boxes. Finally, clinicians review a pre-rendered clip to accept or reject the window and verify/edits the clinical description. This funnel reduces the initial 1,325 windows to 528 high-quality ones, enriching annotations at each stage.

The benchmark construction aggregates the accepted windows into four evaluation tasks: lesion binary classification (frame-level), lesion detection (bounding boxes), open-vocabulary video object segmentation (OV-VOS) using box prompts to drive EdgeTAM, and visual question answering (VQA) with multiple-choice questions generated from clinical text and debiased to avoid textual shortcuts. For VQA, both prompted (with windows) and unprompted (without context) settings are evaluated. Additionally, the authors distill common error patterns from multiple MLLMs into a structured "colon-skill" document, which is prepended to VQA prompts to boost zero-shot performance.

## Key Results
- Colon-Bench dataset: 528 full-procedure videos, 14 lesion categories, >300,000 bounding boxes, >213,000 segmentation masks, >133,000 words of clinical descriptions.
- SOTA MLLM evaluation: Gemini 3 Flash achieves 76.6% prompted VQA accuracy and 48.3% mean IoU on segmentation; detection F1 is 51.8%.
- Colon-skill prompting improves zero-shot VQA performance by up to 9.7% (e.g., Qwen3.5-235B) across most tested models.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** An agentic, multi-stage filtering pipeline can scalably produce high-quality, dense spatiotemporal annotations for full-procedure colonoscopy videos.
- **Mechanism:** The pipeline sequentially applies a Vision-Language Model (VLM) for temporal lesion window proposals, an AI verification agent to reject obvious false positives, bounding-box tracking (EdgeTAM) to localize lesions spatially across frames, a cued AI confirmation agent (using box overlays as visual cues) to validate the localized lesion, and finally a human-in-the-loop review for final quality assurance. Each stage progressively prunes the candidate set (from 1,325 windows to 528) while enriching annotations (adding boxes, masks, text).
- **Core assumption:** AI stages can achieve sufficient precision/recall to reduce human burden dramatically, and human review at the end is more efficient than frame-by-frame annotation.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "combines temporal proposals, bounding-box tracking, AI-driven visual confirmation, and human review to scalably produce high-quality spatiotemporal and textual annotations." [section 2.1] Details the filtering funnel (Table 2) showing progressive gains in precision (30.9%→55.4%) and specificity (78.6%→94.4%) with manageable human rejection (11.6%). [corpus] Related work (e.g., Med-CRAFT, VideoARM) also explores agentic video annotation, but Colon-Bench is specific to the medical, long-sequence, sparse-lesion domain. Evidence for generalizability is weak (no direct citations yet, FMR scores ~0.52).
- **Break condition:** If lesion prevalence is extremely low or visual quality is severely degraded (e.g., excessive blur, stool), the initial VLM proposal recall may drop, causing the pipeline to miss lesions regardless of downstream filtering.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Distilling cross-model error patterns into a structured textual "colon-skill" prompt improves zero-shot VQA performance of MLLMs on colonoscopy tasks.
- **Mechanism:** Errors from multiple MLLMs on the VQA benchmark are stratified by lesion category. A frontier LLM synthesizes these shared failure modes into a concise skill document ("Colon-Skill") containing morphological cues, common confusion traps (e.g., "diverticulum vs. depressed lesion"), and a decision checklist. Prepending this skill to the VQA prompt provides structured domain knowledge at inference time without model retraining.
- **Core assumption:** The distilled skill captures generalizable, clinically relevant heuristics that models failed to learn implicitly, and sufficiently capable MLLMs can integrate this textual guidance.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 3.1] Explicitly describes the two-stage extraction and augmentation pipeline. Figure 5 shows accuracy gains up to +9.7% for Qwen3.5 397B. [section 3.2] Ablation shows bounding-box presence has minimal effect on VQA, implying the skill's value is in clinical reasoning, not spatial grounding. [corpus] Work on "skill" extraction for VQA is nascent (e.g., TaskCraft's task generation). Colon-Bench provides a concrete medical instance; cross-domain skill transfer is unproven.
- **Break condition:** If the error analysis identifies conflicts between models (no consistent patterns) or if the skill document becomes too long/complex for the MLLM's context window to effectively utilize.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A comprehensive, multi-task benchmark with dense, long-sequence annotations is necessary to realistically evaluate MLLMs for colonoscopy AI and reveal their true capabilities and failure modes.
- **Mechanism:** Colon-Bench spans 528 full-procedure videos, providing bounding boxes, segmentation masks, and clinical text for 14 lesion categories. It defines four tasks: binary classification, detection, Open-Vocabulary Video Object Segmentation (OV-VOS), and VQA (prompted/unprompted). The design forces models to handle long sequences, class imbalance, and rich clinical semantics.
- **Core assumption:** Performance on single-image, single-class polyp detection datasets does not predict real-world utility; generalization requires testing on diverse lesions in long-form videos with language-based clinical queries.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "Rigorous evaluation... reveals Gemini 3 Flash achieves 76.6% prompted VQA accuracy and 48.3% mean IoU on segmentation." [section 2.2] Describes the filtering to create evaluation splits with specific frame counts and question generation with debiasing to prevent text-only shortcuts. [section 3.2] Ablation shows temporal context (full video vs. single frame) significantly impacts classification for models like Seed 1.6 (-22.4% F1), proving the need for video benchmarks. [corpus] GI-Bench is a direct contemporary on gastrointestinal MLLM evaluation but focuses on image-level tasks and knowledge-experience dissociation. Colon-Bench's video and segmentation focus fills a distinct gap.
- **Break condition:** If the benchmark's class distribution (long-tailed; Fig. 2) or question generation process (even with debiasing) embeds unintended biases that models exploit, scores may not reflect true clinical reasoning.

## Foundational Learning
- **Agentic Annotation Pipelines**: Why needed here: The core contribution is a scalable workflow using multiple sequential AI agents for video annotation. Understanding how agents filter, refine, and pass data (with different modalities—temporal, spatial, textual) is key to reproducing or extending the method. Quick check: What is the purpose of the "cued AI confirmation agent" and how does it differ from the initial "verification agent"? (Answer: The confirmation agent uses spatial bounding-box overlays as visual cues to re-examine a tracked window, focusing on validating the *localized* lesion, whereas the verification agent reviews a raw temporal window for lesion presence.)
- **Open-Vocabulary Video Object Segmentation (OV-VOS)**: Why needed here: Colon-Bench establishes the first OV-VOS benchmark in colonoscopy. This task requires segmenting lesion instances described by free-form text (category names from clinical descriptions) across video frames without pre-defined class lists. It tests a model's ability to link language to visual concepts over time. Quick check: How does the segmentation benchmark work? (Answer: MLLMs first detect the target lesion via 3 bounding-box prompts; these boxes prompt the EdgeTAM tracker to propagate segmentation masks across the video window. Performance is measured by mIoU/Dice against ground-truth masks.)
- **Curriculum/Progressive Refinement in Data Curation**: Why needed here: The annotation funnel (Table 2, Fig. 8) is a form of curriculum learning for the data itself: easy, high-precision filters (longer windows, AI confirmation) are applied before expensive human review. The statistics show rejected windows are shorter and have sparser text, confirming the curriculum effect. Quick check: Based on Table 6, what empirical evidence suggests the AI filters are selecting for "easier" or higher-quality lesion instances? (Answer: Curated windows are longer on average (878.9 vs. 414.1 frames) and have much richer textual descriptions (252.4 vs. ~177 words), indicating more complete, well-documented lesion occurrences.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: VLM Proposal Agent → Verification Agent → Tracking & Spatial Localization Module → Cued AI Confirmation Agent → Human Review Interface → Dataset & Benchmark Builder
- **Critical path**: VLM Proposals → Verification Agent → Tracking → Confirmation Agent → Human Review → Benchmark Splits
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - **Cost vs. Quality**: Using different Gemini variants (Flash Lite, Pro, Flash) balances API cost and accuracy per stage. Cheaper, faster models for high-volume filtering; more capable models for nuanced confirmation.
  - **Automation vs. Human Labor**: The funnel aims to minimize human time (11.6% rejection at final stage). Tradeoff: risk of AI-filter false negatives. The retention rate (39.8%) indicates a high-precision, lower-recall final set—acceptable for a benchmark but not for exhaustive clinical annotation.
  - **Temporal Resolution vs. Context**: The ablation shows more detection frames improve segmentation but increase per-window cost. The paper picks 3 frames as a trade-off.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - High rejection rate at Verification Agent (>50%): Indicates initial VLM proposals are too noisy or the dataset has very few lesions.
  - Low mask coverage (<40%) after Tracking: Suggests lesions are frequently occluded or the tracker (EdgeTAM) fails in colonoscopy conditions (motion, blur).
  - Human review rejection >25%: AI confirmation is not aligned with clinician judgment; requires retraining or prompt tweaking.
  - Poor model performance on "easy" lesion categories (e.g., sessile polyps): Suggests fundamental dataset issues (e.g., bad masks) or that "easy" is not actually easy for MLLMs.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Pipeline Stage Ablation on REAL-COLON**: Re-run the Table 7 analysis on your own target dataset (if a subset has ground-truth boxes) to validate each filter's impact on temporal (frame-level) and spatial (box F1) metrics before full-scale deployment.
  2. **Human-AI Agreement Study**: Before committing to pipeline, have clinicians independently review a sample of 100 windows from the "AI Confirmation Accepted" set. Compute Cohen's Kappa. Low agreement (<0.6) signals the need to adjust the confirmation agent's prompt or threshold.
  3. **Colon-Skill Transfer Test**: Apply the *exact* Colon-Skill prompt from Fig. 16 to a different medical VQA benchmark (if available) to test its generalizability. A significant drop in gain suggests the skill is overfit to Colon-Bench's specific error patterns.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can the agentic annotation workflow be optimized for real-time deployment in clinical settings without compromising annotation quality?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusions state that future work will focus on optimizing the agent-driven pipeline for real-time use in clinical environments.
- **Why unresolved:** The current pipeline involves multiple sequential AI stages and human review, likely introducing latency unsuitable for live procedures. The trade-off between speed and maintaining the reported high precision (e.g., 94.4% specificity) is not explored.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Benchmark stage-wise processing times on full-procedure videos under hardware constraints common in clinical settings. Conduct a clinical study comparing annotation quality (e.g., lesion recall, false positive rate) between the optimized real-time pipeline and the original Colon-Bench generation protocol.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Why do Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) exhibit a significant performance gap between lesion detection and segmentation tasks on Colon-Bench, despite using the same bounding-box prompts?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Results in Table 3 and Fig. 4 show models like Gemini 3 Flash achieve 51.8% F1 for detection but only 48.3% mIoU for segmentation when prompted with identical bounding boxes. The paper notes MLLMs excel at "high-level reasoning and spatial grounding" but implies a limitation in fine-grained mask generation.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not analyze whether the gap stems from MLLMs' architectural bias toward coarse localization, inherent limitations in generating pixel-accurate masks from text/box prompts, or the specificity of the EdgeTAM tracker's dependence on box quality.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An ablation study comparing mask quality when MLLMs generate masks directly vs. when their detections prompt a segmentation model (like EdgeTAM). Probing internal representations to see if MLLM features encode sufficient spatial resolution for segmentation.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can the "colon-skill" prompting strategy, distilled from error patterns in colonoscopy VQA, be generalized to other medical imaging domains (e.g., endoscopy, radiology) to improve MLLM performance without domain-specific retraining?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The colon-skill was created by analyzing shared failure modes across MLLMs on Colon-Bench's VQA tasks and improved performance by up to 9.7%. The methodology is described as training-free and based on cross-model error patterns, suggesting potential transferability. However, the paper only evaluates it within colonoscopy.
- **Why unresolved:** Medical domains have vastly different visual cues, artifacts, and clinical questions. The effectiveness of colon-specific rules (e.g., for NBI interpretation, distinguishing diverticula) in other contexts is unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply the identical colon-skill prompting framework to VQA benchmarks in other domains (e.g., a lung X-ray dataset or a gastroscopy video dataset). Measure statistically significant changes in accuracy. A negative result would indicate strong domain specificity; a positive result would identify which skill components are transferable.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How robust is the agentic pipeline's filtering efficiency across the long-tailed distribution of lesion categories in Colon-Bench, particularly for rare lesions like "Parasites" or "Crohn's"?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The pipeline's overall retention rate and human review agreement are reported (e.g., 88.4% acceptance, 11.6% rejection). However, the paper presents only aggregate statistics (Table 2, 4) and the overall lesion distribution (Fig. 2), not a breakdown of acceptance/rejection rates by specific lesion category.
- **Why unresolved:** The long-tail implies rare categories have fewer training examples for the AI agents. They might be disproportionately rejected at early proposal or verification stages due to lower confidence or atypical features, biasing the final dataset against them. The paper does not analyze if the 11.6% human rejection rate holds for rare vs. common lesions.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Re-analyze the pipeline logs to compute recall and precision per lesion category at each stage (proposal, verification, confirmation, human review). Statistically test if acceptance rates for rare categories are significantly lower than for common ones, controlling for window duration and initial proposal confidence.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** To what extent do the Colon-Bench VQA tasks, based on short, pre-selected clips and multiple-choice questions, evaluate the continuous, holistic understanding required for real-time decision support during a full colonoscopy procedure?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper contrasts its "long-sequence" videos with prior single-image datasets. However, the VQA benchmark is built from discrete 737-frame clips on average (Table 9), and the "unprompted" split still uses pre-defined clips, not continuous streams. The introduction states colonoscopies last up to two hours, but the evaluation framework does not model temporal accumulation or longitudinal reasoning across an entire procedure.
- **Why unresolved:** Clinical decision-making in colonoscopy depends on synthesizing findings over time (e.g., monitoring a lesion's progression, correlating findings from different colonic segments). Short-clip VQA may not test this capability.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Develop a new evaluation protocol using hours-long procedure videos with questions requiring integration of information from widely separated timestamps (e.g., "Has this bleeding site recurred?"). Compare MLLM performance on this longitudinal task against their clip-based scores to measure the temporal generalization gap.

## Limitations
- Data access and licensing unclear; reliance on proprietary models hinders broad reproducibility.
- Key pipeline details (model versions, thresholds, merging logic) are not released, requiring inference for any recreation.
- The funnel's emphasis on precision (94.4% specificity) may sacrifice recall, potentially missing rare lesions and biasing the dataset toward common appearances.

## Confidence
- Scalable agentic annotation pipeline: **Medium**
- Colon-skill prompting improves zero-shot VQA: **Medium**
- Multi-task dense-video benchmark necessity: **High**
