# When LLMs get significantly worse: A statistical approach to detect model degradations

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.10144
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10144
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: An exact one-sided McNemar test detects accuracy drops as small as 0.3% (p < 1e-5) while controlling false positives and enabling near 50% dataset size reduction.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the critical challenge of statistically detecting small but significant accuracy degradations in optimized large language models (LLMs). Traditional metrics can be misleading due to non-determinism from nominally lossless changes (e.g., quantization). The authors introduce a rigorous, per-sample statistical framework built on an exact one-sided McNemar test, which properly accounts for the dependence between repeated model evaluations. Their method, implemented within the LM Evaluation Harness, reliably flags harmful degradations (like severe quantization) while passing benign variants. For multi-benchmark assessment, they propose aggregation strategies (pooled, max-drop, Fisher’s method) and show that trimming datasets to "flip-prone" examples retains detection power while drastically cutting evaluation cost.

## Method Summary
The core method uses a conditional degradation probability: given that two models (baseline and candidate) disagree on a sample, what is the probability the baseline is correct? This is estimated via an exact one-sided McNemar test on paired predictions, which correctly models the dependence between the two model evaluations and avoids the variance overestimation of naïve binomial tests. For evaluation across multiple benchmarks, three aggregation strategies are proposed: 1) **Pooled** (combining all disagreements), 2) **Max-drop** (focusing on the task with the largest individual degradation), and 3) **Fisher’s combined p-value** (statistically combining per-benchmark p-values). The framework is extended to non-binary metrics via permutation tests. A key efficiency innovation identifies a subset of "flip-prone" examples—those where the baseline model's prediction is marginally correct/incorrect—which, when retained, reduces dataset size by nearly 50% with negligible loss in detection power.

## Key Results
- **High sensitivity**: The method detects accuracy drops as small as 0.3% with strong statistical significance (p < 1e-5) on Llama models.
- **Controlled false positives**: It correctly passes lossless model variants (e.g., minor quantizations) while flagging severely degraded ones (e.g., w4a16 quantization).
- **Efficiency**: Dataset trimming to flip-prone examples reduces benchmark size by ~50% without compromising the ability to detect degradations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The approach works because it directly targets the statistical dependency inherent in paired model evaluations. A naïve test treating each sample as independent would overestimate variance and lose power. The exact McNemar test conditions on the total number of disagreements, using the binomial distribution under the null hypothesis that disagreement outcomes are equally likely. This provides a precise p-value for whether the baseline's conditional accuracy on disagreements is worse than chance (0.5). The aggregation strategies then sensibly combine evidence across benchmarks, either by total signal (pooled), worst-case impact (max-drop), or meta-analytic combination (Fisher). Dataset trimming is effective because degradation signals are concentrated in examples where the baseline model is already borderline; focusing on these maximizes the signal-to-noise ratio for the McNemar test.

## Foundational Learning
- **McNemar's Test**: A statistical test for paired nominal data, used here in its exact one-sided form. *Why needed*: To test if the probability of the baseline being correct on disagreements is >0.5, while correctly modeling dependence. *Quick check*: Verify that under the null (no degradation), the number of times baseline is correct on disagreements follows a Binomial(n, 0.5) distribution, where n is total disagreements.
- **Conditional Degradation Probability**: The core quantity of interest: P(baseline correct | models disagree). *Why needed*: It isolates the subpopulation where degradation can manifest, ignoring samples where both models are right/wrong. *Quick check*: Ensure this is estimated only from the discordant pairs table (2x2 contingency of correct/incorrect per model).
- **Aggregation of p-values**: Combining evidence from multiple benchmarks. *Why needed*: A single benchmark may be noisy; aggregation yields a more robust overall decision. *Quick check*: Understand the trade-off: pooled uses all data but may dilute a catastrophic drop on one task; max-drop is sensitive to any single severe failure; Fisher's method assumes independence between benchmark p-values.
- **Flip-Prone Example Selection**: Identifying samples where baseline prediction is marginally correct. *Why needed*: These samples are most likely to flip under degradation, thus enriching the test set for degradation signal. *Quick check*: Confirm selection is based on baseline's prediction confidence or margin (e.g., logit difference between top two classes) being small.
- **Permutation Test for Non-Binary Metrics**: A non-parametric method to compute significance. *Why needed*: When metrics aren't binary (e.g., BLEU, F1), the McNemar test doesn't apply directly. *Quick check*: For a metric like F1, the test randomly permutes the assignment of "before/after" labels to the paired model scores and builds a null distribution of the aggregated metric difference.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**: Baseline Model Evaluation -> Candidate Model Evaluation -> Per-Sample Comparison (Contingency Table) -> Exact McNemar Test (p-value) -> [Aggregation Strategy] -> Final Decision

**Critical Path**: The statistical validity hinges on the correct construction of the 2x2 contingency table from paired predictions. Errors here (e.g., ignoring ties) invalidate the test. The aggregation step is secondary but crucial for multi-benchmark reporting.

**Design Tradeoffs**:
- *Exact vs. Asymptotic McNemar*: Exact is computationally feasible here (uses binomial CDF) and correct for small disagreement counts; asymptotic chi-square is simpler but inaccurate for small n.
- *Aggregation Strategy Choice*: Pooled maximizes power for uniform small drops; max-drop is conservative, guarding against any single catastrophic failure; Fisher’s method is a balanced meta-analytic approach but assumes independence.
- *Dataset Trimming*: Greatly reduces cost but discards examples where baseline is confidently correct. This is safe for detecting *accuracy degradation* but could mask *fine-grained* subgroup analysis if those subgroups reside in confidently correct regions.

**Failure Signatures**:
- High disagreement count n but McNemar p-value not significant: suggests no systematic bias in disagreements (baseline not better on flips).
- Aggregated p-value significant only via max-drop: indicates a single benchmark is driving the result; requires manual inspection.
- Trimming leading to missed detection: if degradation causes *new* errors on previously confident examples, the flip-prone selection may exclude them.

**First Three Experiments**:
1. **Sanity check on synthetic data**: Generate paired predictions with known conditional degradation probability (e.g., 0.55). Verify exact McNemar test controls Type I error at 5% and has power >80% to detect this shift with sufficient disagreements (n>100).
2. **Reproduce core Llama result**: Run the full pipeline (LM Evaluation Harness) on Llama-2-7B (baseline) vs. a severely quantized variant (e.g., w4a16) across MMLU, HellaSwag, TruthfulQA. Confirm p-value < 1e-5 for pooled aggregation.
3. **False positive control test**: Compare two nominally identical "lossless" checkpoints (e.g., same seed, different hardware). The aggregated p-value should not reject the null (p > 0.05) across multiple random seeds.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- The paper does not explicitly list open questions beyond its scope. The primary unaddressed direction is the generalizability of the method's robustness under natural distribution shifts, across architectures, and its impact on subgroup analysis after dataset trimming.

## Limitations
- **Scope of validation**: Experiments are confined to Llama family models on standard English benchmarks. Applicability to other architectures (e.g., encoder-decoders, multimodal) and non-English tasks is not established.
- **Aggregation guidance**: The practical trade-offs between pooled, max-drop, and Fisher’s methods when benchmark suites conflict (e.g., one improves, another degrades) are not deeply explored.
- **Subgroup analysis risk**: The ~50% dataset reduction via flip-prone example selection's impact on the statistical power for detecting degradation in specific demographic or topical subgroups is unknown.
