# Gender and Race Bias in Consumer Product Recommendations by Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.08124
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08124
- Reference count: 13
- Primary result: Implicit gender and race biases are present in GPT-4o's product recommendations, as shown by statistically significant group-specific terms, high SVM accuracy for race, and divergent word distributions.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates implicit gender and race biases in consumer product recommendations generated by large language models (LLMs), focusing on GPT-4o. The authors simulate 15 demographic personas (5 races × 3 genders) via prompt engineering and collect 15 recommendations per group (225 total). Each response includes 10 product categories with two-sentence reasoning. The anonymized text is analyzed using three complementary methods: Marked Words (statistically over-represented terms), Support Vector Machines (binary classification accuracy and discriminative features), and Jensen-Shannon Divergence (distributional differences). Results reveal clear biases: Marked Words highlight stereotypical associations (e.g., “rice” for Asian, “hair” for Black), SVM classifiers distinguish race groups with 98% ±3% accuracy but gender groups only 70% ±21%, and JSD shows tech-focused terms for White personas versus grooming and cultural items for minority groups. The work quantifies biases in LLM-generated recommendations and underscores the need for equitable system design.

## Method Summary
The research defines 15 demographic groups (e.g., Asian Female, White Male) and uses a template prompt to instruct GPT-4o to act as a member of that group and suggest 10 products with reasoning. After generating 15 independent responses per group, the text is preprocessed: lowercasing, removal of non-word characters, and anonymization (stripping gender pronouns, race terms, titles). The cleaned item and reason texts are concatenated. Three analytical lenses are applied: (1) For each marked group vs. an unmarked baseline, Marked Words computes a weighted log-odds ratio with Dirichlet prior smoothing; words with |z| > 1.96 are deemed significantly associated. (2) Binary linear SVMs (TF-IDF vectorized, stratified 80/20 split) are trained to distinguish groups; test accuracy and top coefficient words are reported. (3) Jensen-Shannon Divergence quantifies the overall word distribution shift; words contributing most to the divergence are identified. This multi-method approach triangulates evidence of bias.

## Key Results
- Marked Words identified statistically significant group-specific terms (e.g., "rice" for Asian, "hair" for Black) with z-scores >1.96.
- SVM classifiers achieved high accuracy for race groups (98%±3%) but lower for gender (70%±21%).
- JSD revealed divergent lexical patterns: White personas were associated with tech-focused terms (e.g., "smartwatch", "speaker") while minority groups received grooming and cultural items (e.g., "rice", "green tea").

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The Marked Words method identifies words that are statistically over-represented in recommendations for marked (e.g., non-White, non-male) demographic groups compared to unmarked groups, implying stereotypical associations.
- **Mechanism:** It computes a weighted log-odds ratio for each word between a marked and an unmarked group's text corpus (item + reason text), using a Dirichlet prior (based on combined corpus frequency) for smoothing. Significance is determined by a z-score threshold (|z| > 1.96), where the variance of the log-odds ratio accounts for uncertainty in word counts.
- **Core assumption:** Words with a statistically significant positive log-odds ratio for a marked group are indicative of a bias that distinctly associates that group with the word's semantic field.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "Marked Words identified statistically significant group-specific terms (e.g., 'rice' for Asian, 'hair' for Black) with z-scores >1.96." [section 4.1 & 5.1] Detailed mathematical formulation and results tables showing words like "rice" (z=2.28), "hair" (z=2.918), and "inclusive" (z=3.962). [corpus] Neighboring papers (e.g., [4] "Marked Personas") use similar log-odds frameworks for stereotype detection, providing methodological precedent.
- **Break condition:** If the z-scores for all words in a group comparison fall below the 1.96 threshold, the method cannot identify statistically significant distinguishing words for that group, suggesting either no detectable bias in the lexical surface or insufficient signal in the generated text.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** A linear Support Vector Machine (SVM) classifier can achieve high accuracy in distinguishing the concatenated text (item + reasoning) of different demographic groups, and its top-weighted features (words) highlight the most discriminative linguistic patterns.
- **Mechanism:** Texts are anonymized (removing explicit demographic references), vectorized (e.g., TF-IDF), and split into train/test sets. A linear SVM is trained for each binary task (e.g., "Asian Woman" vs. "White Man"). The highest positive coefficients in the learned weight vector indicate words most predictive of the marked class.
- **Core assumption:** If an SVM can accurately classify text by demographic persona, the features (words) with the strongest weights are those systematically leveraged by the LLM to generate group-distinct recommendations, revealing latent bias.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "SVM classifiers achieved high accuracy for race groups (98%±3%) but lower for gender (70%±21%)." [section 4.2 & 5.2] Description of anonymization, preprocessing, and results showing top words (e.g., "sunscreen, bb, green, tea" for Asian Woman vs. "moisturizer, headphones, noisecanceling" for White Man). [corpus] Neighbors [6, 10] discuss fairness in rankings and classification, contextualizing SVM use in bias analysis.
- **Break condition:** Classification accuracy approaches random chance (e.g., ~50% for binary, or chance level for multi-class). This would indicate the model's generated text lacks consistent, separable patterns across demographic groups, weakening the claim of embedded bias in the recommendation language.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD) quantifies the overall difference in word frequency distributions between marked and unmarked groups. The words contributing most to this divergence are those whose usage is most asymmetric, highlighting biased topical or categorical focuses.
- **Mechanism:** Preprocessed, anonymized text from each group is converted into word probability distributions. JSD, a symmetric and smoothed measure based on KL divergence, is computed between the marked and unmarked distributions. The top words with the highest individual contribution to the total JSD score are extracted.
- **Core assumption:** A non-zero JSD value indicates a distributional shift in language. Words that maximally contribute to this shift are those whose frequency disparity most strongly differentiates the groups' recommendations.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "JSD showed divergent word distributions, such as tech-focused terms for White personas versus grooming and cultural items for minority groups." [section 4.3 & 5.3] Formulation of JSD/KL and figures/analysis showing top contributing words (e.g., "rice," "green tea" for Asian vs. "speaker," "smartwatch" for White). [corpus] Neighbor papers on measuring bias via distributional methods (e.g., [2] on embeddings) support the use of divergence metrics.
- **Break condition:** The JSD score for a group comparison is effectively zero (or within numerical noise). This indicates the word distributions are nearly identical, suggesting no measurable divergence in the language of recommendations for those groups.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Prompt Engineering for Persona Simulation**
  - **Why needed here:** The entire study relies on crafting prompts that successfully elicit the LLM to generate recommendations as if it were advising a specific demographic persona (e.g., "Asian Female"). The validity of the bias measurement hinges on the prompt's ability to activate the model's internal associations for that group without triggering refusal or overly generic responses.
  - **Quick check question:** What specific elements in the provided prompt template (e.g., "[race/gender group]", pronoun specification, "without including specific brand names") are designed to enforce demographic specificity while avoiding brand-related noise? How might their omission affect results?

- **Concept: Statistical Significance in High-Dimensional Text Data (Marked Words)**
  - **Why needed here:** The Marked Words method operates on thousands of word types. Without a significance test (z-score) and smoothing (Dirichlet prior), rare words could produce wildly unstable log-odds ratios, leading to false positive identifications of "biased" words purely due to random variation in small sample counts.
  - **Quick check question:** In the Marked Words calculation example, what is the role of the Dirichlet prior α_w (derived from the combined corpus), and why is adding 0.5 (Laplace smoothing) to counts necessary before applying the log-odds formula?

- **Concept: Text Vectorization for Supervised Classification (SVM)**
  - **Why needed here:** To train the SVM, the raw text (concatenated item + reason) must be transformed into a fixed-length numerical feature vector. The choice of vectorization (e.g., bag-of-words, TF-IDF) directly impacts what "features" the SVM learns and thus what "top words" are reported.
  - **Quick check question:** Why is anonymization (removing gender/race-specific pronouns and terms) performed *before* vectorization for the SVM analysis? What bias would be detected if this step were skipped?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Prompter → LLM Client → Text Store → Preprocessor → Analysis Modules → Visualizer

Critical path: Define demographic groups → Design & validate prompt template → Bulk generate recommendations via LLM API → Parse & store responses → Preprocess all text → Run all three analysis modules → Synthesize cross-method results.

Design tradeoffs:
- Prompt specificity vs. creativity: A highly constrained prompt ensures consistency but may not probe deeper biases; a more open prompt introduces variability that complicates analysis.
- Group granularity: 15 intersectional groups provide intersectional insights but multiply the data collection burden (225 responses). Analysis per group also reduces statistical power per comparison.
- Model choice: Using a single model (GPT-4o) limits generalizability. A multi-model study is more robust but requires significant additional API cost and coordination.

Failure signatures:
- Prompt failure: LLM rejects persona ("I cannot role-play based on demographic"), returns identical lists for all groups, or produces malformed JSON.
- Data sparsity: For a group (e.g., Latino), Marked Words yields no significant words and JSD is near zero, suggesting the model's output for that persona is not lexically distinct from the unmarked baseline.
- Analysis incoherence: The top words from SVM and Marked Words for the same group show no semantic overlap, suggesting one method's output is unstable or the preprocessing/vectorization is flawed.

First 3 experiments:
1. Prompt Sanity Check: For 3 diverse groups (e.g., White Man, Black Woman, Middle-Eastern Man), generate 5 responses each. Human inspection: Do products and reasoning phrases vary meaningfully by group? Are there obvious stereotypes?
2. Replication of Marked Words Example: Using the small example corpus in Section 5.1, manually recalculate the z-score for "rice." Does the code implementation match the paper's step-by-step? This validates the implementation before full-scale runs.
3. SVM Stability Test: Train the SVM classifier for "Asian Woman" vs. "White Man" with 5 different random train/test splits (e.g., 80/20). Do the top 10 weighted words remain largely consistent across splits? High volatility suggests the model's distinguishing signal is weak or the dataset too small.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Open Question 1: How do implicit gender and race biases in LLM-generated product recommendations vary across different LLM architectures (e.g., Llama, Gemini, Mistral) compared to GPT-4o? Basis: Conclusion states future work will extend analysis to other models. Why unresolved: Only GPT-4o was studied; biases may differ. Evidence needed: Replicate with multiple LLMs using identical prompts and compare metrics.
- Open Question 2: Why did the Latino demographic group show no statistically significant marked words, and does this indicate underrepresentation in training data or a limitation of the method? Basis: Results note "Latino No significant words" and speculate. Why unresolved: No causal test. Evidence needed: Analyze training data demographics or apply alternative bias detection methods.
- Open Question 3: What causes the lower accuracy of SVM classifiers for gender groups (70%±21%) compared to race groups (98%±3%), and how can prompt design be improved to better capture gender biases? Basis: Results show disparity. Why unresolved: Cause not investigated. Evidence needed: Error analysis on misclassifications, alternative prompt formulations, feature analysis.
- Open Question 4: Can instruction fine-tuning or reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) effectively mitigate the identified biases in LLM recommendations, and what are the trade-offs? Basis: Conclusion proposes exploring mitigation. Why unresolved: No experiments conducted. Evidence needed: Implement fine-tuning/RLHF with bias objectives; evaluate post-mitigation bias and recommendation quality.

## Limitations
- Small per-group sample size (n=15) limits statistical power and may inflate variance in metrics.
- Incomplete documentation of preprocessing (anonymization rules, tokenization) hinders exact replication.
- Single-model focus (GPT-4o) restricts generalizability across LLM architectures.

## Confidence
- Marked Words identification of group-specific terms: Medium-High
- SVM classification accuracy (race groups): Low-Medium
- SVM classification accuracy (gender groups): Low-Medium
- Jensen-Shannon Divergence patterns: Medium
