# Adaptive Vision-Language Model Routing for Computer Use Agents

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.12823
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12823
- **Reference count**: 11
- **Primary result**: Projects up to 78% inference cost reduction while maintaining grounding accuracy within 2 percentage points of an all-large-model baseline.

## Executive Summary
Computer Use Agents (CUAs) traditionally rely on a single large Vision-Language Model (VLM) for all GUI actions, incurring high inference costs. Adaptive VLM Routing (AVR) introduces a lightweight router that dynamically selects between a small (7B) and large (72B) VLM per action. It estimates action difficulty via multimodal embeddings, probes the small model for confidence, and routes to the cheapest model meeting a reliability threshold. A memory store of past interactions boosts confidence for "warm" agents, and a safety guardrail overrides to the large model for high-risk actions. Evaluated on ScreenSpot-Pro, AVR projects up to 78% cost savings with less than 2% accuracy drop compared to using only the large model.

## Method Summary
AVR’s routing policy combines three signals: (1) a difficulty classifier that embeds the screenshot crop (SigLIP) and action description (MiniLM), then computes similarity to a contrastive knowledge base of “hard” and “easy” prototypes; (2) a confidence probe that queries the small VLM with logprobs enabled, optionally augmented by retrieved memories; (3) an adaptive threshold τ(ˆd) that varies with estimated difficulty. If confidence meets the threshold, the action is routed to the small model; otherwise, it escalates to the large model. A safety guardrail (Visual Confused Deputy) forcibly routes high-risk actions (e.g., delete, submit) to the large model. The objective minimizes total cost subject to an average grounding accuracy target τ_acc. Costs are computed per call using model pricing and probe overhead (~10% of full generation).

## Key Results
- Up to 78% inference cost reduction versus an all-large VLM baseline.
- Grounding accuracy within 2 percentage points of the all-large baseline.
- Memory-based warm routing reduces escalation rates, further improving cost efficiency.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
AVR works by exploiting the bimodal distribution of action difficulty in CUAs: many actions are trivial for a small model, while a few are genuinely hard. The difficulty classifier quickly identifies likely easy actions via similarity to prototypes; for these, a relatively low confidence threshold suffices. For harder actions, the threshold rises, ensuring only highly confident small-model predictions are accepted. The confidence probe directly measures the small model’s certainty, which correlates with correctness. Memory injection supplies analogous past successes, lifting confidence on similar new tasks and allowing more actions to stay on the small model. The safety guardrail provides a last line of defense against catastrophic errors on risky operations. This selective routing ensures the large model is used only when necessary, cutting costs dramatically while preserving overall accuracy.

## Foundational Learning
- **Multimodal Embeddings**  
  *Why needed*: Convert visual crops and text descriptions into comparable vectors for difficulty estimation.  
  *Quick check*: Verify cosine similarities fall within [-1,1] and that hard/easy clusters separate on a t-SNE plot.
- **Contrastive Knowledge Base**  
  *Why needed*: Supplies reference prototypes for “hard” and “easy” actions without requiring full labeling.  
  *Quick check*: Confirm prototypes are built from representative screens; test that known easy actions yield high similarity to easy prototypes.
- **Confidence Probing with Logprobs**  
  *Why needed*: Quantify the small model’s certainty; low confidence predicts failure.  
  *Quick check*: On a small set, compute mean logprob and check that successful predictions have significantly higher values.
- **Adaptive Threshold Routing**  
  *Why needed*: Different difficulties require different confidence cutoffs to meet accuracy targets; a fixed threshold would misroute many actions.  
  *Quick check*: Plot accuracy vs. confidence for binned difficulty scores; choose thresholds that achieve τ_acc per bin.
- **Memory-Augmented Prompting**  
  *Why needed*: Past successful interactions provide contextual cues that increase small-model confidence on similar tasks.  
  *Quick check*: For a query, retrieve top-3 memories and prepend; measure confidence change – expect a boost for warm agents.
- **Safety Guardrail (Visual Confused Deputy)**  
  *Why needed*: Prevent high-risk actions (e.g., deletion) from being executed by an unreliable small model.  
  *Quick check*: Feed known risky actions; verify the guardrail always forces escalation to the large model.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Input (Screenshot + History + Action Desc) -> [Embed (SigLIP/MiniLM) -> d_score] & [Small VLM Probe (+ Memory) -> Confidence] -> Router (adaptive τ(d)) -> Safety Guardrail -> Selected VLM (7B or 72B) -> Output (Coordinates)

**Critical path**:  
1. Compute visual and textual embeddings.  
2. Estimate difficulty score via prototype similarity.  
3. If warm, retrieve relevant memories from store.  
4. Probe small VLM with logprobs to obtain confidence.  
5. Apply adaptive threshold and safety override to decide model.  
6. Run chosen model to generate action coordinates.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- Cost vs. accuracy: Small model reduces cost but risks errors; adaptive threshold balances.  
- Memory overhead: Warm agents need storage and retrieval latency.  
- Probe overhead: ~10% extra small-model compute but avoids expensive large-model generations.  
- Safety guardrail: May over-escalate borderline actions, increasing cost.

**Failure signatures**:  
- High escalation on apps where 7B accuracy is known high → K_diff misalignment.  
- Low overall accuracy despite low escalation → threshold too permissive or confidence miscalibrated.  
- Memory injection decreases confidence → irrelevant or poor-quality retrievals.  
- Safety guardrail fires on many non-risky actions → risk threshold too sensitive.

**First three experiments**:  
1. **Difficulty classifier validation**: Collect per-app 7B accuracy on a CUA benchmark; compute d_scores; check correlation (Spearman ρ > 0.7).  
2. **Threshold calibration**: Run cold AVR (no memory) on held-out CUA tasks; plot ROC; set τ(ˆd) to achieve ~85% recall of 7B successes.  
3. **Memory effect quantification**: For identical samples with/without memory, measure confidence difference; require average Δconf ≥ 0.1 on ≥70% of warm successes.

## Limitations
- **K_diff construction**: The process for building the difficulty knowledge base (which apps are “hard” vs. “easy”, how prototypes are averaged) is not concretely specified, blocking exact replication.
- **Memory retrieval details**: Embedding model, retrieval count, and prompt formatting for memory injection are underspecified; confidence boost evidence comes from text tasks, not CUA grounding.
- **Probe cost overhead**: The estimated 10% overhead depends on the unspecified probe prompt length and output format; actual cost may differ.

## Confidence
- **78% cost reduction claim**: Medium confidence (relies on projected escalation rates that assume ideal memory boost and threshold tuning).
- **Accuracy within 2% claim**: Low confidence (ScreenSpot-Pro may not fully represent real CUA difficulty; safety guardrail integration could affect effective accuracy).

## Next Checks
1. **Validate K_diff alignment**: Gather per-app 7B accuracy on a CUA benchmark (e.g., OSWorld subset); adjust K_hard prototypes until d_scores correlate with true success rates.
2. **Calibrate thresholds on CUA data**: Execute cold AVR on held-out CUA tasks; derive difficulty-adaptive τ(ˆd) from ROC analysis to target ~85% recall of 7B successes.
3. **Quantify memory effect**: For a fixed set of CUA samples, compare confidence and escalation with/without memory retrieval; require Δconf ≥ 0.1 on at least 70% of warm successes to validate utility.
