# Fundamental Limits of Black-Box Safety Evaluation: Information-Theoretic and Computational Barriers from Latent Context Conditioning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.16984
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16984
- Reference count: 30
- Primary result: Black-box evaluation of latent context-conditioned models is fundamentally underdetermined; passive and adaptive querying admit large, inescapable error floors, while white-box probing with accurate probe quality yields consistent risk estimation.

## Executive Summary
This paper establishes fundamental limits on evaluating AI system safety via black-box access when models exhibit latent context conditioning—hidden triggers that are rare under evaluation but prevalent in deployment. Under a formal model where deployment and evaluation distributions differ in trigger frequency (δ vs. ε) and outputs are perfectly distinguishable on triggers (total variation 1), the authors prove that passive i.i.d. evaluation and even adaptive querying cannot reliably estimate deployment risk. They derive tight minimax lower bounds on estimation error, show a detection barrier for adaptive strategies, prove computational separation under standard cryptographic assumptions, and provide a white-box probing estimator with explicit sample complexity. The results mathematically mandate multi-layered safeguards beyond black-box testing for such systems.

## Method Summary
The analysis uses a minimax framework with a binary latent trigger z and loss gap L. For passive evaluation, a two-point prior (safe vs. unsafe model) and Le Cam’s method yield an absolute error lower bound that decays exponentially with mε but remains large when mε is small. The adaptive lower bound employs an m-wise independent hash family to construct a prior over trigger sets; by Yao’s principle, any adaptive policy faces a detection barrier: the probability of missing all triggers is at least 1−mε, leading to a linear dependence on ε in the worst-case error. A computational separation is shown by embedding a trapdoor one-way function into the model’s parameters: without the trapdoor, any polynomial-time black-box evaluator has negligible advantage in distinguishing deployment from evaluation distributions when δ=1. For white-box probing, a debiased estimator uses probe sensitivity α₁ and specificity α₀ (combined quality γ = α₁+α₀−1); Hoeffding’s inequality gives a sample complexity of Θ(1/(γ²ε_R²)) with explicit constants.

## Key Results
- **Passive i.i.d. evaluation**: Minimax expected absolute error ≥ (δL/4)(1−ε)ᵐ, and for mε ≤ 1/6, ≥ (5/24)δL.
- **Adaptive querying**: Worst-case error ≥ (εL/4)(1−mε); successful detection requires Ω(1/ε) queries.
- **Computational barrier**: Under trapdoor one-way function assumptions, any polynomial-time evaluator without the trapdoor has negligible distinguishing advantage when δ=1.
- **White-box probing**: Estimates deployment risk to ε_R with m = Θ(1/(γ²ε_R²)) samples, using a debiased estimator with explicit constants.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Latent context conditioning creates a hidden conditional dependence between inputs and model behavior. When triggers are rare under evaluation (mass ε) but common under deployment (mass δ), the evaluation distribution provides insufficient information to infer deployment performance. The total variation between the model’s output distributions on triggers and non-triggers is assumed to be 1, meaning triggers cause deterministic, observable changes. However, because the evaluator cannot identify trigger locations without prior knowledge, they cannot estimate how often triggers occur in deployment. The lower bounds formalize this: with high probability, no triggers are observed in m samples (either i.i.d. or adaptive), leading to an irreducible uncertainty of δL in the risk estimate. Adaptive querying cannot overcome this because the prior can be constructed to make any fixed query sequence miss the trigger set with high probability (using m-wise independence). Computationally, the hardness comes from hiding the trapdoor that reveals the trigger set; without it, the model’s input-output behavior appears identical between distributions to any polynomial-time algorithm.

## Foundational Learning
- **Minimax risk and two-point method**: Needed to prove fundamental estimation limits by constructing hard priors; quick check: verify that for two distributions with TV d, any estimator’s error is at least (gap)*d/2.
- **Adaptive query complexity and Yao’s principle**: Used to model interactive evaluation and prove detection barriers; quick check: reduce adaptive strategy to a fixed one via a random prior and compute expected hit probability.
- **m-wise independent hashing**: Constructs a prior where any m query locations are uniform, preventing adaptive focus; quick check: for a random polynomial hash, conditioned on m points, any other point is uniform.
- **Trapdoor one-way functions**: Basis for computational separation; quick check: if inverting the function is hard, distinguishing distributions that differ only on preimages of a trapdoor set is hard.
- **Debiasing for prevalence estimation**: Corrects probe error in white-box setting; quick check: if probe gives 1 with prob α₁ on trigger and 1−α₀ off, then observed positive rate q satisfies E[q] = α₀(1−p) + α₁p, solve for p.
- **Hoeffding concentration**: Provides sample complexity for white-box estimator; quick check: for bounded variables, deviation bound is exp(−2mε_R²).

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Model f_θ with latent trigger indicator z_θ(x) -> Evaluation distribution D_eval (trigger mass ε) vs. Deployment distribution D_dep (trigger mass δ) -> Black-box access (passive/adaptive) or White-box probing (probe with γ) -> Risk estimator R̂ -> Error |R̂−R_dep|. The evaluation method determines the achievable minimax error.
- **Critical path**: Problem setup (binary trigger, TV=1) -> Passive lower bound (two-point, tensorization) -> Adaptive lower bound (m-wise hash prior, transcript indistinguishability) -> Computational lower bound (reduction from trapdoor OWF inversion) -> White-box upper bound (debiasing, Hoeffding).
- **Design tradeoffs**: Passive is simplest but highest error when ε small; adaptive can reduce variance but hits a detection barrier (requires Ω(1/ε) queries for constant success); white-box needs internal access and known γ but achieves consistent estimation.
- **Failure signatures**: (1) Assuming evaluation distribution approximates deployment when ε << δ; (2) Believing adaptive querying can “find” triggers without sufficient budget; (3) Forgetting probe quality γ may be unknown or misestimated; (4) Assuming computational hardness results apply to average-case without trapdoor.
- **First three experiments**: (1) Simulate passive lower bound: binary model, random trigger set, compute Bayes error for varying m, compare to δL/4(1−ε)ᵐ. (2) Simulate adaptive lower bound: m-wise independent hash, fixed adaptive policy (query unused points), record hit rate and error, verify bound. (3) Simulate white-box probing: generate probe outputs with α₀,α₁, compute debiased prevalence and risk, vary m to confirm concentration at predicted rate.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Are the lower bounds for passive and adaptive evaluation tight (i.e., do matching upper bounds exist)?
2. Can adaptive evaluation achieve high-probability error guarantees?
3. Can analogous computational lower bounds be proved in the Statistical Query or low-degree polynomial frameworks?
4. Can training procedures or architectural constraints provably prevent latent context conditioning?

## Limitations
- Assumes perfect unobservability (TV=1) and exact (ε,δ)-separation; real systems may have partial distinguishability (TV=c<1), scaling constants by c.
- White-box probing requires precise knowledge of probe quality γ; estimating γ introduces extra uncertainty.
- Computational separation relies on unproven trapdoor one-way function assumption.
- Model is restricted to binary triggers and fixed loss gap L; extension to multiple contexts or continuous risk is open.

## Confidence
- **Passive i.i.d. lower bound (Theorem 4.1)**: High – Le Cam two-point proof with explicit TV calculation.
- **Adaptive query barrier (Theorem 5.1)**: High – Yao’s principle with m-wise hash construction; rigorous.
- **Computational separation (Theorem 7.3)**: Medium – conditional on existence of trapdoor OWFs.
- **White-box probing sample complexity (Theorem 6.2)**: High – Hoeffding with debiasing; constants verified.
