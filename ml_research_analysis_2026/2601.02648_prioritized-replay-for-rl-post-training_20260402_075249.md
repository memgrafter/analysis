# Prioritized Replay for RL Post-training

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.02648
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.02648
- Reference count: 23
- Primary result: Prioritized replay using a success‑rate‑based priority score (ω = p(1−p)) consistently outperforms uniform sampling on MATH500 and AIME2024 when fine‑tuning Qwen2.5‑7B with GRPO.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles inefficient data selection in reinforcement learning (RL) post‑training for large language models (LLMs). Existing approaches waste compute on uninformative problems by using uniform or manually designed curricula. The authors propose a simple, model‑driven priority score ω = p(1−p) (plus a small bias when p≥0.5) that peaks at an empirical success rate p ≈ 0.5, thereby focusing training on problems that yield the largest gradient signal from GRPO’s zero‑sum advantage. The system is implemented with a max‑heap for sampling and a periodic retesting mechanism to avoid starvation of solved/unsolved problems. In a proof‑of‑concept experiment fine‑tuning Qwen2.5‑7B on a 1,000‑problem subset of GURU, prioritized replay shows superior learning efficiency compared to uniform sampling, as measured by pass@1 and pass@4 on MATH500 and AIME2024.

## Method Summary
The method integrates Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with prioritized replay. Each problem’s priority is computed as ω = p(1−p) + ε (ε=1e−4 if p≥0.5), where p is the exponential moving average of success rates. Problems are stored in a max‑heap and sampled with high priority, while 12.5% of each batch is drawn uniformly to maintain exploration. To prevent solved (p=1) and unsolved (p=0) problems from being permanently neglected, they are placed in separate pools and retested every 10 training steps: one solved and three unsolved problems are selected, re‑evaluated with rollouts, and reintegrated into the heap with updated priorities. Training proceeds for 250 steps with N=8 rollouts per problem, batch size 4, learning rate 1e−6, and other hyperparameters fixed. Performance is evaluated on MATH500 and AIME2024 using pass@1 and pass@4.

## Key Results
- Prioritized replay surpasses uniform sampling on both pass@1 and pass@4 metrics across MATH500 and AIME2024 over the full 250‑step training.
- The advantage is particularly pronounced in the early stages of training, indicating more efficient learning.
- The simple priority function effectively concentrates compute on problems with intermediate success rates, maximizing gradient information.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The mechanism hinges on the relationship between the success probability p and the gradient magnitude in GRPO. Because GRPO uses a zero‑sum advantage (i.e., centered within each group of rollouts for a given prompt), the variance of the advantage is proportional to p(1−p). This variance directly scales the expected gradient update: when p is near 0.5, updates are largest; when p is near 0 or 1, updates vanish. The priority score ω = p(1−p) therefore approximates the per‑problem learning potential. By biasing sampling toward problems with ω near its maximum, the algorithm allocates more training steps to where they yield the greatest improvement. The periodic retesting of solved and unsolved problems ensures that these extreme cases are occasionally re‑evaluated, preventing permanent starvation and allowing recovery from noisy p estimates. The uniform fraction provides a safety net for exploration. Together, these elements lead to a more data‑efficient use of compute.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Problem Set → Success Rate Tracker → Priority Calculator → Max‑Heap → Batch Sampler (with Uniform Sampler) → Training Loop; Retest Manager ↔ Max‑Heap and solved/unsolved pools.

**Critical path**  
1. Sample a batch from the max‑heap (12.5% uniform).  
2. For each problem, generate N rollouts and compute GRPO loss.  
3. Update the model parameters.  
4. Update each problem’s success rate p based on rollout outcomes.  
5. Recompute priorities ω and update the heap.  
6. Every 10 steps: run retest – sample 1 solved and 3 unsolved from their pools, generate rollouts, update their p, and reintegrate into the heap.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Priority function ω = p(1−p) is simple but sensitive to noise; EMA smooths at the cost of lag.  
- Retest frequency (10 steps) balances revisiting stale problems versus extra computation.  
- Uniform fraction (12.5%) guarantees exploration but dilutes the prioritization signal.  
- Initial ω=0.2 sets a baseline for never‑sampled problems; too low risks starvation, too high reduces focus.

**Failure signatures**  
- Starvation: Heap size shrinks; many problems never visited. Monitor distinct problem count.  
- Instability: Loss or gradient norms spike; may require lower learning rate.  
- Forgetting: Performance on easy/solved problems drops over time; check retest frequency.  
- Priority bugs: ω does not peak near p=0.5; verify after update.

**Three first experiments**  
1. **Validate priority dynamics**: Run a short training (e.g., 10 steps) with detailed logging of p and ω per problem. Confirm ω peaks when p≈0.5 and that solved/unsolved pools are correctly reintegrated.  
2. **Baseline uniform consistency**: Execute the uniform baseline (priority disabled) and inspect the sampling distribution to ensure uniformity (with or without replacement as intended).  
3. **Retest sensitivity**: Vary the retest interval (5, 10, 20 steps) and compare performance and starvation metrics to assess robustness.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*The paper does not explicitly enumerate open questions; however, several directions for future work are implied by its limitations and suggested next checks.*
- **Bias term impact**: The paper introduces a small bias ε=1e−4 when p≥0.5, but does not isolate its effect on priority ordering and final performance. Determining whether this term is necessary and how it interacts with the core ω = p(1−p) signal remains an open question.
- **Scalability and domain generalization**: Evaluation was limited to a 1,000‑problem subset of GURU and two math benchmarks (MATH500, AIME2024). It is unknown whether the findings generalize to larger problem sets, different domains (e.g., code generation, factual QA), or other model architectures and sizes.
- **Hyperparameter robustness**: The algorithm uses fixed hyperparameters (retest interval=10 steps, uniform fraction=12.5%, EMA smoothing α=0.8). Their optimal values may vary with dataset characteristics; the sensitivity of prioritized replay to these choices is an open question.
- **Integration with other RL algorithms**: The study focuses on GRPO. It is an open question whether the same priority design is effective with other policy gradient methods (e.g., PPO, DPO) or with different advantage estimators.
- **Long‑term learning dynamics**: While the paper demonstrates early advantages, the long‑term behavior (e.g., after many more training steps) and potential for catastrophic forgetting or saturation are not explored.

## Limitations
- Implementation details are ambiguously reported (e.g., EMA update direction, uniform sampling mode, retest thresholds), hindering exact replication.
- Evaluation is limited to a small (1k‑problem) stratified GURU subset and two math benchmarks (MATH500, AIME2024); scalability and generalization remain unverified.
- The conditional bias term ε (added only when p≥0.5) may subtly alter priority ordering, and its isolated impact was not studied.
