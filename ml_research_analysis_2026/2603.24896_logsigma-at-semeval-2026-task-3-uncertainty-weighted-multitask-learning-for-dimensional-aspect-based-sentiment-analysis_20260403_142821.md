# LogSigma at SemEval-2026 Task 3: Uncertainty-Weighted Multitask Learning for Dimensional Aspect-Based Sentiment Analysis

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.24896
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24896
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: LOGSIGMA achieved 1st place on five of seven submitted datasets across both tracks.

## Executive Summary
Dimensional Aspect-Based Sentiment Analysis (DimABSA) requires predicting continuous Valence and Arousal scores for aspects in text, with Arousal consistently harder to predict. LogSigma addresses this through learned homoscedastic uncertainty to automatically balance regression losses, combined with language-specific encoders and multi-seed ensembling. The system ranked first on five of seven datasets and outperformed baselines including large LLMs, achieving up to 11.2% RMSE reduction where the Valence-Arousal difficulty gap was widest.

## Method Summary
LogSigma employs multitask learning with two regression heads for Valence and Arousal. It uses language-specific pretrained transformers (e.g., GBERT for German, Twitter-RoBERTa for English) to capture linguistic nuances. A key innovation is learned homoscedastic uncertainty: task-specific log-variance parameters weight the MSE losses, preventing the noisier Arousal task from dominating training. Additionally, predictions from three independently trained seeds are ensembled to reduce variance.

## Key Results
- LOGSIGMA achieved 1st place on five of seven submitted datasets.
- Arousal had a consistently lower Pearson Correlation Coefficient than Valence, with an average gap of +0.29 across all 15 datasets.
- Uncertainty weighting yielded up to 11.2% relative RMSE reduction over baselines, especially on datasets with large Valence-Arousal difficulty gaps.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Automatic Loss Balancing via Learned Uncertainty
The model learns task-specific log-variance parameters (s = logσ²) to weight each task's MSE loss by 1/(2σ²). This downweights the noisier task (Arousal) to prevent it from destabilizing training. The homoscedastic assumption treats noise as constant per task. Evidence: ablation shows RMSE improvements up to 11.2% on high-difficulty-gap datasets (e.g., Pidgin), and learned variance weights vary by language (0.66× for German to 2.18× for English). Break condition: if Valence and Arousal difficulty are similar, variance ratios converge to ~1, reducing adaptive benefit.

### Mechanism 2: Language-Specific Encoders
Dedicated pretrained transformers per language (e.g., GBERT, Twitter-RoBERTa) capture linguistic and cultural nuances, outperforming a single multilingual model (XLM-R) by 8.2% on Track A. The approach assumes language-specific pretraining (especially in-domain) yields better representations. Evidence: consistent top rankings across German, English, Swahili; ablation shows dedicated encoders beat XLM-R. Break condition: if languages are highly similar or a single multilingual model is pretrained on all target domains, the gap narrows.

### Mechanism 3: Multi-Seed Ensembling
Averaging predictions from three independently trained models reduces variance from random initialization, smoothing out convergence to different local minima. Evidence: ensemble reduces RMSE by 3–5% over the best single seed (e.g., -4.8% on eng_restaurant). Break condition: if training is deterministic or dataset is very large/homogeneous, ensembling benefits diminish.

## Foundational Learning
### Multitask Learning (MTL)
**Why needed**: DimABSA requires predicting two continuous targets from the same input. MTL shares encoder representations, improving data efficiency and regularization.
**Quick check**: If you remove one task head and train only for Valence, would the shared encoder representations become less useful for Arousal, or vice versa?

### Homoscedastic vs. Heteroscedastic Uncertainty
**Why needed**: The paper uses homoscedastic (task-specific constant) uncertainty to weight losses, assuming noise level is input-independent per task. This is simpler than heteroscedastic (input-dependent) uncertainty.
**Quick check**: Could the noise in Arousal annotations depend on the input text (e.g., more ambiguous sentences have higher label variance)? If yes, homoscedastic uncertainty is an approximation.

### Regression vs. Generation for Continuous Output
**Why needed**: The task is regression to a continuous 1-9 scale. The paper rejects framing as text generation due to tokenization discretization and structural mismatch.
**Quick check**: What are the fundamental limitations of using an autoregressive LLM's next-token prediction to output a floating-point number?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:
1. Input Formatter: `[CLS] text [SEP] aspect [SEP]`
2. Language-Specific Encoder (e.g., `twitter-roberta-large` for English).
3. Dual Regression Heads: Two independent linear layers projecting `[CLS]` hidden state to scalar Valence and Arousal.
4. Learned Uncertainty Module: Two scalar `logσ²` parameters (`s_V`, `s_A`) that weight the loss contributions.
5. Training Loop: Compute `L_V` (MSE), `L_A` (MSE). Combined loss `L = (exp(-s_V)/2)*L_V + (exp(-s_A)/2)*L_A + s_V/2 + s_A/2`. Update encoder weights (low LR) and `s_V`, `s_A` (high LR) separately.
6. Inference Ensemble: For each language, load 3 seed checkpoints. Average their `(V_out, A_out)` tensors.

**Critical path**: The learned uncertainty parameters and their high learning rate (2,500× encoder LR) are critical for dynamic loss balancing during limited fine-tuning epochs. Without the high LR, `s_V`/`s_A` may not adapt quickly enough.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Language-specific vs. Multilingual Encoder: Specific encoders give +8.2% avg gain on Track A but require maintaining 5-7 model binaries. Multilingual (XLM-R) is simpler but weaker.
- Homoscedastic vs. Heteroscedastic Uncertainty: Homoscedastic is simple (2 scalars); heteroscedastic could capture input-dependent noise but adds complexity and overfitting risk.
- 3-Seed Ensemble: Provides ~3-5% RMSE reduction at 3× inference cost; a single high-quality seed with early stopping might approach performance with higher variance.

**Failure signatures**:
- **High Arousal error consistently**: Check if `σ²_A` is being down-weighted (high). Indicates Arousal is too noisy due to poor encoder or dataset ambiguity.
- **Unstable learned `σ²_V / σ²_A` ratios across seeds**: Indicates no clear V/A difficulty signal; could be due to small dataset or high label noise.
- **Domain transfer failure**: Same language encoder performs well on reviews but poorly on stance; encoder pretraining domain may not align with target domain.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Baseline without uncertainty**: Train a single seed per language with `L = L_V + L_A`. Verify Arousal RMSE/PCC is worse than Valence, and overall RMSE is higher than the final system.
2. **Single-seed vs. Ensemble**: Take best trained seed per language. Compare dev RMSE to 3-seed average. Expect 3-5% relative RMSE drop from ensembling.
3. **Encoder selection ablation**: For a language with dedicated pretrained model (e.g., German with GBERT), replace with XLM-R-Large and retrain with uncertainty. Expect significant RMSE increase (e.g., GBERT-L 0.940 vs. XLM-R-L 1.143 on German politics).

## Limitations
- Homoscedastic uncertainty assumption may be invalid if Arousal annotation noise is input-dependent (e.g., ambiguous sentences).
- Language-specific encoders increase maintenance overhead and may not generalize to new languages/domains without dedicated pretrained models.
- Multi-seed ensembling triples inference cost, with likely diminishing returns on larger, more homogeneous datasets.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (Uncertainty Weighting)**: High confidence in observed RMSE improvements; Medium confidence in the homoscedastic assumption's validity across all languages.
- **Mechanism 2 (Language-Specific Encoders)**: High confidence—consistent ranking gains and ablation results strongly support this design.
- **Mechanism 3 (Multi-Seed Ensembling)**: Medium confidence—standard ensemble benefits are demonstrated, but cost-benefit at scale is untested.

## Next Checks
1. **Heteroscedastic Stress Test**: Replace homoscedastic loss with a heteroscedastic head predicting σ per sample. Compare stability of learned weights and final RMSE/PCC on high-ambiguity subsets (e.g., neutral or conflicting aspect expressions).
2. **Universal Encoder Comparison**: Train a single XLM-R (or LLM-based) model on all combined languages/domains using the same uncertainty-weighted multitask loss. Evaluate against the language-specific ensemble to quantify the trade-off between simplicity and performance, especially for low-resource languages.
3. **Ensemble Scaling Analysis**: For one high-resource language (e.g., English), measure ensemble variance (std dev across seeds) and relative RMSE gain as training data size increases (e.g., 25%, 50%, 100% of training set). Identify the data size where ensembling’s marginal benefit falls below 1%.
