# Transformers converge to invariant algorithmic cores

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.22600
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22600
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Transformer training converges to low-dimensional, invariant algorithmic cores that capture necessary and sufficient computation across seeds and model scales.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the non-identifiability problem in mechanistic interpretability—different weight configurations can implement the same function. It introduces Algorithmic Core Extraction (ACE), which identifies low-dimensional subspaces of internal activations that are both necessary and sufficient for task performance, using a cross-covariance SVD and causal ablations. Across synthetic Markov-chain transformers, modular addition, and GPT-2 subject-verb agreement, the authors show that independently trained models embed functionally equivalent cores, often just 1–3 dimensions, even when their weight matrices are nearly orthogonal. These cores remain stable under weight decay and across scale, suggesting that gradient descent converges to shared algorithmic essences.

## Method Summary
ACE operates on a trained model: collect final-layer activations \(H\) and output Jacobians \(J\) over a test set. Compute the SVD of \(L^\top \Gamma\), where \(L\) and \(\Gamma\) are Cholesky factors of the activation covariance \(H^\top H\) and sensitivity matrix \(J^\top J\). The left singular vectors define a core subspace of rank \(r\) (chosen via energy threshold). Causal validation uses ablations: projecting activations onto the core (core-only) and removing it (core-removed) test sufficiency and necessity, respectively. The extracted core is further characterized by fitting a linear operator to its state transitions and comparing spectra to ground-truth dynamics. This pipeline is applied to multiple models trained on the same task to assess invariance via geometric and statistical alignment (e.g., CCA).

## Key Results
- Single-layer Markov-chain transformers embed a 3D core in nearly orthogonal subspaces across seeds, yet these cores share identical transition spectra (eigenvalue error < 1%) and high CCA (> 0.98), indicating functional equivalence.
- For modular addition, cores crystallize at grokking into compact cyclic operators; extended weight decay inflates the core dimension (~ 15 → 60) by spreading signal across redundant rotational modes.
- Across GPT‑2 scales (117 M–774 M parameters), subject‑verb agreement relies on a 1D core in late layers—its removal collapses agreement (AUC ≤ 0.25), its preservation suffices (AUC ≥ 0.97), and flipping it inverts grammatical number throughout generation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### 1. Core extraction via cross-covariance
ACE computes the SVD of \(HJ^\top\) to find subspaces that are both highly activated (input-driven) and output‑sensitive. These subspaces are tested for necessity (removal drops accuracy to chance) and sufficiency (alone preserves accuracy). This works if the network learns an efficient, compressible computation.  
*Evidence*: abstract; Methods § “Algorithmic Core Extraction”; ablations show cores meet both criteria.  
*Break condition*: If the extracted subspace fails both necessity and sufficiency on held‑out data, or rank selection yields a dimension that does not meet causal criteria.

### 2. Functional equivalence across seeds
Different training runs find parameterizations that are geometrically distinct (orthogonal subspaces) but implement the same input‑output map. CCA aligns the subspaces by maximizing correlation between projected activations, revealing shared functional structure invariant to rotation.  
*Evidence*: abstract; Results § “Cores are embedded in orthogonal subspaces but statistically aligned”.  
*Break condition*: If CCA between cores from different seeds falls below a threshold (e.g., < 0.9) despite both passing necessity/sufficiency.

### 3. Core inflation under weight decay
After grokking, weight decay minimizes norm while preserving performance. When multiple rotational modes are functionally equivalent, the minimum‑norm solution distributes amplitude across all modes rather than concentrating in one, increasing the number of *necessary* core dimensions while *sufficient* dimensions remain stable.  
*Evidence*: abstract; Results § “Weight decay inflates core dimension”; Box 1.  
*Break condition*: If disabling weight decay after grokking still causes core inflation, or if core dimension increases without a widening gap between necessary and sufficient ranks.

## Foundational Learning
- **Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA)**: *Why needed*: Distinguishes geometric alignment (subspace angle) from functional alignment (correlation of projected activations). The paper’s core invariance claim hinges on high CCA despite orthogonal subspaces. *Quick check*: If two subspaces have near‑zero cosine similarity but CCA > 0.95, what does that imply about their relationship?
- **Linear System Minimal Realization (Kalman Decomposition)**: *Why needed*: Theoretically motivates ACE—only the reachable/observable subspace determines input‑output behavior. The necessary/sufficient criterion mirrors controllable/observable subspaces in control theory. *Quick check*: In a linear system \(\dot{x}=Ax+Bu, y=Cx\), which states can be removed without affecting input‑output maps?
- **Functional Equivalence & System Drift**: *Why needed*: Explains why different seeds yield different weights but same function. Understanding drift (orthogonal subspaces) vs. invariance (shared core) is central. *Quick check*: If two networks have identical test accuracy but weight cosine similarity ≈ 0, what might differ and what must be conserved?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Hidden states (h) -> Jacobians (J) -> Core extractor (SVD of HJᵀ) -> Validator (ablations) -> Dynamics fitter (A) -> Alignment tool (CCA)

**Critical path**  
1. Train multiple models (different seeds) on same task, verify equivalent test accuracy.  
2. Extract core from each via ACE; ensure it passes necessity/sufficiency ablations.  
3. Compare cores geometrically (subspace angles/overlap) and statistically (CCA).  
4. Fit linear dynamics in core; interpret spectrum.  
5. For scaling: align cores across model sizes, test steering interventions.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Rank selection: Energy threshold vs. ablation‑refined minimal rank.  
- Layer choice: Deeper layers hold more abstractions but may mix functions.  
- Jacobian sampling: Exact vs. finite differences; noise vs. feasibility.  
- Core alignment: >1D needs multi‑dimensional CCA/Procrustes; 1D sign matters.

**Failure signatures**  
- Core fails necessity: removing core does not drop accuracy to chance.  
- Core fails sufficiency: core‑only accuracy < baseline.  
- No spectral recovery: fitted eigenvalues do not match expected task structure.  
- Cross‑run CCA low (< 0.8) despite identical performance.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. *Markov chain validation*: synthetic task with known transition matrix; extract 3D core; validate ablations, geometric orthogonality but CCA ≈ 1, spectrum recovery.  
2. *Modular addition grokking*: track core dimension and fitted operator spectrum across training; expect condensation at grokking, eigenvalues on unit circle; test weight‑decay inflation.  
3. *GPT‑2 agreement scaling*: extract 1D core from each model size at layer with maximal flip effect; validate necessity/sufficiency; test steering; align cores across scales.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1**: Do low‑dimensional algorithmic cores persist for complex multi‑step reasoning tasks? *Basis*: "whether cores remain low‑dimensional for complex multi‑step reasoning tasks is untested" (discussion). *Resolution*: Apply ACE to models trained on diverse complex reasoning benchmarks, measure core dimensionality, and show necessity/sufficiency.  
- **Question 2**: How can algorithmic cores be extracted and validated in large, multifunctional language models without fine‑tuning on single‑task datasets? *Basis*: "extracting task‑specific cores from multifunctional models requires framing precise mechanistic inquiries" (discussion). *Resolution*: Develop systematic task decomposition and intervention methods.  
- **Question 3**: Can ACE be given a rigorous mathematical foundation (e.g., via observability theory) to guarantee properties like minimality and uniqueness? *Basis*: "tighter connections to observability theory ... could place the method on more rigorous mathematical footing" (discussion). *Resolution*: Link to Kalman decomposition, prove consistency.  
- **Question 4**: What universal invariant quantities (spectral, topological, information‑geometric) characterize algorithmic cores across diverse tasks and architectures? *Basis*: "future work might discover invariants empirically through cross‑model comparison" (discussion). *Resolution*: Large‑scale cross‑model analysis to identify repeating invariants.

## Limitations
- ACE’s sensitivity to noise in Jacobian estimation and whether low‑rank subspaces truly capture nonlinear algorithmic structure.
- Whether core sufficiency holds for more complex, multi‑component tasks beyond controlled benchmarks.
- The generality of 1D cores in large models; subject‑verb agreement may be a special case, and scaling to diverse capabilities remains open.

## Confidence
- **Synthetic Markov‑chain validation**: High
- **Modular addition grokking dynamics**: Medium
- **GPT‑2 scaling claims**: Low

## Next Checks
1. **Nonlinear task stress test**: Apply ACE to a transformer solving a clearly nonlinear algorithmic task (e.g., parity or nested loops) to probe the limits of linear core extraction.
2. **Method comparison**: Re‑analyze the same models using SVCCA or mean‑CCA; compare ACE subspaces to top SVCCA components to validate ACE as an efficient summary.
3. **Cross‑task intervention**: For GPT‑2, extract cores for multiple syntactic/semantic tasks (e.g., tense agreement, pronoun resolution). Test whether flipping a task‑specific core dimension selectively corrupts that task without collapsing general fluency.
