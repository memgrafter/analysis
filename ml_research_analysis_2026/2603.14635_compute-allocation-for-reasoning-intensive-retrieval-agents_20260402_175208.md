# Compute Allocation for Reasoning-Intensive Retrieval Agents

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.14635
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14635
- **Reference count:** 5
- **Primary result:** For reasoning-intensive retrieval, concentrating compute on the re-ranking stage (via stronger models and deeper candidate pools) yields substantially higher quality gains than distributing compute across query expansion or inference-time "thinking".

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how to optimally allocate computational resources in multi-stage LLM-augmented retrieval pipelines for reasoning-intensive queries. Using the BRIGHT benchmark and Gemini 2.5 models, the authors systematically ablate three key axes: model capacity (Flash-Lite to Pro), inference-time "thinking" (CoT), and re-ranking depth. They find that re-ranking is the high-leverage stage: gains from using a stronger re-ranking model (+7.5 NDCG@10) and increasing the candidate pool size (+21% from k=10 to 100) are substantial. In contrast, query expansion exhibits rapidly diminishing returns beyond lightweight models, and inference-time thinking provides negligible improvement at both stages while incurring high cost and latency. The core recommendation is to avoid uniform compute distribution and instead prioritize budget for deeper and more capable re-ranking.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates a standard retrieval pipeline: Query Expansion (QE) → Initial Retrieval (BM25) → Re-ranking (RR). For QE, an LLM expands the original query. BM25 retrieves a candidate pool of depth *k*. An LLM re-ranker then list-wise ranks the top-*k* candidates, with the final top-10 evaluated. The primary evaluation metric is NDCG@10, with cost ($/query) and latency (s) as constraints. Experiments vary the QE model (Flash-Lite, Flash-No-Think, Flash-Think, Pro), RR model (same variants), and re-ranking depth *k* (10, 20, 50, 100). This isolates the marginal contribution of each component against its associated inference cost.

## Key Results
- Re-ranking with a stronger model (Pro vs. Flash-Lite) improves NDCG@10 by **+7.5** points.
- Increasing re-ranking depth from *k*=10 to *k*=100 yields a **+21%** NDCG@10 gain.
- Query expansion saturates quickly: moving from no-QE to Flash-Lite gives a large jump, but upgrading from Flash-Lite to Pro yields only **+1.1** NDCG@10 at 27x the cost.
- Inference-time "thinking" provides minimal quality gains (<+0.6 NDCG@10 in RR) while increasing cost by ~20-52% and latency by ~40%.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Compute concentrated in the re-ranking stage yields the largest quality gains for reasoning-intensive retrieval.
- **Mechanism:** Re-ranking is a multi-document comparative evaluation task. A stronger model can perform nuanced reasoning across candidates to identify relevance that depends on implicit constraints, which are missed by lexical matching in initial retrieval. Deeper candidate pools increase the chance that relevant "bridge" documents are present for the re-ranker to discover.
- **Core assumption:** The bottleneck for final quality is the deliberation and comparison step (re-ranking), not the vocabulary expansion step (query expansion). Initial retrieval recall can be scaled cheaply (e.g., via BM25 depth), but recognizing latent relevance among those candidates requires expensive model inference.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "re-ranking benefits substantially from stronger models (+7.5 NDCG@10) and deeper candidate pools (+21% from k=10 to 100)"
  - [section 3.2.1] Table 2 shows NDCG@10 increases from 36.54 (Flash-Lite RR) to 41.19 (Pro RR) with fixed QE and k=100.
  - [section 3.2.2] Figure 2 shows consistent ~20% NDCG@10 gains when increasing k from 10 to 100 across QE variants.
- **Break condition:** If the initial candidate pool (from BM25) is so poor that relevant documents never enter the top-k for re-ranking, then increasing re-ranking compute is wasted. This is mitigated by ensuring query expansion and initial retrieval recall are adequate.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Query expansion exhibits rapidly diminishing returns beyond lightweight models.
- **Mechanism:** Query expansion primarily serves to inject relevant terminology and surface implicit constraints into the query string, acting as a vocabulary booster. This is largely a lexical/surface-coverage task. Once basic constraint terms are added by a small model, larger models add minimal new "keywords" that BM25 can exploit, leading to a plateau in recall and downstream NDCG.
- **Core assumption:** The value of query expansion is bounded by the initial retrieval algorithm's (BM25's) ability to match those expanded terms in the corpus. It does not require complex reasoning, just effective term generation.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "query expansion shows diminishing returns beyond lightweight models (+1.1 NDCG@10 from weak to strong)"
  - [section 3.1] Table 1: NDCG@10 jumps from 14.52 (no QE) to 28.87 (Flash-Lite QE), but from Flash-Lite to Pro only increases by 1.14 points (28.87 -> 30.01), while cost increases 27x.
  - [section 3.1] "Counterintuitive finding: Flash (Think) produces a lower Recall@10 than Flash (No-Think)" suggests deliberation harms simple lexical expansion.
- **Break condition:** If the reasoning required to identify relevant constraints is extremely complex and cannot be captured by simple term expansion, then a stronger QE model might be needed. The BRIGHT benchmark's "reasoning-intensive" nature seems to be handled later in the pipeline.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Inference-time "thinking" (extended chain-of-thought) provides minimal to negative improvement at either QE or RR stages.
- **Mechanism:** "Thinking" adds internal deliberation steps before final output. In QE, this may cause over-composition of the query (e.g., generating verbose, out-of-distribution phrases) that hurts BM25 matching. In RR, while the task is complex, the model may already be capable of the needed reasoning in a single forward pass; extra steps add noise or cause hedging without improving the ranking decision. The cost/latency overhead is substantial.
- **Core assumption:** The Gemini 2.5 Flash models are sufficiently capable for the core reasoning in these tasks without explicit CoT scaffolding, and the benchmark's relevance judgments may not reward nuanced multi-step justification.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Inference-time thinking provides minimal improvement at either stage."
  - [section 3.1] Flash (Think) has lower Recall@10 (57.73) than Flash (No-Think) (58.56) and is 52% more costly.
  - [section 3.2.1] Flash (Think) RR has only a +0.56 NDCG@10 gain over Flash (No-Think) RR (+20% cost, +40% latency).
- **Break condition:** If the task requires verifiable multi-step justification (e.g., mathematical proof checking) or if the base model is smaller/less capable, thinking might become essential. The optimal "thinking" configuration is likely task- and model-specific.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Raw Query -> Query Expansion (QE) -> Expanded Query -> BM25 (initial retrieval, depth k) -> Candidate Pool (top-k) -> Re-ranker (RR) -> Final Ranked List (top-10).
- **Critical path:** The sequential flow from raw query through QE, initial BM25 retrieval, and final LLM re-ranking defines the pipeline. Compute is allocated via model variant selection (QE, RR) and the *k* hyperparameter for re-ranking depth.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **QE Model Strength vs. Cost:** Stronger QE models provide marginal NDCG gains at high cost. Default to Flash-Lite unless a specific need for more expansion is identified.
  - **Re-ranking Depth (k) vs. Linear Cost:** Each doubling of *k* provides significant, predictable NDCG gains (Figure 2). This is a primary lever for quality.
  - **Re-ranking Model Strength vs. Thinking:** Flash-No-Think offers best cost-effectiveness. Pro model is highest quality but expensive. "Thinking" mode in Flash offers negligible ROI.
  - **Uniform vs. Asymmetric Allocation:** Avoid splitting budget equally. Prioritize RR depth and model strength over QE strength.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Symptom:** High re-ranking NDCG but low Recall@10 overall. **Cause:** Initial retrieval/ QE failed to place relevant docs in the candidate pool (*k* too small or QE too weak). Fix: Increase *k* or use slightly stronger QE (Flash-Lite/No-Think).
  - **Symptom:** System is too slow/expensive. **Cause:** Using Pro models or "Think" modes, or setting *k* excessively high. Fix: Switch RR to Flash-No-Think, reduce *k* to 50 if 20% gain from 50->100 is not critical.
  - **Symptom:** No improvement when scaling QE model. **Cause:** Expected behavior on BRIGHT; QE is saturated. Fix: Re-allocate QE budget to increase *k* or upgrade RR model.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **QE Saturation Test:** For a fixed RR setup (e.g., Flash-No-Think, k=100), run full pipeline with QE models: No-QE, Flash-Lite, Flash-No-Think, Pro. Plot NDCG@10 vs. QE cost. Expect steep initial rise then flat line.
  2. **Re-ranking Depth Sweep:** Fix QE (Flash-Lite) and RR model (Flash-No-Think). Run with k = {10, 20, 50, 100}. Plot NDCG@10 and cost vs. k. Expect consistent positive slope.
  3. **Thinking Ablation:** For a fixed QE and fixed *k* (e.g., 100), compare RR models: Flash-No-Think vs. Flash-Think. Measure delta NDCG, delta cost, delta latency. Expect minimal NDCG gain for significant cost/latency increase.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question:** Do the observed asymmetric returns (stronger re-ranking vs. query expansion) generalize beyond the BRIGHT benchmark, Gemini 2.5 model family, and BM25 retrieval?
  - **Basis in paper:** The Future Work section explicitly states the study’s limitation to a single benchmark, model family, and retrieval algorithm, calling for validation across diverse settings.
  - **Why unresolved:** The findings are derived from one reasoning-intensive benchmark and one provider’s model family; different domains, architectures, or retrieval methods may alter the cost-performance trade-offs.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic replication using multiple reasoning-intensive benchmarks (e.g., HotpotQA, scientific QA datasets), varied model families (e.g., GPT, Claude), and alternative retrievers (dense, hybrid), measuring whether re-ranking concentration remains optimal.
- **Question:** How does the choice of initial retriever (e.g., BM25 vs. dense vs. hybrid) moderate the optimal compute allocation between query expansion and re-ranking?
  - **Basis in paper:** The methodology selects BM25 specifically because LLM-generated queries may be out-of-distribution for dense retrievers, but this design choice may skew the observed diminishing returns in query expansion.
  - **Why unresolved:** Only BM25 was tested; different retrievers have distinct error profiles (e.g., lexical vs. semantic mismatch), which could change the marginal value of query expansion and thus the allocation strategy.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments varying only the initial retriever while keeping query expansion and re-ranking models fixed, to see if the plateau in query expansion benefits persists or if certain retrievers shift the optimal compute distribution.
- **Question:** Under what query or document characteristics (e.g., single-hop vs. multi-hop reasoning, constraint complexity) does inference-time "thinking" provide non-marginal gains in query expansion or re-ranking?
  - **Basis in paper:** The paper reports minimal improvement from thinking modes, speculating that query expansion is primarily a vocabulary task. This speculation is not validated across finer-grained reasoning types within BRIGHT.
  - **Why unresolved:** BRIGHT contains diverse reasoning-intensive queries, but the analysis aggregates results; thinking may help for specific subcategories (e.g., multi-hop, numerical reasoning) but this is unexplored.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Error analysis stratified by query reasoning type (e.g., using BRIGHT’s domain annotations) to correlate thinking benefits with specific reasoning patterns, or controlled synthetic queries varying reasoning depth.
- **Question:** How do compute allocation principles change in dynamic agent memory scenarios where query distributions and memory contents evolve over long horizons?
  - **Basis in paper:** The study uses a static benchmark, but real agent memories grow and query distributions shift, potentially affecting retrieval difficulty and the marginal value of deeper re-ranking pools.
  - **Why unresolved:** BRIGHT represents a fixed snapshot; agent trajectories involve temporal dependencies and concept drift, which may alter the optimal depth or model strength for re-ranking.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Simulations or datasets of agent interactions (e.g., from MemGPT or generative agents) tracking memory growth and query evolution, measuring how optimal *k* and model allocation shift over time.

## Limitations
- Findings are restricted to the Gemini 2.5 model family and the BRIGHT benchmark, limiting generalizability.
- Exact prompts for query expansion and re-ranking are unreported, introducing a potential source of variance in reproduction.
- The analysis treats "thinking" as a binary mode; finer-grained control over reasoning steps was not explored.
- The role of BM25 hyperparameters (k1, b) and the size of the full candidate pool (before top-*k* selection) are unspecified and may affect results.

## Confidence
- **Re-ranking gains from model strength & depth:** High (strong tables/figures, convergent corpus support).
- **Query expansion saturation:** High (clear Table 1 trends, convergent corpus support).
- **Inference-time thinking minimal benefit:** Medium (paper-specific evidence, may be model/task-bound).
- **Compute concentration on re-ranking:** High (direct experimental conclusion and cost-benefit analysis).
