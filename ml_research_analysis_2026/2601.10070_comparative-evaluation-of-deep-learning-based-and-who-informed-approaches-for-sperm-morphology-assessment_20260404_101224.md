# Comparative Evaluation of Deep Learning-Based and WHO-Informed Approaches for Sperm Morphology Assessment

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.10070
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.10070
- **Reference count:** 26
- **Primary result:** HuSHeM CNN achieved ROC-AUC 0.975 (95% CI 0.914–1.000) vs WHO+SIRI 0.721 (0.631–0.804), PR-AUC 0.993 vs 0.097, with superior calibration and net clinical benefit.

## Executive Summary
Sperm morphology assessment remains subjective and variable, limiting its reliability in fertility diagnostics. This study compared a deep learning image-based model (HuSHeM CNN) against a clinical baseline that combines WHO strict morphology criteria with the systemic inflammation response index (SIRI). The CNN was trained on a separate dataset of high-resolution sperm head images and both models were evaluated on an independent clinical cohort of 719 images (4% normal). The CNN dramatically outperformed the baseline in discrimination, calibration, and decision curve analysis. At an optimal threshold (0.4–0.5), it achieved 96% sensitivity and 87.5% specificity. These findings suggest that deep learning can provide robust, automated decision support for sperm morphology assessment, though prospective validation across diverse settings is still needed.

## Method Summary
The authors trained a custom shallow CNN on ~800 sperm head images from the HuSHeM benchmark and lab-acquired sources. Data augmentation (random rotations, flips, intensity jitter) and class-weighting addressed the severe imbalance (4% normals). The baseline model used logistic regression on two clinical features: manual percentage of normal sperm (WHO 5th ed.) and SIRI (neutrophils × monocytes / lymphocytes). Both models were fitted on appropriate training subsets of a clinical cohort. Evaluation occurred on an independent hold-out test set of 719 labeled images. Performance metrics included ROC-AUC, PR-AUC, reliability curves, decision curve analysis, and threshold-dependent sensitivity/specificity/F1-score.

## Key Results
- **Discrimination:** HuSHeM CNN achieved ROC-AUC 0.975 (95% CI 0.914–1.000) vs WHO+SIRI 0.721 (0.631–0.804); PR-AUC 0.993 vs 0.097, reflecting excellent identification of the rare normal class.
- **Calibration & clinical utility:** Reliability analysis showed the CNN’s predicted probabilities closely matched observed frequencies, while WHO+SIRI was poorly calibrated. Decision curve analysis indicated higher net benefit for the CNN across a wide threshold range.
- **Threshold performance:** At a threshold of 0.4–0.5 (maximizing F1), the CNN reached 96% sensitivity and 87.5% specificity (F1=0.96). The baseline could not simultaneously achieve high sensitivity and specificity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The HuSHeM CNN achieves superior discriminative performance by learning fine-grained morphological features directly from image pixels, patterns not explicitly encoded in WHO criteria or captured by SIRI.
- **Mechanism:** Convolutional neural networks hierarchically extract spatially local features (edges, textures, shapes) and compose them into complex, discriminative representations of sperm head morphology. This allows the model to detect subtle aberrations (e.g., slight head tapering, irregular acrosome) that define normality/abnormality, whereas WHO+SIRI relies on a single coarse percentage and a systemic blood index.
- **Core assumption:** Sperm head images contain a rich set of visual cues beyond the binary WHO classification that are consistently predictive of morphological normality when learned by a sufficiently flexible model.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract]: "Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) can automatically learn discriminative features from sperm images, potentially identifying subtle morphological and texture patterns beyond human perception." [section 2.5]: Describes the CNN architecture (convolutions, pooling, ReLU) designed to learn from raw pixels. [corpus]: Weak signal; neighboring papers focus on other medical imaging tasks (cardiac MRI, skin sweat glands) but broadly support that deep learning captures nuanced morphological features.
- **Break condition:** If the training image set lacks sufficient variability in abnormal morphologies or if image acquisition protocols (staining, magnification) shift significantly, the learned features may not generalize.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The model’s robustness under severe class imbalance (4% normals) stems from its training protocol, which combines data augmentation and loss weighting to prevent the network from defaulting to the majority class.
- **Mechanism:** During training, random rotations, flips, and intensity variations artificially expand the minority (normal) class dataset. Simultaneously, the binary cross-entropy loss is weighted to penalize errors on the minority class more heavily. This forces the CNN to learn features specific to normal morphology rather than ignoring it.
- **Core assumption:** The underlying data augmentation transformations (rotations, flips) are valid for sperm head morphology (i.e., a rotated normal sperm remains normal) and thus teach the model invariant features.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 2.5]: "Data augmentation (random rotations, flips, and intensity variations) was applied during training... Class imbalance... was addressed by applying a higher loss weight to the minority class." [section 3.1]: The CNN’s PR-AUC of 0.993 indicates excellent performance on the minority (normal) class under imbalance, where the baseline PR-AUC of 0.097 is near prevalence (4%).
- **Break condition:** If the augmentation policy introduces unrealistic sperm appearances (e.g., non-biological rotations if asymmetry is critical) or if the minority class is too sparse (<1%), learning may remain inadequate.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The CNN’s superior calibration (predicted probabilities matching observed frequencies) arises because its continuous output is optimized directly against the binary ground truth via cross-entropy loss, producing well-scaled confidence estimates.
- **Mechanism:** Softmax final layer coupled with cross-entropy loss encourages the network to output probabilities that reflect true likelihoods. The calibration curve near the diagonal (Fig 3) indicates that for a bin of predictions with, say, 80% probability, ~80% are actually normal. This contrasts with WHO+SIRI, whose logistic regression on two coarse features yields poorly separated, miscalibrated scores.
- **Core assumption:** The training set provides a representative distribution of morphological difficulty; if the test set contains systematically harder/easier cases, calibration may degrade.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 3.2]: "Calibration analysis indicated closer agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes for HuSHeM." [section 3.2] & Fig 3: Reliability curve comparison showing HuSHeM’s proximity to the ideal diagonal.
- **Break condition:** If the deployment population has a different prevalence or feature distribution than the training/test set (e.g., a fertility clinic with a higher rate of normals), the calibrated probabilities may become misaligned without post-hoc recalibration.

### Class imbalance and evaluation metrics (ROC-AUC vs. PR-AUC)
**Why needed here:** Only 4% of images are normal; accuracy is misleading. PR-AUC is more informative for the rare positive class, explaining why CNN’s PR-AUC (0.993) is vastly better than baseline (0.097) despite both having high accuracy.  
**Quick check question:** In a highly imbalanced dataset, which curve (ROC or PR) better reflects performance on the minority class, and why?

### Model calibration and decision curve analysis (DCA)
**Why needed here:** Discrimination (AUC) alone doesn’t guarantee reliable probability estimates. Calibration (Fig 3) and DCA (Fig 4) assess whether predicted probabilities are trustworthy and clinically useful across thresholds.  
**Quick check question:** What does a model with excellent discrimination (AUC=0.95) but poor calibration look like on a reliability curve, and why is this problematic for clinical decision support?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input (high-resolution sperm head image) -> Preprocessing/resizing -> Data augmentation (random rotations, flips, intensity jitter, training only) -> CNN backbone (5 convolutional layers with 3x3/5x5 kernels, ReLU, BatchNorm, MaxPooling) -> Dropout (rate=0.5) -> Fully-connected layers (128 units -> 2 units) -> Softmax -> Output probability of normal.

**Critical path:**  
Image ingestion -> augmentation (if training) -> convolutional feature extraction -> flatten -> fully-connected layers -> softmax -> probability. Ensure no color-space mismatches (staining consistency) and that image dimensions are fixed (e.g., 128×128 pixels after resizing).

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Shallow vs deep network:** Paper uses a relatively shallow CNN to mitigate overfitting on ~800 training images. A deeper ResNet might learn richer features but risks overfitting without more data.
- **Dropout rate:** 0.5 is high; reduces capacity but improves generalization. Lower rate may overfit; higher may underfit.
- **Threshold selection:** Operational threshold (0.4–0.5 for max F1) trades sensitivity/specificity. A screening tool might favor sensitivity (lower threshold), while a confirmatory tool favors specificity (higher threshold).

**Failure signatures:**
- **Overfitting:** Training loss continues to decrease while validation loss plateaus/increases (see Fig 2 for desired convergence).
- **Poor calibration:** Reliability curve bows below diagonal at high probabilities (overconfident normals) or above (underconfident).
- **Mode collapse:** CNN outputs cluster near 0 or 1 for almost all images, indicating failure to learn discriminative features (check activation histograms).
- **Dataset shift:** Performance collapses on new lab’s imaging protocol (different stain, microscope). Verify with external validation.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Hold-out validation:** Train on 80% of HuSHeM+lab images, validate on 20%. Confirm ROC-AUC >0.95 and PR-AUC >0.98 before proceeding. If not, increase augmentation or reduce model capacity.
2. **Grad-CAM sanity check:** Run Grad-CAM on a sample of correctly and incorrectly classified images. Confirm heatmaps focus on head morphology, not background or tail. If attention is scattered, the model may be learning spurious correlations.
3. **Threshold sweep and F1 optimization:** On validation set, compute F1-score across thresholds 0.1–0.5. Identify optimal threshold (paper finds ~0.4–0.5). Also note the threshold where specificity >0.95 while sensitivity remains >0.90—this defines a potentially clinically useful operating point.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1: Prospective validation for fertility outcomes?
Does prospective validation of the HuSHeM CNN in a real-world clinical setting demonstrate improved prediction of fertility outcomes (e.g., natural pregnancy, IVF success) compared to WHO+SIRI–based assessment?  
*Basis:* The authors state that future work should prioritize prospective validation to assess generalizability.  
*Why unresolved:* The study evaluates the model’s ability to classify sperm morphology against embryologist labels but does not link predictions to actual fertility treatment results.  
*Evidence needed:* A prospective, multi-center trial where the CNN’s sperm morphology scores guide clinical decisions and are compared against standard WHO-based assessment in terms of time-to-pregnancy, IVF fertilization rates, or cumulative live-birth rates.

### Open Question 2: Generalization across diverse populations and imaging protocols?
To what extent does the HuSHeM CNN generalize across diverse populations, imaging protocols, and staining methods when evaluated on external, multi-center datasets?  
*Basis:* Authors acknowledge a key limitation: the image dataset is modest and derived from a single source, and they explicitly call for "larger, multi-center image cohorts" to assess generalizability.  
*Why unresolved:* Model performance was evaluated on a single independent cohort (719 images) from one institution. Variation in microscope quality, staining techniques (Papanicolaou vs. Giemsa), and patient demographics across fertility centers could affect image characteristics and model accuracy.  
*Evidence needed:* External validation studies using sperm images from multiple international andrology laboratories with standardized and non-standardized acquisition protocols, reporting stratified performance metrics (AUC, calibration) and analyzing failure cases to identify dataset shift sources.

### Open Question 3: Multimodal integration of image and clinical features?
Can a multimodal model integrating CNN-derived image features with clinical variables (e.g., SIRI, other blood markers) on a patient-level basis outperform either unimodal approach for predicting fertility potential?  
*Basis:* The paper compares two models trained on completely independent cohorts (image-based CNN vs. clinical WHO+SIRI), preventing direct assessment of feature complementarity. The authors note that "paired multimodal datasets may further clarify the complementary value."  
*Why unresolved:* It is unknown whether image features and systemic inflammation markers provide synergistic information when available for the same patient. The current design cannot test if adding SIRI to the CNN—or CNN predictions to a clinical model—improves discrimination or calibration.  
*Evidence needed:* A study collecting both high-resolution sperm images and blood-based inflammatory indices from the same cohort, then developing and comparing a multimodal logistic regression or deep learning model (combining CNN embeddings with clinical features) against unimodal baselines using cross-validation and net benefit analysis.

### Open Question 4: Aggregation of per-sperm probabilities to sample-level scores?
How should per-sperm CNN probability outputs be aggregated to generate a clinically meaningful sample-level morphology score that aligns with or improves upon WHO’s strict criteria (e.g., ≥4% normal forms)?  
*Basis:* The study evaluates at the single-sperm level but acknowledges that WHO criteria are sample-level percentages. They suggest aggregation is the "natural next step," but the optimal method (e.g., mean probability, threshold-based proportion) and its relationship to WHO thresholds are unexplored.  
*Why unresolved:* The study does not report how CNN predictions across multiple sperm from the same sample correlate with manual morphology percentages or fertility outcomes. The choice of aggregation method could significantly impact sensitivity/specificity at the sample level.  
*Evidence needed:* An analysis where multiple sperm images from the same semen sample are processed by the CNN, various aggregation strategies are tested, and the resulting sample-level scores are compared to (a) embryologist-reported morphology percentages and (b) clinical pregnancy outcomes in a validation cohort. Receiver operating characteristic curves could identify optimal cutoff values that might differ from the WHO 4% threshold.

## Limitations
- Incomplete reporting of CNN architecture and training hyperparameters prevents exact replication.
- Unclear dataset splits increase risk of data leakage between training and evaluation sets.
- Evaluation limited to a single, highly imbalanced cohort without external validation, raising concerns about overfitting and generalizability.
