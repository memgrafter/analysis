# Entropy-Based Data Selection for Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.17465
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17465
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: EUDS reduces training data volume by up to 84% while improving accuracy on text classification tasks.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces EUDS, an unsupervised framework for fine-tuning language models more efficiently by selecting high-value training samples. EUDS leverages three entropy measures—Information Entropy (lexical), Generative Entropy (perplexity), and Semantic Entropy (semantic equivalence)—to score both original and synthetic data. It determines optimal entropy intervals empirically on a representative subset and applies them globally to filter data. Evaluated on 11 text classification datasets with BERT fine-tuning, EUDS achieves substantial data reduction (often over 70%) while maintaining or even improving accuracy, outperforming existing selection baselines. The approach offers a practical path to resource-efficient model training without requiring labeled data or task-specific supervision.

## Method Summary
EUDS operates in four stages: first, it computes three entropy scores for every candidate sample (original and LLM-generated synthetic). Second, on a small random subset, it bins samples by entropy intervals, trains a quick validation model for each interval, and selects the interval(s) that best balance data reduction and validation performance. Third, it applies this discovered interval to the full dataset to filter samples. Finally, it fine-tunes the target language model (e.g., BERT) on the filtered data. The framework is model-agnostic and handles synthetic data by applying the same entropy-based curation, ensuring that only informative, diverse samples are retained.

## Key Results
- On MMLU-Pro, Information Entropy selection reduced data by 84.05% and improved accuracy by 4.00%.
- On RaceQA, Semantic Entropy achieved 71.25% data reduction with a 0.40% accuracy gain.
- Combined entropy strategies further optimized performance, and EUDS consistently surpassed baselines like SELECT and FDKT across all 11 evaluated datasets.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Entropy-Interval Filtering Identifies High-Information Samples
The framework does not assume low or high entropy is universally beneficial. Instead, it empirically discovers an optimal entropy range on a subset by evaluating downstream performance per interval. This data-driven approach captures the "sweet spot" where samples are neither trivially easy nor hopelessly ambiguous, but rather informative and learnable for the specific model and task. The core assumption is that the entropy-performance relationship generalizes from the subset to the full dataset. Evidence shows that optimal intervals often align between subset and full sets (Section IV.F), enabling scalable filtering. However, this break if the full data’s entropy distribution diverges from the subset (e.g., due to non-i.i.d. shifts).

### Mechanism 2: Synergistic Combination of Original and Selected Synthetic Data
Synthetic data generated via LLM prompts augments scarce original data, but uncurated augmentation introduces noise. EUDS filters both pools using the same entropy criteria, ensuring synthetic samples are high-quality and complementary. Experiments (Section IV.D.2) show that merging a small set of original data with entropy-selected synthetic data can match or exceed performance using the full original dataset, drastically cutting total data needs. The assumption is that filtered synthetic data provides diverse, non-redundant patterns. A break condition arises if synthetic data distribution is misaligned with true data, even after filtering, potentially introducing harmful shifts.

### Mechanism 3: Subset Generalization via Representative Sampling
Exhaustively evaluating all entropy intervals on a large dataset is computationally prohibitive. EUDS sidesteps this by determining the optimal interval on a small, representative subset and applying it globally. The paper demonstrates that the optimal interval for a subset often matches that of the full dataset (Section III.C.b, Table 10), making the two-phase process efficient and effective. This relies on the stability of the entropy-performance curve across scales. If the subset fails to capture rare modes present in the full data, the generalized interval may exclude critical samples, harming performance.

## Foundational Learning
- **Entropy as a Proxy for Sample Utility**  
  *Why needed*: EUDS repurposes entropy (uncertainty measures) as signals for data selection. Understanding what each entropy captures—lexical diversity (IE), predictive difficulty (GE), semantic ambiguity (SE)—is key to interpreting sample selection.  
  *Quick check*: For a clear, common text classification example vs. a nuanced, rare one, how would IE and SE intuitively differ? Which would EUDS likely select?
- **Data-Driven Thresholding vs. Static Thresholding**  
  *Why needed*: The method avoids fixed entropy cutoffs, instead empirically finding optimal ranges per task. This shifts from heuristic to evidence-based selection.  
  *Quick check*: What risks exist if one applies a static threshold (e.g., IE < 5) across all datasets without subset validation?
- **Synthetic Data Quality and Filtering**  
  *Why needed*: Synthetic data from LLMs is useful but noisy; entropy-based curation aims to retain only high-value samples.  
  *Quick check*: Based on cited literature, what factor most affects synthetic data utility for classification, and how might entropy filtering mitigate it?

## Architecture Onboarding
Data Source Layer (OriData, SynData) -> Entropy Computation Module (IE, GE, SE) -> Interval Discovery Module (subset binning, validation training, interval selection) -> Global Filtering Module (apply interval to full data) -> Fine-tuning Layer (BERT training)

**Critical path**: Data Generation -> Entropy Scoring (on subset) -> Interval Discovery -> Interval Application -> Fine-tune & Evaluate. The interval discovery step is the pivotal decision point preceding global filtering.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- **Computation vs. Selection Fidelity**: GE and SE require expensive LM inference/generation; cheaper IE may suffice for some tasks.  
- **Subset Size vs. Discovery Cost**: Larger subsets improve interval reliability but increase discovery overhead.  
- **Data Volume vs. Performance Stability**: Aggressive filtering maximizes reduction but risks excluding critical samples; interval discovery seeks a stable middle ground.

**Failure signatures**:  
- **Interval collapse**: Optimal interval is extremely narrow (<5% of data), indicating non-discriminative entropy or unrepresentative subset.  
- **Performance degradation**: Model fine-tuned on globally filtered data underperforms the subset-discovery model, signaling poor interval generalization.  
- **Synthetic data concentration**: Selected synthetic samples overwhelmingly cluster in one narrow entropy band, risking diversity loss.

**First 3 experiments**:  
1. Reproduce single-entropy (IE) selection on SST-2: discover optimal interval on a 2000-sample subset, apply globally, compare accuracy and reduction against random 20% baseline.  
2. Test synthetic data utility: for SST-5, compare (a) full original, (b) original+full synthetic, (c) original+EUDS-selected synthetic. Improve in (c) over (b) confirms filtering value.  
3. Validate subset-to-full generalization: on MMLU, discover IE interval on a 2000-sample subset, apply to remaining full data and entire full set; similar performance confirms robustness.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **What theoretical principles can explain the task-specific optimal entropy intervals in EUDS and enable their prediction without empirical subset evaluation?**  
   *Basis*: The discussion states that "a unified optimal entropy range suitable for all tasks cannot be found" and that "further theoretical exploration and explanation are required."  
   *Why unresolved*: The framework relies on data-driven interval determination via representative subsets, which is computationally expensive and lacks generalization; no theoretical model exists to predict intervals a priori.  
   *What evidence would resolve it*: A formal theory correlating task characteristics (e.g., domain, data size) with entropy-performance curves, empirically validated to predict optimal intervals for new tasks.

2. **How does the concentration of selected samples in low or high entropy ranges impact model robustness to distribution shifts and out-of-distribution data?**  
   *Basis*: The authors note that entropy-based selection can lead to "concentration in the data distribution," which "may limit the effectiveness," but robustness is not evaluated.  
   *Why unresolved*: Experiments only measure in-distribution accuracy; concentration might reduce data diversity, harming generalization to unseen distributions.  
   *What evidence would resolve it*: Evaluate models on OOD benchmarks after EUDS selection; analyze correlation between entropy concentration metrics and performance drop under distribution shift.

3. **What is the quantitative trade-off between data reduction percentage and training convergence stability in EUDS, and how can it be automatically optimized for given computational constraints?**  
   *Basis*: The paper warns that "if excessive emphasis is placed on significantly reducing the data volume, the model may fail to converge," but no quantitative analysis is provided.  
   *Why unresolved*: Interval selection balances reduction and accuracy without explicit convergence modeling; safe reduction thresholds for different tasks are unknown.  
   *What evidence would resolve it*: Systematic study of convergence rates versus reduction levels across tasks; develop a convergence risk estimator based on entropy statistics and data size.

4. **Does the EUDS framework generalize to decoder-based language models for generative tasks, and do the entropy measures require adaptation for autoregressive settings?**  
   *Basis*: EUDS is evaluated only on BERT for classification; it is claimed model-agnostic, but generative tasks involve decoding where entropy definitions (e.g., GE) may differ.  
   *Why unresolved*: No experiments on GPT-style models; semantic entropy for generation might not directly inform data selection for training generative models.  
   *What evidence would resolve it*: Replicate experiments with GPT models on text generation tasks; adapt entropy measures if needed and compare selection effectiveness.

## Limitations
- Prompt templates and hyperparameters for synthetic data generation and Semantic Entropy computation are undisclosed, hindering exact replication.
- The subset-to-full interval generalization assumption may fail on non-i.i.d. data or datasets with rare subgroups not captured in the subset.
- The computational cost of Generative and Semantic Entropy is not weighed against their marginal gains; no cost-benefit analysis is provided.
- Synthetic data often concentrates in narrow entropy bands after selection (e.g., AGNews SynData), potentially reducing diversity and limiting effectiveness.

## Confidence
- **Overall efficacy** (data reduction with accuracy maintenance/improvement): High  
- **Entropy-interval selection** (including subset discovery): Medium  
- **Synthetic data synergy** (original + selected synthetic): Medium
