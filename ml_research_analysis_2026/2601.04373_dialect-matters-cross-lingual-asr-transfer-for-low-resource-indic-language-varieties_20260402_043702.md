# Dialect Matters: Cross-Lingual ASR Transfer for Low-Resource Indic Language Varieties

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.04373  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04373  
- **Reference count:** 11  
- **Primary result:** Fine‑tuning on small amounts of dialectal speech often yields comparable or better WER than fine‑tuning on larger amounts of phylogenetically closer standardized languages; orthographic variability (hapax legomena %) strongly predicts WER (ρ=0.705); Garhwali w2vBERT model achieved 49.3% WER but normalized 22.9% of non‑Hindi words to Hindi forms.

## Executive Summary
This study tackles the challenge of automatic speech recognition (ASR) for low‑resource Indic dialects, which are characterized by spontaneous speech, noise, and code‑mixing. The authors empirically compare cross‑lingual transfer via fine‑tuning on dialectal data versus standardized high‑resource languages. Key findings demonstrate that using smaller amounts of dialect‑specific speech can match or exceed the performance obtained by transferring from phylogenetically closer standardized languages, even when the latter are available in larger quantities. A strong positive correlation (Pearson ρ = 0.705) is identified between orthographic variability—measured by hapax legomena percentage—and word error rate. In a detailed Garhwali case study, the best model (w2vBERT fine‑tuned on Garhwali) reached 49.3% WER, but analysis revealed a systematic bias toward Hindi: nearly a quarter of non‑Hindi words were incorrectly normalized to valid Hindi forms, highlighting the lingering influence of pre‑training on standardized languages.

## Method Summary
The research uses the VAANI dataset, which contains spontaneous, noisy, code‑mixed speech in Devanagari‑script Indic varieties. Multiple self‑supervised pre‑trained speech models (e.g., IndicWav2Vec‑Hindi, w2vBERT, XLS‑R, HuBERT, Whisper) are fine‑tuned on either dialectal or standardized language data, with training sets ranging from 1 to 7 hours. Zero‑shot evaluation is performed across 30 languages/dialects. Primary metrics are word error rate (WER) and character error rate (CER). Orthographic variability is quantified via hapax legomena percentage and type‑token ratio on transcripts. To probe Hindi bias, a Hindi spell‑checker tags non‑Hindi words in ground truth and predictions, computing conversion rates. Controlled experiments isolate in‑group dialect transfer (e.g., Bundeli+Khariboli vs. Hindi) and analyze error patterns at the character level.

## Key Results
- Dialect‑only fine‑tuning often matches or outperforms models fine‑tuned on larger amounts of phylogenetically close standardized languages (e.g., Bundeli+Khariboli fine‑tuning beats Hindi fine‑tuning on Haryanvi).
- Orthographic variability correlates strongly with WER across languages (Pearson ρ = 0.705, p = 4e‑4).
- In Garhwali, w2vBERT fine‑tuned on the dialect achieves the best WER (49.3%), yet 22.9% of non‑Hindi words are normalized to Hindi forms, indicating persistent pre‑training bias.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Dialectal data conveys transferable variation
**Claim:** Fine‑tuning on dialectal speech, even with less data, yields comparable or better zero‑shot ASR performance on other dialects than fine‑tuning on larger amounts of phylogenetically‑related, high‑resource standardized languages.  
**Mechanism:** Dialectal speech captures shared acoustic‑phonetic and lexical reduction patterns absent in standardized language data. The model learns to map acoustic variability to a more generalizable set of dialectal transcriptions, avoiding overfitting to standardized orthographic and lexical conventions that do not transfer well to spontaneous, non‑standard speech.  
**Core assumption:** Dialects constitute distinct acoustic‑linguistic entities with shared variation patterns not fully predicted by phylogenetic proximity to a standardized language.  
**Evidence:** Abstract statement; Figure 3 heatmap shows Marwari (dialect) → Kumaoni transfer outperforms Hindi (standardized, closer) → Kumaoni; Tables 1‑3 consistently show dialect‑only fine‑tuning matches/surpasses models including mainstream languages (e.g., Bundeli+Khariboli > Hindi+Bundeli+Khariboli on Haryanvi).

### Mechanism 2: Orthographic variability increases WER
**Claim:** Higher orthographic variability (measured by hapax legomena percentage and type‑token ratio) correlates with increased word error rate in Indic dialect ASR.  
**Mechanism:** Orthographic inconsistency inflates vocabulary sparsity and ambiguity. The model encounters many low‑frequency or unique word forms, hindering robust acoustic‑to‑grapheme mapping and enlarging the decoding search space, leading to more substitutions and deletions.  
**Core assumption:** Orthographic variability in transcripts proxies underlying lexical and phonological non‑standardization that complicates sequence modeling.  
**Evidence:** Abstract reports ρ = 0.705; Figure 2 plots hapax legomena % vs. WER showing a clear positive trend; Table 9 lists high TTR and hapax % for languages like Gondi (74.78% hapax) and Thethi (71.71%), which also have high baseline WER.

### Mechanism 3: Pre‑training induces Hindi normalization bias
**Claim:** Pre‑training on standardized mainstream Indic languages (e.g., Hindi) induces a systemic bias that normalizes dialect‑specific and code‑mixed non‑Hindi words toward valid Hindi forms during decoding.  
**Mechanism:** The decoder (or language model component), trained on vast standardized Hindi text, develops a strong prior over valid Hindi word sequences. When fine‑tuned on a dialect, the acoustic encoder may correctly recognize non‑Hindi phonemes, but the language model over‑generates Hindi vocabulary, overriding dialect‑specific tokens.  
**Core assumption:** The modular architecture allows pre‑trained language model biases to persist and dominate during fine‑tuning on small dialectal datasets, especially for out‑of‑vocabulary or non‑Hindi tokens.  
**Evidence:** Abstract notes 22.9% of non‑Hindi words incorrectly normalized to Hindi; Table 5 shows Garhwali‑fine‑tuned w2vBERT preserves 34.8% of non‑Hindi words but still converts 23.3% to Hindi, while Hindi‑fine‑tuned preserves only 4.2%; Table 6 character errors reveal halant and vowel length confusions consistent with Hindi orthographic regularization.

## Foundational Learning
- **Phonological and Orthographic Variation in Indic Dialects**  
  *Why needed:* The paper’s conclusions hinge on differences between standardized languages (e.g., Hindi) and dialects (e.g., Garhwali). Understanding features like consonant clipping (halant usage), vowel length distinctions, and inconsistent transliteration of English is essential to interpret error patterns.  
  *Quick check:* Why might the halant (◌्) be a frequent source of deletion/insertion errors for a Garhwali‑fine‑tuned model, even after seeing Garhwali data?

- **Cross‑Lingual Transfer vs. In‑Group Dialect Transfer**  
  *Why needed:* The paper challenges the default assumption that phylogenetic similarity is the primary predictor of transfer success. Engineers must grasp why transfer within a dialect continuum (e.g., Marwari→Jaipuri) can be more effective than transfer across subgroups (e.g., Hindi→Kumaoni), even if the latter is phylogenetically closer.  
  *Quick check:* According to the results, what is a more reliable predictor of zero‑shot performance on a dialect than the phylogenetic distance of the fine‑tuning language?

- **Self‑Supervised Speech Model Architectures (w2vBERT, XLS‑R, etc.)**  
  *Why needed:* The Garhwali case study compares different pre‑trained architectures (w2vBERT, XLS‑R, HuBERT, Whisper). Understanding their differences (e.g., w2vBERT’s combined contrastive and masked prediction, Whisper’s encoder‑decoder with multilingual supervision) explains why w2vBERT performed best and informs model selection for new dialect projects.  
  *Quick check:* In the Garhwali experiments, why might a model pre‑trained with a multilingual supervised objective (Whisper) underperform compared to a self‑supervised model (w2vBERT) when fine‑tuned on very limited dialectal data?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Raw waveform -> Self‑supervised encoder (e.g., w2vBERT, XLS‑R) -> Fine‑tuning head (CTC or seq2seq decoder) -> Output tokens (character/word, may include code‑mixed English in Devanagari)

### Critical path
1. **Data audit:** Inspect VAANI dialect splits for orthographic consistency (hapax legomena, TTR), code‑mixing rate, and acoustic noise profiles.  
2. **Baseline selection:** Start with a model pre‑trained on the closest standardized language (e.g., IndicWav2Vec‑Hindi for Devanagari dialects) to establish the phylogenetic transfer baseline.  
3. **Dialectal fine‑tuning:** Fine‑tune the selected model on the target dialect’s data (e.g., Garhwali). Monitor WER/CER on a held‑out dialectal test set; if data is very small (<5 h), consider noise/speed augmentation.  
4. **Bias & error diagnosis:** Post‑evaluation, analyze non‑Hindi word preservation (using a Hindi spell‑checker) and character‑level error tables to quantify Hindi normalization bias and orthographic mismatch patterns.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Data volume vs. dialect specificity:** Using 7 h of a standardized language (e.g., Hindi) vs. 2 h of the target dialect; evidence suggests the latter is often better for dialectal test sets.  
- **Vocabulary scope:** Including all transcript tokens (high OOV, high hapax) vs. normalizing spellings. Normalization may reduce hapax% and improve WER but could discard linguistically valid variation.  
- **Architecture choice:** Encoder‑only (HuBERT) + CTC vs. encoder‑decoder (Whisper). The former may be more flexible for low‑resource fine‑tuning; the latter has a stronger built‑in language model but may reinforce pre‑training biases.

### Failure signatures
- **High WER but moderate CER; frequent space insertion/deletion and vowel/consonant length confusions.** Likely cause: word‑boundary and orthographic regularization issues due to mismatched script conventions.  
- **Non‑Hindi/Garhwali lexical items consistently replaced by valid Hindi words (e.g., “गार्हवाली” → “हिंदी”).** Likely cause: pre‑training language model bias overpowering acoustic evidence for dialect‑specific tokens.  
- **Good performance on standardized language test sets but poor results on spontaneous dialectal speech from the same region.** Likely cause: domain mismatch (clean read vs. noisy, code‑mixed spontaneous speech) and unmodeled orthographic variability.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Reproduce RQ1 baseline:** Run IndicWav2Vec‑Hindi on all 30 Devanagari VAANI test sets. Verify high WERs (e.g., ~50% on Hindi itself) and variation across dialects, confirming poor generalization to spontaneous speech.  
2. **Correlate orthography & WER:** Compute hapax legomena percentage and type‑token ratio for each language’s VAANI test transcripts. Replicate Pearson correlation (ρ ≈ 0.7) between hapax% and WER from Figure 2.  
3. **Controlled in‑group transfer:** For the Western Hindi subgroup (Hindi, Haryanvi, Bundeli, Khariboli), fine‑tune separate models on (a) 7 h of Hindi and (b) 1.7 h of Bundeli+Khariboli (dialects only). Evaluate both on the Haryanvi test set; expect (b) to match or beat (a) despite less data, replicating Table 3.

## Limitations
- Exact train‑test splits and full fine‑tuning hyperparameters (beyond steps) are not provided, hindering faithful reproduction.  
- Hapax legomena measurement depends on transcript normalization; the paper lacks details on transcription consistency protocols and handling of code‑mixed English.  
- The classification of languages as “dialect” vs. “standard” appears curated without transparent linguistic criteria.  
- Bias quantification relies on a single Hindi spell‑checker (version unspecified), which may affect the reported Hindi normalization rates.

## Confidence
- **Dialectal transfer advantage:** Medium – strong within‑group evidence but limited to Devanagari VAANI; generalizability uncertain.  
- **Orthographic variability correlation:** High – robust Pearson ρ = 0.705 (p = 4e‑4) across 30 languages, though causality not proven.  
- **Hindi normalization bias:** Medium – Garhwali case study clearly demonstrates bias; magnitude across other dialects needs further validation.

## Next Checks
1. **Replicate in‑group transfer:** Using identical VAANI splits and hyperparameters for the Western Hindi subgroup, confirm that fine‑tuning on Bundeli+Khariboli (1.7 h) matches/exceeds fine‑tuning on Hindi (7 h) on the Haryanvi test set.  
2. **Orthographic causality test:** Apply forced spelling normalization to a high‑hapax dialect’s (e.g., Gondi) transcripts; if WER drops and the hapax‑WER correlation weakens, orthographic variability is a key driver.  
3. **Bias across dialects:** For at least three additional non‑Hindi dialects, use a language identifier to classify non‑Hindi words in predictions and compute Hindi conversion rates; compare to Garhwali’s 22.9% to assess generalizability.
