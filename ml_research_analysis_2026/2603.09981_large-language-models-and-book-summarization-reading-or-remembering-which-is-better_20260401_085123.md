# Large Language Models and Book Summarization: Reading or Remembering, Which Is Better?

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.09981
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09981
- Reference count: 27
- Primary result: Providing the full text generally yields more complete summaries than relying on internal knowledge, but for certain complex books internal knowledge can perform comparably.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates how large language models (LLMs) summarize books when given either their full text (external context) or only the title and author (relying on internal memory). Using two advanced models with large context windows (GPT-4.1 and Gemini-2.5-Flash) on a corpus of 25 classic books, the authors generate multiple summaries under each condition and evaluate them using an LLM-as-a-judge pairwise comparison protocol. The results show that external context typically produces more complete summaries. However, for a subset of structurally complex works—such as poetry, theater, and nonlinear narratives—summaries from internal knowledge are on par with or better than those from the full text. The findings highlight challenges in processing long, complex texts and suggest that internal knowledge may sometimes compensate for limitations in context utilization.

## Method Summary
The authors selected 25 public-domain classic books representing various genres. For each book, they generated 5 summaries using internal knowledge (prompt: "Summarize the book {title} by {author}") and 5 summaries using external context (prompt: full text appended, with instructions to avoid using internal knowledge). They employed two large-context models, GPT-4.1 and Gemini-2.5-Flash, at two temperature settings (0.4 and 0.9). All 25 internal-external pairs per book were evaluated by judge models (the same two models in cross configurations) using a pairwise completeness scoring rubric (-1 if external better, 0 tie, 1 if internal better). Scores for each book range from -25 (external wins all pairs) to 25 (internal wins all). They aggregated results, analyzed per-book patterns, and manually inspected outliers.

## Key Results
- Across the entire corpus, external summaries (with full text) are consistently rated as more complete, yielding negative aggregate scores.
- Four books—*The Divine Comedy*, *Hamlet*, *Ulysses*, and *The Count of Monte Cristo*—show neutral or positive scores, indicating that internal-knowledge summaries can match or exceed external ones, particularly for works with poetic, dramatic, or nonlinear structures.
- Performance varies with the choice of generator model, judge model, and temperature; for instance, Gemini-2.5-Flash exhibits less preference for external summaries regardless of judge, while GPT-4.1 judges tend to favor internal summaries more.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Providing the full text as external context generally yields more complete and detailed summaries compared to relying solely on the model’s internal knowledge.
- **Mechanism:** The external context gives the model direct, unambiguous access to all plot elements, character actions, and narrative details. This reduces reliance on potentially incomplete, biased, or conflated memories from training data and allows the model to extract information faithfully from the source.
- **Core assumption:** The model can effectively process the entire context window without significant degradation (e.g., “lost in the middle” effects) or fragmentation.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “having the full text provides more detailed summaries in general”
  - [section 4] Average scores across all books are negative, indicating external summaries are consistently preferred in pairwise comparisons.
  - [corpus] Neighbors like *FABLES* and *BooookScore* highlight challenges in evaluating faithfulness for long-text summarization, supporting the need for full-context grounding.
- **Break condition:** For texts with high structural complexity (e.g., poetry, non-linear narratives) or extreme length, the model’s ability to parse and integrate the full text may fail, causing internal knowledge to become competitive or superior.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** For certain books—particularly those with poetic form, theatrical structure, or non-linear narratives—summaries based on internal knowledge can match or exceed the quality of full-text summaries.
- **Mechanism:** The model’s internal knowledge encodes high-level narrative patterns, thematic summaries, and canonical interpretations learned during training. For complex works, these learned abstractions may be more coherent and complete than the model’s ability to reconstruct a linear plot directly from a dense, structurally challenging full text.
- **Core assumption:** The training data contained sufficient high-quality summaries, analyses, or reference material on these specific works, allowing the model to form robust internal representations.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “text complexity (poetry, theater, nonlinear narratives) correlating with stronger internal-knowledge performance”
  - [section 4] Outlier books (*The Divine Comedy*, *Hamlet*, *Ulysses*, *The Count of Monte Cristo*) show neutral or positive scores for internal summaries; manual analysis suggests external summaries struggle with chapter-by-chapter structure or non-linearity.
  - [section 3.2] Book selection includes varied genres and styles; complexity features are explicitly discussed.
- **Break condition:** For straightforward, linear prose narratives, internal knowledge is generally inferior, as the model can easily extract details from the full text without needing to rely on memory.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The relative performance of internal vs. external summarization is heavily influenced by model-specific capabilities and evaluation biases.
- **Mechanism:** Different LLMs have varying architectures and training that affect context utilization (e.g., susceptibility to “lost in the middle”) and propensity to rely on parametric memory. Additionally, the choice of judge model in LLM-as-a-judge evaluations introduces systematic biases (e.g., a model may favor its own stylistic outputs).
- **Core assumption:** Models differ in their ability to attend to long contexts and in their internal knowledge robustness, and these differences are not merely due to scale.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4] “Gemini-2.5-Flash receives less negative scores regardless of who performs the evaluation” suggesting it is less capable of leveraging full text or more consistent with internal knowledge; evaluator model choice changes score magnitudes (GPT-4.1 favors internal summaries more).
  - [section 3.4] Temperature variations modestly affect scores but preserve relative rankings.
  - [corpus] Neighbors like *Evaluating book summaries from internal knowledge* directly compare internal vs. external, confirming that model choice matters.
- **Break condition:** If models had uniform context processing and indistinguishable internal knowledge, the performance gap would disappear and evaluator biases would vanish.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Internal vs. External Memory in LLMs  
  - **Why needed here:** The core comparison hinges on whether the model uses its parametric memory (trained knowledge) or processes provided text. Understanding this dichotomy is essential to interpret results.  
  - **Quick check question:** If a model generates a summary without being given the text, which type of memory is it using?
- **Concept:** LLM-as-a-Judge Evaluation  
  - **Why needed here:** The study relies on automated pairwise comparisons by another LLM to assess summary completeness. This method introduces potential biases that must be considered when interpreting scores.  
  - **Quick check question:** What is a known risk of using a model to judge its own outputs?
- **Concept:** “Lost in the Middle” Phenomenon  
  - **Why needed here:** For long inputs, models often neglect middle content, explaining why full-text summarization may fail for lengthy or complex books. This underlies Mechanism 1’s break condition.  
  - **Quick check question:** How might a model’s attention patterns degrade as input length increases?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Book Corpus -> Summary Generator (internal/external prompt) -> Judge Model -> Aggregator

- Book corpus: 25 public-domain texts, preprocessed into plain text.
- Summary generator: Either GPT-4.1 or Gemini-2.5-Flash, prompted either with book title/author only (internal) or with full text appended (external).
- Judge model: Same two models used in cross configuration to evaluate pairwise summary completeness via -1/0/1 scoring.
- Aggregator: Sums 25 pairwise scores per book to produce final comparison metric (-25 to 25).

### Critical Path
1. For each book, generate 5 internal summaries (different seeds/temperature).
2. Generate 5 external summaries.
3. For every internal–external pair (25 pairs), run judge prompt with chosen judge model.
4. Aggregate scores and visualize per-book and per-model/evaluator matrix.

### Design Tradeoffs
- Temperature (0.4 vs 0.9): Lower increases fidelity to text but may be rigid; higher adds creativity but risks hallucination.
- Judge model selection: Cross-judging detects self-bias but doubles evaluation cost; using a single judge may be biased.
- Prompt specificity: Explicit instructions (“do NOT use internal knowledge”) are critical for external condition; wording affects compliance.

### Failure Signatures
- **External summary incomplete:** Model omits major plot points, especially from middle/late chapters → check for “lost in the middle”.
- **Internal summary adaptation bias:** Summary describes film/adaptation rather than original text → verify by checking for anachronistic elements.
- **Judge inconsistency:** Same summary pair yields different scores across seeds → indicates LLM-as-a-judge instability.
- **High score variance for a book:** Suggests the book’s complexity amplifies stochastic effects.

### First 3 Experiments
1. Baseline replication: Reproduce Figure 3 for all books at temperature 0.4 with both generators and judges; confirm aggregate external preference.
2. Complexity subgroup analysis: Isolate books by genre (poetry: *Divine Comedy*; theater: *Hamlet*; nonlinear: *Ulysses*; long plot-driven: *Count of Monte Cristo*) and compute average internal vs. external score differences; test if complexity correlates with smaller external advantage.
3. Context position probe: For *The Count of Monte Cristo*, extract external summaries and ask judge model to rate coverage of early vs. late events; quantify “lost in the middle” effect by comparing mention rates of key plot points from first, middle, and last thirds.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How do summaries generated from internal memory compare to those derived from the full text across different LLM architectures and literary genres?
- **Basis in paper:** The abstract and Section 4 directly compare internal vs. external summaries, finding external generally better but with exceptions for specific books (e.g., *The Divine Comedy*, *Ulysses*).
- **Why unresolved:** The study only tests two commercial models (GPT-4.1, Gemini-2.5) on a fixed set of 25 Western classics; it remains unclear whether observed patterns hold for open-weight models, non-Western literature, or other genres.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A larger-scale experiment with diverse models (open/closed) and a broader, culturally varied book corpus, measuring summary quality across multiple dimensions (completeness, faithfulness, coherence).

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Does prior knowledge from training inadvertently override or interfere with the provided full text during summarization, and how prevalent is this?
- **Basis in paper:** The introduction and abstract raise this question, noting that "prior knowledge can interfere with the book text provided as input" and that internal summaries sometimes outperform external ones.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not quantify the degree of interference or identify specific mechanisms (e.g., hallucination, omission) where internal knowledge conflicts with the source text.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Error analysis of external summaries to detect discrepancies from the source text that align with training-data artifacts, possibly using factual consistency metrics or targeted probes.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Why do structurally complex texts (e.g., poetry, nonlinear narratives, drama) tend to show relatively better performance for internal-knowledge summaries compared to straightforward prose?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 4's analysis of outliers (*The Divine Comedy*, *Hamlet*, *Ulysses*) suggests genre and narrative complexity correlate with stronger internal summary performance, possibly due to "lost-in-the-middle" issues in processing full text.
- **Why unresolved:** The hypothesis is based on observation without controlled testing; the precise role of structure (e.g., verse form, act/scene divisions, temporal jumps) versus length is not isolated.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Comparative experiments on abridged/modified versions of complex books (e.g., linearized *Ulysses*, prose translations of *Divine Comedy*) to measure how structural features affect the internal/external gap.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How reliable is the LLM-as-a-judge evaluation method for detecting summary quality differences in this task, and do biases (e.g., self-preference, leniency) skew the results?
- **Basis in paper:** The limitations section acknowledges LLM judge bias and inconsistency, and the study uses cross-judging but lacks human validation; scatter plots in Section 4 show evaluator disagreement, especially for certain books.
- **Why unresolved:** Without a robust human evaluation baseline, it is uncertain whether LLM scores truly reflect completeness or reflect judge-specific preferences.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Human expert evaluations on a stratified sample of summary pairs (especially outlier cases) to correlate with LLM judgments and calibrate the scoring methodology.

## Limitations
- Model version ambiguity: "GPT-4.1" and "Gemini-2.5-Flash" may be specific previews not publicly accessible, hindering faithful reproduction.
- Evaluation bias: LLM-as-a-judge introduces systematic, unquantified biases (e.g., self-preference, stylistic favor) that may skew results.
- Causality of complexity: The correlation between internal performance and textual complexity is suggestive but not causally established; it is unclear whether failures arise from context processing limits or other factors.

## Confidence
- **High confidence:** Providing the full text generally yields more complete summaries (aggregate negative scores).
- **Medium confidence:** For some complex books, internal-knowledge summaries can match or exceed external ones (observed outliers).
- **Low confidence:** Assertions about model-specific capabilities (e.g., Gemini-2.5-Flash's reduced reliance on external text) due to confounding judge biases and unmeasured architectural differences.
