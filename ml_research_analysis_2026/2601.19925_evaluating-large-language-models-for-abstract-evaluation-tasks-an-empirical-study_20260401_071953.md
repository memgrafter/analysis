# Evaluating Large Language Models for Abstract Evaluation Tasks: An Empirical Study

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.19925
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.19925
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: LLMs show moderate agreement with humans on objective criteria but fair agreement on subjective dimensions, enabling scalable and low-cost abstract evaluation as a complementary tool.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluated whether large language models (LLMs) can reliably assess academic abstracts compared to human reviewers. Three LLMs (ChatGPT-5, Gemini-3-Pro, Claude-Sonnet-4.5) and 14 human graders evaluated 160 abstracts using a standardized rubric. Inter-rater reliability was measured via intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) and Bland-Altman plots. LLMs demonstrated good-to-excellent agreement with each other (ICCs 0.59–0.87). Against human reviewers, ChatGPT and Claude achieved moderate reliability on overall quality and objective criteria (ICCs ~0.45–0.60) but only fair reliability on subjective dimensions (ICCs 0.23–0.38). Gemini performed poorly, showing no reliability on impact and applicability. LLMs processed all abstracts in under 10 minutes at a cost of less than $1. The findings suggest LLMs can consistently apply rubrics for objective criteria at scale but are limited for subjective judgments, supporting a complementary rather than replacement role for human experts.

## Method Summary
The study used 160 abstracts from the Abigail Wexner Research Institute Research Retreat 2025, structured with title, 50-word summary, and sections on importance, objective, methods, results, and conclusions. A 7-criteria rubric on a 5-point Likert scale assessed impression, clarity, objective, results, impact, engagement, and applicability, plus a composite mean score. Fourteen human reviewers each graded subsets, with each abstract rated by two reviewers independently. Three LLMs graded all abstracts via API calls, batching 10 abstracts per prompt with identical instructions. No model training was performed; the task was zero-shot inference. Reliability was quantified using ICCs: ICC(2,k) for LLM-LLM agreement (two-way random, absolute agreement, average rater) and ICC(1,k) for LLM-human agreement (one-way random). Bland-Altman plots assessed systematic bias relative to the mean human score per abstract. Cost and time were recorded for LLM processing.

## Key Results
- LLMs showed strong mutual consistency (ICCs 0.59–0.87).
- ChatGPT and Claude achieved moderate LLM-human agreement on overall quality and objective criteria (ICCs ~0.45–0.60) but fair agreement on subjective dimensions (ICCs: 0.23–0.38).
- Gemini had no reliability (ICC=0.00) on impact and applicability.
- Mean differences from human composite scores were small (ChatGPT: +0.24, Gemini: +0.42, Claude: –0.02).
- LLMs completed all 160 abstracts in under 10 minutes at a cost of less than $1.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Consistent Rubric Application
LLMs apply evaluation rubrics with consistent internal standards across all abstracts, reducing rater variability. Identical prompt engineering and deterministic processing ensure each LLM uses the same rule-based logic for every abstract, unlike human reviewers whose standards drift due to fatigue, expertise gaps, or personal biases. The core assumption is that LLM token prediction is stable given fixed prompts and temperature settings, creating reproducible scoring behavior. Evidence includes the paper's observation that LLMs maintained consistent scoring patterns with narrow interquartile ranges and few outliers, and neighboring studies (e.g., paper 2183 on OLIVER) show LLMs maintain calibration across large batches. A break condition occurs if rubric criteria are ambiguously worded or require implicit domain knowledge not in the training data, causing LLMs to infer different interpretations.

### Mechanism 2: Objective vs. Subjective Criteria Disparity
LLMs achieve moderate reliability with humans on objective, content-specific criteria but fair reliability on subjective dimensions. This occurs because LLMs excel at pattern-matching explicit textual elements (e.g., presence of results, clarity of structure) but lack real-world experiential context to assess significance, engagement, or applicability, which require synthesis beyond the text. The core assumption is that training data contains statistical regularities for objective writing quality but sparse signals for subjective impact or audience engagement. Evidence: the paper reports ChatGPT/Claude ICCs for objective criteria ~0.45–0.60 versus impact ~0.23–0.31, and similar patterns are found in paper 267681 (STROBE checklists). A break condition: if subjective criteria are redefined with explicit, text-anchored descriptors, LLM reliability may improve but likely still trails human consensus.

### Mechanism 3: Scalable Economic Processing
LLMs enable economically scalable evaluation by processing large volumes rapidly at low marginal cost. API-based batch processing allows parallel scoring of multiple abstracts per request, minimizing per-abstract compute and eliminating human time costs. The core assumption is that LLM inference costs scale sublinearly with batch size due to hardware batching efficiencies and fixed context window utilization. Evidence: the paper states LLMs completed all 160 abstracts in under 10 minutes at a cost of less than $1, while human reviewers took over two weeks. Paper 271922 (fine-tuning for systematic reviews) confirms cost reduction for high-volume screening tasks. A break condition: if abstracts exceed context window limits or require extensive custom parsing (e.g., complex tables, non-English text), batch efficiency drops due to preprocessing overhead and increased API calls.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Abstract ingestion -> prompt templating (rubric + abstract) -> API batch dispatch (ChatGPT/Gemini/Claude) -> score extraction/parsing -> aggregation (composite scores) -> reliability analysis (ICC, Bland-Altman) -> bias reporting (means, limits of agreement).

### Critical path
Ensure prompt fidelity to the original rubric; handle API rate limits; validate score format consistency before aggregation; compute ICC using correct model (two-way random for AI-AI, one-way random for AI-human).

### Design tradeoffs
Larger batch sizes (e.g., 20 abstracts/request) reduce cost but risk context window overflow; precise prompt wording improves accuracy but increases token usage; aggregating multiple LLM scores may boost reliability but adds complexity.

### Failure signatures
ICC <0.4 for key criteria; systematic positive/negative bias >0.5 in Bland-Altman mean difference; high variance in LLM composite score distributions (IQR >1.5 points); API errors or truncated outputs for long abstracts.

### First 3 experiments
1. Replicate the paper's ICC calculations using the provided score data (or simulated data) to validate the analysis pipeline and understand the impact of ICC model choice (two-way vs. one-way).
2. Test batch size effects: run the same 160 abstracts with batch sizes of 1, 5, 10, and 20 to measure cost, latency, and score consistency (within-LLM ICC).
3. Conduct a prompt ablation study: vary the specificity of subjective criteria definitions (e.g., "impact" with vs. without examples) to measure changes in LLM-human ICC and systematic bias.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Generalizability to diverse conferences and fields:** To what extent do the findings generalize to larger, more diverse conferences and to academic fields beyond pediatric health research? The study used a single, specialized conference's abstracts, limiting external validity. A multi-site, multi-disciplinary study across STEM, social sciences, and humanities with consistent rubrics is needed.
2. **Model characteristics driving performance differences:** What underlying model characteristics explain the significant performance differences between models, particularly Gemini's failure to achieve any reliability on impact and applicability? The study compares outputs but does not analyze internal model properties. Controlled experiments fine-tuning or prompting the same base model, combined with ablation studies, could identify relevant features.
3. **Impact of granular, outcome-oriented rubrics:** Can a highly granular, outcome-oriented rubric (30–40 detailed items) emphasizing objective and quantitative criteria substantially improve LLM-human agreement and utility? The current "one-size-fits-all" 7-item rubric may not leverage LLMs' strengths. A randomized trial comparing the existing rubric against an expanded rubric with explicit anchors and quantitative checklists is required.
4. **Validity without a gold standard:** Without a consensus "gold standard" for abstract quality, how can we definitively assess whether LLM judgments are valid, or are they merely replicating a specific, potentially flawed, human rater consensus? Human reviewers themselves had only moderate inter-rater reliability. Longitudinal studies correlating LLM and human scores with real-world outcomes (publications, grants, impact) or adjudicated expert panels could address this.

## Limitations
- Model identifiers like "ChatGPT-5" may not match current public APIs, risking behavioral mismatches.
- The published prompt lacks full rubric descriptors and output format details, hindering exact replication.
- Raw human scoring data per abstract and rater assignments are not publicly accessible, blocking direct ICC replication.
- Analysis details (ICC formula unit, handling of batch processing) are ambiguously reported.
- Potential batch effects where LLMs grade relatively within a prompt rather than absolutely across all abstracts.

## Confidence
- LLMs maintain consistent scoring: Medium (dependent on unknown model versions and prompt fidelity).
- Moderate agreement on objective criteria: Medium (raw data and precise rubric needed for verification).
- Scalability (sub-10-minute, <$1 cost): Medium (assumes exact batch size and model pricing; unverified).
