# IncompeBench: A Permissively Licensed, Fine-Grained Benchmark for Music Information Retrieval
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.11941
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11941
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Introduced a large-scale, permissively licensed MIR benchmark with LLM-generated graded relevance judgments, revealing current models struggle with fine-grained query nuances as shown by a significant performance drop in the strict evaluation setting.

## Executive Summary
IncompeBench addresses the scarcity of high-quality, redistributable benchmarks for fine-grained music information retrieval (MIR). It comprises 1,574 permissively licensed (CC-BY) 30-second audio snippets from the IncompeTech corpus, 500 diverse natural language queries, and over 125,000 graded relevance judgments (scale 0–3) generated via a multi-stage LLM pipeline (Gemini 3 Pro). Human verification confirmed strong agreement with the annotations (quadratic weighted Cohen's κ=0.94). Two evaluation variants are provided: Lenient (relevance 1–3 as positive) and Strict (relevance 2–3 as positive). Baseline evaluations on models like CLAMP3, CLAP, and TTMR++ show a substantial performance decline in the Strict setting—e.g., CLAMP3's nDCG@10 drops from 0.6981 to 0.5699—indicating existing retrievers often capture only tangential relevance and fail on precise attribute matching.

## Method Summary
The benchmark construction employs a scalable, automated pipeline. First, a "song card"—a structured summary of ~30 musical attributes (genre, instruments, tempo, mood)—is generated for each audio clip using Gemini 3 Pro. These cards then seed a DSPy-based query generator, which produces 500 queries with controlled diversity in style, length, and use of negation. Candidate query-document pairs for annotation are selected via Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) of results from multiple retrievers (including CLAMP3, TTMR++, CLAP, ColQwen-Omni, and an internal model) to ensure broad coverage. Finally, the same LLM assigns a graded relevance score (0–3) to each candidate pair using an UMBRELA-inspired prompt. Post-analysis of human verification revealed systematic over-leniency for the lowest positive score ("1"), leading to the creation of two qrel files: Lenient (scores 1–3 positive) and Strict (scores 2–3 positive).

## Key Results
- The LLM annotation pipeline achieves strong agreement with expert human judgment (quadratic weighted Cohen's κ=0.94).
- All evaluated baseline models exhibit a significant performance drop when moving from the Lenient to the Strict evaluation variant, with CLAMP3's Precision@100 falling from 0.9027 to 0.5906.
- This performance gap indicates current MIR models retrieve music that is only tangentially relevant but fail to capture the precise, multi-attribute constraints of complex queries.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The multi-stage LLM pipeline (song card → query generation → candidate fusion → pointwise labeling) generates fine-grained, graded relevance annotations at scale that strongly align with expert human judgment.
- **Mechanism:** The "song card" intermediary step grounds the LLM in concrete, extracted musical attributes (e.g., specific instruments, sub-genres, tempo) before it performs query generation or relevance labeling. This reduces abstraction and improves specificity. Candidate retrieval uses reciprocal rank fusion (RRF) across diverse models to surface a broad yet relevant set of songs for labeling, avoiding reliance on any single model's biases.
- **Core assumption:** A frontier multimodal LLM (Gemini 3 Pro), when provided with a detailed, structured summary of an audio clip, can perform nuanced, attribute-based relevance assessment more reliably than when queried with raw audio and text alone.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "annotations were generated via an LLM pipeline (Gemini 3 Pro) with human verification showing strong agreement (quadratic weighted Cohen's κ=0.94)."
  - [section 3.3.1] Describes how the two-stage process with song cards greatly enhanced query diversity and attribute targeting compared to direct prompting.
  - [corpus] The related "JamendoMaxCaps" work also uses LLM-generated captions with metadata, supporting this pattern for scaling music-language annotations.
- **Break condition:** If song card generation consistently fails to capture salient or discriminative musical attributes (e.g., missing key instruments), downstream relevance judgments will be grounded in flawed premises, leading to high systematic error not fully captured by the final κ score.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Releasing two evaluation variants (Strict and Lenient) based on the same underlying annotations mitigates the primary LLM annotation noise (over-leniency on "tangentially relevant" scores) and allows separate diagnosis of coarse vs. fine-grained retrieval capability.
- **Mechanism:** Human verification identified the most common LLM error as labeling irrelevant tracks as "tangentially relevant" (score 1). By creating Lenient (1–3 positive) and Strict (2–3 positive) qrels, the benchmark isolates two failure modes: inability to retrieve *any* relevant music (poor on both) and inability to rank *precisely relevant* music above tangential matches (specific drop in Strict). The large performance delta is the causal signal.
- **Core assumption:** The score-1 annotations are systematically noisy and less reliable for ranking quality than scores 2–3. Discarding them for the Strict variant yields a cleaner evaluation of fine-grained understanding.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] Performance drop in Strict indicates models "retrieve tangentially relevant music but fail to capture query nuances."
  - [section 4.2] Identifies "a tendency towards leniency at the lower end of the scale" as the most common mistake.
  - [section 4.3] Analysis shows "the vast majority of queries have multiple strong matches" and model mistakes are "related to over-leniency on the 'tangentially relevant' ('1') label."
- **Break condition:** If score-1 annotations represent a valid middle tier of relevance (e.g., for very broad queries), the Strict variant incorrectly discards true positives, inflating the measured gap and misdiagnosing model capabilities.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Building on the IncompeTech corpus (permissively licensed, diverse, high-quality instrumentals) solves the licensing barrier that cripples reproducibility and redistribution in MIR benchmarking, while its characteristics define a specific, realistic evaluation scope.
- **Mechanism:** The corpus's CC-BY license ensures audio, queries, and qrels can be freely shared and used without legal risk, removing a key practical obstacle to adoption. Its diversity in genre/mood/instrumentation provides a realistic testbed for attribute-based retrieval (e.g., "upbeat jazz with piano"), the primary query type.
- **Core assumption:** IncompeTech, despite being instrumentals from one composer, is sufficiently representative of the *musical attribute space* (rhythm, harmony, instrumentation, genre) to evaluate the core technical challenge of fine-grained text-to-music retrieval.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 1] States licensing is a "persistent obstacle" limiting reproducibility.
  - [section 3.1] Selects IncompeTech for its "high quality, broad genre coverage and permissive licensing."
  - [limitations] Acknowledges the single-composer instrumental limitation but argues "emphasizing musical diversity over authorship diversity is beneficial."
- **Break condition:** If IncompeTech's attribute distribution (e.g., over-representation of film-score genres, under-representation of modern electronic or vocal-centric genres) is systematically different from music in real-world commercial MIR systems, performance on IncompeBench may not generalize to deployment contexts.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Graded Relevance in Information Retrieval**
  - **Why needed here:** IncompeBench uses a 4-point scale (0–3) instead of binary relevance. Music similarity is a spectrum (e.g., "swinging jazz trio" vs. "bebop" for "upbeat jazz"), making graded metrics like nDCG essential for interpreting performance and the Strict/Lenient split.
  - **Quick check question:** When might a song be graded "2 (partially relevant)" instead of "3 (fully relevant)" for the query "relaxing acoustic guitar for studying"?
- **Concept: LLM-as-a-Judge for Scalable Annotation**
  - **Why needed here:** The core innovation uses a frontier LLM to generate 125k+ judgments. Understanding risks (model bias, prompt sensitivity) and validation (human verification with κ) is key to assessing reliability.
  - **Quick check question:** Why might an LLM judge show "over-leniency" on score 1, and how does the dual-variant design address this specific failure mode?
- **Concept: Permissive Licensing in Dataset Construction**
  - **Why needed here:** The paper's value is a *redistributable* benchmark. Knowing the difference between CC-BY and restrictive research licenses explains why this enables long-term community use and extension.
  - **Quick check question:** What would be the primary consequence if IncompeTech used a "non-commercial" license instead of CC-BY?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Audio Corpus (IncompeTech chunks) -> Song Card Generator (Gemini 3 Pro extracts attributes) -> Query Generator (DSPy program, controlled by persona/style/negation) -> Candidate Retriever & Fuser (RRF across CLAMP3, TTMR++, CLAP, ColQwen-Omni, internal model, song-card retrieval) -> LLM Labeler (Gemini 3 Pro pointwise 0-3 scoring) -> Variant Splitter (creates Strict/Lenient qrels) -> Baseline Models (CLAP, TTMR++, CLAMP3, ColQwen-Omni for eval)
- **Critical path:** Song Card -> Query Generator -> Candidate Fuser -> LLM Labeler -> Variant Split. Song cards are the pivotal intermediary; their quality propagates to query diversity and labeling consistency.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Scale vs. Annotation Depth: Annotating ~128k of ~850k possible pairs provides breadth but risks false negatives for unjudged pairs.
  - Automation vs. Noise: Full automation enables scale and permissive release but introduces LLM biases (over-leniency), addressed by the dual-variant solution.
  - Corpus Diversity vs. Licensing: Prioritized permissive licensing and musical attribute diversity, accepting the limitation of single-composer instrumentals.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Annotation Collapse:** Queries or song cards become formulaic/lacking diversity (indicates prompt or generation pipeline failure).
  - **Label Distribution Skew:** Extreme imbalance in score 0/1/2/3 distribution (e.g., few score 3s) suggests poor candidate selection or labeling model failure to identify strong matches.
  - **Model-Eval Mismatch:** All baselines show very high Precision@100 in Lenient (~0.9+) but a massive drop in Strict (~0.5–0.7). If this gap is absent/small for a new model, it may indicate incorrect protocol usage.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Replicate baseline gap: Load released qrels and audio, compute nDCG@10 and Precision@100 for CLAMP3 on both Strict and Lenient. Verify reported drop (e.g., nDCG@10 0.6981 → 0.5699) to confirm environment setup.
  2. Song card sanity check: Sample 20 song cards and corresponding audio. Manually verify accuracy and specificity of extracted attributes (e.g., "bpm: 128", "instruments: synthesizer").
  3. Query-attribute coverage: For a sample of queries, check if explicitly queried attributes (e.g., "upright bass") are present in ground-truth relevant songs per Strict qrels.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How can MIR benchmarks be expanded to include vocal music and a more diverse set of composers while maintaining permissive licensing and high audio quality?
  - **Basis:** Limitations section states songs are "largely instrumentals from a single prolific composer"; future work should cover "music containing vocals and a larger volume of sources."
  - **Why unresolved:** Finding/creating a comparable permissively licensed corpus with vocal diversity is a significant challenge.
  - **What evidence would resolve:** Release of a new large-scale music corpus with diverse vocal tracks from multiple artists under a permissive license (e.g., CC-BY), with a similar benchmark structure.
- **Open Question 2:** What model architectures or training objectives are necessary for text-to-music retrievers to move beyond retrieving tangentially relevant results and better capture nuanced, multi-attribute constraints?
  - **Basis:** Baseline results show a large performance drop (e.g., CLAMP3 nDCG@10 0.6981 → 0.5699) from Lenient to Strict, indicating systemic limitation.
  - **Why unresolved:** All current models exhibit this behavior.
  - **What evidence would resolve:** A new model with a significantly smaller Strict/Lenient gap on IncompeBench, plus ablations identifying responsible components.
- **Open Question 3:** Can the risk of false negatives in large-scale MIR benchmarks—where relevant songs are not annotated—be systematically reduced without proportional cost increase?
  - **Basis:** Limitations acknowledge "the risk of false negative therefore remains" because not all pairs are annotated and queries are "purposefully broad."
  - **Why unresolved:** Fully annotating all pairs is infeasible; current candidate selection (RRF) may miss relevant songs not retrieved by any baseline.
  - **What evidence would resolve:** A study using a different candidate strategy (e.g., query expansion, embedding-space sampling) measuring reduction in missed pairs via exhaustive human judgment on a sample.
- **Open Question 4:** How generally applicable is the multi-stage LLM-based annotation pipeline to constructing fine-grained retrieval benchmarks in other multimodal domains (e.g., text-to-image, text-to-video)?
  - **Basis:** The pipeline's high human-LLM agreement (κ=0.94) suggests it could be a template for other domains.
  - **Why unresolved:** The design is coupled with domain-specific elements (e.g., "song cards" for musical attributes).
  - **What evidence would resolve:** Successful adaptation of the same core pipeline structure to build a benchmark in another domain (e.g., video retrieval), with a comparable human validation study.

## Limitations
- The IncompeTech corpus consists largely of instrumentals from a single prolific composer, which may not generalize to vocal-centric or multi-author music spaces.
- The dual-variant design assumes score-1 ("tangentially relevant") annotations are predominantly noisy; this label's true semantic validity across query types remains unvalidated.
- The candidate set for annotation relies on a proprietary internal model in the fusion pipeline, whose absence or differences could alter coverage and introduce unmeasured gaps.

## Confidence
- **LLM pipeline validity (Mechanism 1):** High. Supported by strong human agreement (κ=0.94), though validation is corpus-specific.
- **Dual-variant efficacy (Mechanism 2):** Medium. The observed leniency trend is well-documented, but the causal link between score-1 noise and the performance gap is correlational.
- **Corpus licensing/scope (Mechanism 3):** Medium-Low. The licensing barrier is solved, but the assumption that musical attribute diversity outweighs authorship diversity for fine-grained retrieval is untested against external datasets.
