# ViTaB-A: Evaluating Multimodal Large Language Models on Visual Table Attribution

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.15769
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15769
- **Reference count:** 23
- **Primary result:** Multimodal LLMs achieve moderate question answering accuracy (48–55%) but suffer from near-random cell-level attribution accuracy, particularly for JSON (<1%), with confidence scores poorly aligned with attribution correctness.

## Executive Summary
The paper identifies a critical gap in multimodal large language models (mLLMs): while they can answer questions about tabular data correctly, they fail to reliably attribute answers to specific supporting cells. To measure this, the authors introduce the ViTaB-A benchmark, evaluating several open-source mLLMs on visual table attribution across three representations (Markdown, JSON, rendered images) and three prompting strategies. Results show a stark disparity—average QA accuracy remains moderate (48–55%), while cell-level attribution accuracy is dramatically lower, near random for JSON (<1%) and only reaching ~33% for images. Models are consistently better at citing correct rows than columns, and model confidence (both internal and verbalized) shows poor alignment with actual attribution correctness. This indicates that current mLLMs lack fine-grained, trustworthy traceability for structured data, limiting their use in transparency-critical applications.

## Method Summary
The study constructs ViTaB-A, a benchmark requiring models to answer questions about tables and cite exact supporting cells. Tables are presented in three formats: Markdown, JSON, and rendered images. Several open-source mLLMs are evaluated in zero-shot settings using three prompting strategies (zero-shot, few-shot, chain-of-thought). Performance is measured on two axes: question answering accuracy and cell-level attribution accuracy (exact match of cited cells). Additionally, the alignment between model confidence (internal probabilities and verbalized certainty) and attribution correctness is analyzed.

## Key Results
- Question answering accuracy ranges from 48% to 55%, while cell-level attribution accuracy is significantly lower: below 1% for JSON, around 33% for images, and intermediate for Markdown.
- Models consistently outperform in row citation compared to column citation, indicating difficulty in identifying correct attributes.
- Confidence scores (both internal and verbalized) exhibit poor correlation with attribution accuracy, demonstrating that high confidence does not imply trustworthy attribution.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Training Objectives Misalignment**
- **Claim:** Standard training objectives for mLLMs do not optimize for fine-grained evidence localization, creating a capability gap between answer generation and attribution.
- **Mechanism:** Models are trained via instruction tuning and RLHF to produce correct, helpful answers, rewarded on downstream task accuracy. This does not provide a direct learning signal for precisely identifying supporting table cells. Attribution ability does not emerge automatically from answer-focused training.
- **Core assumption:** Training objectives are the primary bottleneck; architectural changes without dedicated attribution objectives will not close the gap.
- **Evidence anchors:** The paper's conclusion states the gap reflects how mLLMs are trained and evaluated, optimizing for correct answers without rewarding precise citation. Section 4.1 shows the stark disparity (QA ~48–55% vs. attribution <1% to ~33%). Related work like *Multimodal Fact-Level Attribution* discusses the need for dedicated grounding objectives.
- **Break condition:** If future work shows that adding attribution-specific fine-tuning (e.g., on ViTaB-A) eliminates the QA-attribution gap without hurting QA performance, the claim gains strong evidence.

**Mechanism 2: Representation-Dependent Spatial Parsing**
- **Claim:** The representational format of tabular data fundamentally changes the spatial parsing demands on the model, with visual (image) inputs providing richer spatial cues that facilitate attribution.
- **Mechanism:** Images preserve the 2D spatial layout (alignment, borders, whitespace), allowing models to use vision-based spatial reasoning to localize cells. Textual formats (Markdown, JSON) encode structure symbolically via nested text/indentation, requiring the model to reconstruct a mental 2D map from a 1D sequence—a known difficult parsing task. The spatial-intelligence requirement is lower for images.
- **Core assumption:** The performance difference is primarily due to the explicitness of spatial information in the input representation, not just modality-specific training data biases.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract notes models struggle more with textual formats than images. Section 4.2 explains that images preserve spatial hierarchy, allowing layout-based cues, while textual formats require understanding structure from sequences. Works like *Efficient Table Retrieval and Understanding with MLLMs* and *TableMoE* note challenges in parsing structure from image vs. text.
- **Break condition:** If models fine-tuned on large-scale synthetic data that forces explicit spatial coordinates from JSON/Markdown achieve textual attribution parity with images, the mechanism is confirmed. If the gap persists, other factors dominate.

**Mechanism 3: Confidence Miscalibration for Grounding**
- **Claim:** Model confidence estimates (internal and verbalized) are poorly calibrated for attribution tasks because confidence is derived from answer-generation probabilities, not evidence-localization certainty.
- **Mechanism:** During generation, token probabilities reflect the model's certainty about the next linguistic token in the citation format (e.g., "B3", "SUM(...)"), not the correctness of the spatial localization decision. The model's internal "belief" about which cell is correct is not directly trained or represented in the probability distribution over output tokens. Verbalized confidence is an additional, uncalibrated head.
- **Core assumption:** Confidence miscalibration stems from a fundamental misalignment between what probabilities measure (linguistic fluency/format adherence) and what attribution requires (spatial precision).
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 4.4 shows no clear advantage of using confidence as an indicator; alignment scores are mostly below 0.7, often near random. The abstract notes confidence shows poor alignment with attribution correctness. *ChartLens* and *RADAR* papers report similar confidence-hallucination issues in visual grounding.
- **Break condition:** If a probing study shows that manipulating the correctness of the spatial reference (while keeping linguistic form valid) changes internal confidence proportionally to attribution error, it supports the mechanism. If confidence remains high regardless, it confirms decoupling.

## Foundational Learning

**Concept: Spatial-Topological Mapping**
- **Why needed:** Attribution requires mapping a 2D grid (table) to symbolic coordinates (e.g., "B3"). The model must maintain a consistent, precise spatial index over the table structure, not just parse content.
- **Quick check:** Given a table with repeated values, does the model's citation change if the target value moves to a different row/column? If it cites the value rather than the position, spatial mapping is weak.

**Concept: Hierarchical Structure Parsing**
- **Why needed:** Both JSON and Markdown encode tables via nested syntax (objects/lists or pipes/plus signs). The model must parse this hierarchy to recover the row-column graph, which is non-trivial and error-prone.
- **Quick check:** Can the model correctly cite a cell if the table JSON is minified (no whitespace) vs. pretty-printed? Failure indicates reliance on formatting cues over logical structure.

**Concept: Uncertainty Calibration for Grounding**
- **Why needed:** Confidence scores from next-token prediction measure linguistic plausibility, not factual grounding. A separate "grounding certainty" signal must be estimated, possibly via consistency checks or multi-path sampling.
- **Quick check:** For a known-attribution case, does the model's verbal confidence ("Very Certain") drop if you add a visually salient but irrelevant row to the table? If not, confidence is not sensitive to distractor evidence.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input Encoder (Vision for images, Text for Markdown/JSON) -> Cross-Modal Bridge (MLP/Perciever) -> LLM (with Spatial Reasoning via Self-Attention) -> Attribution Head (auto-regressive decoder for coordinates) -> Output (cell references)
- **Critical path:** Table (T) → Encoding (Xᵣ) → LLM reasoning (f_θ) → Output tokens (cell refs) → Parse → Set ˆS. Core challenge: LLM reasoning step building a spatial query (q, a) → (row, col) map.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Representation: Images give strong spatial cues but increase compute and may suffer OCR errors; Text is cheap but forces more structural parsing.
  - Prompting: CoT may help reasoning but risks hallucination; Zero-shot is cleaner but may not trigger spatial reasoning.
  - Confidence source: Internal confidence is cheap but misaligned; verbalized confidence is prompt-sensitive and ungrounded.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - JSON: Attribution accuracy ~0–2% (near random). Model outputs syntactically plausible but semantically unrelated indices.
  - Row vs. Column: Row accuracy 1.5–2× higher than column. Model identifies row correctly but picks wrong column, especially with ambiguous headers.
  - Confidence: High confidence (>70%) paired with low attribution (<30%). Model expresses certainty despite wrong citations.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Baseline replication:** Run the paper's zero-shot setup on one 4B model (e.g., InternVL3.5) across all representations. Verify QA-attribution gap and image > text performance.
  2. **Representation ablation:** For image tables, apply visual perturbations (font/color changes). Does attribution drop? Tests reliance on superficial vs. deep layout cues.
  3. **Confidence-accuracy decoupling:** For confident (>80% verbal) and QA-correct cases, manually check attribution. Expect high misattribution rate. Compute Brier score to quantify misalignment.

## Limitations
- Causation unproven: The QA-attribution gap is correlational; no experiments rule out alternative causes like pretraining data distribution or architectural biases.
- Representation confounders: Image vs. text differences may be influenced by training biases (e.g., more image-table examples) rather than solely spatial cue availability.
- Confidence probe depth: Misalignment analysis is aggregate; it does not isolate why confidence is miscalibrated (e.g., token probability smoothing, prompt wording effects).

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (Training objectives): Medium
- Mechanism 2 (Spatial cues in images): Medium
- Mechanism 3 (Confidence miscalibration): High

## Next Checks
1. **Fine-tuning ablation:** Add ViTab-A-style attribution examples to instruction tuning data. If QA-attribution gap closes without hurting QA, Mechanism 1 gains support.
2. **Synthetic representation control:** Convert image tables to minified JSON with enforced spatial coordinate tokens (e.g., `[R=3,C=2]`). If textual attribution improves to image parity, Mechanism 2 is confirmed.
3. **Confidence perturbation probe:** For correctly answered QA instances, insert a visually salient distractor row/column. Measure change in internal and verbalized confidence. A drop indicates grounding sensitivity; no change confirms decoupling.
