# A Dataset and Resources for Identifying Patient Health Literacy Information from Clinical Notes

## Quick Facts

- arXiv ID: 2603.19082  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19082  
- Reference count: 30  
- Primary result: HEALIX dataset (589 notes) enables health literacy classification with best macro F1=0.63 on LLaMA 3.3-70B-Instruct (lenient 3-class).

## Executive Summary

This paper introduces HEALIX, the first public dataset for automatically identifying patient health literacy from unstructured clinical notes. The dataset comprises 589 real clinical notes across nine types, manually annotated with low, normal, high, or not-related health literacy labels, along with supporting sentences. Curation combined social worker sampling, keyword filtering, and LLM-based active learning to ensure label diversity and real-world relevance. Benchmarking zero-shot and few-shot prompting across four open-source LLMs yielded a best macro F1-score of 0.63 (LLaMA 3.3-70B-Instruct) under a lenient 3-class evaluation. Models performed well on direct statements of understanding but struggled with nuanced, context-dependent cues, highlighting the challenging, holistic reasoning required for the task.

## Method Summary

The study evaluates zero-shot and few-shot prompting for health literacy classification using the HEALIX dataset. The dataset was derived from MIMIC-III and created through a three-pronged curation: social worker sampling to seed relevant documents, keyword filtering to capture explicit terminology, and LLM-based active learning (entropy sampling) to increase diversity and include uncertain examples. Notes were annotated by two annotators following guidelines that distinguished levels based on demonstrated vs. verbalized understanding, with reconciliation to produce gold labels and supporting sentences. For evaluation, macro-averaged precision, recall, and F1 were computed under a strict 4-class scheme and a lenient 3-class scheme (merging high and normal). Four open-source LLMs (LLaMA 3-8B/70B-Instruct, Qwen3-8B/32B) were used pre-trained without fine-tuning; an SVM baseline was also included. The data was split into 20% for prompt development and 80% for testing. Prompts included label definitions and optionally one example per class. Models were required to output exactly one label.

## Key Results

- Best macro F1: 0.63 (LLaMA 3.3-70B-Instruct, lenient 3-class).  
- Models performed better on direct statements of understanding but failed on nuanced, indirect cues requiring broader note context.  
- Confusion was highest between high vs. normal and normal vs. not related labels.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The three-pronged curation strategy (social worker sampling, keyword filtering, LLM active learning) produces a dataset with sufficient label diversity and real-world relevance for training health literacy classifiers.
- **Mechanism:** Social worker notes seed likely-relevant documents; keyword filtering captures explicit terminology; active learning via entropy sampling introduces hard-to-classify examples, balancing the dataset and covering varied expression patterns.
- **Core assumption:** Health literacy evidence in notes is sparse and heterogeneous; a single sampling method would yield a narrow distribution of expressions.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] "curated through a combination of social worker note sampling, keyword-based filtering, and LLM-based active learning."  
  [section] "The next sample was collected by taking a random sample of up to 3 notes for each keyword... An additional 200 notes were selected using an LLM based active learning approach to increase the sample diversity to include notes where the model exhibited greater uncertainty."  
  [corpus] weak—neighbors focus on clinical note generation/other tasks, not health literacy annotation curation.
- **Break condition:** If the target population's note structure or documentation style differs substantially from MIMIC-III (e.g., different EHR systems, linguistic norms), the curated distribution may not generalize.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Annotation guidelines that explicitly define indirect, narrative cues for health literacy enable consistent labeling of nuanced evidence beyond explicit terminology.
- **Mechanism:** Guidelines distinguish levels by emphasizing *demonstrated* understanding (high) vs. *verbalized* understanding (normal) vs. *lack of* understanding (low), and provide concrete rules for proxy decision-makers and boilerplate text exclusion.
- **Core assumption:** Clinicians document health literacy indirectly through patient behaviors, family interactions, and care management descriptions; capturing this requires structured interpretation rules.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [section] "Annotators were asked to label the sentences which directly or indirectly indicated the patient’s health literacy level... To distinguish between normal and high health literacy we defined high health literacy as the patient having demonstrated their understanding in a way that goes beyond a verbal confirmation."  
  [section] "Note: If the evidence for HL low is related to a mental condition or the patient’s cognitive function, this is considered HL not related."  
  [section] "Inter-annotator agreement was calculated under both strict and lenient label mappings. The note-level strict agreement F-score was 0.52, which increased to 0.63 under the lenient agreement scheme." (This indicates the guidelines reduced—but did not eliminate—subjectivity, especially for the normal/high distinction.)
- **Break condition:** If annotators cannot reliably apply the nuanced rules (as shown by low strict agreement), the gold standard may reflect annotator bias more than ground truth, limiting model learning.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Zero/few-shot LLM prompting fails on context-dependent health literacy cues because the task requires note-level synthesis of distributed, indirect evidence, which prompts may not adequately induce.
- **Mechanism:** The prompt instructs models to use "the full note context," but model attention and reasoning may default to local keyword matching or fail to integrate non-contiguous cues (e.g., family's partial understanding vs. quality-of-life gap).
- **Core assumption:** Health literacy assessment from notes is a holistic, contextual judgment; models need to connect disparate sentences to infer understanding depth.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [section] "The models performed better when health literacy was conveyed through direct statements about patient understanding, but struggled with more nuanced, indirect examples where broader note context was necessary to make an accurate classification."  
  [figure 3] Example 2: Family inquiry about transplant/amputation → annotators: HL normal, model: HL not related. Example 3: Family demonstrates partial understanding but misses quality-of-life implications → annotators: HL low, model: HL normal.  
  [results] "misclassifications most frequent between high and normal labels" and confusion "between normal and not related."
- **Break condition:** If the model architecture lacks sufficient context window or reasoning depth to connect non-adjacent evidence, or if prompt engineering does not explicitly force contextual reasoning, performance on indirect cases will remain poor.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Data Pipeline: MIMIC-III note extraction → keyword/active learning sampling → context window extraction (5 sentences before/after keyword) → sentence-level support labeling  
Annotation Layer: Dual independent annotation of full notes → reconciliation → gold label + supporting sentences  
Benchmark Layer: Prompt template (strict/lenient) → LLM inference (4 models, zero/few-shot) → macro-F1 evaluation + confusion analysis

### Critical Path
Sampling strategy → Annotation guideline finalization (using 15 dev notes) → Full dataset annotation → Prompt development (using 20% held-out) → Final 80% evaluation

### Design Tradeoffs
- Excerpt vs. full note: Extracting ±5 sentences reduces annotation noise but may omit crucial distal context that rebuts local evidence.  
- Strict vs. lenient evaluation: Collapsing high/normal improves metric reliability but discards clinically meaningful granularity.  
- Active learning uncertainty: Using a small model (LLaMA 3-8B) for sampling is efficient but may bias diversity toward its own error modes.

### Failure Signatures
- Model confuses "not related" with normal/high: Failing to distinguish boilerplate/irrelevant statements from genuine evidence.  
- High↔normal confusion: Inability to detect "demonstrated understanding" vs. "verbalized understanding" without explicit keywords.  
- Low→normal/high false negatives: Missing subtle indicators like "limited insight," "avoidance of care," or family concerns.

### First 3 Experiments
1. Reproduce dataset statistics: Verify label distribution across note types and patient demographics match Table 6; confirm sentence-level extraction offsets.  
2. Prompt sensitivity test: Ablate the "Important Classification Rule" and "When uncertain between HL normal and HL high" clauses to measure their impact on high/normal discrimination.  
3. Error analysis on confused pairs: For all model misclassifications between (normal, not related) and (high, normal), extract the shortest context window containing all supporting sentences to quantify minimum necessary context length.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- Does fine-tuning open-source LLMs on the HEALIX training set improve macro F1-score for health literacy classification compared to zero-shot and few-shot prompting?  
- Does using full clinical notes (rather than keyword-context excerpts) improve health literacy classification accuracy?  
- What specific contextual cues distinguish high from normal health literacy in clinical notes, and can a model that leverages these cues achieve higher accuracy on this distinction?  
- How well do models trained on HEALIX generalize to clinical notes from other hospitals or healthcare systems?

## Limitations

- Generalization: Dataset derived from MIMIC-III social work notes may not generalize to other EHR systems or documentation styles.  
- Annotation reliability: Strict note-level agreement F1=0.52 indicates high subjectivity in labeling health literacy from notes.  
- Reproducibility: Exact few-shot examples and train/test split not provided, hindering exact replication.
