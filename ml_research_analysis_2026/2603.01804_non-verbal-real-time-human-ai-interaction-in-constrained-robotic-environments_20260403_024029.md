# Non-verbal Real-time Human-AI Interaction in Constrained Robotic Environments

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.01804
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01804
- **Reference count**: 17
- **Primary result**: Lightweight AI models achieve real-time, statistically authentic non-verbal human motion generation and emotion recognition, with synthetic pretraining reducing keypoint prediction error.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether AI can generate statistically authentic non-verbal human motion for real-time interaction in constrained robotic environments. The authors develop a dual-task framework that predicts future 2D body keypoints and classifies emotion from a 60‑frame input sequence. Using four lightweight architectures (LSTM, CNN‑LSTM, Transformer, MLP), trained on 437 human videos and synthetically pretrained with MotionLCM, the models reach up to 100 FPS on an NVIDIA Orin Nano. Pretraining on 90 k synthetic samples significantly lowers motion error on real human data for sequence‑aware models. However, a domain gap remains when evaluating on keypoints from SORA and VEO video generators, with performance degrading more on SORA, indicating that temporal coherence—not image fidelity—is critical for keypoint‑level tasks. Emotion recognition is near‑perfect on human data but varies on synthetic data, highlighting the need for further validation of perceptual authenticity.

## Method Summary
The study employs a dual‑task learning setup where a single model ingests a sequence of 60 consecutive 2D body keypoints (extracted from video frames) to perform two tasks: (1) predicting the next N future keypoints (motion continuation) and (2) classifying the emotional state of the human. Four backbone architectures are compared: LSTM, CNN‑LSTM, Transformer, and MLP. Models are first pretrained on a large synthetic dataset (90 k sequences) generated by MotionLCM, then fine‑tuned on 437 in‑the‑wild human videos. Performance is measured using mean absolute error (MAE) for keypoint prediction and classification accuracy for emotion. To probe the synthetic‑to‑real domain gap, keypoints are also extracted from videos generated by SORA and VEO and used for testing. Real‑time capability is benchmarked on an NVIDIA Orin Nano embedded platform.

## Key Results
- Models achieve up to 100 FPS inference on NVIDIA Orin Nano, satisfying real‑time constraints for robotic interaction.
- Synthetic pretraining significantly reduces motion MAE for sequence‑aware architectures (LSTM, CNN‑LSTM, Transformer) on human test data, demonstrating the benefit of large‑scale synthetic priors.
- Emotion recognition accuracy is near‑perfect on human data but degrades on synthetic data; pretraining helps most when synthetic motions share temporal statistics with the pretraining source.
- A measurable domain gap exists when testing on SORA‑ and VEO‑derived keypoints, with SORA causing a larger performance drop, underscoring the importance of temporal coherence over image fidelity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The approach succeeds due to several synergistic factors. First, synthetic pretraining exposes the model to a broad distribution of plausible human motions, enriching its temporal prior and reducing overfitting to the limited real dataset. Second, the dual‑task framework encourages the shared encoder to learn representations useful for both predicting future motion and inferring emotion, which improves feature quality and generalization. Third, the use of lightweight architectures (LSTM, CNN‑LSTM, Transformer, MLP) ensures that inference meets the strict latency and power budgets of embedded robotic hardware. Finally, the comparative analysis across video generators reveals that preserving temporal coherence is essential for keypoint‑level prediction, guiding future synthetic data generation toward more temporally consistent outputs.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Input 60‑frame keypoint sequence → [Optional CNN for spatial features] → Temporal Encoder (LSTM / CNN‑LSTM / Transformer / MLP) → Two task‑specific heads: Motion Predictor (dense layers → future keypoints) and Emotion Classifier (dense layers → emotion label).

### Critical Path
The most heavily used data flow is: Input keypoints → Temporal Encoder → both heads. The temporal encoder is the central shared component; any architectural change here impacts both tasks.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **LSTM vs. Transformer**: LSTM offers lower compute and adequate short‑term memory but may miss long‑range dependencies; Transformer captures long‑range interactions at higher memory/compute cost.
- **CNN inclusion**: Adding a CNN can model local joint correlations when raw frames are used; with already extracted keypoints its benefit is limited and adds overhead.
- **Model size vs. speed**: Larger models improve accuracy but may exceed real‑time budget on Orin Nano; a trade‑off is made between MAE reduction and FPS.
- **Dual‑task loss weighting**: Balancing motion MAE and emotion classification loss is crucial; improper weighting can cause one task to dominate and hurt overall performance.

### Failure Signatures
1. **High motion MAE** on human test data → model fails to learn realistic dynamics.
2. **Low emotion accuracy** on human data → poor affective representation.
3. **Large performance drop** on SORA vs. VEO keypoints → indicates sensitivity to temporal incoherence.
4. **FPS below required threshold** (e.g., <30 FPS) → real‑time deployment becomes infeasible.
5. **No improvement after pretraining** → likely a severe domain mismatch between synthetic source and real data.

### First Experiments
1. **Baseline without pretraining**: Train each architecture solely on the 437 human videos to establish a performance baseline for both tasks.
2. **Ablation of synthetic pretraining**: Compare the baseline with models that receive the 90 k synthetic sample pretraining to quantify its impact on convergence speed and final accuracy.
3. **Domain gap quantification**: Run the fine‑tuned models on keypoints extracted from SORA and VEO videos to measure the performance discrepancy and correlate with temporal coherence metrics (e.g., frame‑to‑frame displacement variance).

## Limitations
- The training set contains only 437 human videos, which may limit the diversity of motions the model learns; the synthetic pretraining source (MotionLCM) might also bias the learned temporal statistics.
- The observed drop in emotion recognition accuracy on synthetic data lacks human validation; it is unclear whether the metrics reflect true perceptual authenticity.
- The domain‑gap analysis relies on keypoints extracted from video generators (SORA/VEO), making it ambiguous whether the gap originates from generator artifacts or limitations of the keypoint extraction pipeline.

## Confidence
- Real‑time performance on Orin Nano: **High** (directly benchmarked FPS).
- Pretraining reduces motion MAE on human test data: **High** (statistically reported improvement).
- Near‑perfect emotion recognition on human data: **High** (high accuracy metrics).
- Emotion recognition variability on synthetic data: **Medium** (variation noted but no statistical test of variance).
- Temporal coherence (not image fidelity) drives SORA/VEO gap: **High** (explicit comparative ablation supports the claim).

## Next Checks
1. Expand synthetic pretraining to diverse motion‑capture datasets (e.g., AMASS) to isolate MotionLCM‑specific biases.
2. Conduct human perception studies comparing model outputs to real motions for both keypoint fidelity and emotional expressiveness.
3. Test model robustness on in‑the‑wild robotic hardware (e.g., NAO, Pepper) with varying latency and sensor noise.
