# Towards Segmenting the Invisible: An End-to-End Registration and Segmentation Framework for Weakly Supervised Tumour Analysis

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2602.05453
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05453
*   **Reference Count:** 14
*   **Primary Result:** A hybrid registration-segmentation framework achieves a Dice score of 0.72 on healthy liver anatomy (CHAOS) but catastrophically fails on clinical tumour data (Dice 0.16) where tumours are invisible in the target CT modality.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles a clinically relevant weak supervision problem: segmenting liver tumours visible on pre-operative MRI but invisible on intra-operative CT. The proposed solution uses a deformable registration network (MSCGUNet) to warp MRI tumour labels onto aligned CT images, creating pseudo-labels to train a UNet segmentation model. While the pipeline successfully segments healthy liver anatomy (Dice 0.72), performance collapses on real tumour data (Dice 0.16). The key finding is that registration-based label transfer fundamentally fails when the target pathology lacks discriminative intensity features in the target modality, representing a hard limit (aleatoric uncertainty) that spatial alignment alone cannot overcome.

## Method Summary
The framework is a two-stage hybrid pipeline. First, a trained MSCGUNet performs cross-modality deformable registration between a moving MRI volume (with tumour labels) and a fixed CT volume, outputting a deformation field ϕ and a warped MRI image. This field is used to resample the MRI tumour mask, generating a pseudo-label in CT space. Second, a standard UNet is trained to segment tumours from the *registered* MRI volume (now in CT coordinates) using these pseudo-labels. Crucially, the training is sequential: the registration network is frozen before training the segmentation network. End-to-end joint optimisation was found to be inferior.

## Key Results
*   On the CHAOS dataset (healthy liver anatomy visible in both MRI and CT), the proposed pipeline achieves a Dice score of **0.72**.
*   When applied directly to the clinical tumour dataset (where tumours are visually absent in CT), performance drops drastically to a Dice score of **0.16**.
*   Sequential training (freezing registration, then training segmentation) substantially outperforms end-to-end training (Dice 0.72 vs. 0.60 on CHAOS).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Registration for Label Propagation
**Claim:** Deformable registration can propagate anatomical labels from a source modality (MRI) to a target modality (CT) when structures are visually consistent.
**Mechanism:** MSCGUNet learns a spatial transformation field ϕ aligning moving MRI to fixed CT. The known MRI tumour mask `y_MR` is resampled via `ỹ_CT = y_MR ∘ ϕ` to create a pseudo-label. The core assumption is that pathology location is spatially invariant across modalities.
**Break Condition:** Fails when ϕ cannot be estimated reliably (e.g., due to large anatomical differences) or when source and target structures are not in true spatial correspondence due to modality-specific tissue effects not captured by alignment.

### Mechanism 2: Segmentation from Spatial Priors Alone
**Claim:** A segmentation network can learn to locate a target structure in the CT image using only spatial priors from propagated pseudo-labels, without requiring the structure to be visually distinct in the CT.
**Mechanism:** The UNet is trained on registered MRI volumes (in CT space) and the corresponding pseudo-labels. It learns to associate spatial patterns in the CT with the tumour location.
**Break Condition:** Fails when the target pathology has no discriminative intensity or textural features in the CT (the "feature absence" problem). No amount of spatial prior can compensate if the input pixel distribution provides no signal. The clinical data result (Dice 0.16) demonstrates this break.

### Mechanism 3: End-to-End Synergy (Invalidated by Evidence)
**Claim:** Connecting registration and segmentation end-to-end, with segmentation loss gradients flowing back to the registration network, creates a synergistic effect by optimising ϕ for the downstream segmentation task.
**Mechanism:** Joint optimisation aims to find a deformation field that better serves segmentation than pure image alignment.
**Evidence Status:** **Not supported.** The paper's own experiments show sequential training significantly outperforms end-to-end training, indicating conflicting objectives between registration (image similarity) and segmentation (label propagation) losses.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept:** Cross-modality deformable image registration
    *   *Why needed:* The entire framework depends on accurately aligning MRI (label source) to CT (segmentation target). MSCGUNet is chosen for multi-scale supervision and cycle consistency to handle inter-modal differences.
    *   *Quick check:* What are the two primary loss components in a typical deformable registration loss (e.g., similarity L_sim and regularisation L_reg), and what does each enforce?
*   **Concept:** Aleatoric vs. Epistemic Uncertainty
    *   *Why needed:* The paper's core argument distinguishes the "invisibility paradox" as *aleatoric* (inherent data noise—tumour has no contrast in CT) versus *epistemic* (lack of data) uncertainty. This means more CT data cannot fix the problem.
    *   *Quick check:* If a tumour is truly invisible in CT (low CNR), is that an aleatoric or epistemic uncertainty limitation? Why can't collecting more CT scans resolve it?
*   **Concept:** Pseudo-label generation via spatial transformation
    *   *Why needed:* This is the weak supervision mechanism. Since CT lacks labels, pseudo-labels are created by warping available MRI labels via the estimated deformation field.
    *   *Quick check:* What is the mathematical operation to generate a pseudo-label for a CT voxel at position *p*, given deformation field ϕ and MR mask *y_MR*?

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:**
    Input CT (Fixed) & Input MRI (Moving) & MRI Labels -> Registration Module (MSCGUNet) -> Deformation Field ϕ & Warped MRI -> Pseudo-label Generation (Warp MRI Labels with ϕ) -> Segmentation Module (UNet) -> Predicted CT Tumour Mask
*   **Critical path:** The segmentation model's input is the *registered* MRI volume (in CT space), not the original CT. Its training targets are the *warped* MRI tumour masks.
*   **Design tradeoffs:**
    *   **Sequential vs. End-to-End Training:** Sequential (freeze registration, train segmentation) is superior; end-to-end causes instability and performance loss.
    *   **Registration Network Choice:** MSCGUNet provides robust multi-scale alignment but adds complexity.
    *   **Loss Function:** Standard Dice Loss for segmentation outperformed Focal Tversky on the CHAOS dataset.
*   **Failure signatures:**
    1.  **Dice Score Collapse:** Drastic drop from ~0.72 (visible anatomy) to ~0.16 (invisible tumours).
    2.  **Qualitative Localisation Only:** Predictions approximately locate the tumour centroid but fail on boundaries.
    3.  **Uncorrectable Error Propagation:** Any error in ϕ directly corrupts the pseudo-label, and the segmentation network has no CT features to correct it.
    4.  **Conflicting Gradients:** Poor performance when attempting end-to-end training indicates registration and segmentation objectives conflict.
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1.  **Training Paradigm Selection:** Compare end-to-end vs. sequential training. **Outcome:** Sequential training yields significantly higher Dice (0.67-0.72 vs 0.53-0.60).
    2.  **Model & Loss Selection:** Cross-validate UNet variants and loss functions (Dice, FTL) on CHAOS. **Outcome:** Standard UNet with Dice Loss is optimal.
    3.  **Domain Shift Stress Test:** Apply the final pipeline (trained on CHAOS) to clinical tumour data. **Outcome:** Catastrophic performance drop (Dice ~0.16), revealing the feature-absence limitation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1.  **Multi-modal Fusion:** Can inputting both registered MRI and CT into the segmentation network overcome feature absence? What is the minimum required registration quality?
2.  **Aleatoric Limit vs. Data Scarcity:** Is the performance collapse (Dice 0.16) an inherent limit due to absent CT features (aleatoric), or could vastly more clinical data and better registration improve it?
3.  **Clinical Utility of Approximate Localisation:** Does low-Dice but roughly correct centroid localisation provide sufficient guidance for interventions like radiofrequency ablation, and what is the minimum accuracy required?
4.  **Isolating Registration Error Contribution:** To what extent do registration errors at tumour boundaries drive failure, versus the pure absence of CT features?
5.  **Uncertainty Quantification for "Blind" Predictions:** Can uncertainty methods reliably flag regions where the network is blindly propagating spatial priors due to feature absence, and would such maps improve clinical trust?

## Limitations
*   **Small Clinical Dataset:** Results on tumour data are based on only 7 paired volumes after exclusions, limiting statistical confidence.
*   **Missing Training Details:** Key hyperparameters (learning rates, batch size, epochs) are not specified, hindering exact reproduction.
*   **Unquantified Registration Quality on Tumour Cases:** No landmark-based or surface-distance metrics report registration accuracy specifically on the challenging clinical tumour dataset.

## Confidence
*   **Registration accuracy for liver anatomy (on CHAOS):** Medium (Supported by qualitative alignment and high CHAOS segmentation scores, but no explicit landmark errors reported).
*   **Pseudo-label training works when pathology is visible in both modalities:** High (CHAOS Dice 0.72 demonstrates proof-of-concept).
*   **Segmentation fails due to aleatoric feature absence in CT:** High (Catastrophic drop to Dice 0.16 on clinical data confirms lack of discriminative signal).
*   **End-to-end joint optimisation yields synergy:** Low (Sequential training significantly outperformed, contradicting the claim).
*   **Registration quality is sufficient on tumour cases:** Low (No quantitative validation; misalignment could contribute to failure).

## Next Checks
1.  **Quantify registration error on clinical data:** Use expert-placed landmarks or liver surface distances to measure alignment accuracy of the tumour region specifically, isolating misalignment as a failure cause.
2.  **Simulate controlled feature absence:** Embed synthetic tumours with varying contrast-to-noise ratios (CNR) into CT images to establish a performance-vs-CNR curve, directly validating the feature-absence hypothesis.
3.  **Acquire sparse CT tumour annotations:** Manually label tumours in a subset of the clinical CT scans to establish an upper bound for fully supervised performance and calibrate the task's intrinsic difficulty.
