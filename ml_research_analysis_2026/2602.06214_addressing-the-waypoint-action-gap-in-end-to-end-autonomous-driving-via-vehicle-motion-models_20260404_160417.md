# Addressing the Waypoint-Action Gap in End-to-End Autonomous Driving via Vehicle Motion Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.06214  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06214  
- **Reference count:** 36  
- **Primary result:** A differentiable vehicle-model framework lifts action sequences into ego-frame waypoints, enabling action policies to match or exceed state-of-the-art waypoint-based methods on NAVSIM and Bench2Drive without changing evaluation protocols.

## Executive Summary
End-to-end autonomous driving policies that output low-level controls (throttle, steer, brake) face a fundamental challenge: standard benchmarks evaluate performance using waypoint trajectories. This "waypoint-action gap" forces either protocol changes or suboptimal training. This paper introduces a deterministic, differentiable lifting operator that maps predicted action sequences to corresponding ego-frame waypoint trajectories via a vehicle motion model. By training action-based policies with a waypoint-space loss computed through this operator, the framework bridges the representation divide. Two model instantiations—a kinematic bicycle model (KBM) and a continuous-curvature planner (CCPP)—are evaluated. Across NAVSIM and Bench2Drive, the approach yields consistent improvements, achieves state-of-the-art on NAVSIM's reactive `navhard`, and demonstrates that lifted waypoint errors correlate far more strongly with closed-loop success than direct action errors, enabling more reliable model selection.

## Method Summary
The core innovation is a lifting operator `F_ϕ` that transforms a sequence of predicted actions into a waypoint trajectory. The operator decomposes into three modular, differentiable components: (i) `ψ_ϕ` maps raw network outputs to bounded physical controls (throttle, steer, brake) via smooth activations; (ii) `f_ϕ` propagates the vehicle state forward in time using a chosen kinematic model (KBM or CCPP) and numerical integration (Euler or RK4); (iii) `h` extracts the `(x, y)` position from the final state at each timestep to form the waypoint sequence. During training, an action-based policy network (e.g., CIL++, MILE) predicts actions over a horizon `Cf`. These actions are lifted to waypoints and supervised with a weighted L1 loss against ground-truth waypoints. Gradients flow back through the fixed, physically plausible vehicle model parameters `ϕ` to update the policy weights `θ`. This allows action policies to be trained and evaluated under waypoint-only benchmarks without any modification to the evaluation code.

## Key Results
- On NAVSIM `navhard` (reactive), the framework achieves state-of-the-art EPDMS, surpassing the previous leader (LTF).
- On NAVSIM `navtest` (non-reactive), it matches the best vision-only waypoint methods within 1.5% PDMS.
- On Bench2Drive closed-loop, it improves Driving Score by up to 23.3 points and Route Completion, with significantly reduced performance variance across evaluation seeds.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Deterministic Lifting Operator
The framework provides a deterministic, differentiable mapping from control sequences to ego-frame waypoint trajectories, directly bridging the output representation gap. The lifting operator `F_ϕ` is formally defined and proven to be `C¹`-smooth. Its decomposition (`ψ_ϕ`, `f_ϕ`, `h`) ensures gradients can propagate from waypoint losses back to the policy's action predictions. This mechanism assumes the chosen kinematic model (KBM/CCPP) and integration scheme accurately approximate the vehicle's motion under the benchmark's timestep `Δt`. The model parameters `ϕ` are fixed to physically plausible values.

### Mechanism 2: Waypoint-Space Supervision
Training an action-based policy with a waypoint-space loss (via the lifting operator) aligns its behavior with the benchmark's true evaluation metric. The loss supervises the accumulated effect of controls over time, rather than per-step action errors, which is a more reliable proxy for safety, progress, and comfort. This mechanism assumes that minimizing lifted waypoint error correlates with improved closed-loop performance, as the waypoints are the direct evaluation target.

### Mechanism 3: Modular Design for Fair Comparison
The framework's separation of control activation, dynamics rollout, and pose projection allows different vehicle models (KBM vs. CCPP) and integration schemes (Euler vs. RK4) to be swapped without altering the training objective. This enables systematic analysis of how model fidelity and numerical accuracy affect learning and evaluation across different benchmarks (open-loop vs. reactive, coarse vs. fine `Δt`). Evidence from experiments shows CCPP with RK4 excels in reactive settings, while KBM is more stable with Euler in non-reactive ones.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Differentiable physics simulation**  
  *Why needed:* The lifting operator integrates vehicle dynamics in a way that allows gradients to propagate from waypoint losses back to the policy's actions. Without differentiability, training action-based policies via waypoint supervision would be impossible.  
  *Quick check:* If the dynamics were non-differentiable (e.g., using a discrete collision check), how would you approximate gradients for training?

- **Concept: Kinematic bicycle model (KBM)**  
  *Why needed:* KBM is one of the two vehicle dynamics instantiations. It approximates a car as a single-track bicycle with state `(x, y, θ, v)` and control `(δ, a_lon)`, capturing essential nonholonomic constraints like turning radius in a simple, differentiable form.  
  *Quick check:* Write the continuous-time KBM equations for `dx/dt`, `dy/dt`, `dθ/dt`, `dv/dt`. What happens when `v = 0`?

- **Concept: End-to-end autonomous driving paradigms**  
  *Why needed:* The paper addresses the divide between output representations: waypoint-based policies predict trajectories directly, while action-based policies output low-level controls. Benchmarks are mostly waypoint-based, creating a gap that the framework bridges.  
  *Quick check:* What are the practical advantages of an action-based policy over a waypoint-based one (e.g., in rural areas)? Why might benchmarks favor waypoint outputs?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Camera Observations + Command -> Policy Network `Nθ` -> Action Sequence `a_t` -> Lifting Operator `F_ϕ` (`ψ_ϕ` -> `f_ϕ` -> `h`) -> Lifted Waypoint Sequence `ŵ_t` -> Waypoint Loss `L_wp` -> Policy Gradient Update

### Critical Path
The end-to-end gradient flow is: `actions → lifting → waypoints → loss → gradients → policy`. The lifting operator (`F_ϕ`) must be implemented correctly to match the benchmark's expected waypoint format (ego-frame, fixed horizon, correct `Δt`). Misalignment in coordinate frame or time discretization will cause poor training.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **KBM vs CCPP:** KBM is computationally cheap and stable; CCPP provides curvature continuity and better long-horizon accuracy at low `Δt` but requires substeps and careful initialization.
- **Euler vs RK4:** RK4 reduces discretization error and stabilizes training (lower variance) but costs ~4× more compute per rollout.
- **`n_int` substepping (CCPP):** More substeps improve accuracy, especially with coarse `Δt`, but increase compute linearly.
- **Control activation:** Sigmoid/tanh impose physical limits; poor scaling (e.g., `δ_max`) may clip useful actions and hinder learning.

### Failure Signatures
- High open-loop waypoint error on validation set, especially at distant horizon.
- Large performance variance across checkpoints (unstable training); RK4 often reduces this.
- Poor closed-loop metrics (e.g., high collision rate, low completion) in reactive benchmarks (`navhard`, Bench2Drive), suggesting lifted trajectories become infeasible under feedback.
- Low correlation between waypoint loss `L_wp` and actual driving score, indicating misalignment.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **NAVSIM `navtest` (non-reactive):** Coupling CIL++/MILE with KBM/CCPP yields PDMS scores within ~1.5% of SOTA waypoint-only methods, showing action policies can compete when trained with lifting.
2. **NAVSIM `navhard` (reactive):** With RK4 integration, framework instantiations (especially MILE w/ CCPP) achieve SOTA EPDMS, demonstrating advantage under feedback and compounding errors.
3. **Bench2Drive closed-loop:** RK4-based framework improves Driving Score by up to 23.3 points and Route Completion substantially while stabilizing variance, validating in a high-fidelity simulator with true closed-loop execution.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
How can the proposed differentiable vehicle model framework be extended to incorporate 3D effects such as road grade, banking, and suspension dynamics to improve accuracy in challenging driving conditions?
- *Basis:* The authors note as a limitation that their formulation assumes planar motion in R², neglecting 3D effects.
- *Why unresolved:* The current framework only considers planar kinematics; adding 3D dynamics requires modeling additional state variables and possibly non-holonomic constraints, which could break differentiability or increase complexity.
- *Evidence to resolve:* Develop an extended model that includes 3D dynamics while preserving differentiability and deterministic properties, then evaluate it on benchmarks with varied terrain (e.g., off-road or hilly environments).

### Open Question 2
Can the initialization of the Continuous Curvature Path Planner (CCPP) within the lifting operator be improved by dynamically estimating initial curvature and speed from recent history, rather than using a fixed nominal state (κ0=0, straight motion), and would this reduce errors in scenarios with strong initial curvature?
- *Basis:* The authors state that their CCPP rollout is initialized with a simple nominal state, which may be suboptimal in scenarios with strong initial curvature or complex manoeuvre onsets.
- *Why unresolved:* The fixed initialization assumes the vehicle is traveling straight at the decision time, which might not hold when the vehicle is already in a curved segment.
- *Evidence to resolve:* Implement a data-driven initializer that predicts initial curvature (or estimates it from past states) and integrate it into the CCPP rollout. Conduct experiments on scenes with high curvature at the decision point, comparing mean waypoint error and closed-loop metrics between the nominal and improved initializations.

### Open Question 3
Does extending the deterministic vehicle model to a stochastic model (e.g., adding Gaussian noise to dynamics or using a probabilistic dynamics model) improve robustness and safety in reactive pseudo-simulation benchmarks like NAVSIM navhard?
- *Basis:* The framework is deterministic; the authors do not consider uncertainty in vehicle dynamics, which can be important under real-world conditions such as tire slip or varying friction.
- *Why unresolved:* Deterministic rollouts assume perfect execution, but real vehicles experience stochastic disturbances. This mismatch could lead to overconfident predictions and degraded performance when the environment reacts.
- *Evidence to resolve:* Augment the lifting operator with a differentiable stochastic dynamics layer and train the policy under this stochastic model. Compare closed-loop safety metrics (collision rates, drivable area compliance) on reactive benchmarks against the deterministic baseline.

### Open Question 4
How does the choice of numerical integration method (Euler vs. higher-order like RK4) interact with different vehicle models (KBM vs. CCPP) and control frequencies (e.g., 2 Hz vs. 10 Hz) in terms of closed-loop performance and training stability?
- *Basis:* The authors compare Euler and RK4 integration for both KBM and CCPP and find that RK4 reduces variance and improves performance, especially for CCPP at low control frequencies.
- *Why unresolved:* The paper does not systematically explore a wider range of integration schemes, control frequencies, or model complexities. The relative benefit of higher-order integration for different dynamics and decision rates remains unclear.
- *Evidence to resolve:* Conduct a systematic study varying integration order (Euler, RK2, RK4, etc.), control intervals (0.1s to 0.5s), and vehicle model fidelity (KBM, dynamic bicycle, CCPP). Measure open-loop rollout error, training dynamics, and closed-loop metrics across multiple benchmarks.

## Limitations
- Vehicle model fidelity: KBM and CCPP simplify dynamics; may fail under high slip, banking, or aggressive maneuvers.
- Loss design: Waypoint L1 ignores heading; its impact remains unablated.
- Reproducibility: Critical hyperparameters, preprocessing, and evaluation protocols are underspecified.
- External validation: Zero citations; independent verification is limited to NAVSIM/Bench2Drive.

## Confidence
- **Gap bridging via lifting operator:** High – strong theory (deterministic, C¹ smooth) and consistent gains across benchmarks.
- **Waypoint loss improves closed-loop:** Medium-High – improvements shown but no direct action-space loss baseline; correlation evidence supports it.
- **Waypoint error correlates with success:** Medium-High – high correlation observed in study, but excludes heading and may not generalize.
- **State-of-the-art results:** Medium – benchmarks are new and may have hidden evaluation biases; results are strong but context-dependent.

## Next Checks
1. **Independent benchmark replication:** Reimplement the framework (KBM & CCPP) and evaluate on an external waypoint-based benchmark (e.g., nuScenes) to test generalization.
2. **Heading ablation:** Add heading supervision to the loss, retrain, and measure performance on maneuvers requiring precise orientation (tight turns, lane changes).
3. **Dynamics stress test:** Introduce controlled mismatches (varying tire friction, road banking, vehicle mass) to probe model robustness and identify failure modes.
