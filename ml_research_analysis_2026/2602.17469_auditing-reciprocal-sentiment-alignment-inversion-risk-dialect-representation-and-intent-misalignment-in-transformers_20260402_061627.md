# Auditing Reciprocal Sentiment Alignment: Inversion Risk, Dialect Representation and Intent Misalignment in Transformers

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.17469
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17469
- **Reference count:** 14
- **Primary result:** mDistilBERT shows 28.7% sentiment inversion rate; IndicBERT suffers a 57% higher alignment error on formal Sadhu Bengali; XLM-R achieves the lowest inversion rate (3.6%).

## Executive Summary
This paper audits cross-lingual sentiment alignment between Bengali and English using four transformer models on a parallel corpus of 7,350 sentence pairs. The study uncovers serious safety issues: compressed models (mDistilBERT) frequently invert sentiment polarity (28.7%), regional models (IndicBERT) display a “Modern Bias” with much worse alignment on formal Sadhu Bengali, and all models show directional empathy asymmetries (systematic dampening or amplification of Bengali affect). XLM-R performs best, achieving 3.6% inversion. The authors argue for incorporating “Affective Stability” metrics into alignment benchmarks, especially for low-resource and dialectal contexts, highlighting that model compression and regional specialization can undermine emotional fidelity.

## Method Summary
The authors evaluate a parallel Bengali‑English sentiment dataset (BanglaBlend) stratified by dialect (Sadhu vs. Cholito). For each of four transformer models (XLM‑R, IndicBERT, mDistilBERT, Tabularis), predictions are obtained independently for the Bengali and English sides of each pair. Raw outputs are normalized to a continuous score in \([-1,1]\) via a function \(\phi\) that maps sentiment classes to extreme or intermediate values. Sentence‑level metrics are then computed: alignment divergence (absolute score difference), inversion indicator (polarity flip with threshold \(\tau=0.1\)), directional bias (English minus Bengali score), and dialect‑specific divergence. These are aggregated across the full set and by dialect to produce inversion rate, mean divergence, mean directional bias, and dialect gap. The within‑model comparative design isolates cross‑lingual misalignment without cross‑lingual supervision.

## Key Results
- mDistilBERT exhibits a 28.7% Sentiment Inversion Rate – the highest among tested models.
- IndicBERT shows a 57% increase in alignment error on formal Sadhu Bengali vs. colloquial Cholito (Modern Bias).
- Asymmetric Empathy: mDistilBERT dampens Bengali affect (negative directional bias), while IndicBERT amplifies it (positive directional bias).
- XLM‑R achieves the lowest inversion rate (3.6%) and smallest dialect gap (7.6%).
- Inversion failures are heavy‑tailed; a few sentences account for most errors.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Compression and Inversion Risk
- **Claim:** Model compression via distillation reduces cross‑lingual alignment stability, increasing the likelihood of sentiment polarity inversions.
- **Mechanism:** Distillation optimizes task accuracy on in‑distribution data while shrinking model capacity, which can degrade the quality of multilingual representations. Decision boundaries become less consistent across languages, producing opposite sentiment polarities for semantically equivalent sentences.
- **Core assumption:** The distillation process loses representational nuances essential for cross‑lingual semantic consistency.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract reports 28.7% inversion for mDistilBERT; Section 4.1 discusses “Compression and Elevated Inversion Risk” and “Heavy‑Tailed Failure Distribution”.

### Mechanism 2: Modern Bias from Dialect Underrepresentation
- **Claim:** Pre‑training corpora dominated by colloquial Bengali (Cholito) lead to poorer alignment for formal Sadhu Bengali because the model’s representations are under‑trained on archaic syntax and lexicon.
- **Mechanism:** Transformers reflect their training distribution. Underrepresentation of Sadhu Bengali yields weaker grammatical and lexical patterns for that dialect, causing larger alignment divergence when translated to English.
- **Core assumption:** Pre‑training data for IndicBERT and Tabularis contain mostly modern Bengali text.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions “Modern Bias” and 57% increase in alignment error for Sadhu; Section 4.2 “Modern‑Register Overfitting” and Table 1 show higher Sadhu divergence for IndicBERT and Tabularis.

### Mechanism 3: Asymmetric Empathy due to English‑centric Fine‑tuning
- **Claim:** The sentiment classification head, typically fine‑tuned on English data, imposes English‑centric intensity scales, causing systematic directional bias when applied to Bengali.
- **Mechanism:** The final layer maps a shared representation to sentiment labels. Calibration on English‑dominant data embeds English‑specific intensity norms. Bengali inputs may occupy different regions in the shared space, leading to consistent over‑ or under‑estimation relative to English.
- **Core assumption:** Sentiment fine‑tuning data is primarily English, misaligning Bengali affect intensity.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions Asymmetric Empathy; Section 4.3 provides directional bias values (mDistilBERT negative, IndicBERT positive).

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
BanglaBlend dataset (7,350 parallel Bengali‑English pairs, stratified by dialect Sadhu/Cholito) → Models (XLM‑R, IndicBERT, mDistilBERT, Tabularis) → Metrics (Divergence \(D_i\), Inversion \(I_i\), Directional Bias \(B_i\)) → Aggregated results (Inversion Rate, Mean Divergence, Dialect Gap).

**Critical path:**  
1. Load dataset \(D = \{(B_i, E_i, D_i)\}\).  
2. For each model \(M\), generate predictions: \(\hat{y}_B = M(B_i)\), \(\hat{y}_E = M(E_i)\).  
3. Normalize scores \(\phi\) to \([-1,1]\) based on sentiment label mapping.  
4. Compute per‑pair metrics: \(D_i = |S_{B,i} - S_{E,i}|\), \(B_i = S_{E,i} - S_{B,i}\), \(I_i = 1\) if scores have opposite polarity and \(|S| > \tau\).  
5. Aggregate over full set and by dialect subsets; compute Inversion Rate, Mean Divergence, Mean Directional Bias, and Dialect Gap \(\Delta\%\).  
6. Compare across models to draw conclusions.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Normalization \(\phi\): Must map multi‑class outputs consistently; scaling intermediate classes (e.g., neutral to ±0.5) is subjective and affects divergence magnitudes.  
- Inversion threshold \(\tau = 0.1\): Avoids false positives from near‑zero scores but may miss subtle inversions; threshold choice influences inversion rate.  
- Dataset choice: BanglaBlend’s creation method may embed biases; results might not generalize to other corpora or domains.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Inversion Rate > 10% signals cross‑lingual instability.  
- Dialect Gap \(\Delta\%\) > 20% indicates Modern Bias.  
- Directional bias \(|\mu_B| > 0.05\) reveals Asymmetric Empathy.  
- High \(\sigma_D\) with right‑skewed divergence distribution suggests heavy‑tailed failure (few sentences cause most errors).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Reproduce Table 1: Compute population‑level metrics for all models to confirm inversion rates and biases.  
2. Dialect gap validation: Split dataset into Sadhu/Cholito; calculate mean divergence per subset and \(\Delta\%\) to quantify formal penalty.  
3. Distribution analysis: For mDistilBERT, plot histogram of \(D_i\); compute skewness and kurtosis to verify heavy‑tail pattern.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. What specific architectural or training differences between mDistilBERT and Tabularis explain the stark contrast in sentiment inversion rates (28.7% vs. 8.6%) despite both being distilled multilingual models?
2. Why does IndicBERT exhibit a “Modern Bias” with 57% higher alignment error on formal (Sadhu) Bengali, while XLM‑R demonstrates dialectal resilience, despite IndicBERT being regionally specialized for Indian languages?
3. How should “Affective Stability” metrics be operationalized to effectively penalize polarity inversions and directional bias in cross‑lingual alignment benchmarks, particularly for low‑resource and dialectal contexts?

## Limitations
- Dataset access and preprocessing details for BanglaBlend are unclear, potentially affecting reproducibility and dialect stratification.
- Tabularis model specification is underspecified; its fine‑tuning data and procedure are unknown, making its behavior uncertain.
- The normalization function \(\phi\) uses arbitrary scaling for intermediate sentiment classes, which may alter metrics.
- Dialect labels (Sadhu vs Cholito) rely on dataset annotations without independent validation, risking misclassification.

## Confidence
- **Compression → Inversion:** Medium (strong correlation but causality not isolated)
- **Modern‑Bias:** High (direct empirical results show large dialect gap)
- **Asymmetric Empathy:** Medium (directional bias observed but external evidence thin)
