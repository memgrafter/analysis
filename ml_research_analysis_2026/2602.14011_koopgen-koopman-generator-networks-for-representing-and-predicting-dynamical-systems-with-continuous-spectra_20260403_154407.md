# KoopGen: Koopman Generator Networks for Representing and Predicting Dynamical Systems with Continuous Spectra

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.14011
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14011
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: KoopGen introduces a state-dependent Koopman generator network with skew-adjoint/self-adjoint decomposition to handle continuous spectra, outperforming baselines on chaotic/spatiotemporal benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
KoopGen addresses a fundamental limitation of conventional Koopman methods: their inability to effectively model dynamical systems with continuous spectra, which are typical in high-dimensional chaotic and spatiotemporal regimes. The core innovation is a neural architecture that learns a state-dependent Koopman generator, decomposed into skew-adjoint (conservative) and self-adjoint (dissipative) components, ensuring operator-theoretic consistency. Evaluated on four benchmark systems—nonlinear pendulum, Lorenz-63, Lorenz-96, and Kuramoto-Sivashinsky—KoopGen achieves lower long-term prediction errors than existing methods (LRAN, DeepKoopman), with the advantage growing in more complex, high-dimensional settings. This demonstrates improved accuracy, stability, and scalability for continuous-spectrum dynamics.

## Method Summary
KoopGen is an autoencoder framework that lifts physical states into a Koopman observable space where dynamics are approximately linear. Unlike static Koopman operators, it models a state-dependent generator \(G(z)\) as a convex combination of learned basis operators: \(N\) skew-adjoint (conservative transport) and \(M\) self-adjoint (dissipation) matrices. These basis operators are parameterized via real block forms that exactly enforce their adjointness properties, guaranteeing a valid generator. The discrete-time Koopman operator is obtained by matrix exponential: \(K(z) = \exp(\Delta t \cdot G(z))\). Given a state \(x(t)\), the encoder \(\Phi\) produces \(z(t)\); gating networks compute convex weights; \(G(z)\) is formed; \(z(t+1) = K(z(t)) z(t)\); and the decoder \(\Psi\) reconstructs the next state. Training uses a Sobolev norm loss on reconstructed trajectories, promoting smoothness and latent consistency. Hyperparameters (latent dimension \(D\), generator counts \(N, M\), learning rates) are system-specific, scaling with dimensionality.

## Key Results
- KoopGen achieves lower long-term prediction error than LRAN and DeepKoopman on all four tested systems.
- The performance gap widens in high-dimensional chaotic regimes (Lorenz-96, Kuramoto‑Sivashinsky), demonstrating superior scalability.
- The structured generator improves stability and provides interpretable separation of conservative versus dissipative mechanisms.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### 1. State-dependent operator for continuous spectra
- **Claim**: A fixed Koopman operator cannot capture dynamics dominated by continuous spectra because such systems lack finite-dimensional invariant subspaces.
- **Mechanism**: KoopGen learns a generator \(G(z)\) that varies with the current state \(z(t)\), allowing the spectral properties to adapt continuously along trajectories.
- **Core assumption**: Continuous-spectrum systems require a family of local linear models rather than a single global operator.
- **Evidence**: The paper states that for continuous spectra, “nontrivial finite-dimensional invariant subspaces generally do not exist” (Sec 2.2). Neighboring work exploring state-dependent approaches supports this limitation.

### 2. Skew-adjoint/self-adjoint decomposition
- **Claim**: Separating the generator into conservative (skew-adjoint) and dissipative (self-adjoint) components yields an interpretable, physically structured representation.
- **Mechanism**: A convex combination of basis operators for each type is used. Skew-adjoint generators produce unitary semigroups (norm-preserving), modeling transport phenomena; self-adjoint generators produce positive semigroups, modeling contraction/dissipation.
- **Core assumption**: Fundamental dynamical processes can be meaningfully split into reversible and irreversible contributions at the generator level.
- **Evidence**: The paper attributes this to operator theory (Stone’s theorem) and shows experimental separation of effects (e.g., Fig 3c for pendulum).

### 3. Exact constraint enforcement via real block parameterization
- **Claim**: Enforcing skew-adjointness and self-adjointness exactly during learning ensures operator-theoretic consistency and numerical stability.
- **Mechanism**: Each learnable matrix is transformed (e.g., for skew: \(\mathbf{b}A = (P - P^\top)/2\)) to yield an exactly skew‑adjoint matrix by construction, avoiding loss penalties or approximate constraints.
- **Core assumption**: Exact adherence to adjointness properties is critical for long-term stability, especially under chaotic evolution.
- **Evidence**: Section 2.3.3 guarantees “exact structural preservation” and the paper’s experiments show stable training without constraint violations.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Koopman Operator & Generator
- **Why needed**: The entire framework relies on lifting nonlinear dynamics to a linear operator (\(K\)) acting on observables; its infinitesimal generator (\(G\)) connects continuous and discrete dynamics and is directly modeled by KoopGen.
- **Quick check**: What is the relationship \(K^{\Delta t} = \exp(\Delta t \cdot G)\), and why might modeling \(G\) be preferable for continuous-spectrum systems?

### Concept: Spectral Decomposition & Continuous Spectrum
- **Why needed**: Continuous spectra distinguish the systems targeted by KoopGen; traditional eigenfunction expansions fail when only continuous spectrum exists, necessitating alternative representations.
- **Quick check**: Why does the absence of nontrivial finite-dimensional invariant subspaces preclude a fixed-operator Koopman model?

### Concept: State-Dependent Operator Learning
- **Why needed**: This is KoopGen’s core innovation: instead of a single linear operator, it learns a map whose spectral properties vary with state, approximating a family of local linear models suitable for continuous spectra.
- **Quick check**: How does a state-dependent \(K(z(t))\) conceptually differ from a fixed \(K\) in representing dynamics across a chaotic attractor?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Encoder \(\Phi(x)\) → latent \(z(t)\) → Gating networks \((bw, ew)\) → weights for generator banks → Generator Combiner: \(G(z)= \sum bwn \cdot bGn + \sum ewm \cdot eGm\) → Koopman operator \(K = \exp(\Delta t \cdot G)\) → Latent Evolver: \(z(t+1)=K \cdot z(t)\) → Decoder \(\Psi\) → reconstruction \(\hat{x}(t+1)\)

### Critical Path
1. \(x(t)\) encoded to \(z(t)\).
2. Gating networks produce convex weights from \(z(t)\).
3. Weighted sum of learned basis matrices forms \(G(z(t))\).
4. Matrix exponential yields \(K(z(t))\).
5. \(z(t+1) = K(z(t)) \cdot z(t)\).
6. Decoder yields \(\hat{x}(t+1)\).

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Number of generators (N,M)** vs. model capacity/overfitting: More generators increase expressivity but risk overfitting; counts scaled with latent dimension \(D\) and system complexity.
- **Latent dimension \(D\)**: Must be large enough to capture continuous-spectrum effects; too high may reduce convex combination effectiveness. Paper uses \(D=2\) for pendulum, \(D=6\) for Lorenz‑63, \(D=64/128/256\) for high‑D systems.
- **Convex combination vs. direct parameterization**: Using fixed basis operators with convex weights is more interpretable and parameter‑efficient (\(O(N \cdot D^2)\)) than predicting a full state‑dependent spectrum (e.g., DeepKoopman’s diagonal plus eigenvalue predictions).

### Failure Signatures
1. **Diverging latent state**: \(K(z)\) has spectral radius \(>1\) → explosion.
2. **Mode collapse**: Gating weights concentrate on few generators → reduces to state‑independent operator.
3. **Uniformly high error**: Encoder fails to produce Koopman‑invariant representation or latent dimension too small.
4. **Rapid error growth in high‑D chaos**: Indicates insufficient dissipation modeling (self‑adjoint bank).

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Nonlinear pendulum**: Verify skew‑adjoint bank yields spectra near unit circle; gating weights correlate with position (turning point vs. equilibrium) as in Fig 3c.
2. **Lorenz-63**: Check eigenfunctions capture (a) amplitude growth along spiral arms (\(eG\)), (b) lobe identification, (c) continuous phase winding from combination.
3. **Lorenz‑96 (high‑D)**: Compare multi‑step prediction error (e.g., 20 steps) against DeepKoopman and LRAN; success means KoopGen’s error grows slower, showcasing scalability.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Computational scaling**: How can KoopGen be made more efficient to handle even higher‑dimensional systems and longer prediction horizons? The current matrix exponential and convex combination costs may become prohibitive.
2. **Theoretical guarantees**: What are the approximation error bounds, stability properties, and generalization behavior under finite data and noise? Rigorous analysis is lacking.
3. **Real‑world applicability**: Does KoopGen maintain performance on real‑world datasets, which often contain noise, missing data, and unmodeled effects? Current evaluation is limited to synthetic benchmarks.

## Limitations
- **Reproducibility gaps**: Key training details (Sobolev loss order, derivative approximation, sequence contiguity, teacher forcing) and the Kuramoto‑Sivashinsky resampling procedure are not fully specified.
- **Theoretical assumptions**: The decomposition assumes separable conservative and dissipative contributions; for systems where these are deeply intertwined, the two‑bank design may impose an artificial constraint.
- **High‑dimensional numerical stability**: Computing \(\exp(\Delta t \cdot G)\) for latent dimension \(D=256\) is \(O(D^3)\) and potentially unstable; the paper does not discuss mitigation strategies.

## Confidence
- **State-dependent necessity**: Medium (theoretical argument strong but may not apply to nearly‑integrable systems).
- **Skew/self‑adjoint decomposition**: Medium (operator‑theoretic foundation solid, but separable assumption restrictive).
- **Exact constraint parameterization**: High (by construction guarantees adherence).
- **Empirical superiority**: Medium (results promising but depend on unreproduced specifics and narrow baseline set).

## Next Checks

2. **Ablation of convex combination**: Replace the state‑dependent convex weighting with a direct, unconstrained state‑dependent generator of equal parameter count; compare performance on Lorenz‑96 to test if the two‑bank structure itself (not just capacity) drives scalability.
3. **Stability stress test**: For the high‑dimensional Kuramoto‑Sivashinsky experiment (\(D=256\)), monitor the spectral radius of \(K(z)\) during training and rollouts; if eigenvalues exceed 1 frequently, try a more stable matrix exponential (e.g., scaling‑and‑squaring) to check for numerical issues.
