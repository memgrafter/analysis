# From Words to Amino Acids: Does the Curse of Depth Persist?

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.21750  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21750  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Modern protein language models exhibit depth inefficiency; early-to-mid layers capture most predictive power for downstream tasks, with later layers providing diminishing returns across objectives and scales.

## Executive Summary
The “Curse of Depth” – a phenomenon observed in autoregressive large language models where later layers contribute minimally to final outputs – is known to affect model efficiency. This paper investigates whether the same pattern holds for protein language models (PLMs), which differ in training objectives (masked, autoregressive, diffusion) and often incorporate multimodal inputs. Using probing and perturbation methods (layer‑skipping interventions, LogitLens, and ProteinGym benchmarks), the authors measure the contribution of each layer across a range of PLMs and scales. They find consistent depth inefficiency: early to intermediate layers encode the majority of information relevant to downstream tasks such as mutation effect prediction, while later layers provide only incremental refinements. Notably, as model size increases, the relative contribution of later layers diminishes further, and performance benefits saturate after mid‑depth. These findings suggest that depth inefficiency is a general characteristic of modern PLMs, opening potential avenues for adaptive compute strategies like early‑exit.

## Method Summary
The authors analyze a diverse set of pretrained PLMs (e.g., ESM2, ProGen2, ESM3) using three complementary layer‑wise evaluation techniques. First, they apply **layer‑skipping interventions**: for a given source layer, they nullify its residual update for earlier token positions and measure the propagated effect (maximum ℓ2 change) on representations and output probabilities at later layers. Second, **LogitLens** tracks the evolution of the output distribution by applying the final layer norm and output projection to the residual stream after each layer, computing KL divergence and top‑1 overlap relative to the final distribution. Third, they perform **layer‑wise ProteinGym** evaluation, where for each layer they compute mutation‑effect scores (masked‑marginals or likelihood ratios) on a benchmark of 217 deep mutational scanning assays, aggregating Spearman correlations across assays. The analysis uses 40 protein sequences from UniRef50 (length ≤ 1024) and accounts for the specific training objectives (masked, autoregressive, diffusion) and multimodal inputs. Together, these methods quantify how layer depth impacts both internal representations and downstream task performance.

## Key Results
- Across multiple PLM families and scales, **early‑to‑mid layers** contain the bulk of signals useful for downstream tasks (e.g., ProteinGym), while **later layers** contribute marginal refinements; performance curves typically plateau after ~50% depth.
- **Propagation effects** from layer‑skipping diminish with skipped layer index, and LogitLens shows KL divergence decreasing and top‑1 overlap increasing toward deeper layers – evidence that later output distributions change little after mid‑depth.
- Depth inefficiency persists across training objectives (masked, autoregressive, diffusion) and in multimodal settings, though models like ESM3 exhibit some deviations. Moreover, **larger models** show a sharper drop in late‑layer contributions, indicating that scaling exacerbates inefficiency.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Residual updates diminish with training convergence
In deep Transformers, residual connections allow each layer to add a small correction to the residual stream. As training progresses, early layers learn broadly useful features, while later layers learn increasingly task‑specific adjustments with smaller effect sizes. Evidence: LogitLens shows KL divergence decreasing toward later layers, meaning layer‑wise output distributions become more similar to the final distribution; layer‑skipping interventions exhibit weaker propagated effects for later skipped layers.

### Mechanism 2: Predictive signals saturate in intermediate layers
The protein modeling objective drives the network to encode essential biochemical and evolutionary information by a certain depth. Beyond that point, additional layers primarily adjust logits without introducing new representational content. Evidence: ProteinGym performance plateaus after ~50% depth for larger PLMs, and the curves show diminishing returns with increasing depth.

### Mechanism 3: Stage‑wise modular organization
Inspired by prior work, the forward pass decomposes into stages where layers within a stage have strong mutual influence, but dependencies across stage boundaries are weaker. Consequently, skipping layers across stage boundaries yields limited impact on later computations. Evidence: Heatmaps from layer‑skipping reveal localized low‑effect regions (early and late), suggesting a stage‑like structure; interventions crossing hypothesized boundaries cause only small changes.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input tokens → embedding layer → N transformer blocks (each: attention + MLP + residual + layer norm) → final layer norm → output projection. For multimodal PLMs, additional modality embeddings (e.g., structure tokens) are merged with sequence tokens early in the stack. Analysis hooks are placed after each layer to extract residual states.

**Critical path:**  
1. Tokenize protein sequence(s) (and structure if multimodal) and pad/truncate to model context length.  
2. Compute embeddings and forward through transformer layers sequentially.  
3. At chosen layer ℓ, optionally:  
   - Skip its residual update for earlier token positions (layer‑skipping intervention).  
   - Read the residual stream and apply output head to obtain layer‑wise logits (LogitLens).  
4. Continue forward pass to the final layer.  
5. Compute metrics: propagated effect (ℓ2 change), output probability change, KL divergence, ProteinGym performance.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Depth vs. efficiency*: Adding layers yields diminishing returns; early‑exit strategies could exploit depth inefficiency.  
- *Objective‑generalizability*: Inefficiency appears across masked, autoregressive, and diffusion objectives, but multimodal models (e.g., ESM3) show variations, suggesting training design alters depth usage.  
- *Modality handling*: Analyzing only the sequence stream of multimodal PLMs may miss depth patterns specific to other streams (e.g., structure).

**Failure signatures:**  
- KL divergence remains high in late layers → later layers still make large shifts, possibly indicating training instability or inefficient depth usage.  
- ProteinGym performance declines after a certain depth → later layers may harm representations (overfitting or noise).  
- Layer‑skipping effects are uniform across depth → all layers contribute similarly; could indicate under‑trained or insufficiently deep model.  
- Discrepancy between representation change and output change (high representation effect but low output effect) may signal output head saturation.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Layer‑skipping intervention:** For a small PLM (e.g., ESM2‑35M), skip each layer for early positions, compute maximum ℓ2 change in future token representations and output norm changes; visualize heatmap and output‑norm curve versus skipped layer depth.  
2. **LogitLens evolution:** After each layer, apply final layer norm and output projection to the residual stream; compute KL divergence and top‑1 overlap relative to the final distribution; plot these metrics versus layer index.  
3. **Layer‑wise ProteinGym:** For each layer (e.g., 10%, 20%, …, 100%), compute mutation scores via masked‑marginals (or likelihood‑ratio for autoregressive); evaluate Spearman correlation on ProteinGym; plot average correlation versus relative depth and compare across scales.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Progressive expansion during pretraining:** Would such training schemes mitigate the observed depth inefficiency? This remains unresolved because no progressive expansion experiments were conducted; training PLMs with progressive expansion and re‑applying the depth analysis would provide evidence.  
- **Multimodal depth usage:** Do different modality streams (e.g., structure vs. sequence) in multimodal PLMs exhibit distinct depth contribution patterns? Unresolved because only the sequence pathway was analyzed; extending measurements to the structure stream is needed.  
- **Adaptive compute methods:** Can practical adaptive‑compute techniques (early‑exit or layer‑skipping) be designed for protein models based on the measured depth contributions? No implementations or evaluations exist yet; developing such policies and testing inference cost vs. performance would address this.  
- **All‑atom co‑generation models:** Do all‑atom models that jointly model sequence and 3D structure show similar depth inefficiency? Unresolved because these models were excluded; applying the analysis framework to a broader set of multimodal and all‑atom models is required.

## Limitations
- Evaluation covers only a few PLM families and a single downstream benchmark (ProteinGym), limiting generalizability to other architectures and tasks.  
- The analysis relies on LogitLens and layer‑skipping, which may miss subtler contributions (e.g., those that are non‑linear or affect only specific downstream heads).  
- Scale is restricted to ≤3B parameters; behavior at larger sizes or with longer sequence contexts (>1024 residues) is unknown.

## Confidence
| Claim | Confidence |
|-------|------------|
| The curse of depth exists in PLMs (i.e., later layers contribute less) | High |
| The pattern holds across masked, autoregressive, and diffusion objectives | Medium |
| Performance saturates after intermediate layers (mid‑depth) | Medium–High |
| Depth inefficiency is universal across all PLMs | Low |

## Next Checks
1. **Broaden model/task coverage:** Replicate the analysis on additional PLMs (e.g., TAPE, UniRep, ESM3) and include tasks such as secondary structure prediction and GO term classification.  
2. **Controlled objective study:** Train identical architectures with and without auxiliary structure heads to isolate the effect of training objective on depth efficiency.  
3. **Scale and length stress test:** Evaluate much larger PLMs (>10B parameters) and sequences exceeding 2048 residues to verify whether the observed depth inefficiency persists or changes.
