# Quality or Quantity? Error-Informed Selective Online Learning with Gaussian Processes in Multi-Agent Systems: Extended Version

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.14275
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.14275
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** The Error-Informed Gaussian Process (EIGP) framework selectively aggregates neighbor models using an error-informed metric, achieving lower standardized mean squared error (SMSE) and faster predictions with fewer agents compared to state-of-the-art distributed GP methods.

## Executive Summary
Distributed Gaussian process regression in multi-agent systems often degrades accuracy when low-quality neighbor models are indiscriminately aggregated. This paper introduces the Error-Informed Gaussian Process (EIGP) framework to address this issue. Each agent evaluates neighbors using a metric that combines prediction errors with spatial data relevance, enabling selective collaboration. Two algorithms are proposed: gEIGP greedily picks the single best neighbor for speed, while aEIGP adaptively selects multiple neighbors within a confidence interval for robustness. A data deletion strategy ensures bounded memory for real-time online learning. Simulations on toy, KIN40K, and POL benchmarks demonstrate that EIGP variants yield lower SMSE and significantly reduced prediction times relative to POE, BCM, MOE, and other baselines, while involving fewer agents per prediction. Theoretical error bounds are derived to guarantee cooperative inference performance.

## Method Summary
EIGP operates in a network of agents each holding a local Gaussian process (GP) with a squared-exponential kernel and a bounded dataset (maximum size ¯N_i). For a query x, an agent computes an error-informed score ε_i(x) for each neighbor (including itself). The score aggregates the neighbor's prediction errors on data points that are highly similar to x (kernel similarity ≥ ρ), normalized by the number of less-similar points. This score reflects neighbor quality. In gEIGP, the agent selects the neighbor with the highest ε and uses its prediction directly. In aEIGP, it selects all neighbors whose ε lies within a confidence interval (mean ± θ·std) and aggregates their predictions using weights that combine ε and posterior variance, controlled by a trade-off parameter ν. Online learning is supported by a data deletion policy: when an agent's dataset reaches ¯N_i, it discards the point with smallest kernel similarity to the new incoming point, updates the Gram matrix via rank-1 operations, and recomputes prediction errors efficiently. This bounded-memory approach allows continuous adaptation without explosion in computational cost.

## Key Results
- EIGP variants (gEIGP and aEIGP) achieve lower standardized mean squared error (SMSE) than state-of-the-art distributed GP methods (POE, BCM, MOE, etc.) on toy, KIN40K, and POL benchmarks.
- Prediction time per iteration is significantly reduced (often by an order of magnitude) due to selective aggregation and fast prediction using only relevant data points.
- Fewer agents are involved per prediction, demonstrating the efficiency of the error-informed selection mechanism.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Error-Informed Quality Metric
The core of EIGP is the ε_i(x) score, which quantifies neighbor model quality by aggregating prediction errors on data points with high kernel similarity to the query x. This combines both error magnitude and spatial relevance, providing a more nuanced assessment than variance- or entropy-based metrics used in prior work. Theoretically, Lemma 1 bounds the relative accuracy loss when approximating the full posterior using only the selected data points, with the bound inversely proportional to ε_i(x) with high probability. Empirically, this metric yields reliable rankings of neighbor models, enabling effective selection. However, the method assumes stationary prediction errors and independent Gaussian noise; if errors are highly non-stationary or data extremely sparse, ε_i(x) may become unstable and misrank neighbors.

### Mechanism 2: Selective Aggregation
Based on ε scores, agents choose which neighbors to incorporate. gEIGP selects the single best neighbor (max ε), minimizing communication and computation overhead at the possible cost of robustness. aEIGP selects all neighbors whose ε exceeds the mean minus θ standard deviations, offering a balance between exploiting high-quality models and maintaining diversity. The selected predictions are aggregated with weights that blend ε and posterior variance, controlled by ν. This selective approach reduces the number of agents involved per prediction, directly cutting prediction time and memory usage. The confidence parameter θ critically shapes the selection set: if too small, aEIGP may exclude all neighbors; if too large, it degenerates to an all-inclusive strategy, losing efficiency. Proper tuning is essential for the trade-off between accuracy and speed.

### Mechanism 3: Data Deletion for Online Efficiency
To enable real-time learning with bounded memory, EIGP employs a deletion strategy: when an agent's dataset reaches capacity ¯N_i, it removes the point with smallest kernel similarity to the new incoming point. This heuristic discards the least relevant data according to the kernel's measure of similarity, aiming to preserve future predictive utility. The Gram matrix is updated via rank-1 modifications, and prediction errors are recomputed efficiently using Property 1. This approach avoids the need for costly retraining from scratch and keeps computational resources constant per update. Nevertheless, under distribution shift, deleted points might later become relevant, leading to catastrophic forgetting. The deletion policy is best suited for stationary or slowly varying data streams.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Each agent comprises a local GP model (kernel, hyperparameters), a bounded dataset D_i and its Gram matrix K_i, an error calculator (maintains e_i via Property 1), a selector (computes ε_i for neighbors and applies gEIGP/aEIGP), an aggregator (combines selected neighbors' predictions), and a data manager (handles deletion and matrix updates). The selector communicates with neighbors to obtain their ε values (and optionally posterior variances), while the aggregator retrieves predictions from the selected neighbors.

**Critical path:** For a query x: compute I_i(x) (set of points with kernel similarity ≥ ρ) → compute ε_i(x) → run selector (choose S_i) → gather predictions from neighbors in S_i → aggregate → output prediction. For online updates: receive new (x,y) → data manager checks capacity; if full, delete point minimizing κ(x, x_old) → update D_i and K_i (rank-1) → recompute e_i via error calculator.

**Design tradeoffs:** gEIGP offers minimal latency (single neighbor) but may be less accurate if the top neighbor is poor. aEIGP improves robustness by considering multiple neighbors, at the cost of higher communication and computation; the ν parameter balances error-informed weighting against variance-based weighting. Data deletion keeps memory bounded but risks losing useful data under distribution shift.

**Failure signatures:** Sudden increase in SMSE across agents; extreme drop in the number of selected neighbors (over-selectivity); dataset size growing uncontrollably (deletion failure); high variance in ε values across neighbors (ranking instability); or prediction latency spikes.

**First three experiments:** (1) Toy example with 4 agents and a 1D function, comparing SMSE and average number of agents used per prediction. (2) KIN40K online benchmark with 8 or 16 agents, tracking SMSE over up to 10^4 iterations and prediction time. (3) POL online benchmark with the same setup.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper identifies several open questions for future research:
1. **Asynchronous integration**: How can EIGP be integrated with asynchronous distributed GP frameworks to eliminate prediction delays caused by synchronous model updates during real-time online learning?
2. **Adaptive thresholding**: How can the static confidence threshold θ in aEIGP be made adaptive to dynamically balance the number of selected neighbors and computational time across varying data distributions and network conditions?
3. **Optimal ρ selection**: How should the spatial relevance threshold ρ and the selection set I_i be chosen to optimally trade off prediction accuracy and computational load in EIGP for different datasets and query distributions?
4. **Robustness to graph structure**: How does EIGP's performance degrade under sparse, time-varying, or disconnected communication graphs, and what modifications are needed for robustness?

## Limitations
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: Performance depends on choices of kernel hyperparameters (length-scale, variance), threshold ρ, confidence θ, and deletion capacity ¯N_i; the paper does not provide systematic guidance.
- **Assumption violations**: The error-informed metric ε assumes stationary prediction errors and independent Gaussian noise; real-world data often violate these, potentially causing instability in neighbor rankings.
- **Forgetting risk**: The data deletion strategy may discard historically important points under distribution shift, leading to performance degradation.
- **Loose theory**: Theoretical error bounds are derived under idealized conditions and may be too loose to predict practical behavior.

## Confidence
- **ε ranking accuracy**: Medium (empirical support strong but theoretical assumptions strict and hyperparameter sensitivity unexamined)
- **Selective aggregation**: High (consistent SMSE reduction and speedup across three benchmarks)
- **Bounded-memory deletion**: Medium (online tests show stable SMSE, but no drift experiments)
- **Overall superiority on presented setups**: High; generalizability to other scenarios (e.g., non-stationary data, sparse graphs) uncertain.

## Next Checks
1. **Hyperparameter ablation**: Systematically sweep ρ, θ, ν, and ¯N_i across multiple datasets, measuring SMSE and prediction time to determine robustness and recommend default settings.
2. **Distribution shift test**: Introduce controlled concept drift in the KIN40K or POL data streams, compare ε stability and SMSE of EIGP against baselines, and quantify forgetting behavior.
3. **Empirical validation of Lemma 1**: For various kernel length-scales, compute the empirical relative error loss when using the fast prediction approximation and compare to the theoretical bound 1/ε_i; assess tightness and identify conditions where the bound holds or fails.
