# Finding Sense in Nonsense with Generated Contexts: Perspectives from Humans and Language Models

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2602.11699
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11699
*   **Reference Count:** 15
*   **Primary Result:** LLM-generated contexts increase human-rated sensicality for ~75% of sentences from standard "nonsensical" datasets, revealing most are merely anomalous.

## Executive Summary
This paper challenges the assumption that established semantically deviant sentence datasets contain primarily "nonsensical" examples. Through a comparative study, the authors find that human annotators rate only 45.5% of such sentences as purely nonsensical (score 1) when judged in isolation, suggesting most are anomalous—semantically unexpected but interpretable under some plausible scenario. When provided with contexts generated by LLMs (Phi-4, Llama 3.1), human sensicality ratings increase significantly in 72-76% of cases. While LLMs assign stricter overall scores than humans, their scoring patterns exhibit a similar contextual modulation, indicating they too adjust sentence fitness based on hypothesized scenarios. The work demonstrates that modern LLMs can effectively hypothesize concrete, sentence-specific contexts that resolve apparent semantic violations, with implications for how we evaluate linguistic competence and construct benchmark datasets.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates sensicality on 200 sentences sampled from five established datasets (ADEPT, BLiMP, PAP, CConS, Cusp). For each sentence, two LLMs (Phi-4, Llama 3.1) generate a contextualizing premise and then provide a sensicality score (1-7) both context-free and with that self-generated context. Human annotators (4 per sentence via Prolific) also score sentences with the LLM-generated contexts. The analysis compares score distributions, calculates context-induced deltas, and thematically labels the generated contexts. A key design involves cross-scoring, where each LLM also scores sentences with the *other* LLM's contexts to control for self-bias.

## Key Results
*   Humans rated only 45.5% of the 200 sentences as purely nonsensical (score 1) without any context, indicating the majority from these datasets are semantically anomalous rather than irredeemably nonsensical.
*   LLM-generated contexts increased human-rated sensicality in 72.7% (Phi-4) and 76.4% (Llama 3.1) of cases, with mean scores rising from ~3.85 to ~5.36.
*   LLMs mirrored the human context effect pattern: both showed a "compression" where contexts helped lower-scoring sentences more than they boosted already-sensible ones, despite LLMs giving uniformly lower absolute scores.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Context as Interpretation Anchor**
LLM-generated contexts transform sentences by hypothesizing a specific real-world or narrative scenario that resolves an apparent semantic violation. The core assumption is that an anomalous sentence possesses latent compositional meaning that can be activated by a sufficiently constraining context. Evidence shows human sensicality rises in ~75% of cases with such contexts. The mechanism breaks when a sentence constitutes a fundamental semantic violation (e.g., a brute category error with no conceivable extension), where no plausible context can be constructed.

**Mechanism 2: Dataset Composition Reveal**
The high rate of non-minimal human scores without context (only 45.5% rated as 1) reveals that standard "nonsensical" datasets are predominantly composed of *anomalous* sentences. Humans implicitly engage in pragmatic reasoning to find latent interpretations even absent explicit context. This suggests these datasets test pragmatic unexpectedness more than pure semantic nonsense. If datasets contained only brute violations, human scores would cluster at the minimum.

**Mechanism 3: Context-Sensitive Scoring in LLMs**
LLMs exhibit a *relative* context effect similar to humans: their sensicality scores are modulated by the provided context in a way that mirrors human cognitive modulation. The model's internal "fit" judgment is not absolute but relational to a hypothesized scenario, likely stemming from the same pretraining objective that enables context generation. Evidence is the highly similar regression slopes of score deltas (with vs. without context) for humans and LLMs, especially for Llama 3.1.

## Foundational Learning

**Concept: Semantic Anomaly vs. Pure Nonsense**
*Why needed here:* The paper's core distinction determines whether context generation is a meaningful intervention. An anomalous sentence has a resolvable semantic tension under *some* conceivable context; pure nonsense violates semantic composition irreparably.
*Quick check:* Can you construct any plausible scenario where "The posterity conducts the origin" would be a true, informative statement? If yes, it's anomalous; if no, it's pure nonsense.

**Concept: Graded Sensicality (Likert Scale 1–7)**
*Why needed here:* Sensicality is operationalized as a continuous spectrum, capturing that some anomalies feel "less weird" than others and that context shifts positions on this spectrum.
*Quick check:* Why use a 7-point scale over binary? What does a score of 4 ("Neutral") typically signify about a sentence's status (e.g., bizarre but grammatically sound)?

**Concept: Context as Hypothesis Generation**
*Why needed here:* The LLM's task is not retrieval but *hypothesizing* a scenario that licenses the sentence, testing compositional generalization beyond memorized contexts.
*Quick check:* How does a generic context ("In a story...") differ from a *Specific Setting* context (e.g., "In a corporate event planning meeting...") in terms of constraints on interpretation?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**
Dataset Loader -> LLM Backend -> Scoring Aggregator -> Human Annotation Simulator/Interface -> Analysis Module. The LLM Backend also feeds its generated contexts back to its own Scoring Aggregator and to the Human Annotation Simulator. Cross-scoring introduces feedback between the two LLM Backend instances.

**Critical path:**
Load sentence -> Generate context (LLM A) -> Score sentence (LLM A, no context) -> Score sentence (LLM A, with own context) -> Repeat for LLM B -> (Parallel) Collect human scores on LLM A's context -> Collect human scores on LLM B's context -> Aggregate and analyze.

**Design tradeoffs:**
*   **Model selection:** Smaller instruct-tuned models (Phi-4 Mini, Llama 3.1 8B) were chosen for cost and reported prompt stability versus potential quality of larger models.
*   **Scoring prompts:** Two prompt variants ("score" vs. "rate") were used to mitigate prompt-specific bias; scores were averaged.
*   **Context specificity:** Using self-generated contexts tests the model's own hypothesis but risks self-bias, partially mitigated by cross-model scoring.
*   **Human validation:** 4 annotations per sentence increase robustness but also cost and noise (low Kendall τ=0.164).

**Failure signatures:**
*   LLM output parsing failure (missing, invalid, or multiple scores).
*   Very low inter-annotator agreement (τ=0.164), indicating high task subjectivity.
*   Cases where context does not improve sensicality (~27-29% of instances).
*   Dataset imbalance: many sentences from datasets like ADEPT are rated fully sensible without context, contradicting their "nonsensical" label.

**First 3 experiments:**
1.  **Replicate Context Effect:** On a held-out dev set, verify that LLM-generated contexts increase human sensicality scores by >70%, confirming the primary result.
2.  **Scoring Pattern Validation:** Compute the regression of LLM score deltas (context vs. no context) against human deltas; expect a slope approximating 1 for Llama and a slightly higher intercept for Phi, replicating Figure 5.
3.  **Theme Distribution Check:** Automate thematic labeling (e.g., "Specific Setting", "Art/Children") via keyword matching. Validate that "Specific Setting" is the most common overall and that CConS shows a majority of "Art"/"Children" contexts, per Table 3.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1**
*   **Question:** To what extent do existing semantically deviant sentence datasets contain truly nonsensical (vs. merely anomalous) examples when evaluated without context?
*   **Basis in paper:** The core RQ1. Only 45.5% were rated purely nonsensical (score 1) by humans without context.
*   **Why unresolved:** Study sampled only 200 sentences from 5 datasets. The composition of other widely used datasets is unknown.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** A large-scale annotation study covering a broader range of existing datasets using the same context-free sensicality scale.

**Open Question 2**
*   **Question:** How do LLMs' abilities to generate *plausible* contexts for anomalous sentences compare to their ability to generate contexts for sentences that are *truly* nonsensical?
*   **Basis in paper:** RQ2 investigates LLMs' distinction ability. Contexts raised sensicality in 72-76% of cases, but analysis aggregates all sentences.
*   **Why unresolved:** The paper does not analyze context generation effectiveness conditioned on the *human-rated ground truth* of a sentence being pure nonsense vs. anomaly.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** An analysis correlating LLM-generated context plausibility with the original human sensicality score (1 vs. 2-7). A failure analysis on the ~25% of sentences still rated nonsensical in-context.

**Open Question 3**
*   **Question:** Do the observed "context compression" effects in LLMs mirror human cognitive mechanisms, or are they an artifact of scoring prompts?
*   **Basis in paper:** Figure 5 shows LLM regression lines are similar to human ones. The paper suggests this may be due to "cognitive load" in humans but does not test if the same mechanism applies to LLMs.
*   **Why unanswered:** LLM scoring was zero-shot prompted, while humans saw alternating pairs. Similarity could be coincidental or prompt-driven.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** A controlled experiment where LLMs are prompted to simulate the human annotation process (judge alone, then with context) versus the current separate-call design. Or probing internal representations for uncertainty signals.

**Open Question 4**
*   **Question:** What drives the dataset-specific patterns in LLM-generated context themes (e.g., CConS→Art/Children, Cusp→Figurative)? Is it residual syntax/vocabulary from original sentence creation or something else?
*   **Basis in paper:** Table 3 shows clear theme imbalances by dataset. The authors speculate it might be due to original metaphoric structures or size-explaining ease.
*   **Why unresolved:** This is an observed correlation, not tested causality. The paper does not isolate linguistic features that might cue specific themes.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** An ablation study correlating context theme choice with measurable lexical/syntactic properties (concreteness, metaphoricity, animacy violations) across all datasets.

**Open Question 5**
*   **Question:** To what extent are the LLMs' context generation capabilities and sensicality scoring influenced by potential contamination of the test sentences in their pre-training corpora?
*   **Basis in paper:** The limitation acknowledges the possibility but deems it minimal because examples are "artificially generated and have no original context." This is an assumption.
*   **Why unresolved:** The "no original context" argument may not hold if the exact or similar sentence strings appeared in training data, which could inflate success.
*   **What evidence would resolve it:** A contamination audit: searching pre-training corpora or large web dumps for exact/near-exact matches of the 200 sentences. Comparing performance on a guaranteed held-out, newly generated set of anomalous sentences.

## Limitations
*   **Dataset & Annotation Subjectivity:** Very low inter-annotator agreement (Kendall τ=0.164) limits strength of absolute claims about "nonsense"; human judgments are inherently noisy.
*   **Model Scope & Bias:** Findings are restricted to two small, instruct-tuned models (Phi-4, Llama 3.1 8B). Larger or differently tuned models may behave differently; self-generated contexts introduce self-bias.
*   **Failure Cases Unexplored:** The 27–29% of contexts that failed to improve human ratings are not deeply analyzed, missing insight into the boundary of "pure nonsense."

## Confidence
*   **Claim:** Most sentences in standard "nonsensical" datasets are anomalous, not purely nonsensical. **Label:** Medium (supported by human data, but limited to 5 datasets).
*   **Claim:** LLM-generated contexts increase human sensicality ratings for most anomalous sentences. **Label:** Medium (strong result within study, but generalizability to other models/datasets is open).
*   **Claim:** LLMs' sensicality scoring exhibits a context modulation pattern similar to humans. **Label:** Medium (statistically significant pattern match, but underlying mechanism uncertain).
*   **Claim:** LLM-generated contexts are largely sentence-specific ("Specific Setting") rather than generic. **Label:** High (directly observed and quantified in thematic analysis).
*   **Claim:** The observed effects are not primarily due to dataset contamination. **Label:** Low (assumed but not tested; a major open question).

## Next Checks
1.  **Generalize to Larger Models:** Replicate the core experiment with state-of-the-art, larger LLMs (e.g., GPT-4, Claude 3.5) to test if the context effect and scoring pattern mimicry hold for more capable systems.
2.  **Analyze Context Failure Cases:** Perform a fine-grained thematic and linguistic analysis on the ~27-29% of sentences where contexts did not improve human ratings to identify properties of "truly nonsensical" items.
3.  **Longitudinal Annotation Study:** Conduct a follow-up human annotation with a stricter training protocol and a higher inter-annotator agreement threshold (e.g., τ>0.6) to validate the baseline distribution of "purely nonsensical" sentences and the robustness of the context effect magnitude.
