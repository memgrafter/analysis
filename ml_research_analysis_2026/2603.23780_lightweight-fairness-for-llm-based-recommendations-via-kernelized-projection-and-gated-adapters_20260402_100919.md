# Lightweight Fairness for LLM-Based Recommendations via Kernelized Projection and Gated Adapters

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.23780  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23780  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Reduces Counterfactual Leakage Gap (ΔCL) to near-zero (e.g., 0.17% for multi-attribute sequential recommendation) while matching or exceeding state-of-the-art recommendation utility (Hit@1,3,10), with minimal computational overhead.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles social bias amplification in LLM-based recommender systems by proposing a lightweight fairness solution. The core of the approach is a kernelized Iterative Null-space Projection (INLP) that leverages Random Fourier Features to non-linearly remove sensitive attribute information from representations in closed form, introducing zero additional trainable parameters. To preserve recommendation utility when debiasing multiple attributes, a two-level gated Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) adapter is incorporated: a Level‑1 gate adaptively combines attribute‑specific projectors to control debiasing intensity, while a Level‑2 gate applies low‑rank LoRA experts to restore task‑relevant signals without re‑introducing bias. Projectors are stored as frozen buffers and recomputed only when a fairness metric exceeds a threshold, ensuring negligible computational overhead. Evaluations on MovieLens and Insurance benchmarks demonstrate near‑zero counterfactual leakage and utility that matches or surpasses strong baselines, offering an effective accuracy‑fairness trade‑off.

## Method Summary
The method integrates two main components into an “erase‑then‑repair” pipeline for LLM‑based recommenders. First, a kernelized INLP with Random Fourier Features lifts the sequence‑level representation \(h\) into a space where non‑linear correlations with sensitive attributes become linearly separable; an orthogonal projection matrix derived via closed‑form least squares is then applied to \(h\) to produce a debiased representation \(h'\). Second, a two‑level gated MoE adapter is inserted into each transformer layer. The Level‑1 gate computes convex weights over attribute‑specific projectors to modulate the debiasing effect; the Level‑2 gate applies gate‑controlled LoRA experts to restore utility. Only adapter parameters and task‑specific LoRA patches are trainable; projectors are frozen buffers updated conditionally based on a fairness threshold. This design yields a parameter‑efficient solution that decouples debiasing and repair, achieving strong empirical results.

## Key Results
- ΔCL reduced to near‑zero on both benchmarks (e.g., 0.17% for multi‑attribute sequential recommendation).
- Recommendation utility (Hit@1,3,10) matches or exceeds state‑of‑the‑art baselines (e.g., UP5).
- Minimal additional parameters (only adapter and projector buffers) and no adversarial training instability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Kernelized INLP for Non‑linear Bias Removal
RFF lifts \(h\) to approximate a Gaussian kernel, making non‑linear bias linearly separable. After isotropic perturbation, a linear INLP probe on the lifted space identifies sensitive‑attribute predictive directions. The null‑space projection matrix (from closed‑form least squares) is mapped back to the original space via block decomposition and applied to \(h\) to get \(h'\). Stored as a frozen buffer, it updates only if fairness degrades. Assumption: kernel lifting enables linear detection of non‑linear bias. Break conditions: poor RFF approximation (small \(D\)) or noise scale \(\eta\) mis‑tuning cause residual bias; over‑projection may lose useful signal.

### Mechanism 2: Two‑level Gated MoE Adapter
For multi‑attribute debiasing, Level‑1 gate computes attribute mixing weights \(\alpha\) from user context \(c_u\) and convexly combines attribute‑specific projectors \(P^{(k)}\) to produce \(h' = (I - \sum \alpha_k (I - P^{(k)})) h\). Level‑2 gate produces sigmoid weights \(\beta_k\) from pooled context \(\tilde{c}_u\) to gate low‑rank LoRA experts \(\Delta O_k = U_k V_k\). Final output: \(y = O_\ell h' + \sum \beta_k \Delta O_k h'\). Regularizers (entropy on \(\alpha\), L1 on \(\beta\)) prevent collapse. Assumption: utility loss is confined to a low‑rank subspace recoverable by gate‑controlled LoRA updates, and gating inputs correlate with residual leakage without re‑encoding protected attributes. Break conditions: \(\alpha\) collapse to one‑hot leaves some attributes unmitigated; all \(\beta_k \to 0\) harms utility; large \(\beta_k\) for attributes with residual leakage may re‑inject bias.

### Mechanism 3: Integrated Pipeline
The full system integrates kernelized INLP and the gated adapter. For each forward pass, the frozen backbone produces \(h\); the (precomputed or conditionally updated) RFF‑INLP projector \(P\) is applied to get \(h'\); the gated adapter then refines \(h'\) before it passes through the layer’s output projection. Only adapter parameters and task‑LoRA patches are trainable; projectors are frozen buffers updated periodically based on \(\Delta CL\). Assumption: projection and repair can be applied sequentially without harmful interactions and attribute‑specific projectors are sufficiently orthogonal for convex combination. Evidence: near‑zero \(\Delta CL\) and utility gains on MovieLens and Insurance. Break conditions: stale or insufficient projectors lead to residual bias; convex combination causing near‑singular transformation collapses expressivity; frequent projector updates could become a bottleneck.

## Foundational Learning

### Counterfactual Fairness (Def 1)
- **Why needed:** Operationalizes fairness as invariance of recommendations to sensitive attributes; the theoretical target for debiased representations.
- **Quick check:** If a model’s top‑K list distribution is identical for a user under all possible assignments of their gender and age, does it satisfy counterfactual fairness? *Yes.*

### Iterative Null‑space Projection (INLP)
- **Why needed:** Foundational technique for linearly removing bias from representations; the paper extends it non‑linearly.
- **Quick check:** After one INLP iteration, is the representation guaranteed to be fair? *No, multiple iterations may be needed to remove all linearly predictable signals.*

### Random Fourier Features (RFF)
- **Why needed:** Enables linear probes to capture non‑linear bias by lifting features into a kernel‑induced space.
- **Quick check:** Does using RFF increase the dimensionality of the representation during projection computation? *Yes.*

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
User prompt → Backbone (Instruct Llama‑3.2 1B, frozen) → h (sequence‑level pooled representation) → Debiasing Engine (RFF‑Lifter adds isotropic noise, applies φ_σ; INLP probe trained on lifted ˆh; Projector P^(k) stored as buffer) → For each layer ℓ: Gated Adapter (Level‑1 Gate α = softmax(G1·c_u) convexly combines P^(k) to produce h'; Level‑2 Gate β_k = σ(g_k^T·˜c_u) gates LoRA ΔO_k; output y = O_ℓ h' + Σ β_k ΔO_k h') → Task head → Item scores.

**Critical path:**  
1. Encode user context/history into prompt; feed to frozen backbone to obtain hidden states.  
2. Pool final hidden states to get h.  
3. Periodically (if ΔCL > τ) recompute RFF‑INLP projectors P^(k) for each sensitive attribute.  
4. For each transformer layer ℓ:  
   a. Take layer input h_ℓ.  
   b. Compute user context vectors c_u and ˜c_u.  
   c. Level‑1: compute α; apply debiasing: h' = (I − Σ α_k (I − P^(k))) h_ℓ.  
   d. Level‑2: compute β_k; apply LoRA experts: y = O_ℓ h' + Σ β_k ΔO_k h'.  
   e. Pass y to next layer.  
5. Final layer output → Task head → item scores.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Projection strength vs. utility: Higher α_k improves fairness but risks losing task‑relevant information; gates balance this.  
- Parameter budget: Adapter adds O(K·d + K·d·r) parameters (gates and LoRA), projectors are d×d buffers; minimal vs. full fine‑tuning.  
- Update frequency: Recomputing P^(k) is costly; threshold τ trades fairness currency with computational load.  
- Attribute correlation: Highly correlated attributes may cause near‑collinearity among P^(k), making Σ α_k (I−P^(k)) low‑rank and potentially collapsing debiased space.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High ΔCL after deployment: stale/insufficient projectors; probe underfits or RFF error too high.  
- Utility collapse (low Hit@k): over‑projection (all α_k ≈ 1) or failed Level‑2 repair (β_k ≈ 0); check gate regularization.  
- Bias reintroduction: Level‑2 gates activate strongly for attributes with residual leakage, causing ΔO_k to re‑encode protected attributes; monitor β_k vs. per‑attribute ΔCL.  
- Training instability: large swings in α or β; ensure stable normalization and appropriate learning rates.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Single‑attribute sanity check on MovieLens with gender only: verify ΔCL drop, non‑zero P^(G), α_G < 1 and β_G > 0.  
2. Multi‑attribute scaling (add age then occupation): check ΔCL_avg near zero, non‑uniform α distribution, utility drop <2% vs. single‑attribute.  
3. Ablation of gates: compare (a) no Level‑1 (fixed α_k=1), (b) no Level‑2 (fixed β_k=1), (c) no both; expected: (a) fairness ok, utility drops; (b) utility ok, ΔCL rises; (c) both fail.

## Limitations
- RFF approximation error may leave residual non‑linear bias; sensitivity to D and η is unstudied.  
- The decoupling assumption of projection and repair may fail under high attribute correlation.  
- Evaluation limited to two small datasets (MovieLens, Insurance); scalability to larger RecLLMs remains open.  
- Projector update threshold τ is heuristic and may affect fairness‑utility trade‑off.

## Confidence
- Kernelized INLP: Medium  
- Gated adapter: Medium  
- System results: High  

## Next Checks
1. Test on larger datasets (Amazon, Yelp) with diverse sensitive attributes.  
2. Ablate RFF: compare with linear INLP and vary D, η to measure robustness.  
3. Audit gate distributions: correlate α, β with per‑attribute ΔCL to validate selective repair without bias reintroduction.
