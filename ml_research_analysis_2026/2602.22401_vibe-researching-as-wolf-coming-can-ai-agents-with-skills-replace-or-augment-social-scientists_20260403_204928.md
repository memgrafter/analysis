# Vibe Researching as Wolf Coming: Can AI Agents with Skills Replace or Augment Social Scientists?

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.22401  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22401  
- **Reference count:** 9  
- **Primary result:** AI agents (via the scholar-skill plugin) excel at speed, coverage, and methodological scaffolding for codifiable research tasks but struggle with theoretical originality and tacit field knowledge; the human–AI delegation boundary is cognitive, not sequential.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether AI agents can replace or augment social scientists by analyzing the capabilities of an autonomous research system, the scholar-skill plugin. It introduces a cognitive task framework classifying research activities along codifiability and tacit knowledge dimensions, concluding that AI effectively handles execution and communication tasks (Types C and D) but cannot originate theory or apply deep field tacit knowledge (Types A and B). The delegation boundary cuts across every research stage, shifting human effort from execution to judgment. While AI dramatically accelerates tasks like literature synthesis, risks include skill atrophy from over-reliance and stratification based on access and training. The author calls for empirical validation of the framework and pedagogical reforms to preserve critical evaluation skills.

## Method Summary
The analysis centers on the scholar-skill system, a Claude Code plugin orchestrating 26 specialist “skills” (e.g., `scholar-lit-review`, `scholar-causal`) across 18 research phases with 53 quality gates and five hard stops. A cognitive task framework categorizes research tasks into four types (A: Formulation, B: Planning, C: Execution, D: Communication) based on codifiability (explicit, rule-based) and tacit knowledge requirement. Empirical evidence draws on reported productivity gains (e.g., 2–3 weeks saved in literature synthesis per Noy and Zhang, 2023) and the system’s demonstrated capabilities (e.g., synthesizing 20,000 references in seconds). The framework predicts delegation potential without assuming full automation of any entire research stage.

## Key Results
- The human–AI delegation boundary is cognitive (based on task characteristics), cutting through every stage of the research pipeline rather than separating stages.
- AI agents deliver substantial speed, coverage, and methodological scaffolding improvements for codifiable tasks, but do not alter the fundamental need for human theoretical originality and tacit field knowledge.
- Effective augmentation requires preserving human judgment for verification, as over-delegation creates a “verification gap” that degrades research quality on tasks requiring tacit expertise.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Research tasks can be classified along two dimensions—codifiability and tacit knowledge requirement—forming a four-type framework that predicts automation potential.  
**Mechanism:** Tasks high in codifiability (explicit, rule-based) and low in tacit knowledge are delegable to AI; tasks low in codifiability and high in tacit knowledge require human judgment. This cross-cutting typology replaces a stage-based delegation model.  
**Core assumption:** Cognitive characteristics of a task, not its position in a sequential pipeline, determine whether AI can effectively execute it.  
**Evidence anchors:** Framework definitions in section 4.1; Table 3 maps tasks to delegation categories (Delegate?, Partially, Never) across all pipeline stages.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** The effective human–AI delegation boundary cuts *through* every research stage, not *between* them.  
**Mechanism:** Every stage contains a mix of delegable and non-delegable subtasks; applying the codifiability/tacit framework within each stage identifies specific tasks to automate (e.g., database querying) versus those needing human oversight (e.g., assessing theoretical significance).  
**Core assumption:** No entire stage is universally automatable or human-only; the boundary is intra-stage.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract statement; Table 3 illustrates intra-stage variation for tasks from literature synthesis to theory generation.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** AI agents fundamentally alter the cost structure of research by excelling at speed, coverage, and methodological scaffolding for codifiable tasks, but they do not alter the fundamental nature of theoretical originality or the necessity of tacit field knowledge.  
**Mechanism:** For Type C (Execution) and Type D (Communication) tasks, AI reduces time and expands feasible methodological repertoire. For Type A (Formulation) and high-tacit components of Type B (Planning), AI can assist but cannot originate. The net effect shifts researcher effort from execution to judgment, not replacement of judgment.  
**Core assumption:** The cognitive distinctions are stable; AI will not soon acquire tacit knowledge or true theoretical creativity.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract on speed/coverage vs. originality limits; section 5.1 on time savings (2–3 weeks vs. seconds); section 5.3 argument that AI theory generation is “recombinative” not “deep theoretical innovation,” citing Granovetter (1973) and Tilly (1998) as counterfactuals.

## Foundational Learning
- **Codifiability & Tacit Knowledge**  
  *Why needed:* Distinguishes what can be written as a rule versus what is known but not easily articulated (e.g., “sense of what is live” in a subfield), forming the basis for delegation decisions.  
  *Quick check:* For a task like “run a regression,” list steps fully specified in a textbook versus judgments relying on experience (e.g., “is this functional form plausible?”).

- **The Four Task Types (A–D)**  
  *Why needed:* Provides a diagnostic matrix to classify tasks as delegable (Type C), drafting partners (Type D), or human-protected (Types A/B).  
  *Quick check:* Classify “selecting an identification strategy for a natural experiment” by codifiability (high/medium/low) and tacit requirement (low/medium/high). What type is it?

- **The Augmentation–Dependency Paradox**  
  *Why needed:* Explains why augmentation is fragile; over-delegation erodes the judgment needed for verification, creating a “verification gap.”  
  *Quick check:* If you always use AI to draft literature reviews, what specific skill atrophies, and why does that make you less able to spot when the AI’s synthesis is superficial or biased?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Orchestrator (Claude Code plugin) → 18 linear phases (Idea → Lit Review → Design → Data → EDA → Analysis → Writing → ... → Submission) with 53 quality gates. Subsystems: Idea Formalization (multi-agent panel), Literature Synthesis (incremental search log, 6-bin thematic map), Causal Identification (13 strategies), Analytic Action Dispatch (parallel task agents for code execution), Verified Citation Pool (5-tier verification), Peer Review Simulation (reviewer agents). 26 specialist skills (e.g., `scholar-lit-review`, `scholar-causal`) are invoked per phase.

**Critical path:**  
Linear phase sequence with five **hard stops** (data safety, lit/theory verification, pre-draft gate, citation verification, ethics compliance) that block advancement. The critical path is human decision/approval points, not AI computation time, due to Analytic Action Dispatch spawning parallel agents for code tasks.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Specialization vs. Integration:* 26 narrowly skilled agents reduce prompt complexity per agent but require robust orchestration and state management.  
- *Verification overhead:* 5-tier citation pipeline and cross-agent review panels add latency but prevent catastrophic errors (fabrication, misapplied methods).  
- *Calibration scope:* Journal-specific norms (22 journals) increase relevance but limit generalizability to niche or non-English venues.

**Failure signatures:**  
- *Silent competence failure:* AI produces syntactically correct but contextually inappropriate analysis (e.g., wrong DiD estimator for data structure).  
- *Theoretical banality:* Output is structurally sound but recombines existing ideas without novelty; over-reliance on canonical theorists (Bourdieu, Granovetter) without cross-field import.  
- *Citation orphan:* Post-draft verification finds `[CITATION NEEDED]` tags because a reference existed in training data but not in the Verified Citation Pool.  
- *Quality gate bypass:* User overrides a hard stop (e.g., ethics gate), creating a submission-blocking violation downstream.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Task Classification Audit:** Take a completed research project. List every discrete action. Classify each using the codifiability/tacit matrix. Calculate the % of time/effort that falls into Type C (delegable) vs. Type A/B (human-protected). Does the observed effort distribution match the framework’s prediction?  
2. **Boundary Stress Test:** For a Type D task (e.g., “draft methods section”), deliberately delegate *only* the prose generation but perform the verification (run code, check citations) yourself. Then, in a separate run, accept the AI’s output with minimal review. Compare the number of errors found and the time taken.  
3. **Full Pipeline Run with Intentional Sabotage:** Run the full `scholar-full-paper` pipeline in code-template mode. In the `scholar-causal` output, manually alter one key assumption statement to be false. Does the subsequent `scholar-analyze` or `scholar-write` detect the inconsistency? Does the quality gate at “pre-draft” catch it?

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Does the cognitive task framework accurately predict which tasks can be delegated to AI agents without compromising research quality or originality?** The framework is theoretically grounded but lacks empirical validation; future controlled experiments are needed to test whether framework-guided delegation leads to better outcomes (e.g., fewer errors, preserved insight) compared to ad-hoc or full automation.

2. **How does the "AI productivity premium" vary across subfields, institutional resource levels, and career stages, and what mechanisms drive any stratification?** The paper warns of a widening gap (cost, language, technical skill, field) but provides no empirical data on the magnitude or distribution of these effects across the social sciences.

3. **Does sustained, heavy delegation of codifiable tasks to AI agents cause atrophy in the human researcher’s tacit knowledge and critical evaluation capacity, ultimately degrading research quality on tasks that require human judgment?** The paper posits a risk of skill erosion (the augmentation–dependency paradox) but offers no longitudinal or experimental evidence on whether AI use systematically weakens foundational skills like manual coding, statistical reasoning, or theoretical critique.

4. **Can future AI agent architectures or training paradigms overcome the current limitation in "theoretical originality"—moving beyond recombinative innovation to contribute genuinely novel frameworks or mechanisms that reshape social science paradigms?** Current systems are argued to excel at theory articulation but not creation; architectural changes (e.g., multi-agent simulation of scholarly debate) or new training objectives might foster deeper innovation, but this remains speculative.

5. **What specific pedagogical interventions (e.g., curriculum design, assessment methods, apprenticeship structures) most effectively prepare graduate students to use AI for augmentation while preserving and developing their capacity for original theoretical judgment and critical evaluation?** The paper diagnoses a pedagogical crisis (traditional execution skills devalued) and suggests principles (e.g., “teach methods as foundations for evaluation”) but provides no concrete, tested curricular models.

## Limitations
- The study relies on a proprietary, underspecified system (scholar-skill plugin); key unknowns (exact prompts, quality gate definitions, journal calibration parameters, 5-tier verification implementation) block faithful reproduction.
- The cognitive task framework is novel but lacks external validation; task classifications are paper-specific with no independent expert ratings or experimental testing.
- Claims about AI’s limitations in theoretical originality are particularly speculative, with weak empirical anchors; neighboring work shows novelty ratings but not deep theory creation.

## Confidence
- **Cognitive delegation boundary (cuts through stages):** Medium (anchored in Table 3 but no external evidence; relies on author-defined classifications).
- **Speed/coverage/scaffolding advantages:** High (strongly supported by cited productivity studies and system capabilities like 20k references/sec).
- **Struggle with theoretical originality/tacit knowledge:** Low (asserted as core assumption with weak empirical anchor; neighboring papers show novelty ratings but not deep theory generation limits).

## Next Checks
1. **Task Classification Audit:** Recruit 3–5 expert social scientists to independently rate a standard set of 10–15 research tasks (from Table 3) on codifiability (1–7) and tacit requirement (1–7). Compute inter-rater reliability and compare quadrant assignments to the paper’s classifications.
2. **Verification Gap Stress Test:** For identical Type D tasks (e.g., “draft methods”), run one delegation with thorough human verification (re-run code, check all citations) and one with minimal review. Measure error rates (fabricated citations, statistical mistakes) and time cost to quantify the augmentation fragility paradox.
3. **Quality Gate Coherence Check:** In a full pipeline run, deliberately inject a subtle error (e.g., mismatched causal identifier in analysis) at an early stage. Monitor whether later quality gates (pre-draft, citation verify) or multi-agent review panels detect the inconsistency, testing intra-phase error propagation checking.
