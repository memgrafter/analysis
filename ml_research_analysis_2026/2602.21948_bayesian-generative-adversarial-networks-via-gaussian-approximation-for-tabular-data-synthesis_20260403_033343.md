# Bayesian Generative Adversarial Networks via Gaussian Approximation for Tabular Data Synthesis

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.21948
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21948
- **Reference count:** 20
- **Primary result:** GACTGAN integrates SWAG into CTGAN to approximate a Bayesian posterior over generator weights, yielding higher utility (up to 6.95% gain) and comparable or lower disclosure risk than CTGAN and BayesCTGAN across seven datasets.

## Executive Summary
Synthetic tabular data generation must balance utility (fidelity to real data) and disclosure risk (privacy). Standard CTGAN, a widely used GAN-based synthesizer, often struggles with this trade‑off. The authors propose GACTGAN, which incorporates Stochastic Weight Averaging‑Gaussian (SWAG) into the CTGAN generator. SWAG efficiently approximates a Bayesian posterior distribution over the generator’s weights by tracking weight statistics during standard training, avoiding the computational burden of MCMC. This posterior enables sampling diverse generator instances, improving mode coverage and preserving multivariate correlations. Evaluated on seven tabular datasets, GACTGAN consistently outperforms CTGAN and BayesCTGAN, achieving up to a 6.95% utility gain while maintaining or reducing disclosure risk. Optimal performance requires a relatively high covariance rank (100–150) and typically only one or two posterior weight samples during synthesis.

## Method Summary
GACTGAN builds on the CTGAN architecture, which uses a fully connected generator and discriminator with PacGAN and mode‑specific normalisation for mixed‑type tabular data. The key addition is the SWAG module, which records the generator’s weight trajectory after a chosen epoch \(t_{\text{collect}}\). It maintains running averages of the weights (\(θ_G\), \(θ_G^2\)) and a low‑rank deviation matrix \(bD\) of maximum rank \(K\). After training, the approximate posterior is \( \mathcal{N}(θ_G, \α_G(\Σ_{\text{diag}} + \Σ_{\text{l‑rank}})) \), where \( \Σ_{\text{diag}} \) derives from \(θ_G^2 - θ_G^2\) and \( \Σ_{\text{l‑rank}} \) from \(bD\). During synthesis, weights are sampled from this posterior and fed into the generator with noise \(z\); typically one or two samples suffice. The training loop uses either Wasserstein or vanilla GAN loss, with Wasserstein generally yielding better stability and utility. Hyperparameters such as covariance rank \(K\), scaling factor \(α_G\), and utility‑risk weight \(\phi\) are tuned per dataset. Evaluation uses utility metrics (ROC, CIO) and disclosure risk (TCAP), combined into a selection score for model comparison.

## Key Results
- Across seven datasets, GACTGAN achieves higher utility (up to **6.95%** gain) and comparable or lower disclosure risk versus CTGAN and BayesCTGAN.
- A higher SWAG covariance rank (100–150) significantly improves utility, with rank 150 delivering the largest gains.
- Using a small number of posterior weight samples (1–2) yields the best utility‑risk trade‑off; increasing the number of samples degrades performance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** SWAG enables efficient Bayesian posterior approximation for GAN generators by tracking weight trajectories during standard training, avoiding high storage cost of MCMC.  
**Mechanism:** SWAG maintains running estimates of the first and second moments of the generator weights after a collection epoch \(t_{\text{collect}}\). The mean vector \(θ_G\) and diagonal covariance \(\Sigma_{\text{diag}}\) are updated via Equations (3) and (5). A low‑rank covariance \(\Sigma_{\text{l‑rank}}\) is built from a deviation matrix \(bD\) that stores recent weight differences, capped at rank \(K\). This yields an approximate Gaussian posterior \( \mathcal{N}(θ_G, \α_G(\Sigma_{\text{diag}} + \Sigma_{\text{l‑rank}})) \) with fixed storage cost \(O(3·P_G)\).  
**Core assumption:** The stochastic trajectory of SGD/Adam optimisation visits a region approximating the posterior’s support, and the Gaussian form with combined diagonal/low‑rank covariance sufficiently captures the posterior geometry for useful weight sampling.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions integration of SWAG to approximate Bayesian posterior efficiently, avoiding MCMC; Section 2.3 details Equations (4)-(7); the mechanism is novel to this application.  
**Break condition:** If the optimiser converges prematurely to a sharp minimum, the SWAG statistics misrepresent the posterior, leading to poor synthetic samples.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** A higher covariance rank \(K\) in SWAG improves synthetic data utility by capturing correlations between generator weights, necessary to preserve complex multivariate structures.  
**Mechanism:** The low‑rank component \(\Sigma_{\text{l‑rank}}\) models interactions between weight parameters. A larger \(K\) (e.g., 100–150) allows \(bD\) to retain more directional information from the training trajectory, enabling sampled generator weights to explore correlated parameter combinations that correspond to different data modes.  
**Core assumption:** Useful generator parameter configurations exhibit correlations; ignoring them (diagonal‑only) forces independent perturbations that disrupt coherent feature generation.  
**Evidence anchors:** Section 5.3 (Table 3) shows utility gains increase with rank (e.g., Wasserstein ϕ=1: 3.63% for diagonal vs 6.95% for rank 150). Sections 5.1 (Figures 2,3) demonstrate better preservation of multivariate correlations and marginals with high rank.  
**Break condition:** If \(K\) exceeds the intrinsic dimensionality of the posterior, \(\Sigma_{\text{l‑rank}}\) overfits noise, increasing cost without utility gain and possibly harming stability.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** For tabular synthesis, using one or two posterior weight samples (not Bayesian Model Averaging over many) maximises utility‑risk trade‑off, as averaging over many samples dilutes high‑quality modes.  
**Mechanism:** During synthesis, noise \(z\) is passed through a generator with weights sampled from the SWAG posterior. Sampling different weights explores different modes. Using more than one sample per batch allows simple mode coverage. However, generating a final dataset by averaging outputs from many posterior samples washes out distinct, high‑fidelity modes into lower‑quality averages.  
**Core assumption:** The SWAG posterior contains a few high‑density modes corresponding to good generator configurations; synthesis should pick representative samples from these modes, not average across low‑density regions.  
**Evidence anchors:** Section 5.4 reports an inverse diagonal trend across the number of posterior samples; using large samples tends to generate less useful data with higher disclosure risk. Section 6.1 suggests exploring a few high‑probability modes is more effective than averaging the entire posterior.  
**Break condition:** If the posterior is highly multi‑modal with no single dominant mode, using only 1–2 samples may fail to cover diversity; more samples might be needed, but the paper’s ablation suggests this is not the case for their datasets.

## Foundational Learning
### Stochastic Weight Averaging (SWA)
**Why needed:** SWA provides the foundation for SWAG by showing that averaging weights along the optimisation trajectory converges to a flat minimum, which generalises better. SWAG extends this to a Gaussian approximation.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
The system consists of five interconnected components:
1. **CTGAN Generator (G):** Takes noise \(z\) and optional condition, outputs synthetic rows via fully connected layers with mode‑specific normalisation for categoricals.
2. **CTGAN Discriminator (D):** Paired network with PacGAN, distinguishes real vs. fake batches.
3. **SWAG Module:** Maintains \(θ_G\) (mean), \(θ_G^2\) (mean squared), and \(bD\) (deviation matrix buffer of size \(K\)). Updated after each generator weight update when training epoch \(> t_{\text{collect}}\).
4. **Synthesis Loop:** Samples \(z\), draws \(\θ_G \sim\) posterior, runs forward pass through \(G\) with sampled weights, optionally averages over \(S\) samples per row.
5. **Evaluation Module:** Computes utility (ROC, CIO) and risk (TCAP→R) metrics to select the best hyperparameter combination via a selection score \(SS\).

Connectivity: Training: \(G \leftrightarrow D\); after \(t_{\text{collect}}\) SWAG updates from \(G\) weights. Post‑training: SWAG → posterior distribution. Synthesis: \(\θ_G \sim\) posterior ∧ \(z\) → \(G\) → synthetic data → Evaluation → selection score.

### Critical path
1. Standard CTGAN training (loss update for \(G\) and \(D\)).  
2. When \(t > t_{\text{collect}}\), update SWA statistics after each \(G\) weight update.  
3. After training completes, compute diagonal and low‑rank covariance from accumulated statistics.  
4. For synthesis, draw weight samples from the posterior and generate synthetic rows.  
5. Evaluate on utility/risk; choose hyperparameters with highest \(SS\).

### Design tradeoffs
- **Covariance rank \(K\):** Higher (100–150) → better utility (captures correlations) but ↑ memory (buffer \(bD\) size) and compute (matrix ops). Lower (diagonal) → faster, less utility.
- **Posterior samples \(S\):** \(S=1\) (or 2) preferred → higher utility, lower risk. \(S>2\) → utility drops, risk may rise (over‑averaging).
- **Loss function:** Wasserstein generally outperforms vanilla loss for stability and utility across ranks.

### Failure signatures
- **High utility variance across seeds:** Indicates unstable SWAG posterior; check if \(t_{\text{collect}}\) is too early or learning rate unsuitable.
- **Synthetic marginal distributions skewed:** Likely \(K\) too low (diagonal) or \(\α_G\) mis‑scaled.
- **Pareto front dominated by CTGAN, not GACTGAN:** SWAG failed to capture posterior; verify \(t_{\text{collect}}\) timing and that discriminator training remained stable (no GAN mode collapse).

### First 3 experiments

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Can the computational overhead of high‑rank SWAG in GACTGAN be reduced without compromising its utility‑risk improvements?**  
*Basis:* The authors acknowledge that higher ranks (100–150) yield best utility but are computationally expensive. They call for more efficient approximations.  
*Why unresolved:* No mitigation method is proposed or evaluated; this is a barrier to practical adoption.

### Open Question 2
**Do the observed advantages of SWAG‑based Bayesian posterior approximation generalise to other tabular generative architectures beyond CTGAN?**  
*Basis:* The authors admit evaluation is “constrained to the CTGAN architecture” and speculate benefits might be larger in more powerful models.  
*Why unresolved:* No empirical validation on other architectures (e.g., TabDDPM, TVAE, flow‑based models) is provided.

### Open Question 3
**What is the precise mechanism by which using multiple posterior weight samples during synthesis degrades utility and increases risk after the first one or two samples?**  
*Basis:* Ablation (Figure 5) shows an “inverse diagonal trend”; the authors hypothesise averaging dilutes distinct high‑quality modes but offer no detailed analysis.  
*Why unresolved:* Understanding this counter‑intuitive phenomenon is critical for principled Bayesian synthesis but remains a theoretical and empirical gap.

### Open Question 4
**Why does the Wasserstein GAN loss interact more favorably with SWAG than vanilla loss?**  
*Basis:* Results consistently show Wasserstein loss yields higher utility. The authors suggest a smoother landscape helps SWAG construct an accurate posterior but call this finding “nuanced” and needing further study.  
*Why unresolved:* The interaction between adversarial loss formulation and Bayesian posterior quality is not theoretically explored.

### Open Question 5
**Can dataset‑dependent hyperparameter tuning (covariance rank \(K\), scale \(\α_G\), utility weight \(\phi\)) be automated or replaced by fixed defaults?**  
*Basis:* The authors state the optimal configuration appears dataset‑dependent and that this diminishes the “out‑of‑the‑box” advantage.  
*Why unresolved:* A method for automatic or robust default configuration is needed for practical deployment but is not provided.

## Limitations
- Exact epoch \(t_{\text{collect}}\) for SWAG statistics is not specified; its choice could affect posterior quality and needs empirical guesswork.
- Evaluation metric definitions (e.g., CIO, TCAP truncation) are not fully described, risking minor deviations in reproduction.
- Experiments are limited to seven census/social datasets; generalisation to other tabular domains (e.g., healthcare, finance) remains uncertain.
- The Gaussian approximation may be inadequate if the true posterior is highly non‑Gaussian or multi‑modal.
