# Large Language Models Reproduce Racial Stereotypes When Used for Text Annotation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.13891
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13891
- **Reference count:** 30
- **Primary result:** Zero-shot LLM annotation systematically reproduces racial stereotypes: minority-associated names are rated less favorable on traits like aggression, self-discipline, and confidence; AAVE texts are judged less professional and more toxic; a rare exception is favorable hireability ratings for minority names, suggesting overcorrection on that single dimension.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates racial bias in large language model (LLM) based text annotation. Across 19 state-of-the-art models and over four million annotation judgments, the authors examine how identity cues—racially associated names and dialect—influence model outputs. In the names experiment, vignettes with Black, Asian, Hispanic, and Arab names trigger stereotype-consistent evaluations (e.g., Black names seen as more aggressive, Asian names as intelligent but unconfident). In the dialect experiment, texts written in African American Vernacular English (AAVE) are consistently rated as less professional, less educated, and more angry or toxic compared to Standard American English equivalents. Notably, all models favor minority names on hireability, indicating a potential overcorrection on a highly visible bias channel while broader stereotypes remain intact. The findings highlight the risk of deploying LLMs for annotation or decision-making without robust bias mitigation.

## Method Summary
The study constructs two stimulus sets. First, 200 racially distinctive names (40 per group: Arab, Asian, Black, Hispanic, White) are selected from U.S. voter data using embedding-based distinctiveness scoring and embedded into 211 neutral biographical vignettes. Second, 35 short texts are transformed into African American Vernacular English (AAVE) using systematic grammatical rules, yielding matched SAE/AAVE pairs. Nineteen instruction-tuned LLMs from multiple providers are queried via zero-shot binary-choice prompts (e.g., "Is the person aggressive?") for each of 39 annotation tasks (names) and 16 tasks (dialect). Models return structured JSON responses at temperature 0. The primary outcome is the within-pair annotation gap: for names, the difference in yes-rates between minority and White names; for dialect, the difference between AAVE and SAE. Statistical significance is assessed with one-sample t-tests (names) or paired t-tests (dialect) and Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction across all tests. The analysis encompasses over 4 million individual judgments.

## Key Results
- **Names:** For Black names, 18/19 models rate them as more aggressive (+0.012 mean gap) and gossipy (+0.019). Asian names show a "bamboo ceiling" profile: 17–18 models judge them as more intelligent (+0.043) but less confident (-0.036). All four minority groups are uniformly penalized on self-discipline (up to 19/19 models).
- **Dialect:** All 19 models evaluate AAVE texts as significantly less professional (-0.774), less educated (-0.688), more angry (+0.153), and more toxic (+0.091) than equivalent SAE texts.
- **Exception:** For the hireability task, every model systematically favors Arab and Black applicants, indicating overcorrection on this dimension despite persistent stereotypes on other traits.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
Zero-shot LLM annotation reproduces stereotype-consistent judgments when identity cues (racially associated names) appear in text. Models infer social group membership from names and retrieve latent stereotype structures from training data, applying them to annotation tasks. These structures are multidimensional (e.g., "bamboo ceiling" for Asian names, aggression for Black names) and survive instruction tuning. Core assumption: training data encodes societal stereotypes, and name cues suffice to activate them during zero-shot classification. Evidence includes the abstract’s report of systematic gaps across 18–19 models and section 1’s claim that subtle identity cues bias outcomes. Break condition: training data is free of US-centric racial stereotypes or instruction tuning completely removes group-based associations.

### Mechanism 2
Dialect (AAVE vs. SAE) triggers near-universal negative evaluation across all models, independent of propositional content. Models treat AAVE as a signal of lower professionalism, education, and higher negative emotionality or toxicity. This reflects a learned devaluation of non-prestige dialects encoded during pre-training, where AAVE is disproportionately linked to negative contexts. Core assumption: pre-training corpora systematically associate AAVE with negative social judgments, which transfer to zero-shot annotation regardless of the prompt. Evidence: abstract shows AAVE texts judged less professional, less educated, more angry, and more toxic; section 1 notes that AAVE is rule-governed but the gap reflects devaluation encoded at scale. Break condition: pre-training data balances portrayals of AAVE and SAE across all domains, or models are explicitly fine-tuned to neutralize dialect as an identity cue.

### Mechanism 3
Overcorrection on a single, highly visible dimension (name-based hireability) can coexist with robust stereotype reproduction elsewhere, creating a false "bias-free" signal. Instruction tuning applies corrective pressure primarily on easily auditable dimensions like hiring discrimination, leading to systematic reversal (favoring minority names) while leaving less-legible stereotype structures (character, self-discipline, dialect penalties) intact. Core assumption: bias mitigation during RLHF/instruction tuning is targeted and asymmetric, focusing on prototypical blatant discrimination scenarios rather than the full stereotype architecture. Evidence: abstract notes the exception favoring minority names on hireability while broader stereotypes persist; section 1 hypothesizes that instruction tuning concentrates corrective pressure on the most visible channel. Break condition: bias auditing and mitigation are systematically applied to all annotation tasks and both identity cues, not just the prototypical hireability task.

## Foundational Learning
- **Zero-shot text annotation with LLMs** – Why needed: the entire methodology relies on using instruction-tuned LLMs to label text without task-specific fine-tuning; understanding this paradigm shows how biases manifest in a general-purpose tool. Quick check: What is the fundamental difference between using an LLM for zero-shot annotation versus a traditional supervised classifier trained on a labeled dataset?
- **Stereotype embodiment in neural language models** – Why needed: the core finding is that LLMs reproduce specific, multidimensional stereotype profiles (e.g., bamboo ceiling, character penalties); this requires understanding how cultural knowledge is stored and activated in model parameters. Quick check: How might a model’s learned representation for “Asian name” simultaneously associate with “intelligent” and “unconfident”?
- **False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction in high-dimensional testing** – Why needed: the study conducts thousands of statistical tests (19 models × 39 tasks × 4 groups); FDR correction is essential to distinguish true signal from noise. Quick check: Why is using a standard p<0.05 threshold across 2,964 tests problematic, and what does FDR correction do instead?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Name Distinction Pipeline → Vignette Engine → Dialect Transformer → Annotation Dispatcher → Response Parser & Gap Calculator → Statistical Engine

**Critical path:** Name selection → Vignette/dialect text generation → API annotation → Gap computation & testing. Failures in name distinctiveness or dialect transformation validity break the experiment.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Name distinctiveness vs. ecological validity*: Highly distinct names maximize signal but may not reflect real-world ambiguity.  
- *Binary tasks vs. richness*: Forced-choice prompts simplify analysis but may obscure nuance.  
- *Cross-model comparability*: Using a unified API routing service standardizes access but may introduce latency/rate-limit biases.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High model-specific refusal rates on negative-attribute tasks (e.g., Claude Haiku 4.5, Qwen2.5-7B, GLM-4.5-Air).  
- Inconsistent directional gaps across models for a given group/task (fewer than 12/19 models agreeing).  
- Non-parsed responses exceeding a small threshold (<2% for high-completion models), indicating prompt formatting issues.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Name Distinctiveness Validation**: Compute embedding similarities for a random subset of selected names; confirm mean inter-group similarity is lower than intra-group similarity. Manually audit 10 names per group for common cultural associations.  
2. **Dialect Transformation Pilot**: Have a linguist fluent in AAVE review 10 transformed texts to ensure propositional identity and grammatical accuracy of AAVE features.  
3. **Annotation Task Inter-Rater Reliability**: For 3 models and 5 tasks, run 500 duplicate text pairs and compute model self-consistency (should be near 100% at temp=0).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. To what extent do these racial stereotype patterns generalize to non-U.S. cultural contexts? The study uses U.S.-coded identity signals; it is unknown whether similar biases appear with other ethnic naming conventions or dialects globally. Replicating with diverse cultural contexts would resolve this.
2. Why does instruction tuning overcorrect on name-based hireability while leaving other stereotype-consistent character attributions and self-discipline penalties largely unaffected? The paper proposes that hireability is a highly visible channel, but the exact mechanism of selective overcorrection is untested. Auditing instruction tuning datasets or performing ablation studies could resolve this.
3. What explains the universal penalty on self-discipline across all four minority groups? The paper notes this as the most consistently penalized attribute but does not investigate its cause. Analyzing co-occurrence patterns in training corpora or designing controlled text manipulations could identify the source.

## Limitations
- **Reproducibility gaps:** Critical methodological details (exact prompts, precise AAVE rule set, full vignette corpus, anchor phrases) are missing, forcing reconstruction that may introduce noise.
- **Scope and generalizability:** Findings are limited to U.S.-centric stereotypes and zero-shot annotation with instruction-tuned models; the overcorrection pattern may be specific to the tasks and RLHF objectives used.
- **Stimulus validity:** The name distinctiveness pipeline and AAVE transformations rely on embedding heuristics and rule-based approximations; ecological validity of highly distinct names and transformed texts for measuring stereotype activation is uncertain.

## Confidence
- **Name-based stereotype reproduction:** High  
- **AAVE dialect penalty:** High  
- **Selective overcorrection on hireability:** Medium

## Next Checks
1. **Prompt sensitivity analysis:** Re-run a subset of tasks with systematically varied prompt phrasings (e.g., “Is the person aggressive?” vs. “Rate aggressiveness”) to quantify robustness of effect sizes.
2. **Linguistic validation of AAVE corpus:** Engage AAVE linguists to evaluate transformed texts for grammatical accuracy, semantic equivalence, and naturalness, confirming stimulus independence.
3. **Fine-tuning mitigation test:** Instruction-tune a base model (e.g., Llama 3) on a balanced dataset explicitly counter-stereotyping both name-based and dialect-based judgments across all attributes; test if all gaps are eliminated.
