# FMBench: Adaptive Large Language Model Output Formatting

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.06384
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06384
- **Reference count:** 8
- **Primary result:** A two-stage post-training pipeline (SFT + GRPO) improves semantic fidelity and structural compliance in LLM Markdown outputs, with larger models (e.g., 8B) balancing the trade-off best.

## Executive Summary
FMBench addresses the problem of Markdown formatting errors in large language model outputs that hinder usability even when the semantic content is correct. The paper introduces the FMBench benchmark containing 1,100 instruction-response pairs with diverse Markdown structures, and proposes an alignment pipeline combining supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and group-relative policy optimization (GRPO). The approach balances semantic preservation and structural compliance by using a composite reward that combines BERTScore-based semantic similarity with a summarization-based structural score. Evaluations on OpenPangu (1B, 7B) and Qwen3 (1.7B, 8B) models show that SFT alone significantly improves semantic scores, while adding GRPO further enhances structure with minimal semantic trade-off, particularly in larger models. The study reveals an inherent tension between semantic and structural objectives, underscoring the importance of careful reward design and model capacity.

## Method Summary
The task is to generate Markdown documents that faithfully preserve the semantic content of a reference while adhering to specified structural constraints (e.g., sections, lists, tables). The authors construct FMBench, a dataset of 1,100 examples split into 800 training and 300 test items. Evaluation uses two automated metrics: Semantic Score (BERTScore-F1 between model output and reference, using a Longformer encoder) and Structure Score (BERTScore between abstractive summaries of output and reference, computed with a frozen language model). Training proceeds in two stages: first, supervised fine-tuning with LoRA (rank 8, alpha 64) for one epoch using a completion-only loss on assistant tokens; second, reinforcement learning with GRPO initialized from the SFT checkpoint. GRPO samples four candidate completions per prompt and optimizes a composite reward r = λ₁·rₛₑₘ + λ₂·rₛₜᵣᵤ꜀ₜ, with λ₁=λ₂=1, using group-relative comparisons. The pipeline is applied to OpenPangu-1B/7B and Qwen3-1.7B/8B models.

## Key Results
- SFT consistently raises semantic alignment; for Qwen3-8B the Semantic Score increases from 0.9347 to 0.9507.
- Adding GRPO to SFT yields structural gains; for Qwen3-8B the Structure Score improves from 0.9700 to 0.9708, with virtually no semantic loss.
- Larger models (7B/8B) manage both objectives well, while smaller models (1B/1.7B) exhibit a slight semantic trade-off when applying GRPO, indicating a capacity-dependent balance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Supervised fine‑tuning (SFT) on instruction‑response pairs significantly improves semantic similarity between generated outputs and reference texts.
- **Mechanism:** By minimizing cross‑entropy loss on target Markdown documents, the model learns to preserve the original content while adapting to formatting instructions. LoRA fine‑tuning updates only query and value projections, which is sufficient for semantic alignment.
- **Core assumption:** The training data contains high‑quality examples where content preservation and basic structural correctness co‑occur, allowing the model to separate semantic content from formatting details.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: “SFT consistently improves semantic alignment.”
  - [section]: Section 4.2 describes the SFT setup; Table 2 shows semantic gains after SFT across all models.
  - [corpus]: Neighboring papers (e.g., “Does Prompt Formatting Have Any Impact on LLM Performance?”) underscore that semantic fidelity is a prerequisite for useful outputs, but they do not directly validate SFT’s effect on Markdown formatting.
- **Break condition:** If reference texts are noisy or the model capacity is insufficient, SFT might overfit to surface patterns without true semantic understanding, leading to degraded generalization on unseen instructions.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Reinforcement learning with group‑relative policy optimization (GRPO), applied after SFT, enhances adherence to Markdown structural constraints.
- **Mechanism:** GRPO samples multiple completions per prompt and updates the policy based on relative rewards that combine semantic and structural scores. This encourages outputs that are both semantically faithful and well‑structured, relative to the model’s own baseline generations.
- **Core assumption:** The structural reward (rₛₜᵣᵤ꜀ₜ), computed by summarizing both generated and reference texts then measuring BERTScore, captures high‑level organizational similarity and correlates with human‑preferred formatting.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: “SFT+GRPO enhances structural compliance (e.g., Qwen3‑8B: 0.9700→0.9708) with minimal semantic trade‑off.”
  - [section]: Section 4.3 defines the composite reward; Section 4.5 ablation shows GRPO adds structural gains, especially for larger models.
  - [corpus]: Works such as “ReFF: Reinforcing Format Faithfulness” demonstrate that programmatic validators can improve format compliance, supporting the feasibility of RL with verifiable rewards, though FMBench uses a different reward design.
- **Break condition:** If the structural reward weight dominates or is mis‑calibrated, GRPO may push the model toward rigidly formatted outputs that sacrifice semantic correctness, particularly in small models.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A carefully balanced composite reward design mitigates the inherent tension between semantic fidelity and structural compliance, enabling joint optimization.
- **Mechanism:** The reward r = λ₁·rₛₑₘ + λ₂·rₛₜᵣᵤ꜀ₜ with λ₁=λ₂=1 provides a steering signal that rewards Pareto improvements. The summarization‑based rₛₜᵣᵤ꜀ₜ is coarse yet robust to lexical variation, reducing reward sparsity.
- **Core assumption:** The trade‑off between content and format is fundamental; however, with sufficient model capacity and appropriate reward shaping, both objectives can be advanced together.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: “Our results also reveal an inherent trade‑off between semantic and structural objectives, highlighting the importance of carefully designed rewards.”
  - [section]: Section 4.5 shows that for larger models (Qwen3‑8B) SFT+GRPO improves both metrics, while smaller models show a slight semantic trade‑off, confirming the capacity dependence.
  - [corpus]: The corpus includes “Checklists are better than reward models” suggesting alternative reward designs; FMBench’s approach aligns with the broader move toward verifiable rewards but uses a similarity‑based structural signal.
- **Break condition:** If the reward components are mismatched to the target format or model capacity, training may converge to a suboptimal equilibrium where one objective is severely compromised.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Supervised Fine‑Tuning (SFT)**  
  - **Why needed here:** SFT establishes a base policy that already captures semantic alignment and basic formatting patterns from demonstration data. Without this stable foundation, RL from a pretrained model often struggles to balance both objectives.  
  - **Quick check question:** In the paper, what LoRA rank and scaling factor were used for SFT, and why might those settings be chosen for efficiency?
- **Concept: Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**  
  - **Why needed here:** GRPO performs reinforcement learning using group-wise relative comparisons, which is more sample-efficient and less sensitive to absolute reward scales than standard PPO. This makes it suitable for combining multiple objectives in formatting tasks.  
  - **Quick check question:** How does GRPO differ from vanilla PPO, and what is the rationale for sampling four parallel candidates per prompt?
- **Concept: BERTScore for semantic evaluation**  
  - **Why needed here:** BERTScore‑F1 is used as the semantic reward signal; it measures contextual similarity beyond surface n‑gram overlap, which is critical for assessing content preservation in long, structured documents.  
  - **Quick check question:** Why did the authors choose a Longformer‑based encoder instead of a standard BERT model for computing BERTScore on long Markdown documents?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
1. **FMBench dataset** – 1,100 instruction‑response pairs with Markdown references, structural specs, and metadata.  
2. **SFT module** – LoRA‑based fine‑tuning (rank 8, α = 64) on the base model using the training split.  
3. **RL module** – GRPO algorithm that samples 4 completions per prompt, computes rewards, and updates the policy.  
4. **Reward computation unit** – Calculates rₛₑₘ via Longformer‑based BERTScore and rₛₜᵣᵤ꜀ₜ by generating summaries (frozen LM) then BERTScore.  
5. **Evaluation suite** – Reports Semantic Score (BERTScore‑F1) and Structure Score (same metric on summaries) on the test set.  

**Data flow:** FMBench → SFT → SFT checkpoint → RL → Final model

### Critical Path
1. Obtain FMBench and split 800/300.  
2. Run SFT for 1 epoch with AdamW (lr = 2e⁻⁵) and LoRA on attention Q/V.  
3. Configure GRPO with λ₁=λ₂=1, 4 candidates per prompt.  
4. Initialize GRPO from the SFT checkpoint (not from pretrained).  
5. Train until convergence on the training set (paper does not specify exact steps but uses full epoch equivalent).  
6. Evaluate on test set using both metrics and compare against baselines (pretrained, SFT‑only, GRPO‑only).

### Design Tradeoffs
- **SFT vs GRPO:** SFT boosts semantics but leaves structure largely unchanged; GRPO adds structure but can slightly harm semantics in small models.
- **Reward weighting:** Equal λ works well for larger models; smaller models may need λₛₜᵣᵤ꜀ₜ reduction to avoid semantic drop.
- **Model scale:** 7B/8B models balance both objectives; 1B/1.7B models show more tension.
- **Structural reward form:** Summarization‑based score is flexible but adds dependency on a frozen LM; validator‑based checks might be more precise but less generalizable.
- **Computation:** GRPO increases training time (4× samples); LoRA reduces parameter overhead but still requires NPU resources.

### Failure Signatures
- **Semantic degradation after GRPO:** Structural reward too strong → outputs become formulaic but lack content.
- **Unstable RL:** Poor reward scaling or missing KL control → loss spikes, no improvement.
- **Overfitting to FMBench:** High test scores but poor robustness to unseen Markdown styles; detect by evaluating on out‑of‑distribution prompts.
- **Reward hacking:** Model might generate summaries that maximize BERTScore without genuine structural quality (not observed in paper but a risk).

### First 3 Experiments
1. **SFT‑only:** Train with SFT and evaluate. Expect: semantic ↑, structure ≈ baseline. Validates SFT’s semantic effect.
2. **GRPO‑from‑pretrained:** Apply GRPO directly to base model. Expect: poor semantics, modest structure gains. Confirms SFT is needed as stable starting point.
3. **Full pipeline (SFT+GRPO):** Combine SFT and GRPO as described. Expect: both metrics improve relative to pretrained (especially in larger models). Demonstrates synergy and the importance of initialization order.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can we fundamentally resolve the inherent tension between semantic fidelity and structural compliance in formatted generation, beyond simple reward weighting?
- **Basis:** The authors reveal an "inherent trade-off between semantic and structural objectives" and note that naively increasing structural reward weight can lead to over-regularized outputs that distort content.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper shows SFT improves semantics while GRPO improves structure, but the trade-off persists, especially in smaller models, indicating a deeper conflict in optimization objectives that is not solved by their composite reward.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic ablations varying the λ₁/λ₂ ratio across diverse model scales, combined with analyses of failure modes (e.g., content omission, template-like outputs) under different reward pressures.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How can reinforcement rewards for formatting be designed to be both verifiable via automatic checks and flexible enough to generalize across heterogeneous, user-specified layout constraints?
- **Basis:** The authors state that existing approaches rely on "task-specific reward engineering or rigid rule definitions, which may limit adaptability," and propose "validator-grounded and instruction-conditioned rewards" as a promising direction.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper's structural reward uses abstractive summarization BERTScore, which may miss fine-grained validator-ground truth (e.g., whitespace-sensitive errors) and is not explicitly conditioned on the varying layout specifications in FMBench instructions.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** New reward functions that integrate executable validators (e.g., Markdown linting rules) with instruction-aware scoring, evaluated for cross-format generalization (e.g., to tables vs. lists) and robustness to unseen specification variants.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How can evaluation metrics for formatting reliably capture the validity of multiple diverse but correct output realizations, given that real-world formatting often lacks a single canonical reference?
- **Basis:** The authors note their evaluation is "reference-based" and acknowledge "there may exist multiple equally acceptable Markdown realizations" that a single target cannot capture, calling this a limitation.
- **Why unresolved:** FMBench uses a single reference per prompt, so metrics like BERTScore and structural reward may penalize semantically equivalent but structurally divergent outputs that still adhere to user constraints, confounding true format compliance with reference matching.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Development of multi-reference benchmarks or parser-based metrics that check constraint satisfaction (e.g., valid nesting, heading consistency) independent of a single reference, correlated with human judgments of acceptability.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** To what extent do formatting alignment gains on Markdown transfer to other structured output formats (e.g., JSON, YAML, hybrid schemas) with different syntactic and semantic constraints?
- **Basis:** The authors list "Beyond Markdown" as a future direction, specifically mentioning expansion to "other structured outputs commonly used in tool calls (e.g., JSON/YAML), as well as hybrid settings."
- **Why unresolved:** Markdown’s hybrid prose-structure nature and soft conventions differ fundamentally from rigid data formats; the SFT+GRPO pipeline’s effectiveness on Markdown may not directly transfer due to different error modes and reward design needs.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Cross-format experiments applying the same pipeline to benchmarks for JSON generation or code-with-documentation tasks, measuring transfer in semantic-structural trade-offs and required reward adaptations.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Can hybrid approaches combining post-training with inference-time constrained decoding achieve better human-preferred formatting than either alone, without sacrificing fluency or introducing prohibitive overhead?
- **Basis:** The authors propose "Hybrid constraint + learning" as a future direction, suggesting combining post-training with "lightweight inference-time validation or constrained decoding to guarantee syntactic validity while preserving flexible, human-preferred organization."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper avoids hard decoding constraints due to overhead and potential fluency issues, but it is unknown if a calibrated hybrid (e.g., constraint-guided beam search) can resolve residual errors post-RLFT while maintaining generation quality, especially for long, multi-section documents.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Comparative studies implementing hybrid systems (e.g., grammar-constrained decoding initialized from RLFT models) versus pure RLFT on FMBench, measuring structural accuracy, semantic preservation, and inference latency trade-offs.

## Limitations
- Structural reward details (identity and prompting of the frozen LM used for r_struct) are omitted, hindering exact replication.
- The validator rules underlying the "versioned validator identifier" are not documented, making the structural scoring process opaque.
- GRPO hyperparameters (number of update steps, KL penalty coefficient, convergence criteria) are not specified, requiring ad‑hoc choices for reproduction.

## Confidence
- **SFT improves semantic fidelity:** High
- **GRPO enhances structural compliance:** Medium
- **Composite reward balances trade-offs:** Medium
- **FMBench benchmark utility:** Medium
