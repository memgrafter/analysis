# When to ASK: Uncertainty-Gated Language Assistance for Reinforcement Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2604.02226
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02226
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: ASK enables robust downward generalization in FrozenLake, achieving 0.95±0.22 reward on 4x4 maps with 32B+ LMs, while PPO and LM alone yield near-zero.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces Adaptive Safety through Knowledge (ASK), a method that combines a pre-trained reinforcement learning (RL) policy with a large language model (LM) to improve out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization. ASK uses Monte Carlo Dropout to estimate the policy's uncertainty and only queries the LM when uncertainty exceeds a threshold. Experiments on FrozenLake show that while ASK does not improve in-domain performance, it dramatically enhances generalization to smaller grid sizes (downward transfer) when using LMs with 32B+ parameters. Smaller LMs (3B–14B) degrade performance due to high overwrite rates, indicating a capability threshold for effective neuro-symbolic integration. The work demonstrates that selective uncertainty-gated intervention can rescue RL agents in OOD states, but requires sufficiently capable LMs.

## Method Summary
ASK integrates a frozen PPO policy with a Qwen2.5 LM without retraining either component. At each step, the policy's uncertainty is estimated via Monte Carlo Dropout (100 forward passes with dropout active), computing total uncertainty as the sum of epistemic (variance of action distributions) and aleatoric (entropy of mean prediction) components. If this total exceeds a pre-determined threshold τ, the current state is translated into a structured natural language prompt (including agent position, goal, immediate neighbors, and one-step look-ahead tiles) and sent to the LM. The LM's action override replaces the policy's action only if a valid direction is returned; otherwise the policy's action is used. Thresholds are tuned per LM size on an OOD validation set using Bayesian optimization. This selective gating prevents the LM from interfering in familiar states while providing rescue in high-uncertainty OOD states.

## Key Results
- ASK with 32B+ LMs achieves 0.95±0.22 mean reward on downward generalization (8x8 train → 4x4 test), while PPO alone and LM-only baselines yield near-zero.
- No in-domain improvement over PPO is observed for any LM size, highlighting that the benefit is specific to OOD scenarios.
- Mid-size LMs (3B–14B) cause performance degradation due to high overwrite rates, revealing a sharp capability threshold around 32B parameters for reliable spatial reasoning.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Uncertainty-gated LM intervention prevents degradation from overconfident LM suggestions in familiar states while providing rescue in high-uncertainty OOD states.
- **Mechanism:** MC Dropout computes total uncertainty (epistemic + aleatoric). When below threshold τ, the agent follows the RL policy. When above τ, the LM's action overrides the policy's. This selective gating ensures the LM is only consulted when the RL policy's knowledge is insufficient (high epistemic uncertainty) or the state is inherently ambiguous (high aleatoric uncertainty).
- **Core assumption:** High total uncertainty reliably correlates with states where the RL policy's learned actions are likely to fail, and where the LM's world knowledge can provide a superior alternative.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "queries the LM only when uncertainty, estimated via Monte Carlo Dropout, exceeds a threshold."
  - [section III.B] "By using uncertainty as the intervention criterion, ASK ensures that the LM is queried only when necessary."
  - [corpus] Neighbors like "Uncertainty-Gated Generative Modeling" and "Ask a Strong LLM Judge when Your Reward Model is Uncertain" support the general paradigm of using uncertainty to gate external model queries, though direct evidence for ASK's specific mechanism is from the paper itself.
- **Break condition:** If uncertainty estimates are miscalibrated (e.g., low uncertainty in OOD states or high uncertainty in-distribution), the gating mechanism will query the LM unnecessarily or fail to query when needed, leading to inefficiency or performance loss.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Effective neuro-symbolic integration via ASK requires a capability threshold in the LM (≥32B parameters), below which the LM's reasoning is too unreliable for the task.
- **Mechanism:** The LM must understand the structured prompt (state description, neighbors, look-ahead) and perform spatial reasoning to select safe, goal-directed actions. Sufficient model scale is hypothesized to be necessary for this robust parsing and reasoning. Below this scale, the LM's suggestions are frequently incorrect, and even when gated by uncertainty, the high overwrite rate (OR) degrades the otherwise-competent PPO policy.
- **Core assumption:** Spatial navigation and safety judgment in a grid-world with sparse rewards require a minimum level of semantic comprehension and multi-step reasoning that emerges only in very large LMs.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "effective neuro-symbolic integration requires sufficient model scale."
  - [section IV.B] "A sharp capability threshold emerges at 32B parameters... This performance transition at 32B reflects a capability threshold for reliable spatial reasoning under distribution shift."
  - [section IV.A] "Mid-size models (3B–14B) exhibit a failure mode characterized by low call rates coupled with high overwrite percentages."
- **Break condition:** If the LM scale is below the capability threshold, its guidance is net harmful when it overrides the policy (high OR), and the gating mechanism's threshold τ must be set very high to suppress these interventions, essentially disabling the LM's contribution.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The hybrid system (PPO + gated LM) achieves robust *downward* generalization (trained on 8x8, tested on smaller grids) not because either component succeeds alone, but because the LM provides critical "semantic critique" that corrects the RL policy's OOD failures in contexts with different hole layouts.
- **Mechanism:** The PPO policy trained on 8x8 has never seen the specific hole configurations of the smaller test maps (different contexts in the CMDP formulation). In these OOD states, its policy becomes highly uncertain, triggering the LM. The large LM (32B+), when prompted with the local state, can reason about safe tile types and goal direction, often overriding the PPO policy's dangerous moves (toward holes) with safe, albeit sometimes suboptimal, navigation. This synergy is only revealed under significant distribution shift (different map sizes), as in-domain tasks are already solved optimally by PPO alone.
- **Core assumption:** The LM's knowledge about "holes are bad" and "move toward goal" is transferable and applicable to the novel grid layouts, even though it was not trained on the specific environment.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "no in-domain improvement... but enables robust downward generalization... ASK with 32B+ parameter LMs achieves 0.95±0.22 reward, while PPO and LM alone yield near-zero."
  - [section IV.B] "PPO alone yields zero reward, and LMs alone fail fundamentally. Yet, their integration at scale (32B+) unlocks robust generalization."
  - [section II.A] The Contextual MDP formulation explicitly defines the problem where context (hole layout) changes between episodes, and the policy does not observe the context.
- **Break condition:** If the OOD shift is too severe (e.g., different action semantics, completely new tile types), the LM's commonsense may not apply, or the prompt may not convey sufficient state information, leading to failure even with large LMs.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) & Training Stability**
  - Why needed here: The baseline RL agent is a trained PPO policy. Understanding that PPO optimizes a surrogate objective with clipping and typically produces stable, near-optimal policies in-distribution is crucial. Its failure mode is not poor optimization but lack of experience in OOD contexts.
  - Quick check question: "Why does a PPO policy that achieves ~0.93 reward on a 6x6 training set fail completely on a 4x4 test set with the same dynamics but different hole positions?"
- **Concept: Epistemic vs. Aleatoric Uncertainty & MC Dropout**
  - Why needed here: ASK's gating signal is total uncertainty. Distinguishing reducible (lack of data) from irreducible (inherent noise) uncertainty explains why high uncertainty can indicate novel states (OOD). MC Dropout is the practical approximation used.
  - Quick check question: "If an observation is highly familiar to the policy (seen often during training), what would you expect the epistemic uncertainty (U_e) from MC Dropout to be? What about in a completely new state configuration?"
- **Concept: Prompt Engineering for Small Language Models**
  - Why needed here: The paper emphasizes that prompt design had to be simplified for smaller models vs. larger ones. The specific structure (position, neighbors, look-ahead) is engineered to fit the limited reasoning capacity of mid-size models.
  - Quick check question: "Why does the prompt include 'LOOK AHEAD' tiles (two steps away) instead of just immediate neighbors? What kind of reasoning does this enable the LM to do?"
- **Concept: Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Generalization in RL**
  - Why needed here: The core problem is OOD states within the same MDP family (different map contexts). This is distinct from domain randomisation; it's about generalizing to new instances of the same task specification.
  - Quick check question: "In the FrozenLake CMDP, what constitutes the 'context' c, and how does changing it between training and test create an OOD scenario for the policy?"

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  1. Trained RL Policy (π_θ): A stable-baselines3 PPO agent. Input: observation (numeric grid state). Output: action distribution.
  2. MC Dropout Wrapper: Attached to the policy's neural network. During inference, performs N forward passes with dropout active. Computes U_e (variance of action distributions) and U_a (mean prediction entropy) per Eq 1 & 2.
  3. Uncertainty Gate: Compares U_total = U_e + U_a to a fixed threshold τ. Outputs binary decision: use PPO action or consult LM.
  4. State-to-Prompt Translator: Converts the numeric observation (grid, agent pos, goal pos, tile types) into the structured natural language prompt (Listing 1).
  5. LM Interface (G_φ): Loads a Qwen2.5 model (e.g., 32B). Receives prompt, generates response. Includes parsing logic to extract the {"action":"..."} JSON.
  6. Action Override Logic: If gate says consult and LM returns a valid action from {UP,DOWN,LEFT,RIGHT}, it replaces π_θ(o); else, falls back to π_θ(o).
- **Critical path (inference step):**
  1. Receive observation o from env.
  2. Compute a_ppo = π_θ(o).
  3. Run MC Dropout on o -> compute U_total.
  4. If U_total < τ: Execute a_ppo.
  5. Else: Generate prompt P_r from o; Query LM a_lm = G_φ(P_r); parse action; If valid: a_final = a_lm; Else: a_final = a_ppo; Execute a_final.
  6. Receive next observation, loop.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Threshold τ: Low τ = more LM calls (higher compute cost, potential for more corrections) vs. High τ = fewer calls (cheaper, but may miss rescue opportunities). Optimal τ is model-dependent and found via Bayesian optimization on an OOD validation set.
  - LM Scale: Larger LMs (72B) are more capable but slower/more memory. Smaller LMs (0.5B) are fast but often unreliable, requiring high τ that neuters their contribution.
  - Prompt Complexity: Richer prompts (more look-ahead, precise coordinates) may help large LMs but overload small ones. The design simplified for smaller models, limiting the depth of reasoning prompted.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - High Overwrite Rate (OR %): Especially for mid-size models (3B-14B). The LM frequently disagrees with PPO in high-uncertainty states, but its suggestions are wrong, leading to worse performance (longer episodes, zero reward). This indicates poor LM capability or poor uncertainty-LM-reliability alignment.
  - Near-100% Intervention Rate (IR %) with Zero Reward: For small models (0.5B) on downward generalization. The LM is queried constantly (τ too low) but is so incapable that it destroys performance, even overriding correct PPO moves.
  - Zero or Near-Zero Reward Despite Queries: The LM is being queried (high IR) but its actions are not leading to the goal. Could be due to: LM fundamentally unable to plan sequential steps, or prompt insufficient to convey state, or LM overconfidently heading into holes.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Calibrate Uncertainty Gate: Run trained PPO on a mixed set of in-distribution and OOD validation contexts. Collect uncertainty distribution per model. This informs the search range for τ and shows if uncertainty actually rises in OOD states.
  2. Establish LM-Only Baseline: For each LM size, run the agent controlled solely by the LM (no PPO, no gate). Prompt exactly as in ASK. Expect near-zero reward on both in-domain and OOD splits, confirming LM's lack of autonomous planning.
  3. Same-Size vs. Transfer Performance: Evaluate PPO and ASK (with each LM size) on the same-size test split (e.g., 8x8 train, 8x8 test) and on a downward transfer split (8x8 train, 4x4 test). This isolates the benefit of the gated LM under distribution shift. Hypothesis: ASK shows no gain same-size, large gain in transfer.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How can we learn adaptive gating mechanisms that dynamically adjust the uncertainty threshold based on context? Basis: Future work includes "learning adaptive gating mechanisms beyond fixed thresholds." Why unresolved: Fixed thresholds require manual tuning per model and environment, limiting adaptability.
- **Open Question 2:** How can we improve the calibration of uncertainty estimates from MC Dropout to better align with language model reliability? Basis: Future work includes "improving uncertainty calibration through ensembles or Bayesian methods." Why unresolved: Poor calibration is evident from mid-size models needing high thresholds to avoid degradation.
- **Open Question 3:** Can joint training of the RL policy and the gating mechanism optimize their interaction and reduce dependence on large language models? Basis: Future work includes "exploring joint training of the policy and gate." Why unresolved: ASK currently treats the policy and gate separately; end-to-end training might discover more efficient synergy.
- **Open Question 4:** Does ASK generalize to partially observable or high-dimensional environments, and what architectural modifications are necessary? Basis: Future work includes "extending our approach to more complex and partially observable environments." Why unresolved: Experiments were limited to fully observable, small grid-worlds; realistic domains involve partial observability and high-dimensional observations.

## Limitations
- Prompt uniformity across LM sizes is unclear; the paper mentions simplified prompts for smaller models but only provides one prompt template.
- Dropout placement in the PPO network is unspecified; standard PPO implementations lack dropout, so the method of inserting dropout layers could affect uncertainty calibration.
- Context generation for test maps (e.g., whether smaller grids are cropped from 8x8 or independently generated) is not detailed, impacting the difficulty of transfer.

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (uncertainty-gated intervention): Medium
- Mechanism 2 (scale threshold): Medium
- Mechanism 3 (synergy): High

## Next Checks
1. Uniform prompt test: Run ASK with identical prompts across all LM sizes. A persistent threshold indicates a true scale requirement; a vanished threshold suggests prompt engineering dominated.
2. Uncertainty calibration check: On a validation set with known optimal actions, bin states by uncertainty and measure PPO suboptimality rate. Expect monotonic increase in suboptimality with uncertainty.
3. Ablation of gating: Compare ASK (gated) vs. always-query and never-query for 32B+ LM on OOD test. Gated should be superior, confirming the necessity of selective intervention.
