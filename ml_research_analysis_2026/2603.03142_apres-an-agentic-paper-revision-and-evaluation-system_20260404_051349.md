# APRES: An Agentic Paper Revision and Evaluation System

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.03142  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03142  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** APRES discovers a rubric that predicts future citations better than human reviews and uses it to revise papers, improving citation prediction MAE by 19.6% and achieving 79% human preference for revised papers.

## Executive Summary
APRES is a system that uses large language models (LLMs) to automatically revise scientific papers to improve their impact—as measured by citation counts—without altering core scientific content. It operates in two stages: first, an agentic search process (MultiAIDE) discovers a scoring rubric that is highly predictive of a paper’s future citations; second, the rubric guides an iterative, diff-based revision loop that enhances presentation while preserving experimental results and claims. The method reduces mean absolute error in citation prediction by 19.6% over the best baseline, and human evaluators strongly prefer the revised papers (79% of the time), demonstrating effective improvements in clarity and presentation.

## Method Summary
APRES employs a two-stage pipeline. Stage 1 (Rubric Discovery) uses a MultiAIDE agentic scaffold: a Rubric Proposer LLM generates candidate rubrics (Python dictionaries of evaluation items and scoring guidelines); a Reviewer LLM scores a training set of papers against each rubric; a Regression Evaluator fits a negative binomial model to predict citation counts from rubric scores and computes mean absolute error (MAE) on a validation set; a Search Controller selects and refines rubrics to minimize MAE. Stage 2 (Paper Revision) applies the final rubric iteratively: a Reviewer scores the current paper version; a Rewriter LLM proposes localized edits via diff blocks, constrained to non-results sections (abstract, introduction, etc.); the revised paper is rescored; the highest-scoring version is retained for the next iteration. This closed loop optimizes the rubric-based impact surrogate while safeguarding scientific content.

## Key Results
- The discovered rubric (via MultiAIDE) predicts future citations with a 19.6% lower MAE (~1.96 with Gemini 2.5 Pro) than baselines using human review scores (~5.0) or SPECTER embeddings (~2.65).
- Human evaluators prefer APRES-revised papers over originals in 79% of pairwise comparisons, indicating substantial gains in clarity and presentation.
- The LLM-based evaluation pipeline shows lower intra-model pairwise disagreement rates (e.g., 19.5% between o1 and o3) than historical human committee baselines (23%–26%) and random chance (35.1%), suggesting greater consistency.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Rubric Discovery via Agentic Search
APRES finds a rubric that better predicts citations than human scores or static embeddings by using an iterative propose-score-evaluate-select loop (MultiAIDE). A Rubric Proposer generates or modifies rubric candidates; a Reviewer LLM applies the rubric to paper texts; a Regression Evaluator computes MAE of predicted versus actual citations; the Search Controller retains and refines the rubric minimizing MAE. This automates rubric design, avoiding human bias, and leverages LLM judgment to extract features correlated with impact. Evidence: Section 4.1 reports MAE ~1.96 for the discovered rubric versus ~5.0 for human scores and ~2.65 for SPECTER. Break condition: Stagnant MAE or lack of correlation on hold-out validation.

### Rubric-Guided Iterative Revision
The discovered rubric guides an LLM Rewriter to improve presentation without altering core scientific content. The closed-loop process scores a paper, generates diff-based edits restricted to narrative sections (excluding results, methods, tables), rescoring, and selects the highest-scoring version for further iteration. The rubric provides actionable feedback, and optimizing its score aligns with human-perceived quality. Evidence: Section 4.2 shows increasing average review scores over iterations (ΔS = 3.33 for o3 on borderline papers) and a 79% human preference for revised papers. Break conditions: Revision fails to increase score, rewriter alters results/methods, or diffs become malformed.

### Consistent Automated Evaluation
An LLM-based review pipeline with a fixed rubric yields more stable evaluations than human peer review. The same rubric and scoring agent are applied uniformly; pairwise disagreement is measured via Glicko2 ratings derived from LLM judgments and compared to historical human committee disagreement. The deterministic nature of the LLM process reduces variability across reviewers and submissions. Evidence: Section A.2 shows intra-model disagreement ~19.5%, below the human 2021 baseline (23%) and random baseline (35.1%). Break condition: LLM disagreement exceeds human baseline or approaches random chance.

## Foundational Learning

### Negative Binomial Regression
**Why needed:** Citation counts are non-negative integers with overdispersion (variance > mean). Linear regression violates assumptions; Poisson may be insufficient. Negative binomial regression includes an extra dispersion parameter, standard in bibliometrics for count data.  
**Quick check:** Why would a linear model predicting citations be misleading, and how does the negative binomial distribution’s extra parameter address overdispersion?

### Agentic Search Scaffolds (MultiAIDE)
**Why needed:** The rubric space is vast and non-linear; manual design introduces bias. MultiAIDE automates exploration with branching, selection, and code debugging to find high-performing rubrics efficiently.  
**Quick check:** What is the functional difference between `initial branch factor`, `branch factor`, `pdebug`, and `Dmax` in controlling the search dynamics?

### Diff-Based Editing with Content Constraints
**Why needed:** To ensure revisions improve presentation (clarity, structure) without altering scientific content (results, claims). Full-text regeneration risks hallucination or content loss. Diff format localizes changes, and explicit prompting plus table-exclusion rules enforce the constraint.  
**Quick check:** How does specifying edits as search/replace blocks, rather than full-text generation, help maintain the integrity of experimental results and tables?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Rubric Proposer LLM -> Reviewer LLM -> Regression Evaluator -> Search Controller (MultiAIDE) -> Final Rubric R*. Then: Original Paper + R* -> Reviewer -> Rewriter LLM (diff) -> Reviewer -> Scoring & Selector -> Best Revised Paper.

### Critical path
- **Stage 1 (Rubric Discovery):** Initial Rubric -> [Proposer -> Reviewer -> Regression -> Select/Refine] (loop) -> Final Rubric R*.
- **Stage 2 (Paper Improvement):** Original Paper + R* -> [Reviewer -> Rewriter (diff) -> Reviewer] (loop) -> Best Revised Paper.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Computational cost vs. stability:** More search iterations/branches improve rubrics but increase API cost; `pdebug` and `Dmax` trade off bug-finding exploration for convergence speed.
- **Rubric generalization vs. overfitting:** Training on a specific citation dataset risks overfitting; the choice of citation count as the target may not generalize to other impact metrics or domains.
- **Constraint enforcement:** Prompt-based constraints (“do not change results”) are weaker than formal verification; diff-based editing mitigates but cannot guarantee zero content drift.

### Failure signatures
- **Stagnant MAE:** Search loop fails to reduce MAE after many iterations, suggesting exhausted rubric space or flawed scoring model.
- **Score degradation:** Revision loop yields S_rev < S_ori, indicating harmful changes or non-actionable rubric.
- **Incoherent edits:** Rewriter output diffs are malformed, cut large sections, or create grammatical errors.
- **Content drift:** Diffs modify numbers in result tables, method descriptions, or core claims.
- **Cache incoherence:** Using different rubrics for discovery and revision (not R*) leads to no improvement.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Validation of Rubric Discovery:** Run Stage 1 search on train/validation split; confirm MAE decreases and final rubric scores correlate with citations on test set; compare MAE of R* vs. human scores vs. SPECTER PCA.
2. **Sanity Check of Revision Constraint:** Manually inspect 100 diff outputs; quantify percentage of diffs that only touch narrative text (abstract, intro, related work, discussion) vs. any in results sections, tables, or methods.
3. **Baseline Rubric Comparison:** Run Stage 2 revision using R* vs. a hand-crafted rubric (e.g., clarity, correctness) vs. no rubric; measure ΔS on borderline/reject papers to isolate value of discovered rubric.

## Limitations
- The rubric is optimized for citation prediction, which may not generalize to other impact metrics or scientific domains.
- Prompt-based constraints during revision might fail to prevent alterations to core scientific content; no systematic content integrity audit is provided.
- Human evaluation details (sample size, evaluator expertise) are sparse, limiting confidence in the 79% preference rate.

## Next Checks
