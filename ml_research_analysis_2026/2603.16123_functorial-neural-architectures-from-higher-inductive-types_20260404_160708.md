# Functorial Neural Architectures from Higher Inductive Types

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.16123
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16123
- **Reference count:** 39
- **Primary result:** Compositional generalization requires decoder functoriality; HIT-compiled functorial architectures achieve 2–10× better length generalization on geometric tasks.

## Executive Summary
Neural networks struggle with compositional generalization because their architectures often fail to preserve the algebraic structure of the input. This paper establishes that compositional generalization is mathematically equivalent to the decoder being a monoidal functor from the input algebra (modeled via the fundamental groupoid of a space) to the space of output loops. By compiling Higher Inductive Type (HIT) specifications into neural architectures—with generator networks for paths, structural concatenation for composition, and learned homotopies for 2-cells—the authors guarantee functoriality by construction. Formal proofs in Cubical Agda confirm that the resulting transport decoders are strict monoidal functors, whereas softmax self-attention is inherently non-functorial. Empirically, functorial decoders maintain constant per‑segment error as word length increases, while non‑functorial baselines degrade; on the torus, wedge of circles, and Klein bottle, the functorial approach outperforms by factors of 2 to 10.

## Method Summary
The method converts a HIT specification of a space’s homotopy 1‑type (generators and relations) into a neural decoder that is provably a monoidal functor. Each generator (a path from the basepoint) becomes an independent neural network that outputs a loop segment. Composition of words translates to structural concatenation of the corresponding generator outputs, ensuring that the decoding of a composite input is exactly the combination of its parts. For spaces with non‑trivial relations (2‑cells), an additional learned natural transformation (an MLP) enforces the homotopy, deforming one concatenation into another to satisfy the relation. The correctness of the construction is verified in Cubical Agda, demonstrating that the decoder satisfies functoriality laws. Training uses standard gradient descent with Chamfer loss between generated and ground‑truth point‑cloud loops.

## Key Results
- Torus \(T^2\): Functorial transport decoder achieves per‑segment Chamfer 0.77 vs. 1.54 for a non‑functorial transformer at length 10 (2× gap).
- Wedge of circles \(S^1 \vee S^1\) (free group \(F_2\)): Functorial decoder attains per‑segment Chamfer 0.054 and 100% circle accuracy, while the transformer reaches 0.537 and 14% accuracy (≈10× gap).
- Klein bottle: Adding a learned 2‑cell reduces error by 46% on words that exercise the relation; functorial decoders keep per‑segment error constant with increasing word length, unlike non‑functorial ones.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Compositional generalization as monoidal functoriality
The decoder must satisfy \(D(w_1 \cdot w_2) = D(w_1) \oplus D(w_2)\) for all words, where \(\oplus\) is structural concatenation of independently generated loop segments. This equation defines a monoidal functor from the input monoid/groupoid to the space of parametric loops, guaranteeing that the output for a composite input is exactly the combination of the outputs of its parts, regardless of grouping. Evidence: Abstract and Section 2.2 frame compositional generalization as requiring such a functor.

### Mechanism 2: HIT compilation enforces functoriality by construction
Given a HIT specifying a space, the architecture maps: (i) generator paths → generator networks, (ii) path composition → structural concatenation, (iii) 2‑cells (homotopies) → learned natural transformations (MLPs) that enforce relations. On the free monoid this yields a strict monoidal functor; with 2‑cells it becomes a functor on the group. The construction is formally verified in Cubical Agda. Evidence: Abstract; Construction 3.2; Section 5.3 shows the learned 2‑cell closing a 46% error gap on the Klein bottle.

### Mechanism 3: Softmax self‑attention is inherently non‑functorial
Softmax attention mixes tokens content‑wise; each position’s representation depends on all input tokens via attention weights. For equivalent words \(w_2 \neq w_2'\) with \([w_2]=[w_2']\), the key vectors differ, causing the output for the \(w_1\)-segment in \(D(w_1\cdot w_2)\) to differ from that in \(D(w_1\cdot w_2')\), violating functoriality. Theorem 4.1 proves this obstruction for any non‑trivial compositional task unless attention is strictly masked, which destroys cross‑segment context.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept: Monoidal Functors**  
*Why needed here:* They formalize preservation of compositional structure. The equation \(D(w_1\cdot w_2)=D(w_1)\oplus D(w_2)\) is the categorical definition of compositional behavior.  
*Quick check:* If \(D\) is a monoidal functor and \(w = a\cdot b\cdot a^{-1}\cdot b^{-1}\) (a word representing the identity in a free group), what must \(D(w)\) equal?

**Concept: Higher Inductive Types (HITs)**  
*Why needed here:* They provide a type‑theoretic language to specify spaces by generators (paths) and relations (2‑cells), mirroring group presentations and allowing practitioners to describe compositional structure without deep category theory.  
*Quick check:* For the torus HIT, what does the `surf` 2‑cell enforce about the loops \(a\) and \(b\)?

**Concept: Parametric Maps (Category \(\textbf{Para}\))**  
*Why needed here:* They model neural networks as parameterized morphisms, letting architectural constraints like functoriality be expressed as functors between categories, making them trainable via gradient descent.  
*Quick check:* How is the parameter space composed for two sequential parametric maps \((\Theta_1, f_1)\) and \((\Theta_2, f_2)\)?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**
- HIT basepoint → trivial loop (constant map)
- HIT generator loop → generator network (MLP taking time \(t\) and outputting a point)
- HIT path composition → structural concatenation of generator outputs (list append)
- HIT 2‑cell (homotopy) → learned natural transformation (separate MLP \(H\) with boundary conditions linking concatenated sequences)

**Critical path:**
1. Specify task’s compositional structure as a HIT (e.g., \(T^2\) with generators \(a,b\) and 2‑cell `surf`).
2. Verify in Cubical Agda that the compiled transport decoder satisfies coherence (e.g., \(\operatorname{tr} a \circ \operatorname{tr} b = \operatorname{tr} b \circ \operatorname{tr} a\)).
3. Compile: instantiate generator networks, set up structural concatenation, optionally add 2‑cell MLP.
4. Train with standard gradient descent on examples of short words; functoriality holds for all parameters.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Functorial vs. expressivity:* Functorial (type‑B) decoders constrain outputs to be assembled from generator segments, reducing geometric freedom per homotopy class but guaranteeing compositional generalization. Non‑functorial (type‑A) decoders allow more intra‑class variation but fail on length extrapolation.
- *2‑cell inclusion:* Adding a learned 2‑cell introduces parameters and training complexity but is necessary when relations are non‑trivial. When relations are trivial (abelian) or absent (free), the 2‑cell is dormant or unnecessary.

**Failure signatures:**
- *Type‑A (non‑functorial) failure:* Per‑segment error increases with word length \(L\) beyond training length; on non‑abelian tasks (e.g., \(S^1\vee S^1\)), circle accuracy drops, indicating loss of segment identity.
- *Type‑B (functorial) behavior:* Per‑segment error plateaus quickly and stays constant as \(L\) grows; on non‑abelian tasks, segment identity is perfectly preserved.
- *Missing 2‑cell:* On spaces with non‑trivial relations (Klein bottle), error spikes on words that exercise the relation (non‑canonical order), while canonical words remain low‑error.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Torus \(T^2\) (abelian, 2‑cell trivial):** Tests monoidal composition. Type‑B (transport) achieves 0.77 vs. type‑A (transformer) 1.54 at \(L=10\) (2× gap). Shows structural concatenation alone suffices when relations are commutative.
2. **Wedge of circles \(S^1 \vee S^1\) (free group \(F_2\), no 2‑cell):** Tests non‑abelian amplification. Type‑B (transport) gets 0.054 per‑segment Chamfer and 100% circle accuracy at \(L=10\); type‑A (transformer) gets 0.537 and 14% accuracy (10× gap). Proves functoriality is essential for preserving non‑abelian structure.
3. **Klein bottle \(Z \rtimes Z\) (non‑trivial 2‑cell):** Tests the learned homotopy \(H\). Transport (no \(H\)) gets 1.52 on non‑canonical words; homotopy (with \(H\)) gets 0.82, closing a 46% gap. On canonical words both are ~0.82, confirming \(H\) is only needed when the relation is exercised.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Extension to higher homotopy groups:** How to incorporate \(\pi_2, \pi_3,\dots\) and corresponding higher‑dimensional proof terms? The current construction only addresses \(\pi_1\) and 2‑cells.
2. **Automated compilation:** Can the pipeline from HIT specification to certified neural architecture be fully automated? Requires bridging proof assistants with deep learning code generation and extracting HIT structure into differentiable constraints.
3. **Linguistic applications:** How to formalize linguistic compositional structures (e.g., SCAN, COGS) as HITs, and can functorial architectures achieve systematic generalization on discrete tasks? The current framework is only demonstrated on continuous geometric generation.
4. **More complex \(\pi_1\):** Does the functorial hierarchy (winding, monoidal composition, 2‑cells) generalize to spaces with more complex fundamental groups (e.g., higher‑genus surfaces), and does the learned 2‑cell yield measurable benefits? Only three simple spaces have been tested.

## Limitations
- Experiments are limited to synthetic geometric tasks; it is unclear whether functoriality yields benefits on real‑world compositional challenges (language, molecules).
- The HIT specification must be provided manually; there is no method to learn the compositional structure from data.
- The expressivity of generator networks may limit geometric fidelity, and comparisons with non‑functorial baselines often lack parameter count parity.

## Confidence
- **Functoriality requirement:** Medium (theoretical argument sound for monoidal tasks, but many real‑world structures are not purely monoidal).
- **HIT compilation:** High (construction explicit and formally verified in Cubical Agda).
- **Attention non‑functorial:** High (Theorem 4.1 provides a clear counterexample under standard assumptions).
- **Empirical gaps:** Medium (large consistent gaps on three tasks, but only on synthetic data and a single baseline; some details omitted hinder full verification).

## Next Checks
1. Evaluate functorial decoders on a diverse set of HIT spaces (e.g., real projective plane, lens spaces, higher‑genus surfaces) to test generalizability.
2. Compare against other compositional architectures (neural module networks, tree decoders) to confirm the performance gap is not transformer‑specific.
3. Ablate the learned 2‑cell on spaces with multiple non‑trivial relations to measure its impact and robustness.
