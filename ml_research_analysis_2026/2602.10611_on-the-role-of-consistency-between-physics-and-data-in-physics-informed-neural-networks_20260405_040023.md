# On the Role of Consistency Between Physics and Data in Physics-Informed Neural Networks

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.10611
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10611
- Reference count: 23
- Primary result: Data-to-PDE mismatch imposes a consistency barrier that fundamentally limits PINN accuracy; only high-fidelity data (sub-1e-6 error) can overcome it, while low-fidelity data causes error saturation regardless of PDE enforcement.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates the impact of data-to-PDE inconsistency in physics-informed neural networks (PINNs). Using the 1D viscous Burgers equation with a manufactured solution, it trains PINNs on datasets of varying fidelity (coarse to fine numerical meshes and analytical data). The results reveal a consistency barrier: when training data contain significant discretization error (low fidelity), PINN error saturates at a level comparable to the data error, even though the PDE loss is enforced exactly. Only with high-fidelity data (error ~1e-6) does the PINN achieve comparable accuracy. The PDE loss can partially recover the dominant physics but cannot overcome the barrier, indicating that data fidelity fundamentally constrains PINN performance. The analysis also employs Pareto front techniques to show how data fidelity controls the tension between minimizing data loss and PDE residual.

## Method Summary
The study uses a steady-state Burgers equation with a manufactured analytical solution $u_{\text{MMS}}(x,\nu)$ to generate reference data. Numerical datasets of three fidelities (C1: 81 nodes, C2: 641, C3: 7121) are produced via a second-order finite-difference solver; their discretization errors are quantified. A standard PINN architecture (7 hidden layers, 200 tanh neurons) is employed, with inputs $(x)$ for fixed viscosity and $(x,\nu)$ for parametric cases. The loss combines data mismatch ($L_D$, $L_{BC}$) and PDE residual ($L_{PDE}$) weighted via the lbPINN method (trainable $\sigma$). Training proceeds in three stages: warm-up (data loss only), AdamW (mini-batch), and L-BFGS (full-batch). Performance is evaluated via test RMSE against the analytical solution and via Pareto front analysis comparing fixed vs. dynamic weighting. Key metrics include error saturation levels, loss component trajectories, and spatial error distributions.

## Key Results
- For low-fidelity C1 data (discretization error $\approx 2.81\!\times\!10^{-1}$), PINN test RMSE plateaued at $9.65\!\times\!10^{-2}$, showing error saturation from inconsistency.
- For high-fidelity C3 data (error $\approx 3.51\!\times\!10^{-6}$), PINN achieved RMSE $5.34\!\times\!10^{-6}$, matching data fidelity and showing no barrier.
- The PDE loss partially corrects low-fidelity data by recovering dominant physical structure but cannot overcome the consistency barrier; the error floor persists at a level comparable to the data inconsistency.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Data Inconsistency Propagates as Loss Bias
Low-fidelity data introduce a systematic bias in the PINN loss function (Eq. 7) that creates an irreducible error floor, the consistency barrier. Data label errors $\varepsilon(x)$ from discretization or noise propagate into the data loss term, adding a bias that shifts the optimization target away from the true PDE solution. Even with exact PDE enforcement, minimizing the effective loss cannot recover the true solution because the data term pulls the network toward the inconsistent data. Evidence: For C1, PINN RMSE plateaued at $9.65\!\times\!10^{-2}$, comparable to the data error of $2.81\!\times\!10^{-1}$, indicating saturation. The bias becomes dominant when $|\varepsilon(x)|$ is comparable to the PDE residual.

### Mechanism 2: PDE Loss Provides Partial But Insufficient Correction
The PDE residual term acts as a regularizer that forces the network to satisfy the governing equation globally, correcting high-frequency errors and smoothing inconsistencies. However, the network remains constrained to compromise between inconsistent data anchors and the exact PDE, so the error saturates at a level proportional to the data inconsistency. Evidence: PINN predictions are closer to the true solution than the numerical data, indicating the PDE loss learns physics, but the improvement is not dramatic, highlighting the barrier. Unit/magnitude mismatch between PDE and data losses also limits correction.

### Mechanism 3: Fidelity Determines Pareto Front Geometry and Tension
Data fidelity controls the compatibility between minimizing PDE loss and data loss, as seen in the Pareto front structure. Lower fidelity increases tension, raising the consistency barrier. In multi-objective optimization terms, the two losses become nearly conflicting under low fidelity; the Pareto front elbow shifts to higher loss values as fidelity decreases. Evidence: In C1, the Pareto front saturates on the LD axis, indicating nearly incompatible objectives. As consistency improves, tension reduces; for analytical data ($\varepsilon\!\approx\!0$), the Pareto front collapses toward the origin.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Consistency Barrier
- **Why needed:** It defines the fundamental limit imposed by data-PDE mismatch; without it, practitioners might misattribute poor performance to architecture or optimization.
- **Quick check:** If training data has a known discretization error of $1\!\times\!10^{-3}$, what is the lowest RMSE expected from a PINN enforcing the PDE exactly? (Answer: It cannot be much below the data error magnitude.)

### Concept: Data-to-PDE Inconsistency Propagation
- **Why needed:** Shows the causal chain from data error to biased loss, explaining why low-fidelity data corrupts training.
- **Quick check:** In the effective loss $L_{\text{eff}}(\theta)$, what extra term appears compared to the ideal loss $L(\theta)$, and why does it prevent convergence? (Answer: A bias term from $\varepsilon(x)$ that pulls the solution toward inconsistent data.)

### Concept: Pareto Front Analysis for PINN Loss Balancing
- **Why needed:** Diagnoses tension between physics and data terms; guides whether dynamic weighting (lbPINN) is needed.
- **Quick check:** What does Pareto front saturation on the LD axis indicate? What does a classic "elbow" shape imply? (Answer: Saturation means data objective cannot be improved further; elbow indicates a trade-off where both objectives can be jointly optimized up to a point.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map (plain text):**
Data generation (analytical or numerical) → Training data loader (spatial points, collocation points) → Neural network ($u_\theta$) → Loss computation (lbPINN: $L_{\text{PDE}}, L_{\text{BC}}, L_{\text{D}}$ with trainable $\sigma$) → Optimizer (Warm-up → AdamW → L-BFGS) → Evaluation (testRMSE, RMSE vs. analytical)

**Critical path:**
1. Generate datasets with quantified fidelity (C1, C2, C3, Analytical). Compute discretization error $\varepsilon(x)$.
2. Define PINN architecture: 7 hidden layers × 200 tanh neurons. Input $(x)$ or $(x,\nu)$. Output $u$.
3. Prepare data loaders: training points ($u$ values), collocation points for PDE residual, boundary points.
4. Initialize loss with lbPINN weighting (trainable $\sigma$) or fixed $\alpha$ for Pareto analysis.
5. Training schedule:
   - Warm-up: optimize only data loss (PDE disabled) for 50 epochs (standard) or 500 (parametric) using AdamW.
   - Main: optimize full loss with AdamW for 2000 epochs (standard) or 20000 (parametric), mini-batch sizes 500/120.
   - Refinement: full-batch L-BFGS for 3000 epochs (standard) or 160000 (parametric).
6. Monitor convergence: compute testRMSE (Eq. 13) and RMSE against analytical solution on mesh nodes.
7. For Pareto front: train with fixed $\alpha \in [0,1]$ and compare to lbPINN trajectory.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Fixed $\alpha$ vs. dynamic $\sigma$ (lbPINN):** Fixed requires Pareto front search; dynamic automates balance but adds trainable parameters and potential instability.
- **Collocation distribution:** Uniform vs. Sobol for parametric; sparse sampling in high-viscosity regions can degrade accuracy.
- **Network capacity:** Deeper/wider networks may reduce approximation error but risk overfitting inconsistent data or increased compute time; $7\!\times\!200$ was sufficient for 1D Burgers.

**Failure signatures:**
- **Error saturation:** testRMSE plateaus near data error level (e.g., C1 $\sim 9\!\times\!10^{-2}$) even as $L_{\text{PDE}}$ decreases. Indicates consistency barrier.
- **Pareto front saturation on LD axis:** Even with $\alpha\!\to\!0$ (prioritizing data), LD cannot drop below a floor, showing incompatibility.
- **Spatial error pattern mimicry:** PINN error distribution resembles $|\tilde{u}-u|$, with reduced amplitude but same shape; suggests PINN inherits data inconsistency.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Baseline consistency barrier validation:** Train four PINNs (Analytical, C1, C2, C3) with lbPINN. Plot testRMSE vs. iteration. Expect C1 to plateau $\sim 9\!\times\!10^{-2}$, C2 to plateau at intermediate level, C3/Analytical to reach $\sim 1\!\times\!10^{-6}$.
2. **Loss weighting sensitivity:** For C1, train with fixed $\alpha$ values (0.1, 0.5, 0.9) and with lbPINN. Plot ($L_{\text{PDE}}$, $L_{\text{D}}$) trajectories on Pareto front. Expect lbPINN to converge to the front; fixed $\alpha$ may miss optimum if poorly chosen.
3. **Generalization in parametric PINN:** Train parametric PINN on mixed viscosities ($10^{-1}, 10^{-3}, 10^{-6}$) with C1/C2/C3/Analytical data. Evaluate RMSE across viscosity range (including interpolated $\nu=10^{-4}, 10^{-5}$). Expect improvement in C1, parity in C2, and slight degradation in C3/Analytical due to interpolation difficulty.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Applicability beyond Burgers:** For which PDE classes (e.g., high-Reynolds Navier-Stokes, systems with shocks) does the consistency barrier manifest, and how does its magnitude scale with nonlinearity and stiffness? The study is limited to 1D smooth Burgers.
2. **Quantitative barrier prediction:** What is the precise relationship between data discretization error (or noise variance) and the attainable PINN error lower bound? The paper shows saturation but lacks a formula linking barrier height to $\varepsilon(x)$ statistics.
3. **Advanced mitigation strategies:** Can adaptive loss weighting or uncertainty quantification techniques (e.g., trainable per-data-point confidences, Bayesian PINNs) overcome the barrier by down-weighting inconsistent data? The paper uses lbPINN but finds the barrier persists.
4. **Spatial data distribution effects:** How does the density and clustering of low-fidelity training data affect the barrier? Does dense sampling in critical regions (boundary layers, shocks) partially override global inconsistency?
5. **Parametric consistency across parameter space:** In parametric PINNs, does the barrier emerge from local inconsistencies at each parameter or from interpolation between parameters with different fidelities? The interaction is unclear.

## Limitations
- Unspecified hyperparameters (FD solver details, AdamW/L-BFGS settings, $\sigma$ initialization) impede exact replication of error values and convergence speed.
- Findings are restricted to a single 1D PDE (viscous Burgers) with smooth solutions; generalizability to higher dimensions, hyperbolic systems, or real-world noisy data remains uncertain.
- The analysis assumes systematic data inconsistency; stochastic noise or model-form errors may alter the bias structure and barrier manifestations.

## Confidence
| Claim | Confidence |
|---|---|
| Empirical demonstration of error saturation across fidelity levels (C1 plateau at $9.65\!\times\!10^{-2}$ vs data error $2.81\!\times\!10^{-1}$; C3 near-zero alignment) – clear and consistent. | High |
| Theoretical derivation of bias propagation (Eq. 7) assumes perfect PDE enforcement and smooth $\varepsilon(x)$ – conditions that may not hold in practice. | Medium |

## Next Checks
1. **Solver invariance test:** Replicate the C1–C3 fidelity ladder using alternative numerical schemes (e.g., spectral, finite element) to confirm the barrier is not an artifact of the specific finite-difference implementation.
2. **Cross-PDE generalization:** Apply the same protocol to at least two other PDE families (e.g., 1D advection-diffusion, 2D Poisson) to test universality of the barrier.
3. **Noise injection study:** Start with high-fidelity analytical data, add controlled zero-mean Gaussian noise with variance matching C1’s $\varepsilon^{2}$. Train PINNs and verify whether the error saturation level aligns with the injected noise magnitude, isolating stochastic effects.
