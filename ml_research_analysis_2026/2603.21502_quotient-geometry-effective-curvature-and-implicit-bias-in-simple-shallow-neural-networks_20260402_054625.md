# Quotient Geometry, Effective Curvature, and Implicit Bias in Simple Shallow Neural Networks

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.21502
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21502
*   **Reference Count:** 9
*   **Primary Result:** Parameter symmetries (permutations, scalings) in overparameterized shallow nets cause representation-dependent geometry; modding these out defines a smooth predictor-class manifold (quotient) with an effective curvature metric that is invariant and better correlates with local training dynamics than Euclidean parameter-space metrics.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the problem that the geometry of the parameter space in overparameterized shallow neural networks is "representation-dependent" due to inherent parameter symmetries—different parameter vectors can represent the same predictive function. To resolve this, the authors construct a quotient manifold by identifying (modding out) all parameters related by hidden-unit permutations and positive scalings. This space of distinct predictor classes is equipped with a natural Riemannian metric induced by the finite-sample realization map. The resulting "effective curvature" on this quotient eliminates spurious flattening along symmetry orbits present in the standard Euclidean parameter space. The paper shows that gradient flow decomposes into a vertical component (moving along symmetry orbits, not changing the function) and a horizontal component (changing the predictor), with only the latter affecting the learned function. This framework argues that notions of model complexity and implicit bias should be defined on predictor classes (the quotient) rather than on raw parameters. For networks with a quadratic activation, the quotient is explicitly shown to correspond to a symmetric matrix `Q`. Experiments confirm that while Euclidean Hessian spectra vary across symmetry-equivalent representatives, the `Q`-space Hessian is invariant. Furthermore, in underdetermined regimes, the local dynamics and final solution norms (Frobenius, nuclear) of `Q` align with a selection principle based on quotient-level curvature.

## Method Summary
The core method is geometric: define an equivalence relation on the parameter space where two sets of parameters are equivalent if they produce the same predictor function. This equivalence relation is generated by the group of hidden-unit permutations and positive scalings. The set of equivalence classes forms a quotient manifold. A metric on this manifold is induced by pulling back the standard Euclidean metric from the ambient parameter space through the realization map that sends parameters to their corresponding predictor on a finite set of training points. This yields an effective Riemannian metric `G` on the quotient. The gradient of the loss with respect to parameters is projected onto the tangent space of the quotient to obtain the horizontal (predictor-changing) component that governs the function evolution. For shallow networks with a quadratic activation, the predictor is a quadratic form, and the quotient can be parametrized by the unique positive semidefinite matrix `Q` representing this form, turning the abstract quotient geometry into concrete matrix geometry.

## Key Results
*   The Euclidean parameter-space Hessian spectrum of the loss varies across points lying on the same symmetry orbit, while the Hessian spectrum on the quotient manifold (in terms of `Q`) is invariant under the symmetry group.
*   Local training dynamics (gradient norm, Hessian condition number) measured using `Q`-space curvature metrics (e.g., trace, Frobenius norm of the `Q`-Hessian) correlate more strongly with convergence behavior than standard parameter-space condition numbers.
*   In underdetermined linear regression settings realized by shallow quadratic networks, different trained solutions (from different random seeds) exhibit significant variation in `Q`-matrix norms (Frobenius, nuclear) and singular value spectra, suggesting a quotient-level selection principle consistent with minimizing a form of complexity defined on predictor classes.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper's mechanism is that the flatness/minimal-norm bias often observed in overparameterized networks is not a property of the predictor function itself, but an artifact of the arbitrary choice of representative within a symmetry orbit in parameter space. By quotienting out the symmetry group, one removes these redundant directions, yielding a geometry where distance and curvature are intrinsic to the predictor class. The effective metric `G` encodes how much a small change in the predictor (in the horizontal direction) costs in terms of the minimal parameter norm required to achieve it. Consequently, gradient flow, when restricted to horizontal motion, naturally moves in directions that minimize this intrinsic predictor-space cost, which can manifest as an implicit bias toward predictor classes with low complexity under this `Q`-space metric (e.g., low nuclear norm). The invariance of the `Q`-Hessian under symmetry confirms that the true local geometry is captured at the quotient level, not in the raw parameter space.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Quotient Manifold:** A space of equivalence classes where points are identified under a group action (here, parameter symmetries). *Why needed:* To obtain a geometry that depends only on the predictor function, not its arbitrary parametrization. *Quick check:* Verify that two different parameter sets related by a hidden-unit permutation/scaling produce the exact same `Q` matrix.
*   **Parameter Symmetry Group:** The set of transformations (permutations, positive scalings of hidden units) that leave the predictor function unchanged. *Why needed:* These are the redundancies causing representation-dependent artifacts in parameter-space geometry. *Quick check:* For a given shallow network, manually permute two hidden units and scale them inversely; confirm the output is identical.
*   **Effective Riemannian Metric (`G`):** The metric on the quotient induced by the finite-sample realization map. *Why needed:* It provides an intrinsic measure of distance and curvature between predictor classes based on minimal parameter change. *Quick check:* Compute `G` for a simple 2-hidden-unit quadratic net; show it is not proportional to the identity, indicating non-flat quotient geometry.
*   **Horizontal/Vertical Decomposition:** Splitting a parameter-space vector into a component tangent to the symmetry orbit (vertical, gauge) and one orthogonal to it (horizontal, predictor-changing). *Why needed:* To isolate the part of gradient descent that actually changes the predictor. *Quick check:* For a gradient step, project the parameter update onto the vertical subspace (spanned by symmetry generators) and horizontal subspace; confirm only the horizontal part changes the `Q` matrix.
*   **Predictor-Class Complexity:** A measure of complexity (e.g., nuclear norm of `Q`) defined on the quotient space, not on raw parameters. *Why needed:* To define a meaningful, representation-independent notion of simplicity that gradient flow might minimize. *Quick check:* Compute nuclear norm for different `Q` matrices obtained from training; see if it correlates with generalization in underdetermined settings.

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component Map:** Input Data -> Finite Training Set -> Realization Map (Params -> Predictor on Set) -> Symmetry Group Action -> Quotient Manifold (Space of Predictor Classes) -> Effective Metric `G` -> Horizontal Gradient Flow -> Trained Predictor Class (`Q` matrix).
*   **Critical Path:** 1) Identify the full symmetry group of the network architecture (permutations, scalings). 2) Construct the quotient space by defining equivalence classes under this group. 3) Derive the effective metric `G` as the pushforward of the Euclidean metric through the realization map. 4) Project gradient flow onto the horizontal subspace of the quotient. 5) For quadratic activation, show the quotient is parametrized by the PSD matrix `Q`. 6) Relate local curvature in `Q`-space to training dynamics and final solution properties.
*   **Design Tradeoffs:** The framework trades the simplicity and familiarity of Euclidean parameter-space analysis for a more complex but semantically correct quotient geometry. The benefit is invariance and meaningful curvature; the cost is the need to work with abstract manifolds and projections, though the quadratic case simplifies this to matrix calculus. A key tradeoff is between generality (works for any architecture in principle) and tractability (explicit `Q` only for quadratic/shallow).
*   **Failure Signatures:** 1) If the identified symmetry group is too small (misses some redundancies), the quotient will still contain flat directions, and Hessian spectra may vary within predictor classes. 2) If the realization map is not sufficiently "generic" (e.g., not full rank for a given `Q`), the induced metric `G` may be degenerate, making the quotient ill-defined. 3) If the network depth or activation nonlinearity changes the symmetry group fundamentally (e.g., ReLU introduces sign flips), the entire `Q`-matrix parametrization breaks down.
*   **First Three Experiments:** 1) **Symmetry Invariance Test:** For a fixed quadratic network, generate several parameter vectors in the same symmetry orbit. Compute and compare their Euclidean Hessian spectra and their corresponding `Q`-Hessian spectra; the latter must match exactly. 2) **Nonlinear Activation Stress Test:** Replace the quadratic activation with a shallow ReLU network. Attempt to define a meaningful quotient (likely requiring sign choices). Measure if a similar symmetry-invariant curvature descriptor can be defined and if it correlates better with dynamics than parameter-space condition numbers. 3) **Underdetermined Solution Analysis:** Train many instances of an underdetermined quadratic regression problem (more hidden units than data points). Collect the final `Q` matrices. Compute their Frobenius and nuclear norms, and plot singular value distributions. Check for a consensus low-complexity solution in `Q`-space across random seeds, contrasting with high variability in raw parameter norms.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
None explicitly stated in the provided excerpt. The paper's focus is on establishing the framework and its validation in a specific setting, leaving broader applicability as an open direction.

## Limitations
*   The explicit quotient manifold construction via the symmetric matrix `Q` is tightly coupled to the quadratic activation and shallow (single hidden layer) architecture. Generalizing to deeper networks or common nonlinear activations (e.g., ReLU) is not derived and may involve more complex symmetry groups or be infeasible analytically.
*   Experimental validation is confined to synthetic, underdetermined regression tasks. The paper does not test whether the quotient-level complexity metrics (e.g., `Q` nuclear norm) predict generalization on standard, overdetermined benchmarks or real-world datasets, limiting claims about practical implicit bias.
*   The connection between observed `Q`-space curvature metrics and an implicit bias *selection principle* remains correlational in the experiments. A direct causal demonstration that the effective metric `G` actively *induces* the gradient flow to prefer low-complexity predictor classes is not established.

## Confidence
*   **High:** The mathematical formulation of the quotient manifold for shallow quadratic networks and the derivation of the effective Riemannian metric are sound and internally consistent. The proof that the Euclidean Hessian spectrum is symmetry-dependent while the `Q`-space Hessian is invariant is robust for this model class.
*   **Medium:** The horizontal/vertical decomposition of gradient flow and the argument that only horizontal motion affects the predictor are compelling for the presented setting. The experimental correlation between local dynamics and `Q`-space curvature metrics is suggestive, but based on a limited set of metrics and specific underdetermined regimes.
*   **Low:** The overarching claim that this quotient-level perspective provides the "correct" or universally applicable framework for understanding implicit bias in *general* shallow neural networks. This extrapolation lacks theoretical support for non-quadratic activations and any empirical evidence beyond the controlled experimental setup.

## Next Checks
1.  **Nonlinear Activation Generalization:** Replicate the core Hessian invariance experiment (comparing Euclidean vs. `Q`-space spectra) using a shallow network with a ReLU activation. Determine if a similar symmetry-invariant curvature metric exists or if the symmetry group and quotient structure change fundamentally.
2.  **Depth Scalability Probe:** For a two-hidden-layer network with quadratic activations, identify the extended symmetry group (including inter-layer permutations). Numerically compute the effective metric `G` on a low-dimensional slice of the quotient and compare its condition number to the full parameter-space condition number to assess if "flattening" mitigation scales with depth.
3.  **Benchmark Bias Correlation:** Train shallow networks (both quadratic and ReLU) on a standard overdetermined regression dataset (e.g., polynomial fitting with more data points than parameters). After convergence from multiple seeds, compute the nuclear/Frobenius norms of the final `Q` (or analogous) matrices. Correlate these predictor-class complexity metrics with held-out test error to test if they better explain implicit bias than parameter-space norms.
