# T-REX: Transformer-Based Category Sequence Generation for Grocery Basket Recommendation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.06631  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06631  
- **Reference count**: 5  
- **Primary result**: Offline recall@10 >90%, exact rank-matching 30%; online A/B test showed 23% sales lift and highest widget clickthrough rates.

## Executive Summary
Online grocery shopping requires next-basket recommendation models that handle repetitive purchases and complex category relationships. T-REX introduces a transformer-based encoder-decoder architecture to jointly model short-term basket dependencies and long-term user preferences. Its key innovations are dynamic sequence splitting for efficient training on sparse histories, adaptive positional encoding for irregular temporal patterns, and category-level modeling to reduce dimensionality and align with customer mental models. Offline evaluations on a large Amazon Grocery dataset demonstrate consistent improvement over the strong P-Top baseline across recall@k and precision@k, with median recall@10 exceeding 90% and 30% exact rank-matching. In production A/B testing, T-REX delivered a 23% lift in sales and achieved the highest clickthrough rates, confirming its real-world effectiveness.

## Method Summary
T-REX uses a transformer encoder-decoder with 6 layers, 6 attention heads, and an embedding dimension of 512 (≈50M parameters). Customer purchase histories are represented as sequences of category sets; products are mapped to a taxonomy of 35–280 categories. The model employs dynamic sequence splitting: a random pivot purchase is chosen, with all earlier purchases fed to the encoder and the next *n* purchases as decoder targets. Positional encodings are computed as normalized days relative to the pivot, adapting to irregular shopping intervals. The decoder features causal masking to prevent information leakage during generation. Training uses teacher forcing with cross-entropy loss, Adam (lr=1e-4, weight decay=0.01), gradient clipping (max norm=0.5), and early stopping. At inference, the encoder processes the full history and the decoder autoregressively generates top-*k* category recommendations.

## Key Results
- Outperforms the P-Top baseline on recall@k and precision@k for all *k* values.  
- Median recall@10 exceeds 90% and exact rank-matching reaches 30% (vs. 27% for P-Top).  
- Production A/B test in Amazon Grocery reports a 23% sales lift and the highest widget clickthrough rates.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Dynamic Sequence Splitting**  
*Claim*: Improves generalization across varying history lengths and enables mid-session recommendation generation.  
*Mechanism*: Randomly sample a pivot purchase; all prior purchases form encoder input, and a fixed number of subsequent purchases form decoder target. This creates diverse, overlapping training contexts from a single history.  
*Core assumption*: Temporal relationships between purchases are meaningful and can be learned from non-contiguous windows.  
*Evidence*: The paper highlights efficiency for sparse patterns, creation of diverse contexts, and improved generalization for new customers (abstract, Section 3.1).  
*Break condition*: If purchase patterns are entirely stochastic, the diverse windows would introduce contradictory signals, degrading performance.

**Mechanism 2: Adaptive Positional Encoding**  
*Claim*: Normalized positions relative to the pivot capture irregular temporal patterns better than absolute or sequential positions.  
*Mechanism*: Positional encodings are computed as days before/after the pivot, normalizing the temporal scale around that reference point while preserving local order.  
*Core assumption*: The most informative temporal signal is relative time to the current decision point, not absolute timestamps.  
*Evidence*: The paper states standard sinusoidal and learnable absolute embeddings proved inadequate, while the relative approach proved effective (abstract, Section 3.2).  
*Break condition*: If absolute recency (e.g., “bought 2 days ago”) is the dominant predictor, the relative normalization would discard that information.

**Mechanism 3: Category-Level Modeling**  
*Claim*: Reduces dimensionality and stabilizes learning by aligning with customer mental models and mitigating sparsity.  
*Mechanism*: Products (29K) are mapped to 35–280 categories; the model predicts the next basket at the category level, collapsing the prediction space and leveraging robust long-term preferences.  
*Core assumption*: Shopping intent is primarily organized by category; product-level repetition within a category is secondary noise.  
*Evidence*: The paper lists advantages (manageable basket variability, direct cross-category dependencies, computational reduction, alignment with how customers plan) and notes neighboring works also use set/category-level modeling (Section 2, Section 5).  
*Break condition*: If the key value lies in item-specific novelty or substitution within categories, this abstraction would lose critical signals.

## Foundational Learning

- **Encoder-Decoder Transformer Architecture**  
  *Why needed*: T-REX’s encoder-decoder structure (unlike BERT4Rec’s encoder-only) allows the decoder to generate baskets causally, while cross-attention fuses history with generation.  
  *Quick check*: Why is a decoder with causal masking essential for next-basket generation, whereas bidirectional encoding (like BERT) suffers from information leakage?

- **Next-Basket Recommendation (NBR) Problem Formalization**  
  *Why needed*: NBR treats a user’s history as a sequence of unordered sets (baskets), not a single item sequence; this distinction explains the category-level output and set-based metrics.  
  *Quick check*: How does the mathematical representation `S(u) = (S_t^(u), ...)` differ from traditional sequential recommendation?

- **Information Leakage in Masked Language Models for Generation**  
  *Why needed*: BERT4Rec-style models use masking that can expose future instances of the same item during training, making them unsuitable for generating new baskets without leakage.  
  *Quick check*: What specific issue with bidirectional attention and masking makes BERT4Rec unsuitable for generating a new basket (Section 3)?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**  
Input Processor → Embedding Layer → Encoder Stack (6 layers) → Decoder Stack (6 layers) → Output Layer

**Critical Path**  
Data flows from Input Processor to Embedding, then through the Encoder (optional cross-attention context), into the Decoder where cross-attention fuses encoder outputs, finally to the Output Layer. The critical learning signal is the decoder’s cross-attention to encoder states.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *Category Granularity*: 35 categories (stable, high-level) vs. 280 (specific, riskier). Production adopted 280 for specificity.  
- *Dynamic Split vs. Session Split*: Dynamic splitting enables mid-session inference but introduces variable-length contexts; session splits are simpler but rigid.  
- *Model Size*: ~50M parameters (6 layers, 6 heads, dim=512) trades compute for capacity; optimal size not ablated.

**Failure Signatures**  
1. Poor performance on new/low-tenure customers if category abstraction is too coarse or dynamic splitting fails on short histories.  
2. Low rank-matching score indicates misalignment with stable long-term preferences, possibly due to overfitting to recent noise.  
3. High variance in recall@k across customers suggests inconsistent personalization; the model may only serve specific user archetypes.

**First 3 Experiments**  
1. Ablate dynamic splitting: replace with session-based splits (using EOS tokens); compare recall@10 and rank-matching.  
2. Ablate positional encoding: use standard sinusoidal absolute positions; measure degradation, especially on users with irregular intervals.  
3. Compare to properly masked BERT4Rec: implement a variant that masks all future occurrences of a target item during training; evaluate generation quality on the test set.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **Comparison with other transformer recommenders** – How does T-REX fare against SASRec or BERT4Rec on the same grocery dataset? No head-to-head experiments are presented.  
2. **Isolated impact of dynamic splitting** – What quantitative gain does dynamic sequence splitting provide? The paper claims superiority but lacks an ablation isolating its effect.  
3. **Optimal category granularity** – What is the best level (35, 280, or intermediate) for balancing specificity, efficiency, and user satisfaction? The paper expands to 280 without systematic evaluation.  
4. **Real-time inference adaptation** – Can T-REX support dynamic basket updates (e.g., as users add items) at scale, and what are the latency/accuracy trade-offs? Real-time generation is left for future work.  
5. **Adequacy of rank-matching metric** – Does rank-matching fully reflect practical utility? It may miss diversity, novelty, or alignment with specific shopping goals.

## Limitations
- Missing hyperparameter specifics (dropout rate, exact decoder length *n*) and dynamic splitting timestamp distribution.  
- Unknown product-to-category mapping details; synthetic data may not capture true relationships.  
- May fail on customers with very short histories or highly irregular gaps; category imbalance risks frequency bias.

## Confidence
- Dynamic splitting: Medium  
- Adaptive positional encoding: Medium  
- Category-level modeling: High  
- Encoder-decoder necessity: High
