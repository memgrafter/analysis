# Errors in AI-Assisted Retrieval of Medical Literature: A Comparative Study

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2603.22344  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22344  
- **Reference count**: 30  
- **Primary result**: AI-assisted retrieval of medical literature exhibits high error rates, with an average multimetric score ratio of 0.29 (SD 0.35) and a 47.8% complete miss rate, varying significantly by LLM platform and journal.

## Executive Summary
The study evaluated five large language models (Grok, ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity, DeepSeek) on retrieving references for 40 articles from top medical journals. Using a multimetric scoring system (valid DOI, PubMed ID, Google Scholar link, relevance) and complete miss rate, they found poor performance overall (average score ratio 0.29, complete miss rate 47.8%). Performance differed substantially across platforms (Grok best, Gemini worst) and journals (NEJM worst). The authors attribute errors to "referential hallucination" and suggest that abstract length may influence retrieval accuracy. They caution against relying on LLMs for literature retrieval without verification.

## Method Summary
The study selected 40 recent articles from four high-impact medical journals (NEJM, Lancet, JAMA, BMJ). For each article abstract, the authors queried five LLM platforms to retrieve 10 references. Retrieved references were manually validated for bibliographic identifiers (DOI, PubMed ID, Google Scholar link) and relevance (presence in the original article’s reference list). A composite score was computed per reference, adjusted for applicable metrics, and summed to yield a total score; the score ratio was total score divided by the maximum possible score (the “score cap”). A “complete miss” was flagged if the total score was zero. Multivariable regression analyzed associations of platform and journal with score ratio and complete miss rate, adjusting for publication year.

## Key Results
- Average multimetric score ratio was 0.29 (SD 0.35) with a 47.8% complete miss rate.
- Platform performance differed significantly: Grok had the highest score ratio (0.57), Gemini the lowest (0.11).
- Journal was independently associated with errors; NEJM articles had lower score ratios and higher complete miss rates compared to BMJ (P<.001).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Referential Hallucination**  
LLMs generate fabricated or incorrect bibliographic data due to inherent tendencies toward “referential hallucination.” When prompted to retrieve references, the model produces plausible-sounding citations by pattern-matching on its training data without verifying against live databases, resulting in non-existent DOIs, PubMed IDs, or Google Scholar links. This occurs because the next-token prediction objective prioritizes fluency over factual accuracy for specific identifiers. Evidence includes the high complete miss rate (47.8%) and prior work documenting fabrication in 50–80% of generated citations. The effect would be mitigated if the system incorporated a verified external knowledge base (e.g., retrieval-augmented generation).

**Mechanism 2: Platform-Specific Variation**  
Retrieval accuracy varies across LLM platforms due to differences in model architecture, training data, and fine-tuning. Each platform’s underlying model (e.g., Grok-2 vs. Gemini Flash 2.5) exhibits different proficiency in parsing academic queries and recalling/validating metadata, leading to score ratios ranging from 0.11 to 0.57. Model-specific factors such as inclusion of biomedical literature in training corpora and instruction tuning directly impact performance. Evidence includes stark differences in valid identifier counts (e.g., Gemini returned 0 valid PubMed IDs for BMJ). Performance convergence might occur with domain-specific fine-tuning or consistent verification pipelines.

**Mechanism 3: Journal Characteristics**  
Journal-specific attributes, possibly abstract length or content density, are associated with retrieval error rates. NEJM abstracts are shorter (≈250 words) compared to BMJ, Lancet, JAMA (300–350 words), and were linked to lower scores and higher miss rates. Shorter abstracts may provide insufficient cues for the LLM to identify key terms, leading to more failures. The study’s regression adjusted for platform and year but not abstract length directly, leaving this as a hypothesized mediator. The journal effect could diminish if abstracts were standardized or if LLMs used full-text articles instead.

## Foundational Learning
- **Referential hallucination**: Understanding why LLMs generate false citations is core to interpreting the study’s error metrics.  
  *Quick check*: If an LLM returns a plausible but non-existent DOI, is this a “retrieval error” or a “generation error” according to the paper’s framework?
- **Multimetric composite scoring**: The study’s score ratio adjusts for applicable metrics and bonus points; confusing this with simple accuracy would misrepresent results.  
  *Quick check*: Why does the score ratio sometimes exceed 1.0 (range 0–1.25), and what does a ratio >1 imply about the reference?
- **Confounding vs. independent association**: The paper claims journal and platform are “independently associated” with errors via multivariable regression; this distinction controls for other factors.  
  *Quick check*: In the regression, what does it mean that “publication year was not associated” after adjusting for platform and journal?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Input → LLM query engine (prompt with abstract) → Reference retrieval (10 per article) → Validation module (manual/automated checks for DOI, PubMed ID, Google Scholar link, relevance) → Scoring engine (composite score ratio, complete miss flag) → Regression analysis (factors: platform, journal, year).

**Critical path**:  
1. Accept article abstract and target journal.  
2. Query 5 LLM platforms with structured prompt for 10 references each.  
3. For each retrieved reference, manually or automatically validate bibliographic IDs and check presence in index article’s reference list.  
4. Compute total score and score cap (applicable metrics), adjust if index article.  
5. Derive score ratio = total score / score cap; flag complete miss if total score = 0.  
6. Run multivariable regression on score ratio and complete miss rate.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- Manual validation vs automated API checks: Manual is thorough but slow; automated scales but may miss relevance nuances.  
- Fixed 10 references per query vs variable: Fixed allows comparison but may penalize platforms that retrieve fewer but higher-quality references.  
- Using abstracts only vs full text: Abstract-only is feasible but may limit retrieval context; full text could improve relevance but dramatically increase validation effort.

**Failure signatures**:  
- Platform-specific failure modes (e.g., Gemini consistently returns 0 valid PubMed IDs for certain journals).  
- Journal-specific failure patterns (NEJM abstracts yield higher complete miss rates).  
- Score ratio distributions with long tails (high SD 0.35) indicating inconsistent performance across references.  
- Prompt confirmation step ineffective (“explicit prompts to double check references were used but appear not effective”).

**First 3 experiments**:  
1. Replication with current LLM versions: Run the same protocol (40 articles, 5 platforms) using latest model versions (e.g., GPT-4o, Claude 3.5) to test if error rates have improved since study period (July 2025).  
2. Prompt variation test: Systematically vary prompt specificity (e.g., “retrieve from PubMed only” vs. “include DOI verification”) to see if structured prompts reduce fabrication.  
3. Full-text vs. abstract retrieval: For a stratified subset (10 NEJM, 10 BMJ articles), run retrieval using full-text instead of abstracts; expect improved score ratios if abstract length is a key confounder.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Abstract length vs full-text impact**: Does using only abstracts underestimate error rates? The study acknowledges this limitation but did not compare with full-text. Resolution: direct comparative study using full-text and abstracts on same LLM platforms.  
2. **Ineffectiveness of verification prompts**: Why do explicit prompts to double-check references not improve accuracy? The paper observes this but doesn’t investigate. Resolution: ablation studies varying prompt structure and integrating live search APIs, plus internal model probing.  
3. **Journal effect mechanism**: Is the correlation between journal (e.g., NEJM) and errors driven by abstract length or other factors like topic complexity or reference list characteristics? The study hypothesizes abstract length but didn’t isolate it. Resolution: multi-variable model including measured abstract characteristics or controlled experiment with artificially modified abstracts.  
4. **Generality of findings**: Do the error patterns and platform rankings persist with newer LLM versions and across non-medical domains? The study is a snapshot for specific models and medical literature. Resolution: replication with updated models and extension to diverse scholarly domains.

## Limitations
- Manual validation bottleneck: manual checks are thorough but not scalable or reproducible.  
- Journal effect inference: the hypothesis that shorter NEJM abstracts cause higher errors is plausible but not directly tested.  
- Prompt dependency: results are tied to an unreported prompt; other designs (e.g., RAG) were not explored.

## Confidence
- Platform performance differences: High  
- Journal-specific error rates: Medium  
- Referential hallucination as primary mechanism: High
