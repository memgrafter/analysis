# On-Policy Self-Distillation for Reasoning Compression

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.05433  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05433  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** OPSDC reduces reasoning token count by 57–59% on MATH-500 while improving accuracy by 9–16 points (14B: 70.0%→86.1%), and by 41% on AIME 2024 with a 10-point gain.

## Executive Summary
Reasoning language models produce long, noisy traces that can compound errors. OPSDC (“On-Policy Self-Distillation for Reasoning Compression”) addresses this by training a student model to imitate a teacher that is the same model but instructed to be concise. The student generates its own rollouts, and training minimizes a per‑token reverse KL divergence to the teacher’s distribution. The teacher is periodically refreshed from the student (every M=50 steps), enabling progressive shortening without any ground‑truth answers. On Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-14B, OPSDC achieves >50% token reduction on MATH-500 and AIME-2024 while simultaneously boosting accuracy, preserving entropy, and maintaining general capabilities.

## Method Summary
OPSDC uses a single base model πθ with two conditioning contexts: the student πθ(·|x) receives the raw problem plus a step‑by‑step instruction, while the teacher π̄θ(·|x,c) receives the same problem prepended with a conciseness instruction. Training begins with θ̄ ← θ. At each step:  
1. Sample a batch of prompts.  
2. Generate one complete rollout y ∼ πθ(·|x) for each prompt (temperature 1.0, max response length 8192 tokens).  
3. For every token position t in y, compute the student token distribution q_t and the teacher distribution p_t (teacher forward with no gradient).  
4. Compute the per‑token reverse KL D_KL(q_t ∥ p_t), sum over t to obtain sequence loss L_i, average over the batch, and backpropagate to update the student.  
5. Every M=50 steps, copy student weights to the trainer (θ̄ ← θ). The loss depends solely on student rollouts; no ground‑truth answers are used. Optimizer: AdamW (lr=1e-6, weight decay=0.01), global batch size 32, gradient clipping 1.0, bfloat16, FSDP with parameter/optimizer offloading, sequence parallelism (Ulysses degree 4) and tensor parallelism (degree 2). Final student checkpoints are evaluated on benchmarks (MATH, AIME) by generating 8 samples per problem (temp=0.6, top-p=0.95, top-k=20, max new tokens up to 30 000) and extracting answers via the veRL math_dapo.py script.

## Key Results
- **MATH‑500:** Qwen3‑8B: 57% token reduction, +9 points accuracy; Qwen3‑14B: 59% reduction, +16 points (70.0% → 86.1%).  
- **AIME 2024:** Qwen3‑14B: 41% token reduction, +10 points accuracy.  
- Compression adapts naturally to problem difficulty: easy problems are compressed far more than hard ones, without sacrificing entropy or general‑purpose performance (MMLU unchanged).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Reverse KL stabilizes on‑policy self‑distillation
**Claim:** Reverse KL divergence stabilizes on‑policy self‑distillation by weighting updates with the student’s own distribution, preventing accuracy collapse during teacher refreshes.  
**Mechanism:** The loss `D_KL(π_θ(·|x,y<t) ∥ π_θ̄(·|x,c,y<t))` scales each token’s gradient by the student’s probability, confining updates to regions the student already explores. When the teacher is refreshed, the student only needs small adjustments because it already covers the teacher’s high‑probability modes.  
**Break condition:** If the student’s distribution fails to cover the teacher’s modes (e.g., early over‑compression), the KL penalty under‑fits and accuracy degrades.

### Mechanism 2: Periodic teacher updates enable progressive compression
**Claim:** Periodic teacher weight updates allow continuous shortening beyond what a frozen teacher can achieve.  
**Mechanism:** Every M steps (e.g., 50), the teacher is synchronized with the current student (θ̄ ← θ). Since the student has already learned to compress, the new teacher (conditioned on conciseness) produces even shorter traces, creating a stronger compression target for the next training window.  
**Break condition:** If M is too small (e.g., 1), the teacher changes faster than the student can adapt, causing a moving‑target instability and entropy explosion.

### Mechanism 3: KL objective yields difficulty‑adaptive compression
**Claim:** The KL objective naturally adapts compression to problem difficulty without explicit estimation.  
**Mechanism:** On easy problems, the concise teacher produces much shorter traces, creating many token positions with high KL signal. On hard problems, both teacher and student require long, similar reasoning, yielding weak signal. Thus the optimization pressures the student more on easy problems.  
**Break condition:** If the conciseness instruction fails to shorten easy problems (teacher not concise), the adaptive signal vanishes.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
`Problem → Student πθ → Rollout y → Compute per‑token reverse KL vs Teacher π̄θ → Update θ; periodically ¯θ ← θ`

### Critical Path
1. Split DAPO‑Math‑17k (80/20) and create two prompt contexts (student & teacher).  
2. Initialize θ_student = θ_teacher.  
3. For each training step:  
   - Generate one rollout per prompt from student (temp=1.0, max_len=8192).  
   - For each token, compute student and teacher logits; sum reverse KL over tokens; average over batch.  
   - Backpropagate and update student.  
   - If step % M == 0, copy student weights to teacher.  
4. Periodically evaluate checkpoints on MATH/AIME benchmarks (8 samples, temp=0.6, top‑p=0.95, top‑k=20, max_new_tokens up to 30 000).

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Reverse KL vs forward KL:** reverse stable, preserves entropy; forward can compress more but risks collapse.  
- **Teacher update interval M:** larger M yields stable convergence; smaller M accelerates compression but may cause instability.  
- **Instruction design:** qualitative “be concise” gives best accuracy‑compression balance; quantitative soft budgets (“use 50% fewer tokens”) compress more but hurt accuracy on hard problems.  
- **Token budget during training:** long max response (8192) ensures full reasoning; inference budgets control serving cost but may truncate.

### Failure Signatures
- **Entropy collapse:** per‑token entropy drops sharply (≥0.1 decrease).  
- **Accuracy saw‑tooth:** accuracy peaks then declines after each teacher refresh (sign of forward KL or M too small).  
- **Over‑compression on hard benchmarks:** token reduction similar to easy benchmarks accompanied by large accuracy drop on AIME.  
- **Training instability:** loss spikes or diverges; check learning rate, gradient clipping, teacher refresh frequency.  
- **Teacher not updating:** verify weight copy operation every M steps.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Main result replication:** Train OPSDC on Qwen3‑14B with M=50 and qualitative concise instruction. Evaluate on MATH‑500 and AIME 2024 (30k token budget). Expect ~59% token reduction on MATH and +16% accuracy; ~41% reduction and +10% gain on AIME.  
2. **Teacher update ablation:** Sweep M ∈ {1,10,20,40,50,60}. Plot entropy and accuracy over 100 steps. Expect M=1 to collapse; M≥40 stable; M=50 optimal.  
3. **Instruction ablation:** Compare qualitative concise instruction vs. soft budgets (p=20%, 50%, 80%). Expect qualitative to achieve highest accuracy with moderate compression; soft budgets to over‑compress and degrade on hard benchmarks.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. What is the minimum instruction‑following capability needed for OPSDC to succeed? (Only Qwen3‑8B/14B tested.)  
2. Does OPSDC generalize to reasoning domains beyond mathematics, where ground‑truth answers are scarce? (Evaluation limited to math benchmarks.)  
3. Under what conditions do conciseness instructions improve accuracy, and when do they degrade it, across different model families? (Only Qwen3 examined.)  
4. How should the teacher update interval M be chosen given model scale and problem difficulty? (Ablation covers limited scales; scaling laws unknown.)

## Limitations
- **Data & prompt fidelity:** Exact DAPO‑Math‑17k train/val split (seed, subset) and precise prompt wording are unspecified; deviations could alter teacher conciseness and learning signal.  
- **Algorithmic details:** Implementation of per‑token KL loss (sum vs. average, padding/EOS handling) and teacher weight synchronization (direct copy vs. EMA) are not fully clarified.  
- **Generalization:** Results only on mathematical reasoning; preservation of general capabilities (MMLU) is claimed but not deeply analyzed; transfer to other domains remains uncertain.

## Confidence

- **Periodic teacher updates enable progressive compression:** **Medium**  
- **Difficulty‑adaptive compression via KL signal:** **Low**
