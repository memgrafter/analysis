# Spectral Edge Dynamics of Training Trajectories: Signal--Noise Geometry Across Scales

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.15678  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15678  
- **Reference count:** 26  
- **Primary result:** Neural network training trajectories concentrate along a few coherent directions; the spectral edge (identified by the maximum consecutive singular value ratio) reveals a universal three-phase pattern (rise, plateau, collapse) and automatically selects a small signal rank (k* = 2 or 3). Larger windows cause the temporal correlation with validation loss to flip sign, and Johnson–Lindenstrauss projection to d = 10W preserves the spectral gap within 5–7%.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces Spectral Edge Dynamics (SED), a method for analyzing the geometry of neural network training trajectories. By applying a rolling-window SVD to parameter updates, SED isolates a low‑dimensional signal subspace from stochastic gradient noise via the sharp boundary in the singular value spectrum, identified by the maximum ratio \(\sigma_k/\sigma_{k+1}\). Experiments on a 51M‑parameter TinyStories model and GPT‑2 124M under distribution shift uncover a universal three‑phase evolution of the spectral edge: a rise as signal accumulates, a plateau of stable signal directions, and a collapse when the signal merges with noise. The automatically determined signal rank \(k^*\) remains very small (2–3). Intriguingly, the cross‑correlation between the spectral edge ratio and validation loss changes sign when the window size increases around \(W \sim 20\), revealing a scale‑dependent temporal coupling. A streaming Johnson–Lindenstrauss projection reduces computational cost while preserving the spectral gap with < 7% error. In follow‑up work, the collapse of the spectral edge predicts grokking events hundreds of steps in advance.

## Method Summary
SED takes a sequence of model checkpoints sampled at regular intervals. It first computes parameter deltas \(\delta_t = \theta_{t+1} - \theta_t\). To avoid direct SVD on the huge \(\mathbb{R}^{W\times p}\) trajectory matrix (\(p \sim 10^8\)), it precomputes all pairwise dot products \(D_{ij} = \langle\delta_i,\delta_j\rangle\) and extracts the \(W\times W\) Gram submatrix \(G = XX^\top\) for each rolling window. Eigendecomposition of \(G\) yields eigenvalues \(\lambda_k\) and singular values \(\sigma_k = \sqrt{\lambda_k}\). The signal rank \(k^*(t) = \arg\max_k \sigma_k/\sigma_{k+1}\) and the edge ratio \(r_{k^*}(t)\) are tracked over time. For scalability, a streaming Johnson–Lindenstrauss projection reduces the deltas to \(d \approx 10W\) dimensions before computing dot products, with a theoretical guarantee that the spectral gap is preserved within a small factor.

## Key Results
- The spectral edge ratio exhibits a universal three‑phase dynamic (rise, plateau, collapse) across model scales and under distribution shift.
- The automatically selected signal rank \(k^*\) is very small (2 for 51M TinyStories, 3 for GPT‑2 124M) and adapts to task dimensionality.
- The temporal coupling between spectral edge ratio and validation reverses sign as the window size increases (lag flip), indicating a timescale‑dependent relationship.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The spectral edge, identified via the maximum consecutive singular value ratio \(\sigma_k/\sigma_{k+1}\) from a rolling‑window SVD of parameter updates, separates coherent optimization directions (signal) from stochastic gradient noise.
- **Mechanism:** For a trajectory matrix \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{W\times p}\), the Gram matrix \(G = XX^\top\) captures inner products. Its singular spectrum has a sharp boundary: a few large singular values correspond to consistent update directions across the window (signal), while the bulk follows a Marchenko–Pastur‑like distribution (noise). The largest ratio \(\sigma_k/\sigma_{k+1}\) marks the transition.
- **Core assumption:** SGD/Adam noise is approximately isotropic *after* preconditioning, but the observed noise spread far exceeds MP predictions, indicating structured noise. The ratio metric remains robust.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] "rolling‑window SVD of parameter updates reveals a sharp boundary—the spectral edge—between coherent optimization directions and stochastic noise, identified via the maximum consecutive singular value ratio."  
  [section 2.2] Formal definition of signal rank \(k^* = \arg\max \sigma_k/\sigma_{k+1}\).  
  [section 3.2] Observed noise eigenvalue CV 0.688 vs. MP prediction ~0.001 (229×–576× excess), confirming structured noise.
- **Break condition:** No clear peak in ratio profile (all \(r_k \approx 1\)); window captures only noise or lacks coherent directions.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Across model scales and tasks, the spectral edge ratio exhibits a universal three‑phase dynamic: rise (signal accumulation), plateau (stable signal subspace), collapse (signal merges with noise).
- **Mechanism:** During initial learning, a few Hessian‑aligned directions dominate updates, increasing \(\sigma_k/\sigma_{k+1}\). As these directions saturate, the ratio plateaus. Later, when productive learning diminishes, the second signal direction (\(k^*\)) becomes indistinguishable from noise (ratio collapses).
- **Core assumption:** The number of active curvature directions (effective signal rank) is finite and adapts to task complexity.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] "the spectral edge exhibits a universal three‑phase pattern (rise, plateau, collapse)."  
  [section 3.3] TinyStories: rise (steps 1000–5000), plateau (5000–7000), collapse (7000–9000), \(k^*=2\).  
  [section 4.2] GPT‑2 124M: \(k^*=3\), analogous pre‑shift/rapid‑improve/overfit phases.
- **Break condition:** Ratio never declines or shows no clear plateau; indicates memorization or absent signal manifold.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The temporal coupling between the spectral edge ratio and validation loss reverses sign as the window size \(W\) increases (lag flip), reflecting a timescale‑dependent relationship.
- **Mechanism:** Small windows (\(W\) ~2000 steps) capture instantaneous dynamics: val‑loss leads spectral changes (negative lag). Larger windows integrate longer history, smoothing stochastic fluctuations and making the spectral edge a leading indicator (positive lag). The flip occurs around \(W \sim 20\).
- **Core assumption:** The spectral edge integrates geometric information over a timescale proportional to \(W\); val‑loss is a more instantaneous metric.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] "The temporal coupling with validation loss reverses sign as the window size increases (lag flip)."  
  [section 2.4, Property 2] Formal statement of the lag flip.  
  [section 3.5] TinyStories: lag shifts from –1 (\(W=10\)) to +1 (\(W=20\)).  
  [section 4.4] GPT‑2: lag from –1 (\(W=10\)) to +2 (\(W=30\)).
- **Break condition:** Correlation sign remains invariant with \(W\); spectral edge has fixed temporal alignment.

## Foundational Learning

**Concept: Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) and the Gram matrix trick**  
- **Why needed:** The full parameter covariance \(X^\top X\) is intractable for \(p \sim 10^8\). The Gram matrix \(XX^\top\) (size \(W\times W\)) shares non‑zero eigenvalues, enabling efficient spectral analysis.  
- **Quick check:** Why can we compute the spectrum via \(XX^\top\) instead of \(X^\top X\) even though \(p \gg W\)?

**Concept: Random Matrix Theory (RMT) and the BBP phase transition**  
- **Why needed:** The BBP threshold predicts when a signal singular value emerges from the noise bulk. The paper uses the Marchenko–Pastur law as a noise null, though observed noise is structured.  
- **Quick check:** How does the signal‑to‑noise ratio (SNR) of a spiked Wishart model determine the detectability of a signal eigenvalue?

**Concept: Cross‑correlation and Granger causality**  
- **Why needed:** To quantify temporal coupling between spectral edge ratio and validation loss, including the lag flip and directional Granger influence.  
- **Quick check:** What does a positive lag in Granger causality imply about the predictive direction between two time series?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Checkpoints → Compute deltas \(\delta_t\) → Build rolling trajectory matrix \(X\) (size \(W\times p\)) → Compute Gram matrix \(G = XX^\top\) → Eigendecompose \(G\) → Extract singular values \(\sigma_k\) → Compute ratios \(r_k = \sigma_k/\sigma_{k+1}\) → Identify \(k^* = \arg\max r_k\) → Track ratio and \(k^*\) over time. (Optional: Apply Johnson–Lindenstrauss projection to reduce \(p\) to \(d = 10W\) before dot products.)

**Critical path:**  
1. Load a sequence of checkpoints and flatten parameters consistently (exclude tied heads, biases as needed).  
2. Compute all pairwise dot products between deltas once: \(D[i,j] = \langle\delta_i,\delta_j\rangle\).  
3. For each window start \(t\), extract the \(W\times W\) submatrix \(G(t) = D[t:t+W, t:t+W]\).  
4. Perform eigendecomposition of \(G(t)\) to obtain eigenvalues \(\lambda_k\); set \(\sigma_k = \sqrt{\lambda_k}\).  
5. Compute consecutive ratios \(r_k\), set \(k^*(t) = \arg\max_k r_k\), and record edge\((t) = r_{k^*(t)}\).  
6. Repeat for all windows to produce time series.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Window size* \(W\): Larger \(W\) integrates longer history, yields smoother edge and positive lag; smaller \(W\) captures fast dynamics but gives negative lag and higher variance.  
- *Projection dimension* \(d\): \(d \approx 10W\) preserves the spectral gap within 5–7%; lowering \(d\) increases distortion.  
- *Checkpoint interval* \(s\): Finer sampling improves temporal resolution but increases storage and pairwise‑dot‑product costs; must align with expected spectral timescales.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Flat ratio profile (\(r_k \approx 1\) for all \(k\)) → no spectral edge; window too small or noise‑dominated.  
- \(k^*\) varies wildly with \(W\) → insufficient integration or unstable signal.  
- No lag flip across \(W\) sweep → stationary geometry or inappropriate window range.  
- JL projection error > 15% at \(d=10W\) → implementation or seeding error.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Replicate three‑phase pattern:** Train a TinyStories‑sized model (\(p\approx50M\)) with \(W=10\); verify the rise–plateau–collapse pattern in \(\sigma_2/\sigma_3\) and \(k^*=2\) across ≥ 2 seeds.  
2. **Test lag flip:** On the same model, sweep \(W\in\{10,15,20,25\}\); compute cross‑correlation of the edge ratio with validation loss per \(W\) and confirm the sign change occurs between \(W=15\) and \(W=20\).  
3. **Verify JL preservation:** For GPT‑2 124M, set \(W=10\) and project deltas to \(d\in\{50,100,200\}\); compare the edge ratio against the full‑dimensional result; expect ≤ 10% error at \(d=100\).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What determines the effective signal rank \(k^*\) of the training trajectory? How does it relate to the underlying loss landscape’s curvature (e.g., number of significant Hessian eigenvalues) or the rank of the Fisher information matrix?  
- **Basis in paper:** Discussion: “If the backbone manifold picture is approximately correct, it raises several natural mathematical questions: what determines the dimension of the backbone (the number of active Hessian modes, the rank of the Fisher information)?” (Page 13).  
- **Why unresolved:** Understanding drivers of \(k^*\) would explain why signal rank is small (2–3) and why it increases with task/model complexity, linking trajectory geometry to intrinsic properties of the optimization problem.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Empirical studies correlating \(k^*\) with Hessian spectra/Fisher information across diverse models; theoretical analysis linking the BBP phase transition to active curvature modes.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What causes the spectral edge (signal–noise boundary) to collapse during late training? Is this collapse inevitable after exhausting a low‑dimensional backbone manifold, or does it reflect a phase transition (e.g., approaching a saddle or local minimum)?  
- **Basis in paper:** Discussion: “When and why does the signal–noise boundary become unstable, triggering collapse?” (Page 13). The three‑phase pattern is observed across seeds and scales.  
- **Why unresolved:** Collapse marks a qualitative change in trajectory geometry and coincides with validation loss stabilization; understanding its mechanism could inform stopping criteria or adaptive learning rates.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** High‑resolution tracking of spectral edge alongside Hessian spectra; interventions that delay or accelerate collapse (e.g., varying weight decay, learning rate); theoretical models of spectral dynamics in non‑convex landscapes.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Does the SED framework scale to much larger models (1B+ parameters) and other architectures (CNNs, state‑space models)? Does signal rank remain as low (2–3), and does the three‑phase pattern persist under different optimizers (SGD, Lion) and data regimes?  
- **Basis in paper:** Limitations: “The framework’s applicability to larger models (1B+), other architectures (CNNs, state‑space models), and other optimizers (SGD, Lion, Shampoo) remains to be tested…” (Page 13). They showed JL projection works up to 124M but not beyond.  
- **Why unresolved:** To establish SED as a general diagnostic, it must be validated across the spectrum of modern deep learning setups; the small signal rank might be specific to transformers with AdamW.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic experiments with models of varying scales (1B, 10B, 70B), different architectures (ResNet, Mamba), and optimizers, measuring spectral edge dynamics.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What is the theoretical explanation for the “lag flip” phenomenon, where the cross‑correlation between spectral edge ratio and validation loss reverses sign as window size \(W\) increases? How does this relate to timescales of trajectory integration versus loss dynamics?  
- **Basis in paper:** They observe and measure the lag flip in both experiments (Property 2) and provide an intuitive explanation: larger windows integrate more history. A rigorous derivation linking window size to effective memory and the sign of the coupling is missing.  
- **Why unresolved:** The lag flip suggests a deeper connection between the geometry of the trajectory manifold and the temporal structure of learning; a formal understanding could guide the choice of \(W\) for monitoring training health.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Theoretical analysis using stochastic differential equation models of optimization; simulations that manipulate the timescale separation; empirical studies varying checkpoint frequency and correlating with loss changes.

## Limitations
- Theory assumes isotropic SGD/Adam noise; empirical noise eigenvalue coefficient of variation is ~0.688 vs. the ~0.001 predicted by Marchenko–Pastur, indicating unmodeled structured residuals.
- Evaluation is restricted to two transformer language models (TinyStories 51M and GPT‑2 124M); applicability to CNNs, RNNs, vision/speech tasks remains unknown.
- Missing implementation details: TinyStories dataset splits/sequence length, exact checkpoint frequency, and streaming Johnson–Lindenstrauss projection specifics (distribution, seeding) hinder full replication.

## Confidence
- **High:**  
  - Spectral edge detection via \(\max_k \sigma_k/\sigma_{k+1}\);  
  - Johnson–Lindenstrauss projection preservation at \(d=10W\).
- **Medium:**  
  - Universal three‑phase pattern (rise, plateau, collapse);  
  - Lag flip between spectral edge and validation loss.
- **Low:**  
  - Prediction of grokking events from spectral collapse (reported in companion work, not directly tested).
