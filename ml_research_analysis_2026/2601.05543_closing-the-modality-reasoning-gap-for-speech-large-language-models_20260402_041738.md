# Closing the Modality Reasoning Gap for Speech Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.05543
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05543
- **Reference count**: 12
- **Primary result**: TARS closes the speech‑text reasoning gap for 7B Speech LLMs, achieving up to 79.80% audio accuracy on OBQA.

## Executive Summary
Speech large language models (LLMs) exhibit a substantial performance gap when processing speech inputs compared to text, particularly on complex reasoning tasks. The authors identify two root causes: representational drift (divergence of hidden states across Transformer layers) and behavioral deviations (differences in final outputs). To close this gap, they introduce TARS, an on‑policy reinforcement learning framework that aligns speech‑conditioned reasoning trajectories with text‑conditioned ones. TARS employs a dual dense reward design combining representation alignment (layer‑wise cosine similarity of hidden states) and behavior alignment (semantic consistency of outputs via embedding similarity). Evaluated on the MMSU and OBQA benchmarks, TARS significantly narrows the modality gap. For Qwen2.5‑Omni‑7B, it reaches 76.84% audio accuracy (MRR 98.89%); for Phi‑4‑MM‑7B, 79.80% audio accuracy (MRR 100.45%), both state‑of‑the‑art among 7B Speech LLMs. Text‑input accuracy also improves, indicating beneficial cross‑modal knowledge transfer.

## Method Summary
TARS uses on‑policy reinforcement learning (GRPO) to align speech and text reasoning. The foundation model is a multimodal LLM (Qwen2.5‑Omni‑7B or Phi‑4‑MM‑7B) with a frozen audio encoder and projector; LoRA adapters (rank 8, alpha 32) are applied to all linear layers for efficient updates. Training data merges the UnifiedQA training set with synthetic speech from CosyVoice2 and openaudio‑s1‑mini, filtered by Whisper‑medium (WER < 10%) to yield 9,953 samples (203 hours). During RL, each prompt samples a group of eight completions balanced across speech and text. For a speech completion, a correct text completion from the same group serves as reference. Dense rewards include a base correctness reward, a representation reward (mean cosine similarity of layer‑wise hidden states between speech and reference text), and a behavior reward (cosine similarity of output embeddings using Qwen3‑Embedding‑0.6B); text completions receive only the base reward. Advantages are computed with modality‑specific normalization (separate means for speech and text) to prevent scale imbalance. Optimization uses the DAPO loss for three epochs with learning rates 1e‑5 (Qwen) or 2e‑5 (Phi), batch size 64. After training, evaluation on MMSU and OBQA with both audio and text inputs uses xFinder for answer extraction; accuracy and Modality Recovery Rate (MRR) are reported, and ASR capability is monitored via LibriSpeech WER.

## Key Results
- TARS achieves state‑of‑the‑art audio accuracy for 7B Speech LLMs: 76.84% (MRR 98.89%) on Qwen2.5‑Omni‑7B and 79.80% (MRR 100.45%) on Phi‑4‑MM‑7B.
- The modality reasoning gap is substantially closed; MRR approaches or exceeds 100%, meaning speech performance matches or surpasses the text baseline.
- Text‑input accuracy also improves, demonstrating cross‑modal knowledge transfer.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The reasoning gap arises because speech inputs cause representational drift (hidden states diverge across layers) and behavioral deviations (final outputs differ from text‑conditioned ones). TARS directly counters these by incorporating two alignment rewards. The representation reward enforces similarity between intermediate representations of speech and text completions on the same reasoning step, stabilizing the latent trajectory. The behavior reward aligns final outputs semantically, ensuring conclusions are consistent irrespective of modality. On‑policy RL with modality‑specific advantage normalization ensures speech updates are not overwhelmed by the typically lower base rewards of speech completions. Together, these mechanisms steer the speech reasoning process toward the text‑optimized pathway, effectively closing the gap while also regularizing the model to improve text performance.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Representational Drift**  
   **Why needed**: Explains internal divergence between speech and text representations across layers, a root cause of the reasoning gap.  
   **Quick check**: For identical reasoning steps delivered via speech and text, compute average cosine similarity of hidden states at each layer; a significant drop in deeper layers indicates drift.

2. **Behavioral Deviation**  
   **Why needed**: Captures discrepancies in final outputs despite similar inputs; addresses the end‑stage manifestation of the gap.  
   **Quick check**: Generate answers for the same question using speech and text inputs; measure embedding cosine similarity; low scores reveal deviation.

3. **On‑policy Reinforcement Learning (RL)**  
   **Why needed**: Enables fine‑tuning the model directly on the reasoning task without requiring labeled outcomes, crucial for aligning trajectories in a streaming fashion.  
   **Quick check**: Implement a simple policy gradient on a small LM for a toy reward (e.g., length) and verify the policy improves the reward over iterations.

4. **Dense Reward Design**  
   **Why needed**: Provides granular feedback for alignment beyond binary correctness, guiding both internal states and final outputs.  
   **Quick check**: Compare learning curves of a model trained with only sparse reward vs. one with added dense alignment rewards on a held‑out set.

5. **Modality‑Specific Advantage Normalization**  
   **Why needed**: Prevents one modality (typically text with higher base rewards) from dominating gradient updates, ensuring balanced improvement.  
   **Quick check**: After computing advantages, plot their distributions for speech and text; re‑centering to zero mean per modality should yield comparable spreads.

6. **LoRA (Low‑Rank Adaptation)**  
   **Why needed**: Efficiently adapts large pretrained models with a small number of trainable parameters, preserving knowledge while enabling specialization.  
   **Quick check**: Apply LoRA to a transformer layer and count trainable parameters; confirm it is orders of magnitude smaller than full fine‑tuning.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Text Prompt → LLM (with LoRA) → Completion  
Speech Prompt → Speech Encoder → Projector → LLM (with LoRA) → Completion  
Completions → Hidden State Extractor (per layer) → Representation Reward (cosine similarity)  
Completions → Embedding Extractor (Qwen3‑Embedding‑0.6B) → Behavior Reward (cosine similarity)  
Correctness Scorer → Base Reward  
Reward Combiner → Total Reward  
Advantage Normalizer (modality‑specific mean) → Policy Gradient (DAPO) → LoRA Update  

**Critical Path**  
1. Sample a batch of prompts.  
2. For each prompt, generate multiple completions (balanced speech/text) using the current policy.  
3. Evaluate base correctness for each completion.  
4. For speech completions, select a random correct text completion from the same prompt group as reference.  
5. Extract hidden states after the last assistant token for each layer; compute average layer‑wise cosine similarity between speech and reference text → R_rep.  
6. Compute output embeddings (via Qwen3‑Embedding‑0.6B) for speech and reference text; cosine similarity → R_beh.  
7. Combine rewards: speech total = base + λ_rep·R_rep + λ_beh·R_beh; text total = base.  
8. Compute advantages by subtracting modality‑specific mean from total rewards.  
9. Apply DAPO loss to update LoRA parameters.  
10. Repeat until convergence.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *Frozen audio encoder*: Preserves robust ASR features but prohibits adaptation of acoustic representations; may be detrimental if audio characteristics differ from pretraining distribution.  
- *Modality‑specific advantage normalization*: Balances updates but introduces extra statistics; with small groups, estimates may be noisy.  
- *Dual dense rewards*: Provide rich guidance but require additional forward passes (hidden states, embeddings), increasing computational cost.  
- *Group size*: Larger groups (e.g., 8) reduce variance of advantage estimates but increase memory for sampling; smaller groups may destabilize training.  
- *Reference selection*: Using a random correct text completion introduces stochasticity but may not perfectly match the speech’s reasoning chain; deterministic pairing could reduce variance but risk overfitting to a specific text.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Speech advantages are all non‑positive while text advantages are positive → likely missing or incorrect modality‑specific normalization.  
- R_rep values are near zero or negative across training → mishandling of token alignment or pooling (e.g., comparing states at different token positions).  
- Very few groups contain any correct text completions → temperature may be too low or text branch underperforms; consider pre‑training text branch or adjusting sampling.  
- Training diverges or text performance degrades → dual reward weights may be too high, causing excessive regularization; or LoRA rank insufficient.  
- Unexpectedly low WER on LibriSpeech after training → indicates forgetting of ASR ability; may need to preserve audio encoder or add an auxiliary ASR loss.

**First Experiments**  
1. **Baseline Gap Measurement**: Evaluate the foundation speech LLM on MMSU and OBQA with both audio and text inputs to quantify the initial performance difference (expected speech accuracy significantly lower than text).  
2. **Reward Signal Validation**: On a small held‑out set, compute R_rep and R_beh for correctly matched speech‑text pairs; verify R_rep is high (close to 1) and R_beh is also high, confirming alignment signals function as intended.  
3. **Ablation of Modality‑Specific Normalization**: Train a variant where advantages are computed globally (no per‑modality mean), compare speech learning speed and final performance to the full TARS; expect speech to lag or degrade without normalization.

## Limitations
- Proprietary status of Phi‑4‑MM‑7B prevents independent verification of SOTA claims.
- Key implementation details (random seeds, group selection logic, handling of groups without correct text references) are omitted, risking reproducibility gaps.
- Training relies on synthetic speech filtered by ASR; any bias or quality issue in this pipeline may affect the learned alignment and limit generalisation to natural speech.

## Confidence
- **Narrowing the reasoning gap**: Medium
- **SOTA among 7B Speech LLMs**: Low
- **Cross‑modal transfer (text accuracy improvement)**: High
- **Dual reward alignment effectiveness**: Medium

## Next Checks
1. Replicate TARS with the publicly available Qwen2.5‑Omni‑7B using the described data pipeline and verify audio accuracy ≥76% and MRR ≥98% on MMSU.
2. Substitute Qwen3‑Embedding‑0.6B with a public sentence encoder (e.g., all‑MiniLM‑L6‑v2) and measure the impact on final accuracy; a drop of less than 2% would indicate robustness to the choice of embedding model.
3. Conduct an ablation study removing either the representation reward or the behavior reward, and also disable modality‑specific advantage normalization; expect a significant regression in closing the modality gap, underscoring the necessity of each component.
