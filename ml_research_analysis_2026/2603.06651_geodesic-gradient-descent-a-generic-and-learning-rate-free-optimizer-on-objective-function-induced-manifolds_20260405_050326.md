# Geodesic Gradient Descent: A Generic and Learning-rate-free Optimizer on Objective Function-induced Manifolds

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.06651  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06651  
- **Reference count:** 30  
- **Primary result:** Compared to Adam, GGD reduces test MSE by 35.79%–48.76% on Burgers' dataset (fully connected networks) and cross-entropy loss by 3.14%–11.59% on MNIST (convolutional networks).

## Executive Summary
Geodesic Gradient Descent (GGD) is a novel optimization algorithm that operates directly on the objective function‑induced manifold. It approximates each local neighborhood of the hypersurface with an *n*-dimensional sphere tangent to the current parameter point, then projects the gradient‑derived tangent vector onto this sphere to follow a geodesic. This geometric approach keeps updates on the hypersurface without requiring a manually tuned learning rate. Empirical results on Burgers' equation and MNIST show substantial improvements over Adam and other baselines, suggesting that manifold‑aware updates can yield better generalization.

## Method Summary
GGD treats the pair *(θ, L(θ))* as a point on a hypersurface defined by *F(θ, L)=0*. At each iteration, it computes the Euclidean gradient *g*, constructs the normal vector *n = [g; -1]* and a tangent vector *v = [g; ||g||²]* orthogonal to *n*. A radius *R_t* is scheduled (e.g., Gaussian RBF: *R_t = R₀·exp(-(t-µ)²/(2σ²))*). The point *P = [θ; L]* is translated to an origin‑centered sphere of radius *R_t* with center *C = P - (R_t/||n||)·n*. The tangent *v* is scaled to have length π*R_t/2* (a quarter of the sphere’s circumference) and then projected onto the sphere to obtain the next point via the geodesic formula:  
*P' = cos(||v||/R_t)*P + (R_t·sin(||v||/R_t)/||v||)*v*.  
After translating back (*P_new = P' + C*), the new parameters are the first *n* components. This process eliminates the learning rate and constrains updates to the manifold.

## Key Results
- On Burgers’ dataset with fully connected networks, GGD lowers test MSE by **35.79%–48.76%** relative to Adam.  
- On MNIST with convolutional networks, GGD reduces cross‑entropy loss by **3.14%–11.59%** and often improves accuracy.  
- GGD achieves these gains without any learning‑rate tuning, using a fixed step size derived from the local sphere radius.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
GGD approximates the complex hypersurface locally with an *n*-dimensional sphere tangent at the current point. The normal *n* (derived from the Euclidean gradient) defines the sphere’s center *C = P - R_t n/||n||*. This spherical model replaces the true manifold’s curvature within a small neighborhood. The core assumption is that the hypersurface’s local geometry can be well captured by a sphere, regardless of global complexity. If the curvature varies rapidly over the radius *R_t*, the approximation breaks down and updates may leave the true surface.

### Mechanism 2
The algorithm projects the gradient‑derived tangent vector *v* onto the tangent sphere to move along a geodesic. After scaling *v* to a fixed length, the update *P_{t+1}* is computed using the geodesic equation on the sphere. This ensures the new point lies on the approximating sphere, which in turn is assumed to stay on the true hypersurface locally. The validity hinges on the sphere’s geodesic accurately representing the true manifold’s shortest path. Non‑spherical curvature or high torsion can cause significant deviation.

### Mechanism 3
GGD fixes the step size as exactly a quarter of the sphere’s arc length (π*R_t/2*) by scaling the tangent vector to that norm. This removes the need for a learning‑rate hyperparameter and makes the update magnitude a deterministic function of the local radius *R_t*. The underlying assumption is that a constant relative step size works universally across different loss landscapes. In practice, the fixed ratio may be too aggressive or conservative in regions with varying curvature, requiring careful choice of the radius schedule parameters *R₀* and *σ*.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**
1. **Gradient & Vector Compute:** Computes Euclidean gradient *g*, then normal *n = [g; -1]* and tangent *v = [g; ||g||²]*.
2. **Radius Schedule:** Updates radius *R_t = R₀·exp(-0.5·(t-µ)²/σ²)* (typically µ = 0).
3. **Tangent Scaling:** Scales *v* to *ṽ = 0.5·π*R_t * v/||v||* so that *||ṽ|| = π*R_t/2*.
4. **Sphere Transport:** Computes sphere center *C = P - (R_t/||n||)·n* and translates *P* to origin‑centered sphere *P' = P - C*.
5. **Geodesic Step:** Computes new origin‑centered point *P_new' = cos(||ṽ||/R_t)*P' + (R_t·sin(||ṽ||/R_t)/||ṽ||)*ṽ*.
6. **Transport Back:** Sets *P_new = P_new' + C* and extracts the first *n* components as *θ_{t+1}*.

**Critical path:** Euclidean gradient → *n, v* → *R_t* → *ṽ* → transport *P* → compute *P_{t+1}* on sphere → transport back → new parameters. Orthogonality of *n* and *v* and correct radius decay are essential.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Sphere approximation vs. complexity:* Simpler than learning a full manifold but may fail on highly anisotropic or non‑spherical curvature.
- *Fixed step vs. adaptivity:* Removes learning‑rate tuning but assumes π/4 is universally suitable; may need problem‑specific *R₀, σ*.
- *Computational cost:* Additional operations (sphere construction, transport) increase per‑iteration cost versus Adam, but could reduce total iterations.

**Failure signatures:**
- *Numerical instability:* Very small *||n||* causes division by near‑zero in *C*.
- *Hypersurface drift:* Violation of *||P_new|| = R_t* after transport indicates spherical approximation failure.
- *Poor convergence:* High/oscillatory test loss suggests *R₀* or *σ* are mismatched to curvature scale.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Burgers' Dataset (FCNs):** Verify GGD reduces test MSE versus Adam; compare three network depths and report hyperparameter values (*R₀, σ*) from the paper.
2. **MNIST Dataset (CNNs):** Verify GGD lowers cross‑entropy loss and improves accuracy on CNNs; examine training/validation curves for overfitting.
3. **Hyperparameter Sensitivity:** Systematically vary *R₀* and *σ* on a small network; expect degraded performance if radius decays too fast or too slow relative to loss curvature.

- **Q2:** Would alternative manifold structures (hyperbolic, Bézier) better approximate complex hypersurface geometries? *Basis:* Authors suggest other structures; *Evidence needed:* Extend GGD to such manifolds and compare on benchmarks with varied curvature.
- **Q3:** How does GGD scale to large‑scale deep learning (e.g., ImageNet, transformers, billions of parameters)? *Basis:* Experiments only on small networks; *Evidence needed:* Implement GGD for ResNet‑ImageNet, BERT, etc., and measure time, memory, accuracy relative to Adam.

Under what conditions (curvature bounds, step schedule) does it converge? *Basis:* No analysis provided; *Evidence needed:* Prove convergence under standard assumptions (bounded curvature, smoothness) and extend to stochastic mini‑batches.

## Limitations
- The sphere approximation has weak theoretical support for arbitrarily complex hypersurfaces; rapid curvature changes within *R_t* can invalidate it.
- Projecting the tangent vector onto a sphere assumes the sphere’s geodesic matches the true manifold’s geodesic, which may not hold for non‑spherical curvature or high torsion.
- The fixed quarter‑arc step size (π*R_t/2*) is a heuristic whose universality is unverified; it may cause overshooting or excessive slowdown in some regions, necessitating careful tuning of the radius schedule.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1** (local sphere approximation): **Low** (lack of evidence for general hypersurfaces)
- **Mechanism 2** (geodesic projection): **Low** (no validation of sphere geodesic fidelity)
- **Mechanism 3** (fixed step size): **Low** (empirical only, no theory on universality)
