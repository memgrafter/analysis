# Risk-sensitive reinforcement learning using expectiles, shortfall risk and optimized certainty equivalent risk

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.09300
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09300
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Developed policy gradient algorithms for three convex risk measures; on MuJoCo Reacher, expectile and quadratic UBSR outperform REINFORCE with mean return -2.29 (vs -8.07) and variance 0.11 (vs 1.25).

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces risk‑sensitive reinforcement learning algorithms for three convex risk measures: expectiles, utility‑based shortfall risk (UBSR), and optimized certainty equivalent (OCE) risk. For each, it derives a policy gradient theorem for finite‑horizon Markov decision processes, proposes sample‑based gradient estimators, and proves mean‑squared error bounds scaling as \(O(1/m)\) with the number of trajectories \(m\). Under standard smoothness and bounded variance assumptions, the risk‑sensitive objective is shown to be smooth, which yields stationary convergence rate bounds for the proposed risk‑aware policy gradient algorithm. Numerical experiments on the MuJoCo Reacher benchmark demonstrate that variants using expectile and quadratic UBSR risk significantly outperform the risk‑neutral REINFORCE baseline, achieving higher average returns and lower variance.

## Method Summary
The paper addresses the problem of optimizing a risk‑sensitive objective \(\rho_\theta[F]\) where \(F\) is the cumulative discounted cost under a parameterized stochastic policy \(\pi_\theta\). For each convex risk measure—expectiles, UBSR, and OCE—the authors derive a policy gradient theorem that expresses the gradient in terms of the score function and the risk‑measure conditional dependence. The resulting estimators (Equation 19 for expectiles, Equation 22 for UBSR, Equation 24 for OCE) achieve an \(O(1/m)\) mean‑squared error bound. The risk objective is proved smooth under standard assumptions, enabling the use of gradient‑based optimization with a finite‑sample convergence guarantee. The proposed RAPG algorithm samples \(m\) trajectories at each iteration, estimates the risk threshold (e.g., the expectile \(\xi_\nu\) or UBSR level \(SR\)), computes the gradient estimator, and updates the policy parameters. Empirical evaluation on MuJoCo Reacher confirms the theoretical advantages, with expectile and quadratic UBSR variants clearly beating REINFORCE.

## Key Results
- Theoretical guarantees: policy gradient theorems, \(O(1/m)\) MSE bounds for the estimators, and smoothness of the risk‑sensitive objective under assumptions (A1)–(A14).
- Empirical performance on MuJoCo Reacher: expectile (\(\nu=0.65\)) and quadratic UBSR achieve average return \(-2.29\) with variance \(0.11\), while REINFORCE obtains \(-8.07\) and \(1.25\).
- Other risk measures studied (CVaR, mean‑variance) underperformed relative to the top two variants.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Expectile Policy Gradient via Implicit Differentiation
- **Claim:** The gradient of the expectile risk objective \(\nabla \xi_\nu(\theta)\) equals \(\mathbb{E}_\theta\left[ \ell_\nu(F - \xi_\nu(\theta)) \, G(\theta) \right] / \mathbb{E}_\theta\left[ \ell'_\nu(F - \xi_\nu(\theta)) \right]\).
- **Mechanism:** The expectile \(\xi_\nu(\theta)\) is defined as the minimizer of \(\mathbb{E}[e_\nu(F - k)]\). Applying the implicit function theorem to the condition \(\mathbb{E}[\ell_\nu(F - \xi_\nu)] = 0\) yields the gradient after swapping gradient and integral under smoothness assumptions. The estimator (Equation 19) uses the same \(m\) trajectories to compute \(\hat{\xi}_\nu\) (via Equation 18) and the ratio.
- **Core assumption:** Assumptions (A1)–(A6): continuously differentiable policy, bounded costs, zero probability that \(F = \xi_\nu\), bounded score function \(G(\theta)\), bounded density of \(F\), and Lipschitz policy gradient.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] "derive a policy gradient theorem… for each of the aforementioned risk measures… prove O(1/m) mean‑squared error bounds"  
  - [section 4.2] Theorem 4 provides the gradient formula; Theorem 5 gives the MSE bound.  
  - [corpus] Corpus neighbors mostly address OCE or dynamic risk measures; expectile policy gradients are novel – prior work (e.g., [7,20,30]) used expectiles in distributional RL but not for direct optimization via policy gradients.
- **Break condition:** Numerical failure to solve Equation 18 for \(\hat{\xi}_\nu\) to sufficient accuracy, or violation of (A3) (positive probability mass at \(\xi_\nu\)) causing derivative issues.

### Mechanism 2: UBSR Policy Gradient with Double‑Sampling
- **Claim:** The UBSR objective gradient \(\nabla SR(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_\theta\left[ \ell(F - SR(\theta)) \, G(\theta) \right] / \mathbb{E}_\theta\left[ \ell'(F - SR(\theta)) \right]\) and can be estimated with MSE \(O(1/m)\) using two independent trajectory batches to avoid correlation bias.
- **Mechanism:** \(SR(\theta)\) is the smallest \(k\) satisfying \(\mathbb{E}[\ell(F - k)] \le \lambda\). Under convex, strictly increasing \(\ell\), implicit differentiation yields the gradient (Theorem 6). Because \(SR(\theta)\) itself must be estimated from data, using the same batch for both numerator and denominator induces bias; the estimator (Equation 22) employs \(2m\) trajectories: one batch to compute \(\hat{SR}_m\) and another for the ratio. Lemma 3 proves the MSE bound under bounded moments.
- **Core assumption:** Assumptions (A7)–(A11): \(\ell\) convex, strictly increasing, continuously differentiable; bounded second moments for \(\ell(F - SR)\) and \(G(\theta)\); smooth policy; and existence of constants \(b_1, B_1\) bounding \(\ell'\).
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] "derive a policy gradient theorem… propose estimators… prove O(1/m) mean‑squared error bounds"  
  - [section 5] Theorem 6 (gradient), Equation 22 (estimator), Lemma 3 (MSE).  
  - [corpus] Prior UBSR work (e.g., [16]) focuses on estimation, not policy gradients; this paper introduces the first policy‑gradient method for directly optimizing UBSR (as opposed to nested variants like [32]).
- **Break condition:** If \(\ell'\) becomes very small near \(SR\), denominator in Equation 22 may lose precision despite \(b_1\); incorrect implementation of double‑sampling (e.g., reusing the same batch) introduces bias.

### Mechanism 3: OCE Policy Gradient via Cancellation
- **Claim:** The OCE gradient simplifies to \(\nabla OCE(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_\theta\left[ \ell(F - SR(\theta)) \, G(\theta) \right]\), where \(SR(\theta)\) is the UBSR with loss \(\ell'\) and \(\lambda=1\). This avoids a ratio and yields an estimator with MSE \(O(1/m)\).
- **Mechanism:** \(OCE(\theta) = \inf_k \{ k + \mathbb{E}[\ell(F - k)] \}\) equals \(SR_{\ell',1}(F) + \mathbb{E}[\ell(F - SR_{\ell',1}(F))]\) (Lemma 4). Differentiating, the \(\nabla SR\) terms cancel because \(SR_{\ell',1}\) satisfies \(\mathbb{E}[\ell'] = 1\) (Theorem 8). The estimator (Equation 24) uses one batch for \(\hat{SR}\) and the same or another batch for the expectation of \(\ell(F - \hat{SR})G(\theta)\). Lemma 6 bounds the MSE.
- **Core assumption:** Assumptions (A12)–(A14): \(\ell\) convex, increasing, continuously differentiable; bounded \(\ell'\) and bounded second moment of \(\ell(F - SR)\).
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] "derive a policy gradient theorem… for optimized certainty equivalent risk"  
  - [section 6] Theorem 8 (gradient), Equation 24 (estimator), Lemma 6 (MSE).  
  - [corpus] Several neighbors (e.g., [129235] "A Reductions Approach to Risk‑Sensitive Reinforcement Learning with Optimized Certainty Equivalents") study OCE, but in model‑based or dynamic settings; this work’s model‑free policy‑gradient approach is distinct.
- **Break condition:** If \(\ell\) is non‑convex or the UBSR \(SR(\theta)\) is not differentiable (or \(\mathbb{E}[\ell'] \neq 1\) exactly due to estimation error), cancellation fails, degrading gradient accuracy.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept:** Policy Gradient Theorem (Risk‑Neutral)  
**Why needed here:** The derivations for risk‑sensitive gradients extend the standard score‑function technique; familiarity with REINFORCE and the gradient of \(J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum \gamma^t r_t\right]\) is essential.  
**Quick check question:** "Express \(\nabla_\theta J(\theta)\) using the score function \(g(\theta,\tau)\) and trajectory returns."

**Concept:** Convex Risk Measures and Loss Functions  
**Why needed here:** Expectiles, UBSR, and OCE are all convex risk measures defined via specific loss functions (\(\ell_\nu\), \(\ell\)). Their properties (e.g., differentiability, Lipschitzness) are used in smoothness analysis and gradient bounds.  
**Quick check question:** "What loss function defines the expectile at level \(\nu\), and how does its derivative \(\ell'_\nu\) behave?"

**Concept:** Implicit Differentiation and the Envelope Theorem  
**Why needed here:** The risk measures are defined as \(\arg\min\) or \(\inf\) over \(k\). Computing \(\nabla_\theta\) of such nested definitions requires the implicit function theorem (for expectiles) or envelope arguments (for UBSR/OCE).  
**Quick check question:** "If \(\alpha(\theta)=\arg\min_k g(\theta,k)\) and \(g\) is smooth, what is \(\nabla_\theta \alpha(\theta)\) when the minimizer is interior?"

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Policy network (π_θ) -> Risk module (ExpectileRisk/UBSRRisk/OCERisk) -> Trajectory buffer -> Optimizer (Adam/SGD) -> Environment (MuJoCo)

Critical path:
1. Sample \(m\) trajectories using current policy \(\pi_\theta\).
2. For the chosen risk measure:
   - Expectile: solve Equation 18 for \(\hat{\xi}_\nu\) (e.g., fixed‑point iteration).
   - UBSR/OCE: use two batches (or same for OCE numerator) to compute \(\hat{SR}\) via root‑finding (Equation 22/24).
3. Compute gradient estimator using score functions (Equation 19/22/24).
4. Update \(\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \hat{\nabla}\).
5. Repeat for \(N\) iterations; return a random iterate.

Design tradeoffs:
- **Batch size (m):** Larger \(m\) reduces gradient variance (MSE \(\sim 1/m\)) but increases wall‑clock time per iteration; choose based on compute budget.
- **Risk parameters:** \(\nu\) for expectile; \(\ell(\cdot)\) and \(\lambda\) for UBSR; \(\ell(\cdot)\) for OCE. Tuning is environment‑specific; e.g., higher \(\nu\) (e.g., 0.65) yields more risk‑averse behavior.
- **Smoothness constant \(L\):** Guarantees require step size \(\eta \le 1/L\). \(L\) depends on the risk measure and policy; in practice \(\eta\) must be tuned.
- **Double‑sampling cost:** UBSR and OCE use \(2m\) trajectories per gradient (Equation 22/24), doubling sample cost relative to expectile (Equation 19).

Failure signatures:
- **Noisy gradients:** High variance of \(\hat{\nabla}\) across iterations, causing oscillating returns. Monitor gradient norms.
- **Numerical instability in root‑finding:** \(\hat{\xi}_\nu\) or \(\hat{SR}\) fail to converge within reasonable iterations (e.g., binary search). Impose max iterations and monitor residual.
- **Denominator near zero:** For expectile, \(\mathbb{E}[\ell'_\nu]\) bounded below by \(\min(\nu,1-\nu)>0\), but finite‑sample estimates can be small if \(m\) is tiny. Add a small epsilon to denominator.
- **Overfitting to batch:** With tiny \(m\), the estimated risk may have high bias. Validate by occasionally evaluating the risk with a large held‑out set.

First 3 experiments:
1. **Gradient sanity check:** On a single‑state MDP with Gaussian costs and linear policy, analytically compute \(\nabla \xi_\nu(\theta)\) and compare to the estimator over increasing \(m\). Plot MSE vs \(m\); confirm \(O(1/m)\) decay.
2. **Synthetic risk descent:** Define a simple risk objective (e.g., expectile of \((\theta+X)^2\) with known solution). Run gradient descent with the estimator and verify convergence to the true minimizer.
3. **Reproduce main result:** Train REINFORCE and the three risk‑aware variants (expectile \(\nu=0.65\); UBSR with \(\ell(x)=[x^+]^2\), \(\lambda=0.5\); OCE with \(\ell(x)=[[x+1]^2-1]/2\)) on MuJoCo Reacher using \(N=10000\) episodes, batch size 100, \(\eta=2\mathrm{e}{-3}\). Record average return and standard deviation over 250 test episodes; expect expectile and quadratic UBSR to beat REINFORCE (returns ≈ –2.3 vs –8.0, lower variance).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can the proposed finite‑horizon results be extended to infinite‑horizon (discounted or average-reward) MDPs, particularly for expectiles which are defined over cumulative costs?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper explicitly restricts analysis to finite‑horizon MDPs (Section 2). The authors note that prior work on expectiles and nested UBSR used different formulations, but their policy gradient theorems and convergence bounds are derived only for finite horizon.
- **Why unresolved:** Infinite‑horizon formulations require different techniques (e.g., Bellman equations for risk measures, or uniform ergodicity arguments) and stronger assumptions for convergence. The current analysis depends on finite sums over trajectories and horizon‑length constants that do not directly translate.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Derivation of an infinite‑horizon policy gradient theorem for each risk measure, coupled with finite‑time convergence bounds under appropriate mixing/ergodicity conditions. Empirical evaluation on continuing tasks would validate the extension.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** To what extent can the smoothness and bounded variance assumptions (e.g., (A8)–(A10)) be relaxed while retaining finite‑sample guarantees for the gradient estimators?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors repeatedly state that assumptions like bounded variance (A9, A10) and bounded derivatives of the loss function are "standard" but "difficult to verify in practice". They use these to obtain \(O(1/m)\) MSE bounds and smoothness.
- **Why unresolved:** Real‑world cost distributions may be heavy‑tailed or unbounded. Relaxing these could widen applicability but might degrade bounds (e.g., to \(O(1/\sqrt{m})\) or require more sophisticated robust estimation). The paper does not explore robustness to misspecification.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Theoretical derivations under weaker moment conditions (e.g., sub‑exponential tails) or using alternative estimators (e.g., clipping, truncation). Empirical studies comparing performance under heavy‑tailed noise would be necessary.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Why do some risk‑aware variants (e.g., CVaR, mean‑variance) underperform relative to expectile and quadratic UBSR in the Reacher experiment, and what theoretical properties explain this relative performance?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper reports that expectile and quadratic UBSR significantly outperform REINFORCE and other measures (CVaR, mean‑variance) in Table 1 and Figure 1. However, no analysis is provided to explain why certain risk measures yield better policies.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper's theoretical results show smoothness and convergence for all three families but do not characterize how the shape of the risk measure (e.g., tail sensitivity, asymmetry) interacts with the MDP's cost structure to affect final performance. The empirical outcome is noted but not investigated.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A deeper empirical study varying risk‑aversion parameters across measures and environments. Theoretical investigation linking the risk measure's properties (e.g., distortion function, elicitability) to optimization landscape properties (e.g., curvature, gradient variance) could offer explanations.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Can the proposed sample‑based gradient estimators be improved to achieve lower variance or bias, especially for UBSR/OCE which use double‑sampling or ratio‑of‑expectations forms?
- **Basis in paper:** The estimators (e.g., equation (19) for expectiles, (22) for UBSR, (24) for OCE) are shown to have MSE \(O(1/m)\), but the constants (e.g., \(C\) in Theorem 5) are large and involve terms like \(F_{\text{max}}\) and \(p_{\text{max}}\). The UBSR estimator requires \(2m\) samples (double‑sampling) due to the ratio structure.
- **Why unresolved:** High variance in estimators can slow convergence. The paper does not explore variance reduction techniques (e.g., baselines, control variates) tailored to these risk‑sensitive gradients. The constants in the bounds suggest potential room for improvement.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Deriving tighter bounds on the estimator variance, proposing variance‑reduced versions (e.g., using a baseline that is risk‑adjusted), and empirically demonstrating improved sample efficiency.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** For non‑smooth or non‑differentiable risk measures (e.g., CVaR as a special case of OCE with a piecewise‑linear loss), do the policy gradient theorems and smoothness results hold, and if not, what modifications are needed?
- **Basis in paper:** The analysis assumes continuously differentiable loss functions (A7, A12) for UBSR/OCE. The paper mentions CVaR as a special case of OCE (Table 2) but does not analyze it directly because its loss function is not differentiable at the threshold. The expectile case uses a non‑smooth loss \(\ell_\nu\) (piecewise linear) but the derivative exists a.s. by assumption (A3).
- **Why unresolved:** Non‑smoothness complicates gradient derivations (e.g., subgradients instead of gradients) and smoothness proofs. The current framework may not apply directly. Theoretical guarantees for such important special cases are missing.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Extending the policy gradient theorem to subgradients for non‑smooth risk measures (e.g., via recourse to Clarke derivatives). Verifying whether the objective remains smooth or analyzing convergence of subgradient methods would be needed.

## Limitations
- Incomplete experimental description (policy architecture, baselines, optimizer) prevents exact replication.
- Theoretical assumptions (A1–A14) may not hold in practice; constants and \(O(1/m)\) scaling remain untested.
- Only one MuJoCo task evaluated; generalizability unknown.
- Risk‑threshold estimation (expectiles, SR) could be numerically unstable; finite‑sample bias not analyzed.
