# How often do Answers Change? Estimating Recency Requirements in Question Answering

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.16544
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16544
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Key outcome:** Recency-stationarity taxonomy reveals LLMs struggle with context-dependent temporal questions, with transition accuracy below 15% when contexts change.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses LLMs' inability to determine when time-sensitive questions require up-to-date information, leading to confidently incorrect answers. The authors introduce a recency-stationarity taxonomy and the RecencyQA dataset (4,031 questions) to systematically study how temporal information needs affect QA performance. Their core finding is that non-stationary questions (where recency expectations depend on context) are significantly harder for LLMs than stationary ones, and models show limited ability to dynamically adapt their recency predictions when contexts change. Providing temporal context improves accuracy for non-stationary questions but often degrades performance on stationary ones, revealing a fundamental gap in dynamic temporal reasoning.

## Method Summary
The authors construct RecencyQA by sampling questions from FreshQA, PATQA, SituatedQA, and LLM-generated sources, then applying a multi-stage LLM annotation pipeline. For each question, 13 recency class predictions from LLaMA 3.3 (70B) generate a distribution, while three other LLMs vote on stationarity. Four cross-validation rules ensure consistency between recency distribution and stationarity labels, with inconsistencies leading to discarding examples. Verified temporal contexts are generated to ground each label. Human evaluation validates the final labels (76% recency, 78% stationarity accuracy). The dataset is then used to evaluate model performance on recency classification, context impact analysis, and recency transition tasks.

## Key Results
- Non-stationary questions are significantly more challenging for LLMs than stationary ones, with performance worsening as required update frequency increases
- Providing temporal context improves accuracy for non-stationary questions (e.g., +44% in some settings) but often degrades performance on stationary questions
- Models show limited ability to adapt recency predictions when context changes, with transition accuracy below 15% for the best model
- Human evaluation validates the labels with 76% recency and 78% stationarity accuracy

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** A two-dimensional taxonomy (recency × stationarity) enables fine-grained modeling of temporal information needs beyond binary freshness.
- **Mechanism:** Recency quantifies expected time until answer changes (12 classes from "an hour" to "never"). Stationarity determines whether that recency expectation is stable (context-invariant) or varies with temporal context. This separates how often an answer updates from whether that frequency itself is stable.
- **Core assumption:** Questions can be reliably classified along these two orthogonal dimensions, and this classification meaningfully predicts difficulty for QA systems.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] Introduces the taxonomy as the core conceptual contribution. [section 3] Defines the 12 recency classes and stationarity concept formally. [corpus] Neighboring papers (e.g., EverGreenQA) explore evergreen vs. mutable classification, suggesting the field recognizes this dichotomy but RecencyQA adds granularity and context-dependence.
- **Break condition:** If human annotators cannot agree on recency/stationarity labels for a significant portion of questions, the taxonomy's reliability collapses. The paper reports 76% recency and 78% stationarity human agreement, suggesting the break condition is not yet met but the margin is narrow.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** An LLM-based annotation pipeline with distributional sampling and cross-validation can produce a large-scale dataset with reliable recency and stationarity labels.
- **Mechanism:** For each question, 13 independent recency class predictions from LLaMA 3.3 (70B) generate a distribution, not a single label. A preliminary stationarity label from three other LLMs is then cross-validated against the recency distribution's entropy (single class → stationary; multiple classes → non-stationary), with inconsistencies leading to discarding the example. Verified temporal contexts are generated to ground each label.
- **Core assumption:** The aggregation and consistency rules (majority vote, distributional variance check) effectively approximate human judgment and mitigate LLM annotation biases.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 4.2] Details the 13-sample recency distribution, the 3-LLM stationarity vote, and the four cross-validation consistency rules. [section 5.2] Reports human evaluation validating the final labels (76% recency, 78% stationarity accuracy). [corpus] Weak; neighbors discuss dataset creation but not this specific multi-LLM verification protocol.
- **Break condition:** If the cross-validation rules overfit to LLM-specific prediction patterns rather than human notions of temporal stability, the dataset would encode model quirks, not human-like temporal reasoning. The human evaluation is the primary guard against this.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Non-stationary questions (context-dependent recency) are fundamentally harder for current LLMs than stationary ones, and models fail to dynamically adapt their recency predictions when context changes.
- **Mechanism:** 1) Performance on non-stationary questions is lower and increases in difficulty with higher recency classes (faster-changing info). 2) Providing a temporal context helps for non-stationary questions (+44% in some settings) but often harms performance on stationary ones. 3) On the recency transition task (RL1→RL2), per-context accuracy is moderate (~45%) but transition accuracy (correctly switching predictions) is very low (<15%), showing static reasoning.
- **Core assumption:** The experimental setup (question-only vs. question+context, multiple contexts per non-stationary question) isolates and measures the specific capabilities of recency awareness and dynamic adaptation.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] States non-stationary questions are "significantly more challenging" and models show "limited ability to adapt recency predictions." [section 7] Tables 6 & 7 provide the quantitative results: context improves non-stationary performance but hurts stationary; transition accuracy is drastically lower than per-context accuracy. [corpus] Weak; neighboring papers study temporal QA but do not measure context-dependent recency shifts or transition accuracy as defined here.
- **Break condition:** If the observed effects are an artifact of the specific prompts, model checkpoints, or the RecencyQA dataset's biases rather than a fundamental limitation of LLM architecture, the mechanism would not generalize. The consistent pattern across multiple models and prompting strategies (Table 6, 7) suggests the break condition is not imminent but requires testing on other architectures.

## Foundational Learning

**Concept: Temporal Grounding in QA**
- *Why needed here:* The entire paper assumes answers are not permanent facts but time-anchored assertions. An engineer must internalize that a "correct answer" is only valid for a specific time window or context.
- *Quick check question:* For the question "Who is the CEO of Company X?", what are three different contexts (e.g., dates, events) that would yield three different correct answers?

**Concept: Taxonomy Design for Evaluation**
- *Why needed here:* The recency-stationarity taxonomy is the lens through which all data is created and all experiments are interpreted. One must understand how to map a real-world information need to a point (or distribution) in this 2D space.
- *Quick check question:* Categorize "What is the price of Bitcoin?" as high/low recency and stationary/non-stationary, justifying based on real-world volatility and event-dependence.

**Concept: LLM-as-a-Judge for Temporal Properties**
- *Why needed here:* The dataset construction relies on LLMs to assign temporal labels. An engineer must grasp the protocol (multiple samples, aggregation, cross-validation) and its pitfalls (model bias, hallucination of temporal patterns).
- *Quick check question:* If 12 LLM samples for a question yield 11 "A-Few-Hours" and 1 "A-Day," what is the primary recency label? What does this distribution suggest about the question's temporal certainty?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**
Question Source Module -> Labeling Module (Recency Distributor, Stationarity Classifier) -> Consistency Engine -> Context Generator & Verification -> Final Curator

**Critical path:** Question Selection → Recency Distribution → Stationarity Vote → Consistency Check → Context Generation & Verification → Final JSON. A failure in Consistency Check (e.g., single recency class but non-stationary vote) leads to discarding the question.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Precision over Recall:* Context verification requires 13/13 label match, eliminating many contexts but ensuring strong temporal grounding. This sacrifices dataset size for label fidelity.
- *LLM Dependency vs. Scalability:* Using a single powerful LLM (LLaMA 70B) for core generation ensures consistency but inherits its temporal knowledge biases. Human validation is limited to a sample.
- *Stationarity Definition:* Stationarity is derived from recency distribution variance, not independently. This operational definition may conflate "genuinely context-dependent" with "LLM uncertainty about recency."

**Failure signatures:**
- High label entropy but "stationary" vote: Indicates the consistency rule correctly overrode a flawed stationarity vote.
- Low per-context accuracy but high transition accuracy (unexpected): Would suggest models are consistently wrong but adapt correctly—likely indicates dataset error or prompt misalignment.
- Context degradation on stationary questions widespread: As seen in Table 6, this is an expected finding, not a failure. A lack of this pattern would suggest the stationary questions are mislabeled.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Recency Classification Replication:** Re-run Table 5 for at least 2 models (e.g., Qwen2.5-72B, Gemma3-27B) with the provided prompts to validate the baseline difficulty of inferring recency from question alone.
2. **Context Impact Analysis:** Replicate the stationary vs. non-stationary split from Table 6 on your chosen models. Verify that context provides a statistically significant gain for non-stationary and a loss or null effect for stationary questions.
3. **Transition Task Sanity Check:** For Gemma3-27B (best in paper), manually inspect 20 examples where the model failed the RL1→RL2 transition. Check if the contexts C1 and C2 are meaningfully different and if the required recency shift is clear to a human. This tests whether the low transition accuracy stems from model limitation or ambiguous dataset examples.

## Limitations

- The taxonomy's reliability hinges on LLM-generated labels, with human validation showing only moderate agreement (76% recency, 78% stationarity)
- The operational definition of stationarity—derived from recency distribution variance rather than independent verification—may conflate genuine context-dependence with LLM uncertainty
- The strict context verification rules (13/13 label match) may have reduced dataset diversity and introduced biases

## Confidence

- **High Confidence:** The core finding that non-stationary questions are harder for LLMs and that context improves non-stationary but often harms stationary performance (based on quantitative results across multiple models)
- **Medium Confidence:** The taxonomy's ability to meaningfully classify temporal information needs (supported by human validation but limited sample size)
- **Medium Confidence:** The conclusion that LLMs show limited ability to adapt recency predictions when context changes (based on transition accuracy results, though the mechanism behind this limitation requires further investigation)

## Next Checks

1. **Cross-Architecture Replication:** Test the recency classification and context impact experiments on a fundamentally different LLM architecture (e.g., transformer vs. Mamba) to determine if observed patterns are architecture-specific.
2. **Temporal Bias Analysis:** Analyze the RecencyQA dataset for temporal knowledge cutoffs by examining model performance on questions about events before and after the model's training cutoff.
3. **Human Temporal Reasoning Benchmark:** Conduct a human study where annotators predict recency and stationarity for a subset of questions, then compare human vs. LLM consistency and reasoning patterns to isolate model-specific biases.
