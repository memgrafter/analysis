# HDPO: Hybrid Distillation Policy Optimization via Privileged Self-Distillation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.23871
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23871
- **Reference count:** 10
- **Primary result:** On OpenMathInstruct-2 with Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B-Instruct, HDPO (λ=0.01) yields pass@4 = 0.7861 (+1.1%) and pass@8 = 0.8271 (+0.4%) while preserving pass@1 ≈ 0.651.

## Executive Summary
Reinforcement learning for math reasoning often encounters “cliff” prompts—problems where all generated rollouts receive zero reward, causing the policy gradient to vanish. HDPO overcomes this by augmenting Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with privileged self‑distillation. For detected cliffs, the same model acts as a teacher by conditioning on the ground‑truth solution, generates correct trajectories (reward = 1), and distills its token distribution into the student via a Jensen‑Shannon Divergence loss. This hybrid signal rescues learning on otherwise unteachable prompts. On a 1.5 B math model, HDPO improves coverage metrics (pass@4, pass@8) while maintaining greedy accuracy, with the distillation weight λ directly balancing exploration and exploitation.

## Method Summary
HDPO starts with standard GRPO: for each prompt, generate \(K\) rollouts, compute binary rewards \(r_k\) (via verifier), and calculate leave‑one‑out advantages to obtain the PPO‑clipped loss \(L_\text{GRPO}\). It then identifies “cliff” prompts where \(\sum_k r_k = 0\). For each cliff, the model re‑generates rollouts using a privileged prompt constructed as \(x \oplus y^*\) (original problem concatenated with the ground‑truth solution). Only trajectories receiving reward = 1 are retained. Over these filtered trajectories, HDPO computes a token‑averaged Jensen‑Shannon Divergence \(L_\text{JSD}\) between the teacher distribution (forward pass on privileged prompt) and student distribution (current policy on standard prompt). The total loss is \(L = L_\text{GRPO} + \lambda L_\text{JSD}\), where \(\lambda\) controls the distillation strength. Because teacher and student share the same model weights (drifting teacher), the realizability gap is provably bounded. Training proceeds with AdamW; distillation occurs only on cliffs.

## Key Results
- At \(\lambda=0.01\): pass@4 = 0.7861 (+1.1%), pass@8 = 0.8271 (+0.4%), pass@1 ≈ 0.651 (baseline unchanged).
- At \(\lambda=0.1\): larger pass@8 gains (+1.4–1.7%) but pass@1 degrades noticeably.
- The distillation weight \(\lambda\) directly trades off greedy accuracy vs. solution diversity; higher \(\lambda\) boosts pass@k at the cost of pass@1.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Cliff Detection
**Claim:** HDPO detects悬崖 prompts where all standard rollouts receive zero reward, pinpointing exactly where the GRPO gradient vanishes.  
**Mechanism:** After generating \(K\) rollouts per prompt, a prompt is flagged as a cliff if \(\sum_k R(x, y^{(k)}) = 0\). This forces the within‑group mean reward \(\mu = 0\), yielding advantages \(A_i = r_i - \mu = 0\) for all rollouts and collapsing the GRPO policy gradient.  
**Core assumption:** Binary reward (0/1), fixed finite \(K\).  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 2.3 describe the vanishing gradient when all rewards are zero.  
**Break condition:** Non‑binary rewards or an accidental correct rollout (due to stochasticity) may obscure the cliff signal.

### Mechanism 2: Privileged Self‑Distillation on Cliffs
**Claim:** On cliffs, HDPO generates privileged rollouts (prompt includes ground truth), filters for correctness (\(R=1\)), and distills the teacher distribution into the student via Jensen‑Shannon Divergence.  
**Mechanism:** For each cliff \(x\), sample \(\bar{y} \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot | x \oplus y^*)\); retain only \((\bar{x}, \bar{y})\) with \(R(x,\bar{y})=1\). Then minimize \(L_\text{JSD} = \frac{1}{N_\text{tok}} \sum \text{JSD}_k\bigl( \pi_T(\cdot |\bar{y}_{<t}) \,\|\, \pi_\theta(\cdot |\bar{y}_{<t}) \bigr)\) over all tokens in the filtered set. The \(R=1\) filter implements rejection sampling from the KL‑regularized optimal policy in the \(\beta \to 0\) limit (Proposition 2).  
**Core assumption:** The model conditioned on \(y^*\) has non‑negligible probability of producing a correct solution; the filter yields samples from \(\pi_\theta(\cdot | x, y^*, R=1)\).  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 3.2; Proposition 2 links the filter to the optimal policy.  
**Break condition:** If the privileged success rate on cliffs is near zero, the teacher set \(T\) becomes empty and no distillation occurs.

### Mechanism 3: Bounded Realizability Gap via Drifting Teacher
**Claim:** Because teacher and student share identical model weights (drifting teacher), the KL divergence between their token distributions is bounded solely by the information content of the ground‑truth, not by any model‑capacity gap.  
**Mechanism:** Let context for student be \(c_S = [x; y_{<t}]\) and for teacher \(c_T = [x; y^*; y_{<t}]\). With same weights, \(f_\theta(c_T)\) vs. \(f_\theta(c_S)\) differ only by input perturbation. By Lipschitz continuity of \(f_\theta\), \(\|f_\theta(c_T)-f_\theta(c_S)\|_\infty \le L_\theta \cdot \Delta(g)\), where \(\Delta(g)\) is the input‑space distance from injecting \(y^*\). Lemma 1 then gives \(D_\text{KL}(P_T \| P_S) \le \frac{1}{2} L_\theta^2 \Delta(g)^2\). Cross‑model distillation would add an extra \(\|f_\phi(c_T)-f_\theta(c_T)\|_\infty\) term.  
**Core assumption:** \(f_\theta\) is locally Lipschitz on bounded input domains; \(y^*\) causes a finite perturbation \(\Delta(g)\).  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract, Section 3.4, Proposition 1; the theoretical bound is provided.  
**Break condition:** If \(f_\theta\) is not Lipschitz (e.g., extreme weight magnitudes or discontinuous activations), the bound may fail. In practice, transformers on bounded embeddings satisfy the condition.

## Foundational Learning

- **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) and advantage normalization**  
  *Why needed:* HDPO augments GRPO; understanding the cliff problem requires knowing that GRPO computes advantages per group (leave‑one‑out). If all rollouts fail, the group mean and variance become zero, leading to zero advantages and a vanishing gradient.  
  *Quick check:* For a prompt with 8 rollouts, if 4 succeed (\(r=1\)) and 4 fail (\(r=0\)), what is the advantage of a failing rollout? What changes if all 8 fail?

- **Binary verifiable reward and KL‑regularized RL objective**  
  *Why needed:* The \(R=1\) filter in HDPO is justified by the \(\beta \to 0\) limit of \(\max_\pi \mathbb{E}[R] - \beta \text{KL}(\pi\|\pi_\text{ref})\). For binary \(R\), the optimal policy concentrates on trajectories where \(\pi_\text{ref}\) assigns non‑zero probability to \(R=1\).  
  *Quick check:* For a reference policy with \(\mathbb{P}(R=1)=0.01\), how does the optimal policy evolve as \(\beta \to 0^+\)? What if \(\mathbb{P}(R=1)=0.5\)?

- **Jensen‑Shannon Divergence (JSD) as a mode‑covering loss**  
  *Why needed:* HDPO uses JSD (not reverse KL) to maintain solution diversity and avoid mode collapse, which would harm coverage metrics like pass@k.  
  *Quick check:* If the teacher distribution is multi‑modal and the student collapses to a single mode, how do reverse KL and JSD differ in their penalty? Which better supports high pass@k values?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
GRPO Core → Cliff Detector → (non‑cliff) Hybrid Optimizer;  
GRPO Core → Cliff Detector → Privileged Generator → R=1 Filter → JSD Loss → Hybrid Optimizer (for cliffs).

**Critical path:**
1. Sample a batch \(B\) of prompts.
2. For each \(x \in B\), generate \(K\) standard rollouts via current policy \(\pi_\theta\).
3. Compute binary rewards \(r_k\) for each rollout and leave‑one‑out advantages \(A_i\).
4. Compute \(L_\text{GRPO}\) using PPO‑clipped objective.
5. Identify cliff set \(C = \{ x \in B \mid \sum_k r_k = 0 \}\).
6. For each \(x \in C\), generate privileged completions with prompt \(x \oplus y^*\); sample trajectories from \(\pi_\theta(\cdot | x\oplus y^*)\).
7. Filter trajectories: keep only those with \(R(x, \bar{y})=1\), forming set \(T\).
8. If \(T \neq \emptyset\), compute \(L_\text{JSD}\) as token‑averaged Jensen‑Shannon Divergence between teacher (privileged forward) and student (standard forward) distributions over all tokens in \(T\), using global token‑count normalization.
9. Total loss \(L = L_\text{GRPO} + \lambda L_\text{JSD}\).
10. Perform AdamW update with gradient clipping and learning‑rate schedule.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **λ (distillation weight):** λ=0.01 gives modest pass@k gain while preserving pass@1; λ=0.1 yields larger pass@8 improvements (+1.4–1.7%) but degrades pass@1. Higher λ increases exploration but risks flattening the policy.
- **Teacher type:** Drifting teacher (shared current weights) enjoys a tighter realizability bound and empirically outperforms a frozen teacher at low λ. Frozen teacher may offer more diverse initial distribution but introduces capacity mismatch.
- **Normalization:** Global token‑count normalization (synchronized across data‑parallel ranks) ensures consistent scaling of \(L_\text{JSD}\); per‑batch normalization can cause instability.

**Failure signatures:**
- **No pass@k improvement:** Likely \(T\) empty or privileged success rate too low. Check cliff detection rate and privileged rollout correctness.
- **pass@1 drops sharply:** Over‑distillation; λ too high or teacher distribution too flat. Reduce λ or stop distillation earlier.
- **JSD loss NaN or unstable:** Incorrect computation (e.g., unnormalized logits, missing tail correction). Verify teacher softmax on top‑k and proper handling of student mass outside top‑k.

**First experiments:**
1. **Cliff detection validation:** Sample a batch, compute fraction of prompts with \(\sum r_k = 0\). Expect ~20–30% on OpenMathInstruct‑2 at early steps for the 1.5B model.
2. **Privileged success rate:** For detected cliffs, generate privileged rollouts and compute fraction yielding \(R=1\). Expect >70% (sufficient to form \(T\)).
3. **λ sweep:** Train HDPO with \(\lambda \in \{0, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2\}\) for 500 steps; monitor pass@1, pass@4, pass@8. Expect monotonic pass@8 increase and pass@1 peak near \(\lambda=0\).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Scaling behavior:** Does the tradeoff between pass@1 and pass@k controlled by λ persist at larger model scales (7B, 14B, 70B)? The paper only tests a 1.5B model; scaling effects are unknown.
2. **Curriculum for re‑injection:** What is the optimal schedule for re‑injecting former cliff prompts (now solvable after distillation) back into RL training for mode‑sharpening? The proposed “delay” lacks concrete hyperparameters (delay steps, frequency).
3. **Empirical realizability gap:** How large is the KL divergence between privileged and unprivileged token distributions in practice, and how does it vary with problem difficulty? The theoretical bound exists but empirical measurement is missing.
4. **Minimum privileged success rate:** What is the threshold of privileged success rate (probability of generating a correct solution given ground truth) below which HDPO yields no net benefit? This threshold likely varies with model scale and domain.

## Limitations
- **Privileged prompt formatting** unspecified (full solution vs. only final answer), directly impacting privileged rollout success.
- **Global token‑count normalization** details are vague; improper synchronization may cause training instability.
- **Generalizability** limited to one model (1.5B) and dataset (OpenMathInstruct‑2); it is unclear whether gains transfer to other scales, architectures, or math benchmarks.

## Confidence
- **Cliff detection mechanism:** High
- **Privileged distillation optimality (β→0):** Medium
- **Bounded realizability gap (drifting teacher):** High
- **Empirical gains at λ=0.01:** Medium
