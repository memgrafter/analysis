# StreamVoiceAnon+: Emotion-Preserving Streaming Speaker Anonymization via Frame-Level Acoustic Distillation

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.06079
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06079
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Achieves 49.2% emotion UAR on VoicePrivacy 2024 (+24% relative over baseline), with 5.77% WER and 49.0% EER privacy, while maintaining ~180 ms streaming latency and zero inference overhead.

## Executive Summary
Stream speaker anonymization systems often degrade the source speaker's emotional expression due to content token bottlenecks and a training paradigm that favors copying prompt acoustics. StreamVoiceAnon+ addresses this with two key advances: supervised finetuning using neutral-emotion utterance pairs from the same speaker, which forces the model to generate emotional acoustics from neutral prompts; and frame-level emotion distillation applied to acoustic token hidden states, avoiding gradient competition with the language modeling loss. On the VoicePrivacy 2024 benchmark, the method yields a 49.2% unweighted average recall for emotion recognition—a 24% relative gain over the baseline—while preserving intelligibility (5.77% WER) and privacy (49.0% EER), and adhering to the strict 180 ms streaming latency constraint without adding inference cost.

## Method Summary
The method builds upon StreamVoiceAnon, a streaming speaker anonymizer that uses a neural audio codec (NAC) to generate interleaved discrete acoustic and semantic tokens via two autoregressive transformers (Slow AR for acoustic tokens, Fast AR for semantic tokens). The authors first restructure the training data: instead of random utterance pairs, they create same-speaker pairs where the prompt is neutral and the source carries an emotion (angry, happy, sad). Learnable separation tokens are inserted to mark the prompt-source boundary. During training, they add a distillation loss that uses a causal transformer to predict frame-level emotion embeddings (from Emotion2Vec+) from the Slow AR acoustic hidden states. The total loss combines the original next-token prediction loss (for both AR branches) with the mean squared error distillation loss (weighted by w=0.01). Only the Slow AR, Fast AR, and the new distillation transformer are finetuned, keeping the rest of the pretrained model frozen. At inference, the distillation components are removed, leaving the original streaming pipeline unchanged.

## Key Results
- Emotion UAR reaches 49.2% on VoicePrivacy 2024, a 24% relative improvement over the baseline (39.7%) and 10% over an emotion-prompt variant (44.6%).
- The acoustic-branch distillation significantly boosts performance for the ‘sad’ emotion (from 8.0% to 42.6%) and corrects an over-prediction bias for ‘happy’.
- The system maintains intelligibility (5.77% WER), high privacy (49.0% EER), and the original ~180 ms streaming latency with no extra inference overhead.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Training Paradigm Restructuring
The emotion degradation in standard NAC-based streaming anonymization stems from the audio continuation training objective, which encourages the model to copy the prompt's acoustic patterns (including its often neutral emotion) rather than extracting emotional cues from the source. By finetuning on same-speaker neutral-emotion pairs, the model is forced to generate emotional output from a neutral prompt, thereby learning to preserve source emotion from acoustic features. Ablations show that this restructuring yields three times larger gains than simply adding more emotional utterances to training (Exp1 vs Exp2).

### Mechanism 2: Acoustic-Branch Distillation Isolation
Applying emotion distillation to the acoustic token hidden states avoids gradient competition with the primary next-token prediction loss, which already supervises the semantic branch. The semantic branch is thus free to focus on content accuracy, while the acoustic branch receives a dedicated emotion learning signal through the distillation loss. Empirically, acoustic distillation (Exp7) outperforms semantic distillation (Exp6) both in emotion UAR (49.2% vs 48.2%) and in WER (5.77% vs 6.23%), confirming that isolating emotion learning to the acoustic branch prevents detrimental interference.

### Mechanism 3: Frame-Level Temporal Modeling
Emotion is a dynamic property; pooling statistics over an utterance discards temporal variations. The proposed frame-level distillation uses a causal transformer to process each acoustic hidden state sequentially, preserving the time-varying emotional trajectory. This temporal modeling yields a 2.2 percentage point UAR gain (48.5% vs 46.3%) over utterance-level pooling (Exp4 vs Exp5) with comparable WER, demonstrating the benefit of fine-grained temporal processing within the streaming constraint.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Source audio -> Acoustic encoder -> A_src; Content encoder -> S_src; Emotion2Vec+ -> E_src (train-only)
Neutral prompt audio -> Acoustic encoder -> A_prompt; Content encoder -> S_prompt
Target speaker audio -> Speaker encoder -> spk_emb
Tokens assembled: [A_prompt, S_prompt, SEP_acoustic, A_src, S_src, SEP_linguistic] + spk_emb -> input embedding
-> Shared causal transformer:
   - Slow AR branch: consumes embeddings, predicts next acoustic token and produces h_acou
   - Fast AR branch: for each acoustic step, predicts n semantic tokens
-> Interleaved A,S token stream -> Neural audio codec decoder -> anonymized waveform
Distillation path (training only): h_acou -> Causal transformer -> predicted emotion embeddings; loss = MSE(predicted, E_src frame features)

### Critical path
For each time step t, the Slow AR generates an acoustic token conditioned on previous tokens and the prompt; the Fast AR then generates a bundle of semantic tokens for that same time frame. The process repeats sequentially in a fully causal manner, producing an interleaved token stream that the codec decoder converts to audio.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Acoustic vs. Semantic distillation:** Distilling on the acoustic branch yields cleaner emotion learning without harming content, but may lose some prosodic nuance that the semantic branch could capture; semantic distillation risks content degradation via gradient conflict.
- **Prompt emotion diversity:** Using a single neutral prompt simplifies deployment (no emotion labeling required) but may cap the maximum achievable emotion fidelity compared to using an emotion-matched prompt.
- **Finetuning vs. full training:** Finetuning for <2 hours on 4 GPUs is efficient and adds zero inference overhead, but inherits any architectural limitations of the base model (e.g., VQ bottleneck).

### Failure signatures
- **WER > 6%:** Indicates likely gradient interference; check distillation weight w or if distillation was applied to the wrong branch.
- **UAR < 45%:** Suggests missing separation tokens (causing prompt emotion copying), absence of same-speaker neutral-emotion pairs, or use of pooling instead of frame-level distillation.
- **EER-lazy < 45%:** Privacy collapse; emotion features may be leaking speaker identity—reduce w or add dropout in the distillation head.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Baseline replication:** Finetune on random neutral-neutral pairs (no same-speaker restriction). Expect UAR ~39.7%, WER ~4.5%.
2. **Paradigm restructuring only:** Add same-speaker neutral-emotion pairs without distillation. Expect UAR ~45.3%, WER ~5.2%.
3. **Full method:** Add acoustic-branch distillation with w=0.01. Expect UAR 49.2%, WER ~5.8%; if WER exceeds 6.5%, reduce w.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can streaming speaker anonymization incorporate longer-range emotional context within causal constraints to close the gap with offline methods? (Offline methods achieve ~63.8% UAR vs. 49.2%)
2. Does the proposed finetuning approach generalize to spontaneous emotional speech, which differs from acted corpora?
3. Can the method be extended to dimensional emotion models (valence-arousal) to capture continuous emotional states?
4. Does adversarial training against adaptive attacks improve privacy without sacrificing emotion preservation or intelligibility?

## Limitations
- Potential speaker overlap between CREMA-D (training) and IEMOCAP (test) could inflate reported emotion UAR.
- The teacher model (Emotion2Vec+) accuracy on the filtered 4-class set is unreported, which may limit the achievable upper bound for distillation.
- Critical hyperparameters (distillation transformer architecture, loss weight w) are not fully specified, hindering exact replication.

## Confidence
- **High:** The training paradigm restructuring and acoustic-branch distillation advantages are strongly supported by ablation experiments (Exp1–Exp7).
- **Medium:** The benefits of frame-level temporal modeling and the method's generalizability to other datasets/emotions are promising but less extensively validated.
- **Low:** Faithful reproduction is uncertain due to missing specifications of the base model architecture and preprocessing details; speaker-disjoint validation was not performed.

## Next Checks
1. **Speaker-disjoint verification:** Re-split CREMA-D and IEMOCAP by speaker, rebuild neutral-emotion pairs, and measure UAR. A drop >5% would indicate data leakage.
2. **Teacher accuracy audit:** Evaluate Emotion2Vec+ large on a held-out subset of CREMA-D (filtered to the 4 emotions). If UAR <85%, the distillation target quality may be suboptimal.
3. **Distillation weight sensitivity:** Sweep the loss weight w across [0.001, 0.1] on a validation set and plot WER/UAR trade-offs. Significant deviation from w=0.01 would suggest reported numbers are hyperparameter-sensitive.
