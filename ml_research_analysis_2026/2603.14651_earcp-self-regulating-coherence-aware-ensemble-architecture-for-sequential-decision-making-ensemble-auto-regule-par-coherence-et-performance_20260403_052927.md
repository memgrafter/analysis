# EARCP: Self-Regulating Coherence-Aware Ensemble Architecture for Sequential Decision Making -- Ensemble Auto-Regule par Coherence et Performance
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.14651
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14651
- Reference count: 17
- Primary result: EARCP dynamically reweights experts using performance and coherence, achieving up to 8.4% lower RMSE vs Hedge, 3.8% higher accuracy vs offline MoE, and 10.5% higher Sharpe ratio vs Hedge.

## Executive Summary
EARCP tackles the limitation of static ensemble weights in non‑stationary sequential decision‑making. It introduces a self‑regulating ensemble that dynamically re‑weights heterogeneous experts by combining individual performance (via multiplicative updates) with inter‑model coherence (agreement signals). The approach enjoys a theoretical regret bound of O(√T log M) and demonstrates significant empirical gains across time‑series forecasting, activity recognition, and financial prediction tasks. The complete implementation is open‑source on GitHub and PyPI.

## Method Summary
EARCP operates in an online fashion: at each timestep t, a pool of pre‑trained experts m_i produces predictions p_i,t. The ensemble output ŷ_t = Σ_i w_i,t p_i,t, where weights are updated after observing the true target y_t and loss ℓ_i,t = L(p_i,t, y_t). Each expert’s performance P_i,t is tracked as an exponential moving average (EMA) of negative loss: P_i,t = α_P P_i,t−1 + (1‑α_P)(‑ℓ_i,t). Coherence C_i,t measures agreement with other experts (e.g., fraction of experts predicting the same class or an exponential kernel over distance) and is similarly smoothed to C̄_i,t with α_C. Both P and C̄ are normalized across experts to [0,1] using rolling min‑max statistics. A combined score s_i,t = β P̃_i,t + (1‑β) C̃_i,t is clipped to [‑10,10] and transformed into an unnormalized weight w̃_i,t = exp(η_s s_i,t). After normalizing and enforcing a minimum weight w_min (followed by renormalization), the final weights w_i,t are obtained. Hyperparameters are typically α_P=0.9, α_C=0.85, β=0.7, η_s=5.0, w_min=0.05.

## Key Results
- RMSE reduced by 8.4% compared to the Hedge baseline.
- Classification accuracy improved by 3.8% relative to an offline mixture‑of‑experts.
- Sharpe ratio increased by 10.5% versus Hedge in financial prediction.
- These gains are consistent across electricity forecasting, HAR activity recognition, and market trend prediction.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Balancing exploitation and exploration via coherence.**  
EARCP computes a score for each expert that is a weighted combination of normalized performance (˜P_i,t) and normalized coherence (˜C_i,t). High performance increases weight, but high coherence—meaning the expert agrees with many peers—also boosts weight. This prevents premature dismissal of an expert that may be temporarily underperforming yet remains broadly aligned with the ensemble, which is valuable during non‑stationary shifts. The coherence signal acts as an exploration guide that is correlated with stability. However, if multiple experts systematically agree on incorrect predictions (adversarial coherence), the mechanism can amplify errors; the w_min floor provides only partial mitigation.

**Mechanism 2: No‑regret guarantee extended with coherence.**  
When β=1, EARCP reduces to the standard Hedge algorithm, which is known to achieve O(√T log M) regret under bounded losses. The theoretical analysis shows that incorporating coherence with β<1 scales the effective learning rate by 1/β, leading to an increased but still bounded regret. The proof relies on assumptions of bounded convex losses and that the best fixed expert in hindsight is a meaningful benchmark. The bound holds even with coherence, but it assumes stationarity of the underlying best expert; in highly volatile environments, the comparator may be unrealistic, though the algorithm may still perform well empirically.

**Mechanism 3: Practical stabilization via normalization, floors, and smoothing.**  
Several engineering choices ensure robustness: (1) Clipping s_i,t to [‑10,10] and normalizing across experts prevent numerical overflow. (2) A minimum weight w_min (e.g., 0.05) guarantees that all experts retain a baseline probability, maintaining diversity and avoiding total collapse. (3) Exponential moving averages (α_P≈0.9, α_C≈0.85) smooth noisy performance and coherence estimates, trading responsiveness for stability. These elements together help the system handle delayed feedback, transient noise, and rapid weight divergence.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Expert Pool → [Performance Calculator & Coherence Calculator] (parallel) → Normalizer → Score Combiner → Weight Engine → Ensemble Aggregator → final prediction. The Weight Engine feeds back to the next timestep via updated weights.

**Critical path:**  
Input x_t → Expert predictions {p_i,t} → On y_t arrival, compute losses ℓ_i,t → Update P_i,t and C_i,t → Normalize P and C → Combine into s_i,t → Clip and exponentiate → Apply floor and renormalize → Obtain w_i,t → Ensemble output ŷ_t = Σ w_i,t p_i,t.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- β: Balances performance vs. coherence; higher β reacts faster to true performance shifts but is less stable under noise.  
- α_P, α_C: EMA smoothing; higher values (0.95) reduce sensitivity to recent noise, lower values (0.7) increase adaptability.  
- w_min: Exploration floor; larger values preserve diversity but may retain poor experts; smaller values risk weight collapse.  
- η_s: Sensitivity of weight updates; large η_s causes rapid concentration on top scorers, small η_s yields near‑uniform weights.

**Failure signatures:**  
1. Weight collapse (entropy → 0): η_s too high or w_min too low; check weight distribution.  
2. Chaotic weight oscillations: η_s too high or α_P too low; monitor weight variance.  
3. Systematic lag behind best expert: β too low or α_P too high; compare cumulative loss offsets.  
4. Adversarial coherence amplification: All experts have high C and high loss; may indicate correlated bias.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline sanity check** on synthetic data with a clear stationary best expert and distractors; compare EARCP to equal weighting and Hedge; expect faster convergence to the best expert with lower variance.  
2. **Coherence ablation (β sweep)** on data with a moderate regime shift; test β ∈ {0, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9, 1.0}; expect optimal β (around 0.6‑0.8) to minimize performance dip and speed recovery.  
3. **Floor constraint test (w_min sweep)** on highly non‑stationary data where expert quality rotates; test w_min ∈ {0, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2}; expect w_min=0 to fail, w_min≥0.05 to succeed, but too high slows convergence.

## Limitations
- Normalization uses rolling min‑max statistics without specifying the window size, which can significantly affect weight dynamics.  
- For regression coherence, the kernel bandwidth γ (in exp(‑γ ‖p_i‑p_j‖²)) is not defined, leaving similarity sensitivity ambiguous.  
- The O(√T log M) regret bound assumes a stationary best fixed expert; in highly volatile non‑stationary settings this comparator may be unrealistic.  
- The mechanism may fail if multiple experts systematically agree on wrong predictions; the w_min floor is a mitigation but its effectiveness is unproven.  
- Empirical support for coherence as a stabilizer is limited; ablations isolating the coherence component are sparse.

## Confidence
- Empirical superiority over baselines (e.g., RMSE −8.4% vs Hedge): **Medium** – results are promising but rely on specific datasets and hyperparameters; independent replication is needed.  
- Regret bound O(√T log M): **High** – proof follows standard multiplicative‑weight analysis under stated assumptions and appears sound.  
- Coherence as a meaningful exploration/regularization signal: **Low** – theoretical justification is modest, empirical evidence thin, and risk of adversarial coherence remains.

## Next Checks
1. **Normalization window sweep** – Train EARCP with various rolling window sizes (e.g., 10, 50, 200, full history) on all datasets to assess performance sensitivity and identify a robust default.  
2. **Coherence bandwidth calibration (regression)** – For continuous‑output tasks, systematically vary γ (based on empirical distance distributions or grid search) and measure impact on adaptation during regime shifts.  
3. **Adversarial coherence stress test** – Construct scenarios where multiple experts share a correlated bias (e.g., all predictions offset by a fixed amount). Evaluate whether the w_min floor and β parameter prevent catastrophic weight collapse, and quantify any performance degradation relative to baselines.
