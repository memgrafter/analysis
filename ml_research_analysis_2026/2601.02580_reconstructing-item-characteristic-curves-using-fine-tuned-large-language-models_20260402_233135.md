# Reconstructing Item Characteristic Curves using Fine-Tuned Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.02580  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.02580  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: Fine‑tuned Qwen‑14B achieves Pearson correlation of 0.503 for 1PL difficulty and 0.446 for 2PL discrimination on a Grade 6 ELA dataset, demonstrating that LLMs can simulate student responses to estimate IRT parameters.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates whether large language models (LLMs) can replace expensive field testing for Item Response Theory (IRT) calibration. The authors fine‑tune Qwen‑3 models with LoRA to simulate student responses across discrete ability descriptors, then generate synthetic Item Characteristic Curves (ICCs) from which IRT parameters (difficulty, discrimination) are estimated. On a Grade 6 English Language Arts dataset (275 items, 754k responses), Qwen‑14B achieved a Pearson correlation of 0.503 for 1PL difficulty and 0.446 for 2PL discrimination. On the BEA 2024 Shared Task test set (201 items), Qwen‑8B achieved a Pearson correlation of 0.381 and an RMSE of 0.288 for difficulty prediction, outperforming baseline models like ELECTRA. The method is particularly effective for modeling item discrimination, a challenging psychometric property. While promising, the results are modest and rely on several design choices that warrant further investigation.

## Method Summary
The pipeline begins with a dataset of multiple‑choice items and historical student responses, including calibrated abilities (θ). Students are binned into 20 discrete ability descriptors (e.g., "Deficient", "Proficient") based on θ intervals. For each item, the counts of choices per ability bin are used to fit a Nominal Response Model (NRM), yielding smooth probability distributions over options for each ability level. A Qwen‑3 LLM is then fine‑tuned with LoRA in two stages: (1) supervised training to generate the correct answer token given an item and an ability descriptor; (2) distribution correction that minimizes the squared error between the model’s token probability distribution over choice tokens and the NRM’s probability distribution for that ability. After training, for a new item, the model is queried with each ability descriptor to produce a set of choice probabilities, forming a discrete ICC. Continuous IRT parameters (difficulty *b*, discrimination *a*) are recovered by fitting a logistic model to these points via weighted least squares. The approach is evaluated on an internal Grade 6 ELA set and the BEA 2024 test set, where 1PL difficulties are derived from provided parameters under a uniform guessing assumption.

## Key Results
- Grade 6 ELA (275 items, 754k responses): Qwen‑14B yields *r* = 0.503 for 1PL difficulty and *r* = 0.446 for 2PL discrimination.  
- BEA 2024 test (201 items): Qwen‑8B yields *r* = 0.381 and RMSE = 0.288 for difficulty prediction, outperforming ELECTRA.  
- The method shows a particular strength in estimating item discrimination, which prior LLM‑based IRT approaches have struggled with.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Fine‑tuning a base LLM with LoRA on ability‑descriptor‑conditioned prompts causes the model's token probability distribution to approximate the empirical response distributions of students at those ability levels.
- **Mechanism**: The model is first supervised‑fine‑tuned to generate a specific answer token for a given question and ability label (Appendix A prompts). A second "distribution correction" phase directly minimizes the squared error between the model's softmax output probabilities over answer tokens and the probabilities from a fitted Nominal Response Model (NRM) for that ability level (Equation 16). This forces the model's internal representations to produce calibrated probabilities.
- **Core assumption**: The base LLM's learned semantics and world knowledge provide a meaningful prior over what a student at a given competency level *might* know or misunderstand, and fine‑tuning can shift this prior to match observed response patterns.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "fine‑tuning Large Language Models (LLMs) to simulate student responses across a spectrum of latent abilities."
  - [section 3.2]: Details the two‑stage fine‑tuning procedure and the loss function (16) that aligns LLM probabilities with NRM probabilities.
  - [corpus]: Weak direct support. Neighboring papers like "Synthetic Student Responses…" (114014) and "SMART…" (105662) explore similar LLM‑simulation for IRT, suggesting an emerging methodological consensus, but do not validate *this specific mechanism*.
- **Break condition**: The model fails to generalize from the training items to unseen items; the fine‑tuned probability distributions do not correlate with empirical ICCs on a held‑out set, indicating the learned mapping is item‑specific rather than ability‑general.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Discretizing continuous latent ability (θ) into a set of semantically rich, ordered descriptors (e.g., "Deficient", "Basic", "Proficient") provides a conditioning signal that an LLM can interpret and respond to more effectively than a numerical θ value.
- **Mechanism**: Instead of regressing on a continuous number, the training data is binned into *N* classes based on θ intervals (Table 2). Each training example is tagged with one descriptor (*L*ₖ). The LLM is prompted to "be a student" at that descriptor level. The semantic meaning of the labels leverages the LLM's pre‑trained knowledge of competency levels.
- **Core assumption**: The semantic distinctions between the chosen descriptors (e.g., "Inadequate" vs. "Minimal") are meaningful and map to non‑trivial differences in problem‑solving behavior, and the LLM can act upon these distinctions.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "conditioned on discrete ability descriptors."
  - [section 3.1]: Defines the mapping from θ intervals to labels *L*ₖ and the expected value θₖ for each label. States: "it is desirable to maintain a direct correspondence with the competency levels they define."
  - [corpus]: No direct evidence from neighbors. This is a novel design choice in this paper.
- **Break condition**: Descriptors are too coarse or semantically indistinct, causing the model to produce similar outputs for adjacent ability levels, flattening the reconstructed ICC. Alternatively, the mapping from descriptor to θₖ (Equation 10) is miscalibrated.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Fitting a smooth Nominal Response Model (NRM) to sparse, binned empirical response counts provides a stable target distribution for each ability level, which is more robust than using raw proportions (Equation 12).
- **Mechanism**: For each item, the empirical counts (*C*ᵢⱼₖ) for each choice (*v*ᵢ) and ability bin (*L*ₖ) are weighted and used to fit NRM parameters (*a*ᵢⱼ, *b*ᵢⱼ) via minimization (13). This yields smooth, analytically derived probabilities *P*ⱼₖ for each option at each ability level (14), which serve as targets in the second fine‑tuning stage. The NRM implicitly shares statistical strength across ability levels and choices.
- **Core assumption**: The NRM is a sufficiently flexible and correct parametric model for the relationship between ability and multiple‑choice response probabilities. The uniform‑random‑guess assumption for incorrect options (Section 2.1) holds approximately.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 3.1]: Presents the NRM formulation (Equation 3) and the weighted minimization (13) to derive smooth probabilities. Contrasts this with the "bad estimate" of raw proportions.
  - [section 4.1/4.2]: The method's success (e.g., 0.446 discrimination correlation) suggests the NRM targets were effective.
  - [corpus]: Neighbors like "β⁴‑IRT" (207122) and "Amortised Design Optimization…" (226384) use different IRT modeling approaches but share the goal of parameter estimation, indicating the field's move beyond simple logistic models, but not direct support for this specific NRM use.
- **Break condition**: The NRM fit is poor (e.g., high residual error on a validation set), meaning the smooth target probabilities do not accurately represent the true underlying response probabilities, leading the LLM to learn a distorted mapping.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Item Response Theory (IRT) 1PL/2PL Models**  
  *Why needed here*: The entire goal is to estimate IRT parameters (difficulty *b*, discrimination *a*) from synthetic data. Understanding the logistic ICC curve (Equation 1) and the meaning of parameters is essential to interpret outputs and design the loss function.  
  *Quick check question*: If an item has a high discrimination parameter (*a*), what does that imply about the shape of its Item Characteristic Curve?

- **Concept: Parameter‑Efficient Fine‑Tuning (LoRA)**  
  *Why needed here*: The paper uses LoRA to adapt large Qwen models with limited compute/data. An engineer must understand that only low‑rank adapters (∆ = *B A*) are trained, not the full model, to grasp resource constraints and optimization dynamics.  
  *Quick check question*: In LoRA, if the rank *r* is set to 64, how many additional parameters are learned for a linear layer of size [*d*, *d*] compared to full fine‑tuning?

- **Concept: Nominal Response Model (NRM) for Multiple Choice**  
  *Why needed here*: The method uses the NRM (Equation 3) as an intermediary to model probabilities for >2 options and generate targets for the LLM. This is a generalization beyond binary 1PL/2PL.  
  *Quick check question*: Under what specific assumption about guessing does the NRM reduce to the 2PL model for the correct option? (See Section 2.1, equation 4).

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:
  1. **Data Module**: Reads item text, correct answer, and historical student responses. Bins students by θ into descriptors (*L*ₖ). Computes counts *C*ᵢⱼₖ.
  2. **NRM Fitter**: For each item, fits {*a*ᵢⱼ, *b*ᵢⱼ} by minimizing weighted loss (13) to produce smooth target probabilities *P*ⱼₖ (Equation 14).
  3. **Prompt Builder**: Constructs system/user prompts (Appendix A) combining item stem, options, correct answer (for SFT), and target ability descriptor (*L*ₖ).
  4. **LLM Wrapper (Qwen3 + LoRA)**: Loads base Qwen3 model, injects LoRA adapters. Handles tokenization, forward passes, and extraction of logits for the answer choice tokens.
  5. **Loss Calculator (Dist. Correction)**: Computes squared error between LLM's softmax over choice tokens and the NRM's softmax target (*a*ᵢⱼ(θₖ − *b*ᵢⱼ)) for the given item and ability level.
  6. **Parameter Extractor**: After fine‑tuning, for a new item, runs the LLM through all 20 descriptors to get probability sequences. Solves minimization (11) using derived θₖ and weights ωₖ to estimate final ˆ*a*ⱼ, ˆ*b*ⱼ.
- **Critical path**: Data Binning → NRM Fitting → SFT Fine‑Tuning → Distribution Correction Fine‑Tuning → ICC Reconstruction → IRT Parameter Estimation.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - **LoRA Rank (*r*=64)**: Higher rank increases capacity to fit NRM targets but risks overfitting to the small item pool. Lower rank is more robust but may underfit.
  - **Descriptor Granularity (*N*=20)**: More bins gives finer‑grained ICCs but drastically reduces counts per bin, making NRM fits and LLM learning noisier. Fewer bins make learning easier but lose resolution.
  - **NRM Smoothing**: The NRM provides stability but introduces model misspecification risk if the true response process isn't well‑captured by its form.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - **Flat ICCs**: LLM outputs similar probabilities across all ability descriptors for an item. Indicates failure in distribution correction phase.
  - **High variance in parameter estimates**: Similar items yield wildly different ˆ*a*, ˆ*b*. Suggests overfitting to item‑specific quirks rather than generalizable difficulty features.
  - **Poor discrimination correlation but ok difficulty correlation**: The method may be capturing mostly difficulty signals; discrimination requires modeling differential error patterns which is harder.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Baseline sanity check**: Run the full pipeline with **randomly initialized LoRA adapters** (no base model knowledge). Expect near‑zero correlation. Confirms that learned signal comes from base model + fine‑tuning, not just the NRM fitting or parameter extraction math.
  2. **Ablation: NRM vs. Raw Proportions**: Replace NRM target probabilities (*P*ⱼₖ) in the loss (16) with raw empirical proportions (*C*ᵢⱼₖ/*C*ⱼₖ). Expected outcome: increased variance and worse parameter estimates, validating the NRM's stabilizing role.
  3. **Descriptor sensitivity test**: Train with a **reduced set of descriptors** (e.g., only 5: Very Low, Low, Medium, High, Very High). Compare resulting ICC smoothness and parameter correlation to the 20‑descriptor model. Expect coarser ICCs and potentially worse discrimination prediction.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How does the granularity and semantic granularity of the discrete ability‑level descriptors (e.g., 20 levels) impact the fidelity of the reconstructed Item Characteristic Curve (ICC) and the accuracy of estimated IRT parameters?  
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The method relies on a fixed set of 20 discrete descriptors with subjective labels (e.g., "Exceptional" vs. "Outstanding"). The paper notes that "the semantic distinction between labels associated with proximate θ values… may be negligible" and that the mapping from discrete labels to expected θ values (θₖ) is derived from a normal distribution assumption. The impact of descriptor choice and binning strategy on the final parameter estimates is not systematically investigated.  
- **Why unresolved**: The paper uses one specific, LLM‑generated set of descriptors without ablation. It is unclear if a different number of levels, different label semantics, or a data‑driven discretization of θ would yield significantly different (and potentially more accurate) ICCs and IRT parameter estimates.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A controlled experiment where the number and semantic definitions of the ability descriptors are varied (e.g., 5, 10, 20 levels; using standard educational proficiency labels vs. LLM‑generated ones), measuring the resulting Pearson/RMSE against ground‑truth IRT parameters. Analysis should also inspect bias/variance trade‑offs in parameter recovery.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: To what extent does the assumption of uniform random guessing for incorrect multiple‑choice options (the basis for using equation 4 on the BEA dataset) limit the model's performance, and can the LLM‑based simulation naturally account for non‑uniform distractor plausibility?  
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] For the BEA 2024 Shared Task, the paper states: "Since we do not have responses or probabilities directly to estimate the other response probabilities, we rely on the approximation that incorrect choices are uniformly random. This allows us to use the correspondence provided by (4)." The core method (equation 3) uses a Nominal Response Model (NRM) which does not assume uniform guessing, but the BEA evaluation forces a 1PL difficulty estimate via this assumption.  
- **Why unresolved**: The assumption of uniform guessing is a known simplification often violated in real test items, where specific distractors attract more students. The paper's primary methodological contribution (simulating full response distributions) is circumvented for the BEA evaluation, limiting the assessment of the model's true capability to model full response vectors.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Evaluation on a dataset with full response vectors (like the ELA dataset) where the NRM is fit directly, comparing the resulting 2PL parameter estimates against those derived via the uniform‑guessing 1PL approximation. Additionally, analyzing the LLM‑simulated choice probabilities for specific distractors to test if they exhibit non‑uniform patterns correlated with real student distractor choices.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Does the proposed fine‑tuning approach genuinely learn to model the cognitive processes underlying item difficulty and discrimination, or is it primarily capturing superficial textual correlations and dataset‑specific artifacts?  
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The discussion states: "The current models serve as useful tools for low‑stakes automated pre‑testing, but further rigorous testing and reliability assessments are required before they can replace live human pre‑testing in formal assessment environments." It also notes the datasets are "relatively small," raising concerns about generalizability. The method conditions on discrete ability descriptors but the mechanism by which the model's internal representations align with these abstract labels is opaque.  
- **Why unresolved**: The correlation results, while positive, are modest (e.g., max ~0.5 for difficulty). The paper does not provide interpretability analysis (e.g., probing model layers for representations of ability, examining how item features like readability or content relate to the generated probabilities). It is uncertain if the model's success is due to learning psychometrically valid simulations or exploiting spurious correlations in the fine‑tuning data.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: 1) Ablation studies where the ability descriptor input is removed or scrambled during inference to measure its causal contribution. 2) Analysis of model‑generated responses for "ability‑inconsistent" errors (e.g., low‑ability descriptors generating correct answers to highly difficult items) to understand failure modes. 3) Testing fine‑tuned models on completely out‑of‑domain item types (e.g., math items vs. ELA) to measure transfer and robustness. 4) Comparing against a baseline that fine‑tunes on the same data but without the ability conditioning.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: What is the relative contribution of the two‑stage training process (supervised fine‑tuning for answer format, followed by distribution correction) to the final performance, and could a simpler, single‑stage objective suffice?  
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The method describes a two‑phase process: first, supervised fine‑tuning to produce the correct answer token given an ability descriptor; second, a "distribution correction" phase to align the model's token probability distribution (softmax over options) with the target probabilities from the fitted Nominal Response Model (NRM). The loss in phase two is the squared difference between the LLM's output probabilities and the NRM probabilities.  
- **Why unresolved**: The paper does not provide an ablation comparing this two‑stage approach to, for example: (a) training only with the standard cross‑entropy loss on the correct answer (ignoring the NRM targets), or (b) directly training to match NRM probabilities from the start. The necessity and separate benefit of the "distribution correction" stage is not quantified.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: An experimental ablation on the ELA dataset using the same model architecture and LoRA setup: 1) Model trained only with standard next‑token prediction (phase 1 only). 2) Model trained with the phase 2 objective from initialization. 3) The proposed two‑stage method. Compare the resulting ICC curves and IRT parameter accuracy (Pearson/RMSE) for all three. Analysis should also track if phase 1 serves as a necessary initialization for phase 2 to converge.

### Open Question 5
- **Question**: How do the scaling laws observed (performance improving with model size from 1.7B to 14B parameters) extrapolate to even larger modern LLMs (70B+ parameters), and is there a point of diminishing returns for this specific psychometric simulation task?  
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] Results show clear performance gains with larger Qwen models (e.g., Qwen‑14B > Qwen‑8B > Qwen‑4B for discrimination modeling). The introduction notes hardware limitations prevented experimentation with the 32B model as frequently, and the 70B Llama variant was "difficult." The paper states: "the rapid evolution of generative models… suggests that the predictive accuracy… will likely improve over time as base model capabilities increase."  
- **Why unresolved**: The largest model evaluated is 14B parameters. The paper posits scaling will help but does not test it. Given the task involves nuanced, fine‑grained ability simulation rather than broad knowledge, it is an open question whether large gains continue with scale or if the task hits a ceiling due to limitations in the training data (small item pool), the discrete ability descriptor framework, or fundamental mismatches between LLM training objectives and IRT theory.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A follow‑up study fine‑tuning the same methodology (LoRA, two‑stage) on a larger, more diverse item pool using state‑of‑the‑art open‑weight models at 70B+ parameter scale (e.g., Llama 3.1 70B, Qwen 3 72B). Performance should be tracked across model sizes (including intermediate sizes like 32B, 70B) to fit a scaling curve and identify inflection points. Comparison must control for training compute and data.

## Limitations
- Implementation details are not fully specified: prompt template and answer tokenization, Stage 2 loss weighting and early stopping, and handling of zero‑count bins in NRM fitting.
- The method still depends on historical response data, undermining the claim of eliminating field testing.
- Reported correlations are modest (≤0.5) with uncertain generalizability to items with many options (e.g., 7‑option BEA items) and missing discrimination evaluation on BEA.

## Confidence
- **Outcome (accurate IRT parameter estimation)**: *Medium*. Positive results exist, but reproducibility hinges on unresolved implementation choices and modest effect sizes.
- **Discrete ability descriptor conditioning**: *Low*. Introduced without ablation; neighboring literature provides no support; risk of coarse or ambiguous labels.
- **NRM smoothing as stable target**: *Medium*. Contrast with raw proportions is argued but not directly validated by a controlled experiment.
- **Two‑stage distribution alignment**: *Medium*. The loss is specified, but no calibration checks confirm that LLM probabilities match NRM targets.

## Next Checks
1. *Prompt template ablation*: Systematically vary answer choice encoding (single letters, full text, token‑level aggregation) and measure impact on ICC reconstruction correlation.
2. *Loss aggregation study*: Compare alternative Stage 2 loss formulations (per‑item mean, per‑batch sum, sample weighting) and assess sensitivity of recovered difficulty/discrimination parameters.
3. *NRM smoothing sensitivity*: Introduce Laplace smoothing (varying epsilon) and L2 regularization during NRM fitting; evaluate robustness of IRT estimates, especially for items with sparse extreme‑ability bins.
