# Systematic Characterization of Minimal Deep Learning Architectures: A Unified Analysis of Convergence, Pruning, and Quantization

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.17987  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17987  
- **Reference Count:** 24  
- **Primary Result:** Minimal stable parameter counts are dataset‑dependent; deeper models tolerate higher pruning rates; 8‑bit quantization gaps increase with dataset complexity.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the challenge of identifying minimal deep learning architectures that reliably solve image classification tasks while accounting for convergence stability, pruning tolerance, and quantization robustness. Through systematic design sweeps over architectural parameters (depth, layer shapes) for DNNs, CNNs, and ViTs across MNIST, Fashion‑MNIST, and CIFAR‑10, the authors uncover universal convergence regimes (unstable, stable learning, overfitting). Key findings include: model size (total learnable parameters) is the dominant factor for stable convergence; deeper networks exhibit greater pruning resilience (up to ~60% redundancy); and quantization‑induced accuracy loss grows with dataset complexity, hitting up to 14.8% for CNNs on CIFAR‑10. The work offers a unified framework for minimal architecture design and compression.

## Method Summary
The study employs a two‑phase experimental protocol. Phase 1 sweeps architectural layer shapes (increasing, decreasing, uniform widths) across various model sizes to test shape invariance. Phase 2 narrows to uniform‑layer networks and systematically varies total parameter count to map convergence regimes. From these, minimal stable models are selected for two compression analyses: (1) iterative L1 pruning applied in 10% increments (10%–100%), measuring accuracy degradation, and (2) 8‑bit quantization‑aware training (W8A8, symmetric weights, asymmetric activations), comparing final accuracy to full‑precision baselines. Experiments are repeated multiple times (30 for DNNs, 10 for CNNs/ViTs) to ensure reliability. This approach isolates the effects of parameter count, depth, and dataset complexity on convergence, pruning tolerance, and quantization robustness.

## Key Results
- Minimal stable parameter counts are dataset‑dependent: ~4 learnable parameters for DNNs on MNIST, ~4 CNN channels, ~3 ViT hidden dimensions.
- Deeper architectures tolerate higher safe pruning rates (up to ~60% parameter redundancy) compared to shallower ones.
- Quantization gaps widen with dataset complexity, reaching up to 14.8% accuracy drop for CNNs on CIFAR‑10, and are most severe for smaller, unstable models.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Size‑Dominance in Convergence
- **Claim:** Total learnable parameters are the dominant factor for convergence stability, overriding architectural shape once a dataset‑specific threshold is exceeded.
- **Mechanism:** Sufficient parameters allow the network to capture the data manifold’s complexity, reducing optimization variance. Below the threshold, shape variations become consequential and lead to unstable learning.
- **Evidence:** The abstract notes that “size of the learnable parameters has a dominant influence on the convergence profile compared to topological variations.” Section III.A observes that “the convergence curves for varying layer shapes largely overlap once the network size exceeds a certain parameter count,” confirming size‑dominance.
- **Break condition:** Parameter count falls below the minimal stable threshold for the dataset (e.g., ~4 parameters on MNIST).

### Mechanism 2: Depth Increases Pruning Tolerance
- **Claim:** Network depth enhances resilience to weight pruning by distributing representational capacity across layers, delaying catastrophic information loss.
- **Mechanism:** Deeper models possess higher inherent parameter redundancy; pruning uniformly removes weights, and each layer can compensate for adjacent losses, yielding a more graceful degradation curve.
- **Evidence:** The abstract states that “deeper architectures are more resilient to pruning than shallower ones, with parameter redundancy as high as 60%.” Section IV.B shows that within each model family, “wider or deeper configurations tolerate higher pruning before failure” and that “the failing region begins at lower pruning ratios” for ViTs compared to CNNs/DNNs.
- **Break condition:** If the model is under‑parameterized (unstable), additional depth may not prevent pruning collapse.

### Mechanism 3: Quantization Gap Scales with Complexity and Capacity
- **Claim:** 8‑bit quantization accuracy loss grows with dataset complexity and shrinks with increasing model capacity (within the stable regime).
- **Mechanism:** Complex datasets (e.g., CIFAR‑10) require finer decision boundaries; quantization introduces discretization noise that erodes subtle weight distinctions. Larger models achieve higher decision margins that absorb this noise.
- **Evidence:** The abstract reports that “quantization impacts models with fewer learnable parameters more severely and has a larger effect on harder image datasets.” Section IV.C quantifies the gap: on CIFAR‑10, CNNs show a 1.5%‑2.5% gap at larger channel counts, while ViTs show 4%‑6%; on MNIST, gaps are “negligible… typically declining by no more than 3%.”
- **Break condition:** For very simple datasets (MNIST), even minimal stable models show near‑zero gaps; for complex datasets (CIFAR‑10), larger stable models may retain a persistent gap (~4‑6%).

## Foundational Learning
- **Three‑phase convergence dynamics**  
  *Why needed:* Interpreting convergence curves is essential to locate the minimal parameter count for stable learning, a prerequisite for meaningful pruning and quantization analysis.  
  *Quick check:* What characterizes the “stable learning” regime in the convergence profiles, and why must pruning only be applied to models within this regime?

- **L1 pruning sensitivity and parameter redundancy**  
  *Why needed:* Understanding the three pruning regimes (stable, sharp decline, collapse) and how they shift with depth is key to determining safe compression rates for a minimal architecture.  
  *Quick check:* How does the “safe pruning window” change as network depth increases, and what does this imply about the relationship between depth and effective redundancy?

- **Quantization‑Aware Training (QAT) and precision robustness**  
  *Why needed:* To interpret the 8‑bit quantization gaps, one must understand how reduced numerical precision interacts with the learned decision margin and model capacity.  
  *Quick check:* Why does 8‑bit quantization hurt performance more on CIFAR‑10 than on MNIST, even for models of similar size?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map** (plain text)

DNN: Input -> Fully Connected layer(s) with ReLU (variable depth, width) -> Output  
CNN: Input -> 3x3 Convolution (C channels) -> ReLU -> Flatten -> Linear classifier -> Output  
ViT: Input -> Patch extraction (7x7 grid) -> Linear projection (hidden dim D) -> 2 Transformer Encoder blocks -> Class token -> Linear classifier -> Output  

**Critical path**
1. **Phase 1 (Shape Sweep):** Train architectures with varying layer shapes (increasing, decreasing, equal) to test shape invariance.  
2. **Phase 2 (Size Sweep):** Based on Phase 1, use uniform‑layer architectures and vary total parameter count to map convergence regimes.  
3. **Pruning:** For each architecture/dataset, select the best performing stable model from Phase 2; apply L1 pruning in 10% increments (10%–100%) and record test accuracy.  
4. **Quantization:** Retrain the same stable models with 8‑bit QAT and compare final test accuracy to the full‑precision baseline.

**Design tradeoffs**
- Sweep breadth vs. cost: Phase 1 explored many shapes for DNNs but was computationally prohibitive for CNNs/ViTs, so Phase 2 narrowed to uniform shapes to isolate parameter‑count effects.  
- Repetition count: DNNs repeated 30 times for statistical robustness; CNNs/ViTs only 10 times due to higher per‑run cost, accepting slightly higher variance.  
- Dataset selection: Used MNIST, Fashion‑MNIST, CIFAR‑10 for increasing complexity; did not explore other modalities or tasks.

**Failure signatures**
- **Convergence:** Mean test accuracy < dataset‑dependent threshold (e.g., 80%) with high variance (±10%+) indicates an unstable region.  
- **Pruning:** Sharp accuracy drop (>5% absolute) within a single 10% pruning increment signals crossing from safe to collapse regime.  
- **Quantization:** Accuracy gap > 2–3% between full‑precision and 8‑bit QAT on a stable model indicates sensitivity; a widening gap with dataset complexity is expected but may be unacceptable for deployment.

**First 3 experiments**
1. Train a 1‑layer DNN on MNIST with hidden nodes = [1,2,3,5,10,20,30,50,100,200,500,1000] for each topology (▷, ◁, □). Repeat 30 times. Plot convergence (test accuracy vs. param count) to observe shape invariance above ~4 parameters.  
2. Train a CNN on MNIST with convolutional channels = [1,2,3,5,10,20,32]. Repeat 10 times. Identify minimal channel count for stable 99%+ accuracy (expected ~4 channels).  
3. For the CNN with 4 channels on MNIST (stable), apply L1 pruning from 10% to 100% in 10% steps. Determine safe pruning rate (expected ~60% where accuracy remains within 1% of baseline).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Why does 8‑bit quantization sometimes improve accuracy in Vision Transformers for certain hidden dimensions, and which architectural components drive this effect?**  
   *Basis in paper:* The conclusion states that “quantizing ViT improved performance for some dimensions, opening a promising research direction to investigate the relationship between its components and quantization.”  
   *Why unresolved:* The phenomenon is reported but not analyzed; it is unclear which ViT modules (e.g., attention, MLP) are responsible or what the underlying mechanism is.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Ablation studies quantizing individual ViT modules (attention heads, feed‑forward networks) across hidden dimensions, comparing accuracy and robustness to full‑precision baselines.

2. **What theoretical explanation accounts for the universal emergence of three learning regimes (unstable, stable, overfitting) as parameter count increases across diverse architectures?**  
   *Basis in paper:* Convergence results consistently show these regimes for DNNs, CNNs, and ViTs, but the paper offers no theoretical justification.  
   *Why unresolved:* Understanding why parameter count alone dictates regime transitions could inform minimal architecture design and optimization; the empirical characterization lacks explanatory power.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Analytical work linking parameter count to loss landscape properties (e.g., curvature, mode connectivity) or optimization dynamics (e.g., gradient norm evolution), validated across architectures.

3. **How do architectural hyperparameters beyond width (e.g., ViT patch size, number of heads; CNN kernel size, stride) affect minimal stable parameter counts and compression robustness?**  
   *Basis in paper:* The study fixed these factors to isolate width effects, leaving their influence unexplored.  
   *Why unresolved:* These factors may significantly interact with width/depth, altering stability thresholds and pruning/quantization sensitivity; ignoring them limits the framework’s applicability.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Systematic sweeps varying these hyperparameters while controlling for total parameter count, measuring convergence stability, pruning tolerance, and quantization gaps.

4. **Do the findings on minimal stable architectures and compression generalize to non‑image data (e.g., text, audio) and more complex tasks (e.g., detection, segmentation)?**  
   *Basis in paper:* Experiments were restricted to image classification on MNIST, Fashion‑MNIST, and CIFAR‑10.  
   *Why unresolved:* Different modalities and tasks have distinct inductive biases and difficulty levels; the identified parameter thresholds and compression behaviors may not hold universally.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Replicate the methodology on diverse datasets (e.g., GLUE for NLP, AudioSet) and tasks, comparing minimal stable parameter counts and compression profiles.

## Limitations
- **Generalization beyond tested datasets:** Minimal stable parameter counts (e.g., ~4) are derived from MNIST/FMNIST/CIFAR‑10; scalability to higher‑dimensional data (e.g., ImageNet) is unclear.
- **Causality of pruning tolerance:** The paper correlates depth with pruning resilience but does not isolate depth from width effects in deeper ViTs/CNNs, leaving the depth‑vs‑effective‑redundancy relationship ambiguous.
- **Quantization mechanism specificity:** The claim that quantization gaps narrow with increased capacity relies on QAT; the relative impact of QAT implementation details (e.g., straight‑through estimator vs. learned rounding) versus architectural scale is unexplored.

## Confidence
- **Size‑dominance in convergence:** High confidence. Supported by extensive parameter sweeps and repeated trials across model families; corpus provides no contradictory evidence.
- **Depth‑pruning tolerance:** Medium confidence. Robust empirical trend within stable regimes, but confounding between depth and total parameters weakens causal attribution.
- **Quantization scaling with dataset complexity:** Low confidence. Observed correlation is strong, but mechanistic explanation (decision margin) is speculative and not validated against alternative hypotheses.

## Next Checks
1. Replicate minimal size threshold on a complex dataset (e.g., ImageNet subset) to test if the ~4‑parameter finding generalizes or is an artifact of low‑dimensional data.
2. Ablate depth vs. width in ViTs: Train ViTs with constant parameter count but varying depth/width ratios, then apply uniform L1 pruning to isolate depth’s independent contribution to pruning tolerance.
3. Compare QAT implementations: Re‑run quantization experiments with alternative QAT methods (e.g., Learned Rounding vs. STE) to confirm whether the observed dataset‑complexity gap persists across techniques.
