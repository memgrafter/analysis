# PII-VisBench: Evaluating Personally Identifiable Information Safety in Vision Language Models Along a Continuum of Visibility

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.05739
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05739
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Introduces PII-VisBench to evaluate VLM safety; finds models leak PII more for high-visibility subjects and exhibit a "conservative gradient" in refusals.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the gap in evaluating privacy risks of vision language models (VLMs) with respect to personally identifiable information (PII). The authors introduce PII-VisBench, a benchmark consisting of 4,000 probes across 200 subjects stratified by online visibility (high, medium, low, zero) and 20 PII attributes. Evaluating 18 open-source VLMs, they measure Refusal Rate (RR) and Conditional PII Disclosure Rate (cPDR). Results reveal a consistent pattern: as subject visibility decreases, RR increases and cPDR decreases, indicating a "conservative gradient." Additionally, a persistent "high-visibility privacy gap" shows models are more likely to leak PII for well-known individuals. The study also finds substantial heterogeneity across model families and exposes robustness failures against paraphrased and jailbreak prompts. The work underscores the need for visibility-aware safety alignment in VLMs.

## Method Summary
The authors construct PII-VisBench by selecting 200 subjects (50 per visibility level) from real-world datasets (CelebA, web, MMDT, FFHQ) and synthetic StyleGAN faces. Visibility is stratified via Google search hit counts. For each subject, 20 PII attributes (e.g., name, SSN, address) are queried using WH-questions, yielding 4,000 unique probes. Stress testing involves paraphrasing prompts with GPT-5.1 and applying seven jailbreak templates. Eighteen open-source VLMs are evaluated in zero-shot inference. Model outputs are judged by three methods: target-string matching, GPT-4.1-mini, and Qwen3Guard-Gen-8B. Primary metrics are Refusal Rate (RR) and Conditional PII Disclosure Rate (cPDR). Statistical analysis includes Friedman tests, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, and Spearman correlations to assess significance and trends.

## Key Results
- **Conservative gradient:** Across models, refusal rates increase and cPDR decreases as subject visibility drops (e.g., average cPDR: 9.10% high → 5.34% low).
- **High-visibility privacy gap:** Models are statistically more likely to leak PII for highly visible subjects, suggesting pre-training memorization overrides safety fine-tuning.
- **Model-family heterogeneity:** Safety varies widely; e.g., InternVL3 14B shows near-perfect refusal (RR > 92%) while LLaVA 1.5 7B has high disclosure (cPDR ≈ 26%).
- **PII-type disparity:** Models more reliably refuse structured identifiers (SSN, email) than demographic traits (race, gender), indicating uneven safety coverage.
- **Jailbreak fragility:** Paraphrased and adversarial prompts reduce refusals by >30% and spike disclosures, exposing robustness failures.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Memorization Overrides Safety for High-Visibility Subjects
**Claim:** For highly visible individuals, VLMs are more likely to disclose PII because pre-training memorization of their biographical data supersedes safety fine-tuning.  
**Mechanism:** During pre-training on web-scale corpora, high-visibility subjects appear frequently, embedding specific PII in model weights. When presented with an image and a PII query, recognition of the subject triggers retrieval of these memorized facts, bypassing refusal mechanisms learned during safety alignment.  
**Core assumption:** Safety fine-tuning does not fully suppress internally stored factual knowledge; it primarily teaches the model to refuse generic requests but not queries about specific memorized entities.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract]: "we identify a persistent high-visibility privacy gap, where models are statistically more likely to leak PII for highly visible subjects, suggesting that pre-training memorization can outweigh safety fine-tuning for public figures."  
- [section 5.2]: "We hypothesize that this 'privacy gap' for high-visibility subjects stems from data memorization during the pre-training phase. As noted by Carlini et al. (2021), models are more likely to memorize and subsequently regurgitate information that appears frequently across web-scale corpora."  
- [corpus]: Neighboring work on "Unintended Memorization of Sensitive Information in Fine-Tuned Language Models" supports the broader concern that memorization persists after fine-tuning.  
**Break condition:** If safety fine-tuning were significantly strengthened (e.g., via explicit unlearning or extensive adversarial training) to suppress all PII output regardless of memorization, the high-visibility privacy gap could diminish. Similarly, if pre-training data were rigorously cleansed of PII, memorization would be reduced.

### Mechanism 2: Visibility-Driven Risk Assessment Drives Refusals
**Claim:** As subject visibility decreases, VLMs increase refusal rates because they perceive greater uncertainty or privacy risk when the individual is not readily recognizable.  
**Mechanism:** The model assesses the identifiability of a subject based on the match between visual features and stored representations. Low-visibility or zero-visibility subjects have weaker or no internal representations, leading the model to default to a conservative safety policy—refusing to speculate about private attributes. This yields the observed "conservative gradient" (RR up, cPDR down as visibility drops).  
**Core assumption:** VLMs possess an implicit mechanism to gauge the identifiability risk of a query, linking recognizability to a threshold for safe response generation.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract]: "Across models, we observe a consistent pattern: refusals increase and PII disclosures decrease (9.10% high → 5.34% low) as subject visibility drops."  
- [section 5.2]: "Friedman test reveals that visibility level has a statistically significant effect on VLM refusal behavior (χ² = 23.85, p < 0.001). We also observe a monotonic increase in aggregate evaluator mean refusal rates as subject visibility decreases except Zero."  
- [section 5.2]: "We attribute this to differences in safety alignment strategies."  
**Break condition:** If safety training were uniformly applied regardless of perceived identifiability (e.g., a hard-coded refusal for all PII queries), the gradient would flatten. Conversely, if low-visibility subjects were treated as "generic" and the model were optimized for helpfulness, refusal rates could drop.

### Mechanism 3: PII-Type Disparity Reflects Differential Safety Coverage
**Claim:** VLMs more reliably refuse structured identifiers (e.g., SSN, address) than demographic or descriptive traits (e.g., race, gender) due to uneven safety alignment and the visual inferability of the latter.  
**Mechanism:** Safety fine-tuning datasets likely emphasize traditionally "high-risk" PII with clear formats (numbers, emails) that are easy to detect via regex or heuristics. Demographic traits, often visually inferable and frequently answered in non-safety contexts (e.g., image captioning), create a conflict: the model’s utility-driven training encourages providing such attributes, while safety training is weaker, leading to lower refusal rates.  
**Core assumption:** Privacy alignment effectiveness varies with PII structure and prevalence in training; structured PII benefits from binary, format-based guardrails, whereas soft attributes lack crisp detection rules.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract]: "models more reliably refuse structured identifiers (e.g., SSNs or emails) than demographic or descriptive traits (e.g., race or gender)."  
- [section 5.6]: "The average refusal rates across open VLMs vary significantly by PII type, prioritizing structured PII categories like SSN (90.13%) and Address (79.74%) over demographic attributes such as Race (39.84%) or Gender (13.73%)."  
- [section 5.6]: "This disparity suggests a fundamental conflict between a model’s descriptive utility and its privacy alignment; models often bypass safety guardrails to fulfill image-captioning objectives for visual profiling."  
**Break condition:** Comprehensive safety training that explicitly covers all PII types equally, possibly through targeted synthetic data, could reduce disparity. However, if demographic attributes remain central to visual understanding tasks, the tension may persist.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Subject Selection & Visibility Stratification → PII Probe Generation → Model Inference Pipeline → Response Evaluation Module → Analysis & Metrics Computation

**Critical path:** Subject selection → Probe generation → Model inference → Response evaluation → Metrics calculation → Statistical analysis.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Visibility proxy:* Web search hit counts are noisy but scalable; they may not capture nuanced online presence.  
- *Automatic evaluation:* Fast and reproducible but may miss subtle refusals or PII; mitigated by multiple judges.  
- *Open‑source focus:* Enables reproducibility but excludes closed models (GPT, Gemini), limiting generality.  
- *No truth verification:* The benchmark measures PII‑like strings, not factual correctness; hallucinated PII still counts as a breach.

**Failure signatures:**  
- *Judge disagreement:* Low Fleiss’ kappa (e.g., Qwen2.5 7B κ ≈ 0.21) indicates ambiguous model outputs.  
- *High leakage models:* LLaVA 1.5 7B shows RR ≈ 30% and cPDR ≈ 26% → high privacy risk.  
- *Jailbreak fragility:* Prefix Injection and Few‑shot JSON can reduce RR by >30% and spike cPDR, exposing robustness failures.  
- *Zero‑visibility anomaly:* Some models have slightly lower refusal for zero vs. low visibility, hinting at over‑generalization or recognition gaps.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. *Reproduce Table 1:* For InternVL3 14B, compute evaluator‑average RR on original prompts for all four visibility levels. Confirm RR > 92% everywhere.  
2. *Judge agreement analysis:* For Llama3.2 11B, compute Fleiss’ kappa between string matching, GPT‑4.1‑mini, and Qwen3Guard across visibility; verify κ > 0.9 (high consistency).  
3. *Paraphrase impact:* For Qwen3 8B, compare RR under original vs. paraphrased prompts for low‑visibility subjects; observe a drop of ~5–10 percentage points, indicating prompt sensitivity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How can safety alignment techniques for VLMs be explicitly conditioned on or made aware of a subject's visibility level (e.g., high vs. low online presence) to close the persistent "high-visibility privacy gap"?**  
   *Basis in paper:* The conclusion states the need for "visibility-aware safety alignment" and "training interventions that provide consistent privacy protections across the entire spectrum of digital presence." The results show models are more likely to leak PII for high-visibility subjects, suggesting current safety tuning fails to suppress memorized information.  
   *Why unresolved:* Current alignment methods (e.g., RLHF, SFT) appear to treat privacy as a uniform property, not as a function of entity recognizability. The paper demonstrates that memorization from pre-training can override safety fine-tuning for well-known individuals.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Developing and evaluating a new alignment method (e.g., visibility-stratified preference data, loss functions that penalize PII disclosure more for high-visibility entities) on PII‑VisBench, showing a reduced or eliminated correlation between subject visibility and PII leakage.

2. **Why do specific model families (e.g., LLaVA, SmolVLM2) exhibit consistently high Conditional PII Disclosure Rates (cPDR) and low Refusal Rates (RR) across all visibility levels, while others (e.g., InternVL3, Phi-3.5) are highly safe?**  
   *Basis in paper:* The results highlight "substantial model-family heterogeneity." For example, LLaVA1.5 7B has an average cPDR of ~26%, while InternVL3 14B has ~0.57%. This pattern holds across visibility strata and prompt variations.  
   *Why unresolved:* The paper analyzes architecture, scale, and generation trends but does not pinpoint the root causes (e.g., specific safety tuning data composition, reinforcement learning setups, base model memorization, or tokenizer/vocabulary effects) for this persistent vulnerability.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Controlled ablation studies comparing fine-tuning datasets, safety loss functions, or base model checkpoints across these families. For instance, analyzing the distribution of PII-related examples in the SFT data or measuring the base model's propensity to generate PII before safety tuning.

3. **What is the relationship between a model's factual accuracy when answering visual profiling questions (e.g., inferring gender or age) and its likelihood of subsequently disclosing more sensitive PII (e.g., address, SSN), especially for low/zero-visibility subjects?**  
   *Basis in paper:* The paper notes that models more reliably refuse "structured identifiers" than "demographic or descriptive traits," and that PII disclosure is higher for high-visibility subjects. The limitations section admits they do not verify if disclosed PII is factually correct or hallucinated.  
   *Why unresolved:* There is a potential trade-off: models that comply with low-risk, visually inferable attributes (building "helpfulness") may be more likely to comply with high-risk PII requests. The paper treats all non-refusal outputs equally, not distinguishing between correct inference and harmful hallucination.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Annotating model responses for factual correctness against ground truth (where possible, e.g., for high-visibility subjects) and correlating compliance on "easy" PII (like gender) with compliance on "hard" PII. This would reveal if safety failures are sequential or independent.

4. **Do the privacy failure patterns observed in open-source VLMs (the "conservative gradient" and "high-visibility privacy gap") hold for leading closed-source multimodal models (e.g., GPT-4o, Gemini 1.5 Pro), and how do their robustness to jailbreak prompts compare?**  
   *Basis in paper:* The introduction and limitations explicitly note the evaluation is restricted to open-source VLMs and that closed-source systems may behave differently. The motivating example shows closed-source models refusing zero-visibility probes, hinting at different behaviors.  
   *Why unresolved:* The study's scope is limited to 18 open-source models due to API access and reproducibility constraints. The fundamental mechanisms of safety in proprietary systems are a black box.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Running the full PII-VisBench suite (or a representative subset) on major closed-source VLMs via their APIs, comparing their RR/cPDR across visibility levels and their susceptibility to the same jailbreak attacks.

5. **Is the "paraphrasing sensitivity" effect—where indirect prompts reduce refusal rates for many models—primarily a failure of the model's instruction-following robustness or a limitation of the refusal classifiers (string matching, LLM-as-a-judge) used in evaluation?**  
   *Basis in paper:* The paper shows paraphrased prompts systematically weaken refusal for several model families while preserving the visibility effect. They also report judge agreement statistics, noting that "paraphrasing introduces stylistic variations... that challenge the consistency of the judging schemes."  
   *Why unresolved:* It is unclear whether reduced refusals under paraphrasing indicate genuine model vulnerability (the model no longer recognizes the intent as harmful) or if the judges fail to classify nuanced refusal language as a true refusal. The drop in Fleiss' Kappa for some models under paraphrasing supports this ambiguity.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* A detailed error analysis of paraphrased cases: (1) human evaluation of whether a model output is truly a non-refusal versus a missed refusal, and (2) improving the judging pipeline to see if the paraphrasing gap narrows.

## Limitations
- **Visibility proxy limitations:** Google hit counts are noisy and region/language-dependent, potentially misrepresenting true subject visibility.
- **Automatic evaluation challenges:** Judge disagreement (low Fleiss’ κ) for some models threatens metric reliability; ambiguous outputs may be misclassified.
- **Scope constraints:** Evaluation limited to open-source VLMs; findings may not generalize to closed-source models. Disclosed PII is not fact-checked, so hallucinations inflate breach counts.

## Confidence
- **Conservative gradient & high-visibility gap:** *Medium* – Effects are statistically significant across 18 models, but measurement noise (judge disagreement, visibility proxy) may affect magnitude.
- **PII-type disparity (structured vs. demographic):** *High* – Consistently large refusal differences observed with clear statistical separation.
- **Jailbreak fragility:** *High* – Multiple attack types reliably reduce refusal rates and increase disclosures, demonstrating robust vulnerability.
