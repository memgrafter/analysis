# SOMP: Scalable Gradient Inversion for Large Language Models via Subspace-Guided Orthogonal Matching Pursuit
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.16761  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16761  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** SOMP achieves state-of-the-art gradient inversion on large-batch LLMs, e.g., 76.4 ROUGE-L on IMDB with GPT-J-6B at batch size 16 (vs. ~31.6 for prior art), and scales up to B=128.

## Executive Summary
SOMP introduces a three-stage framework to reconstruct private training texts from a single aggregated gradient under federated learning with large language models. The attack leverages head-wise geometric structure in transformer gradients to guide token selection, beam decoding, and final sparse recovery via Orthogonal Matching Pursuit (OMP). By treating the mixed gradient as a sparse combination of candidate gradient atoms, SOMP overcomes the signal mixing problem that plagues prior methods in large-batch regimes. Evaluations across multiple models, datasets, and batch sizes demonstrate substantial improvements over DAGER, LAMP, and GRAB, with robustness to long sequences, multilingual data, and FedAvg.

## Method Summary
SOMP operates on a white-box aggregated gradient `g_mix` from a batch of size `B`. **Stage I** decomposes the first-layer query gradient into per-head subspaces (via SVD) and scores every vocabulary token at each position using three components: (1) subspace alignment (distance of token’s query projection to head subspace), (2) cross-head consistency (standard deviation of alignment across active heads), and (3) head-aware sparsity (projection onto FFN gradient slices). The top-`k` tokens form a pool `P`. **Stage II** performs diverse beam search over `P`, generating candidate sentences. At each step, candidates are scored by a combination of geometry (distance of head-wise hidden states to second-layer query subspaces) and a language model prior, with penalties for token and n-gram repetitions. **Stage III** computes gradient atoms for each candidate using a fixed surrogate label (e.g., all-zeros), models `g_mix` as a sparse linear combination of these atoms, and applies Orthogonal Matching Pursuit to select the `B` most aligned atoms. Final coefficients are refined via ridge regression, and the corresponding sentences are output as the reconstructed batch.

## Key Results
- SOMP dramatically outperforms prior attacks: e.g., 76.4 vs. 31.6 ROUGE-L on GPT-J-6B/IMDB at B=16.
- The method maintains high reconstruction fidelity up to B=128 and shows strong multilingual performance.
- Ablations confirm that both OMP and geometric guidance are essential; removal causes severe degradation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core insight is that large-batch gradients, while mixed, preserve low-dimensional geometric structure across transformer heads. Head-wise query gradients span informative subspaces that reflect token identity and position. By projecting tokens and hidden states onto these subspaces, SOMP measures how well a candidate explains the mixed gradient, a signal robust to averaging. The sparsity of the gradient mixture (only `B` contributing examples) justifies the OMP step, which greedily selects the best-matching atoms and solves a regularized least-squares problem to de-mix. The three-stage cascade progressively narrows the search space without sacrificing true tokens, enabling scalable recovery even when `B` is large.

## Foundational Learning
- **Gradient Inversion Attacks**: *Why needed*: Reveal privacy risks in federated learning; demonstrate that gradients leak data. *Quick check*: Can you reconstruct a single image by matching its gradient via optimization?  
- **Transformer Head-Wise Analysis**: *Why needed*: Different attention heads specialise; their gradients separately encode features. *Quick check*: Do SVD singular values of a head’s query gradient decay rapidly, indicating low rank?  
- **Orthogonal Matching Pursuit**: *Why needed*: Efficiently solves sparse approximation problems. *Quick check*: Given a signal that is a linear combination of `s` atoms from a dictionary, does OMP recover the correct support within `s` iterations?  
- **Sparse Signal Recovery**: *Why needed*: The mixed gradient is a sparse combination of per-example gradients in a suitable basis. *Quick check*: Is the representation of `g_mix` using true per-sample gradients approximately sparse (few non-zero coefficients)??  
- **Large-Batch Gradient Aggregation**: *Why needed*: Federated averaging aggregates over many clients; the attack must disentangle this mixture. *Quick check*: How does reconstruction quality change as batch size increases?  
- **Beam Search with Diversity**: *Why needed*: To generate many plausible text candidates from a limited token pool. *Quick check*: Do diversity penalties reduce repeated n-grams in beam outputs?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:
g_mix & model weights → Stage I (head subspace estimation, token scoring, top‑k) → token pool P → Stage II (second‑layer subspace, diverse beam search) → candidate set X → Stage III (gradient atom generation, OMP with ridge regression) → final reconstruction.

Critical path: Stage I narrows the vocabulary to a promising token pool; Stage II expands this into a diverse set of sentence candidates; Stage III uses sparse recovery to pick exactly `B` candidates that best explain `g_mix`.

Design tradeoffs:
- **Token pool size (`k`)**: Larger `k` improves recall but increases beam search and OMP cost.
- **Surrogate label**: Must be fixed and independent of true labels; a neutral label (e.g., all zeros) reduces leakage but may misalign atoms.
- **Geometry vs. LM score**: Strong geometry guidance improves discrimination but can derail if subspaces are noisy; LM prior keeps candidates fluent.

Failure signatures:
- **Stage I**: Low token recall (e.g., <80% true tokens in pool) suggests thresholds are too strict or `k` too small.
- **Stage II**: Beam outputs contain many repetitions or low linguistic quality, indicating insufficient diversity or poor token pool.
- **Stage III**: OMP selects wrong candidates; inner products of true atoms with residual are low, signalling misaligned atoms or candidate set lacking ground truth.

First experiments:
1. **Token Pool Recall Test**: On a known batch, compute the fraction of true tokens present in the pool per position; aim for >90% at the chosen `k`.
2. **Atom Alignment Check**: For a candidate set containing the true sentences, compare normalized inner products `|⟨g_i, g_mix⟩|/||g_i||`; true candidates should rank near top.
3. **Small‑Batch Baseline**: Run SOMP with B=1 (single‑example gradient) to verify near‑perfect reconstruction, validating gradient atom computation and OMP.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper does not explicitly list open questions. However, its limitations and next checks suggest the following open questions:
- Surrogate label sensitivity: How does the fixed label choice affect reconstruction quality across different models, datasets, and batch sizes?
- Hyperparameter generalization: Do the tuned parameters (e.g., token pool size, beam width, geometry/LM weights) transfer to unseen architectures, languages, or larger batch sizes?
- Computational scaling: Can Stage I be efficiently approximated for very large vocabularies (e.g., >50k tokens) without sacrificing token recall or reconstruction fidelity?

## Limitations
- **Surrogate label ambiguity**: The exact fixed label for gradient atom generation is not specified; its choice may bias OMP.
- **Hyperparameter specificity**: Weights, beam width, and pool size are tuned for evaluated settings; generalisation to other architectures/languages is uncertain.
- **Computational scaling**: Stage I linearly scans the vocabulary; for huge vocabularies this becomes expensive and is not addressed.

## Confidence
- **Scalability to large batches (B up to 128)**: Medium (empirical evidence but no theoretical guarantee)
- **Superior reconstruction vs. prior attacks**: High (consistent gains across multiple models and datasets)
- **Necessity of subspace‑guided OMP**: Medium (ablations show strong contribution, but alternative sparse methods might match)

## Next Checks
1. **Surrogate label sensitivity**: Systematically vary fixed labels (zero, uniform, random class) and measure ROUGE‑L to confirm robustness.
2. **Sparsity threshold procedure**: Implement two versions of τ_m^(h) (full‑vocabulary median vs. random subset median) and compare token recall@k in Stage I.
3. **Scalability profiling**: Profile runtime/memory of Stage I across vocabulary sizes (e.g., 50k vs. 50257) and explore coarse‑filter approximations.
