# Cross-Modal Robustness Transfer (CMRT): Training Robust Speech Translation Models Using Adversarial Text

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.11933
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11933
- **Reference count**: 26
- **Primary result**: CMRT improves robustness to an adversarial inflectional attack by an average of more than 3 BLEU points across four language pairs without using adversarial speech data.

## Executive Summary
End-to-end speech translation (ST) models are vulnerable to inflectional morphology variations common in non-native speech. Generating adversarial speech for training is computationally expensive. The proposed Cross-Modal Robustness Transfer (CMRT) framework circumvents this by first aligning speech and text representations via contrastive learning and mixup training, then fine-tuning by injecting adversarial text embeddings (from a text-based attack) into the speech manifold. CMRT achieves average gains of over 3 BLEU points on an adversarial inflectional test set for four language pairs, matching the performance of models fine-tuned on synthetic adversarial speech while suffering less degradation on clean data. Effectiveness strongly correlates with the strength of speech-text semantic alignment, and the residual modality gap limits the ceiling of transfer.

## Method Summary
CMRT comprises two stages. **CMRT-TR (alignment training)** jointly optimizes an ST model and an MT model using three objectives: Word-Aligned Contrastive Learning (WACO) pulls speech frame embeddings and text token embeddings into a shared space, while mixup training stochically replaces a word’s speech representation with its text embedding, teaching the decoder to handle hybrid inputs. **CMRT-FN (robust fine-tuning)** freezes the speech encoder, generates an adversarial transcription via Speech-MORPHEUS, and builds an adversarial mixup input by injecting the corresponding adversarial text embeddings into the clean speech stream at perturbed word positions. The model is trained on this input with the standard ST loss plus asymmetric KL-divergence losses that force the output distribution to match those from clean speech and clean text inputs. No adversarial speech is synthesized, drastically cutting computational cost.

## Key Results
- **Robustness**: CMRT improves BLEU on the adversarial Morpheus test set by >3 points on average across English→German, French, Spanish, and Chinese.
- **Efficiency**: Achieves results comparable to fine-tuning with synthetic adversarial speech generated by TTS, but with significantly less clean test set degradation.
- **Alignment correlation**: Improvement in adversarial robustness strongly correlates (high cosine similarity) with speech-text word embedding alignment quality.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Shared Aligned Space Enables Transfer
A shared, aligned speech‑text semantic space allows adversarial robustness learned from textual examples to transfer to the speech modality. CMRT-TR uses WACO (contrastive loss on aligned words) and mixup to create a manifold where speech and text representations of the same concept are proximate. The decoder is trained on hybrids, so it sees inputs that combine both modalities. The core assumption is that inflectional sensitivity is a semantic/syntactic weakness independent of modality; if the representations are aligned, injecting adversarial text embeddings into the speech stream mimics non‑native speech errors. **Break condition**: Poor alignment (low cosine similarity) leaves adversarial embeddings off the speech manifold, preventing transfer.

### Mechanism 2: Adversarial Mixup with KL Regularization
Robustness is imparted by fine‑tuning on hybrid inputs where adversarial text embeddings replace clean speech segments for attacked words, with asymmetric KL divergence enforcing output consistency. The speech encoder is frozen; for each training example, Speech‑MORPHEUS generates an adversarial transcript. The adversarial text embeddings for perturbed words replace the corresponding speech embeddings in the input stream. The loss combines standard ST cross‑entropy with two asymmetric KL terms that penalize divergence of the adversarial model’s output from the outputs of clean‑speech and clean‑text models. This forces the model to produce unchanged translations despite the perturbation. **Break condition**: Mis‑tuned KL weight (λ_kl) or mismatch between the text‑based attack and natural speech errors will hinder robustness.

### Mechanism 3: Modality Gap Bounds Transfer
The effectiveness of CMRT is limited by a residual “modality gap” that contrastive learning and mixup cannot fully close. Speech embeddings inherently encode acoustic details, while text embeddings capture lexical‑syntactic content. Some robustness features are more naturally expressed in text. When adversarial text embeddings are injected, the model must reconcile them with acoustic context from the frozen speech encoder, leaving a performance gap compared to using real adversarial speech. **Break condition**: If initial alignment is weak, the modality gap remains large, capping transfer gains; advanced alignment techniques would be needed to surpass this ceiling.

## Foundational Learning

### Cross‑Modal Contrastive Learning (WACO)
**Why needed**: Aligns the speech encoder’s continuous frame embeddings with the text embedder’s discrete token embeddings, creating a common semantic manifold without which injecting text embeddings into speech would be meaningless.
**Quick check**: For a word like “running”, given its pooled speech embedding $f_s$ and text embedding $f_t$, the contrastive loss (e.g., InfoNCE) pulls this positive pair together while pushing apart mismatched word pairs from the same batch.

### Mixup Training (Manifold Interpolation)
**Why needed**: Acclimates the translation decoder to inputs that are stochastic hybrids of speech and text embeddings – exactly the format used during CMRT‑FN.
**Quick check**: With mixup probability $p^*=0.8$, a given word’s representation is chosen as speech with 80% probability and as text with 20% probability. Roughly 20% of words in a sequence will be represented by their text embeddings.

### Asymmetric KL Divergence Regularization (VAT‑style)
**Why needed**: Enforces output invariance by penalizing large changes in the predicted distribution between clean and adversarially perturbed inputs, acting as a consistency regularizer.
**Quick check**: Symmetric KL divergence would also push clean outputs toward the adversarial one; asymmetric KL allows only the adversarial output to be regularized toward each clean output (speech‑based and text‑based) independently, which is necessary to preserve clean model behavior.

### Component Map

### Critical Path
1. **Alignment Training (CMRT‑TR)**: Pre‑train MT on parallel text. Initialize ST with HuBERT encoder and shared or separate MT decoder. Jointly train on ST triples using ST loss, MT loss, WACO, and mixup loss to align modalities and teach hybrid‑input processing.
2. **Robust Fine‑Tuning (CMRT‑FN)**: Freeze speech encoder. For each batch, generate adversarial $\tilde{x}$, build adversarial mixup $e_m$, and train with ST loss + asymmetric KL against clean $a$ and $e$ outputs.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Alignment strength vs. clean performance**: Stronger alignment (higher contrastive loss weight, lower mixup probability) can slightly hurt clean ST BLEU.
- **Robustness vs. clean accuracy (λ_kl)**: Raising the KL weight improves adversarial robustness but damages clean performance; tuning is required.
- **Computational vs. data efficiency**: CMRT avoids costly TTS‑based adversarial speech generation but adds forced alignment and multi‑objective training overhead.
- **Generality vs. attack specificity**: Method targets inflectional morphology; extension to other perturbations (noise, disfluencies) is not proven.

### Failure Signatures
1. **Poor alignment**: Low word‑level cosine similarity on dev set → <1 BLEU improvement on adversarial test after CMRT‑FN.
2. **Decoder instability**: High ST loss during CMRT‑TR or clean drop >3 BLEU after CMRT‑FN without commensurate adversarial gain → mis‑configured mixup or KL.
3. **No representation convergence**: CKA similarity between CMRT‑FN and TTS‑Morpheus‑FN does not increase relative to CMRT‑TR → failed transfer.
4. **Attack coverage issues**: In homophonic languages (e.g., French), if Speech‑MORPHEUS filtering is too aggressive, few perturbations → weak robustness signal.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Alignment Strength Ablation**: Vary contrastive loss weight (λ_ctr) and mixup probability (p*). Measure dev‑set word‑level cosine similarity, clean ST BLEU, and adversarial BLEU (without fine‑tuning). Goal: establish correlation between alignment strength and robustness potential.
2. **KL Weight Sweep (λ_kl)**: For fixed CMRT‑TR model, run CMRT‑FN with λ_kl ∈ {1,2,5,8,10}. Evaluate clean and Morpheus test BLEU. Goal: replicate the robustness‑accuracy trade‑off and identify optimal λ_kl.
3. **Modality Gap Probe**: Compute CKA similarity between (a) CMRT‑TR and TTS‑Morpheus‑FN representations, and (b) CMRT‑FN and TTS‑Morpheus‑FN representations on clean and adversarial sentences. Goal: verify that CMRT‑FN moves internal representations closer to those of a model trained on real adversarial speech.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **Unified Training**: Can the two‑stage CMRT pipeline be collapsed into a single‑stage objective without sacrificing robustness? The current sequential design may be suboptimal.
2. **Alignment‑Robustness Decoupling**: Why does mixup (weaker alignment) yield comparable robustness gains to WACO alone after CMRT‑FN? Understanding this could yield simpler, more efficient alignment strategies.
3. **Modality‑Specific Features**: To what extent do acoustic or speaker‑specific cues in speech embeddings persist in the aligned space and cap cross‑modal transfer? Quantifying this gap could guide better alignment objectives.
4. **Breaking the Robustness‑Accuracy Trade‑off**: Can advanced regularization (e.g., curriculum, confidence penalties) shift the Pareto frontier to maintain clean performance at high robustness?
5. **Generalization Beyond Morphology**: Does CMRT transfer to other speech corruptions (noise, accents, disfluencies) if corresponding text‑based attacks are used? The current validation is limited to inflectional errors.

## Limitations
- **Dependency on pre‑trained HuBERT and forced alignment**, which may be inaccurate or unavailable for low‑resource languages.
- **Attack scope limited to inflectional morphology**; generalizability to other robustness scenarios (acoustic noise, semantic errors) is unproven.
- **Correlation vs. causation**: The empirical link between alignment strength and robustness does not establish a causal mechanism; confounding factors may exist.

## Confidence
- **CMRT significantly improves adversarial robustness without adversarial speech data**: *High*.
- **Robustness gains correlate with speech‑text semantic alignment strength**: *Medium*.
- **Cross‑modal transfer operates via an aligned space and adversarial mixup with KL regularization**: *Medium*.
- **Residual modality gap bounds the achievable robustness**: *Medium*.
- **Generalization beyond inflectional attacks or to low‑resource settings**: *Low*.

## Next Checks
1. **Causal Alignment‑Robustness Probing**: Train CMRT‑TR models across a wide range of λ_ctr and p*, then measure alignment quality (cosine similarity) and subsequent CMRT‑FN adversarial BLEU to test direct causality.
2. **Cross‑Attack and Cross‑Linguistic Evaluation**: Apply CMRT to new threat models (e.g., additive noise, speed perturbations) and to typologically diverse languages (e.g., Inuktitut, Mandarin) using suitable pre‑trained speech models. This tests broader generalizability.
3. **Modality Gap Reduction Interventions**: Compare standard CMRT‑FN (frozen encoder) with a variant that fine‑tunes the speech encoder slightly during CMRT‑FN. Evaluate clean/adversarial BLEU and CKA similarity to TTS‑Morpheus‑FN. Improved transfer would indicate that narrowing the modality gap can boost cross‑modal robustness.
