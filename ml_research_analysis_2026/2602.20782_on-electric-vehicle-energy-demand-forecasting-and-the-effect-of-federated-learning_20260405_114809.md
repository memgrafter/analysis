# On Electric Vehicle Energy Demand Forecasting and the Effect of Federated Learning
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.20782
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20782
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Centralized XGBoost delivers superior EVSE demand forecasting accuracy; federated bidirectional RNNs balance privacy with acceptable performance, while FL "light" configurations significantly cut energy and carbon costs.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles EVSE energy demand forecasting, complicated by intermittent patterns and data fragmentation across entities with privacy constraints. It benchmarks ARIMA, XGBoost, LSTM, GRU, and bidirectional variants under centralized and federated learning (FL) paradigms using four real-world datasets. Centralized XGBoost consistently outperforms all others in accuracy. Under FL, bidirectional RNNs (BiGRU/BiLSTM) are optimal for three datasets, though all FL models lag behind centralized XGBoost. Crucially, a lightweight FL setup (one local epoch) reduces training energy by up to 68.21% and carbon emissions by 80.6% on average versus heavier FL, with minimal accuracy sacrifice. The trade-off is clear: XGBoost excels centrally for accuracy and efficiency; FL offers privacy in heterogeneous settings but at higher communication/computation costs.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates conventional time series and machine learning models—specifically ARIMA, XGBoost, LSTM, GRU, and their bidirectional counterparts—using four actual EVSE energy consumption datasets. Models are trained in two paradigms: a traditional centralized approach and a federated learning framework where local clients train on private data and share only model updates with a central server for aggregation. Performance is measured via MASE, MAE, and R². Energy consumption and carbon emissions are tracked under two FL configurations: “light” (one local epoch per round) and “heavy” (multiple local epochs). This design isolates the effects of data distribution, model architecture, and FL hyperparameters on both predictive accuracy and operational efficiency.

## Key Results
- Centralized XGBoost significantly outperforms statistical (ARIMA) and neural network models (LSTM/GRU variants) across all error metrics and datasets.
- In FL settings, bidirectional RNNs (BiGRU/BiLSTM) achieve the best accuracy on three of four datasets, though all FL models remain less accurate than centralized XGBoost.
- FL “light” (one local epoch) reduces training energy overhead by up to 68.21% and lowers carbon emissions by 80.6% on average compared to “heavy” FL, with only a minor drop in prediction fidelity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
XGBoost’s centralized dominance stems from its ensemble strength in modeling nonlinear feature interactions and robustness to hyperparameter settings on tabular time series data. In FL, bidirectional RNNs likely succeed because their two-directional sequence processing better captures the complex temporal dependencies in intermittent EVSE demand when data is distributed non-IID across clients. The “light” FL configuration’s energy savings arise from drastically fewer local computations per round, reducing overall CPU/GPU utilization despite potentially more communication rounds.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Time Series Forecasting**  
   *Why needed:* EVSE demand exhibits trends, seasonality, and noise.  
   *Quick check:* Does the model capture autocorrelation? (ARIMA does explicitly; tree models use lag features.)
2. **Federated Learning (FL)**  
   *Why needed:* Enables model training without centralizing sensitive charging data.  
   *Quick check:* How does aggregation (FedAvg) handle statistical heterogeneity? (Performance may drop if client data distributions diverge.)
3. **Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)**  
   *Why needed:* Highly effective on tabular data with complex interactions.  
   *Quick check:* Is it prone to overfitting on small EVSE datasets? (Paper shows consistent superiority, suggesting robustness.)
4. **Recurrent Neural Networks (LSTM/GRU)**  
   *Why needed:* Designed for sequential data with memory of past inputs.  
   *Quick check:* Can they handle long-term dependencies in EVSE patterns? (Bidirectional variants help by using future context within a window.)
5. **Bidirectional RNNs**  
   *Why needed:* Process sequences both forward and backward, enriching temporal context.  
   *Quick check:* Do they improve forecasting accuracy over unidirectional RNNs in FL? (Yes, on three datasets.)
6. **Model Aggregation (FedAvg)**  
   *Why needed:* Combines local updates into a global model without raw data exchange.  
   *Quick check:* Does weighted averaging based on client data size improve fairness? (Implied but not deeply analyzed.)

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:  
Local Clients (EVSE data) → Local Model Training (ARIMA/XGBoost/LSTM/GRU/BiLSTM/BiGRU) → Parameter Update → Federated Server (aggregates updates) → Global Model → Clients (update local models)

Critical path:  
1. Each client performs local training for specified epochs.  
2. Clients transmit model parameters to server.  
3. Server computes weighted average (FedAvg) or specialized aggregation (FedXGBllr).  
4. Server broadcasts global parameters to clients.  
5. Repeat for multiple communication rounds.

Design tradeoffs:  
- **Accuracy vs. privacy:** FL sacrifices some accuracy for data privacy; centralized XGBoost is more accurate but requires data pooling.  
- **Communication vs. computation:** FL “heavy” (more local epochs) reduces rounds but increases per-client energy; FL “light” does the opposite.  
- **Model complexity:** Neural RNNs adapt better to FL’s distributed data shifts but are heavier; XGBoost is simpler but less FL-friendly.

Failure signatures:  
- **Non-IID data:** If clients have highly heterogeneous demand (e.g., residential vs. commercial), global model performance degrades.  
- **Stragglers:** Slow clients delay aggregation, especially in “heavy” FL with longer local training.  
- **Hardware heterogeneity:** Energy savings of “light” FL assume similar device capabilities; uneven hardware may skew results.

First experiments:  
1. Compare centralized vs. FL performance for each model type to quantify the privacy-accuracy gap.  
2. Test FL “light” (1 local epoch) vs. “heavy” (≥3 epochs) on energy use and accuracy trade-offs.  
3. Evaluate bidirectional vs. unidirectional RNNs in FL to confirm the bidirectional advantage across varying data heterogeneity.

## Limitations
- **Dataset scope:** Only four EVSE datasets used; findings may not generalize to other regions, charging speeds, or user behaviors.
- **FL simulation:** “Light” vs. “heavy” FL configurations may not capture real-world constraints like variable network latency, intermittent connectivity, or device heterogeneity.
- **Energy measurement:** Reported energy savings are tied to specific hardware and training protocols; robustness across diverse computational platforms is uncertain.
- **Statistical rigor:** Claims of “significant” superiority lack explicit statistical tests (p-values, confidence intervals), weakening evidence for observed differences.
- **Model coverage:** Excludes modern architectures like Transformers, which could alter performance rankings, especially in FL.

## Confidence
- **Centralized XGBoost superiority:** High – consistent across all metrics and datasets.
- **Bidirectional RNNs optimal under FL:** Medium – holds for three datasets but fails on one; reason unclear.
- **FL “light” reduces energy/carbon footprint:** Medium – reductions are large but environment-specific.
- **Overall privacy-accuracy-efficiency trade-off:** Medium – reasonable synthesis, but broader scenarios needed.

## Next Checks
1. **External validation:** Replicate the benchmark on at least two additional EVSE datasets from distinct geographic regions and infrastructure types to test generalizability.
2. **Statistical testing:** Apply rigorous paired significance tests (e.g., Wilcoxon signed-rank with multiple-testing correction) to validate reported performance differences between centralized and FL models.
3. **Energy robustness:** Measure training energy across varied hardware (CPU, GPU, edge accelerators) and realistic network conditions (bandwidth throttling, packet loss) to verify FL “light” savings claim.
