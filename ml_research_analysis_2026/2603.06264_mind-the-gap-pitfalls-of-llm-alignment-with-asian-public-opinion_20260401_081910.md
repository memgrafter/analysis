# Mind the Gap: Pitfalls of LLM Alignment with Asian Public Opinion

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.06264
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06264
- **Reference Count:** 21
- **Primary Result:** LLMs like GPT-4o-Mini and Gemini-2.5-Flash achieve high representativeness (>91%) on non-religious topics across Asia but drop to ~90% on religious items, with sharper declines for minority groups; local-language prompting reduces distributional divergence but fails to fully close gaps, and downstream bias benchmarks confirm persistent stereotyping and negative framings of some religious identities.

## Executive Summary
This paper audits the cultural alignment of large language models (LLMs) across diverse Asian societies, using religious opinions as a critical lens. Comparing model-generated opinion distributions—extracted from log-probabilities—against ground-truth Pew survey data via Jensen-Shannon Divergence and Hellinger Distance, the study reveals systematic misalignment on religious topics, especially for minority groups. While prompting in local languages and with demographic context partially improves alignment, it does not eliminate deep gaps. Downstream bias benchmarks (C.g., CrowS-Pairs, IndiBias) further confirm that these distributional mismatches manifest as stereotyping and implausible negative framings. The findings underscore how English-centric training data and majority-oriented alignment can marginalize non-Western perspectives, and why lightweight prompting interventions are insufficient for genuine cultural representation.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates multiple LLMs (GPT-4o-Mini, Gemini-2.5-Flash, Gemma-3, Llama-3.2, Mistral-7B) across 12 Asian countries/towns. Survey questions from Pew Research microdata—covering religion, governance, and demographics—are translated into local languages (e.g., Hindi, Korean, Thai) via a validated crowd-sourcing process (Cohen’s κ=0.82). For each multiple-choice question, models are prompted with temperature=0, and token-level log-probabilities are aggregated to form a probability distribution over answer options (`D_M`). Human ground-truth distributions (`D_O`) are derived from weighted Pew responses. Alignment is quantified using three metrics: representativeness (R_M, Wasserstein-based), Jensen-Shannon Divergence (AJSD), and Hellinger Distance (AHD). The same models are then evaluated on culturally specific bias benchmarks (CrowS-Pairs, IndiBias, ThaiCLI, KoBBQ) to assess downstream harms. The critical path involves baseline alignment measurement, local-language intervention testing, and correlation with benchmark performance.

## Key Results
- Non-religious topics: Most models achieve R_M > 0.91, indicating high representativeness.
- Religious items: R_M drops to ~0.90 on average, with larger declines for minority religious groups (e.g., Shia, Jain, Parsi).
- Local-language prompting reduces AJSD (distributional divergence) for some models (e.g., Gemma-3 in Sri Lanka) but AHD (total probability mass overlap) remains stable, indicating superficial rather than fundamental alignment.
- Downstream benchmarks show persistent bias: models rate negative framings of certain religious identities as more plausible than positive or neutral ones (e.g., high positive ΔELO for minority groups on IndiBias).
- Gemini exhibits high invalid/refusal rates (15–19/105) on some language-specific benchmarks, suggesting culturally inconsistent safety filters.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: English-Centric Training Data Skew
**Claim:** LLMs trained on predominantly English internet corpora systematically misalign with non-Western cultural values, particularly on religion.
**Mechanism:** Training data over-represents English-language, Western-centric viewpoints, shaping the model’s prior probability distribution to favor majority or Western-secular narratives. This under-represents minority or locally salient perspectives.
**Core Assumption:** The distribution of views in the training corpus directly determines the model’s learned likelihood function for generating opinions.
**Evidence Anchors:** The paper’s abstract and introduction explicitly state that reliance on English-centric data risks misalignment and marginalizing non-Western views. Neighboring work (e.g., Camellia, Multilingual != Multicultural) empirically documents Western/US-centric bias in multilingual models.
**Break Condition:** Training data includes sufficiently large volumes of locally-authored, culturally-grounded text from target populations so that the prior over opinions matches human survey distributions.

### Mechanism 2: Post-Training Alignment Amplification
**Claim:** Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) and instruction-tuning amplify training data skews by optimizing for majority preferences, failing to preserve minority viewpoints.
**Mechanism:** Human feedback data typically reflects the norms of rater populations (often Western, educated, internet-fluent). The resulting reward models and fine-tuned LLMs prioritize outputs aligned with these majority preferences, sharpening under-representation of minority cultural or religious perspectives already present in the base model.
**Core Assumption:** Alignment objectives applied after pretraining are not demographically neutral and can accentuate pre-existing biases.
**Evidence Anchors:** The abstract and discussion sections explicitly state that post-training alignment techniques amplify issues rather than resolve them. Indirect support is drawn from cited literature (e.g., Guo et al. 2024, Ovalle et al. 2024) on RLHF perpetuating gender/identity biases.
**Break Condition:** Alignment datasets explicitly include diverse, locally-situated human raters from target demographic groups, and the optimization objective is constrained to preserve distributional alignment with surveyed population opinions.

### Mechanism 3: Superficiality of Prompt-Based Interventions
**Claim:** Demographic priming and native-language prompting only provide surface-level alignment by activating latent knowledge but cannot reshape the model’s internal probability landscapes for deeply misaligned topics.
**Mechanism:** Changing prompt language or adding demographic context steers activations toward existing pathways, reducing distributional divergence (e.g., JSD) by concentrating probability mass on the most probable option. However, it cannot create novel, well-calibrated probability mass for under-represented minority viewpoints where the base model’s internal representations are sparse or noisy, hence Hellinger Distance remains stable.
**Core Assumption:** The model’s internal representation of minority opinions is sparse or noisy; prompting can only select from existing pathways, not generate new, accurate ones.
**Evidence Anchors:** Experimental results show AJSD decreases with local language but AHD remains resistant. The discussion notes that lightweight interventions can lead to partial improvements but are insufficient.
**Break Condition:** Requires intervention at training/fine-tuning stage (e.g., culturally-specific datasets) or architecture-level steering (e.g., activation engineering) to reshape the core representation space.

## Foundational Learning

**Distributional Alignment Metrics (Jensen-Shannon Divergence, Hellinger Distance, Wasserstein-based Representativeness)**
- **Why needed here:** These are the core quantitative tools measuring the gap between model opinion distributions (from logits/log-probs) and human survey distributions. Understanding their differences (JSD/HD sensitive to overall shape, Wasserstein accounts for option ordering) is key to interpreting results like "AJSD decreased but AHD remained stable."
- **Quick Check:** If local-language prompting lowers JSD but not Hellinger Distance, what does that imply about how the model's probability distribution changed?

**Demographic Priming & Native-Language Prompting as Lightweight Interventions**
- **Why needed here:** The paper tests these as primary, accessible methods to mitigate bias. Results show they are partial and inconsistent, which is a central finding. Understanding their mechanism (contextual steering) vs. limits (cannot fix representation gaps) is crucial.
- **Quick Check:** Why might prompting in a local language reduce Jensen-Shannon Divergence for a model like Gemma-3 in Sri Lanka but not improve its Hellinger Distance?

**Downstream Bias Benchmarks (CrowS-Pairs, IndiBias, ThaiCLI, KoBBQ) as Manifestation Tests**
- **Why needed here:** The paper argues that high-level distributional gaps (measured via survey alignment) should manifest as concrete harms in these culturally-specific benchmarks. They test stereotyping, plausibility bias, and cultural norm adherence. Results show persistent negative framing preferences.
- **Quick Check:** What does a high positive ΔELO (ELO_neg - ELO_pos) for a religious identity on IndiBias indicate about the model's behavior?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map:**  
Survey Data Pipeline → Model Query Engine → Metric Calculator → Bias Benchmark Harness → Analysis & Visualization

**Critical Path:**  
1. Survey translation & validation  
2. Baseline R_M on non-religious vs. religious items  
3. Local-language prompting experiment (measure ΔA_JSD, ΔA_HD)  
4. Downstream benchmark evaluation (CrowS-Pairs, IndiBias, etc.)  
5. Cross-metric correlation analysis

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- Breadth vs. Depth: Covering 12 countries/towns with multiple languages vs. deep analysis of 1–2 contexts; paper chooses breadth for generalizability.  
- Metric choice: JSD/HD are symmetric but don’t account for option ordering; Wasserstein R_M does but may be less interpretable for divergence. Using all three provides a multi-faceted view.  
- Prompt design: Simple "answer the question" prompts vs. complex demographic priming; paper uses simple prompts for baseline and priming as a variable, avoiding overfitting to a specific steering technique.  
- Model access: API-based (GPT, Gemini) vs. local (Llama, Mistral) requires different extraction logic (log-probs vs. logits), adding complexity but enabling broader model coverage.

**Failure Signatures:**  
1. High invalid/refusal rates on CrowS-Pairs or ThaiCLI (e.g., Gemini's 15–19/105 invalid in some languages), indicating safety filters may over-trigger or be culturally inconsistent.  
2. Persistently high A_HD despite A_JSD improvement with local prompting, signaling fundamental probability mass misplacement.  
3. Large positive ΔELO for specific minority identities (Shia, Sunni, Jain, Parsi) showing normalized negative framings.  
4. Minimal change in R_M on religion items even after local prompting, indicating deep misalignment.

**First 3 Experiments:**  
1. **Baseline alignment audit:** For each model, compute R_M, A_JSD, A_HD on the full Pew question set. Stratify by keyword topic (religion, governance, demographics). *Hypothesis:* Lower R_M and higher divergences on religion-tagged questions.  
2. **Local-language intervention test:** For countries with local language prompts, compute ΔA_JSD = A_JSD(eng) - A_JSD(loc) and ΔA_HD. *Hypothesis:* ΔA_JSD > 0 (improvement) but ΔA_HD ≈ 0.  
3. **Downstream harm correlation:** For models/countries with high divergence on religion items, evaluate on religion-related subsets of CrowS-Pairs, IndiBias, KoBBQ. *Hypothesis:* High correlation between high A_JSD on survey religion items and high bias rates/ΔELO on benchmark religion subsets.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Beyond religion, how accurately do LLMs represent other cultural dimensions (e.g., political ideology, caste, regional identity) across Asian societies? The audit exclusively uses Pew religion-focused surveys, leaving broader cultural values unmeasured.
2. Can deeper interventions (e.g., activation steering, culturally specific fine-tuning) close the representational gaps that prompt engineering alone cannot? The study only evaluates prompt-based steering, not methods that alter model internals or weights.
3. Do translation choices and forced-choice response formats in the survey instrument introduce artifacts that bias the measured LLM divergence? The methodology assumes translations are semantically equivalent without quantifying how translation variance might correlate with alignment metrics.
4. Are the observed misalignment patterns (especially negative stereotyping of minority religions) unique to Asian contexts, or do they generalize to Western democracies? Without parallel data from Western nations using identical surveys and metrics, it is unclear if the gaps are universal or region-specific.
5. Do LLMs contain latent, culturally specific knowledge that is not reliably activated by standard prompting, and can it be systematically elicited? The paper shows prompting helps but does not probe internal representations to confirm the existence or structure of latent cultural knowledge.

## Limitations
- Translated survey prompts and exact tokenization rules are not fully specified; reproduction may require reverse-engineering.
- API-specific log-prob extraction (e.g., top_logprobs limits) could bias distribution estimates.
- Human distribution D_O weighting depends on correct application of Pew survey weights; errors would invalidate metrics.
- Findings are restricted to Asian countries and religion; generalizability to other regions/topics is unknown.

## Confidence
- English-centric data misalignment: **Medium** – supported by literature and results, but break condition (sufficient local data) not tested.
- RLHF amplifies skews: **Low** – indirect evidence; no ablation on alignment data demographics.
- Prompting is superficial: **High** – consistent differential between JSD and Hellinger distance across models and languages.
- Downstream benchmark correlation: **Medium** – observed association but not causative; confounders possible.

## Next Checks
1. **Tokenization audit:** For each model and language, explicitly sum probabilities of all tokens comprising each answer option; report sensitivity to tokenization choices.
2. **Translation validation re-run:** Using the same crowd-sourcing protocol, recompute inter-rater agreement (Cohen’s κ) on a subset; ensure κ≥0.8.
3. **Fine-tuning intervention:** Perform lightweight supervised fine-tuning on a curated set of locally-authored religious opinion texts; measure pre/post change in A_JSD and A_HD to test break condition.
