# Beyond Subtokens: A Rich Character Embedding for Low-resource and Morphologically Complex Languages

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.21377  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21377  
- **Reference count**: 10  
- **Primary result**: Rich Character Embeddings (RCE) and a hybrid RCE+convolutional model consistently outperform FastText on intrinsic metrics (TopK, OddOneOut) and downstream tasks for low-resource, morphologically complex languages.

## Executive Summary
Tokenization-based embeddings like BERT and word2vec struggle to capture orthographic and morphological similarities, especially in low-resource and highly inflected languages. The authors propose Rich Character Embeddings (RCE), a transformer-based method that computes word vectors directly from character strings, and a hybrid RCE+convolutional (c2v) variant. RCE uses a modified alphabet that includes uppercase and diacritic modifiers, encodes each character as a one-hot vector, and processes the sequence with a transformer encoder; the embedding is the output at the `[BEG]` token. The model is trained with multiple objectives: predicting surrounding words’ character sequences, reconstructing the input word, and optionally predicting dictionary entries of context words.

Evaluated on Faroese, Latin, Uzbek, and German, RCE and c2v achieve clear gains over FastText. On intrinsic measures, they improve TopK (up to 0.27 vs. 0.25) and OddOneOut (up to 0.39 vs. 0.10). On Latin declension prediction, RCE/c2v reaches ~0.90 accuracy versus 0.85 for FastText. In downstream tasks such as stylistic device detection and a BERT-like next‑sentence prediction (SWAG), RCE-based models perform better, particularly in cross‑lingual and low‑resource settings, with the hybrid model often the strongest. These results demonstrate that character‑level modeling can effectively replace token‑based embeddings for morphologically rich languages.

## Method Summary
RCE treats each word as a sequence of characters. The alphabet includes base letters (a‑z), digits, special symbols, and special tokens (`[BEG]`, `[END]`, `[PAD]`, `[UNK]`). Uppercase letters and diacritics are decomposed into modifier tokens combined with the base lowercase character (e.g., “T” → `[UP] + "t"`, “é” → `"e" + [DIAC]`). Character sequences are padded to the maximum length in a batch and converted to one‑hot vectors. A linear layer projects these into a dense embedding of size 64, to which positional encodings are added. Three transformer encoder layers with two attention heads process the sequence; the `[BEG]` token’s output is the word embedding.

Training uses three auxiliary objectives:  
1. **Context prediction** – given a target embedding, predict the character sequences of surrounding context words via a separate transformer encoder and linear decoder.  
2. **Identity prediction** – reconstruct the input word’s character sequence from its embedding (autoencoder style).  
3. **Context dictionary prediction** (optional) – predict the dictionary ID of a context word from a frequency‑based dictionary.  
The total loss (weighting unspecified) combines these. The hybrid c2v model adds a convolutional layer over the character embeddings before the transformer to capture local n‑gram patterns. After pretraining on large corpora (e.g., CC100 for Faroese, Latin, Uzbek), the embeddings serve as drop‑in replacements for token‑based embeddings in downstream tasks.

## Key Results
- Intrinsic evaluation: RCE and c2v surpass FastText on TopK and OddOneOut across all tested languages, with TopK up to 0.27 vs. 0.25 and OddOneOut up to 0.39 vs. 0.10.
- Latin declension prediction: RCE/c2v achieve ~0.90 accuracy, beating FastText’s 0.85.
- Downstream tasks: RCE‑based models outperform WordPiece‑based BERT on SWAG‑like next‑sentence prediction (e.g., Faroese: 0.285 vs. 0.277) and show improved F1/accuracy for stylistic device detection; the hybrid c2v variant often yields the best performance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Character‑level embedding preserves morphological structure and orthographic features that token‑based subword methods discard. By operating directly on raw character strings, RCE can generalize across inflected forms (e.g., verb conjugations, noun declensions) because words sharing roots or suffixes have overlapping character sequences and thus similar embeddings. This is critical for low‑resource languages where subword vocabularies are limited and many word forms are unseen. The multi‑task training objectives further encourage the model to capture both local character patterns (identity reconstruction) and broader contextual usage (context prediction), yielding richer semantic representations. Modifier tokens for uppercase and diacritics ensure that orthographic nuances are retained, which is essential for languages with extensive diacritic use or case distinctions.

## Foundational Learning
- **Character-level modeling**: Treating text as a sequence of characters handles out‑of‑vocabulary words and captures morphological regularities. Why needed: Token embeddings fail for low‑resource languages with many rare inflected forms. Quick check: Are embeddings of “walk” and “walked” similar due to shared characters?
- **Transformer architecture**: Self‑attention models dependencies across character sequences, capturing long‑range morphological interactions better than convolutions. Why needed: Morphological processes can span non‑contiguous characters. Quick check: Do attention weights highlight morpheme boundaries?
- **Multi‑task objective**: Combining context prediction and identity reconstruction forces the model to learn both lexical and contextual information. Why needed: Single‑task training risks representations that are either too shallow or context‑agnostic. Quick check: Does adding identity loss improve performance on morphological analogy tasks?
- **Modifier tokens for orthography**: Decomposing uppercase and diacritics into separate tokens retains fine‑grained character information. Why needed: Diacritics can change meaning; ignoring them loses information. Quick check: Are words differing only by an accent (e.g., “résumé” vs. “resume”) given distinct embeddings?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Input word → Character tokenizer → One‑hot encoding → Linear projection → Positional encoding → Transformer encoder (3 layers, 2 heads) → `[BEG]` token output (word embedding).  
Branches:  
- *Context prediction*: embedding + context word embeddings → Transformer encoder → Linear head → Predict character sequence of context words.  
- *Identity prediction*: embedding → Transformer decoder → Predict input character sequence.  
- *Dictionary prediction* (optional): embedding → Linear → Predict token ID from frequent dictionary.

**Critical path**:  
1. Tokenize words into characters (with modifiers), create one‑hot matrices, pad to max length.  
2. Forward through base transformer to obtain embeddings.  
3. Compute losses from three prediction heads (context, identity, dictionary).  
4. Backpropagate total loss and update parameters with Adam (lr=0.001).  
For downstream use, only the embedding extraction path is needed.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- Character sequences are longer than subword sequences, increasing computational cost and requiring robust handling of long‑range dependencies.  
- One‑hot representation is simple but sparse; an alphabet‑level embedding could be more efficient but is not used.  
- Multi‑task loss may cause gradient interference if objectives are unbalanced.  
- The hybrid c2v adds a convolutional layer to capture local patterns at the expense of extra parameters and tuning.

**Failure signatures**:  
- High `[UNK]` rate → insufficient alphabet coverage.  
- Poor TopK/OOO scores → embeddings fail to capture semantic/morphological similarity.  
- Training loss divergence → unbalanced objectives or learning‑rate issues.  
- Slow or stalled convergence → unsuitable loss weighting or lack of character‑level embedding.  
- Downstream performance on par with FastText → training corpus mismatch or suboptimal hyperparameters.

**First experiments**:  
1. *Intrinsic replication*: Train RCE on CC100 Faroese with the paper’s hyperparameters (embedding size 64, 3 layers, 2 heads) and evaluate TopK/OOO using the provided category lists; verify improvement over FastText.  
2. *Objective ablation*: Train models with only context prediction, only identity prediction, and both; compare intrinsic scores to isolate each objective’s contribution.  
3. *Hybrid c2v test*: Implement the convolutional extension (1D conv over character embeddings before transformer) and compare its performance against base RCE on Latin declension and SWAG tasks to confirm the reported hybrid gains.

## Limitations
- **Missing corpus details**: Exact sizes, preprocessing steps, and train/validation/test splits for the CC100 subsets (Faroese, Latin, Uzbek) and the Uzbek news corpus are not described, hindering faithful replication.  
- **Undisclosed training configuration**: Loss weighting for the auxiliary objectives and the threshold for constructing the context dictionary (`DT`) are unspecified, leaving critical hyperparameters ambiguous.  
- **Limited evaluation scope**: Experiments cover only four languages and do not include modern subword tokenizers (e.g., BPE, SentencePiece); the SWAG-like next‑sentence prediction gains are small and rely on unclear dataset construction and architectural choices.
