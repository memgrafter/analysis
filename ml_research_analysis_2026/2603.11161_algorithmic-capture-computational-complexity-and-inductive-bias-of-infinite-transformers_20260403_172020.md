# Algorithmic Capture, Computational Complexity, and Inductive Bias of Infinite Transformers

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.11161
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11161
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Infinite-width transformers exhibit an inductive bias toward algorithms of at most O(T^{3+ε}) (lazy) or O(T^{2+ε}) (scaled) complexity, enabling algorithmic capture for simple tasks but not for higher-complexity ones like Shortest Path and MinCut.

## Executive Summary
Algorithmic capture is formally defined as a model's ability to generalize to arbitrarily large problem sizes with only a logarithmic number of additional training samples, distinguishing genuine algorithmic learning from statistical interpolation. This paper establishes a two-stage protocol to test capture and analyzes infinite-width transformers through the lens of the Neural Tangent Kernel. Theoretical results show that inference complexity is bounded by O(T^{3+ε}) in the lazy regime, tightening to O(T^{2+ε}) under scaling assumptions (width ∝ log T). Consequently, only tasks whose average-case heuristic complexity lies within these bounds can be captured. Experiments on induction heads, sorting, Shortest Path, and MinCut confirm the theory: captured tasks exhibit logarithmic sample growth, while uncaptured tasks require superlinear samples. The work clarifies fundamental limitations of transformers for algorithmic reasoning and provides a rigorous framework for evaluating their generalization.

## Method Summary
The evaluation uses a two-stage learning protocol. First, decoder-only transformers (GeLU activation, RoPE positional encoding, multi-head softmax attention) are pre-trained on instances of size at most T₀ to reach a target accuracy δ (e.g., 90%). Then, fine-tuning on larger sizes T > T₀ is limited to O(log(T/T₀)) additional samples, testing whether the model can adapt without relearning the task logic. Tasks include:
- Induction heads: predicting a trigger token injected at a random position and repeated at the end.
- Sorting: mapping an unsorted integer sequence to its sorted version.
- Shortest Path and MinCut on random geometric graphs with fixed source/target.
Architectures vary in depth (2, 4, 40 layers) and width (d=128/256). Performance is measured by the extra sample complexity P_extra(T) needed to maintain δ-accuracy as T increases. Logarithmic growth indicates algorithmic capture; superlinear growth indicates failure.

## Key Results
- **Definition**: Algorithmic capture requires generalization to arbitrary T with only logarithmic additional samples, separating algorithmic learning from interpolation.
- **Theoretical bound**: Infinite-width transformers have an inductive bias toward algorithms in EPTHS with complexity O(T^{3+ε}) (lazy) or O(T^{2+ε}) under scaling, excluding higher-complexity tasks.
- **Empirical validation**: Sample complexity scales logarithmically for induction and sorting (captured) but superlinearly for Shortest Path and MinCut (uncaptured), matching the theory.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
The two‑stage protocol tests algorithmic capture by allowing only a logarithmic number of extra samples when scaling from T₀ to larger T. If fine‑tuning requires ω(log T) samples, the model fails to capture the algorithm, indicating reliance on statistical interpolation or architectural misalignment. The core assumption is that the input distribution µX,T is scalable and efficiently samplable.

### Mechanism 2
Inference in infinite-width transformers corresponds to evaluating a kernel predictor. Monte Carlo estimation of the kernel incurs O(P T³) FLOPs due to token‑to‑token covariance propagation. Under scaling assumptions (width N ∝ P^γ, P ∝ log T), a finite‑width forward‑pass approximation reduces complexity to O(T^{2+ε}). Hence only algorithms whose average‑case heuristic time is within these bounds can be captured; otherwise the required computation exceeds the network’s capacity.

### Mechanism 3
Empirical measurements of P_extra(T) directly reflect whether the learned algorithm fits within the EPTHS class. Captured tasks (induction, sorting) show logarithmic growth, while uncaptured ones (Shortest Path, MinCut) show superlinear growth, confirming the theoretical bounds. This pattern holds across varying depths, demonstrating that depth alone cannot overcome the inductive bias.

## Foundational Learning
- **Algorithmic Complexity Classes (EPTHS)**
  - *Why needed*: Provides a distributional complexity measure for average‑case heuristic runtime under µX,T, beyond worst‑case expressivity.
  - *Quick check*: Given an algorithm with average‑case O(T^k) under µX,T, does it belong to EPTHS? How does error tolerance δ affect its implementation time?
- **Infinite‑Width Limits (NTK/NNGP)**
  - *Why needed*: Removes finite‑depth bottlenecks to isolate the inductive bias of the architecture; infinite‑width networks are universal approximators whose behavior is captured by a kernel.
  - *Quick check*: In the NTK limit, how does prediction scale with training samples P and sequence length T? What replaces the standard forward pass?
- **Monte Carlo Kernel Estimation**
  - *Why needed*: Evaluating the NTK kernel for transformers requires sampling from Gaussian scores with Kronecker covariance, and its complexity determines inference bounds.
  - *Quick check*: Why does naive MC cost O(T⁶) while structured sampling achieves O(T³)? What role does LayerNorm play in bounding outputs?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Input embedding + positional encoding → Transformer blocks (multi-head softmax attention + pointwise MLP) → Output projection

**Critical path**:
1. Formalize the task as a scalable distribution µX,T over instances of size T.
2. Determine the target algorithm's average‑case heuristic complexity under µX,T (e.g., SPP O(T log T)).
3. Derive kernel evaluation complexity: O(P T³) for the lazy regime via Monte Carlo.
4. Apply scaling assumptions (P ∝ log T, width N ∝ P^γ) to tighten the bound to O(T²).
5. Compare the target complexity to the derived bound; capture is possible only if the target lies within O(T^{2+ε}) or O(T^{3+ε}).

**Design tradeoffs**:
- *Lazy vs. rich*: The lazy regime yields a clean O(T³) bound but may be loose; a rich regime with feature learning tightens to O(T²) but relies on unproven scaling assumptions (N ∝ P^γ).
- *Depth*: More blocks increase the Lipschitz constant but remains T‑independent; deeper transformers may still fail capture if the algorithm’s complexity exceeds the bound.
- *Positional encoding*: Crucial to avoid attention dilution; RoPE is used for induction/sorting, while special token encoding suffices for graph problems.

**Failure signatures**:
- *Empirical*: P_extra(T) grows superlinearly; accuracy drops when T exceeds T₀ without extensive fine‑tuning.
- *Theoretical*: Target algorithm’s heuristic complexity > O(T^{3+ε}) (lazy) or > O(T^{2+ε}) (scaled); e.g., MinCut O(T³) pushes against the O(T^{2+ε}) bound.
- *Architectural*: Even deep transformers (40 layers) fail on SPP/MinCut, indicating depth alone cannot overcome the inductive bias.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Induction heads**: Vocabulary size V=1024; a random trigger token appears at an arbitrary index and must be predicted at the final position. Measure P_extra(T) after pre‑training on T₀. Expect logarithmic growth → capture.
2. **Sorting**: Input a sequence of distinct integers followed by a SEP token and the sorted list; loss is masked on the unsorted prefix. Expect logarithmic P_extra(T).
3. **Shortest Path on random geometric graphs**: Source and target are fixed at indices 0 and T+1; unit‑weight edges exist between points within a critical radius. Expect superlinear P_extra(T); compare shallow (4-layer) vs. deep (40-layer) models to show depth insufficient.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: Is the O(T^{3+ε}) inference-time complexity bound for algorithmic capture by infinite-width transformers tight, or can it be improved to O(T^{2+ε}) or lower via a more refined kernel analysis?
- **Open Question 2**: What are the fixed points of the kernel recursion equations for deep transformers, and how do they influence the inductive bias toward algorithms of a particular complexity?
- **Open Question 3**: Why do graph algorithms like shortest path and min-cut, whose heuristic complexities are below the theoretical upper bound, fail to be captured experimentally even with deep transformers?
- **Open Question 4**: Does the scaling assumption N ∝ P^{γ} (width vs. data) ensure that finite-width transformers accurately approximate their infinite NTK limits when problem size T grows arbitrarily (i.e., in OOD settings)?

## Limitations
- Protocol specifics (δ, T₀) are not provided, hindering exact replication.
- Theoretical analysis relies on infinite‑width NTK/NNGP limits and scaling assumptions (width ∝ log T); finite‑width models may deviate.
- The EPTHS complexity class is novel and lacks connections to standard algorithmic complexity; experiments cover only four tasks, limiting generalizability.
