# Constrained Density Estimation via Optimal Transport
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.06830  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06830  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: Solves constrained density estimation via Wasserstein-2 minimization with smoothing inequalities, reducing exotic option pricing errors by up to ~90% versus KL-based methods while producing smoother target distributions.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the problem of estimating a target probability density subject to expectation constraints by minimizing the 2-Wasserstein distance to a given prior distribution. Unlike KL-divergence, which rescales densities vertically and preserves support smoothness, the Wasserstein metric shifts mass horizontally, leading to different singularity structures (e.g., atomic accumulations at boundaries for indicator constraints). To handle non-smooth constraints (e.g., Heaviside functions), the authors propose an annealing algorithm that mollifies constraints and uses gradient descent with logarithmic barrier terms to regularize density estimates. These barriers penalize high-density spikes and low-density gaps, mitigating singularities. Empirical results on synthetic examples and a quantitative finance case study (recovering risk-neutral measures) show superior performance: the Wasserstein approach yields smoother target densities and reduces relative errors in exotic option pricing by up to ~90% compared to KL-divergence-based alternatives.

## Method Summary
The method formulates the problem as min_µ W₂(ρ,µ) subject to E_µ[f_k] = f̄_k. Discretized via samples {x_i}~ρ and {y_i}=T(x_i)~µ, the objective becomes an empirical transport cost (1/n)∑∥x_i−y_i∥² plus squared penalties for constraint violations. Non-smooth constraint functions f_k are replaced by mollifications f_k^ϵ (e.g., tanh(x/ϵ)). Smoothing inequalities—to avoid high-density spikes (∫p_µ²dy ≤ M_δ) and low-density gaps within a domain D (∫_D 1/p_µ dy ≤ M_0)—are enforced via logarithmic barrier terms B_δ and B_0^ϵ computed from kernel density estimates on {y_i}. Optimization proceeds by annealing: for a schedule of decreasing ϵ, gradient descent with backtracking line search minimizes the composite objective L^ϵ,t, starting from {y_i}={x_i} and using the final state of one ϵ as the initial point for the next. This gradually sharpens constraint enforcement while maintaining gradient information and regularizing the density to avoid singularities.

## Key Results
- The Wasserstein-2 framework produces target densities with different singularity structures (mass accumulations/gaps) than KL-divergence, enabling better alignment with ground-truth prices in financial applications.
- An annealing-based algorithm effectively handles non-smooth constraints via progressive mollification, avoiding gradient stalls that plague direct optimization.
- In a quantitative finance case study for recovering risk-neutral measures, relative errors in exotic option pricing (e.g., up-and-out, cash-or-nothing) are reduced by up to ~90% compared to KL-based methods, with smoother and more plausible target distributions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Horizontal vs. Vertical Displacement
- **Claim:** The Wasserstein distance yields target densities with different singularity structures (mass accumulations/gaps) compared to KL divergence's vertical stretching, due to its "horizontal" metric.
- **Mechanism:** Minimizing ∫∥x−T(x)∥²dρ under constraint ∫fₖ(T(x))dρ=̄fₖ induces transport maps T that move mass. For indicator constraints (e.g., support in [a,b]), the optimal T shifts all mass outside [a,b] to the nearest boundary, creating atomic accumulations at a/b. KL minimizes ∫log(pₘ/pₚ)dρ, which vertically rescales density pointwise, preserving support smoothness but creating sharp corners.
- **Core assumption:** The prior ρ is atomless; constraints are consistent (Assumption 1).
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract]: "minimizes the Wasserstein distance... KL-divergence-based methods... superior performance... smoother target distributions"; [section 3.1]: OT solution (9) has δ-terms at boundaries; KL solution (8) is vertically scaled.
- **Break condition:** Incompatible constraints or non-atomless prior may yield non-monotone T, invalidating Lemma 1.

### Mechanism 2: Annealing via Mollification
- **Claim:** Annealing via mollification of non-smooth constraint functions fₖ prevents gradient stalls and enables convergence to the true constrained solution.
- **Mechanism:** Non-smooth fₖ (e.g., Heaviside) have zero gradients in flat regions, causing samples yᵢ to remain at xᵢ (prior values) and violate constraints. Mollifying fₖ→fₖ^ϵ with broader support (e.g., tanh(x/ϵ)) yields non-zero gradients everywhere. Gradual ϵ reduction (annealing) allows initial mass movement under coarse constraints, then refinement.
- **Core assumption:** The mollifier sequence fₖ^ϵ converges pointwise to fₖ as ϵ→0; gradient descent steps are sufficiently small.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract]: "annealing-based algorithm to handle non-smooth constraints via mollification"; [section 5]: "mollify them into {fₖ^ϵ}... gradually reducing the mollification allows the solution to satisfy the constraints".
- **Break condition:** If annealing schedule is too aggressive (ϵ↓ too fast) or gradient descent gets stuck in local minima before constraints are satisfied.

### Mechanism 3: Smoothing Inequalities
- **Claim:** Smoothing inequality constraints (∫pₘ²dy ≤ M_δ, ∫_{D}1/pₘ dy ≤ M₀) mitigates singularities (accumulations/gaps) by regularizing the empirical density estimate from samples.
- **Mechanism:** The unregularized OT solution can have δ-masses on boundaries or zero-density gaps. Penalizing pₘ² (via barrier B_δ) discourages high-density spikes; penalizing 1/pₘ on domain D (via barrier B_0) discourages near-zero densities within D. These are estimated via kernel density estimation on {yᵢ} and plugged into the objective L^ϵ,t as logarithmic barriers.
- **Core assumption:** Kernel bandwidth h is well-chosen; domain D appropriately specifies where smoothness is desired.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract]: "smoothing inequalities to mitigate singularities like mass accumulations or gaps"; [section 4]: Constraints (27) explicitly defined; [section 5.2]: 2D examples (Figures 3,4) demonstrate regularization spreads mass from boundaries.
- **Break condition:** Poor h (too large/small) distorts density estimates; overly restrictive M_δ/M₀ may violate feasibility of equality constraints.

## Foundational Learning
- **Optimal Transport (Wasserstein-2 distance)**: *Why needed:* The core objective is min_µ W₂(ρ,µ). Understanding Monge vs. Kantorovich formulations, and that optimal maps T are monotone in 1D (Lemma 1), is essential. *Quick check:* Given ρ~N(0,1) and constraint ∫y dµ=1, what qualitative shape does T* have? (Ans: shifts positive x more than negative, preserving monotonicity.)
- **Density Estimation via Optimization**: *Why needed:* The framework solves min_µ d(ρ,µ) s.t. constraints. Must distinguish "vertical" (KL) vs. "horizontal" (OT) discrepancies. *Quick check:* Why does KL produce p_KL(y) ∝ p_ρ(y) exp(∑λₖfₖ(y)) while OT produces p_OT(y)=p_ρ(T^{-1}(y)) with piecewise-constant T? (Ans: KL is pointwise reweighting; OT is deterministic displacement.)
- **Constrained Optimization & KKT Conditions**: *Why needed:* Equality constraints handled via Lagrange multipliers (λₖ) in both formulations; inequalities via barrier functions. *Quick check:* In the single RELU case (Proposition 2), what do the two candidate systems (1) and (2) correspond to in terms of the shift pattern τ(x)? (Ans: Whether the shift region starts at ω or ω−λ/2, affecting where mass accumulates.)
- **Numerical Optimization with Mollifiers**: *Why needed:* Algorithm 1 uses gradient descent on L^ϵ with mollified fₖ^ϵ and annealing. Understanding why non-smooth fₖ break gradient-based methods is critical. *Quick check:* If fₖ is Heaviside at 0, why would gradient descent on L with yᵢ initialized at xᵢ never move points across 0? (Ans: fₖ'=0 almost everywhere, so ∂L/∂yᵢ=0 for yᵢ>0 unless some yᵢ are exactly at 0.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Prior samples {x_i} ~ ρ, constraint functions {f_k}, targets {f̄_k}, smoothing parameters (M_δ, M_0, D) -> Initialize Y = X -> For each ϵ in annealing schedule (ϵ←ϵ/β): Compute L^ϵ(Y) = (1/n)∑∥x_i−y_i∥² + ∑λ_k((1/n)∑f_k^ϵ(y_i)−f̄_k)² + λ_δ^t B_δ + λ_0^t B_0 -> Gradient descent with backtracking line search until convergence -> Update Y -> After final ϵ, output final Y.
- **Critical path**: Prior samples -> initialize y -> annealed gradient descent on L^ϵ,t -> converge y.
- **Design tradeoffs**: Mollification ϵ (large ensures gradients but biases; small respects constraints but may stall). Smoothing hyperparameters M_δ, M_0 (larger relaxes regularization, risking singularities; smaller may over-regularize and violate constraints). Kernel bandwidth h (affects density estimates; rule-of-thumb on {x_i} recommended). Lagrange multipliers λ_k, λ_δ, λ_0 (balance transport cost vs. constraints; too small violates constraints, too large dominates objective).
- **Failure signatures**: Constraint violation after convergence |(1/n)∑f_k(y_i)−f̄_k| > tolerance -> insufficient ϵ reduction or poor λ_k. Singularities persist (mass spikes/gaps in histogram) -> M_δ/M_0 too extreme or h poorly chosen. Stalled gradient (∥∇L∥≈0 but constraints unsatisfied) -> ϵ not small enough or local minimum. Numerical instability in B_δ/B_0 (NaN/Inf) -> M_δ/M_0 too small relative to kernel density estimates or h too small.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **1D indicator constraint** (Section 3.1): With ρ=N(0,1), f(y)=1_{ℝ\[a,b]}, f̄=0. Verify unregularized OT solution has δ-masses at boundaries; with smoothing inequalities, mass spreads inside [a,b]. Compare to analytic solution (Proposition 1).
  2. **1D single RELU** (Section 3.2): f(y)=max(y−ω,0). Visualize y_i movement as ϵ decreases. Confirm without mollification, many y_i=x_i (stuck); with annealing, ∫f(y)dµ̂ ≈ f̄.
  3. **Compare against KL baseline** (Section 3.3, Figure 2): Use multiple RELU constraints. Compute p_KL via formula (17) and p_OT via Algorithm 1. Plot both densities; observe KL's smooth corners vs OT's piecewise-constant shifts. Introduce smoothing inequalities and show they fill OT's gaps.

## Limitations
- Hyperparameter choices (annealing schedule, kernel bandwidth h, barrier thresholds M_δ, M_0) lack systematic study and may cause failures in new settings.
- Theoretical analysis limited to 1D maps; no guarantees for higher dimensions where optimal transport maps are nonlinear and may not be unique.
- Algorithm complexity is O(n²) per iteration due to pairwise kernel terms in barriers, raising scalability concerns for large sample sizes.
- Validation primarily on synthetic 1D/2D examples and a single finance case study; lacks diversity in real-world high-dimensional benchmarks.

## Confidence
- **Wasserstein singularity structure**: Medium (supported by 1D monotonicity theory and 2D experiments, but no high-dimensional analysis; break conditions include inconsistent constraints or non-atomless priors).
- **Annealing via mollification**: Medium (empirical evidence shows it overcomes gradient stalls for non-smooth constraints; no convergence proof; annealing too aggressive or poor initialization can lead to local minima).
- **Smoothing inequalities**: Low-Medium (they visibly reduce singularities in examples, but are heuristic with ad‑hoc thresholds (M_δ, M_0) and lack theoretical justification; choice of h also ambiguous).

## Next Checks

Record constraint violation, singularity metrics (mass at boundaries/gaps), and objective value to establish robust defaults and failure regimes.
2. **Convergence theory for 1D monotone constraints**: For constraints yielding a monotone optimal transport map (e.g., cumulative distribution constraints), prove that as ϵ→0 and with a sufficiently slow annealing schedule, gradient descent on the mollified objective converges to the true constrained OT solution.

Compare against state‑of‑the‑art neural OT solvers in terms of constraint satisfaction, Wasserstein‑2 distance, and runtime.
