# How Do Large Language Models Learn Concepts During Continual Pre-Training?

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.03570
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03570
- **Reference count:** 20
- **Primary result:** Circuit topology (graph metrics) predicts concept learning and forgetting, revealing a trade-off: dense, robust circuits learn faster but forget more; interference scales with semantic similarity and circuit overlap, and knowledge-type transfer is asymmetric.

## Executive Summary
This paper studies how Llama-2 models acquire and retain new conceptual knowledge during continual pre-training. The authors introduce a synthetic dataset (FICO) of fictional concepts derived from ConceptNet relations, enabling isolation of concept learning from prior knowledge. Using the EAP-IG method, they extract Concept Circuits—minimal subgraphs responsible for each concept’s predictions—and compute four topological metrics (eigenvector centrality variance, density, global efficiency, k-core). They find that these metrics correlate with both the speed of concept acquisition and the degree of subsequent forgetting, evidencing a structural trade-off: circuits that are denser and more robust learn quickly but are more vulnerable to interference. During sequential training (FICO then an unrelated corpus, BIO), circuit metrics follow a non-monotonic trajectory—an early increase, then decay and stabilization. Interference experiments show that semantically related concepts share more circuitry (higher Jaccard similarity) and experience stronger forgetting (57.5% vs. 75.9% performance drop for weakly related). Finally, pretraining on certain knowledge types (Property & Affordance) significantly facilitates later learning of others (Hyponym & Hypernym, +63.74%), indicating asymmetric transfer. The work links behavioral dynamics to internal circuit structure and suggests principles for interference-aware continual pre-training.

## Method Summary
The study uses a two-stage continual pre-training pipeline on GPT-2 Large and LLaMA-3.2-1B-Instruct. Stage 1 involves training on FICO, a dataset of 1,500 fictional concepts (1,000 train, 500 test) mapped from five ConceptNet relation types (e.g., Hyponym/Hypernym, Property/Affordance). Concept names are replaced with invented tokens to avoid pretrained knowledge, and natural-language templates for each relation are split into disjoint train/test pools to prevent memorization. After training, model knowledge of each concept is evaluated via average logit change on test triples, yielding a Concept Learning Degree. The EAP-IG algorithm identifies a Concept Circuit for each concept from the checkpoint by selecting edges that preserve ≥70% of full-model performance. Four graph metrics are computed on each circuit. Stage 2 continues training on the unrelated BIO corpus; forgetting is measured similarly. Interference is tested by co-training a target concept with groups of concepts of high, medium, or low semantic similarity (based on embedding cosine) and evaluating the target’s performance. Knowledge-type transfer is measured by ordered training on pairs of relation types and computing the transferability score.

## Key Results
- Circuit metrics (eigenvector centrality variance, density, global efficiency, k-core) positively correlate (Spearman, p<0.001) with both concept learning and forgetting degrees, revealing a trade-off: structurally robust circuits enable faster learning but increase vulnerability.
- Circuit metrics during the forgetting stage exhibit a consistent three-phase pattern: initial rise, gradual decay, and eventual stabilization.
- A concept’s learning degree positively predicts its subsequent forgetting degree—larger initial gains lead to greater loss.
- Co-training with highly semantically related concepts causes significantly more forgetting (57.5% vs. 75.9% performance drop for weakly related), correlated with higher Jaccard similarity between their circuits.
- Knowledge-type transfer is asymmetric: pretraining on Property & Affordance improves later Hyponym & Hypernym learning by 63.74%, while the reverse direction shows minimal transfer.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The topological structure of a concept’s internal computational subgraph (Concept Circuit) systematically influences both how effectively it is learned and how vulnerable it is to forgetting.
- **Mechanism:** Graph metrics capture properties such as hub concentration (eigenvector centrality variance), redundancy (density), information flow efficiency (global efficiency), and core depth (k-core). Dense, efficient circuits with centralized hubs offer redundant pathways and stable cores, promoting strong initial learning. However, their high integration makes them more susceptible to interference during subsequent training, leading to greater forgetting. More modular circuits, while slower to learn, may be more resilient.
- **Evidence anchors:** Correlations between circuit metrics and learning/forgetting degrees; positive correlation between learning and forgetting degrees themselves.
- **Break condition:** Absence of significant (p<0.001) Spearman correlations between circuit metrics and learning/forgetting degrees across concepts would falsify the trade-off.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** During continued training on new data, circuits for previously learned concepts undergo a predictable reorganization: an early increase in metric values, followed by decay and stabilization.
- **Mechanism:** The early rise may reflect transient entanglement of old pathways with new knowledge as the model adapts. Over time, interference and overwriting degrade the circuit (decay), eventually converging to a stable, degraded state.
- **Evidence anchors:** Figure 4 shows the pattern across multiple metrics and random seeds.
- **Break condition:** A monotonic (e.g., only decay) or inconsistent trajectory across metrics/seeds would invalidate the claimed universal pattern.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Interference and synergy between concepts are driven by semantic relatedness and knowledge-type transferability, with circuit overlap quantifying interference strength.
- **Mechanism:** Semantically similar concepts share more circuitry (higher Jaccard similarity of edge sets), indicating representational competition; co-training thus causes more forgetting. Knowledge types differ in their scaffolding: certain types (e.g., Property & Affordance) provide foundational representations that facilitate learning of other types (e.g., Hyponym & Hypernym) asymmetrically.
- **Evidence anchors:** Stronger forgetting for highly related concept pairs; higher circuit Jaccard for related pairs; asymmetric transferability in the heatmap.
- **Break condition:** (a) No correlation between semantic similarity and circuit Jaccard; (b) No performance difference between relatedness groups in co-training; (c) No asymmetric positive cells in transfer heatmap.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Concept Circuit**  
  *Why needed:* It is the core unit of internal analysis—a minimal, extracted subgraph (via EAP-IG) that faithfully predicts all knowledge triples for a concept, isolating the model’s computation for that concept.  
  *Quick check:* Is the circuit defined by edges that, when ablated, cause a >30% drop in concept performance, or by the top-scoring edges that preserve ~70% performance? (Answer: the latter.)
- **Concept: Graph Metrics (Node Importance, Density, Global Efficiency, k-core)**  
  *Why needed:* These topological measures characterize circuit structure and serve as independent variables in correlating circuit shape with behavior.  
  *Quick check:* What does a higher *global efficiency* specifically indicate? (Shorter average shortest path lengths, i.e., faster information flow.)
- **Concept: FICO Dataset**  
  *Why needed:* Provides controlled, novel concept learning by fictionalizing real ConceptNet relations, isolating acquisition from prior knowledge.  
  *Quick check:* Why replace concept names with fictional ones, and what is the role of disjoint template pools? (Fictional names avoid preexisting knowledge; disjoint train/test templates prevent memorization.)
- **Concept: EAP-IG (Edge Attribution Patching with Integrated Gradients)**  
  *Why needed:* The algorithm extracts circuits by computing edge importance scores and selecting a subgraph that maintains ≥70% of full model accuracy on the concept’s triples.  
  *Quick check:* Does EAP-IG use gradient-based attribution (integrated gradients) to score edges? (Yes.)
- **Concept: Learning/Forgiving Degree**  
  *Why needed:* These behavioral metrics quantify performance change on a concept’s knowledge triples (average logit difference) between checkpoints, linking circuit metrics to learning dynamics.  
  *Quick check:* Are degrees computed as averages over all triples for a given concept? (Yes.)
- **Concept: Interference Degree**  
  *Why needed:* Measures performance degradation of a target concept when jointly trained with others, testing the effect of relatedness.  
  *Quick check:* Is interference defined as the post-training performance drop relative to solo training? (Yes.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Base Model (GPT-2 Large / LLaMA-3.2-1B) -> FICO training -> checkpoint π₁ -> EAP-IG Circuit Extraction -> Graph Metrics & Learning Degrees -> Correlation analysis.  
  π₁ -> BIO training -> checkpoint π₂ -> Circuit Extraction -> Graph Metrics & Forgetting Degrees -> Trajectory analysis.  
  For interference: Relatedness groups (high/medium/low) -> Joint training with target -> Performance evaluation -> Circuit Jaccard similarity.  
  For transfer: Knowledge type pairs (Rᵢ, Rⱼ) -> Ordered training (Rᵢ then Rⱼ) -> Transferability T(Rᵢ→Rⱼ).
- **Critical path:**  
  1. Load pretrained model.  
  2. Train on FICO for fixed steps; save checkpoints.  
  3. Evaluate on FICO test to compute Concept Learning Degrees.  
  4. Apply EAP-IG to extract circuits from the final FICO checkpoint.  
  5. Compute four graph metrics; correlate with learning degrees.  
  6. Continue training on BIO; repeat extraction and computation of forgetting degrees.  
  7. Track metric evolution across BIO checkpoints for held-out concepts.  
  8. For interference: for each target concept, co-train with high/medium/low related groups; evaluate and compare target performance; compute pairwise circuit Jaccard.  
  9. For transfer: for each ordered pair of knowledge types, train sequentially and compute T(Rᵢ→Rⱼ).
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Circuit faithfulness vs. size: The 70% performance preservation threshold balances interpretability (smaller circuit) against risk of omitting relevant pathways.  
  - Concept aggregation: Learning/forgetting degrees average over all triples, assuming uniform importance; may mask heterogeneity within a concept.  
  - Relatedness proxy: Token embedding cosine similarity is simple and scalable but may not capture all forms of representational overlap.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - High variance in circuit metrics across random seeds or pruning thresholds, destabilizing correlations.  
  - No consistent early-rise-then-decay trajectory in metric evolution; suggests noise or insufficient training.  
  - High Jaccard similarity even for randomly paired (unrelated) concepts, refuting interference hypothesis.  
  - Zero-shot performance on FICO test >10% indicates fictional names still trigger pretrained knowledge.  
  - Test accuracy >90% suggests template leakage between train/test pools.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Replicate FICO Learning Dynamics:** Train GPT-2 Large on FICO; verify unimodal, wide distribution of Concept Learning Degrees (Figure 2) and positive correlations with circuit metrics (Figure 3a).  
  2. **Validate Stage-Wise Trajectory:** For a held-out concept, extract circuits at multiple BIO checkpoints; plot four metrics over training steps; confirm early rise, decay, stabilization pattern (Figure 4).  
  3. **Test Interference:** For a target concept, sample 100 highly related (high embedding cosine) and 100 weakly related concepts. Run three joint-training runs (target + each group). Compare target post-training performance; expect significantly lower performance in high-relatedness condition (Figure 6). Compute circuit Jaccard to confirm higher overlap for related pairs.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How do concept learning and forgetting dynamics generalize to larger LLMs beyond the 1B parameter scale? The study was limited to GPT-2 (0.7B) and LLaMA-3.2-1B; scaling behavior may differ.
- **Open Question 2:** Can circuit metrics be turned into training interventions—like interference-aware batching or robustness-based learning rate scheduling—to mitigate catastrophic forgetting? The paper establishes correlational links but provides no implemented strategies.
- **Open Question 3:** What internal mechanisms explain the asymmetric transfer between knowledge types (e.g., Property & Affordance facilitating Hyponym & Hypernym)? The paper documents the behavioral asymmetry but not the circuit-level causes.

## Limitations
- FICO dataset: Fictional concept names may still activate pretrained knowledge; zero-shot baseline not reported.
- EAP-IG circuit extraction: 70% faithfulness threshold is arbitrary; reproducibility across seeds is unclear.
- Graph metrics: Potential multicollinearity; unclear which metrics are independent drivers.
- Model selection: Only small-to-medium LLMs (≤1B); scaling effects unknown.
- Relatedness measure: Embedding cosine simplicity may not fully capture circuit overlap.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (circuit trade-off):** Medium. Correlations are statistically significant but correlational; cross-dataset validation needed.
- **Mechanism 2 (stage-wise trajectory):** Low. Based on sparse checkpoint sampling; could be artifact of training duration or extraction noise.
- **Mechanism 3 (interference & transfer):** Medium. Interference aligns with Jaccard similarity, and asymmetric transfer is observed, but relatedness grouping is coarse and only five knowledge types tested.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-model replication:** Repeat the entire pipeline on a larger model (e.g., Llama 2 13B) and a newly created synthetic concept dataset to verify generalizability of circuit-behavior correlations and trajectories.
2. **Circuit extraction sensitivity:** Systematically vary the EAP-IG faithfulness threshold (e.g., 50%–90%) and assess stability of metric–behavior correlations; if correlations collapse, the 70% choice is critical.
3. **Trajectory reproducibility:** Extract circuit metrics at every training step (or every 500 steps) for multiple random seeds during the forgetting stage; apply statistical tests (e.g., time-series clustering) to confirm the early-rise-then-decay pattern is consistent and not noise-driven.
