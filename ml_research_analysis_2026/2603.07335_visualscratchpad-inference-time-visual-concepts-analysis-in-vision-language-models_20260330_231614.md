# VisualScratchpad: Inference-time Visual Concepts Analysis in Vision Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.07335
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07335
- Reference count: 16
- Core contribution: A method for debugging vision-language models by analyzing visual concepts through sparse autoencoders and attention-based linking

## Executive Summary
VisualScratchpad addresses the fundamental interpretability challenge in vision-language models (VLMs) by providing an inference-time interface for analyzing visual concepts. The method applies sparse autoencoders (SAEs) to the vision encoder and links extracted concepts to text tokens via text-to-image attention, enabling causal analysis through concept ablation. Through case studies, it reveals three underexplored failure modes: limited cross-modal alignment, reliance on misleading visual cues, and unused hidden cues. The approach transforms black-box VLMs into interpretable systems where users can systematically debug model failures by manipulating specific visual concepts.

## Method Summary
VisualScratchpad applies sparse autoencoders directly to vision encoder outputs to extract interpretable visual concepts. It then links these concepts to text tokens using text-to-image attention weights, creating a bridge between visual and textual representations. A token-latent activation heatmap guides effective concept ablation by highlighting which visual concepts are most relevant to specific tokens. The core innovation enables inference-time debugging by allowing users to ablate or steer individual concepts and observe how model outputs change, providing causal insights into model behavior.

## Key Results
- Reveals three failure modes in VLMs: limited cross-modal alignment, reliance on misleading visual cues, and unused hidden cues
- Demonstrates causal influence of visual concepts on model outputs through systematic ablation experiments
- Provides an interactive interface for inference-time debugging of VLM responses
- Shows that vision encoders can capture richer visual information than what models ultimately use

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The method works by decomposing visual representations into interpretable concepts using sparse autoencoders, then establishing connections between these concepts and text outputs through attention mechanisms. This creates a causal pathway where manipulating visual concepts directly influences textual responses, revealing the model's reasoning process and failure modes.

## Foundational Learning
- Sparse autoencoders for concept extraction: SAEs decompose high-dimensional visual features into sparse, interpretable components that capture meaningful visual patterns
- Text-to-image attention linking: Attention weights serve as a bridge between visual concepts and textual tokens, enabling cross-modal analysis
- Causal ablation analysis: Systematically removing or modifying concepts reveals their functional importance to model outputs
- Token-latent activation heatmaps: Visual indicators guide users to the most relevant concepts for targeted debugging
- Inference-time debugging: Enables real-time analysis of model behavior rather than post-hoc interpretation

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:** Vision Encoder -> Sparse Autoencoder -> Text-to-Image Attention -> Concept Linking -> Ablation Interface
**Critical Path:** Vision features → SAE decomposition → Attention-based linking → Concept selection → Ablation → Output analysis
**Design Tradeoffs:** SAE complexity vs. interpretability, attention-based linking vs. alternative attribution methods, interactive vs. automated analysis
**Failure Signatures:** Misaligned attention weights, spurious concept activations, insufficient concept granularity
**First Experiments:** 1) Apply SAE to vision encoder outputs and visualize concept activations, 2) Compute text-to-image attention weights for concept linking, 3) Perform initial ablation on a simple VQA example

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: How can we extend VisualScratchpad's concept tracing to capture the transformation of visual representations inside the language model's cross-attention layers?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The limitation section states: "Connecting vision SAEs to language models does not explicitly capture how image-token representations are transformed inside the language model during cross-modal processing."
- Why unresolved: The current method links concepts via attention weights but does not decode how the language model modifies or recombines visual information after cross-attention. Understanding this transformation is crucial for diagnosing failures where the vision encoder provides correct cues but the language model misinterprets them.
- What evidence would resolve it: Developing SAEs or similar disentangling methods for cross-attention outputs, and validating that the traced concepts accurately predict model behavior when ablated at different layers.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can we automate the discovery and classification of VLM failure modes using the token-latent heatmap and causal ablation, scaling beyond manual case studies?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that the interface is interactive and "does not directly scale to large, fully automated experiments," but they suggest "automating large-scale causal analyses" as a future direction.
- Why unresolved: Manual case studies are time-consuming and may miss systematic patterns. An automated pipeline could analyze thousands of examples to identify common failure patterns, quantify their prevalence, and suggest targeted interventions.
- What evidence would resolve it: Implementing an automated system that processes a benchmark dataset (e.g., VQA v2, MMVP), applies concept ablation heuristically, clusters resulting output changes, and validates that the discovered modes correlate with human-annotated error types.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Do the three identified failure modes (limited cross-modal alignment, misleading visual concepts, unused hidden cues) cover the majority of VLM errors, or are there additional systematic failure modes?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors present three case studies and state they "reveal three underexplored failure modes." The use of "underexplored" implies these are not the only ones; the paper does not claim exhaustiveness.
- Why unresolved: The case studies are illustrative but limited in scope. Without a large-scale analysis, we cannot know if these three modes account for most errors or if other modes (e.g., failures in reasoning, temporal confusion, bias exploitation) are equally or more prevalent.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conducting a systematic study using VisualScratchpad on a diverse set of VLM errors, coding the type of failure based on concept ablation results, and determining the distribution of failure modes. This could be combined with error analysis on standard benchmarks.

### Open Question 4
- Question: How does the choice of linking method (e.g., attention-based weighting vs. alternative attribution techniques like gradient-based methods or SHAP) affect the identification of causally relevant visual concepts?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper's method uses text-to-image attention maps to weight SAE latents. This is a design choice, not compared to other linking strategies. It's an assumption that attention correlates with importance.
- Why unresolved: Attention weights may not reliably indicate feature importance; they can be diffuse or misaligned. Other attribution methods might yield different concept rankings and thus different ablation results. Understanding the robustness of the linking method is key for trustworthy interpretation.
- What evidence would resolve it: Running comparative experiments where the same VLM responses are analyzed using different linking methods (e.g., attention, gradient norms, Integrated Gradients), then performing causal ablations based on each method's top concepts, and measuring agreement in which concepts are deemed causally influential.

## Limitations
- Does not capture how visual representations are transformed inside language model cross-attention layers
- Interactive interface does not scale to fully automated large-scale experiments
- Interpretation of "unused hidden cues" may conflate correlation with causation
- Reliance on attention weights may not accurately reflect feature importance
- Limited validation across diverse VLM architectures and datasets

## Confidence
**High:** The core technical contribution of applying sparse autoencoders to vision encoders and using text-to-image attention for concept linking is well-supported and reproducible. The inference-time debugging interface demonstrably enables concept manipulation.

**Medium:** The case studies showing three failure modes are compelling but may reflect specific dataset biases or model peculiarities rather than universal VLM behaviors. The interpretation of what constitutes "limited cross-modal alignment" versus "reliance on misleading cues" requires careful subjective judgment.

**Low:** Claims about the richness of "unused hidden cues" and their relationship to final VLM outputs are speculative without rigorous quantification of concept importance or ablation studies across multiple model families.

## Next Checks
1. Conduct ablation studies across multiple VLM architectures (e.g., CLIP, Flamingo, BLIP) to verify that the identified failure modes are not model-specific artifacts but generalizable patterns.

2. Implement quantitative metrics to measure concept importance and cross-modal alignment beyond qualitative case studies, such as correlation between concept ablation strength and output degradation across diverse prompts.

3. Validate whether the sparse autoencoder concepts extracted from vision encoders align with human-annotated visual attributes through controlled experiments with ground-truth concept labels, establishing whether the method captures meaningful rather than spurious visual features.
