# Contextual Rollout Bandits for Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.08499  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08499  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: Up to 8.5% relative improvement in average score and 50.4% reduction in actor update time with <4% scheduling overhead across six mathematical reasoning benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) suffers from inefficient use of rollout data: within each prompt group, responses of varying quality are used indiscriminately, and historical rollouts are discarded after a single step, leading to noisy gradients and poor sample efficiency. This paper reformulates rollout scheduling as a contextual bandit problem, proposing a neural scheduler (CBS) that assigns each rollout an arm score based on a 10-dimensional representation capturing reward, advantage, entropy, clipping statistics, and usage history. CBS selects top-scoring rollouts both within groups (intra-group) and from a FIFO replay buffer (global reuse), enabling data-aware filtering and long-horizon reuse. The reward signal is the empirical train reward improvement between consecutive updates, regularized by an entropy penalty to avoid collapse. Experiments on six mathematical reasoning benchmarks using Qwen3 models and three RLVR algorithms show consistent gains, with ablations highlighting the importance of EMA reward smoothing and entropy control.

## Method Summary
CBS encodes each rollout into a 10-dimensional feature vector: reward, advantage, mean/std group reward, normalized length, truncation flag, entropy, clip ratio, usage count, and sample age. An MLP scheduler maps this representation to a score. Two selection regimes operate: (1) intra-group (CBS*), selecting the top *p*% of rollouts per prompt; and (2) global (CBS), selecting the top *K* rollouts from a FIFO replay buffer of size *M*. The reward for a round is defined as the difference in training reward between consecutive updates, smoothed via EMA and standardized, minus an entropy penalty term when entropy exceeds a threshold. The scheduler is updated via gradient descent on per-sample advantages (advantage-dispersed reward assignment), with epsilon-greedy exploration decaying over a warm-up period. Buffer operations and feature updates (usage count increment, age increment) occur at the end of each round.

## Key Results
- Consistent improvements across six math reasoning benchmarks (AIME24, AIME25, AMC23, MATH500, Minerva, Olympiad) using Qwen3 (1.7B–8B) and three RLVR algorithms (GRPO, DAPO, GSPO).
- Up to 8.5% relative improvement in average score, 50.4% reduction in actor update time, and scheduling overhead below 4%.
- Ablations confirm that EMA reward smoothing and entropy regularization are critical components; removing either degrades performance.

## Why This Works
CBS directly addresses the two core inefficiencies in standard RLVR. First, indiscriminate use of heterogeneous-quality rollouts within a prompt group introduces noise into policy gradients. By scoring rollouts with a learned contextual bandit, CBS filters out low-quality samples, ensuring that updates are driven by rollouts predicted to improve training reward. Second, historical rollouts are typically discarded after one use, wasting data. The global replay buffer allows reuse across prompts and time steps, increasing sample efficiency. The bandit’s reward signal—empirical train reward improvement—aligns the scheduler’s objective with the ultimate goal of policy progress. Entropy regularization prevents the scheduler from collapsing to overly deterministic (low-entropy) selections, maintaining exploration diversity crucial for stable learning. Together, these mechanisms yield noisier gradients, faster convergence, and lower compute cost per effective update.

## Foundational Learning
- **Contextual bandits**: A sequential decision framework where an agent selects an arm based on context to maximize reward. *Why needed*: Models rollout selection as an adaptive scoring problem. *Quick check*: How does a contextual bandit differ from full reinforcement learning in terms of feedback and state transitions?
- **RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR)**: Reinforcement learning where rewards are computed by a verifier (e.g., checking correctness of a math answer). *Why needed*: Defines the problem domain with dense, binary, or programmatic rewards. *Quick check*: Why is RLVR particularly suited for mathematical reasoning and code generation?
- **Rollout scheduling**: The process of choosing which generated responses (rollouts) to include in policy updates. *Why needed*: Mitigates noise from low-quality rollouts and improves data efficiency. *Quick check*: What are the risks of using all rollouts versus a selective scheduler?
- **Entropy regularization**: Adding the negative entropy of the policy’s action distribution to the reward to encourage exploration. *Why needed*: Prevents premature convergence to suboptimal deterministic policies. *Quick check*: How might entropy regularization interact with the scheduler’s own exploration (epsilon-greedy)?
- **FIFO replay buffer**: A fixed-size queue that discards oldest entries when full. *Why needed*: Enables reuse of historical rollouts while bounding memory usage. *Quick check*: Why might a FIFO buffer be preferred over a prioritized replay buffer in this context?
- **Advantage estimation**: Measuring how much better a rollout’s reward is compared to an average baseline. *Why needed*: Central to policy gradient methods and used here to assign per-sample rewards for scheduler updates. *Quick check*: In standard PPO, what is the purpose of the advantage function?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Policy Model → Rollout Generator → Feature Extractor → Scheduler (MLP) → Selection Module → Policy Update  
                        ↑                            
                      Replay Buffer (FIFO) ← Selected Rollouts  
                                             ↑  
                      Reward Computation (train improvement + entropy penalty) → Scheduler Update  

**Critical path**: Rollouts are generated for a batch of prompts → each rollout is converted into a 10D feature vector → the MLP scheduler scores all rollouts → top *p*% per prompt (intra-group) and top *K* from the global buffer are selected → policy is updated using only selected rollouts → the training reward before and after the update is computed → per-sample advantages are derived → the scheduler undergoes a gradient step based on these advantages.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- *Feature representation*: Hand-crafted 10D features offer interpretability and low computational overhead but may miss deeper patterns; a learned semantic representation could improve accuracy at the cost of speed and stability.
- *Exploration schedule*: Epsilon-greedy with decay balances early exploration and later exploitation; too rapid decay risks converging to a suboptimal scheduler, while too slow wastes samples.
- *Buffer size vs. staleness*: Larger buffers increase reuse opportunities but may introduce stale data; the age feature attempts to mitigate this but requires careful scaling.
- *Intra-group vs. global selection*: Strict per-prompt selection ensures diversity but loses cross-prompt transfer; global selection leverages historical data but risks overfitting to older patterns. The *p*% and *K* parameters balance these effects.

**Failure signatures**:
- Scheduler outputs nearly uniform scores: indicates insufficient exploration (epsilon too small) or MLP underfitting; check scheduler loss and score distribution.
- Performance degrades as buffer size grows: suggests staleness overwhelms benefits; inspect selection age distribution and consider increasing decay on age feature.
- Scheduling overhead exceeds 5%: feature extraction or buffer management is too slow; profile to ensure operations are vectorized and caching is used.
- Policy entropy steadily decreases without entropy penalty: confirms the penalty is necessary; monitor entropy per round to ensure it stays above threshold.

**First experiments** (exactly three):
1. **Baseline replication**: Train GRPO on the DAPO dataset with Qwen3-1.7B for 500 steps, logging average score on MATH500 and actor update time per step.
2. **CBS integration**: Add the scheduler with default hyperparameters (MLP: 2 hidden layers, 256 units, ReLU; *p*=30%; *M*=2048; ε-greedy: ε₀=1, ε_min=0.1, warm-up=50; η₂=1e-4). Compare against baseline on identical seeds.
3. **Ablation study**: Remove EMA smoothing from reward computation and disable entropy penalty (set *w*ₑ=0). Measure changes in final average score and training stability (e.g., reward variance, entropy trajectory).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1**: Can replacing the 10-dimensional hand-crafted training dynamics features with a deep, semantic representation from the LLM policy itself (e.g., hidden states from a specific layer) improve scheduler performance?  
  *Basis*: Conclusion states future work could explore integrating deep semantic representations.  
  *Why unresolved*: The hand-crafted features are efficient proxies, but a learned representation might capture more nuanced signals.  
  *Evidence needed*: Ablation comparing original 10D CBS against variant using pooled policy hidden state embeddings, measuring performance and overhead.

- **Question 2**: Can the theoretical regret bound be extended from the single-rollout selection setting to the full batch/K-armed selection setting used in practice?  
  *Basis*: Theorem 5.4 and Appendix C note the analysis assumes single-sample selection and batch selection only has a heuristic factor-K degradation.  
  *Why unresolved*: Batch selection introduces combinatorial complexities and non-linear group rewards not captured by the single-arm proof.  
  *Evidence needed*: A formal regret bound for K-armed selection per round with the advantage-dispersed reward, validated against empirical regret curves.

- **Question 3**: Does the proposed CBS framework generalize beyond mathematical reasoning benchmarks to other RLVR domains (e.g., code generation, instruction following) and to different model architectures?  
  *Basis*: Experiments are exclusively on six math benchmarks with Qwen3; introduction mentions code generation but no tests.  
  *Why unresolved*: Feature correlations and rollout noise characteristics may differ across domains and model families.  
  *Evidence needed*: Replication on a code benchmark (e.g., HumanEval) with a different base model (e.g., Llama), measuring consistent improvement.

## Limitations
- **Missing hyperparameters**: MLP architecture details (layers, width), epsilon-greedy decay schedule (ε_min, Δε), exact buffer management (when usage/age update), reward normalization initialization, and evaluation sampling settings (temperature, top-p) are not fully specified, hindering exact replication.
- **Theoretical assumptions**: Regret analysis assumes idealized conditions (linear reward structure, infinite samples) that may not align perfectly with finite LLM training dynamics.
- **Performance uncertainty**: Reported gains (e.g., 8.5% improvement, 50.4% time reduction) depend on undisclosed settings; confidence in precisely replicating these numbers is low.

- **Claim**: EMA smoothing and entropy control are critical. **Confidence**: Medium – ablations are plausible but could interact with unspecified scheduler design choices.
