# CPUBone: Efficient Vision Backbone Design for Devices with Low Parallelization Capabilities

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.26425  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26425  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** CPUBone achieves state-of-the-art accuracy‑latency trade‑offs on CPUs; e.g., CPUBone‑B0 attains 77.6% ImageNet top‑1 accuracy in 24.2 ms on a Raspberry Pi 5, and CPUBone‑B3 reaches 83.1% in 199.8 ms, outperforming prior CPU‑optimized backbones.

## Executive Summary
Modern vision backbones are optimized for high‑parallelism hardware (GPUs) and suffer high latency on CPUs due to excessive MAC operations and poor hardware efficiency (MACs per second). This paper introduces **CPUBone**, a family of backbones tailored for CPU inference. The core idea modifies MBConv blocks in two ways: (1) set convolution groups to two to reduce per‑group channel width, and (2) replace the 3×3 depthwise kernel with a 2×2 kernel in later stages. These changes cut MAC counts by up to 45% and 50% respectively while preserving or even improving MACpS on CPUs—unlike GPUs where grouping typically harms efficiency. The macro‑architecture combines grouped fused MBConv (for channels < 256) and grouped MBConv (for channels ≥ 256), and integrates LowFormer attention (with nearest‑neighbor upsampling) in the final two stages. Trained from scratch on ImageNet‑1K, CPUBone models (B0–B3) achieve superior accuracy‑latency trade‑offs on ImageNet classification, ADE20K segmentation, and COCO detection compared to baselines such as FasterNet‑T1 and RepViT‑M2.3.

## Method Summary
CPUBone explores two structural modifications to MBConv blocks:

1. **Grouped MBConv variants:**  
   - *Grouped Fused MBConv (GrFuMBConv)*: groups = 2, expansion = 4, depthwise conv followed by pointwise; used when channel count < 256.  
   - *Grouped MBConv (GrMBConv)*: groups = 2, expansion = 4, separate pointwise and depthwise; used when channels ≥ 256.  
   Both reduce MACs (by ~45% for GrFuMBConv vs. fused MBConv; ~23% for GrMBConv vs. standard MBConv) and maintain high MACpS on CPUs.

2. **2×2 depthwise kernels** applied only in the last two stages (Stages 3 & 4), where feature resolutions are already lower; earlier stages retain 3×3 kernels to preserve receptive field.

The backbone macro‑architecture (B0–B3) follows a LowFormer‑like layout: stem → Stage 1 (GrFuMBConv) → Stage 2 (GrFuMBConv) → Stage 3 (GrMBConv with 2×2 kernels + LowFormer attention) → Stage 4 (GrMBConv with 2×2 kernels + LowFormer attention) → task‑specific head. LowFormer attention replaces the transpose convolution in the original with nearest‑neighbor upsampling to avoid checkerboard artifacts and further reduce MACs.

Models are trained from scratch on ImageNet‑1K for 320 epochs using AdamW (cosine decay, 20‑epoch warm‑up) without distillation or pretraining. Downstream tasks (ADE20K with Semantic FPN, COCO with RetinaNet) adopt standard 1× schedules.

## Key Results
- **CPUBone‑B0** (10.4 M, 519 M MACs): 77.6% top‑1 ImageNet accuracy, 24.2 ms latency on Raspberry Pi 5, beating FasterNet‑T1 (76.2% at 32.8 ms).  
- **CPUBone‑B3** (40.7 M, 4054 M MACs): 83.1% top‑1, 199.8 ms, outperforming RepViT‑M2.3 (82.5% at 227 ms).  
- **ADE20K segmentation:** CPUBone‑B0 yields 37.9% mIoU with backbone latency 131.5 ms (up to 3× faster).  
- **COCO detection (RetinaNet):** CPUBone‑B0 achieves 37.5% AP at 131.5 ms backbone latency (up to 4× faster).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Depthwise convolutions, while low in MAC count, are memory‑bound on CPUs because they suffer from poor data locality and high memory traffic, leading to low MACpS. Grouping (groups = 2) reduces the number of channels processed per group, increasing cache reuse and improving MACpS on CPUs. The 2×2 kernels further cut MACs and memory accesses with limited accuracy impact when used only in later stages where the receptive field is already sufficiently large. On CPUs these modifications preserve or increase hardware efficiency; on GPUs, grouping typically reduces parallelism and hurts efficiency. Thus, CPUBone achieves a better accuracy‑latency trade‑off on CPU devices by explicitly targeting the memory bottleneck.

## Foundational Learning
Understanding CPUBone relies on several key concepts:

1. **MAC (Multiply‑Accumulate) Operations**  
   - *Brief:* The fundamental arithmetic in deep learning convolutions; used to quantify computational cost.  
   - *Why needed:* CPUBone explicitly reports MAC reductions; accurate computation of MACs is essential to evaluate theoretical efficiency.  
   - *Quick check:* Compute MACs for a 3×3 convolution with 64 input channels, 128 output channels, and a 224×224 input feature map (ignore bias).  

2. **Hardware Efficiency (MACs per second, MACpS)**  
   - *Brief:* Metric of how many MAC operations a hardware device can perform per second; reflects utilization of compute units.  
   - *Why needed:* CPUBone aims to maintain high MACpS on CPUs despite reducing MAC counts; MACpS reveals whether a layer is compute‑ or memory‑bound.  
   - *Quick check:* If a layer executes 100 million MACs in 10 ms, what is its MACpS?  

3. **Depthwise Separable Convolution**  
   - *Brief:* Factorization of a standard convolution into a depthwise (per‑channel) convolution followed by a pointwise (1×1) convolution.  
   - *Why needed:* MBConv blocks (used in CPUBone) rely on depthwise convolutions; their efficiency on CPUs is a central observation.  
   - *Quick check:* Decompose a 3×3 convolution with 128 input and 128 output channels into depthwise and pointwise parts; compute MACs for each part.  

4. **Grouped Convolution**  
   - *Brief:* Splitting input channels into groups and performing separate convolutions on each group, then concatenating outputs.  
   - *Why needed:* CPUBone modifies MBConv to use groups = 2, which reduces per‑group channel count and improves cache efficiency on CPUs.  
   - *Quick check:* For a conv with 128 input channels, groups = 2, and 256 output channels, determine input/output channels per group.  

5. **CPU vs GPU Parallelism**  
   - *Brief:* CPUs have few complex cores optimized for sequential tasks; GPUs have thousands of simple cores for massive data parallelism.  
   - *Why needed:* Design choices for CPUBone are CPU‑specific; what works on GPUs (e.g., depthwise conv) often performs poorly on CPUs.  
   - *Quick check:* List two architectural reasons why depthwise convolutions achieve lower MACpS on CPUs than on GPUs.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Input → Stem (3×3 conv, stride 2) → Stage 1 (GrFuMBConv blocks) → Stage 2 (GrFuMBConv blocks) → Stage 3 (GrMBConv blocks with 2×2 kernels + LowFormer attention) → Stage 4 (GrMBConv blocks with 2×2 kernels + LowFormer attention) → Task‑specific head (global avg‑pool + classifier for ImageNet; Feature Pyramid + detection/segmentation heads for downstream tasks).

### Critical Path
The forward pass flows sequentially through the stem, then each stage’s MBConv blocks (stacked as per Table 5). In Stages 3‑4, each MBConv block is followed by a LowFormer attention module that upsamples via nearest‑neighbor instead of transpose convolution. The final stage’s output is either pooled for classification or fed into task‑specific heads.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Group count (2 vs. 4):** Groups = 2 offered the best speed‑accuracy trade‑off; groups = 4 reduced accuracy with marginal MACpS gains.  
- **Kernel size scheduling:** 2×2 kernels used only in Stages 3‑4 to preserve receptive field early; earlier use would degrade accuracy more than latency savings.  
- **Block type selection:** GrFuMBConv (fused) for smaller channel counts (<256) reduces operator overhead; GrMBConv (separate pointwise) for larger channels (>256) maintains accuracy.  
- **Attention upsampling:** Nearest‑neighbor replaces transpose conv to eliminate checkerboard artifacts and lower MACs at a slight cost in expressiveness.

### Failure Signatures
- **MAC count mismatch:** Total MACs differ strongly from reported (e.g., B0 should be ~519 M). Compute per‑stage MACs and compare.  
- **Latency anomaly:** On Raspberry Pi 5, B0 latency > 30 ms or GrFuMBConv showing lower MACpS than FuMBConv on CPU (opposite of paper).  
- **Accuracy drop:** ImageNet top‑1 below 77% for B0 suggests wrong block config (e.g., 2×2 kernels in Stage 1) or missing training heuristics (augmentation, weight decay).  
- **Block misuse:** GrFuMBConv used for channels ≥ 256 or GrMBConv for < 256; or 2×2 kernels applied before Stage 3. Verify against Table 5.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Optimal group count:** The paper only evaluates groups = 2 and 4; a systematic sweep across group values (1, 2, 4, 8, 16) and channel dimensions could reveal Pareto‑optimal configurations for different CPUs.  
- **Kernel size placement:** CPUBone restricts 2×2 kernels to later stages under the assumption that larger kernels are essential early for receptive field; direct ablations of earlier 2×2 introduction are needed to validate this trade‑off.  
- **Generalization to other block types:** All modifications are applied to MBConv; it remains unknown whether similar CPU efficiency gains hold for other micro‑architectures (e.g., PConv in FasterNet, MLPs in ViTs).  
- **Hardware bottleneck analysis:** The paper attributes low MACpS of depthwise convs on CPUs to memory access costs but does not isolate the contribution of cache hierarchy vs. bandwidth; profiling with synthetic benchmarks would clarify the root cause.

## Limitations
- Training protocol specifics (exact augmentation pipeline, weight decay, EMA usage) are not fully disclosed, making exact reproduction of ImageNet accuracies difficult.  
- LowFormer attention implementation details (embedding dimension, number of heads, layer‑norm placement) are only referenced from [22] with the upsampling modification; precise configuration for CPUBone is missing.  
- Latency results are hardware‑specific (Raspberry Pi 5, Pixel 4, etc.); absolute numbers may not generalize to other CPUs due to differences in microarchitecture, compiler flags, and BLAS libraries.
