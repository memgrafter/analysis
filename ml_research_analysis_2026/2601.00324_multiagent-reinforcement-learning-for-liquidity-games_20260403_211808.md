# Multiagent Reinforcement Learning for Liquidity Games

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.00324
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.00324
- Reference count: 7
- Primary result: Difference rewards enable self-interested agents to achieve high market liquidity without coordination, particularly effective under partial clearing.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates how self-interested agents in decentralized financial markets can collectively sustain high market liquidity without explicit coordination. It unifies two frameworks—Liquidity Games and Rational Swarms—by employing difference rewards within a Markov team game formulation. Difference rewards give each agent its marginal contribution to the total cleared volume, aligning individual incentives with the global liquidity objective. The study finds that agents trained with difference rewards achieve the highest cumulative liquidity and throughput under partial clearing (MinFill regime). Under stricter exact-match constraints, they still reach about 70% trade success, outperforming alternative reward designs and non-learning baselines. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of difference rewards in incentivizing liquidity-provision behaviors in bilateral markets.

## Method Summary
The simulation uses a custom LiquidityGameEnv where 1300 agents (33% large with balance cap 40, 66% small with cap 10) are randomly paired each episode. Agents select integer offers up to their current balance. Two clearing rules are considered: MinFill (partial execution, quantity = min(offers)) and Exact Match (trade only if offers are equal and positive). After clearing, balances are updated. Agents employ tabular Q-learning with state = current balance and actions = feasible offers. Four reward modes are tested: difference reward (agent’s marginal contribution to total liquidity), local reward (2q - a_i - penalty), global reward (total market liquidity), plus baseline agents: random and greedy (full balance with penalty for over-offering under MinFill). Training proceeds for 10,000 episodes with fixed hyperparameters (α=0.1, γ=0.99, ε=0.2). Metrics include cumulative liquidity, hit rate, and clearing efficiency.

## Key Results
- Under MinFill, difference-reward agents achieve the highest cumulative liquidity and per-agent throughput, significantly outperforming local, global, and baseline agents.
- Under exact-match constraints, difference-reward agents converge rapidly to ~70% trade success, while alternatives struggle with sparse rewards.
- The advantage of difference rewards holds across heterogeneous agent populations, demonstrating robust incentive alignment in bilateral markets.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Difference Rewards Align Individual and Global Objectives**
- Claim: Difference rewards (G(z) - G(z_{-i})) provide each agent a signal equal to its marginal contribution to total cleared volume, making the best response for the agent also beneficial to the system.
- Core assumption: The counterfactual G(z_{-i}) (system liquidity if agent i’s offer were zero) can be accurately computed or approximated while holding the partner’s offer fixed.
- Evidence: Abstract and Section 5 (Learning Agents) formalize D_i and state it quantifies agent i’s improvement while preserving independence. Corpus shows novelty of applying difference rewards to bilateral OTC markets.
- Break condition: In highly non-linear interactions (strong complementarities), marginal estimates become noisy; also computationally intractable for large populations without approximation.

**Mechanism 2: Markov Team Game Framing Enables Stochastic Learning**
- Claim: Modeling the market as a Markov game with state = current balances allows tabular Q-learning to capture the sequential decision problem while keeping the team objective G (total cleared volume) as the global reward.
- Core assumption: The state representation (balances) is sufficient to capture all relevant dynamics (Markov property). Past pairing histories or order flow are ignored.
- Evidence: Abstract mentions the Markov team games framework; Section 3 discusses the Markov property and its limitations for tractability.
- Break condition: If environments exhibit significant path dependence (e.g., strategies depend on past counterparties), the Markov state becomes inadequate, leading to suboptimal policies.

**Mechanism 3: Clearing Rule Determines Reward Landscape and Equilibrium**
- Claim: The choice of clearing rule (MinFill vs. Exact Match) fundamentally shapes the global liquidity objective and the effectiveness of difference rewards.
- Core assumption: Global liquidity G is the sum of traded quantities across pairs; MinFill yields smoother, more frequent trades, while Exact Match imposes sparse matching.
- Evidence: Section 4 defines the two Liquidity Games and their Nash equilibria; Section 6 shows MinFill leads to higher cumulative liquidity and hit rates, while Exact Match yields ~70% success with difference rewards.
- Break condition: If clearing rules are too mismatched with real market frictions (e.g., transaction costs, adverse selection), learned policies may not transfer; in Exact Match many actions yield zero marginal contribution, weakening the difference reward signal.

## Foundational Learning

**Difference Rewards** (Multiagent RL / Swarm Intelligence)
- Why needed: Standard local rewards (agent’s own trade) ignore positive externalities; global rewards are not centrally available. Difference rewards offer a local signal that credits an agent for its marginal increase in system liquidity, aligning selfish learning with collective efficiency.
- Quick check: Under MinFill, if agent offers a_i and partner a_j, what is the difference reward if the agent had offered 0? (Answer: traded quantity q, since G(z_{-i}) = 0 for that pair.)

**Markov Decision Processes and Tabular Q-Learning**
- Why needed: Agents face a sequential decision problem: choose offer based on current balance to maximize cumulative reward. Tabular Q-learning provides a simple, interpretable foundation for comparing reward signals in discrete state/action spaces.
- Quick check: What violates the Markov assumption in this bond market model? (Answer: The assumption that future states depend only on current balances; in reality, past trading partners or order flow history may influence future matching.)

**Nash Equilibrium in Congestion/Coordination Games**
- Why needed: The Liquidity Game is a one-shot simultaneous game. Its equilibria (e.g., equal positive offers in Exact Match; maximum trade in MinFill) provide theoretical benchmarks for rational behavior and help interpret learning outcomes.
- Quick check: In an exact-match game with two agents having balances 5 and 10, what are the pure Nash equilibria? (Answer: profiles (a,a) where a ∈ {1,...,5} and (0,0); unequal positive offers yield 0 payoff, so both can deviate to equal a to get a>0.)

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**:
LiquidityGameEnv -> Agent (Q-learning) -> RewardCalculator (computes signal)
Baselines: RandomAgent, GreedyAgent; MetricsLogger gathers episode statistics

**Critical Path** (per episode):
1. Reset: Initialize agent balances (random within size-specific caps).
2. Random pairing: Form n/2 disjoint pairs.
3. For each pair: Agents simultaneously select offer action using own Q-table (ε-greedy).
4. Apply clearing rule (MinFill or Exact Match) to compute traded quantity q per pair.
5. Update each agent's balance (subtract q).
6. Compute reward for each agent according to its reward mode (Diff/Local/Global).
7. Learning agents update Q(s,a) using TD error: Q(s,a) += α (r + γ max_a' Q(s',a') - Q(s,a)).
8. Log metrics: total liquidity, hit rate, clearing efficiency.
9. Repeat from step 1 for next episode.

**Design Tradeoffs**:
- Tabular Q-learning vs. function approximation: Tabular is interpretable but doesn't scale to large/continuous state-action spaces; real systems would need DQN or policy gradients.
- MinFill vs. Exact Match: MinFill yields smoother reward landscapes, aiding learning but may be less realistic; Exact Match is sparser but mirrors some OTC markets.
- Difference reward computation: Simplified under MinFill (D_i = q); under Exact Match requires a counterfactual simulation, increasing complexity.

**Failure Signatures**:
- Learning agents converging to zero offers: indicates too harsh environment (e.g., exact match with high mismatch) where any positive offer risks zero reward.
- Greedy baseline underperforming due to mis-specified penalty: check whether penalty reduces agent reward or global liquidity; verify penalty logic.
- Global reward agents failing to learn: noisy/delayed signal may not credit individual contributions, causing slow convergence.
- Metrics instability: cumulative liquidity plateaus or declines, indicating broken reward or bad hyperparameters.

**First 3 Experiments**:
1. **Baseline comparison under MinFill**: Run all agent types (Diff, Local, Global, Random, Greedy) for 10k episodes. Expect Diff to achieve highest cumulative liquidity and clearing efficiency.
2. **Exact match robustness test**: Repeat with exact fill clearing rule. Expect Diff to converge faster to ~70% hit rate, outperforming Local/Global.
3. **Heterogeneity sensitivity**: Vary proportion of large vs. small agents (e.g., 50/50 split instead of 33/66). Test if Diff maintains advantage across population compositions.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How do persistent state variables (e.g., inventory holdings) and transaction frictions (e.g., costs) affect liquidity emergence under difference rewards?
2. Do alternative, more complex market clearing mechanisms (beyond MinFill and exact match) alter the effectiveness of difference rewards for liquidity provision?
3. How sensitive are the performance gains of difference rewards to increased agent heterogeneity and partial observability of market state?
4. Can the Financial Swarm model with difference rewards be calibrated and validated using real-world OTC market data, or within hybrid ABM-RL frameworks?

## Limitations
- Exact-match difference reward definition is ambiguous; counterfactual G(z_{-i}) not formally specified, risking implementation bias.
- Greedy baseline penalty details are unclear: whether it reduces agent reward or global liquidity, and exact application logic.
- Markov assumption (state = current balances) may omit relevant history (e.g., past partners), limiting realism and transferability to actual OTC markets.
