# LIBERO-Para: A Diagnostic Benchmark and Metrics for Paraphrase Robustness in VLA Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.28301  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.28301  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** VLA models fine-tuned on limited demonstrations suffer a 22–52 percentage point drop in success rate on paraphrased commands; PRIDE reveals binary success rates overestimate robustness by up to 22% absolute.

## Executive Summary
Vision-language-action (VLA) models often overfit to the exact surface forms of instructions seen during limited demonstration fine-tuning, leaving their robustness to natural paraphrasing unmeasured. This paper introduces **LIBERO-Para**, a diagnostic benchmark built on the LIBERO-Goal suite that systematically varies both object references and action expressions across 43 fine-grained paraphrase types, yielding 4,092 instances. It also proposes **PRIDE** (Paraphrase Robustness Index), a difficulty-adjusted metric that combines lexical and structural similarity between original and paraphrased instructions. Evaluating seven state-of-the-art VLA models (0.6B–7.5B) reveals a consistent 22–52 percentage point drop in success rate under paraphrasing, with object-level substitutions causing the sharpest declines. Moreover, 80–96% of failures are classified as planning-level (trajectory divergence) rather than execution errors, demonstrating that paraphrase misalignment disrupts task identification. PRIDE correlates negatively with binary success rates, exposing that standard evaluations drastically overestimate robustness, particularly on easy paraphrases. Together, LIBERO-Para and PRIDE establish a rigorous foundation for measuring and improving linguistic generalization in embodied agents.

## Method Summary
The authors construct **LIBERO-Para** by taking the 10 canonical instructions of LIBERO-Goal and generating paraphrases using linguistically grounded taxonomies: for objects, addition and same-polarity substitution; for actions, lexical, structural, and pragmatic variations. Each paraphrase is labeled by type and paired with precomputed similarity scores: **SK** (keyword similarity via Sentence-BERT) and **ST** (structural similarity via normalized dependency tree edit distance). The **PRIDE** metric weights these by a parameter α (default 0.5) to compute a paraphrase distance PD = 1 − (α·SK + (1−α)·ST). PRIDE = PD only if the task succeeded, thereby adjusting the binary success rate for difficulty.

Evaluation uses the LIBERO simulation (MuJoCo). For each VLA model, the authors realize 5 random seeds per (original task, paraphrase) pair, recording success/failure and the 3‑D end‑effector trajectory. They then aggregate cell‑wise success rates, compute overall PRIDE, and analyze failures: a pseudo‑ground‑truth trajectory per task (mean of successful runs) is compared to failed trajectories using Dynamic Time Warping (DTW); failures with DTW below a task‑specific threshold are deemed execution‑level (Near‑GT), and those above are planning‑level (Far‑GT). This pipeline enables fine‑grained diagnosis of where paraphrases break the VLA pipeline.

## Key Results
- **Object-level lexical substitutions cause the sharpest decline:** success rate drops of 19.8–51.0 percentage points across models; action indirectness adds incremental degradation.
- **80–96% of failures are planning-level:** paraphrased commands mostly lead to task misunderstanding (divergent trajectories), not motion execution errors.
- **Binary success rate overestimates robustness:** PRIDE reveals an absolute overestimation of up to 22%, as models succeed primarily on easy paraphrases (high lexical/structural similarity).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
VLA models finetuned on limited demonstrations overfit to the exact phrasing of object nouns present in the training data. Each object is typically referenced by a single canonical name (e.g., “stove”), so the model learns a tight association between that token sequence and the corresponding visual target. When a synonym (“range”) is used, the vision‑language backbone yields a different language representation that the policy head fails to normalize, leading to failure to locate the correct object. This accounts for the largest observed drops.

PRIDE corrects the misleading binary success rate by explicitly accounting for paraphrase difficulty. It combines keyword similarity (task‑critical tokens) and structural similarity (dependency tree divergence) into a single difficulty score. Because success sharply drops with even minor lexical changes (evidence: strong negative correlation between PRIDE and raw success rate), the weighted metric reveals that binary SR overestimates robustness—models succeed only on the easiest paraphrases.

The two‑axis benchmark design isolates the dominant bottleneck (object paraphrases) and quantifies incremental degradation from action indirectness. By generating paraphrases that vary only one aspect at a time, the experiment shows that object‐level variation consistently causes a larger performance gap than any action‑level variation, and the combination of both often yields additive, not multiplicative, damage. This clean decomposition points directly to the need for better object grounding.

- **Paraphrase Taxonomies**: The benchmark draws from linguistic typologies (e.g., lexical substitution, structural changes, pragmatic indirectness) to generate systematic variations. *Why needed here:* The 43 variation types are derived from these taxonomies; knowing what “coordination paraphrase” or “question directive” means clarifies the scope of robustness tested. *Quick check:* Give an example of a coordination paraphrase and a question directive paraphrase for “Pick up the bowl.”
- **Trajectory-Level Failures**: Failures are classified as Near‑GT (execution‑level) or Far‑GT (planning‑level) by comparing failed trajectories to a pseudo‑ground‑truth via DTW. *Why needed here:* The key finding that 80–96% are planning‑level depends on this classification; the notion of trajectory divergence relative to successful returns is central. *Quick check:* If a model reaches the wrong object but then performs the correct motion (e.g., grasping), is that Near‑GT or Far‑GT? Why?
- **Difficulty‑Adjusted Evaluation Metrics**: PRIDE contrasts with standard text metrics (BLEU, BERTScore) by weighting each paraphrase by its combined lexical and syntactic distance from the original. *Why needed here:* The paper argues binary SR is insufficient because models succeed on easy phrases but fail on hard ones; PRIDE attempts to capture this nuance. *Quick check:* Why might BLEU score poorly reflect the difficulty of a paraphrase like “Is the range hot yet?” compared to “Carefully turn on the stove”?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** LIBERO-Para dataset (CSV/JSON) → LIBERO simulation (MuJoCo) → VLA model inference (OpenVLA, π0.5, VLA‑Adapter, etc.) → trajectory recording → PRIDE calculation (Sentence‑BERT embeddings for SK, spaCy dependency parser for ST, fastdtw for DTW) → analysis (heatmaps, PRIDE‑SR plots, failure classification).

**Critical path:**  
1. Install LIBERO and baseline VLA checkpoints; verify >90% success on LIBERO‑Goal (unparaphrased).  
2. For each paraphrase, run 5 seeds per task, record success and full end‑effector trajectory.  
3. Aggregate success rate per variation cell (object × action).  
4. Compute PRIDE per instance: extract content‑word embeddings, compute SK; parse sentences, compute normalized tree edit distance ST; combine with α = 0.5 into PD; PRIDE = PD if success=1 else 0.  
5. Compare SR and PRIDE; compute absolute overestimation.  
6. Classify failures: compute mean successful trajectory per task (pseudo‑GT), resample trajectories to 50 points, compute DTW to pseudo‑GT; failures with DTW ≤ task τ are Near‑GT, else Far‑GT.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Keyword extraction can be manual (ensuring only task‑relevant tokens) or automatic via POS tagging; manual is stricter but labor‑intensive.  
- Structural similarity (TED) depends on parser quality; spaCy’s `en_core_web_trf` is robust but may still fail on non‑standard paraphrases.  
- α weighting (default 0.5) governs lexical vs. syntactic importance; the α‑sweep shows keyword dominance, but domains with strong structural constraints may need adjustment.  
- Pseudo‑GT construction: simple mean of resampled trajectories assumes consistent paths; if trajectories vary, DTW barycenter averaging may be more appropriate.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Large gap between raw SR and PRIDE indicates the model only succeeds on easy paraphrases (high SK, low PD).  
- If PRIDE is non‑zero but success also occurs, the model may be robust to that paraphrase type.  
- High Far‑GT percentage (>>20%) points to task‑identification failures; high Near‑GT suggests execution issues (e.g., π0.5 expert‑only).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Reproduce Table 2: Evaluate OpenVLA‑OFTmixed on LIBERO‑Goal and LIBERO‑Para; confirm drop of ~33 pp.  
2. Replicate PRIDE–SR correlation: For all 7 models, plot PRIDE vs. SR per paraphrase cell; expect strong negative correlation (Pearson r < −0.8, p < 0.0001).  
3. Perform α‑sensitivity sweep on VLA‑Adapter: vary α from 0.0 to 1.0, compute average PRIDE; observe monotonic decrease as α increases (keyword dominance).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Physical robot transfer:** Do the observed robustness failures (22–52 pp drop) persist on real hardware? The Limitation section notes further validation is required, as all evaluations were in simulation.  
- **Compound paraphrases:** How do VLA models handle co‑occurring multiple variation types (e.g., object substitution combined with indirect speech act)? The benchmark isolates single variations; natural language often combines them.  
- **Data augmentation:** Can paraphrase‑based data augmentation improve robustness without contaminating the evaluation? The authors avoided augmentation due to risk of distributional overlap with the test set.  
- **Architectural mitigation:** Which architectural modifications (e.g., more diverse training object names, frozen VLMs, better grounding modules) can best address the object‑level lexical bottleneck? The current study isolates the phenomenon but not the optimal fix.

## Limitations
- **Simulation‑only evaluation:** The benchmark and all experiments are conducted in LIBERO (MuJoCo); generalizability to physical robots is unknown.  
- **PRIDE weighting and keyword extraction:** The α = 0.5 parameter and the method for task‑critical keyword selection are not domain‑validated; alternative weightings could change conclusions.  
- **Underspecified evaluation details:** Exact hyperparameters (e.g., max episode length, inference horizons) and pseudo‑GT construction (mean vs. DTW barycenter) are not fully described, hindering exact replication.

## Confidence
- VLA models overfit to surface forms of instructions (object paraphrases cause large drops) → **High**  
- PRIDE metric reveals binary SR overestimation (strong negative correlation shown) → **Medium‑High**  
- Two‑axis design isolates bottlenecks (object primary, action secondary) → **Medium**  
- Planning‑level failures dominate (80‑96% Far‑GT) → **Medium**
