# Topology-Preserving Data Augmentation for Ring-Type Polygon Annotations

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.14764
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14764
- **Reference count:** 15
- **Primary result:** The proposed method achieves near‑perfect Cyclic Adjacency Preservation (CAP > 0.97) across common geometric augmentations and yields measurable gains in segmentation performance (e.g., YOLOv11‑Seg mIoU 90.8 vs. 88.4).

## Executive Summary
Standard geometric data augmentation (rotation, cropping, scaling, flipping) applied directly to polygon vertices often breaks the cyclic connectivity of ring‑type annotations—common in architectural floorplans—by discarding vertices at clip boundaries. This disrupts the single‑chain encoding of regions with holes. The paper introduces a topology‑preserving approach: augmentations are performed in mask space, then surviving original vertices are projected back onto the transformed mask contour and their adjacency is repaired by enforcing successor consistency within the original cyclic index sequence. The repair restores correct adjacency without altering traversal order. The method attains CAP scores above 0.97 for all tested augmentations, far surpassing baseline pipelines (YOLO: 0.33, Roboflow: 0.55). Models trained with the augmented data also show improved segmentation accuracy, demonstrated with YOLOv11‑Seg and Mask R‑CNN.

## Method Summary
The pipeline converts each input polygon into a binary mask, applies the desired geometric transformation in image space, and then extracts the resulting contour. For every original vertex, the algorithm locates the nearest point on the transformed contour (within a tolerance) and records the vertex’s original index if the point is retained. The surviving indices are sorted according to their original cyclic order. The repaired polygon is constructed by connecting each surviving vertex to the next surviving vertex in that order, closing the loop to form a single cyclic chain. This “successor consistency” step guarantees that adjacency relationships among the retained vertices match those in the source polygon, thereby preserving ring topology. The quality of preservation is measured by the Cyclic Adjacency Preservation (CAP) score, which computes the fraction of edges whose original successor is still adjacent in the repaired polygon.

## Key Results
- CAP scores exceed 0.97 for rotations, crops, scaling, and flips, dramatically outperforming standard augmentation pipelines (0.33–0.55).
- Training YOLOv11‑Seg with topology‑preserving augmentations yields mIoU = 90.8, compared to 88.4 with standard augmentation.
- The method is robust across a range of augmentation intensities and polygon complexity levels.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The repair mechanism leverages the fact that the original polygon is a single cyclic chain. After mask‑space augmentation, the transformed contour approximates the geometrically distorted polygon, but vertex‑to‑contour correspondence is lost. By projecting original vertices onto the transformed contour and retaining only those with sufficient proximity, we obtain a subset of vertices that survived the transformation. Sorting these survivors by their original indices restores the correct cyclic order. Enforcing that each vertex is connected to the next surviving vertex in that order replicates the original adjacency pattern among the retained vertices. Because the ordering is derived from the ground‑truth indexing, the adjacency structure is guaranteed to be consistent with the source polygon’s topology. The algorithm does not modify the traversal order, it merely re‑establishes connections that may have been broken by clipping or discretization.

## Foundational Learning
- **Ring‑type polygon encoding**: A single cyclic vertex sequence that represents both outer and inner boundaries via implicit bridge edges. *Why needed*: The repair algorithm assumes this encoding; it is the basis for ordering and adjacency restoration. *Quick check*: Load a sample polygon and verify that it forms a single closed loop; compute the number of self‑intersections (should be zero for simple rings).
- **Cyclic Adjacency Preservation (CAP)**: Metric measuring the proportion of edges whose original successor remains adjacent after augmentation. *Why needed*: Quantifies structural integrity beyond standard geometric similarity. *Quick check*: On a perfect copy, CAP = 1; on a randomly shuffled vertex order, CAP ≈ 0.
- **Mask‑space augmentation**: Applying geometric transforms to rasterized binary masks instead of vertex coordinates. *Why needed*: Enables use of well‑tested image augmentation libraries but destroys exact vertex correspondence, necessitating repair. *Quick check*: Apply a rotation to a mask, extract its contour, and compare to analytically rotated vertices; discrepancies appear near boundaries due to rasterization.
- **Vertex projection onto contours**: Finding the closest point on the transformed mask boundary for each original vertex. *Why needed*: Recovers approximate positions of surviving vertices after mask transformation. *Quick check*: For a simple square polygon, rasterize, rotate, project a corner vertex; the projected point should lie near the exact rotated corner within a pixel or two.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Input Polygon → Rasterization → Binary Mask → Geometric Augmentation → Augmented Mask → Contour Extraction → Vertex Projection → Index Mapping → Successor Consistency → Repaired Polygon

### Critical Path
1. Rasterize the input polygon to a binary mask.
2. Apply the geometric transformation (rotation, crop, scale, flip) to the mask.
3. Extract contours from the transformed mask.
4. For each original vertex, locate the nearest contour point; if the distance is below a threshold, keep the vertex and its original index.
5. Sort the surviving vertices by their original cyclic indices.
6. Construct the repaired polygon by connecting each surviving vertex to the next surviving vertex in the sorted order, forming a closed cycle.
7. Output the repaired polygon.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Mask‑space vs. vertex‑space**: Mask augmentation allows reuse of image processing tools but introduces quantization and contour approximation errors. The repair step corrects topology but may yield vertices slightly off the true transformed boundary.
- **Topology vs. geometric fidelity**: Enforcing successor consistency guarantees cyclic adjacency but does not ensure that the repaired polygon exactly follows the mask contour; tradeoffs emerge when many vertices are lost (e.g., aggressive cropping).
- **Encoding assumption**: The method relies on the single‑chain ring representation. Data stored as separate outer/inner rings requires an additional merging step, increasing preprocessing complexity and potential for error.

### Failure Signatures
- **CAP near 0 or undefined**: No surviving vertices (e.g., polygon completely cropped) or adjacency not preserved.
- **Non‑monotonic index order**: After sorting, vertex indices jump erratically (e.g., 5, 2, 8), indicating projection mismatches or duplicate assignments.
- **Visual discrepancies**: Repaired polygon deviates significantly from the augmented mask (gaps, self‑intersections), detectable by overlaying both.
- **Breakdown under extreme parameters**: Very small crop windows or extreme scales cause CAP to drop sharply, signaling the limits of the repair heuristic.

## Limitations
- Dataset access: The AI Hub floorplan dataset used in the paper requires registration and is not openly available; alternative datasets with validated ring‑type encodings are not provided.
- `polyaug` API details: The exact interface, input/output formats, and index handling are not described, forcing implementers to infer or reverse‑engineer the library.
- Missing training specifications: Hyperparameters, data splits, and integration method (online vs. offline) for segmentation experiments are omitted, hindering exact replication.
- Encoding constraint: The repair algorithm assumes a single cyclic chain; polygons stored as independent rings (e.g., GeoJSON MultiPolygon) must first be converted, adding a preprocessing step that may be non‑trivial.
