# Mean Estimation from Coarse Data: Characterizations and Efficient Algorithms
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.23341
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23341
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Complete geometric characterization of identifiability for convex partitions and a polynomial-time algorithm achieving optimal sample complexity eO(d/ε²) for Gaussian mean estimation from coarse data.

## Executive Summary
This paper resolves two fundamental questions in Gaussian mean estimation from coarse data, where observations are reported as sets in a partition of the space, under the assumption that the partition sets are convex. The authors provide a necessary and sufficient geometric condition for the mean to be uniquely recoverable: identifiability fails if and only if almost all partition sets are unbounded slabs (parallel infinite strips) in a single direction. On the algorithmic side, they introduce a polynomial-time method based on projected stochastic gradient descent with variance reduction and a truncation mechanism to handle unbounded sets. The algorithm attains an ε-accurate estimate using eO(d/ε²) samples and runs in polynomial time, matching the information-theoretic lower bound. This result enables efficient learning in applications such as linear regression with market frictions.

## Method Summary
The algorithm treats each coarse observation (a set from the partition) as a constraint that the mean must satisfy with some probability. It optimizes the negative log-likelihood using projected stochastic gradient descent (SGD). To accelerate convergence, a variance reduction technique (akin to SVRG) is employed, which reuses gradient information from previous iterations to reduce noise. Unbounded partition sets are handled via a careful truncation scheme that limits the influence of extreme points, leveraging the Gaussian distribution's sub-Gaussian tails and the fact that convex truncation reduces variance. The method iterates between computing full gradients at reference points and performing SGD updates with minibatch gradients, projecting onto the feasible region after each step. This yields an efficient estimator that converges to the true mean under the identifiability condition.

## Key Results
- **Identifiability characterization:** For a convex partition, the Gaussian mean is uniquely recoverable if and only if the partition does not consist almost entirely of unbounded slabs in a single direction.
- **Algorithmic guarantee:** A polynomial-time algorithm exists that, given samples from a Gaussian with known covariance and unknown mean observed through a convex partition, outputs an estimate with error ε using eO(d/ε²) samples.
- **Optimality and tractability:** The sample complexity matches the known lower bound, and the algorithm runs in polynomial time, resolving the open problem of efficient estimation under convex partitions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The characterization follows from analyzing the shape of the log-likelihood: if the partition contains many unbounded slabs aligned in one direction, the likelihood becomes flat along that direction, making the mean unidentifiable. Conversely, any deviation from this structure yields a strictly concave log-likelihood with a unique maximum. The algorithm exploits the concavity of the log-likelihood for known covariance. Projected SGD navigates the feasible region defined by the partition. Variance reduction (via control variates) dramatically lowers the gradient variance, leading to the optimal sample complexity. Truncation ensures that gradients from unbounded sets remain bounded and stable, as convex truncation of a Gaussian reduces variance and preserves concentration.

## Foundational Learning
- **Gaussian mean estimation:** The fundamental problem of estimating the mean of a Gaussian distribution; needed because the Gaussian log-likelihood is concave and leads to well-behaved optimization.
- **Convex partitions:** Assumption that each set in the partition is convex; ensures the log-likelihood is concave and enables geometric arguments for identifiability.
- **Identifiability:** Condition that the statistical model (coarse data distribution) uniquely determines the parameter (mean); essential for any estimation to be possible.
- **Stochastic gradient descent (SGD):** Optimization algorithm using stochastic gradients; scalable to high dimensions and forms the backbone of the estimator.
- **Variance reduction:** Technique (e.g., SVRG) to reduce the variance of stochastic gradients; crucial for achieving the information-theoretically optimal sample complexity.
- **Truncation for unbounded sets:** Method to bound the influence of observations from unbounded partition sets; necessary because unbounded sets can produce outliers that break standard SGD convergence.

## Architecture Onboarding
The algorithm's architecture can be viewed as a pipeline:

Data input (coarse observations) -> Preprocessing (map each sample to its partition set) -> Variance-reduced SGD loop -> Truncation module (clip gradient contributions) -> Projection onto feasible region (convex hull of partition sets) -> Mean estimate output.

**Critical path:** The variance-reduced SGD loop dominates runtime; each iteration samples a minibatch of sets, computes gradients, applies truncation, and projects, with occasional full gradients for variance reduction.

**Design tradeoffs:** Truncation threshold trades robustness to outliers for potential bias; step size affects convergence speed and stability; variance reduction batch size influences memory usage and communication overhead.

**Failure signatures:** Diverging loss (SGD instability), high variance across independent runs (inadequate variance reduction or truncation), and sensitivity to initialization (possible if truncation introduces non-convexities, though unlikely under identifiability).

**First experiments:**
1. Synthetic validation: Generate Gaussian data with known mean, observe through a convex partition (e.g., axis-aligned hypercubes), and verify that the estimate converges to the true mean as sample size increases.
2. Ablation study: Compare the proposed algorithm against plain SGD (without variance reduction) and against a version without truncation to demonstrate the contribution of each component.
3. Stress test: Apply the algorithm to partitions that are nearly degenerate (e.g., many thin slabs) to observe how identifiability failure manifests in estimation error.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What is the computational complexity of Gaussian mean estimation with unknown covariance matrix, where the log-likelihood becomes non-convex?
- **Basis:** [explicit] The authors state in the Conclusion: "While we study Gaussian estimation with a known covariance, understanding the computational complexity of estimation with unknown covariance is an interesting direction. Developing efficient algorithms for this problem would require new ideas since the log-likelihood for this case can be non-convex."
- **Why unresolved:** Non-convexity of the log-likelihood objective hinders the application of convex-optimization-based methods used in this work; it is unclear whether polynomial-time algorithms exist or if the problem is inherently harder.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A polynomial-time algorithm (or a complexity-theoretic lower bound) for unknown-covariance coarse Gaussian mean estimation under convex partitions, potentially using techniques for non-convex optimization that still achieve sample efficiency.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can the algorithmic and characterization results be extended beyond Gaussian distributions to other exponential families or general log-concave distributions?
- **Basis:** [explicit] The authors mention in the Conclusion: "Another interesting question is to extend our guarantees beyond Gaussians to other distribution families (see Appendix F for preliminary results in this direction)."

## Limitations
The paper's results are restricted to convex partitions and known covariance matrices. The identifiability condition reveals that if the partition consists almost entirely of unbounded slabs aligned in one direction, the mean is fundamentally unrecoverable. The algorithm does not handle unknown covariance, which introduces non-convexity and remains an open challenge. Additionally, practical implementations must carefully choose truncation thresholds and step sizes to avoid bias and ensure convergence.

## Confidence
The theoretical guarantees are proven with high probability and match information-theoretic lower bounds, providing strong confidence in the optimality of the sample complexity. The algorithm's convergence is established under the stated assumptions, and the use of variance reduction and truncation is well-motivated by Gaussian properties. However, confidence in real-world performance depends on the applicability of the convex partition model and the accurate specification of the covariance matrix.
