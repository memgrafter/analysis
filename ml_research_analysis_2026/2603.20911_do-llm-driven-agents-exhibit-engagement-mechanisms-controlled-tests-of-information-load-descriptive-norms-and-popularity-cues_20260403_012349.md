# Do LLM-Driven Agents Exhibit Engagement Mechanisms? Controlled Tests of Information Load, Descriptive Norms, and Popularity Cues
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.20911
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20911
- **Reference count:** 15
- **Primary result:** LLM agents exhibit human-like engagement mechanisms: they reduce participation under high information load, shift allocation according to descriptive norms, and show conditional bandwagon effects from popularity cues.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates whether large language model (LLM) driven agents can replicate human behavioral patterns in social media engagement. Using a Weibo-like simulation with 558 Qwen-8B agents, the authors experimentally manipulate information load (number of posts presented) and descriptive norm prompts (prevalence of likes vs. reposts) while allowing popularity cues (likes/reshares) to emerge endogenously. Engagement is analyzed in two stages: the decision to participate (like/repost/quote) versus read, and the allocation among specific engagement types. Results show that higher information load significantly reduces the odds of participation (OR=0.273 for high load vs. lowest load). Descriptive norm prompts reliably shift the distribution of engagement actions—repost-dominant norms increase reposting from 1.6% to 5.5% under high load. Popularity cues have a massive positive effect on engagement (OR=101.87), but this effect is moderated by both information load and descriptive norms, indicating conditional bandwagon dynamics. The findings validate that LLM agents can simulate mechanism-sensitive human behavior, supporting their use for controlled theory testing in social media contexts.

## Method Summary
The study uses the OASIS simulation framework with real Weibo user data (558 agents, follower network) and 50 seed posts about DeepSeek. Agents are activated asynchronously (1% per timestep) over 480 timesteps representing 24 hours. At each activation, the recommendation module presents 3 fixed posts from followed accounts plus a variable number of algorithmic posts (4, 7, 15, or 30) to manipulate information load. The agent module uses Qwen-8B to generate one action (read, like, repost, quote) based on displayed posts, their dynamic popularity counts, the agent’s profile and recent history, and a system prompt encoding the descriptive norm condition (no-norm, like-dominant, repost-dominant). Engagement actions update popularity metrics and create new repost/quote posts. The analysis models engagement as a two-stage outcome: binary logistic regression for participation vs. reading, and multinomial logistic regression for allocation among like/repost/quote, with predictors including load, norm, log-transformed popularity, and their interactions.

## Key Results
- Higher information load (larger choice set) reduces participation odds: high load OR=0.273 compared to lowest load (p<.001).
- Descriptive norms shift engagement allocation: repost-dominant norms increase reposting odds by 89.1× relative to liking, while like-dominant norms increase liking odds by 5.4× (both p<.001), without altering total participation rates.
- Popularity cues strongly boost overall engagement (OR=101.87, p<.001), but their effect is conditional: interactions with load and norm regimes show that responsiveness to popularity varies, e.g., under repost-dominant norms and high load, bandwagon sensitivity is amplified.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Information Load and Participation Thresholds**  
Higher information load (larger post choice-set) lowers the probability that agents engage versus read. The mechanism posits that increased load raises a simulated participation threshold due to capacity-limited attention, making agents more selective. Evidence: Logistic regression shows decreasing engagement odds across load conditions (ORs 0.645, 0.469, 0.273; all p<.001). A break would be if load increased engagement.

**Mechanism 2: Descriptive Norms and Engagement Allocation**  
Descriptive norm prompts (stating what others typically do) shift the distribution of engagement types toward the norm-emphasized action without changing overall participation. The mechanism: prevalence information biases the choice among actions by altering perceived typicality. Evidence: Multinomial logit shows repost-dominant norm increases reposting odds vs. like (OR=89.1, p<.001) and decreases liking; like-dominant norm increases liking (OR=5.4, p<.001). A break would be if norms produced no shift or inverted shifts.

**Mechanism 3: Popularity Cues and Conditional Bandwagon Effects**  
Popularity cues (cumulative likes/reshares) greatly increase engagement likelihood, but their marginal effect depends on information load and descriptive norms. The mechanism: cues serve as informational signals that lower participation thresholds, with sensitivity conditioned by the attentional environment and normative context. Evidence: Main effect of popularity OR=101.87 (p<.001); significant three-way interactions (e.g., popularity×high load×repost-dominant: B=3.649, p<.001). A break would be if popularity had a constant multiplicative effect (no interactions).

## Foundational Learning
- **Limited Capacity Models of Attention**: Provides theoretical basis for expecting information load to affect participation thresholds. Quick check: How would you operationally define "information load" in a social media feed to test a capacity mechanism, and what pattern of results would support it?
- **Descriptive vs. Injunctive Norms**: Critical for distinguishing the norm manipulation as descriptive (prevalence) rather than injunctive (prescriptive). Quick check: If the "like-dominant" prompt said "most people here like posts," is that descriptive or injunctive? How could you design a prompt to isolate descriptive norm effects?
- **Bandwagon Dynamics and Informational Cascades**: Underlies the expected effect of popularity cues as self-reinforcing signals. Quick check: Why is it methodologically important that popularity cues emerge endogenously rather than being exogenously imposed in the simulation?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Real agent profiles and network -> Recommendation module selects posts (3 followed + N algorithmic) -> Agent module (LLM) decides action based on posts + popularity + norm prompt + agent context -> State updater increments popularity and generates repost/quote posts -> Feed pool updates; loop.

**Critical path:**  
1. Timestep tick selects active agent (1% probability).  
2. Recommendation module pulls posts (3 from followed accounts + N algorithmic per load condition).  
3. Agent module constructs prompt with posts, their dynamic popularity counts, descriptive norm info, and agent context; LLM decodes one action (read/like/repost/quote).  
4. State update: if engagement, increment post’s popularity counts; if repost/quote, create new post object added to feed pool.  
5. Repeat.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Fixed activation rate vs. endogenous timing: constant 1% controls activity level but ignores burstiness; tradeoff between identification and ecological timing.  
- Prompt language (English) vs. content language (Chinese): ensures instruction consistency across norm conditions but adds potential cultural/linguistic misalignment.  
- Single-stage action choice vs. two-stage analytical model: agent picks one action from all options; analysis later splits into engage/read and allocation; tradeoff: simpler agent design vs. need for post-hoc analytic separation.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Mechanism 1 fail: Engagement rates flat or increasing with load across norm conditions.  
- Mechanism 2 fail: No significant shift in allocation hierarchy between norm regimes; agents ignore prevalence cues.  
- Mechanism 3 fail: No significant interactions involving popularity cues; effect purely additive and constant.  
- Systemic fail: High variance in realized load (number of posts shown) across conditions, violating manipulation.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Baseline replication: Run no-norm, lowest-load condition; verify aggregate engagement rates (read ~90-95%) and allocation (like > quote >> repost) match paper’s descriptive stats.  
2. Isolate load effect: Hold norm constant (no-norm); test if engagement probability monotonically decreases from lowest to high load; check OR gradient.  
3. Isolate norm effect: Hold load constant (e.g., medium); compare allocation across three norm regimes; confirm repost-dominant increases reposting share, like-dominant increases liking share.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Commenting behavior**: How does including commenting as an engagement behavior affect sensitivity to information load, descriptive norms, and popularity cues? The current study focuses on visibility-producing actions (like, repost, quote) and notes that comments may have different motivational drivers.
2. **Default prompt norms**: How do default prompts implicitly encode descriptive norms, and how does this affect interpretation of no-norm baselines? The authors acknowledge that even "no-norm" conditions are not neutral because prompt framing contains implicit expectations.
3. **Recommendation algorithm variation**: How do alternative recommendation algorithms condition the effects of load, norms, and endogenous popularity? The current design uses a fixed recommendation logic; varying it could test robustness and generalizability.

## Limitations
- **Dataset access**: Full 558-agent network and 50 seed posts are not publicly available, hindering exact replication.
- **Prompt design**: Exact system/agent prompts (beyond norm framing) and decoding parameters (e.g., top_p) are unreported, risking subtle cue contamination.
- **Ecological validity**: Fixed 1% activation rate ignores real-world bursty behavior; English prompts on Chinese content may introduce cultural/linguistic misalignment.

## Confidence
- Information load effect on participation: **High** (consistent OR gradient, p<.001)
- Descriptive norms reallocating engagement: **High** (strong norm-specific ORs, directional replication)
- Popularity cues as conditional bandwagon: **Medium** (massive main effect but interaction patterns depend on unreported prompt nuances and model specificity)

## Next Checks
1. Cross-model replication: Re-run key conditions (high vs. lowest load; repost-dominant norm) with additional LLM architectures (e.g., Llama-3-8B, GPT-4o-mini) to test robustness beyond Qwen-8B.
2. Prompt language control: Translate full prompts to Chinese and re-run the no-norm baseline to quantify any artifact from English instruction on Chinese content.
3. Endogenous activation timing: Replace fixed 1% activation with a bursty Poisson process (mean interval=100 timesteps) to test whether load/norm effects persist under more realistic activity patterns.
