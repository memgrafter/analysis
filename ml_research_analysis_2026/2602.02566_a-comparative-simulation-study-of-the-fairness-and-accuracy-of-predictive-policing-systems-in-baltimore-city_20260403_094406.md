# A Comparative Simulation Study of the Fairness and Accuracy of Predictive Policing Systems in Baltimore City

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.02566
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02566
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** PredPol achieves higher accuracy and fairness on average than hot-spots methods but amplifies racial and neighborhood bias fastest, with bias direction flipping based on crime type.

## Executive Summary
This study simulates predictive policing (PredPol) and traditional hot-spots policing (short- and long-term kernel density estimation) in Baltimore City using real crime data from 2019–2022. Over 300 simulated days, PredPol consistently delivers superior accuracy and better average fairness metrics compared to KDE variants. However, it accelerates bias amplification through feedback loops between police deployment and crime detection. Notably, the direction of racial bias reverses when training on all crimes versus aggravated assault alone: the former leads to over-policing of White neighborhoods (e.g., Mount Vernon), the latter to Black neighborhoods (e.g., Sandtown-Winchester). These findings reveal a trade-off between short-term fairness/accuracy and long-term bias dynamics, and underscore how crime-type selection critically shapes outcomes.

## Method Summary
The authors conduct an agent-based simulation using Baltimore crime records (2019–2022). Each day, a predictive model (PredPol, short-term KDE, or long-term KDE) is trained on a historical dataset of "detected" and "reported" crimes to produce a spatial risk map. Police officers are allocated to high-risk areas. A Noisy-OR detection model then determines which of that day’s actual crimes are detected, based on the number of officers in each neighborhood (each officer contributes a fixed 0.5 probability of detection). Detected crimes are fed back into the training data for the next day. Scenarios vary officer numbers (40 vs. 400), reporting probability (0 vs. 0.4), and crime types (aggravated assault only vs. all crimes). Fairness is measured via racial police-share gaps, police-crime ratio (PCR) gaps, and neighborhood Gini coefficients; accuracy is the proportion of crimes detected.

## Key Results
- PredPol is the most accurate (best coverage in 87.5% of scenarios) and fairest on average, with lower racial gaps in 62.5–75% of scenarios and lower neighborhood Gini in 75%.
- PredPol amplifies bias fastest: neighborhood fairness deteriorates in 100% of scenarios and racial fairness (PCR) in 75%, compared to slower deterioration under KDE.
- Bias direction flips with crime type: aggravated assault training targets Black neighborhoods (e.g., Sandtown-Winchester), while all-crime training leads PredPol to over-police White neighborhoods (e.g., Mount Vernon).
- Long-term KDE slows bias amplification but sacrifices accuracy, illustrating a time-dependent accuracy-fairness trade-off.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Feedback Loop Amplification via Detection-Dependent Training
Predictive policing amplifies bias because police presence increases the number of *detected* crimes in already-policed neighborhoods, creating a reinforcing cycle. The simulation uses a Noisy-OR model: each officer in a neighborhood adds a fixed probability (p=0.5) of detecting any ongoing crime. More officers raise detection rates, which inflates the recorded crime count in those areas. Since the model is trained solely on detected (and reported) crimes, higher counts lead to higher predicted risk, prompting more officers to be assigned the next day. This positive feedback loop is the core driver of rapid bias amplification. Evidence: The abstract identifies feedback loops as the source of bias; Section 3.2 defines the Noisy-OR detection; Section 4 shows PredPol amplifies neighborhood bias in all scenarios and racial bias in most. The break condition would be if detection probability were independent of officer count or if the model were trained on true (undetected) crime data.

### Mechanism 2: Crime-Type-Dependent Bias Direction Reversal
The direction of racial bias (over-policing Black vs. White neighborhoods) can reverse depending on which crime types are included in the training data. Different crimes have distinct spatial patterns: aggravated assault is concentrated in majority-Black neighborhoods (e.g., Sandtown-Winchester), while property crimes and other offenses are more prevalent in White neighborhoods (e.g., Mount Vernon). Predictive models aggregate past crime locations; thus, including all crimes shifts the aggregated risk toward areas with high property crime rates, flipping the over-policing target. Evidence: The abstract explicitly notes the flip; Section 4 and Figures 24–25 show PredPol focusing on Black neighborhoods with aggravated assault but on White neighborhoods with all crimes. The break condition would be if crime types were evenly distributed across racial neighborhoods or if the model used socioeconomic covariates uncorrelated with race.

### Mechanism 3: Temporal Fairness-Accuracy Divergence
Short-term accuracy and fairness are positively correlated (more accurate models are fairer initially), but long-term bias amplification rates differ across models, creating a trade-off over time. Models that tightly align with recent crime patterns (PredPol, short-term KDE) achieve higher initial accuracy and more equitable police distributions. However, their responsiveness to recent *detected* crimes (which are influenced by prior police deployment) accelerates the feedback loop. In contrast, long-term KDE smooths predictions over a longer history, slowing amplification but at the cost of some accuracy. Evidence: Section 4 finds higher neighborhood fairness gaps accompany lower accuracy, but racial fairness does not correlate with accuracy; PredPol shows the fastest trend of neighborhood bias worsening while long-term KDE slows it. The break condition would be a static true crime distribution or models with explicit fairness constraints that decouple allocation from recent detected counts.

## Foundational Learning
**Positive Feedback Loops in Socio-Technical Systems**: Why needed: The bias mechanism hinges on a reinforcing cycle between police deployment and detected crime counts. Without recognizing such loops, one might misinterpret correlation (more police where more crimes are detected) as causation (police cause crime). Quick check: If a neighborhood receives more police today, how does that affect its *detected* crime count tomorrow, and how does that change the model’s prediction for the next day?

**Point Process Models for Spatiotemporal Event Prediction**: Why needed: PredPol uses an Epidemic-Type Aftershock Sequence (ETAS) model, a self-exciting point process where each crime increases the intensity of nearby future crimes. This differs from simpler Kernel Density Estimation, which only aggregates past events. Quick check: What is the mathematical difference between KDE (summing historical crimes) and PredPol (adding a self-excitation term) in how they score a location for tomorrow’s risk?

**Group Fairness vs. Individual (Neighborhood) Fairness Metrics**: Why needed: The paper uses both group fairness (racial police-share gap) and individual/neighborhood fairness (Gini coefficient of police-to-crime ratios). These capture different notions: equal resource allocation across groups vs. proportional treatment across units. Quick check: Would a model that assigns police exactly proportional to each neighborhood’s crime share achieve a Gini of 0 for police-to-crime ratios? What about the racial police-share gap if neighborhoods are racially segregated?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: DataLoader loads initial crime and neighborhood data into History. Each simulation day: (1) ReportSimulator (if reporting enabled) subsamples real crimes to generate reported crimes, added to History. (2) Model fits on History (reported + detected crimes) and outputs officer allocations. (3) DetectionEngine applies the Noisy-OR model using officer allocations to that day’s real crimes, producing detected crimes added to History. (4) MetricsCalculator computes daily fairness and accuracy metrics from allocations and detection outcomes. SimulationLoop orchestrates this 300‑day cycle.

Critical path: The SimulationLoop runs 300 iterations. Each iteration: Model fitting (computationally heavy for PredPol/ETAS) → officer assignment → DetectionEngine loops over all crimes that day, applying Noisy-OR per neighborhood. The detection step dominates runtime if not vectorized by aggregating crimes by neighborhood first.

Design tradeoffs:
- **Data history**: Short-term KDE uses only 1 month → faster fits, volatile predictions; long-term KDE uses all history → slower fits, smoother.
- **Model complexity**: PredPol (ETAS) is O(n²) in crime events without approximations; KDE is O(n) with grid evaluation. With >200k crimes, spatial binning or sampling is necessary.
- **Stochasticity**: 20 runs per scenario for averaging increase computational cost; could reduce if variance is low.
- **Detection model**: Noisy-OR with p=0.5 is arbitrary; could calibrate to real clearance rates.

Failure signatures:
- **Numerical instability**: Long-term KDE with large bandwidth produces near-uniform densities → random officer assignment; check if all neighborhoods get exactly 1 officer when resources are low.
- **Feedback explosion**: PredPol’s self-excitation too high → >90% of officers concentrate in one neighborhood by day 100; monitor max police share per neighborhood.
- **No reporting**: If report_probability=0, only detected crimes feed back → purely deployment-driven feedback, likely exaggerating bias.
- **Cold start**: First day uses pre-2019 crimes assumed fully detected; if pre-2019 data is sparse or biased, initial allocation is arbitrary; check if initial distribution matches pre-2019 crime distribution.

First 3 experiments:
1. **Baseline replication**: 40 officers, report_prob=0.4, all crimes, 300 days for all models. Verify PredPol shows higher accuracy and neighborhood fairness on day 300 but faster bias amplification (negative Gini slope) vs. long-term KDE.
2. **Crime type sensitivity**: Repeat with aggravated assault only. Confirm bias direction flips: PredPol now over-polices Black neighborhoods (check Sandtown-Winchester in top-3 over-policed).
3. **Resource scaling**: Double officers to 400. Check if accuracy-fairness correlation strengthens (per Figure 10) and whether speed of bias amplification increases with more officers.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1:** How do specific crime categories (beyond aggravated assault vs. all crimes) influence the direction and magnitude of racial bias amplification? The study only compared two broad sets; other crime types might yield different patterns.
- **Question 2:** What underlying model or data characteristics causally determine the *speed* of bias amplification? PredPol amplifies faster than KDE, but the mechanistic cause (temporal weighting, granularity, interaction with reporting sparsity) is not isolated.
- **Question 3:** How does incorporating *variable* neighborhood-specific crime reporting rates (instead of uniform 0.4) alter fairness and accuracy trajectories? Uniform reporting likely masks spatial heterogeneity in under-reporting, which could interact uniquely with feedback loops.
- **Question 4:** Over longer simulation horizons (>300 days), do observed temporary reductions in racial fairness gaps inevitably lead to renewed amplification, or can they stabilize? The 300-day window may capture only a transient phase of a longer cycle.

## Limitations
- Implementation details for PredPol (kernel bandwidths, decay parameters) and the police sampling strategy are unspecified, hindering exact replication.
- Detection probability (p=0.5) and reporting rate (0.4) are arbitrary and not calibrated to real Baltimore clearance rates.
- Neighborhood racial classification by majority may obscure intra-neighborhood diversity and mask biases within groups.
- Results are tied to Baltimore’s specific crime and demographic patterns; generalizability to other cities remains uncertain.
