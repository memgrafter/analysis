# Online Statistical Inference of Constant Sample-averaged Q-Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.26982  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26982  
- **Reference count:** 1  
- **Primary result:** Sample-averaged Q-learning with constant batch updates achieves accurate statistical inference via a functional central limit theorem and random scaling, yielding confidence intervals with superior coverage and narrower widths compared to vanilla Q-learning.

## Executive Summary
Reinforcement learning algorithms often suffer from high variance in their updates, making statistical inference for the optimal Q‑function difficult. This paper proposes **sample‑averaged Q‑learning**, a variant that replaces single‑sample updates with batch averages drawn from a generative model. Under bounded rewards, the authors prove a **functional central limit theorem (FCLT)** for the resulting process, establishing its asymptotic normality. They then construct **online confidence intervals** using a random scaling technique that avoids explicit variance estimation. Experiments on a grid world and a dynamic resource‑matching problem show that the batch method maintains coverage near the nominal 95% (e.g., 99% for the grid world) while vanilla Q‑learning’s coverage degrades, and it produces substantially narrower intervals in the matching task. The results demonstrate that variance reduction through batch averaging enables reliable uncertainty quantification.

## Method Summary
The algorithm operates under a **generative model** that can sample i.i.d. next‑state/reward pairs for any state‑action. At each iteration *t*, it draws a constant batch size *B* of independent samples and updates the Q‑table using the batch‑averaged Bellman operator (Eq. 4). The step size *η* satisfies *η ∈ (0, η₀)* to ensure stationarity. After *T* iterations, the final estimator is the average `\bar{Q}_T`. For inference, the method computes `m_T = √(∑_{t=1}^T B_t^{-1}) ≈ √(T/B)` and an estimate `\hat{D}_T` of the asymptotic covariance by integrating the empirical process of centered Q‑values. Under the FCLT, the statistic  
`\hat{κ} = T( \bar{Q}_{T,j} - Q^*_j ) / ( m_T √{ \hat{D}_{T,jj} } )`  
converges to a pivotal random variable `κ`. Confidence intervals for `Q^*_j` are then  
`\bar{Q}_{T,j} ± κ_{α/2} (m_T/T) √{ \hat{D}_{T,jj} }`.  
The approach requires storing past Q‑values (or their sum) to compute `\hat{D}_T`.

## Key Results
- **Coverage rates:** In a 3×4 grid world, sample‑averaged Q‑learning maintains ~99% coverage at the 95% nominal level as the number of iterations grows; vanilla Q‑learning’s coverage declines to ~96% at `n=10000`, while interval lengths remain comparable.
- **Interval width:** In a dynamic resource‑matching problem (2×2 reward matrix), both methods achieve ~99.9% coverage, but sample‑averaged yields dramatically narrower intervals (e.g., 19.1 vs. 113.8 at 2000 iterations).
- **Theoretical confirmation:** The empirical results align with the asymptotic normality established by the FCLT and the validity of random scaling for online inference.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Variance reduction via batch averaging**  
Each update uses the average of *B* independent reward/next‑state samples, computing `\hat{T}_t(Q_t)` as in Eq. 4. This averaging acts as a variance reduction technique, making the Markov process `(Q_t)` less noisy. The batch estimator remains unbiased for the Bellman operator `T`, so the bias is unchanged while the variance shrinks by a factor of *B*. Under bounded rewards, this yields a more stable learning process. *Break condition:* If the generated samples are not i.i.d. (e.g., from a single non‑stationary trajectory), the variance reduction effect deteriorates.

**Mechanism 2: Functional Central Limit Theorem (FCLT)**  
With constant batch size *B* and step size `η` small enough, the process `(Q_t)` converges to a stationary distribution `Q_η`. Theorem 1 proves that the scaled, centered, and time‑averaged process  
`(1/√∑ B_t^{-1}) ∑_{t=1}^T (Q_t - E Q_η)`  
converges weakly to a Brownian motion with covariance `Σ_{Q_η}`. This FCLT is adapted from stochastic approximation theory for Markovian processes (Xie & Zhang 2022) and implies asymptotic normality of the final averaged estimator. *Break condition:* Requires bounded rewards (or uniformly integrable second moments), geometric ergodicity of the Markov chain induced by `T`, and `η` within `(0, η₀)`. Violating contraction (e.g., `γ=1` in some MDPs) or unbounded rewards can invalidate the result.

**Mechanism 3: Random scaling for online confidence intervals**  
The FCLT leads to a mixed‑normal limiting distribution for `\bar{Q}_T` because the scaling factor `m_T` is random. Theorem 2 shows that  
`\hat{κ} = T( \bar{Q}_{T,j} - Q^*_j ) / ( m_T √{ \hat{D}_{T,jj} } )`  
converges to a pivotal random variable `κ` (from Abadir & Paruolo 1997). Consequently, confidence intervals can be constructed as `\bar{Q}_{T,j} ± κ_{α/2} (m_T/T) √{ \hat{D}_{T,jj} }` without separate variance estimation; `\hat{D}_T` is computed online via the integrated empirical process. *Break condition:* Finite‑sample accuracy depends on correct estimation of `\hat{D}_T` and sufficiently large *T*; the method also needs O(*T*) memory to store past `Q_t`.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept: Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) and Bellman Optimality**  
*Why needed:* The whole problem is set in an infinite‑horizon MDP. Q‑learning targets the optimal Q‑function `Q*`, the unique fixed point of the Bellman optimality operator `T` under bounded rewards (a contraction). Understanding this fixed‑point equation explains why the update rule converges to `Q*`.

**Concept: Stochastic Approximation (SA) and Robbins‑Monro**  
*Why needed:* Q‑learning is a stochastic approximation algorithm: it solves the fixed‑point equation `T(Q)=Q` using noisy samples. The theoretical analysis (stationarity, FCLT) builds on SA theory for Markovian processes, including step‑size conditions and averaging techniques.  
*Quick check:* How does using a constant batch size `B>1` instead of `B=1` change the noise structure of the SA updates?

**Concept: Central Limit Theorem (CLT) and Functional CLT (FCLT)**  
*Why needed:* The inference in this paper relies on asymptotic normality of the averaged estimator, derived from an FCLT for the entire process `(Q_t)`. The FCLT extends the CLT to stochastic processes and is essential for the random scaling method.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Generative Model Interface -> Batch Updater -> Running Statistics Accumulator -> Random Scaling Module -> CI Reporter

**Critical Path**  
The generative model supplies i.i.d. `(R, S')` samples to the Batch Updater, which computes the Q‑update. The Running Statistics Accumulator stores the deviations `Q_t - \bar{Q}` needed for `\hat{D}_T`. As `T` grows, the Random Scaling Module computes `m_T`, `\hat{D}_T`, and the κ statistic; the CI Reporter then outputs intervals using tabulated κ quantiles.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *Batch size B:* Larger *B* reduces per‑iteration noise and improves inference accuracy, but raises computational cost and may require a smaller step size to prevent bias. The paper assumes constant *B*; adaptive schedules are left for future work.  
- *Step size η:* Must lie in `(0, η₀)` to ensure stationarity. Too large → divergence; too small → slow convergence.  
- *Memory vs. Accuracy:* Computing `\hat{D}_T` demands O(*T*) storage for the entire Q‑history, which can become prohibitive for large state‑action spaces.

**Failure Signatures**  
- *Coverage collapse:* If `\hat{D}_T` underestimates the true asymptotic covariance, CIs become too narrow and coverage falls far below nominal. Often caused by insufficient mixing or use of early non‑stationary points.  
- *Instability:* Violating the step‑size bound (η too large) makes `Q_t` explode; monitor `||Q_t||∞`.  
- *Memory overflow:* For large |S×A| and very long runs, the O(*T*) memory for Qt history may exceed available memory.

**First Experiments**  
1. **Reproduce Grid World (Section 5.1).** Verify that sample‑averaged Q‑learning yields ~99% coverage at 95% nominal level (e.g., with `n=10000`) while vanilla Q‑learning drops to ~96%; also compare interval lengths.  
2. **Reproduce Dynamic Resource Matching (Section 5.2).** Confirm both methods achieve ~99.9% coverage; check that sample‑averaged produces much narrower intervals around `Q*` (e.g., 19.1 vs. 113.8 at 2000 iterations). Stress test with larger state spaces.

## Limitations
- The exact batch size used in the grid world experiments is not reported, and the state‑action representation for the dynamic resource‑matching problem is incompletely specified, preventing perfect replication.  
- The critical values (κ quantiles) for the pivotal distribution are not provided; relying on a normal approximation may bias coverage.  
- Theoretical guarantees assume a generative model with i.i.d. samples and bounded rewards, conditions often violated in online RL. The allowable step‑size range `η₀` is also not made explicit.
