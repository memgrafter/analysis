# Implicit Bias and Convergence of Matrix Stochastic Mirror Descent

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.18997
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18997
- **Reference count:** 13
- **Primary result:** Stochastic Mirror Descent with matrix parameters converges exponentially to the unique interpolator that minimizes the Bregman divergence induced by the mirror from the initialization.

## Executive Summary
This paper studies Stochastic Mirror Descent (SMD) for overparameterized matrix-valued prediction tasks, such as multi-class classification and matrix completion. The core update rule is ∇ψ(W_t)=∇ψ(W_{t-1})-η∇L_t(W_{t-1}), where ψ is a strongly convex mirror function on matrices. The authors prove that under standard conditions—including full row rank of the linear measurement operator and a local bound on the Hessian of ψ—SMD converges exponentially fast to a global interpolator. Furthermore, the limit point is characterized as the unique solution that minimizes the Bregman divergence D_ψ(W,W_0) among all matrices fitting the training data, extending the concept of implicit bias to matrix-valued models. For the important case of matrix completion, using a Schatten‑p mirror with p≈1 (approximating the nuclear norm) yields strong empirical performance, outperforming standard convex methods like SVT and Soft‑Impute, especially at low sampling rates.

## Method Summary
The method is Matrix Stochastic Mirror Descent. The iterate lives in a matrix space W∈ℝ^{d×k}. A differentiable, strongly convex mirror ψ induces a Bregman divergence D_ψ. At each step, a stochastic gradient ∇L_t(W_{t-1}) (from a sampled loss) is computed and the update is performed in the dual space: Z_t = Z_{t-1} - η∇L_t(W_{t-1}), where Z_t := ∇ψ(W_t). To recover W_t from Z_t, one must invert the gradient mapping ∇ψ, which is typically done via singular value thresholding for Schatten‑p mirrors. For experiments on matrix completion, the mirror is ψ(W)=∑σ_i(W)^p + ν∥W∥_F² with p=1.05 and ν small (e.g., 1e‑5). The linear operator A encodes sampling: A(W) = (W_{ij})_{(i,j)∈Ω}. The goal is to find W such that A(W)=b (interpolation), where b are the observed matrix entries. The algorithm is run for a fixed number of iterations (e.g., 200) with a fixed step size η (e.g., 50).

## Key Results
- **Exponential convergence:** Under Assumptions 1–6 (full row rank of A, strong convexity of ψ, local Hessian boundedness, appropriate step size), the expected squared distance decays exponentially: E[∥W\*-W_t∥_F²] ≤ C·(1-ρ)^t D_ψ(W\*,W_0), with rate ρ depending on η, σ_p(A), and a local Lipschitz constant.
- **Implicit bias:** The limit W* is the unique minimizer of D_ψ(W,W_0) subject to A(W)=b. This generalizes classical implicit bias results from vector to matrix parameters.
- **Empirical advantage:** For matrix completion with a Schatten‑p mirror (p≈1), SMD achieves lower relative Frobenius error than SVT and Soft‑Impute across sampling probabilities, with the largest gains at low sampling rates (e.g., 10–30%).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Matrix SMD converges exponentially to a global interpolator under specific conditions.
- **Mechanism:** The update ∇ψ(W_t)=∇ψ(W_{t-1})-η∇L_t(W_{t-1}) combined with the strong convexity of ψ and the properties of the linear operator A leads to a monotonic decrease in the Bregman divergence D_ψ(W*,W_t) toward the unique interpolator W* that minimizes D_ψ(W,W_0). The rate depends on the step size η, the smallest singular value of A (σ_p(A)), the strong convexity parameter of the loss (µ), and a local Lipschitz constant L derived from the bounded Hessian of ψ in a region B.
- **Core assumption:** ψ is ν-strongly convex; A has full row rank (σ_p(A)>0); the Hessian of ψ is bounded in the region B containing the iterates (Assumption 6); η is small enough that ψ-ηL_t remains convex.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "SMD converges exponentially to a global interpolator under assumptions including full row rank of the linear operator A and bounded Hessian of ψ in a local region"
  - [section III] Theorem 1 states the exponential convergence rate (5) under Assumptions 1-6.
- **Break condition:** If A loses full row rank or the Hessian of ψ becomes unbounded near the iterates (violating Assumption 6), the exponential rate guarantee fails.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The limit point W* is the unique solution minimizing the Bregman divergence D_ψ(W,W_0) among all interpolators of the training data.
- **Mechanism:** The accumulated gradient updates ∇ψ(W_t)-∇ψ(W_0) = η∑_{s=1}^t ∇L_s(W_s) lie in the row space of A^T because each ∇L_s(W_s) is a linear combination of the rows of A. In the limit, this yields ∇ψ(W_∞)-∇ψ(W_0) = A^T λ, which matches the KKT conditions for minimizing D_ψ(W,W_0) subject to A(W)=b. Strong convexity of ψ ensures this solution is unique.
- **Core assumption:** ψ is differentiable and strongly convex; the constraints A(W)=b are linear; the stochastic gradients ∇L_s(W_s) are unbiased and lie in the range of A^T.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "generalize classical implicit bias results ... converges to the unique solution minimizing the Bregman divergence induced by ψ from initialization"
  - [section IV.B] Derives KKT conditions (10) and shows SMD limit satisfies them.
- **Break condition:** If ψ is not strongly convex (so minimizer may not be unique), or if the constraint set is nonlinear, the implicit bias characterization via KKT fails.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Using a Schatten‑p mirror ψ(W)=∑σ_i(W)^p with p≈1 promotes low‑rank solutions in matrix completion, empirically outperforming proximal methods.
- **Mechanism:** As p→1, the Schatten‑p norm approximates the nuclear norm, whose minimization tends to yield low‑rank matrices. In the overparameterized regime, the implicit bias of SMD drives W_t toward the low‑rank interpolator that minimizes D_ψ(W,W_0). The geometry of the Schatten‑p mirror thus encodes a low‑rank prior without explicit constraints.
- **Core assumption:** The true matrix is low‑rank; p is chosen close to 1; the problem is overparameterized; the SMD dynamics are stable.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "For matrix completion with ψ(W)=∑σ_i(W)^p (p≈1) Schatten‑p SMD outperforms SVT and Soft‑Impute ... largest gains at low sampling rates."
  - [section V] Experimental setup and results: Uses p=1.05, shows lower relative Frobenius error.
- **Break condition:** If the true matrix is not low‑rank, the low‑rank bias is mismatched. Also, for p<2, exponential convergence is not proven; if Assumption 6 fails (e.g., singular matrices appear in the region B), convergence may be slower or not guaranteed.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Overparameterization**
  - **Why needed:** The theory relies on dk > p (more parameters than constraints), so infinitely many matrices satisfy A(W)=b. This multiplicity is essential for implicit bias to be meaningful; otherwise there is a unique solution and optimizer choice does not matter.
  - **Quick check:** "For a given linear system A(W)=b, does the number of free parameters (dk) exceed the number of independent constraints (p)?"
- **Concept: Bregman Divergence**
  - **Why needed:** It generalizes squared distance to the geometry induced by the mirror ψ. The implicit bias is defined as minimizing D_ψ(W,W_0) among interpolators. The convergence proof hinges on showing D_ψ(W*,W_t) decreases monotonically.
  - **Quick check:** "Given ψ(W)=∑σ_i(W)^p, write D_ψ(U,V). How does it differ from ∥U-V∥_F² when p=2?"
- **Concept: Schatten Norms**
  - **Why needed:** Matrix analogs of vector p‑norms, defined via singular values. They serve as the primary example of matrix mirrors. For p≈1, they approximate the nuclear norm, inducing low‑rank solutions; for p≥2, they are smooth and strongly convex, ensuring exponential convergence.
  - **Quick check:** "What is the Schatten‑∞ norm of a matrix? How does it relate to the operator norm?"

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Inputs (A, b, ℓ_i, ψ) → Core loop (Initialize W_0; sample batch; compute ∇L_t; update ∇ψ; solve for W_t) → Output (W_t converging to W*)
**Critical path:** (1) Validate assumptions: σ_p(A)>0, ψ strongly convex, choose η small for ψ‑ηL_t convex. (2) Initialize W_0 (influences bias). (3) For each batch: compute ∇L_t, update Z_t = ∇ψ(W_t) = Z_{t-1} - η∇L_t, then invert ∇ψ to get W_t. (4) Terminate when L(W_t)≈0 or iterates stable.
**Design tradeoffs:** Choice of ψ: p≥2 gives exponential convergence but weaker low‑rank promotion; p≈1 gives stronger low‑rank bias but no proved exponential rate. Step size η must satisfy local convexity condition; too large → divergence, too small → slow. Batch size B: larger reduces variance but increases computational cost.
**Failure signatures:** Loss not decreasing to zero → η too large or A rank-deficient. Solution has high rank when p≈1 → p not close enough to 1 or Assumption 6 violated. Numerical instability in inverse mapping → singular values near zero; add small ν∥W∥_F².
**First 3 experiments:** (1) Convergence rate test: use ψ(W)=∥W∥_F², random full‑row‑rank A; plot log(E[∥W*‑W_t∥_F²]) vs. t to see linear decay. (2) Implicit bias verification: for a tiny overparameterized system (d=k=2, p=1), compute all interpolators analytically; run SMD with different W_0 and ψ; verify W_t approaches minimizer of D_ψ(W,W_0). (3) Matrix completion replication: implement SMD with ψ(W)=∑σ_i(W)^{1.05} (plus ν∥W∥_F², ν=1e‑5), η=50; compare against SVT and Soft‑Impute on 100×100 rank‑5 matrix at sampling prob = 0.2; measure relative Frobenius error.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Prove exponential convergence for matrix SMD with Schatten‑p mirrors for 1 < p < 2 under conditions that avoid Assumption 6 (bounded Hessian).
- **Basis in paper:** The conclusion explicitly identifies “proving exponential rates in this regime requires relaxing assumption 6” as future work.
- **Why unresolved:** The current rate proof relies on Assumption 6, which may fail for 1<p<2 because the Hessian of ψ can become unbounded near singular matrices; thus the exponential rate is not guaranteed.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A new analysis that eliminates the Hessian bound, or a demonstration that for all data/initialization the set B (where D_ψ(W*,W) ≤ D_ψ(W*,W0)) contains no singular matrices.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** For 1<p<2, characterize when the set B = {W: D_ψ(W*,W) ≤ D_ψ(W*,W0)} avoids singular matrices, as this condition is necessary for Assumption 6 and exponential rates.
- **Basis in paper:** Example 4 notes that Assumption 6 holds iff B contains no singular matrices, but the paper does not investigate when this occurs.
- **Why unresolved:** Without knowing whether B contains singular matrices for specific problems, we cannot ascertain the validity of the exponential convergence guarantee.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A theorem providing sufficient conditions on A and W0 that enforce a minimum singular value on all W∈B, or a counterexample showing the need for alternative approaches.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How does the exponent p in the Schatten‑p mirror influence the rank or singular value distribution of the implicit bias solution W*? Can we theoretically explain why p≈1 yields low‑rank solutions?
- **Basis in paper:** The experiments use p≈1 to promote low rank, but the theory only defines W* as the minimizer of D_ψ subject to A(W)=b and does not analyze its rank or singular values.
- **Why unresolved:** Linking p to the structure of W* would connect the general theory to the practical advantage of near‑nuclear‑norm mirrors and inform the choice of p.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Deriving KKT conditions that tie the singular values of W* to p and showing a thresholding effect as p→1+, or proving that for typical random sampling the rank of W* matches the true rank with high probability as p→1+.

## Limitations
- Experimental details are partially unspecified: exact mirror (inclusion/value of ν∥W∥_F²), gradient inversion method, batch sampling, and loss function (likely squared) are not fully described.
- Assumption 6 (bounded Hessian of ψ in a local region) may fail for Schatten‑p with 1<p<2 when singular values approach zero, threatening the exponential rate guarantee.
- Theory assumes linear measurements A(W)=b; extensions to nonlinear settings are not covered.

## Confidence
- **Exponential convergence:** High. The proof under Assumptions 1–6 is rigorous, though the local boundedness condition can be restrictive, especially for p<2.
- **Implicit bias:** High. The KKT‑based argument cleanly extends classical SMD results to matrix parameters.
- **Low‑rank bias & empirical gains:** Medium. Results are promising but lack full reproducibility (missing details) and statistical validation; theory does not yet support p≈1.
