# Targeted Syntactic Evaluation of Language Models on Georgian Case Alignment

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.10661
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10661
- Reference count: 21
- Primary result: Transformer-based language models show a performance hierarchy on Georgian case alignment: nominative (88.6% word-level accuracy) > dative (48.8%) > ergative (24.4%), strongly correlating with in-language case frequency.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a targeted syntactic evaluation framework for transformer language models focusing on Georgian's split-ergative case alignment, a rare syntactic phenomenon. Using a treebank-based approach with Grew queries, the authors generate 370 minimal-pair tests across seven tasks, each comparing nominative, dative, and ergative noun forms in subject/object positions. Five encoder-only and two decoder-only models are evaluated with word-level and sentence-level accuracy metrics. The results reveal a robust performance gradient aligned with case frequency, a pronounced nominative bias in model errors, and particularly low accuracy on the ergative due to its infrequent and contextually restricted use. The study demonstrates the feasibility of using UD treebanks to create fine-grained syntactic probes and highlights the challenges low-resource, typologically rare phenomena pose for language models.

## Method Summary
The authors leverage the Georgian Language Corpus Universal Dependencies (GLC UD) treebank to generate minimal-pair test sets. For each of seven syntactic constructions (e.g., intransitive with nominative subject, transitive with dative object, etc.), they use Grew query language to extract sentences matching specific dependency patterns and morphological features. They then create minimal sets by swapping the case form (nominative, dative, ergative) of a target noun, producing one grammatical and two ungrammatical variants. The evaluation involves computing conditional probabilities for each target word form given the sentence context, using both word-level (WL) and sentence-level (SL) metrics. Encoder-only models are assessed with WL and SL, while decoder-only models use SL due to autoregressive constraints. The dataset comprises 370 tests (50–70 per task) and covers a range of verbs and nouns.

## Key Results
- Models achieve highest accuracy on nominative case (average 88.6% WL), followed by dative (48.8% WL), and lowest on ergative (24.4% WL).
- Performance strongly correlates with the frequency of case forms in the training data (NOM > DAT > ERG).
- A consistent nominative bias appears in errors: when the grammatical case is not nominative, models often incorrectly prefer the nominative form.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Treebank-based minimal-pair generation via Grew queries reliably creates syntactically valid test sets for rare phenomena like Georgian split ergativity.
- Mechanism: Grew matches dependency patterns (e.g., `SUBJ[Case="Erg"]`) in a UD treebank, then modifies a single morphological feature (case) to produce grammatical/ungrammatical minimal sets. This leverages existing syntactic annotations to ensure structural fidelity.
- Core assumption: The UD treebank contains sufficient exemplars of the target constructions to generate diverse tests, and the Grew pattern language can precisely isolate the relevant syntactic configurations.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "A treebank-based approach for the generation of minimal pairs using the Grew query language is implemented."
  - [section 4] Details the Grew patterns for matching intransitive and transitive constructions and the manual validation step for rare ergative forms.
  - [corpus] Neighbor papers (e.g., "Controlled Evaluation of Syntactic Knowledge in Multilingual Language Models") use similar treebank-probing methods, but direct corpus support for Grew-based Georgian case tests is weak; this is a novel application.
- Break condition: If the treebank lacks sufficient instances of a specific construction (e.g., ergative transitive), the number of generated tests becomes too small for robust evaluation, as seen with the need for human-annotated ergative forms.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Model performance on case assignment correlates strongly with the in-language frequency of the case forms, reflecting data-driven learning over rule-based generalization.
- Mechanism: During pre-training, models optimize for predicting frequent tokens/patterns. The nominative (most frequent) is learned robustly; the rare ergative receives minimal signal, leading to poor accuracy. This frequency bias manifests as a "nominative default" in errors.
- Core assumption: The models' training corpora reflect the same case frequency distribution as the GLC UD treebank, and learning is primarily distributional rather than rule-based for these morphological distinctions.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Performance correlated with the overall frequency distribution of the three forms (NOM > DAT > ERG)."
  - [section 7.1] Explicitly states the correlation between word-level accuracy and frequency: nom (88.6%) > dat (48.8%) > erg (24.4%).
  - [corpus] Neighbor work on multilingual LMs ("Controlled Evaluation of Syntactic Knowledge...") finds performance aligns with resource representation, supporting the frequency-learning link.
- Break condition: If a model were trained with artificially balanced case frequencies or explicit grammatical rules, the performance hierarchy would flatten, breaking the frequency-accuracy correlation.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The ergative case's highly specific syntactic role (only in past transitive constructions) combined with overall low-resource data scarcity creates a "double sparsity" that models cannot overcome.
- Mechanism: The ergative is both globally rare and contextually restricted to a single screeve (past tense). Models fail to learn the precise conditional trigger (perfective/past screeve) because co-occurrence signals are too sparse in training data. This leads to overgeneralization to the default nominative.
- Core assumption: The models lack inductive biases that would allow them to infer a low-frequency, high-specificity rule from limited examples, and transfer learning from other languages does not help because split ergativity is typologically rare.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "The low frequency of the ergative case, its highly specific use case, as well as an overall lack of available training data make this a particularly difficult syntactic phenomenon for models to learn."
  - [section 7.2] Discusses the ergative's "very specific cases" and "limited scope for improvement through transfer learning" due to its rarity across languages.
  - [corpus] Neighbor paper on Urdu ("A Paradigm Gap in Urdu") examines a similar phenomenon of a specific construction becoming ungrammatical, highlighting how narrow syntactic contexts are vulnerable to data gaps.
- Break condition: If ergative frequency were increased (e.g., via data augmentation) without altering its specific syntactic trigger, performance might improve; if it remained low despite augmentation, the issue would be more fundamental to model architecture.

## Foundational Learning
- Concept: Split Ergativity in Georgian
  - Why needed here: The entire evaluation targets this rare alignment system; understanding which case marks subject/object in which screeve (tense/aspect) is essential to interpret the test design and error patterns.
  - Quick check question: Given a past transitive sentence, which case marks the subject and which marks the object? (Answer: ergative and nominative, respectively).
- Concept: Targeted Syntactic Evaluation (TSE) with Minimal Pairs
  - Why needed here: TSE is the core methodology for isolating model knowledge of a single syntactic feature (case). The paper extends standard binary minimal pairs to minimal *sets* of three forms.
  - Quick check question: What is the key difference between a typical minimal pair and the minimal *set* used in this paper? (Answer: Instead of grammatical vs. ungrammatical, it's one grammatical form vs. two ungrammatical alternatives, testing discrimination among all three cases).
- Concept: Universal Dependencies (UD) Treebanks as a Data Source
  - Why needed here: The Grew queries operate on UD's standardized dependency relations and morphological features (e.g., `Case=Nom`). This provides a structured, cross-linguistically consistent way to find syntactic patterns.
  - Quick check question: What two aspects of a UD treebank are exploited for test generation? (Answer: dependency relations between words and the morphological annotations on words).

## Architecture Onboarding
- Component map: GLC UD treebank -> Grew query engine -> Lexicon lookup -> Minimal set generation -> Model evaluator -> WL/SL accuracy metrics
- Critical path:
  1. Define Grew patterns for each alignment (intransitive, transitive nom-dat, transitive erg-nom, transitive dat-nom).
  2. Extract sentences, identify target nouns.
  3. Generate minimal sets by changing `Case` feature.
  4. For each model and test, compute WL/SL probabilities for the three target word forms.
  5. Determine if grammatical form has highest probability.
  6. Aggregate accuracy by case and task.
- Design tradeoffs: WL accuracy is more precise for token choice but invalid for decoder-only models if target noun isn't final token (requires SL). SL is consistent but conflates target choice with downstream context effects. Treebank size limits test diversity; augmentation (human validation) was needed for rare ergative forms.
- Failure signatures:
  1. Ergative accuracy near random chance (~33%) or strongly biased toward nominative.
  2. High nominative accuracy but high error rate when nominative is incorrect (default bias).
  3. No performance gap between WL and SL for decoder models (suggests context confusion).
- First 3 experiments:
  1. **Pattern Validation**: Run each Grew query (e.g., Figure 2) on the GLC UD treebank. Manually inspect 10 matches per pattern to confirm the dependency structure and case assignments align with Georgian grammar descriptions.
  2. **Baseline Model Sweep**: Evaluate all seven models on the `intransitive-nom-subj` task (simplest, only nominative subject). This establishes a clean baseline for nominative performance and checks for obvious tokenization or implementation bugs.
  3. **Frequency-Accuracy Correlation Check**: For each model, plot case accuracy (WL) against case frequency counts from the *full* treebank. Compute Pearson's r. A strong positive correlation (r > 0.7) would support Mechanism 2; a weak correlation would suggest other factors dominate.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. What is the impact of including additional Georgian screeves and a more diverse set of intransitive constructions on the performance of language models in case alignment tasks?
2. To what extent do the test sentences derived from the GLC UD treebank overlap with the training corpora of the evaluated language models, and how does this potential data leakage affect the measured accuracy?
3. Is the observed nominative bias primarily due to the higher frequency of nominative forms in the training data, or does it reflect an inherent inductive bias of transformer models towards nominative alignment?
4. How effective is the Grew query-based minimal-pair generation methodology for evaluating syntactic phenomena in other low-resource languages, and what factors determine its success?

## Limitations
- The test set relies on a single UD treebank; underrepresentation of rare ergative constructions may limit generalizability.
- Tokenization of multi-subword nouns could affect word-level accuracy; implementation details vary across models.
- Potential data leakage: Georgian-specific models may have seen the GLC treebank during pre-training.

## Confidence
- Treebank-based minimal pairs: Medium confidence (manual validation limited; Grew patterns might miss edge cases).
- Frequency-accuracy correlation: High confidence (strong empirical evidence across models and aligns with prior multilingual LM studies).
- Ergative difficulty from double sparsity: Medium confidence (plausible but not directly tested; requires controlled experiments).

## Next Checks
1. Data leakage control: Train a Georgian LM from scratch excluding GLC treebank. If ergative accuracy stays low, leakage is not inflating results.
2. Tokenization audit: For a random sample, recompute probabilities using alternative tokenizers (BPE vs. WordPiece) and compare WL accuracy. Large variance indicates sensitivity to tokenization.
3. Ergative frequency ablation: Artificially oversample ergative constructions in training (e.g., via back-translation or synthetic generation) and re-evaluate. Improved ergative accuracy would confirm the frequency hypothesis.
