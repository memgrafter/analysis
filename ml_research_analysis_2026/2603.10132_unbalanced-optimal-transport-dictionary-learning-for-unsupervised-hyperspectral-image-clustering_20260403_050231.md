# Unbalanced Optimal Transport Dictionary Learning for Unsupervised Hyperspectral Image Clustering

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.10132  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10132  
- **Reference count:** 0  
- **Primary result:** UBCSC improves unsupervised HSI clustering accuracy (e.g., 89% on Salinas A vs 86% for balanced methods) by preserving mass variations without normalization.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles a key limitation of balanced Wasserstein dictionary learning for unsupervised hyperspectral image clustering: the need to normalize spectral profiles, which blurs class boundaries and reduces robustness to outliers. The authors introduce Unbalanced Optimal Transport Dictionary Learning (UBCSC), which employs unbalanced barycenters to retain total mass variations in pixel distributions. The method learns a dictionary of prototype spectra and sparse weight vectors, which are then used to construct a graph for spectral clustering. On standard benchmarks, UBCSC outperforms balanced approaches, achieving 89% accuracy on Salinas A (vs 86%) and 84% on Pavia Centre (vs 77%), with purity scores up to 93%. Effective hyperparameters include τ for mass matching and ε in [0.07,0.12]. However, the quadratic time complexity O(n²/ε) presents scalability challenges for large datasets.

## Method Summary
The pipeline begins by converting each HSI pixel into a non-negative spectral distribution and rescaling total masses to [0,1] for stability. A dictionary learning process then iteratively updates a set of k atoms (D) and weight vectors (Λ). For each pixel, an unbalanced optimal transport barycenter of its weights on the atoms is computed using entropic regularization (ε) and a marginal penalty (τ). The reconstruction loss between this barycenter and the original pixel drives updates via ADAM; Λ rows are softmax-normalized and D is kept positive. After convergence, a k‑NN graph is built on the weight vectors Λ using Euclidean distance. The normalized graph Laplacian’s eigenvectors are clustered by k‑means to label a subset of pixels. These labels are propagated to the full image via majority voting among spectral neighbors. The final clustering is evaluated by matching to ground truth.

## Key Results
- UBCSC achieves 89% accuracy on Salinas A compared to 86% by balanced methods.
- On Pavia Centre, accuracy improves from 77% (balanced) to 84%.
- Purity scores reach up to 93% with slight cluster overestimation, revealing latent material classes.
- Effective hyperparameters: τ matching total data mass and ε in [0.07,0.12].
- Time complexity O(n²/ε) poses scalability challenges for large datasets.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Unbalanced optimal transport (UOT) barycenters preserve meaningful total mass variations in spectral distributions that are lost during normalization required by balanced OT. UOT introduces KL divergence penalties on marginal constraints, allowing mass creation or destruction during transport instead of strict conservation. This enables barycentric reconstructions that respect the original total reflectance (mass) of each pixel’s spectral profile. The core assumption is that total spectral energy is a discriminative feature; ignoring it via normalization blurs classes and harms outlier robustness. Evidence: the paper states normalization “blurs the classes, and sacrifices robustness to outliers and noise,” and notes that by incorporating total reflectance, “we expect to get weights which better capture the underlying data.” Break condition: if τ is too high, the method approaches balanced OT and reintroduces normalization problems; if τ is too low, mass variation may dominate spectral shape and cause sensitivity to illumination changes.

### Mechanism 2
The learned barycentric weight vectors (Λ) provide a more faithful lower‑dimensional representation than balanced OT weights, capturing both spectral shape and total energy. Dictionary learning minimizes reconstruction loss L(P(D,Λ), μ) where P is the UOT barycenter. The softmax‑normalized weights sum to 1 but are derived from unbalanced comparisons, encoding similarity to dictionary atoms while implicitly retaining mass information. The assumption is that similar material spectra (including scale) yield similar Λ vectors, and this geometry is more meaningful post‑UOT. Evidence: the paper notes that “if learned weights Λi and Λj are close then the underlying pixels, μi and μj, must also have similar distributions. Notably, this was not necessarily true for when using balanced optimal transport.” Break condition: if dictionary atoms collapse or learning diverges, Λ becomes uninformative (e.g., poor initialization, unsuitable ε or excessive k).

### Mechanism 3
Spectral clustering on the Euclidean graph constructed from UOT weight vectors (Λ) yields better cluster separation due to improved representation of material heterogeneity. A k‑NN graph W is built using d(μi, μj)=||Λi−Λj||²; the normalized graph Laplacian L is computed, and k‑means is applied to its smallest eigenvectors. The UOT‑derived Λ space is hypothesized to have tighter intra‑cluster connectivity and clearer inter‑cluster separations. Evidence: algorithmic details and results showing UBCSC outperforms BCSC (e.g., 89% vs 86% on Salinas A) support that the Λ‑space graph is more discriminative. Break condition: if Λ vectors are too dense or overlapping (e.g., from poor dictionary learning), the k‑NN graph becomes noisy and spectral clustering fails; mismatched NN relative to scene sparsity also degrades connectivity.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Data Preprocessing -> Barycentric Dictionary Learner -> Graph Construction -> Spectral Clustering -> Post-processing  
Critical path: Preprocessed pixels -> UOT dictionary learning (get Λ) -> Graph from Λ -> Laplacian eigenvectors -> k‑means labels.  
Design tradeoffs:  
- τ (marginal penalty): Higher enforces mass conservation (balanced‑like); lower allows mass variation (robust).  
- ε (entropy): Larger speeds Sinkhorn but blurs transport plan.  
- k (atoms): Too few → poor reconstruction; too many → overfitting, higher O(n²) cost.  
- NN (neighbors): Should match scene spatial coherence (dense vs sparse).  
Failure signatures:  
- Low accuracy (< balanced baseline): Likely τ too high or k too small.  
- Many tiny clusters: K too large or NN too small (over‑segmentation).  
- Runtime explosion: n > 10,000 causes O(n²) Sinkhorn bottleneck; need GPU parallelization.  
- Weights Λ all similar: ε too high or learning failed (check loss convergence).  
First 3 experiments:  
1. Baseline replication: Run BCSC (balanced) on Salinas A to verify ~86% accuracy.  
2. τ sensitivity sweep: On Salinas A, vary τ ∈ [100, 1000, 10000, 100000] with fixed ε=0.1, k=24, NN=25; expect accuracy peak near τ=1000.  
3. ϵ and scalability test: On Pavia Centre, run UBCSC with ϵ = 0.05, 0.1, 0.15; record accuracy and runtime to observe trade‑off.

3. **What is the theoretical or empirical cause of UBCSC’s insensitivity to the choice of loss function (TV, quadratic, KL, unbalanced transport), despite the unbalanced transport loss’s higher computational cost?**  
   *Basis:* Inferred from Section 4’s observation that all loss functions perform similarly in accuracy while unbalanced transport is costlier.  
4. **To what extent does UBCSC’s improvement over balanced methods stem from mass preservation versus outlier robustness, and can these factors be disentangled?**  
   *Basis:* Inferred from claims about preserving mass variations and robustness, but experiments compare whole systems without isolating drivers.

## Limitations
- **Implementation gaps:** Missing details on exact POT barycenter usage, cost matrix handling, graph symmetrization, subset size n, ADAM hyperparameters, and iteration count.  
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity:** Acceptable ranges for τ, ε, k, NN may be dataset‑dependent; only coarse defaults are provided.  
- **Scalability:** O(n²/ε) complexity hinders application to full‑scene HSIs (n > 10000); no large‑scale runtime results reported.

## Confidence
- UOT preserves mass variations: **Medium** confidence (theoretically sound, supported by gains, but assumes total reflectance is a key discriminator).  
- Barycentric weights are a faithful low‑dimensional representation: **Medium** confidence (inference based on authors’ experiments; no external validation of weight geometry).  
- Spectral clustering on these weights improves accuracy: **High** confidence (consistent improvements on two standard datasets).
