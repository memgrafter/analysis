# The Astonishing Ability of Large Language Models to Parse Jabberwockified Language

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.23928  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23928  
- **Reference count**: 11  
- **Primary result**: LLMs recover original meaning from Jabberwockified English with mean translation similarity 0.59 vs. random baseline 0.43 (p≪.0001)

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether large language models (LLMs) can reconstruct the meaning of English passages after all content words (nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs) are replaced with random nonsense strings—a process termed “Jabberwockification.” The method preserves function words, morphology, punctuation, and numbers, creating syntactically well-formed but lexically opaque texts. When prompted to translate such passages into standard English, state-of-the-art models like GPT‑5.1 and Gemini‑3‑Pro achieve a mean embedding similarity of 0.59 to the original texts, far exceeding a random baseline of 0.43. Accuracy varies by genre (spoken language is hardest) and improves with longer context, suggesting that models leverage deep statistical regularities linking syntax to meaning rather than memorizing specific texts.

## Method Summary
The study used 150 English passages from three genres (spoken transcripts, TV/movie screenplays, fiction). Content-word lemmas were replaced with nonce words from the ARC Nonword Database while retaining function words, inflectional morphology, punctuation, capitalization, and numerals. Five degradation variants were created (e.g., all content words replaced by “BLANK”, removal of punctuation or prepositions). LLMs were prompted to translate each degraded passage to regular English and optionally provide a gloss for each nonce word. Translation quality was measured by cosine similarity between passage embeddings of the original and generated text (OpenAI text‑embedding‑3‑large). A baseline similarity was computed between the translation and a randomly selected original passage from the same genre. No model training was performed; inference used medium reasoning effort and standard sampling parameters.

## Key Results
- Mean translation similarity 0.59 vs. random baseline 0.43 (p≪.0001).
- Genre effect: spoken texts yielded significantly lower accuracy than fiction/screenplays.
- Context length positively correlated with recovery (β=0.08, t>9).
- Even extreme degradation (all content words replaced by “BLANK”) produced statistically indistinguishable accuracy.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim**: LLMs recover meaning by using morphosyntactic “fingerprints” (function words, inflectional morphology, word order) to constrain the possible meanings of nonsense content words.  
The model leverages high‑probability syntactic frames and constructional patterns learned during pretraining. For a nonce word in a specific syntactic position (e.g., after a determiner, before a verb), the model activates a distribution over likely lexical semantics based on the co‑occurrence statistics of real words in identical structural roles across its training corpus.  
**Core assumption**: The mapping between syntactic position/construction and abstract semantic role (e.g., AGENT, PATIENT, LOCATION) is statistically stable enough across real texts that a model trained on them can generalize this mapping to novel lexical items.  
**Evidence anchors**:  
  - Spoken texts (fewer function words, lower type‑token ratio) are significantly harder to translate than fiction/screenplays (Figure 1).  
  - Removing auxiliary verbs & prepositions causes a significant accuracy drop (Table 4).  
**Break condition**: Removing critical structural scaffolding (function words, morphology, punctuation) severely degrades performance (No verbs/prepositions, No punctuation conditions in Figure 3).

### Mechanism 2
**Claim**: Meaning recovery is an iterative, context‑wide constraint satisfaction process where later context disambiguates earlier nonce words, and vice versa.  
The model processes the entire passage in parallel/auto‑regressively. The representation of each nonce word is informed not just by its local syntactic frame but by the global coherence constraints imposed by the entire degraded passage, creating a mutually reinforcing network of probabilistic guesses.  
**Core assumption**: A coherent discourse creates a consistent latent “topic” and “semantic field” that can be inferred from preserved function words and syntax alone, providing top‑down constraints on individual word meanings.  
**Evidence anchors**:  
  - Subsequent context helps resolve earlier text; longer context improves recovery (β=0.08, t>9).  
  - Figure 4 shows a clear positive relationship between context length (tokens) and translation similarity.  
**Break condition**: Providing only single, isolated sentences prevents global constraint propagation, leading to lower accuracy.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim**: The ability is a product of massive pretraining on real language, enabling the model to learn deep statistical regularities linking form (syntax, morphology) to meaning, not mere memorization.  
The model has internalized thousands of lexical‑syntactic‑semantic “constructions” and their typical argument structures, world‑knowledge associations, and discourse patterns. Faced with Jabberwocky, it performs sophisticated pattern‑matching over these stored constructions, filling nonce slots with the most probable real words given the surrounding structural pattern and need for overall coherence.  
**Core assumption**: The space of possible human utterances is highly constrained by the interplay of syntax, world knowledge, and genre conventions; a model that has ingested a vast enough sample can reconstruct a plausible original from its “skeleton.”  
**Evidence anchors**:  
  - Online (in pretraining) vs. offline (not in pretraining) passages showed no reliable difference in accuracy (M=0.69 vs. M=0.61, p=0.75).  
  - BLANKs condition (all content words replaced by the same token “BLANK”) yields performance statistically indistinguishable from standard Jabberwocky, proving recovery does not rely on intra‑passage repetition cues.  
**Break condition**: Using a model with insufficient capacity or pretraining (e.g., base models, non‑reasoning instruction‑tuned models) which the paper states “largely fail.”

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Syntactic Bootstrapping & Construction Grammar
**Why needed here**: This is the core theoretical foundation. The paper directly builds on the idea that syntax provides a scaffold for meaning. A verb’s syntactic subcategorization frame (e.g., transitive *NP V NP*) imposes strong semantic constraints (e.g., caused transfer), explaining how a nonce verb in a transitive frame can be approximated.  
**Quick check**: Given the sentence “The *gostak* *distims* the *doshes*”, what can you infer about the meanings of *gostak*, *distims*, and *doshes* based solely on syntax and morphology?  
*Answer*: *gostak* is likely an agentive noun (subject), *distims* is a transitive verb, *doshes* is a plural count noun object.

### Concept: Distributional Semantics & Vector Space Representations
**Why needed here**: The evaluation relies on embedding similarity. The mechanism assumes that the model’s translation produces a vector close to the original text’s vector because it has recovered the same semantic neighborhood. Understanding that word/utterance meaning is a point in a high‑dimensional space shaped by co‑occurrence is key to grasping the evaluation metric.  
**Quick check**: If two sentences have nearly identical meaning but use different words (synonyms), should their embedding vectors be closer or farther than two sentences with different meanings but similar words? Why?  
*Answer*: Closer, because distributional models place semantically similar items in nearby regions of the vector space regardless of lexical form.

### Concept: Constraint Satisfaction & Connectionist Processing
**Why needed here**: The authors frame the process as a constraint‑satisfaction problem (connectionist/statistical‑physics roots). It’s not a step‑wise rule application but a parallel, probabilistic resolution where multiple weak constraints (syntactic, semantic, pragmatic) interact to converge on a stable interpretation.  
**Quick check**: In the example “He *lorped* it on the *molp*”, what are at least three different *types* of constraints that collectively narrow the meaning of *lorped* and *molp*?  
*Answer*: (1) Syntactic: *lorped* is a past‑tense verb taking a direct object (*it*) and a prepositional phrase (*on the molp*). (2) Selectional: *lorped* likely involves an action that can be performed on an object (*it*). (3) World knowledge: *molp* is a location (object of preposition *on*), so it is probably a place or surface.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**:  
  Tokenizer & Preprocessor → Embedding Layer → Transformer Stack (Core) → Output/Decoding Head

- **Critical path**:  
  Jabberwockified Text → Tokenization → Token Embeddings → **Transformer Layers (Attention + MLP)** (where structural patterns are matched and nonce meanings are constrained) → Final contextual embeddings → Decoder generates standard English translation.

- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - **Context Window vs. Compute**: Longer context improves recovery (β=0.08) but increases O(n²) attention cost; a system must balance accuracy with latency/memory.
  - **Granularity of “Stop Word” Definition**: Defining what counts as a “function word” directly impacts which structural cues are preserved; over‑ or under‑inclusion changes degradation difficulty.
  - **Evaluation Metric**: Using embedding similarity is efficient but coarse and may reward gist over detail; production systems may need granular metrics (BLEU, ROUGE, human eval).

- **Failure signatures**:
  - **Genre Sensitivity**: Sharp drop for spoken transcripts (fragmented syntax, low function‑word density).
  - **Dependency on Auxiliaries/Prepositions**: Performance plummets when these are removed (No verbs or prepositions condition).
  - **High Variance in BLANKs Condition**: Wider variance suggests over‑reliance on repetitive nonce tokens as a crude cue.
  - **Poor Short‑Context Recovery**: Sentence‑by‑sentence translation yields low similarity, indicating inability to propagate information across turns.

- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Baseline Sanity Check**: Jabberwockify a simple sentence (e.g., “The cat chased the mouse.”). Verify model returns a translation with correct syntactic structure and plausible noun/verb semantics; expect similarity >0.7.
  2. **Structural Cue Ablation**: For a medium‑difficulty passage, systematically remove preserved elements (punctuation, numerals, auxiliary verbs, prepositions). Re‑run translation; expect largest drop when prepositions/auxiliaries are removed.
  3. **Context Window Gradient Test**: For a complex paragraph, translate incrementally (1 sentence, then 2, then 4, then full paragraph). Plot similarity vs. context length; expect a positive trend (β≈0.08).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper highlights several open questions for future research:

- How do these results generalize to languages beyond English, particularly those with different morphological and syntactic properties?
- What is the effect of model scale and architecture? Do smaller or non‑instruction‑tuned models exhibit the same ability?
- Can the proposed mechanisms (morphosyntactic fingerprints, global constraint satisfaction, pretraining regularities) be experimentally disentangled, for example by designing targeted ablation studies?
- How does the choice of nonce word generation (random strings vs. phonotactically plausible nonwords) influence recovery performance?
- To what extent does embedding similarity capture fine‑grained semantic recovery, and how does it correlate with human judgments?

## Limitations
- Dataset and stimuli (150 passages, exact nonce mappings) are unreproducible without the Human‑AI‑Parallel corpus.
- Prompt wording, model parameters (“medium reasoning effort”), and embedding model version are underspecified.
- Evaluation relies on a single embedding similarity score, which is coarse and may conflate gist with detail.
- Findings are restricted to English and a few high‑capacity LLMs; lower‑resource languages or smaller models are unexplored.
