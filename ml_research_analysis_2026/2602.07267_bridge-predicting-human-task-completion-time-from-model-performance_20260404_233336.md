# BRIDGE: Predicting Human Task Completion Time From Model Performance

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.07267
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07267
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** BRIDGE predicts human task completion time from model performance via IRT difficulty calibration, achieving R²=0.81 on METR and accurate estimates on SWE-bench (F1≈0.23) and Cybench (92% within 0.5×–2×).

## Executive Summary
BRIDGE is a framework that estimates how long a human would take to complete a task based on AI model performance, eliminating the need for costly human annotations. It uses a two‑parameter logistic Item Response Theory (2PL IRT) model to infer latent task difficulty from binary success/failure data across multiple benchmarks. The difficulty scores are calibrated against log‑transformed human completion times on the METR benchmark, yielding a strong linear relationship (R²=0.81). This mapping allows BRIDGE to predict human completion times for new tasks. Evaluations on SWE‑bench Verified and Cybench show that BRIDGE substantially outperforms baselines like success‑rate and LLM‑based predictors. Furthermore, by tracking frontier model capabilities over time, BRIDGE reveals an exponential growth in the solvable task‑length horizon (doubling roughly every six months), independently confirming trends observed by METR.

## Method Summary
The method comprises four main steps. First, a binary response matrix is constructed from model–task outcomes across a diverse set of benchmarks (METR, SWE‑bench Verified, Cybench, etc.). Second, a two‑parameter logistic Item Response Theory (2PL IRT) model is fitted to this matrix using MCMC inference, yielding latent estimates of task difficulty (b) and discrimination (a), and model ability (θ). Third, for tasks with human completion time annotations (METR), a linear regression of log(human time) against the IRT difficulty b is performed, establishing a calibration mapping. Fourth, this mapping is applied to the difficulty estimates of unseen tasks to predict human completion time. For forecasting, the best model per release window is identified; its ability defines the maximum solvable difficulty (b = θ for 50% success), which is then transformed via the calibration to the solvable task‑length horizon. The approach avoids direct human annotation for new benchmarks and enables capability scaling analysis.

## Key Results
- Calibration strength: IRT task difficulty correlates strongly with log(human time) on METR (R² = 0.81).
- Predictive accuracy: On SWE‑bench Verified, BRIDGE achieves weighted macro F1 ≈ 0.23, beating success‑rate and LLM baselines (≤ 0.20). On Cybench, 92% of predicted times fall within a 0.5×–2× tolerance of actual first‑solve times (R² = 0.45).
- Scaling trend: The solvable task‑length horizon (human time) doubles roughly every 6 months, consistent with exponential improvement in frontier model capabilities.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Unified latent scale via 2PL IRT
- **Claim:** A two‑parameter logistic Item Response Theory (2PL IRT) model provides a unified latent scale that jointly captures task difficulty and model ability from binary success/failure data across multiple benchmarks.
- **Mechanism:** The 2PL model defines success probability as σ(a_i(θ_j – b_i)), where a_i is task discrimination, b_i is difficulty, and θ_j is model ability. Bayesian inference (MCMC) estimates these parameters from a sparse binary matrix, placing tasks and models on a shared continuum identified up to an affine transformation.
- **Core assumption:** Binary outcomes follow the 2PL process; latent traits are stable across the benchmark set.
- **Break condition:** Poor IRT model fit (e.g., high residuals, non‑convergence) invalidates latent estimates.

### Mechanism 2: Linear calibration to human time
- **Claim:** Latent task difficulty (b) is linearly related to the logarithm of human completion time, enabling the IRT scale to be anchored to human‑interpretable units.
- **Mechanism:** For METR tasks with human time annotations, a linear regression log(h) = slope × b + intercept is fitted. This mapping translates any task’s IRT difficulty into an estimated human time without new human studies.
- **Core assumption:** The difficulty–time relationship generalizes across benchmarks; human time annotations are reliable.
- **Break condition:** If the calibration fails to transfer to out‑of‑distribution benchmarks (low R², misaligned predictions), the mapping lacks generality.

### Mechanism 3: Exponential capability horizon growth
- **Claim:** Frontier model ability increases exponentially over time, so the solvable human task‑length horizon (derived from calibrated difficulty) also grows exponentially, enabling forecasting without human annotations.
- **Mechanism:** For each model release window, the best model’s ability θ_max equals the task difficulty at 50% success (b = θ). After calibration, b_max maps to human time h_max. Tracking h_max over release dates reveals exponential growth (doubling ~6 months).
- **Core assumption:** The IRT latent scale remains stable over time; the best model per window is representative; calibration does not drift.
- **Break condition:** A plateau or deviation from exponential growth (e.g., scaling slowdown) would undermine future predictions.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Data collector -> IRT module -> Calibration module -> Prediction engine -> Forecasting module

**Critical path:**  
Response matrix -> IRT fitting -> Calibration -> New task time estimation -> Frontier track over time.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- 2PL vs. 1PL: 2PL adds discrimination for richer modeling but risks overfitting on sparse data.  
- Calibration scope: Using only METR assumes transferability; domain shift could bias predictions.  
- Binary aggregation: Collapsing multiple trials to a single binary outcome discards variance information.

**Failure signatures:**  
- IRT diagnostics: high R‑hat, low effective sample size, poor posterior predictive checks.  
- Calibration: R² (METR) far below 0.8, or systematic residuals.  
- Transfer: Human time predictions for SWE‑bench or Cybench fall outside plausible ranges (e.g., all <15 min for complex bugs).  
- Forecasting: Inferred doubling time varies widely or recent models fall below the exponential fit.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Validate IRT model: Compare predicted vs. observed success rates across tasks; check that task difficulty and ability are on a shared scale.  
2. Reproduce calibration: Fit log(h) vs. b on METR; confirm R²≈0.81 and visualize as in Figure 2.  
3. Test transfer: Predict human time buckets for SWE‑bench Verified (macro F1 >0.2) and Cybench tolerance (≥90% within 0.5×–2×).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can BRIDGE be extended to model human–AI collaborative settings, where task responsibility is shared and human interventions provide signals about effective task difficulty?
2. How can BRIDGE incorporate task attributes like information requirements, tool use, or reliance on out‑of‑scope knowledge to better evaluate knowledge‑intensive benchmarks?
3. How can BRIDGE produce uncertainty‑aware predictions of human completion time, especially for longer tasks where inter‑individual variability grows?
4. Does the unidimensional latent trait assumption of the 2PL IRT model hold across the heterogeneous benchmarks combined in BRIDGE, or would a multidimensional IRT model be more appropriate?

## Limitations
- The unidimensional IRT assumption may not hold across heterogeneous task domains, potentially distorting difficulty estimates.
- Calibration relies solely on METR tasks; domain shift to benchmarks like SWE‑bench or Cybench could weaken the linear mapping.
- Binary aggregation of model trial outcomes discards variance and uses an arbitrary 50% success threshold, which may introduce noise.
- Frontier model selection and windowing choices may bias the estimated exponential growth rate.

## Confidence
- 2PL IRT latent scale: Medium (method sound but missing convergence diagnostics and fit assessment).  
- Log-linear calibration on METR: High (R²=0.81 strong on in-distribution).  
- Transfer to new benchmarks: Medium (moderate F1/tolerance; limited annotation quality).  
- Exponential growth of solvable horizon: Medium (observational trend; may plateau).
