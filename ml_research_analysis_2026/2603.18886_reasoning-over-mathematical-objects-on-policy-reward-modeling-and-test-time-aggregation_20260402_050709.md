# Reasoning over mathematical objects: on-policy reward modeling and test time aggregation

## Quick Facts

- **arXiv ID:** 2603.18886  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18886  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Introduces PrincipiaBench/Principia Collection; on-policy RL with a strong model-based verifier yields 7–18% gains on mathematical object derivation and transfers to AIME (+7–18%) and GPQA (+12–25%); ParaGator sets state-of-the-art.

## Executive Summary

We introduce *PrincipiaBench* and the *Principia Collection*, new resources for training and evaluating language models on deriving complex mathematical objects (equations, inequalities, intervals, sets, matrices, piecewise functions) from graduate-level STEM problem statements. Existing benchmarks focus on numerical or multiple‑choice answers; our benchmark requires expressive output in mathematical notation. We train models with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) using a powerful model‑based verifier (e.g., GPT‑OSS‑120B) as reward signal. This on‑policy reinforcement learning approach improves base models by 7–18 percent points on *PrincipiaBench* and transfers to standard math benchmarks (AIME, GPQA) with similar gains. Two extensions are proposed: *RLLM*, which learns an on‑policy reward model, and *ParaGator*, which jointly optimizes parallel generation and online aggregation. Frontier models (o3, Qwen3‑235B) achieve only 56–62% on *PrincipiaBench*, indicating substantial room for improvement.

## Method Summary

Training uses GRPO, an on‑policy RL algorithm, with a strong verifier providing binary correctness rewards for generated mathematical objects. The training corpus, *Principia Collection*, consists of 248K synthetic instances from MSC2020/PhySH taxonomies, each requiring a mathematical‑object answer. For each problem, the policy generates multiple chain‑of‑thought (CoT) responses; the verifier compares each response to the reference answer using a reference‑based prompt, delivering a reward. The policy is updated with advantages normalized within groups of rollouts, together with a KL regularizer. Evaluation reports mean@k accuracy over k generations. The paper introduces two enhancements: (1) *RLLM* replaces the static external verifier with an on‑policy‑trained language‑model‑as‑reward, and (2) *ParaGator* trains an aggregator end‑to‑end in an online fashion: it first optimizes pass@k and then pass@1, aggregating multiple samples at test time. All methods avoid rule‑based verifiers (e.g., math‑verify) due to fragility.

## Key Results

- On‑policy GRPO with GPT‑OSS‑120B verifier yields +7–18% absolute improvement on *PrincipiaBench* across base models (Qwen2.5‑7B, Qwen3‑4B, OctoThinker‑8B) and transfers to AIME (+7–18%) and GPQA (+12–25%).
- *RLLM* (on‑policy LM‑as‑RM) outperforms both scalar RLHF and rule‑based RLVR, achieving up to +8% absolute gain on competition math scores.
- *ParaGator*’s online two‑stage aggregation surpasses offline baselines and establishes new state‑of‑the‑art on *PrincipiaBench* and math benchmarks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

The superiority of the approach stems from three factors. First, a strong model‑based verifier provides a dense and reliable reward signal that can assess equivalence of mathematical objects, overcoming the brittleness of symbolic rule‑based verifiers that often fail on nuanced representations. Second, on‑policy RL ensures that the rollout distribution used for reward collection matches the current policy, avoiding the distribution shift that can degrade learning in off‑policy methods. This alignment is crucial because the reward model (or verifier) is highly sensitive to the style and formatting of the outputs. Third, test‑time aggregation (pass@k) and its online extension *ParaGator* effectively harness multiple diverse samples from the policy, selecting the most likely correct answer; training the aggregator on‑policy further refines this selection process. Together, these elements encourage the model to produce more precise and mathematically sound derivations, which transfer to tasks requiring numerical or multiple‑choice reasoning.

## Foundational Learning

- **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**  
  *Why needed:* The core RL algorithm used for on‑policy training with group‑normalized advantages.  
  *Quick check:* Verify implementation by computing advantages within a group of rollouts and confirming policy loss matches reference code.

- **On‑policy vs. Off‑policy RL**  
  *Why needed:* On‑policy training avoids distribution mismatch between generated rollouts and the policy being updated.  
  *Quick check:* Compare performance gains when using rollouts from the current policy versus a fixed data buffer; on‑policy should yield better reward alignment.

- **Model‑based Reward Modeling**  
  *Why needed:* A strong verifier (GPT‑OSS‑120B) is required to judge correctness of nontrivial mathematical objects where symbolic rules are insufficient.  
  *Quick check:* Measure verifier agreement with human judgments on a held‑out set; target >90% accuracy.

- **Mathematical Object Equivalence**  
  *Why needed:* The task demands recognizing when two mathematical expressions (e.g., different but equivalent forms) are semantically identical.  
  *Quick check:* Test the verifier on known equivalent/non‑equivalent pairs; F1 > 0.9 desirable.

- **Test‑time Aggregation (pass@k)**  
  *Why needed:* Aggregating multiple samples from the same policy increases chance of obtaining a correct answer.  
  *Quick check:* For a fixed model, compute pass@1 and pass@k on a validation set; k should yield significant gains.

- **Synthetic Dataset Construction (Principia Collection)**  
  *Why needed:* Provides large‑scale training data of problems requiring mathematical‑object answers.  
  *Quick check:* Sample instances and verify that answers are indeed objects (e.g., sets, matrices) and not just numbers.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**  
Problem Statement → Policy Language Model → Multiple CoT Responses → Verifier Language Model (GPT‑OSS‑120B) → Binary Rewards → GRPO Optimizer → Updated Policy LM.  
For *ParaGator*: Policy LM → Multiple Responses → Aggregator LM → Final Answer; both policy and aggregator are updated online.

**Critical Path**  
1. Load a batch of problems from *Principia Collection*.  
2. Generate `n` CoT completions per problem using the current policy (temperature 0.7–1.4).  
3. For each completion, send the problem, reference answer, and response to the verifier; receive a binary correctness label.  
4. Compute group‑relative advantages: normalize rewards within the set of completions for a given problem.  
5. Perform a GRPO update: policy gradient loss with KL penalty against a reference model (usually the initial checkpoint).  
6. Periodically evaluate on *PrincipiaBench* validation set (mean@8) and checkpoint the policy.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- **Verifier choice:** Using a proprietary model like GPT‑OSS‑120B maximizes accuracy but introduces cost, rate limits, and reproducibility issues; open‑source alternatives lower cost but may underperform.  
- **On‑policy vs. off‑policy:** On‑policy learning requires generating fresh rollouts each step, increasing compute; off‑policy reuse reduces cost but risks reward mismatch.  
- **Rule‑based vs. model‑based verification:** Rule‑based methods are fast and deterministic but brittle to formatting variations; model‑based verifiers are robust but slower and may have their own biases.  
- **Aggregation strategy:** Simple voting (e.g., majority) is cheap but may not capture nuanced correctness; learning an aggregator (*ParaGator*) improves performance but adds complexity and training time.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Verifier agreement on a random subset of model outputs falls below 80%, indicating a weak or mis‑aligned reward signal.  
- Validation accuracy plateau or decline after a few GRPO steps, suggesting instability or distribution shift.  
- KL divergence explodes, causing training crashes; this often stems from too large a learning rate or insufficient reference‑model weighting.  
- Aggregator fails to beat simple pass@k voting, implying that the two‑stage online training is not properly implemented.

**First Experiments**  
1. **Baseline GRPO pipeline:** Implement the GRPO loop with a mock verifier (exact string match) on a tiny subset (e.g., 100 problems) to ensure end‑to‑end functionality.  
2. **Open‑source verifier integration:** Replace the mock verifier with an accessible model (e.g., J1) using the reference‑based prompt from Appendix Figure 26; evaluate verifier F1 on *Principia VerifyBench*.  
3. **Reproduce core result:** Train Qwen2.5‑7B with GRPO on 1k *Principia Collection* samples for a few hundred steps, report mean@8 on the *PrincipiaBench* validation set; expect a few points gain over the base model.

## Limitations

- Exact GRPO hyperparameters (learning rate, KL schedule, PPO clip, batch size) for different base models are not disclosed, hindering direct replication.  
- Details on verifier implementation at scale (batching, rate limits, retry logic) and full prompt templates for equivalence checking are missing.  
- The precise two‑stage online training procedure for *ParaGator* is only described conceptually, leaving algorithmic gaps for faithful implementation.
