# Multi-Domain Riemannian Graph Gluing for Building Graph Foundation Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.00618
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00618
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: GraphGlue achieves 68.4% ACC on Computers and 67.1% on Reddit in 1-shot node classification, outperforming prior methods by +4.9% and +2.3% respectively.

## Executive Summary
Multi-domain graph pre-training lacks a theoretical understanding of cross-domain knowledge integration. This work introduces a Riemannian geometry perspective to merge any graph dataset into a unified, smooth Riemannian manifold. The core contribution is neural manifold gluing, which uses metric compatibility and trivial holonomy to glue local geometries, with Ricci curvature controlling smoothness. The GraphGlue framework supports batched pre-training with EMA prototyping and Riemannian MoE, and provides a Geometric Transfer Metric (GTM). Experiments show superior cross-domain transfer, with significant gains in few-shot tasks, and validate a geometric scaling law.

## Method Summary
GraphGlue pre-trains in three stages: first, local construction via graph contrastive learning and EMA-updated Riemannian prototypes to distinguish domain semantics; second, global manifold skeleton by building a cross-dataset KNN graph and applying holonomy and curvature losses to enforce consistency; third, per-dataset refinement with the same geometric regularizations. For adaptation, the target graph is aligned to the manifold using learnable prompts and a Riemannian MoE that aggregates knowledge from nearest prototypes, with geometric losses on a transfer graph providing regularization. The overall objective combines contrastive, prototype, and geometric losses.

## Key Results
- **Superior transfer performance:** In 1-shot node classification, GraphGlue attains 68.4% ACC on Computers (+4.9% over prior) and 67.1% on Reddit (+2.3%).
- **Geometric scaling law:** Increasing the number of pre-training datasets yields a smoother manifold, raising transfer accuracy following a logarithmic trend.
- **Interpretable transfer metric:** The proposed Geometric Transfer Metric (GTM) correlates with transfer difficulty, offering a quantitative assessment of integration effort.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Knowledge integration across domains is achieved by constructing a unified, smooth Riemannian manifold where each graph’s local geometry is embedded and glued together via metric compatibility and trivial holonomy, with Ricci curvature controlling smoothness.
- **Mechanism:**  
  - **Local Geometry:** For each node/graph, use a (k, M)-sparse perturbation (M virtual nodes, top‑k connected) to generate M tangent vectors from the encoder. Apply QR‑decomposition with length recovery to obtain an adaptive orthogonal frame. The local metric tensor is G_i = W^⊤W, where W is the basis matrix.  
  - **Gluing:** For each edge (i,j), define an edge tangent translation P(i,j) = G_j^{-1/2}(G_j^{1/2} G_i G_j^{1/2})^{1/2} G_j^{-1/2} that satisfies P^⊤ G_j P = G_i, acting as an isometry. Applied along all edges, this yields a global continuous metric (Theorem 4.6).  
  - **Triangle Consistency:** Enforce trivial holonomy on every triangle: H(T)=I, where H(C) = ∏ P along cycle C. This is implemented as ℒ_holo = (1/|A|) Σ_{triangles} ||P(k,i)P(j,k)P(i,j) - I||_F^2 .  
  - **Smoothing:** Control volume element changes via log‑det determinants: ℒ_curv = (1/|A|) Σ_{triangles} |log(r_ij) - log(r_jk)|^2 with r(z_i,z_j)=detG_i/detG_j, which approximates Ricci curvature sign.
- **Core assumption:** Graph data lies on (or can be embedded into) a low‑dimensional Riemannian manifold; local tangent frames learned by the GNN capture the true intrinsic geometry; holonomy triviality plus smoothness ensures global consistency.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "...merge any graph dataset into a unified, smooth Riemannian manifold... gluing is achieved through metric compatibility along edges and triangle triviality via holonomy. Smoothness is enforced by controlling volume change ratios (Ricci curvature)."
  - [section 4.1] defines the adaptive orthogonal frame and local metric G_i = W^⊤W; [section 4.2] presents edge tangent translation, holonomy loss ℒ_holo, and curvature loss ℒ_curv.
  - [corpus] Multiple neighboring works explore Riemannian graphs (258222) and multi‑domain pre‑training (143931, 41399), indicating a known gap: integrating across domains via geometric techniques, which this mechanism directly addresses.
- **Break condition:** Failure modes include:
  - Holonomy loss does not converge → manifold glued with twists, violating continuity.
  - Curvature loss remains high → abrupt volume changes, causing "folds" that hinder gradient flow for downstream transfer.
  - Isometry condition violated (numerical instability in matrix square root) → non‑preserving metrics, breaking global consistency.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Pre‑training with Exponential Moving Average (EMA) prototypes and contrastive loss organizes domain semantics into distinct, stable regions of the manifold, while batched computation enables scalability.
- **Mechanism:**  
  - For each domain k, maintain a Riemannian prototype (z_Sk, log G_Sk) representing the global location and average local geometry.  
  - Update prototypes via EMA: z_Sk ← β z_Sk + (1-β) avg(z_G) over batch; log G_Sk ← β log G_Sk + (1-β) avg(log G(z_G)).  
  - Apply a sample‑prototype contrastive loss ℒ_proto that encourages each graph to match its own prototype and avoid others, separated by temperature τ.  
  - Batched pre‑training iterates over mixed data; after warm‑up epochs, domain prototypes become discriminative anchors on the manifold.
- **Core assumption:** Different domains occupy nearby but distinguishable locations on the unified manifold; EMA stabilizes prototype trajectories; contrastive loss is sufficient to separate them.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 5.1] explicitly introduces EMA prototyping and prototype loss ℒ_proto: "distinguishes domain semantics through different locations on the manifold and efficiently handles large‑scale graphs in a batched manner."
  - [abstract] mentions "batched pre‑training with EMA prototyping" yielding superior transfer.
  - [corpus] EMA is a standard technique for stabilizing representations (e.g., in BYOL), but its Riemannian adaptation (using log for SPD matrices) is novel here.
- **Break condition:** 
  - Prototypes collapse to a single point → cannot distinguish domains, leading to negative transfer (e.g., Figure 4 shows GCOPE degrades with dissimilar data; GraphGlue avoids this via EMA + contrastive).
  - EMA momentum β too low → noisy prototypes; too high → slow adaptation.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** During adaptation, Riemannian Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) and geometric gluing losses align a target graph to the pre‑trained manifold, while the Geometric Transfer Metric (GTM) quantifies integration effort via holonomy and curvature disagreements.
- **Mechanism:**  
  - For a target graph, obtain its embeddings z_T and local basis W_T via the frozen encoder.  
  - Introduce a learnable prompt matrix Q ∈ ℝ^{d×d}. Adapt coordinates: z_adapt = Q z_T. Adapt metric: apply Q to basis vectors, giving G_adapt = diag(||Q w_T^1||^2, ..., ||Q w_T^M||^2).  
  - Construct a transfer graph G^0 connecting the target to its k‑nearest prototypes (anchors on the manifold). Apply ℒ_holo(G^0) and ℒ_curv(G^0) as geometric regularization (ℓ_glue).  
  - Riemannian MoE: each prototype (z_Sk, log G_Sk) is an expert; compute gating weights β_k = g_k(z_adapt, G_adapt). Combine expert metrics: log G_align = Σ_k β_k log G_Sk. Final representation: z_task = [z_T; vec(log G_adapt); vec(log G_align)].  
  - GTM = ΔH + ΔC, i.e., ℒ_holo(G^0) + ℒ_curv(G^0).
- **Core assumption:** Target domain can be integrated into the pre‑trained manifold by aligning its local metric to the prototype region; the sum of holonomy and curvature violations on the connecting subgraph indicates transfer difficulty.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 5.2] details prompt adaptation, Riemannian MoE, and defines GTM as ΔH + ΔC.
  - [section 6.2] shows GTM correlates with test loss (Figure 3); lower GTM → better transfer.
  - [abstract] "provides a geometric transfer metric (GTM) based on holonomy and curvature losses."
  - [corpus] GTM fills a gap: existing similarity measures (Wang et al. 2024) do not directly assess geometric compatibility; GTM is derived from the model’s own construction.
- **Break condition:**
  - High GTM ( ℒ_holo or ℒ_curv significant) → target graph far from manifold, likely poor transfer unless more fine‑tuning data is added.
  - Gating weights collapse to one expert → MoE degenerate, losing benefit of multi‑domain knowledge.

## Foundational Learning
- **Riemannian Manifold:** Why needed: All geometric operations (gluing, smoothing, MoE) occur on a learned manifold; understanding tangent spaces, metrics, and volume elements is essential to grasp how local geometry is extracted and integrated. Quick check: How does the adaptive orthogonal frame relate to the tangent bundle of the manifold?
- **Holonomy (Parallel Transport around loops):** Why needed: Holonomy measures the failure of path‑independent transport; trivial holonomy on triangles ensures the glued manifold is globally consistent (flat connection locally). It underpins the gluing condition. Quick check: Why is holonomy defined on triangles sufficient if every edge lies in a triangle? (Hint: cycle space generated by triangles.)
- **Exponential Moving Average (EMA):** Why needed: Stabilizes prototype estimates in batched pre‑training, smoothing domain centers over time, which is critical for consistent manifold anchoring when data arrives in mini‑batches. Quick check: Why is the update on the log‑metric (not the metric itself) used for SPD matrices?
- **Mixture of Experts (MoE):** Why needed: Riemannian MoE aggregates geometric knowledge from multiple domain prototypes, allowing the target to inherit manifold structure from relevant sources; gating based on (z_adapt, G_adapt) determines which domains contribute. Quick check: How does the MoE gate decide which prototype to use for a given target sample?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Encoder generates embeddings -> Adaptive Orthogonal Frame module produces local metric G -> Prototype Manager (EMA) stores domain prototypes -> Gluing Module computes edge translations and holonomy loss -> Smoothing Module enforces curvature loss -> Adaptation Module aligns target via prompts and Riemannian MoE -> Loss Aggregator combines all losses -> GTM Calculator reports transfer difficulty.

**Critical path:**
1. Load mixed batch, encode graphs, apply AOF to get W and G.
2. Pre-training: compute contrastive loss, prototype loss, geometric losses over cross-dataset KNN; backprop; update encoder and AOF; EMA update prototypes.
3. Adaptation: freeze encoder, compute target embeddings, prompt adaptation, build transfer graph to prototypes, compute ℒ_glue, apply Riemannian MoE, fine-tune.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Manifold dimension M: small M insufficient, large M costly; empirically set to 32.
- Neighborhood size k: larger k improves manifold approximation but increases triangle sampling cost.
- λ balance: high λ stresses geometric consistency, low λ risks ignoring alignment; tuned per task.
- EMA momentum β: tradeoff between stability and adaptability; defaults 0.9–0.99.
- Triangle vs pair sampling: exact triangles expensive; pairs approximate but may weaken condition on sparse graphs.

**Failure signatures:**
- Diverging ℒ_holo: manifold twists due to insufficient connections.
- Oscillating/high ℒ_curv: folds from abrupt volume changes, often from high LR or small M.
- Prototype collapse: all domains same prototype, leading to mixed semantics.
- Numerical instability in P(i,j): near-singular G_i; add ε·I.
- GTM not correlating with loss: geometric losses may not capture transfer difficulty.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Ablation of ℒ_holo and ℒ_curv: remove each loss, evaluate 1-shot ACC; expect significant drop.
2. Manifold visualization: reduce embeddings to 2D, color by domain; expect distinct, smooth clusters.
3. GTM vs task loss curve: plot GTM and test cross-entropy over pre-training epochs; expect inverse relationship.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **What is the fundamental mechanism by which knowledge from multiple source domains integrates and transfers to a target domain?** The paper proposes a Riemannian framework but does not establish a universal principle; evidence for resolution would be a formal theory with broader validation.
2. **Why does the transfer performance follow a logarithmic scaling law with respect to the number of pre-training datasets?** Empirical observation lacks theoretical derivation linking manifold smoothness and dataset quantity.
3. **Can GTM be theoretically guaranteed to correlate with actual transfer difficulty, and used to select optimal source domains?** Correlation shown empirically but no proof; evidence would be a theorem or extensive cross-domain validation.
4. **Under what conditions does neural manifold gluing fail to produce a smooth manifold preserving domain semantics?** Framework assumes gluing is always possible but doesn't characterize failure modes; evidence would be a characterization of limitations or a diagnostic test based on GTM/curvature.

## Limitations
- Theoretical assumptions (triangular coverage, metric invertibility) may fail on sparse graphs, risking manifold inconsistency.
- Hyperparameter generalization (λ, k, M, EMA β) to unseen domains is untested; optimal values likely dataset-dependent.
- GTM's predictive power for out-of-distribution targets remains unknown; correlation only validated on six datasets.

## Confidence
- Neural manifold gluing (metric compatibility + trivial holonomy): Medium – Theorem guarantees continuity under ideal conditions, but practical convergence and triangle coverage are unproven.
- EMA prototyping for domain discrimination: High – Built on stable techniques; ablations confirm gains.
- GTM as transfer difficulty metric: Medium – Strong empirical correlation but lacks theoretical foundation and universality.
