# Surprisal-Guided Selection: Compute-Optimal Test-Time Strategies for Execution-Grounded Code Generation
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.07670
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07670
- **Reference count**: 31
- **Primary result**: Best-of-N sampling (K=64) achieves 90% task success on KernelBench L1, while Test-Time Training (TTT) reaches only 30.6% and even underperforms single-sample inference; among correct samples, selecting the highest‑surprisal (lowest confidence) yields 80% success versus 50% for confidence‑guided, and a top‑3 variant matches oracle performance at 100%.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates compute‑optimal test‑time strategies for verifiable execution‑grounded (VEG) tasks, using GPU kernel optimization as a case study. It compares sampling‑based search (Best‑of‑N), gradient‑based test‑time training (TTT), and self‑distillation (SDPO) under matched compute budgets. The core finding is that simple search decisively outperforms TTT, and that a novel selection heuristic—choosing the highest‑surprisal (lowest model confidence) among correct samples—significantly boosts success. The results challenge the assumption that gradient adaptation is necessary at test time for dense‑reward coding tasks and instead advocate for investing compute in diverse sampling and intelligent selection.

## Method Summary
The study builds on a strong base model obtained by fine‑tuning GPT‑OSS‑120B with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) on 80 KernelBench L1 training tasks (outer loop). This RLVR‑trained checkpoint is then evaluated under various test‑time strategies (inner loop) on 20 held‑out tasks. Strategies are compared using a fixed total number of code rollouts (e.g., 320). Best‑of‑N generates K independent completions per task, filters for functional correctness via a deterministic CUDA evaluator, and selects a final sample according to a rule (oracle speedup, surprisal, confidence, etc.). Batch TTT performs a small number of GRPO steps on a batch of tasks, updating LoRA adapters, and selects the checkpoint that maximizes validation fast1 across steps. SDPO variants distill token‑level policies from rollouts. The primary metric is fast1: the proportion of tasks for which a correct sample achieves speedup >1×.

## Key Results
- **Best‑of‑N scaling**: Task success saturates by K=16, achieving ~99.9% coverage; larger K gives marginal gains.
- **TTT vs. Best‑of‑N** (matched 320 rollouts): Best‑of‑N (K=64) reaches 90% task success, TTT’s best checkpoint only 30.6%, and TTT even underperforms single‑sample inference due to over‑sharpening that collapses solution diversity.
- **Surprisal‑guided selection**: Among correct samples, picking the highest‑surprisal (lowest log‑prob) yields 80% success vs 50% for confidence‑guided (absolute +30%). A top‑3 variant matches oracle performance (100%) with negligible extra cost.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Search Diversity Trumps Adaptation
Gradient‑based TTT causes **over‑sharpening**: after 1–2 updates the policy concentrates on early mediocre successes, destroying the diversity needed to locate high‑quality tail solutions. Best‑of‑N maintains independent samples, preserving diversity; selection then picks the best among a broad set. The highest‑quality kernel optimizations lie in the low‑probability tail of the model’s original distribution, which adaptation moves away from. Evidence: TTT’s performance peaks at step 2 then regresses; equivalent K < 1; NLL‑speedup correlation deepens (ρ from -0.198 to -0.275), especially for bottom‑quartile solutions.

### Mechanism 2: Surprisal‑Guided Selection
The model’s probability distribution reflects training frequency, not quality. High‑speedup kernels are rare and thus carry high surprisal (low log‑prob). Filtering for correctness first isolates valid solutions; among them, the most surprising sample is more likely to be the fastest. This inverts the usual confidence‑quality relationship. Evidence: surprisal‑guided yields 80% success vs 50% for confidence‑guided; quartile analysis shows high‑surprisal half attains 64.2% fast1 vs 58.1% for low‑surprisal; length‑control rules out token count confound.

### Mechanism 3: Over‑Sharpening in Dense‑Reward VEG Tasks
Dense continuous speedup rewards provide immediate, strong gradients. Minimal GRPO steps (1–2) already compress feedback enough to narrow the distribution; further updates overfit to the narrow subset of early successes, causing performance regression. This explains why TTT underperforms even single‑sample inference. Evidence: per‑task TTT trajectories show heterogeneous peaks at step 1–2 followed by collapse; NLL probe reveals progressive anti‑calibration (bottom‑quartile ρ from -0.237 to -0.442).

## Foundational Learning

### Compute‑Optimal Allocation
- **Why needed**: Challenges the default that test‑time compute should be spent on gradient adaptation; instead advocates allocating compute to sample diversity and intelligent selection for dense‑reward VEG tasks.
- **Quick check**: What are the three canonical ways to spend test‑time compute, and which does this paper advocate for VEG tasks? (Answer: sampling diversity, selection intelligence, gradient adaptation; advocate: diversity + selection, not adaptation.)

### Surprisal as Inverse Quality Indicator
- **Why needed**: The core selection strategy flips the usual preference for high‑confidence outputs; understanding that surprisal correlates with rarity, and that rare strategies are often high‑performing, is key.
- **Quick check**: Why might a lower‑probability (higher‑surprisal) correct kernel be faster than a higher‑probability one? (Answer: The model’s training data contain many naive implementations; high‑quality optimizations are under‑represented, hence low probability.)

### Over‑Sharpening and Distribution Collapse
- **Why needed**: Explains why TTT fails under dense rewards; recognizing that gradient updates can too quickly concentrate the distribution helps avoid misapplying TTT.
- **Quick check**: What is over‑sharpening, and how does it differ from the beneficial sharpening that RL sometimes achieves? (Answer: Over‑sharpening is premature collapse onto suboptimal modes due to excessive concentration; beneficial sharpening refines without losing tail diversity.)

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
RLVR Training (GRPO + LoRA on 80 tasks) → Base Checkpoint → Test‑Time Strategy (Best‑of‑N / Batch TTT / SDPO) → Deterministic CUDA Evaluator (correctness & speedup) → fast1 metric.

### Critical Path
For a given strategy and total rollout budget (e.g., 320):
- **Best‑of‑N**: For each test task, sample K completions (e.g., K=64), filter correct, apply selection rule (oracle, surprisal, confidence). Combine results across tasks.
- **Batch TTT (BoA)**: Initialize with base checkpoint; for each step s (up to 5), sample K rollouts per task from current policy on a batch (e.g., 5 tasks), compute GRPO update on LoRA adapters, evaluate all rollouts so far to compute per‑step fast1. After completing steps, choose the checkpoint (step) with highest validation fast1. Total rollouts must match the budget (e.g., 2 steps × 160 rollouts = 320).
- **SDPO**: Distill a token‑level policy from rollout data (with/without execution feedback) and sample from it; details in appendix.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Sampling vs. adaptation**: Sampling spends compute on diverse candidates; adaptation spends on weight updates. Search wins for dense‑reward VEG.
- **Selection cost**: Confidence/surprisal‑guided selection are zero‑cost (use existing logprobs); oracle selection requires full speedup evaluation; surprisal‑guided top‑3 needs 3 extra evaluations per task but matches oracle.
- **Hyperparameters**: Temperature 0.25 balances diversity; K controls coverage; TTT learning rate and steps must be minimal (1–2) to avoid over‑sharpening.

### Failure Signatures
- **TTT**: Non‑monotonic fast1 trajectory peaking at step 1–2 then regressing; high seed variance; deepening negative NLL‑speedup correlation; equivalent K < 1.
- **Selection**: Confidence‑guided underperforms random when logprob variance low; surprisal‑guided fails if all correct samples have similar surprisal (e.g., cuDNN‑based layers).
- **General**: Tasks with no speedup headroom (baseline optimal) or extremely low correctness rate are failure cases for any selection.

### First Three Experiments
1. **Best‑of‑N scaling**: Vary K (1–64) on task subsets; observe saturation at K=16 (99.9% success).
2. **TTT vs. Best‑of‑N (matched budget)**: Compare BoA (TTT) against Best‑of‑N (K=64) under 320 rollouts; TTT best checkpoint 30.6% vs Best‑of‑N 100% (surprisal‑guided 80%).
3. **Selection strategy ablation**: Within Best‑of‑N (K=64), compare oracle, confidence‑guided, surprisal‑guided, random‑correct, and surprisal‑guided‑top3; confirm 30% absolute gain and top3 matching oracle.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Entropy regularization in TTT**: Does adding an entropy bonus prevent over‑sharpening in dense‑reward VEG tasks, or is the collapse inherent?
2. **Deployable surprisal‑guided pipeline**: Can we rank candidates by surprisal *before* correctness filtering to reduce evaluation cost while preserving oracle‑level performance?
3. **Reward sparsification**: Does thresholding the continuous speedup reward shift the optimal TTT adaptation steps upward, validating the proposed reward compression principle?

## Limitations
- Evaluation limited to a single benchmark (KernelBench L1), restricting generalizability to other VEG domains.
- Results may depend on the specific 120B GRPO‑finetuned model; other scales or training methods could behave differently.
- Surprisal‑guided selection requires a sufficient number of correct samples per task; in low‑correctness regimes the signal vanishes.
- Test‑time training variants explored are minimal; other algorithms might avoid over‑sharpening.

## Confidence
- **Best‑of‑N superiority over TTT** for dense‑reward VEG tasks: Medium (strong single‑benchmark evidence).
- **Surprisal‑guided selection improvement**: Medium overall; High for tasks with high intra‑task logprob variance.
- **Over‑sharpening as the cause of TTT failure**: Medium (correlative evidence from trajectories and NLL probes).

## Next Checks
1. **Cross‑benchmark replication**: Run the same comparison on a different VEG dataset (e.g., other GPU kernel optimization suites) and across multiple model sizes (e.g., 7B, 34B).
2. **Base model ablation**: Evaluate surprisal‑guided selection using a pretrained model without RLVR fine‑tuning, and with alternative fine‑tuning (SFT, DPO) to isolate the effect of the outer loop.
3. **TTT diversity intervention**: Introduce an entropy bonus or larger batch size during TTT; measure whether performance still regresses and whether code‑level diversity correlates with final fast1.
