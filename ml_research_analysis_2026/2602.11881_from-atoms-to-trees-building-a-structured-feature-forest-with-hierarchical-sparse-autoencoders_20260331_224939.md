# From Atoms to Trees: Building a Structured Feature Forest with Hierarchical Sparse Autoencoders

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.11881
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11881
- **Reference count:** 28
- **Primary result:** HSAE reduces the Hamming distance between parent activations and their children’s logical-OR reconstructions by ~40% and improves parent-child co-activation probabilities by 15–25% compared to post-hoc baselines, while preserving standard SAE performance.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces Hierarchical Sparse Autoencoders (HSAE) to capture hierarchical feature structures in large language models that standard sparse autoencoders (SAEs) miss. HSAE jointly trains multiple SAE levels with increasing dictionary sizes and explicitly models parent-child relationships via a structural constraint loss and a random feature perturbation mechanism. By alternating between parameter updates and hierarchical reassignment, HSAE learns semantically coherent multi-level hierarchies (e.g., broad concepts like “Science” splitting into specialized sub-features) without sacrificing reconstruction quality. Experiments show significant improvements in hierarchical consistency metrics—parent-child Hamming distance drops from 36.1 to 21.3 and co-activation probabilities rise by 15–25%—while variance explained remains comparable to independent SAEs.

## Method Summary
HSAE extends standard SAEs by stacking several levels, each with a different dictionary size. At each level ℓ, a standard JumpReLU SAE encodes activations into sparse features fℓ. The core innovation is a hierarchical constraint that couples adjacent levels: for each parent feature at level ℓ, the activation should be reconstructible as the sum (or logical OR) of its assigned children’s activations at level ℓ+1. This is enforced through a mean squared error loss LPC between parent and summed children. Additionally, a random perturbation swaps a parent’s contribution with its children’s with small probability r during reconstruction, encouraging functional interchangeability. The parent-child assignments are not fixed but updated periodically—every 5000 training steps—by assigning each child to its most similar parent according to encoder cosine similarity, while leaving a fraction (e.g., 20%) unassigned to accommodate atomic features. The total loss combines standard SAE reconstruction/sparsity terms with the hierarchy loss, and training alternates between optimizing SAE parameters and updating the assignment tree. This joint, dynamic approach yields aligned, semantically interpretable feature hierarchies.

## Key Results
- HSAE reduces the parent-child Hamming distance from 36.1 to 21.3 (~40% improvement) compared to post-hoc baselines.
- Parent-child co-activation probabilities increase by 15–25%, and standard SAE metrics (variance explained, absorption) remain unchanged.
- Qualitative analysis reveals coherent multi-level semantic hierarchies; broad concepts systematically decompose into specialised sub-features (e.g., “Science” → “Biology”, “Physics”, …).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper identifies three key mechanisms that enable HSAE to learn hierarchical feature structures.

### Mechanism 1: Structural constraint loss
A mean squared error loss `LPC` penalises the discrepancy between a parent’s activation and the sum of its children’s activations: `LPC,ℓ,i(x) = ∥fℓ,i(x) - Σ_{j∈Cℓ,i} fℓ+1,j(x)∥²`. This directly incentivises the parent to summarise the collective behaviour of its children, under the assumption that hierarchical subsumption is a learnable prior in LLM representations. Evidence: The loss definition in Section 3.1 and neighbouring papers on feature splitting and hierarchical structure. The constraint would fail if assigned child pairs were semantically mismatched, resulting in high Hamming distances.

### Mechanism 2: Random feature perturbation
With probability r (default 5%), a parent’s output in the reconstruction is stochastically replaced by the sum of its children’s outputs. This exposes the decoder to mixed representations, teaching that parent and children are interchangeable at different granularities. The core assumption is that both capture the same underlying phenomenon. Evidence: Mention in abstract and Section 3.2; ablation in Figure 4 shows removing perturbation increases Hamming distance (29.7 vs 21.3). If r is too high, reconstruction becomes unstable, indicating misalignment.

### Mechanism 3: Alternating optimization
Parent-child assignments are updated periodically (every 5000 steps) based on current feature similarity (encoder cosine similarity). This co‑evolution allows the hierarchical structure to adapt as features refine, avoiding suboptimal fixed assignments. A static post‑hoc tree is inferior: Fix Tree ablation yields higher Hamming distance (24.2 vs 21.3). Training hyperparameters (update frequency, partial tree with ~20% unassigned) balance flexibility and stability.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**
- Level SAEs (`SAE_ℓ`): Standard two‑layer JumpReLU SAEs at each level ℓ with dictionary size nℓ, each having encoder `e_ℓ,i`, decoder `d_ℓ,i`, threshold `σ_ℓ,i`.
- Hierarchy (`C(ℓ,i)`): Set of child indices for parent feature `(ℓ,i)`; children are disjoint across parents at the same level.
- Losses: `LSAE,ℓ` (reconstruction + sparsity) per level; `LPC` (parent‑child constraint) for each parent with children; total `LHSAE = E[Σ LSAE,ℓ + ρ Σ LPC]`.
- Perturbation: With probability r, parent activation is replaced by sum of children’s activations during LSAE computation.
- Similarity metric: Encoder cosine similarity used in hierarchy updates.

**Critical path**
1. Initialize all SAE parameters and random parent‑child assignments (using a quantile filter to leave ~20% of children unassigned).
2. For many steps: optimize `LHSAE` with current assignments (Adam, batch 1024, dynamic sparsity weight λℓ to target L0=50).
3. Every 5000 steps: freeze parameters, recompute similarity between level ℓ and ℓ+1 features, reassign each child to its most similar parent (respecting disjointness), re‑apply quantile filter.
4. Repeat until convergence.

**Design tradeoffs**
- *Partial vs. full tree:* Forcing 100% assignment harms consistency (Hamming 24.0 vs 21.3); partial tree accommodates “atomic” features.
- *Constraint weight ρ:* Higher ρ improves alignment but risks bottlenecking representation (variance explained drops if too large).
- *Perturbation rate r:* Higher r strengthens alignment but adds noise; 5% is optimal.
- *Similarity metric choice:* Encoder, decoder, or co‑activation similarity all work similarly; encoder is simplest.

**Failure signatures**
- High parent‑child Hamming distance (>30) despite training.
- Low `P(activ_parent | activ_child)` (<0.6) indicating children activate without parent.
- Variance explained significantly below baseline SAEs (>0.02 drop).
- Many features remain unassigned (>40%) due to poor similarity distribution.

**First 3 experiments**
1. **Reproduce core result:** Train HSAE on Gemma2‑2b layer 13 with dictionary sizes 2048→16384 (L0=50). Expected Hamming distance ~21.3 vs ~36.1 for post‑hoc baseline.
2. **Ablate mechanisms:** Train variants without constraint loss (ρ=0) and without perturbation (r=0). Hamming distance should rise to ~29.7 and ~22.2 respectively (Figure 4).
3. **Test generalizability:** Apply HSAE to Gemma2‑2b layers 6/20 and Qwen3‑4b layer 18. Verify variance explained remains comparable to baseline while alignment metrics improve (Table 3).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Variable-depth hierarchies** – The fixed number of levels may not reflect varying semantic abstraction depths. The authors suggest a dynamic level allocation mechanism as future work.
2. **Residual misalignments** – Some parent‑child co‑activation gaps persist; it is unclear whether these stem from methodological constraints or genuinely non‑hierarchical activations in the LLM.
3. **Scaling with model size** – Experiments are limited to a few small models; it is unknown whether hierarchical patterns generalize to larger or deeper architectures.
4. **Branching factor and interpretability** – Features with more children show slightly higher interpretability, but it is unknown if branching causes clarity or is merely correlated.

## Limitations
- **Partial tree design** – The 20% unassigned heuristic lacks theoretical justification; it is unclear whether these truly represent “atomic” features or noisy leftovers.
- **Generalizability** – Evaluations cover only two models (Gemma2‑2b, Qwen3‑4b) and up to three layers; hierarchical patterns may not hold across architectures or all depths.
- **Implementation gaps** – Key details are underspecified: dynamic sparsity control (λ updates), ghost gradient mechanics, decoder normalization schedule, and quantile filtering procedure hinder faithful reproduction.

## Confidence
- **High:** Quantitative alignment metrics (Hamming distance, co‑activation probabilities). Reason: Direct measurements with clear baselines and ablations.
- **Medium:** Mechanistic claims (constraint loss, perturbation, alternating optimization). Reason: Supported by ablations but causal pathways not fully isolated from co‑adaptation effects.
- **Medium:** Qualitative hierarchical interpretations. Reason: Human‑labelled examples show coherence but lack systematic large‑scale validation.
- **Low:** Cross‑model/layer generalisation. Reason: Insufficient diversity in tested models and layers to claim broad applicability.

## Next Checks
1. **Scale testing** – Train HSAE on ≥5 layers across ≥3 model families (e.g., Gemma, Llama, Pythia). Expect hierarchical consistency improvements to persist but vary by layer depth.
2. **Unassigned feature analysis** – Probe the semantics of unassigned features (e.g., via cosine similarity to known datasets, clustering). Verify that forcing 100% assignment degrades coherence; hypothesise unassigned features are semantically diffuse or highly specialised.
3. **Hyperparameter sensitivity** – Sweep ρ (0.001–0.1) and r (0–20%). Predict a Pareto frontier where alignment improves until variance explained drops >0.02, with optimal values near ρ≈0.01, r≈5%.
