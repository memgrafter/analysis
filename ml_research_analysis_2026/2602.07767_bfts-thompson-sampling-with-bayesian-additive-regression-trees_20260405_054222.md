# BFTS: Thompson Sampling with Bayesian Additive Regression Trees

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.07767
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07767
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: BFTS integrates BART into Thompson Sampling, achieving O(√T) Bayesian regret, state-of-the-art average rank 1.63 on 9 OpenML datasets, near‑nominal 95% uncertainty calibration (coverage 0.944), and >30% user engagement improvement in a mobile health trial with feasible runtime (~33 minutes for 10k steps).

## Executive Summary
BFTS (Bayesian Forest Thompson Sampling) addresses the challenge of applying contextual bandits to personalized mobile health interventions, where user behaviors are highly nonlinear and standard linear models are biased while neural Thompson Sampling can be brittle. The core innovation is to integrate Bayesian Additive Regression Trees (BART)—a fully probabilistic, non‑parametric sum‑of‑trees model—directly into the Thompson sampling exploration loop. Each arm has its own independent BART model, preserving posterior factorization; batched MCMC updates with cold starts keep computational cost logarithmic in the number of rounds. Theoretically, under Gaussian noise and i.i.d. contexts, BFTS enjoys a Bayesian regret bound of O(√T), and a “feel‑good” variant attains frequentist minimax optimality. Empirically, BFTS sets a new state of the art on synthetic nonlinear benchmarks and on nine OpenML classification bandit datasets, maintains well‑calibrated uncertainty (≈95% coverage), and in the Drink Less mHealth trial increases user engagement by over 30% relative to the original deployed policy—all with a runtime of about 33 minutes for 10,000 decisions.

## Method Summary
In BFTS, each arm \(a \in \{1,\dots,K\}\) has an independent BART prior: \(f_a(x) = \sum_{j=1}^m g_{a,j}(x)\), where each \(g\) is a regression tree. The prior on tree depth is exponential (psplit ∝ α<sup>depth</sup>) and leaf values follow \(N(0, \sigma_µ^2)\). Gaussian noise with unknown variance (inverse‑Gamma prior) completes the likelihood. At each round \(t\), after an initial round‑robin allocation of \(\tau K\) samples, the algorithm draws a posterior sample \(\theta\) from a pre‑computed pool (approximated via Metropolis‑within‑Gibbs MCMC) and selects the greedy arm: \(a_t = \arg\max_a f_\theta(x_t, a)\). Posterior updates are not performed every round; instead a refresh schedule \(r(t)=\lceil 8 \log t\rceil\) triggers a cold‑start MCMC run (burn‑in + post iterations) on the full history, producing a fresh pool of size \(n_{\text{post}}\). The pool is then reused for subsequent rounds until the next refresh. This batched strategy reduces the number of expensive MCMC calls from \(O(T)\) to \(O(\log T)\) while preserving exploration efficacy, as the pool remains sufficiently accurate within each batch. The separate‑arm design is essential for the theoretical analysis, which bounds mutual information \(I(\theta^*; H_T)\) by decomposing it across arms and leveraging the tree structure’s entropy growth rate to achieve the \(\sqrt{T}\) regret guarantee.

## Key Results
- BFTS attains the best average rank (1.63) across nine OpenML classification bandit datasets, outperforming NeuralTS (3.00) and other baselines.
- Uncertainty calibration is near nominal: 95% coverage of 0.944 on the Friedman‑Sparse benchmark, with low Expected Calibration Error.
- In the Drink Less mobile health trial, BFTS improves user engagement by over 30% compared to the deployed policy and by >5% over the best baseline, with a runtime of ~33 minutes for 10k decision points.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: BART posterior for effective exploration
Claim: BART provides a principled, fully probabilistic posterior that balances exploration and exploitation in nonlinear bandits.
Mechanism: Independent BART priors per arm; Thompson Sampling draws from posterior; the ensemble’s inherent uncertainty drives exploration toward high‑uncertainty or high‑potential regions.
Core assumption: The posterior concentrates around the true reward function at a rate sufficient for optimal learning (Lemma 3: mutual information grows only logarithmically, yielding \(O(\sqrt{T})\) Bayesian regret).
Evidence anchors: Abstract and Section 3.2 define arm‑wise BART; Section 4 bounds mutual information via tree entropy and leaf‑parameter capacity; prior contraction guarantees from BART literature support Lemma 3.
Break condition: If posterior fails to contract (severe misspecification, too few trees), mutual information bound breaks and regret may degrade to linear.

### Mechanism 2: Batched MCMC with cold starts
Claim: Batched MCMC updates with cold starts provide computationally feasible posterior approximation while retaining near‑optimal regret.
Mechanism: Refresh schedule \(r(t)=\lceil 8 \log t\rceil\); when \(r(t)\) increases, run cold‑start MCMC (\(n_{\text{burn}} + n_{\text{post}}\) iterations) on all data, store \(n_{\text{post}}\) samples, and reuse without replacement. Total MCMC calls limited to \(O(\log T)\).
Core assumption: Batches are frequent enough for posterior accuracy; cold start avoids poor modes from warm‑started chains.
Evidence anchors: Algorithm 1 and Section 3.3 describe schedule; Section 7 reports stable policies (Policy ∆‑TV 0.03–0.08) despite elevated bootstrap R̂; related batched TS theory yields near‑optimal regret with logarithmic batches.
Break condition: Too long batches → stale posterior; too short → high runtime.

### Mechanism 3: Separate‑arm modeling for factorization
Claim: Independent arm models preserve posterior factorization, enabling a tight information‑theoretic regret bound.
Mechanism: Model \(f(x,a)=g_a(x)\) with separate BART per arm; posterior factorizes \(\Pi(\theta|H_T)=\prod_a \Pi_a(\theta_a|H_T)\). Mutual information \(I(\theta^*;H_T)\) sums over arms, each bounded by tree‑structure entropy and leaf‑parameter capacity.
Core assumption: Arm‑wise factorization holds; cross‑arm pooling not needed (\(K\) small relative to \(T\)).
Evidence anchors: Section 3.3 chooses separate‑arm for factorization; Remark 1 notes factorization specific to this design; sensitivity (Fig 8a) shows one‑hot encoding drastically increases normalized regret.
Break condition: Using joint model (one‑hot/multi‑encoding) breaks factorization and likely destroys \(O(\sqrt{T})\) guarantee.

## Foundational Learning
### Bayesian Nonparametrics (BART)
Why needed here: BART allows flexible, data‑driven modeling of complex, nonlinear reward interactions without a fixed parametric form. Its sum‑of‑trees prior with depth‑exponential splitting provides strong regularization and a well‑defined posterior.
Quick check question: Why does BART’s depth‑exponential splitting prior \((\text{psplit}(\Omega)=\alpha^{d(\Omega)}/q^d)\) ensure the prior entropy on tree structures is only logarithmic in the maximum number of leaves \(N_{\text{max}}\)?

### Thompson Sampling (TS)
Why needed here: TS is the exploration‑exploitation algorithm that samples actions from a posterior over reward models. BFTS replaces the usual parametric posterior with BART’s non‑parametric posterior to handle nonlinear contexts.
Quick check question: How does the “lifted information ratio” bound \(E[\text{Regret}_T]\) in terms of mutual information \(I(\theta^*; H_T)\) for Gaussian rewards?

### Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) for Posterior Approximation
Why needed here: Exact sampling from the BART posterior is intractable; MCMC (Metropolis‑within‑Gibbs) provides an approximate posterior that can be refreshed in batches to keep computation feasible.
Quick check question: What are the trade‑offs between using many short MCMC batches versus fewer long batches in terms of posterior accuracy and runtime?

## Architecture Onboarding
#### Component map
Context stream -> History buffer -> MCMC refresher -> Posterior sample pool -> TS action selector -> Chosen arm -> Reward (feeds back to history)

#### Critical path
1. Observe context \(x_t\).
2. If within initial \(\tau K\) round‑robin steps, select predefined arm.
3. Else draw a posterior sample \(\theta\) uniformly from the current pool.
4. Compute predicted rewards \(\{f_\theta(x_t, a)\}_{a=1}^K\).
5. Select arm \(a_t = \arg\max_a f_\theta(x_t, a)\).
6. Observe reward \(r_t\), append \((x_t, a_t, r_t)\) to history.
7. Check if \(\lceil 8 \log t\rceil > \lceil 8 \log(t-1)\rceil\); if so, run cold‑start MCMC (\(n_{\text{burn}} + n_{\text{post}}\) iterations) on full history to create a new pool; otherwise continue.

#### Design tradeoffs
- **Separate vs. joint modeling**: Separate arms preserve posterior factorization needed for the \(\sqrt{T}\) bound but ignore potential information sharing across arms; joint encoding (one‑hot or multi‑) inflates input dimensionality, harms nonparametric rates, and empirically increases regret.
- **Refresh frequency**: Logarithmic schedule (default \(r(t)=\lceil 8 \log t\rceil\)) balances freshness and computational cost; static or too‑frequent refreshes lead to either stale posteriors or excessive runtime.
- **MCMC budget**: Increasing the number of trees \(m\) or iterations (\(n_{\text{burn}}\), \(n_{\text{post}}\)) improves posterior fidelity but yields diminishing returns on regret; \(m=100\), \(n_{\text{burn}}=n_{\text{post}}=500\) is a practical compromise.

#### Failure signatures
- **Poor MCMC mixing**: \(bR > 1.1\), acceptance rate \(< 0.1\), Policy \(\Delta\)-TV \(> 0.1\) at \(T=10000\).
- **Calibration drift**: 95% coverage consistently below 0.90 indicates underestimated uncertainty, likely causing premature exploitation.
- **Action encoding mismatch**: Using one‑hot instead of separate‑arm leads to significantly higher regret (as shown in sensitivity analysis).
- **Runtime escalation**: Cumulative time growing superlinearly if refresh schedule not properly batched.

#### First experiments
1. **Synthetic nonlinear benchmarks** (Friedman variants): Demonstrate BFTS’s superiority over linear and neural baselines; verify coverage near 0.94–0.95.
2. **OpenML classification bandits** (9 datasets): Show BFTS achieves best average rank (1.63) among all methods.
3. **Drink Less mHealth trial (OPE)**: Using replay OPE, confirm BFTS yields >30% engagement improvement over the original deployed policy.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
Question: How does the use of approximate MCMC (e.g., batched updates, limited mixing) affect the regret guarantees of BFTS in practice?
Basis in paper: The authors note in the conclusion that their analysis assumes perfect inference and call for studying how MCMC mixing rates interact with regret accumulation.
Why unresolved: Existing theory for approximate Thompson Sampling (e.g., via Particle Filters or Langevin) often requires strong assumptions on approximation quality; for BART‑MCMC specifically, no regret analysis exists.
What evidence would resolve it: A theoretical bound on the additional regret due to approximate posterior sampling, perhaps using Markov chain approximation tools; plus empirical diagnostics linking mixing metrics (e.g., ESS, bR) to regret degradation.

### Open Question 2
Question: Can scalable inference methods (e.g., variational approximations, GPU‑accelerated samplers) be integrated into BFTS without sacrificing the calibrated uncertainty needed for effective Thompson sampling?
Basis in paper: The conclusion explicitly lists “Scalable inference … integrating advances in methodology or software for accelerated BART inference” as an important direction.
Why unresolved: While fast BART approximations exist (e.g., StochTree, XBART), it is unclear whether they preserve the posterior properties (correct uncertainty, tail behavior) required for Thompson sampling performance.
What evidence would resolve it: An implementation of a scalable inference variant for BFTS, with theoretical justification (e.g., showing the approximation induces a pseudo‑posterior within a certain KL divergence from the exact one) and empirical demonstration of comparable regret and calibration on larger benchmarks.

### Open Question 3
Question: What is a principled approach for sharing information across arms in BART‑based bandits that enjoys both strong empirical performance and theoretical guarantees?
Basis in paper: The authors model arms independently to enable posterior factorization and a regret bound; they note that “designing arm pooling in a principled, theory‑supported manner for nonparametric bandits remains an open problem.”
Why unresolved: Joint modeling with action encodings inflates dimensionality and breaks the factorization used in the proof, while separate models ignore potentially useful statistical strength across arms. No satisfactory middle ground currently exists.
What evidence would resolve it: A new modeling framework (e.g., hierarchical BART, multi‑task priors) that allows information sharing yet still admits a bound on mutual information or decoupling coefficient; accompanied by a regret analysis and empirical superiority on sparse or low‑data regimes.

### Open Question 4
Question: How can BFTS be extended to handle non‑stationary reward functions, such as user habituation or drift in mobile health interventions?
Basis in paper: The conclusion states that the Bayesian framework is naturally extensible to non‑stationary settings but leaves the details open.
Why unresolved: Although Bayesian methods can incorporate forgetting (sliding windows, time‑varying priors), the resulting posterior approximations and exploration‑exploitation trade‑offs in a non‑stationary bandit environment are not well understood theoretically.
What evidence would resolve it: A non‑stationary contextual bandit algorithm built on BART (e.g., with exponential forgetting or change‑point detection), together with a dynamic regret analysis (e.g., competing with the best non‑stationary policy) and validation on synthetic drifting data and real mHealth trials.

### Open Question 5
Question: To what extent do the theoretical guarantees of BFTS depend on the i.i.d. context assumption and Gaussian noise? Can the results be extended to dependent contexts or heavy‑tailed noise with only sub‑Gaussian tails?
Basis in paper: The regret analysis relies on Assumptions (A1) Gaussian noise and (A2) i.i.d. contexts. The paper does not explore these relaxations.
Why unresolved: Standard Thompson Sampling analyses often crucially use i.i.d. contexts to bound mutual information; relaxing this may require new information‑theoretic tools. Heavy‑tailed noise could invalidate Gaussian‑based concentration.
What evidence would resolve it: Extension of the mutual information bound (Lemma 2) to mixing or stationary ergodic contexts; and/or derivation of regret bounds under sub‑Gaussian or sub‑exponential noise via martingale or peeling arguments. Empirical robustness tests on data with temporal dependence and heavy‑tailed rewards would also be informative.

## Limitations
- MCMC details omitted (proposal types, probabilities, tuning) may cause posterior bias and affect reproducibility.
- Drink Less context features and OPE update rule are ambiguous; the reported >30% engagement gain may be fragile under different implementations.
- Theoretical assumptions (BART posterior contraction, factorization) might not hold in practical scenarios, potentially degrading regret or calibration.
