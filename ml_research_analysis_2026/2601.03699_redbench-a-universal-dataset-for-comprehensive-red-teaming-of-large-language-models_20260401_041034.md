# RedBench: A Universal Dataset for Comprehensive Red Teaming of Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.03699
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03699
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: RedBench unifies 37 red-teaming benchmarks (29,362 samples) under a standardized taxonomy (22 risk categories, 19 domains) to enable systematic, reproducible evaluation of LLM vulnerabilities across attack and refusal scenarios.

## Executive Summary
RedBench addresses fragmentation in red-teaming evaluation by aggregating 37 existing benchmarks into a single, consistently categorized dataset covering 22 risk types and 19 domains. The resource includes both attack prompts (designed to elicit harmful outputs) and refusal prompts (designed to test over-defensiveness). Annotations were semi-automated using Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct with human oversight, achieving high agreement (84.68% risk category, 97.73% domain). Evaluations on six modern LLMs reveal open-source models are highly vulnerable to adversarial attacks (attack success rates up to 97.81%), while closed-source models show stronger resistance. The dataset is publicly released to standardize future red-teaming research and promote safer LLM deployment.

## Method Summary
The authors constructed RedBench by aggregating 33 attack datasets (e.g., HarmBench, AdvBench) and 4 refusal datasets (CoCoNot, ORBench, SGXSTest, XSTest). They developed a unified taxonomy mapping all samples to 22 risk categories and 19 domains. Annotation was performed semi-automatically: Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct generated initial labels, which were then reviewed and corrected by human annotators. Inter-annotator agreement was high. For evaluation, they tested six target models (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Gemma-2-9B-It, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Ministral-8B-Instruct-2410, GPT-4o-Mini, GPT-4.1-Nano) using four red-teaming methods (Direct, ZeroShot, HumanJailbreak, RainbowPlus) on the HarmBench subset. Attack success was judged by Llama-Guard-3-8B; refusal behavior was judged by GPT-4o. Inference used vLLM on NVIDIA A40 GPUs. The full dataset and code are released at https://github.com/knoveleng/redeval.

## Key Results
- RedBench provides a unified resource of 29,362 samples across 22 risk categories and 19 domains, covering both attack and refusal prompts.
- Open-source models exhibit high vulnerability to adversarial attacks, with attack success rates (ASR) reaching up to 97.81% using the RainbowPlus method; closed-source models show much lower ASR (e.g., 0.00% on HumanJailbreak).
- Refusal evaluations reveal divergent behaviors: Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct is over-defensive (average rejection rate 28.53%), while Gemma-2-9B-It is more permissive (13.46%).
- Coverage analysis indicates overrepresentation in Abusive Content and General Knowledge, suggesting imbalance and future expansion needs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
RedBench works by resolving three core deficiencies in prior red-teaming evaluation: inconsistent risk categorization, limited domain coverage, and outdated benchmarks. Its aggregation of 37 datasets into a single resource with a standardized taxonomy ensures consistent labeling across diverse harm types and contexts. Including both attack and refusal prompts enables a two-dimensional assessment of model safety (resistance to attacks vs. propensity to refuse safe requests). The semi-automated annotation pipeline leveraging a powerful LLM (Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct) with human oversight scales labeling while maintaining quality, as evidenced by high agreement scores. By providing a comprehensive, reproducible benchmark, RedBench facilitates apples-to-apples comparisons across models and red-teaming techniques, revealing clear performance gaps between open- and closed-source models.

## Foundational Learning
- **Red teaming**: The practice of intentionally probing LLMs with adversarial inputs to discover vulnerabilities. *Why needed*: Proactive identification of harmful behaviors is essential for safe deployment. *Quick check*: Can you design a prompt that causes an LLM to output disallowed content?
- **Risk taxonomy**: A hierarchical classification system for types of harm (e.g., hate speech, misinformation). *Why needed*: Enables consistent categorization and measurement across datasets. *Quick check*: Given a harmful scenario, assign it to the correct RedBench risk category.
- **Dataset aggregation**: Combining multiple benchmark datasets into a unified whole. *Why needed*: Overcomes fragmentation and provides broad coverage. *Quick check*: Name at least five datasets included in RedBench.
- **Semi-automated annotation**: Using an LLM to generate labels with human oversight. *Why needed*: Balances scalability with accuracy. *Quick check*: Why is human oversight necessary even when using a powerful LLM for labeling?
- **Attack Success Rate (ASR)**: Fraction of attack prompts that elicit a harmful response. *Why needed*: Primary metric for quantifying vulnerability to jailbreaks. *Quick check*: How is ASR calculated? (Number of harmful outputs / total attack prompts)
- **Rejection Rate (RR)**: Fraction of safe prompts that are refused by the model. *Why needed*: Measures over-defensiveness, which can degrade utility. *Quick check*: What does a high RR on a refusal benchmark indicate?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:  
  Data Sources (37 benchmarks) → Aggregation & Cleaning → Taxonomy Mapping (22 risk, 19 domains) → Semi-automated Annotation (Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct + human) → RedBench Dataset → Evaluation Pipeline (target models + judge models) → Metrics (ASR, RR)
- **Critical path**: The most resource-intensive steps are the semi-automated annotation (due to human review) and the evaluation with evolutionary red-teaming methods like RainbowPlus (computationally heavy). Dataset aggregation and taxonomy alignment are foundational but less time-consuming.
- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  *Scalability vs. quality*: Semi-automated annotation scales to ~30k samples but requires human oversight to correct errors.  
  *Breadth vs. depth*: Covering 19 domains and 22 risk categories provides breadth but may sacrifice depth in niche areas (as noted in coverage imbalance).  
  *Open vs. closed model evaluation*: Including both types enables comparisons but introduces API costs and rate limits for closed-source models.
- **Failure signatures**:  
  *Taxonomy misalignment*: Poor mapping from source datasets to RedBench categories can introduce inconsistency.  
  *Judge bias*: Llama-Guard-3-8B and GPT-4o may have their own biases, distorting ASR/RR.  
  *Data leakage*: If target models were trained on RedBench or similar data, evaluation results become overoptimistic.  
  *Resource exhaustion*: Running RainbowPlus or large models may exceed GPU memory without careful batching/quantization.
- **First three experiments**:  
  1. Verify dataset integrity: Download RedBench, confirm total samples (29,362), and check distribution across risk categories and domains.  
  2. Pilot attack evaluation: Run HarmBench Direct and ZeroShot methods on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct with a fixed seed (10 prompts); manually compute ASR and compare with reported values.  
  3. Assess refusal behavior: Evaluate Gemma-2-9B-It on a subset of CoCoNot; manually review a sample of outputs to see if refusals align with GPT-4o judgments.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: Why is there no universal dataset that provides consistent risk categorization and comprehensive evaluation in diverse domains?  
  *Basis*: Introduction identifies fragmentation as a critical gap.  
  *Why unresolved*: RedBench solves the immediate need, but sustaining a universal benchmark depends on community adoption and ongoing updates.  
  *Resolution evidence*: Track adoption metrics, update frequency, and dataset evolution over time.
- **Open Question 2**: Which risk categories and domains do existing datasets prioritize and how do they align with real-world LLM deployment scenarios?  
  *Basis*: Introduction notes prior work lacks systematic mapping to real-world harms.  
  *Why unresolved*: The paper maps coverage but does not correlate with actual incident reports or deployment frequencies.  
  *Resolution evidence*: Map LLM incident reports and use-case surveys to RedBench categories to assess alignment.
- **Open Question 3**: How do modern LLMs perform under red-teaming evaluations and what new insights can be gained from benchmarking their robustness?  
  *Basis*: Introduction states prior work evaluated older models; new insights require testing current models.  
  *Why unresolved*: The study tests only six models and four attack methods, leaving many architectures and techniques unexplored.  
  *Resolution evidence*: Evaluate a broad spectrum of state-of-the-art models with diverse red-teaming strategies across all RedBench subsets.

## Limitations
- Data accessibility: Exact download URL and storage layout are unspecified beyond the GitHub repository link.
- Inference settings: Hyperparameters (temperature, top-p, max_new_tokens, batch size) for target and judge models are unreported, affecting reproducibility.
- Evaluation scope: Unclear whether attack evaluation covers all 33 attack datasets in RedBench or only the HarmBench subset.
- Red-team configurations: Details of RainbowPlus (population size, mutation rate) are omitted, relying on external citation.
