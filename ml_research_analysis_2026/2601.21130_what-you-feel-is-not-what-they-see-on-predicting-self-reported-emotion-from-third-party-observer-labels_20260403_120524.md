# What You Feel Is Not What They See: On Predicting Self-Reported Emotion from Third-Party Observer Labels

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.21130
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.21130
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Models trained on third-party emotion labels fail to generalize to self-reported labels, except for valence when the spoken content is personally significant to the speaker (CCC ≈ 0.6–0.8); self-reported activation is nearly unpredictable (CCC ≈ 0).

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates a critical mismatch in automatic emotion recognition: models trained on labels from third-party observers (how emotion appears) perform poorly when tasked with predicting self-reported emotion (how emotion is felt). Through rigorous cross-corpus evaluation on speech datasets, the authors find that self-reported activation (arousal) is effectively unpredictable from such models, while valence (positivity/negativity) shows only moderate generalization. The key exception is when the speaker's content is personally significant—a factor that increases alignment between internal state and external expression, boosting valence prediction to high levels. Furthermore, combining multiple training datasets (e.g., acted and naturalistic) harms generalization to new, held-out corpora compared to training on a single, coherent dataset. The findings underscore that perceived and felt emotions are fundamentally different constructs, with personal significance being a crucial bridging factor for valence.

## Method Summary
The study employs a cross-corpus evaluation framework using three English speech datasets: MSP-Podcast (naturalistic, used for primary training), IEMOCAP (acted, some self-reports), and MuSE (naturalistic monologues with self-reports). Models include fine-tuned audio encoders (WavLM), text encoders (Longformer, ModernBERT), and large language models (GPT, Qwen). All emotion labels are continuous valence and activation dimensions scaled to [0,1]. Models are first trained on third-party labels from MSP-Podcast. For main experiments, these models are directly evaluated on self-report labels from MuSE and IEMOCAP without further fine-tuning. Personal significance for each utterance is estimated post-hoc using a fixed prompt with GPT-4-oss-20B, and model performance is stratified by significance score. The primary metric is the Concordance Correlation Coefficient (CCC).

## Key Results
- Models trained on third-party labels show a severe performance drop when predicting self-reported emotion, confirming a fundamental "perceiver-experiencer" gap.
- Self-reported activation is nearly unpredictably from any third-party-trained model (CCC ≈ 0), while valence shows modest cross-corpus predictability (CCC ≈ 0.3).
- For utterances with high estimated personal significance, third-party-trained models achieve high performance on valence self-reports (CCC ≈ 0.6–0.8), demonstrating that content significance aligns internal and external states.
- Mixing training data from different corpora (e.g., acted IEMOCAP with naturalistic MSP-Podcast) harms generalization to naturalistic self-reports compared to models trained on a single, naturalistic corpus.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Personal significance of content bridges the gap between third-party perception and self-reported emotion, specifically for valence.
**Mechanism:** Appraisal theory suggests that when an event is personally significant, the resulting emotional state is more intentionally or inadvertently expressed through observable cues (linguistic/paralinguistic). This makes the internal state more legible to an external observer and to a model trained on third-party labels.
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 5.3 show CCC ≈ 0.6–0.8 at highest significance levels; the paper's own GPT-based stratification analysis is primary evidence.
**Break condition:** Low-significance content (e.g., hypothetical, externally focused) yields near-chance model performance.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Valence is more cross-corpus predictable from third-party labels than activation because it is more reliably encoded in linguistic content, whereas activation is predominantly paralinguistic and highly speaker/context-dependent.
**Mechanism:** Valence (positive/negative) is often directly stated or implied in language (e.g., "thrilled" vs. "awful"), which LLMs capture robustly. Activation (arousal/calm) relies on prosody and subtle acoustic patterns that vary with speaking style, recording conditions, and cultural norms, making learned features brittle across corpora.
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 5.2 report near-zero CCC for activation self-reports but modest CCC for valence; divergence persists across audio and text modalities.
**Break condition:** When language is ambiguous or semantically neutral, valence predictability would degrade.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Mixing datasets from different sources harms cross-corpus generalization to self-reports due to fundamental distribution shifts in label meaning and emotional expression style.
**Mechanism:** Third-party and self-report labels have different semantics even for the same utterance. Datasets also differ in elicitation (acted vs. naturalistic) and label collection (momentary vs. retrospective). A model trained on a mixture learns features tied to specific label distributions and expression styles that do not transfer to a target corpus with a different emotional ecology.
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 5.1; Table 2 shows Mixed-trained WavLM on MuSE self-report (Val CCC=0.04) underperforms Podcast-trained (0.258) and LLMs (~0.34).
**Break condition:** Source and target corpora are highly similar in elicitation, label type, demographics, and acoustics.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Dimensional Emotion Model (Valence/Activation)**
  - **Why needed:** The entire analysis models emotion as continuous dimensions (valence: negative→positive; activation: calm→excited). This is essential to interpret CCC scores and the differential findings.
  - **Quick check:** Why might the same facial expression be high-activation to a third party but low-activation to the speaker? (Answer: The speaker's internal baseline or context dampens felt arousal despite expressive display.)

- **Concept: Self-Report vs. Third-Party (Perceived) Emotion Labels**
  - **Why needed:** This is the core distinction. Self-reports ground internal experience but are noisy/scarce; third-party labels are abundant but represent external perspective. Grasping this ontological gap is key.
  - **Quick check:** If a comedian says a sad line with a smile, which label captures the intended emotion and why? (Answer: Self-report, if the comedian *feels* amusement; third-party, if the observer *perceives* sadness from the words.)

- **Concept: Cross-Corpus Generalization**
  - **Why needed:** RQ1 tests whether a model trained on one dataset's labels works on another's. ML models are sensitive to dataset-specific biases (acoustics, annotation protocols, speaker populations).
  - **Quick check:** Name two reasons a model trained on acted IEMOCAP speech might fail on naturalistic podcast speech. (Answer: 1) Different acoustic conditions (microphone, environment); 2) Different emotional expression styles (exaggerated vs. spontaneous).)

- **Concept: Concordance Correlation Coefficient (CCC)**
  - **Why needed:** The primary metric; measures agreement between predicted and true continuous values, penalizing both bias and lack of correlation. Different from Pearson's r.
  - **Quick check:** When would CCC be low even if a model's predictions are perfectly linearly correlated with true labels? (Answer: If there is a systematic bias, i.e., the prediction line does not pass through the origin with slope 1.)

- **Concept: Personal Significance (Appraisal Theory)**
  - **Why needed:** The paper's key moderator. An event's relevance to one's goals/well-being increases emotional expressivity, aligning internal state with external cues.
  - **Quick check:** How could an utterance about a mundane daily task have low personal significance, affecting emotion predictability? (Answer: It is routine, goal-irrelevant, and evokes minimal emotional intensity, leading to subtle or ambiguous expressive cues.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**
Raw Audio -> WavLM (frozen CNN + fine-tuned transformers) -> Linear Head -> Valence/Activation Prediction
Text Transcript -> Longformer/ModernBERT (from scratch) -> Linear Head -> Valence/Activation Prediction
Text Prompt -> LLM (GPT, Qwen) -> Regression from Hidden States -> Valence/Activation Prediction
All -> CCC Loss -> AdamW Optimizer

**Critical path:**
1. **Data Preparation:** Load MSP-Podcast (train/dev/test), IEMOCAP, MuSE. Generate MuSE transcripts with Whisper. Min-Max scale all valence/activation labels to [0,1].
2. **Podcast-Training:** Freeze WavLM's CNN layer; fine-tune last 3 transformer layers on MSP-Podcast third-party labels. Train Longformer/ModernBERT from scratch on podcast transcripts. Select best model on podcast dev set.
3. **Mixed-Training:** Continue fine-tuning the best Podcast-trained model on *all* IEMOCAP self-report data (2 epochs).
4. **Evaluation:** For MuSE self-report (monologue-level), apply two strategies: (a) **Seg-Mono**: assign monologue label to each segment within it; (b) **Avg-Segs**: average segment predictions for monologue label. For IEMOCAP, use segment-level labels directly.
5. **Significance Stratification:** Use fixed prompt with gpt-oss-20B to score each MuSE/IEMOCAP segment for personal significance (1-5). Re-evaluate model performance within each significance bin.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Audio vs. Text:** WavLM (audio) is primary for activation prediction (per modality hypothesis) but sensitive to acoustic shifts. Text models (LLMs) often outperform on valence, especially for significant content, but lose prosodic cues.
- **Aggregation Strategy:** Seg-Mono provides more evaluation points but misaligns training (segment) and evaluation (monologue) resolutions. Avg-Segs aligns resolutions but reduces sample size and may smooth extreme predictions.
- **Training Data:** Podcast-trained (single naturalistic corpus) generalizes better to naturalistic MuSE self-reports. Mixed-trained (adding acted IEMOCAP) helps on IEMOCAP but severely hurts cross-corpus transfer to MuSE.

**Failure signatures:**
- **Activation Collapse:** CCC ≈ 0 for self-reported activation across all models and datasets.
- **Generalization Gap:** Large performance drop from third-party evaluation (e.g., WavLM on MuSE third-party Val CCC=0.42) to self-report evaluation (MuSE self-report Val CCC=0.258).
- **Mixed-Training Penalty:** For MuSE self-report, Mixed-trained WavLM Val CCC=0.04, far below Podcast-trained (0.258) and LLMs (~0.34).
- **Significance Sensitivity:** Performance for low-significance (1-2) samples is near zero; high-significance (4-5) samples show high CCC. A non-stratified model shows mediocre average performance masking this bimodal behavior.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Baseline Replication:** Train WavLM on MSP-Podcast third-party labels. Evaluate on MSP-Podcast test third-party labels to reproduce prior work (expected Act/Val CCC ~0.6-0.65). Confirms setup.
2. **Cross-Corpus Third-Party:** Take Podcast-trained models and evaluate on third-party labels for IEMOCAP and MuSE (Table 1). Establishes a ceiling for cross-corpus generalization when label type is consistent.
3. **Cross-Corpus Self-Report (MuSE):** Evaluate Podcast-trained WavLM/Longformer on MuSE self-reports using Seg-Mono and Avg-Segs. Expect dramatic drop from third-party results, especially for activation (CCC ~0). Validate RQ1 and RQ2. Follow by stratifying by significance to test RQ3.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can models trained on third-party labels generalize across corpora to predict self-reports?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Stated as RQ1 in the Introduction, with cross-corpus evaluation showing performance drops.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper demonstrates poor generalization but does not isolate contributing factors (e.g., acoustic mismatch, label distribution shifts, content differences).
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments varying one corpus characteristic at a time (e.g., matched acoustic conditions) or detailed error analysis by speaker/dataset attributes.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Are activation and valence equally predictable from self-reports, or does one generalize more reliably than the other?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] RQ2 in the Introduction, with results showing activation CCC≈0 and valence CCC≈0.3.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper documents the predictability gap but does not investigate the underlying mechanisms (e.g., activation's internal nature, modality effectiveness).
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablation studies controlling for content and modality, or collecting additional signals (e.g., physiological data) to probe activation's observability.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Do speaker-centered factors beyond personal significance determine when third-party models align with self-reports?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] RQ3 in the Introduction, focusing on personal significance from appraisal theory.
- **Why unresolved:** Only personal significance was evaluated; other appraisal dimensions (e.g., novelty, pleasantness) may also influence alignment but were not tested.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Annotate datasets for multiple appraisal factors and stratify model performance by each dimension.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How reliable are LLM-generated personal significance scores compared to human expert annotations?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Section 4 uses GPT for significance scoring, and the Conclusion notes future work should incorporate expert annotations.
- **Why unresolved:** Validity of LLM-based scoring is unverified; only 10% of GPT labels were manually reviewed for obvious errors.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Collect expert significance ratings on a shared subset; correlate with GPT scores and assess impact on model performance when using expert vs. LLM scores.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Why does fine-tuning on acted self-reports (IEMOCAP) degrade cross-corpus generalization to naturalistic self-reports (MuSE)?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Table 2 and Section 5.1 show Mixed-trained models underperform on MuSE compared to Podcast-trained.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper speculates about acted vs. naturalistic differences but lacks direct evidence linking dataset characteristics to the performance drop.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Compare feature distributions (audio/text) between IEMOCAP and MuSE; conduct domain adaptation experiments or train on matched/mismatched data with controlled content.

## Limitations
- Personal significance is operationalized via GPT-4 scoring without human-annotated ground truth; the causal mechanism (increased expressivity vs. rater bias) remains inferential.
- Near-zero CCC for self-reported activation may stem from intrinsic noisiness of self-reported arousal, model limitations, or both; the paper cannot disentangle these.
- The observed penalty for mixing datasets could be due to mismatched label semantics (self vs. third-party) or corpus-specific acoustic/linguistic shifts; the paper separates these only partially.
- Findings are limited to English speech and dimensional emotion models; generalizability to other languages, modalities, or discrete emotion categories is unknown.

## Confidence
- **Personal significance bridges the gap for valence:** Medium. Strong empirical correlation (stratified CCC 0.6–0.8), but mechanism relies on untested theoretical assumptions and proxy scoring.
- **Valence vs. activation divergence in predictability:** High. Consistent, large-magnitude performance gaps (CCC ~0.3 vs. ~0) across multiple models and datasets.
- **Mixing datasets hinders cross-corpus generalization:** Medium. Clear empirical penalty in Table 2, but causal attribution to label meaning vs. corpus style is not fully isolated.

## Next Checks
1. **Human-annotated significance validation:** Replace GPT-4 significance scores with human annotations on a subset; re-run stratification to test robustness of the significance-performance link.
2. **Test alternative emotion representations:** Evaluate using discrete emotion categories (e.g., Ekman) instead of dimensions; does personal significance improve generalization for specific categories?
3. **Controlled acoustic normalization:** Apply vocal tract length normalization or style transfer to reduce corpus-specific acoustic variation; does this improve mixed-training generalization for activation?
