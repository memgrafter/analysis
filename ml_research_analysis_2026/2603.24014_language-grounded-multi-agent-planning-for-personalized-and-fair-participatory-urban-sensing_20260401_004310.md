# Language-Grounded Multi-Agent Planning for Personalized and Fair Participatory Urban Sensing

## Quick Facts

- arXiv ID: 2603.24014
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24014
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: MAPUS achieves coverage utility on par with state-of-the-art baselines (e.g., 5.115 vs. 5.102 on T‑Drive Medium) while significantly improving path satisfaction (0.875 vs. 0.651) and fairness.

## Executive Summary

Participatory urban sensing harnesses crowdsourced data to monitor urban environments, yet conventional centralized planning often neglects participant heterogeneity and fairness, resulting in low satisfaction and imbalanced task distribution. MAPUS (Language-Grounded Multi-Agent Planning) addresses these gaps by introducing a multi-agent framework where participant agents leverage personal profiles and urban context to generate preference-aware routes, while a coordinator agent ensures equitable task allocation and mediates negotiation to minimize spatial overlap. The system integrates classical optimization (TSPTW) for baseline feasibility with LLM-driven iterative refinement for personalization, and employs a fairness-aware selection score that incorporates historical participation counts.

Experimental evaluation on two large-scale taxi trajectory datasets (T‑Drive and Grab‑Posi) demonstrates that MAPUS attains coverage utility comparable to leading baselines (e.g., 5.115 vs. 5.102 on T‑Drive Medium) while substantially boosting path satisfaction (0.875 vs. 0.651) and promoting more uniform participant selection. Ablation studies validate the necessity of all three core modules—Preference-aware Route Generation, Fairness-aware Participant Selection, and Negotiation-based Route Refinement—for balancing coverage, personalization, and fairness. Despite higher runtime due to LLM calls, MAPUS maintains stable performance, underscoring the viability of language-grounded multi-agent planning for urban sensing.

## Method Summary

MAPUS orchestrates a multi-agent system where each participant agent (PA) represents a potential contributor with a personal profile (preferences over land-use types, demographic attributes) and a schedule. For a given sensing task, the coordinator agent (CA) broadcasts the task specification (grid cells to cover, time horizon) and budget. PAs first check schedule feasibility; feasible ones invoke a classical Traveling Salesman Problem with Time Windows (TSPTW) solver to produce a baseline route that covers a subset of required cells within time constraints. Then, the PA enters a Preference-aware Route Generation (PRG) loop: an LLM proposes route adjustments by reasoning over the task, personal profile, and urban attribute data (land-use distribution, crime risk). Proposed routes are evaluated for feasibility (via TSPTW) and improvement in a Path Satisfaction Score (PSS), which measures alignment with preferences minus a risk penalty; accepted refinements replace the current route. After PRG, the CA collects all refined routes and performs Fairness-aware Participant Selection (FPS): it greedily selects participants up to budget, maximizing a composite score J(u) = β·f(Δϕ(u)) + (1−β)·e(F(u)), where Δϕ(u) is marginal coverage utility and F(u)=1/(1+h_u) is a fairness factor based on historical selection count h_u. Selected participants then undergo Negotiation-based Route Refinement (NRR) to reduce spatial overlap: the CA iteratively identifies the most overlapping pair and proposes adjusted routes (via LLM) that lower overlap while preserving feasibility and respecting preferences. PAs accept proposals only if they improve their PSS and remain feasible; negotiations continue until overlap falls below a threshold or a round limit is reached. Finally, the CA outputs the set of optimized routes.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Preference-aware Route Generation (PRG)

PRG allows participant agents to iteratively refine baseline routes using natural language reasoning over personal profiles and urban context, leading to higher path satisfaction. Concretely, after obtaining a feasible route from a classical TSPTW solver, an LLM proposes adjustments that consider task requirements, the participant's preference distribution over land-use categories, and grid-level attributes like land-use mix and crime risk. Refinements that are feasible and improve the Path Satisfaction Score (PSS) are accepted; invalid or non-improving proposals are discarded. The core assumption is that LLMs can interpret semantic preferences and contextual factors to make route adjustments that pure numerical optimization might overlook. Evidence from the paper includes the algorithm specification in Section 3.3 and the significant PSS gains observed (e.g., 0.875 vs. 0.651). Ablation studies (Figure 3) show that removing PRG dramatically lowers PSS while coverage remains similar. The approach may fail if the LLM consistently generates infeasible routes, if participant profiles are incomplete or misleading, or if the PSS utility function is poorly aligned with true preferences.

### Fairness-aware Participant Selection (FPS)

FPS balances coverage utility with long-term fairness by incorporating historical participation counts into the selection score. The coordinator agent selects participants under budget by greedily maximizing J(u) = β·f(Δϕ(u)) + (1−β)·e(F(u)), where Δϕ(u) is the marginal coverage gain and F(u)=1/(1+h_u) rewards less frequently chosen participants. This biases selection toward under-served participants while preserving overall coverage. The core assumption is that evenly distributing tasks across participants promotes sustainable, inclusive sensing. Evidence: Section 3.4 details the FPS algorithm and fairness score; Figure 4 shows that FPS yields a more uniform selection distribution than coverage-only greedy (UR, LS, CG) while maintaining comparable coverage and higher PSS. The method defaults to coverage-only at cold start (no historical data) and may be sensitive to the trade-off parameter β; overemphasis on fairness could reduce coverage, while too little may fail to achieve equity. Additionally, count-based fairness does not address demographic disparities.

### Negotiation-based Route Refinement (NRR)

NRR reduces spatial overlap among selected participants' routes through language-based negotiation, thereby improving coverage utility by avoiding redundant sensing. After selection, the coordinator computes pairwise overlap and iteratively selects the most overlapping pair. It proposes refined routes (via LLM) that lower overlap while preserving feasibility and respecting preferences. Each participant agent evaluates the proposal through its LLM; if both accept, routes are updated. The process repeats until overlap falls below a threshold or negotiation rounds are exhausted. The core assumption is that participants can be persuaded to adjust routes for collective benefit and that the coordinator can generate acceptable proposals. Evidence: Section 3.5 describes the NRR algorithm; Table 4 demonstrates overlap reduction (from 3.482% to 1.267% on T‑Drive Medium) and increased spatial entropy; coverage utility improves compared to no negotiation (ablation). The mechanism may falter if participants consistently reject proposals due to strong preferences or infeasible adjustments, or if overlap reduction does not directly translate to coverage gains (e.g., temporal separation might be more relevant).

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map (plain text):**  
TaskConfig → Coordinator Agent (CA) → Participant Agent (PA) → Classical Route Planner  
PA ↔ Urban Attribute Service  
CA ↔ Historical Selection Tracker  
CA ↔ Participant Profile Store (via PA profiles)  
CA ↔ PA (negotiation round messages)

**Critical path:**

1. Coordinator agent reads task configuration and broadcasts to all participant agents.
2. Each participant checks schedule feasibility; if feasible, calls the classical route planner to obtain a baseline route.
3. Participant enters the PRG loop: the LLM proposes route adjustments; if the proposal is feasible (validated by the planner) and improves the PSS-related utility, the route is updated; loop continues until max iterations or no improvement.
4. Coordinator collects accepted routes from participants, computes marginal coverage gains Δϕ(u) and fairness scores F(u) for each, and greedily selects participants up to the budget using the composite score J(u).
5. Coordinator computes pairwise spatial overlap among selected routes; for the most overlapping pair, it enters the NRR loop:
   a. Coordinator generates a refined route proposal (via LLM) aiming to reduce overlap.
   b. Each participant evaluates the proposal through its LLM, considering schedule, profile, and coordinator’s incentive; they accept only if the new route is feasible and improves their PSS.
   c. If both accept, routes are updated; otherwise, the coordinator collects feedback and revises the proposal.
   d. The process repeats for that pair up to K negotiation rounds or until overlap is acceptable.
   e. If the overlap threshold is not met after exhausting a maximum number of pairs, the process terminates.
6. Coordinator outputs the final set of selected participants and their refined routes.

**Design tradeoffs:**

- LLM stochasticity vs. deterministic baselines: MAPUS gains flexibility for semantic preferences and context but introduces variability and requires careful prompting to ensure feasibility.
- Decentralized generation vs. global optimization: Local autonomy improves satisfaction but can lead to suboptimal coverage; mitigated by negotiation.
- Fairness via historical count: Simple and effective for uniform distribution but ignores demographic equity and cold-start participants.
- Runtime overhead: LLM calls increase computational cost and latency, making the system suitable for offline planning but not real‑time operation.
- Profile fidelity: Synthetic profiles may not fully mimic real human decision‑making, potentially limiting external validity.

**Failure signatures:**

- Consistently low PSS: PRG ineffective due to LLM not adhering to preferences or mis‑specified utility.
- High coverage variance across runs: Indicative of LLM instability; may require multiple rollouts or more deterministic prompting.
- High negotiation rejection rate: Coordinator’s proposals are repeatedly rejected, suggesting poor proposal generation or overly strict participant constraints.
- Persistent selection imbalance despite FPS: β may be too low or historical counts not updated correctly.
- Excessive runtime exceeding practical limits: Could be due to large number of participants or too many LLM iterations.

## Limitations

- Reliance on synthetic participant profiles may not accurately capture real human preferences and behavior.
- LLM stochasticity introduces reproducibility challenges, variable performance, and high computational cost.
- Fairness defined solely by historical selection count overlooks other equity dimensions (e.g., demographic parity) and does not address cold‑start fairness.
