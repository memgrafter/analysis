# Spectral Archaeology: The Causal Topology of Model Evolution

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.03424  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03424  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** A single forward pass yields attention-graph spectra (λ2, smoothness, entropy) that fingerprint training regimes, revealing Passive-Triggered Connectivity Collapse (PTCC) in Phi-3/3.5 models where English passive voice causes a severe drop in algebraic connectivity localized to Layer 2, with spectral metrics strongly correlating (r=‑0.976) with performance and tracking tokenization density across scripts.

## Executive Summary
This work introduces a spectral archaeology framework that extracts high‑fidelity fingerprints of model training and internal processing from attention‑graph eigenvalues computed during a single forward pass. By measuring algebraic connectivity (λ2), smoothness, and entropy per layer, the authors uncover a phenomenon they term Passive‑Triggered Connectivity Collapse (PTCC) in the Phi‑3/3.5 family: English passive constructions induce a dramatic collapse in λ2 at Layer 2 (Δλ2 ≈ ‑0.76), which is only partially reversible (38% recovery) via steering. The method further identifies four recurrent processing strategies distinguishable by simple spectral rules, and demonstrates that λ2 systematically varies with tokenization density—logographic scripts yield high λ2 (~0.78) while alphabetic scripts yield low (~0.25), a relationship confirmed by script‑swap controls. A strong negative correlation (r=‑0.976) between spectral metrics and performance suggests that topology may serve as a practical audit tool for model evaluation and training verification.

## Method Summary
The core method constructs attention graphs for each transformer layer by treating tokens as nodes and using the sum of attention weights across heads as edge weights. From these graphs, spectral properties are derived: algebraic connectivity (λ2) indicating how well‑connected the graph is, smoothness reflecting the variance of attention, and entropy measuring the uncertainty of the attention distribution. These metrics are computed per layer from a single forward pass on a given input. By aggregating these metrics across diverse prompts and model families, the authors compare spectral fingerprints to reveal structural differences attributable to training data, architecture, and linguistic features.

## Key Results
- PTCC: English passive voice triggers a drastic drop in algebraic connectivity (Δλ2 ≈ ‑0.76) isolated to Layer 2 of Phi‑3/3.5 models; steering vectors restore only ~38% of the lost connectivity.
- Processing strategies: Four recurrent processing patterns are identified across the model family, and simple spectral rules achieve perfect separation of the families.
- Script‑density link: Tokenization density correlates with λ2 (logographic scripts ≈ 0.78, alphabetic ≈ 0.25); script‑swap experiments confirm the relationship holds for identical linguistic content. Spectral metrics also show a strong negative correlation (r=‑0.976) with downstream performance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Spectral properties of attention graphs capture how information flows between tokens during processing. Training data and architectural inductive biases shape these connectivity patterns over time. For instance, passive‑voice constructions reorder syntactic relationships, which may lead the attention mechanism in Layer 2 to disperse focus across a broader set of tokens, thereby reducing graph connectivity (λ2). The strong correlation with performance suggests that such topological measures reflect overall model coherence and efficiency. The partial restoration via steering indicates that the collapse is not a hard structural constraint but can be modulated by activating alternative patterns—still, the incomplete recovery hints at entrenched biases learned during training.

## Foundational Learning
**Attention Graph** – Graph representation where nodes are tokens and edge weights are summed attention scores. *Why needed:* Encodes relational dynamics between tokens; the basis for spectral analysis. *Quick check:* Visualize the attention matrix for a single head on a short sentence.  
**Algebraic Connectivity (λ2)** – The second‑smallest eigenvalue of the Laplacian matrix; measures how well‑connected a graph is. *Why needed:* Sensitive to bottlenecks in information flow; collapses indicate disrupted connectivity. *Quick check:* Compute λ2 for a fully connected vs. disconnected graph to see the difference.  
**Smoothness** – Variance of attention weights across tokens; higher values indicate more uniform attention. *Why needed:* Distinguishes focused vs. diffuse processing patterns. *Quick check:* Compare smoothness on a copy task (highly focused) vs. a summarization task.  
**Entropy** – Shannon entropy of the attention distribution per token. *Why needed:* Captures unpredictability of token interactions; high entropy may signal noisy or exploratory processing. *Quick check:* Compute entropy for a random‑attention baseline and for a trained model.  
**Tokenization Density** – Average number of tokens per linguistic unit (e.g., per word or concept). *Why needed:* Different writing systems produce different granularities, which affect attention graph sparsity. *Quick check:* Tokenize identical English sentences using different scripts (e.g., Chinese characters vs. Latin alphabet) and compare average token counts.  
**Steering** – Intervention that adds a vector to activations to probe causal effects. *Why needed:* Tests whether a spectral change is necessary for model behavior. *Quick check:* Apply a known steering vector (e.g., for sentiment) and observe output changes.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Input text -> Tokenizer -> Token embeddings -> Transformer blocks (Multi‑Head Attention -> LayerNorm -> MLP -> LayerNorm) repeated N times -> LM head -> Output logits  

**Critical path**  
The data flow passes through each transformer block sequentially; within each block, attention computes weighted sums of values based on token interactions, and the MLP applies a position‑wise transformation. The final LM head projects to vocabulary logits.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Layer depth vs. spectral stability: Deeper models may accumulate distortions, making late‑layer spectra more volatile.  
- Attention head count vs. spectral complexity: More heads increase expressive power but can blur per‑layer spectral signals.  
- Parameter efficiency vs. interpretability: Compact models (like Phi) may exhibit sharper spectral signatures, but scaling can dilute them.

**Failure signatures**  
- PTCC (abrupt λ2 drop in Layer 2 on passive voice) indicates a susceptibility to syntactic reordering.  
- Extremely high entropy in early layers may suggest training data mismatch or poor initialization.  
- Script‑swap inducing abnormal λ2 changes points to tokenization‑level bias.

**First three experiments**  
1. Reproduce PTCC: Compute Layer 2 λ2 for pairs of semantically matched active/passive sentences; verify Δλ2 ≈ ‑0.76.  
2. Script‑swap test: Translate a short English passage into Chinese, Japanese, and Korean; tokenize using respective scripts; measure λ2 per layer and compare to original alphabetic version.  
3. Steering recovery: Apply a steering vector trained to enhance connectivity at Layer 2 on passive sentences; quantify the fraction of λ2 recovered relative to active‑sentence baseline.

## Limitations
- The mechanistic cause of PTCC (syntactic, semantic, or data‑distribution artifact) remains unexplained.  
- Steering recovers only 38% of λ2; the factors limiting full recovery are unknown.  
- Generalizability of findings to non‑Phi architectures (e.g., Llama, Claude) has not been tested.

## Confidence
- PTCC existence and localization to Layer 2: **High**  
- Passive voice *causes* PTCC (vs. correlates): **Medium**  
- Script‑density relationship with λ2: **High**  
- Performance‑spectra correlation (r=‑0.976): **High**  
- Processing strategy separation rules (perfect discrimination in sample): **High** (generalizability **Medium**)

## Next Checks
1. **Architecture generality test:** Compute spectra for ≥ 3 non‑Phi model families (e.g., Llama‑3, Mistral, Claude) using identical passive/active prompt sets; check if PTCC recurs.  
2. **Synthetic syntax intervention:** Generate controlled sentence pairs differing only in voice (passive/active) with matched semantics; verify Δλ2 persists without lexical/semantic drift.  
3. **Longitudinal spectral tracking:** Record λ2 trajectories during training (multiple seeds) for models with/without passive‑heavy data; test if PTCC emergence predicts overfitting or downstream performance plateaus.
