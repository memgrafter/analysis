# Stochastic Dimension-Free Zeroth-Order Estimator for High-Dimensional and High-Order PINNs
## Quick Facts
**ArXiv ID:** 2603.24002  
**Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24002  
**Reference count:** 40  
**Primary result:** SDZE enables training of a 10‑million‑dimensional fully‑dense PINN on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU with orders‑of‑magnitude improvements in wall‑clock speed and peak memory efficiency over state‑of‑the‑art baselines.

## Executive Summary
Physics‑Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) provide a flexible way to solve partial differential equations but face the curse of dimensionality in both spatial derivative computation (cost O(d^k)) and backpropagation memory (O(P)). This paper presents the Stochastic Dimension‑Free Zeroth‑Order Estimator (SDZE), a meta‑optimizer that merges randomized spatial derivative estimation with zeroth‑order optimization. Its core innovations are Common Random Numbers Synchronization (CRNS), which cancels the O(1/ε²) variance explosion arising from double stochasticity, and an implicit matrix‑free subspace projection that reduces parameter‑side variance from O(P) to O(r) while keeping memory usage constant. Empirically, SDZE successfully trains a 10‑million‑parameter fully‑dense PINN on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU, achieving ~2100 iterations/sec and ~485 MB peak memory at 100 dimensions, outperforming all existing first‑order and naive zeroth‑order methods in both speed and memory. Ablations confirm the necessity of CRNS and the robustness to subspace rank choice. SDZE thus completes the missing piece for dimension‑independent PDE solvers, opening doors to extreme‑scale simulations in high‑dimensional finance and quantum many‑body physics.

## Method Summary
SDZE is a zeroth‑order optimizer that replaces backpropagation in any randomized spatial PINN solver. It constructs a low‑rank perturbation Δθ = α δ_t·(U_t Z_t V_t^⊤) implicitly: each layer ℓ has orthonormal bases U_t, V_t (refreshed lazily every F steps) and a Gaussian core Z_t∼𝒩(0,I_r). The finite‑difference gradient estimate δ_t = (L(θ+εΔθ) − L(θ−εΔθ))/(2ε) uses identical spatial random seeds for both losses (CRNS), causing the spatial truncation error to cancel exactly. The forward pass for a perturbation computes H_ℓ = σ(H_{ℓ‑1}W_ℓ ± ε (H_{ℓ‑1}U_t)Z_t V_t^⊤), requiring only O(1) operations per layer independent of d and derivative order k. Memory usage is O(1) relative to total parameters P because the global projection matrix is never materialized. Hyperparameters include subspace rank r, lazy frequency F, spatial batch size |B| and dimension sample |I| (with recommended |B|≈|I|), perturbation scale ε∝1/√q, and learning rate α_t typically annealed as O(1/√(t q)).

## Key Results
- Enabled training of a 10‑million‑dimensional fully‑dense PINN on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU; all first‑order and naive zeroth‑order baselines failed due to out‑of‑memory errors.
- At 100 dimensions, achieved ~2100 iterations/sec and ~485 MB peak memory, improving ~6% in speed and ~4.5% in memory over the best baseline (STDE+Adam: ~1974 it/s, ~507 MB).
- Ablation studies showed that disabling CRNS causes immediate NaN divergence, and that performance remains robust across a wide range of subspace ranks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Common Random Numbers Synchronization (CRNS)
- **Claim:** CRNS exactly cancels the O(1/ε²) variance explosion caused by the interplay between randomized spatial operators and zeroth‑order perturbations.
- **Mechanism:** By locking the spatial random seeds (i.e., the same set of sampled dimensions) across the two symmetric perturbations θ + εPz and θ − εPz, the spatial truncation noise field η_ω(θ) becomes identical in both forward passes and subtracts out algebraically in the finite‑difference numerator.
- **Core assumption:** Spatial random seeds can be synchronized perfectly; the spatial truncation noise is a smooth zero‑mean random field with bounded variance.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states “CRNS to algebraically cancel the O(1/ε²) variance…”; Section 4.3 guarantees exact algebraic cancellation; Table 3 ablation shows that independent seeds cause NaN divergence.
- **Break condition:** If spatial seeds are independent (ω⁺ ≠ ω⁻), variance blows up as ε → 0, leading to divergence.

### Mechanism 2: Implicit Matrix‑Free Subspace Projection
- **Claim:** SDZE reduces parameter exploration variance from O(P) to O(r) without storing an O(P × r) projection matrix by leveraging tensor associativity and lazy subspace updates.
- **Mechanism:** For each layer ℓ, random orthonormal bases U_t∈ℝ^{m_ℓ×r_ℓ} and V_t∈ℝ^{n_ℓ×r_ℓ} are generated via QR. The perturbation matrix ΔW_ℓ is never materialized; instead, the forward pass uses the contraction (H^{(ℓ‑1)}U_t) Z_t V_t^⊤, where H^{(ℓ‑1)} is the previous activation and Z_t∼𝒩(0,I_{r_ℓ}). The global projection matrix P_t is implicit; the parameter update is applied layer‑wise as ΔW_ℓ = α δ_t·(U_t Z_t V_t^⊤). Lazy updates (every F steps) reduce QR overhead.
- **Core assumption:** The low‑rank subspaces capture sufficient gradient directions; the lazy frequency F balances expressivity and computation.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract: “implicit matrix‑free subspace projection … reduce … variance from O(P) to O(r) while maintaining an O(1) optimizer memory footprint.” Section 4.2 describes the Kronecker‑isomorphic subspace and annealed lazy update. Section 4.4 notes that the physical dimension m_ℓ is instantaneously collapsed by the inner product (H^{(ℓ‑1)}U_t).
- **Break condition:** If r is too small, the subspace may not span useful gradient directions → stagnation; if F is too large, bases become stale → tracking failure.

### Mechanism 3: Unified Framework for Dual Dimension‑Independence
- **Claim:** SDZE achieves dimension‑independent complexity in both spatial derivative evaluation and memory overhead, enabling training of extreme‑scale (e.g., 10M‑dimensional) fully‑dense PINNs on a single GPU.
- **Mechanism:** SDZE acts as a meta‑optimizer that replaces first‑order updates in existing randomized spatial PINN solvers. It combines CRNS (to kill double‑stochastic variance) and implicit subspace projection (to avoid O(P) memory) into a fully forward‑only dynamical system. The underlying spatial estimator already amortizes spatial complexity to O(1); the ZO subspace provides O(1) memory relative to P.
- **Core assumption:** The spatial estimators are unbiased and already dimension‑free; SDZE adds the parameter‑side independence.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract: “SDZE … achieves dimension‑independent complexity in both space and memory.” Section 1: “SDZE … completes the final missing piece of dimension‑independent PDE solvers.” Section 6.4: empirical scaling to 10M‑dimensional PINNs with orders‑of‑magnitude improvements.
- **Break condition:** For extremely stiff PDEs or highly non‑smooth solutions, spatial variance may remain problematic; overly restrictive low‑rank subspaces may degrade accuracy.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
SDZE consists of: (1) layer‑wise random orthonormal bases U_t, V_t; (2) a lazy update scheduler (frequency F); (3) a CRNS seed manager; (4) an implicit forward pass that computes (H^{(ℓ‑1)}U_t) Z_t V_t^⊤; (5) a finite‑difference controller (ε). Data flow: θ → generate z_t → compute θ⁺ and θ⁻ via implicit forward → compute spatial loss with identical seeds → δ_t → update θ layer‑wise: W_ℓ ← W_ℓ − α_t δ_t·(U_t Z_t V_t^⊤).

**Critical Path:**  
At each optimization step t:  
(a) If t mod F = 0, refresh U_t and V_t for each layer via QR decomposition of Gaussian matrices.  
(b) Draw z_t ∼ 𝒩(0, I_q) and split into layer‑wise blocks Z_t^{(ℓ)}.  
(c) For each sign s∈{+, −}, run the forward pass: for ℓ=1..L, compute H_{ℓ}^{(s)} = σ( H_{ℓ-1}^{(s)} W_ℓ + s ε (H_{ℓ-1}^{(s)} U_t^{(ℓ)}) Z_t^{(ℓ)} V_t^{(ℓ)⊤} ).  
(d) Compute loss L_s using a spatial estimator (e.g., STDE) with identical sampled dimension indices I, J for both s.  
(e) Compute δ_t = (L⁺ − L⁻)/(2ε).  
(f) Update each layer: W_ℓ ← W_ℓ − α_t δ_t·(U_t^{(ℓ)} Z_t^{(ℓ)} V_t^{(ℓ)⊤}).

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- **Subspace rank r:** Larger r lowers gradient variance but increases per‑iteration compute and memory (O(∑ m_ℓ r + n_ℓ r)). Smaller r improves speed but may cause stagnation.  
- **Lazy update frequency F:** Larger F reduces QR overhead but makes bases stale, potentially harming convergence; smaller F keeps bases adaptive but increases cost.  
- **Spatial batch sizes:** Collocation batch size |B| and dimension sample size |I| both contribute memory O(|B|·|I|). Parallel efficiency favors |B|≈|I|; too small |I| increases spatial variance.  
- **Perturbation scale ε:** Theory suggests ε ∝ 1/√q; too large harms finite‑difference accuracy, too small amplifies noise.  
- **Learning rate α_t:** Often annealed as O(1/√(t q)) to counter ZO variance.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- **NaN loss immediately:** Likely due to missing CRNS (spatial seeds differ) or ε too small.  
- **Out‑of‑memory (OOM)** errors: Usually caused by inadvertently materializing the full perturbation matrix or the global projection; ensure implicit formulation.  
- **Very slow convergence or plateau:** Subspace rank r too small, lazy F too large, or α_t too low.  
- **Poor final accuracy:** Increase r or |I|, check that the spatial estimator’s bias is acceptable for the PDE.

**First 3 Experiments:**  
1. **Baseline sanity check:** Train a small (e.g., 10‑D) Allen‑Cahn PINN with SDZE and compare loss curve and final L² error against a first‑order baseline (e.g., STDE+Adam). Expect comparable accuracy with lower memory usage.  
2. **CRNS ablation:** Disable CRNS (use independent spatial seeds for θ⁺ and θ⁻) on a moderate 100‑D Allen‑Cahn. Observe rapid divergence to NaN within a few iterations, confirming CRNS necessity.  
3. **Subspace scaling:** For a fixed 1 000‑D Allen‑Cahn, sweep subspace rank r ∈ {8, 16, 32, 64, 128} while holding other hyperparameters constant. Measure iterations to reach target loss and per‑iteration time. Expect a clear elbow; very small r stagnates, very large r yields diminishing returns.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Adaptive step size:** Can adaptive step size schemes accelerate SDZE convergence while maintaining stability? The paper notes that fixed/annealed rates may be suboptimal in non‑uniform PINN landscapes and calls for empirical and theoretical studies (e.g., AdaGrad‑style variance normalization).

**Variance reduction:** How can variance reduction techniques (e.g., control variates, antithetic sampling) be integrated to improve sample efficiency? The current gradient estimates retain spatial and ZO noise; the paper did not explore such techniques.

**Optimal batch allocation:** What is the optimal trade‑off between spatial batch size |I| and residual point batch size |B| under a fixed memory budget? Observations show lower randomization batch sizes improve speed and memory, but the theoretical variance‑optimal allocation is unknown.

**Extension to quantum/finance:** Can SDZE be applied to many‑body Schrödinger equations or high‑dimensional Black‑Scholes? The paper suggests these domains but provides no validation.

**Asymptotic sample complexity:** Under what conditions does ZO optimization with SDZE have finite‑time advantages or disadvantages compared to first‑order methods? While wall‑clock speedups are demonstrated, the relative sample complexity for reaching ε‑accuracy depends on subspace dimension q, physical dimension d, PDE smoothness, and FO memory constraints; a finite‑time analysis is lacking.

## Limitations
- CRNS seeding details and hyperparameter values are vaguely described, hindering exact replication.  
- Theoretical assumptions (e.g., smoothness of the solution, bounded truncation error) lack rigorous validation for arbitrary PDEs.  
- Generalization beyond the Allen‑Cahn equation, especially to stiff or discontinuous problems, remains uncertain.
