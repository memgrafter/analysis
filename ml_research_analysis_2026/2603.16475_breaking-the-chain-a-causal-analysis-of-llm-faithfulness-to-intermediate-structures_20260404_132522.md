# Breaking the Chain: A Causal Analysis of LLM Faithfulness to Intermediate Structures

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.16475
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16475
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** LLMs show high self-consistency with their own intermediate reasoning structures but substantial drops in faithfulness under controlled edits (average gap Δ≈0.27), indicating intermediates act as influential context rather than stable causal mediators.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether large language models' intermediate reasoning structures (e.g., rubrics, checklists) causally determine final outputs or merely correlate with them. The authors introduce a causal intervention protocol: on tasks where a deterministic function maps intermediate structures to decisions, they edit the model-generated mediator and check if the output updates accordingly. Across eight models and three benchmarks, models exhibit high self-consistency (FID up to 0.93) but significant faithfulness drops under intervention (FStrong lower by up to 0.64). Delegating the final decision to an external tool nearly eliminates the gap (residual Δ≤0.03), while stronger prompts yield minimal improvement. Models also show directional asymmetry, being more sensitive to counterfactual than corrective edits. The findings reveal that apparent faithfulness is fragile and that intermediate structures function as influential context rather than stable causal mediators.

## Method Summary
The evaluation protocol treats the intermediate structure as a causal mediator. For each instance: (1) the model generates a mediator (ˆM) and decision (ˆY) from input X; (2) a controlled intervention I(ˆM) produces a modified mediator M* with a unique target Y* = C(M*), where C is a deterministic evaluator; (3) the model is re-prompted with (X, M*) to produce a new decision Y*'; (4) faithfulness is measured via FID (proportion where C(ˆM)=ˆY) and FStrong (proportion where both C(ˆM)=ˆY and C(M*)=Y*). The gap Δ = FID – FStrong quantifies fragility. Interventions are of two types: Correction (COR) replaces an incorrect ˆM with gold M, while Counterfactual (CNF) flips a correct element in a correct ˆM. Variations test externalizing C as a tool call and increasing prompt strength.

## Key Results
- Models exhibit high self-consistency (FID up to 0.93) but substantial faithfulness drops under intervention, with average gap Δ≈0.27 and up to 0.64.
- Externalizing the deterministic computation as a tool nearly eliminates the gap (residual Δ ≤ 0.03), pointing to computational difficulty.
- Stronger prompts yield minimal improvement in FStrong (average gain 0.01–0.05), ruling out instruction ambiguity as the primary cause.
- Models show directional asymmetry: they are more faithful to counterfactual interventions (removing correct elements) than corrective ones (fixing incorrect elements).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Causal Intervention Protocol
- **Claim**: The paper evaluates faithfulness by treating the intermediate structure as a causal mediator and applying controlled interventions.
- **Mechanism**: For tasks where a deterministic function C maps M to Y, the protocol: (1) has the model generate (ˆM, ˆY) from X; (2) applies an intervention I to produce M*; (3) computes target Y* = C(M*); (4) re-prompts the model with M* to get Y*'. Faithfulness requires Y*' = Y*.
- **Core assumption**: C is deterministic and each M* uniquely determines Y*.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "by selecting tasks where a deterministic function maps intermediate structures to decisions, every controlled edit implies a unique correct output."
  - [section 3.1/Algorithm 1]: Formalizes the evaluation procedure.
  - [corpus]: Neighbors such as "Making Reasoning Matter" and "C2-Faith" also employ intervention-based faithfulness evaluation.
- **Break condition**: If C is not truly deterministic or interventions alter multiple aspects of M, the causal test becomes ambiguous.

### Mechanism 2: Self-consistency Gap as Unfaithfulness Indicator
- **Claim**: High self-consistency (FID) does not guarantee causal mediation; the gap Δ = FID – FStrong quantifies fragility.
- **Mechanism**: FID measures C(ˆM)=ˆY (model’s own mediator matches decision). FStrong additionally requires C(M*)=Y* after intervention. A positive Δ reveals cases where initial consistency fails when M is edited.
- **Core assumption**: The drop from FID to FStrong indicates the model relies on pathways bypassing M (e.g., direct X→Y or internal knowledge).
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "models show high self-consistency (FID up to 0.93) but substantial drops in faithfulness under intervention (FStrong lower by up to 0.64, average gap Δ≈0.27)."
  - [section 5, Table 2]: Positive Δ across all 24 model–dataset pairs.
  - [corpus]: "Measuring the Faithfulness of Thinking Drafts" finds similar fragility in reasoning drafts.
- **Break condition**: If the intervention itself causes confusion rather than causal bypass, Δ may overestimate unfaithfulness.

### Mechanism 3: Computational Difficulty vs. Instruction Ambiguity
- **Claim**: The faithfulness gap stems primarily from the model’s inability to execute the deterministic mapping C internally; externalizing C as a tool nearly closes the gap, while stronger prompts yield minimal improvement.
- **Mechanism**: In tool-externalized mode, the model passes M* as an argument to a tool that computes C exactly. This removes the need for in-context evaluation. The near-zero residual gap indicates computational limits in the standard setting. Prompt-strength ablations (Detailed, Max Detailed) show only small gains, ruling out instruction ambiguity as the main cause.
- **Core assumption**: The tool accurately implements C, and internal computation of C is the primary bottleneck.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 6]: "When derivation of the final decision from the structure is delegated to an external tool, this fragility largely disappears; however, prompts which ask to prioritize the intermediate structure over the original input do not materially close the gap."
  - [section 6.1, Figure 4]: Residual gaps Δ ≤ 0.03 under tool use across most models.
  - [section 7]: Max Detailed improves FStrong by only 0.01–0.05 on average.
  - [corpus]: "Beyond Correctness" demonstrates tool use improves faithful reasoning in RAG.
- **Break condition**: If tool calls are frequently malformed or the tool implementation deviates from C, the comparison may be invalid.

## Foundational Learning
- **Causal mediation and intervention**  
  **Why needed here**: The paper frames faithfulness as a causal mediation problem; intervening on M tests whether X→Y flows through M.  
  **Quick check question**: If M is a faithful mediator, what should happen to Y when we fix X and change M? (Answer: Y should change to match C(M).)  
  **Assumption**: The intervention affects only M, not other paths from X to Y.

- **Self-consistency vs. causal faithfulness**  
  **Why needed here**: Distinguishes correlation (ˆY matches C(ˆM)) from causation (Y updates with M*). Prevents mistaking initial alignment for reliable mediator use.  
  **Quick check question**: Can FID = 1.0 while FStrong < 1.0? Yes — model always agrees with its own mediator initially but often ignores edited mediators.  
  **Assumption**: The drop reflects true bypass rather than intervention-induced confusion.

- **Deterministic evaluator C**  
  **Why needed here**: Provides unambiguous targets for intervention; every edit to M yields a single correct Y*.  
  **Quick check question**: Why must C be deterministic? If C were stochastic, the same M* could imply multiple Y*, making faithfulness assessment ambiguous.  
  **Assumption**: C is a correct and complete specification of the decision rule.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:  
  Input X -> Model pθ -> Mediator ˆM -> Deterministic evaluator C (applied to ˆM) -> Decision ˆY  
  Intervention: I(ˆM) -> M* -> C(M*) = Y* -> Re-prompt pθ(X, M*) -> Y*'  
  Tool mode: pθ outputs tool call with M* as argument -> Tool returns C(M*) directly

- **Critical path** (per dataset instance):
  1. Generate (ˆM, ˆY) from pθ(X).
  2. Determine intervention type: if ˆM incorrect → COR (apply gold correction); if correct → CNF (apply local edit). Compute M* = I(ˆM) and target Y* = C(M*).
  3. Construct intervention prompt with M* and query pθ for Y*' (or tool call).
  4. Evaluate metrics using (ˆY, Y*, Y*').
  5. For tool mode, verify tool argument matches M* and tool output equals C(M*).

- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - **Dataset choice**: Requires deterministic C for controlled interventions → may not generalize to open-ended domains.
  - **Mediator source**: Using model-generated ˆM tests real behavior but introduces generation noise.
  - **Intervention scenarios**: COR tests error correction; CNF tests counterfactual sensitivity. Both needed to expose different failure modes.
  - **Tool vs. in-context**: Tool externalization removes computational confound but requires correct formatting; in-context evaluation is harder for the model but simpler to implement.
  - **Prompt engineering**: Stronger instructions test whether ambiguity causes unfaithfulness, but may not override ingrained behaviors.

- **Failure signatures**:
  - High Δ: Model ignores M* and repeats original decision (bypass).
  - Low FID: Model’s own M and Y inconsistent (internal confusion).
  - Tool mode mismatch: Tool argument ≠ M* even if Y*' correct.
  - Refusal or confusion: Model declines to follow intervention (e.g., reverts to original reasoning).
  - C errors: Incorrect implementation of deterministic evaluator.
  - Overly broad interventions: Changing multiple mediator elements makes Y* ambiguous.

- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Baseline faithfulness study**: Run standard protocol across all models and datasets; compute FID, FStrong, Δ (answers RQ1).
  2. **Tool externalization study**: Repeat with tool calls; compare Δ to baseline (answers RQ2).
  3. **Prompt strength study**: Test Standard / Detailed / Max Detailed instructions; measure changes in FStrong (answers RQ3).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Why do LLMs exhibit a systematic gap between self-consistency and strong faithfulness, indicating apparent but fragile reliance on intermediate structures?
- **Basis in paper:** The core finding is a consistent dissociation: FID exceeds FStrong across all models and datasets. The authors conclude intermediates act as "influential context rather than stable causal mediators," but do not diagnose the underlying mechanism.
- **Why unresolved:** The causal intervention protocol measures the *effect* but not the *cause*. The paper rules out prompt ambiguity and isolates computational difficulty, yet cannot disentangle whether bypass stems from architectural inductive biases, training objectives, or inherent uncertainty.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Follow-up experiments that probe internal representations during interventions, vary the determinism/complexity of C(M), or compare models trained with explicit mediator-supervision.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What explains the directional asymmetry in intervention sensitivity, where models are more responsive to counterfactual edits (removing correct elements) than corrective edits (fixing incorrect elements)?
- **Basis in paper:** Figure 3 shows most models above the diagonal, indicating stronger faithfulness to counterfactual interventions. The effect is consistent in direction for some models but offers no causal explanation.
- **Why unresolved:** Asymmetry contradicts a symmetric causal mediation model. Potential explanations (loss-function asymmetries, prior biases) are not explored. The design may confound direction with initial error patterns.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A balanced design where the same initial mediator state is subjected to both counterfactual and corrective interventions, controlling for change magnitude. Analyzing model confidence on corrected vs. counterfactual targets could reveal reluctance to override a previously generated mediator.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can the causal faithfulness evaluation framework be extended to tasks or domains where gold-standard, deterministic mediators (C(M)) are not available, such as open-ended reasoning or creative generation?
- **Basis in paper:** The methodology relies on datasets with explicit, gold-annotated intermediate structures and a deterministic evaluator C. The Limitations section explicitly states this is not available in many real-world datasets.
- **Why unresolved:** Most real-world LLM applications lack such gold mediators and deterministic outcomes. Without them, the intervention protocol cannot define unique counterfactual targets. The paper does not propose proxy methods for approximating C.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Develop and validate a generalized protocol where C is approximated (e.g., via a separate expert model) and test whether unfaithfulness gaps persist when C is noisy. Alternatively, design annotation schemes for complex tasks.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** To what extent does the near-elimination of the faithfulness gap under tool externalization reflect a fundamental limitation of autoregressive in-context computation versus a specific architectural shortcoming of current LLMs?
- **Basis in paper:** Case Study 2 shows externalizing C as a tool call reduces Δ to ≤0.03, whereas in-context execution suffers large gaps. Authors attribute this to "difficulty executing C in context" but do not isolate the specific limitation.
- **Why unresolved:** Tool use offloads computation but also changes the interface: the model must only encode the mediator correctly for the tool call, not perform the computation. The remaining small residual gaps hint at deeper issues, but the experiment cannot separate "cannot compute C" from "cannot format for tool use." It also leaves open whether future architectures would close the gap without tools.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Compare in-context execution of C with tool use on mediators of graduated complexity to pinpoint the scaling limit of in-context computation. Evaluate models with native tool-use capabilities or fine-tuned for function-calling to see if gap closure is specific to prompting.

## Limitations
- The framework requires datasets with gold-structured mediators and a deterministic evaluator C, limiting generalizability to open-ended domains.
- Interventions may inadvertently alter X→Y pathways or introduce instruction confusion, potentially confounding the measured gap.
- Tool externalization introduces formatting demands and reliance on correct tool call syntax, which may affect results independently of causal mediation.

## Confidence
- **Causal protocol validity**: High (aligned with established intervention methodology).
- **Self-consistency vs. faithfulness gap**: Medium (empirically robust but confounded by potential intervention-induced confusion).
- **Tool externalization nearly closes gap**: High (effect size large; residual Δ ≤ 0.03 suggests computational bottleneck).
- **Prompt strength minimal improvement**: Medium (small gains observed, but may vary with model scale or prompt optimization).
- **Directional asymmetry (CNF > COR sensitivity)**: Medium (consistent trend but dataset-specific flip rules may contribute).

## Next Checks
1. **Intervention Integrity Audit**: For a random subset, verify that M* differs from ˆM only in the intended single flipped element (for CNF) or uses exact gold mediator (for COR). Confirm C(M*) yields a different Y* than C(ˆM).
2. **Tool Call Parsing Stress Test**: Systematically vary formatting of tool invocation (JSON vs. plain text, argument order) to ensure observed near-zero Δ is robust to syntax.
3. **Confusion Control Condition**: Run a "null intervention" where M* is identical to ˆM but re-prompted. If FStrong drops significantly here, it indicates baseline re-prompting instability; otherwise, Δ more reliably reflects causal bypass.
