# Standard Acquisition Is Sufficient for Asynchronous Bayesian Optimization
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.13501
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13501
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Standard acquisition functions (UCB, Expected Improvement) are theoretically sound and empirically superior for asynchronous Bayesian optimization, matching or outperforming specialized async methods.

## Executive Summary
This paper challenges the common belief that asynchronous Bayesian optimization (BO) requires specialized acquisition functions to avoid redundant queries when pending evaluations are unknown. It shows that standard UCB enjoys Bayes simple regret bounds asymptotically equivalent to sequential Thompson sampling, and that penalization-based async methods (e.g., LP-UCB) are approximations of Kriging Believer and may over-explore. Crucially, updating the Gaussian Process surrogate after each completed evaluation naturally shifts the acquisition optimum away from busy points, providing implicit diversity. Empirical evaluations on synthetic functions (5–100 dimensions) and real-world tasks with up to 20 workers confirm that standard UCB and Expected Improvement perform on par with or better than all tested purpose-built asynchronous methods. The work suggests that existing approaches may be over-engineered and that simple, standard BO pipelines can be highly effective in asynchronous settings.

## Method Summary
The study focuses on asynchronous BO with $q$ parallel workers, where the algorithm selects a new query as soon as any worker finishes, without knowledge of other pending evaluations. The proposed simple approach uses a Gaussian Process (GP) surrogate; upon each completion, the GP is refitted with the new datapoint, and then a standard acquisition—Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) with fixed $\beta=2$ or log Expected Improvement (LogEI)—is maximized to select the next query, with no explicit handling of busy locations. This is contrasted with specialized asynchronous methods: Thompson Sampling (TS), penalization-based UCB variants (LP-UCB, LLP-UCB), Kriging Believer (KB-UCB), expected LogEI (E-LogEI), and AEGIS. All methods are implemented within a shared framework (BoTorch/GPyTorch) using ARD-RBF kernels, marginal likelihood hyperparameter optimization, and multi-start L-BFGS-B for acquisition optimization. Experiments span synthetic functions (Ackley, Hartmann, etc.) across dimensions $d=5$ to $d=100$, and real-world tasks: PROTEUS, Pest Control, XG-Boost, and CNN hyperparameter tuning.

## Key Results
- **Theoretical:** Asynchronous UCB achieves a Bayes simple regret bound that asymptotically matches sequential Thompson sampling; the bound contains a constant term $\xi_q$ (due to pending evaluations) that does not grow with $n$, while the dominant information gain term is identical to the sequential case.
- **Analytical:** For UCB, the expected acquisition conditioning on pending evaluations equals Kriging Believer; penalization methods (LP/LLP) are distinct approximations that can misrepresent the expected acquisition landscape.
- **Empirical:** Across synthetic functions ($d=5$–$100$, $q=2$–$20$) and four real-world tasks, standard UCB and LogEI consistently achieve lower or equal log simple regret compared to purpose-built async methods. Query-distance analysis shows standard methods do not systematically repeat nearby queries and mirror the exploration-exploitation trade-off of sequential optimals, while penalization methods often over-explore.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Intermediate posterior updates drive diversity.** Each time a worker completes, the GP is immediately refitted with the new datapoint. This posterior update changes both the mean (shifting toward the new point) and the variance (reducing uncertainty nearby, and possibly globally via lengthscale adaptation). The acquisition is then re-optimized under this updated posterior, which naturally moves its optimum away from the just-queried point and other busy points, without explicit penalization. The key assumption is that new data provide non-negligible information. Evidence: Figure 3 visualizes the UCB curve shift after a posterior update, and the abstract notes that existing works neglect these intermediate updates, which are found sufficient to avoid redundancy.
**Mechanism 2: Regret bound matches sequential TS.** The Bayes simple regret bound for asynchronous UCB (Theorem 2) is of order $\sqrt{\Psi_n/n}$ plus $\sqrt{\xi_q \beta_n \Psi_n / n}$, where $\Psi_n$ is the maximum information gain (sublinear for common kernels) and $\xi_q$ bounds missing information from pending evaluations. Because $\xi_q$ is independent of $n$ and can be made small via uncertainty-based initialization, the asymptotic rate matches that of sequential Thompson sampling (Theorem 4). This holds under standard GP assumptions (bounded RKHS norm, bounded kernel) and with an appropriate $\beta$ schedule (e.g., $\beta_j = O(\log j)$); the paper notes that using a constant $\beta=2$ works well in practice but does not satisfy the theorem’s conditions.
**Mechanism 3: Penalization methods may over-explore.** LP-UCB and LLP-UCB multiply the base acquisition by penalizers derived from a global Lipschitz constant $L$ and current best estimate $y_n^*$. Proposition 1 shows that for UCB, the true expected acquisition (averaging over pending $y$) equals Kriging Believer (KB-UCB); LP-UCB is a different, coarse approximation. If $L$ is underestimated—as can happen with long learned lengthscales—the exclusion cones become too wide, forcing queries into distant, less promising regions and sacrificing exploitation. Empirically, Figure 4 and Figure 8 show LP-UCB over-exploring and querying farther from busy points than standard methods or sequential baselines.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Gaussian Process Posterior Update**  
  *Why needed here:* The argument hinges on how adding one datapoint changes the GP’s mean ($\mu$) and variance ($\sigma$). Understanding this local (mean shift) and global (lengthscale adaptation) effect explains why the acquisition optimum moves.  
  *Quick check question:* If you add a new observation at $x_{\text{new}}$ with value $y_{\text{new}}$, what happens to $\mu(x)$ and $\sigma(x)$ exactly at $x_{\text{new}}$? How about at a point $x_{\text{far}}$ where $x_{\text{far}}$ is distant from all data?
- **Concept: Acquisition Function (UCB/EI)**  
  *Why needed here:* These are the decision engines. UCB’s balance via $\beta$ and EI’s reliance on $y_n^*$ must be understood to see why their optima shift with posterior updates.  
  *Quick check question:* For UCB $\alpha(x)=\mu(x)+\sqrt{\beta}\sigma(x)$, if $\sigma(x)$ collapses at a location due to a new nearby point, how does $\alpha(x)$ at that location change relative to a distant point with high $\sigma$?
- **Concept: Asynchronous vs. Synchronous/Batch BO**  
  *Why needed here:* The core problem is making a decision with incomplete information ($q-1$ pending $y$ values). The paper’s thesis is that the intermediate update resolves this incompleteness for the next decision.  
  *Quick check question:* In synchronous batch BO (selecting all $q$ points at once), you must account for all $q$ pending evaluations simultaneously. How is the async setting (selecting one, waiting for one) different in terms of information availability at decision time?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Global shared state (dataset $D_n$, busy set $B$) and $q$ worker processes. Each worker executes: wait for a completed query, read/append to $D_n$, refit the GP surrogate, maximize acquisition to get new $x$, add to $B$, and launch the query. The GP (ARD‑RBF kernel) and acquisition optimizer (multi‑start L‑BFGS‑B) are stateless utilities.
**Critical path**:
1. Initialization: $D_0$ gets $3d$ quasi‑random (Halton) points; $q$ workers start on random points.
2. Async loop (triggered by a worker completion):
   - Lock, append $(x_{\text{finished}}, y_{\text{finished}})$ to $D_n$, remove from $B$, unlock.
   - Refit GP on $D_n$.
   - Compute new query $x' = \argmax \alpha(x|D_n)$ (using standard or async acquisition).
   - Lock, add $x'$ to $B$, unlock.
   - Launch new worker at $x'$.
3. Termination when wall‑clock budget $T$ reached; return best observed $(x_i^*, y_i^*)$.
**Design tradeoffs**:
- Simplicity vs. Theory: Standard acq. (UCB/LogEI) require no busy‑location logic, but their theoretical guarantees (Theorem 2) assume a $\beta$ schedule; purpose‑built methods have tailored theory but often rely on strong assumptions (e.g., known Lipschitz constant).
- Exploration control: Standard methods rely on hyperparameters ($\beta$, EI’s $y_n^*$). Diversity‑enforcing methods add explicit penalization or sampling, which can inadvertently over‑explore (as shown empirically).
- Computational cost: TS and E‑LogEI entail sampling or Monte Carlo, making them orders of magnitude slower per iteration than analytic UCB/LogEI.
**Failure signatures**:
1. Repeated/nearby queries: very low distance $\Delta$ to busy points across many iterations.
2. Over‑exploration: $\Delta$ remains very high, indicating avoidance of promising regions.
3. Stagnant lengthscales: $\Delta\ell_n$ (change in lengthscales) remains high throughout, suggesting GP not adapting.
4. Poor scaling with $q$: log regret improvement sub‑linear in number of workers.
**First 3 experiments**:
1. 1D Ackley ($q=2$): Visualize posterior mean, UCB curve, and busy points across iterations to observe the shift after a completion.
2. 2D Michalewicz ($d=2$, $q=2$): Compare query trajectories of standard UCB/LogEI vs. LP‑UCB/TS over 15 iterations; verify standard methods transition from exploration to exploitation while LP‑UCB remains spread out.
3. Synthetic high‑D: Ackley $d=10$, $q=8$, 30s budget. Run all methods, plot log regret curves, and compute win‑rates and statistical tests to establish baseline superiority of standard methods.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **What is the precise relationship between penalization strength in LP/LLP and the degree of over‑exploration?** The paper shows LP/LLP over‑explore empirically but does not theoretically bound how penalization parameters affect exploration metrics like query distances. Resolution would require formal analysis (e.g., bounds on minimum distance to busy points) or systematic ablations.
2. **Can the theoretical Bayes regret bound for asynchronous UCB be extended to constant $\beta=2$?** Theorem 2 requires $\beta_j = O(\log j)$, but the paper uses $\beta=2$ in practice. It is unclear whether constant $\beta$ works universally or only under certain conditions. Empirical studies across diverse problems or a tighter bound accommodating constant $\beta$ would resolve this.
3. **Why do asynchronous standard acquisition functions exhibit query distance profiles nearly identical to their sequentially optimally‑informed counterparts?** Figures show $\Delta(n)$ for async standard acquisitions matching $\Delta_{\text{seq}}(n)$ for sequential baselines. A theoretical connection between the async update operator and the sequential one could explain this similarity.
4. **Do these findings extend to non‑GP surrogates?** All analysis and experiments use GP surrogates. It is unknown whether standard async acquisition works with Random Forests, neural networks, or any‑cost models, which have different update and uncertainty properties. Replication with alternative surrogates is needed.
5. **Can we design a theoretically motivated async acquisition that leverages busy location information without over‑exploration?** Existing busy‑aware methods underperform; the paper suggests future work on mechanisms that take busy locations into account while avoiding the pitfalls of penalization. Proposing a new acquisition rule with improved regret bounds and empirical performance would address this.

## Limitations
- Exact ARD lengthscale hyperprior (from Hvarfner et al., 2024) is unspecified in the reproducibility notes; mismatched priors could alter GP adaptation dynamics and query diversity, potentially affecting conclusions.
- AEGIS’s NSGA‑II parameters (population size, generations) are unreported; its performance may be sensitive to these settings, making comparisons less reliable.
- Real‑world experiments report wall‑clock times on an unknown machine; hardware differences could bias time‑based budgets, though evaluation count might serve as a proxy.

## Confidence
- Redundancy avoidance via posterior updates: **Medium** (supported by visuals but depends on correct GP learning; hyperprior unspecified).
- Theoretical equivalence to sequential TS: **High** under stated assumptions (GP assumptions, uncertainty initialization); **Medium** in practice due to possible model misspecification.
- Over‑exploration of penalization methods: **Medium–High** (consistent empirical evidence across figures; LP/LLP may suffer from $L$ misestimation).
- Empirical dominance of standard methods: **Medium** (comprehensive experiments but reproducibility details missing and low external validation).

## Next Checks
1. **Lengthscale prior robustness**: Run Ackley ($d=20$, $q=8$) with lengthscale priors $\text{LogNormal}(0, \sigma)$ for $\sigma \in \{0.5, 1, \sqrt{d}\}$. Compare standard UCB on log($R$) and $\Delta$; robustness would strengthen the claim.
2. **Fixed evaluation budget benchmark**: Use HPO‑bench to run all methods with exactly 300 completed evaluations per method (instead of time budget). Report median simple regret and $\Delta$ to eliminate wall‑clock variance.
3. **Refit frequency ablation**: Implement standard UCB that refits the GP only every $k \in \{2,5,10\}$ completions. Compare against $k=1$ (standard) to quantify the contribution of intermediate posterior updates to regret and redundancy.
