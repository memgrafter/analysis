# SUSD: Structured Unsupervised Skill Discovery through State Factorization

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.01619
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.01619
- **Reference count:** 19
- **Primary result:** SUSD significantly outperforms five state-of-the-art USD baselines on downstream hierarchical RL tasks, achieving higher per-factor state coverage and lower factor decoding error.

## Executive Summary
SUSD introduces a structured approach to unsupervised skill discovery (USD) by factorizing the state space into independent components (e.g., objects or entities) and assigning distinct latent skill variables to each factor. A curiosity-driven weighting mechanism adaptively emphasizes under-explored factors during training based on a learned conditional density model. This combination addresses key limitations of prior USD methods: mutual information approaches tend to learn static skills, while distance-maximizing methods often neglect certain controllable factors in complex environments. Evaluated on three multi-factor environments (Multi-Particle, Kitchen, 2D-Gunner) with up to ten factors, SUSD demonstrates superior downstream task performance via hierarchical RL, higher worst-agent state coverage, and lower factor decoding error compared to five strong baselines. The method also remains competitive on standard unfactorized benchmarks (Ant, HalfCheetah), indicating broad applicability.

## Method Summary
SUSD operates under the assumption that the environment state can be factored into independent subspaces \(S = \{s_1, ..., s_N\}\), each corresponding to an entity or controllable feature. For each factor \(s_i\), a dedicated embedding network \(\phi_i\) maps the state component to a latent vector \(z_i \in \mathbb{R}^D\); the full skill vector is the concatenation \(z = [z_1, ..., z_N]\). The skill policy \(\pi(a|s,z)\) is conditioned on the entire state \(s\) and the skill \(z\). The intrinsic reward for a transition \((s,s')\) is computed per factor as \(r_i = (\phi_i(s_i') - \phi_i(s_i))^\top z_i\). A conditional density model \(q_\theta(s'|s)\) (e.g., diagonal Gaussian) is trained on collected transitions; its per-factor negative log-likelihood \(\sqrt{-\log q_\theta(s_i'|s)}\) serves as a curiosity weight \(w_i\), scaling each factor’s contribution. The total reward is \(R = \sum_i w_i r_i\). The optimization maximizes the expected sum of these rewards subject to a Lipschitz constraint \(\sum_i \|\phi_i(s_i') - \phi_i(s_i)\|^2 \leq 1\) on adjacent states. Updates are performed via dual gradient descent for the embeddings and Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda\), while the policy is trained with SAC using \(R\).

## Key Results
- Outperforms five state-of-the-art USD baselines on downstream hierarchical RL tasks across three factored environments (Multi-Particle, Kitchen, 2D-Gunner) with up to 10 factors.
- Achieves higher worst-agent state coverage per factor and lower factor decoding error (e.g., 0.060 vs 0.147 in Multi-Particle).
- Remains competitive with baselines in standard unfactorized domains (Ant, HalfCheetah).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Factorization and Skill Allocation
- **Claim:** Factorizing the state space and allocating distinct skill variables per factor enables fine-grained control over individual entities and promotes discovery of skills that jointly cover all controllable aspects of the environment.
- **Mechanism:** The observation space \(S\) is decomposed into \(N\) independent subspaces \(\{s_i\}\) (e.g., each object/entity). Each \(s_i\) is mapped by a dedicated embedding network \(\phi_i\) to a latent skill component \(z_i\). The skill policy \(\pi(a|s, z)\) is conditioned on the concatenated skill vector \(z = [z_1, ..., z_N]\). The intrinsic reward for a transition is computed per factor as \((\phi_i(s_i') - \phi_i(s_i))^\top z_i\).
- **Core assumption:** The environment’s state can be meaningfully factored into entities whose dynamics are at least partially independent, and the optimal skill set can be composed by independently controlling each factor.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "factorizes the state space into independent components (e.g., objects or controllable entities)... allocates distinct skill variables to different factors"; [section 4.1] "We factorize the observation space into N factors... each corresponding to a distinct subset of controllable features... This design enables the agent to learn localized skills specialized to each subspace"; [corpus] Strong corpus evidence for factorization in USD (e.g., SkiLD, DUSDi), but no direct evidence that factorized embeddings *cause* better coverage; correlation shown in ablation studies.
- **Break condition:** If factors are not meaningfully independent or if significant inter-factor dynamics exist that cannot be captured by linear combinations of independent skill components, the factorization imposes a restrictive inductive bias that may hinder learning complex, coordinated behaviors.

### Mechanism 2: Curiosity-Driven Factor Weighting
- **Claim:** A curiosity-driven, factor-wise weighting mechanism adaptively emphasizes under-explored or hard-to-control state factors during training, preventing the skill discovery from collapsing onto easily controllable factors.
- **Mechanism:** A conditional density model \(q_\theta(s'|s)\) is trained on transitions collected by the current skill policy. For each factor \(i\), the negative log-likelihood \(-\log q_\theta(s_i'|s)\) is computed, treating the marginal Gaussian parameters of \(q_\theta\) partitioned by the predefined factorization. The square root of this value serves as a per-factor distance/weight \(w_i\). The final intrinsic reward is \(R = \sum_i \sqrt{-\log q_\theta(s_i'|s)} \cdot r_i^{\text{SUSD}}\), scaling each factor’s contribution by its novelty/uncertainty.
- **Core assumption:** Factors associated with rare, high-negative-log-likelihood transitions (under the current model) correspond to aspects of the state space that are under-learned and require more exploration effort. Assigning higher weight to these factors incentivizes the policy to improve coverage of those subspaces.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "a dynamic model also tracks learning across factors, adaptively steering the agent’s focus toward underexplored factors"; [section 4.2] "we propose a fine-grained factor-wise weighting mechanism... The square root of `-log q_θ(s_i'|s)` can be interpreted as a valid distance metric... The curiosity-based factor weighting module is shown on the bottom of Figure 1."; [corpus] The concept of curiosity/novelty as intrinsic motivation is well-established (Park et al., 2023 uses a coarse version). The factor-wise novelty application is novel to SUSD; corpus shows no prior work applying density-model-based weighting per factor in DSD.
- **Break condition:** If the density model \(q_\theta\) is poorly calibrated or if the factorization misaligns with the true sources of controllability/difficulty, the factor-wise weights could misdirect exploration, over-emphasizing irrelevant factors or under-emphasizing critical ones.

### Mechanism 3: Integrated DSD Objective with Factor Weights
- **Claim:** Recasting the DSD optimization to incorporate factor-wise curiosity weights directly into the reward, alongside the factorized embedding, yields a scalar objective that simultaneously maximizes dynamic skill coverage *and* balanced exploration across factors.
- **Mechanism:** The standard DSD objective \(\max \mathbb{E}\left[ \sum_t (\phi(s_{t+1}) - \phi(s_t))^\top z \right]\) (with a Lipschitz constraint on \(\phi\)) is transformed (Lemma 4.1) to include a distance term as a coefficient. SUSD then specializes this: \(\phi\) is replaced by \(\{\phi_i\}\), the distance term becomes the factor-specific curiosity weight \(w_i = \sqrt{-\log q_\theta(s_i'|s)}\), and the objective becomes \(\max \mathbb{E}\left[ \sum_t \sum_i w_i \cdot (\phi_i(s_i') - \phi_i(s_i))^\top z_i \right]\) subject to a factorized Lipschitz constraint \(\sum_i \|\phi_i(s_i') - \phi_i(s_i)\|^2 \leq 1\).
- **Core assumption:** The linear combination of factor-wise intrinsic rewards, weighted by their respective curiosity scores, is a valid and effective scalarization of the multi-objective problem of (1) learning dynamic skills and (2) achieving balanced coverage across factors. The constraint \(\sum_i \|\cdot\|^2 \leq 1\) sufficiently regularizes the embedding.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "reformulate the DSD optimization problem and integrate the concept of curiosity-based factor weighting"; [section 4.3] "we optimize our objective using dual gradient descent... with... \(R = \sum_i \sqrt{-\log q_\theta(s_i'|s)} \cdot r_i^{\text{SUSD}}\)"; [corpus] DSD methods (METRA, CSD) optimize variants of the Wasserstein-based distance objective. SUSD’s factorized, weighted combination is novel; corpus has no direct precedent for integrating per-factor novelty weights into the DSD reward.
- **Break condition:** If the factor-wise rewards \(r_i\) are not commensurable (different scales) or if the curiosity weights \(w_i\) are highly correlated, the sum could still collapse onto a subset of factors. The Lipschitz constraint may be insufficient to prevent degenerate embeddings if factors have vastly different dimensionalities or scales.

## Foundational Learning
- **Unsupervised Skill Discovery (USD)**
  - **Why needed:** SUSD is a USD method; understanding the core problem—learning diverse, reusable behaviors without extrinsic rewards—is prerequisite. The paper contrasts USD with standard RL and positions itself against MI-based and prior DSD-based USD.
  - **Quick check:** *What is the key limitation of Mutual Information (MI)-based USD methods that DSD methods aim to address?*
- **Distance-Maximizing Skill Discovery (DSD)**
  - **Why needed:** SUSD is built upon the DSD framework. The core objective (Eq. 2) and its reliance on a state-space distance metric (Wasserstein) are foundational. The paper’s innovation is modifying this DSD core with factorization and weighting.
  - **Quick check:** *In the DSD objective \(\max \mathbb{E}[ (\phi(s') - \phi(s))^\top z ]\), what role does the mapping function \(\phi\) play?*
- **Factored Markov Decision Processes (FMDPs)**
  - **Why needed:** The method assumes the environment state can be factored into independent components \(S = S^1 \times ... \times S^N\). This structural assumption is the primary inductive bias leveraged. Understanding factorization is key to grasping the architecture’s design.
  - **Quick check:** *Why might factorization be a natural assumption in multi-object environments like the 2D-Gunner or Multi-Particle?*

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Input Processor (splits state \(s\) into \(\{s_i\}\)) -> Factor Embedding Networks \(\phi_i\) (each processes \(s_i\)) -> concatenated representation -> Skill Policy \(\pi\) (outputs action) and Intrinsic Reward Calculator; Transition \((s,s')\) -> Density Model \(q_\theta\) -> Factor Weight Calculator -> weights \(w_i\); Intrinsic Reward Calculator combines \(w_i\), \((\phi_i(s_i')-\phi_i(s_i))\), and \(z_i\) to produce \(R = \sum_i w_i (\phi_i(s_i')-\phi_i(s_i))^\top z_i\); \(R\) used by SAC Trainer to update \(\pi\) and by Dual Optimizer to update \(\phi_i\) and Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda\) under constraint \(\sum_i \|\phi_i(s_i')-\phi_i(s_i)\|^2 \leq 1\); for evaluation, a High-Level Controller uses frozen \(\pi\).
- **Critical path:**  
  1) Collect rollouts with current \(\pi\) and random/fixed skill prior.  
  2) Fit density model \(q_\theta\) on all collected transitions.  
  3) Compute per-factor intrinsic rewards \(r_i\) and curiosity weights \(w_i\) from \(q_\theta\).  
  4) Compute total intrinsic reward \(R = \sum_i w_i r_i\).  
  5) Update \(\pi\) with SAC using \(R\).  
  6) Update each \(\phi_i\) and \(\lambda\) via dual gradient descent using constraints from adjacent state pairs.  
  7) Repeat until skill policy converges.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Factorization fidelity vs. simplicity:* More factors capture finer details but risk over-factorization; fewer factors may miss key entities.  
  - *Curiosity model capacity:* A more expressive \(q_\theta\) (e.g., normalizing flows) may better capture dependencies but requires more data and could overfit. The paper uses a simple diagonal Gaussian.  
  - *Constraint slack \(\varepsilon\):* Balances constraint adherence vs. gradient signal strength; too small \(\varepsilon\) may harm learning (paper uses \(\varepsilon=10^{-6}\)).  
  - *Skill dimension per factor \(D\):* Uniform \(D\) simplifies architecture but may not match true factor complexity; the paper uses \(D=2\) consistently.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Poor state/factor coverage: Some factors remain largely unaffected by any skill; check per-factor reward magnitudes and curiosity weights.  
  - High factor decoding error: Factor-specific embeddings \(\phi_i\) fail to capture information about their assigned factor; possibly due to insufficient data or gradient interference.  
  - Skill collapse: All skills produce similar trajectories; may occur if curiosity weights vanish (density model overconfident) or Lipschitz constraint is violated.  
  - Downstream task failure: Zero-shot or HRL performance near random; could be due to uncontrollable skills, insufficient diversity, or factor misalignment with task requirements.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Ablation of Curiosity Weighting (SUSD-w): Train without \(w_i\) (set all \(w_i=1\)). Compare factor coverage and downstream performance; expect drop, especially on hard-to-control factors.  
  2. Ablation of Factorization (SUSD-wf): Use a single non-factorized \(\phi(s)\) and single skill \(z\) (vanilla DSD). Compare against full SUSD; expect significant drop in complex multi-factor environments.  
  3. Factor-wise Reward/Weight Analysis: Track per-factor reward magnitudes \(\|r_i\|\) and curiosity weights \(w_i\) over epochs. Expect initially low \(w_i\) for hard factors to rise over time, with corresponding \(\|r_i\|\) increasing, demonstrating adaptive re-weighting.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** *How can SUSD be extended to environments where the true state factorization is unknown or only high-dimensional pixel observations are available?*  
  **Basis:** The method assumes direct access to a factored state (Section 3, Section 5.1). The authors mention that an encoder could be used but do not implement or evaluate this extension.  
  **Why unresolved:** Without a factored state, the core inductive bias of SUSD is lost, and it is unclear how well the method would perform if factorization had to be learned jointly with skills.  
  **What evidence would resolve it:** Modify SUSD to incorporate an unsupervised representation learning module (e.g., object-centric encoder) and evaluate on pixel-based environments (e.g., Atari, robotic manipulation) by comparing learned skill diversity and downstream task performance against the original factored version.
- **Open Question 2:** *Does the additive factor-wise reward structure in SUSD’s objective sufficiently capture cross-factor interactions that are necessary for complex, synergistic skills?*  
  **Basis:** The optimization sums contributions from each factor (Eq. 6), and the authors claim not to impose a strict independence assumption, but the additive form inherently treats factors as separable in the reward.  
  **Why unresolved:** In multi-object settings, optimal skills may require coordinated changes that affect multiple factors simultaneously; a purely additive reward may not incentivize such coordination.  
  **What evidence would resolve it:** Design synthetic environments where the only way to achieve maximal state change is through joint factor interactions, then compare SUSD against variants with explicit interaction terms (e.g., pairwise products in \(\phi\) or reward) using metrics such as state coverage and downstream task success.
- **Open Question 3:** *How robust is the curiosity-based factor weighting to inaccuracies in the learned density model, particularly as the number of factors increases?*  
  **Basis:** The curiosity weight for each factor is derived from a diagonal Gaussian density model \(q(s'|s)\) (Section 4.2). The model is trained on trajectories collected by the current policy (Algorithm 1).  
  **Why unresolved:** With many factors, modeling joint transitions becomes more challenging; the diagonal assumption may fail to capture correlations, leading to misleading per-factor curiosity signals. This could cause misguidance in the weighting.  
  **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablate the density model by varying its expressiveness (e.g., vanilla Gaussian, mixture density networks, autoregressive models) while keeping the rest of SUSD fixed, and measure the correlation between density model likelihood error and final skill diversity/coverage across a range of factor counts.
- **Open Question 4:** *Does SUSD truly learn disentangled skills that enable independent control over individual state factors?*  
  **Basis:** SUSD claims to yield a factorized skill representation for fine-grained control, but evaluation relies on hierarchical RL and reconstruction error rather than causal intervention on skill factors.  
  **Why unresolved:** Disentanglement is typically verified by measuring that manipulating one skill dimension affects only its corresponding state factor while leaving others unchanged. The paper provides indirect evidence (accidental task completion, factor decoding error) but not direct intervention results.  
  **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct factor intervention experiments: fix all but one skill factor (or context) and vary the free factor across a range, then compute statistics of other state factors’ deviations. A disentangled method should show near-zero change in non-target factors.
- **Open Question 5:** *What is the computational and sample complexity of SUSD as the number of factors grows, and does the linear scaling assumption hold in practice?*  
  **Basis:** SUSD trains separate embedding networks \(\phi_i\) per factor and a shared density model (Algorithm 1). Experiments are limited to up to 10 factors (Section 5).  
  **Why unresolved:** Real-world domains can involve hundreds of entities; training cost per factor and the density model’s complexity could become prohibitive, and the additive constraint might interact with scale in unforeseen ways (e.g., concentration of \(\lambda\) updates, gradient magnitudes).  
  **What evidence would resolve it:** Run systematic experiments in scalable synthetic environments (e.g., Multi-Particle with increasing number of agents) measuring wall-clock time, memory consumption, sample efficiency (steps to achieve coverage), and quality of learned skills (coverage, decoding error) as a function of factor count.

## Limitations
- Unspecified details (density model architecture, high-level controller training) hinder faithful reproduction and full understanding of implementation nuances.
- Evaluation is limited to five environments, with no reported statistical significance or variance, reducing confidence in the generalizability and effect size of the reported improvements.
- Core assumptions—state factor independence and a simple diagonal Gaussian density model—may not hold in environments with strong inter-factor couplings or complex state distributions, potentially degrading performance.

## Confidence
- **Claim 1:** Factor+weighting improves coverage/downstream performance in factored MDPs: *Medium-High*  
- **Claim 2:** Curiosity weighting adaptively balances factor exploration: *Medium*  
- **Claim 3:** SUSD competitive in unfactorized domains: *Medium*

## Next Checks
1. **Reproduce core experiments with clarified hyperparameters** – Obtain missing details (e.g., \(q_\theta\) architecture, high-level controller steps) from authors and run an independent replication on at least two factored environments to confirm performance gaps and estimate variance.
2. **Stress-test factor independence** – In a controlled environment like Multi-Particle, introduce varying degrees of inter-factor coupling (e.g., correlated particle motions) and compare SUSD against non-factorized baselines to characterize breakdown conditions of the factorization assumption.
3. **Density model sensitivity study** – Ablate the capacity and structure of \(q_\theta\) (e.g., diagonal Gaussian vs. deeper networks or normalizing flows) and measure impacts on curiosity weights, factor coverage, and downstream performance to assess robustness to model misspecification.
