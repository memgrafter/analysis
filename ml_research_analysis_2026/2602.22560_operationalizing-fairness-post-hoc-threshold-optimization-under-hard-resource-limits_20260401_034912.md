# Operationalizing Fairness: Post-Hoc Threshold Optimization Under Hard Resource Limits

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.22560
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22560
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Under a 25% capacity limit, the proposed framework achieves recall values between 0.409 and 0.702, while standard unconstrained fairness heuristics collapse to near-zero utility.

## Executive Summary
Deploying fair machine learning systems often faces strict resource constraints, such as limits on the number of interventions (e.g., social support slots). Existing methods either ignore these capacity limits or rely on group-specific thresholds that may be illegal. This paper introduces a post-hoc, model-agnostic framework that optimizes a single global decision threshold using a composite ethical loss balancing false negatives, false positives, and subgroup disparity, while enforcing a mathematically rigorous hard cap on the total number of positive predictions. Experiments across multiple high-stakes datasets show that the capacity constraint frequently dictates the final threshold (over 80% of configurations). Under a restrictive 25% capacity cap, the framework yields recall between 0.409 and 0.702, whereas standard fairness heuristics degrade to random performance. The authors argue that operational feasibility must be central to designing practically deployable fairness interventions.

## Method Summary
The framework operates on the output scores of any pre-trained binary classifier. It formulates an optimization problem that directly incorporates a hard capacity constraint (maximum number of positive decisions allowed) alongside a composite ethical loss function. This loss combines standard classification errors (false negatives and false positives) with a disparity metric to promote fairness across sensitive subgroups. The optimization yields a single threshold applied globally to all instances. By being post-hoc, the approach avoids retraining and is model-agnostic. The capacity constraint is enforced as a hard limit, ensuring resource feasibility, while the composite loss navigates the trade-off between utility and fairness.

## Key Results
- Capacity constraint dominates the final threshold choice in over 80% of tested configurations.
- Under a 25% capacity limit, the proposed method attains recall between 0.409 and 0.702.
- Standard unconstrained fairness heuristics collapse to near-zero utility (≈ random) under the same restrictive capacity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The method works because it explicitly integrates the resource limitation into the decision-making process. A hard capacity cap forces the optimizer to select a threshold that respects the maximum number of interventions. This constraint often overrides fairness and accuracy considerations, explaining its dominance. The composite ethical loss simultaneously penalizes disparate impacts and classification errors, allowing the framework to find a threshold that balances these objectives within the capacity bound. The post-hoc nature means the threshold can be tuned without altering the underlying model, making it practical for real-world deployment where models are already trained and validated.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Pre-trained Model -> Score Output -> Threshold Optimizer (Composite Loss + Capacity Constraint) -> Optimal Threshold -> Applied to All Instances -> Final Decisions

**Critical Path:**  
1. Generate scores for a validation set using the pre-trained model.  
2. Iterate over candidate thresholds, computing the composite ethical loss and checking the hard capacity constraint.  
3. Select the threshold that optimally balances loss and capacity.  
4. Deploy the fixed threshold to classify future instances.

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- Global vs. Group-Specific Thresholds: A single threshold ensures legal compliance but may not fully address subgroup needs. Group-specific thresholds could improve fairness but risk being illegal or complex.  
- Fairness Metric Choice: The composite loss must balance multiple fairness notions; weighting choices affect outcomes and may introduce new biases.  
- Capacity Cap Strictness: Tighter caps improve resource feasibility but may drastically reduce utility; the optimizer navigates this trade-off.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- Infeasible capacity: No threshold satisfies the cap, leading to failure or degenerate thresholds (e.g., 0% or 100%).  
- Mis–specified loss: Over–penalizing one component skews thresholds, ignoring critical fairness or accuracy goals.  
- Numerical instability: Hard capacity constraint may cause non‑smooth optimization, preventing convergence.

**First Experiments (3):**  
1. **Reproduce Core Claim:** Use a public dataset (e.g., COMPAS) to replicate the dominance of capacity constraint by sweeping capacity caps and observing threshold selection frequency.  
2. **Sensitivity to Loss Weights:** Vary the weights in the composite ethical loss (e.g., relative importance of FNR vs. disparity) and measure resulting recall and fairness metrics under fixed capacity caps.  
3. **Comparison to Group‑Specific Methods:** Implement a legally non‑compliant group‑specific thresholding approach and compare utility and fairness under identical hard capacity caps to assess the cost of compliance.

## Limitations

- The exact formulation of the composite ethical loss (weights, disparity metric) is not fully described.
- The reliance on a single global threshold may still mask intra‑group harms; real‑world factors like dynamic resource allocation are not addressed.
