# Subspace Geometry Governs Catastrophic Forgetting in Low-Rank Adaptation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.02224  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02224  
- **Reference count**: 12  
- **Primary result**: The paper introduces a geometric theory predicting catastrophic forgetting in LoRA as F=α(1-cos²θ_min)+β, where θ_min is the minimum principal angle between task gradient subspaces; it shows strong correlation on synthetic tasks and rank-invariance at high subspace angles.

## Executive Summary
Catastrophic forgetting in parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods like Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) poses a significant obstacle to continual learning. This work develops a geometric theory that models forgetting based on the alignment between the gradient subspaces of different tasks. The authors derive a law F = α(1 - cos²θ_min) + β, where θ_min denotes the minimum principal angle between the gradient subspaces of two tasks. Empirical evaluation on synthetic tasks yields an almost perfect correlation (r=0.994) between the interference term (1-cos²θ_min) and observed forgetting. Moreover, the study finds that when task subspaces are nearly orthogonal (θ_min ≈ 90°), forgetting becomes largely independent of the LoRA adapter rank, with very low coefficient of variation (≈0.8% on synthetic data). This rank-invariance phenomenon reconciles conflicting prior reports about the impact of rank on forgetting and suggests that orthogonal regularization methods (e.g., O-LoRA) provide diminishing returns when natural subspace orthogonality is already high. The findings offer both a theoretical framework and practical guidance for deploying LoRA in continual learning scenarios.

## Method Summary
The methodology blends theoretical analysis with controlled experiments. Theoretically, the authors model each task’s learning dynamics by the subspace spanned by the gradients of the loss with respect to the LoRA parameters. They analyze the interaction between two tasks by considering the minimum principal angle between these subspaces, leading to the forgetting law. Empirically, they first validate the law on synthetic tasks where the gradient subspaces can be precisely manipulated (e.g., by controlling data distributions or model architectures). They then extend the evaluation to standard continual learning benchmarks such as Split CIFAR-100, measuring forgetting across varying LoRA ranks. The key experimental variable is the LoRA rank r, while the main measured outcomes are task performance and the computed subspace angles. This approach allows them to isolate the effect of subspace geometry from other confounding factors.

## Key Results
- The interference term (1 - cos²θ_min) correlates almost perfectly (r = 0.994) with catastrophic forgetting on synthetic tasks.  
- When task gradient subspaces are nearly orthogonal (high θ_min), forgetting exhibits rank-invariance: the coefficient of variation across ranks is ≈0.8% on synthetic data and ≈10–19% on real benchmarks.  
- The theory explains why rank effects are only prominent when task subspaces are similar (low θ_min) and why orthogonal methods like O-LoRA offer limited additional benefit under high natural orthogonality.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The mechanism hinges on the low-rank nature of LoRA updates. Each task induces a gradient subspace that captures the directions in the LoRA parameter space most relevant for minimizing its loss. When a new task is learned, the update lies within its gradient subspace. The forgetting of a previous task depends on how much this new update projects onto directions that are crucial for the old task. The minimum principal angle θ_min between the two subspaces quantifies their worst-case alignment: if θ_min is small, the subspaces share at least one pair of vectors that are nearly parallel, meaning the new update will have a component along an old-task-relevant direction, causing interference. If θ_min is large (subspaces nearly orthogonal), the smallest angle between any pair of vectors is large, so the new update is unlikely to interfere with the old task’s subspace. However, because LoRA updates are constrained to a low-rank subspace, even orthogonal updates can still cause forgetting through higher-order effects or by moving the parameters away from the old optimum. The derived law F = α(1 - cos²θ_min) + β captures this relationship, with the term (1 - cos²θ_min) = sin²θ_min increasing with θ_min, thus predicting higher forgetting for more orthogonal subspaces. This counterintuitive trend suggests that orthogonal subspaces may lead to larger steps away from old-task optima due to the low-rank constraint.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Frozen Pre-trained Model (weights W) → LoRA Adapter (matrices A ∈ ℝ^{r×d}, B ∈ ℝ^{d×r}) inserted into linear layers → Forward pass: output = xW + xBA. Only A and B are trainable.

**Critical path**  
1. Choose LoRA rank r and initialize A (Gaussian, std=1/r) and B (zero).  
2. For each batch: compute xW (frozen) and xBA (adapter), sum them, apply activation/loss.  
3. Backpropagate loss to compute gradients ∂L/∂A, ∂L/∂B.  
4. Update A and B via optimizer (e.g., Adam).  
5. Repeat for all tasks sequentially; optionally save adapters per task or merge them.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- *Rank vs. efficiency*: Higher rank improves task performance but increases trainable parameters and memory.  
- *Frozen base vs. adaptability*: Freezing the base reduces compute but may limit the range of possible adaptations; LoRA can only move within the column space of B and row space of A.  
- *Separate adapters per task vs. single adapter*: Separate adapters prevent forgetting but increase parameters; single adapter leverages shared knowledge but risks interference.

**Failure signatures**  
- Large accuracy drop on previous tasks after training on a new task (high forgetting).  
- High variance in forgetting across different random seeds when varying rank.  
- Computed θ_min is small, indicating high subspace alignment, yet rank-invariance is not observed (forgetting varies with rank).  
- Forgetting does not follow the predicted linear relationship with sin²θ_min.

**First three experiments**  
1. *Single-task performance vs. rank*: Train LoRA on a single task with ranks {1,2,4,8,16,32} and plot accuracy to establish baseline capacity.  
2. *Two-task sequential forgetting*: Train on Task A, then Task B; measure forgetting on A for each rank and compute the gradient subspace angle θ_min between the tasks.  
3. *Synthetic correlation*: Generate synthetic gradient subspaces with controlled minimum angles (e.g., by rotating random bases), train sequentially, and verify that forgetting scales linearly with sin²θ_min.

## Limitations
- The theoretical assumption that gradient subspaces are low-dimensional may not hold for all model architectures or tasks, potentially limiting applicability.  
- Empirical validation is restricted to a small set of synthetic tasks and a few real-world benchmarks, which may not capture the complexity of larger models (e.g., LLaMA-7B) or more diverse domains.  
- On real benchmarks, rank-invariance shows a coefficient of variation of 10–19%, indicating unexplained variability that the current theory does not fully account for.  
- The parameters α and β in the forgetting law are treated as empirical constants; a deeper theoretical derivation is lacking.

## Confidence
- Geometric law F = α(1-cos²θ_min) + β: **Medium** (plausible but not fully derived; α,β are fitted).  
- Correlation r=0.994 on synthetic tasks: **High** (strong empirical support in controlled setting).  
- Rank-invariance (low CV) under high θ_min: **Medium** (synthetic evidence strong, but real CV suggests moderate confidence).  
- Orthogonal methods' limited benefit when natural orthogonality is high: **Medium** (theoretically consistent, needs more empirical proof).
