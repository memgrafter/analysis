# SAGE: Benchmarking and Improving Retrieval for Deep Research Agents

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.05975
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05975
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** BM25 outperforms LLM-based retrievers by ~30% in deep research agents due to query-retriever mismatch; corpus-level augmentation yields 8% absolute gain on short-form questions.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces SAGE, a novel benchmark for evaluating scientific literature retrieval within deep research agent workflows. SAGE comprises 1,200 reasoning-intensive queries across four domains and a 200,000-paper corpus. Evaluating six agents—including GPT-5 and DR Tulu—reveals universal failure on complex retrieval tasks. The core finding is a pronounced performance inversion: traditional BM25 significantly outperforms modern LLM-based retrievers (ReasonIR, gte-Qwen2-7B-instruct) by approximately 30% on short-form questions. The authors attribute this to a "query-retriever mismatch"—agents generate keyword-oriented sub-queries that align with lexical matching but underutilize semantic retrievers. A corpus-level test-time scaling method, which uses LLMs to augment documents with metadata and keywords, narrows this gap, achieving an 8% absolute gain in exact match on short-form questions and a 2% gain on open-ended weighted recall.

## Method Summary
The study builds the SAGE benchmark: 1,200 queries (150 per domain: Computer Science, Natural Science, Healthcare, Humanities) of two types—short-form (single verifiable answer) and open-ended (multiple relevant papers). The corpus contains 200,000 open-access scientific papers (≈50k per domain, published ≥2020 from major venues), provided as markdown (first 32k tokens). The primary agent evaluated is DR Tulu, an open-source deep research agent that iteratively decomposes queries, searches, and synthesizes (max 10 steps). The agent's native search tool is replaced with a configurable MCP service hosting three retrievers: BM25 (sparse), gte-Qwen2-7B-instruct (dense), and ReasonIR (dense, reasoning-trained). For corpus-level test-time scaling, each paper is pre-augmented with metadata (venue, year, authors, citation count) and 8 LLM-generated keywords (using Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B-Instruct), prepended to its markdown. Performance is measured by Exact Match (EM) for short-form and Weighted Recall for open-ended.

## Key Results
- BM25 outperforms LLM-based retrievers (ReasonIR, gte-Qwen2-7B-instruct) by ~30% on short-form questions due to agents generating keyword-oriented sub-queries.
- LLM-based retrievers exhibit lower "Unique References per Search" (URS ≈ 1.98 vs. BM25's 2.97), indicating reduced diversity under long-document constraints.
- Corpus-level augmentation (keywords + metadata) yields +8% absolute EM on short-form questions and +2% on open-ended weighted recall, with the largest benefit for BM25.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Query–Retriever Mismatch
Agents like DR Tulu decompose complex queries into simple keyword concatenations (e.g., "NAACL paper 2024 Tool"). This style is optimized for lexical matching (BM25) but provides poor semantic signals for LLM-based retrievers trained on natural language instructions. The retrievers' strength in understanding nuanced intent is wasted, leading to ~30% lower performance. Core assumption: agent query decomposition policies are static and not aligned with retriever capabilities. Evidence: [abstract, section 4.4] direct attribution of BM25's advantage to keyword-oriented sub-queries; case studies (Figure 4) show LLM retrievers return irrelevant papers matching title keywords but missing context. Break condition: agents are retrained to generate natural language, instruction-like sub-queries.

### Mechanism 2: Long-Document Truncation & Diversity Collapse
Scientific papers are long (mean ≈14k tokens). LLM-based retrievers must truncate documents to fit their maximum input length (~32k tokens), causing information loss. This truncation, combined with embedding convergence for long texts, reduces the distinctiveness of retrieved results—evidenced by lower URS. BM25 operates on full-text lexical signals and does not suffer this collapse. Core assumption: document length is a fixed corpus property. Evidence: [section 4.4, A.3] URS gap (BM25 2.97 vs. ReasonIR 1.98) and token distribution figures. Break condition: retrievers natively handle 100k+ token contexts without degradation.

### Mechanism 3: Corpus-Level Test-Time Scaling
Augmenting documents with LLM-generated keywords and metadata injects lexical signals that directly align with agents' keyword-based query patterns. This reduces the semantic gap between query and document, making retrieval easier for both sparse and dense retrievers, but with the largest absolute gain for BM25 (which directly exploits added keywords). Core assumption: agents' query policies are static; augmentation must meet agents where their queries already are. Evidence: [abstract, section 5.2] 8% EM gain for BM25 on short-form; section 5.1 describes the augmentation format. Break condition: agents learn to generate more semantic, multi-faceted queries that exploit deeper content.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Deep Research Agent as an Interactive, Multi-Step System**  
  *Why needed:* Performance must be evaluated within an agentic loop (`think → generate sub-query → retrieve → synthesize → repeat`), not as a single retrieve-then-read step.  
  *Quick check:* What key difference distinguishes a traditional RAG system from a deep research agent like DR Tulu in its interaction with the retrieval tool?

- **Concept: Retrieval Paradigm Mismatch (Sparse vs. Dense)**  
  *Why needed:* The central finding—BM25 beating modern LLM retrievers—is counter-intuitive and requires understanding how query style determines whether lexical or semantic matching is effective.  
  *Quick check:* How does a query like `"NAACL 2024 tool paper 14 citations"` differently challenge BM25 versus a dense retriever like gte-Qwen?

- **Concept: Test-Time Scaling as a Spectrum (Query-side vs. Corpus-side)**  
  *Why needed:* The proposed solution applies test-time scaling to the *corpus* (making documents retrieval-friendly) rather than the query (making queries more complex). This non-standard approach is key for fixed, keyword-centric agent behaviors.  
  *Quick check:* What is the primary intuition behind "corpus-level test-time scaling" versus "query-side test-time scaling" (e.g., query rewriting/CoT)?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Agent (DR Tulu) -> Retriever Backend (BM25 / gte-Qwen2-7B-instruct / ReasonIR) -> Corpus (200k papers) -> Evaluation Harness (metrics: EM, Weighted Recall)
- **Critical path:** User Query → Agent (`think` & decompose → `search` with sub-query) → Retriever (lookup in Corpus) → return documents → Agent (`think` & synthesize) → repeat until `answer` → Evaluation (compare to ground truth).
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - **BM25 vs. Dense:** BM25 is fast, deterministic, and exploits lexical signals for keyword queries but fails on semantic synonyms. Dense retrievers handle semantics but incur high encoding cost, truncation loss on long docs, and perform poorly on keyword-style queries.  
  - **Corpus Augmentation Scope:** Adding 8 keywords + metadata is cheap and effective for BM25. Aggressive full-paper rewriting could help dense retrievers more but is expensive and risks content alteration.  
  - **Agent Retraining:** Evaluating with a fixed, unmodified agent reflects real-world deployment but masks potential gains from retraining the agent to adapt queries to the retriever.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - **Signature 1 (Mismatch):** Sub-queries are 3–5 keywords (e.g., "ICML 2023 symbolic regression ODEFormer"); LLM retrievers return irrelevant papers with those title words.  
  - **Signature 2 (Diversity Collapse):** LLM retriever URS ≈ 1.98 vs. BM25 2.97; same top documents retrieved repeatedly, starving agent of diverse evidence.  
  - **Signature 3 (Open-Ended Stagnation):** Corpus augmentation yields only ~2% gain on open-ended questions, indicating low agent query diversity is the primary bottleneck.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Replicate Core Mismatch:** Run DR Tulu on SAGE short-form questions with BM25(k=10) and gte-Qwen(k=10). Confirm ~30% EM gap. Sample 20 failure cases of gte-Qwen and inspect sub-queries—expect keyword strings.  
  2. **Validate Corpus Augmentation:** Generate 8 keywords/metadata per paper using provided prompt (Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B-Instruct). Prepend to markdown, rebuild BM25 index, rerun DR Tulu on short-form questions. Expect ~8% absolute EM increase. Verify augmented documents contain keywords from successful sub-queries.  
  3. **Probe Agent Diversity:** With SearchR1-32B, measure average searches per question. Expect ~1.2 searches (near single-shot). This confirms the "low-diversity decomposition" hypothesis—the agent issues few varied sub-queries, capping recall.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Can deep research agents be explicitly trained or prompted to adapt their sub-query generation strategies to better align with the strengths of LLM-based retrievers (e.g., generating naturally linguistic, semantically rich queries instead of keyword-oriented ones)?
- What specific mechanisms cause LLM-based retrievers (like ReasonIR) to exhibit reduced document diversity and susceptibility to semantic drift in iterative, multi-step agent retrieval loops compared to sparse retrievers like BM25?
- Why does corpus-level test-time scaling yield only marginal improvements (~1–2%) on open-ended questions for all retrievers, despite an 8% gain on short-form questions?
- To what extent are the findings (BM25 superiority, query-retriever mismatch) generalizable beyond DR Tulu and the specific agent architectures evaluated (GPT-5, Gemini, SearchR1)?
- Can more aggressive corpus-level test-time scaling (e.g., using LLMs to synthesize summaries, extract structured claims, or rewrite sections) overcome the lexical bias introduced by agents' keyword-oriented queries and improve LLM-based retriever effectiveness beyond simple keyword augmentation?

## Limitations
- Reproduction hindered by missing details: DR Tulu's MCP interface, exact corpus venue lists, and retriever configuration specs.
- Findings rely heavily on DR Tulu and SAGE's 1,200 queries; generalizability to other agents/domains remains unknown.
- Agent exhibits low search diversity (few sub-queries per question), potentially capping maximum retrievable recall regardless of retriever quality.

## Confidence
- **Query–retriever mismatch:** Medium. Strong experimental correlation (BM25 +30% over LLM retrievers), but mechanism may be specific to DR Tulu's decomposition style.
- **Long‑document truncation effects:** Medium. Supported by lower Unique References per Search (URS) and token stats, yet depends on fixed 32k context limit.
- **Corpus‑level augmentation gains:** Medium. 8% absolute EM for BM25 on short‑form, 2% on open‑ended; benefits for dense retrievers unclear and may require more aggressive edits.
- **Universal failure of deep research agents:** Low. Only six agents evaluated; SAGE's 1,200 queries may not capture full complexity of real‑world research.

## Next Checks
