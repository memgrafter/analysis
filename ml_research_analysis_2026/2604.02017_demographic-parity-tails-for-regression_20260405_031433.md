# Demographic Parity Tails for Regression
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2604.02017
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02017
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Introducing Demographic Parity Tails, a fairness notion that enforces demographic parity only above a prediction threshold α, achieving improved accuracy‑fairness trade‑offs compared to global DP while guaranteeing perfect fairness in the tail region.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the accuracy‑fairness trade‑off in regression under demographic parity (DP). Enforcing DP across the entire prediction distribution often harms predictive performance, especially when fairness concerns are limited to high‑risk or high‑prediction regions. The authors propose Demographic Parity Tails (DP‑tails), which imposes DP constraints only on the tail of the prediction distribution defined by a threshold α. They derive the optimal fair predictor via optimal transport theory, showing it modifies conditional quantiles by clipping the lower portion and averaging the tail across sensitive groups. A practical post‑processing algorithm uses unlabeled data to estimate necessary conditional distributions and achieves perfect tail fairness asymptotically. Theoretical results provide convergence rates for tail unfairness and risk bounds. Experiments on synthetic and real datasets demonstrate that DP‑tails reduces mean squared error compared to global DP while maintaining (approximately) zero tail unfairness for thresholds above a certain level. The framework also allows optimizing the proportion p of mass below α to further minimize risk under the tail constraint.

## Method Summary
The method proceeds as follows. First, a base regressor \(\hat{f}\) is trained on labeled data \(\mathcal{D}_n\). Then, an unlabeled calibration set \(\mathcal{D}_N\) (containing only \(X\) and sensitive attribute \(S\)) is used to estimate the conditional distribution of \(\hat{f}(X,S)\) given each sensitive group \(s\). Specifically, for each group the predictions (with a tiny jitter to ensure continuity) are split into two halves: one half builds an empirical conditional CDF \(\hat{F}_{\hat{f}|s}\), the other builds an empirical conditional quantile function \(\hat{Q}_{\hat{f}|s}\). Group proportions \(\hat{p}_s\) are computed. For a chosen tail threshold \(\alpha\) and tail mass \((1-p)\), the adjusted predictor is defined as
\[
\hat{g}_{\alpha,p}(x,s) = \hat{Q}_s\big(\hat{F}_{\hat{f}|s}(\hat{f}(x,s)+\varepsilon)\big),
\]
where \(\hat{Q}_s(u)\) equals \(\min(\alpha, \hat{Q}_{\hat{f}|s}(u))\) for \(u\in[0,p]\) and \(\max\big(\alpha+\xi, \sum_{s'} \hat{p}_{s'} \hat{Q}_{\hat{f}|s'}(u)\big)\) for \(u\in(p,1]\). This transformation clips lower quantiles to \(\alpha\) and replaces upper quantiles by a group‑averaged quantile, thereby enforcing equal conditional CDFs for all \(t\ge\alpha\). Optionally, one can optimize \(p\) (relaxed DP‑tails) by minimizing the empirical mean‑squared error on the calibration set. The approach is a post‑processing step that requires no additional labeled data.

## Key Results
- Theoretical characterization: The optimal fair predictor \(g^*_{\alpha,p}\) is given by an explicit quantile formula derived from a 2‑Wasserstein barycenter problem.
- Tail unfairness \(U^\alpha\) of the plug‑in estimator \(\hat{g}_{\xi,\alpha,p}\) converges at rate \(O(1/\sqrt{N})\) with unlabeled sample size \(N\), independent of the base predictor’s quality.
- Risk bounds: The mean squared error degradation relative to the Bayes optimal fair predictor scales with \(\|\hat{f}-f^*\|_\infty\) and \(1/\sqrt{N}\).
- Empirical findings: On synthetic and three real‑world datasets (Law School, Crime, California Housing), DP‑tails yields lower MSE than global DP (implemented via OT) while achieving perfect tail fairness for \(\alpha\) above a data‑dependent threshold; optimizing \(p\) further reduces risk.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The effectiveness of DP‑tails rests on three pillars.

1. **Tail‑focused fairness constraint.** By requiring demographic parity only for predictions at or above a threshold \(\alpha\), the method targets the region where interventions are most consequential, leaving lower predictions unconstrained. This avoids the blanket fairness enforcement of global DP that unnecessarily distorts accurate predictions in low‑risk zones. Core assumption: fairness concerns are confined to high‑prediction outcomes. If \(\alpha\) is set so high that the tail mass \((1-p)\) becomes negligible, the constraint may cease to be meaningful.

2. **Optimal transport formulation.** The risk minimization problem under \((\alpha,p)\)-DP‑tails is equivalent to minimizing a weighted sum of 2‑Wasserstein distances between the conditional distribution \(\nu_{f^*|s}\) and a common tail distribution plus group‑specific head distributions. This barycenter problem admits a closed‑form solution in terms of quantile functions: below \(p\) the quantiles are clipped to \(\alpha\), above \(p\) they are replaced by the group‑weighted average of the original conditional quantiles. Existence of a minimizer holds when \(\alpha\) does not exceed a certain threshold; otherwise an \(\xi\)-approximation introduces a vanishing risk gap. This builds on established OT methods for global DP (Chzhen et al., 2020).

3. **Post‑processing with unlabeled data.** The oracle solution can be approximated using only unlabeled examples. The algorithm estimates conditional CDFs and quantiles from the base predictor’s outputs on the calibration set (with small jitter) and then applies the quantile transformation. Theoretical guarantees (Theorem 3.1 and 3.5) show that tail unfairness decreases as \(O(1/\sqrt{N})\) and the excess risk scales with the base predictor’s error plus \(1/\sqrt{N}\). Hence DP‑tails is practical when labeled data are limited but unlabeled data are plentiful.

## Foundational Learning
- **Optimal Transport (Wasserstein distance)**  
  *Why needed*: Provides a geometric framework for minimizing L2 risk under distributional constraints; the barycenter problem yields the minimal fair predictor.  
  *Quick check*: The 2‑Wasserstein distance \(W_2(P,Q)\) equals \(\inf_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)\sim\pi}[(X-Y)^2]^{1/2}\) over couplings \(\pi\); thus minimizing \(W_2^2\) is equivalent to minimizing the mean squared difference under an optimal transport map.

- **Quantile Functions**  
  *Why needed*: The optimal fair predictor is expressed via conditional quantile functions (clipping/averaging), making the solution tractable.  
  *Quick check*: For a continuous random variable with CDF \(F\), its quantile function \(Q(u)=\inf\{x: F(x)\ge u\}\) satisfies that \(Q(U)\) has distribution \(F\) when \(U\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]\). In DP‑tails, we apply \(Q\) to transformed uniform variables to remap distributions.

- **Post‑processing for Fairness**  
  *Why needed*: Enables fairness adjustment after model training without retraining, relying only on unlabeled data—crucial when labeled data are scarce.  
  *Quick check*: Post‑processing modifies the predictor’s outputs based on the sensitive attribute, whereas in‑processing incorporates fairness constraints directly into the training loss. Post‑processing is model‑agnostic and can be applied to any base learner.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Labeled data \(\mathcal{D}_n\) → train base regressor \(\hat{f}\); unlabeled data \(\mathcal{D}_N\) → for each group \(s\): compute \(\hat{f}(X,s)\) with jitter, split into two halves \(\mathcal{I}_s^0,\mathcal{I}_s^1\); from \(\mathcal{I}_s^1\) estimate conditional CDF \(\hat{F}_{\hat{f}|s}\); from \(\mathcal{I}_s^0\) estimate conditional quantile \(\hat{Q}_{\hat{f}|s}\); compute group proportions \(\hat{p}_s\); construct group‑specific quantile adjuster \(\hat{Q}_s\) using clipping at \(\alpha\) and averaging above \(p\) (with small \(\xi\)); final predictor \(\hat{g}_{\alpha,p}(x,s)=\hat{Q}_s\big(\hat{F}_{\hat{f}|s}(\hat{f}(x,s)+\varepsilon)\big)\).

**Critical path**:
1. Train \(\hat{f}\) on labeled \(\mathcal{D}_n\).
2. For each sensitive group \(s\): split the jittered predictions on \(\mathcal{D}_s\) into two disjoint halves; use one half to build empirical CDF \(\hat{F}_{\hat{f}|s}\), the other to build empirical quantile \(\hat{Q}_{\hat{f}|s}\).
3. Compute group frequencies \(\hat{p}_s\).
4. For given \(\alpha\) and \(p\), construct \(\hat{Q}_s\) with clipping and averaging (and \(\xi>0\) if needed).
5. Form \(\hat{g}_{\alpha,p}\) by plug‑in composition.
6. (Optional) Optimize \(p\) by minimizing empirical risk \(\hat{I}_\alpha(p)\) on the calibration set via Brent’s method, then set \(\hat{g}_\alpha = \hat{g}_{\alpha,\hat{p}}\).

**Design tradeoffs**:
- \(\alpha\): Larger \(\alpha\) restricts fairness to more extreme predictions, preserving accuracy but reducing fairness coverage; smaller \(\alpha\) broadens the constrained region, increasing fairness at potential accuracy cost.
- \(p\): The mass placed in the “unfair” head; fixing \(p\) provides direct control over the fraction of individuals not subject to tail fairness. Optimizing \(p\) can lower risk but requires extra computation and may overfit if calibration data are limited.
- \(\xi\): Small positive value ensuring existence of the quantile transform when \(\alpha\) is too large; larger \(\xi\) increases the risk gap but may improve numerical stability.
- Jitter scale \(\sigma\): Must be tiny (e.g., \(10^{-6}\)) to avoid bias while breaking ties and satisfying density assumptions; too large \(\sigma\) distorts predictions.

**Failure signatures**:
- Tail unfairness \(U^\alpha\) remains high after deployment: unlabeled sample size \(N\) too small (recall \(O(1/\sqrt{N})\) rate).
- Excess MSE significantly above baseline: base predictor \(\hat{f}\) is inaccurate, or \(\alpha\) is set too low (over‑constraining the model).
- Empirical conditional CDFs are non‑monotonic or quantiles inconsistent: estimation error due to insufficient group samples or implementation mistakes in splitting/ordering.
- Optimized \(p\) yields unstable or boundary solutions: calibration set too small or Brent’s method not converging; indicates possible overfitting.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: Extending the DP‑tails framework to enforce fairness over a finite union of intervals in the prediction space. The current single‑threshold approach is mathematically convenient; handling multiple intervals would require new optimal transport characterizations.
- **Open Question 2**: Data‑driven selection of the threshold \(\alpha\) and proportion \(p\). The paper leaves the choice of \(\alpha\) to domain expertise; an automatic tuning method (e.g., via cross‑validation or a theoretical criterion) is needed to avoid mis‑specification.
- **Open Question 3**: Benefits of relaxing exact DP‑tails equality to approximate distribution matching (e.g., bounded CDF differences). Exact equality may overly restrict accuracy; an approximate version could improve utility while still controlling unfairness, but its optimal solution and trade‑offs remain unknown.

## Limitations
- Tail threshold \(\alpha\) requires careful domain‑driven selection; inappropriate values render the constraint either vacuous or overly restrictive.
- The data‑splitting design (using separate halves for CDF and quantile estimation) effectively halves the usable unlabeled sample size per task, potentially increasing variance; the impact of this trade‑off on finite‑sample performance is not quantified.
- Experimental validation is limited to three tabular datasets with binary sensitive attributes; the method’s effectiveness for multi‑valued sensitive attributes or high‑dimensional input spaces remains untested.

## Confidence
- Optimal transport formulation (Theorems 2.4, 2.5): High
- Tail unfairness convergence \(O(1/\sqrt{N})\) (Theorem 3.1): Medium
- Risk bound scaling (Theorem 3.5): Medium
- Empirical superiority over global DP: Medium

2. Ablate the base predictor quality by varying its capacity (e.g., Random Forest depth, regularization) to measure how the MSE gap between DP‑tails and global DP shrinks as base accuracy improves, thereby testing the theoretical dependence on \(\|\hat{f}-f^*\|_\infty\).
