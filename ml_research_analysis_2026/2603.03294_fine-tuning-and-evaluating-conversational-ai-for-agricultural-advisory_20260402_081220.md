# Fine-Tuning and Evaluating Conversational AI for Agricultural Advisory

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.03294
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03294
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Hybrid pipeline (LoRA fine-tuning + stitching) doubles fact recall (26.2%→50.3%) and improves F1 (37.2%→51.8%), with smaller models achieving frontier quality at ~85% lower cost.

## Executive Summary
Large language models (LLMs) often produce hallucinated, generic, and culturally misaligned agricultural advice, posing risks to farmer outcomes. This paper presents a hybrid two-stage pipeline to address these issues: first, a LLM is fine-tuned via LoRA on a corpus of expert-curated atomic "GOLDEN FACTS" to optimize fact recall; second, a separate stitching layer transforms the retrieved facts into culturally appropriate and safety-aware responses. Evaluation with DG-EVAL decomposes responses into atomic facts and verifies them against expert ground truth, measuring recall, precision, and contradiction detection. On a dataset of Bihar crop queries, fine-tuning (GPT-4o Mini) increased fact recall from 26.2% to 50.3% and F1 from 37.2% to 51.8% while maintaining ~88% relevance. Smaller fine-tuned models achieved comparable or better factual quality than frontier models at approximately 85% cost reduction. The stitching layer improved safety subscores by 19% relative, and human experts preferred fine-tuned outputs in 65.9% of 308 comparisons. The authors release the farmerchat-prompts library to support reproducible domain-specific AI development.

## Method Summary
The proposed system comprises a fact retriever and a response generator. The retriever is a LLM fine-tuned via LoRA on a set of atomic facts curated by agricultural experts (GOLDEN FACTS). This fine-tuning targets improved recall of relevant facts for a given query. The retrieved facts are then passed to a stitching layer, which assembles them into a coherent response while incorporating cultural appropriateness and safety constraints (e.g., avoiding harmful recommendations) through rule-based filters or additional prompting. Evaluation employs DG-EVAL, a framework that breaks down generated responses into atomic propositions and checks each against expert-curated ground truth, enabling measurement of fact recall, precision, and detection of contradictions (e.g., numeric or polarity conflicts). The approach also includes subscores for safety and relevance.

## Key Results
- Fact recall increased from 26.2% to 50.3% and F1 from 37.2% to 51.8% after fine-tuning on Bihar crop queries.
- Smaller fine-tuned models matched or exceeded frontier model factual quality at ~85% lower cost.
- The stitching layer improved safety subscores by 19% relative, and human experts preferred fine-tuned outputs in 65.9% of 308 comparisons.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The hybrid pipeline succeeds by addressing two distinct challenges: factual accuracy and contextual appropriateness. Fine-tuning via LoRA on expert-curated atomic facts forces the model to internalize a reliable knowledge base, substantially improving recall of relevant information while keeping the parameter footprint small. The separate stitching layer then has the flexibility to rephrase and restructure these facts according to cultural norms and safety guidelines without distorting the underlying content. Moreover, the DG-EVAL evaluation provides targeted feedback on atomic fact correctness, enabling iterative refinement focused on precision and contradiction reduction. The observed gains in fact recall, F1, and safety subscores demonstrate that this decoupled strategy effectively balances the competing demands of accuracy, relevance, and safety in a domain where errors can have serious real-world consequences.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: User Query → Fine-tuned LLM (via LoRA on GOLDEN FACTS) → Retrieved Atomic Facts → Stitching Layer (cultural & safety rules) → Final Response.
- **Critical path**: The sequence from query to final response: fact recall accuracy directly impacts the final response’s correctness; stitching layer then adds cultural and safety context.
- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - Decoupling fact retrieval and response generation enables independent optimization but adds latency and potential inconsistency.  
  - LoRA balances adaptation and retention of general knowledge; too little adaptation hurts recall, too much may cause overfitting.  
  - Smaller fine-tuned models reduce cost but may lose nuanced understanding; frontier models are expensive and may hallucinate more.  
  - The recall–precision trade-off: maximizing recall can introduce noise, while high precision may omit critical facts.
- **Failure signatures**:  
  - Low fact recall: missing key recommendations (e.g., fertilizer type).  
  - Contradictions: conflicting facts within the retrieved set.  
  - Stitching layer over-generation: adding unsourced information not present in retrieved facts.  
  - Cultural misalignment: phrasing that disregards local practices or literacy levels.
- **First experiments**:  
  1. Quantify the relationship between fine-tuning data size (e.g., 1k, 5k, 12k, 62k, 130k) and fact recall/precision on a held-out test set to identify optimal data scale.  
  2. Ablate the stitching layer by disabling its cultural and safety rules, measuring changes in safety subscores and expert-rated relevance.  
  3. Compare the proposed pipeline against a RAG system (using identical GOLDEN FACTS) on accuracy, inference latency, and cost per query under varying knowledge update scenarios.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How can we ensure factual accuracy in agricultural LLM responses while minimizing hallucination?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] RQ1 in Section 1: "How can we ensure factual accuracy in agricultural LLM responses while minimizing hallucination?"
- **Why unresolved**: Fine-tuning improves recall but precision drops (~11 pp) and contradictions persist (Section 5.2). The recall–precision trade-off remains suboptimal for safety-critical domains where omissions (low recall) may be riskier than marginally incorrect details (low precision). Current contradiction detection only covers explicit numeric/polarity conflicts and misses subtle contextual errors.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Longitudinal field trials correlating specific model error types (e.g., dosage errors, missing safety steps) with actual farmer outcomes (yield loss, health incidents). Ablation studies isolating fine-tuning data characteristics (e.g., fact specificity, confidence scoring) and architectural changes (e.g., constrained decoding, fact-grounded prompting) to reduce hallucinations without sacrificing recall.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: How should domain-specific outputs be evaluated beyond holistic scoring to capture safety-critical errors?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] RQ2 in Section 1: "How should domain-specific outputs be evaluated beyond holistic scoring to capture safety-critical errors?"
- **Why unresolved**: DG-EVAL improves on prior frameworks by using expert-curated atomic facts and contradiction detection, but it still has gaps: (1) it does not account for temporal validity (e.g., outdated pesticide recommendations), (2) "safety" is narrowly defined as direct factual contradiction, missing cultural misalignment or omission of critical context (e.g., soil type dependencies), and (3) evaluation is static, not assessing real-world applicability or farmer decision-making impact.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Development of time-aware evaluation datasets with versioned ground truth (tracking regulatory updates, new cultivars). Expert audits of model responses in controlled scenario-based evaluations simulating farmer constraints (e.g., input availability, literacy). Integration of agricultural outcome proxies (e.g., "would this advice prevent crop loss?") into evaluation metrics.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Can fine-tuning with curated agricultural facts improve retrieval without sacrificing conversational quality?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] RQ3 in Section 1: "Can fine-tuning with curated agricultural facts improve retrieval without sacrificing conversational quality?"
- **Why unresolved**: The paper decouples fact retrieval (fine-tuned model) and conversational delivery (stitching layer), showing maintained conversationality. However, it is unknown whether end-to-end fine-tuning on both factuality and persona could yield better overall utility, or if decoupling inevitably creates suboptimal trade-offs. The stitching layer also sometimes adds unsourced information (Appendix A.6), suggesting the boundary between retrieval and generation is not fully controlled.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Direct comparison of decoupled vs. jointly fine-tuned models on a unified metric combining fact recall, precision, and conversationality (e.g., weighted sum). Analysis of stitching layer failures: quantifying added information rate, and testing constrained decoding or fact-aware prompts to prevent over-generation. Human studies with farmers comparing decoupled vs. end-to-end outputs for trust and usability.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: How does the fine-tuning approach compare to retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) in terms of accuracy, cost, and deployability for agricultural advisory?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] Section 2.2 discusses RAG as an alternative but states "a direct empirical comparison remains important future work." Section 6.1 acknowledges the need to determine "when to use this approach vs. RAG."
- **Why unresolved**: The paper only evaluates fine-tuning (with stitching). RAG could offer dynamic knowledge updates and provenance, potentially improving factuality for rapidly changing topics (e.g., pest outbreaks), but at the cost of inference-time retrieval infrastructure. The trade-offs in low-connectivity settings, cost per query, and robustness to corpus gaps are unexplored. The hybrid architecture claims to avoid RAG dependencies, but this is not empirically validated.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Head-to-head benchmark on the Bihar test set comparing: (1) fine-tuned+stitched model, (2) RAG using the same curated GOLDEN FACTS corpus, (3) a true hybrid (fine-tuned retriever + RAG). Metrics: fact recall/precision, latency, cost per 500-token response, and sensitivity to simulated knowledge updates (e.g., injecting new pesticide recommendations). Field deployment pilots in low-connectivity areas measuring reliability and maintenance overhead.

### Open Question 5
- **Question**: What enables the non-monotonic performance as training data scale increases, and how can we optimize the quality–quantity trade-off in domain-specific fine-tuning?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] Section 5.4 ablation shows 12k human-curated data outperforms 62k mixed-source data, but 130k mixed data recovers and exceeds performance (F1 52.7% vs 51.8%). The authors hypothesize a distributional shift at mid-scales but do not test it.
- **Why unresolved**: The ablation reveals that mixing synthetic data initially harms performance before volume compensates, but the mechanisms are unclear. Is it due to label noise, distribution shift, or model capacity? Without understanding, curation strategies remain empirical. The "quality–quantity threshold" is noted but not characterized, leaving practitioners without guidance on optimal data mixes for given resource constraints.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Controlled experiments varying the proportion of human-curated vs. synthetic data at finer granularity (e.g., 20k, 40k, 80k, 160k) with detailed noise analysis (e.g., fact error rates in synthetic sources). Measuring model confidence distributions and fact diversity across scales. Studying whether specific synthetic sources (e.g., video RAG vs. LLM synthesis) drive the mid-scale degradation.

## Limitations
- Generalizability beyond Bihar’s specific crops and socio-cultural context is untested.
- The stitching layer’s precise mechanisms for ensuring cultural alignment and safety are opaque, hindering reproducibility.
- Long-term real-world impact on farmer outcomes (e.g., yield, income) remains unmeasured; response quality does not guarantee adoption or efficacy.

## Confidence
- Fact recall/F1 gains (26.2%→50.3%, 37.2%→51.8%): **High**
- Cost reduction (~85% vs. frontier models): **High**
- Human expert preference (65.9% in 308 comparisons): **Medium**
- Safety subscore improvement (+19% relative): **Medium**

## Next Checks

- Ablation of stitching layer: Systematically remove/modify components (e.g., cultural rules, safety filters) to parse their individual contributions to safety and relevance scores.
