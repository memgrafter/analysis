# When Models Manipulate Manifolds: The Geometry of a Counting Task
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.04480
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04480
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Character counts are represented on sparse, low‑dimensional curved manifolds; attention heads twist these manifolds to estimate line boundaries, and orthogonal subspace arrangement yields a linear separator for newline insertion.

## Executive Summary
The paper uncovers how Claude 3.5 Haiku performs linebreaking in fixed‑width text—a task requiring implicit character counting. Using sparse crosscoders, the authors discover that character counts are encoded on a one‑dimensional curved manifold within a low‑dimensional subspace, tiled by discrete sparse features analogous to biological place cells. Boundary detection is achieved by attention heads that “twist” the count manifold to align with a line‑width manifold, producing a sharp signal proportional to the characters remaining. The final newline decision combines this estimate with the length of the next token; these two quantities occupy nearly orthogonal subspaces, making the condition (next‑token length > characters remaining) linearly separable. Causal interventions—ablating the count subspace, activation patching—confirm the mechanisms, while visual distractors (e.g., “@@”) hijack attention sinks and disrupt counting. The work delivers a detailed geometric picture of a simple arithmetic process in a language model.

## Method Summary
The analysis uses a synthetic dataset created by stripping newlines from a text corpus and reinserting them at word boundaries such that each line contains between 15 and 150 characters (targets vary per example). Activations from Claude 3.5 Haiku are collected for all tokens. A weakly causal crosscoder with 10 million features is trained on the residual stream across layers; its sparse features automatically expose interpretable patterns (e.g., “count is in range X‑Y”). Linear probes and PCA elucidate the geometry: a linear probe after layer 1 predicts the line character count with R² = 0.985, and mean activations per count trace a helical curve in the top six PCA dimensions. Attention heads are examined via QK/OV decomposition: query/key matrices rotate the count and line‑width representations so their dot product peaks when the count is near the line width. Late‑layer activations show that vectors for “characters remaining” and “next‑token length” are nearly orthogonal; their sum yields a linearly separable signal for newlines. Causal validation includes ablating principal components of the count subspace (which harms newline loss but not other tokens), activation patching to shift count estimates, and testing visual‑illusion distractors.

## Key Results
- Character counts lie on a 1D curved manifold in a low‑dimensional subspace, tiled by sparse features analogous to place cells.
- Boundary detection heads twist the count manifold via QK rotations, creating off‑diagonal alignments with line‑width features that estimate characters remaining; multiple heads cover overlapping offsets.
- The final newline decision is enabled by arranging “characters remaining” and “next‑token length” in near‑orthogonal subspaces, yielding a linear separator.
- Ablating the count subspace increases loss on newline tokens; activation patching causally shifts predictions; inserting “@@” disrupts attention sinks and impairs counting.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Character counts lie on sparse, low‑dimensional, curved 1D manifolds
The model accumulates token lengths via Layer 0 attention heads into an initial estimate. Multiple heads with different offsets (“sinks”) sum their outputs, each contributing a linear component. Together they form a high‑curvature curve—a manifold—in a low‑dimensional subspace (top 6 PCA dimensions capture ~95% variance). Sparse crosscoder features parametrize this curve locally, with each feature activating for a specific count range. This curved embedding optimizes the trade‑off between capacity (few dimensions) and resolution (distinguishing adjacent counts), allowing a norm‑stable representation of ~150 discrete values. **Evidence:** a linear probe after layer 1 achieves R² = 0.985; PCA of mean count vectors shows a twisting helix; crosscoder features tile the count space with overlapping tuning curves.

### Mechanism 2: Boundary detection via QK manifold twisting
A separate family of features represents the line width (total allowed characters, inferred from prior newlines). In boundary detection heads (e.g., L1H2, L1H3, L1H6), the query matrix (W_Q) transforms the count representation and the key matrix (W_K) transforms the line‑width representation. The learned W_Q and W_K act as a joint rotation/twist: they align the vector for count *i* with the vector for line width *k* only when *i ≈ k − ϵ* (i.e., when characters remaining is small and positive). The attention score then spikes, and the head’s output (via its OV circuit) activates a “characters remaining” feature proportional to that difference. **Evidence:** after multiplying count and line‑width probes by W_Q and W_K, the cosine similarity matrix shows an off‑diagonal peak whose offset matches each head’s functional range; multiple heads tile the “characters remaining” space with overlapping coverage.

### Mechanism 3: Linear decision via orthogonal subspace arrangement
The outputs of the boundary heads (characters remaining) and a separate circuit estimating the length of the next predicted token are summed into the residual stream at a late layer (~90% depth). Their respective mean activation vectors occupy approximately orthogonal subspaces—PCA of their union shows two distinct clusters. Because addition in orthogonal directions preserves separation, the sum’s norm/direction depends monotonically on the difference between the two values. A downstream linear readout (e.g., in the logits) can then separate “break line” from “continue” with a simple hyperplane. **Evidence:** PCA of pairwise sums yields two clusters; a linear classifier on the two mean vectors achieves high AUC; the cosine similarity between the two probe vectors is near zero.

## Foundational Learning
### Sparse Autoencoders / Dictionary Learning
**Why needed:** The discovery process begins with a weakly causal crosscoder—a sparse dictionary model trained across layers—that automatically identifies ~10 million interpretable features (e.g., “current character count is 35‑50”). These features are far more mechanistically useful than raw neurons or linear probes and reveal the existence of feature families that tile the count space.  
**Quick check:** If you train a sparse autoencoder and find a feature that activates for a specific character count range, how would you verify it is causal (not just correlated) using the paper’s intervention methodology?

### Feature Manifolds & Duality
**Why needed:** The discrete feature view (many tiling features) and the continuous manifold view (a curved 1D curve) are dual descriptions of the same representation. Features are how you discover the representation; the manifold geometry explains how computation (twisting, orthogonality) is performed.  
**Quick check:** Given decoder vectors of features that tile a scalar variable (like character count), what geometric property would you compute on their pairwise cosine similarities to confirm they lie on a “rippled” low‑dimensional manifold?

### QK/OV Circuit Decomposition
**Why needed:** To understand how attention heads manipulate representations, the paper decomposes each head into a QK circuit (where it attends, a linear transform) and an OV circuit (what it writes, another linear transform). This is the standard mechanistic interpretability lens for attention.  
**Quick check:** If an attention head’s QK circuit twists a count manifold, what would you expect to see when you multiply linear probes for character count and line width through its W_Q and W_K matrices and compute pairwise cosine similarities?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Token embedding (initial token length) → Layer 0 counting heads → Layer 1 boundary heads → Late‑layer orthogonal addition → Output logits.

**Critical path:** The core flow starts with token embeddings that encode each token’s character length. Layer 0 attention heads sum these lengths over tokens since the last newline, producing a coarse character count. Layer 1 boundary heads twist this count representation against line‑width features to estimate characters remaining. At a late layer (~90% depth), this “remaining” vector is combined with a “next‑token length” vector in nearly orthogonal subspaces; the summed representation is then read out by the final linear layer (logits) to predict newline vs. continue.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Curvature vs. capacity*: A curved manifold provides high resolution between adjacent counts but requires more dimensions; the model embeds ~150 values in ~6 dimensions, accepting some overlap.  
- *Distributed vs. monolithic computation*: No single head produces the full curved manifold; curvature is built by summing outputs of multiple early heads, increasing circuit complexity but enabling flexible tuning.  
- *Orthogonalization vs. robustness*: Placing the two relevant quantities in orthogonal subspaces ensures linear separability of their sum but may reduce the robustness of each representation to noise or interference from MLPs.

**Failure signatures:**  
- *Visual illusions*: Inserting strings like “@@” hijacks the attention sink mechanism of counting heads, causing them to attend to the distractor instead of the previous newline, corrupting the count estimate.  
- *Loss spikes on newlines*: Ablating the top six PCA components of the mean character‑count vectors dramatically increases cross‑entropy loss specifically on newline tokens.  
- *Loss of tiling*: If boundary heads do not have staggered offsets (i.e., they all cover similar remaining‑ranges), the representation of “characters remaining” becomes coarse, leading to higher loss on linebreak decisions near the gaps.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Probe validation:** Train a linear probe to predict line character count from the residual after layer 1; expect R² > 0.98.  
2. **Ablation of count subspace:** Zero‑out the top 6 PCA components of the mean activation vectors for each count and measure the increase in cross‑entropy loss on newline versus non‑newline tokens.  
3. **Visual illusion test:** For a prompt that normally predicts a newline, insert “@@” 20 characters before the end. Observe the drop in newline probability and use QK attribution to confirm that counting/boundary heads now attend to “@@” instead of the preceding newline.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How does the model quantify and represent uncertainty in its character count estimates, and how does this uncertainty propagate through the linebreaking decision?  
2. What algorithm does the model use to estimate global line width from multiple prior lines of text, and how does it update this estimate across a document?  
3. To what extent do MLP layers contribute to the character counting and boundary detection algorithms, beyond the attention heads examined?  
4. What precise mechanism causes sequences like “@@” to disrupt attention sinks and create visual illusions, and how general is this hijacking effect across different distracting tokens?

## Limitations
- The analysis is restricted to a proprietary model (Claude 3.5 Haiku), preventing exact replication of activations and the 10‑million‑feature crosscoder.  
- Details of the synthetic dataset (source corpus, word‑boundary algorithm) are not fully described, which may affect count distributions and activation statistics.  
- Causal validation is limited to ablating the character‑count subspace; the twisting and orthogonality mechanisms lack direct head‑level interventions.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross‑model manifold replication:** Train linear probes on multiple open‑source models (e.g., Pythia‑70M, GPT‑2) using the same synthetic linebreaking dataset. Compute mean activations per count, perform PCA, and verify whether a similar curved manifold (helical trajectory) appears in the top 6 dimensions.  
2. **Causal test of QK twisting:** For an identified boundary head (e.g., L1H2), replace its W_Q matrix with a random orthogonal rotation while keeping W_K unchanged. Measure linebreaking accuracy and the cosine similarity matrix of count vs. line‑width probes after transformation. A disappearance of the off‑diagonal peak and a performance drop would confirm the twisting mechanism’s necessity.  
3. **Orthogonality intervention:** At the late layer where “characters remaining” and “next‑token length” are combined, apply a learned linear rotation that aligns their subspaces (minimizing cosine similarity between their mean vectors). Compare cross‑entropy loss on newline tokens before and after alignment; a significant loss increase would indicate that orthogonality is functionally critical.
