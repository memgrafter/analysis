# Digital Linguistic Bias in Spanish: Evidence from Lexical Variation in LLMs
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.09346
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09346
- Reference count: 13
- Primary result: LLMs exhibit variable accuracy across Spanish dialects, with Spain, Equatorial Guinea, Mexico & Central America, and La Plata River varieties recognized best, while Chilean Spanish is the most challenging; performance does not correlate with country-level digital resource volume.

## Executive Summary
This paper evaluates how well large language models (LLMs) capture geographic lexical variation in Spanish, a language with rich regional diversity. The authors use the expert-curated VARILEX database, which contains 934 items and 9,057 variants across 21 Spanish-speaking countries, to probe LLMs as virtual informants. Two survey-style question formats are employed: Yes-No questions (YNQF) and multiple-choice questions (MCQF). Performance is measured by F1 score for YNQF and a chance-corrected adjusted Jaccard coefficient (Jadj) for MCQF. Across GPT models, systematic performance differences emerge: dialects from Spain, Equatorial Guinea, Mexico & Central America, and the La Plata River are recognized most accurately, while Chilean Spanish is consistently the most difficult. Notably, model performance does not correlate with estimated country-level digital resource volume, suggesting that factors beyond data quantity shape dialectal representation in LLMs.

## Method Summary
The study adopts a zero-shot probing framework. For each item in VARILEX, the LLM is prompted to act as a native speaker from a specific country. In the YNQF format, the model judges whether a given variant is typically used for a concept (e.g., “¿Suele utilizar el término ‘coche’ para referirse a ‘automóvil’?”), requiring a binary “Sí” or “No”. In MCQF, all variants for an item are listed (with randomized order) and the model selects all that apply, outputting a slash-separated list of numeric indices. A sample of 10,000 YNQF instances (out of a possible 190,158) is used to limit API calls, while all 17,911 MCQF items are evaluated. Responses are validated for format compliance; invalid outputs are discarded. For YNQF, country-level F1 is computed; for MCQF, the adjusted Jaccard coefficient (Jadj) corrects raw Jaccard overlap for the expected agreement under a hypergeometric null model, set-size bias, and chance. Experiments were conducted on GPT‑4o (YNQF) and GPT‑5.1 (MCQF) with temperature 0.

## Key Results
- Spain, Equatorial Guinea, Mexico & Central America, and La Plata River dialects achieve the highest accuracy (e.g., Spain: YNQF F1 = 0.723, MCQF Jadj = 0.508).
- Chilean Spanish is the most difficult dialect (YNQF F1 = 0.372, MCQF Jadj = 0.141).
- Overall LLM performance shows no correlation with estimated country-level digital resource volume, contradicting the assumption that more data leads to better dialectal representation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The evaluation’s validity rests on using a gold‑standard resource that faithfully captures Spanish lexical variation across countries.
- **Mechanism:** The VARILEX database (934 items, 9,057 variants, 21 countries) provides binary (“+” vs “–”) annotations of predominant usage. These labels serve as the ground truth for both Yes‑No and multiple‑choice probing.
- **Core assumption:** VARILEX reflects real‑world, contemporary dialectal usage patterns.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “We exploited VARILEX, a large‑scale, expert‑curated database of Spanish lexical variation.”
  - [section] Section 3.1 details VARILEX’s structure, annotation scheme, and coverage.
  - [corpus] (weak) The surrounding literature mentions other dialectal resources but does not directly validate VARILEX’s reliability for LLM probing; note limited corroboration.
- **Break condition:** If the expert curation is biased toward certain regions or outdated, the results may systematically misrepresent model abilities.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Survey‑style question formats (YNQF and MCQF) allow systematic, comparable probing of an LLM’s dialectal knowledge.
- **Mechanism:** YNQF asks whether a specific variant is typically used in a given country (binary judgment). MCQF asks for all variants typically used for a concept (set selection). The prompts explicitly instruct the model to ignore previous questions, treating each item independently.
- **Core assumption:** The LLM’s response to these prompts approximates the judgment of a native speaker from the specified country.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “We probe their dialectal knowledge using two survey‑style question formats — Yes–No questions and multiple‑choice questions.”
  - [section] Sections 3.2 and 4.2.1 describe the templates and the fact that all models outperformed baselines, indicating they capture some dialectal signal.
  - [corpus] (weak) Related studies like SESGO and DialectalArabicMMLU use similar probing, but direct support is sparse.
- **Break condition:** If the model frequently violates format constraints (e.g., non‑Yes/No answers) or shows sensitivity to option order, the measured performance may be artefactual.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The adjusted Jaccard coefficient (Jadj) provides a fair, chance‑corrected metric for the multiple‑choice set‑selection task.
- **Mechanism:** Jadj subtracts the expected Jaccard overlap under a hypergeometric random model from the raw Jaccard index and normalizes by (1 – expected). This removes inflation from large variant sets.
- **Core assumption:** The null model (random, uniform selection of s gold and t predicted variants from N total) accurately approximates chance agreement.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section] Section 3.3 derives Jadj; Section 5.2 contrasts conventional Jaccard (which correlates with number of variants, ρ = 0.655) with Jadj (no significant correlation, ρ = 0.183), demonstrating its correction.
  - [section] The metric shows expected properties: zero when observed similarity equals chance; negative values clipped to zero.
  - [corpus] (weak) The corpus includes bias‑evaluation work but does not directly assess Jadj; evidence is limited to internal validation.
- **Break condition:** If the hypergeometric assumption fails (e.g., non‑uniform variant probabilities), Jadj may over‑ or under‑correct, distorting comparisons.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Geographic lexical variation in Spanish  
  - Why needed here: Spanish exhibits multiple regional variants for a single concept (e.g., “car” = coche, carro, auto). Understanding this polyvocality is essential to interpret why a model might correctly identify some countries’ usages and fail others (e.g., Chile’s distinct lexical set).  
  - Quick check: “List three Spanish words for ‘vehicle’ and indicate the countries where each is predominant.”

- **Concept:** Prompt engineering for dialectal probing  
  - Why needed here: The entire experimental design treats LLMs as virtual informants; the prompts must simulate a dialect survey and produce constrained outputs (Sí/No; numeric selections). Without careful design, the model’s response format could introduce noise.  
  - Quick check: “Draft a Yes‑No prompt that asks an LLM to respond as a speaker from Mexico about the term ‘coche’. How would you enforce strict format compliance?”

- **Concept:** Evaluation metrics for multi‑label classification  
  - Why needed here: MCQF yields sets of selected variants; raw overlap measures (e.g., simple Jaccard) are biased by the number of correct options. The adjusted Jaccard accounts for chance, enabling fair cross‑item aggregation.  
  - Quick check: “Why does the number of correct variants per item matter when choosing an evaluation metric for MCQF? What does Jadj correct for?”

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
- Data loader: reads VARILEX (JSON/TSV) and yields items, variants, country annotations.  
- Prompt generator: for each (item, country) produces YNQF and MCQF strings following templates in Appendix A, randomizing option order for MCQF.  
- LLM client: wraps API (e.g., OpenAI chat completion) with temperature 0, max‑tokens tuned, and handles retries.  
- Response parser: validates YNQF (only “Sí”/“No”), parses MCQF numeric lists (split by “/”, sort ascending, map to variant indices).  
- Evaluator: computes F1 (binary) for YNQF, Jadj for MCQF; aggregates at country and dialect‑area levels.  
- Analyzer: produces tables, correlates with digital resource volume, generates plots.

**Critical path:**  
1. Load VARILEX and pre‑process (extract variants, gold “+” masks).  
2. Generate all required prompts (sample 10 k YNQF, full MCQF) with randomized order.  
3. Call LLM API, collect responses, filter invalid.  
4. Parse responses, compare to gold masks.  
5. Compute metrics, aggregate, and output results.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Coverage vs. cost: YNQF uses a random sample to limit API calls; MCQF uses full set but could be sampled if needed.  
- Randomization: shuffling variant order is essential to avoid positional bias (see Pezeshkpour and Hruschka, 2024).  
- Strict validation: discarding non‑conforming responses improves metric reliability but reduces sample size; monitor rejection rates.  
- Extensibility: current pipeline is hard‑coded to GPT‑family; abstracting the LLM client would allow testing other models.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High invalid response rate (>5 %) indicates model not adhering to format; revisit prompt wording or temperature.  
- F1 scores strongly correlated with label prevalence (ρ > 0.8) suggest metric artefact; switch to ∆F1 or use Jadj for consistency.  
- Extreme country‑level swings (e.g., Chile very low) could reflect data issues; verify VARILEX coverage and sampling.  
- Correlation between model response set size and accuracy disappearing after chance‑correction would indicate spurious matches.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline replication:** Run the pipeline on a tiny subset (e.g., 50 items) with gpt‑4o and confirm that F1 and Jadj are roughly in line with paper’s values (e.g., F1 ≈ 0.5 for Spain).  
2. **Metric sanity check:** Generate synthetic MCQF data with known gold sets of varying sizes; ensure Jadj does not correlate with number of gold variants, whereas raw Jaccard does.  
3. **Country profiling:** Execute full YNQF and MCQF; reproduce Table 5 and Table 6, checking that Spain and Argentina rank high and Chile ranks low. If patterns diverge, investigate sampling or parsing bugs.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Why is Chilean Spanish the most challenging dialect for LLMs to recognize lexically, despite Chile having one of the largest digital footprints?
   - Basis in paper: [explicit] "Chile proves particularly difficult... This unexpected outcome warrants further investigation" (Section 5.3.1).
   - Why unresolved: The inverse relationship with digital resource volume contradicts assumptions that more data improves dialectal representation, pointing to unexamined factors like lexical uniqueness or corpus biases.
   - What evidence would resolve it: Analyze training corpora for Chilean Spanish token frequency and lexical overlap with other dialects; conduct targeted error analysis on Chilean-specific items from VARILEX.

2. How do sociolinguistic variables (e.g., age, gender, socioeconomic status) modulate LLM responses in dialectal lexical tasks?
   - Basis in paper: [explicit] "Explicitly encoding such variables in prompts may affect variant selection and warrant systematic investigation" (Limitations section).
   - Why unresolved: The study treats LLMs as monolithic "virtual informants" per country, abstracting away within-dialect variation that influences real-world language use and may skew model outputs.
   - What evidence would resolve it: Systematically vary prompts to include sociolinguistic descriptors (e.g., "as a young person from Chile"), collect model responses, and correlate with known sociolinguistic patterns from speaker surveys.

3. To what extent does subnational dialectal variation within Spanish-speaking countries affect LLM performance on lexical recognition?
   - Basis in paper: [explicit] "Future work could refine this approach by incorporating subnational dialectal divisions" (Limitations).
   - Why unresolved: Country-level evaluation masks regional diversity (e.g., Andean vs. coastal Spanish in Peru), potentially inflating or deflating performance scores and misrepresenting dialectal knowledge.
   - What evidence would resolve it: Use subnational data from dialectological resources (e.g., regional VARILEX annotations), probe models at finer geographic scales, and compare aggregation effects against current country-level metrics.

4. How do open-weight language models (e.g., Llama, Mistral) capture Spanish dialectal lexical variation compared to proprietary GPT models?
   - Basis in paper: [explicit] "extending the analysis to open-weight models would allow for finer-grained probabilistic evaluation and greater transparency" (Limitations).
   - Why unresolved: Findings are restricted to GPT-family models; open-weight models may differ in training data composition or architecture, affecting dialectal bias, and transparency is needed for reproducible bias assessment.
   - What evidence would resolve it: Replicate the YNQF and MCQF experiments with state-of-the-art open-weight models, compare performance and error profiles, and analyze publicly available training data sources for dialectal representation.

## Limitations
- VARILEX may not fully represent contemporary informal usage that LLMs encounter in training data.
- Discarding non‑conforming responses could skew results if certain dialects elicit more formatting errors.
- The study evaluates only closed‑source GPT families; open‑weight or non‑autoregressive models might behave differently.
