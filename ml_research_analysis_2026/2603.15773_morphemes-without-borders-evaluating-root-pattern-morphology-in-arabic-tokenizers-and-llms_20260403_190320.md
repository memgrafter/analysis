# Morphemes Without Borders: Evaluating Root-Pattern Morphology in Arabic Tokenizers and LLMs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.15773
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15773
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** High-fertility tokenizers (e.g., GPT-4) achieve near-perfect nonce root-pattern generation (>96%) despite poor morphological alignment, while morphology-aware tokenizers (e.g., ALLAM) exhibit sharp drops on nonce words (20%/52%), indicating memorization over productive rule learning.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates how Arabic tokenizers and large language models (LLMs) handle root-pattern morphology, a non-concatenative process central to Arabic derivational morphology. Counterintuitively, tokenizers that over-segment words (high fertility) and poorly align with morpheme boundaries enable LLMs to achieve the highest accuracy on novel (nonce) root-pattern generation, while models with morphology-aligned tokenizers perform poorly on nonce words. Correlation analysis shows standard morphological alignment metrics are weak or zero predictors of generalization. The findings challenge the assumption that morphology-aware tokenization is necessary for effective morphological processing in Arabic and suggest that instruction-tuned LLMs can learn productive rules from fragmented subword representations.

## Method Summary
The study employs a two-part evaluation. First, tokenizer morphological alignment is measured using gold-standard segmented Arabic corpora (ATB3, BOLT) via metrics: fertility (tokens per word), boundary F1, morpheme F1, and morpheme coverage rate (MCR). Second, LLM performance is tested on a novel dataset of real and nonce Arabic roots combined with derivational patterns, assessing exact-match accuracy of generated forms under English/Arabic prompts. Correlation between alignment metrics and generation scores is computed across seven models (Arabic-centric and multilingual).

## Key Results
- GPT-4 tokenizer, with highest fertility (>3) and lowest MCR, achieves 96.92% (real roots) and 97% (nonce roots) accuracy in one-shot English prompts.
- Arabic-centric models (ALLAM, FANAR) with better alignment show drastic nonce accuracy drops (20%, 52% respectively).
- Alignment metrics (boundary F1, MCR) have zero or weak negative correlation with generation performance; fertility shows only weak positive correlation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Over-segmentation in tokenizers (high fertility) does not hinder and may even facilitate morphological rule learning in LLMs for non-concatenative languages like Arabic.  
**Mechanism:** Tokenizers that produce many short, sub-morphemic tokens allow the LLM to recombine these atomic units flexibly during instruction tuning, enabling it to abstract templatic patterns from statistical co-occurrences rather than relying on intact morpheme chunks.  
**Core assumption:** LLMs can learn productive morphological transformations from distributional patterns in subword sequences without requiring explicit, linguistically correct morpheme boundaries.  
**Evidence anchors:** GPT-4 with fertility >3 achieves >96% nonce accuracy; correlation shows weak positive fertility link.  
**Break condition:** Models with high fertility consistently fail on patterns requiring strict morpheme integrity (e.g., infix attachment to specific root positions), but GPT-4 succeeds.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Standard morphological alignment metrics (e.g., Boundary F1, Morpheme Coverage Rate) are poor predictors of an LLM's ability to generalize Arabic root-pattern morphology productively.  
**Mechanism:** These metrics measure static segmentation fidelity against a gold standard but fail to capture the dynamic, compositional reasoning an LLM performs during generation. An LLM may learn to manipulate internal consonantal structures and apply templatic rules from fragmented token representations, making alignment scores irrelevant to its functional competence.  
**Core assumption:** The relationship between tokenizer structure and downstream morphological performance is non-linear and mediated by the model's architecture and training objective, not directly by segmentation accuracy.  
**Evidence anchors:** Correlation analysis confirms zero or weak negative correlation between alignment metrics and generation performance; paper argues for defining morphological competence by generalization beyond training vocabulary.  
**Break condition:** A consistent, strong positive correlation (>0.7) across diverse models and languages in a controlled study where tokenizer design is the only variable.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Strong instruction-following capability in modern LLMs can compensate for poor morphological tokenization, acting as a functional substitute that enables rule-like morphological generation.  
**Mechanism:** Instruction tuning teaches the model to interpret and execute abstract transformation rules (e.g., "apply pattern X to root Y"). The model then uses its internal subword representations—even if poorly aligned with morphemes—to reconstruct the target word by manipulating characters within tokens based on the prompt's template, effectively performing on-the-fly morphological analysis.  
**Core assumption:** The prompting framework and the model's alignment to human instructions are primary drivers of morphological behavior, overshadowing tokenizer effects.  
**Evidence anchors:** GPT-4 and GPT-4o show minimal differences across shot settings; most models perform better with English prompts than Arabic, suggesting prompt language and instruction data matter more than tokenizer Arabic-specificity.  
**Break condition:** Models with strong general instruction-following show catastrophic performance on root-pattern tasks when prompted in a highly trained language (e.g., English) or with maximally explicit instructions.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Non-concatenative morphology**  
  *Why needed:* Arabic root-pattern system is the core phenomenon under study. Understanding that roots are abstract consonant templates (e.g., /k-t-b/) and patterns are templatic vowel/consonant frameworks (e.g., /ma-...-u:b/) is essential to grasp why standard BPE tokenization is mismatched.  
  *Quick check:* If the root is /q-l-ˤ/ ("heavy") and the pattern is /ma-...-a:ʃ/, what is the resulting word stem? Answer: /maqla:ʃ/.
- **Concept: Tokenizer fertility**  
  *Why needed:* Fertility (average tokens per word) is the primary metric used to characterize tokenizer behavior (over-segmentation vs. compression). It's central to the paper's surprising finding that high fertility correlates weakly with performance.  
  *Quick check:* Would a tokenizer with fertility 1.2 for Arabic text be under-segmenting or over-segmentation relative to word boundaries? Answer: Likely under-segmenting/compressing, as the average word has more than 1.2 morphemes.
- **Concept: Productivity vs. memorization in evaluation**  
  *Why needed:* The paper's key methodological innovation is using nonce roots to test productive generalization versus lexical memorization. Distinguishing these is crucial for interpreting results.  
  *Quick check:* Why is performance on nonce roots (e.g., /*s-l-ˤ*/ with pattern /fi-...-a:l/) a stronger probe of morphological knowledge than performance on attested roots? Answer: Nonce roots cannot be memorized; success requires applying the pattern template to novel consonant slots.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Tokenizer -> LLM (Backbone + Instruction Tuning) -> Evaluation Dataset -> Metrics Engine -> Prompting System

**Critical path:**  
1. Select tokenizer (from model) → segment alignment corpus → compute alignment metrics.  
2. For each LLM: feed productivity probe prompts → collect generations → compare to gold forms → aggregate accuracy.  
3. Correlate alignment metrics (from step 1) with generation scores (from step 2) across models.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Arabic vs. Multilingual Tokenizer: Arabic-centric tokenizers (ALLAM, FANAR) may better preserve morphemes but could have less balanced vocabularies. Multilingual tokenizers (GPT-4, LLaMA) may fragment more but benefit from larger, more diverse training data.  
- Prompt Language: English prompts generally yield higher scores, possibly due to model's instruction-tuning data being English-heavy or the cognitive ease of processing templatic instructions in a non-templatic language.  
- Evaluation Leniency: Accepting correct words embedded in longer outputs (lenient matching) controls for chatter but may inflate scores for models that don't follow instructions precisely.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High fertility + low nonce accuracy: Extreme over-segmentation destroys all morphemic signal (not observed; GPT-4 defies this).  
- High alignment + low nonce accuracy: Tokenizer preserves morphemes but model memorizes only whole words, failing to recombine (observed in ALLAM: high MCR, 20% nonce accuracy).  
- Low affix accuracy but high root-pattern accuracy: Model understands templatic root-pattern insertion but fails on concatenative affix ordering (e.g., LLaMA3: 10% nonce root-pattern, 68% affix).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Why do high-fertility tokenizers (e.g., GPT-4) enable superior nonce word generation despite poor morphological alignment?**  
   Basis: Paper shows GPT-4 has highest fertility and >96% nonce accuracy, while morphology-aligned tokenizers fail. Correlation is not causal; confounded by model scale, training data, architecture.  
   Evidence needed: Controlled experiments training identical architectures with tokenizers varying only in fertility, holding training data constant.
2. **Do LLMs develop internal representations of Arabic root-pattern morphology that operate independently of token boundaries?**  
   Basis: Authors speculate "character-level interpolation" but study cannot probe deep representations due to conflating instruction-following with reasoning.  
   Evidence needed: Direct representational analysis (probing hidden states, attention patterns) when generating root-pattern forms to test if embeddings encode root consonants and pattern templates as separable units.
3. **Is the poor nonce word performance of morphology-aware tokenized models (e.g., FANAR) caused by tokenization design, model scale, or instruction-following limitations?**  
   Basis: FANAR uses morpheme-informed tokenizer but scores only 52% on nonce roots; could stem from smaller size or instruction-tuning gaps.  
   Evidence needed: Ablation training multiple model scales with same morphology-aware tokenizer on identical data.
4. **Can an adaptive hybrid tokenizer—preserving intact morphemes where possible but falling back to character-level processing for novel forms—improve morphological generalization without harming overall language modeling?**  
   Basis: Authors suggest this direction, noting rigid morphemic segmentation may hinder flexibility for unseen combinations.  
   Evidence needed: Develop and evaluate a tokenizer that dynamically selects between morpheme-based and character-level segmentation, tested on Arabic productivity alongside standard benchmarks.

## Limitations
- Small model sample (7) yields weak correlation statistics; no confidence intervals.
- Focus solely on Arabic derivational morphology; findings may not generalize to other language types.
- English prompts may bias results toward models with richer English instruction data.

## Confidence
- **High:** High-fertility tokenizers enable rule learning (GPT-4 >96% nonce accuracy).
- **Medium:** Alignment metrics poorly predict generalization (weak/zero correlations observed); instruction-following compensates (English-prompt advantage).
- **Low:** Causal mechanism behind fertility's positive effect; generalizability beyond Arabic.

## Next Checks
1. **Exact reproducibility:** Use authors’ code and pinned tokenizer versions to verify all scores.
2. **Wider model coverage:** Test additional recent models (e.g., GPT-4.5, Claude-3, Arabic-focused open-source) to improve correlation robustness.
3. **Ablation study:** Train identical architectures with controlled tokenizers (over-segmented, morpheme-aligned, random) on fixed data to isolate tokenizer impact.
