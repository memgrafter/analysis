# Teleological Inference in Structural Causal Models via Intentional Interventions
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.18968
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18968
- **Reference count:** 32
- **Primary result:** Standard structural causal models (SCMs) cannot represent intentions of state-aware, goal-directed agents. The authors introduce intentional interventions and structural final models (SFMs) to enable teleological inference, allowing empirical detection of agents and discovery of their goals.

## Executive Summary
Standard structural causal models (SCMs) lack the formalism to represent the intentions of state-aware, goal-directed agents, leading to flawed reasoning about interventions and their underlying motivations. This paper introduces **intentional interventions**, a novel time-agnostic operator that constructs a **Structural Final Model (SFM)** by twinning the original SCM and intervening on the copy via a deterministic function of a goal variable. This formalism cleanly separates observational and interventional quantities, preserves acyclicity, and enables two key forms of **teleological inference**: (1) detecting the presence of an agent by observing violations of Markovianity in collider structures, and (2) discovering an agent's specific intention by experimentally probing descendants of the goal in fork structures. The approach is validated through simulations on canonical heating and smoking examples.

## Method Summary
The core method constructs an SFM from a base acyclic SCM `M` and a hypothesized goal variable `Y` (a descendant of an intervention target `X`). The **intentional intervention** `do⋆(X⋆=f(Y))` first creates a twin replica `M_rep` of the original model, conditioned on the goal value `Y=y`. A mechanism change `do(X⋆=f(y))` is then applied to this replica. The resulting SFM `M_fin` treats the original variables (`X`, etc.) as counterfactual outcomes and the twin variables (`X⋆`, etc.) as the factual result of the agent's goal-directed action. This twin construction preserves acyclicity (Proposition 1) and generates specific statistical dependencies that signal an agent's presence (Proposition 2) or reveal their goal (Proposition 3). Reproduction involves implementing the base SCMs (e.g., heating, smoking), applying the twin construction per Definition 6, and conducting statistical tests (e.g., independence tests for agent detection, distribution-shift tests for intention discovery) on data sampled from the SFM.

## Key Results
- Standard SCMs cannot represent state-aware, goal-directed agency; intentional interventions resolve this via a twin construction that yields a Structural Final Model (SFM).
- SFMs always produce an acyclic combined graph (Proposition 1), ensuring compatibility with standard causal inference tools.
- Teleological inference is feasible: Markovianity violations in collider structures detect agents (Proposition 2), and interventional shifts on descendants of a fork identify the agent's goal (Proposition 3).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The intentional intervention operator `do⋆(X⋆=f(Y))` generates an SFM by twinning the original SCM and intervening on the copy via a deterministic function of the goal variable `Y`, thereby encoding state-awareness and goal-directedness while avoiding explicit time.
- **Mechanism:** The construction (Definition 6) first replicates the original model `M` conditioned on the goal value `Y=y` (creating `M_rep`, a twin where factual variables are `X⋆` and counterfactual are `X`). Then it performs a mechanism change `do(X⋆=f(y))` on the replica. The resulting `M_fin` treats observed outcomes as due to the agent's intervention and retains the unintervened copy as a counterfactual, cleanly separating observational and interventional quantities.
- **Core assumptions:** (A1) Perfect knowledge of the true causal DAG; (A2) High-frequency agent (exogenous variables stable across twins); also `Y ∈ Desc(X)`.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract] "intentional interventions, a new time-agnostic operator that induces a twin SCM we call a structural final model (SFM)."
  - [section] Section 5.2 (Definition 6) explicitly defines the twin construction and role of `f`.
- **Break condition:** If A1 fails (agent has incorrect causal model) the SFM's counterfactual predictions are invalid. If A2 fails (exogenous variables change faster than agent), the conditioning on `Y=y` is inappropriate. If `Y ∉ Desc(X)`, the intervention cannot affect the goal.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** SFMs induced by intentional interventions preserve acyclicity (i.e., produce a DAG), ensuring compatibility with standard causal inference tools.
- **Mechanism:** Proposition 1 proves the twin graph `G_fin` (combining `G_M` and `G_M⋆`) contains no cycles. Any directed path either stays within one component (already acyclic) or crosses from `G_M` to `G_M⋆` via the intervention edge but never returns, because the intervention removes incoming edges to `X⋆` and adds only a functional edge from `Y` (which lies in `G_M`). Hence, the combined graph remains a DAG.
- **Core assumption:** The original SCM `M` is acyclic (DAG) and Markovian; intentional intervention is single-variate as defined.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "avoiding cyclic graphs and preserving acyclicity"
  - [section] Section 5.3 (Proposition 1) provides the formal proof.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** SFMs enable two basic forms of teleological inference: (i) empirical detection of agents via violations of Markovianity in collider structures; (ii) discovery of an agent's intention by intervening on descendants of the goal in fork structures.
- **Mechanism:** In a collider (e.g., `W → T ← H`), if an agent intervenes on `H` based on goal `T`, the SFM introduces a dependence between `W` and `H⋆` through their common descendant `T⋆`, violating the d-separation `H ⟂ W` from the original DAG. For forks (e.g., `S → P, D`), the agent's goal `Y` (say `P`) drives the intervention on `S`. Performing `do(Z=¯z)` on a descendant `Z` of `S` shows that only intervening on the goal descendant (here `P`) alters the distribution of `S⋆`.
- **Core assumptions:** For detection: agent intervenes on a parent of a collider and Assumptions 1,2 hold. For discovery: fork structure holds, the agent's `f` depends only on `Y`, and `Y` is a descendant of `X`.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "SFMs enable teleological inference: empirical detection of agents via violations of Markovianity in collider structures (Proposition 2) and discovery of intentions by intervening on descendants of the goal in fork structures (Proposition 3)."
  - [section] Section 6 (Proposition 2 and 3) formalizes the identifiability results.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Structural Causal Models (SCMs)
  - **Why needed:** The paper extends the standard SCM framework; grasp of endogenous vs exogenous variables, structural equations, and DAGs is essential to understand the twin construction and the distinction between observational and interventional distributions.
  - **Quick check:** In an SCM, what graphical property corresponds to the Markov condition, and how does an intervention `do(X=x)` alter the graph?

- **Concept:** Interventions and Counterfactuals (Twin Models)
  - **Why needed:** The intentional intervention builds on both the `do` operator and counterfactual twin construction; recognizing the algebraic and graphical differences between perfect interventions, mechanism changes, and counterfactuals is key.
  - **Quick check:** How does the twin graph for a counterfactual differ from that of an intentional intervention in terms of which variables are treated as factual vs counterfactual?

- **Concept:** Markovianity and d-Separation
  - **Why needed:** Teleological inference relies on violations of expected independencies (Markovianity) and on the ability to identify colliders and forks using d-separation rules.
  - **Quick check:** For the graph `X → Z ← Y`, are `X` and `Y` d-separated marginally? Conditioned on `Z`? If `Z` has a child, how does conditioning on that child affect the marginal independence between `X` and `Y`?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Original SCM (M) -> Goal Specification (Y) -> Mechanism Function (f(Y)) -> Twin Operator -> Intentional Intervention Step -> Structural Final Model (M_fin) -> Inference Components (Markovianity Tests, Interventional Experiments)

- **Critical path:**
  1. Build an acyclic SCM for the domain and determine its DAG `G_M`.
  2. Choose a candidate intervention target `X` and hypothesize a goal `Y ∈ Desc(X)`.
  3. For agent detection: compare empirical independencies in collected data against those implied by `G_M`; a violation in a collider suggests an intentional intervention on a parent of that collider.
  4. For intention discovery: identify fork structures with `X` as a common cause; for each descendant `Z` of `X`, perform `do(Z=¯z)` and measure the distribution of `X⋆`; `Z` belongs to the agent's goal if the distribution changes compared to baseline.
  5. Optionally, construct the full SFM `M_fin` for simulation or further counterfactual reasoning.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Single vs multi-variate:** Formalism limits to one intervened variable and one goal; multi-variate extensions capture more complex intentions but risk cyclic graphs and identifiability issues.
  - **Time-agnostic vs time-based:** Avoiding explicit time simplifies data requirements (i.i.d.) but assumes the agent acts much faster than exogenous changes (Assumption 2). When violated, dynamic SCMs increase complexity.
  - **Perfect knowledge vs bounded rationality:** Perfect causal knowledge (A1) makes the agent's actions fully transparent; relaxing it requires modeling possibly incorrect agent models, complicating inference.
  - **Acyclicity vs cycles:** Maintaining DAGs preserves compatibility with existing causal tools; allowing cycles necessitates different inference machinery.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Agent detection:** Observed data shows no Markovianity violation in any collider despite suspecting an agent → possible false negative (e.g., agent acts randomly or matches natural dynamics).
  - **Intention discovery:** Intervening on a presumed non-goal descendant (e.g., `D` in smoking example) unexpectedly shifts `S⋆` distribution → either the fork structure is wrong or the agent's aim includes that descendant.
  - **Assumption violation:** High variability in exogenous variables across observations (e.g., weather changes rapidly in heating example) can break the twinning relation, leading to mismatched independencies.
  - **Graph mismatch:** Using wrong causal graph `G_M` yields incorrect expected independencies and misleads both detection and discovery.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Heating example replication:** Implement `M_caus` and an agent `f_H` with goal `T<0.5`. Sample data `D_fin` and test for `H ⋆ ⟂ W ⋆`; confirm violation (p<0.05) under original `G_M` and restoration when using `G_fin`. Also compute the distribution of `H⋆` under the SFM.
  2. **Smoking example replication:** Build SFM with `f_S` depending on `P>1` (goal = pleasure). Sample `D_fin`. Generate `D_fin|do(P=0)` and `D_fin|do(D=0)`. Compare `P(S⋆)` distributions; significant shift under `do(P=0)` but not `do(D=0)` indicates goal `P`.
  3. **Robustness to imperfect knowledge:** Replace `f` with a stochastic/noisy version (e.g., `f_act = f(Y)` with probability 0.9, else flip). Measure detection rate (Markovianity violation) and intention discovery accuracy over 1000 runs; demonstrate degradation relative to perfect knowledge case.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **What are necessary and sufficient conditions for identifiability of intentional interventions from data in general causal graphs?** (Section 6.1) Only simple collider structures are currently understood; real-world systems have complex dependencies.
- **How can intention discovery be made computationally efficient for large causal systems?** (Section 6.2) Proposed method tests all descendant combinations, causing combinatorial explosion.
- **Can intentions be discovered using only observational data, without active interventions?** (Appendix C.2) Active interventions may be infeasible; observational methods would broaden applicability.
- **How does imperfect causal knowledge on the agent's side affect teleological inference?** (Section 7) Real agents have incomplete or wrong causal models; current SFMs assume perfect alignment.

## Limitations
- Strong assumptions: perfect causal knowledge (A1) and high-frequency agent (A2) are often unrealistic.
- Scope limited to single-variate intentional interventions and acyclic graphs; extensions to multi-variate or cyclic cases are non-trivial.
- Teleological inference procedures rely on specific graph structures (colliders for detection, forks for discovery) being present.
- Statistical tests near-deterministic variables (e.g., heating example) may be sensitive to implementation choices.
