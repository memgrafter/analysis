# How to Set the Batch Size for Large-Scale Pre-training?

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: `2601.05034`
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05034
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: The original Critical Batch Size theory fails under the Warmup-Stable-Decay (WSD) schedule; a revised E(S) formula and a dynamic batch size scheduler reduce token consumption and improve downstream task performance.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles batch size selection for large-scale language model pre-training under the modern Warmup-Stable-Decay (WSD) learning rate schedule. It reveals that the established Critical Batch Size theory, encapsulated by the E(S) relationship, breaks down during the Stable phase because gradient noise scale varies over time, invalidating key assumptions. The authors derive a revised, piecewise E(S) formula specific to WSD, leading to two critical metrics: B_min (minimum batch size to reach a target loss) and B_opt (batch size that minimizes total tokens consumed). They observe that B_opt increases monotonically as training loss decreases and propose a dynamic batch size scheduler that tracks this curve. Extensive experiments across model architectures (MoE and Dense) and learning rate schedules confirm the theory and show consistent improvements in training efficiency and downstream accuracy (MMLU/CMMLU) over fixed-batch baselines.

## Method Summary
The methodology begins with collecting step-loss data from training several model scales (122M to 1B parameters) under a constant learning rate, using a wide range of batch sizes (64K to 7.5M). These data are used to fit a new piecewise E(S) function with continuity constraints, achieving high R² (e.g., 0.992 for the 1B model). From the fitted curve, B_opt and B_min are extracted as functions of target loss. The authors then design a dynamic batch size scheduler (Algorithm 1) that periodically increases the batch size at fixed token intervals (125B) according to the rising B_opt trajectory. This scheduler is evaluated on Qwen3 MoE and Dense models, comparing against fixed-batch baselines under both constant and cosine learning rates, and during continued training with a decay phase. Performance is measured via training loss and downstream task scores (MMLU/CMMLU).

## Key Results
- The original E(S) relationship fails during the Stable phase of WSD, causing loss curves for different batch sizes to intersect.
- The revised piecewise E(S) formula fits empirical data with high accuracy (R² = 0.992 on a 1B model).
- Dynamic batch scheduling consistently lowers training loss and improves MMLU/CMMLU scores compared to any fixed batch size baseline, across MoE/Dense models, constant/cosine schedules, and continued training with decay.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Failure of Original E(S) under WSD**  
The original Critical Batch Size theory assumes a fixed interplay between steps (S) and data consumption (E) that holds under a slowly decaying learning rate (e.g., cosine). In WSD, the Stable phase maintains a constant learning rate, causing the gradient noise scale to vary significantly over time. This breaks the monotonicity of E(S), meaning the ordering of data efficiency for different batch sizes is not preserved as loss decreases. Consequently, the simple hyperbolic E(S) formula no longer holds, as evidenced by crossing loss curves for different batch sizes during the Stable phase.

**Mechanism 2: Revised Piecewise E(S) and B_opt/B_min**  
Re-deriving the expected per-step loss reduction under SGD with a constant learning rate and a time-varying gradient noise scale yields a piecewise E(S) function. This function has three stages: inverse linear near the minimum step count, approximately quadratic in a transition regime, and linear at large steps with slope A1. The minimum of E(S) defines B_opt (batch size minimizing tokens), while the asymptotic slope defines B_min. These emerge from continuity and smoothness constraints. The piecewise model fits empirical data very well (R²=0.992 for 1B), confirming its validity under WSD's Stable phase.

**Mechanism 3: Dynamic Scheduling Exploits Monotonic B_opt**  
B_opt increases monotonically as the training loss decreases. A static batch size either undershoots B_opt (leaving data efficiency on the table) or overshoots it (wasting compute). By increasing the batch size along the empirically observed B_opt curve while keeping the learning rate constant, the training remains near the data-optimal point throughout the Stable phase. This avoids reintroducing extra gradient noise (which would occur if LR were scaled with batch), and relies on sufficient weight decay to regularize the larger batches. The strategy consistently outperforms fixed batches in experiments.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
E(S) Fitter -> B_opt Extracter -> Batch Size Scheduler -> Training Loop -> Evaluation Module

**Critical path:**  
Identification of original E(S) failure under WSD -> derivation of new piecewise E(S) model -> empirical fitting of parameters across model scales -> extraction of B_opt as a function of loss -> design of dynamic batch schedule -> large-scale training runs (dynamic vs fixed baselines) -> evaluation of training loss and downstream tasks -> comprehensive ablations (LR scaling, seqlen, WD, decay).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Constant LR vs LR scaling: Keeping LR constant while increasing batch size is crucial; scaling LR (e.g., √B rule) reintroduces gradient noise and negates benefits.  
- Micro-batch expansion vs sequence-length switching: Increasing micro-batch size preserves the data distribution; extending sequence length causes a distribution shift and training instabilities.  
- Weight decay strength: Dynamic batch sizing relies on moderate-to-high weight decay for regularization; low WD (e.g., 0.01) significantly reduces the advantage.  
- Dynamic vs fixed: Dynamic scheduling adds complexity (curve fitting, schedule generation) but yields consistent gains; fixed baselines are simpler but suboptimal.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Loss divergence or instability when batch size is changed abruptly, especially via sequence-length switching.  
- Diminishing or vanishing advantage compared to fixed baseline when weight decay is reduced too much.  
- No benefit or performance regression if learning rate is scaled with batch size (e.g., √B).  
- Poor fit of the piecewise E(S) model (low R²), indicating violated assumptions (e.g., non-SGD optimizer, extreme batch sizes, insufficient data range).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **E(S) Fitting:** Train five model sizes (122M–1B) with thirteen batch sizes (64K–7.5M) under a constant learning rate; collect loss-step data; fit the piecewise E(S) with continuity constraints via Huber loss; validate with high R² (e.g., 0.992 for 1B).  
2. **Dynamic vs Fixed (constant LR):** Train Qwen3 MoE and Dense models with a dynamic batch schedule (e.g., 2M→4M→5M→6M at 125B-token intervals) against fixed baselines (2M, 4M, 5M, 6M); compare final training loss and MMLU/CMMLU scores.  
3. **Ablations:** (a) Apply dynamic scheduling under a cosine learning rate decay; (b) Scale learning rate with batch size (√B) during dynamic scheduling; (c) Switch sequence length instead of micro-batch size; (d) Reduce weight decay; (e) Continue training into a decay phase; measure impact on loss and downstream performance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. Does the revised E(S) formula derived for the WSD stable phase hold for learning rates other than the single value (6×10⁻⁴) used in fitting? The paper notes that computational costs restricted fitting to one learning rate, leaving its behavior under other LRs unexplored.
2. Is there a theoretical foundation that explains or guarantees the effectiveness of the proposed dynamic batch size scheduling strategy? The current work is empirical; a formal proof or theoretical analysis of why the monotonic B_opt trend leads to optimal scheduling is lacking.
3. What is the root cause of training instabilities when switching sequence length in dynamic batch size scaling, and how can they be prevented? The paper observed immediate performance drops upon increasing seqlen but did not analyze the underlying mechanism, limiting flexibility.

## Limitations
- The revised E(S) derivation assumes SGD dynamics; its extension to AdamW (used in experiments) lacks theoretical justification and may fail under extreme batch regimes.
- Scheduler parameters (the exact f(N,D) curve, momentum list {α_i}, switch points) are undisclosed, hindering exact replication of the dynamic strategy.
- The observed advantages require constant learning rate, sufficiently high weight decay, and micro-batch scaling; other LR scaling rules, sequence-length changes, or low WD break the gains.
- Details of the decay phase in continued training (e.g., decay schedule specifics) are vague, limiting reproducibility of those experiments.

## Confidence
- Original E(S) failure under WSD: **High** (clearly demonstrated with crossing loss curves).
- Revised E(S)/B_opt validity: **Medium** (strong empirical fits but narrow assumptions: SGD-like dynamics, specific LR, limited model sizes).
- Dynamic scheduler effectiveness in evaluated settings: **High** (consistent gains across multiple architectures and schedules).
- Broader transfer of dynamic scheduler: **Medium** (unclear for other optimizers, LRs, or extreme scales).

## Next Checks

2. On larger models (2B+ parameters), conduct sensitivity analysis on scheduler hyperparameters (α_i values, switching intervals, fitting data range) to determine their impact on final loss and downstream scores.
3. Transfer the dynamic batch scheduling strategy to cosine decay schedules and lower weight decay regimes (e.g., WD=0.01) to map the boundary conditions where its advantages hold or vanish.
