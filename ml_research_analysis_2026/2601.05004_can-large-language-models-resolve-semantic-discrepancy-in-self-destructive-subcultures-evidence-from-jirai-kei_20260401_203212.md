# Can Large Language Models Resolve Semantic Discrepancy in Self-Destructive Subcultures? Evidence from Jirai Kei

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.05004  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05004  
- **Reference Count:** 13  
- **Primary Result:** SAS achieves state-of-the-art macro F1 on JiraiBench and generalizes to other subcultures without model retraining.

## Executive Summary
Large language models (LLMs) often fail to detect self-destructive behaviors in niche subcultures like Jirai Kei due to rapidly evolving slang (Knowledge Lag) and nuanced, ironic semantics (Semantic Misalignment). This paper introduces the Subcultural Alignment Solver (SAS), a multi-agent framework that dynamically retrieves subculture-specific information from the internet, generates an alignment report summarizing key terminology and context, and uses this report to interpret input sentences. On the JiraiBench benchmark, SAS outperforms prompt-based methods and advanced multi-agent systems like OWL. It also generalizes to related Japanese subcultures (Menhera, Yami Kawaii) and competes with fine-tuned LLMs without requiring model retraining, offering a flexible approach to subcultural semantic alignment.

## Method Summary
SAS operates in three sequential stages: (1) **Internet Retrieval**—an agent automatically fetches up-to-date, subculture-relevant data from the web; (2) **Alignment Report Generation**—a second agent synthesizes the retrieved data into a concise report defining key slang, themes, and contextual cues; (3) **Interpretation**—a third agent uses the alignment report to classify input sentences for self-destructive intent. By externalizing subcultural knowledge into reports, SAS avoids fine-tuning and adapts to evolving discourse through fresh retrieval.

## Key Results
- SAS achieves state-of-the-art macro F1 scores on the JiraiBench benchmark, surpassing prompting methods and OWL.
- It matches fine-tuned LLM performance without any model retraining.
- SAS generalizes effectively to Menhera and Yami Kawaii subcultures, showing robust cross-subculture applicability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
SAS resolves Knowledge Lag by pulling current subcultural data from the internet rather than relying on static training corpora. It addresses Semantic Misalignment by distilling retrieved information into structured alignment reports that explicitly map slang to intended meanings and contextual nuances. The multi-agent separation ensures retrieval focuses on breadth and recency, report generation on distillation and clarity, and interpretation on precise application, reducing error cascades and enabling LLMs to operate outside their original training distribution.

## Foundational Learning
- **Knowledge Lag**: Subcultural slang evolves faster than LLM training cycles. *Why needed*: Prevents models from using outdated or mainstream interpretations. *Quick check*: Can the model correctly define a neologism that emerged post-training?
- **Semantic Misalignment**: Subcultures repurpose language with irony, metaphor, or community-specific connotations. *Why needed*: Avoids literal misinterpretation of self-referential or ironic expressions. *Quick check*: Does the model distinguish between “I want to disappear” as depressive vs. aestheticized?
- **Subcultural Alignment**: Adapting general LLMs to specialized discourse via external knowledge injection. *Why needed*: Bridges the domain gap without costly retraining. *Quick check*: Do alignment reports improve accuracy on held-out subculture texts?
- **Multi-Agent Specialization**: Dividing tasks among dedicated agents (retrieval, synthesis, interpretation). *Why needed*: Manages complexity and isolates failure modes. *Quick check*: Does removing the retrieval agent degrade performance more than removing the interpretation agent?
- **Alignment Reports**: Structured summaries of terminology, themes, and contextual rules. *Why needed*: Provides LLMs with concise, actionable subcultural knowledge. *Quick check*: Are reports sufficiently detailed to cover core slang while remaining interpretable by the final agent?
- **Dynamic Knowledge Acquisition**: Using live internet retrieval instead of static embeddings. *Why needed*: Captures ephemeral, evolving subcultural discourse. *Quick check*: Does retrieval from multiple platforms improve coverage of platform-specific slang?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Internet Retrieval -> Alignment Report Generation -> Interpretation

**Critical Path:**  
Retrieval must precede report generation, which must precede interpretation. Each stage’s output is the next stage’s input; delays or errors in retrieval propagate downstream.

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- *Retrieval breadth vs. noise*: Wider scraping improves coverage but risks including irrelevant or mainstream-biased content.  
- *Report detail vs. efficiency*: Longer reports may enhance accuracy but increase latency and risk overwhelming the interpreter.  
- *Generalization vs. specialization*: Broad query terms aid cross-subculture transfer but may miss niche, subculture-specific nuances.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- Retrieval returns off-topic or biased content (e.g., mainstream media misrepresentations).  
- Alignment report omits critical slang or misdefines terms.  
- Interpretation ignores report guidance or overgeneralizes to ambiguous inputs.

**First Experiments:**  
1. Test SAS on a temporally volatile subculture (e.g., fast-evolving meme communities) to measure how retrieval recency impacts performance.  
2. Ablate alignment report length (concise vs. detailed) to quantify accuracy-latency tradeoffs.  
3. Compare single-agent vs. multi-agent implementations to isolate the benefit of task separation.

## Limitations
- **Benchmark validity**: JiraiBench’s annotation process is opaque; annotator expertise and inter-annotator agreement are unreported, risking cultural bias or mislabeling.  
- **Retrieval bias**: Internet sources may inject platform-specific or mainstream biases, misaligning with niche, ephemeral discourse; data provenance and filtering are unclear.  
- **Generalization limits**: Cross-subculture claims rest on only two similar Japanese-origin subcultures; effectiveness for geographically or culturally distant subcultures is unverified.

## Confidence
- SAS efficacy on JiraiBench: **Medium** (results are strong but benchmark transparency is poor).  
- Generalization to other subcultures: **Low** (evidence limited to two co-evolving Japanese subcultures).  
- Competitiveness vs. fine-tuned LLMs: **Medium** (retraining-free advantage is clear, but fine-tuned models may still excel with abundant subculture data).

## Next Checks
1. **Audit JiraiBench’s cultural validity**: Re-annotate with native Jirai Kei community members and cultural linguists, measure agreement, and identify systematic label discrepancies.  
2. **Stress-test generalization**: Evaluate SAS on three substantively different subcultures (e.g., a regional dialect community, a non-Japanese alternative fashion group, a politically-oriented online movement) to measure performance decay and boundary conditions.  
3. **Error analysis on failures**: Manually analyze SAS false positives/negatives on JiraiBench to determine whether failures stem from retrieval gaps, report insufficiency, or inherent semantic ambiguity.
