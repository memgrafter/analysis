# Censored LLMs as a Natural Testbed for Secret Knowledge Elicitation
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.05494
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05494
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Censored Chinese LLMs (e.g., Qwen3) provide a natural testbed for honesty elicitation and lie detection; simple techniques like next-token completion and few-shot prompting significantly improve truthfulness, and prompted self-classification achieves detection accuracy near that of uncensored models.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces censored Chinese large language models (LLMs), such as those in the Qwen3 family, as a realistic testbed for evaluating honesty elicitation and lie detection methods. These models have undergone training to refrain from discussing politically sensitive topics and often produce falsehoods when they do answer, creating a scenario with naturally suppressed factual knowledge and known false outputs. This setting overcomes limitations of artificial misalignment benchmarks that require manual fabrication of dishonest behavior.

The authors systematically evaluate a variety of elicitation and detection techniques on Qwen3-32B and Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking, finding that simple black-box approaches—next-token completion without chat formatting, few-shot prompting, and fine-tuning on generic honesty data—substantially increase honesty scores. For lie detection, prompting the model to self-classify its responses yields 80.9% detection of untruthful outputs and a balanced accuracy of 86.3%, approaching the uncensored-model upper bound of 89.8%. The most effective methods transfer to other open-weight models (DeepSeek-R1, MiniMax-M2.5, Qwen3.5-397B), though no technique entirely eliminates false responses. This work demonstrates that censored LLMs serve as a valuable, scalable resource for auditing model truthfulness.

## Method Summary
The evaluation uses a curated set of 90 held-out test questions spanning 12 sensitive political topics. Ground-truth factual answers are derived from GPT-4.1-mini responses, providing a reliable reference. Primary models are Qwen3-32B and Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking; transfer experiments include DeepSeek-R1, MiniMax-M2.5, and Qwen3.5-397B. For each question, ten responses are generated with temperature = 1 using standard settings or specific intervention prompts.

A suite of black-box elicitation techniques is tested: next-token completion (removing chat template and ending with an unbiased prefix), prefill attacks (user-provided answer start), few-shot prompting (examples of honest answers), and system-prompt incentives. White-box methods include abliteration (removing a precomputed refusal direction from activations), activation steering (adding steering vectors from contrastive pairs or probe means), and honesty fine-tuning (LoRA on generic honesty-oriented datasets). Lie detection approaches consist of prompted self-classification (asking the model if its response contains a lie), fine-tuned classifiers, and linear probes trained on contrastive pairs across all layers. Metrics include honesty score (0–100), percentage of facts mentioned, percentage of responses containing at least one lie, refusal rate, and for detection: balanced accuracy, true-positive rate, and true-negative rate.

## Key Results
- Next-token completion on Qwen3-32B increased honesty score from 42.8% to 62.9% and raised the fraction of facts mentioned from 44.9% to 53.5%.
- Prompted self-classification detected 80.9% of untruthful outputs with a balanced accuracy of 86.3%, nearly matching the uncensored model upper bound (89.8%).
- Effective elicitation techniques (e.g., few-shot prompting, next-token completion) transferred to frontier open-weight models (DeepSeek-R1, MiniMax-M2.5, Qwen3.5-397B), significantly reducing censorship and false responses, although no method completely eliminated lies.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Effective techniques often succeed by interfering with the model's censorship circuitry rather than by directly promoting honesty. For instance, next-token completion avoids the chat template that triggers refusal training, and few-shot prompting may overload the context and disrupt refusal execution. White-box interventions like activation steering and abliteration explicitly remove refusal-related activations. Lie detection methods exploit the model's internal consistency: prompted self-classification asks the model to judge its own truthfulness, while linear probes capture deception-related activation patterns across layers.

## Foundational Learning
- **Censorship in LLMs**  
  *Why needed:* To understand why models refuse certain queries and how such training affects internal representations.  
  *Quick check:* Does the model refuse to answer a politically sensitive question in a standard chat format?

- **Honesty elicitation**  
  *Why needed:* To develop methods that coax models into revealing suppressed truthful knowledge without fabricating answers.  
  *Quick check:* Does removing the chat template increase the proportion of factual statements?

- **Lie detection**  
  *Why needed:* To automatically identify when a model produces false or misleading outputs, which is crucial for auditing.  
  *Quick check:* Can the model accurately classify its own responses as truthful or deceptive when prompted?

- **Activation steering / abliteration**  
  *Why needed:* To directly manipulate the model's internal activations and test causal roles of refusal directions.  
  *Quick check:* Does subtracting a precomputed refusal direction reduce the refusal rate on sensitive topics?

- **Linear probes for deception**  
  *Why needed:* To build lightweight detectors that can be trained on contrastive pairs and generalize to unseen data.  
  *Quick check:* Does a probe trained on honest vs. dishonest activations achieve >80% balanced accuracy on a held-out set?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Question Dataset -> Model Generation (with intervention) -> Response Collection -> Autorater Evaluation -> Metrics Computation

**Critical Path**  
The core pipeline flows from the question dataset through model inference (with or without elicitation technique) to the autorater (Gemini 3 Flash) that produces honesty scores, fact verification, and lie classification, culminating in aggregated metrics.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- **Autorater choice:** Using a single powerful model (Gemini 3 Flash) ensures consistency but may introduce bias; using multiple raters improves reliability at higher cost.  
- **Black-box vs white-box:** Black-box techniques (prompting) are easy to apply but may be less effective; white-box interventions require model access and careful hyperparameter tuning.  
- **Metrics aggregation:** Honesty score combines multiple aspects; simplifying to a single number may obscure nuanced performance differences.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Very high refusal rate (>90%) indicates that the elicitation technique failed to bypass censorship.  
- Low honesty score (< baseline) suggests the intervention degraded truthfulness or introduced new falsehoods.  
- Large discrepancy between prompted self-classification and ground truth indicates poor calibration of the detection method.

**Three First Experiments**  
1. **Baseline evaluation:** Generate responses with standard chat template and compute metrics to establish reference honesty score and refusal rate.  
2. **Next-token completion:** Remove chat formatting and end with "Unbiased AI:"; measure change in honesty score and facts mentioned.  
3. **Prompted self-classification:** Ask the model to classify each of its own responses as truthful or deceptive; compute balanced accuracy against ground truth.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Direct training for lie detection** – *Basis:* "An alternative to honesty fine-tuning could involve training directly for lie detection, encouraging models to be well-calibrated. We leave this for future work." (Section 4.3)  
   *Why unresolved:* The authors only evaluated honesty fine-tuning and prompted classification; direct training might yield better detectors but was not explored.

2. **Effectiveness of Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs)** – *Basis:* "While we do not find evidence of SAEs as an effective elicitation technique, and thus do not include them in the main body of the paper, we do not rule out the possibility of them working on future models." (Section 5)  
   *Why unresolved:* Initial investigation found features tied to tokens rather than abstract knowledge; it remains unclear whether improved SAEs or training regimes could unlock hidden knowledge.

3. **Mechanism of simple elicitation success** – *Basis:* "Some of the most successful techniques appear to work not by directly encouraging honesty, but by impairing the model’s ability to hide information." (Section 5); also "it is possible that models forget their censorship training in multi-turn conversations, as opposed to single-turn ones." (Section 3.3)  
   *Why unresolved:* Speculations are not validated; understanding the mechanism could lead to more robust auditing and help design defenses.

4. **Ineffectiveness of user prefill attacks** – *Basis:* "User prefill attacks are highly unsuccessful in our setting. Despite reported success in prior work on auditing LLMs, we find this technique very unsuccessful when benchmarking both Qwen3 models... We attribute this to several factors." (Section 3.3)  
   *Why unresolved:* Proposed explanations are not systematically tested; the failure may reveal unique aspects of censorship training.

5. **Fundamental limits to eliminating false responses** – *Basis:* "Notably, no technique entirely eliminated false responses." (Abstract) and "No single technique fully eliminates false responses." (Section 3.3)  
   *Why unresolved:* Unclear whether the failure is due to incomplete technique or fundamental constraints (e.g., knowledge gaps or incentive mismatches).

## Limitations
- **Autorater version uncertainty:** The exact Gemini 3 Flash checkpoint used is unspecified; newer versions may rate differently, shifting metrics.
- **Missing hyperparameter details:** Optimal values for steering strength/layer, probe layer selection, etc., are not fully tabulated, hindering exact replication.
- **Model version drift:** Qwen3 models may have been updated, altering censorship strength and baseline performance.

## Confidence
- Natural testbed premise: High
- Elicitation effectiveness (next-token completion, few-shot, fine-tuning): Medium
- Lie detection (self-classification, linear probes): Medium
- Transfer to frontier open-weights: Medium

## Next Checks
1. **Autorater calibration:** Evaluate a human-annotated subset with multiple Gemini 3 Flash versions and a fixed alternative (e.g., GPT-4o) to measure rating variance and calibrate metrics.
2. **Hyperparameter recovery:** Replicate the development sweeps for steering vectors, probe layers, and regularization; publish full optimal settings and confidence intervals to verify reported scores.
3. **Cross-version robustness:** Run top techniques on multiple historical Qwen3 checkpoints (if available) and on other model families (Llama 3, Mistral) to assess stability of gains across updates and architectures.
