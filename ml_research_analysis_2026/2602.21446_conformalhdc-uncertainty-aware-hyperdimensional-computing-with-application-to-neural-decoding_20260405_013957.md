# ConformalHDC: Uncertainty-Aware Hyperdimensional Computing with Application to Neural Decoding

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.21446
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21446
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Integrates conformal prediction with Hyperdimensional Computing to provide finite-sample, distribution-free coverage guarantees, yielding valid marginal coverage (e.g., 0.950 with α=0.05), compact prediction sets, and high out-of-distribution detection (AUC up to 0.986) on benchmarks and neural decoding.

## Executive Summary
Hyperdimensional Computing (HDC) offers lightweight classification via prototype-based similarity, but lacks uncertainty quantification. ConformalHDC bridges this gap by wrapping HDC classifiers with conformal prediction, enabling rigorous, distribution-free uncertainty guarantees. The framework introduces tailored nonconformity scores, notably the CHDC-discount, which balances absolute similarity and relative confidence to produce compact prediction sets and effective abstention. Evaluated on MNIST, ISOLET, language datasets, and hippocampal neural decoding, ConformalHDC achieves marginal coverage at nominal levels, outperforms standard HDC and other baselines in set efficiency, and detects out-of-distribution samples with high AUC. The approach retains HDC’s linear scaling in hypervector dimension and class count, making it practical for real-world applications requiring trustworthy uncertainty estimates.

## Method Summary
ConformalHDC operates in a split-calibration regime. First, an HDC classifier is trained on a dedicated training set: inputs are encoded into high-dimensional bipolar hypervectors (e.g., via random projection and bundling), and class prototypes are computed as the sign of aggregated training hypervectors per class. Then, on an independent calibration set, a nonconformity score S(h, y) is computed for each sample with its known label. The score used in the paper is CHDC-discount: S(h,y) = -[δ(h, H_y)^2 / Σ_{y'} δ(h, H_{y'})], where δ is a scaled cosine similarity in [0,1]. The (1−α) empirical quantile of these scores is stored. At test time, for a new sample, S(h, y) is computed for all candidate classes; the prediction set includes all classes whose score does not exceed the quantile. This set-valued output guarantees marginal coverage under exchangeability. A point-valued variant first forms the set, then resolves multi-class cases by selecting the class with the smallest score, and returns an empty set when abstention is allowed. The method supports marginal and label-conditional coverage, with minimal computational overhead beyond the base HDC classifier.

## Key Results
- Achieves valid marginal coverage at the desired confidence level (e.g., 0.950 coverage with α=0.05) on MNIST, ISOLET, and language benchmarks.
- Produces significantly smaller prediction sets than standard HDC and other conformal baselines, improving usability.
- Attains high out-of-distribution detection AUC (up to 0.986), demonstrating strong abstention capability.
- In neural decoding from hippocampal activity, ConformalHDC provides calibrated uncertainty estimates and correctly abstains on non-task (“running”) states, while maintaining decoding accuracy.
- Maintains HDC’s computational efficiency with linear scaling in hypervector dimension and number of classes.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: ConformalHDC provides finite-sample, distribution-free marginal coverage guarantees for HDC classifiers by wrapping them with a conformal prediction layer.
- **Mechanism**: The data are split into training and calibration sets. The HDC model (encoder and class prototypes) is trained on the training set. For each calibration point, a nonconformity score (e.g., CHDC-discount) is computed. The (1−α) empirical quantile of these scores is stored. At test time, the score for each possible class is computed; the prediction set includes all classes whose score does not exceed the quantile. Exchangeability ensures that the true label is captured with probability ≥1−α.
- **Core assumption**: The data points (training, calibration, test) are exchangeable.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - Abstract: “providing finite-sample, distribution-free coverage guarantees”
  - Section 3.2: Algorithm 1 and Theorem 1 formally establish marginal coverage under exchangeability.
  - Corpus: Weak – no direct prior work on conformal HDC.
- **Break condition**: Violations of exchangeability or a calibration set not representative of the test distribution can cause coverage to fall below the nominal level.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: The CHDC-discount nonconformity score creates enclosed decision boundaries and robust out-of-distribution (OOD) detection by balancing absolute similarity to a class with its relative confidence among all classes.
- **Mechanism**: For a test hypervector h and candidate class y, the score combines a similarity term (−δ(h, H_y)) and a ratio term (δ(h, H_y) / Σ_{y'} δ(h, H_{y'})). The product amplifies differences when the true class is much more similar than others, while also penalizing points that are globally dissimilar, enabling abstention.
- **Core assumption**: Similarity measures are non-negative and normalized so that ratios are meaningful.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - Section 3.1: Introduces CHDC-discount and contrasts with other scores; Figure 2 visualizes enclosed boundaries.
  - Section 4 and Table E.2: Demonstrates smaller sets and higher OOD AUC vs. baselines.
  - Corpus: No existing HDC nonconformity scores.
- **Break condition**: Poorly learned prototypes or unnormalized similarity metrics distort the ratio, leading to failure to abstain on OOD inputs.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Point-valued ConformalHDC improves predictive accuracy over standard HDC while retaining the ability to abstain on uncertain or OOD inputs by trimming set-valued outputs.
- **Mechanism**: After obtaining the set-valued prediction, if the set contains multiple classes, the class with the smallest nonconformity score is selected. If the set is empty and abstention is allowed, an empty set is returned; otherwise the most similar class is forced. This leverages nuanced boundaries from the score to resolve overlaps, boosting accuracy, and still defers when no class meets the calibrated threshold.
- **Core assumption**: The nonconformity score reliably ranks classes for a given test point.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - Section 3.3: Describes Algorithm 3 and its two modes.
  - Section 4 and Table E.2: Shows higher accuracy than standard HDC while maintaining abstention.
  - Section 5.2: Neural decoding experiment correctly abstains on “running” state.
  - Corpus: No direct evidence.
- **Break condition**: Inaccurate quantile (due to small calibration) or poorly discriminative score may cause over/under-abstention; trimming may not improve accuracy.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:
  Encoder (ϕ) → Hypervector (h) → Compute δ(h, H_y) for each class y → Apply nonconformity score S(h,y) → Compare to quantile(s) → Prediction set C → Point-valued via trimming if desired.
- **Critical path**:
  1. Split data into D_train and D_cal.
  2. Train HDC on D_train: learn ϕ and {H_y}.
  3. Compute calibration scores S_i = S(ϕ(X_i), Y_i) for i ∈ D_cal.
  4. Compute quantile q_{1−α} (or per-class q_y).
  5. For each test X:
     a. Encode h = ϕ(X).
     b. Compute s_y = S(h, y) ∀y.
     c. Build set C = { y : s_y ≤ q } (or ≤ q_y).
     d. If point-valued: resolve multi-class set by picking argmin s_y; empty set becomes argmin if abstention disallowed.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Set-valued vs point-valued: Set-valued provably covers but may be less actionable; point-valued yields a decision but may sacrifice some coverage.
  - Marginal vs label-conditional: Marginal easier with smaller sets; conditional ensures fairness across rare classes but often increases set size.
  - Score choice: CHDC-discount gives compact sets and strong OOD detection; similarity-only yields larger sets; ratio-only loses abstention.
  - Computation: Adding calibration costs O(n_cal K d) and test cost O(m K d), still linear in hypervector dimension d and class count K.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Coverage significantly below 1−α → exchangeability violated or calibration mismatch.
  - Excessively large prediction sets → poor score discriminativity or mis-calibrated quantile.
  - Low OOD AUC → score insensitive to outliers; consider CHDC-discount or adjust α.
  - High abstention on in-distribution data → quantile too high or score not well-calibrated; increase calibration size.
  - Point-valued accuracy worse than standard HDC → over-trimming due to unreliable calibration; check score quality and quantile.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Synthetic Gaussian mixture classification evaluating coverage, set size, accuracy, and OOD AUC under controlled heteroscedasticity.
  2. MNIST benchmark: digits 0–5 in-distribution, 6–9 OOD; measures marginal coverage, average set size, OOD AUC.
  3. ISOLET benchmark: spoken letters A–V in-distribution, W–Z OOD; same metrics, showcasing multimodal applicability.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Full conformal / jackknife+ integration** – To improve data efficiency in small-data regimes, as split-conformal wastes calibration data that could enhance prototype learning.
2. **Online conformal prediction** – Adapting ConformalHDC to non-stationary streams with incremental updates of prototypes and quantiles.
3. **Applicability to other VSAs** – Whether the tailored nonconformity scores remain valid and effective for alternative vector symbolic architectures (e.g., Tensor Product Representations, Binary Spatter Codes).
4. **Impact of temporal dependence** – How autocorrelation in neural spiking data affects the exchangeability assumption and therefore coverage guarantees.

## Limitations
- The exchangeability assumption may be violated in temporally correlated neural data, threatening coverage guarantees in real-world decoding.
- The CHDC-discount score’s design (e.g., squared similarity) lacks theoretical justification; its advantages may be dataset-dependent.
- Point-valued abstention policy (forcing a label when sets are empty) breaks coverage guarantees and can degrade calibrated uncertainty; the trade-off is not thoroughly analyzed.
- Calibration requires a representative in-distribution set, which can be scarce in specialized domains, leading to unreliable quantile estimates.

## Confidence
- **Theoretical coverage guarantees**: High confidence in the provable marginal coverage under exchangeability; Low confidence in practical coverage when exchangeability is violated (e.g., neural time series).
- **CHDC-discount efficacy**: Medium confidence; empirical results show improvements on several benchmarks, but comparative evaluations are limited and may not generalize.
- **Point-valued accuracy + abstention**: Low confidence; reported accuracy gains are modest and abstention behavior is only qualitatively demonstrated.

## Next Checks
1. **Test Coverage Under Temporal Dependence** – Re-run neural decoding with calibration set that includes preceding timepoints from the same trial; measure empirical coverage on later timepoints to quantify exchangeability violations.
2. **Ablate CHDC-discount Components** – On MNIST/ISOLET, compare CHDC-discount against its constituent terms (−δ only, δ/∑δ) and other rational forms (−δ/∑δ). Measure set size, OOD AUC, and calibration error to isolate the contribution of each component.
3. **Quantile Sensitivity & Calibration Size** – Vary calibration set size (5%, 15%, 30%) and test coverage; also use calibration data from a different session/subject. This probes robustness to quantile estimation and the need for subject-specific calibration.
