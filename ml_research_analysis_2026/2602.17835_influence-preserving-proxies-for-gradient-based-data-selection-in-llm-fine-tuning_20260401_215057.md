# Influence-Preserving Proxies for Gradient-Based Data Selection in LLM Fine-tuning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.17835  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17835  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** IPROX creates compact proxy models that preserve the target model's influence for gradient-based data selection, reducing compute by over 50% while exceeding the full 3B model's selection accuracy on several benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
IPROX tackles the high computational cost of gradient‑based data selection (e.g., TracIn, Influence Functions) for fine‑tuning large language models. It introduces a two‑stage pipeline: first, Influence‑Preserving SVD (IPSVD) compresses the target model’s weights into a low‑rank proxy that retains gradient‑relevant components; second, the proxy is aligned to the target via gradient matching in the low‑rank space and output anchoring through KL divergence. Extensive evaluation across Llama, Gemma, and Qwen models on MMLU, BBH, and TyDiQA shows IPROX consistently outperforms off‑the‑shelf proxies and baseline selection methods, achieving substantial compute savings without sacrificing selection quality.

## Method Summary
The method builds a tiny proxy to approximate the target model’s influence scores for efficient data selection. **Stage 1** applies IPSVD: on a small probe set, hidden states (`hℓ`) and upstream gradients (`δℓ`) are gathered; second‑moment matrices `C_{h,ℓ}`, `C_{δ,ℓ}` are estimated; and a reweighted SVD solves `min_{cWℓ} || Cδ,ℓ^{1/2} (Wℓ−cWℓ) Ch,ℓ^{1/2} ||_F`, yielding low‑rank factors `Aℓ`, `Bℓ` that preserve directional influence. **Stage 2** aligns the proxy: the target’s gradients are projected onto the low‑rank subspace, and the proxy factors are optimized to match these projected gradients while also minimizing a KL divergence between the proxy’s and target’s output logits. The final proxy is orders of magnitude smaller than the target and can compute influence scores for the entire candidate set at low cost, enabling effective selection of high‑influence samples for fine‑tuning.

## Key Results
- A 1.5B IPROX proxy outperforms a 1.7B off‑the‑shelf proxy on Qwen3‑4B.  
- On Llama3.2, IPROX reduces compute by over 50% while exceeding the full 3B model’s selection accuracy.  
- Consistently beats off‑the‑shelf proxies and baseline methods across Llama, Gemma, and Qwen models on MMLU, BBH, and TyDiQA.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Influence‑Preserving Low‑Rank Approximation via Reweighted SVD
The directional effect of a weight perturbation `Eℓ` on a sample `z` is `eℓ(z)=δℓ(z)⊤Eℓhℓ(z)`. Under smoothness assumptions, the expected change in influence is bounded by `√E[eℓ(z)²]`. Minimizing this expectation leads to the weighted Frobenius norm objective `min_{cWℓ} || Cδ,ℓ^{1/2} (Wℓ−cWℓ) Ch,ℓ^{1/2} ||_F`. Solving via SVD of `Sℓ = Cδ,ℓ^{1/2} Wℓ Ch,ℓ^{1/2}` and truncating to rank `rℓ` yields low‑rank factors that retain the components most relevant to gradient‑based influence. This reweighting focuses compression on directions that actually affect influence.

### Mechanism 2: Gradient Alignment in the Low‑Rank Parameter Space
After IPSVD initialization, the proxy’s factors `Aℓ`, `Bℓ` are refined by aligning their gradients to the target’s gradients projected into the same low‑rank subspace. Specifically, the loss minimizes a distance (e.g., `1−cosine`) between the proxy’s gradients (`∇_{Aℓ}L = (∇_{Wℓ}L) Bℓᵀ`, `∇_{Bℓ}L = Aℓᵀ (∇_{Wℓ}L)`) and the target’s projected gradients (with stop‑gradient). This ensures the proxy’s local training dynamics mirror the target’s in the reduced space, which is critical for accurate influence estimation.

### Mechanism 3: Output Anchoring via KL Divergence
The alignment loss is combined with a KL divergence term between the proxy’s and target’s output logits (with temperature `τ`): `min L_GA + λ_KL L_KL`. This knowledge‑distillation‑style regularization anchors the proxy’s external behavior, preventing output collapse or semantic drift during gradient alignment and complementing the internal gradient matching.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Probe data collector -> IPSVD module -> Gradient alignment module -> Influence calculator

### Critical Path
1. Warm‑up target model on a small random subset (5% of `D_train`).  
2. Collect probe data (≈1% of `D_train`) from the warmed model.  
3. Run IPSVD on all target layers (excluding embeddings/LM head) to obtain low‑rank factors.  
4. Assemble proxy model: replace target layers with `Aℓ Bℓ`, copy other layers unchanged.  
5. Align proxy on the remaining probe data (90% of probe set) using loss `L_GA + λ_KL L_KL`.  
6. Use aligned proxy to compute influence scores for all candidate samples.  
7. Select top‑k samples and perform full fine‑tuning of the original target model.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Rank `r`:** Lower rank → smaller/faster proxy but risk under‑fitting influence patterns; higher rank → better accuracy but higher cost. Choose `r` to match budget (Table 2).  
- **Probe set size `N`:** Larger `N` improves second‑moment estimates but linearly increases Stage 1 time; saturation beyond ~3× default (Table 7).  
- **KL coefficient `λ_KL`:** Too large (≥0.5) over‑regularizes, too small (≤0.001) causes instability; default 0.1 is safe.  
- **Layer coverage:** Embedding and LM head typically excluded due to small rank and criticality.

### Failure Signatures
- **Low influence retention:** Spearman correlation between proxy‑ and target‑computed influence < 0.8 on a held‑out set; indicates poor IPSVD or alignment.  
- **Divergence during alignment:** Loss spikes or NaNs; suggests missing KL anchoring, excessive `λ_KL`, or bad learning rate.  
- **No downstream improvement:** Proxy‑selected data underperforms random/off‑the‑shelf selection; check rank, probe diversity, alignment data distribution.  
- **Proxy collapse:** Output probabilities become uniform or extreme; monitor KL loss – if high, increase `λ_KL`.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **IPSVD retention validation:** On a synthetic MLP task, compute TracIn for target; compress with standard SVD and IPSVD at various sparsities; plot influence retention (Spearman) vs. sparsity; expect IPSVD curve above SVD.  
2. **End‑to‑end single‑task test:** Build 1.8B IPROX proxy (`ρ=0.5`) for Llama3.2‑3B; use TracIn to select 5% of DOLLY; fine‑tune target; evaluate BBH; expect performance to exceed 1B off‑the‑shelf proxy and approach full‑model selection.  
3. **Ablation study:** Repeat experiment 2 with (a) no KL anchoring (`λ_KL=0`), (b) no alignment (only IPSVD‑initialized proxy); expect significant drops on BBH/TyDiQA, confirming both stages are necessary.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **How does rank r affect influence error?** The paper gives a bound without explicit r‑dependence and notes performance drops below 10% rank but lacks a theoretical link.  
- **Do layer‑wise errors accumulate in deep networks?** IPROX compresses layers independently; cumulative effect on final influence is unknown.  
- **How does distributional shift between candidate data and target tasks affect selection?** Theory includes a κ term capturing shift but it is not quantified or studied.

## Limitations
- Rank allocation per layer is unspecified; uniform allocation may be suboptimal.  
- Stage 2 hyperparameters (learning rate, `λ_KL`, etc.) are not fully disclosed, requiring empirical tuning.  
- Evaluation limited to three model families and three tasks; performance on other architectures (e.g., MoE) and domains (code, math) remains unknown.

## Confidence
- **High confidence:** The IPSVD objective is formally derived (Proposition 4.1) and aligns with the goal of preserving directional influence; gradient alignment and KL anchoring are well‑motivated by prior work.  
- **Medium confidence:** Empirical results are strong but limited in scope; compute savings are plausible but may vary with implementation details.  
- **Low confidence:** Optimal rank allocation and robustness to probe set distribution shifts are not thoroughly explored; these are practical gaps that could affect real‑world deployment.
