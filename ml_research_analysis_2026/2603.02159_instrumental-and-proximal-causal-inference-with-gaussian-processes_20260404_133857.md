# Instrumental and Proximal Causal Inference with Gaussian Processes

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.02159
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02159
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: The Deconditional Gaussian Process framework provides well‑calibrated epistemic uncertainty and state‑of‑the‑art accuracy for instrumental and proximal causal inference under unobserved confounding.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles causal inference with instrumental variables (IV) or proximal variables when unobserved confounding is present. Existing kernel methods yield point estimates but lack reliable uncertainty quantification. The authors develop a Deconditional Gaussian Process (DGP) framework that places a GP prior on the structural function (or bridge function). The posterior mean recovers known kernel estimators (KIV, KNC), while the posterior variance gives principled epistemic uncertainty. Hyperparameters are optimized via marginal log‑likelihood, enabling use of all data without splitting. On synthetic and demand datasets, DGP methods (GPIV, GPProxy) achieve predictive accuracy matching or exceeding baselines, and their 95% credible intervals exhibit near‑nominal coverage and informative rejection‑rate curves, outperforming bootstrap and Bayesian competitors.

## Method Summary
We synthesize the core method: The unknown causal function (or bridge) is modeled as a Gaussian process. For IV, the observed response Y is linked through E[f(X)|Z], estimated via conditional mean embedding (CME). The joint GP of f(X) and Y yields a posterior GP; conditioning on Y gives posterior mean and variance. The posterior mean algebraically simplifies to the KIV estimator under fixed hyperparameters. For proxy, a bridge function h is similarly treated, using a deconditional operator based on both X and Z. Hyperparameters (length scales of X, W, and noise variance) are tuned by maximizing the marginal likelihood of Y, while the instrument’s length scale is fixed by median heuristic to avoid identifiability issues. The resulting framework simultaneously provides point estimates of the average treatment effect and calibrated uncertainty, and it avoids data‑splitting used in frequentist kernel methods.

## Key Results
- Predictive accuracy comparable to or better than state‑of‑the‑art baselines on synthetic and real‑world demand data.
- Well‑calibrated 95% credible intervals, with empirical coverage close to the nominal level.
- Accuracy‑rejection curves show more informative uncertainty than bootstrap and Bayesian competitors, leading to better decision‑making under uncertainty.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Deconditional Kernel Embedding as Fredholm Solver
- **Claim**: The causal ATE under unobserved confounding is recovered by inverting a conditional expectation operator via deconditional mean embeddings, turning identification conditions into solvable integral equations.
- **Mechanism**: For instrumental variable (IV) settings, the equation \(E[Y|Z] = E[f(X)|Z]\) is a Fredholm equation of the first kind. The deconditional mean operator \(D_{X|Z}\) acts as a pseudo‑inverse of the conditional mean operator \(C_{X|Z}\), satisfying \((D_{X|Z})^T E[f(X)|Z=\cdot] = f\). The paper adapts this to causal inference: for IV it constructs \(f\) directly from \(g(z)=E[Y|Z]\); for proxy it first recovers a bridge function \(h\) and then integrates out the proxy.
- **Core assumption**: Regularity conditions ensuring boundedness of the deconditional operator and existence of RKHS embeddings (Assumptions C.1, C.2 for IV; C.5, C.6 for Proxy). Completeness is required for identification in the proxy setting (Assumption A.2).
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "Our formulation recovers popular kernel estimators as the posterior mean"
  - [section 2.3]: Formal definitions of CME, DME, and operators \(C_{X|Z}, D_{X|Z}\); representation of DME using cross‑covariance and CME.
  - [section 3]: Derivation of deconditional estimators (DIV, DProxy) and their equivalence to GP posteriors.
- **Break condition**: Violation of completeness (e.g., using \(C_{X,W|X,Z}\) instead of \(C_{W|X,Z}\) in proxy first stage) leads to biased estimates. Extremely small lengthscale for the instrument due to data‑splitting or poor regularization can overfit the CME, destroying the operator inversion.

### Mechanism 2: GP Deconditional Model for Uncertainty Quantification
- **Claim**: Placing a Gaussian process prior on the structural function \(f\) (or bridge \(h\)) and assuming an additive noise model on the conditional mean process yields a posterior GP whose mean matches existing kernel‑based point estimators and whose variance gives well‑calibrated epistemic uncertainty.
- **Mechanism**: The joint distribution of the latent function values \(f(x)\) and the observed responses \(y\) (via \(g(z)=E[f(X)|Z=z]\) in IV or \(g(x,z)=E[h(x,W)|X=x,Z=z]\) in proxy) is Gaussian. Conditioning on \(y\) produces a posterior GP over \(f\). The posterior mean simplifies to a closed form that coincides with KIV or KNC; the posterior variance captures curvature of the kernel and regularization, providing natural uncertainty bounds.
- **Core assumption**: The GP prior covariance is positive definite; the additive noise model holds; the conditional mean embedding estimators are sufficiently accurate (regularity and completeness). The derivation treats hyperparameters and CME estimates as fixed (first‑stage uncertainty is ignored).
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "the posterior mean recovers existing kernel estimators ... while the posterior variance yields principled and well‑calibrated EU."
  - [section 3.1]: Proposition 3.2 gives explicit posterior mean and variance for IV; Proposition 3.3 proves equivalence to KIV. Section 3.2 does the same for proxy.
  - [section 4]: Mentions that Bayesian competitors (e.g., QBIV) rely on artificial data‑generating mechanisms, while this approach uses a full nonparametric model.
- **Break condition**: Poor CME estimation (small sample, misspecified kernel, violated completeness) will bias both mean and understate variance. Ignoring first‑stage uncertainty (as noted in the Discussion) is an approximation; if the CME is highly uncertain, the nominal coverage may drop below 95%.

### Mechanism 3: Marginal Likelihood for End‑to‑End Model Selection
- **Claim**: The GP framework naturally supports hyperparameter selection via penalized marginal log‑likelihood optimization, eliminating the need for data‑splitting strategies and enabling better predictive performance.
- **Mechanism**: The marginal likelihood \(\log p(y|\theta) \propto -\log|\tilde{Q}| - y^T\tilde{Q}^{-1}y\) (with \(\tilde{Q}\) the covariance of \(y\)) balances data fit and model complexity. Hyperparameters (lengthscales of \(X,W\), noise \(\sigma^2\)) are optimized using this objective, while fixing the instrument’s lengthscale via median heuristic to avoid identifiability issues. This allows using all data for both stages simultaneously.
- **Core assumption**: The Gaussian likelihood for \(y\) given the conditional mean process is correct; the marginal likelihood approximation treats the CME as fixed (i.e., first‑stage error is negligible).
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 3.3]: Presents the log marginal likelihood formula and the recommended hyperparameter optimization scheme, noting identifiability of \(l_Z\) (Appendix G.5).
  - [section 5.1]: Ablation showing that optimizing the lengthscale of \(X\) improves MSE; KIV’s data‑splitting causes worse performance.
  - [section 4]: Contrasts with cross‑validation used in frequentist kernel methods.
- **Break condition**: Optimization can become unstable if initial values are poor or the likelihood surface is flat. Over‑fitting might occur for very flexible kernels (small lengthscales) in small samples, though the marginal likelihood has an automatic Occam factor. Fixing \(l_Z\) may limit fit if the instrument lengthscale truly varies.

## Foundational Learning

### Gaussian Processes
- *Why needed here*: The entire model is built on a GP prior for the structural/bridge function and leverages GP conditioning for posterior inference.
- *Quick check question*: When conditioning a GP on observed data, what changes in the mean and covariance functions, and how does the kernel matrix structure affect the computation?

### Kernel Mean Embeddings & Conditional Mean Embeddings
- *Why needed here*: CMEs provide a nonparametric representation of conditional distributions, enabling the definition of the conditional mean process \(g(z)\). Their empirical estimators (regularized by \(\eta\)) are core to computing the mediation matrices.
- *Quick check question*: Given samples \(\{(x_i,z_i)\}_{i=1}^n\), how do you estimate the conditional mean embedding \(\mu_{X|Z=z}\), and why is Tikhonov regularization required?

### Deconditional Statistical Inference
- *Why needed here*: Deconditional mean embeddings are the mathematical tool that inverts the conditional expectation operator, turning identification equations into estimable forms. This perspective unifies IV and proxy approaches.
- *Quick check question*: What is the relationship between the operators \(C_{X|Z}\) and \(D_{X|Z}\), and why does \(D_{X|Z}\) correspond to solving a Fredholm equation of the first kind?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Component map: The pipeline starts with pre‑processing that standardizes covariates and computes kernel Gram matrices \(K_{XX}, K_{ZZ}, K_{WW}\). For instrumental variable (IV) models, conditional mean embedding estimation computes the matrix \(A = (K_{ZZ} + \eta I)^{-1}K_{ZZ}\); for proxy models, it computes \(B = (K_{XX} \odot K_{ZZ} + \eta I)^{-1}(K_{XX} \odot K_{ZZ})\). Posterior mean is then derived: for IV, \(\hat{\mu}(x) = K_{xX} A (A^\top K_{XX} A + \sigma^2 I)^{-1} y\); for proxy, \(\hat{\mu}(x) = (K_{xX} \odot \bar{K}_{WW} B) (K_{XX} \odot (B^\top K_{WW} B) + \sigma^2 I)^{-1} y\), where \(\bar{K}_{WW}\) averages rows of \(K_{WW}\). Posterior variance is obtained from the kernel diagonal minus a quadratic form involving the posterior mean. Hyperparameter optimization maximizes the marginal log‑likelihood over length scales \(l_X, l_W\) and noise \(\sigma^2\), while fixing \(l_Z\) via median heuristic. The optimization loop repeats the computation of kernels, CME, and marginal likelihood until convergence.

### Critical path
1. Pre‑process data and select kernel type.
2. Initialize hyperparameters (median heuristic for lengthscales, \(\sigma^2=0.25\)).
3. Compute kernel matrices and mediation matrices (A or B).
4. Evaluate marginal likelihood; if optimizing, use gradient‑based method (e.g., L‑BFGS) to update hyperparameters and repeat from step 3.
5. After convergence, compute posterior mean and variance for test points.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Data splitting vs full‑data**: Frequentist two‑stage methods (e.g., KIV) split data, reducing sample size; the GP approach uses all data but depends on quality of CME estimation.
- **Hyperparameter optimization**: Fixing \(l_Z\) avoids instability but may underfit; optimizing all hyperparameters improves accuracy but requires careful initialization.
- **Kernel choice**: RBF enforces smoothness; Matérn offers flexibility but adds a smoothness hyperparameter.
- **Scalability**: Inverting \(n \times n\) matrices is cubic; large datasets need approximations (not covered).

### Failure signatures
- **Under‑dispersed variance** (coverage <95%): CME overfitting (η too small, \(l_Z\) too small) or ignored first‑stage error.
- **High test MSE**: Kernel misspecification (lengthscale too large/small), η too large, or violation of additive noise assumption.
- **Optimization instability**: Marginal likelihood plateaus or diverges; try different initializations, increase regularization, or fix \(l_Z\).
- **Bias in proxy** (if posterior matches KNC‑orig instead of KNC): Likely completeness violation; use correct CME operator \(C_{W|X,Z}\).

### First 3 experiments
1. **Synthetic IV**: Test on sine, linear, and log structural functions. Show GPIV matches KIV point estimates but yields better‑calibrated intervals; present accuracy‑rejection curves.
2. **Demand IV**: Evaluate on a larger real‑world dataset. Compare MSE against KIV, MMRIV, QBIV; demonstrate full‑data GPIV reduces error relative to data‑splitting KIV.
3. **Synthetic & Demand Proxy**: Evaluate GPProxy against KNC, KPV, and bootstrap methods on synthetic and modified demand designs. Highlight superior coverage and ARC.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How can epistemic uncertainty from the first‑stage conditional mean operator estimation be propagated into the GP posterior for the structural function without sacrificing the closed‑form solution?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors state in the discussion (page 17) that incorporating first‑stage uncertainty would break the closed‑form GP structure and necessitate more complex inference and optimization procedures, which they leave for future investigation.
- **Why unresolved**: The current framework treats first‑stage estimates as fixed, potentially leading to overconfident posterior intervals, especially with limited first‑stage data. A joint Bayesian treatment of both stages is needed.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Derive a hierarchical GP or variational Bayes model that jointly learns the conditional mean operator and structural function; validate on synthetic data where first‑stage uncertainty is manipulated, showing improved coverage while maintaining predictive accuracy.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Why does marginal likelihood optimization in GPIV/GPProxy lead to degenerate lengthscale values for the instrument/proxy variables (Z or W), and can this be avoided without resorting to heuristic fixes like the median heuristic?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors note in Section 3.3 and Appendix G.5 that optimizing the lengthscale for Z results in extremely small values, harming predictive performance; they therefore recommend fixing it via median heuristic due to identifiability issues.
- **Why unresolved**: The degeneracy likely arises from the marginal likelihood's sensitivity to the kernel matrix structure, but a theoretical analysis is lacking; alternative automatic tuning strategies (e.g., hierarchical priors, different objectives) are not systematically explored.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Analyze the curvature of the marginal likelihood as a function of hyperparameters, particularly lz; propose modified objectives or priors that ensure well‑behaved optima; empirically demonstrate stable, improved performance across diverse datasets without ad hoc fixes.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: How robust are the uncertainty estimates from GPIV and GPProxy to violations of key identification assumptions (e.g., exclusion restriction, completeness, additive noise model)?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The methods rely on standard IV/Proxy assumptions plus additive noise; however, the paper does not consider sensitivity to these assumptions, despite their known fragility in practice (see references on partial identification and sensitivity analysis).
- **Why unresolved**: Assumption violations could bias point estimates and mis‑calibrate uncertainty; the GP framework's Bayesian structure might offer some implicit robustness, but this is untested and theoretically unclear.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Conduct simulation studies where assumptions are gradually weakened (e.g., add small direct effects of Z on Y, introduce non‑additive noise, weaken instrument strength); evaluate degradation in coverage, ARC, and MSE compared to robust methods (e.g., set identification approaches).

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Can the deconditional GP framework be extended to target other causal quantities besides the average treatment effect (ATE), such as conditional average treatment effects (CATE) or dynamic treatment regimes, under unobserved confounding?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The paper focuses solely on ATE estimation via Fredholm integral equations; while it mentions "causal estimands" broadly, extensions to CATE or sequential decisions are non‑trivial and not discussed.
- **Why unresolved**: CATE involves additional conditioning on covariates, requiring different operator constructions (e.g., conditional deconditioning) and possibly new identifiability conditions; the current formulation is tailored to ATE.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Develop analogous GP models for CATE under IV/Proxy assumptions, prove they recover known kernel‑based CATE estimators (if any), and evaluate uncertainty quality on synthetic data with heterogeneous effects.

## Limitations

- Baseline implementations (splitting ratios, CV folds, hyperparameter ranges) are incompletely specified, making reported improvements sensitive to these choices.
- The GP marginal likelihood ignores error from first‑stage CME estimation, potentially leading to overconfident uncertainty estimates with limited data or misspecified kernels.
- The method relies on a strong completeness assumption in the proxy setting; its robustness to violations is not explored.

## Confidence

- **Equivalence to kernel estimators (posterior mean = KIV/KNC)**: High confidence
- **Uncertainty calibration**: Medium confidence
- **Model selection benefit**: Medium confidence
- **Overall superiority over baselines**: Medium confidence

## Next Checks

1. Joint CME‑function inference: Extend the model to simultaneously infer the CME (e.g., hierarchical GP) and evaluate coverage across varying sample sizes and instrument strength to test robustness of uncertainty quantification.
2. Completeness violation stress test: Systematically introduce confounding between the proxy W and unobserved variable U and measure decay in bias and coverage to assess sensitivity to the completeness assumption.
3. Scalable approximate GP: Implement inducing‑point or random Fourier feature approximations and test on synthetic datasets with n>10k to verify that predictive accuracy and calibration remain comparable to the full‑GP while reducing computational cost.
