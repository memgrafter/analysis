# Small molecule retrieval from tandem mass spectrometry: what are we optimizing for?

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.16507
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16507
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: The paper uncovers a fundamental Pareto trade‑off between fingerprint similarity and retrieval hit rate in tandem mass spectrometry, supported by theoretical Bayes‑risk analysis and practical guidance on loss and fingerprint selection.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how the choice of loss function influences deep learning models that predict molecular fingerprints from tandem mass spectra for compound retrieval. Through extensive experiments on the MassSpecGym benchmark, it reveals a trade‑off: models optimized for fingerprint similarity (e.g., via IoU loss) achieve high Tanimoto scores but poor retrieval hit rates (HR@k), while models optimized for retrieval (contrastive losses) achieve high HR@1 but low fingerprint similarity. Theoretical analysis using Bayes‑risk decision theory explains this as a consequence of differing Bayes‑optimal predictors for the two objectives, with regret bounds that depend on the similarity structure of candidate sets. Empirically, the study shows that the molecular fingerprint representation affects the severity of the trade‑off—for example, MAP4 fingerprints exhibit lower regret compared to Morgan fingerprints. These findings provide actionable insights for practitioners to align model training with the intended downstream task.

## Method Summary
The study addresses the task of predicting continuous molecular fingerprints (4096‑dimensional Morgan radius‑2) from binned tandem mass spectra (10050‑dimensional vectors) and retrieving the correct molecule from a candidate pool. The dataset is MassSpecGym, containing 231,104 spectrum‑molecule pairs with a clustered split to avoid data leakage; candidate sets include up to 256 molecules either with equal mass or equal chemical formula. The core model is a feed‑forward MLP embedder (three hidden layers of 1024 units with GeLU, dropout 0.25, LayerNorm) followed by either a linear output layer with sigmoid (for bitwise/vectorwise losses) or learned projection heads (for contrastive losses). Loss functions span bitwise (BCE, focal), vectorwise (cosine, IoU), and listwise (contrastive with cosine or MLP scoring). Training uses Adam with gradient clipping and checkpointing every 1000 steps; hyperparameters (learning rate, focal γ, temperature τ) are tuned via grid search on validation HR@20. Evaluation metrics are binarized Tanimoto for fingerprint accuracy and hit rate @k (HR@1, HR@5, HR@20) for retrieval; for non‑contrastive models, retrieval uses cosine similarity, while contrastive models use their trained scoring function g. The experimental protocol includes separate models for equal‑mass and equal‑formula candidate sets and, for the Fp‑Cos variant, separate temperature choices optimized for retrieval versus Tanimoto.

## Key Results
- **Pareto trade‑off confirmed**: Models trained with IoU loss achieve high average Tanimoto (~0.194) but low HR@20 (~26.5%); contrastive models (Emb‑Cos) achieve high HR@20 (~45.0%) but low Tanimoto (~0.12). The negative correlation holds across loss families and candidate set types.
- **Theoretical explanation**: Bayes‑optimal predictors differ: for expected cosine similarity, the optimal support set is given by sorted marginal contributions, while for HR@1 it is the mode (MAP) of the conditional distribution p(y|x). Regret bounds (Theorems 4.2, 4.3) show the gap depends on the similarity band width (σmax−σmin) and the uncertainty of the top conditional probability (1−p*).
- **Fingerprint choice matters**: MAP4 fingerprints have a narrower similarity band (σmax≈0.20, band≈0.17) than Morgan (r=2) (σmax≈0.37, band≈0.34) on MassSpecGym candidate sets, leading to a smaller HR@1 gap between IoU‑ and contrastive‑trained models.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Divergence of Bayes‑optimal predictors**  
The predictor that maximizes expected fingerprint similarity (e.g., cosine) selects a support set based on sorted marginal contributions of candidates (Theorem 4.1), whereas the predictor that maximizes expected HR@1 selects the mode (MAP) of the conditional distribution p(y|x). Since both objectives optimize different utility functions over the same p(y|x), a model trained to minimize a loss aligned with one objective will converge to its Bayes‑optimal predictor, not the other’s. The decisions only coincide when p(y|x) is degenerate (p(y_true|x)=1) or when all candidates have identical similarity to the true molecule (σmax=σmin). Empirical evidence is drawn from the consistent trade‑off across loss families on MassSpecGym.

**Mechanism 2: Regret bounds governed by similarity band**  
The performance loss (regret) incurred when using a predictor optimal for one objective under the other grows with the width of the similarity band (σmax−σmin) and the uncertainty in the top conditional probability (1−p*). Theoretically, Theorem 4.2 bounds the regret of HR@1 under a vectorwise similarity utility, and Theorem 4.3 bounds the reverse. A larger spread of similarity values among false candidates (high σmax−σmin) and higher uncertainty (low p*) inflate the inevitable gap. This explains why candidate set design (which influences σmin/σmax) impacts the trade‑off severity.

**Mechanism 3: Fingerprint representation shapes the similarity band**  
Different molecular fingerprints produce different empirical distributions of similarity between true and false candidates, i.e., different σmin and σmax on a given candidate set. Since regret bounds depend on σmax−σmin, a fingerprint with a narrower band and lower σmax can reduce the trade‑off. Empirically, MAP4 fingerprints exhibit lower σmax and a smaller band than Morgan (r=2) on MassSpecGym, and the HR@1 gap between IoU‑ and contrastive‑trained models is correspondingly smaller (Section H). This provides a practical handle to mitigate the Pareto trade‑off.

## Foundational Learning
- **Bayes risk and decision theory**: Needed to grasp why different utility functions (similarity vs. retrieval) lead to different optimal predictors and to quantify the inevitable performance gap (regret). Quick check: Given p(y|x), which candidate maximizes expected HR@1? (Answer: the one with highest conditional probability, i.e., the mode.)
- **Multi‑label classification loss functions**: Needed to understand how surrogate losses (BCE, focal, contrastive) approximate the target metrics and why some align better with retrieval. Quick check: How does contrastive loss differ from binary cross entropy? (Answer: Contrastive loss is listwise, operating over a set of candidates to directly optimize ranking, while BCE decomposes over bits independently.)
- **Molecular fingerprints and similarity metrics**: Needed because fingerprints are the predicted representation and retrieval relies on similarity measures (Tanimoto, cosine); different fingerprints have varying sparsity and correlation structures affecting the similarity band. Quick check: What does a Tanimoto similarity of 1 between two binary fingerprints imply? (Answer: They have identical sets of on‑bits; i.e., identical substructure encoding.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Binned tandem mass spectrum (10050‑dim) → Embedder E (3‑layer MLP, 1024‑dim) → (a) Non‑contrastive: Linear layer W_out (4096×1024) + sigmoid → 4096‑dim soft fingerprint; (b) Contrastive: Projection W_sp (1024→256) for spectrum, candidate fingerprint projected by W_c (4096→256) → similarity function g (cosine or MLP).  
Training loss: BCE / Focal / Cosine / IoU (non‑contrastive) or contrastive loss (Eq. 4).  
Retrieval module: At inference, compute scores between predicted representation (or embedding) and candidate fingerprints, return top‑k.

**Critical path**:  
1. Preprocess raw MS/MS spectrum → fixed‑length binned vector.  
2. Pass through E to obtain embedding.  
3a. For non‑contrastive: apply W_out and sigmoid to produce soft fingerprint.  
3b. For contrastive: compute embedding e = W_sp(E(x)) and candidate projection e_c = W_c(c); score g(x,c).  
4. Compute loss against ground‑truth fingerprint (non‑contrastive) or over candidate set (contrastive).  
5. Backpropagate to update all learnable parameters.  
6. For new spectrum, generate representation, score all candidates, and evaluate HR@k.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- **Loss family**: Bitwise losses ignore inter‑bit correlations; vectorwise losses directly optimize fingerprint similarity but still misalign with retrieval under varying candidate similarities; contrastive (listwise) losses align with HR@k but depend on negative sampling and temperature τ.  
- **Candidate set construction**: For contrastive training, negative pool should be challenging; MassSpecGym uses a cascade favoring natural products, which may limit generalization.  
- **Similarity function at inference**: Cosine similarity consistently outperforms Tanimoto even for models trained with IoU loss, because it better exploits continuous predictions.  
- **Fingerprint choice**: Higher‑radius Morgan fingerprints increase sparsity and prediction difficulty; MAP4 is denser and exhibits lower regret.

**Failure signatures**:  
- **Overfitting**: Training metric (Tanimoto or HR@20) far exceeds validation; remedy with stronger regularization or early stopping.  
- **Model collapse**: Predictions collapse to all zeros or a constant; use focal loss or adjust learning rate.  
- **Candidate‑set mismatch**: Model trained on equal‑mass candidates performs poorly on equal‑formula; ensure candidate distribution matches deployment.  
- **Temperature sensitivity**: Contrastive models with FP‑Cos show Pareto curves that reverse with τ; careful tuning needed.  
- **Binarization effect**: Fixed 0.5 threshold for Tanimoto scoring may be sub‑optimal; consider threshold optimization per fingerprint.

**First 3 experiments**:  
1. **Reproduce the Pareto trade‑off**: Train BCE, IoU, and Contrastive Emb‑Cos models on MassSpecGym (equal‑mass). Expect IoU: Tanimoto ~0.194, HR@20 ~26.5%; Contrastive: HR@20 ~45.0%, Tanimoto ~0.12.  
2. **Hyperparameter sensitivity (temperature τ)**: For Contrastive Emb‑Cos, vary τ ∈ {1/256, 1/64, 1/16, 1/4, 1, 4}. Plot HR@20 vs. Tanimoto; lower τ favors retrieval, higher τ favors similarity. Choose τ based on primary objective.  
3. **Fingerprint comparison and regret estimation**: Compute σmin/σmax on candidate sets for Morgan (r=2) and MAP4. Train IoU and Contrastive Emb‑Cos models for both fingerprint targets. Measure HR@1 gap (contrastive minus IoU); expect smaller gap for MAP4 due to narrower similarity band.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: How can aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty be quantified and incorporated into deep learning models for fingerprint prediction to improve the reliability of molecular retrieval?  
  *Basis*: Explicit discussion in the paper.  
  *Why unresolved*: Current models output point predictions without uncertainty estimates; the impact on retrieval is unknown.  
  *Evidence to resolve*: Comparative studies with uncertainty‑aware methods (e.g., MC dropout, ensembles) showing improved calibration or hit rates.

- **Open Question 2**: Does the observed Pareto trade‑off between fingerprint similarity and molecular retrieval hold for other retrieval tasks that rely on binary representations, such as item retrieval with learned hashes or document retrieval with binary codes?  
  *Basis*: Conclusion notes empirical verification in other settings is future work.  
  *Why unresolved*: Theoretical bounds are general but have only been validated in mass spectrometry; other domains may have different similarity structures.  
  *Evidence to resolve*: Replicate the study across multiple domains (e.g., image retrieval with binary descriptors, recommendation with binary embeddings) to test trade‑off emergence.

- **Open Question 3**: Can the empirical similarity band (σmin, σmax) of candidate sets be used to select molecular fingerprints or loss functions that minimize the regret between retrieval and similarity objectives?  
  *Basis*: Inferred from Section 4.3; the paper notes that while unknown, these quantities can be computed and may guide model design.  
  *Why unresolved*: Only correlations are shown; no practical framework exists for leveraging σmin/σmax in model selection or training.  
  *Evidence to resolve*: Large‑scale study correlating estimated σmin/σmax across fingerprint types with observed HR@1 gaps; develop a predictive model for choosing loss/fingerprint based on these statistics.

- **Open Question 4**: How does the design of contrastive candidate sets (choice of negatives, candidate size, hardness) influence the severity of the trade‑off between retrieval performance and fingerprint accuracy?  
  *Basis*: Inferred from experiments with different candidate sets and comments on open‑world generalization.  
  *Why unresolved*: The paper tested limited strategies (equal mass, equal formula, PubChem hard negatives) without formal analysis of how properties like hardness or diversity affect the Pareto front.  
  *Evidence to resolve*: Systematic experiments varying negative sampling (curriculum, hard vs easy), candidate set size, and diversity metrics; measure resulting HR@k and Tanimoto; possibly also measure σmin/σmax.

## Limitations
- **Candidate set dependence**: The trade‑off and fingerprint regret are tied to the specific candidate pools (equal‑mass, equal‑formula) used in MassSpecGym. Generalizability to larger, more diverse candidate sets (e.g., entire chemical databases) remains unproven.
- **Theoretical assumptions**: The Bayes‑risk analysis assumes a plug‑in predictor (estimate p(y|x) then optimize utility) and that the same conditional distribution underlies both objectives. Deep neural networks trained with surrogate losses may not exactly follow this decision rule, potentially weakening the bound’s practical tightness.
- **Fingerprint generalizability**: The guidance favoring MAP4 over Morgan (r=2) is based on a single comparison; other fingerprints (e.g., Morgan r=3, RDKit, AtomPairs) were not evaluated, so the conclusion may not extend broadly.
