# Do Metrics for Counterfactorial Explanations Align with User Perception?

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.15607
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15607
- **Reference Count:** 26
- **Primary Result:** Standard automated metrics for counterfactual explanations show weak, dataset-dependent correlations with human perception and fail to predict user-rated quality even when combined.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether commonly used automated metrics for counterfactual explanations (CFs) correspond to how humans perceive explanation quality. Through a user study with 167 participants evaluating 85 CFs across three datasets, the authors measure five quality dimensions (Accuracy, Understandability, Plausibility, Sufficiency, Satisfaction). They then correlate these human judgments with seven standard computational metrics and test if the metrics can jointly predict user ratings. The findings reveal consistently weak and variable correlations across datasets, and combinations of metrics perform poorly in predictive models (best out-of-sample R² = 0.067). The study concludes that current automated metrics are unreliable proxies for human evaluation and calls for more human-centered approaches to assessing explanation quality.

## Method Summary
The study uses three tabular datasets (Mushroom, Obesity Levels, Heart Disease). For each, an XGBoost classifier is trained, and counterfactuals are generated for test instances using Counterfactuals Guided by Prototypes (from Alibi Explain). From a large pool of generated CFs, 85 are sampled via k-means clustering on seven computed metrics to ensure diversity. These 85 CFs (30 from Mushroom, 30 from Obesity, 25 from Heart Disease) are presented to 167 Prolific participants in a within-subjects design. Each CF is rated on five 4-point Likert scales. The inter-rater reliability is high (ICC), and internal consistency across dimensions is strong (Cronbach’s α = 0.88), allowing aggregation into a Combined Quality Score (CQS). For analysis, Pearson correlations are computed between each of the seven automated metrics and each human rating (including CQS) per dataset. Additionally, an exhaustive search over all 127 non-empty subsets of the seven metrics is performed, training five model classes (linear regression, kNN, Random Forest, XGBoost, GAM) with 5-fold cross-validation to predict each human target, recording out-of-sample R².

## Key Results
- Correlations between individual automated metrics and human ratings (CQS) are generally weak (e.g., Trust Score: r = 0.307) and vary substantially across datasets in strength and direction.
- Predictive models combining multiple metrics perform poorly; the best mean R² across all settings is 0.067 (Random Forest), and increasing the number of metrics often degrades performance.
- No single metric or combination reliably proxies human judgments across all datasets and quality dimensions, indicating a fundamental misalignment.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The controlled comparison design isolates the metric–perception relationship by evaluating the same fixed set of counterfactuals with both automated metrics and human raters.
- **Mechanism:** A diverse set of 85 CFs is generated via one method, sampled by clustering on metric values. Human participants rate these exact instances, and standard metrics are computed for them. This directly correlates algorithmic scores with aggregated human judgments, controlling for generation variation.
- **Core Assumption:** The aggregated Combined Quality Score (CQS) represents a stable, underlying construct of "perceived explanation quality."
- **Evidence Anchors:** [abstract] direct comparison of human ratings to automated metrics; [section 3.2 & 3.6] same CF set used for both evaluations.
- **Break Condition:** If CQS lacks internal consistency or unidimensionality, the comparison baseline is invalid.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Weak, dataset-dependent correlations arise because human perception is multi-dimensional and context-sensitive, while standard metrics capture narrow computational properties.
- **Mechanism:** The study measures five distinct human-rated dimensions but correlates single-number metrics (e.g., Sparsity) with the aggregated CQS. Metrics are designed for optimization (e.g., minimal change), not psychological alignment, leading to systematic mismatch.
- **Core Assumption:** Standard CF metrics are intended as generalizable proxies for human-perceived quality.
- **Evidence Anchors:** [section 3.5] high Cronbach’s α and unidimensionality justify CQS aggregation; [section 4.1] correlations weak and patterns differ substantially across datasets.
- **Break Condition:** If human judgments were unidimensional and perfectly aligned with a single computational property, correlations would be strong and consistent.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The failure of metric combinations to improve prediction indicates that current metrics do not capture complementary, independent aspects of human-perceived quality; they overlap or encode irrelevant variance.
- **Mechanism:** Exhaustive powerset analysis tests if any linear or non-linear combination of the seven metrics can approximate CQS. Performance does not improve with added metrics, suggesting metrics either measure noise, are highly inter-correlated but not correlated with human judgment, or miss core latent factors.
- **Core Assumption:** If multiple automated metrics jointly capture different facets of quality, their combination should explain more variance in human judgments than single metrics.
- **Evidence Anchors:** [section 4.2] best R² = 0.067, performance degrades with more metrics; [section 4.3] metrics cannot serve as reliable proxies.
- **Break Condition:** Discovery of a metric subset with substantially higher R² (e.g., >0.3) that stabilizes with more metrics would contradict this.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Human-centered evaluation vs. algorithmic metrics
  - **Why needed:** The core finding is a mismatch between computational metrics (optimizing proximity, sparsity) and psychological factors (trust, satisfaction) that drive user perception.
  - **Quick check:** What are two rating dimensions from the user study not directly measured by any of the seven automated metrics? (Plausibility, Sufficiency)
- **Concept:** Correlation vs. predictive power analysis
  - **Why needed:** Correlation tests simple association; predictive modeling tests whether metrics collectively capture variance in human judgments. Both are necessary to assess metric utility.
  - **Quick check:** Why did the authors use out-of-sample R² from cross-validated regression instead of just reporting correlation coefficients for metric combinations? (Correlation measures linear association for single variables; R² from predictive modeling measures joint explanatory power of multiple metrics while avoiding overfitting.)
- **Concept:** Dataset-dependent effects in XAI evaluation
  - **Why needed:** Results vary dramatically across datasets (Mushroom, Obesity, Heart Disease), showing explanation quality is context-dependent and findings may not generalize.
  - **Quick check:** Which dataset showed the most positive correlations between several metrics and user satisfaction, and what might this indicate? (Obesity Levels; may indicate that explanation properties align better with user expectations in a multi-class health domain.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Data & Model Training → Counterfactual Generation → Metric Computation → Clustering & Sampling → User Study Deployment → Rating Collection → Rating Aggregation → Correlation Analysis → Predictive Modeling (Exhaustive Subsets + CV) → Interpretation
- **Critical path:** Dataset selection → XGBoost training → CF generation with CounterfactualPrototypes → compute seven metrics → k-means clustering → sample 85 CFs → run Prolific study → collect ratings → compute CQS → run correlations and exhaustive regression → draw conclusions.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Sample size vs. coverage: 85 CFs balances diversity and study length (~20 ratings per CF) but limits statistical power.
  - Generalizability vs. depth: Three datasets and one CF generator provide cross-dataset evidence but limit conclusions about other methods or data types.
  - Aggregation vs. dimensionality: Combining five ratings into CQS simplifies analysis but may hide dimension-specific metric alignments.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Negative mean R² for linear models across all metric subsets.
  - Best non-linear model R² < 0.1, with performance peaking around 3–4 metrics then declining.
  - Correlation signs and magnitudes flipping across datasets (e.g., Sparsity negative in Mushroom, positive in Obesity).
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Replicate the full analysis on a fourth tabular dataset (e.g., Adult Income) using the same CF generation method to test pattern generalizability.
  2. Replace CounterfactualPrototypes with a different generator (e.g., DiCE) and repeat to check if misalignment is method-agnostic.
  3. Conduct the user study on one dataset (e.g., Heart Disease) with domain experts (e.g., clinicians) instead of lay users to assess expertise impact.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How can automated counterfactual explanation metrics be redesigned to better align with human perception of quality? (Basis: conclusion calls for human-centered approaches; current metrics fail.)
- **Open Question 2:** Do the observed weak metric–human alignment findings generalize to counterfactuals generated by methods other than Counterfactuals Guided by Prototypes? (Basis: limitation acknowledges single generator; need to test others.)
- **Open Question 3:** What specific, unmeasured factors (e.g., actionability, narrative coherence) most strongly influence human perception? (Basis: future work identifies actionability as a missing factor.)
- **Open Question 4:** What underlying dataset characteristics (e.g., binary vs. multi-class, feature distributions) drive the stark differences in alignment patterns across datasets? (Basis: results vary dramatically across Mushroom, Obesity, Heart Disease; no analysis of dataset properties.)
- **Open Question 5:** Is there any theoretically motivated combination or transformation of existing metrics that can achieve acceptable predictive performance, or are fundamentally new metrics required? (Basis: exhaustive combination search yielded uniformly poor R²; may indicate need for novel metrics.)

## Limitations
- Raw data (metric values and human ratings for the 85 CFs) is not public, preventing direct replication.
- Study uses one CF generation method (CGP) and only three tabular datasets; generalizability to other methods, modalities, or domains is uncertain.
- Participants are lay users; expert judgments in high-stakes domains (e.g., medicine) may differ.
- Aggregating five rating dimensions into CQS assumes unidimensionality, potentially masking dimension-specific metric alignments.
- Small per-dataset samples (25–30 CFs) and exhaustive subset searches risk overfitting and unstable estimates.

## Confidence
- **Weak, dataset-dependent correlations exist between standard metrics and human perception.** High (directly supported by empirical correlations across three datasets).
- **Combining multiple metrics fails to substantially improve predictive power for human judgments.** Medium (observed with one generation method; may not hold for others).
- **Current automated CF metrics fundamentally fail to capture user-perceived quality and require human-centered evaluation.** Low (broad conclusion beyond specific scope; needs broader validation).

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-dataset replication:** Use a fourth tabular dataset (e.g., Adult Income), generate CFs with the same CGP method, and replicate the correlation and predictive analyses to confirm the weak, dataset-dependent alignment pattern.
2. **Alternative CF generator:** Replace CounterfactualPrototypes with a different method (e.g., DiCE) while keeping the pipeline identical; assess whether metric–perception misalignment persists.
3. **Domain expert evaluation:** Repeat the user study on the Heart Disease dataset with medical professionals; compare their rating patterns and metric correlations against the lay-user baseline to evaluate expertise effects.
