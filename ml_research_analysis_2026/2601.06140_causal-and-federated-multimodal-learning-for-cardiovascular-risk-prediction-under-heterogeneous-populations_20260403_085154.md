# Causal and Federated Multimodal Learning for Cardiovascular Risk Prediction under Heterogeneous Populations

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.06140
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06140
- **Reference count:** 32
- **Primary result:** Achieved ROC-AUC 0.994, F1-score 0.981, Brier score 0.008 with fair performance across sexes and ethnicities (ΔAUC < 0.001) on UK Biobank (50k) and federated multi-hospital data (200k+), with <1% AUC drop out-of-distribution and 25 ms inference.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a causal and federated learning framework for multimodal cardiovascular risk prediction that integrates genomic, imaging (MRI), ECG, wearable, and electronic health record data. Using cross-modal transformers and graph neural networks, the model achieves state-of-the-art discrimination (ROC-AUC 0.994) and calibration (Brier 0.008) while maintaining fairness across sexes and ethnicities (ΔAUC < 0.001). The approach incorporates causal invariance regularization and differential privacy to ensure robustness under distribution shifts and data heterogeneity across federated sites. Evaluated on large cohorts (UK Biobank and multi-hospital data), it demonstrates strong out-of-distribution generalization and reliable uncertainty quantification. The work highlights the potential of combining causal principles with federated multimodal learning for trustworthy AI in healthcare.

## Method Summary
The proposed framework fuses heterogeneous data sources through modality-specific encoders followed by a cross-modal transformer that aligns embeddings. Graph neural networks capture relational structure among features, while a causal invariance loss based on conditional mutual information regularizes the latent space to be independent of site-specific confounders. Training occurs in a federated manner with secure aggregation and differential privacy (DP-SGD) to protect patient data. The final predictor outputs cardiovascular risk scores along with uncertainty estimates. This multimodal architecture enables integrative analysis of genomic, imaging, physiological, and clinical data while preserving privacy and promoting fairness.

## Key Results
- **Discrimination & calibration:** ROC-AUC 0.994, F1-score 0.981, Brier score 0.008 on held-out test sets.
- **Fairness:** Performance stability across sexes and ethnicities (ΔAUC < 0.001).
- **Robustness & efficiency:** Out-of-distribution AUC drop <1%; 95.3% prediction interval coverage; 25 ms inference per patient.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The model’s high performance stems from three synergistic components. First, causal invariance regularization forces the learned representations to be unaffected by spurious correlations tied to specific sites or populations, improving generalization to unseen environments. Second, cross-modal transformers effectively integrate diverse data types by learning attention across modalities, while graph neural networks exploit known biological and clinical relationships (e.g., between cardiac measurements and risk factors). Third, federated learning with differential privacy allows training on decentralized data without centralizing sensitive information, reducing privacy leakage risks and encouraging participation from multiple institutions. Together, these mechanisms address the key challenges of heterogeneity, privacy, and confounding in multimodal healthcare AI.

## Foundational Learning
- **Causal invariance** – *Why needed*: To ensure model predictions remain stable across different population strata and data collection sites despite hidden confounders. *Quick check*: Compare performance on multiple held-out sites; invariance loss should correlate with reduced site-specific variance.
- **Federated learning (FL)** – *Why needed*: Enables training on sensitive patient data across hospitals without data leaving local premises, complying with privacy regulations. *Quick check*: Verify that global model performance matches a centralized baseline while keeping local data private.
- **Cross-modal transformers** – *Why needed*: Capture interactions between heterogeneous data types (e.g., genomic and imaging) that are crucial for comprehensive risk assessment. *Quick check*: Ablate the transformer module and measure drop in multimodal fusion gains.
- **Graph neural networks (GNNs)** – *Why needed*: Represent structured relationships among clinical variables (e.g., disease hierarchy, organ systems) to improve predictive power. *Quick check*: Replace GNN with a fully connected baseline and assess impact on performance and interpretability.
- **Differential privacy (DP-SGD)** – *Why needed*: Provide mathematical guarantees against membership inference and reconstruction attacks. *Quick check*: Audit the privacy budget (ε) and conduct attacks to ensure leakage is within acceptable limits.
- **Uncertainty quantification** – *Why needed*: Reliable confidence estimates are essential for clinical decision-making. *Quick check*: Check calibration curves and coverage probabilities on out-of-distribution data.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map (plain text):**
Raw heterogeneous data → Modality encoders (genomic, MRI, ECG, wearable, EHR) → Cross-modal transformer → Graph neural network → Causal invariance regularization → Federated aggregation (with DP-SGD) → Risk predictor → Output (risk score, uncertainty)

**Critical path:** The essential flow for training and inference is: local data encoding → cross-modal fusion → graph propagation → causality‑aware latent representation → (federated averaging across sites) → final prediction. Any break in this pipeline (e.g., misaligned embeddings or failed invariance regularization) can degrade fairness or robustness.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Model complexity vs. computational efficiency*: Deep multimodal transformers increase accuracy but raise inference latency; the authors report 25 ms, but hardware specifications are missing.
- *Privacy vs. utility*: Stronger differential privacy (lower ε) protects data more but may harm model performance; the paper does not explore this trade‑off explicitly.
- *Causal regularization vs. predictive power*: Imposing invariance constraints might discard useful site‑specific patterns; proper tuning is necessary.

**Failure signatures:**
- Large performance variance across federated sites indicates poor invariance.
- Significant drop in OOD AUC (>5%) suggests overfitting to training environments.
- High membership inference attack success points to insufficient privacy guarantees.
- Missing‑data patterns (e.g., MNAR) not handled could cause biased predictions.

**First three experiments:**
1. **Multimodal ablation** – Train with unimodal inputs and all combinations to quantify contribution of each modality and the fusion gains.
2. **Causal invariance ablation** – Remove the invariance regularization term and compare fairness metrics (ΔAUC) and OOD robustness.
3. **Privacy‑utility trade‑off** – Vary the DP‑SGD noise scale or clipping norm and measure effect on AUC, fairness, and attack success rates.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Does the proposed multimodal framework maintain its high discrimination, calibration, and fairness metrics in a prospective clinical trial setting, rather than retrospective cohort analysis?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors explicitly list "Lack of prospective and interventional validation" as a key limitation, noting that current results are from retrospective cohorts and that prospective trials are necessary to confirm real-world reliability and safety.
- **Why unresolved:** Retrospective validation on curated biobank and federated EHR data may not capture the complexity, data drift, and deployment challenges of live clinical environments, including patient selection bias and evolving practice patterns.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A multi-center prospective study where the model is integrated into clinical workflows, with outcomes measured against standard of care, including hard endpoints like major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), clinician adoption rates, and impact on patient management decisions.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How can the framework's causal invariance guarantees be formally verified in the presence of *unmeasured* or *hidden confounders* that differ across federated sites, which are not accounted for by the current regularization approach?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors acknowledge "distribution shift and hidden confounding" as a limitation, stating that despite multi-institutional data, confounding factors and unobserved variables may persist. Their causal latent alignment uses conditional mutual information regularization but does not specify validation against ground-truth causal structures.
- **Why unresolved:** Federated learning across heterogeneous sites amplifies risks of latent distribution shifts from site-specific unmeasured variables (e.g., local scanner protocols, unrecorded socioeconomic factors). Current invariance constraints are heuristic and lack formal guarantees under complex, unknown confounding.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Development and application of sensitivity analysis frameworks (e.g., using synthetic data with injected hidden confounders) or incorporation of negative control variables/exposures to quantify the robustness of causal claims. Formal verification using causal discovery on per-site data to identify and adjust for latent variables.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can the integrated differential privacy and federated learning protocol provide *certified* robustness against sophisticated membership inference and model inversion attacks in high-dimensional multimodal settings, where privacy utility trade-offs are poorly understood?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper states that "model inversion and membership inference attacks are still feasible" despite DP-SGD and FL, and identifies "Privacy and robustness to adversaries" as a limitation requiring "certified defenses."
- **Why unresolved:** Standard DP-SGD guarantees (ε, δ)-privacy are often loose and may not hold under multimodal data heterogeneity and iterative FL updates. Attack surfaces expand with cross-modal attention mechanisms and graph structures, making privacy leakage pathways complex and context-dependent.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Empirical evaluation using state-of-the-art privacy attack benchmarks (e.g., membership inference with shadow models, inversion via generative models) on the proposed architecture. Theoretical analysis deriving tighter, modality-aware privacy bounds, followed by implementation of privacy auditing pipelines and adaptive defense mechanisms (e.g., gradient clipping tuned for multimodal gradients).

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** To what extent are the SHAP-based feature attributions and counterfactual explanations *causally valid* for clinical decision-making, and how can they be systematically validated against known cardiovascular pathophysiology or expert clinician judgment?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors note that "explanations obtained through SHAP/counterfactuals are not necessarily causally correct" and propose future work involving "formal causal graphs do-calculus based verification, and clinician centered interpretability studies."
- **Why unresolved:** Post-hoc explainability methods like SHAP highlight associative importance but do not guarantee causal mechanisms. In multimodal healthcare AI, a feature's attribution could reflect confounding or proxy relationships rather than true causal drivers (e.g., left ventricular mass might be a mediator, not a cause).
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A two-part validation: (1) causal consistency checks using domain knowledge graphs (e.g., does the model's top feature align with established causal pathways in cardiology?) and (2) prospective user studies with cardiologists assessing the clinical plausibility and actionability of generated explanations, particularly counterfactuals (e.g., "If LV mass were reduced by 10%, predicted risk would decrease by X%").

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** How does the framework handle *missing not at random* (MNAR) data patterns across modalities—common in real-world EHR and wearable streams—and can the proposed generative imputation extensions preserve both predictive performance and causal invariances?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The limitation "Handling missingness and data irregularity" is raised, with future work suggested on "generative imputation, temporal point processes, and uncertainty aware modeling" for MNAR scenarios.
- **Why unresolved:** Current preprocessing assumes complete embeddings; real-world multimodal data often have structured missingness (e.g., no ECG during asymptomatic periods, missing genomic data for certain ancestries). MNAR mechanisms can induce bias that violates causal invariance if not properly modeled, and standard imputation may distort inter-modal relationships.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Simulation studies introducing clinically realistic MNAR patterns (e.g., missing wearables in less adherent patients, missing MRI due to contraindications) and benchmarking imputation strategies (VAEs, GANs, Bayesian models) on downstream tasks. Evaluation should include metrics for bias in causal effect estimates (e.g., using synthetic ground truth) and fairness across subgroups with differential missingness.

## Limitations
- Claims of near-perfect discrimination and fairness lack independent validation on external cohorts.
- Critical implementation details of the federated protocol (number of sites, data distribution, privacy budget) are omitted, hindering reproducibility and privacy assessment.
- The reported ΔAUC < 0.001 for fairness is unusually small and may be underpowered or not corrected for multiple testing.

## Confidence
- **Methodology:** Medium – conceptually sound but underspecified.
- **Predictive performance and robustness:** Low – extraordinary numbers without external or statistical validation.
- **Interpretability (SHAP):** Low – anecdotal analysis lacking formal validation.
- **Inference efficiency:** Medium – reported 25 ms but without hardware context.
