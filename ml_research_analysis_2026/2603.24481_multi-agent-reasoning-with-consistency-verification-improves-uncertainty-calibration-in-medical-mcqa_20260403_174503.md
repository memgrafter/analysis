# Multi-Agent Reasoning with Consistency Verification Improves Uncertainty Calibration in Medical MCQA

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.24481
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24481
- **Reference count:** 28
- **Primary result:** MARC reduces Expected Calibration Error (ECE) by 49–74% relative to a single-specialist baseline on high-disagreement medical QA datasets, achieving ECE = 0.091 on MedQA-250.

## Executive Summary
Miscalibrated confidence in medical AI models hinders clinical trust and deployment. This paper introduces the Multi-Agent Reasoning with Consistency Verification (MARC) framework to improve uncertainty calibration in medical multiple-choice question answering (MCQA). MARC employs four domain-specialist agents (respiratory, cardiology, neurology, gastroenterology) whose reasoning is validated by a Two-Phase Verification process. This process measures internal consistency to produce per-specialist confidence scores (S-scores). A weighted fusion strategy then selects the final answer and calibrates confidence using these S-scores. Across four high-disagreement medical QA datasets, MARC substantially reduces calibration error (by 49–74%) while improving accuracy. Ablation studies confirm that consistency verification is the primary driver of calibration improvement, while multi-agent reasoning boosts accuracy. The results demonstrate that label-free consistency signals yield more reliable uncertainty estimates for safety-critical clinical AI, though accuracy on knowledge-recall benchmarks can be limited.

## Method Summary
MARC is an inference-time framework without training. Four specialist agents (based on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) independently answer a medical question using a 5-step chain-of-thought (CoT) and provide an initial confidence score. Each specialist’s reasoning undergoes Two-Phase Verification: first, four factual claims are extracted; then, each claim is answered independently (no context) and with the original reasoning as context. An inconsistency score \(I_k\) is computed via Jaccard similarity between these two answer sets (after stop-word filtering). The specialist’s verified S-score is \(S_k = C_k^{(0)} \cdot (1 - I_k)\), downweighting confidence when the model’s answers conflict under slight context changes. Final answer selection and confidence calibration use S-Score Weighted Fusion: the winning answer is chosen by vote count weighted by the mean S-score of its supporters; final confidence blends the vote fraction with the mean and minimum S-score among supporters. The system is evaluated on high-disagreement subsets of MedQA-USMLE and MedMCQA, comparing four ablations: single specialist (no verification), single specialist with verification, multi-agent without verification, and the full MARC system.

## Key Results
- MARC reduces Expected Calibration Error (ECE) by 49–74% relative to a single-specialist baseline across four datasets.
- On MedQA-250 (n=250), the full system achieves ECE = 0.091 (74.4% reduction) and AUROC = 0.630 (+0.056) at 59.2% accuracy.
- Ablation analysis shows Two-Phase Verification is the primary driver of calibration improvement, while multi-agent reasoning drives accuracy gains.
- On MedMCQA-250, adding verification to the multi-agent system (C3→C4) reduces accuracy (−2.8 pp) but still improves ECE, indicating a trade-off between calibration and absolute correctness on knowledge-recall benchmarks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Consistency-Based Confidence Penalization
- **Claim:** Two-Phase Verification reduces miscalibration by converting internal inconsistency into a penalty on initial confidence, compressing overconfident scores toward the model’s true accuracy.
- **Mechanism:** A specialist’s reasoning is decomposed into factual claims. Each claim is answered independently (without original reasoning) and again with the reasoning as context. Cross-condition inconsistency is measured via Jaccard similarity; the inconsistency score \(I_k\) is subtracted from 1 and multiplied by the initial confidence \(C_k^{(0)}\) to produce the S-score \(S_k = C_k^{(0)} \cdot (1 - I_k)\). This proportionally downweights confidence when the model’s own answers conflict across conditions.
- **Core assumption:** Internal inconsistency (different answers to the same factual query under slight context changes) is a label-free indicator of epistemic uncertainty that correlates with likelihood of error.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] describes the process; [section 3.3] details claim extraction, answering, and Jaccard computation; ablation (Config 1→2) shows ECE drops 31–51% without accuracy change.
- **Break condition:** A specialist produces self-consistent but factually wrong reasoning (e.g., on knowledge-recall questions like MedMCQA), receiving a high S-score despite an incorrect answer. The mechanism fails when consistency does not imply correctness.

### Mechanism 2: Multi-Agent Error Suppression
- **Claim:** Multi-agent reasoning improves accuracy by pooling independent specialist perspectives, allowing correct answers to emerge from majority voting even when individual specialists err.
- **Mechanism:** Four domain-specialist agents generate independent diagnoses. The S-Score Weighted Fusion selects the answer by vote count weighted by mean S-score of supporters. Diversity in specialty expertise increases the probability that at least one agent possesses relevant knowledge; voting suppresses idiosyncratic errors.
- **Core assumption:** Errors across independently prompted specialist agents are sufficiently un-correlated that majority vote improves the chance of a correct collective answer.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] notes accuracy gains; [section 5.1] explains the pooling and voting rationale; neighboring multi-agent medical QA papers show analogous benefits.
- **Break condition:** All specialists share a common knowledge gap or bias (e.g., all fail on narrow factual recall), or the question lies outside all four covered specialties, potentially reinforcing a systematic error.

### Mechanism 3: Consensus-Aware Confidence Blending
- **Claim:** S-Score Weighted Fusion calibrates final confidence by blending vote fraction with the distribution of S-scores among supporters, producing lower confidence for weak majorities and higher for strong consensus.
- **Mechanism:** For the winning answer \(\hat{a}\), final confidence \(\hat{C} = v \cdot \bar{S}_{\hat{a}} + (1-v) \cdot \min_{k:a_k=\hat{a}} S_k\), where \(v\) is the vote fraction. This ties reported confidence to both the proportion of agreeing specialists and the minimum verified confidence among them.
- **Core assumption:** The S-score distribution among supporters signals reliability: a tight, high-S cluster indicates robust consensus, while a spread or low minimum indicates fragility.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] describes the fusion; [section 3.4] presents the formula; internal evidence shows Config 3 (multi-agent, no verification) has mean confidence ~0.90 despite ~50% accuracy, while Config 4 (full) reduces mean confidence to ~0.55, aligning better with accuracy.
- **Break condition:** S-scores are systematically mis-calibrated relative to answer correctness (e.g., all specialists receive similar S-scores regardless of correctness), causing the fusion to mis-weight a wrong majority with high S-scores.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Uncertainty Calibration vs. Discrimination (ECE vs. AUROC)**
  - *Why needed here:* The paper’s core finding is a large ECE reduction, yet AUROC sometimes drops when adding verification alone. Understanding that calibration (confidence matches accuracy) and discrimination (confidence ranks correct higher than incorrect) are distinct properties is essential to interpret the ablation patterns.
  - *Quick check:* “If a model assigns 70% confidence to all predictions and is correct 70% of the time, is it well-calibrated? Is it useful for deferral?”

- **Concept: Internal Consistency as a Label-Free Uncertainty Signal**
  - *Why needed here:* Two-Phase Verification is the primary calibration driver. It does not use gold labels; instead, it treats inconsistency across subtly different query formulations as an epistemic uncertainty estimate. Grasping this label-free nature is key to understanding why it can be applied at inference time without additional data.
  - *Quick check:* “Why might a model give different answers to the same factual question when the prompt context changes slightly? What does that imply about its confidence?”

- **Concept: High-Disagreement Questions as a Stress Test for Calibration**
  - *Why needed here:* The evaluation filters for questions where the four specialists initially disagree. These are precisely the cases where uncertainty quantification matters most for deferral decisions. Recognizing this design choice clarifies why calibration gains are the central finding.
  - *Quick check:* “If all specialists always agree, how useful are their confidence scores for a clinician deciding whether to defer?”

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:**
  Specialist Agents (4 × Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct with domain prompts) -> Two-Phase Verification (claim extraction, independent/reference answering, Jaccard inconsistency) -> S-Scores -> S-Score Weighted Fusion (vote-weighted selection, confidence blending) -> Final Answer & Confidence
- **Critical path:** Input question → Parallel specialist calls (4 ×) → Parallel verification sub-calls per specialist (≈4 × each) → Compute all S_k → Fusion to select answer and compute confidence.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Latency vs. calibration: Verification multiplies LLM calls (~16× vs. single specialist), increasing wall-clock time by ~7×; parallelization across GPUs is the primary mitigation.
  - Accuracy vs. calibration on knowledge-recall benchmarks: On MedMCQA, adding verification to multi-agent (C3→C4) reduces accuracy (−2.8 pp on 250-q set) while improving ECE. Trade-off: better uncertainty estimates at the cost of absolute correctness on fact-heavy questions.
  - Specialty coverage: Only four specialties are covered. Extending to other domains requires defining new specialist prompts and ensuring high-disagreement subsets exist.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Calibration failure: Mean predicted confidence remains far above observed accuracy in unverified configurations (C1, C3). S-score distribution does not compact toward the empirical accuracy.
  - Discrimination failure: AUROC drops when adding verification alone (C2 vs. C1 on MedMCQA-100: −0.064). Indicates S-scores are not reliably ranking correct vs. incorrect predictions.
  - Accuracy collapse on MedMCQA: Full system (C4) accuracy lower than multi-agent without verification (C3) on MedMCQA-250 (44.0% vs. 46.8%). Suggests verification over-penalizes specialists whose consistent reasoning is factually wrong.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Baseline run: Execute Config 1 (single specialist, no verification) on MedQA-250. Document accuracy (~54.4%), ECE (~0.355), AUROC (~0.574), and mean confidence (~0.899). Confirms severe overconfidence.
  2. Verification isolation: Execute Config 2 (single specialist + verification). Expect identical accuracy, significantly lower ECE (≈−50%), and possibly lower AUROC. Validates that Two-Phase Verification alone compresses confidence without harming correctness.
  3. Multi-agent isolation: Execute Config 3 (four specialists, no verification). Expect accuracy improvement (~+2–3 pp) but still poor calibration (ECE similar to C1). Demonstrates that diversity improves correctness but does not fix miscalibration.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** Does grounding Two-Phase Verification against an external medical knowledge base (e.g., PubMed) reduce overconfidence on factually incorrect but self-consistent reasoning, thereby improving calibration without sacrificing accuracy? (Basis: authors identify self-consistent but wrong reasoning as the core limitation and propose retrieval augmentation as the most direct path forward.)
- **Open Question 2:** Do the calibration improvements (ECE reduction) from MARC persist when scaling to larger language models (e.g., 13B, 70B, or GPT-4 class), or do they diminish because base models are already better calibrated? (Basis: authors explicitly ask whether improvements hold at larger model scales.)
- **Open Question 3:** Can learned fusion weights (trained on a small specialist performance validation set) outperform the current heuristic S-score weighted fusion in balancing accuracy and calibration? (Basis: authors suggest vote-count fusion can be improved with learned weights from a validation set.)
- **Open Question 4:** Does MARC's calibration robustness extend to medical domains beyond the four evaluated specialties (respiratory, cardiology, neurology, gastroenterology), such as anatomy, pharmacology, or surgery? (Basis: evaluation is restricted to four specialties due to high-disagreement curation; authors question transferability.)
- **Open Question 5:** How does MARC perform on medical questions that inherently require multi-domain integration (e.g., a cardiology case with neurological complications), where specialist agents might each hold partial but conflicting knowledge? (Basis: specialists are domain-isolated; interaction with cross-domain questions is untested.)

## Limitations
- Curation pipeline details (seed, sampling, agreement mix) are missing → reproducibility unclear.
- Parsing success rates for claim extraction and answering are unreported → potential bias in S-score distribution.
- Evaluation limited to four specialties and a 7B model (Qwen2.5-7B) → generalizability unknown.
- Over-penalization on knowledge-recall benchmarks (MedMCQA) shows S-scores may miss some errors.
- High computational cost (~16× LLM calls) may hinder deployment without significant parallelization.

## Next Checks
1. **Curation reproducibility:** Execute the exact high-disagreement selection (specify seed, thresholds, and agreement mix) and measure variability in the resulting subset composition.
2. **Verification robustness:** Quantify extraction and answering success rates; analyze how failures impact S-score distribution and ECE.
3. **Generalization:** Test MARC on other specialties (e.g., pediatrics, psychiatry) and with a different LLM scale (e.g., Llama-3-70B) to assess transferability of calibration gains.
