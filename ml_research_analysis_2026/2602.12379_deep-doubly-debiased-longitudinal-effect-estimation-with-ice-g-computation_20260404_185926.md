# Deep Doubly Debiased Longitudinal Effect Estimation with ICE G-Computation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.12379
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12379
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: D3-Net reduces bias and variance for longitudinal treatment effect estimation, e.g., mean absolute bias drops from ~1.2 to ~0.4 at τ=15 under expanded confounding on MIMIC-III benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
Longitudinal causal inference with time‑varying confounders is hindered by treatment‑confounder feedback. Deep ICE G‑computation, while flexible, suffers from error propagation through shared neural representations. To address this, the paper introduces D3‑Net, a two‑stage debiasing framework. First, an ICE sequence is trained using Sequential Doubly Robust pseudo‑outcomes, stabilized by a target network and an auxiliary covariate simulator. Second, the trained nuisance models are fed into Longitudinal Targeted Minimum Loss‑Based Estimation (LTMLE) for final inference. Extensive experiments on semi‑synthetic MIMIC‑III data across multiple horizons and confounding intensities show that D3‑Net consistently outperforms state‑of‑the‑art ICE‑based baselines, achieving substantial bias and variance reductions.

## Method Summary
D3‑Net employs a multi‑task Transformer backbone with three heads: Q predicts the counterfactual outcome given treatment and history; G estimates the propensity of treatment given history; and S simulates next‑step covariates. The architecture processes sequences of historical covariates and treatments with positional encodings and causal masking. During training, the Q‑head is supervised with Sequential Doubly Robust (SDR) pseudo‑outcomes that recursively incorporate bias corrections from estimated outcomes and propensities, reducing error propagation. A slowly updated target network supplies stable pseudo‑outcomes, while the auxiliary S‑head regularizes the shared representation by predicting future covariates. The total loss combines the SDR‑supervised Q loss with cross‑entropy for G and mean squared error for S, weighted by an auxiliary factor α. After convergence, D3‑Net discards the SDR correction and applies Longitudinal TMLE to the learned Q and G models, performing a one‑step targeted update with L1 regularization to produce the final CAPO estimates. This two‑stage procedure yields robust and efficient estimates even under long horizons and strong confounding.

## Key Results
- D3‑Net achieves significantly lower bias and variance across τ=10, 15, 20 under both limited and expanded confounding compared to G‑comp, LTMLE, DeepACE, and DeepLTMLE.
- For τ=15 with expanded confounding, mean absolute bias across counterfactual sequences decreases from ~1.2 (DeepLTMLE) to ~0.4 (D3‑Net).
- The method maintains strong performance even with longer horizons and increased noise from latent confounders.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: SDR pseudo-outcome debiasing
- **Claim**: Training ICE regressions with SDR pseudo‑outcomes reduces error propagation compared to standard ICE.
- **Mechanism**: Instead of using raw next‑step outcome \(\hat{Q}_{t+1}\) as the regression target, SDR adds an influence‑function‑based correction term that accounts for upstream bias from outcome and propensity models. This correction is applied recursively at each time step, making each regression locally doubly robust.
- **Core assumption**: The correction term accurately estimates the bias, requiring that at each step either the outcome or propensity model is consistently estimated (2τ multiply robust).
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: “train the ICE sequence using Sequential Doubly Robust (SDR) pseudo‑outcomes, which provide bias‑corrected targets for each regression”
  - [section]: Section 4.1 and Eq. (7) define the SDR transformation; Lemma 3 states 2τ multiply robust consistency.
  - [corpus]: Corpus includes general doubly robust papers but the specific longitudinal SDR formulation (Díaz et al. [12]) is not directly represented; evidence is indirect.
- **Break condition**: If propensity scores are extreme (near 0 or 1), the correction term can amplify variance; the mechanism also requires stable estimation of nuisance models at each step.

### Mechanism 2: Target network stabilization
- **Claim**: A target network (slowly updated copy) stabilizes training by smoothing the moving targets in recursive ICE.
- **Mechanism**: In shared‑parameter ICE, the pseudo‑outcome \(\hat{Q}_{t+1}\) depends on current parameters; as parameters update, the target shifts, causing instability. The target network provides lagged targets, decoupling the target from immediate parameter changes.
- **Core assumption**: The target network update rate β is small enough that targets change slowly relative to the learning rate.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: “stabilized by a target network”
  - [section]: Section 4.1 describes using a target network with Polyak averaging; Algorithm 2 shows its use in generating pseudo‑outcomes.
  - [corpus]: Corpus does not contain the deep RL works that introduced target networks; evidence is weak.
- **Break condition**: If β is too large, the target network tracks too quickly and loses smoothing; if too small, targets become stale and slow convergence.

### Mechanism 3: Auxiliary covariate simulator regularization
- **Claim**: Predicting next‑step covariates with an auxiliary S‑head regularizes the shared representation, improving robustness of nuisance models.
- **Mechanism**: The multi‑task architecture includes a simulator that predicts \(L_{t+1}\) from history and treatment. This directly supervises the dynamics of time‑varying confounders, encouraging representations that capture temporal structure, which benefits outcome and treatment modeling.
- **Core assumption**: Covariate dynamics are predictive of outcome and treatment, and the auxiliary task is not too noisy.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: “multi‑task Transformer with a covariate simulator head for auxiliary supervision, regularizing representations against corruption by noisy pseudo‑outcomes”
  - [section]: Section 4.2 defines the S‑head and its loss \(L_S\); Figure 1 shows architecture.
  - [corpus]: Corpus lacks direct evidence; auxiliary tasks are common in representation learning but not specifically in longitudinal causal inference in the provided papers.
- **Break condition**: If the auxiliary task is mis‑specified (e.g., covariates are not Markovian) or the weight α is too high, it may dominate and degrade primary task performance.

## Foundational Learning

### Iterative Conditional Expectation (ICE) G‑computation
- **Why needed**: ICE is the backbone of D3‑Net; it decomposes the CAPO into a backward recursion of conditional outcome regressions.
- **Quick check**: Write the recursive equation: \(Q_t(A_t, H_t) = \mathbb{E}[Q_{t+1}(a_{t+1}, H_{t+1}) \mid A_t, H_t]\) under a fixed counterfactual sequence.

### Sequential Doubly Robust (SDR) estimation
- **Why needed**: SDR provides bias‑corrected targets for each step, crucial for reducing error propagation during training.
- **Quick check**: What is the SDR pseudo‑outcome \(Q^\dagger_{t+1}(a_{t+1}, H_{t+1})\)? It equals \(\hat{Q}_{t+1} + \sum_{s=t+1}^\tau \left( \prod_{k=t+1}^s \hat{w}_k \right) [\hat{Q}_{s+1} - \hat{Q}_s]\) where \(\hat{w}_k\) are inverse propensity weights.

### Longitudinal Targeted Minimum Loss‑Based Estimation (LTMLE)
- **Why needed**: Final debiasing step ensures multiply robust consistency and efficient finite‑sample properties.
- **Quick check**: What is the influence function for CAPO? \(D^*(O) = \sum_{s=1}^\tau \left( \prod_{k=1}^s w_k \right) [Q_{s+1}(a_{s+1}, H_{s+1}) - Q_s(A_s, H_s)]\). LTMLE targets this score equation.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Input: sequence of history (L_t, A_t) for t=1..τ. Shared Transformer backbone: processes embedded sequence with positional encodings and causal masking. Heads: Q‑head (predicts outcome \(Q_t(A_t, H_t)\)), G‑head (predicts propensity \(G_t(H_t)\)), S‑head (predicts next‑step covariates \(S_t(A_t, H_t) \approx L_{t+1}\)). Target network: delayed copy of backbone and Q/G heads (used to generate stable pseudo‑outcomes during training). Losses: L_Q (MSE), L_G (binary cross‑entropy), L_S (MSE). Total loss L = L_Q + α(L_G + L_S). Inference: after training, use main network’s Q and G outputs, then apply LTMLE algorithm.

### Critical path
1. Preprocess data into trajectories.
2. Training loop:
   a. Forward main network → Q, G, S.
   b. Forward target network → Q′, G′.
   c. Construct SDR pseudo‑outcome \(Q^\dagger_{t+1}\) using target outputs (recursive computation from t=τ down to 1) as per Algorithm 2; clip Q† to [0,1].
   d. Compute losses L_Q, L_G, L_S.
   e. Backpropagate to update main network parameters.
   f. Update target network via Polyak averaging (θ′ ← βθ + (1‑β)θ′) with small β (e.g., 0.02).
3. After training, run Longitudinal TMLE (Algorithm 3) on evaluation data using the trained Q and G to produce final CAPO estimates; TMLE performs a one‑step targeted update with L1 penalty λ.

### Design tradeoffs
- SDR vs raw ICE: SDR reduces error propagation but inverse propensity products may inflate variance; clipping needed but may bias.
- Target network update rate β: smaller β improves stability but slows adaptation; too large loses smoothing effect.
- Auxiliary task weight α: balances representation learning; too high may dominate primary tasks.
- Model capacity: Transformer depth/width tradeoff between expressiveness and overfitting.
- LTMLE targeting: uses uncorrected Q and G; if SDR training overfits, LTMLE may not fully correct.

### Failure signatures
- Training divergence: check gradient norms, loss spikes; remedy by adjusting clipping, learning rate, or β.
- High bias on test: model underfitting; increase capacity, training epochs, or adjust α.
- High variance across seeds: instability; increase β or batch size.
- LTMLE not improving: may indicate poor quality of nuisance models or inappropriate λ.
- SDR computation off‑by‑one: verify indices and counterfactual sequences.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How robust is D3‑Net to unmeasured confounding, and can its assumptions be relaxed?
- **Basis in paper**: As with all observational studies, our results rely on standard causal assumptions and may be affected by unmeasured confounding, so they should not be interpreted as direct clinical recommendations without further investigation.
- **Why unresolved**: The method assumes sequential ignorability; the impact of violations is not studied.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Perform sensitivity analyses; incorporate methods for unmeasured confounding (e.g., instrumental variables, negative controls); test on datasets with known hidden confounders.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Does hard clipping of SDR pseudo‑outcomes bias the estimates, and are there better stabilization alternatives?
- **Basis in paper**: we recognize that such hard clipping risks introducing bias.
- **Why unresolved**: Hard clipping is used for stability but may systematically distort the debiasing targets; no alternative explored.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Compare clipping to bounded activations, robust losses, or adaptive scaling; measure bias‑variance trade‑off in simulations where truth is known.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Can D3‑Net be generalized to continuous or multi‑valued treatment regimes?
- **Basis in paper**: The setup restricts A_t to binary values and all derivations (e.g., weights) assume a binary action space.
- **Why unresolved**: Many applications involve continuous dosing; extending requires redefining propensity scores and SDR/LTMLE for continuous actions.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Derive continuous‑treatment analogues of SDR and LTMLE; implement and evaluate on synthetic data with continuous treatments.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Does the two‑stage SDR+LTMLE procedure inherit the multiply robust consistency of LTMLE?
- **Basis in paper**: The paper combines SDR and LTMLE without formal consistency proof; it separately cites Lemma 2 (LTMLE) and Lemma 4 (SDR error bound).
- **Why unresolved**: It is unknown whether the SDR‑initialized models satisfy the error conditions for LTMLE’s consistency; the second‑order bound may not be sufficient.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Theoretically link the SDR error bound to LTMLE’s requirements; prove consistency under weaker conditions; simulate with various nuisance misspecifications to empirically verify.

## Limitations
- Missing implementation details (e.g., exact S‑head input, TMLE hyperparameters, SDR clipping thresholds) hinder full replication.
- The multiply robust consistency guarantee may break if nuisance models are poorly estimated at any time step, especially under extreme propensity values.
- Evaluation is restricted to a single medical dataset (MIMIC‑III/MIMIC‑IV), limiting claims about generalizability.

## Confidence
- Overall performance (bias/variance reduction): Medium
- SDR pseudo‑outcome debiasing: Medium
- Target network stabilization: Low
- Auxiliary covariate simulator: Medium
- LTMLE final debiasing: High
- Generalizability: Low

## Next Checks

2. **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: Systematically vary auxiliary weight α, target decay β, IPW clipping quantiles, and TMLE λ to identify robust configurations and failure modes.
