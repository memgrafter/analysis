# Explainability for Fault Detection System in Chemical Processes

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.16341
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16341
- Reference count: 13
- Primary result: Integrated Gradients and SHAP explanations are physically plausible for an LSTM fault classifier on the Tennessee Eastman Process, with SHAP sometimes indicating root-cause-proximal variables.

## Executive Summary
The study evaluates two model-agnostic post-hoc explainable AI methods—Integrated Gradients (IG) and SHAP—for interpreting an LSTM-based fault classifier applied to the Tennessee Eastman Process (TEP). The LSTM achieves 99% accuracy across 20 process faults. The authors compare the feature attributions from both methods and validate their plausibility against known process dynamics and subsystem groupings. Results show that in most cases, both methods highlight features consistent with the affected process subsystem, while SHAP occasionally identifies variables closer to the fault root cause. The work demonstrates the broad applicability of model-agnostic XAI for black-box models in chemical process monitoring.

## Method Summary
The authors use a pre-trained LSTM classifier (from Gravanis et al., 2022) trained on multivariate time-series data from the TEP simulator. The dataset comprises 52 measured/manipulated variables under normal operation and 20 injected faults (IDV 1–20). Data is segmented into fixed-length sequences (e.g., 99 samples). For each fault, the first 100 post-fault sequences are selected. Integrated Gradients computes gradients along a straight-line path from a baseline (e.g., normal operating point) to the input, accumulating attributions. SHAP values are computed via a model-agnostic explainer (KernelExplainer) using a background dataset of normal samples. The per-sequence attributions are averaged and normalized to identify highly-contributed features. The resulting feature importance rankings are qualitatively compared between IG and SHAP and validated by mapping top features to known TEP subsystems (e.g., reactor, separator). The analysis focuses on plausibility rather than formal metrics.

## Key Results
- Both XAI methods identified process-subsystem-consistent features for most faults, confirming their explanations align with known TEP dynamics.
- For complex multi-variable faults (e.g., IDV 8), SHAP highlighted variables with larger deviations, suggesting it may provide root-cause-closer insights compared to IG.
- The LSTM fault classifier achieved 99% test accuracy, indicating strong predictive performance that supports the reliability of the explanations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- Claim: **Model-agnostic post-hoc XAI methods can generate physically plausible explanations for an LSTM fault classifier in a chemical process.**
- Mechanism: The trained LSTM learns temporal patterns from multivariate process data. Post-hoc XAI (IG, SHAP) then examines the trained model's input-output relationships, attributing the classification prediction back to input time-series features. This attribution reveals which measured/manipulated variables (xmeas, xmv) the model deems most influential for a specific fault diagnosis.
- Core assumption: The LSTM has internalized meaningful, causal process dynamics such that its decision logic correlates with real-world physical relationships (e.g., a reactor coolant fault will manifest via reactor temperature and cooling valve position).
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Both XAI methods identified process-subsystem-consistent features in most cases... XAI outputs were validated as physically plausible against known process dynamics."
  - [section 4.1] "For faults that affect the reactor, there are three features... that according to XAI methods play a crucial role in classifier decisions." Also, validation via grouping faults by affected subsystem (Table 3).
  - [corpus] Weak anchor: Corpus shows related work (e.g., "Combining SHAP and Causal Analysis...") exploring SHAP for industrial processes, indicating community validation of the approach. Explicit: **Corpus evidence is indirect; the paper itself provides the primary validation against TEP dynamics.**
- Break condition: If XAI-attributed "important" features consistently contradict established chemical engineering principles for a given fault (e.g., attributing a feed composition fault primarily to a distant purge valve with no plausible causal link), the mechanism fails.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: **For certain complex, multi-variable faults, SHAP may provide finer-grained, root-cause-closer explanations than Integrated Gradients.**
- Mechanism: SHAP computes Shapley values from coalition game theory, considering all possible feature subsets and their marginal contributions. This can better capture non-linear, interactive effects among process variables in complex disturbances. IG computes accumulated gradients along a straight-line path from baseline to input, which may average out or miss subtle interactive signals.
- Core assumption: The fault manifestation in IDV 8 (random Stream 4 composition change) involves subtle, distributed effects across multiple measured variables where feature interactions are key. SHAP's exhaustive subset analysis is more suited to disentangle this than IG's path integral.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "in some cases the SHAP method seems to be more informative and closer to the root cause of the fault."
  - [section 4.3] Direct comparison for IDV 8: SHAP highlights `xmeas_20` (Compressor Work) and `xmeas_22` (Separator Cooling Water Temp) with large scores. Authors argue these have "greater error variation" and are potentially closer to the composition fault's root effect than IG's highlights (`xmeas_21`, `xmv_10`).
  - [corpus] Weak anchor: Neighbor paper "Combining SHAP and Causal Analysis..." suggests SHAP is being integrated with causal methods for root cause analysis, implying its perceived utility for this purpose. Explicit: **Corpus does not directly compare SHAP vs. IG for TEP; the paper's IDV 8 analysis is the sole evidence.**
- Break condition: If SHAP's computational burden (exponential in feature count) becomes intractable for high-dimensional process data, or if subsequent studies show IG performs equally well or better on a broader set of TEP faults, this mechanism's generality is broken.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: **XAI explanations are validated by aligning them with the known process topology and control structure of the Tennessee Eastman Process.**
- Mechanism: The TEP's P&ID (Figure 1) and control strategies define expected causal pathways. XAI outputs (feature importance maps like Figure 2) are interpreted by domain experts who check if top-attributed variables reside in the physical subsystem(s) directly impacted by a fault or its immediate control loops. For example, a reactor cooling water inlet temperature fault (IDV 11) should highlight reactor temperature and cooling water valve.
- Core assumption: The implemented control strategy (Bathelt et al., 2015) stabilizes the process, so deviations in controlled variables (e.g., reactor temperature) are direct consequences of the disturbance and are monitored by the controller. The LSTM fault classifier detects these deviation patterns, and XAI correctly surfaces them.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.1] "Given that the process is under control... it is normal for both the classifier and the explanation method to recognize important features both in measured and manipulated values." Explicit grouping in Table 3 and analysis.
  - [section 4.2] For IDV 11, XAI highlights `xmeas_9` (Reactor Temp), `xmeas_21` (Reactor CW Outlet Temp), `xmv_10` (Reactor CW Valve), all directly in the reactor cooling water subsystem.
  - [corpus] Explicit: **No direct corpus evidence; this validation is purely based on the authors' domain expertise applied to TEP's documented dynamics.**
- Break condition: If controlled variables show no significant deviation after a known fault (due to failed control or different fault dynamics), but XAI still highlights them based on spurious correlations, the alignment with process physics fails. More critically, if an XAI method highlights a variable with no physical or control connection to the fault's known area, the validation breaks.

## Foundational Learning
- Concept: **Post-Hoc Explainable AI (XAI) for Deep Learning**
  - Why needed here: The LSTM is a "black box." To trust its decisions in a safety-critical chemical process, operators/engineers need to understand *which process variables* and *what patterns* led to a fault alarm. Post-hoc methods like SHAP and IG provide this by explaining a *pre-trained* model's predictions without altering its architecture.
  - Quick check question: What is the key difference between SHAP (based on Shapley values) and Integrated Gradients (based on integrated gradients along a baseline) in how they attribute importance to features?
- Concept: **Multivariate Time-Series Classification with LSTMs**
  - Why needed here: Process data is sequential (samples over time) and multivariate (52 variables). An LSTM is chosen for its ability to learn long-range temporal dependencies in this sensor data, mapping a time window of measurements to a fault class.
  - Quick check question: Why might an LSTM be preferred over a simple feedforward neural network for this TEP fault diagnosis task?
- Concept: **Tennessee Eastman Process (TEP) Dynamics & Control Strategy**
  - Why needed here: To judge if an XAI explanation is "reasonable" or "plausible," one must know the *true* process: how disturbances propagate (e.g., a feed composition change affects reactor conversion and temperature), and how the distributed control system (DCS) reacts (e.g., adjusting coolant valves to maintain reactor temperature).
  - Quick check question: For a step change in reactor cooling water inlet temperature (IDV 4), which measured variables would you *expect* to deviate first, and why? How would the controller respond?

## Architecture Onboarding
- Component map: TEP Data Simulator -> Preprocessing & Windowing -> LSTM Classifier -> XAI Wrapper -> Attribution Aggregator -> Plausibility Validator
- Critical path: Simulated TEP Data → Sequence Windowing → Trained LSTM → (For test fault) Generate predictions & explanations → Aggregate attributions → Compare against known fault-subsystem mapping (Table 3).
- Design tradeoffs:
  - *Model-Agnostic vs. Inherently Interpretable*: Chose model-agnostic XAI (SHAP/IG) to keep complex LSTM. Tradeoff: attribution approximations vs. potentially lower accuracy of a simpler, inherently interpretable model (e.g., decision tree on engineered features).
  - *SHAP vs. IG*: SHAP is theoretically more rigorous (satisfies local accuracy, missingness, consistency) but is **computationally expensive** (requires many model evaluations). IG is **faster** but path-dependent and may not capture feature interactions as well. Choice impacts scalability to higherDimensions.
  - *Validation Heuristic*: Using subsystem grouping (Table 3) is pragmatic but coarse. It cannot validate *why* a variable is important, only *whether* it's in the right area.
- Failure signatures:
  - **Classifier accuracy drops** (<90% on test faults) → LSTM hasn't learned; explanations are meaningless.
  - **XAI outputs are noisy/unstable** for similar sequences → Aggregation insufficient or XAI method too sensitive.
  - **Top-attributed features consistently fall in "wrong" subsystem** (e.g., IDV 6 (A Feed Loss) highlights only stripper variables) → Indicates either LSTM learned spurious correlations or XAI method is broken for this model/data.
  - **SHAP/IG completely disagree** on top features for a fault → Suggests instability in explanations; need more background data for SHAP or different baseline for IG.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. **Replicate LSTM Training**: Train the Gravanis et al. (2022) LSTM on standard TEP dataset (training/normal + fault data). Verify achieved ~99% test accuracy on faults as reported.
  2. **Sanity Check XAI on Normal Operation**: Apply IG/SHAP to normal operating data. Expect **low, diffuse attributions** (no single feature should dominate). If strong features emerge, model may be exploiting simulation artifacts.
  3. **Replicate Key Case Studies**: For IDV 11 and IDV 8, exactly reproduce the comparative analysis (Figure 4, Figure 6, Table 5, Table 6). Confirm: a) Both methods highlight reactor variables for IDV 11. b) For IDV 8, SHAP highlights `xmeas_20/22` and IG highlights `xmeas_21/xmv_10`, with SHAP scores suggesting larger deviations. This validates the paper's core comparative finding.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Do Integrated Gradients (IG) and SHAP generally agree on which features are most important for fault classification decisions in the Tennessee Eastman Process (TEP)?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors explicitly pose this as RQ1 and state that "in most cases the same features are indicated as the most important for the decision," while noting differentiation on faults like IDV 8, IDV 12, IDV 18, and IDV 20.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper provides qualitative heatmap comparisons and specific case studies but lacks a systematic, quantitative measure of agreement across all 20 faults. The observed agreements and disagreements are noted but not statistically analyzed.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A comprehensive quantitative comparison (e.g., correlation or rank agreement metrics like Kendall's tau) across all faults between the normalized feature importance rankings produced by IG and SHAP, along with statistical tests to determine if agreement levels are significant.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Are the explanations produced by IG and SHAP for the LSTM fault classifier physically and mechanistically plausible with respect to the known process dynamics and root causes of each TEP fault?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] This is RQ2. The authors subjectively validate the results against their process knowledge, stating the results are "reasonable" and the indicated variables match affected subsystems. They also suggest SHAP may be "more informative and closer to the root cause" for faults like IDV 8.
- **Why unresolved:** The validation is largely qualitative and based on the authors' expert interpretation. There is no formal, objective benchmark against established TEP fault diagnosis knowledge (e.g., a gold-standard list of primary affected variables per fault from process engineering literature or control diagrams).
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A blinded evaluation where domain experts (unaware of which XAI method produced which explanation) score the plausibility of explanations for a representative set of faults against an agreed-upon reference of process behavior. Alternatively, a controlled study using simulated faults with analytically known propagation paths.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Why does SHAP appear to identify variables with greater deviation from normal operation (and potentially closer to the fault root cause) compared to IG for certain faults (e.g., IDV 8), and is this a consistent and reliable pattern?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors observe that for IDV 8, SHAP highlights `xmeas_20` and `xmeas_22` which have "greater error variation" compared to IG's highlights (`xmeas_21`, `xmv_10`). They hypothesize this might make SHAP "more informative" for faults where effects propagate from the root cause. This is an inference from their comparative case study, not a systematically tested hypothesis.
- **Why unresolved:** The observation is based on a few case studies (IDV 8, 12, 18, 20). The underlying reason for the methodological difference in these specific contexts is not explored (e.g., sensitivity to non-linearities, handling of time-series dependencies in LSTM, or baseline definitions). It's unclear if SHAP's selections are consistently more accurate or just different.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Experiments with a larger set of faults, especially those with well-defined, spatially distinct root causes. Analysis of the correlation between the magnitude of a variable's deviation from normal operation and the attribution scores from both methods. A direct comparison of which method's top-ranked features better match a ground-truth ranking of variables by their causal proximity to the fault inception point.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How do the explanations from model-agnostic post-hoc methods (IG, SHAP) compare to those from inherently interpretable models or ante-hoc explainable architectures when applied to the same fault classification task on TEP?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper uses model-agnostic post-hoc methods (IG, SHAP) on a black-box LSTM, stating this offers broad applicability. However, they cite other works (e.g., Wu & Zhao 2021's PTCN) that build transparency into the model architecture. The trade-off between performance, explanation fidelity, and trustworthiness between these two paradigms is not addressed.
- **Why unresolved:** The study is confined to evaluating explanations from two post-hoc methods on one model type. It does not compare the quality, computational cost, or user trust generated by explanations from a transparent model versus a black-box model with post-hoc explanations on the identical problem.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A comparative user study or benchmarking framework where the same TEP fault classification task is performed by (a) a black-box LSTM explained with IG/SHAP and (b) an interpretable model (e.g., a decision tree with inherent rules, or a PTCN). The explanations from both approaches would be evaluated for accuracy in reflecting process physics, consistency, and actionability by domain experts.

## Limitations
- Missing implementation details for the LSTM architecture/hyperparameters (referenced from Gravanis et al., 2022) and undefined XAI parameters (IG baseline, SHAP background size) impede exact replication.
- Validation of explanation plausibility is qualitative and relies on coarse subsystem groupings; no objective benchmark or quantitative agreement metric between IG and SHAP across all faults is provided.
- The corpus shows low direct citation engagement, indicating limited external validation of the findings; the paper's claims are based on a single case-study dataset (TEP) and a single model.

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (Model-agnostic post-hoc XAI can be physically plausible): High
- Mechanism 2 (SHAP may offer root-cause-closer explanations for complex faults): Medium
- Mechanism 3 (Validation via process topology alignment): Medium
