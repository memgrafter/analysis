# Learning Under Extreme Data Scarcity: Subject-Level Evaluation of Lightweight CNNs for fMRI-Based Prodromal Parkinsons Detection

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.00060  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00060  
- **Reference count:** 29  
- **Primary result:** Naive image-level splitting yields >99% accuracy due to severe information leakage; strict subject-level splitting reveals realistic accuracies of 60–81% and shows MobileNet V1 generalizing best among lightweight CNNs.

## Executive Summary
Functional MRI (fMRI) offers promise for early Parkinson’s detection, but data scarcity can lead to misleading results. This study exposes a critical pitfall: splitting fMRI data at the image (slice) level instead of the subject level causes dramatic information leakage, inflating accuracy to near‑perfect levels. When a strict subject‑level split is enforced, performance drops substantially to a realistic range of 60–81%. Among the tested ImageNet‑pretrained CNNs, the lightweight MobileNet V1 consistently outperforms deeper models despite having far fewer parameters. The findings demonstrate that proper evaluation protocol and matching model capacity to data size are paramount in low‑data neuroimaging regimes.

## Method Summary
ImageNet‑pretrained CNNs (VGG19, Inception V3, Inception ResNet V2, MobileNet V1) were fine‑tuned on 2D axial slices extracted from 4D fMRI volumes of 40 subjects (28 prodromal Parkinson’s, 12 controls). Slices were resized to each model’s expected dimensions. Three data‑splitting strategies were compared: naive image‑level (shuffled slices), subject‑level single‑draw (random partition of subjects), and subject‑level best‑case. Training used the Adam optimizer (learning rate 1e‑4, batch size 16) for up to 20 epochs with early stopping on validation loss. Performance was assessed via slice‑level accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 on the held‑out test set. The methodology clearly highlights the danger of information leakage and the impact of model capacity under extreme scarcity.

## Key Results
- Image‑level splitting produces >99% test accuracy, revealing severe information leakage.
- Subject‑level splitting yields realistic test accuracies between 60% and 81% across architectures.
- MobileNet V1 achieves the highest subject‑level test accuracy (67.20%), outperforming deeper models such as VGG19 (58.36%).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Information Leakage via Improper Data Partitioning
Naive image‑level splitting allows slices from the same subject to appear in both training and test sets. The network then learns subject‑specific anatomical or scanner artifacts instead of disease‑related patterns, effectively performing subject identification. This inflates accuracy to >99% as shown in the study. Evidence: all architectures achieved uniformly high accuracy with image‑level splits but dropped to 60–81% under subject‑level splits (abstract, Sec 5.1). The core assumption is that 2D slices from the same fMRI volume are not independent—they share low‑level correlations (e.g., brain shape). The break condition is to enforce that all slices from a given subject reside exclusively in either the training or test set (strict subject‑level split).

### Implicit Regularization via Architectural Simplicity
Under extreme data scarcity (~32 training subjects), lightweight MobileNet V1 (4.2 M parameters) generalizes better than deeper models (e.g., Inception ResNet V2 with 55.8 M parameters). Its smaller capacity acts as an implicit regularizer, limiting the ability to fit noise and subject‑specific quirks, thereby forcing the learning of robust, high‑level features. Evidence: MobileNet achieved the highest subject‑level test accuracy (67.20%) while VGG19 performed worst (58.36%) (Sec 5.2, 6.2). The break condition: with many more training subjects (e.g., >500), deeper models may surpass due to greater representational power.

### High Variance in Single‑Split Subject‑Level Evaluation
With only about four test subjects, the performance estimate from a single random subject‑level split is highly unstable; inclusion or exclusion of a single atypical subject can drastically change accuracy. This variance reflects population heterogeneity and the tiny test sample. Evidence: for MobileNet V1, test accuracy ranged from ~67% to >80% depending on which four subjects were in the test set (Sec 5.3). The break condition: increase the number of test subjects (≥10) or employ repeated random splits/averaged cross‑validation.

## Foundational Learning
### Hierarchical Data Independence
**Why needed:** The dataset consists of many 2D slices but only 40 independent subjects; the algorithm must treat subjects as the atomic unit for generalization. Violating this causes information leakage.  
**Quick check:** "If you shuffle all slices randomly, can a slice from a subject in the training set appear in the test set? If yes, you have leakage."

### Model Capacity Calibration
**Why needed:** With ~32 training subjects, the effective sample size is tiny. High‑capacity models (millions of parameters) will overfit unless capacity is matched to data quantity.  
**Quick check:** "Does your model have more than 10× the number of parameters as the number of training subjects? If yes, extreme overfitting risk exists."

### Evaluation Protocol as a Hyperparameter
**Why needed:** The choice of evaluation strategy (image vs. subject‑level) drastically changes reported performance. The protocol must be chosen before seeing results, not optimized post‑hoc.  
**Quick check:** "Is your test set composed of completely unseen subjects, or could it contain slices from a subject also in training? The answer must be 'unseen subjects' for trustworthy results."

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
- **Data pipeline:** Input 4D fMRI NIfTI → extract 2D axial slices per time frame → resize to model‑specific dimensions (e.g., 224×224) → assign subject ID and label.
- **Split managers:** three strategies – naive image‑level, subject‑level single‑draw, subject‑level best‑case.
- **Model zoo:** Pre‑trained ImageNet CNNs (VGG19, Inception V3, Inception ResNet V2, MobileNet V1) with head replaced by Global Average Pooling + sigmoid dense layer.
- **Training controller:** Adam optimizer (lr = 1e‑4, batch = 16), up to 20 epochs, early stopping on validation loss; end‑to‑end fine‑tuning.
- **Evaluation engine:** compute slice‑level metrics (accuracy, precision, recall, F1) on held‑out test partition; optional subject‑level majority‑vote aggregation.

### Critical Path
1. **Preprocess:** Convert all fMRI volumes to 2D slices while preserving subject IDs.
2. **Split:** Choose a subject‑level split (Segmentation 2 or 3). Never use image‑level for final reporting.
3. **Train:** Fine‑tune a lightweight architecture (MobileNet V1) first.
4. **Validate:** Monitor validation loss for early stopping; beware large gap between validation and test accuracy.
5. **Evaluate:** Report slice‑level metrics on the held‑out subjects; compute subject‑level consensus if needed.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Capacity vs. Data:** MobileNet V1 (4.2 M parameters) vs. Inception ResNet V2 (55.8 M parameters). With ≤32 training subjects, lower capacity wins.
- **Split stringency:** Image‑level maximizes training slices but invalidates generalization; subject‑level is correct but yields noisy estimates with few test subjects.
- **Ensembling:** Combining high‑capacity (IRv2) and low‑capacity (MobileNet) did not help. Likely because both learn similar limited‑signal patterns under scarcity; ensemble increases capacity without adding complementary information.

### Failure Signatures
- **Leakage present:** Test accuracy >95% on subject‑level split; near‑zero training/validation loss; confusion matrix nearly diagonal.
- **Overfitting high‑capacity model:** Training accuracy ≈100% but test accuracy <65%; large gap (>15%) between validation and test metrics.
- **Unstable estimate:** Test accuracy changes by >10% when re‑running with a different random subject‑level seed.
- **Ensemble fails:** Ensemble performance does not exceed best single model (MobileNet).

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Leakage detection:** Train with image‑level split and a subject‑level split. If image‑level accuracy >95% and subject‑level <70%, leakage is confirmed. Stop and correct splits.
2. **Capacity benchmark:** Under a fixed subject‑level split, train MobileNet V1, Inception V3, and VGG19. Expect MobileNet to achieve highest test accuracy with lowest variance across multiple seeds.
3. **Ensemble sanity check:** Train MobileNet V1 and Inception ResNet V2 independently. Average their predicted probabilities on the test set. If ensemble accuracy ≤ MobileNet alone, discard ensembling for this data regime.

## Limitations
- **Generalizability:** Findings are based on a single small cohort (40 subjects); it is unknown if MobileNet’s advantage extends to larger, more diverse datasets or different scanners.
- **Mechanism ambiguity:** The specific reason for MobileNet’s reliability (fewer parameters, depthwise convolutions, or pretraining compatibility) is not isolated.
- **Statistical uncertainty:** Test sets with only four subjects produce high‑variance estimates, limiting confidence in the reported performance range.

## Confidence
- Information leakage via image‑level splitting: **High**
- MobileNet V1 superiority under data scarcity: **Medium**
- High variance of single subject‑level splits: **Medium**

## Next Checks
1. External replication on an independent prodromal Parkinson’s fMRI dataset (e.g., PPMI) to confirm both the performance collapse from image‑ to subject‑level splitting and MobileNet’s relative advantage.
2. Capacity ablation: train MobileNet V1 with systematically varied width/depth multipliers (0.5×, 1.0×, 2.0×) under identical subject‑level splits to isolate whether parameter count alone drives generalization.
3. Variance quantification: perform 100 random subject‑level splits and report the distribution (mean, standard deviation, 95% confidence interval) of test accuracies for each architecture.
