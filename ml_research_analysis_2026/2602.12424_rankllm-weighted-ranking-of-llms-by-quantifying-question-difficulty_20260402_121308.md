# RankLLM: Weighted Ranking of LLMs by Quantifying Question Difficulty

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.12424
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12424
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** RankLLM achieves 90% agreement with human difficulty judgments, outperforming IRT baselines.

## Executive Summary
Current LLM benchmarks rely on aggregate accuracy metrics that ignore question difficulty, resulting in flat scores that fail to distinguish model capabilities. This limits diagnostic insight and progress tracking. RankLLM addresses this by jointly estimating question difficulty and model competency through bidirectional score propagation on a bipartite graph of model-question interactions. Evaluated on 30 models and 35,550 questions, it reaches 90% agreement with human difficulty judgments—far above the 52.9% maximum of IRT baselines. The method converges in approximately 0.006 seconds on consumer hardware, scales linearly, and remains stable under model removals and dataset changes. It further reveals distinct difficulty profiles across benchmarks and shows that using a diverse model pool (by size and architecture) reduces extreme misestimation by 83% and yields more accurate difficulty rankings.

## Method Summary
RankLLM takes a binary outcome matrix \(A\) (questions \(\times\) models) as input. It filters out questions with all-correct or all-incorrect responses and models with no failures to ensure a connected bipartite graph. From the filtered matrix it constructs two row-stochastic transition matrices:  
- \(P^{Q\to M} = \text{diag}(S)^{-1} A\) spreads a question's score to models that solved it, where \(S(q)\) is the number of solvers for question \(q\).  
- \(P^{M\to Q} = \text{diag}(F)^{-1} (1 - A)^{\top}\) spreads a model's score to questions it failed, where \(F(m)\) is the number of failures for model \(m\).

The algorithm initializes uniform distributions \(\pi_Q\) (questions) and \(\pi_M\) (models). Using a damping factor \(\alpha \in (0,1)\), it iteratively updates:  
\(\pi_Q \leftarrow \alpha (P^{M\to Q})^{\top} \pi_M + (1-\alpha)/Q\)  
\(\pi_M \leftarrow \alpha (P^{Q\to M})^{\top} \pi_Q + (1-\alpha)/M\)  
The updates are coupled, with \(\pi_M\) using the newly computed \(\pi_Q\) in the same iteration. Convergence is monitored via the L1 norm difference between successive distributions. The stationary \(\pi_Q\) gives normalized difficulty scores (higher = harder) and \(\pi_M\) gives normalized competency scores.

## Key Results
- 90% agreement with human difficulty judgments across 35,550 questions.
- Outperforms Item Response Theory baselines, which achieve at most 52.9% agreement.
- Converges in ~0.006 seconds, scales linearly, robust to model/dataset perturbations; diverse model pools reduce extreme misestimation by 83%.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
#### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Question difficulty and model competency are mutually determined through a bidirectional score propagation process on a bipartite graph of model-question interactions.
- **Mechanism:** The framework constructs two directed matrices: `A` for correct answers (competency edges, Q→M) and `ˆA` for incorrect answers (difficulty edges, M→Q). Scores propagate iteratively: a question's difficulty score is distributed to models that solved it (weighted by number of solvers), and a model's competency score is distributed to questions it failed (weighted by number of failures). A damping factor `α` introduces teleportation, converting a 2-periodic walk into a unique stationary distribution.
- **Core assumption:** Question difficulty is not an intrinsic property but emerges from *which* models fail it; a question is hard if even competent models fail. Model competency is defined by solving hard questions.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] "RankLLM's core mechanism facilitates bidirectional score propagation between models and questions... a model earns a competency score when it correctly answers a question, while a question's difficulty score increases when it challenges a model."  
  - [section 2.2] Defines the directed bipartite graph G=(V,E) with `E_Comp` and `E_Fail` edges, formalizing the two reciprocal information flows.  
  - [section 2.3] Presents the iterative update equations with damping factor `α` and convergence guarantee via ergodic Markov chain.
- **Break condition:** If the graph becomes disconnected (e.g., all models universally solve or universally fail a question), propagation fails. The paper mitigates this by filtering questions with `0 < Σ A_ij < M` (section 2.2).

#### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Non-parametric, graph-based propagation outperforms parametric IRT by avoiding per-item curve fitting and handling small per-model sample sizes.
- **Mechanism:** Unlike IRT, which fits a logistic function per item with parameters (difficulty, discrimination), RankLLM uses simple row-stochastic normalization (`diag(S)^{-1}A` and `diag(F)^{-1}ˆA`). No item-specific parameters are estimated; scores emerge from global network flow.
- **Core assumption:** The relative pattern of successes/failures across a diverse model pool contains sufficient signal for difficulty estimation without assuming a specific response function (e.g., logistic).
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] "Compared to Item Response Theory (IRT), RankLLM is non-parametric, avoids per-item logistic fits, and runs in linear time... making it practical when per-model sample sizes are small and datasets are large."  
  - [section 1] "RankLLM isn’t non-parametric, avoids per-item logistic fits, and runs in linear time... when per-model sample sizes are small and datasets are large."  
  - [section 3.2] Empirical results show RankLLM (90% human agreement) outperforms IRT baselines (max 52.9%).
- **Break condition:** If the model pool is homogeneous (all similar ability), the bipartite graph loses discriminating power; propagation may converge to trivial or unstable distributions. The paper shows homogeneity increases "dead nodes" and extreme difficulty estimates (section 3.2).

#### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Diverse model pools (by size and architecture) yield more accurate and human-aligned difficulty estimates via complementary error patterns.
- **Mechanism:** Models of different scales fail on different subsets of questions. A diverse pool creates a "wisdom of the crowd" effect where extreme over-/under-estimation by any single model subgroup is averaged out during propagation, leading to a balanced difficulty distribution and a valid gradient flow (reduced dead nodes by 83%).
- **Core assumption:** Model capability is not a single scalar "size" dimension; architectural and training differences cause heterogeneous failure modes that, when combined, better approximate true difficulty.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [section 3.2] "Mixed-scale model pools reduce extreme misestimation by 83% relative to homogeneous pools and best align with human judgments (90% consensus)."  
  - [section 3.2] Figure 7: Whole group (mixed sizes) reaches 90% human consensus vs. 38.6%-64.3% for size-homogeneous groups.  
  - [section 3.2] Figure 6: Open-weight pool difficulty rankings correlate ρ=0.96 with full-pool rankings, showing diverse open models capture landscape well.
- **Break condition:** If the model pool is extremely small (e.g., 1-2 models), the bipartite graph is too sparse; difficulty estimates become highly sensitive to individual model failure modes and fail to generalize.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Bipartite Graph & Random Walk**  
  **Why needed here:** RankLLM’s entire mechanism rests on modeling model-question interactions as a directed bipartite graph. Understanding nodes (models, questions), directed edges (success/failure), and the random walk interpretation is essential to grasp score propagation.  
  **Quick check question:** In the bipartite graph, what does an edge from a model node to a question node signify, and why is it directed that way?

- **Concept: Stationary Distribution of a Markov Chain**  
  **Why needed here:** The converged difficulty/competency scores are the stationary distribution of a damped Markov chain. This guarantees unique, positive scores regardless of initialization.  
  **Quick check question:** Why is the damping factor `α` (teleportation) necessary for convergence in this bipartite graph?

- **Concept: Item Response Theory (IRT)**  
  **Why needed here:** IRT is the primary baseline. Understanding IRT’s parametric approach (per-item logistic curves) is key to appreciating RankLLM’s non-parametric, graph-based alternative and its advantages (no fitting, scales linearly).  
  **Quick check question:** What are the two parameters in 2-Parameter Logistic IRT, and what does each represent?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
1. Input: Binary performance matrix A (Q×M) from model-question evaluations.  
2. Preprocessing: Filter questions with Σ A_ij = 0 or = M. Compute S(q)=Σ A_qj (solvers) and F(m)=Σ (1-A_jm) (failures).  
3. Transition Matrices: P_{Q→M} = diag(S)^{-1} A (competency flow) and P_{M→Q} = diag(F)^{-1} (1-Q×M - A)^⊤ (difficulty flow).  
4. Iteration: Initialize π_Q and π_M uniformly. Loop: π_Q ← αP_{M→Q}^⊤ π_M + (1-α)1_Q/Q; then π_M ← αP_{Q→M}^⊤ π_Q + (1-α)1_M/M. Check Δ = ||π_new - π_old||_1.  
5. Output: Stationary π_Q (question difficulty) and π_M (model competency).

**Critical path:** Matrix construction → Transition matrices → Damped iterative updates → Convergence check. The two update equations are coupled; π_M uses the *new* π_Q from the same iteration.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Damping factor α: Higher α slows convergence but reduces sensitivity to initialization/teleportation. Paper shows small α (e.g., 0.1) converges in ~9 iterations (Table 8). Tradeoff: speed vs. stability.  
- Filtering threshold: Strict filtering (0 < ΣA < M) ensures graph connectivity but removes easy/hard extremes. Looser thresholds risk trivial scores.  
- Model pool diversity: Homogeneous pools create "dead nodes" and extreme score distributions; mixed pools improve alignment but require more models.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Non-convergence: If Δ never drops below ε, suspect disconnected graph (excess filtered questions) or numerical instability. Check max S(q) and F(m) are >0.  
- Extreme score distributions: >30% questions at 0 or 100 difficulty indicates homogeneous model pool or dataset with universal pass/fail.  
- High variance in leave-one-out: If ρ (pool perturbation) drops <0.95, the model pool is too small/non-diverse for stable estimates.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Smoke test: Run on a tiny synthetic matrix (3 models, 5 questions). Verify π_Q and π_M are positive, sum to 1, and that a known hard question (solved only by top model) gets higher difficulty than an easy one (solved by all).  
2. Convergence sanity: Vary α from 0.1 to 0.9 on a medium dataset (e.g., GSM8K subset). Plot Δ per iteration; confirm ~9 iterations typical and Δ decreases geometrically.  
3. Stability test: Perform leave-one-model-out (LOO) on full pool. Compute Spearman ρ between LOO π_M and full-pool π_M. Expect ρ > 0.99 (Table 5). If lower, your model pool is likely too homogeneous or too small.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Stability when adding new models** – The paper only tests robustness under model removal; it is unknown how difficulty estimates change when new, unseen models are incorporated, which could alter propagation dynamics.
2. **Multidimensionality of difficulty** – RankLLM outputs a single scalar difficulty per question, but questions may possess multiple independent difficulty facets (e.g., reasoning skills, knowledge domains). The scalar may collapse important nuances.
3. **Continuous score extension** – While a continuous formulation exists, experiments used only binary outcomes. It is unclear whether partial credit inputs lead to better difficulty discrimination or human alignment.
4. **Human agreement ceiling** – The reported 90% consensus with human majority votes did not measure inter-rater reliability; the true upper bound of human consensus on difficulty remains unquantified.
5. **Temporal stability** – As new, more capable models emerge, the relative difficulty landscape may shift. The study’s static snapshot does not address how stable difficulty estimates are over time.

## Limitations
- No independent replication: the paper has zero citations and relies solely on self‑reported experiments.
- Hyperparameter ambiguity: the exact damping factor α and convergence tolerance ε used for the main 90% agreement result are not disclosed, hindering exact reproducibility.
- External validation gap: although neighboring literature motivates difficulty‑aware benchmarking, no cited work directly validates RankLLM’s specific graph‑based propagation mechanism.

## Confidence
- Bidirectional propagation mechanism: **High**
- Non‑parametric advantage over IRT: **Medium**
- Diverse model pools improve estimates: **Medium**

## Next Checks
1. **Replicate convergence & scores:** Implement the algorithm on a small matrix (e.g., 5 models × 20 questions). Verify that stationary distributions sum to 1, a known hard question receives the highest difficulty, and convergence occurs within 20 iterations with α=0.15.
2. **Hyperparameter ablation:** Sweep α ∈ [0.05, 0.5] and filtering thresholds on a medium dataset (e.g., GSM8K). Measure human agreement and number of filtered questions to assess sensitivity and identify robust settings.
3. **Group‑wise perturbation:** Perform leave‑one‑group‑out by removing all models of a specific size class (e.g., <10B parameters) from a mixed pool. Compute Spearman correlation between full‑pool and reduced‑pool difficulty estimates; a significant drop would indicate that the removed group contributes unique information.
