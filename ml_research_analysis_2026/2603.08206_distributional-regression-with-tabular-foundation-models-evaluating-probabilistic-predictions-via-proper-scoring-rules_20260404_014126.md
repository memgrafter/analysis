# Distributional Regression with Tabular Foundation Models: Evaluating Probabilistic Predictions via Proper Scoring Rules
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.08206
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08206
- **Reference count:** 23
- **Primary result:** Tabular foundation models that output full predictive distributions are inadequately assessed by standard point-estimate metrics (RMSE, R²); fine-tuning with proper scoring rules yields consistent metric-specific improvements and reveals divergent model rankings.

## Executive Summary
The paper identifies a critical evaluation mismatch: modern tabular foundation models like TabPFN and TabICL produce full predictive distributions, yet established benchmarks rely solely on point estimates (RMSE, R²). This conflation obscures distributional quality—such as aleatoric uncertainty, multimodality, and tail behavior—which is vital for risk-sensitive decisions. The authors advocate supplementing point metrics with proper scoring rules (e.g., CRPS, CRLS, Interval Score), which evaluate the entire forecast distribution. Experiments on 20 OpenML regression datasets demonstrate that model rankings reverse across different scoring rules due to their distinct inductive biases. Furthermore, fine-tuning TabPFN with scoring rules that were not part of its pretraining loss (e.g., β=1.8 energy score, CRLS) improves performance on those metrics, confirming that aligning the training objective with the evaluation rule is beneficial. The work underscores that point-estimate leaderboards are insufficient for distributional models and calls for more nuanced, application-specific evaluation frameworks.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates two pretrained tabular foundation models—realTabPFNv2.5 (a prior-data fitted network) and TabICLv2 (an in-context learner)—on 20 OpenML regression datasets (each subsampled to ≤3000 instances). Models are tasked with producing predictive distributions, represented as discretized probability mass functions (PMFs) over fixed bins (TabPFN) or quantiles (TabICL). Standard point metrics (MAE, RMSE, R²) and proper scoring rules (CRPS, CRLS, IS95) are computed on held-out folds via 5-fold cross-validation. To assess the impact of training objectives, realTabPFNv2.5 is fine-tuned on each dataset with two scoring rules unseen during its original pretraining: a β=1.8 energy score and the CRLS loss. Fine-tuning replaces the default loss, uses a low context size (1 example), and employs early stopping on the same scoring rule (up to 600 epochs). Improvements are measured as relative percentage changes in each metric compared to the unfine-tuned baseline.

## Key Results
- Model rankings are metric-dependent: TabICLv2 leads on CRLS and IS95, while β=1.8 energy fine-tuned TabPFN excels on MAE and CRPS.
- Fine-tuning with a scoring rule that matches the evaluation metric yields consistent improvements: β=1.8 energy fine-tuning improves MAE by +4.28% and CRPS by +2.76%; CRLS fine-tuning improves RMSE and CRLS; TabICLv2 achieves +6.01% in CRLS and +5.14% in IS95 over TabPFN.
- Point-estimate metrics (RMSE, R²) fail to capture distributional performance, as models optimized for means can exhibit poor tail calibration or miss multimodality, which proper scores penalize.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Different proper scoring rules induce distinct gradient updates during optimization because each rule defines a unique loss landscape over the predictive distribution. For example, CRPS integrates squared CDF discrepancies uniformly across quantiles, while CRLS applies a logarithmic penalty that heavily weights regions with very low forecast probability. These mathematical differences cause gradients to emphasize different error types (e.g., central vs. tail errors), steering model parameters toward different configurations even on the same finite dataset. Consequently, rankings diverge because finite-sample optimization does not guarantee convergence to the same global optimum across strictly proper rules, and each rule implicitly regularizes the solution by favoring certain distributional features.

Using point-estimate metrics like RMSE (equivalent to squared error loss) implicitly selects for models that approximate the conditional mean. However, RMSE is insensitive to variance, calibration, or multimodality—aspects that proper scores like CRPS or IS95 directly penalize. Thus, a model with accurate means but poor distributional representation can rank highly on point metrics yet fail under proper scoring.

## Foundational Learning
- **Proper Scoring Rules**
  - *Why needed:* The paper’s core thesis relies on proper scoring rules as both evaluation metrics and training losses. Strict propriety ensures the true distribution uniquely minimizes expected loss; elicitation determines which statistical functional (mean, median, quantiles) is targeted.
  - *Quick check:* What is the key difference between CRPS (β=1 energy) and the logarithmic score in terms of gradient behavior for outlier observations?
- **Distributional Regression**
  - *Why needed:* Tabular foundation models output full distributions (discretized PMFs), shifting the modeling goal from estimating a single functional (e.g., mean) to the entire conditional distribution F(Y|X).
  - *Quick check:* Why can’t aleatoric uncertainty be captured by a point estimate alone, even with infinite data?
- **Inductive Bias from Loss Functions**
  - *Why needed:* The paper argues that the pretraining loss (an implicit scoring rule) shapes the model’s internal biases, making it specialized for certain error types; fine-tuning alters that bias.
  - *Quick check:* If two strictly proper scoring rules both minimize at the true distribution, why might a model trained on one perform worse on the other when evaluated?

**Critical path:** The base model is pretrained on an unspecified loss (likely cross-entropy on bins). To adapt to a downstream scoring rule, implement it as a differentiable loss, fine-tune the model with early stopping on that metric, then evaluate across all metrics to characterize trade-offs.

**Design tradeoffs:** (1) Generalization vs. specialization – fine-tuning improves target-metric performance but risks overfitting on small datasets; (2) Metric selection – reporting only point metrics incentivizes mean-optimizing models and hides distributional deficiencies; (3) Computational cost – some scoring rules (e.g., energy score) require pairwise computations and careful numerical handling.

**Failure signatures:** (1) High RMSE but low CRPS/CRLS suggests ignored variance or multimodality; (2) Fine-tuning fails to improve target metric – possibly due to implementation error, misaligned early stopping, or insufficient data; (3) Near-random win/loss/tie counts across models indicate no meaningful differences, perhaps due to misaligned scoring rules with data characteristics.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **What is the optimal framework for selecting and adapting scoring rules in tabular foundation models to match asymmetric risk structures in downstream applications?**  
  *Basis:* The paper notes it is unclear how to choose scoring rules for asymmetric error severity.  
  *Why unresolved:* The space of proper scoring rules is vast; domain-specific risk asymmetries require tailored criteria, and the paper provides no systematic guidance.  
  *Evidence needed:* Large-scale empirical studies linking risk profiles to scoring rule families, or meta-learning methods that recommend rules based on dataset characteristics and utility functions.

- **Can task-token conditioning in pretraining enable tabular foundation models to generalize to arbitrary proper scoring rules at inference time without fine-tuning?**  
  *Basis:* The paper proposes task tokens as a potential solution but questions generalization to unseen rules.  
  *Why unresolved:* Pretraining on a finite set of rules may not extrapolate to arbitrary ones; theoretical understanding of interpolation in scoring rule space is lacking.  
  *Evidence needed:* Train models with tokens representing entire families (e.g., β-energy) and evaluate held-out members; theoretical analysis of generalization across rule families.

- **Why does fine-tuning with scoring rules unseen during pretraining (e.g., CRLS, β=1.8 energy) yield consistent improvements, and does this indicate a misalignment between current pretraining objectives and downstream evaluation metrics?**  
  *Basis:* Fine-tuning improvements imply the original pretraining loss was suboptimal for these rules.  
  *Why unresolved:* The exact composition of the pretraining loss is unspecified, so the source of misalignment—e.g., under-weighting tails, gradient norm differences—remains unknown.  
  *Evidence needed:* Ablations varying weighting of CRPS vs. point losses during pretraining; comparative analysis of optimization dynamics under different pretraining objectives.

- **How can tabular foundation models better capture epistemic uncertainty and tail behavior, given that scoring-rule training optimizes expected error over observed data and may neglect rare events?**  
  *Basis:* The paper states that expected scoring rule minimization is ill-suited for tail-risk elicitation.  
  *Why unresolved:* Many high-stakes applications involve risks concentrated in tails, but foundation models may under-represent scarce events seen in pretraining.  
  *Evidence needed:* Novel objectives that explicitly penalize tail miscalibration (e.g., tail-weighted scores) or combine expected scoring rule minimization with uncertainty regularization; evaluation on heavy-tailed or out-of-distribution test sets.

## Limitations
- **Implementation opacity:** The paper does not detail discrete approximations for CRLS and β-energy losses within TabPFN’s pipeline, hindering reproducibility.
- **Hyperparameter secrecy:** Random seeds, fold assignments, subsampling seeds, and fine-tuning hyperparameters (except learning rate) are unreported, blocking exact replication.
- **Discretization mismatch:** TabPFN uses adaptive bins while TabICL uses quantiles; scoring comparisons may be unfair without a common output grid.
- **Scope constraints:** Findings are limited to two models and 20 datasets; generalization to other architectures or scoring rules is uncertain.

## Confidence
- Point-estimate metrics are insufficient: **Medium** (theoretically sound but lacks systematic evaluation across diverse distribution shapes).
- Proper scoring rules yield divergent model rankings: **Medium‑High** (supported by synthetic and real experiments, but limited to two models).
- Fine-tuning with the evaluation scoring rule improves performance: **Medium** (observed gains, but fine-tuning regimen may not generalize and risks overfitting).

## Next Checks
1. **Rule implementation verification:** On a synthetic dataset with known continuous distribution (e.g., Gaussian mixture), compute CRPS, CRLS, and β-energy using both the paper’s method and a reference library (e.g., `properscoring`); compare for numerical equivalence and assess discretization bias.
2. **Controlled replication with fixed splits:** Re-run the 20‑dataset evaluation using identical random seeds and 5‑fold splits for all models; apply paired statistical tests to confirm significance of ranking differences and fine‑tuning gains.
3. **Unified discretization grid experiment:** Force both TabPFN and TabICL to output predictive distributions on a common grid (e.g., 1000 equally spaced points covering the target range); recalculate all scoring rules to determine whether ranking changes persist after eliminating discretization discrepancies.
