# R$^2$Energy: A Large-Scale Benchmark for Robust Renewable Energy Forecasting under Diverse and Extreme Conditions

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.15961
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15961
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Under extreme weather, simple autoregressive models (e.g., GRU) achieve higher robustness than complex Transformers, revealing a robustness-complexity trade-off driven by meteorological integration strategy.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces R²Energy, a large-scale benchmark for renewable energy forecasting that addresses thecritical gap in evaluating model performance during extreme weather events. The benchmark comprises 10.7 million hourly records from 902 stations across four Chinese provinces, coupled with a standardized, leakage-free NWP-assisted forecasting protocol. It introduces regime-wise evaluation using expert-aligned extreme weather annotations (rainstorm/heatwave). Key findings show that autoregressive (AR) models like GRU significantly outperform Transformer-based architectures in reliability metrics (Qualification Rate, Forecast Skill Score) during extreme conditions, challenging the assumption that model complexity improves robustness. The work underscores that a model’s meteorological integration strategy—not its architectural sophistication—is paramount for safety-critical grid operations under climate stress.

## Method Summary
The study reformulates renewable forecasting as an NWP-assisted mapping problem: given a historical window of capacity factors and covariates `X` and future NWP context `C`, predict the future capacity factor `Ŷ`. The R²Energy dataset provides hourly data with strict quality control (no missing, frozen, or outlier timesteps). A leakage-free protocol enforces chronological splits (70/10/20) and sample construction that prevents future information from leaking into the input window. Models are trained with AdamW (learning rate and dropout tuned) and L1 loss, evaluated on multiple horizons (short-, medium-, long-term) and weather regimes (normal, heatwave, rainstorm). Two model families are compared: Autoregressive (e.g., GRU) that dynamically inject future NWP at each decoding step, and Non-Autoregressive (e.g., Transformer) that ingest NWP as a global context. Performance metrics include MAE, Qualification Rate (Q, error within tolerance τ=0.25), and Forecast Skill Score (S, improvement over physics baseline).

## Key Results
- **Robustness gap:** Under extreme rainstorms, GRU achieves a Qualification Rate of ~79% while Autoformer drops to ~31% on wind data, revealing that aggregate MAE improvements mask catastrophic failures in high-stress regimes.
- **AR superiority in extremes:** AR models consistently yield positive Skill Scores during rainstorms (e.g., GRU S=41.36% on W1) whereas Transformers degrade severely (Autoformer S=-78.57%).
- **Complexity trade-off:** Model complexity does not equate to robustness; the dynamic NWP injection in AR models better preserves physical causality, leading to more reliable predictions when weather forcing is volatile.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Autoregressive (AR) models maintain robustness under extreme weather by dynamically integrating NWP signals at each decoding step, anchoring recursive predictions to physically plausible states and mitigating error accumulation.
- **Mechanism:** During iterative forecasting, the future NWP vector `c_t` for the target timestamp is concatenated with the model’s internal state at step `t`. This creates a tight temporal coupling where each output step is immediately corrected by known exogenous weather forcing, simulating a step-wise physical reaction. In contrast, Non-Autoregressive (NAR) models ingest the entire NWP sequence as global context, which can dilute precise forcing signals within attention or convolution operations, especially under high-frequency stochastic fluctuations.
- **Core assumption:** Renewable generation during extreme events is a causal, step-wise reaction to instantaneous meteorological forcing; preserving this causality in the model’s computational graph is more critical for reliability than capturing long-range dependencies.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [Section 3.3]: "For AR models... the NWP data is inserted dynamically at each future time step... This simulates the operational availability of weather forecasts for each specific look-ahead hour."
  - [Section 5.2]: Table 6 shows AR models (GRU/RNN) achieve positive Skill Scores (S) during rainstorms for wind (e.g., GRU S=41.36% on W1) while Autoformer degrades severely (S=-78.57%). The text states: "AR models... mirror this physical causality. Conversely, generative NAR models appear prone to signal dilution."
- **Break condition:** If the NWP signal itself becomes highly erroneous or the extreme event’s physical response has significant inertia/lag beyond the model’s recurrence window, the dynamic injection advantage may diminish.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Regime-wise evaluation with expert-aligned extreme weather annotations uncovers a "robustness gap" in aggregate metrics, revealing that a model’s meteorological integration strategy—not its complexity—determines operational safety.
- **Mechanism:** By annotating timelines with rainstorm/heatwave labels (Table 2), performance is stratified by physical regime. Models are then compared on metrics like Qualification Rate (Q) and Forecast Skill Score (S) within these high-stress regimes. This isolates failure modes (e.g., Autoformer’s collapse in high-entropy wind rainstorms) that are masked by overall MAE improvements in normal conditions.
- **Core assumption:** Extreme weather imposes distinct, non-linear modalities on power generation (e.g., wind increases during convective rainstorms, solar drops during cloud occlusion). A model’s ability to adapt its internal representation to these modality shifts is a key determinant of safety-critical performance.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [Abstract]: "Beyond aggregate accuracy, we incorporate regime-wise evaluation with expert-aligned extreme weather annotations, uncovering a critical 'robustness gap' typically obscured by average metrics."
  - [Section 3.1]: Figure 3 visualizes divergent capacity factor distributions under normal vs. extreme events, confirming "profound physical divergences." The paper notes: "forecasting models optimized solely for 'average' conditions may fail catastrophically during these high-impact events."
  - [Section 5.2]: Explicit comparison of MAE, Q, and S across Normal/Heatwave/Rainstorm regimes (Table 6) demonstrates the gap; e.g., on W1 Rainstorm, Autoformer Q=31.26% vs. GRU Q=78.73%.
- **Break condition:** If extreme event definitions are poor (e.g., annotations don’t correlate with actual power impact) or the dataset lacks sufficient extreme event samples, the regime-wise signal becomes noisy and the gap may not be detectable.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The standardized, leakage-free NWP-assisted forecasting protocol enables fair comparison of AR and NAR models by granting identical access to future exogenous signals, eliminating confounding variables that previously obscured architectural trade-offs.
- **Mechanism:** The protocol rigorously defines how future NWP data `C` is injected, tailored to model type (dynamic for AR, static for NAR). Crucially, it uses a "perfect-prognosis" ERA5 reanalysis proxy for NWP and enforces strict sample construction rules (Equation 5) to prevent any information leakage from future observations into the historical input window `X`. This ensures all models operate under the same operational constraints and information horizon.
- **Core assumption:** Prior benchmarks unfairly compared AR and NAR models by either giving NAR models an unrealistic "cheat" access to future NWP or not standardizing injection, leading to misleading conclusions about complexity vs. robustness.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [Section 3.3]: Detailed injection protocols for AR ("dynamically at each future time step") and NAR subtypes. "This ensures all models have identical access to future NWP signals, enabling fair and reproducible 'stress testing'."
  - [Section 2]: Formal problem definition `(X, C) -> Ŷ` and sample construction with validity mask (Equation 5) explicitly prevents leakage: "a sample is retained only if all involved time steps are valid."
  - [Section 5.1]: The consistent outperformance of lightweight AR models (GRU) over complex Transformers *across all datasets and horizons* under this protocol is presented as the key finding, attributed to the protocol’s fairness.
- **Break condition:** If the perfect-prognosis assumption (ERA5 as error-free NWP) is violated in deployment (real NWP has bias/noise), the robustness hierarchy observed may not hold; the protocol evaluates mapping capability, not full end-to-end robustness including NWP errors.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: NWP-Assisted Forecasting Formulation**  
  - Why needed here: The paper redefines the task from pure time-series extrapolation to `(X, C) -> Ŷ`, where `C` is future NWP. This shifts the model’s purpose from learning historical autocorrelation to learning a physical mapping from meteorological forcings to power output. Understanding this is essential to grasp why dynamic NWP injection matters.  
  - Quick check question: If the future NWP vector `c_t` for a specific hour is corrupted (e.g., sudden wind speed error), how would an AR model’s error propagate differently than a NAR model’s?

- **Concept: Regime-Wise Evaluation Metrics (Qualification Rate, Forecast Skill Score)**  
  - Why needed here: Average MAE/RMSE hide safety-critical failures. The Qualification Rate (Q) measures industrial compliance (error within tolerance τ). The Forecast Skill Score (S) quantifies improvement over a physics baseline, making the value of deep learning explicit in extreme regimes where physics may fail. These metrics directly operationalize "robustness."  
  - Quick check question: Why might a model have a lower MAE but also a lower Q compared to another model? What does a negative Skill Score (S) imply during an extreme event?

- **Concept: Physical Characteristics of Renewable Generation (Capacity Factor, Power Curve)**  
  - Why needed here: The target is normalized Capacity Factor (CF), not raw power. This accounts for station-specific rated capacity changes. For wind, the non-linear GE 2.5MW power curve (Figure 4) defines the physical relationship between wind speed and power. Models must learn or approximate these physical constraints to be robust; simple heuristics (Seasonal-Naive) fail.  
  - Quick check question: During a wind rainstorm (high wind speeds), what region of the power curve (I, II, or III) is typically operating? How does this affect the volatility of the CF signal?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input (Historical window `X` + Future NWP `C`) -> Core (For AR: Recurrent cell with dynamic NWP injection at each step; For NAR: Encoder processes `X`, fuses with `C` in decoder/head) -> Output (Regression head predicting continuous CF per horizon step)
- **Critical path:** Data ingestion -> Feature standardization (using training set stats only) -> Sample construction with strict validity mask -> Model selection & hyperparameter tuning (learning rate, dropout, hidden size) -> Training with MAE loss -> Evaluation on held-out test sets across Normal/Extreme regimes using MAE, Q, S.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  **Dynamic NWP Injection (AR):** + Aligns with physical causality, robust in high-noise regimes. - Recursive error accumulation can occur if NWP is poor or horizon very long; slower inference.  
  **Global Context Fusion (NAR):** + Parallel decoding, fast inference, good for long-horizon pattern capture. - Prone to signal dilution in volatile conditions; attention may smooth critical transient features.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - **Transformer variants (Autoformer, Informer):** Sharp degradation in Q and S during high-entropy wind rainstorms (Table 6, W1 Rainstorm Q ~31%, S ~-78%). High training variance (std dev) in volatile datasets.  
  - **CNN-based (TCN):** Can outperform in short, gusty wind regimes (good at local patterns) but may miss longer cycles.  
  - **RNN variants (GRU):** Exceptionally stable convergence (low std dev) and consistent Q across regimes; failure mode is typically underfitting on complex seasonal patterns in stable solar regimes.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Baseline Replication & Protocol Validation:** Implement the leakage-free protocol. Reproduce Table 4/5 results for GRU and Autoformer on a single dataset (e.g., S1 USTF). Confirm AR advantage in MAE/Q holds under standardized NWP injection.  
  2. **Extreme Regime Stress Test:** Isolate Level 2/3 Rainstorm samples from W1. Compare GRU, TCN, and Autoformer on MAE and Q. Verify the "Robustness Gap" (GRU Q >75%, Autoformer Q <35%).  
  3. **NWP Injection Ablation:** For a GRU, modify to inject NWP only at the first decoding step (not dynamically). Measure ΔMAE and ΔQ on W1 Rainstorm (H=24). Expect significant Q drop, confirming dynamic injection is key to robustness.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Question:** How can forecasting models dynamically switch between different inductive biases (e.g., AR vs. NAR) to maintain robustness across stable and extreme weather regimes?  
   - **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion states the need for models "capable of autonomously adapting their strategies when transitioning between stable regimes and high-entropy weather fronts."  
   - **Why unresolved:** The paper identifies the robustness-complexity trade-off and shows AR models excel in extremes while NAR models may be better for longer horizons, but provides no mechanism for dynamic adaptation.  
   - **What evidence would resolve it:** Development and benchmarking of a hybrid or meta-learned architecture that can, for instance, route inputs to an AR or NAR sub-module based on a detected weather regime (e.g., using the provided extreme event labels), with performance evaluated on the regime-wise metrics of R²Energy.

2. **Question:** What is the precise mechanistic reason for the "signal dilution" and instability observed in Transformer-based models under high-entropy (e.g., rainstorm) conditions?  
   - **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper hypothesizes that generative NAR models suffer from "signal dilution, where the precise NWP forcing at time t is washed out by the global attention mechanism." It also notes pronounced training volatility in Transformers in high-noise wind regimes.  
   - **Why unresolved:** The claim is based on empirical performance degradation but lacks a diagnostic analysis (e.g., attention weight visualization, gradient flow analysis) to confirm the proposed mechanism of over-smoothing or mis-attribution.  
   - **What evidence would resolve it:** An ablation study and interpretability analysis on Transformer variants within the R²Energy framework, measuring the consistency and focus of attention weights on relevant future NWP covariates during normal vs. extreme periods, correlated with error spikes.

3. **Question:** To what extent do the robustness findings (AR superiority in extremes) hold when models are exposed to realistic, imperfect Numerical Weather Prediction (NWP) signals containing forecast errors, rather than the "perfect prognosis" ERA5 proxy used in the benchmark?  
   - **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The protocol uses ERA5 reanalysis as a "perfect-prognosis" proxy to isolate the model’s mapping capability from NWP noise. This is a stated simplification: "Using reanalysis data as a 'perfect prognosis' proxy isolates the forecasting model’s mapping capability from the noise of weather prediction errors."  
   - **Why unresolved:** Real-world deployment relies on operational NWP forecasts which are noisy and biased. A model’s robustness to the physical regime shift of an extreme event may be undermined or masked by its sensitivity to NWP errors.  
   - **What evidence would resolve it:** A follow-up study using the same R²Energy evaluation protocol but injecting synthetic or real NWP error profiles (e.g., from a separate NWP model verification dataset) into the future context vectors `C`, then re-evaluating the robustness gap and Qualification Rate.

4. **Question:** Is the observed "robustness-complexity trade-off" a universal property across all spatial and temporal scales of renewable assets (e.g., single turbines vs. entire wind farms, regional solar fields), or is it specific to the station-level data in this benchmark?  
   - **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The benchmark uses 902 individual stations. The conclusion generalizes the finding to "safety-critical power systems," but the analysis aggregates station-level forecasts. Spatiotemporal aggregation (e.g., forecasting a regional aggregate) can inherently smooth local volatility.  
   - **Why unresolved:** The physical causality argument (step-wise reaction to immediate forcing) may be strongest at the single-asset level. Aggregation could change the signal-to-noise ratio and the optimal model bias, potentially narrowing the robustness gap.  
   - **What evidence would resolve it:** Creating aggregated target variables (e.g., capacity factor of all stations in a province) within the R²Energy framework and re-running the comparative analysis of model families to see if the AR advantage in extremes persists or diminishes.

5. **Question:** Can the superior training stability of lightweight AR/MLP models be formally linked to specific architectural properties (e.g., Lipschitz continuity, gradient norm behavior) in the context of high-variance renewable time series?  
   - **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper highlights "training stability as a first-class evaluation dimension" and notes stark contrasts: "Lightweight architectures... exhibit highly stable convergence surfaces... Transformer-based models demonstrate pronounced volatility."  
   - **Why unresolved:** The observation is empirical (standard deviation across seeds) but not connected to theoretical properties of the architectures. Understanding this could inform the design of stable models without sacrificing accuracy.  
   - **What evidence would resolve it:** A theoretical and empirical analysis of gradient propagation and loss landscape smoothness for the evaluated architectures when trained on the high-noise (e.g., W2, W3) vs. low-noise (e.g., S1) regimes of R²Energy. Metrics could include spectral norms of Jacobians or eigenvalue distributions of the Hessian.

## Limitations
- **Dataset & Protocol:** The benchmark’s reliance on ERA5 reanalysis as a "perfect-prognosis" NWP proxy may overstate model robustness; real-world NWP errors are not evaluated. Extreme event annotations (rainstorm/heatwave) lack quantified inter-rater reliability or correlation with actual CF disruptions.
- **Reproducibility:** Exact model hyperparameters and training seeds are undisclosed, requiring expensive sweeps. The Qualification Rate (Q) metric definition is ambiguous (threshold τ=0.25 vs. implied |error|≤0.25), risking inconsistent scoring.
- **Generalizability:** Findings are confined to four Chinese provinces; geographic and climatological transferability is untested. The AR robustness advantage may diminish for longer horizons or with poor NWP skill.

- **Mechanism 2 (Regime-wise evaluation):** Medium-High

## Next Checks
1. **Ablation: NWP Injection Timing.** Train GRU with static NWP injection (only at first decoding step) versus dynamic injection. Expect major Q degradation (>15% drop) on W1 Rainstorm test set, confirming dynamic injection is necessary for robustness.
2. **Annotation Reliability Check.** Compute inter-annotator agreement (Cohen’s κ) for a subset of extreme labels; correlate annotation timestamps with CF volatility spikes (e.g., >2× normal std). κ<0.6 or low correlation would invalidate the regime-wise analysis premise.
3. **NWP Error Sensitivity.** Perturb ERA5 wind speed by ±5% (simulating NWP bias) and re-evaluate GRU vs. Autoformer on W1 Rainstorm. If AR advantage diminishes (Q gap <10%), the protocol’s perfect-prognosis assumption is a major limitation.
