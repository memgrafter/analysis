# MT-PingEval: Evaluating Multi-Turn Collaboration with Private Information Games

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.24188
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.24188
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** MT-PingEval introduces a private information game benchmark and isotoken evaluation; state-of-the-art language models generally fail to leverage additional dialogue turns, with performance flat or declining, revealing a strategic communication gap.

## Executive Summary
MT-PingEval proposes a novel benchmark for evaluating multi-turn collaboration using private information games (PINGs). The core methodology, isotoken evaluation, fixes the total token budget while varying the number of turns, isolating interactive capability from raw task skill. Across five tasks (chess, COVR, MD3, tangram, and name‑game) and five language models, the study finds that accuracy does not improve—and often declines—as the turn count increases. This contrasts with human performance, which achieves high accuracy with far fewer tokens and more coherent dialogue. The results highlight a fundamental gap in language models’ ability to engage in strategic, adaptive communication.

## Method Summary
The evaluation employs five diverse tasks where two agents (each holding private information) communicate via text to solve a shared problem. Private information is encoded in non‑textual forms (e.g., board positions, images, or structured tables) to prevent trivial extraction. Each agent receives a total token budget of 256 tokens, partitioned into a variable number of turns \(T \in \{2,4,8,16\}\); the per‑turn limit is \(\lfloor 256/T \rfloor\). After each turn, the agent’s utterance is truncated to fit the limit. Dialogue proceeds alternately until the final turn, at which point a structured answer (e.g., “SELECT ROW i” or “ANSWER: …”) must be produced. Success is measured by exact match to the ground truth. The study also computes secondary analyses: sycophancy (via an LLM autorater), lexical density (TF‑IDF novelty × content‑word ratio), and centering coherence (based on discourse entity transitions). A human benchmark on MD3 provides a reference for token efficiency and dialogue quality.

## Key Results
- **Flat or inverse scaling:** Across chess, COVR, MD3, and tangram, models show no improvement or even declining accuracy as turns increase; only name‑game improves, but this is due to random guessing.
- **Strategic communication gap:** Humans achieve comparable success on MD3 with far fewer tokens and higher coherence, indicating that models fail to use interaction strategically.
- **Local coherence without global strategy:** Linguistic metrics (centering, lexical density) often remain stable or improve with turns but do not correlate with task success, suggesting models produce superficially coherent dialogue without effective information exchange.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Isotoken evaluation isolates interactive capability
By fixing the total communication budget and varying the number of turns, any performance improvement with more turns must stem from adaptive use of dialogue (e.g., asking clarifying questions, updating beliefs) rather than simply having more compute or longer outputs. The core assumption is that models can modify their responses based on dialogue history; if not, performance will plateau at the baseline regardless of turn count. Evidence is provided by the methodology paper and formal arguments that any dialogue doable in \(t\) turns can also be done in more turns, so a positive slope would indicate effective interaction.

### Models fail to leverage multi‑turn interaction
Private information games require strategic communication such as selective feature sharing and targeted questions. The observed flat or decreasing accuracy curves demonstrate that models treat each turn independently and do not build a shared understanding. The non‑interactive baseline (2‑turn summarization) already captures what models can convey; extra turns are wasted, confirming a lack of adaptive interaction. Evidence includes the main results across four tasks where accuracy never rises with turns.

### Linguistic coherence does not imply strategic effectiveness
Centering scores (measuring local discourse focus) and lexical density (measuring content‑weighted novelty) remain stable or improve with turns but show no correlation with task success. This decoupling indicates that models can maintain entity consistency and produce information‑dense utterances without using them to solve the game strategically. The mechanism explains why dialogues look coherent yet fail to achieve the goal.

## Foundational Learning
- **Private Information Games (PINGs):** Asymmetric information held by each player must be exchanged via dialogue to succeed. *Why needed:* defines the core experimental paradigm. *Quick check:* What design ensures private information cannot be simply read out as text? (It is encoded in non‑textual modalities like images or structured tables, making compression into a short text span difficult.)
- **Isotoken Evaluation:** Total token budget fixed, number of turns varies, isolating interactive skill from absolute length. *Why needed:* enables scaling analysis of interactive capability. *Quick check:* How does it differ from merely limiting turns? (It holds tokens constant while varying turns; a model that uses interaction effectively should improve with more turns because it can refine communication.)
- **Levels of Interactivity:** Theoretical framework classifying games by the minimum turns required to transmit necessary information. *Why needed:* guides task design and expected success patterns. *Quick check:* What distinguishes a level‑2 interactive game from level‑1? (Level‑1: player 2 can transmit all needed info in one turn; level‑2: player 1 must first share a partial summary that enables player 2 to respond with the critical information.)
- **Centering Theory:** Formal measure of local discourse coherence based on transitions of discourse centers. *Why needed:* provides a linguistically grounded metric to analyze dialogue quality. *Quick check:* Which transition type is considered most coherent? (Continue, where the backward‑looking center is maintained and also becomes the preferred center of the next utterance.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Game definitions -> Budget manager -> Agent wrappers -> Dialogue loop -> Evaluator -> Analysis modules

**Critical path:** For each task, initialize two agents (same model) with private inputs. For a given turn budget T and per‑turn limit L, run a loop: Agent 1 speaks (token‑limited) → Agent 2 speaks → repeat until the final turn, where an explicit answer is forced. After the final turn, evaluate the answer against the ground truth and store the dialogue and metrics.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Token budget (256) vs. turn count: Larger tokens allow more informative turns but reduce the number of turns; the chosen size highlights interactive failure.  
- Task selection: Mix of symmetric/asymmetric, visual/textual to cover diverse interactive demands.  
- Thinking mode: Allowed as a free scratchpad (tokens do not count) to test if internal reasoning substitutes for interaction.  
- Prompt engineering: Extensive tuning (answer format, final‑turn warnings) was required to ensure protocol compliance.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Flat or decreasing accuracy vs. turns (main expected failure).  
- Premature termination: models stop before using full turn budget, especially in image‑selection tasks.  
- Token under‑utilization: total tokens used drop as turns increase.  
- Sycophantic patterns: spurious apologies or uncritical agreement.  
- Inverse scaling: performance worse with more turns, indicating distraction by excess speaking opportunities.

**First three experiments:**  
1. Replicate isotoken on COVR: run 1000 instances with 2, 4, 8, 16 turns; verify that accuracy does not significantly improve (p > 0.05) for at least two models.  
2. Ablate token budget: double total tokens (e.g., 512) while keeping turn counts fixed; check whether models use extra tokens to repeat themselves rather than improve accuracy, confirming the bottleneck is interactivity.  
3. Human vs. model efficiency on MD3: obtain human dialogue transcripts (e.g., from Eisenstein et al. 2023), compute tokens per dialogue and centering scores; confirm humans achieve higher accuracy with far fewer tokens and higher coherence.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **What specific communicative strategies or planning mechanisms are missing in current LLMs that prevent them from leveraging increased turn budgets to improve task performance in private information games?**  
   The paper shows flat/inverse scaling and notes a “strategic communication gap,” but the root cognitive or architectural deficits remain unclear. Evidence needed: diagnostic experiments where models are explicitly taught or prompted to adopt strategies (e.g., hypothesis testing, explicit belief tracking) with isotoken evaluation; analysis of internal representations during dialogue; comparisons with multi‑agent reinforcement learning or self‑play models.

2. **Why do models exhibit inverse scaling with turn count on image selection tasks (MD3, Tangram), and what triggers premature dialogue termination despite incentives to continue?**  
   Inverse scaling is documented, but the decision‑making behind early termination is not investigated. Potential causes include overconfidence, inability to generate useful follow‑up questions under token constraints, or optimization for brevity. Evidence needed: process‑tracing of confidence scores; controlled experiments varying the cost of incorrect answers or penalty for extra turns; correlating termination decisions with dialogue patterns; testing forced continuation until a confidence threshold is met.

3. **Can the isotoken evaluation methodology uncover scaling laws for multi‑turn collaboration, and if so, what model architectures, training data, or inference‑time techniques (e.g., thinking budgets) might overcome the observed performance plateaus?**  
   The study is diagnostic, not interventional; it only evaluates a small set of existing models, leaving open what improvements could change the scaling curves. Evidence needed: systematic evaluation across a broader spectrum of model scales and families (including those trained specifically for dialogue); ablations adding synthetic or human‑human dialogues about private information to training data; comparing standard decoding with beam search, sampling, or planning‑augmented generation.

4. **What precise elements of human collaborative dialogue enable superior token efficiency and accuracy in private information games, and can these be distilled into trainable objectives or prompts for LLMs?**  
   Humans achieve comparable success at superior token efficiency on MD3, but the specific conversational maneuvers behind this are not extracted. Evidence needed: fine‑grained qualitative and quantitative analysis of human dialogues to identify recurring patterns (e.g., highly discriminative questions, rapid consensus‑building, implicit grounding). Then incorporate these patterns as rewards in RL fine‑tuning or as constraints in prompted generation; train models to imitate human dialogues and re‑evaluate with isotoken scaling.

## Limitations
- Exact data generation parameters (e.g., number of random moves for chess, image selection criteria) are not specified, hindering faithful reproduction.
- Token counting relies on a heuristic scaling factor (1.3) between whitespace and subword tokens; its precise value may vary across tokenizers and affect per‑turn limits.
- The benchmark includes only five tasks and uses self‑play; results may not generalize to human‑model collaboration or heterogeneous agent populations.
- Model API settings (temperature, max_tokens) and the sycophancy autorater’s validation dataset are not fully described, introducing reproducibility uncertainty.

## Confidence
- Isotoken evaluation isolates interactive capability from raw task skill: **High**
- Language models fail to leverage additional turns in private information games: **High**
- Linguistic coherence metrics explain the failure as superficial coherence without strategy: **Medium**

## Next Checks
1. **Isotoken methodology stress test:** Deploy a simpler task that explicitly requires clarification (e.g., ambiguous reference resolution). If models show positive scaling with turns, the isotoken setup is validated; if not, it reinforces the claim that models cannot leverage interaction.
2. **Token counting robustness:** Repeat key experiments using each model’s native subword tokenizer instead of the whitespace‑based scaling factor. Consistent trends would confirm results are not an artifact of token estimation.
3. **Human‑model collaboration:** Conduct MD3 and name‑game sessions pairing one human with one model. Compare accuracy, token efficiency, and dialogue coherence to model‑model and human‑human baselines; significant degradation when paired with a human would indicate a general strategic communication deficit.
