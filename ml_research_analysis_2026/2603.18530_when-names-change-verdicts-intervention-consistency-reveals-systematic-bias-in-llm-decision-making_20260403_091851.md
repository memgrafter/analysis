# When Names Change Verdicts: Intervention Consistency Reveals Systematic Bias in LLM Decision-Making

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.18530
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18530
- **Reference Count:** 20
- **Primary Result:** An iterative ICE-guided mitigation loop achieves cumulative 78% bias reduction by identifying and patching spurious signal leakage in structured decision pipelines.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces ICE-Guard, a framework that detects systematic bias in LLM decision-making by measuring intervention consistency—specifically, whether decisions flip when irrelevant features (like names, credentials, or phrasing) are swapped. Testing across 3,000 vignettes in ten domains reveals that authority bias (5.8%) and framing bias (5.0%) are more prevalent than demographic bias (2.2%), with finance showing extreme rates (22.6% authority, 20.4% framing). Mitigation via structured decomposition—using an LLM to extract features followed by a deterministic rubric—reduces flip rates by up to 100% (median 49%). An iterative ICE-guided loop that diagnoses and patches residual flips in the extraction prompt yields further cumulative reductions, demonstrating a practical path toward fairer high-stakes AI decisions.

## Method Summary
The core method, Intervention Consistency Evaluation (ICE), constructs paired vignettes where only a single irrelevant feature (e.g., a candidate's name or the source of their credential) differs between versions. The target LLM's decisions on these pairs are compared; a "flip" indicates sensitivity to that spurious feature. After detecting significant bias, mitigation uses structured decomposition: an LLM first extracts a predefined set of decision-relevant features from the vignette into a constrained JSON schema, and a separate, deterministic rubric applies fixed rules to those features to produce a final decision. If flips persist under structured evaluation, an iterative loop identifies which extracted fields correlate with the bias, refines the extraction prompt to exclude those signals, and re-tests, repeating until flip rates plateau.

## Key Results
- Authority bias (5.8%) and framing bias (5.0%) substantially exceed demographic bias (2.2%) across all domains and models.
- Finance exhibits extreme bias: 22.6% authority flip rate and 20.4% framing flip rate.
- Structured decomposition reduces flip rates by up to 100% (median 49% reduction across nine models).
- An iterative ICE-guided loop applied to Claude Sonnet 4.5 achieves a cumulative 78% bias reduction (6.0% → 1.7% → 1.3%).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Intervention consistency testing detects systematic bias by measuring decision flips when irrelevant features are swapped.
- **Mechanism:** The framework operationalizes an invariance principle: decisions should remain unchanged under interventions on spurious features. The flip rate (FR) quantifies violations; an FR significantly above a noise baseline (~5%) indicates the model relies on the swapped feature.
- **Core assumption:** Decision-irrelevant features (e.g., name, credential source, phrasing) are non-causal parents of the outcome in a correct causal graph.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] Reports distinct bias magnitudes (authority 5.8%, framing 5.0%, demographic 2.2%). [section 3.1] Formalizes invariance and FR metric. [section 4.1] Details vignette construction and control baseline to isolate systematic sensitivity.
- **Break condition:** FR falls within the noise baseline (≤5% with statistical confidence), indicating either true invariance or an inadequately designed swap (e.g., feature not salient).

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Structured decomposition (LLM feature extraction + deterministic rubric) mitigates bias by preventing spurious signals from reaching the decision logic.
- **Mechanism:** The pipeline separates extraction (LLM outputs structured features) from decision (rubric applies fixed rules). The constrained JSON schema limits output to decision-relevant fields, filtering implicit bias cues. The rubric is immune to prompt variation.
- **Core assumption:** The extraction schema can capture all genuinely decision-relevant information without leaking spurious signals, assuming objective domain criteria.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] Reports up to 100% flip rate reduction (median 49%). [section 3.3] Describes safeguards (schema constraint, deterministic rules, ICE feedback). [section 5.3] Shows Kimi-K2.5 (5.5%→0.0%) and Gemini 2.5 Pro (5.3%→0.2%).
- **Break condition:** Extraction is inconsistent or leaks spurious signals into structured output, potentially increasing bias (e.g., GPT-5.2: 3.7%→6.2%).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** An ICE-guided iterative loop (detect→diagnose→mitigate→verify) achieves cumulative bias reduction by patching extraction prompts based on residual flips.
- **Mechanism:** Residual flips in structured evaluation indicate leakage in the extraction stage. The loop identifies these cases, diagnoses which extracted fields correlate with bias, and refines the prompt to exclude such signals. Verification re-tests to measure reduction.
- **Core assumption:** Residual flips stem from inadequate prompt instructions, not domain subjectivity or rubric flaws, implying prompt engineering can seal leakage paths.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] Reports 78% reduction for Claude Sonnet 4.5 via iterative patching (6.0%→1.7%→1.3%). [section 5.3] Details the Sonnet case study with five diagnosed flips and prompt patches. [section 5.3] Notes education and finance retain higher residual flips, suggesting domain subjectivity limits.
- **Break condition:** Iterative patching yields diminishing/no returns, indicating irreducible bias due to subjectivity, schema limits, or model constraints.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Counterfactual Invariance**
  - **Why needed:** The ICE-Guard framework rests on the principle that decisions should be invariant to interventions on spurious attributes. This causal notion is key to interpreting flip rates as evidence of bias.
  - **Quick check:** If swapping a candidate's name changes the decision, what does that imply about the model's reliance on that name as a decision factor?

- **Concept: Structured Decomposition Trade-off**
  - **Why needed:** Mitigation trades model flexibility for consistency. Engineers must judge when domain criteria are sufficiently objective for a deterministic rubric versus when free-form reasoning is necessary.
  - **Quick check:** For which high-stakes domain (e.g., medical triage vs. creative content moderation) would structured decomposition likely fail, and why?

- **Concept: Statistical Noise Baseline Estimation**
  - **Why needed:** Distinguishing true bias from stochastic model behavior requires a baseline. The paper uses control perturbations (synonym/punctuation swaps) to estimate noise (~5%). Misestimation leads to false positives/negatives.
  - **Quick check:** Why might a flip rate of 4% still indicate systematic bias if the noise baseline is 5%? What statistical test helps decide?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Vignette Bank -> Intervention Generator -> Decision Model (free-form eval) / Extraction Schema -> Deterministic Rubric (structured eval) -> ICE Feedback Loop (patches extraction prompt) -> Re-evaluation
- **Critical path:** 1) Run free-form evaluation on vignettes to identify high flip-rate domains/bias types. 2) For high-flip cases, implement structured decomposition (extraction + rubric) and compare flip rates. 3) If residual flips remain, run ICE loop: diagnose correlated extracted fields, patch extraction prompt, and re-evaluate. Repeat until plateau.
- **Design tradeoffs:** *Structured vs. free-form:* Structured decomposition sacrifices flexibility for consistency; unsuitable for domains requiring nuanced judgment (e.g., legal edge cases). *ICE loop cost:* Each iteration requires full re-evaluation of the vignette set (thousands of LLM calls). *Schema engineering:* Creating comprehensive extraction schemas requires domain expertise; incomplete schemas may omit critical features, leading to incorrect rubric decisions.
- **Failure signatures:** High free-form flips → near-zero structured flips: successful mitigation. Higher structured flips than free-form (e.g., GPT-5.2): extraction inconsistency or schema leakage. No reduction after ICE patching: possibly irreducible bias or flawed rubric. Finance/education domains consistently show higher residual structured flips: may need hybrid human-in-the-loop.
- **First 3 experiments:** 1) **Baseline detection:** Run free-form evaluation on 50 vignettes per domain (500 total) to identify FR >5%. Prioritize high-flip domains (e.g., finance) for mitigation. 2) **Structured decomposition test:** Implement extraction schema and rubric for one high-flip domain (e.g., finance authority). Compare flip rates on same 50 vignettes. Expect ≥40% reduction if mitigation works. 3) **Single ICE iteration:** For a domain with residual structured flips (e.g., education framing), run one ICE loop: diagnose which extracted fields differ between flipped pairs, patch extraction prompt to exclude those fields, and re-evaluate. Measure Δ flip rate.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper does not explicitly enumerate open questions, but the limitations highlight several unresolved issues: generalizing beyond synthetic vignettes, mitigating bias in highly subjective domains, and scaling the ICE loop for practical use.

## Limitations
- Evaluation relies on synthetic vignettes; real-world decisions involve richer context and stakeholder dynamics not captured.
- Domain subjectivity (e.g., education, finance) may cap achievable mitigation, as rubrics cannot fully encapsulate nuanced criteria.
- ICE loop's computational cost scales poorly (thousands of LLM calls per iteration), limiting practical deployment.
- Generalizability beyond tested model families (Claude, Gemini, etc.) is unclear; some models (e.g., GPT-5.2) showed increased bias under structured decomposition.

## Confidence
- **Detection methodology (ICE flips reveal bias):** High — well-specified invariance principle, controlled swaps, noise baseline.
- **Mitigation efficacy (structured decomposition + ICE loop):** Medium — strong median reduction but model-specific failures and domain ceilings.
- **Domain-specific extremes (finance authority/framing >20%):** Medium — based on one model set; needs replication on newer LLMs.

## Next Checks
1. Replicate finance domain bias on **GPT-4.1, Claude 3.7 Sonnet, and o3-mini** using identical vignettes to test stability across model families.
2. Apply structured decomposition to **legal sentencing** and **medical triage** vignettes—domains with high subjectivity—to quantify failure modes when rubrics cannot capture nuanced criteria.
3. Measure ICE loop's **marginal reduction per iteration** for high-bias domains to identify practical stopping points vs. diminishing returns.
