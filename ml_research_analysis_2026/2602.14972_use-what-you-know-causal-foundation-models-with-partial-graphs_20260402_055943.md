# Use What You Know: Causal Foundation Models with Partial Graphs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.14972
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14972
- **Reference count:** 31
- **Primary result:** Conditioning CFMs on partial ancestral matrices via soft attention bias and a GCN significantly improves causal effect prediction on synthetic and semi-synthetic benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
Causal Foundation Models (CFMs) are neural networks trained to perform causal inference across a distribution of structural causal models. However, they typically ignore domain-specific causal knowledge at test time, leading to overly conservative predictions. This paper introduces methods to condition CFMs on available causal information, ranging from full graphs to more practical partial ancestral matrices (PAMs). The authors propose injecting learnable soft attention biases derived from the PAM into the transformer’s feature-wise attention, and optionally combining this with a graph convolutional network (GCN) that encodes global ancestral structure via adaptive layer normalization. Experiments on synthetic linear‑Gaussian and complex nonlinear data, as well as the RealCause semi‑synthetic benchmark, demonstrate that conditioning on partial causal knowledge yields substantial improvements in predictive performance and effect estimation, matching or exceeding specialized models while retaining flexibility.

## Method Summary
The method builds on transformer‑based CFMs that take a dataset (features, treatment, outcome) and an interventional query. The core innovation is conditioning on a partial ancestral matrix (PAM) that encodes known ancestors, known non‑ancestors, and unknowns. The PAM is used in two ways: (1) a soft attention bias module computes a bias matrix B from the PAM using learnable scalars β_anc and β_non‑anc, which are added to attention logits during feature‑wise self‑attention, encouraging or discouraging attention between nodes according to their ancestral status; (2) a graph convolutional network (GCN) processes the PAM (augmented with initial node role embeddings) to produce a global structural embedding Z_graph, which is injected into each transformer block via adaptive layer normalization (AdaLN), modulating feature statistics globally. The model is trained as a prior‑data fitted network (PFN) over a causal prior, making it amortized: it learns to infer interventional distributions from context and query across a distribution of synthetic causal datasets spanning various graph sizes, mechanisms, and sample sizes. At test time, the provided PAM can be complete, partially known, or even empty.

## Key Results
- Soft attention bias (with or without GCN) substantially outperforms an unconditioned CFM on linear‑Gaussian data, improving negative log‑likelihood (NLL), mean squared error (MSE), and R².
- Conditioning on full graphs on a complex nonlinear prior reduces error; using partial ancestral information yields monotonic improvements as the fraction of known relations increases.
- On RealCause semi‑synthetic benchmarks (IHDP, ACIC, CPS, PSID), encoding the unconfoundedness assumption via a PAM lowers √PEHE and relative ATE error compared to models without graph conditioning.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Partial ancestral matrices as a flexible representation
Partial ancestral matrices (PAMs) encode known cause‑effect relationships (ancestors), known non‑ancestors, and unknown relationships. This allows specification of common assumptions (e.g., unconfoundedness, temporal order) without requiring a complete DAG. Experts can reliably assert some ancestral relations even if the full graph is unknown. Evidence: The paper theoretically shows how PAMs represent unconfoundedness (Theorem A.1) and argues practically for PAMs over adjacency matrices. Break condition: If no reliable ancestral relationships can be specified (all entries 0), the mechanism provides no benefit.

### Mechanism 2: Soft attention bias as the most effective conditioning strategy
During feature‑wise self‑attention, additive biases (+β_anc for known ancestors, –β_non‑anc for known non‑ancestors) are applied to attention logits. This steers information flow without prohibiting it entirely, allowing the model to respect causal constraints while remaining robust to incomplete information. Evidence: Empirical results on linear‑Gaussian data show soft attention bias (with or without GCN) consistently outperforms hard masking and GCN‑only approaches across multiple node counts. Break condition: If the PAM contains systematic errors (e.g., incorrectly specified non‑ancestors), the bias may actively mislead the model, potentially worsening performance below the unconditioned baseline.

### Mechanism 3: GCN + soft attention yields strongest performance on complex data
A GCN processes the PAM (with initial node role embeddings) to produce structure‑aware node embeddings Z_graph, which are injected into each transformer block via AdaLN, modulating feature statistics globally. This complements the local, pairwise guidance from soft attention bias. Evidence: On the complex causal prior, GCN + soft attention noticeably reduces NLL relative to soft attention alone; Table 1 shows systematic improvements on RealCause benchmarks. Break condition: On simpler data (linear‑Gaussian, small graphs), the GCN’s added complexity may offer diminishing returns compared to soft bias alone.

## Foundational Learning
- **Ancestral Relationships & Partial Ancestral Matrices (PAMs)**  
  *Why needed here:* The core innovation is conditioning on partial, not complete, causal knowledge. Understanding that an ancestor matrix is the transitive closure of the adjacency matrix, and that it can be partially specified ( ~T ), is fundamental.  
  *Quick check question:* Given a DAG A→B→C, what is the ancestor matrix? If you know only B causes C, how would you fill a PAM for {A,B,C}?
- **Transformer Self‑Attention Mechanism**  
  *Why needed here:* The model uses a transformer with factorised attention (sample‑wise and feature‑wise). Conditioning happens by modifying feature‑wise attention logits; understanding standard attention is essential to grasp bias injection.  
  *Quick check question:* In a self‑attention layer, what are the roles of the Query, Key, and Value matrices? Where does the bias matrix B get added in the soft attention variant?
- **Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) & Adaptive Layer Normalization (AdaLN)**  
  *Why needed here:* The GCN provides a global graph representation that is injected via AdaLN. Understanding GCN message passing and how AdaLN uses external embeddings to modulate activations is key to implementing the architecture.  
  *Quick check question:* In a simple GCN layer, how are node features updated? If Z_graph contains structural embeddings, how does AdaLN use them to scale and shift a feature map H?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Input Pre-processor → Embedding Layer → Graph Conditioning (Soft Bias Module & GCN Encoder) → Transformer Backbone (L blocks: Feature‑wise Attention with bias B and/or AdaLN(Z_graph), Sample‑wise Attention, Feed‑Forward) → Decoder

**Critical path**  
The input tensor flows through embedding, graph conditioning (producing bias B and embeddings Z_graph), transformer blocks that integrate these conditioners, and finally the decoder to produce the outcome distribution.

**Design tradeoffs**
- *Soft vs Hard Bias:* Soft bias (learnable β) is more robust to partial/incorrect info; hard masking (–∞) is stricter but can break if the PAM has errors.
- *GCN vs No GCN:* GCN adds parameters (~3 M) and computation but captures non‑local structure, yielding gains on complex nonlinear data and larger graphs; on simpler data benefits are smaller.
- *PAM Completeness:* The model is amortised over hide fractions; it can handle any amount of known relations, but performance degrades monotonically as the fraction of hidden (unknown) entries increases.

**Failure signatures**
- *No conditioning effect:* β parameters do not diverge from zero or Z_graph has no predictive signal. Check gradient norms on β and variance of Z_graph.
- *Catastrophic forgetting with partial PAM:* If trained with high hide fraction, the model may fail to utilise bias when full PAM is provided. Monitor performance vs. hide fraction during validation.
- *Inconsistent PAM handling:* If the diagonal of ~T is not set to 1, self‑attention may be suppressed. Ensure diagonal is 1.

**First 3 experiments**
1. Sanity check on synthetic linear‑Gaussian data: Train with/without soft bias on a 5‑node graph where T→Y. Verify bias improves NLL/MSE and that β_anc > 0, β_non‑anc < 0 after training.
2. Partial knowledge amortisation: Train a single model with randomised hide fractions (0 %, 50 %, 100 %). Evaluate on fixed hide fractions (0 %, 50 %, 100 %). Expect monotonic degradation as hide fraction increases, and comparable performance to specialised models at extremes.
3. Benchmark replication: Replicate Table 1 on IHDP/ACIC/CPS using the provided unconfoundedness PAMs. Confirm “Ancestral Info.” outperforms “No Ancestral Info.” on √PEHE and ϵ_ATE. Validate PAM construction per Theorem A.1.

## Limitations
- Limited external validation: Low citation count (0.0) and moderate neighbor FMR (0.457) indicate the work is novel but not yet widely validated or built upon by other high‑impact research.
- Sensitivity to PAM errors: The method’s benefit depends on accurate PAM; systematic errors (e.g., incorrect non‑ancestor assertions) could degrade performance, and robustness to such errors is not deeply explored.
- Scalability trade‑off: Adding a GCN increases model size and computation (~3 M parameters) with variable cost‑effectiveness; gains are most pronounced on complex, nonlinear, large‑scale problems.

## Confidence
- **Claim:** Partial ancestral matrices are a practical representation for encoding partial domain knowledge in CFMs. **Confidence:** Medium.
- **Claim:** Soft attention bias is the most effective single conditioning strategy. **Confidence:** High.
- **Claim:** Combining soft bias with a GCN via AdaLN yields the strongest performance on complex data. **Confidence:** Medium.
