# Not All Candidates are Created Equal: A Heterogeneity-Aware Approach to Pre-ranking in Recommender Systems

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.03770
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03770
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Deployed HAP framework achieved a 0.4% increase in user app usage duration, 3.0% CTR gain, and 6% reduction in serving CPU usage.

## Executive Summary
Industrial pre-ranking systems face a critical challenge: candidate heterogeneity from multiple sources (exposure, ranking, pre-ranking, random negatives) induces gradient conflicts during training, where hard negatives dominate optimization and suppress learning from easy negatives. This paper introduces HAP, a heterogeneity-aware framework combining Gradient-Harmonized Contrastive Learning (GHCL) to balance gradient contributions across difficulty groups and Difficulty-Aware Model Routing (DAMR) to apply a lightweight model universally and a complex model only to hard candidates. A nine-month deployment in Toutiao’s feed pre-ranking system demonstrates significant gains in user engagement and click-through rate while reducing computational cost.

## Method Summary
HAP addresses gradient conflict via GHCL, which stratifies negative samples into hard (exposed, random) and easy (pre-ranking, random) groups and applies separate InfoNCE losses. A correction factor mathematically reduces the gradient ratio between hard and easy groups, preventing hard-sample dominance. DAMR then exploits the finding that model capacity matters most for hard samples: a lightweight model scores all candidates, but only the top-G hardest are re-scored by a more complex model. This routing decouples accuracy from efficiency, as the expensive model is invoked only where performance gains are meaningful.

## Key Results
- Online user app usage duration increased by 0.4% and active days by 0.05%.
- Click-through rate (CTR) improved by 3.0%.
- Serving CPU usage reduced by 6% versus the previous state-of-the-art.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Gradient Conflict from Heterogeneous Negatives
- **Claim:** Mixing heterogeneous negative sample sources causes gradient conflicts where hard samples dominate training.
- **Mechanism:** Under standard contrastive losses (BCE/InfoNCE), gradient magnitude correlates positively with sample difficulty (higher model scores for harder negatives). This leads to disproportionate parameter updates from a small hard subset, causing unstable convergence.
- **Core assumption:** Model logits for hard negatives are systematically higher, and loss gradients scale monotonically with these scores.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "indiscriminately mix heterogeneous samples, suffer from gradient conflicts"; [section 3.2] Table 1 shows empirical gradient norms and logit hierarchy (EN > RN > PRN > GN); related work (HCCP, COPR) expands negative diversity but does not address gradient dominance.

### Mechanism 2: Gradient Harmonization via Decoupled Losses
- **Claim:** Splitting negatives into difficulty-based subgroups and applying separate contrastive losses (GHCL) reduces gradient coupling.
- **Mechanism:** By computing InfoNCE losses separately for hard and easy sets, gradients are decoupled. A correction factor `C` in Eq. 15 reduces the gradient ratio `R_GHCL = R_ORG * C < R_ORG`, balancing contributions.
- **Core assumption:** Gradient dominance arises from softmax normalization across all negatives within one loss; isolating groups breaks destructive interference.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 3.3] Derivation proves reduced gradient ratio; [section 4.5.1] Figure 6 shows GHCL stabilizes inter-set cosine similarity and balances intra-set gradient norms; [section 4.4.1] Table 4/Figure 5 show GHCL outperforms mixing baselines.

### Mechanism 3: Difficulty-Aware Model Routing
- **Claim:** Routing candidates by predicted difficulty (lightweight model for all, complex model only for hard) optimizes the accuracy-efficiency trade-off.
- **Mechanism:** DAMR exploits that model capacity correlates with performance primarily on hard samples (Fig 3). A cheap lightweight model filters easy negatives; the expensive expressive model focuses on the hard subset.
- **Core assumption:** There exists a pronounced performance-efficiency gap between simple and complex models for hard samples but minimal for easy ones.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 3.4.1] "lightweight models perform comparably to complex ones on easy samples but degrade sharply on hard ones"; [section 4.5.2] Figure 7 shows CTR gains peak with routing ratio before overload; [section 4.4.2] Table 5: HAP outperforms Unified Large Model on THard with lower cost.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Multi-stage recommender systems**
  - *Why needed here:* Understands pre-ranking's role as a latency-critical filter between retrieval (millions) and ranking (hundreds), and how data heterogeneity stems from signals at different stages.
  - *Quick check:* What are typical latency constraints and candidate set sizes at pre-ranking vs. ranking, and why does this pressure model design?
- **Concept: Contrastive learning (InfoNCE)**
  - *Why needed here:* GHCL modifies standard InfoNCE; understanding gradient coupling via softmax normalization is key to the problem.
  - *Quick check:* In InfoNCE, how does the denominator containing all negative scores create interdependence between gradients of different negatives?
- **Concept: Gradient-based optimization dynamics**
  - *Why needed here:* The core problem is gradient magnitude imbalance and direction conflict; requires understanding how loss gradients propagate and interact during multi-sample training.
  - *Quick check:* Why would larger gradient norms from hard negatives lead to "dominance" in parameter updates if per-sample gradients are summed?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Lightweight model (f_l) processes all retrieval candidates -> score-based routing keeps top-G -> Expressive model (f_c) rescoring top-G -> top-K sent to ranking.
- **Critical path:** Retrieve candidates → f_l scores all → keep top-G → f_c rescore top-G → send top-K (e.g., 200) to ranking.
- **Design tradeoffs:** 
  - Routing threshold G: Higher G improves hard-sample coverage but increases latency/compute risk.
  - Model capacity split: Too small f_c hurts hard-sample accuracy; too large f_l wastes compute.
  - GHCL weighting: Implicit set sizes affect loss balance; paper uses equal weighting but notes λ tuning may be needed.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Online: Latency spikes when G exceeds serving capacity; CTR gain erodes.
  - Offline: AUC gap between THard and TEasy remains large (DAMR not helping hard samples) or TEasy drops (f_l underfitting).
  - Training: Gradient norm plots show persistent imbalance; validation loss unstable.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. GHCL ablation: Remove GHCL (use standard InfoNCE mix), retrain HAP. Expect significant AUC drop across all test sets, especially THard/TEasy.
  2. DAMR ablation: Compare HAP (two-stage) against a single-stage Unified Large Model with same total capacity trained on all samples. Expect HAP to match/exceed THard accuracy with lower serving cost.
  3. Gradient dynamics validation: Track per-difficulty gradient norms and inter-set cosine similarity during training with/without GHCL. Expect GHCL to show balanced norms and higher cosine.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Does the HAP framework generalize to recommendation domains beyond news feeds (e.g., e-commerce, video platforms) where candidate heterogeneity patterns differ?
- **Basis in paper:** The study exclusively uses Toutiao's feed scenario data and the released ToutiaoRec dataset, with no cross-domain validation.
- **Why unresolved:** The defined negative types and difficulty thresholds are tailored to Toutiao's pipeline; other domains may exhibit different distributions or user intents, affecting GHCL and DAMR efficacy.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply HAP to other multi-stage recommendation datasets (e.g., from Amazon or Taobao) with comparable heterogeneity, measuring performance improvements and gradient conflict reduction.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Could sample difficulty be dynamically learned rather than heuristically categorized based on ranking positions, to improve GHCL's gradient harmonization?
- **Basis in paper:** GHCL splits negatives into hard (EN, RN) and easy (PRN, GN) sets using fixed pipeline-derived types, without exploring data-driven difficulty estimation.
- **Why unresolved:** A fixed split ignores continuous difficulty spectra and may not adapt to model learning progress or domain-specific nuances, potentially limiting optimization.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replace the heuristic split with a learnable difficulty module (e.g., using model confidence or a separate difficulty predictor) and train end-to-end; compare against fixed-split GHCL on multiple metrics.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What is the impact of HAP's candidate routing on downstream ranking and re-ranking stages? Could the altered pre-ranking distribution cause distribution shift issues?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper reports overall online gains but does not isolate downstream effects or analyze pre-ranking output distribution changes in detail.
- **Why unresolved:** Ranking models are typically trained on pre-ranking outputs; a significant shift (e.g., more hard samples) might degrade ranking performance if not accounted for.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Examine the distribution of pre-ranking scores and candidate composition before and after HAP; retrain ranking models on HAP's outputs and evaluate ranking-specific metrics (e.g., NDCG) and end-to-end performance.

## Limitations
- **Generalization gap:** HAP's validation is confined to Toutiao's pre-ranking context; candidate heterogeneity patterns and latency constraints may differ in other systems (e.g., e-commerce, video).
- **Difficulty stratification fragility:** GHCL and DAMR rely on accurate hard/easy sample splits; source-specific biases or distribution shifts could misclassify samples, undermining gradient harmonization and routing efficiency.
- **Threshold sensitivity:** DAMR's routing ratio G demands empirical tuning; suboptimal values risk system overload (if too high) or discarded viable candidates (if too low), with no universal tuning protocol provided.

## Confidence
- **Gradient conflict existence:** High. Empirical evidence (Table 1) confirms logit-based gradient dominance across heterogeneous negatives.
- **GHCL's gradient harmonization:** High. Mathematical reduction in gradient ratio (Eq. 15) and stable inter-set cosine similarity (Fig 6) validate decoupled loss design.
- **DAMR's efficiency-accuracy trade-off:** High. Deployment metrics (6% CPU reduction, 3% CTR lift) and ablation vs. unified models (Table 5) demonstrate tangible benefits, though long-term stability remains unverified.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-platform transfer:** Implement HAP on at least two diverse recommendation systems (e.g., Amazon product pre-ranking, YouTube video funnel) to measure consistency in gradient conflict mitigation and routing gains.
2. **Longitudinal drift analysis:** Track online metrics (CTR, latency) over 12 months post-deployment to detect degradation from candidate source evolution or model staleness.
3. **Noise robustness test:** Artificially corrupt difficulty labels (e.g., 20% random hard/easy swaps) and quantify impact on GHCL's gradient balance (norms, cosine similarity) and final AUC on THard/TEasy splits.
