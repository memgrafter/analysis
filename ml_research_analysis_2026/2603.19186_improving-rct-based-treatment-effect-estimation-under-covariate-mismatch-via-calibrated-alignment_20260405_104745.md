# Improving RCT-Based Treatment Effect Estimation Under Covariate Mismatch via Calibrated Alignment
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.19186
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19186
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** CALM bypasses imputation via embedding alignment and double calibration; for nonlinear CATEs, CALM-NN achieves the lowest RMSE in all 22 simulation settings, with reductions up to 39%.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the problem of augmenting a randomized controlled trial (RCT) with a larger observational study (OS) when the two sources measure different, partially overlapping covariates (covariate mismatch). Directly applying OS outcome models to the RCT is impossible. Existing methods impute the missing OS-only covariates in the RCT, which can be difficult. The authors propose CALM (Calibrated Alignment), a method that learns low-dimensional embeddings mapping each source’s covariates into a common space. OS outcome models trained on OS embeddings are transferred to the RCT embedding space and calibrated using RCT data. This double-calibration pipeline preserves the RCT’s causal identification while leveraging OS data. Theoretical risk bounds show CALM outperforms imputation when outcome-relevant information is low-dimensional. Across 51 simulations, all calibration-based methods are equivalent for linear CATEs, but CALM-NN dominates for nonlinear CATEs. A semi-synthetic benchmark confirms these findings.

## Method Summary
CALM addresses covariate mismatch without imputing missing covariates. It uses a four-stage pipeline: (1) Train OS outcome models (per treatment arm) on the full OS covariate set. (2) Learn an RCT-specific encoder that maps RCT covariates into the same embedding space as the OS models, while aligning the distributions of OS and RCT embeddings via a penalty (e.g., MMD). Simultaneously, fit small discrepancy functions to calibrate the frozen OS outcome models to the RCT. (3) Combine the calibrated OS predictions into a pseudo-outcome that is unbiased for the true CATE. (4) Regress this pseudo-outcome on the original RCT covariates to obtain the final CATE estimate. Two instantiations exist: CALM-Lin (linear reduced-rank projections) and CALM-NN (neural encoders with alignment losses). The method decouples nuisance estimation (OS model transfer) from final estimation (CATE correction), ensuring consistency even if the transferred model is misspecified.

## Key Results
- For linear CATEs (29 settings), all calibration-based methods (RACER, SR-OSCAR, MR-OSCAR, CALM-Lin) achieve nearly identical RMSE (differences < 10⁻³).
- For nonlinear CATEs (22 settings), CALM-NN achieves the lowest RMSE in every setting, with reductions up to 39% compared to the best linear method (e.g., RMSE 0.71 vs 1.16).
- A semi-synthetic IHDP benchmark confirms the equivalence of calibration-based methods in the linear regime and the superiority of CALM-NN for nonlinear effects.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The double-calibration pipeline yields a consistent CATE estimator even if the OS outcome model is poorly transferred, because the second-stage correction absorbs any residual discrepancy.
- **Mechanism:** A pseudo-outcome is constructed using an augmentation function *m*. Under RCT randomization, this pseudo-outcome is unbiased for the true CATE regardless of *m*. The first calibration stage adjusts *m* to approximate the counterfactual mean outcome (CMO) using OS data and a small RCT sample. The second stage learns a correction term directly from the RCT pseudo-outcomes to recover the full CATE. This decoupling ensures that misspecification in the OS outcome model only affects variance, not bias.
- **Core assumption:** The CATE correction function class has enough capacity to capture the true CATE (Assumption 1). Pseudo-outcome regression uses cross-fitting.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "preserving causal identification" and "double-calibration pipeline". [section 3.2] Pseudo-outcome construction and risk decomposition showing *E*[τₘ|*X*] = τ(*X*). [section 3.3] R-OSCAR pipeline and negative-transfer protection.
- **Break condition:** If the CATE correction function class is misspecified (e.g., too restrictive), the second-stage correction cannot fully recover the true CATE, leading to bias.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Learning embeddings that map each source’s covariates into a common low-dimensional space is easier than imputing the full set of missing covariates, because it only needs to preserve outcome-relevant information.
- **Mechanism:** Instead of reconstructing OS-only features *V* from shared covariates *Z* (imputation), CALM learns encoders ϕₒ and ϕᵣ that map *X*ᵒ and *X*ʳ into a shared *d*-dimensional space. OS outcome models are trained on embeddings ϕₒ(*X*ᵒ) and evaluated on ϕᵣ(*X*ʳ) during calibration. An alignment loss (e.g., MMD, contrastive) encourages the two embedding distributions to match, so the OS outcome model provides a useful initial prediction for RCT units. The calibration step then corrects residual discrepancies.
- **Core assumption:** Embeddings are outcome-sufficient (Assumption 2), a discrepancy function exists (Assumption 3), and alignment error *r²*ϕ is finite (Assumption 4).
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "learn feature embeddings... bypasses imputation". [section 4.1] Core idea of replacing imputation with alignment. [section 4.2] Formal assumptions (2–4). [section 4.4] Alignment objectives.
- **Break condition:** If outcome-relevant information is high-dimensional so that low-*d* embeddings incur a large sufficiency gap ϵ²*suff*, or if distributional shift is severe and alignment fails (large *r²*ϕ), the transferred model becomes uninformative or harmful.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The finite-sample risk bound for CALM decomposes into alignment error, sufficiency gap, and complexity terms, theoretically identifying when embedding alignment outperforms imputation.
- **Mechanism:** Theorem 1 bounds CATE estimation error by: CATE approximation error, outcome-model and calibration complexities (Rademacher terms), alignment error ((*L*ₘᵤ+*L*δ)² *r²*ϕ), and sufficiency gap ϵ²*suff*. Comparing to the MR-OSCAR bound (Corollary 2) shows CALM gains when alignment+sufficiency costs are lower than the imputation cost *L*² *r²*ᵢₘ plus complexity of outcome models in the full OS feature space. In linear Gaussian models, CALM strictly generalizes imputation (Proposition 4).
- **Core assumption:** Function classes for outcome models, discrepancies, and CATE have comparable complexity; Lipschitz constants finite; sufficiency gap and alignment error small.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "Finite-sample risk bounds decompose...". [section 5.1] Theorem 1 and proof sketch. [section 5.2] Corollary 2 comparing CALM and MR-OSCAR. [section 5.4] Linear specialization.
- **Break condition:** When imputation is easy (small *r²*ᵢₘ) and outcome-relevant dimension is high (large ϵ²*suff*), the bound favors imputation. Also, if alignment penalty is poorly tuned, *r²*ϕ may be large.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Pseudo-outcome regression for CATE estimation.
  - **Why needed:** Enables any augmentation function *m* to be used while maintaining unbiasedness of the CATE estimator, separating nuisance estimation from final estimation. The variance depends on the closeness of *m* to the CMO.
  - **Quick check:** Why does the pseudo-outcome remain unbiased for the CATE even if *m* is not the true CMO?

- **Concept:** Representation alignment under covariate mismatch.
  - **Why needed:** Covariate mismatch means the two sources measure different variables; direct application of OS outcome models to RCT units is impossible. Alignment learns a common representation space where transfer is possible without reconstructing missing covariates.
  - **Quick check:** What is the key difference between imputation (MR-OSCAR) and alignment (CALM) for handling covariate mismatch?

- **Concept:** Finite-sample risk decomposition for method comparison.
  - **Why needed:** The risk bound reveals trade-offs between alignment error, sufficiency gap, and complexity, guiding when to prefer embedding alignment over imputation.
  - **Quick check:** According to Corollary 2, under what conditions does CALM achieve a tighter risk bound than MR-OSCAR?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** RCT/OS data -> Stage 1 (OS outcome model training on full X^o) -> Stage 2 (Learn ϕᵣ, δₐ with alignment penalty; freeze ϕₒ, μₒₐ) -> Stage 3 (Compute calibrated CMO m(x^r); construct pseudo-outcomes ψ) -> Stage 4 (Regress ψ on original X^r to get δ) -> Final CATE τ̂(x^r) = Σₐ a μᶜᵃˡₐ(x^r) + δ(x^r).
- **Critical path:** 1. Train Stage 1 on OS only. 2. Jointly optimize ϕᵣ and δₐ with alignment penalty (Stage 2). 3. Compute m and pseudo-outcomes (Stage 3). 4. Fit δ on original X^r (Stage 4).
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Embedding dimension *d*:** Smaller *d* reduces variance but increases bias (sufficiency gap). Select via cross-validated OS prediction error (CALM-Lin) or RCT calibration error (CALM-NN).
  - **Alignment loss:** MMD is robust but needs kernel bandwidth; contrastive is simpler but sensitive to neighborhood radius ε. For small nᵣ, contrastive may be noisier; for large nᵣ, MMD preferred.
  - **Calibration model complexity:** Use simple models (linear, shallow trees) for δₐ and δ to protect against negative transfer. Increasing complexity risks overfitting.
  - **Neural vs linear:** CALM-NN flexible for nonlinear CATEs but may distort embeddings under severe shift; CALM-Lin more robust but limited to linear effects.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Alignment failure:** Alignment loss (e.g., MMD) does not decrease during Stage 2 training. Check: compute MMD between frozen ϕₒ(X^o) and current ϕᵣ(X^r).
  - **Poor calibration:** High residual error in Stage 2 regression after adding δₐ suggests OS model not transferable. Increase δₐ capacity or λ.
  - **Dominant CATE correction:** If ‖δ̂‖ ≫ ‖τ̃‖, OS augmentation ineffective; estimator reverts to RCT-only (safe but indicates little benefit).
  - **Instability in CALM-NN under severe shift:** Performance degrades relative to Naive, especially when outcome shift ∥δʳₐ−δᵒₐ∥ is large. Monitor by comparing to Naive baseline.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Equivalence in linear regime:** Run CALM-Lin on linear-CATE simulation grid. Expect RMSE differences <10⁻³ vs RACER, SR-OSCAR, MR-OSCAR.
  2. **Neural advantage in nonlinear regime:** Use shared-latent DGP with sinusoidal CATE (ω=1.5), nᵣ=500. CALM-NN should achieve RMSE ~0.71 vs ~1.16 for best linear method.
  3. **Alignment objective ablation:** On same nonlinear DGP, compare MMD vs contrastive alignment for CALM-NN. MMD should be more stable; contrastive performance may vary with ε.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1:** How can practitioners directly estimate or bound the alignment error \(r^2_\phi\) in CALM, given that \(V\) is unobserved in the RCT? The discussion identifies "unobservability of the alignment quality \(r^2_\phi\) in practice" as a limitation.
- **Question 2:** Under what conditions does CALM-NN fail to provide negative-transfer protection, and how can the framework be modified to guarantee robustness under severe distributional shift? The discussion notes "incomplete negative-transfer protection for CALM-NN under severe distributional shift."
- **Question 3:** How can CALM be extended to incorporate multiple observational studies with heterogeneous covariate spaces and different degrees of overlap with the RCT? The conclusion lists "multi-source settings" as a natural extension.
- **Question 4:** How can we construct valid confidence intervals for CALM's CATE estimates that account for the two-stage estimation and representation learning? The conclusion suggests "formal inference via de-biased ML" as a future direction.

## Limitations
- **Simulation parameters not fully disclosed:** Exact data generating processes (coefficients, noise levels) are missing, hindering exact replication.
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity:** CALM-NN depends on embedding dimension, alignment loss weight, and network architecture; optimal values not specified.
- **Assumption fragility:** Outcome-sufficiency and finite alignment error may not hold in real mismatched data, especially under severe covariate shift.

## Confidence
- **Double-calibration pipeline consistency:** High. Theoretical guarantee via pseudo-outcome unbiasedness; supported by simulations. Break condition: misspecified CATE correction class.
- **Embedding alignment easier than imputation:** Medium. Theory and nonlinear simulations show advantage when outcome-relevant dimension low. Sensitive to alignment quality; may fail if alignment error large or sufficiency gap large.
- **Finite-sample risk bound:** High. Rigorous decomposition identifies conditions favoring CALM; proof is sound.
- **Nonlinear empirical superiority:** Medium. CALM-NN reduced RMSE up to 39% across 22 settings, but replication needed due to undisclosed tuning specifics.
