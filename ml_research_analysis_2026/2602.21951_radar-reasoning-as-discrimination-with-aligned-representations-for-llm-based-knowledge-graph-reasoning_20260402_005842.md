# RADAR: Reasoning as Discrimination with Aligned Representations for LLM-based Knowledge Graph Reasoning

## Quick Facts

- **ArXiv ID**: 2602.21951  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21951  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: RADAR achieves average relative improvements of 5–6% in link prediction (MRR) and triple classification accuracy over state-of-the-art LLM baselines across four benchmarks.

## Executive Summary

Knowledge graph reasoning (KGR) with large language models (LLMs) often fails to generalize beyond co‑occurrence patterns present in training data. RADAR addresses this by reformulating KGR as a discriminative entity selection task rather than generative pattern matching. Given a query (head, relation, ?), the model selects the correct tail from a fixed small candidate set. Training proceeds in two stages: first, supervised fine‑tuning (SFT) with chain‑of‑thought (CoT) rationales instills structured output; second, reinforcement learning (GRPO) maximizes a reward that enforces relational separability between correct entities and hard negatives. Inference bypasses autoregressive decoding entirely: hidden states from an intermediate layer are scored by a learned binary classifier, directly yielding validity judgments. On FB15K‑237, FB15K‑237N, WN18RR, and UMLS, RADAR delivers average relative gains of 5–6% in MRR and triple classification accuracy. A task‑adaptive mutual information probe reveals a 62.9% average increase in relational signal within the intermediate representations, and ablation studies confirm that the synergy of discriminative data, outcome‑based optimization, and representation‑based inference is essential for the observed improvements.

## Method Summary

RADAR converts KGR into a discriminative selection problem. For each query (h, r, ?), a candidate set C(h, r) of size K=4 is built, containing one or more true tails and random negatives sampled via TuckER scoring. The training pipeline:

1. **Data construction**: Prompts containing CoT rationales (generated by a proprietary LLM such as GPT‑4) are created for the discriminative setting.
2. **Supervised fine‑tuning (SFT)**: A LLaMA backbone is fine‑tuned with LoRA on next‑token prediction of the CoT traces, producing an initial policy π_θ.
3. **Error‑focused reinforcement learning**: The SFT model is evaluated on the training set; failures are collected into D_error. GRPO (group‑relative policy optimization) is then applied to update the policy using a composite reward R = α·R_fmt + (1−α)·R_acc (F1‑based), with KL regularization to the SFT reference policy.
4. **Representation classifier**: From the final model, hidden states of an intermediate layer (l=15) are extracted for both positive and negative triples and used to train a 2‑layer MLP binary classifier f_ϕ.
5. **Inference**: For triple classification, (h, r, t) is scored directly by f_ϕ. For link prediction, a retrieve‑then‑rerank pipeline first obtains top‑15 candidate tails using a pretrained TuckER model; these are reranked by f_ϕ scores, avoiding autoregressive generation entirely.

## Key Results

- Average relative improvement of 5–6% in link prediction MRR and triple classification accuracy compared to state‑of‑the‑art LLM baselines on four benchmarks.
- Task‑adaptive mutual information (probe of relational signal) increases by 62.9% on average after the full RADAR training pipeline.
- Ablation studies show that removing any of the three core components (discriminative data formulation, GRPO RL, representation‑based inference) degrades performance by more than 3% absolute, confirming their synergistic necessity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Discriminative Reformulation
- **Claim**: Reformulating KGR as discriminative entity selection eliminates reliance on surface co‑occurrence shortcuts.
- **Mechanism**: The model must explicitly compare relational evidence among a fixed candidate set, rather than generating triple strings that can be produced by memorizing frequent entity‑relation co‑occurrences.
- **Core assumption**: Generative training on serialized triples encourages memorization without genuine relational reasoning.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - Abstract: “recasts KGR as discriminative entity selection, where reinforcement learning enforces relative entity separability beyond token‑likelihood imitation.”  
  - Section 2.2: The reformulation “forces the optimization trajectory toward inducing discriminative latent structures governed by relational validity, rendering co‑occurrence shortcuts insufficient.”  
  - Corpus: Neighboring studies identify co‑occurrence as a key failure mode (e.g., “Co‑occurrence is not factual association”).
- **Break condition**: If negative candidates are too easy (Tier 1), discrimination remains superficial; gains diminish (Table 3 shows Tier 2 performs best).

### Mechanism 2: Outcome‑Based Reinforcement Learning
- **Claim**: Reinforcement learning with an entity‑level reward aligns optimization with relational correctness, not token statistics.
- **Mechanism**: After SFT, GRPO updates the policy to maximize a reward that measures selection accuracy (F1‑based) on hard negatives, shifting gradients from next‑token imitation to global relational validity.
- **Core assumption**: Token‑level cross‑entropy cannot resolve the mismatch between generative objective and relational reasoning; outcome‑based RL can directly optimize the desired behavior.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - Abstract: “reinforcement learning (GRPO) to maximize a reward that enforces relational separability between correct entities and hard negatives.”  
  - Section 2.3: Describes two‑stage training, noting “RL… pivots the learning signal from token likelihood to global outcome validity” and presents the composite reward (Eq. 2).  
  - Corpus: GRPO from DeepSeekMath supports viability of policy gradient methods.
- **Break condition**: Improper reward weighting (α mis‑set) or excessive KL regularization can prevent RL from improving separability, potentially degrading performance.

### Mechanism 3: Representation‑Based Inference
- **Claim**: Inference in representation space using a learned classifier bypasses generation errors and extracts the discriminative relational signals induced during training.
- **Mechanism**: Hidden states from an intermediate LLM layer (l=15) are fed to a binary classifier trained on triple validity. This avoids autoregressive hallucinations and aligns inference with the discriminative objective.
- **Core assumption**: Autoregressive decoding evaluates plausibility indirectly and is prone to hallucination; discriminative relational information is better captured in intermediate representations.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - Abstract: “Leveraging this separability, inference operates directly in representation space, ensuring consistency with the discriminative optimization and bypassing the generation‑induced hallucinations.”  
  - Section 2.4: Details extraction from layer 15 and binary classifier f_ϕ; for link prediction, uses retrieve‑then‑rerank.  
  - Section 3.3 (ablation): Table 4 shows Serial‑SFT‑Extraction outperforms Serial‑SFT‑Generation, confirming benefit of representation extraction.
- **Break condition**: Suboptimal extraction layer (e.g., final layer) or inadequate classifier training fails to capture discriminative signal (Figure 9 shows layer‑wise sensitivity).

## Architecture Onboarding

Component map:  
Data constructor → SFT trainer (LoRA) → RL trainer (GRPO) → Representation classifier (2‑layer MLP) → Inference engine (retrieve‑then‑rerank for link prediction, direct scoring for triple classification)

Critical path:  
Construct dataset → SFT → evaluate SFT → build D_error → GRPO → train f_ϕ → deploy inference (with retrieval for link prediction)

Design tradeoffs:
- Candidate set size K: Larger K increases discrimination difficulty; K=4 optimal (Table 9).
- Negative hardness: Random negatives (Tier 2) outperform easier (Tier 1) and harder (Tier 3) (Table 3).
- Extraction layer: Intermediate layers (e.g., 15) capture relational information best; final layers lose signal (Figure 9).
- Retrieval vs recall: top‑n=15 balances efficiency and recall; lower n risks missing true tails.

Failure signatures:
- Task‑adaptive SMI drops below ~0.25, indicating poor relational encoding.
- Inductive split: Unseen‑entity accuracy similar to seen; RADAR maintains a noticeable gap (Figure 6).
- Ablation: Removing RL or using generation yields >3% absolute accuracy loss (Table 4).
- Overfitting: High seen accuracy but low overall/inductive accuracy suggests reliance on entity‑specific co‑occurrence.

First three experiments:
1. Ablation study isolating data formulation, training, and inference (Table 4).
2. Task‑adaptive mutual information measurement across training stages (Figure 5, Figure 7).
3. Inductive generalization under varying entity exclusion rates (Figure 6).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1**  
- **Question**: How can the computational cost of the reinforcement learning stage in RADAR be reduced without sacrificing its ability to promote relational separability and generalization?  
- **Basis in paper**: The authors note that “our discriminative alignment via reinforcement learning incurs higher training costs than standard fine‑tuning” and state that future work will focus on optimizing training efficiency.  
- **Why unresolved**: High training costs limit scalability and accessibility, especially for large models and datasets.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A thorough analysis of the RL component’s computational profile, combined with algorithmic improvements (e.g., more sample‑efficient RL algorithms, gradient checkpointing, or staged training adjustments) that demonstrate comparable or better performance at lower compute.

**Open Question 2**  
- **Question**: Can a retriever‑free discriminative KGR framework be developed that eliminates the recall bottleneck inherent in the current retrieve‑then‑rerank approach while maintaining inference efficiency?  
- **Basis in paper**: The authors identify the retrieve‑then‑rerank paradigm as introducing an inherent recall bottleneck and mention exploring retriever‑free LLM reasoning mechanisms as future work.  
- **Why unresolved**: The recall bottleneck means if the retriever fails to include the true answer in the candidate set, the method cannot recover it, limiting completeness. A retriever‑free approach could score all entities directly but must do so efficiently.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A novel architecture or scoring function that allows exhaustive or near‑exhaustive entity scoring without a separate retrieval step, while keeping computational complexity manageable (e.g., via learned index structures or efficient attention patterns), and demonstrating improved recall on large KG benchmarks.

**Open Question 3**  
- **Question**: Is the fixed choice of intermediate layer (e.g., layer 15 in LLaMA) for extracting representations optimal for discriminative KGR across diverse datasets and tasks, or can we design an adaptive mechanism to select the most informative layer?  
- **Basis in paper**: The paper uses layer l=15 based on prior work and reports layer‑wise performance (Figure 9) showing variation across datasets, but does not investigate adaptive selection.  
- **Why unresolved**: The optimal layer may depend on dataset characteristics (e.g., relation complexity, entity description length). A fixed layer might be suboptimal for some scenarios, and an adaptive approach could extract more relational signal.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: An analysis correlating layer features with task‑specific metrics, and a developed method (e.g., learned layer selection or multi‑layer aggregation) that consistently outperforms the fixed layer choice across multiple benchmarks.

**Open Question 4**  
- **Question**: To what extent does the increase in task‑adaptive mutual information (I_task) directly cause the improved generalization of RADAR, versus being a correlated but non‑causal indicator of relational reasoning?  
- **Basis in paper**: The paper uses I_task to show that RADAR increases relational signal in representations, but does not establish causality between this increase and performance gains.  
- **Why unresolved**: Understanding whether boosting I_task is necessary or sufficient for better generalization could inform future probing and training objectives. It might be that other factors also contribute.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Controlled experiments where I_task is manipulated independently (e.g., via intervention on representations) while keeping training fixed, to see if changes in I_task directly affect downstream performance; or training objectives specifically optimized to increase I_task and measuring resulting generalization.

## Limitations

- **Incomplete reproducibility details**: CoT trace generation LLM, prompt template, temperature, and validation of reasoning quality are unreported, threatening exact replication of the supervised signal.
- **Missing critical hyperparameters**: GRPO parameters (α, ε, β, group size G), LoRA configuration, classifier hidden size, negative sampling thresholds, and retrieval top‑n are not disclosed, preventing faithful reproduction.
- **Limited evaluation scope**: Experiments are restricted to four medium‑scale benchmarks; scalability to very large, dense, or cross‑domain knowledge graphs and behavior under extreme inductive conditions remain unexplored.

## Confidence

- **Performance superiority**: Medium (results are consistent but rely on proprietary GPT‑4 and lack full hyperparameter disclosure)
- **Mechanistic explanation**: Medium (ablation supports contributions, but synergistic alignment narrative is not directly measured)
- **Representation analysis**: Medium (probe methodology described but robustness without code or alternative estimators is uncertain)
