# Spilled Energy in Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.18671
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18671
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** Min‑pooled spilled energy ΔE detects hallucinations with 73.16% average AuROC across nine benchmarks, outperforming logits and probing classifiers.

## Executive Summary
Detecting hallucinations in large language models (LLMs) is a critical challenge. This paper introduces a training‑free method that reinterprets the softmax classifier as an Energy‑Based Model (EBM). The core concept is “spilled energy” ΔE—the discrepancy between the marginal energy of a prefix (computed from the next‑step logits) and the energy of the token sampled at the current step. ΔE is computed directly from the logits emitted during autoregressive generation, requiring no additional training or access to internal activations. Across nine diverse benchmarks using LLaMA, Mistral, and Gemma models, the authors find that min‑pooling ΔE over the exact answer span yields an average AuROC of 73.16%, significantly surpassing raw logit confidence (54.62%) and probing classifiers (64.16%). The approach generalizes robustly across tasks and model types, works for both pretrained and instruction‑tuned variants, and introduces minimal computational overhead beyond reading the logits.

## Method Summary
The pipeline begins with standard autoregressive generation while storing the logits at every step. For each generated token \(x_i\) (position \(i\)), two energy values are computed: the energy of the sampled token \(E_\ell = -\text{logit}_i[x_i]\) and the marginal energy of the prefix \(E_m = -\log\sum_k \exp(\text{logits}_{i+1}[k])\), the latter coming from the next step’s logits. The spilled energy \(\Delta E_i = E_\ell - E_m\) (or equivalently \(\log\sum\exp(\text{logits}_{i+1}) - \mathbf{l}_i[x_i]\)) measures the inconsistency between these two perspectives. After generation, the exact answer tokens within the output are localized (e.g., by string matching or an auxiliary extractor). The \(\Delta E\) values over this answer span are aggregated—typically by taking the minimum—to produce a single score. A threshold, determined on validation data, then classifies the response as likely correct or hallucinated. The entire process requires only the model’s output logits and no training.

## Key Results
- Min‑pooled \(\Delta E\) averages 73.16% AuROC across nine benchmarks, clearly beating logits (54.62%) and probing classifiers (64.16%).
- The method generalizes robustly across different tasks (e.g., QA, math) and model families (LLaMA, Mistral, Gemma), including both pretrained and instruction‑tuned variants.
- It introduces no training overhead or activation access requirements; marginal energy alone provides a complementary but weaker signal.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The softmax layer of an autoregressive LLM can be formally reinterpreted as an Energy‑Based Model (EBM), exposing a theoretical equality between the energy of the sampled token (\(E_\ell\)) and the marginal energy (\(E_m\)) that should hold across consecutive decoding steps but does not in practice.
- **Mechanism:** By applying the chain rule of probability, the conditional \( p(x_i \mid x_{i-1:1}) \) is expressed as a ratio of joint and marginal probabilities. Following Grathwohl et al. (2020), these are mapped to EBMs: \( E_\ell = -\text{logit}(x_i) \) (energy of the sampled token) and \( E_m = -\log\sum_k \exp(\text{logits}_k) \) (marginal energy of the prefix). For a consistent joint probability, the energies should satisfy \( E_\ell(x_{i:1}) = E_m(x_{i-1:1}) \) across steps. However, during inference, \( E_\ell \) is computed from the logits at step \(i\) (predicting \(x_i\)), while \( E_m \) for the same prefix \(x_{i:1}\) is derived from the logits at step \(i+1\) (with \(x_{i:1}\) as context). The paper defines **spilled energy** as the discrepancy \( \Delta E(x_{i:1}) = -E_m(x_{i:1}) + E_\ell(x_{i:1}) = \log\sum\exp(\text{logits}_{i+1}) - \text{logit}_i[x_i] \), which should be zero for perfect adherence but is nonzero in practice.
- **Core assumption:** Autoregressive training implies that the model’s predicted probabilities obey the chain rule, but the separate computation of logits and normalization during decoding does not enforce that the energy of a token matches the marginal energy of its prefix measured in the next step. This mismatch arises from the model’s internal approximations.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “We reinterpret the final Large Language Model (LLM) softmax classifier as an Energy‑Based Model (EBM), decomposing the sequence‑to‑sequence probability chain into multiple interacting EBMs at inference.”
  - [section] Section 4.1, Eq. (3)–(8), particularly Eq. (6) shows the EBM decomposition and Eq. (8) defines \( \Delta E \).
  - [corpus] Corpus neighbors such as “Semantic Energy: Detecting LLM Hallucination Beyond Entropy” (arXiv:2508.14496) and “Energy Landscapes Enable Reliable Abstention…” (arXiv:2509.04482) explore energy‑based diagnostics for LLM uncertainty, but the specific reinterpretation of softmax as an EBM with cross‑step energy comparison is novel and lacks direct prior evidence.
- **Break condition:** If the LLM were explicitly trained to enforce \( E_\ell = E_m \) across steps (e.g., via a regularizer), the spill would collapse to zero, erasing the signal. Non‑autoregressive or encoder‑decoder architectures might not exhibit the same spill pattern.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The magnitude of spilled energy \( \Delta E \) computed over the exact answer tokens correlates robustly with hallucination likelihood; higher \( \Delta E \) values indicate a greater probability that the answer is incorrect.
- **Mechanism:** After generating a response, the method localizes the span \([u, w]\) of tokens that constitute the exact answer (e.g., “Rome” in a QA task). For each token \(x_i\) in that span, \( \Delta E_i \) is computed from the logits of the preceding step \(i\) (for \(E_\ell\)) and the subsequent step \(i+1\) (for \(E_m\)). Intuitively, a confident correct answer yields a high logit relative to the log‑sum‑exp of the next‑step logits, producing a low \( \Delta E \). A hallucinated answer often has a suppressed logit, leading to elevated \( \Delta E \). Pooling (e.g., min‑pooling) over \([u, w]\) aggregates these values into a single score per response.
- **Core assumption:** Factual errors are accompanied by a mismatch between the model’s immediate token preference (logit) and the marginal distribution over continuations measured one step later, reflecting a misallocation of probability mass.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “min‑pooled \( \Delta E \) achieves an average AuROC of 73.16%... On nine benchmarks… surpasses logits (54.62%).”
  - [section] Section 4.2 and Section 5.2: Fig. 2(d) shows histograms with clear separation; Table 1 reports per‑dataset AuROC values up to 97% (Mistral‑Instruct on HotpotQA‑WC). Synthetic arithmetic experiments (Section 5.1) demonstrate separation even for subtle errors (hard offset range [1, 10]).
  - [corpus] “Semantic Energy: Detecting LLM Hallucination Beyond Entropy” uses semantic entropy—a token‑level uncertainty metric derived from multiple rollouts—which also correlates with hallucination. This provides indirect support that energy‑like signals are predictive, though the exact \( \Delta E \) definition differs.
- **Break condition:** The correlation is empirical and may weaken for highly calibrated models (e.g., heavily RLHF‑tuned). Misidentification of the answer span dilutes the signal; Section 5.2 notes that using the exact answer tokens yields a ~24% AuROC boost for \( \Delta E \).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Min‑pooling the per‑token \( \Delta E \) values over the exact answer span yields the most robust and generalizable hallucination detector across tasks and model families.
- **Mechanism:** The answer span may contain multiple tokens; pooling combines their \( \Delta E \) scores. Min‑pooling selects the smallest \( \Delta E \) within the span. Because correct answers tend to have uniformly low \( \Delta E \), the minimum is also low. For incorrect answers, the answer tokens typically exhibit elevated \( \Delta E \) across the board, so even the minimum remains relatively high. This yields a clean separation. Min‑pooling outperforms max‑pooling (which may over‑react to a single noisy token) and mean‑pooling (which averages with non‑answer tokens that may have naturally high \( \Delta E \)).
- **Core assumption:** The strongest evidence for truthfulness is the presence of at least one answer token with very low \( \Delta E \); conversely, if all answer tokens have elevated \( \Delta E \), the answer is likely fabricated.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “min‑pooled ΔE achieves an average AuROC of 73.16%.”
  - [section] Table 1 consistently shows Min‑pooling \( \Delta E \) surpassing Max‑pooling (e.g., LLaMA‑Instruct: 73.16% vs 48.87%). Section D.3.2 includes an ablation confirming min is best.
  - [corpus] No direct evidence; this pooling strategy is an operational insight validated solely within the paper.
- **Break condition:** If correct answers occasionally contain answer tokens with high \( \Delta E \) (e.g., due to lexical rarity or context), min‑pooling could produce false negatives. Also, the approach assumes answer spans are accurately identified and contiguous; mis‑localization can cause the min to fall on a non‑answer token with spuriously low \( \Delta E \), reducing detection power.

## Foundational Learning

**Concept: Autoregressive Decoding & Logits**  
- *Why needed:* The method directly reads the logits produced during token prediction. Understanding the relationship between logits, softmax probabilities, and how they are generated step‑by‑step is essential to compute \( E_\ell \) and \( E_m \).  
- *Quick check question:* Given an LLM’s next‑token logits vector \( \mathbf{l} \), how would you compute the probability of the sampled token? How does log‑sum‑exp relate to the marginal energy \( E_m \)?

**Concept: Energy‑Based Models (EBMs)**  
- *Why needed:* The reinterpretation hinges on treating the softmax as an EBM where probability is proportional to \( \exp(-\text{energy}) \). This conceptual shift justifies defining \( E_\ell \) and \( E_m \) and their expected equality.  
- *Quick check question:* In an EBM, what is the role of the partition function \( Z \)? How does a lower energy value affect the probability of a configuration?

**Concept: Sequence Probability Chain Rule**  
- *Why needed:* The cancellation argument that \( E_\ell(x_{i:1}) \) should equal \( E_m(x_{i-1:1}) \) derives from the chain rule: \( p(x_{i:1}) = p(x_i \mid x_{i-1:1})\,p(x_{i-1:1}) \). Without this, the “spill” definition would not follow.  
- *Quick check question:* Write the joint probability \( p(x_2, x_1, x_0) \) using the chain rule. Identify where the marginal \( p(x_1, x_0) \) appears and how it relates to \( p(x_2 \mid x_1, x_0) \).

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:** `Prompt -> Model Generation (logits) -> Answer Span Extraction -> ΔE Computation (Eℓ, Em) -> Min‑Pooling -> Classification`

**Critical path:**  
1. Run full autoregressive generation while storing logits at each step.  
2. Localize the exact answer tokens in the generated string, obtaining token index range \([u, w]\).  
3. For each token \(i\) in \([u, w]\), compute \(\Delta E_i\) using logits from step \(i\) (for \(E_\ell\)) and step \(i+1\) (for \(E_m\)).  
4. Take the minimum of the \(\Delta E_i\) values to obtain the final score \(S\).  
5. Compare \(S\) against a threshold (determined on validation data) to decide if the response is hallucinated.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Pooling strategy*: Min‑pooling is empirically best but assumes correct answers have uniformly low \(\Delta E\) across answer tokens; max‑pooling is sensitive to outliers, mean‑pooling dilutes with non‑answer tokens.  
- *Answer localization*: Using an instruction‑tuned LLM extractor is flexible for open‑ended tasks but adds latency and may fail; heuristic matching is faster but limited to closed‑form answers.  
- *Temperature*: Sampling temperature flattens distributions and can push \(\Delta E\) toward a constant; the paper uses default settings and does not explore temperature scaling.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Elevated \(\Delta E\) on punctuation, sentence‑initial tokens, or other non‑semantic tokens even for correct answers, necessitating answer‑span restriction.  
- Performance collapses to near chance on bias/subjective datasets (e.g., Winobias, IMDB).  
- Probing classifiers fail to generalize across datasets, while \(\Delta E\) remains robust.  
- Base (non‑instruct) models show a smaller separation; instruction tuning amplifies the signal.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Synthetic arithmetic replication:** Generate multi‑digit sums, introduce offsets of varying magnitude (Easy/Medium/Hard). Compute \(\Delta E\) histograms and ROCs to confirm separation.  
2. **Cross‑dataset generalization:** Train probing classifiers separately on each benchmark and evaluate on others; compare their off‑diagonal collapse with the single‑run min‑pooled \(\Delta E\).  
3. **Pooling ablation:** On TriviaQA, compare min, max, mean, and last‑token pooling for \(\Delta E\) and marginal energy; also test using the full response tokens vs. only exact answer tokens to quantify the localisation gain.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1:** How does exact answer token extraction impact robustness and generalizability? The paper shows a ~24% AuROC improvement when using exact answer tokens, but extraction success varies (<95% on some datasets). The sensitivity to extraction errors and the feasibility of task‑agnostic extraction remain unknown. Resolving this requires systematic evaluation under controlled error rates and alternative extraction methods.

**Open Question 2:** What is the theoretical relationship between spilled energy and model calibration? \(\Delta E\) is derived from a consistency condition of the chain rule, but why it correlates with hallucinations is not explained. It is unclear whether \(\Delta E\) measures uncertainty, confidence, or something distinct. A formal analysis linking \(\Delta E\) to calibration metrics or controlled experiments decoupling uncertainty would clarify its nature.

**Open Question 3:** Why does min‑pooling consistently outperform other strategies? Min‑pooling yields the best average AuROC, but the underlying reason is not discussed. Potential explanations include that the minimum \(\Delta E\) in a correct answer span is very low, or that min‑pooling avoids dilution by non‑answer tokens. Understanding this could lead to principled aggregation methods.

**Open Question 4:** Can spilled energy detect other failure modes beyond factual hallucinations? The method shows promise on reasoning (Math) and bias benchmarks, but its scope is unclear. Different error types (e.g., subtle biases, unsupported claims) might produce distinct \(\Delta E\) patterns. Testing on diverse error‑annotated datasets would determine generalizability.

**Open Question 5:** What is the computational cost and can it be approximated efficiently? Computing the log‑sum‑exp over the full vocabulary at each step adds overhead, especially for large models. The paper does not report runtime or explore approximations. Benchmarking costs and developing approximate variants (e.g., top‑k) are needed for practical deployment.

## Limitations
- **Answer extraction dependency:** The method requires accurately identifying the exact answer token span; failures in open‑ended tasks introduce noise and degrade detection.
- **Architectural scope:** The EBM reinterpretation assumes autoregressive softmax classifiers; its applicability to encoder‑decoder or non‑autoregressive models is untested.
- **Training effects:** The signal may diminish for heavily RLHF‑tuned models or if models are explicitly trained to enforce \(E_\ell = E_m\), as the spill would collapse.

## Confidence
- **Claim 1 (EBM reinterpretation and ΔE definition):** High confidence. The derivation follows directly from the chain rule and softmax properties; the break condition is well‑specified.
- **Claim 2 (ΔE correlates with hallucination):** Medium confidence. Empirical results are strong but depend on answer localization and may not generalize to all error types.
- **Claim 3 (min‑pooling robustness):** Medium confidence. Ablations show consistent advantage, but the assumption that correct answers have uniformly low \(\Delta E\) might not always hold.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross‑architecture test:** Apply \(\Delta E\) to encoder‑decoder models (e.g., T5, BART) to verify if the energy spill pattern exists and remains predictive.
2. **RLHF stress test:** Evaluate on state‑of‑the‑art RLHF‑tuned models (e.g., GPT‑4) to determine if the signal persists or collapses under strong alignment.
3. **Open‑ended extraction robustness:** Use an LLM‑based answer extractor on a diverse open‑ended QA dataset (e.g., ELI5) to measure sensitivity to localization errors.
