# Learning to Negotiate: Multi-Agent Deliberation for Collective Value Alignment in LLMs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.10476  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10476  
- **Reference count:** 0  
- **Primary result:** A multi-agent negotiation framework using self-play and RLAIF aligns LLMs to collective agency while improving conflict-resolution performance, reducing negotiation rounds, and preserving general language capabilities.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the challenge of aligning large language models (LLMs) for multi-stakeholder scenarios with conflicting values, where single-agent methods like RLHF or RLAIF are insufficient. The authors introduce a multi-agent negotiation framework where two self-play instances of the same LLM, assigned opposing personas, engage in structured turn-based dialogue to synthesize mutually beneficial solutions. Training leverages Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback (RLAIF) with Group-Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), applying gradients directly to dialogue tokens based on a Collective Agency (CA) reward model that scores final completions and imposes zero reward for failed negotiations. Evaluations on Qwen3-14B-Instruct show the aligned model achieves CA alignment comparable to a single-agent baseline (~62% win rate vs base) while substantially improving conflict-resolution performance (72.8% win rate vs single-agent), reducing average negotiation rounds by 20–25%, and preserving general language abilities on IFEval, GPQA, and AIME. Sampling inference yields larger gains, indicating robust performance across diverse negotiation outcomes.

## Method Summary
The method begins with a dataset of synthetic moral dilemmas (1,100) and adversarial persona pairs (25 pairs) generated automatically. During training, for each prompt and persona pair, a trainable agent (Agent 1) negotiates against a frozen copy of the same policy (Agent 2) in a turn-based dialogue conditioned on the last two turns. After each turn, a lightweight agreement judge (LLM) checks for mutual consensus; negotiation ends on agreement or a turn limit. If agreement is reached, Agent 1 produces a final summary solution. A heavyweight CA reward judge (LLM) scores this summary on a detailed rubric (0–5); failed negotiations receive a reward of 0 regardless of content. Multiple trajectories are sampled per prompt, group-relative advantages are computed via GRPO, and the policy is updated using a loss applied only to the dialogue tokens. This directly optimizes negotiation dynamics toward high‑CA, convergent solutions. The training loop iterates over the synthetic dataset, alternating between sampling, scoring, and policy updates.

## Key Results
- CA alignment: ~62% win rate versus base model on conflict-resolution tasks (sampling).  
- Conflict-resolution improvement: 72.8% win rate versus single‑agent baseline (sampling).  
- Efficiency & generalization: reduces average negotiation rounds by 20–25% and maintains performance on IFEval, GPQA, and AIME benchmarks, with larger gains under sampling indicating robustness.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Structured turn-based negotiation between agents with opposing personas improves conflict-resolution capability by forcing the policy to reconcile competing value perspectives during interaction.
- **Mechanism:** The policy instantiated as two self‑play agents (Agent 1 trainable, Agent 2 frozen) each receive a conflicting persona from a predefined library. They engage in multi‑turn dialogue where each utterance must propose a solution respecting (i) the Collective Agency (CA) objective, (ii) their own persona, and (iii) the inferred opposing persona’s objective. Negotiation terminates upon mutual agreement or a turn limit.
- **Core assumption:** Exposure to and practice in reconciling fixed, opposing value directives during dialogue will improve the policy’s ability to generate convergent, mutually beneficial solutions in value‑conflict scenarios.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “two self-play instances of the same LLM, assigned opposing personas, engage in structured turn-based dialogue to synthesize mutually beneficial solutions.”; [section 3.1] persona pairs (e.g., “data vs. intuition”) and negotiation phase structure; [corpus] neighboring works on multi‑agent deliberation support the paradigm.
- **Break condition:** If the agreement rate does not improve or if negotiation rounds increase without quality gains (e.g., more verbose but non‑convergent dialogues), the mechanism may be failing to teach synthesis.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Applying reinforcement learning gradients directly to the dialogue tokens (using GRPO), rather than only the final completion, improves negotiation dynamics by shaping the interactive process that leads to a high‑CA outcome.
- **Mechanism:** After each negotiation, a final completion is generated and scored by the CA reward model. The scalar reward is assigned to the entire trajectory. Using GRPO, group‑relative advantages are computed across multiple sampled trajectories for the same prompt/persona pair. Critically, the loss is computed over the dialogue tokens, not just the final completion tokens. This trains the policy to generate utterances that are more likely to lead to high‑reward outcomes.
- **Core assumption:** The quality of the final completion is sufficiently determined by the quality of the negotiation dialogue that preceded it, such that optimizing the dialogue tokens improves the probability of producing high‑CA, convergent solutions.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 3.2.2] “Importantly, gradients are computed from the dialogue tokens rather than the final completion… This design directly trains the model to improve its negotiation dynamics rather than final completion generation capability.”; [section 4.3.1] training dynamics show increasing CA scores and decreasing rounds to agreement, suggesting improved dialogue quality.
- **Break condition:** If CA scores improve but negotiation agreement rates or rounds to agreement do not improve, the gradient signal may be misattributed or the final completion may not be a reliable proxy for dialogue quality.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Using an outcome‑based CA reward model combined with a zero reward for failed negotiations provides an effective training signal that aligns the policy to CA *and* incentivizes successful convergence, even within a dynamic alignment objective.
- **Mechanism:** A frozen external LLM judges the final completion using a detailed CA rubric (0–5). Crucially, if the negotiation failed to reach agreement within the turn limit, the reward is forced to zero, regardless of the final completion’s content. These zero‑reward trajectories receive negative group‑relative advantages, pushing the policy away from non‑convergent dialogue behaviors.
- **Core assumption:** The CA rubric applied to the final completion is a valid proxy for the quality of the entire negotiation process. Furthermore, explicitly punishing failed negotiations is necessary because a high‑CA final completion could be produced even after a non‑convergent or acrimonious dialogue, which is undesirable.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 3.2.2] “For negotiations that fail to reach agreement… we assign a reward of rᵢ = 0. This design provides explicit negative learning signals for unsuccessful negotiation trajectories...” ; [section 4.3.1] simultaneous improvement in CA score and agreement rate (~91% to ~97%) with fewer rounds, suggesting the dual signal is effective.
- **Break condition:** If agreement rate plateaus or CA scores diverge (e.g., high CA but low agreement), the zero‑reward signal may be insufficiently weighted or the agreement judge may be unreliable.

## Foundational Learning
- **Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback (RLAIF)**  
  *Why needed here:* The framework uses RLAIF (no human feedback) with an external LLM as the reward model for both the CA score and the agreement check, enabling scalable, automated training.  
  *Quick check question:* How does using an LLM as a reward model introduce different biases or failure modes compared to a human‑derived reward model?

- **Group‑Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**  
  *Why needed here:* GRPO normalizes advantages within a group of sampled trajectories for the same prompt/persona pair, which is suited to the negotiation setting where absolute reward scale is less meaningful than relative quality within a context.  
  *Quick check question:* Why is group‑relative normalization preferred over using absolute reward values for this task, and what could go wrong if the group distribution is skewed?

- **Collective Agency (CA) as a Dynamic Alignment Objective**  
  *Why needed here:* CA is the target alignment objective (with its four aspects: Knowledge, Benevolence, Power, Vitality). The entire training loop is designed to improve policy performance on this open‑ended, non‑static goal.  
  *Quick check question:* How does training for CA in a multi‑agent negotiation differ conceptually from training for CA in a single‑agent “open‑ended question” setting?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
1. **Prompt & Persona Generator:** Synthesizes moral‑dilemma prompts (1,100) and adversarial persona pairs (25 pairs) using Gemini‑3‑Pro.  
2. **Negotiation Engine:** Implements the `Negotiate` function. Instantiates two agents (πθ trainable, πθ frozen) from the same policy, assigns opposing personas, and manages the turn‑based dialogue loop with truncated context (last 2 turns).  
3. **Agreement Judge (Lightweight):** An external LLM (GPT‑4o‑mini) called after each turn to assess `IsAgree(D_t)`. Terminates negotiation on “YES” or turn‑limit.  
4. **Final Completion Generator:** Agent 1 generates a final summary `y` conditioned on the full dialogue and personas.  
5. **CA Reward Judge (Heavyweight):** An external LLM scores `y` on the 0–5 CA rubric. Assigns `r=0` if negotiation failed.  
6. **GRPO Trainer:** Samples `G` trajectories per prompt, computes group‑relative advantages `Â_i`, and updates πθ via the token‑normalized loss (Eq. 1) over dialogue tokens only. KL penalty (`β`) is set to 0.

**Critical path:**  
Dataset Generation → For each training step: Sample (prompt, persona pair) → Generate `G` negotiation dialogues (with agreement check) → Generate final completions for successful negotiations → Score with CA Judge (0 for failures) → Compute advantages → Update policy on dialogue tokens.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Synthetic Data vs. Diversity:* Using generated dilemmas and personas scales training but may lack the complexity and true adversarial nature of real‑world stakeholder conflicts.  
- *Self‑Play vs. Heterogeneous Opponents:* Using a frozen copy of the same policy (self‑play) is simple and stable but limits exposure to diverse negotiation strategies compared to training against independently trained or human opponents.  
- *Outcome Reward vs. Process Reward:* Rewarding only the final completion with a zero for failure is computationally simple but provides no fine‑grained feedback on specific negotiation moves (e.g., a good concession vs. a bad one).  
- *Truncated Context vs. Full History:* Limiting conditioning to the last 2 turns reduces memory but may lose important long‑range conversational dependencies.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Stagnant agreement rate (<90%) or increasing rounds to agreement during training.  
- Divergence between CA score and conflict‑resolution win rate.  
- Sharp drop in general NLP benchmark scores (IFEval, GPQA, AIME).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. *Component Validation – Agreement Judge:* Run the trained model on a small set of prompts and manually verify the `IsAgree` judgments. Ensure the judge’s “YES” correlates with human perception of convergence.  
2. *Ablation – Token‑Level Gradients:* Train a baseline where gradients are applied only to the final completion tokens (not dialogue tokens). Compare its negotiation agreement rate and rounds‑to‑agreement against the main method. Expect worse efficiency.  
3. *Ablation – Zero Reward for Failure:* Train a variant where failed negotiations receive the *group‑mean reward* instead of zero. Compare agreement rates and final CA scores. Expect lower incentive to converge.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What is the individual contribution of each framework component (adversarial persona pairs, dilemma prompts, structured turn‑based dialogue, GRPO optimization) to the improvements in Collective Agency alignment and conflict‑resolution performance?  
- **Basis in paper:** Section 7 (Limitations): “Our current experiments do not isolate the contribution of individual design components in the framework.”  
- **Why unresolved:** Without ablation studies, it is unclear whether observed gains primarily arise from negotiation dynamics, exposure to value‑conflict scenarios, trajectory diversity from relative RL, or their combination.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled ablation experiments where each component (e.g., persona pairs, negotiation structure, GRPO) is systematically removed or replaced while measuring CA scores, agreement rates, and conflict‑resolution win rates on held‑out tasks.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Does the multi‑agent negotiation framework extend to multi‑party (N > 2) settings with heterogeneous agent personas, and how do credit assignment and coalition formation affect alignment and convergence?  
- **Basis in paper:** Section 7 (Negotiation Setting): “real‑world decision processes often involve more than two stakeholders… Extending the framework to multi‑party (N > 2) interactions would introduce additional complexities such as coalition formation, asymmetric information, and multi‑agent credit assignment.”  
- **Why unresolved:** The current two‑agent self‑play may not capture the complexities of real‑world multi‑stakeholder negotiations, where group dynamics and asymmetric power/information can alter outcomes.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implementing N‑agent negotiations (e.g., three or more agents with diverse, conflicting personas), measuring convergence rates, solution quality (via CA and conflict‑resolution metrics), and analyzing credit allocation mechanisms (e.g., Shapley values) in the group‑relative RL framework.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can fine‑grained, per‑turn or trajectory‑based reward signals (e.g., intrinsic incentives for integrative proposals, Shapley credit assignment) improve sample efficiency and targeted learning of negotiation strategies compared to the outcome‑based final‑completion CA score?  
- **Basis in paper:** Section 7 (Training Signal Design): “the reward signal is outcome‑based… More fine‑grained reward decomposition—such as per‑turn intrinsic signals or Shapley‑based credit allocation—could improve sample efficiency and enable more targeted learning of negotiation strategies.”  
- **Why unresolved:** The current coarse reward assigns a single score to the final completion, limiting the ability to attribute credit to specific negotiation moves and potentially slowing learning of effective intermediate tactics.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Designing and integrating per‑turn rewards (e.g., for acknowledging opponent’s points, proposing trade‑offs) or credit allocation methods (e.g., Shapley values over dialogue turns), then comparing sample efficiency, negotiation strategy diversity, and final outcome quality against the baseline outcome‑only reward.

## Limitations
- LLM‑based judges for reward and agreement may not align with human judgment; their biases are unexamined.  
- Synthetic dilemmas and persona pairs, though diverse, may not generalize to real‑world conflicts.  
- Self‑play with a frozen copy limits exposure to varied negotiation strategies.

## Confidence
- Multi‑agent negotiation improves conflict‑resolution performance: **Medium**  
- General language capabilities are preserved: **High**  
- The specific mechanisms (dialogue‑token gradients, zero‑reward failure signal) are necessary and effective: **Medium**

## Next Checks
- **Human‑Judge Concordance:** Recruit human evaluators to judge a sample of negotiation dialogues for convergence and solution quality; correlate with LLM judge decisions.  
- **Mechanism Ablations:** Train model variants without dialogue‑token gradients (only final completion) and without zero‑reward (using group mean) to isolate their contributions.  
- **Real‑World Generalization:** Evaluate on a new benchmark of authentic multi‑stakeholder conflicts (e.g., environmental policy disputes) to test robustness beyond synthetic dilemmas.
