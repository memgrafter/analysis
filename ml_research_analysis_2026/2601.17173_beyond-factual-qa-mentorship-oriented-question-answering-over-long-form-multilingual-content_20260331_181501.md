# Beyond Factual QA: Mentorship-Oriented Question Answering over Long-Form Multilingual Content
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.17173
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17173
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A Multi-Agent pipeline for generating mentorship-oriented QA pairs consistently outperforms simpler architectures across four languages, achieving a mean human-rated mentorship score of 4.40/5.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a new problem: generating mentorship-oriented question-answer pairs from long-form, multilingual video content. Unlike standard factual QA, mentorship QA aims to provide guidance, reflection, and learning value. To study this, the authors create the MentorQA dataset—8,990 QA pairs from 180 hours of video across English, Chinese, Hindi, and Romanian—and propose new evaluation dimensions (Clarity, Alignment, Mentorship Value). They systematically compare four architectures (Single-Agent, Dual-Agent, RAG, Multi-Agent) using a fixed base model. The Multi-Agent system, which uses adaptive question allocation based on a segment-level mentorship strength score and a two-step question filtering process (Scorer + Justifier), achieves the highest quality. Results show particularly strong improvements for complex topics and lower-resource languages like Hindi. The work establishes mentorship QA as a distinct research challenge and provides a multilingual benchmark.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates four LLM-based architectures for generating QA pairs from video transcripts. **Inputs** are 120 long-form videos (180 hours) spanning six mentorship topics and four languages. Transcripts are created with Whisper. **Task:** For a given transcript, generate ~20 non-extractive QA pairs where questions seek guidance/reflection and answers are grounded, clear, and mentorship-oriented. **Metrics:** Human evaluation on 1–5 Likert scales for Fluency, Clarity, QA Alignment, Question Mentorship, and Answer Mentorship (12 annotators, 3 per language). Automatic evaluation uses nine multilingual LLM judges. **Architectures:** 1) Single-Agent (full transcript in one prompt), 2) Dual-Agent (topic-based chunking, then QA per segment), 3) RAG (retrieve relevant chunks for answering), 4) Multi-Agent (chunking, brainstorming, scoring, adaptive quota allocation, answering). All use Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct-1M as the base model.

## Key Results
- The **Multi-Agent pipeline** achieves the highest mentorship quality, with a mean score of 4.40 on a 1–5 Likert scale, significantly outperforming other architectures.
- Gains are **especially strong for complex topics** and for the **lower-resource language (Hindi)**, narrowing the performance gap seen with simpler models.
- Automated LLM-based evaluation shows **variable alignment with human judgments**; Qwen and Phi-4 judges reach the highest agreement (up to 0.72), but correlation is lower for some metrics and languages.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Adaptive Allocation via Segment Strength Scoring
- **Claim:** Allocating more final questions to transcript segments with high, consistent mentorship potential improves overall QA quality compared to uniform allocation.
- **Mechanism:** The system computes a segment strength score `S(i) = µ(q) − σ(q)` from the mentorship scores of candidate questions `q` brainstormed for that segment. A final quota per segment `quota(i) = round(S(i)/∑S(j) × T)` is then set, focusing generation effort on segments where high-scoring questions are plentiful and consistent (low variance). This assumes mentorship value is unevenly distributed in long-form content.
- **Evidence:** [section 4.4] Details the scoring and quota formula. [abstract] Notes Multi-Agent’s strong gains for complex topics and Hindi. [section 4.4, 6] Multi-Agent outperforms Dual-Agent (which uses uniform per-segment generation), supporting the allocation’s benefit.
- **Break condition:** Fails if mentorship value is uniformly high or low across all segments, making `S(i)` uninformative. Also fails if the Scorer Agent cannot reliably distinguish mentorship quality.

### Mechanism 2: Explicit Question Scoring and Justification
- **Claim:** A dedicated Scorer Agent rating questions 1–10, followed by a Justifier Agent providing selection reasons, reduces noise in question selection and increases transparency, leading to higher-value final QA pairs.
- **Mechanism:** After brainstorming, each candidate question receives an integer mentorship score. The Justifier then generates a short explanation for why a question is "Selected" or "Rejected." This two-step filtering aims to ensure only high-value, non-redundant questions are kept.
- **Evidence:** [section 4.4] Describes the Scorer and Justifier roles. [section 6] Multi-Agent achieves strongest performance on Mentorship and QA-Alignment metrics.
- **Break condition:** Fails if the Scorer’s ratings are inconsistent or misaligned with true mentorship value (as suggested by moderate human agreement on metrics). Justifications may become templated without nuanced understanding.

### Mechanism 3: Topic-Aware Segmentation as Foundation
- **Claim:** Partitioning the transcript into coherent, topic-based segments ensures balanced coverage of distinct conversational themes, preventing over-concentration in any one part of the video.
- **Mechanism:** A Chunking Agent creates segments with dynamic lengths, adhering to coherence and coverage rules. This structure is used by subsequent agents (brainstorming, allocation) to operate on thematic units rather than arbitrary windows.
- **Evidence:** [section 4.2] Dual-Agent’s Chunking Agent is described. [section 4.4] Multi-Agent reuses this same Chunking Agent, indicating its foundational role. [section 6] Dual-Agent outperforms Single-Agent, supporting segmentation’s benefit for coverage.
- **Break condition:** Fails if segmentation produces incoherent, fragmented, or incomplete segments, propagating errors downstream.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Mentorship-focused evaluation dimensions (Clarity, Alignment, Mentorship Value)
  - **Why needed:** Standard QA metrics (factual accuracy) are insufficient. These new dimensions assess whether responses provide actionable guidance, reflection, and learning value.
  - **Quick check:** What is the key difference between "QA Alignment" (question-answer coherence) and "Answer Mentorship" (guidance/reflection value in the response)?
- **Concept:** Mentorship value is unevenly distributed in long-form conversations
  - **Why needed:** This insight justifies adaptive allocation; uniform generation dilutes quality by including many low-value segments.
  - **Quick check:** Why might generating a fixed number of QA pairs per equal-length transcript segment harm overall mentorship quality?
- **Concept:** Topic-aware segmentation as a prerequisite for balanced QA generation
  - **Why needed:** Raw transcript order doesn’t reflect thematic importance; segmentation allows sampling from distinct conversational themes.
  - **Quick check:** How does topic-based segmentation differ from sliding-window chunking for QA generation over long content?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Transcript -> Chunking Agent -> Per-segment Question Brainstorming -> Scorer Agent (rate 1–10) -> Justifier Agent (explain) -> Quota Calculator (using `S(i)=µ−σ`) -> Top-K Selection per Segment -> Answering Agent -> Final QA Pairs
- **Critical path:** The pipeline is sequential with branching: chunking first; then for each segment, brainstorm all questions, score and justify each, compute segment strength `S(i)`, determine quota, select top questions, and generate answers.
- **Design tradeoffs:** 
  - *Quality vs. Cost:* More LLM calls (brainstorming, scoring, justifying per question) versus single-pass generation.
  - *Control vs. Flexibility:* Explicit scoring/allocation provides control but may overfit to the Scorer’s biases.
  - *Generality vs. Language Performance:* Fixed base model (Qwen2.5) has known weaknesses (e.g., Hindi tokenization), bounding architectural gains.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Low variance in segment strength `S(i)` → quotas become nearly uniform → Multi-Agent degrades to Dual-Agent.
  - Poor Chunking output (incoherent segments) → downstream agents operate on wrong thematic units → coverage/relevance drop.
  - Scorer Agent rates trivial questions highly → final QA lacks depth despite high scores.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Ablate the adaptive allocation: In Multi-Agent, replace `S(i)`-based quotas with uniform quotas. Compare mentorship scores; expect a drop, especially on complex topics.
  2. Probe segmentation quality: Manually annotate segment coherence for a sample. Correlate segment-level `S(i)` with human-rated mentorship value; expect strong positive correlation.
  3. Language stress test: Evaluate Multi-Agent vs. Single-Agent on Hindi using a model with better Devanagari support (e.g., Hindi-fine-tuned LLM). Check if Multi-Agent’s relative advantage persists.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- What specific components within the Multi-Agent pipeline are most responsible for its superior mentorship quality? The paper lacks an ablation isolating mechanisms like scoring, allocation, or justification.
- How can automated evaluation for mentorship QA be made more reliable and culturally robust, especially for lower-resource languages? The authors note variable LLM-human alignment but do not propose remedies.
- Does mentorship-focused QA lead to measurable improvements in downstream learning outcomes (e.g., comprehension, reflection) compared to factual QA? Evaluation stops at response quality, not learner impact.
- What are the computational efficiency versus quality trade-offs of Multi-Agent pipelines? The paper acknowledges higher cost but provides no detailed profiling.
- How does subjectivity in mentorship evaluation and cultural variation affect training objectives and metrics? The work treats subjectivity as noise, not a design factor.

## Limitations
- Moderate human agreement on mentorship metrics suggests inherent subjectivity, especially across cultures.
- Hindi gains may partially reflect base model (Qwen2.5) tokenization issues rather than pure architectural benefit.
- Small human evaluation set (12 annotators, 3 per language) limits statistical power and cross-cultural generalizability.
- Key LLM judges used for automatic evaluation may be inaccessible or deprecated, hindering reproduction.
- The core assumption that mentorship value is unevenly distributed may not hold for all content types.

## Confidence
- **Adaptive allocation improves quality**: **Medium** (supported by Multi-Agent vs. Dual-Agent difference, but no direct ablation).
- **Scoring + justification improves question selection**: **Medium** (strong mentorship scores, but Scorer validity undermined by moderate metric agreement).
- **Topic segmentation is foundational**: **Medium** (Dual-Agent beats Single-Agent, but no comparison to alternative segmentation).
- **Multi-Agent overall superiority**: **Medium** (consistent gains shown, but limited to one dataset, one base model, small human sample).
- **LLM judges can approximate human mentorship evaluation**: **Low** (alignment is variable and language-dependent).

## Next Checks
1. **Ablation of adaptive allocation:** Run Multi-Agent with uniform quotas instead of `S(i)`-based quotas. Measure delta in mentorship scores, particularly on complex topics.
2. **Scale human evaluation:** Recruit 50+ annotators per language to re-rate a stratified subset. Validate LLM-judge correlations and reduce noise, especially for Hindi.
3. **Cross-base-model test:** Replace Qwen2.5 with a model having stronger multilingual support (e.g., mT5, Hindi-fine-tuned LLM). Re-run architecture comparison to check if Multi-Agent’s advantage persists beyond base-model quirks.
