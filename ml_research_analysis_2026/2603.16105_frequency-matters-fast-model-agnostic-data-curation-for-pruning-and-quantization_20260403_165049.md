# Frequency Matters: Fast Model-Agnostic Data Curation for Pruning and Quantization

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.16105
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16105
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: ZipCal matches state-of-the-art perplexity-based methods in downstream performance with <0.5% mean accuracy difference while being ~240× faster; multi-domain variant improves average scores by >2% over single-domain approaches.

## Executive Summary
ZipCal introduces a model-agnostic approach to selecting calibration data for post‑training compression of large language models. By maximizing lexical diversity through a Zipfian tail capture strategy, it produces small calibration sets that yield robust statistics for pruning and quantization. The method achieves performance on par with expensive model‑dependent techniques (e.g., COLA) while reducing curation time by approximately 240× due to its linear‑time greedy algorithm. A hierarchical multi‑domain extension further enhances inter‑task generalization, boosting average scores by over 2%. Experiments across multiple compression methods (Wanda, 2SSP, GPTQ, AWQ) confirm ZipCal’s effectiveness and scalability.

## Method Summary
Given a large text corpus, ZipCal first tokenizes each sample and applies sanitization (lowercasing, removal of special tokens) to build a set of unique tokens per sample. The core single‑domain algorithm then greedily selects k samples to maximize marginal vocabulary gain—i.e., the number of previously unseen tokens added to the covered set. This prioritizes rare tokens from the Zipfian tail, ensuring the calibration set approximates full‑dataset lexical diversity. For multi‑domain scenarios, ZipCal runs the single‑domain selector on each domain to obtain candidate pools of size k, then pools these and applies a greedy k‑centers algorithm on lightweight sample embeddings to maximize cross‑domain semantic spread. The resulting indices are fed to standard compression pipelines (e.g., Wanda, GPTQ) to collect activation statistics and apply the compression. The entire curation runs in O(nk) time, orders of magnitude faster than model‑dependent baselines.

## Key Results
- ZipCal matches COLA’s downstream accuracy with <0.5% mean difference while being ~240× faster.
- Multi‑domain ZipCal improves average task scores by >2% compared to single‑domain baselines.
- ZipCal demonstrates consistent gains across pruning (Wanda, 2SSP) and quantization (GPTQ, AWQ) with runtimes in seconds versus hours for baselines.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Maximizing lexical diversity via Zipfian tail capture yields calibration data that better represents the full dataset’s vocabulary manifold, leading to more robust compression statistics.
- **Mechanism**: The algorithm greedily selects samples that introduce the most new sanitized tokens, iteratively expanding the covered vocabulary. This explicitly forces coverage of low‑frequency words that might be omitted by random sampling.
- **Core assumption**: Rare tokens and diverse semantic contexts are critical for triggering representative activation outliers in LLMs during compression; covering them yields statistics closer to the full data distribution.
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] “captures the sparse tail of the Zipfian distribution”; [section 3.1] Equation (1) and Algorithm 1 define marginal vocabulary gain; coverage plots in Fig. 1 show longer tail vs. random.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Model‑agnostic selection using token frequency eliminates the computational cost of model‑dependent metrics, enabling scalable curation.
- **Mechanism**: ZipCal operates solely on tokenized text, computing vocabulary sets and performing greedy selection. Complexity is O(nk), in contrast to model‑dependent methods that require full forward passes (quadratic or higher).
- **Core assumption**: Lexical diversity is a stable, model‑independent proxy for calibration utility; high‑diversity subsets provide sufficient statistics without model‑specific signals.
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] “~240× faster due to its tractable linear complexity”; [section 3.1] Lemma 3.1 proves O(nk) time; [section 4.2] runtime comparisons show orders‑of‑magnitude speedups.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Hierarchical multi‑domain sampling (per‑domain ZipCal + k‑centers on embeddings) produces a unified calibration set that maintains representativeness across heterogeneous sources, improving inter‑task generalization.
- **Mechanism**: Stage 1 extracts lexically diverse subsets from each domain. Stage 2 pools candidates and uses greedy k‑centers on embeddings to maximize semantic diversity, preventing any single domain from dominating.
- **Core assumption**: A single calibration set for multi‑domain deployment must balance lexical and semantic diversity across domains; naive concatenation would bias toward larger or linguistically denser domains.
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] “Multi‑Domain variant improves average scores by over 2% compared to single‑domain approaches”; [section 3.2] describes the two‑stage hierarchical process; [section 4.2] Table 4 shows multi‑domain ZipCal outperforming single‑domain COLA and ZipCal on average.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
- **Sanitization module**: Tokenizes raw text, lowercases, strips special tokens (e.g., EOS), and builds per‑sample vocabulary sets V_s.
- **Single‑Domain ZipCal**: Greedy selector. Starts with S = ∅ and V_covered = ∅. Repeats k times: for each candidate s ∉ S, compute gain = |V_s \ V_covered|; pick s* with max gain (break ties by |V_s|); add s* to S, update V_covered with V_s*. Outputs S.
- **Multi‑Domain extension**: Runs Single‑Domain ZipCal on each domain to get local pools P_i (size k). Pools all candidates. Computes embeddings (e.g., using a lightweight sentence transformer). Applies greedy k‑centers on the pooled set to select k final samples maximizing embedding diversity. Outputs final indices.
- **Integration layer**: Selected sample indices are passed to downstream compression toolkits (e.g., Wanda, GPTQ), which run forward passes to collect activation statistics and apply the compression algorithm.

### Critical Path
- **Single‑domain**: Dataset tokenization → vocabulary precomputation → k iterations of max‑gain selection → output set.
- **Multi‑domain**: Parallel single‑domain runs on each domain → pooling → embedding computation → greedy k‑centers → final set.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Speed vs. specificity**: ZipCal gives up model‑specific activation signals for massive speed gains; may underperform when model dynamics are not well‑captured by lexical diversity.
- **Multi‑domain balancing**: The hierarchical approach prevents domain domination with negligible overhead, but performance depends on the choice of embedding model.
- **Context‑length sensitivity**: Some compression methods (e.g., 2SSP) are highly sensitive to context window; ZipCal does not directly optimize context length, leaving it as a hyperparameter.

### Failure Signatures
- **Domain mismatch**: If calibration domains are disjoint from target tasks, performance degrades measurably (though gracefully).
- **Non‑textual data**: The token‑level diversity assumption may break for multimodal or code data; the method would need adaptation.
- **Extreme sparsity**: At very small k (e.g., 16), ZipCal may still miss domain‑specific jargon; vocabulary coverage gap remains.

1. **Baseline validation**: Apply ZipCal and uniform random sampling to Wanda pruning (25% sparsity) on Llama‑3.1‑8B; compare downstream accuracy on reasoning tasks (ANLI, GSM8K). Expect consistent wins for ZipCal per Table 2.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Generalization to morphologically rich and non‑alphabetic languages**: The reliance on English data and simple sanitization (lowercasing, subword stripping) may not transfer to languages with complex morphology or logographic scripts; the Zipfian assumption might hold but the sanitization tuning is untested.
- **Performance on Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) models**: MoE routing may require a different data balance to calibrate all experts; lexical diversity might not uniformly cover expert‑specific activation patterns.
- **Extension to multimodal LLMs**: For models with visual, audio, or video tokens, a multimodal analogue of lexical diversity is needed; the current token‑frequency approach is inapplicable.
- **Broader architecture and compression coverage**: Results are limited to Llama‑3.1‑8B and Gemma‑2‑9B and to four compression methods; it is unknown whether ZipCal generalizes to other architectures (e.g., encoder‑decoder) or extreme compression schemes (1‑bit, N:M sparsity).

## Limitations
- Unreported dataset sources and the choice of lightweight embedding model hinder exact replication of multi‑domain experiments.
- Comparison to the model‑dependent COLA baseline lacks implementation details, making performance parity approximate.
- The method’s reliance on lexical diversity may not generalize to non‑English text, code, or multimodal data.

## Confidence
- **Speedup (~240×)**: High confidence (theoretical O(nk) complexity and measured runtimes).
- **Parity with COLA (<0.5% mean difference)**: Medium confidence (empirical but dependent on unspecified COLA implementation).
- **Multi‑domain benefit (>2% average gain)**: Medium confidence (shown on undefined domain mixtures; embedding choice and domain definitions affect reproducibility).
- **General mechanism (lexical diversity → robust statistics)**: Medium confidence (supported by coverage analysis but not causally proven).
