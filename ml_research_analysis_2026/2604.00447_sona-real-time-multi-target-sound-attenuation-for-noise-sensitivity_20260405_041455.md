# Sona: Real-Time Multi-Target Sound Attenuation for Noise Sensitivity

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.00447
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00447
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Real-time multi-target sound attenuation on mobile with user-extensible classes, achieving ~3 dB SI-SNR improvement and high user satisfaction (bothersome sound reduction 5.7/7, wanted sound preservation 5.8/7).

## Executive Summary
Noise-sensitive individuals often need to reduce specific bothersome sounds while maintaining awareness of their environment—a capability conventional noise cancellation cannot provide. Sona is a mobile system that enables real-time, selective attenuation of multiple overlapping sound sources on commodity devices. It uses a target-conditioned neural pipeline that decouples the specification of what to suppress (via dynamic target embeddings) from the suppression mechanism itself. The framework supports user-extensible sound classes: users can record examples of new bothersome sounds, and the system generates embeddings on the fly without retraining the model.

Technical evaluation on an iPhone 16 Pro Max shows a stable end-to-end latency of 42 ms and consistent signal-to-noise ratio improvement of ~3 dB across 1–3 simultaneous targets. An in-situ user study with 10 noise-sensitive participants reported meaningful reduction of bothersome sounds (mean 5.7/7) while preserving wanted sounds (mean 5.8/7), with strong ratings for usability and perceived fit for daily use.

## Method Summary
Sona's core is a target-conditioned deep complex convolutional recurrent network (DCCRN) that takes as input a raw audio waveform and a fused embedding vector representing the user's selected sound targets. The fused embedding is computed by projecting each target's 768-dimensional embedding (derived from a pretrained AudioSep model) through a two-layer network and applying permutation-invariant max-pooling across up to K targets, allowing variable target sets without model retraining. The model is trained on synthetic mixtures constructed from VGGSound, ESC-50, and FSD50K datasets, each containing 2–3 sound sources with random signal-to-interference ratios, using a negative scale-invariant signal-to-noise ratio (SI-SNR) loss.

For deployment, Sona employs a streaming inference pipeline with a 250 ms sliding window and a 25 ms hop, resampling device audio to 16 kHz. The model runs locally on a commodity mobile device (iPhone 16 Pro Max) with optimizations (e.g., Core ML) to achieve an end-to-end latency of 42 ms. Personalization is handled at the representation level: users record short clips of a new sound, which are processed offline by the pretrained AudioSep encoder to extract utterance-level embeddings; averaging these yields a class-level embedding that is added to the dictionary and used identically to built-in classes during runtime conditioning.

## Key Results
- End-to-end latency: 42 ms on iPhone 16 Pro Max.
- SI-SNR improvement: ~3 dB consistent across 1–3 simultaneous targets.
- User study: bothersome sound reduction mean 5.7/7, wanted sound preservation 5.8/7, strong usability and daily fit ratings.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Selective sound attenuation via target-conditioned neural suppressor
- **Claim:** Selective sound attenuation is achieved by conditioning a single neural suppressor on dynamic target embeddings, decoupling *what* to suppress from the *how* of suppression.
- **Mechanism:** A target-conditioned DCCRN model processes a raw waveform and a fused embedding vector representing the user's currently selected sound targets. The fused embedding is computed via permutation-invariant max-pooling over individual target embeddings, allowing variable target sets at runtime without model retraining.
- **Core assumption:** A single model can generalize to suppress arbitrary combinations of sound classes if those classes are represented in a shared conditioning space and the training data includes diverse multi-source mixtures.
- **Evidence:** [abstract] "Sona is built on a target-conditioned neural pipeline that supports simultaneous attenuation of multiple overlapping sound sources." [section 4.1] "The model implements ˆ𝑥=𝐹 𝜃 (𝑥, 𝑒fused)... The central technical idea is to separate *what* should be attenuated from the *how* attenuation is performed." [corpus] "No direct evidence; neighboring papers focus on related but distinct audio filtering or hearable concepts."
- **Break condition:** If the acoustic characteristics of a novel user-added target are too far from the training distribution (e.g., an extremely rare mechanical sound), the conditioning embedding may fail to activate appropriate suppression pathways, leading to ineffective attenuation.

### Mechanism 2: User-extensible personalization via embedding storage
- **Claim:** User-extensible personalization is enabled by storing target representations as external embeddings derived from a pretrained audio model, avoiding costly retraining.
- **Mechanism:** Users record short examples of a new bothersome sound. These recordings are processed offline using a pretrained AudioSep model to extract utterance-level embeddings, which are averaged to create a class-level embedding. This embedding is stored in a dictionary and treated identically to built-in class embeddings during runtime conditioning.
- **Core assumption:** The pretrained AudioSep model produces embeddings where acoustically similar sounds (even from different instances) cluster in the embedding space, allowing a single class embedding to represent a sound category.
- **Evidence:** [abstract] "supports user-extensible sound classes via in-situ recordings without model retraining." [section 4.3] "We therefore designed the framework so that personalization happens at the level of *target representation*, not network weights... teaching Sona a new bothersome sound amounts to adding a new embedding." [section 4.4] "We derive class-level embeddings from a pretrained AudioSep framework... average these utterance-level embeddings across multiple recordings from the same class."
- **Break condition:** If a user's recording of a novel sound is too short, noisy, or unrepresentative, the derived class embedding may not accurately capture the sound's perceptual qualities, leading to poor suppression when that class is selected.

### Mechanism 3: Real-time low-latency through streaming pipeline and optimized inference
- **Claim:** Real-time, low-latency performance on commodity mobile hardware is achieved through a streaming inference pipeline with fixed buffer management and optimized model operations.
- **Mechanism:** Audio is processed in 250 ms sliding windows with a 25 ms hop. The model operates on 16 kHz mono audio. An output buffer is capped at 50 ms to prevent latency drift. The DCCRN model is optimized for on-device execution (e.g., via Core ML or similar).
- **Core assumption:** The computational complexity of the DCCRN model with conditioning is low enough to complete inference within the hop duration on a modern mobile SoC, and the buffer management strategy can absorb minor processing variations.
- **Evidence:** [abstract] "Technical evaluation shows a stable end-to-end latency of 42 ms... on a commodity device." [section 4.5] "The streaming pipeline operates on a 250 ms sliding window with a 25 ms hop... achieves an end-to-end latency of 42 ms while running locally on a commercial mobile device (iPhone 16 Pro Max)." [section 6.2] Reports inference time averaging 11.18 ms and stable end-to-end latency (41.56–42.03 ms) across nine 10-minute runs.
- **Break condition:** If the device is under heavy thermal throttling, running other demanding applications, or has a significantly less powerful chipset than the test device, inference time could exceed the hop duration, causing audio glitches or increased latency.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
- **Live Listen Layer:** Captures microphone stream, blends current target embeddings, runs suppressor model, mixes attenuated output with pass-through based on strength slider.
- **Context Reasoning Layer:** Runs parallel sound classifier (Apple SoundAnalysis), maps classes to Sona targets, triggers suggestions. For unknown sounds, uses Audio Flamingo 3 (server) + on-device LLM judge against user profiles to prompt for recording.
- **Personalization Layer:** Manages storage/creation of custom class embeddings (from user recordings) and sensitivity profiles (text descriptions). Interface for recording and labeling.
- **Shared Resources:** Target embedding dictionary (built-in + user-defined), sensitivity profile store, rolling audio buffer.

### Critical Path
Mic Input -> [Context Reasoning: detection/suggestion] -> User selects targets -> Retrieve embeddings -> Fuse (Eq 1) -> Suppressor (𝐹𝜃) -> Blending (Eq 2) -> Playback.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Latency vs. Contextual Intelligence:** The Context Reasoning layer's server-based audio description (Audio Flamingo 3) introduces potential delay/connectivity issues for novel sound prompts. Tradeoff: richer description vs. offline reliability.
- **Number of Targets vs. Speech Quality:** Allowing more simultaneous targets (the model supports K>3) risks greater speech distortion and artifacts as more sources are suppressed.
- **Personalization Effort vs. Accuracy:** Users can add a class with one recording, but more recordings improve embedding robustness. Tradeoff: quick setup vs. long-term efficacy.

### Failure Signatures
- **Artifact buildup:** whooshing, distortion, or "muffled" speech when multiple strong filters are active, especially in noisy environments.
- **Missed suggestions/triggers:** Context Reasoning fails to detect a bothersome sound due to classifier taxonomy mismatch, lowvolume, or overlapping acoustic scenes.
- **Embedding drift:** A user-defined class (e.g., "refrigerator hum") becomes ineffective if the appliance degrades or is replaced with a different-sounding model.
- **Battery drain:** CPU usage spikes (reported up to 23%) in complex scenes could shorten usable session time below user expectations.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Validate single-target attenuation quality:** Run the model on held-out test mixtures for one built-in class (e.g., "keyboard typing"). Measure SI-SNRi and audit for artifacts. Verify that changing the embedding to a different class (e.g., "dog bark") produces the corresponding suppression.
2. **Measure streaming latency stability:** Profile the full pipeline (mic input to playback) on the target device for a 60-minute continuous run with 1, 2, and 3 active targets. Confirm max E2E latency stays below 50ms and does not drift.
3. **Test personalization workflow end-to-end:** Record 3 examples of a novel sound (e.g., a specific fan). Use the offline embedding generation script to create the class embedding. Inject it into the dictionary and verify that selecting this new class in Live Listen attenuates that sound in a fresh mixture, compared to a baseline where it is not selected.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** How can Sona improve speech preservation when attenuating multiple simultaneous bothersome sounds, especially in noisy environments?
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 8 (Future Work) identifies "improve speech preservation under heavy multi-target filtering" as a key direction.
**Why unresolved:** In the user study (RQ1), participants reported that speech intelligibility degraded when multiple filters were active or in louder settings, sometimes making speech muffled or incomprehensible.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments measuring speech comprehension (e.g., word accuracy, listener ratings) across varied target counts and noise levels; iterative model adjustments that minimize speech distortion while suppressing targets.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** How can residual artifacts (e.g., whooshing, distortion, unnatural speech) in Sona’s output be reduced to enhance soundscape naturalness?
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 8 (Future Work) calls for "reducing residual artifacts in the processed soundscape."
**Why unresolved:** Participants frequently noticed processing artifacts that detracted from the experience, such as whooshing sounds and unnatural voice quality, particularly with multiple active targets.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Perceptual evaluations using audio quality metrics (e.g., PESQ, ViSQOL) alongside qualitative user feedback; technical refinements to the neural pipeline to smooth transitions and eliminate processing cues.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** How can Sona account for previously backgrounded sounds becoming more noticeable and potentially distracting once dominant targets are attenuated?
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 8 (Future Work) highlights the need to "account for the way previously backgrounded sounds can become more noticeable once dominant sounds are removed."
**Why unresolved:** User study findings (RQ1) showed that suppressing several targets made minor ambient sounds (e.g., page-turning) unexpectedly salient and disruptive.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Studies characterizing soundscape dynamics pre- and post-attenuation; developing adaptive mechanisms (e.g., dynamic gain control, secondary target detection) to prevent over-attenuation and maintain balanced auditory scenes.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** What interaction models better align with how users conceptualize their acoustic environments—e.g., through broad acoustic qualities (hums, thuds) rather than discrete sound-source labels?
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 8 (Future Work) suggests moving "beyond sound-by-sound selection toward interaction models that better match how users conceptualize their acoustic environments."
**Why unresolved:** Participants often described bothersome sounds in terms of qualitative attributes (e.g., "sharp repetitive," "mechanical whirring") instead of specific sources, indicating a mismatch with the current sound-class catalog interface.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Design and evaluation of alternative interfaces (e.g., quality sliders, acoustic profile selectors, hierarchical descriptors) via user studies assessing conceptual fit, learnability, and effectiveness.

### Open Question 5
**Question:** How do personalization workflows (e.g., creating and managing custom sound classes) evolve over sustained daily use, and what supports long-term engagement and adaptation?
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 8 (Future Work) states that "longer-term field studies are needed to understand how personalization workflows evolve over time"; Section 7 (RQ3) notes participants viewed personalization as a longitudinal process.
**Why unresolved:** The user study was a single 2-hour session, limiting insight into how users build, refine, and reuse custom classes over weeks or months, or whether motivation and needs shift.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Longitudinal deployments (e.g., 4–8 weeks) tracking custom class creation, modification, and usage patterns; longitudinal interviews on changing soundscapes, personalization habits, and system trust.

## Limitations
- **Generalization to novel user-recorded sounds:** Poor-quality or out-of-distribution recordings may yield ineffective embeddings, causing suppression failure.
- **Hardware and thermal constraints:** Latency and battery impact only validated on a high-end device; older or throttled devices may not meet real-time specs.
- **User study limitations:** Small sample (N=10) and single 2-hour session; long-term effectiveness and diverse needs unknown.

## Confidence
- **Selective attenuation efficacy** (SI-SNRi ~3 dB, 1–3 targets): **High** (consistent quantitative results).
- **End-to-end latency** (42 ms): **Medium** (single device, no stress testing).
- **User-extensibility** (add classes without retraining): **Medium** (mechanism described, limited custom-class validation).
- **User-perceived effectiveness** (reduction 5.7/7, preservation 5.8/7, usability): **Medium** (small N, lab setting).

## Next Checks

2. **Cross-device latency and thermal resilience:** Deploy on a matrix of devices (old/new, iOS/Android) with background load. Run 60-minute sessions, log end-to-end latency, CPU, battery. Flag runs >50 ms or crashes.
