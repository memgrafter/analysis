# Impact of Markov Decision Process Design on Sim-to-Real Reinforcement Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.09427
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09427
- **Reference count:** 16
- **Primary result:** Including the target color in the state is critical; without it, real-world success is 0%, while physics-based dynamics achieve up to 50% under strict precision requirements.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates how the formulation of the Markov Decision Process (MDP) affects sim-to-real transfer in reinforcement learning. Using a color mixing testbed where an agent must match a target RGB color by dispensing inks, the authors trained DDPG policies in simulation with systematic variations in state, reward, termination, and dynamics. Policies were then deployed on real hardware. The key finding is that the inclusion of the target color in the state space is absolutely necessary for successful transfer—its omission causes complete failure despite reasonable simulation performance. Under strict color-matching tolerances, high-fidelity physics-based dynamics models (Kubelka-Munk) enable up to 50% real-world success, whereas a simplified linear interpolation model fails entirely. Furthermore, simple distance-based rewards and relative state representations promote training stability and better transfer.

## Method Summary
The experimental testbed involves an agent that controls the dispensing of cyan, magenta, and yellow inks to produce a target RGB color in a mixing vessel. The MDP components were varied: state representations included different encodings of current color, volumes (absolute, relative, normalized ratios), and crucially, the inclusion or exclusion of the target color `c_target`; action space was discrete (choice of base color + volume increment); reward functions ranged from simple normalized Euclidean distance (R1) to variants with action penalties (R2, R3); termination horizons (T) and success tolerances (τ) were adjusted; and dynamics models for color prediction included a linear interpolation (Lerp), the physics-based Kubelka-Munk (KM) model, and a Whiteness–Glossiness Model (WGM). To improve robustness, fixed observation noise and Adversarial Robustness Learning (ARL) were applied. After training in simulation, policies were evaluated on real hardware under exact target-matching criteria defined by τ.

## Key Results
- Excluding the target color from the state (`c_target` omitted) results in 0% real-world success, despite achieving moderate simulation performance.
- Under strict precision requirements (τ=7.5), the high-fidelity Kubelka-Munk dynamics model achieves 50% hardware success, while the simplified Lerp model fails completely (0%).
- Simple distance-based rewards (R1) and normalized ratio state representations (State 4) yield stable training and are employed by the top-performing hardware models.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Goal Conditioning is Non-Negotiable
**Claim:** Including the target color (`c_target`) in the state space is **necessary** for successful sim-to-real transfer; its omission causes complete failure (0% success) despite moderate simulation performance.
**Mechanism:** Without `c_target`, the policy must learn a single, time-invariant behavior that optimizes the *average* reward over all possible targets (Eq. 4). This yields a compromise policy. In simulation (where dynamics match the training model), this compromise can achieve acceptable average rewards. However, in the real world, the distribution over achievable colors shifts due to unmodeled physical variations. The policy, lacking explicit goal information, cannot adapt its strategy for a specific target and fails entirely.
**Core assumption:** The real-world achievable color distribution differs sufficiently from the simulation's distribution that a goal-agnostic policy cannot generalize.
**Evidence anchors:**
- [abstract] "...including the target color in the state is critical; without it, real-world success is 0%."
- [section V-B.1] M2 (target excluded) achieves 0% success on hardware, while M1 (target included) achieves 43.75% success, despite M2 having reasonable simulation performance (FP ~6.99).
**Break condition:** If a goal-agnostic policy (target excluded) achieves non-zero, comparable success to a goal-conditioned policy on hardware *for the same strict precision requirements*, this mechanism is invalidated. This would require either (a) the real-world dynamics shift to align perfectly with the simulation's average-optimal compromise, or (b) the task is such that a single fixed policy is optimal for all targets.

### Mechanism 2: Dynamics Fidelity Enables Precision
**Claim:** Using a high-fidelity, physics-based dynamics model (Kubelka-Munk or WGM) in simulation is a **key enabler** for successful transfer under strict color precision constraints (`τ=7.5`), where a simple linear interpolation (Lerp) model fails entirely.
**Mechanism:** The Lerp model's inaccurate color prediction creates a simulation bias. Under strict tolerances, the policy trained with Lerp learns behaviors that exploit this inaccuracy, producing colors that are "close" in the flawed simulation but are physically impossible or far from the target in reality. Physics-based models (KM, WGM) constrain the policy to actions that produce *physically plausible* color mixes, making the learned control strategy more robust to the sim-to-real gap in pigment optics and dispensing accuracy.
**Core assumption:** The dominant source of the sim-to-real gap for this precision task is the inaccuracy of the color mixing dynamics model, not other factors like observation noise or camera calibration (which are fixed via robustness mechanisms).
**Evidence anchors:**
- [abstract] "physics-based dynamics models (Kubelka-Munk) achieve up to 50% success, whereas simplified linear models fail entirely."
- [section V-B.3 & Table VI] Under strict parameters (`T=5, τ=7.5`), M3 (Lerp) has 0% success. M4 (KM) achieves 50% success, and M5 (WGM) achieves 31.25%. M1 (Lerp with relaxed `τ=10`) achieves 43.75%, suggesting strict thresholds break Lerp-based policies.
**Break condition:** If policies trained with Lerp achieve success rates comparable to KM/WGM under the *same strict precision parameters*, this mechanism fails. This could occur if the robustness mechanisms (noise, ARL) sufficiently over-regularize the policy to ignore simulation bias, or if the real-world system's dynamics happen to align with Lerp.

### Mechanism 3: Simplicity Promotes Robustness
**Claim:** Simple, distance-based reward functions (R1: normalized Euclidean distance) and relative state representations (State 4: normalized ratios) **promote training stability and better sim-to-real transfer** compared to more complex reward shaping or absolute state encodings.
**Mechanism:** Complex rewards (e.g., R2/R3 with action penalties) introduce an inductive bias that may be misaligned with the true goal (minimizing RGB distance) and can be sensitive to the specific dynamics model. They risk overfitting to simulation-specific artifacts (e.g., exact volume-dispensing costs). Relative state representations (normalized ratios) are scale-invariant, making the policy less sensitive to absolute volume changes and more focused on the proportional mixing strategy that generalizes. Simplicity reduces the chance of learning spurious correlations that do not transfer.
**Core assumption:** The true objective is simply to minimize RGB distance to target, and auxiliary terms in complex rewards, or absolute volume scales, are unnecessary constraints that hurt generalization.
**Evidence anchors:**
- [abstract] "Simple distance-based rewards and relative state representations enhance stability."
- [section V-A.2 & Table IV] Under KM dynamics, State 4 with R1 led to stable learning (CV=0.06), while other states (2,3) or reward R2 resulted in unstable policies (high CV, negative FP) or failure. The final chosen configuration uses State 4 and R1.
- [section V-B] The top-performing hardware models (M4, M5) both use R1 and State 4.
**Break condition:** If a complex reward (e.g., R2) combined with an absolute state representation (e.g., State 1) achieves equal or better hardware success rates *and* training stability (lower CV) compared to R1+State4 under the same high-fidelity dynamics and strict constraints, the claim is weakened. This would suggest the inductive bias of the complex reward is beneficial, not detrimental.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning**  
  **Why needed here:** The core of Mechanism 1 hinges on whether the policy can condition on a specific target (`c_target`). Understanding how goal signals are integrated into the state space (making the MDP Markov) versus being marginalized (creating a POMDP-like problem) is essential for designing the state space.  
  **Quick check question:** If a reward function depends on a goal `g` but `g` is not in the state, what does the optimal policy converge to, and why might this fail when the environment dynamics change?

- **Concept: Markov Decision Process (MDP) Formulation**  
  **Why needed here:** The paper systematically varies the MDP tuple (S, A, P, R, T). An engineer must understand how each component (state S, reward R, horizon T) shapes the learning problem and its susceptibility to overfitting to simulation artifacts.  
  **Quick check question:** How would changing the termination horizon `T` from 20 to 5 steps, while keeping the physical task the same, effectively change the reward function the agent optimizes?

- **Concept: Dynamics Model Fidelity & Sim-to-Real Gap**  
  **Why needed here:** Mechanism 2 is about choosing a simulator's transition model `P(s'|s,a)`. The engineer must grasp that a low-fidelity model (`Lerp`) can be a *mis-specification* that teaches the wrong behavior, not just a noisier version of the truth.  
  **Quick check question:** If a policy trained on a biased simulator succeeds on real hardware, does that prove the simulator was "good enough," or could it indicate the task was insensitive to the specific bias?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
State Space (S: `c_current`, volume encoding, `c_target`) -> Action Space (A: discrete base color + volume increment) -> Dynamics Model (P: Lerp / KM / WGM) -> Reward Function (R: R1 / R2 / R3) -> Termination (T: horizon `T`, tolerance `τ`).  
Robustness mechanisms: fixed observation noise & Adversarial Robustness Learning (ARL) applied to observations/rewards.

**Critical path:**  
1. **Fix Goal Information:** First decision: include `c_target` in `S`? (Must be YES based on evidence).  
2. **Select Dynamics Fidelity:** Choose `P` (KM/WGM preferred for precision). This dictates expected learning difficulty.  
3. **Design Reward & State Simplicity:** Opt for R1 (simple distance) and State 4 (normalized ratios) to stabilize learning under chosen `P`.  
4. **Align Episode Parameters:** Set `T` and `τ` based on `P`'s capability (stricter `τ` requires higher fidelity `P`).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **State Complexity vs. Markov Property:** Adding `c_target` makes state larger but restores Markov property. Omitting it creates a POMDP.  
- **Dynamics Fidelity vs. Sample Efficiency:** KM/WGM are more accurate but slower to learn from (higher T7.5). Lerp is fast but risks policy overfitting to its bias.  
- **Reward Simplicity vs. Guidance:** Simple R1 is robust but provides less shaping. Complex R2/R3 may speed learning in simulation but harm transfer.  
- **Strict Termination vs. Success Rate:** Tight `τ` promotes precision but may make the task unsolvable in simulation/real if `P` or hardware is inaccurate.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **0% hardware success with moderate simulation FP:** Classic sign of `c_target` omission.  
- **High variance (CV) and non-monotonic learning (high NM):** Indicates unstable policy, often from complex rewards (R2/R3) or absolute states (0,1) under KM.  
- **Good simulation success but poor hardware under strict `τ`:** Suggests dynamics model (`P`) is too simplistic (Lerp)—policy exploits simulation bias.  
- **Hardware success only with relaxed `τ`:** Indicates `P` is insufficiently accurate for the required precision.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline with Target:** Train a policy with `c_target` included, Lerp dynamics, R1 reward, State 4, `T=20`, `τ=10`. Verify it learns in simulation and achieves ~44% hardware success (replicate M1).  
2. **Target Ablation:** Repeat Experiment 1 with `c_target` **excluded**. Expected result: simulation FP may be decent (~7), but hardware success drops to 0% (replicate M2). This isolates Mechanism 1.  
3. **Dynamics Swap with Fixed Parameters:** Using the configuration from Exp 1 (with target), change only the dynamics model from Lerp to KM, keep all else identical (including strict `τ=7.5` from Phase 2). Expected: training becomes unstable/high T7.5 in sim, and hardware success may drop or become 0% if `τ` is too strict for KM's learning curve. This tests the interaction in H4/H5.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** How should MDPs be designed when real-world target states are not achievable in simulation, and what alternative metrics can evaluate transfer success in such cases?
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors discovered that all hardware target colors lay outside the simulation's producible range for all dynamics models, making exact target attainment impossible. They suggest focusing on reachable targets or alternative gap metrics.
**Why unresolved:** The paper's experimental evaluation relies on exact target achievement (via tolerance τ), which was unattainable in reality. This mismatch means policies may optimize for simulation-specific goals that do not align with real-world feasibility, leaving the proper handling of unreachable targets unexplored.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct a systematic study where target reachability is varied (e.g., by selecting targets inside and outside simulation ranges). Develop and validate metrics that account for simulation limitations (e.g., distance to the closest achievable color, or relative improvement). Compare how different MDP designs (state, reward, dynamics) perform under these conditions to derive guidelines.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** Does curriculum learning—gradually increasing task difficulty—improve sample efficiency and robustness for sim-to-real transfer under strict precision constraints?
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] The Future Work section explicitly proposes investigating curriculum learning to improve sample efficiency and robustness.
**Why unresolved:** The paper used fixed termination horizons (T=5 or 20) and tolerances (τ=7.5 or 10) throughout training, without any gradual increase in difficulty. The potential benefits of a curriculum for bridging the sim-to-real gap remain untested.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Implement a curriculum that starts with relaxed tolerances (e.g., τ=20) and/or longer horizons, then progressively tightens to the target strict values. Train policies with and without the curriculum across multiple MDP configurations (e.g., different dynamics models). Compare sample complexity, training stability (CV, NM), and real-world success rates to determine if curricula yield more robust and efficient transfer.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** How can physics-based dynamics models like Kubelka-Munk be effectively calibrated using real-world data to reduce simulation bias and enhance sim-to-real transfer?
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] The Future Work explicitly calls for calibrating dynamics models to reduce simulation bias.
**Why unresolved:** The study compared off-the-shelf models (Lerp, KM, WGM) but did not adapt any to the specific hardware. KM achieved 50% real-world success, but calibration could further close the gap by aligning simulation predictions with actual color mixing outcomes.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Collect a dataset of real-world mixing experiments (e.g., varying ink volumes and measuring resulting RGB). Fit the parameters of the Kubelka-Munk model to this data. Integrate the calibrated model into simulation, retrain policies, and evaluate transfer performance compared to the uncalibrated KM model. Analyse how calibration data quantity and diversity affect success rates.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** Is the inclusion of the target state in the observation universally necessary for successful sim-to-real transfer in goal-conditioned RL, or does it depend on task characteristics such as goal space size or dynamics nonlinearity?
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper strongly supported target inclusion for color mixing based on theory and experiments, but the study was limited to a single task domain. The theoretical argument (Eq. 4) suggests a general principle, but its applicability across diverse tasks is unverified.
**Why unresolved:** The conclusion that target state inclusion is essential is drawn solely from color mixing. Other tasks might allow goal inference from state transitions or provide dense rewards that indirectly encode goals, potentially mitigating the need for explicit goal conditioning.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Replicate the experimental design on multiple sim-to-real benchmarks (e.g., robotic peg-in-hole, locomotion with velocity goals) with varying properties (discrete vs. continuous goals, linear vs. nonlinear dynamics). For each, train policies with and without goal conditioning, measuring the sim-to-real gap. Identify task features that moderate the necessity of target inclusion.

## Limitations
- **Single-task validation (color mixing); domain transfer unknown.** Findings may not generalize to other robotic control problems.
- **Hardware results lack statistical rigor (small N, no CI).** Success rates are based on limited trials, reducing confidence in effect sizes.
- **Low citation/FMR indicates limited external validation.** The paper has not been widely tested or replicated by the community.
- **Algorithm dependence not assessed.** Only DDPG was used; other RL algorithms might yield different sensitivity to MDP design.
- **No reproducibility details provided.** Implementation specifics are insufficient for independent verification.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (target inclusion necessity):** High (0% vs 43.75% stark contrast, strong theoretical grounding).
- **Mechanism 2 (dynamics fidelity importance):** Medium (paper evidence strong but domain-specific; may not generalize).
- **Mechanism 3 (simple rewards/state):** Medium (stability observed, but hardware link is indirect and confounded by other factors).
