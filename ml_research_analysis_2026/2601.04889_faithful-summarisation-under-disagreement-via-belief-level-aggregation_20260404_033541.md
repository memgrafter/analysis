# Faithful Summarisation under Disagreement via Belief-Level Aggregation

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2601.04889  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04889  
- **Reference count**: 30  
- **Primary result**: Belief-level aggregation using distance-based merging yields consistently strong and stable performance on disagreement-aware metrics across diverse LLM families and scales (3B to GPT-5).

## Executive Summary
Multi-document opinion summarisation often fails to preserve genuine disagreement, as LLMs tend to smooth conflicts and amplify majority views. This paper introduces a pipeline that separates belief aggregation from natural language generation. It first extracts structured aspect-level binary opinions from each review, then merges them via a distance-based belief merging operator to compute a fair compromise belief state, and finally verbalises the merged beliefs into a fluent summary using simple prompting. The upstream resolution of conflict makes the summarisation robust to the generator’s capacity, ensuring faithful representation of minority and contested opinions. Evaluations on Rotten Tomatoes critic reviews show that the approach achieves stable, high scores on disagreement-aware metrics (e.g., Coverage, Polarity Fidelity, Contrast Preservation) across models of varying size. In contrast, implicit aggregation methods (Fusion-of-N, QA-prompting) exhibit brittle, model-dependent behaviour, with smaller models failing to preserve disagreement. The work demonstrates that explicit belief-level aggregation is key to faithful summarisation under disagreement.

## Method Summary
The proposed three-stage pipeline operates as follows:  
(1) **Opinion extraction**: For each source review, a large LLM (e.g., Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct) prompts to extract a set of ⟨aspect, polarity⟩ pairs for seven predefined aspects (Plot, Themes, Script, Performances, Visuals, Pacing, Overall Success), binarising sentiment.  
(2) **Belief merging**: Given the collection of per-review belief bases, enumerate all 2^7 = 128 possible worlds (binary assignments over aspects). For each world, compute total L1 distance to each critic’s belief base; select all worlds minimising this total distance. The merged probabilistic belief state K* is obtained by averaging the binary values across all optimal worlds, yielding a [0,1] strength for each aspect.  
(3) **Summary realisation**: Convert each aspect’s merged belief into a natural‑language hypothesis using a world conversion prompt, then combine these hypotheses into a final summary via a second LLM prompt. The separation ensures that any LLM, regardless of size, can produce a faithful summary because the conflict resolution is already performed upstream.

## Key Results
- Belief merging produces consistent, high disagreement‑aware scores across a wide range of generator models (from 3B to GPT‑5), with tightly clustered performance.  
- Implicit aggregation methods (Fusion‑of‑N, QA‑prompting) show large performance swings depending on model capacity, often smoothing over minority opinions.  
- The pipeline’s effectiveness is robust to the choice of generator; even small models achieve comparable disagreement‑aware scores to larger ones.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The approach’s success rests on three interconnected mechanisms.  
First, **explicit conflict resolution** through distance‑based belief merging: the operator minimises total L1 disagreement across all critics, producing a compromise belief state that respects both majority and minority views, avoiding the bias of majority voting.  
Second, **decoupling aggregation from generation**: by determining the exact content (merged beliefs) before verbalisation, the language model only needs to translate a clear, structured representation into fluent text, a task that even modest models can perform reliably.  
Third, **binary aspect‑level representation** reduces the merging space to a manageable size and aligns L1 distance with Hamming distance, providing a natural measure of disagreement. While this abstraction simplifies merging, it also limits nuance, as intensity or conditional opinions are not captured. Empirical results (Section 5) confirm that this combination yields stable performance across diverse model families, validating the mechanism.

## Foundational Learning

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Opinion Extractor → Belief Merger → Summary Realizer  

**Critical path**: Extraction (parallelisable per review) → Merger (sequential across all belief bases) → Realization (sequential)  

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Granularity of representation: Binary polarity simplifies merging but discards intensity nuances.  
- Distance metric: L1 vs. alternatives (L2, cosine); L1 corresponds to Hamming but may not capture weighted aspects.  
- Realisation prompt design: Two‑stage (hypothesis then summary) improves traceability; direct summary is shorter but risks losing structure.  
- Model choice for extraction: Larger extractors (e.g., Qwen‑72B) improve accuracy but increase cost; smaller models may miss aspects or implicit opinions.  

**Failure signatures**  
- Extraction failures (missing aspects, incorrect polarities) propagate to an incomplete belief base, leading to summaries that omit entire facets.  
- Merging anomalies (many optimal worlds) produce near‑uniform K* values (~0.5), yielding vague or indecisive hypotheses.  
- Generation failures (LLM deviates from input beliefs, adds unsupported claims, or flattens contrast) indicate poor transfer from structured beliefs to language.  
- Model‑dependent variance in implicit methods signals that aggregation is not truly explicit.  

**First 3 experiments**  
1. Main replication: Implement belief merging, Fusion‑of‑N, and QA‑prompting pipelines; run all generator models (GPT‑5, Qwen‑14B, Llama‑8B) on the test set; compute GEval disagreement‑aware metrics to confirm the stability pattern.  
2. Extraction quality ablation: Replace the Qwen‑72B extractor with a smaller model (Llama‑8B) or a rule‑based extractor; measure downstream disagreement metrics to quantify sensitivity to belief base fidelity.  
3. Merging operator ablation: Swap L1 distance with L2 or cosine similarity; compare resulting merged beliefs and final summaries for changes in coverage, contrast preservation, and model invariance.

## Limitations
- **Aspect definition dependency**: The 7 fixed aspects were derived via LDA, but exact parameters and topic‑to‑aspect mapping are unreported, making it unclear whether performance gains stem from belief merging or the specific aspect set.  
- **Integrity constraint µ ambiguity**: The formal merging operator includes an integrity constraint µ, yet the reported implementation (Appendix E) appears unconstrained; the effect of possible constraints on outcomes is unknown.  
- **Extraction fragility**: The pipeline relies on high‑fidelity ⟨aspect, polarity⟩ extraction; sensitivity to extractor model size, prompt design, and handling of implicit/neutral opinions is not fully characterised.

## Confidence
- Belief merging yields stable disagreement‑aware performance across model families and scales: **High**  
- Separating aggregation from generation reduces sensitivity to generator capacity: **Medium**  
- Distance‑based merging fairly preserves minority opinions vs. majority voting: **Medium‑Low**

## Next Checks
1. **Aspect‑set ablation**: Run the pipeline with a fixed, expert‑defined aspect list (bypassing LDA) and compare disagreement‑aware metrics to isolate whether gains are due to merging or the specific aspect granularity.  
2. **Constraint ablation**: Implement two merging variants—(a) unconstrained as in Appendix E, (b) with a minimal integrity constraint (e.g., at least one positive aspect); evaluate summaries for changes in contrast preservation and prevalence calibration.  
3. **Extraction stress test**: Replace the Qwen‑72B extractor with smaller models (Llama‑8B, 3B) and a simple rule‑based extractor (sentiment lexicon + keyword matching); measure the drop in downstream disagreement metrics and identify which extraction errors most degrade merging quality.
