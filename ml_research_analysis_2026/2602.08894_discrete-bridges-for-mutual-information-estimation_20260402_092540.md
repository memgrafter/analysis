# Discrete Bridges for Mutual Information Estimation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.08894
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08894
- Reference count: 36
- Primary result: DBMI introduces a discrete bridge framework for mutual information estimation, achieving accurate scaling to high-dimensional discrete data where neural estimators fail.

## Executive Summary
Mutual information (MI) estimation between high-dimensional discrete random variables remains a challenge for standard neural approaches like MINE and InfoNCE, which often suffer from high variance, bias, or failure to converge. This paper presents the Discrete Bridge Mutual Information (DBMI) estimator, which reframes the problem as a KL divergence between two reciprocal Markov processes—one modeling the joint distribution and one modeling independence. DBMI employs a single neural network with a binary conditioning input to learn the transition kernels of both processes, leveraging a lazy random walk reference that yields analytically tractable bridge distributions.

Empirical evaluation on low-dimensional benchmarks demonstrates that DBMI maintains low absolute error as the number of dimensions and alphabet sizes increase, while competing estimators deteriorate significantly. Moreover, on a novel 32×32 binary image benchmark derived from latent rectangle coordinates, DBMI successfully estimates ground-truth MI and generates high-quality samples, confirming its scalability and robustness. The method thus provides a promising new direction for discrete MI estimation in high dimensions.

## Method Summary
DBMI formulates MI as KL[r_joint || r_ind], where r_joint and r_ind are reciprocal Markov chains that share the same transition model rθ(xt|xt-1, x0, v) but differ in the binary flag v (1 for joint, 0 for independent). The chains start from the marginal of x0 and end at x1 after N steps, with intermediate states drawn from a lazy walk bridge posterior that can be computed analytically. Training involves sampling batches of joint (x0,x1) pairs and constructing independent pairs via permutation. For each pair, a random diffusion step n is chosen and inner bridge samples are generated. The loss compares the reference next-step distribution to rθ’s prediction; for n=N, the loss is the negative log-likelihood of x1. An optional cross-entropy term on x1 stabilizes the image experiment. After training, MI is estimated by averaging KL[rθ(·|x0,1) || rθ(·|x0,0)] over test samples, with multiple inner samples to reduce variance.

## Key Results
- DBMI achieves accurate MI estimates on low-dimensional discrete distributions even as the number of dimensions D and alphabet size S grow, whereas MINE and InfoNCE exhibit large errors.
- On a high-dimensional 32×32 binary image benchmark, DBMI matches the ground-truth MI and scales without failure; baselines either diverge or provide unreliable estimates.
- Generated x1 samples from the learned joint process closely reproduce the rectangle images, demonstrating that DBMI captures the underlying dependency structure.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
DBMI works by transforming MI estimation into a comparison of two Markov processes that share a common reference bridge. The use of a lazy random walk ensures that the bridge distributions have full support and are analytically tractable, avoiding the instability of direct density ratio estimation. The binary conditioning on a single network forces the model to learn the difference between joint and independent transitions without requiring separate architectures, which improves data efficiency and encourages consistent representations. The per-dimension factorization simplifies the transition modeling and enables linear scaling with dimensionality. Moreover, the KL divergence objective aligns with the mutual information definition and, combined with the closed-form bridge posteriors, yields a stable gradient signal even for large state spaces. The optional cross-entropy loss on the endpoint (used for images) further regularizes the model by explicitly anchoring the final state to x1.

## Foundational Learning
These concepts underpin DBMI and are essential for a correct implementation:

- **Mutual Information (MI): The target quantity measuring dependency between x0 and x1.**  
  *Why needed:* DBMI’s goal is to estimate MI; understanding its definition as KL(π(x0,x1) || π(x0)π(x1)) is essential.  
  *Quick check:* Compute MI for two independent Bernoulli variables (should be 0) and for two perfectly coupled variables (should equal entropy of one).

- **KL Divergence:** The core objective comparing the joint and independent transition distributions.  
  *Why needed:* The loss function in DBMI is a KL divergence; understanding its asymmetry and how to compute it between categorical distributions is necessary.  
  *Quick check:* Implement KL(p||q) for two small categorical distributions; verify it is non-negative and zero only when p=q.

- **Markov Chains & Reciprocal Processes:** DBMI constructs two Markov chains that bridge from x0 to x1.  
  *Why needed:* The derivation of MI as an integral over n of conditional KLs relies on the chains being Markov and reciprocal (same forward transition).  
  *Quick check:* Simulate a simple lazy walk and verify that the probability of reaching a state after n steps depends only on the current state, not the entire history.

- **Bridge Processes (Discrete Bridge Matching):** Conditioning a Markov chain on both endpoints yields a bridge process.  
  *Why needed:* Training samples are drawn from these bridge distributions; the loss uses the posterior over next states given the bridge.  
  *Quick check:* For a small state space and lazy walk, compute the bridge marginal P(xt=s) ∝ Q^n_{x0,s}·Q^{N-n}_{s,x1} and compare with empirical counts from sampling.

- **Lazy Random Walk Reference:** A simple Markov chain with high self-loop probability (1-α) and uniform jumps.  
  *Why needed:* Its transition matrix has a closed form for powers, and its irreducibility ensures all states are reachable, avoiding zeros in bridge probabilities.  
  *Quick check:* Compute Q for S=5, α=0.1, and verify that Q^n converges to the uniform distribution as n increases.

- **Neural Parameterization of Discrete Distributions:** The network rθ outputs per-dimension categorical probabilities.  
  *Why needed:* The transition kernels are represented by a neural network; correct parameterization and numerical stability (e.g., using log_softmax) are crucial.  
  *Quick check:* Feed random inputs to the network and check that for each dimension the output logits produce a valid probability vector (sum to 1, no NaNs).

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input pre-processing → Reference bridge computation → Neural network rθ (DiT/UNet) → Loss computation → Optimization → MI estimation & sampling

**Critical path:**  
1. For each training batch of K joint pairs, generate independent pairs by shuffling x1.  
2. For each pair (x0,x1,v), sample a diffusion step n∼U[1,N] and draw M inner states from the lazy walk bridge posterior P(xtn|x0,x1).  
3. Compute the loss: if n<N, average KL between reference next-step distribution q_ref(·|x_m^{(n)},x1) and rθ(·|x_m^{(n)},x0,v); if n=N, use -log rθ(x1|x_m^{(n)},x0,v). Add optional cross-entropy λ·(-log rθ(x1|x0,v=1)) for images.  
4. Backpropagate and update θ with Adam.  
5. For evaluation, sample (x0,x1), draw n and inner samples, then compute mean KL[rθ(·|x0,1) || rθ(·|x0,0)] as the MI estimate.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Factorization vs. expressivity*: Assuming per-dimension conditional independence reduces parameters and scales linearly, but may limit modeling of cross-dimension dependencies. This works for the reported factorized benchmarks but could hinder performance on more entangled data.  
- *Lazy walk parameter α*: Smaller α yields slower mixing and more spread-out bridges, improving numerical stability but requiring larger N. Larger α makes bridges concentrate near endpoints, potentially reducing gradient signal. Tuning α for the dataset is important.  
- *Single network with binary flag*: Sharing parameters between joint and independent processes reduces memory and encourages contrast, but the flag must be properly injected (e.g., via learned embedding). If the flag is ignored by the network, the two processes may collapse, harming MI estimation.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **NaNs in loss**: Typically caused by zero probabilities in rθ (leading to log(0)). Remedy: add epsilon to logits, use log_softmax, or clip probabilities.  
- **Consistently underestimated MI**: Might indicate insufficient model capacity, poor choice of α or N, or a mismatch between the factorization assumption and the true joint distribution. Check training loss decrease, increase network size, tune hyperparameters, and validate bridge posterior calculations.  
- **Poor sample quality** (especially for images): Could be due to incorrect conditioning on x0, unstable AdaGN injection, or inadequate training steps. Verify that the conditioning information is properly incorporated and consider more training epochs or a higher weight λ for the auxiliary CE loss.

**First three experiments:**  
1. **Bridge posterior sanity check**: For a tiny state space (S=3) and small N, compute the bridge marginal analytically and compare to samples drawn from the closed-form posterior.  
2. **Low-dimensional verification**: Train DBMI on a 1‑dimensional discrete Gaussian-like conditional distribution (σ=0.5) and confirm that the estimated MI matches the analytically computable value within tolerance.  
3. **Scaled image pilot**: Implement the 32×32 binary image data generation, but first train on a reduced resolution (e.g., 8×8) to ensure the full pipeline (UNet, conditioning, training) works before committing to full-size images.

## Limitations
- The number of diffusion steps N is unspecified and may require problem-specific tuning.
- The conditioning details for the image UNet architecture are ambiguous, leaving the exact input pipeline unclear.
- The factorization assumption on transitions may limit applicability to data with strong inter-dimensional dependencies.
