# Uncovering Cross-Objective Interference in Multi-Objective Alignment
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.06869
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06869
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Cross-objective interference is pervasive; a local covariance law characterizes first-order improvement, and Covariance Targeted Weight Adaptation (CTWA) dynamically adjusts scalarization weights to keep per-objective covariance positive, yielding balanced performance.

## Executive Summary
Multi-objective alignment aims to satisfy multiple reward objectives but suffers from cross-objective interference where scalarized training improves some objectives at the cost of others. This paper systematically studies classical scalarization methods (linear, Lagrangian, Tchebycheff, PAMA, dynamic weighting, MGDA, GradNorm) on mathematical reasoning tasks and finds interference to be pervasive and model‑dependent. The authors derive a local covariance law: an objective improves at first order if and only if its true reward exhibits positive covariance with the scalarized score (or clipped advantage weight). Leveraging this, they propose Covariance Targeted Weight Adaptation (CTWA), a plug‑and‑play controller that adjusts scalarization weights during reinforcement learning fine‑tuning (e.g., GRPO) to maintain positive per‑objective covariance. Experiments on Math500 with Qwen2.5‑1.5B and Qwen3‑1.7B show CTWA achieves balanced improvements across accuracy, conciseness, and clarity, while baselines sacrifice at least one objective. CTWA requires minimal overhead and converges faster, consistent with a global convergence analysis under the Polyak‑Łojasiewicz condition.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates seven scalarization algorithms across two model sizes on Math500, measuring per‑objective rewards (accuracy, conciseness, clarity). They observe that all static methods exhibit cross‑objective interference—some objectives improve while others degrade—indicating a general failure mode. The authors establish a first‑order condition linking objective improvement to the covariance between the objective’s true reward and the scalarized advantage. Based on this, they design CTWA, which periodically estimates these covariances during RL fine‑tuning and increases the weight of any objective whose covariance falls below a preset target. The controller is model‑agnostic and operates alongside any gradient‑based RL algorithm (e.g., GRPO). Theoretical analysis under the Polyak‑Łojasiewicz condition connects the covariance sign to the curvature of the alignment objective, explaining why unfavorable model geometry causes interference.

## Key Results
- Cross‑objective interference appears across all tested scalarization methods and both model architectures.
- The local covariance law provides a precise first‑order signal: an objective improves iff its reward covariance with the scalarized score (or clipped advantage) is positive.
- CTWA successfully maintains positive covariances, delivers balanced accuracy/conciseness/clarity, converges faster than baselines, and incurs negligible computational overhead.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The mechanism hinges on the local covariance law: during gradient‑based RL fine‑tuning, the direction of improvement for a scalarized objective is proportional to the dot product between the per‑objective reward gradients and the overall gradient. By expressing this in terms of advantages and the scalarization weights, the authors show that the first‑order change in a true reward is positive exactly when the covariance between that reward and the scalarized score is positive. CTWA directly monitors this covariance and increases the weight of objectives with negative covariance, thereby steering the optimization toward directions that benefit all objectives. This adaptive reweighting prevents the dominant gradients of high‑reward objectives from overwhelming the others, eliminating the interference observed with fixed scalarization.

## Foundational Learning
- **Multi‑objective optimization**: Simultaneously optimizing several reward functions. *Why needed*: Alignment often requires balancing competing goals (e.g., accuracy vs. brevity). *Quick check*: Can we define at least two independent reward models for the same policy?
- **Scalarization**: Combining multiple objectives into a single scalar loss via weighted sum, Chebyshev, etc. *Why needed*: Standard RL optimizes a single objective; scalarization enables the use of single‑objective algorithms. *Quick check*: Does a fixed weight vector always yield Pareto improvements? (Usually not.)
- **Cross‑objective interference**: When scalarized training raises some objectives but worsens others. *Why needed*: Recognizing interference is essential for designing robust alignment strategies. *Quick check*: Track per‑objective rewards during training; if any drop consistently, interference may be present.
- **Covariance‑based adaptation**: Using the covariance between an objective’s reward and the scalarized score to adjust scalarization weights. *Why needed*: Provides a data‑driven, first‑order signal about whether an objective is being helped or hurt. *Quick check*: Compute rolling covariance of per‑objective advantage with the scalarized advantage; negative values indicate the objective is being degraded.
- **GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization)**: A variant of policy gradient RL used in the experiments. *Why needed*: Serves as the underlying RL algorithm to which CTWA is applied. *Quick check*: Does the algorithm use group‑based advantages or standard per‑token advantages? (Here it’s group‑relative.)

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map (plain text):  
Reward Models → Scalarization (weights) → GRPO → Policy; CTWA: (GRPO → per‑objective advantages) → Covariance → weight update → Scalarization.

Critical path:  
1. Sample trajectories from current policy.  
2. Compute per‑objective rewards and advantages.  
3. Form scalarized advantage as weighted sum.  
4. Perform GRPO update.  
5. Estimate per‑objective covariance between each advantage and scalarized advantage.  
6. If covariance < target, increase corresponding weight.  
7. Repeat.

Design tradeoffs:  
CTWA adds minimal overhead (covariance computation O(num_objectives)) but requires careful choice of target covariance and adaptation frequency. Too aggressive weight increases can cause oscillations; too conservative may fail to correct interference. The method is agnostic to the underlying RL algorithm but assumes access to per‑objective advantages.

Failure signatures:  
- Any objective’s covariance remains negative over many steps despite weight updates → that objective will degrade.  
- Weights explode or vanish → numerical instability; may require clipping.  
- Training loss shows high variance or divergence → indicates overly aggressive adaptation.  
- Per‑objective rewards diverge from target trends → interference not mitigated.

First three experiments:  
1. **Baseline comparison**: Run CTWA and static scalarization (linear, Tchebycheff) on Math500 with Qwen2.5‑1.5B; report per‑objective scores and compute an "interference index" (max drop relative to oracle).  
2. **Target covariance ablation**: Study impact of target covariance values (0.0, 0.05, 0.1) on balance (standard deviation across objectives) and convergence speed (steps to reach 80% accuracy).  
3. **Adaptation frequency and overhead**: Vary CTWA update period (every step, every 10 steps, every 100 steps); measure wall‑clock time per step and stability (variance of weights and covariances).

## Limitations
- Limited to small models (1.5B/1.7B); interference and CTWA may differ for larger LLMs.
- Single dataset (Math500); no validation across diverse domains.
- Only three objectives (accuracy, conciseness, clarity); safety, creativity, and real‑world trade‑offs remain unexplored.

## Confidence
- **Interference is pervasive**: Medium
- **Local covariance law holds (first‑order improvement condition)**: Medium
- **CTWA achieves balanced improvements across objectives**: Medium
- **Polyak‑Łojasiewicz‑based convergence analysis explains interference**: Low

## Next Checks
1. Scale and domain diversity: Replicate on models ≥7B and on tasks beyond math (e.g., code generation, summarization) to test interference ubiquity and CTWA robustness.
2. Sensitivity analysis: Systematically vary CTWA hyperparameters (target covariance, adaptation frequency, learning rate) and report performance variance across random seeds.
3. Extended objectives: Incorporate non‑verifiable rewards (e.g., harmlessness, creativity) and evaluate whether CTWA maintains covariance targets and balanced improvements.
