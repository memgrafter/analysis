# Swiss-Bench SBP-002: A Frontier Model Comparison on Swiss Legal and Regulatory Tasks
## Quick Facts
*   arXiv ID: 2603.23646
*   Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23646
*   Reference count: 8
*   Primary result: The best-performing model (Qwen 3.5 Plus) achieves only 38.2% correct on this challenging Swiss regulatory benchmark, indicating no current frontier model is reliable for unassisted work.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Swiss-Bench SBP-002, a novel 395-item benchmark designed to evaluate frontier large language models on applied Swiss regulatory compliance tasks. Distinct from academic legal reasoning or translation, the benchmark spans three regulatory domains (FINMA, Legal-CH, EFK) and seven practitioner-oriented task types across German, French, and Italian. A structured three-dimension scoring framework (Legal Accuracy, Citation Accuracy, Completeness) is assessed by a blind, heterogeneous three-judge LLM panel. Results show the benchmark is extremely difficult, with top-model performance at 38.2% correct and wide variation across task types. The authors conclude that no current model can be trusted for unassisted Swiss regulatory compliance under zero-retrieval conditions.

## Method Summary
The evaluation uses a newly created, expert-curated dataset of 395 items. Items are trilingual (German, French, Italian) and cover three Swiss regulatory domains (FINMA, Legal-CH, EFK) across seven task types (e.g., regulatory Q&A, gap analysis, legal translation). Ten frontier models (March 2026) are evaluated in a zero-retrieval setting (temperature 0, no system prompt). Each model response is scored by a blind panel of three heterogeneous LLM judges (GPT-4o, Claude Sonnet 4, Qwen3-235B) on three discrete dimensions (0.0/0.5/1.0). A deterministic formula computes a weighted score (S = 0.5*Legal Accuracy + 0.3*Citation Accuracy + 0.2*Completeness), which is thresholded into a final grade (Correct/Partial/Incorrect). Final grades per item are determined by majority vote across the three judges. The scoring framework's reliability (weighted Cohen's κ = 0.605) and the reference answers' validity were partially confirmed by a single human legal expert (73% Correct rate on a 100-item subset).

## Key Results
*   The top model (Qwen 3.5 Plus, open-weight) achieves only 38.2% correct, establishing the benchmark's high difficulty.
*   Three performance tiers emerge: Tier A (35–38%), Tier B (26–29%), Tier C (13–21%). Open-weight models are competitive at the top.
*   Task-type difficulty varies dramatically: legal translation (72.3% mean) and case analysis (69.4%) are easiest, while regulatory Q&A (7.5%), hallucination detection (8.3%), and gap analysis (6.4%) are extremely challenging.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
*   **Claim:** Structured numeric scoring on three dimensions (Legal Accuracy, Citation Accuracy, Completeness) improves inter-rater reliability over holistic grading for Swiss regulatory tasks.
*   **Mechanism:** Decomposing evaluation into discrete, factual dimensions with deterministic thresholds removes subjective partial-credit boundaries. Judges assess specific components rather than holistic correctness, reducing ambiguity at the Partial/Incorrect boundary.
*   **Core assumption:** Swiss regulatory compliance can be operationalized through measurable sub-dimensions where ground truth is defined by expert reference answers.
*   **Evidence anchors:** The structured framework achieved weighted κ = 0.605, an improvement from 0.512 under holistic grade mode. Calibration is described in Section 3.3.

### Mechanism 2
*   **Claim:** A blind, heterogeneous three-judge LLM panel with majority-vote aggregation mitigates provider-specific biases and approximates expert human judgment.
*   **Mechanism:** Using judges from distinct model families (OpenAI, Anthropic, Alibaba) with anonymized responses reduces position and self-preference biases. Majority vote on discrete grades aggregates independent applications of the scoring thresholds.
*   **Core assumption:** Frontier LLMs can serve as reliable proxies for human legal experts on Swiss regulatory tasks when ensemble methods and blinding are applied.
*   **Evidence anchors:** The panel achieved weighted κ = 0.605. Pairwise κ values are reported (0.707, 0.556, 0.553). The human expert validation subset showed 73% agreement on Correct grades.

### Mechanism 3
*   **Claim:** A trilingual, multi-domain dataset with stratified task types creates a challenging, ecologically valid probe of applied Swiss regulatory compliance under zero-retrieval conditions.
*   **Mechanism:** Items span Switzerland's official languages (German, French, Italian) and three key regulatory domains, covering seven practitioner-oriented task types. Zero-retrieval evaluation tests parametric knowledge only, isolating model capability from retrieval-augmentation.
*   **Core assumption:** Applied Swiss regulatory competence requires integrated knowledge across languages, domains, and tasks that cannot be reduced to academic legal reasoning or translation.
*   **Evidence anchors:** Results show wide performance variation by domain (Legal-CH vs. FINMA/EFK) and task type (6.4% vs. 72.3% mean C), supporting ecological validity. The paper identifies a gap in prior benchmarks for such applied tasks.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Structured Evaluation Dimensions**
    *   **Why needed here:** Swiss regulatory compliance demands correctness of principle, precision of citation, and completeness of coverage. Holistic grading obscures which specific component fails.
    *   **Quick check:** If a model correctly states a principle (LA=1), cites the wrong article (CA=0), and omits an exception (C=0.5), what is its combined score S and final grade under the 0.8/0.5 thresholds?
*   **Concept: Zero-Retrieval Evaluation**
    *   **Why needed here:** The benchmark isolates parametric knowledge. Real-world compliance often uses RAG; this baseline shows unassisted unreliability.
    *   **Quick check:** Would performance on gap analysis likely improve, degrade, or stay the same if models were provided with the relevant FINMA circular as context? Why?
*   **Concept: Inter-Rater Reliability (Weighted Cohen's κ)**
    *   **Why needed here:** LLM-as-judge outcomes are only meaningful if judges agree substantially. κ quantifies consistency in applying the scoring framework.
    *   **Quick check:** With κ=0.605 (substantial) and 91% of non-unanimous cases being P↔I disagreements, what does this suggest about the discriminative power of the 0.5 and 0.8 score thresholds?
*   **Concept: Task-Type Difficulty Confounding**
    *   **Why needed here:** The dataset's imbalanced composition (e.g., all EFK items are Regulatory Q&A) makes it hard to isolate whether a task type is inherently hard or just paired with a difficult domain/language.
    *   **Quick check:** If Italian items are enriched with easier task types like translation, how would that bias the observed performance difference between Italian and German?
*   **Concept: Legal Ground Truth Validation**
    *   **Why needed here:** The benchmark's validity hinges on the correctness of expert-authored reference answers. Single-expert validation is a major limitation.
    *   **Quick check:** A 73% Correct rate on the human validation subset means what for our confidence in the 38.2% top-model score? Does it suggest the benchmark is too hard or the answers are wrong?

## Architecture Onboarding
Dataset Construction -> Model Execution -> Judge Panel -> Scoring Engine -> Validation & Analysis. The judge panel (3 judges x 10 models x 395 items) is the primary computational bottleneck.

*   **Critical path:** Item authoring & reference validation -> model inference (10 x 395) -> judge scoring (3 x 395 x 3) -> grade aggregation -> statistical analysis.
*   **Design tradeoffs:**
    *   *Structured vs. holistic grading:* Structured improves κ but may miss holistic coherence; thresholds (0.8/0.5) are arbitrary.
    *   *Judge diversity vs. cost:* Frontier judges increase depth but raise cost (~$65); simpler judges risk ceiling effects.
    *   *Dataset balance vs. ecological validity:* Stratified sampling targets quotas but realized composition is imbalanced (e.g., EFK only Regulatory Q&A), confounding interpretation.
*   **Failure signatures:**
    *   **High P↔I disagreement** (91% of non-unanimous): Indicates threshold ambiguity; inspect boundary items.
    *   **Anomalous language performance** (Italian > German/French): Likely compositional bias or translation smoothing; decompose by task.
    *   **Domain collapse** (FINMA/EFK near-zero C%): May indicate reference answer gaps or judge knowledge limits for niche circulars.
    *   **Self-family bias risk**: Claude judge scores Claude Sonnet 4; sensitivity analysis (Appendix A.5) is required.
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1.  **Human validation replication:** Have a second Swiss legal expert independently score the 100-item validation subset. Disagreement patterns will reveal systemic issues with reference answers.
    2.  **Judge panel ablation:** Re-compute rankings using only two non-Claude judges (GPT-4o + Qwen3-235B) and with a single judge. Quantify how Claude's inclusion affects rankings, especially its own.
    3.  **Task-type factorial analysis:** Re-weight the dataset to have equal items per task type (e.g., 56 each) and re-run. This isolates whether task-type difficulty differences are real or artifact of the current distribution.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Question:** To what extent does retrieval augmentation improve model performance on Swiss regulatory tasks compared to the zero-retrieval baseline?
    *   **Basis in paper:** The conclusion states no model is reliable unassisted and future work will investigate RAG for the hardest tasks.
    *   **Why unresolved:** The benchmark evaluated only standalone responses. Real-world workflows use retrieval, so low scores may not reflect practical utility with augmentation.
    *   **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicate the benchmark with a RAG setup using a curated corpus of Swiss legal documents. Compare C% gains across task types.
*   **Question:** Are the observed task-type difficulty differences driven by the cognitive demands of the task itself or by confounding with domain and language composition?
    *   **Basis in paper:** Section 5.3 notes task-type difficulty may outweigh capability but that EFK is confounded with Regulatory Q&A and Italian has a compositional bias.
    *   **Why unresolved:** Imbalanced cross-tabs (e.g., all EFK items are Regulatory Q&A) prevent isolating the effect of task type from domain or language.
    *   **What evidence would resolve it:** A new balanced dataset that fully crosses all seven task types with all three domains and languages. A mixed-effects analysis could then test task-type as an independent predictor.
*   **Question:** Are the scoring thresholds (C ≥ 0.8) and dimension weights (0.5/0.3/0.2) optimal for distinguishing legally sufficient from insufficient responses?
    *   **Basis in paper:** The framework thresholds and weights were chosen without formal validation. The human expert review produced some P-grades despite formula scores ≥0.8.
    *   **Why unresolved:** Thresholds reflect author judgment, not empirical alignment with expert legal evaluation. No sensitivity analysis checked ranking stability.
    *   **What evidence would resolve it:** A threshold-sensitivity analysis on the 100-item expert-validated subset. Compute rankings under multiple threshold sets and measure correlation (e.g., Kendall’s τ) between LLM grades and expert C/P/I grades.
*   **Question:** Does machine translation of German source items into French and Italian introduce semantic or legal nuance shifts that affect performance and evaluation validity?
    *   **Basis in paper:** Section 6.1 acknowledges FR/IT items were machine-translated with a legal audit by the single author, not an independent translator. The Italian C-rate anomaly is noted as potentially due to "translation smoothing."
    *   **Why unresolved:** Quality of multilingual legal terminology and preservation of regulatory nuance were not independently validated. Human expert review covered only German items.
    *   **What evidence would resolve it:** Independent expert review of a stratified sample of FR/IT items, evaluating both reference answers and translation quality against the German source. Discrepancy analysis would reveal translation-induced noise.

## Limitations
*   **Dataset composition:** Uneven domain/task distribution (e.g., EFK only Regulatory Q&A) and potential Italian translation bias confound results.
*   **Scoring parameters:** The chosen thresholds (0.8/0.5) and weights (0.5/0.3/0.2) lack grounding in Swiss legal practice; high P↔I disagreement (91%) indicates boundary ambiguity.
*   **Validation scope:** LLM judge panels may lack niche regulatory knowledge; human validation is limited to one expert and only for German items.
*   **Evaluation setting:** The zero-retrieval condition, while isolating parametric knowledge, limits direct applicability to real-world RAG-augmented compliance workflows.

## Confidence
*   **Benchmark validity (κ=0.605, 73% human C-rate):** Medium (supported by reliability metrics but undermined by dataset/threshold concerns).
*   **Unreliability conclusion (top model 38.2% C):** High (the result is robust and stark).
*   **Task-type difficulty hierarchy:** Medium-Low (clear variation exists but is confounded by dataset composition).
*   **Open-weight model competitiveness:** Medium (top model is open-weight, but ranking stability under judge ablation is unknown).

## Next Checks
1.  **Full human reference validation:** Have a second Swiss legal expert independently validate all 395 reference answers for legal soundness and completeness. This establishes a more reliable ground truth.
2.  **Judge panel ablation study:** Re-compute final rankings using all combinations of the three judges (e.g., exclude Claude, use only GPT-4o+Qwen). Measure rank correlation (Kendall's τ) to quantify the impact of individual judges and detect self-family bias.
3.  **Task-type rebalancing replication:** Create a version of the dataset with an equal number of items (e.g., ~56) for each of the seven task types, maintaining trilingual and domain diversity as much as possible. Re-run the full evaluation to see if the task-type difficulty order persists when composition is balanced.
