# Causality is Key for Interpretability Claims to Generalise
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.16698
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16698
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A causal inference framework using Pearl’s ladder reveals that 53.5% of empirical claims in mechanistic interpretability papers exhibit a "rung gap," where the language implies a stronger causal conclusion (e.g., "encodes") than the evidence (e.g., probing) justifies, explaining common generalisation failures.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses a critical reliability issue in large language model (LLM) interpretability: findings that do not generalise and causal interpretations that exceed the supporting evidence. It introduces a diagnostic framework based on Pearl’s causal ladder, classifying interpretability claims and methods into three rungs—association (L1), intervention (L2), and counterfactual (L3)—and emphasising that each method targets a specific causal estimand whose identifiability must be established. A large-scale pilot study of 186 claims from 50 papers finds most claims overreach relative to their evidence. The framework provides a checklist to align claim strength with methodological support, clarifying what additional evidence (e.g., controlled interventions, necessity/sufficiency tests) is needed for generalisable, reliable findings crucial for AI safety.

## Method Summary
The authors conduct a pilot corpus study to quantify a "rung gap" between interpretability claims and the evidence supporting them. They first construct a corpus of 50 mechanistic interpretability papers (2021–2026) from major venues, extracting 186 empirical claims about model internals. Using a structured codebook, they annotate each claim: the *method rung* (1=observational, 2=interventional, 3=counterfactual) based on the employed technique (e.g., probing=L1, patching=L2), and the *claim rung* based on linguistic markers (e.g., "encodes"=R3, "correlates"=R1). The gap is computed as max(0, claim rung – method rung). Annotation is performed via an LLM-in-the-loop with human calibration and consensus resolution. The analysis aggregates gap rates overall and by claim location/paper type, measuring inter-annotator agreement.

## Key Results
- **Widespread rung elevation:** 53.5% of claims exhibit a positive gap, where claim language implies a higher causal level than the method justifies.
- **Framework utility:** The causal ladder cleanly partitions evidential strength; a gap predicts specific failure modes (e.g., probe accuracy not implying model use, patching not implying necessity).
- **Bridging to safety:** Aligning claim-evidence rungs specifies the exact additional evidence needed for generalisation, moving interpretability toward reliable control for AI safety.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Causal Ladder as Diagnostic
- **Claim:** Interpretability claims can be classified by Pearl's causal ladder rungs, exposing when claim language exceeds method evidence.
- **Mechanism:** Map common verbs (e.g., “encodes”, “mediates”, “correlates”) to L1/association, L2/intervention, L3/counterfactual. The gap (claim rung – method rung) predicts generalization failure.
- **Core assumption:** The causal ladder cleanly partitions causal questions; higher rungs require strictly stronger evidence.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and section 2.1 provide a worked example mapping refusal queries across rungs; Table 1 shows method-to-rung mapping. Neighbouring concepts “Missed Causes and Ambiguous Effects” and “Causality ≠ Decodability” reinforce rung mismatch as a core pitfall.
- **Break condition:** If claim uses L3 terms (“the circuit”, “represents”) but method only supplies L1 (probing) or L2 (patching) evidence, the claim will not generalize beyond tested inputs.

### Mechanism 2: Identifiability Narrows Equivalence Classes
- **Claim:** Causal representation learning (CRL) specifies which latent variables are uniquely recoverable from activations under what assumptions.
- **Mechanism:** Identifiability results (from interventions, natural variation, or inductive biases) constrain the equivalence class of solutions. Two representations behaving identically under all afforded interactions are equivalent; identifiability makes recovered features invariant across all solutions within the class.
- **Core assumption:** Data or intervention set satisfies sufficient support/variability conditions (e.g., disentangled shifts in SSAEs).
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and section 2.2 discuss identifiability; the SSAE example shows how paired perturbations guarantee elementwise identification up to permutation/scaling. Section A.3 details three sources of identifying structure: interventions, natural variation, inductive biases.
- **Break condition:** If identifiability conditions fail (entangled concepts, insufficient variation), multiple non-unique features satisfy the objective, and individual feature semantics become unstable under reparameterization.

### Mechanism 3: Affordances Define Interpretability Bounds
- **Claim:** What can be discovered is bounded by available interactions (affordances), not just observed data statistics.
- **Mechanism:** Gibson’s affordance concept + CRL → interpretation is interaction-relative. The equivalence class is defined by invariance under admissible interventions. Without sufficient interventions, latent structure remains underdetermined.
- **Core assumption:** The set of admissible interventions is known and sufficiently rich to break equivalence classes.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 2.2’s “Identifiability as Interaction-Relative” and iron-filings analogy. Section A.2 gives a detailed discussion of features as affordances. Weak corpus support; “Causality-Driven Neural Network Repair” mentions repair via interventions but not affordances explicitly.
- **Break condition:** If intervention set is sparse (only forward passes), identifiability fails and any assigned feature semantics is underdetermined.

## Foundational Learning
- **Pearl’s Causal Ladder**  
  Why needed: To classify interpretability methods and claims by evidential strength (association, intervention, counterfactual).  
  Quick check: Does your method involve actively changing the model (intervention) or just observing correlations?

- **Identifiability**  
  Why needed: To know when a learned representation is uniquely determined by data and assumptions, preventing arbitrary remixing.  
  Quick check: Can two different feature sets explain your observations equally well under your interventions?

- **Affordances**  
  Why needed: To understand that interpretability is bounded by what manipulations are possible; not all structure is visible without the right interactions.  
  Quick check: What specific interventions can you perform on the model to test your claim?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Estimand → Method rung → Claim rung → Gap score → Failure pattern → Additional evidence recommendation

Critical path:
1. Extract claim verb and causal commitment → map to claim rung
2. Identify method used → map to evidence rung
3. Compute gap (claim rung – method rung)
4. If gap>0, match pattern (e.g., probing→“encodes” = L1→L3)
5. Recommend additional L2/L3 evidence to close gap

Design tradeoffs:
- Stronger claims (L3) require costlier evidence (counterfactuals, uniqueness proofs)
- Scope vs precision: Narrower L1/L2 claims often generalize more reliably than overreaching L3 claims
- Pragmatic utility vs ontological commitment: Focus on intervention affordances rather than “discovering” true latent variables

Failure signatures:
- Rung gap >0 in abstract/body
- High SAE reconstruction but poor steering generalization
- Patching works in-distribution but fails OOD
- Multiple circuits yield same patching effect (non-uniqueness)
- Probe transfers to random labels (decodability ≠ use)

First 3 experiments:
1. Replicate the pilot study: Classify 20 recent MI papers by claim/method rungs; compute gap rate by paper type.
2. Bridge L1→L2: Train linear probe for concept C, construct steering vector from probe direction, test steering reliability in-distribution and on paraphrases.
3. SAE intervention correlation: Train SAE on toy model with known ground-truth features; compare reconstruction loss vs feature intervention effectiveness; test composability of steering directions.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** When does activation patching coincide with counterfactual conditioning in transformers?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 4.1 asks: "When does activation patching coincide with counterfactual conditioning in transformers?" and seeks conditions for L2/L3 equivalence.
- **Why unresolved:** Counterfactuals require structural assumptions beyond controlled interventions; patching may diverge from true counterfactuals due to residual couplings or confounding.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Build controlled tasks with known ground-truth counterfactuals, systematically compare patching effects; analyze model architectures to identify where standard structural assumptions fail.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What minimal equivalence classes guarantee reliable steering interventions?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 4.2 asks: "What are the weakest equivalence classes empirically sufficient for reliable steering?" contrasting blockwise vs. elementwise identifiability.
- **Why unresolved:** Steering often succeeds despite entangled features, but identifiability theory suggests elementwise guarantees are needed for robustness; practical sufficiency thresholds are unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematically vary representation constraints (e.g., block-sparse vs. element-sparse) and measure steering transfer across distribution shifts; identify when weaker classes suffice.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Under what conditions do representation-level interventions compose additively?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 4.3 asks: "Under what conditions do steering vectors compose additively?" and whether identifiability improves compositionality.
- **Why unresolved:** Model edits often fail to compose, but theoretical conditions from causal abstraction (e.g., independent causal mechanisms) are not empirically validated for interventions.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Test composition of steering/interchange interventions on features with varying overlap/orthogonality; derive composition rules from representation geometry (e.g., feature support).

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Why do claim-evidence "rung gaps" persist despite widespread recognition?
- **Basis in paper:** The pilot study found 53.5% of claims exhibit rung elevation, suggesting linguistic conventions outpace methodological alignment; the paper notes this reflects field-wide norms.
- **Why unresolved:** No investigation into cultural or incentives driving rung-elevated language; the diagnostic checklist's impact on writing practices remains untested.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct surveys or writing experiments with intervention groups (e.g., enforce checklist use); correlate gap rates with factors like venue, author seniority, or replication success.

## Limitations
- **Replication fidelity limited:** The original paper's exact corpus list, annotation protocol details, and calibration anchors are not fully disclosed, making precise replication difficult.
- **Claim extraction ambiguity:** The boundary for "empirical claims about model internals" is not rigorously defined, risking inclusion/exclusion bias.
- **Discrete rung oversimplification:** Real interpretability claims often blend multiple causal levels; assigning a single rung may under- or over-estimate the evidential gap.

## Confidence
- **Medium** for the reported 53.5% rung-gap prevalence: empirical but sample- and protocol-dependent.
- **Low‑Medium** for the causal link between rung gap and generalization failure: evidence is correlational (observed failure modes), not interventional.
- **Medium** for identifiability ensuring unique representations: theory is strong, but practical affordances often fall short of assumptions.
- **Medium‑High** for affordances bounding discovery: conceptually robust, yet empirically under-validated in large models.
