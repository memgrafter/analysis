# On the Formal Limits of Alignment Verification

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.08761
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08761
- Reference count: 13
- Primary result: No verification procedure can be simultaneously sound, general, and tractable for certifying AI alignment.

## Executive Summary
This paper establishes a fundamental trilemma for the formal certification of AI alignment. By defining alignment as a semantic property of parameterized functions, the authors prove that any verification procedure can satisfy at most two of three desiderata: soundness (no false positives), generality (full input domain coverage), and tractability (polynomial runtime). The result holds for overparameterized neural networks and transformer-based architectures, revealing inherent computational, representational, and informational barriers to complete alignment verification.

## Method Summary
The paper formalizes alignment verification within a framework where AI systems are parameterized functions, alignment is a semantic metric, and verifiers are procedures with properties soundness, generality, and tractability. Using three independent lemmas, it shows that satisfying any two properties forces the failure of the third: (1) soundness+generality implies intractability due to NP-hardness/undecidability; (2) soundness+tractability implies lack of generality because alignment depends on internal structure invariant under behavioral equivalence; (3) generality+tractability implies unsoundness because finite evaluation cannot cover an infinite domain. Constructive examples demonstrate that each pairwise combination is achievable, establishing the trilemma's tightness.

## Key Results
- Alignment verification cannot be simultaneously sound, general, and tractable.
- The trilemma arises from three barriers: computational (NP-hard/undecidable), representational (symmetry groups), and informational (finite evidence vs. infinite domain).
- Relaxations are possible: bounded domains (exponential time), probabilistic guarantees, or accessing internal representations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Computational Barrier (S+G ⇒ ¬T)
- **Claim:** Soundness and Generality together force the failure of Tractability.
- **Mechanism:** A verifier that is sound (no false positives) and general (covers all inputs) must decide a semantic property over the entire input domain. For feedforward ReLU networks, this requires reasoning about all linear regions, exponential in depth. For Turing-complete architectures (e.g., transformers with chain-of-thought), the problem becomes undecidable by Rice’s theorem.
- **Core assumption:** The function class includes expressive networks with non-trivial semantic alignment properties.
- **Evidence anchors:** NP-hardness for ReLU nets (Katz et al.), undecidability for transformers (Pérez et al.).
- **Break condition:** Relax T (allow exponential/undecidable) or relax G (bound input domain).

### Mechanism 2: Representational Barrier (S+T ⇒ ¬G)
- **Claim:** Soundness and Tractability together force the failure of Generality.
- **Mechanism:** A sound verifier must give identical verdicts for behaviorally equivalent parameters (same input-output function), because alignment depends on internal representations which differ across symmetry-equivalent parameters (e.g., neuron permutations). Thus, a sound+tractable verifier cannot distinguish aligned from misaligned systems that are behaviorally identical on all inputs it can check.
- **Core assumption:** Alignment depends on internal structure and is not invariant under representation symmetries.
- **Evidence anchors:** Non-identifiability from behavioral observation (D’Amour et al.), mesa-optimization (Hubinger et al.), causal non-equivalence (Geiger et al.).
- **Break condition:** Relax S (accept false positives/negatives) or design a verifier that breaks behavioral equivalence (e.g., by accessing internals)—then T or G may fail.

### Mechanism 3: Informational Barrier (G+T ⇒ ¬S)
- **Claim:** Generality and Tractability together force the failure of Soundness.
- **Mechanism:** A general and tractable verifier can only evaluate a finite set of inputs (polynomial runtime). For any such finite evaluation set S, there exist networks that agree on S but differ in alignment off-S. The verifier, seeing identical evidence, must issue the same verdict for both, thus certifying the misaligned one when it certifies the aligned one.
- **Core assumption:** Alignment requires full-domain behavior and verifier has only finite evaluation access.
- **Evidence anchors:** Finite evidence cannot cover infinite domain; proxy gap examples in RLHF and reward hacking (Skalse et al., Gao et al.).
- **Break condition:** Relax S (use probabilistic guarantees) or accept that certification only covers a bounded domain (relax G).

## Foundational Learning
- **Semantic Property**: Alignment is defined as a semantic property of the computed function, not syntactic features. This distinction is crucial for the representational barrier: behaviorally equivalent networks can have different alignment.
  - Quick check: Can two neural networks produce identical outputs on every input yet have different alignment scores? Yes, by symmetry and internal goal divergence.
- **Trilemma Properties (S, G, T)**: These are the minimal formal contents of a certificate. Understanding their pairwise achievability and joint impossibility frames the result.
  - Quick check: If a verification procedure is sound and tractable, which property must it sacrifice? Generality—it cannot cover all inputs while remaining sound.
- **Symmetry Group G(θ)**: This group captures all parameter transformations that leave the input-output function unchanged but change internal representations. It is the engine of the representational barrier.
  - Quick check: Does permuting neurons in a hidden layer change the network’s function? No—but it changes internal activations, which can affect alignment.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: A* (alignment objective) → V (verifier) → F (function class) → G (symmetry group) → D (input domain)
- **Critical path**: Definitions (1–4) → Structural assumptions (1–3) → Pairwise constructions (Section 4) → Lemmas 2–4 (each forces one property to fail given the other two) → Theorem 2 (trilemma) → Corollary 1 (independence).
- **Design tradeoffs**: Which property to relax? Relax T → use expensive SMT tools (S+G). Relax G → bounded domain verification (S+T). Relax S → statistical/probabilistic guarantees (G+T). Each relaxation changes the meaning of "certification."
- **Failure signatures**: Any claim that simultaneously promises soundness, generality, and tractability. For example: "Our verifier runs in polynomial time and certifies alignment for all inputs with zero error." This implicitly violates at least one assumption.
- **First three experiments**:
  1. Demonstrate S+G without T: Implement a simple SMT-based verifier for a small ReLU network and a linear alignment spec. Show it is sound and general but runtime grows exponentially with depth.
  2. Demonstrate S+T without G: Use a bounded verifier (e.g., Reluplex) on a network where alignment depends on off-boundary behavior. Show it misses misalignment occurring outside the bounded domain.
  3. Demonstrate G+T without S: Train a proxy reward model via RLHF on a finite dataset. Show it generalizes to all inputs and runs quickly but has a non-zero proxy gap—certifies some misaligned policies as aligned.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How do the S, G, T properties trade off when relaxed? Specifically, what is the Pareto frontier of achievable guarantee quality across joint relaxations?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 9 identifies this as the central open problem the trilemma defines.
- **Why unresolved:** The trilemma proves no verifier can satisfy all three simultaneously, but it does not characterize how much relaxation in one property enables improvement in the others.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Formal characterizations of trade-off curves, e.g., quantifying how maximum achievable soundness degrades as verification domain size increases.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can mechanistic interpretability produce a representation map Φ(θ) that is both G-invariant and alignment-discriminating?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 6.4 states such a Φ would escape Lemma 3's barrier, but current methods only achieve partial progress.
- **Why unresolved:** Existing interpretability techniques may fail one condition; G-invariance is possible via invariant polynomials, but alignment-discrimination may be inherently unidentifiable.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A constructive algorithm for Φ on standard architectures, or a proof that no such map exists under assumptions. Empirical validation would require showing Φ clusters parameters by alignment score.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What is the computational complexity of verifying nonlinear semantic alignment properties for feedforward ReLU networks?
- **Basis in paper:** Limitations section notes the intractability direction is established for linear specifications; complexity of nonlinear semantic verification remains open.
- **Why unresolved:** Nonlinear properties may be harder (beyond NP) or admit different bounds due to piecewise linear structure.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Complexity reductions from known hard problems to specific nonlinear alignment verification tasks, or algorithms showing membership in a complexity class.

## Limitations
- The exact symmetry group for transformer architectures with attention remains unspecified; permutation symmetry for MLPs is well-known, but attention introduces parameter couplings that may alter the representational barrier.
- No concrete alignment objective A* satisfying required non-triviality and full-domain dependence is instantiated; the diagonal construction in Lemma 4 is abstract without a specific A*.
- The notion of tractability for bounded verification is imprecise; SMT solvers still have exponential worst-case runtime, and the claim may rely on fixed-parameter rather than polynomial dependence.

## Confidence
- Trilemma Theorem (no verifier can satisfy S, G, T simultaneously): **High**
- Computational Barrier (Lemma 2: S+G ⇒ ¬T): **High**
- Representational Barrier (Lemma 3: S+T ⇒ ¬G): **High**
- Informational Barrier (Lemma 4: G+T ⇒ ¬S): **Medium**
- Pairwise achievability (S+G, S+T, G+T): **High**

2. Instantiate a minimal A*: Provide a concrete alignment objective (e.g., worst-case expected loss over a Lipschitz distribution) that meets Assumptions 4 and 7 and verify that the Lemma 4 diagonal construction yields a strictly positive proxy gap Δ(θ)>0.
