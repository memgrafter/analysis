# From Trial by Fire To Sleep Like a Baby: A Lexicon of Anxiety Associations for 20k English Multiword Expressions

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.18692
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18692
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** A high-reliability lexicon, WorryMWEs, provides anxiety association scores for 20,530 English multiword expressions, revealing that MWEs are more emotionally polar than single words and that anxiety-associated MWEs frequently have neutral constituent words.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces WorryMWEs, the first large-scale lexicon mapping over 20,000 English multiword expressions (MWEs) to continuous anxiety association scores. The lexicon was created by crowdsourcing annotations from native speakers on Amazon Mechanical Turk, employing rigorous quality control via gold standard questions and annotator filtering. The resulting resource demonstrates high split-half reliability (0.81–0.95). Analysis shows that a higher proportion of MWEs (53%) are associated with anxiety or calmness compared to unigrams (39.9%), and that many anxiety-linked bigrams are composed of words with neutral anxiety scores, indicating substantial non-compositionality.

## Method Summary
The core method involves selecting frequent MWEs from an existing resource (Muraki et al., 2023a) and adapting a validated word-level anxiety annotation protocol into a questionnaire. This questionnaire, deployed on Mechanical Turk, includes 2% gold-standard MWEs with known anxiety labels to monitor annotator quality. Annotators must achieve at least 80% accuracy on these gold items to have their responses retained. For each MWE, nine valid annotations are averaged to produce a final real-valued anxiety score on a -3 (calm) to +3 (anxious) scale. The reproducibility of these scores is assessed via split-half reliability (SHR) over 1000 random splits.

## Key Results
- **Lexicon Creation:** Produced WorryMWEs, a lexicon with anxiety scores for 20,530 English MWEs (9,891 bigrams, 7,287 trigrams, 3,352 fourgrams).
- **High Reliability:** Split-half reliability scores range from 0.81 to 0.95 (Spearman's ρ) and 0.89 to 0.96 (Pearson's r) across n-gram lengths.
- **Polarity & Non-Compositionality:** 53% of MWEs are anxiety- or calmness-associated (vs. 39.9% of unigrams). Most anxiety-associated bigrams (e.g., in the "neutral–neutral" constituent bin) have constituent words with low anxiety scores from a separate unigram lexicon, suggesting non-compositional meaning.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** A large-scale, reliable anxiety lexicon for MWEs can be created via structured crowdsourcing with robust quality control.
- **Mechanism:** A predefined set of frequent MWEs is annotated by nine native English speakers per item via Mechanical Turk. The questionnaire includes gold questions: 50% provide immediate pop-up feedback on errors, while 50% are silent. Annotators with <80% accuracy on the silent gold set are discarded. Final MWE scores are the mean of retained annotations.
- **Core Assumption:** This filtering and aggregation protocol yields a stable "descriptive norm" (common perception) of anxiety association, mitigating individual bias and noise.
- **Evidence Anchors:** [abstract] & [section 4] describe the full pipeline. [corpus] The high SHR scores (0.81–0.95) are presented as evidence of a stable resource.
- **Break Condition:** Flawed gold question design (ambiguous or incorrect labels) could incorrectly filter annotators or retain noise. A non-diverse or insufficient annotator pool also risks bias.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Split-half reliability (SHR) over 1000 random splits is a valid metric for assessing the reproducibility of the aggregated anxiety scores.
- **Mechanism:** For each MWE, its set of valid annotator scores is randomly split into two halves. The mean score per half is computed. The correlation (Spearman's ρ, Pearson's r) between the two sets of means across all MWEs is calculated and averaged over 1000 trials. High correlation indicates the aggregation is stable.
- **Core Assumption:** SHR effectively models expected variation if the annotation were repeated with an equally large, different annotator sample. A score >0.8 indicates a reliable descriptive norm.
- **Evidence Anchors:** [section 5] defines the SHR procedure. [Table 1] reports the resulting high scores.
- **Break Condition:** If most annotator scores are identical (low variance), SHR will be artificially high even if absolute scores are erroneous. The metric measures consistency of relative ordering, not absolute accuracy.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The non-compositionality of anxiety in MWEs is evidenced by the prevalence of anxiety-associated bigrams whose constituent words are themselves neutral.
- **Mechanism:** For bigrams with both words present in the external unigram lexicon *WorryWords*, each MWE is binned in a 7x7 grid based on the anxiety class of its first and second word. The percentage of anxiety-associated MWEs in each bin is computed. A high percentage in the "neutral–neutral" bin indicates non-compositionality.
- **Core Assumption:** The anxiety score of a word in the unigram lexicon is a valid proxy for its contribution in isolation within an MWE, and compositionality can be approximated by a simple combination of constituent classes.
- **Evidence Anchors:** [section 5.2] & [Figure 4] show the binning analysis, with ~11.35% of anxious bigrams in the neutral–neutral bin versus <0.3% in the anxious–anxious bin.
- **Break Condition:** The unigram lexicon scores may not apply to a word's specific sense within the MWE context. The simple binning model may miss more complex, non-linear compositional interactions (e.g., negation).

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Multiword Expressions (MWEs)**
  - **Why needed here:** The study focuses on phrases (e.g., idioms, collocations) whose meaning is often not predictable from individual words. This is central to interpreting the non-compositionality findings.
  - **Quick check question:** Why might "butterflies in my stomach" (anxious) not derive its anxiety from the literal meanings of "butterflies," "in," "my," and "stomach"?
- **Concept: Descriptive Norms vs. Prescriptive Rules**
  - **Why needed here:** The lexicon captures common *perceptions* of anxiety, not "correct" usage. This is crucial for ethical interpretation (e.g., for tracking language trends, not diagnosing individuals).
  - **Quick check question:** If an anxiety-associated MWE is used sarcastically in a calm context, does the lexicon score reflect the common association or the intended meaning?
- **Concept: Crowdsourcing Annotation & Quality Control**
  - **Why needed here:** The primary data collection method relies on non-expert crowd workers. Understanding gold questions, filtering thresholds, and multi-annotation aggregation is key to evaluating reliability.
  - **Quick check question:** Why can't we simply average nine Mechanical Turk responses without any filtering or gold-standard checks?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Term Selection Module -> Questionnaire Design Module -> Gold Question Generation & QC Logic Module -> Crowdsourcing Interface & Data Collection Module -> Annotation Filtering & Aggregation Module -> Reliability Assessment Module -> Analysis Module
- **Critical path:** Select frequent MWEs -> Design MWE-specific questionnaire with gold items -> Deploy on Mechanical Turk -> Filter low-quality annotators -> Aggregate scores per MWE -> Compute SHR -> Analyze (join with unigram lexicon, binning, distributions).
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Coverage vs. Frequency:** Using only top-frequency MWEs ensures recognizability but excludes less common, potentially anxiety-relevant expressions.
  - **Cost vs. Reliability:** Nine annotations per MWE improves robustness but is expensive. An 80% accuracy threshold is stringent; a lower threshold might increase data but risk quality.
  - **Simplicity of Compositionality Model:** The 7x7 binning is interpretable but may miss subtle semantic interactions like negation or metaphor.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Low number of valid annotations collected for many MWEs.
  - Poor SHR scores (e.g., <0.7), indicating unstable aggregation.
  - Extreme skew in final anxiety scores (e.g., >90% neutral), suggesting task or MWE list issues.
  - Very high annotator rejection rate (>50%), pointing to ambiguous gold items or instructions.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1.  **Replication of Core Reliability:** Re-compute the SHR on the published WorryMWEs data using the same 1000-split procedure to confirm the reported 0.81–0.95 scores.
  2.  **Compositionality Baseline Test:** Manually annotate 100 random "neutral–neutral" bigrams (from the intersection with WorryWords) to verify if they are truly low-anxiety, testing the non-compositionality claim.
  3.  **Gold Question Sensitivity Analysis:** Compare annotator accuracy on pop-up vs. no-popup gold items. A significant difference suggests feedback during the task influences later performance, threatening the independence of the filtering metric. Also, test the impact of a 75% vs. 80% accuracy threshold on the final lexicon.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **How do anxiety associations for MWEs differ across languages and cultures?** WorryMWEs is English-only, annotated by a narrow native speaker pool. Cross-linguistic/cultural patterns are unknown.
- **Is the non-compositionality of anxiety observed in bigrams also present in trigrams and fourgrams?** The compositionality analysis was performed only on bigrams. Longer MWEs, especially idioms, may have different properties.
- **Does adding MWE-level anxiety features improve automatic anxiety detection in text?** The lexicon's utility in downstream applications like sentiment analysis or affective computing remains unevaluated.

## Limitations
- **Reproducibility Blockers:** The exact list of 20,530 MWEs (from Muraki et al., 2023a) and the specific gold items with their labels are not public, preventing faithful recreation of the annotation cohort.
- **Reliability Metric Caveat:** Split-half reliability measures consistency of *relative rankings*, not absolute accuracy against a true anxiety norm. High SHR does not guarantee the scores are "correct."
- **Dependency Risk:** The compositionality analysis critically depends on the external *WorryWords* unigram lexicon; any bias or error there propagates to the non-compositionality conclusion.

## Confidence
- **Lexicon Creation via Crowdsourcing:** Medium (Protocol detailed but missing key inputs for full validation)
- **High Reliability (SHR 0.81–0.95):** Medium-High (SHR computation is explicit and standard, though the metric's limitations temper interpretation)
- **Substantial Non-Compositionality:** Medium (Finding hinges on *WorryWords* validity and a simplistic binning model; alternative explanations like lexical sense mismatch are plausible)

## Next Checks
1.  **SHR Replication:** Using the published WorryMWEs data, recompute split-half reliability over 1000 splits and confirm the reported Spearman's ρ and Pearson's r values for each n-gram length.
2.  **Neutral-Neutral Spot-Check:** For 100 randomly sampled "neutral–neutral" bigrams (from the 8,323 intersection set with WorryWords), conduct a small human annotation study to assess whether they truly exhibit low anxiety.
3.  **Gold Question Dynamics:** Analyze the distribution of annotator accuracy on the "pop-up" versus "no-popup" gold questions. A significant accuracy gap would indicate task learning effects, invalidating the independence assumption for the filtering metric.
