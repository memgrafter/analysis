# When Are RL Hyperparameters Benign? A Study in Offline Goal-Conditioned RL

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.05459  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05459  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Offline goal-conditioned RL hyperparameter landscapes are generally more benign than online RL; bootstrapped TD (HIQL) yields sharp optima and sensitivity to data quality shifts, while quasimetric learning (QRL) maintains broad, stable near‑optimal regions even with modest expert data.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether hyperparameter sensitivity in reinforcement learning is intrinsic or exacerbated by training dynamics, using offline goal‑conditioned RL with controlled data quality shifts. The authors compare two algorithms: HIQL (which relies on bootstrapped temporal‑difference learning) and QRL (which learns quasimetric representations). Across multiple OGBench environments and mixed datasets (varying ratios of expert and exploratory trajectories, plus a non‑stationary schedule), they measure the geometry of the hyperparameter landscape via metrics such as ε‑optimality mass, phase‑to‑phase drift, and gradient alignment. Their findings show that offline RL is generally more robust than online RL. However, HIQL exhibits sharper optima and significant performance drift under data quality schedules, whereas QRL maintains broad, stable near‑optimal regions with as little as 20% expert data. Gradient diagnostics reveal that HIQL suffers stronger destructive inter‑goal interference, which correlates with its brittleness. The study concludes that bootstrapping dynamics, rather than RL itself, drive hyperparameter sensitivity.

## Method Summary
The study conducts a controlled experiment across OGBench environments (antmaze‑medium/large, pointmaze‑medium, humanoidmaze‑medium, antmaze‑teleport) using offline datasets that mix expert (“navigate”) and exploratory (“explore”) trajectories. For each environment, constant expert ratios of 0%, 40%, 80%, 90% and a scheduled sequence (100%→80%→40%→0%) are created. A fixed set of 128 hyperparameter configurations is sampled from a broad space via scrambled Sobol. Training proceeds in phases: the total number of steps \(N\) for a given mixture is estimated as the point where a reference agent reaches 95% of its final success rate. For constant mixtures, phases correspond to fractions of \(N\) (e.g., quarters); for the schedule, each phase uses its own \(N_i\) (from a lookup table). At the start of each phase, all configurations are initialized from the checkpoint of the best configuration (by success ratio) from the previous phase. Each phase is trained for its allocated steps, followed by evaluation on 6 goals × 10 episodes. At regular intervals, gradient diagnostics are recorded: for a batch and a set of goals, critic loss gradients are computed, normalized, and pairwise cosine similarities are stored. After all phases, landscape geometry is analyzed using ε‑optimality mass (proportion of configs achieving near‑optimal return), phase‑to‑phase drift, early‑selection regret, and fANOVA hyperparameter importance.

## Key Results
- Offline RL hyperparameter landscapes are generally more benign than those observed in online RL.  
- HIQL exhibits sharper optima and significant phase‑to‑phase drift under data quality schedules; QRL maintains broad, stable near‑optimal regions with modest expert data (~20%).  
- QRL achieves higher ε‑optimality mass (e.g., 0.80 vs. 0.52 at ε=0.9 in the last phase) and lower drift.  
- Gradient diagnostics show HIQL suffers stronger destructive inter‑goal interference (gradients for different goals are more negatively aligned), correlating with its sensitivity.  
- The results indicate that bootstrapping dynamics, not RL per se, drive hyperparameter brittleness.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
HIQL’s reliance on bootstrapped TD learning creates a loss landscape where gradients derived from different goals interfere destructively. This interference yields sharp optima and high sensitivity to hyperparameters and shifts in data distribution. QRL, by learning a quasimetric representation, avoids cross‑goal bootstrapping; it structures the value function as a goal‑dependent distance, which decouples the objectives for different goals and produces a broad plateau of good hyperparameters. The offline setting further contributes to benign landscapes by fixing the data distribution, eliminating the compounding effects of policy‑dependent data collection.

## Foundational Learning
**Offline Goal‑Conditioned RL** – Learns from a static dataset without environment interaction; policy is conditioned on a goal state. *Why needed:* Enables safe and practical deployment where exploration is expensive or dangerous. *Quick check:* Verify that the training loop never queries the environment for new transitions and that each batch includes goal information.

**Bootstrapped TD (HIQL)** – Standard RL approach that learns value functions by regressing onto targets constructed from the same network (e.g., Bellman backup). *Why needed:* Provides a baseline to contrast with representation‑based methods. *Quick check:* Confirm critic loss includes a target from a target network and Bellman error (e.g., \(\|q_\theta(s,a,g) - (r + \gamma \bar{q}_{\theta^-}(s',\pi(s'))\|^2\)).

**Quasimetric Representation (QRL)** – Learns an asymmetric distance function \(d(g,s)\) that satisfies the quasimetric axioms; used to shape rewards or value functions. *Why needed:* Offers a structured alternative to bootstrapping that reduces inter‑goal interference. *Quick check:* Ensure the representation is regularized (e.g., triangle inequality penalty) and that the loss includes a quasimetric consistency term.

**Hyperparameter Landscape Geometry** – Quantifies robustness by measuring the volume of hyperparameter space that yields near‑optimal performance. *Why needed:* Directly characterizes sensitivity; a broad plateau indicates benign tuning requirements. *Quick check:* Sample many random hyperparameter configurations, evaluate their final returns, and compute the proportion that achieve within ε of the best found return (ε‑optimality mass).

**Phase‑to‑Phase Drift** – The change in best‑configuration performance between successive phases when the data distribution shifts (e.g., via a schedule). *Why needed:* Captures stability under non‑stationarity; high drift suggests brittle performance. *Quick check:* Compare the success ratio of the best configuration at the end of each phase; drift \(\Delta\) is the absolute difference between consecutive phases.

**Destructive Inter‑Goal Interference** – Phenomenon where gradients from different goals oppose each other during critic updates, causing optimization instability and sharp optima. *Why needed:* Explains the underlying cause of brittleness in bootstrapped methods. *Quick check:* For a set of goals, compute the normalized critic loss gradients and their pairwise cosine similarities; a heavy negative tail (values near -1) indicates destructive interference.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Data Collection -> Dataset Mixing (ratio/schedule) -> Hyperparameter Sampling (Sobol) -> Phased Training Loop (Checkpoint Reset) -> Evaluation (Success Ratio, Return) -> Gradient Diagnostics (Normalized Gradient Pairs) -> Landscape Analysis (ε‑mass, drift, fANOVA)

**Critical Path**  
1. Generate mixed offline datasets for required environments and expert ratios (including schedule).  
2. For each algorithm (HIQL, QRL) and regime, sample a fixed configuration set \(C\).  
3. Determine total steps \(N\) per mixture (convergence to 95% of final success).  
4. For each phase (either quarters of \(N\) or schedule‑based lengths): load the best checkpoint from previous phase, train all configs in \(C\) for the phase length, evaluate goals, log gradients.  
5. After all phases, compute landscape metrics (ε‑optimality mass, phase drift, early‑selection regret, fANOVA) and gradient alignment statistics.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *Fixed vs. resampled hyperparameter set:* Fixed \(C\) enables direct phase‑to‑phase comparison but may miss high‑performing regions that only appear later.  
- *Phase boundaries:* Using fixed fractions of a reference agent’s convergence simplifies setup but may not align with algorithm‑specific learning curves.  
- *Checkpoint reset from best config:* Isolates phase effects but risks inheriting a suboptimal representation if the best is not representative.  
- *Evaluation on 6 goals:* Balances computational cost and goal coverage; a small set may not capture full task diversity.  
- *Gradient logging frequency:* 10× per phase yields reliable CDFs with moderate overhead; less frequent logging risks undersampling high‑variance gradients.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Dataset ratios cannot be verified (e.g., trajectory origins not tracked) → incorrect data quality shifts.  
- Hyperparameter set changes across phases → incomparable landscape metrics.  
- Gradients not normalized before cosine similarity → misleading interference measures.  
- Using constant‑phase fractions for a schedule → distorted drift estimates.  
- Deviating from specified network architecture (e.g., missing layer norm) → performance diverges from reported baselines.

**First Experiments**  
1. **Baseline verification:** Train HIQL and QRL on standard OGBench antmaze‑medium with default hyperparameters; confirm success rates match published numbers (e.g., >90% on expert data).  
2. **Landscape sanity check:** For antmaze‑medium 0% expert, run phased training with 128 sampled configs; compute ε‑optimality mass at final phase. Expect QRL > HIQL (e.g., 0.80 vs. 0.52 at ε=0.9) and lower drift.  
3. **Gradient alignment validation:** During the same run, collect normalized gradient pairs; plot empirical CDFs of cosine similarities. Confirm HIQL exhibits a heavier negative tail than QRL, indicating destructive interference.

## Limitations
- Phase boundary fractions for constant regimes are unspecified (assumed quarterly); different timings could alter drift metrics.  
- Total‑step values \(N\) for several environment/ratio combinations are missing from Table 10, requiring additional convergence experiments.  
- Critical implementation details (gradient extraction protocol, quasimetric penalty scheduling, whether \(C\) is strictly fixed) are not fully documented and may affect reproducibility.  
- The causal claim that bootstrapping drives brittleness rests on a comparison of only two algorithms, limiting generalizability.

## Confidence
- Offline landscapes are more benign than online RL: **Low** – comparison is indirect; no direct online baseline tested.  
- HIQL exhibits sharper optima/stronger drift; QRL shows broad stable regions: **Medium** – metrics within tested regimes are clear, but depend on unspecified phase fractions and missing convergence data.  
- Bootstrapping dynamics, not RL itself, drive hyperparameter brittleness: **Low** – overgeneralizes from two‑algorithm comparison; causal isolation incomplete.

## Next Checks
1. **Phase boundary sensitivity test:** Re‑run analysis with alternative phase fractions (e.g., thirds, halves) for constant regimes to confirm drift metrics are robust to timing shifts.  
2. **Fill missing convergence data:** For every environment/ratio lacking \(N\) in Table 10, independently determine convergence steps (95% final success rate) and re‑execute the full phased training pipeline.  
3. **Implementation audit:** Inspect OGBench codebase to verify gradient extraction (number of goals, normalization before dot‑product), quasimetric penalty scheduling, and that \(C\) is unchanged across phases; if code unavailable, request these details from the authors.
