# Assessing Large Language Models for Medical QA: Zero-Shot and LLM-as-a-Judge Evaluation

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2602.14564
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14564
*   **Reference count:** 23
*   **Primary result:** Llama 3.3 70B Instruct achieved the highest zero-shot medical QA performance, while the smaller Mixture-of-Experts Llama-4-Maverick 17B demonstrated strong efficiency, attaining 94% of the larger model's score.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates five large language models (LLMs) on a medical question-answering task in a zero-shot setting to determine their suitability for low-resource healthcare environments. Using 3,000 samples from the iCliniq dataset, performance is measured through a dual framework: standard n-gram overlap metrics (BLEU, ROUGE) and an LLM-as-a-Judge evaluation using Claude Sonnet 4, which rates responses on accuracy, completeness, safety, clarity, and helpfulness. Results indicate that larger parameter counts generally correlate with better performance, but architectural innovations like Mixture-of-Experts can significantly narrow the gap. The findings underscore that n-gram metrics alone are insufficient for clinical assessment and that multi-dimensional evaluation is essential for gauging deployment readiness.

## Method Summary
The study employs a zero-shot evaluation protocol on a randomly selected subset of 3,000 question-answer pairs from the iCliniq dataset. Each of the five target models generates a response to a patient question using a fixed prompt. Performance is assessed in two ways: (1) automatically computed BLEU and ROUGE scores against expert reference answers, and (2) a multi-dimensional evaluation where Claude Sonnet 4 scores each generated response on five clinical criteria. The final analysis compares model rankings across both metric types and investigates correlations between lexical overlap and holistic quality.

## Key Results
*   Llama 3.3 70B Instruct attained the highest scores across BLEU-1 (0.2207), ROUGE-1 (0.2761), and the LLM-as-a-Judge overall score (4.40/5).
*   Llama-4-Maverick 17B, a Mixture-of-Experts model, achieved competitive performance (ROUGE-1 0.2597, judge score 4.23) with less than a quarter of the parameters, demonstrating significant efficiency.
*   GPT-5-mini severely underperformed (BLEU-1 0.0124), highlighting that model name or recency does not guarantee capability in this domain.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Scaling with Parameter Count
*   **Claim:** Larger model parameter counts correlate with superior zero-shot medical QA performance as measured by n-gram overlap metrics.
*   **Mechanism:** Increased parameters enhance the model's capacity to encode and retrieve complex medical knowledge and linguistic patterns during pre-training, leading to generated responses with higher lexical similarity to expert references.
*   **Core assumption:** Performance is primarily determined by the breadth and depth of knowledge acquired during pre-training, which scales with parameter count.
*   **Evidence anchors:** The paper's results show Llama 3.3 70B Instruct surpassed all others in BLEU and ROUGE, attributing this to its 70B parameter size. This aligns with observed scaling benefits in clinical tasks.
*   **Break condition:** The correlation breaks for GPT-5-mini (an outlier) and is non-linear when comparing the 70B model to the 17B Maverick, suggesting architectural innovations can decouple performance from raw parameter count.

### Mechanism 2: Architectural Efficiency via Mixture-of-Experts
*   **Claim:** Architectural innovations like Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) can mitigate the performance gap between large and smaller models, enabling competitive efficiency for resource-constrained deployment.
*   **Mechanism:** MoE architectures activate only a subset of parameters (experts) per inference, increasing effective model capacity for knowledge storage without a proportional increase in computational cost (FLOPs) during generation.
*   **Core assumption:** The efficiency gains from sparse activation preserve enough representational power to maintain medical reasoning capability as measured by lexical and judge metrics.
*   **Evidence anchors:** Llama-4-Maverick 17B achieved competitive results with less than a quarter of the 70B model's parameters. The paper suggests the MoE architecture provides these efficiency benefits.
*   **Break condition:** The mechanism fails if activation sparsity leads to incorrect expert selection for complex queries, causing inconsistent reasoning. The claim is specific to the Maverick MoE implementation.

### Mechanism 3: Necessity of Multi-Dimensional LLM-as-a-Judge Evaluation
*   **Claim:** A multi-dimensional evaluation framework (LLM-as-a-Judge) is necessary to assess clinical utility beyond what n-gram metrics can capture.
*   **Mechanism:** N-gram metrics measure surface-level lexical overlap but cannot evaluate factual correctness (accuracy), risk mitigation (safety), or practical utility (helpfulness). An LLM judge, prompted with a rubric, simulates a clinical expert's multidimensional assessment.
*   **Core assumption:** A powerful LLM (Claude Sonnet 4) can reliably act as a proxy for a human medical expert when evaluating dimensions like accuracy and safety.
*   **Evidence anchors:** The paper states n-gram metrics are insufficient for clinical reliability. It finds ROUGE-1 has strong correlation (r=0.89) with overall judge scores, while BLEU has moderate correlation (r=0.67) with medical accuracy. GPT-5-mini's high safety score despite poor n-gram scores highlights the need for multi-dimensional assessment.
*   **Break condition:** Invalid if the judge model has biases, applies the rubric inconsistently, or cannot grasp subtle medical nuances. The rubric's validity is also conditional on review by a single medical professional.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept:** Zero-shot evaluation.
    *   **Why needed here:** To test models' inherent capabilities acquired during pre-training without the confounder of task-specific fine-tuning, isolating the effect of architecture and scale on general medical knowledge.
    *   **Quick check question:** If a model is fine-tuned on medical QA, what aspect of its performance would zero-shot evaluation specifically fail to measure?
*   **Concept:** N-gram based evaluation metrics (BLEU, ROUGE).
    *   **Why needed here:** They provide a standardized, automated, and reproducible baseline for measuring lexical overlap with reference answers, allowing comparison across many models and serving as a common ground for historical comparison.
    *   **Quick check question:** Why would a model with a high BLEU score potentially still generate a medically dangerous or incorrect answer?
*   **Concept:** Clinical safety and risk-aware response generation.
    *   **Why needed here:** Medical QA carries risk of harm if models provide definitive diagnoses, omit disclaimers, or suggest unsafe actions. Evaluation must explicitly assess safety alongside accuracy to gauge deployment viability.
    *   **Quick check question:** What specific elements in a response (beyond factual correctness) would a "safety" score of 5/5 versus 1/5 likely reflect?

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:** Dataset (iCliniq 3k subset) -> Models (5 LLMs) -> Evaluation Pipeline (Generate response -> Compute BLEU/ROUGE -> LLM-Judge scoring) -> Scoring & Analysis (Aggregate metrics, compare rankings, analyze trends).
*   **Critical path:** Data Loading → Zero-shot Inference Loop (per model) → Traditional Metric Calculation → Judge Inference & Scoring → Statistical Aggregation & Comparison.
*   **Design tradeoffs:**
    *   **Automatic (BLEU/ROUGE) vs. LLM-Judge:** Automatic metrics are cheap, fast, reproducible but clinically shallow. LLM-Judge is expensive, slower, potentially biased but clinically relevant. The study uses both complementarily.
    *   **Zero-shot vs. Fine-tuned:** Zero-shot tests general capability but likely underperforms a fine-tuned model. The tradeoff is evaluating pre-training power vs. task-specific optimization.
    *   **Model Scale vs. Deployment Cost:** Larger models (70B) score higher but require significant GPU memory. Smaller/MoE models offer efficiency at some cost to peak accuracy. The 94% performance/parameter ratio for Maverick is a key data point.
*   **Failure signatures:**
    *   **Judge-Derived:** High variance in judge scores for the same model (inconsistent rubric application). Judge model refusing to score certain responses.
    *   **Metric-Derived:** Extremely low BLEU (<0.05) coupled with low judge scores (like GPT-5-mini) indicates fundamental failure in generating coherent medical text. Disconnect between high ROUGE-1 and low judge accuracy scores suggests lexical overlap without semantic correctness.
    *   **Operational:** Inference timeouts or crashes on large models (70B). Judge API rate limits or cost overruns.
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1.  **Sanity Check:** Run the full pipeline on 10 sample Q&A pairs. Verify all 5 models produce non-empty responses, BLEU/ROUGE are calculable, and the judge returns 5 scores. Check that Llama 3.3 70B is highest on n-grams and overall.
    2.  **Metric Correlation Deep Dive:** Compute Pearson/Spearman correlations between all BLEU/ROUGE scores and each judge dimension (accuracy, safety, etc.) across all 3000 samples. Confirm the paper's finding of strong ROUGE-1/judge score correlation and moderate BLEU/accuracy correlation. Identify any outlier dimensions.
    3.  **Efficiency Frontier Test:** For the two highest-performing models (Llama 3.3 70B, Llama-4-Maverick 17B), measure inference latency (tokens/sec) and memory footprint on your target hardware. Calculate the performance-per-parameter and performance-per-unit-time ratios to validate the "competitive efficiency" claim in your specific environment.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Open Question 1:** What metrics beyond BLEU/ROUGE are necessary to evaluate medical accuracy and safety in LLM-generated QA responses?
    *   **Basis in paper:** The authors state that n-gram metrics cannot assess factual correctness or clinical utility, and note a gap between benchmark scores and real-world effectiveness.
    *   **Why unresolved:** Current evaluations rely on automated or LLM-based scores without direct clinical validation, leaving uncertainty about actual patient safety.
    *   **What evidence would resolve it:** Studies correlating evaluation scores with expert clinician assessments and patient health outcomes, or development of gold-standard benchmarks validated through medical trials.
*   **Open Question 2:** Which architectural innovations (e.g., mixture-of-experts) allow efficient models like Llama-4-Maverick to achieve performance close to larger models in medical QA, and how can these be optimized for low-resource settings?
    *   **Basis in paper:** Results show Maverick (17B) achieves 94% of the 70B model's ROUGE-1, but the paper provides no analysis of why this efficiency occurs.
    *   **Why unresolved:** Unknown which specific architectural components or training techniques contribute to parameter efficiency in medical reasoning.
    *   **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablation studies and mechanistic interpretability analyses comparing Maverick and larger models, measuring expert utilization, attention patterns, and knowledge representation.
*   **Open Question 3:** Is zero-shot evaluation sufficient for predicting clinical deployment success, or is domain-specific fine-tuning indispensable for safe medical LLM use?
    *   **Basis in paper:** The study uses zero-shot to test "inherent medical knowledge" but cites prior work highlighting a gap between benchmark performance and real-world clinical effectiveness.
    *   **Why unresolved:** No direct comparison between zero-shot and fine-tuned models on clinically relevant metrics like safety or patient comprehension.
    *   **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments comparing zero-shot, few-shot, and fine-tuned LLMs on medical QA, evaluated by clinicians for factual accuracy, safety, and helpfulness in simulated consultations.

## Limitations
*   Unspecified dataset split and random seed for the 3,000-sample subset introduce variance risk.
*   Incomplete reporting of the LLM-as-a-Judge protocol (exact Claude version, rubric text, weighted formula) hinders faithful replication.
*   Model accessibility issues (GPT-5-mini, Llama-4-Maverick may not be publicly available) may block exact reproduction.

## Confidence
*   **Scaling with parameters:** Medium — Direct evidence from Llama 3.3 70B superiority, but outliers (GPT-5-mini, Maverick) show non-linear scaling.
*   **MoE efficiency:** Medium — Maverick achieves 94% of larger model’s performance, but only one MoE model tested; broader validation needed.
*   **Necessity of multi-dimensional evaluation:** Medium — Correlation gaps and GPT-5-mini safety anomaly support claim, but judge reliability not independently verified.

## Next Checks
1.  **Reproducibility audit:** Release the exact 3,000 sample IDs and random seed; recompute metrics to confirm reported scores.
2.  **Judge reliability study:** Have two board-certified physicians independently rate a stratified sample (n=300) using the same rubric; compute agreement (Cohen’s κ) with Claude’s scores.
3.  **Generalization test:** Apply the same pipeline to a different medical QA dataset (e.g., MedQA-USMLE) to verify if scaling and efficiency trends hold across domains.
