# ECG Classification on PTB-XL: A Data-Centric Approach with Simplified CNN-VAE

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.07558
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07558
- Reference count: 9
- Primary result: Achieved 87.01% binary accuracy and 0.7454 weighted F1-score on PTB-XL, but with low HYP recall (50.2%).

## Executive Summary
This study challenges the reliance on complex deep learning architectures for ECG classification by implementing a data-centric approach on the PTB-XL dataset. The core method combines systematic lead-wise z-score normalization, targeted oversampling of the minority HYP class and downsampling of the majority NORM class, and a simplified 1D CNN-VAE classifier with only 197,093 trainable parameters. The model achieves competitive overall performance (87.01% binary accuracy) but reveals a specific challenge in detecting cardiac hypertrophy (HYP), with a recall of only 50.2%. This underscores the persistent difficulty of classifying subtle ECG patterns in imbalanced medical datasets, even with careful preprocessing and rebalancing.

## Method Summary
Preprocessing involves lead-wise z-score normalization computed solely on the training set to preserve lead-specific diagnostic information while standardizing amplitude disparities. To address severe class imbalance (NORM 43.7%, HYP 12.2%), the training set is rebalanced to approximately 4,000 samples per major class: HYP is oversampled from 2,392 to 4,000, and NORM is downsampled from 8,564 to 4,000. The architecture is a simplified CNN-VAE: three Conv1D blocks (64→128→256 filters) with BatchNorm, MaxPooling, and Dropout extract hierarchical temporal features; GlobalAveragePooling yields a 256-D vector projected to a 32-D latent space (`z_mean` used directly, without stochastic sampling); two dense layers with high Dropout (0.5) process this before a 5-class sigmoid output. Training uses binary cross-entropy loss with per-class sample weights, including a 1.5× multiplier for HYP, and standard callbacks (early stopping, reduce LR on plateau).

## Key Results
- Binary accuracy: 87.01% on PTB-XL test set.
- Weighted F1-score: 0.7454 across five diagnostic superclasses.
- HYP recall: 50.2%, indicating poor detection of hypertrophy despite balancing efforts.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Lead-wise z-score normalization** standardizes each ECG channel independently, preventing amplitude disparities from dominating learning and preserving lead-specific diagnostic information. For each of the 12 leads, the mean (µ) and standard deviation (σ) are computed only on the training set and applied to normalize all splits. This removes lead-specific baselines and gain variations while maintaining relative waveform morphology. The core assumption is that distinct physiological signal ranges per lead (e.g., aVL vs. V5) are relevant features; common global normalization would distort these patterns. Evidence: the abstract and section 3.3 define the formula `x' = (x - µ_j) / (σ_j + ε)` per lead `j`; neighboring papers also emphasize per-lead normalization. Break condition: if leads are already consistently calibrated or if normalization inadvertently removes clinically relevant amplitude differences (e.g., QRS voltage in hypertrophy).

**Mechanism 2: Targeted sampling and class weighting** mitigate severe class imbalance, forcing the model to learn minority class features without overwhelming bias toward NORM. The training set is rebalanced to ~4,000 samples per major class: HYP oversampled from 2,392 to 4,000, NORM downsampled from 8,564 to 4,000. Additionally, a sample weight `w_j = n_total / (n_classes * n_j)` is applied during training, with a 1.5× multiplier for HYP. Core assumption: the model’s default behavior on imbalanced data is to prioritize majority-class accuracy; explicit rebalancing and weighting are necessary to boost sensitivity for rare conditions. Evidence: abstract and sections 3.2 & 3.3 provide counts and weight formula; Table 2 shows HYP recall remains low despite balancing, indicating inherent difficulty. Break condition: if oversampling introduces significant overfitting to synthetic/repeated HYP samples, or if downsampling NORM discards rare but important normal variations.

**Mechanism 3: Simplified CNN-VAE architecture** achieves competitive performance by focusing on hierarchical local feature extraction via 1D convolutions, while the VAE-like latent space (using `z_mean` directly) provides regularization and compact representation without reconstruction loss overhead. Three Conv1D blocks (64→128→256 filters) with BatchNorm, MaxPooling, Dropout extract temporal features; GlobalAveragePooling creates a 256-D vector projected to a 32-D latent space; two dense layers with high Dropout (0.5) process this before the sigmoid output. The model avoids a full VAE decoder and custom Lambda layers, simplifying training and serialization. Core assumption: ECG diagnostic patterns (e.g., QRS complex, ST-segment) are captured by local temporal filters; a deep but narrow network with strong regularization is sufficient when inputs are well-preprocessed and balanced. The latent space imposes a smoothness prior that aids generalization. Evidence: abstract highlights simplicity and performance; section 4.1 details layer configuration and the choice to use `z_mean`; section 5 Table 4 shows this model matches ResNet-50 with far fewer parameters. Break condition: if ECG patterns require modeling very long-range temporal dependencies that 1D convolutions with local kernels cannot capture, or if the latent dimension (32) is too small to preserve discriminative features for subtle classes like HYP.

## Foundational Learning

Quick check: Why might normalizing all 12 leads together with a single global mean/std be harmful to model performance?

**Concept: Class Imbalance in Medical ML**  
Why needed: The paper’s core intervention addresses the skew where NORM is 43.7% and HYP is 12.2%. Without this foundation, one might misinterpret overall accuracy as success while missing the failure on critical minority classes.  
Quick check: What is a potential downside of naive random oversampling (without downsampling NORM) on this dataset?

**Concept: Multi-label vs. Multi-class Classification**  
Why needed: The PTB-XL labels are not mutually exclusive (a recording can have multiple superclasses). This affects loss function choice (binary cross-entropy) and metric interpretation (subset vs. binary accuracy).  
Quick check: How does subset accuracy (59%) differ in meaning from binary accuracy (87%) for this task, and which is more stringent?

## Architecture Onboarding

Component map: Input (12-lead ECG, 1000 samples) -> Lead-wise z-score normalization -> Targeted sampling (oversample HYP, downsample NORM) -> Encoder (3 Conv1D blocks with BN/MP/Dropout) -> GlobalAveragePooling -> Latent projection (Dense to 32-D z_mean) -> Classifier Head (2 Dense blocks with ReLU/BN/Dropout) -> Output (5-D sigmoid)

Critical path: Data loading -> lead-wise normalization (fit on train, transform all) -> apply sampling indices -> model forward -> loss weighting -> backpropagation.

Design tradeoffs:
- Simplicity vs. performance: 197k parameters vs. millions in ResNet/transformer models. Gains: fast inference, small footprint, less overfitting risk. Cost: lower HYP recall (50.2%).
- VAE simplification: Using `z_mean` skips stochastic sampling and decoder loss, easing training/serialization but losing explicit regularization from KL divergence and reconstruction objective.
- Sampling hybrid: Combining over/downsampling with loss weights helps but may not resolve feature overlap for HYP.

Failure signatures:
1. HYP recall remains ~50% despite balancing → indicates feature indistinctness or label noise.
2. Training/validation loss diverge after sampling → oversampling artifacts causing overfit to synthetic/repeated samples.
3. High false positives in NORM (225 FP) → model may be too sensitive to minor artifacts; consider threshold tuning.

First 3 experiments:
1. Ablation 1 (No balancing): Train on original imbalanced data → expect NORM recall >95%, HYP recall <40%. Confirms imbalance impact.
2. Ablation 2 (Global normalization): Apply single µ/σ across all 12 leads → expect overall accuracy drop, especially on lead-sensitive patterns.
3. Ablation 3 (No VAE latent): Remove the latent projection (feed encoder output directly to classifier) → test if the 32-D bottleneck helps or hurts generalization.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can the detection of cardiac hypertrophy (HYP) be significantly improved given its subtle ECG patterns and the current model's low 50.2% recall? The authors suggest future work on advanced resampling (SMOTE), focal loss, and domain-specific feature engineering, but the current hybrid balancing strategy is insufficient.
2. Does the proposed data-centric, simplified CNN-VAE approach generalize to other ECG databases with different patient demographics, recording hardware, and annotation standards? The authors explicitly state that generalization remains unexplored but essential for clinical deployment, and mention testing on CPSC2018 and Georgia datasets.
3. What are the specific ECG features and temporal patterns the model uses for its predictions, and can this be made interpretable to clinicians without sacrificing performance? The paper provides no interpretability analysis (e.g., saliency maps, latent space visualization); future work includes attention mechanisms and SHAP/LIME.
4. Would incorporating longer-range temporal modeling (e.g., recurrent or transformer layers) or attention mechanisms improve classification performance, particularly for conditions with subtle or evolving patterns like HYP and STTC? The authors acknowledge that 1D convolutions use local receptive fields only and that recurrent or transformer layers might capture longer-range patterns.

## Limitations
- Unreported details: exact HYP oversampling method (with/without replacement?) and empirical basis for the 1.5× HYP weight multiplier (described as ad-hoc).
- Persistent HYP recall of 50.2% despite balancing suggests feature indistinctness or label noise, but error patterns (e.g., confusion with STTC/MI) are not analyzed.
- The claimed "simplified VAE" contribution (using `z_mean` only) is not isolated via ablation; the benefit versus a non-VAE CNN with equivalent dimensionality remains speculative.

## Confidence
- Data-centric preprocessing (lead-wise z-score + targeted sampling): High (procedures mathematically defined, aligned with domain practice).
- Simplified CNN-VAE architecture & parameter count: Medium-High (layer specs complete, but VAE bottleneck contribution not isolated).
- HYP detection failure as a dataset limitation: High (result reported; "why" inferred but not validated via feature visualization or clinician review).
- Competitive performance vs. complex models: Medium (fair comparison but limited to one dataset; generalizability unaddressed).

## Next Checks
1. Ablation: Global vs. lead-wise normalization. Retrain with a single global mean/std across all 12 leads; expect drop in overall accuracy and HYP recall if lead-specific patterns are degraded.
2. Sampling integrity audit. From the balanced training indices, compute exact class counts and verify HYP oversampling indices contain duplicates (indicates sampling with replacement). Also check for unintended data leakage between train/validation splits post-sampling.
3. HYP error case analysis. Manually inspect false-negative HYP samples (predicted non-HYP). Assess whether confusion stems from overlapping STTC/MI features, low voltage criteria not captured, or label ambiguity in PTB-XL’s multi-label schema.
