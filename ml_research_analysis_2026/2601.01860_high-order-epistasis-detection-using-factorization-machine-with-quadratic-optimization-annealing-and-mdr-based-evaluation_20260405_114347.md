# High-Order Epistasis Detection Using Factorization Machine with Quadratic Optimization Annealing and MDR-Based Evaluation
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.01860
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.01860
- **Reference count**: 29
- **Primary result**: FMQA with MDR achieves 100% success for additive 3‑5 locus epistasis, 70‑100% for threshold models, and scales efficiently with problem size compared to exhaustive search.

## Executive Summary
The paper presents FMQA, a black‑box optimization framework for detecting high‑order epistatic interactions in genetic association studies. By iteratively training a Factorization Machine surrogate on Classification Error Rates from Multifactor Dimensionality Reduction (MDR), FMQA guides a QUBO‑based Ising solver toward promising SNP combinations. Experiments on simulated case‑control datasets with up to 1000 loci and interaction orders up to 5 demonstrate near‑perfect identification of ground‑truth epistasis for additive models and strong performance (70‑100%) for threshold models, while requiring orders of magnitude fewer evaluations than exhaustive search.

## Method Summary
FMQA starts with ten random d‑locus combinations and computes their full‑sample MDR classification error. A Factorization Machine (FM, rank K=10) is trained on these (combination, error) pairs. The FM parameters are transformed into a QUBO matrix with a penalty term λ=2 to enforce exactly d selected loci. A simulated‑annealing Ising solver (1 s timeout) returns a binary vector; if its cardinality is not d, a neighbor is generated by swapping one selected and one unselected locus. MDR evaluates the candidate(s), and the results are added to the training set. This loop—FM training → QUBO construction → Ising solve → neighbor generation → MDR evaluation—repeats for up to 1000 iterations or until the ground‑truth combination is found. The entire process is run over ten random seeds to estimate success rates and iteration counts.

## Key Results
- **Success rates**: 100% for additive models (d=3,4,5) across varying numbers of loci; 70–100% for threshold (no‑marginal‑effect) models, with slight decline for d=5 and larger N.
- **Efficiency**: Average iterations to success range from 28 (smallest problems) to 658 (largest), growing polynomially versus the exponential blow‑up of exhaustive search (e.g., from ~10⁷ to ~10¹² candidate sets).
- **Robustness**: The method remains effective even when epistatic interactions lack marginal effects, though performance degrades for the most challenging d=5 threshold cases.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Factorization Machines offer a compact low‑rank representation of high‑order interactions, allowing the surrogate to learn the MDR error landscape from few samples. The QUBO formulation translates this continuous model into a binary optimization problem that maps naturally onto an Ising machine, enabling fast stochastic exploration via simulated annealing. The neighbor‑swap step maintains feasibility while probing local variations, and retraining the FM with new data refines the surrogate throughout the search. MDR provides a non‑parametric, model‑free error measure that is sensitive to nonlinear epistasis. Together, these components create a feedback loop that concentrates evaluations on promising regions of the combinatorial space, yielding high detection rates with dramatically fewer function calls than exhaustive enumeration.

## Foundational Learning
- **Epistasis**: Interaction among genetic loci where the combined effect deviates from the sum of individual effects. Why needed: The paper’s core objective is to detect such interactions in genome‑wide data. Quick check: Can you describe a scenario where two SNPs show epistasis but no marginal effect?
- **Multifactor Dimensionality Reduction (MDR)**: Non‑parametric method that pools multi‑locus genotypes into high‑and‑low‑risk groups and computes classification error. Why needed: MDR supplies the objective (CER) that quantifies predictive performance of a candidate SNP set. Quick check: Given a 2×2 table of case/control counts for two genotypes, how would you compute MDR error?
- **Factorization Machine (FM)**: Linear‑model‑based approach that factorizes pairwise interaction parameters into low‑dimensional embeddings, enabling efficient modeling of high‑order effects. Why needed: FM acts as a surrogate that can be trained on a small set of evaluated combinations and then optimized to propose new ones. Quick check: How does FM reduce the number of parameters compared to a full quadratic model?
- **Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO)**: Optimization problem with a quadratic objective over binary variables, equivalent to an Ising model. Why needed: The learned FM can be expressed as a QUBO, allowing the use of specialized hardware/heuristics (Ising solvers) to search for optimal binary vectors. Quick check: Write a simple QUBO for a max‑cut problem on two nodes.
- **Simulated Annealing**: Probabilistic metaheuristic that mimics physical annealing to escape local minima. Why needed: Provides a generic, time‑bounded solver for the QUBO, yielding diverse candidate solutions. Quick check: What effect does the cooling schedule have on solution quality?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**: Data Generation -> Initial Random Sampling -> FM Training -> QUBO Construction -> Ising Solver -> Candidate Solution -> (Cardinality Check) -> Neighbor Generation -> MDR Evaluation -> Dataset Update -> (loop back to FM Training)

**Critical Path**: The iterative core consists of FM training (updates surrogate), QUBO construction (encodes objective), Ising solve (generates candidate), and MDR evaluation (provides true objective value). The loop terminates when the ground‑truth combination is found or the iteration limit is reached.

**Design Tradeoffs**:
- *Surrogate capacity vs data efficiency*: Higher FM rank K captures more complex interactions but requires more samples to train reliably.
- *Penalty λ*: Too small yields infeasible solutions (wrong number of loci); too large may dominate the objective and distort the search.
- *Solver timeout*: Longer annealing improves solution quality but increases per‑iteration cost; the paper’s fixed 1 s may be suboptimal for larger N.
- *Neighbor swap vs full resampling*: Swapping preserves cardinality but may limit exploration; random re‑sampling could introduce infeasibility.

**Failure Signatures**:
- Frequent infeasible solutions (∑xᵢ ≠ d) despite penalty, indicating mis‑scaled QUBO terms.
- Stagnant CER values across iterations, suggesting the surrogate fails to learn useful structure.
- Inability to find ground truth for no‑marginal‑effect models (d=5 threshold), hinting at lack of informative lower‑order signals to guide search.
- High variance in iteration counts across seeds, reflecting sensitivity to random initialization or solver nondeterminism.

**First Three Experiments**:
1. *Baseline replication*: Run FMQA on an additive d=3, N=100 dataset with default parameters (K=10, λ=2, 1 s timeout) and verify 100% success within 1000 iterations over 10 seeds.
2. *Penalty sensitivity*: Sweep λ ∈ {0.5, 1, 2, 3} and record infeasible solution rate and success rate to identify the minimum λ that reliably enforces exactly d selected loci.
3. *Solver timeout impact*: Compare 1 s, 5 s, and 10 s annealing timeouts on a larger problem (d=5, N=1000) to assess effects on average iterations to success and solution quality distribution.

## Limitations
- Omitted details on Ising solver (annealing schedule, spin updates) and FM training (learning rate, optimizer) hinder exact replication.
- Evaluation relied on full‑sample MDR error without cross‑validation and used only 10 random seeds, limiting statistical confidence.
- Experiments were restricted to simulated data with fixed minor allele frequency (0.4) and heritability (0.2); real genetic data may exhibit different characteristics.
- Performance notably declines for high‑order no‑marginal‑effect epistasis (d=5 threshold), indicating limited robustness to weak signals.
