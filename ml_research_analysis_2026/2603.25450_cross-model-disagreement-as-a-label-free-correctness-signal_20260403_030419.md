# Cross-Model Disagreement as a Label-Free Correctness Signal

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.25450  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25450  
- **Reference count:** 31  
- **Primary result:** Cross-model disagreement metrics (CMP/CME) provide a strong label-free signal for detecting confident errors, outperforming within-model uncertainty on MMLU (AUROC 0.75 vs. 0.59) and enabling effective model routing.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the problem of identifying when a language model is confidently wrong without access to ground-truth labels. Existing self-uncertainty measures (e.g., entropy, perplexity) often fail to correlate with correctness. The proposed solution uses a second, separate verifier model: the generator's output is evaluated by a single forward pass through the verifier, measuring its surprise (Cross-Model Perplexity, CMP) or uncertainty (Cross-Model Entropy, CME). This cross-model disagreement signal consistently surpasses within-model baselines across multiple benchmarks. The approach enables label-free applications like deployment monitoring, selective prediction, and dynamic model routing, working effectively even with same-sized models from different architectural families.

## Method Summary
Given a generator model that produces an answer, a verifier model (which did not generate the answer) processes that answer in a single forward pass. The verifier computes token-level probabilities, from which two metrics are derived: Cross-Model Perplexity (CMP), the exponentiated average negative log-likelihood, and Cross-Model Entropy (CME), the average token entropy. Higher CMP/CME values indicate that the verifier is more surprised or uncertain about the generator's output, which correlates with the generator being incorrect. The method requires no labeled data, no additional generation from the verifier, and only one forward pass through the verifier per generator output.

## Key Results
- On MMLU, CMP achieves a mean AUROC of 0.75 for distinguishing correct vs. incorrect answers, substantially outperforming generator entropy (0.59) and perplexity.
- For model routing (selecting between a weak and strong model based on uncertainty), CMP recovers most of the performance gap, yielding a mean APGR of 0.915 on MMLU versus 0.848 for generator entropy.
- The signal remains effective with same-sized models from different families (e.g., Llama vs. Falcon), demonstrating that architectural diversity often suffices; even modest cross-family differences provide a useful correctness signal.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Cross-model disagreement works because independent model architectures develop distinct error patterns and knowledge representations. A verifier model's surprise at the generator's output reflects a form of consensus: if the generator's answer is incorrect, the verifier—trained on different data, with different inductive biases—is more likely to assign low probability to the erroneous tokens or sequence. This signal is less miscalibrated than a model's self-uncertainty, which can be overconfident or influenced by the model's own biases. The verifier does not need to be larger or more capable; it simply needs sufficient architectural or training divergence to provide an independent perspective on correctness.

## Foundational Learning
- **Cross-Model Perplexity (CMP):** The exponentiated average negative log-likelihood that a verifier assigns to a generator's answer. *Why needed:* Provides a scalar surprise score for label-free error detection. *Quick check:* Feed generator output into verifier, compute token-wise log-probs, average, and exponentiate.
- **Cross-Model Entropy (CME):** The average entropy of the verifier's token distribution over the generator's answer. *Why needed:* Captures distributional uncertainty; less sensitive to single-token anomalies than CMP. *Quick check:* For each token, compute entropy of verifier's next-token distribution, average across answer.
- **Selective Prediction:** The task of deciding when to trust a model's output. *Why needed:* Enables safe deployment by abstaining on uncertain inputs. *Quick check:* Threshold CMP/CME to abstain on high-scoring examples.
- **Model Routing:** Dynamically selecting which model (e.g., weak vs. strong) to use per input. *Why needed:* Optimizes accuracy-compute trade-offs. *Quick check:* Route to stronger model when CMP/CME exceeds a threshold.
- **Architectural Diversity:** Using models with fundamentally different designs (e.g., decoder-only vs. encoder-decoder). *Why needed:* Increases independence of error patterns, strengthening the disagreement signal. *Quick check:* Compare CMP performance for same-family vs. cross-family model pairs.
- **Label-Free Signal:** A monitoring metric that does not require ground-truth labels. *Why needed:* Real-world deployment where labels are unavailable. *Quick check:* Correlate CMP with correctness on a small labeled validation set to calibrate.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Generator Model -> Generated Answer -> Verifier Model -> CMP/CME Score -> Decision (Abstain / Route)

Critical path: The verifier performs a single forward pass on the generator's complete answer text to compute token probabilities; these are aggregated into CMP or CME, which is then thresholded or used for routing.

Design tradeoffs: 
- Verifier capability: More capable verifiers may yield stronger signals but increase computational cost. The paper finds same-sized cross-family models work well, suggesting capability matching is not critical.
- Cross-family diversity: Greater architectural difference improves signal independence but may limit available verifier options for a given generator.
- Metric choice: CMP is more sensitive to token-level anomalies; CME captures broader distributional uncertainty. Choice may depend on error type susceptibility.

Failure signatures: 
- If verifier is too similar to generator (e.g., same architecture, similar training data), CMP/CME collapse toward within-model uncertainty and lose advantage.
- If verifier is severely under-capable (e.g., much smaller), it may not assign meaningful probabilities, weakening the correlation with correctness.
- On tasks where both models share systematic biases (e.g., social stereotypes), disagreement may not indicate correctness.

First experiments:
1. **Verifier Size Variation:** Fix a strong generator (e.g., Llama-2-70B). Test CMP with verifiers of varying sizes (7B, 13B, 70B) from the same family vs. a different family. Measure AUROC change to see if size difference alone helps.
2. **Extreme Architectural Pairing:** Use a decoder-only generator (e.g., GPT-style) and a verifier with a non-transformer architecture (if accessible, e.g., Mamba or a hybrid). Evaluate CMP on MMLU to test the boundary of "sufficient" diversity.
3. **Open-Ended Generation:** Apply CMP to generator outputs from tasks like summarization or dialogue (using TruthfulQA or human preference datasets). Compare AUROC to QA results to assess generalizability.

## Limitations
- **Generalizability:** Core evaluations are on curated QA benchmarks (MMLU). Performance on real-world, open-ended, high-stochasticity tasks (e.g., creative writing, complex code) remains unproven.
- **Verifier Dependency:** Method requires a separate, accessible verifier model. Efficacy depends on verifier capability and architectural divergence; the minimal diversity threshold is not mapped.
- **Theoretical Gap:** The precise mechanistic reason why cross-model disagreement outperforms self-uncertainty is not fully explained; failure modes when verifier shares generator biases are unknown.

## Next Checks
1. **Benchmark Diversification:** Replicate core AUROC/APGR results on non-QA, open-ended generation benchmarks (e.g., TruthfulQA, BIG-Bench Hard, human preference datasets) to test generalizability beyond factual recall.
2. **Verifier Diversity Stress Test:** Systematically vary verifier model architecture (e.g., small dense vs. large MoE, or encoder-decoder vs. decoder-only) against a fixed generator to identify the minimal diversity threshold and failure cases where similarity erodes the signal.
3. **Cost-Benefit Analysis in Routing:** In a model routing scenario, directly measure the computational latency increase of using a CMP-based router versus a generator-uncertainty router, and evaluate if the recovered accuracy (vs. using the strongest model) justifies the added inference cost across different hardware budgets.
