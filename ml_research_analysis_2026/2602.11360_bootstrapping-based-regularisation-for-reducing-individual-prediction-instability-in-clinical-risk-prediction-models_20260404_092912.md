# Bootstrapping-based Regularisation for Reducing Individual Prediction Instability in Clinical Risk Prediction Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.11360  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11360  
- **Reference count**: 6  
- **Primary result**: A bootstrapping‑based regularisation framework reduces individual prediction instability in interpretable deep neural networks without sacrificing discriminatory performance.

## Executive Summary
Clinical risk prediction models often suffer from instability—predictions for the same patient can vary substantially when the model is trained on slightly different data. This undermines reliability in practice. Existing remedies such as ensembles improve stability but sacrifice interpretability because feature attributions must be aggregated across many models. This study proposes a novel regularisation technique that directly embeds bootstrapping into the training of a single deep neural network. The method penalises deviations between the network’s predictions and those from multiple bootstrap‑trained alternatives, steering the model toward predictions that are robust to sampling variation. Evaluated on simulated data and three real-world clinical datasets (GUSTO‑I, Framingham, SUPPORT), the “stable” model consistently achieved lower mean absolute differences (MAD) and fewer significantly deviating predictions than a standard model, while preserving AUC and maintaining high SHAP correlation with the bootstrapped median. Ensembles offered marginally greater stability but lost a coherent explanation. The approach thus delivers an interpretable, robust single model with no predictive trade‑off.

## Method Summary
The method leverages a large pool of bootstrap models to quantify prediction variability. First, B=200 independent deep neural networks (two hidden layers, 64 and 32 ReLU units, sigmoid output) are pre‑trained on bootstrap resamples of the development set. These models approximate the distribution of plausible predictors. The target model is then trained on the full development set using a penalised loss: standard binary cross‑entropy plus a regularisation term λ · (1/N) ∑ᵢ E_B[|log f_θ_b(xᵢ) − log f_θ(xᵢ)|]. The expectation is approximated by randomly sampling M=100 of the pre‑trained models for each batch. The logarithmic transformation makes the penalty scale‑invariant. The optimiser is Adam. At evaluation, a model’s predictions on a held‑out test set are compared to the median of the 200 bootstrap predictions to compute MAD (instability). Discrimination is measured by AUC, and interpretability consistency by the Spearman correlation of SHAP values between the model and the median‑bootstrap model. Baselines include a standard DNN (no regularisation) and a bootstrap ensemble (average of the 200 models). The framework is validated on simulated data (p=15, mix of binary/continuous/noise, logistic β~Uniform(3,6)) and three clinical datasets.

## Key Results
- **Reduced instability**: The stable model achieved substantially lower MAD than the standard model (e.g., GUSTO‑I: 0.019 vs 0.059; Framingham: 0.057 vs 0.088; SUPPORT: 0.034 vs 0.062).  
- **Fewer extreme deviations**: The proportion of test samples with bootstrap p‑value < 0.05 was markedly lower for the stable model.  
- **Preserved discrimination**: AUC values for stable and standard models were comparable, with no statistically significant drop.  
- **Interpretability maintained**: SHAP correlations between stable and median‑bootstrap models remained high (e.g., 0.894 for GUSTO‑I). In contrast, ensemble models showed variable feature attributions across constituents, complicating interpretation.  
- **Ensemble trade‑off**: While ensembles were marginally more stable, they lacked a single set of feature importances, highlighting the advantage of the stable single model.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Model instability arises from high variance in learned parameters under finite data. By explicitly penalising the deviation of a model’s predictions from those of many bootstrap alternatives, the regulariser forces the optimisation towards a region of parameter space where predictions are consistent across plausible training sets. The log‑transform ensures the penalty is relative, avoiding dominance by extreme probability values. Consequently, the model’s own variance is reduced without necessarily increasing bias, yielding robust individual predictions. Because the approach outputs a single model, its feature attributions remain fixed and interpretable, unlike ensembles where explanations must be aggregated over diverse models.

## Foundational Learning

**Bootstrapping**:  
*Why needed*: To create multiple resampled datasets and approximate the distribution of model predictions under data variability.  
*Quick check*: Generate 1000 bootstrap samples of a dataset and compute a 95% confidence interval for the mean.

**Prediction Stability**:  
*Why needed*: In clinical settings, a patient’s risk score should not change dramatically with minor changes to the training data.  
*Quick check*: Train two models on two bootstrap samples and compute the correlation of their predictions on a held‑out set.

**Regularisation via Deviation Penalty**:  
*Why needed*: Directly targets prediction variance, unlike weight regularisers that indirectly affect stability.  
*Quick check*: For linear regression, add a penalty equal to the mean squared difference between the model’s predictions and those of several bootstrap regressions; verify reduced prediction variance on new data.

**SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)**:  
*Why needed*: To evaluate whether the stable model’s feature attributions remain faithful and consistent.  
*Quick check*: Compute SHAP values for two models on the same data and calculate their Spearman correlation; high correlation indicates similar feature rankings.

**Mean Absolute Difference (MAD)**:  
*Why needed*: Provides a scale‑appropriate measure of individual‑level deviation from a reference (e.g., bootstrap median).  
*Quick check*: For two prediction vectors `p1` and `p2`, compute `np.mean(np.abs(p1 - p2))`.

**Deep Neural Networks (DNNs)**:  
*Why needed*: Offer a flexible, non‑linear baseline to which the stability regulariser can be applied.  
*Quick check*: Train a two‑hidden‑layer DNN on a toy binary classification task (e.g., XOR) and achieve >90% accuracy.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**  
Data preprocessing → Development set → Bootstrap sampling (B=200) → Independent DNN training (Pretrained models) → Target DNN with stability regularisation (λ, M) → Trained stable model → Evaluation (MAD, AUC, SHAP)

**Critical path**
1. Split data into development and test sets.  
2. Generate B bootstrap samples from the development set.  
3. Train B independent DNNs on each bootstrap sample (pre‑computation, parallelizable).  
4. Initialize target DNN; for each epoch:  
   a. Randomly sample M pretrained models.  
   b. For each batch compute binary cross‑entropy loss and the stability penalty (mean absolute log‑difference against the M bootstrap predictions).  
   c. Update parameters with Adam.  
5. After convergence, evaluate on test set: compute predictions, compare to median of the B bootstrap predictions for MAD, compute AUC, and compute SHAP values.

**Design tradeoffs**
- **B vs precomputation cost**: Larger B yields a more accurate bootstrap distribution but increases memory and training time linearly.  
- **M vs gradient variance**: Smaller M reduces per‑iteration cost but introduces noise into the regulariser gradient; M=100 balances this.  
- **λ vs bias‑stability trade‑off**: High λ strongly enforces alignment with the bootstrap median, potentially increasing bias and harming AUC; moderate λ preserves discrimination.  
- **Model capacity**: Two hidden layers (64, 32 units) were chosen to provide flexibility without overfitting on modest clinical sample sizes.  
- **Interpretability**: A single stable model retains a fixed set of feature weights; ensembles require aggregating explanations from many models, which can be ambiguous.

**Failure signatures**
- **Regulariser does not decrease**: The stability term remains high, suggesting the target model cannot align with any bootstrap model (maybe λ too high or insufficient capacity). Check gradient norms; consider reducing λ.  
- **Bootstrap models lack diversity**: Pairwise MAD among bootstrap predictions is low, making the median uninformative. Inspect diversity; if absent, train longer or increase model capacity.  
- **AUC drop**: Significant loss in discrimination indicates over‑regularisation; reduce λ or increase model size.  
- **Low SHAP correlation**: The stable model altered feature interactions unexpectedly; compare global feature importance plots. Could also stem from unstable SHAP estimation (increase background samples).  
- **Training instability (NaNs)**: Log of probabilities near zero; add epsilon clipping inside the log operation.

**First experiments**
1. **Baseline instability quantification**: On a small simulated dataset, train a standard DNN and 200 bootstrap DNNs. Compute MAD of the standard model’s predictions relative to the median of the bootstrap predictions. Confirm that MAD is non‑negligible, establishing the baseline problem.  
2. **Regularisation sanity check**: Train a stable DNN with λ=0.1 and M=10. Monitor the stability penalty per epoch; it should decrease and converge. Compare final MAD to baseline; expect a sizable reduction with minimal AUC change.  
3. **λ sensitivity sweep**: Train stable models with λ∈{0.01, 0.1, 1.0} on the same dataset. Record MAD and AUC for each. Plot the trade‑off; the curve should show MAD decreasing with λ and AUC stable for λ≤0.1, possibly dropping at λ=1. This mirrors the paper’s Figure 5.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Hyperparameter details**: The paper omits specific values for learning rate, batch size, weight decay, and number of training epochs. Without these, exact replication is challenging. Assumption: standard values were used, but this is not confirmed.
- **Calibration impact**: The evaluation focuses on AUC and SHAP correlation but does not measure calibration (e.g., via Brier score or calibration plots). It is unknown whether the stability regularization affects the reliability of predicted probabilities.
- **Scalability**: The pre‑training of B=200 bootstrap DNNs is computationally intensive. The paper does not discuss the time or memory requirements, nor does it propose approximations for larger datasets. Unknown: whether the method scales to high‑dimensional data or larger sample sizes.
- **Generality to other models**: The experiments are limited to a specific two‑hidden‑layer DNN architecture. It remains an open question whether the approach generalizes to other model families (e.g., logistic regression, random forests, gradient boosting) or to other types of clinical prediction tasks (e.g., survival analysis).
- **Systematic hyperparameter tuning**: Only a few λ values (0.01, 0.1, 1.0) are tested. The full spectrum of the bias‑stability trade‑off and the interaction with M are not fully explored.
- **SHAP value stability**: While SHAP correlation between the stable model and the bootstrap median is high, the stability of SHAP values across bootstrap samples for the standard vs. stable models is not quantified. This would further validate the interpretability claim.

## Limitations
- **Implementation details omitted**: Exact hyperparameters (learning rate, batch size, epochs, weight decay) are not specified, hindering exact replication of reported numbers.  
- **Preprocessing pipelines unclear**: How missing data, categorical encoding, and scaling were handled for the real clinical datasets is left undescribed, introducing ambiguity in data preparation.  
- **Statistical test underspecified**: The procedure for flagging “significantly deviating” predictions (bootstrap p‑value) lacks methodological detail, making that metric difficult to reproduce verbatim.

## Next Checks
1. **Gradient flow verification**: Confirm that the gradient of the stability penalty is non‑zero and decreases during training; a vanishing gradient would indicate the regulariser is not influencing learning.  
2. **λ sensitivity replication**: On the GUSTO‑I dataset, train stable models with λ∈{0.01, 0.1, 1.0} and verify that MAD decreases monotonically while AUC stays roughly constant for λ≤0.1 and drops for λ=1, matching the paper’s trade‑off curve.  
3. **SHAP correlation consistency**: Compute SHAP values for both standard and stable models using the same background dataset; the global Spearman correlation of feature attributions should be high (≥0.89) and the stable model’s SHAP values should exhibit less variance across bootstrap replicates than the standard model’s.
