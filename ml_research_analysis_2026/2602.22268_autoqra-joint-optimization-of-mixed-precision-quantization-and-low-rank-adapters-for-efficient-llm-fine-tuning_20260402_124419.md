# AutoQRA: Joint Optimization of Mixed-Precision Quantization and Low-rank Adapters for Efficient LLM Fine-Tuning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.22268
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22268
- **Reference count:** 38
- **Primary result:** AutoQRA jointly optimizes per-layer quantization bit-width and LoRA rank for efficient LLM fine-tuning under memory constraints, achieving near full-precision performance with memory comparable to uniform 4-bit methods; for example, on LLaMA-3.1-8B it achieves 69.83% accuracy with 13.08 GB memory, surpassing QLoRA's 67.45% with 15.22 GB.

## Executive Summary
AutoQRA addresses the challenge of fine-tuning large language models under tight memory budgets by automatically determining per-layer quantization bit-widths and LoRA adapter ranks. Its two-phase search first performs a global, multi-fidelity evolutionary exploration guided by layer importance signals, then locally refines elite configurations via trust-region Bayesian optimization. The method enforces the memory constraint through a sensitivity-aware repair operator. Experiments on LLaMA-3.1-8B and Qwen-2.5-7B show that AutoQRA recovers most of the full-precision fine-tuning accuracy while using memory competitive with uniform 4-bit quantization. The approach demonstrates that higher bit-widths can be compensated by lower LoRA ranks and vice versa, enabling more flexible resource allocation.

## Method Summary
AutoQRA formulates the problem as finding a configuration \(C = \{(q_\ell, r_\ell)\}\) for each layer \(\ell\) that maximizes expected downstream accuracy subject to a strict memory budget \(M(C) \le B_{\text{max}}\). The search proceeds in two phases. Phase I uses NSGA‑II with constrained domination. The initial population is warm‑started from layer‑wise importance scores: \(I_q(\ell)\) (sensitivity to low‑bit quantization) and \(I_r(\ell)\) (gradient spectral energy from a short probe fine‑tuning). Mutations are guided by these scores and include a memory‑balanced coupled variation to stay near the constraint boundary. Evaluations use a fidelity ladder of training steps; a surrogate model \(\Phi_s\) predicts high‑fidelity performance from low‑fidelity observations, allowing only promising candidates to be promoted. A repair operator (REPAIR) projects infeasible configurations to feasibility by repeatedly downgrading the bit or rank choice that minimizes \((\text{sensitivity}+\epsilon)/\Delta\text{memory}\). Phase II takes top candidates from Phase I as seeds for multiple trust regions. A Gaussian process surrogate models performance in an ordinal embedding space; discrete neighborhoods are explored via Expected Improvement, again using REPAIR. The best measured configuration across all high‑fidelity evaluations is returned.

## Key Results
- AutoQRA (≤4‑bit) on LLaMA‑3.1‑8B: 69.83% accuracy, 13.08 GB memory vs. QLoRA (4‑bit, rank 16) 67.45%, 15.22 GB.
- Surrogate screening in Phase I improves top‑3 hit rate from 44.7% to 67.3%; AutoQRA reaches target performance with only 6 high‑fidelity evaluations vs. 107 for random search.
- Higher bit‑widths are paired with lower LoRA ranks and vice versa, confirming a compensatory allocation strategy.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Coarse-to-Fine Multi-Fidelity Screening
- **Claim:** Low‑fidelity short fine‑tuning runs filter candidates before expensive high‑fidelity evaluations, making the combinatorial search feasible.
- **Mechanism:** Phase I uses a Hyperband‑style schedule: candidates first evaluated at few steps \(T_1\); a surrogate \(\Phi_s\) trained on paired low/high‑fidelity data predicts high‑fidelity performance (\(T_S\)). Only top candidates are promoted to higher fidelities, resuming from checkpoints. This reserves full \(T_S\) evaluations for a small elite set. ([section 3.2])
- **Core assumption:** Low‑fidelity performance correlates sufficiently with high‑fidelity performance.
- **Evidence:** Surrogate screening boosts top‑3 hit rate (44.7% → 67.3%) in experiments (Fig 4). Related methods (QuEPT, MLoRQ) use multi‑fidelity, but AutoQRA uniquely integrates it with evolutionary search and Bayesian refinement.
- **Break condition:** If low‑fidelity performance has high variance or poor correlation, screening becomes unreliable and may discard good candidates or promote poor ones.

### Mechanism 2: Importance‑Guided Evolutionary Operators
- **Claim:** Injecting layer‑wise importance priors into initialization and mutations steers search toward trainable regions, accelerating convergence.
- **Mechanism:** Compute \(I_q(\ell)\) (sensitivity to low‑bit quantization) and \(I_r(\ell)\) (gradient spectral energy during probe). Warm‑start population by mapping these scores to discrete bit/rank ladders under the memory constraint. Mutations sample layers proportionally to \(I_q\) (bits) or \(I_r\) (ranks) with concentration \(\gamma\). A memory‑balanced coupled mutation allows global compensation to stay near the constraint boundary.
- **Core assumption:** \(I_q\) and \(I_r\) predict how much a layer benefits from higher precision or higher rank in final performance.
- **Evidence:** Warm‑start and guided mutations reduce Phase I generations compared to random initialization (Fig 5). Importance signals are derived from first‑order statistics (Appendix A).
- **Break condition:** If \(I_q\) or \(I_r\) are poorly estimated (e.g., unrepresentative calibration set), search may bias toward suboptimal layers, missing better trade‑offs.

### Mechanism 3: Sensitivity‑Aware Feasibility Repair
- **Claim:** When configurations exceed memory, REPAIR selectively downgrades less sensitive layers, preserving capacity in critical layers.
- **Mechanism:** For an infeasible candidate, compute for every layer \(\ell\) and knob \(t\) the memory saved by a single downgrade \(\Delta M_t(\ell)\) and the associated sensitivity \(eI_t(\ell)\). Choose the downgrade minimizing \((\text{sensitivity}+\epsilon)/\Delta M_t(\ell)\) and repeat until \(M(C) \le B_{\text{max}}\). (Eq 8)
- **Core assumption:** The same importance scores used for proposals are valid proxies for the harmful impact of capacity reduction on fine‑tuning performance.
- **Evidence:** Repair intensity shows strong negative correlation with sensitivity (\(\rho = -0.68\), \(p < 0.01\)) (Appendix E.1).
- **Break condition:** If importance scores are inaccurate, REPAIR may degrade critical layers, yielding poor performance even for feasible configurations.

## Foundational Learning

### Mixed-Precision Quantization
- **Why needed:** Layers vary in robustness to low‑bit quantization; uniform low precision wastes memory or harms accuracy in sensitive layers.
- **Quick check:** Why not use the same bit‑width everywhere? Some layers (e.g., attention heads) require higher precision to preserve representation quality.

### Low‑Rank Adaptation (LoRA)
- **Why needed:** Full fine‑tuning of LLMs is memory‑prohibitive; LoRA reduces trainable parameters by learning low‑rank updates applied to frozen weights.
- **Quick check:** How does LoRA reduce memory? It replaces a full weight update \(\Delta W\) (size \(d \times k\)) with \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}\) and \(B \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}\), where \(r \ll d,k\).

### Multi‑Fidelity Black‑Box Optimization
- **Why needed:** Exhaustive evaluation with full fine‑tuning is too expensive; need cheap approximations to screen many candidates.
- **Quick check:** What fidelity levels does AutoQRA use? Short training runs (few steps) for low fidelity and longer runs for high fidelity; promotion uses a surrogate trained on paired data.

### Constraint‑Handling in Search
- **Why needed:** Memory budget is a hard constraint; the search must only consider feasible configurations.
- **Quick check:** How does AutoQRA enforce constraints? Via REPAIR operator projecting infeasible candidates to feasible, and NSGA‑II's constrained domination.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Calibration/Probe → Importance Calculator → Warm‑Start Generator → (Evolutionary Engine ↔ Multi‑Fidelity Evaluator → Surrogate Model) with REPAIR → Trust‑Region Refiner → Output; Memory Accountant used throughout.

### Critical Path
1. Compute \(I_q\) (gradient‑weighted quantization residual) and \(I_r\) (singular value energy of gradients from probe fine‑tuning) using a calibration set.
2. Warm‑start initial population by mapping importance scores to discrete bit/rank ladders under the memory constraint; repair to ensure feasibility.
3. Phase I: For each generation, generate offspring via importance‑guided and memory‑balanced mutations; repair; evaluate at low fidelity; use surrogate \(\Phi_s\) to screen for promotion; evaluate promoted candidates at high fidelity (resuming from checkpoints); update NSGA‑II population via constrained domination; terminate when hypervolume stabilizes.
4. Collect all high‑fidelity evaluations into dataset \(D_{\text{hi}}\).
5. Phase II: Select diverse elite configurations from \(D_{\text{hi}}\) as trust‑region centers. Iterate: enumerate discrete neighbors, repair, compute Expected Improvement using a GP surrogate fitted on \(D_{\text{hi}}\) in an ordinal embedding space; evaluate best neighbor at high fidelity; update GP and trust‑region radius; stop when EI saturates or iteration limit reached.
6. Return the best feasible configuration according to utility \(f(C;\alpha)\).

### Design Tradeoffs
- Phase I budget vs. coverage: Larger Phase I explores more but delays Phase II refinement.
- Multi‑fidelity rung count: More rungs improve correlation but add scheduling complexity.
- Importance‑guided mutation: Speeds convergence but risks premature convergence; memory‑balanced mutation mitigates.
- Trust‑region size: Larger regions explore more but may include poor points; adaptive radius balances.
- Repair aggressiveness (\(\epsilon\)): trade‑off between protecting sensitive layers and meeting constraint.

### Failure Signatures
- Surrogate correlation degrades: promotion hit rate drops, hypervolume stagnates.
- Excessive repair: many candidates needing heavy repairs indicate search generating infeasible configurations excessively.
- Phase I hypervolume stagnation: no improvement over several generations → premature convergence; increase population or mutation.
- Phase II EI saturation: max EI below \(\epsilon_{\text{ei}}\) early → trust regions exhausted; consider reinitializing with different \(\alpha\).

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Main results** (Table 1, Fig 3): AutoQRA (≤4‑bit) outperforms uniform 4‑bit baselines (e.g., LLaMA3.1‑8B: 69.83% vs. 67.45% accuracy, with lower memory); negative correlation between bit‑width and rank.
2. **Surrogate screening benefit** (Fig 4): Surrogate‑guided promotion increases top‑3 hit rate from 44.7% to 67.3%, proving screening effectiveness.
3. **Search efficiency** (Fig 5): AutoQRA reaches target performance with only 6 high‑fidelity evaluations vs. 107 for random search, demonstrating sample efficiency.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How does the computational overhead of AutoQRA's two‑phase search scale with model size (number of parameters/layers), and is the method feasible for very large language models (e.g., >30B parameters) given the exponential growth of the search space?
- **Basis in paper:** (inferred) The paper evaluates up to 8B/7B models; the search space is \((|Q|\times|R|)^L\), exponential in layers.
- **Why unresolved:** For deployment on massive models, search cost must be amortized; unknown scaling limits hinder adoption.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Empirical scaling studies measuring search time (GPU‑hours) and final performance relative to model size; theoretical complexity analysis of Phase I/II with respect to \(L\) and candidate pool sizes.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Is the negative correlation between bit‑width and LoRA rank (the "compensation pattern") a universal property across different fine‑tuning tasks and model architectures, or does it depend on the specific task distribution and model design?
- **Basis in paper:** (inferred) The pattern is observed on instruction tuning (Alpaca52k + HC3) for LLaMA and Qwen models.
- **Why unresolved:** If task‑specific, AutoQRA may need retraining per task, reducing portability; a universal pattern would enable pre‑computed configurations.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic experiments across diverse fine‑tuning scenarios (classification, summarization, code generation) and model families; compute Pearson correlation between bit‑width and rank per layer; test statistical consistency.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can we formulate a predictive model (e.g., based on layer sensitivity metrics or optimization theory) that directly maps a layer's characteristics (e.g., Hessian spectrum, activation statistics) to an optimal (bit‑width, rank) pair, thereby eliminating or drastically reducing the need for costly black‑box search?
- **Basis in paper:** (inferred) AutoQRA relies on evolutionary search plus Bayesian optimization, which requires many partial fine‑tuning evaluations; importance signals \(I_q, I_r\) are only used for warm‑start/proposals, not as reliable predictors.
- **Why unresolved:** A reliable analytical or surrogate predictor would make joint optimization orders of magnitude cheaper, critical for large models or tight time constraints.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Develop a new sensitivity metric that strongly correlates with post‑fine‑tuning performance for mixed‑precision LoRA configurations; validate by comparing predictions against AutoQRA results on multiple models/tasks, achieving similar performance with far fewer evaluations.

## Limitations
- Exact hyperparameters for search (multi‑fidelity rungs, population size, GP settings, etc.) are not disclosed, hindering exact reproduction and sensitivity analysis.
- The method's scalability to very large models (>30B parameters) is not addressed; the exponential search space may become computationally infeasible.
- The universality of the negative correlation between bit‑width and LoRA rank across tasks and architectures is unverified; the observed pattern may be limited to instruction tuning on LLaMA/Qwen.

## Next Checks
1. **Fidelity correlation robustness:** Replicate Phase I surrogate screening with varying step ratios \(T_1/T_S\); measure Pearson \(\rho\) between low‑ and high‑fidelity performance across 50 random configurations. Expect \(\rho > 0.7\) for stable promotion.
2. **Repair operator sensitivity:** For 100 infeasible candidates, log the rank of chosen downgrades by \((\text{sensitivity}+\epsilon)/\Delta\text{memory}\) among all possible downgrades. Expect strong negative correlation (\(\rho \approx -0.68\)) and >80% of repairs targeting the bottom‑20% sensitive layers.
3. **Comparison with MLoRQ/QuEPT:** Run AutoQRA and MLoRQ on LLaMA‑3.1‑8B with identical memory budgets (e.g., 13 GB) and seeds. Compare accuracy drop vs. full precision and per‑layer bit‑rank distributions. Expect AutoQRA to achieve ≥0.5% better accuracy with more varied bit‑rank pairing.
