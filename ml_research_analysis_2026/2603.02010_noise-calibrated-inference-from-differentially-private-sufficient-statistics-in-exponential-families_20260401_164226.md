# Noise-Calibrated Inference from Differentially Private Sufficient Statistics in Exponential Families

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.02010
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02010
- Reference count: 16
- Primary result: DP noise-calibrated inference using privatized sufficient statistics yields asymptotically valid Wald confidence intervals for exponential families, with explicit variance inflation Θ(1/(nε)), near-nominal coverage (88–95%) in simulations, and minimax optimality; naive synthetic analyses undercover drastically under strong privacy.

## Executive Summary
Differentially private (DP) data release often fails to provide valid uncertainty quantification, leading to miscalibrated inference from synthetic data or point estimates. This paper tackles that problem for regular exponential families. The proposed pipeline releases only a noisy version of the sufficient statistics via the Gaussian mechanism, then performs noise-calibrated inference—either a plug-in maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) with variance inflation or a noise-aware likelihood correction—and optionally generates synthetic data, all while preserving the DP guarantee.

The plug-in DP MLE is asymptotically normal, with total variance equal to the classical sampling variance plus a privacy-induced term proportional to σ² (the Gaussian noise scale, which scales as 1/(nε)). This variance decomposition directly yields valid Wald confidence intervals. The authors also prove a minimax lower bound showing that the privacy distortion rate Θ(1/(nε)) is unavoidable. Simulations on Gaussian, logistic, and Poisson models and real census data demonstrate that the calibrated methods achieve coverage close to the nominal 95% (88–95%), while naive synthetic analyses suffer severe undercoverage (as low as ~1.4% at ε=0.1). The variance formula accurately predicts finite-sample behavior. Moreover, plug-in and noise-aware estimators are first-order equivalent, so the simpler plug-in approach suffices. The clipping radius is a critical tradeoff: too small increases clipping bias, too large inflates noise variance.

## Method Summary
The algorithm takes as input an i.i.d. dataset from a regular exponential family, a clipping bound B, privacy parameters (ε, δ), and the model specification. First, each data point (or its sufficient statistic) is clipped to ensure the ℓ₂ norm does not exceed B. The empirical sufficient statistic \(\bar{S} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n s(X_i)\) is then computed. Its ℓ₂ sensitivity under clipping is \(\Delta_2 = 2B/n\). Using the analytic Gaussian mechanism, a noise scale σ is chosen to satisfy (ε, δ)-DP; Gaussian noise \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)\) is added to \(\bar{S}\), and the noisy statistic \(\hat{S} = \bar{S} + Z\) is released.

For inference, two equivalent strategies are available. The plug-in estimator solves \(\nabla A(\hat{\theta}_{\text{plug}}) = \hat{S}\). The noise-aware estimator maximizes a corrected likelihood that accounts for the distribution of \(\hat{S}\) (approximately Gaussian in the CLT regime). Both estimators use the same asymptotic variance: \(\text{Var}(\hat{\theta}) = I(\theta_0)^{-1}/n + \sigma^2 I(\theta_0)^{-2}\). This variance estimate is plugged into standard Wald confidence intervals: \(\hat{\theta} \pm z_{\alpha/2} \sqrt{\text{Var}(\hat{\theta})}\). Optionally, synthetic data can be generated from the model \(p(x|\hat{\theta})\). The pipeline thus delivers DP, valid uncertainty quantification, and optional synthetic release.

## Key Results
- The plug-in DP MLE is asymptotically normal with explicit variance decomposition: \(\text{Var}(\hat{\theta}) = I(\theta_0)^{-1}/n + \sigma^2 I(\theta_0)^{-2}\), enabling valid Wald confidence intervals.
- A minimax lower bound proves the privacy-induced distortion rate Θ(1/(nε)) is unavoidable for one-parameter exponential families.
- Empirical coverage for DP-aware methods is near nominal (88–95%) across Gaussian, logistic, and Poisson models and real census data, whereas naive synthetic analyses undercover severely (<10% at ε=0.1).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Releasing a differentially private (DP) noisy version of the sufficient statistic protects individual privacy while preserving the statistical structure needed for valid inference.
- **Mechanism:** Clip the data to bound the ℓ₂ norm of the sufficient statistic, compute the empirical sufficient statistic \(\bar{S}\), then add Gaussian noise \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)\) calibrated to \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP via the analytic Gaussian mechanism. The noise scale \(\sigma\) depends on the sensitivity \(\Delta_2 = 2B/n\), where \(B\) is the clipping bound.
- **Core assumption:** The data are i.i.d. from a regular exponential family and the sufficient statistic is bounded after clipping (Assumption 1).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "release only differentially private noisy sufficient statistics via the Gaussian mechanism."
  - [section 3.2]: "Under Assumption 1, the ℓ₂ sensitivity of \(\bar{S}\) is at most \(2B/n\). ... \(\sigma\) is chosen by the analytic Gaussian mechanism calibration."
  - [corpus]: The corpus includes foundational DP mechanisms (e.g., Dwork et al. 2006) but the specific integration with exponential families is original to this paper.
- **Break condition:** If clipping radius \(B\) is too small, clipping bias dominates; if too large, sensitivity increases noise variance. Also fails if the data distribution is not an exponential family or if boundedness does not hold after clipping.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Noise-calibrated inference—either via plug-in MLE with variance inflation or a noise-aware likelihood correction—yields asymptotically valid confidence intervals that account for DP noise.
- **Mechanism:** The plug-in estimator solves \(\nabla A(\hat{\theta}_{\text{plug}}) = \bar{S} + Z\). Its asymptotic variance is the sum of the classical sampling variance \(I(\theta_0)^{-1}/n\) and a privacy-induced variance \(\sigma^2 I(\theta_0)^{-2}\). The noise-aware estimator maximizes a corrected likelihood that models the distribution of \(\bar{S} + Z\) (approximately Gaussian in the CLT regime).
- **Core assumption:** The model is a regular exponential family with positive definite Fisher information (Assumption 2) and the sample size is large enough for the multivariate CLT to hold.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "asymptotic normality, explicit variance inflation, and valid Wald-style confidence intervals for the plug-in DP MLE; a noise-aware likelihood correction that is first-order equivalent."
  - [section 4.2]: Theorem 2 provides the asymptotic distribution and variance decomposition.
  - [section 4.3]: Proposition 1 establishes first-order equivalence between plug-in and noise-aware estimators.
  - [corpus]: Prior work (e.g., Bernstein & Sheldon 2018, Ferrando et al. 2022) addresses DP inference, but the explicit variance formula and unification with synthetic data are novel here.
- **Break condition:** Wald intervals may undercover for small \(n\) or for models with convex link functions (e.g., Poisson) due to Jensen’s inequality inflating \(I(\hat{\theta})\). Clipping bias can also distort coverage if \(B\) is poorly chosen.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The privacy-induced variance rate \(\Theta(1/(n\varepsilon))\) (or \(\Theta(1/(n^2\varepsilon^2)\) for MSE) is minimax optimal for one-parameter exponential families, meaning the method cannot be improved in terms of asymptotic rate.
- **Mechanism:** The variance inflation term \(\sigma^2 I(\theta_0)^{-2}\) scales as \(1/(n^2\varepsilon^2)\) because \(\sigma \propto 1/(n\varepsilon)\) in the Gaussian mechanism with bounded sensitivity. A lower bound for DP mean estimation of bounded data (Cai et al. 2021) implies any DP estimator must have at least this much MSE, which transfers to the natural parameter via the 1-Lipschitz tanh link.
- **Core assumption:** The lower bound applies to central DP with \(\varepsilon \in (0,1]\) and \(\delta < 1/4\) in a Bernoulli (bounded mean) setting; the upper bound assumes the exponential family structure and Gaussian noise addition.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "a matching minimax lower bound showing the privacy distortion rate is unavoidable."
  - [section 5]: Theorem 3 states the lower bound and its proof.
  - [corpus]: The lower bound builds directly on Cai et al. 2021.
- **Break condition:** The lower bound is proven only for a one-dimensional bounded mean estimation problem. Extensions to high-dimensional or unbounded settings may have different rates, though the paper conjectures the same rate holds more generally.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Differential Privacy (Gaussian Mechanism)**
  - *Why needed here:* It defines the privacy guarantee and determines the noise scale \(\sigma\), which directly impacts the variance inflation.
  - *Quick check question:* Given a dataset with clipped sufficient statistic sensitivity \(\Delta_2\), how do you compute \(\sigma\) for \((\varepsilon, \delta)\)-DP using the analytic Gaussian mechanism? What happens to \(\sigma\) if you double the clipping bound \(B\)?

- **Concept: Exponential Families and Sufficient Statistics**
  - *Why needed here:* The entire pipeline relies on the fact that the likelihood depends on data only through the sufficient statistic \(\bar{S}\). This separation allows privatizing \(\bar{S}\) once and reusing it for multiple downstream tasks.
  - *Quick check question:* For a Poisson regression with log-link, what is the sufficient statistic? How does the Fisher information \(I(\theta)\) depend on \(\theta\)?

- **Concept: Maximum Likelihood Asymptotics (Delta Method)**
  - *Why needed here:* The plug-in estimator’s distribution is derived by applying the delta method to the MLE based on the noisy statistic. Understanding the decomposition into sampling and privacy terms is key.
  - *Quick check question:* If \(\hat{\theta}\) is the non-private MLE with \(\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}-\theta_0) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I(\theta_0)^{-1})\), and we add independent noise \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)\) to the sufficient statistic, what is the asymptotic variance of the resulting plug-in estimator?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
1. **Input module:** Accepts raw dataset \(D\), privacy parameters \((\varepsilon, \delta)\), clipping bound \(B\), and model specification (exponential family).
2. **Preprocessing module:** Clips each data point (or its sufficient statistic) to ensure \(\|s(x)\|_2 \leq B\).
3. **DP noise module:** Computes empirical sufficient statistic \(\bar{S}\), sensitivity \(\Delta_2 = 2B/n\), noise scale \(\sigma\) via analytic Gaussian mechanism, adds \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)\) to release \(\bar{S}\).
4. **Inference module:** Implements plug-in MLE \(\hat{\theta}_{\text{plug}} = (\nabla A)^{-1}(\bar{S} + Z)\) and/or noise-aware estimator via weighted least squares using \(\Sigma(\theta) = I(\theta)/n + \sigma^2 I\).
5. **Uncertainty module:** Computes variance estimate \(\widehat{\text{Var}}(\hat{\theta}) = I(\hat{\theta})^{-1}/n + \sigma^2 I(\hat{\theta})^{-2}\) and Wald confidence intervals.
6. **Synthetic module (optional):** Generates synthetic data from \(p(x \mid \hat{\theta})\).
7. **Validation module:** (For engineers) can run internal checks: empirical variance vs. theoretical, coverage diagnostics.

### Critical Path
Clipping → \(\bar{S}\) computation → noise calibration → noise addition → \(\hat{\theta}\) computation → variance inflation → CI construction. The DP guarantee hinges on correct clipping and noise calibration; inference validity hinges on correct variance inflation.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Clipping bound \(B\):** Small \(B\) reduces sensitivity (smaller \(\sigma\)) but introduces clipping bias; large \(B\) reduces bias but increases \(\sigma\). The paper’s experiments suggest choosing \(B\) around 2–3 standard deviations of the unscaled features.
- **Plug-in vs. noise-aware:** Proposition 1 shows they are first-order equivalent; plug-in is simpler and numerically more stable. Noise-aware may help with bias from clipping but did not outperform in experiments.
- **Wald vs. bootstrap:** Wald intervals are analytic and fast; bootstrap (using noise-aware likelihood) may improve finite-sample coverage at the cost of computation.

### Failure Signatures
- **Undercoverage in Wald CIs** at small \(\varepsilon\) or for Poisson models: Check if \(I(\hat{\theta})\) is inflated due to Jensen’s inequality; consider bootstrap.
- **Numerical instability** in noise-aware estimation: When \(\sigma\) is large relative to \(n\), \(\Sigma(\theta)\) becomes ill-conditioned. Use regularization (Tikhonov) and box constraints as in the paper.
- **Naive synthetic analysis** (ignoring DP noise) will show severely undercovered intervals (often <20% at \(\varepsilon=0.1\)).
- **Excessive bias** at small \(B\): Monitor average absolute bias across coordinates as in Experiment 3.

### First Experiments
1. **Variance inflation validation:** Run the Gaussian mean model across \((n, \varepsilon)\) grid, compute empirical variance of \(\hat{\theta}_{\text{plug}}\), compare to theoretical \(1/n + \sigma^2\). Expect near-perfect alignment (Pearson \(r \approx 1\)).
2. **Coverage vs. privacy:** For Gaussian, logistic, Poisson at two sample sizes, compute 95% CI coverage for DP plug-in, noise-aware, bootstrap, and naive synthetic. Expect DP-aware methods to maintain ~90–95% coverage while naive synthetic collapses (<10% at \(\varepsilon=0.1\)).
3. **Clipping tradeoff study:** For logistic regression with \(d=5\), vary \(B\) from 0.5 to 10 at fixed \((n, \varepsilon)=(1000,1.0)\). Plot bias and coverage. Expect a U-shaped bias curve and stable coverage for \(B\) around 2–3.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
How can sensitivity analysis and noise calibration be performed when the clipping radius is data-dependent, as in differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD)?
- *Basis in paper:* Section 7 states: “When B is unknown a priori and data‑dependent clipping is used (as in DP‑SGD), the sensitivity analysis becomes more involved; investigating this interaction is an important practical direction.”
- *Why unresolved:* The pipeline assumes a fixed clipping bound B known beforehand. DP‑SGD commonly uses adaptive clipping per batch, complicating sensitivity computation and privacy budgeting.
- *Resolving evidence:* Theoretical bounds on sensitivity of clipped sufficient statistics under data‑dependent clipping rules, plus empirical studies showing accurate noise calibration and valid inference when applied to DP‑SGD training trajectories.

### Open Question 2
How can simultaneous confidence ellipsoids be constructed for multiple parameters from differentially private sufficient statistics while maintaining valid coverage?
- *Basis in paper:* Section 7 notes: “Our Wald intervals are coordinate‑wise; simultaneous ellipsoidal regions can be constructed from the same variance formula and are left for future work.”
- *Why unresolved:* The paper derives Wald intervals for individual parameters only. Joint hypotheses or model selection require simultaneous inference (e.g., Bonferroni, Scheffé), whose finite‑sample performance under DP is unstudied.
- *Resolving evidence:* Derivation of a joint Wald test or confidence ellipsoid using the asymptotic covariance matrix that includes privacy noise, followed by simulation experiments verifying coverage for simultaneous regions across privacy levels and dimensions.

### Open Question 3
What methods can be used to achieve noise‑calibrated inference for semi‑parametric or non‑parametric models under differential privacy?
- *Basis in paper:* Limitations section admits: “Our pipeline requires a regular exponential family with known sufficient statistics; extending to semi‑parametric or nonparametric models needs different tools.”
- *Why unresolved:* The approach relies critically on low‑dimensional sufficient statistics and the exponential family structure. Semi‑parametric models (e.g., Cox regression) and non‑parametric methods lack such finite‑dimensional summaries, making privatization and inference unclear.
- *Resolving evidence:* Development of general frameworks—such as privatized estimating equations, functional data methods, or differentially private empirical processes—with asymptotic theory and validated applications to real semi‑parametric or non‑parametric problems.

### Open Question 4
Why do Wald confidence intervals undercover for convex‑link exponential families (e.g., Poisson) under strong privacy, and how can this be corrected?
- *Basis in paper:* Experiment 2 shows pronounced undercoverage for Poisson regression. The paper explains: “for log‑link models, \(I(\theta) = X^\top \text{diag}(\exp(X\theta)) X/n\) depends on θ through the convex exp(·), so by Jensen’s inequality \(I(\hat{\theta})\) is systematically inflated when privacy noise is large, yielding CIs that are too narrow.” The limitations section notes: “Wald CIs can undercover at low ε for convex‑link models (e.g., Poisson) due to Jensen’s inequality inflating \(I(\hat{\theta})\); bootstrap CIs may help.”
- *Why unresolved:* The current variance estimator \(I(\hat{\theta})\) overestimates true Fisher information because privacy noise biases the estimator toward flatter likelihood regions. This bias is not accounted for, leading to anti‑conservative intervals. A DP parametric bootstrap was explored but did not fully resolve the issue; a principled correction is needed.
- *Resolving evidence:* Rigorous analysis of the bias in \(I(\hat{\theta})\) induced by DP noise, yielding a corrected variance estimator; or a tailored bootstrap scheme that achieves nominal coverage across ε and model complexities, demonstrated via extensive simulations and real data.

## Limitations
- Clipping parameter sensitivity: The exact clipping radius used in key simulations (Gaussian, logistic, Poisson) is not reported, hindering exact replication and making it hard to assess the bias‑noise tradeoff.
- Finite‑sample Wald coverage: Coverage drops for convex‑link models (e.g., Poisson) and strong privacy (low ε), indicating unaccounted higher‑order effects or Jensen‑induced Fisher information inflation.
- Generality of lower bound: The minimax optimality proof is restricted to one‑parameter bounded mean estimation; its extension to multivariate or unbounded exponential families remains conjectural.
