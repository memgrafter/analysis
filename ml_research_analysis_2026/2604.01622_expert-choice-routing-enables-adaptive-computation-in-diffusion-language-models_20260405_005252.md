# Expert-Choice Routing Enables Adaptive Computation in Diffusion Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.01622
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01622
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Expert-choice routing eliminates structural load imbalance in diffusion language models, delivering 2.0× faster training (10.6h vs 20.7h) and 1.5–2.1× higher throughput (52.1 vs 24.9–44.4 TFLOP/s/GPU) compared to token-choice routing.

## Executive Summary
Diffusion language models (DLMs) generate text by iteratively denoising masked sequences. Token-choice (TC) routing in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) DLMs suffers from structural load imbalance because token-level expert selection leads to uneven distribution of work across experts, reducing hardware efficiency. This paper introduces expert-choice (EC) routing, where each expert selects a fixed number of top-scoring tokens globally, eliminating this imbalance. Through extensive experiments, the authors show EC dramatically improves training efficiency: it halves wall-clock time (10.6h vs 20.7h) and boosts throughput by 1.5–2.1× (52.1 vs 24.9–44.4 TFLOP/s/GPU) compared to TC, while preserving or improving model quality.

The study also demonstrates that EC’s capacity can be made timestep-dependent in the diffusion process. By allocating more experts to low-mask-ratio denoising steps—where learning is inherently faster—the authors achieve a new state-of-the-art validation perplexity of 36.5 on OpenWebText, beating static EC (37.1). Mechanistic analysis reveals that low-mask-ratio contexts converge an order of magnitude faster. Moreover, retrofitting a pretrained TC DLM by simply replacing its router with EC, freezing the experts, and finetuning yields faster convergence and higher accuracy on code (HumanEval), math (GSM8K), and medical (MedQA) tasks. These results establish EC routing as a robust and efficient alternative for scaling diffusion language models.

## Method Summary
The authors conduct a controlled comparison between TC and EC routing using diffusion language models. All models share a Transformer architecture with 16 layers, a hidden size of 2048 (for the 8B-A1B configuration) or 512 (for OpenWebText), and a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) feed-forward network comprising 64 fine-grained experts (FFN hidden size 1280 or 384) and 2 shared experts (FFN hidden size 768). Sequence lengths are 2049 for large-scale training and 513 for OpenWebText. Training data consists of Nemotron-CC (split 50/50 High-Quality/Diverse-QA) for scaling studies and OpenWebText (~9B tokens, multiple epochs) for scheduling experiments. Evaluation metrics include wall-clock time, throughput (TFLOP/s/GPU), per-GPU memory variance, validation perplexity, and downstream task accuracy on MMLU, ARC-Challenge, HumanEval, HumanEval+, GSM8K, and MedQA.

Several TC baselines are implemented: dropless (no capacity limit), dropless with an auxiliary load-balancing loss, and capacity-bounded TC with capacity factors 1.0, 1.25, and 1.5. For EC, both static (constant capacity k) and dynamic (timestep-dependent k(r)) are explored. Dynamic schedulers (Linear-Rev., Cosine-Rev., Gaussian, Linear, Cosine, Gaussian-Rev.) are defined as k(r) = clamp(k_min + (k_max - k_min) s(r), k_min, k_max) with s(r) being the schedule function; all are engineered to have equal expected FLOPs. Additionally, the authors retrofit a pretrained TC DLM (LLaDA-MoE) by swapping its router for an EC router, freezing all expert weights, and finetuning on task-specific data (GSM8K-AUG-NL for math, OpenCodeInstruct for code, MedQA for medical) using identical finetuning hyperparameters.

## Key Results
- EC reduces training time by half (10.6h vs 20.7h) and increases throughput up to 2.1× (52.1 vs 24.9–44.4 TFLOP/s/GPU).
- Timestep-dependent capacity scheduling (e.g., Linear-Rev.) lowers validation perplexity to 36.5 from 37.1 (static EC) on OpenWebText.
- Replacing only the router in a pretrained TC DLM with EC and finetuning yields faster convergence and higher accuracy on code (HumanEval), math (GSM8K), and medical (MedQA) benchmarks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
In TC routing, each token selects its top-k experts independently, leading to a long-tail distribution: a few experts receive many tokens while others remain underutilized, causing load imbalance. EC routing flips the selection: each expert picks its top-c tokens from the entire batch, guaranteeing that every expert processes exactly c tokens. This structural property eliminates imbalance, ensuring all experts are equally busy, which maximizes hardware utilization and eliminates stragglers, thereby increasing throughput and reducing training time. The dynamic capacity scheduling further exploits the varying difficulty across diffusion timesteps. The authors find that steps with low mask ratios (i.e., little noise) exhibit much higher learning efficiency (convergence rate up to 7× faster). By allocating more expert capacity to these steps via a schedule that increases k(r) when r is small, the model can spend more compute where it is most effective, leading to lower perplexity. Retrofit works because expert weights are already pretrained to be useful; only the router needs adaptation to the new EC routing pattern. Freezing experts preserves their knowledge while the new router learns to distribute tokens effectively, resulting in faster convergence and better downstream performance.

## Foundational Learning
- **Diffusion Language Models (DLMs)**  
  - *Why needed:* Framework for generative modeling that iteratively denoises text, enabling parallel decoding and flexible control.  
  - *Quick check:* Can you explain how a noise schedule γ(t) determines the masking ratio at training timestep t and how the loss is computed for different mask ratios?

- **Mixture of Experts (MoE)**  
  - *Why needed:* Scales model capacity efficiently by sparsely activating a subset of parameters per token, reducing FLOPs while increasing model size.  
  - *Quick check:* Understand the difference between shared and routed experts, and how expert parallelism is implemented across GPUs.

- **Token-Choice (TC) Routing**  
  - *Why needed:* Classic MoE routing where each token selects its top-k experts, often leading to load imbalance because popular experts become bottlenecks.  
  - *Quick check:* Simulate a small example: given gating scores for 4 tokens and 3 experts, pick top-2 for each token and compute how many tokens each expert gets.

- **Expert-Choice (EC) Routing**  
  - *Why needed:* Reverses the selection: each expert picks its top-c tokens globally, ensuring each expert processes exactly c tokens, eliminating structural load imbalance.  
  - *Quick check:* Given the same scores, determine which tokens each expert would take if c=2, and verify each expert gets exactly 2 tokens.

- **Timestep-Dependent Capacity Scheduling**  
  - *Why needed:* In DLMs, learning difficulty varies with mask ratio; adaptive capacity allocation improves sample efficiency.  
  - *Quick check:* Design a simple schedule k(r) that increases capacity for low mask ratios (e.g., k = k_max for r<0.3, k=k_min otherwise) and check expected FLOPs equality.

- **Load Balancing**  
  - *Why needed:* Imbalanced token distribution across experts under TC causes underutilization of some GPUs and reduces throughput.  
  - *Quick check:* Compute per-expert token counts and their variance for a given assignment; interpret what high variance indicates.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Token Input -> Embedding Layer -> Transformer Stack (each layer: Multi-Head Attention + MoE FFN) -> Final LayerNorm -> LM Head -> Logits  
MoE FFN subcomponents: Token Representations -> Router (Token-Choice or Expert-Choice) -> Dispatching (all-to-all) -> Expert Networks (64 routed + 2 shared) -> Combining (weighted sum) -> MoE Output

### Critical Path
During a forward pass: tokens are embedded, then processed sequentially through each Transformer layer. Within a layer, self-attention is applied, then the MoE FFN. The MoE FFN's critical path is: compute gating scores, perform top selection (per token for TC or per expert for EC), route tokens to experts via all-to-all communication, execute expert FFNs in parallel, combine results, and return to the main path. The backward pass mirrors this with gradient propagation and all-reduce operations.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **EC vs TC**: EC guarantees load balance but requires a global top-c reduction across all tokens, increasing communication overhead. TC uses local top-k, which is cheaper but prone to imbalance.
- **Static vs Dynamic Capacity**: Static capacity is simpler; dynamic scheduling adds complexity (need to define and match FLOPs) but can improve quality by allocating compute adaptively.
- **Retrofitting**: Freezing experts retains pretrained knowledge and speeds up finetuning, but limits adaptation; only the router is trained. This may be insufficient if the routing pattern change requires minor expert adjustments.

### Failure Signatures
- **TC load imbalance**: High variance in per-GPU memory usage or FLOP counts during training; throughput significantly below the reported ~40 TFLOP/s/GPU.
- **Dynamic scheduling mismatch**: If average capacity per step deviates from the intended FLOP budget, perplexity comparisons become invalid. Monitor E[k] closely.
- **Retrofitting instability**: Router gradients may vanish if initialization is poor; convergence stalls or downstream accuracy does not improve. Verify that only the router is updated and that the new router's top-c logic matches EC exactly.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Mechanism of Low-Mask-Ratio Learning Efficiency**  
   The paper observes that denoising steps with low mask ratios converge faster but does not establish causality. It remains unclear whether the speedup stems from richer visible context, token predictability, or other factors. Resolution would require controlled interventions that isolate context or mask properties.

2. **Learned Timestep-Dependent Capacity Scheduling**  
   The optimal capacity schedule likely depends on model scale, dataset, and task. The paper uses hand-designed schedules and suggests replacing them with learned ones (e.g., a neural network predicting capacity from mask ratio). No such learned scheduler was implemented or compared.

3. **Robustness of Retrofitting across Checkpoints and Architectures**  
   The retrofit results are limited to one pretrained TC checkpoint and three tasks. It is unknown whether benefits generalize to larger models, different MoE configurations, or varied pretraining data. The necessary finetuning protocol for stable conversion also needs investigation.

4. **Generalization to Continuous Diffusion Language Models**  
   Findings are based on discrete masked diffusion. Continuous diffusion (e.g., DiffusionBERT) uses additive Gaussian noise; the analogous of mask ratio is continuous noise level σ. Whether allocating more capacity to low-noise steps yields similar gains is an open question.

## Limitations
- Masking schedule γ(t) is not explicitly defined, preventing exact replication of dynamic routing and mechanistic analysis.
- EC router implementation details (gating, top-c tie-breaking, shared expert integration) in Megatron-LM are not fully described and must be extracted from code.
- Retrofitting procedure (finetuning steps, learning rate, data preprocessing, router initialization) is underspecified in the appendix.
