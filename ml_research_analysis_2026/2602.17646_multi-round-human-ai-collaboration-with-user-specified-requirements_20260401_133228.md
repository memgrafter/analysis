# Multi-Round Human-AI Collaboration with User-Specified Requirements

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.17646  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17646  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** A user-centric framework formalizes counterfactual harm and complementarity via rule-based prediction sets, and an online, distribution-free algorithm enforces user-specified violation rates (ε, δ) across multi-round interactions; empirical studies show these constraints act as reliable levers for guiding collaboration quality without modeling human behavior.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the challenge of providing principled guarantees in multi-round human‑AI collaboration. The authors introduce a framework that defines two key principles—counterfactual harm (CH) and complementarity (Comp)—through user‑specified binary rules applied to the AI’s prediction sets and the dialogue transcript. They propose an online, distribution‑free algorithm that dynamically adjusts set thresholds to meet prescribed violation rates ε and δ. The method is evaluated on an LLM‑simulated medical diagnosis task and a human crowdsourcing study for visual reasoning. Results demonstrate that the algorithm maintains the target error rates even under non‑stationary human behavior, and that tightening or loosening the constraints yields predictable, monotonic shifts in final human decision accuracy. This validates the rules as effective, interpretable controls for steering collaborative outcomes without requiring an explicit model of the human partner.

## Method Summary
The protocol proceeds in discrete “days”. Within each day, a human iteratively proposes prediction sets over the label space, while the AI responds with its own sets at each dialogue round. The AI’s sets are constructed using a nonconformity score (e.g., one minus model probability) and a linear combination of user‑defined rule activations, with thresholds τ and λ that are fixed for the entire day. After the true label is revealed, two errors are computed: the maximum across rounds of a CH rule indicator when the truth is omitted, and similarly for the Comp rule. Then a projected subgradient update (τ ← max(0, τ + η (E_CH − ε)), similarly for λ) adjusts the thresholds for the next day. Under the assumption that each rule’s online activation upper‑bounds the eventual rule value (Assumption 5.1), the cumulative average errors are guaranteed to remain within ε + O(1/T) and δ + O(1/T). The procedure is distribution‑free and treats the human as a black box; flexibility comes from encoding task‑specific intuitions about harmful omissions and complementary coverage directly into the rules.

## Key Results
- The online algorithm successfully maintained the specified violation rates ε and δ for both counterfactual harm and complementarity across an LLM‑simulated medical diagnosis task and a human crowdsourcing study, even when human behavior exhibited strong non‑stationarity.
- Systematic variation of ε and δ produced monotonic effects on downstream human accuracy: decreasing ε reduced human ground‑truth loss, while decreasing δ improved correct label recovery; this confirms the constraints act as interpretable levers for controlling collaboration quality.
- Theoretical finite‑sample guarantees (Theorem 5.2) were empirically validated, with error averages converging to the targets as predicted, despite the black‑box, adaptive human partner.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: User-Defined Rule Formalization
- **Claim:** Task‑specific definitions of counterfactual harm and complementarity are encoded via binary functions over the interaction transcript, replacing fixed indicator definitions from single‑round settings.
- **Mechanism:** For a given label 𝑦 and day 𝑡, rules \(R_{\text{CH}}(y, H_{t,1:N_t}, r)\) and \(R_{\text{Comp}}(y, H_{t,1:N_t}, r)\) evaluate to 1 when the AI must include 𝑦 at round 𝑟 to avoid a violation. Errors \(E_t^{\text{CH}}\) and \(E_t^{\text{Comp}}\) are defined as the maximum over rounds of the rule’s output times an indicator that \(y_t \notin C_{t,r}\). This allows flexible, application‑grounded constraints (e.g., “only at final round” or “if human ever proposed truth”).
- **Core assumption:** Rules satisfy Assumption 5.1: \(R(y, H_{t,1:N_t}, r) \le \bar{R}(y, H_{t,1:r})\) for all \(r \le N_t\), where \(\bar{R}\) is the online activation (treating current round as terminal). This ensures the algorithm’s online evaluation never under‑triggers relative to the eventual post‑hoc rule.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  Abstract: “formalize these concepts via user‑defined rules, allowing users to specify exactly what harm and complementarity mean”  
  Section 4.2: defines \(R_{\text{CH}}\), \(R_{\text{Comp}}\), and error indicators.  
  Corpus: Prior work on human‑AI complementarity explores formal definitions but not rule‑based, user‑specifiable ones.
- **Break condition:** If a user‑defined rule violates Assumption 5.1 (e.g., depends on future rounds beyond \(r\)), the online activation \(\bar{R}\) may not upper‑bound the true rule, potentially allowing undetected violations during the interaction.

### Mechanism 2: Online Threshold Update Algorithm
- **Claim:** A simple online projected subgradient method maintains prescribed violation rates \(\varepsilon, \delta\) over arbitrary interaction sequences without distributional assumptions.
- **Mechanism:** At the end of each day \(t\), after observing \(E_t^{\text{CH}}\) and \(E_t^{\text{Comp}}\), thresholds \(\tau_t\) and \(\lambda_t\) are updated via \(\tau_{t+1} = \max\{0, \tau_t + \eta (E_t^{\text{CH}} - \varepsilon)\}\) (similarly for \(\lambda\)). Within a day, the AI constructs prediction sets \(C_{t,r} = \{ y : s(T_{t,r}, y) \le \tau_t \bar{R}_{\text{CH}}(y, H_{t,1:r}) + \lambda_t \bar{R}_{\text{Comp}}(y, H_{t,1:r}) \}\). The projections keep thresholds non‑negative; the step size \(\eta\) controls adaptation speed.
- **Core assumption:** (i) The nonconformity score \(s\) yields valid uncertainty quantification (e.g., from a well‑calibrated model); (ii) Thresholds start at \(\tau_1 = \lambda_1 = 0\); (iii) Scores are exchangeable across rounds within a day—the key is that daily updates treat each day as an i.i.d. sample for the error rates, even if rounds within a day are dependent.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  Abstract: “online, distribution‑free algorithm with finite‑sample guarantees that enforces the user‑specified constraints.”  
  Section 5: presents the update rule and Theorem 5.2 bounding cumulative errors by \(\varepsilon + \frac{1+\eta}{\eta T}\).  
  Corpus: Connects to online conformal prediction literature (e.g., “Online conformal prediction with decaying step sizes”) but here applied to multi‑round, rule‑based errors.
- **Break condition:** If the nonconformity scores are invalid (e.g., LLM probabilities are miscalibrated such that \(s\) does not reflect true likelihood), the set construction may systematically exclude truth even when thresholds are high, causing violations independent of threshold adjustment.

### Mechanism 3: Multi‑Round Protocol with Transcript‑Aware Sets
- **Claim:** The AI’s prediction sets dynamically evolve across dialogue rounds by conditioning on the growing transcript, enabling the AI to adapt its uncertainty communication to the human’s evolving proposals while still enforcing daily threshold constraints.
- **Mechanism:** At round \(r\) on day \(t\), the AI constructs \(C_{t,r}\) based on the current transcript \(T_{t,r}\) (including all prior sets and messages) and the human’s current set \(H_{t,r}\). The thresholds \(\tau_t, \lambda_t\) are fixed for the entire day, but the set changes because \(s(T_{t,r}, y)\) and the rule activations \(\bar{R}(y, H_{t,1:r})\) depend on the transcript prefix. This decouples round‑to‑round adaptation (via transcript) from day‑to‑day calibration (via thresholds).
- **Core assumption:** The human’s prediction sets \(H_{t,r}\) are provided as black boxes; the AI does not model how they evolve. The algorithm only needs the sets to evaluate rules post‑hoc after the day ends.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  Abstract: “multi‑round dialogue… AI’s uncertainty sets can adapt dynamically to the evolving conversational transcript.”  
  Section 4.1: defines the interaction protocol: alternating \((H_{t,r}, U_{t,r})\) and \((C_{t,r}, A_{t,r})\), with \(N_t\) chosen by human.  
  Section 5: “the AI constructs its prediction set as… with thresholds \(\tau_t, \lambda_t\) fixed throughout each day \(t\).”
- **Break condition:** If the human strategically manipulates their sets (e.g., proposes truth early then stops to force a CH error later) and the rules do not capture this pattern, the AI’s sets may not align with the intended spirit of harm/complementarity despite meeting formal definitions.

## Foundational Learning

### Conformal Prediction (CP)
- **Why needed:** The AI’s prediction sets are built using a nonconformity score \(s(T_{t,r}, y)\), a core CP construct that turns model outputs (e.g., LLM probabilities) into distribution‑free uncertainty sets. The algorithm’s guarantees rely on the score’s exchangeability property.
- **Quick check:** In a classification setting with a softmax output, how would you define a nonconformity score to produce a prediction set that contains the true label with ~90% coverage?

### Online Calibration / Adaptive Learning
- **Why needed:** The thresholds \(\tau_t, \lambda_t\) are updated online based on streaming error signals \(E_t^{\text{CH}}, E_t^{\text{Comp}}\) without i.i.d. assumptions. This is an online subgradient method to track quantiles of the error distribution over time.
- **Quick check:** If the step size \(\eta\) is too large in the update \(\tau_{t+1} = \max\{0, \tau_t + \eta (E_t^{\text{CH}} - \varepsilon)\}\), what might happen to the stability of the thresholds and the empirical error rate?

### Single‑Round Human‑AI Collaboration (Noorani et al. [48])
- **Why needed:** This paper extends the prior single‑round framework where the AI acts once per instance. The multi‑round setting breaks the separation: AI actions influence human behavior, invalidating offline calibration. The single‑round optimal form \(C^*(x) = \{ y : s(x,y) \le a \cdot \mathbf{1}\{y \notin H\} + b \cdot \mathbf{1}\{y \in H\} \}\) inspires the multi‑round parameterization with rule‑dependent thresholds.
- **Quick check:** Why does the single‑round “exchangeable calibration” approach fail when the AI intervenes multiple times per problem instance?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Human Agent (H_{t,r}, U_{t,r}) → AI Agent (p_{t,r}(y), A_{t,r}) → Nonconformity Scorer (s(T_{t,r}, y)) → Rule Evaluator (\bar{R}_{\text{CH}}, \bar{R}_{\text{Comp}}) → Threshold Manager (τ_t, λ_t) → Set Constructor (C_{t,r}) → returned to Human Agent for next round.

### Critical path
Daily loop → (within day: for each round \(r\): compute \(C_{t,r}\) using current \(\tau_t,\lambda_t\) and transcript \(T_{t,r}\)) → day ends → compute \(E_t^{\text{CH}}\), \(E_t^{\text{Comp}}\) using full transcript and \(y_t\) → update \(\tau_{t+1}, \lambda_{t+1}\) → next day.

### Design tradeoffs
- *Distribution‑free vs. set size:* The algorithm makes no assumptions on \(P\) or human behavior, but does not optimize set size efficiency; it only enforces error constraints. Smaller sets require more accurate scores or smaller thresholds.
- *Black‑box human vs. rule expressivity:* Treating human as black‑box allows arbitrary behavior, but rules must be carefully designed to capture meaningful harm/complementarity. Complex rules may increase computational cost for evaluation.
- *Multi‑round transcript awareness vs. computational overhead:* Using full transcript history \(T_{t,r}\) in \(s(\cdot)\) can capture context but may lead to large input representations for the LLM scorer.

### Failure signatures
- **Diverging error curves:** Running average of \(E_t^{\text{CH}}\) or \(E_t^{\text{Comp}}\) steadily drifts away from \(\varepsilon\) or \(\delta\)—indicates score invalidity or rule violation of Assumption 5.1.
- **Human override harm:** Even with low \(E_t^{\text{CH}}\), human final decisions lose correct answers—may indicate human distrust or misinterpretation of sets despite formal harm avoidance.
- **Set explosion:** Prediction sets \(C_{t,r}\) become large (all labels) while thresholds rise—suggests score function is degenerate (e.g., uniform LLM probabilities) or target \(\varepsilon,\delta\) too stringent for the model’s discriminative power.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Convergence validation (LLM sim):** Run fixed \(\varepsilon, \delta\) for many days; plot running average of \(E_t^{\text{CH}}\) and \(E_t^{\text{Comp}}\). Should stabilize near targets. Verifies algorithm works in controlled, non‑stationary human sim.
2. **Lever effect (LLM sim):** Vary \(\varepsilon\) or \(\delta\) across runs; measure downstream human final accuracy (GT gain/loss). Expect monotonic relationships: lower \(\varepsilon\) → less human GT loss; lower \(\delta\) → higher GT recovery. Confirms principles are effective levers.
3. **Real human stress test (crowdsourcing):** Implement with human participants, use sequential participation to create strong non‑stationarity. Check that error rates still converge despite varying individual accuracy, speed, trust. Validates distribution‑free guarantees in wild.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1**  
- **Question:** How can the rule‑based framework be extended to uncertainty representations beyond prediction sets (e.g., confidence intervals, probability distributions) for tasks like regression or generative modeling?  
- **Basis:** The authors state in Section 7 (Discussion) that a key limitation is controlling collaboration dynamics via prediction sets and that extending to alternative uncertainty objects is a future direction.  
- **Why unresolved:** The current algorithm relies on set membership checks derived from nonconformity scores, which do not directly translate to continuous or structured uncertainty representations.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A new method that maps user‑defined rules to constraints on alternative uncertainty representations (e.g., intervals or densities) with provable finite‑sample guarantees, validated on non‑classification tasks.

**Open Question 2**  
- **Question:** What practical guidance can be given to non‑expert users for designing effective rule functions (R_CH and R_Comp) that avoid vacuous or overly restrictive constraints?  
- **Basis:** The paper allows arbitrary user‑defined rules but does not address how to choose them or their impact on set size and collaboration quality.  
- **Why unresolved:** Poorly chosen rules might rarely trigger (undermining the principles) or impose excessive set size inflation, but the paper offers no methodology for rule design or evaluation.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A set of principled rule templates for common task types, coupled with user studies measuring how rule granularity affects both error rates and downstream human accuracy.

**Open Question 3**  
- **Question:** How does intra‑session non‑stationarity (e.g., human fatigue, trust evolution) within a single multi‑round dialogue affect the algorithm’s ability to maintain target violation rates?  
- **Basis:** The algorithm updates thresholds only at the end of each problem instance (day), assuming stationarity across rounds within a session. However, human behavior may drift during a dialogue, violating the exchangeability needed for the online calibration guarantees.  
- **Why unresolved:** Theorem 5.2 guarantees control over cumulative errors across instances, but intra‑session shifts could cause localized spikes in violations within a session that the fixed daily thresholds cannot correct.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Simulations or experiments that induce controlled intra‑session changes (e.g., gradually increasing human reliance on AI) and measure whether violation rates exceed targets in later rounds; alternatively, an algorithm with round‑level threshold adaptation.

**Open Question 4**  
- **Question:** Do counterfactual harm and complementarity remain meaningful and enforceable levers for collaboration quality in domains where the output space is continuous or open‑ended (e.g., collaborative text editing or parameter tuning)?  
- **Basis:** The experiments use discrete classification tasks with finite label spaces. The definitions of harm and complementarity rely on set membership, which is ambiguous for continuous or generative outputs.  
- **Why unresolved:** The rule‑based formulation may not generalize to tasks where “including the truth” is not a discrete event, and the nonconformity score approach may require fundamental reinterpretation.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Reformulations of harm and complementarity for continuous domains, implemented as new rule types and score functions, with empirical validation showing predictable effects on human decisions.

## Limitations
- User‑defined rules must satisfy Assumption 5.1; otherwise theoretical guarantees fail, and the paper provides no automated check or design guidance.
- The algorithm relies on well‑calibrated model probabilities; LLMs are often miscalibrated, which can lead to invalid prediction sets and uncontrolled error rates.
- No optimization of prediction‑set size is performed; under stringent thresholds, sets may become overly large, reducing practical utility.

## Confidence
- **Rule formalization (soundness & completeness):** Medium  
- **Algorithmic guarantee (Theorem 5.2):** High  
- **Empirical maintenance of error rates:** Medium  
- **Constraints as effective levers:** Medium  

## Next Checks
1. **Assumption 5.1 violation test:** Construct a rule that depends on future rounds (e.g., “include label if human will propose it later”). Run the algorithm; if empirical violation rates exceed the bound, it confirms the assumption’s necessity.
2. **Score calibration robustness:** For each LLM used, apply temperature scaling or Platt calibration to improve probability calibration. Compare error‑rate adherence and set size against raw probabilities; miscalibrated scores should degrade performance.
3. **Multi‑task human replication:** Recruit at least 200 participants across three distinct tasks (e.g., medical diagnosis, natural language inference, mathematical problem solving). Verify that the algorithm maintains ε, δ across tasks and that tightening/loosening them monotonically shifts final human accuracy, controlling for set size.
