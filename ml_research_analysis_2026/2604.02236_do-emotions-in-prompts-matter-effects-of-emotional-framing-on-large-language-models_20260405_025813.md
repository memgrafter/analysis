# Do Emotions in Prompts Matter? Effects of Emotional Framing on Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.02236
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02236
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Adaptive emotion selection via EmotionRL yields reliable accuracy gains over static emotional prompting and no-emotion baselines.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether first-person emotional framing in user prompts systematically influences large language model (LLM) performance across six benchmarks spanning mathematical reasoning, medical QA, reading comprehension, commonsense reasoning, and social inference. The authors find that applying a static emotional prefix (one of six basic emotions) produces only small and inconsistent accuracy changes (±0.02–0.06), with the largest variations occurring in socially grounded tasks like SocialIQA. In contrast, an adaptive method called EmotionRL—which learns to select the most beneficial emotion for each specific input—achieves more consistent improvements over the no-emotion baseline (e.g., +0.05 to +1.10 percentage points). The key conclusion is that emotional tone acts as a weak, input-dependent signal rather than a dominant factor, and its benefits can be reliably harnessed through per-instance adaptive control.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates emotional framing through two main approaches. First, **static prompting** prepends a short first-person emotional sentence (e.g., "I am very happy...") to each test question and measures accuracy using frozen LLMs (Qwen3-14B, Llama 3.3-70B, DeepSeek-V3.2) in zero-shot mode. Six basic emotions are tested across six benchmark datasets. Second, **EmotionRL** trains a lightweight MLP policy on the training split of each dataset. The policy receives frozen sentence embeddings of the input and is trained via reward-weighted cross-entropy to predict which of the six emotion-conditioned prompts yields a correct answer from the frozen backbone LLM. At test time, the policy selects an emotion per input, which is then prepended to the query for a single LLM generation.

## Key Results
- Static emotional prefixes induce small (±0.02–0.06) and inconsistent accuracy deltas relative to no-emotion baselines, with the largest fluctuations in SocialIQA.
- Varying emotional intensity or using human-written versus LLM-generated prefixes does not materially change the pattern of weak static effects.
- EmotionRL’s instance-adaptive emotion selection produces reliable and sometimes substantial gains (up to +1.10 percentage points) over both static averaging and no-emotion baselines.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Emotional framing provides a subtle affective signal that interacts with input-specific features. Static prompts impose a uniform emotional tone regardless of the query, leading to averaged-out or even harmful effects because the wrong emotion may hinder reasoning. EmotionRL succeeds by learning a mapping from the semantic content of each input (captured by sentence embeddings) to the emotion that historically maximized correctness for similar queries. This adaptive matching exploits the fact that sensitivity to emotional tone is not uniform across tasks; socially oriented queries (e.g., SocialIQA) show more variable responses, making per-instance selection beneficial. The mechanism thus shifts from a global, one-size-fits-all application to a localized, data-driven alignment of affective context with query characteristics.

## Foundational Learning
- **Emotional Framing**: Prepending affective first-person statements to prompts; *why needed*: Tests whether LLMs parse and utilize emotional context as part of user intent; *quick check*: Compare accuracy with and without an emotional prefix on a held-out set.
- **Static vs. Adaptive Prompting**: Fixed emotion across all inputs vs. per-instance selection; *why needed*: Static may neutralize average gains, while adaptation can capture heterogeneity; *quick check*: Compute mean accuracy across six static emotions and compare to adaptive policy’s test accuracy.
- **Reward Caching**: Precomputing binary correctness labels for each emotion-input pair; *why needed*: Enables efficient policy training without repeated LLM calls; *quick check*: Verify each training sample has six reward entries (0/1) stored.
- **Policy Training via Cross-Entropy**: Using rewards as weights to train an MLP to predict optimal emotions; *why needed*: Simple supervised approach to learn from offline feedback; *quick check*: Ensure policy’s predicted emotion matches highest-reward choice on training instances.
- **Sentence Embeddings**: Dense vector representations of input text; *why needed*: Provides compressed semantic state for policy decision; *quick check*: Check embedding dimensionality and that similar inputs have high cosine similarity.
- **Zero-Shot Frozen Evaluation**: Running LLM without gradient updates and deterministic decoding; *why needed*: Isolates framing effect from model adaptation; *quick check*: Confirm model weights remain unchanged and temperature=0 during test inference.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Sentence Encoder (frozen) -> Policy MLP (trainable) -> Emotion Selection -> LLM Backbone (frozen). During training, a Reward Cache sits parallel to provide labels.

**Critical path**: Training: For each sample, encode input with Sentence Encoder → lookup six precomputed rewards from Reward Cache → compute weighted cross-entropy loss → update Policy MLP. Test: Encode input → Policy MLP selects emotion → prepend emotional prefix → LLM Backbone generates answer.

**Design tradeoffs**: 
- Policy simplicity (MLP) vs. expressiveness: MLP is parameter-efficient but may miss non-linear interaction between embedding and optimal emotion.
- Reward caching vs. computational cost: Caching avoids repeated LLM inference but requires upfront compute and storage.
- Frozen LLM ensures fair comparison but prevents end-to-end fine-tuning that might further improve performance.

**Failure signatures**: 
- Policy accuracy on training set near random (1/6) indicates poor learning.
- Test gains from EmotionRL are less than the average static delta, suggesting policy is not capturing useful signal.
- Large performance drop when switching sentence encoders indicates encoder sensitivity.

**First three experiments**: 
1. Validate Reward Cache completeness: Loop through all training samples and assert each has six non-null reward entries.
2. Monitor Policy loss decay: Plot training loss per epoch; expect steady decrease and convergence within a few epochs.
3. Check test-time emotion distribution: Compare histogram of emotions selected by policy vs. uniform baseline; deviation indicates learned bias.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **What mechanisms explain the greater sensitivity of social reasoning tasks (e.g., SocialIQA) to emotional framing compared to other benchmarks?** [Inferred] The results show larger accuracy shifts in SocialIQA across models and emotions, but the paper does not investigate why socially grounded tasks exhibit more variable effects. Identifying the linguistic or cognitive factors that amplify emotional influence in social contexts could clarify how LLMs integrate affective cues with interpersonal reasoning and guide the design of adaptive prompting for such tasks. What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments isolating social vs. non-social elements within identical task structures, or interpretability analyses probing model attention and hidden states under emotional prompts in social versus abstract reasoning scenarios.

- **How does the EmotionRL policy select emotions per instance, and what input features predict when a specific emotional framing improves accuracy?** [Inferred] EmotionRL demonstrates that instance-adaptive selection yields reliable gains, but the paper does not analyze the policy’s internal decisions or the characteristics of inputs that benefit from particular emotions. Understanding the policy’s decision boundaries could reveal general principles for matching emotional tone to query properties, improving transparency and enabling rule-based approximations without retraining. What evidence would resolve it: Post-hoc analysis correlating selected emotions with semantic features (topic, question type, difficulty), visualizing policy activations via dimensionality reduction, or feature attribution methods to identify influential tokens or concepts in the embedding space.

- **Do emotional framing effects generalize to multi-turn dialogues, open-ended generation, or evaluations emphasizing safety, empathy, or stylistic alignment rather than accuracy?** [Explicit] The discussion explicitly notes the study’s focus on single-turn, accuracy-oriented benchmarks and suggests that larger or qualitatively different effects may emerge in multi-turn interactions, open-ended generation, or safety-sensitive dialogue. Real-world human-LLM interactions often involve extended conversations and require balancing correctness with social and ethical considerations; emotional framing might have stronger or divergent impacts in these settings. What evidence would resolve it: Replicating the experimental design in multi-turn dialogue benchmarks (e.g., persuasion, counseling simulations) and measuring metrics like user trust, harmlessness, or perceived empathy alongside accuracy. Testing emotional framing in safety-critical applications (e.g., mental health support, customer dispute resolution).

- **Would alternative emotion representations—such as continuous valence-arousal dimensions or cross-cultural emotion taxonomies—produce different patterns of LLM sensitivity compared to the six basic emotions tested?** [Inferred] The study operationalizes emotion using six discrete categories from Plutchik and Russell but does not compare other frameworks, leaving open whether the observed weak and heterogeneous effects are specific to this taxonomy. Emotions are multifaceted and culturally variable; different representations might align better with LLM training data or internal affective representations, potentially uncovering more robust or interpretable effects. What evidence would resolve it: Running experiments with prompts varying along valence (positive/negative) and arousal (calm/excited) continua, or including emotions from non-Western taxonomies (e.g., interdependent emotions like “amae” in Japanese). Comparing model accuracy distributions across these schemes to assess whether granularity or cultural specificity changes sensitivity.

## Limitations
- GPT‑4o generation parameters for emotional prefixes are unreported; different sampling settings may alter prefix style and affect results.
- The pretrained sentence‑embedding model used in EmotionRL is not named; embedding choice could influence policy training and final gains.
- Full prompt templates for each benchmark are not provided; formatting variations might interact with emotional prefixes.
- Evaluation is limited to large proprietary models in zero‑shot settings; other architectures, scales, or training paradigms remain unexplored.

## Confidence
- **Static emotional prefixes yield small, inconsistent accuracy changes:** Medium (tiny deltas susceptible to unreported generation parameters and template details).
- **EmotionRL provides reliable gains over baselines:** Medium (depends on unspecified encoder and hyperparameters; gains may not transfer to other models/datasets).
- **Emotional tone is a weak, input‑dependent signal exploitable via adaptive control:** Medium (conclusion holds across tested benchmarks but scope is bounded by task and model selection).

## Next Checks
- **Static replication:** On SocialIQA with Qwen3-14B, manually create six emotional prefixes (5–8 words, first-person). Confirm deltas relative to no‑emotion baseline are within ±0.06 and the gap between best and worst emotions is at least 0.04.
- **Encoder ablation:** Train EmotionRL on MedQA using `all-MiniLM-L6-v2` as the sentence encoder. Verify that test accuracy beats the average static emotion baseline by at least 0.05 percentage points on at least 4 out of 6 tasks.
- **Generation robustness:** Generate GSM8K emotional prefixes with temperature 0 and 0.7. Show that the difference in static accuracy delta between these two settings is less than 0.02, indicating insensitivity to generation randomness.
