# TSR: Trajectory-Search Rollouts for Multi-Turn RL of LLM Agents

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.11767  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11767  
- **Reference count:** 34  
- **Primary result:** TSR improves success rates by up to 15% absolute (e.g., 85.3% vs 70.3% on WebShop) and stabilizes training with smoother gradient norms, while enabling a 0.5B model to surpass GPT-4o and 72B models on Sokoban and FrozenLake.

## Executive Summary
Multi-turn reinforcement learning for LLM agents is hampered by sparse rewards and unstable training due to low-quality rollouts. TSR addresses this by integrating lightweight tree-style search (best-of-N, beam, lookahead) directly into the rollout generation phase. It selects high-scoring actions at each turn using task-specific feedback without altering the policy optimization objective. TSR yields significant gains across Sokoban, FrozenLake, and WebShop: up to 15% absolute improvement in success rate, superior performance of a 0.5B model over 72B baselines, and improved inference efficiency. These benefits come from a one-time increase in training compute, after which convergence time remains comparable to standard baselines.

## Method Summary
TSR modifies only the rollout generation process. Given a prompt and the current policy πθ, the TSR module iterates over turns: at each step it samples a set of candidate actions, executes them to obtain next states, scores each (state, action) pair using a task-specific scoring function (environment reward or proxy heuristic), and then applies a search strategy (best-of-N, beam, or shallow lookahead) to pick the action that will be taken in the trajectory. The resulting high-quality trajectory is stored for policy updates. The policy optimizer (PPO or GRPO) and its loss remain unchanged, making TSR optimizer-agnostic and compatible with complementary techniques like instance filtering.

## Key Results
- On WebShop with Qwen2.5-3B, TSR achieves 85.3% success vs 70.3% for the best baseline.
- A 0.5B model trained with TSR surpasses GPT-4o and 72B models on Sokoban and FrozenLake.
- Training stabilizes with gradient norms free of spikes, and inference becomes more efficient (shorter responses, fewer turns).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
TSR improves multi-turn RL through three interrelated mechanisms. First, by performing tree search (best-of-N, beam, or shallow lookahead) at each turn, it steers rollouts away from irreversible dead ends (e.g., pushing a box into a corner). This is possible because a local scoring function—even if coarse—predicts long-term viability of actions, pruning catastrophic branches early. Second, the search biases the rollout distribution toward higher-reward trajectories, increasing the signal-to-noise ratio of advantage estimates and stabilizing gradient updates; indeed TSR eliminates the spikes in gradient norms observed with naive sampling. Third, TSR is fully modular: it intercepts only the rollout generator while leaving the policy gradient objective untouched, so it can be combined with any optimizer (PPO, GRPO) and with complementary techniques like instance filtering. The one-time compute overhead during rollout generation yields faster convergence and better final performance.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Task -> (Instance Filter) -> TSR Rollout Generator -> Trajectory Buffer -> Policy Optimizer (PPO/GRPO) -> Policy Network

**Critical Path:**  
1. Sample a batch of task prompts.  
2. Optionally apply instance filtering to select the most uncertain task groups.  
3. For each selected task, generate trajectories via TSR: at each turn, sample candidate actions from πθ, score them using a task-specific scoring function, and select actions using a search strategy (best-of-N, beam, or lookahead) to construct a full trajectory.  
4. Store trajectories in a buffer.  
5. Compute advantages and perform a policy gradient update (PPO/GRPO) on πθ.  
6. Repeat from step 1 with the updated policy.

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- Search Budget vs. Compute: Larger candidate sets (higher N, B, M, D) improve rollout quality but increase training time; diminishing returns observed beyond moderate budgets.  
- Proxy Score Design vs. Task Fidelity: Hand-crafted heuristics are needed for sparse-reward tasks but may mislead search if they disregard long-term consequences.  
- Trajectory Quality vs. Diversity: Aggressive search can overfit to narrow high-scoring paths; instance filtering is used to maintain diversity.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- Spiking gradient norms indicate rollout collapse or insufficient search budget.  
- Stagnant success rates may result from misaligned scoring functions or overly aggressive pruning.  
- Poor inference efficiency (long responses/turns) suggests the policy hasn't internalized the efficient strategies demonstrated by TSR rollouts.

**First 3 Experiments:**  
1. Compare TSR (best-of-N, beam, lookahead) against instance filtering baseline on Sokoban to validate convergence speed and final performance.  
2. Ablate search budget (vary beam width B and branching M) to identify optimal compute-quality tradeoff.  
3. Evaluate TSR across environments with varying reward sparsity (Sokoban, FrozenLake, WebShop) to confirm robustness, expecting largest absolute gain on WebShop.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How effectively can TSR be integrated with complementary multi-turn RL frameworks (e.g., ScalingInter-RL, hierarchical RL), and does it maintain its performance and stability benefits when combined?**  
   **Basis in paper:** The authors state in Section 3.3: "Since TSR is modular, it can be combined with other mechanisms that encourage exploration (e.g., ScalingInter-RL (Xi et al., 2025a)) or hierarchical RL methods (e.g., (Luo et al., 2025)). A systematic study of integrating TSR with complementary methods is an interesting direction for future work."  
   **Why unresolved:** The paper only evaluates TSR with instance-level filtering and does not empirically explore integration with other frameworks, leaving the synergistic effects and potential trade-offs unknown.  
   **What evidence would resolve it:** Running experiments that combine TSR with those frameworks across the same environments, measuring success rate, training stability, and wall-clock time to see if benefits compound or interfere.

2. **Does combining TSR with rejection-sampling-style selection methods (e.g., GFPO, RAFT, StarPO) lead to further improvements in multi-turn RL, and if so, under what conditions?**  
   **Basis in paper:** The abstract notes that TSR is "complementary to existing frameworks and rejection-sampling-style selection methods," but the paper does not test such combinations.  
   **Why unresolved:** The interaction between trajectory search at training and reward-based response filtering has not been studied; it is unclear whether the two approaches improve performance additively, multiplicatively, or conflict.  
   **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement hybrid setups where TSR-generated rollouts are further filtered by methods like GFPO's length-or-reward-per-token criteria, then compare final success rates and sample efficiency against each method alone.

3. **What is the compute-optimal allocation of search budget (branching factor, beam width, lookahead depth) in TSR across different tasks and model scales, and does the optimal point shift when moving from training to inference?**  
   **Basis in paper:** The authors remark in the related works and conclusion that they "do not claim compute-optimal allocation" and perform only moderate ablations. They observe diminishing returns when increasing search parameters.  
   **Why unresolved:** The paper uses a fixed moderate budget (e.g., M=4, B=2) without exploring the full spectrum of possible budgets or deriving a theoretical model; thus the frontier between compute cost and performance gain remains vague.  
   **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct extensive scaling experiments varying M, B, D over a wider range (including much larger values) and fit a performance-vs-compute curve per environment. This could be complemented by a theoretical analysis of how search depth interacts with horizon length and sparsity.

4. **Do the benefits of TSR extend to larger language models (e.g., 7B, 13B) and more complex, real-world domains such as tool-use or embodied navigation?**  
   **Basis in paper:** The experiments are limited to Qwen2.5-0.5B and -3B models and three synthetic environments. The paper hypothesizes that TSR is general, but this has not been validated beyond the chosen setups.  
   **Why unresolved:** Scaling to larger models introduces different dynamics (e.g., longer contexts, more capabilities) and potentially different failure modes; complex domains may require richer search spaces and scoring functions.  
   **What evidence would resolve it:** Train larger LLMs (e.g., 7B+) with TSR on tasks like AgentBench, WebArena, or embodied simulators, and compare success rates and stability against naive rollouts and strong baselines.

5. **Can the scoring function S in TSR be learned (e.g., via a value network or reward model) to automatically adapt to environment dynamics and improve rollout quality beyond hand-designed heuristics?**  
   **Basis in paper:** The text states that S "may also incorporate heuristic signals, learned value estimates, or proxy feedback," but all experiments used fixed environment rewards or simple hand-crafted proxies. The potential of learned guidance is unexplored.  
   **Why unresolved:** Hand-crafted proxies may not be available for new environments; a learned S could generalize and reduce manual engineering, but might introduce instability or additional compute.  
   **What evidence would resolve it:** Introduce a secondary network that predicts a score for candidate actions (e.g., trained on intermediate outcomes or final returns) and use it to drive TSR. Compare performance against the hand-designed baseline across the three tasks, controlling for total compute.

## Limitations
- TSR relies on carefully designed scoring functions; poorly chosen heuristics can misguide search and degrade performance.
- The approach incurs additional compute during rollout generation, which may be prohibitive for very long-horizon tasks without careful budget tuning.
- Evaluation is currently limited to small models (≤3B) and synthetic environments, leaving generalization to larger models and real-world domains unproven.
