# Activation Matters: Test-time Activated Negative Labels for OOD Detection with Vision-Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.25250
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25250
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** On ImageNet-1k with CLIP ViT-B/16, TANL reduces average FPR95 from 17.5% to 9.8% and raises AUROC to 97.97% across iNaturalist, SUN, Places, Textures.

## Executive Summary
Negative-label based out-of-distribution (OOD) detection with vision-language models (VLMs) typically relies on a static set of negative labels, which may not adequately capture the semantics of unseen OOD data. This paper introduces Test-time Activated Negative Labels (TANL), a training-free method that dynamically selects and reweights negative labels during test time based on their activation on the current distribution. TANL maintains two FIFO queues to accumulate high-confidence in-distribution (ID) and OOD image features from the test stream, computes an activation difference score for each candidate label from a large corpus, and picks the most OOD-relevant labels. An activation-aware scoring function implicitly weights these labels by their activation rank. The method also incorporates batch-level statistics for fine-grained adaptation. On ImageNet-1k with CLIP ViT-B/16, TANL reduces average FPR95 from 17.5% to 9.8% and raises AUROC to 97.97% across multiple OOD benchmarks, outperforming existing methods and showing robustness across backbones and batch sizes.

## Method Summary
TANL is a zero-shot, test-time OOD detection method for frozen vision-language models like CLIP. It assumes access to a large corpus of candidate negative labels (e.g., WordNet nouns/adjectives). During testing, it initializes two FIFO queues: `Xpos` with ID text features, and `Xneg` with random noise image features. For each incoming test batch, it first computes OOD scores `Saa(v)` for all images using the currently selected negative labels (initially derived from activation scores based on the queues). It then splits the batch into pseudo-positives (`Xb_pos`) and pseudo-negatives (`Xb_neg`) by thresholding `Saa` around a dynamically estimated `γ`. Next, it computes a batch-adaptive activation score `dActb(yi)` for each candidate label `yi` by blending historical activation from the queues and activation from the current batch positives/negatives. The top-`M` labels with highest `dActb` are selected as the activated negative set. The OOD score for an image is the average of the softmax-normalized similarity to these selected labels (implicitly ranking them). Finally, the queues are updated by enqueuing the features of `Xb_pos` and `Xb_neg`, discarding the oldest. This online adaptation allows TANL to track distribution drift without any training.

## Key Results
- Reduces average FPR95 from 17.5% to 9.8% and increases AUROC to 97.97% on ImageNet-1k with CLIP ViT-B/16 across iNaturalist, SUN, Places, Textures.
- Outperforms training-required methods and demonstrates robustness across different backbones, near/far-OOD shifts, and batch sizes.
- Enables online adaptation to test distribution shifts via FIFO queue updates and theoretical analysis confirms activated labels reduce false positives.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Dynamically selecting negative labels based on test-time activation scores overcomes the limitation of static negative labels that poorly activate on specific OOD distributions.
- **Mechanism:** TANL maintains two FIFO queues (`Xpos`, `Xneg`) storing high-confidence ID and OOD image features from the test stream. It computes an activation metric `dActd(byi) = Act(Xneg, byi) - Act(Xpos, byi)` for each candidate label `byi` in a large corpus dataset, where `Act(X, byi)` is the average softmax-normalized similarity between `byi` and images in `X`. Labels with the highest `dActd` are selected as activated negatives.
- **Core assumption:** High `dActd` indicates a label that is more characteristic of the current (approximated) OOD distribution than the ID distribution, thus better capturing OOD semantics.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] "it dynamically measures label activation by accumulating assignment probabilities from high-confidence ID/OOD images stored in FIFO queues, selecting negative labels with high (Act(OOD)-Act(ID)) scores"  
  - [section 3.3] Equation 8 defines `dActd` using cached queues; Equation 6 selects top-M labels by this score.  
  - [corpus] Evidence is strong: corpus neighbors (AdaNeg, NegLabel) confirm the premise that static negative labels are suboptimal; TANL's novelty is dynamic activation measurement.
- **Break condition:** The corpus dataset must contain labels semantically related to the potential OOD distribution. If the OOD concepts are absent from the corpus, no activated labels can be found.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The activation-aware score function `Saa` implicitly weights the contribution of each selected negative label by its activation rank, boosting performance and robustness to the number of negative labels.
- **Mechanism:** For a test image feature `v`, `Saa(v) = (1/M) * sum_{m=1 to M} [ sum_{i=1 to C} exp(v·ti) / ( sum_{j=1 to C} exp(v·tj) + sum_{j=1 to m} exp(v·etj) ) ]`. Because labels are pre-ranked by `dActb` (descending), the denominator incrementally includes terms for increasingly less-activated labels. Labels with higher activation (smaller `m`) appear in more terms, thus receiving higher implicit weight.
- **Core assumption:** The rank-based summation effectively up-weights negative labels with stronger activation on the test distribution, which should be more discriminative for OOD detection.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] "scoring with an activation-aware function that implicitly weights selected labels by rank"  
  - [section 3.3] Equation 15 defines `Saa`; text following it explains the ranking guarantee `dActb(eyi) >= dActb(eyj)` for `i < j`.  
  - [corpus] Theoretical analysis (Section 3.3, Eq. 16) links reduced FPR to `p1 - p2 < 0`, which `dActb` aims to ensure.
- **Break condition:** If the activation ranking is noisy or unstable (e.g., due to extreme early errors corrupting the queues), the implicit weighting may amplify incorrect label contributions.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Incorporating batch-level activation statistics provides fine-grained adaptation to immediate test batch characteristics and temporal shifts, complementing the historical distribution knowledge from queues.
- **Mechanism:** For each test batch `B`, TANL identifies possible ID/OOD samples (`Xb_pos`, `Xb_neg`) using the current `Saa`. It then defines a batch-adaptive activation score `dActb(byi) = α * Act(X_queues, byi) + (1-α) * Act(X_batch, byi)`, blending historical and current batch activation estimates (Eq. 12-14).
- **Core assumption:** The current batch, even if small, contains useful statistical information about the immediate test distribution that can correct for long-term queue drift or sudden distribution shifts.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] "it also leverages batch-level activation for fine-grained adaptation"  
  - [section 3.3] Equations 11-14 define the batch-adaptive variant and the blending parameter `α`.  
  - [section 4.3] Fig 3c shows α=0.95 is optimal; ablation (Tab 4) shows "Batch-adapt" provides marginal but consistent gains.
- **Break condition:** With very small batch sizes (e.g., batch=1), the batch statistics are highly noisy and may degrade performance if `α` is too low. The method relies on having at least a few confident samples in a batch to estimate `Act(X_batch, byi)` meaningfully.

## Foundational Learning
### Activation Metric (`Act(X, byi)`)
- **Why needed here:** Quantifies how "active" or representative a candidate text label `byi` is for a set of images `X`. It is the core quantity used to select effective negative labels. Without understanding it, one cannot grasp how TANL identifies which corpus words are "OOD-like".
- **Quick check:** If `Act(X_ood, byi)` is high and `Act(X_id, byi)` is low, what does that suggest about label `byi` for OOD detection?  
  *Answer: It suggests `byi` is more characteristic of OOD data than ID data, making it a good activated negative label.*

### FIFO Queue for Test-Time Adaptation
- **Why needed here:** Provides a lightweight, non-parametric memory to accumulate historical high-confidence ID/OOD evidence during testing. This enables dynamic estimation of `Act(X_pos, byi)` and `Act(X_neg, byi)` without retraining.
- **Quick check:** What would happen if the FIFO queue was replaced with a fixed, pre-computed set of ID/OOD images (i.e., no test-time update)?  
  *Answer: The activation estimates would become static, losing the ability to adapt to distribution drift over the test stream.*

### Test-Time Adaptation (TTA)
- **Why needed here:** TANL is a TTA method. It assumes the test distribution may drift over time (concept drift, changing environments). The online queue update and batch adaptation are mechanisms to track this drift.
- **Quick check:** How does TANL's use of FIFO queues differ from a method that requires test-time gradient optimization?  
  *Answer: TANL uses simple queue updates (non-parametric, no gradients) while gradient-based TTA adjusts model parameters, which is computationally heavier and may cause catastrophic forgetting.*

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map

Activation Score Calculator -> Negative Label Selector

High-Confidence Sample Filter -> FIFO Queues  

### Critical Path
1. Initialize `Xpos` (ID text features), `Xneg` (noise images).
2. For each test batch `B`:
   a. Compute `Saa` for batch using *current* selected negatives (from previous step).
   b. Use `Saa` to split `B` into `Xb_pos`, `Xb_neg`.
   c. Compute batch-adaptive `dActb` using `Xpos`, `Xneg`, `Xb_pos`, `Xb_neg`.
   d. Select new top-M negative labels `Y-` from `Y_cor` based on `dActb`.
   e. Update `Xpos` and `Xneg` queues by enqueuing high-confidence features from `Xb_pos`, `Xb_neg` (dequeuing oldest).
   f. Store `Saa` scores for batch.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Stability vs. Adaptability:** Large FIFO queue (L) provides stable activation estimates but adapts slowly. Small L adapts faster but is noisier. α controls blend of historical (stable) vs. batch (adaptive) info.
- **Precision vs. Coverage (M):** Small M uses only highly activated labels (precise) but may miss some OOD concepts. Large M covers more but includes less-activated/noisy labels. `Saa`'s implicit weighting makes it more robust to M than baselines.
- **Thresholding (`γ`, `g`):** Tight thresholds (large `g`) yield purer `Xb_pos/neg` but fewer samples to update queues. Loose thresholds provide more data but risk contaminating queues with misclassified samples.

### Failure Signatures
- **Poor initial performance:** First few batches may have high error if initialization (noise in `Xneg`) is too far from true OOD distribution.
- **Degradation under extreme temporal shift:** If OOD distribution changes drastically and `Xneg` becomes contaminated with ID-like features, `dActb` may select poor labels. Queue length L must be long enough to forget old OOD but short enough to adapt.
- **Corpus mismatch:** If `Y_cor` lacks synonyms/semantic neighbors for the true OOD concepts, `dActb` cannot select meaningful labels, leading to performance close to NegLabel.
- **Batch size = 1 with low α:** `Act(Xb_neg, byi)` becomes a single-sample probability, extremely noisy, causing erratic label selection.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Main Results Comparison (Tab 1, 2):** Validate that TANL outperforms static-label (NegLabel, CSP) and other test-time adaptive (AdaNeg, OODD) methods on ImageNet benchmarks (FPR95, AUROC). This confirms the core claim that activated labels improve detection.
2. **Ablation Study (Tab 4):** Isolate contributions of (a) Distribution-adaptive activation (`Dis-adapt`), (b) Batch-adaptive (`Batch-adapt`), (c) Activation-aware score (`AAScore`). Shows each component contributes, with `AAScore` and `Dis-adapt` being primary drivers.
3. **Label Number Sensitivity (Fig 3a):** Vary M (number of selected negatives). Shows TANL (w/ `Saa`) is robust to M; performance degrades gracefully as M increases and noisy labels are included, unlike NegLabel which degrades more steeply. Validates the implicit weighting design.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What corpus dataset properties (semantic diversity, domain relevance, size) most significantly determine TANL's effectiveness, and can we derive principled guidelines for corpus construction in novel domains (e.g., medical imaging)?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors explicitly state as a key limitation that their method assumes "the corpus dataset covers words related to the OOD distribution and that the pre-trained text encoder understands these words," noting that WordNet lacks medical terminology and CLIP has limited medical image understanding. They experiment with three corpora but do not analyze what properties drive performance.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper provides neither theoretical analysis linking corpus characteristics to activation metric properties nor systematic empirical studies beyond coarse size variation. It remains unclear whether semantic diversity, term specificity, or domain alignment is most critical.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments varying corpus properties (e.g., using domain-specific vs. general corpora, measuring semantic diversity via embedding metrics) across diverse OOD scenarios, especially domain-shifted ones like medical images. Theoretical work relating corpus coverage to activation score variance and OOD separability.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What is the theoretical trade-off between distribution-adaptive (historical queue) and batch-adaptive activation information, and can the balancing parameter α be set adaptively based on test stream characteristics rather than fixed at 0.95?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors introduce α to combine historical and batch activation but determine its value (0.95) via validation on ImageNet. They show that decreasing α further degrades performance (Fig 3c), suggesting a sensitivity to batch noise, but offer no theoretical justification for this trade-off.
- **Why unresolved:** There is no analysis of estimation error or convergence properties as α varies. The optimal α likely depends on batch size, distribution drift rate, and OOD difficulty, but the paper treats it as a static hyperparameter.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Theoretical bounds on activation estimation error under different α, empirical studies across varying stream dynamics (abrupt vs. gradual shifts), and adaptive α schemes that respond to detected changes in the test distribution (e.g., via distribution discrepancy metrics).

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What are the sample complexity requirements for reliable activation estimation—specifically, how many test samples and what queue length L are needed—and how do these requirements scale with OOD difficulty (near vs. far) and ID dataset size?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors empirically find that L=300 saturates performance (Fig A5a) and ~10K test samples lead to convergence (Fig A7b) on ImageNet-scale data. However, these observations are dataset-specific and lack generalization theory.
- **Why unresolved:** No confidence intervals or convergence rates for activation estimates are provided. Requirements for smaller ID datasets (e.g., CIFAR) or more subtle near-OOD shifts may differ, but this is not analyzed. The paper does not relate sample needs to statistical properties of the activation metric.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Theoretical sample complexity bounds linking activation estimation error to OOD distinguishability (e.g., p1–p2 gap), experiments across varied ID scales (CIFAR-10 to ImageNet) and OOD difficulties, and evaluation of early-stage performance to determine minimal viable samples.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Is the implicit rank-based weighting in TANL's activation-aware score (Eq. 15) theoretically optimal, or could explicit or learned weighting schemes improve robustness to the number of negative labels M?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors propose an implicit weighting via rank repetition but test explicit variants (Sew1, Sew2) and note that "more sophisticated explicit weighting formulations... could potentially achieve comparable or even superior performance." Their implicit method shows better robustness to M (Fig 3a).
- **Why unresolved:** There is no theoretical analysis of why implicit ranking outperforms the tested explicit normalization. The relationship between activation magnitude and optimal weight is not modeled, and learned weighting (e.g., via a small network on validation data) is unexplored.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Theoretical characterization of the score function's properties (e.g., monotonicity, variance), comprehensive comparison of explicit weighting strategies (different normalizations, activation functions), and experiments with learned weights evaluated across varying M and OOD types.

## Limitations
- **Corpus dependency:** Performance hinges on the external corpus containing semantically relevant labels for the OOD distribution. Mismatch (e.g., medical OOD with generic WordNet) may collapse to static-baseline performance.
- **Initialization sensitivity:** Early batches rely on noise-initialized `Xneg` and dynamic threshold `γ`; extreme initial skew could cause erroneous label selection before queues stabilize.
- **Batch-size constraint:** Batch adaptation (`α<1`) is fragile at very small batches (e.g., batch=1) due to noisy per-batch activation estimates.
