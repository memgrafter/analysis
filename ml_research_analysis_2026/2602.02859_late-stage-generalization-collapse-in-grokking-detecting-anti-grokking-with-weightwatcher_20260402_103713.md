# Late-Stage Generalization Collapse in Grokking: Detecting anti-grokking with Weightwatcher
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.02859
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02859
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Identification of anti-grokking—a late-stage collapse of test accuracy—via Correlation Traps in shuffled weight spectra and deviations in the HTSR metric α.

## Executive Summary
Grokking, the delayed generalization phenomenon observed in neural network training, has been thought to lead to stable generalization. This paper reveals a previously unreported third phase: anti-grokking, where continued optimization causes test accuracy to collapse back to chance while training accuracy remains perfect. The collapse is demonstrated in two canonical settings—a three-layer MLP on a subset of MNIST and a transformer on modular addition—by extending training up to 10⁷ steps. To diagnose anti-grokking, the authors employ WeightWatcher, a tool grounded in Heavy-Tailed Self-Regularization (HTSR) and SETOL theory. The primary signal is the emergence of Correlation Traps—anomalously large eigenvalues beyond the Marchenko–Pastur bulk in element-wise shuffled weight matrices. A secondary indicator is the HTSR layer quality metric α drifting from its optimal value near 2.0. Competing metrics (e.g., ℓ₂ norms, sparsity) fail to detect this late-stage transition. Weight decay (WD=0.01) is shown to suppress traps and mitigate the collapse. Similar spectral anomalies (α < 2, traps) are observed in large language models (OSS GPT‑20B/120B), hinting at broader relevance.

## Method Summary
The paper replicates two standard grokking experiments. First, a 3‑layer MLP (width 200, ReLU) is trained on 1,000 MNIST samples (100 per class) using AdamW (lr=5e‑4, weight decay=0) with MSE loss for up to 10⁷ steps, batch size 200. Second, a single-layer transformer decoder (d_model=128, d_mlp=512, 4 attention heads of size 32, ReLU, no LayerNorm) is trained on the modular addition task (p=113) with identical optimizer settings. Checkpoints are saved throughout training. The core diagnostic is WeightWatcher (v0.7.5.5). For each linear layer in a checkpoint, two metrics are computed: (a) the HTSR power‑law exponent α by fitting the tail of the empirical spectral density (ESD) of the weight matrix W; (b) the number of Correlation Traps by element‑wise shuffling W to obtain W_rand, fitting a Marchenko–Pastur distribution to the ESD of W_rand to derive the bulk edge λ_+^rand, and counting eigenvalues of that ESD that exceed λ_+^rand. Standard training and test accuracies are tracked as well. The study also performs ablations with weight decay (WD=0.01) and compares these metrics against alternatives like ℓ₂ norm and activation sparsity.

## Key Results
- **Anti‑grokking observed:** After extended training, test accuracy collapses to chance while training accuracy stays perfect, in both the MLP (after ~10⁶ steps, no weight decay) and the transformer.
- **Correlation Traps emerge robustly:** At anti‑grokking onset, shuffled weight matrices exhibit anomalously large eigenvalues beyond the Marchenko–Pastur bulk—a signal absent in earlier phases.
- **α deviates from optimal:** The average HTSR layer quality metric α shifts from ≈2.0 at grokking to below 2 in the MLP (≈1.1) and above 2 in the transformer (≈3.9) during anti‑grokking. Competing metrics do not capture this transition.
- **Weight decay mitigates collapse:** WD=0.01 suppresses Correlation Traps and prevents or softens anti‑grokking; similar spectral patterns (α < 2, traps) are also found in large language models (OSS GPT‑20B/120B).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Correlation Traps as the primary cause
Anti‑grokking is triggered by the formation of Correlation Traps—rank‑one (or higher) perturbations in weight matrices that grow to extreme magnitudes during prolonged optimization. These perturbations survive element‑wise shuffling and produce eigenvalues λ_trap that lie far beyond the Marchenko–Pastur bulk edge λ_+^rand of the shuffled matrix. According to SETOL theory, such atypical weight configurations impair generalization. Evidence: Figure 4 and Table 1 show that traps appear precisely at the onset of anti‑grokking in both models. Regularization (e.g., weight decay) that limits extreme weight growth prevents trap formation and thereby mitigates the collapse.

### Mechanism 2: HTSR α as a secondary phase indicator
The HTSR metric α (power‑law tail exponent of a weight matrix’s ESD) acts as a secondary signal of training phase. The optimal generalization state corresponds to α ≈ 2.0. During anti‑grokking, α systematically deviates: it drops below 2 in the MLP, indicating over‑correlated “prototype‑memorizing” layers, and rises above 2 in the transformer, suggesting catastrophic forgetting of learned rules. This provides a complementary, though less robust, diagnostic (especially for transformers with extremely heavy‑tailed ESDs).

### Mechanism 3: A distinct memorization collapse phase
Grokking is not a terminal state; extended SGD on the same data can destroy generalization. The paper distinguishes three phases: pre‑grokking (initial overfitting), grokking (successful generalization), and anti‑grokking (late‑stage catastrophic memorization). After reaching peak test accuracy, further optimization drives weights into pathological states: the MLP memorizes specific digit prototypes (α < 2), while the transformer disrupts modular addition rules (α > 2). Training accuracy remains perfect because the model fits the training set, but test performance reverts to chance.

## Foundational Learning
- **Random Matrix Theory (RMT) & Marchenko–Pastur Distribution**  
  *Why needed:* Provides the null model for random weight matrices; deviations in the ESD indicate learned structure or pathology. Shuffling weights destroys correlations, and the resulting ESD should follow the MP law if no structure remains.  
  *Quick check:* After element‑wise shuffling, should the eigenvalue distribution match MP? A significant deviation implies leftover structure (e.g., outliers) that survive shuffling.
- **Heavy‑Tailed Self‑Regularization (HTSR) & Power‑Law Exponent α**  
  *Why needed:* α quantifies the tail heaviness of the ESD; it predicts generalization performance. α ≈ 2 is optimal; α → ∞ indicates random/unlearned; α < 2 signals over‑correlation/overfitting; α ≫ 6 signals underfitting.  
  *Quick check:* For a layer with α = 1.5, is the layer more likely underfit, optimally generalizing, or overfitting? (Answer: overfitting.)
- **SETOL Theory & Correlation Traps**  
  *Why needed:* Predicts that certain atypical weight configurations (traps) cause generalization failure. Detecting traps involves comparing the shuffled matrix’s ESD to the MP bulk edge.  
  *Quick check:* What is the operational definition of a Correlation Trap in WeightWatcher? An eigenvalue in the shuffled matrix’s ESD that exceeds the MP bulk edge λ_+^rand, beyond Tracy‑Widom fluctuations.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Weight matrices (W) → compute ESD_original (from X = (1/N) W^T W) → fit α tail; in parallel W → W_rand (element‑wise shuffle) → ESD_shuffled → fit Marchenko–Pastur to obtain bulk edge λ_+^rand → count eigenvalues > λ_+^rand (Correlation Traps).

**Critical path:** For each saved checkpoint and each linear layer: (1) compute ESD of W and W_rand; (2) fit power‑law exponent α on the original ESD tail using automated methods; (3) fit MP distribution to the shuffled ESD to derive λ_+^rand and count traps. Then average α across layers and sum trap counts across layers to monitor training dynamics.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Pros:* Metrics rely solely on weights; no test data required, enabling continuous monitoring.  
- *Cons:* α fitting is sensitive to the minimal eigenvalue threshold and to the tail selection; extremely heavy‑tailed ESDs (e.g., in transformers) can yield unreliable α.  
- *Tradeoff:* Weight decay stabilizes α toward 2 and reduces traps, but may suppress the emergence of grokking altogether.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Pre‑grokking: α ≳ 5 (random‑like), zero traps.  
- Grokking: average α ≈ 2.0, zero traps.  
- Anti‑grokking: average α < 2 (MLP) or > 2 (transformer) + non‑zero trap count in multiple layers; MP fit on shuffled ESD shows high KS statistic (poor fit).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Replicate the canonical grokking setups (3‑layer MLP on MNIST subset, transformer on modular addition) and train each for up to 10⁷ steps with AdamW (lr=5e‑4, WD=0). Save checkpoints regularly. Verify the three‑phase pattern in accuracy curves.  
2. Apply WeightWatcher to all checkpoints. Compute per‑layer α and trap counts; aggregate and plot against steps to confirm correlation with accuracy phases (α → 2 at grokking, traps appear only at anti‑grokking).  
3. Repeat experiment 1 with weight decay WD=0.01. Compare: anti‑grokking should be weaker or absent, trap counts lower, and α should stabilize near 2 without dropping below (MLP) or rising excessively (transformer).

## Limitations
- Exact MNIST subset indices are not provided; different stratified samples could alter the timing of anti‑grokking.
- WeightWatcher’s automated α fitting and MP bulk estimation lack transparency; sensitivity to tail fraction and aspect‑ratio choices is unknown.
- Transformer ESDs are extremely heavy‑tailed, making α fits methodologically questionable; reliance on trap counts alone provides weaker evidence.

## Confidence
- Anti‑grokking existence: **High**
- Correlation Traps as primary signal: **Medium**
- α deviation as secondary signal: **Medium** for MLP, **Low** for transformer
- Weight decay suppression of collapse: **Medium**
- LLM spectral anomalies: **Low**
