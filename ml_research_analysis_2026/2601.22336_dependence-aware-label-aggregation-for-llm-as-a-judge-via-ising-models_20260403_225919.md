# Dependence-Aware Label Aggregation for LLM-as-a-Judge via Ising Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.22336
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22336
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: The paper introduces Ising-based dependence-aware label aggregation for LLM-as-a-Judge, achieving test accuracies of 0.90/0.79/0.68 on relevance, toxicity, and summarization tasks—substantial improvements over CI-weighted majority vote (0.82/0.72/0.60).

## Executive Summary
When multiple LLM judges label the same item, their votes are often dependent due to shared training data, architectures, or prompts. Classical aggregation methods (e.g., Dawid-Skene or weighted majority voting) assume conditional independence given the true label, which can lead to miscalibrated posteriors and non‑vanishing excess risk. This paper proposes to model the dependence explicitly using Ising graphical models: class‑dependent couplings yield a quadratic Bayes decision boundary, while class‑independent couplings still adjust weights in a linear rule. Theoretically, under dependent models, CI‑based predictors can suffer constant error even with infinitely many judges, whereas Bayes‑optimal under the true Ising attains vanishing risk. Practically, fitting these models via Expectation‑Maximization with pseudo‑likelihood yields significant accuracy gains on three real‑world evaluation tasks (relevance, toxicity, summarization) using six diverse LLM judges.

## Method Summary
The work addresses binary label aggregation from K binary judges under label‑dependent dependence. It defines a hierarchy of probabilistic models:
- Conditional Independence (CI): judges independent given true label Y, with class‑specific per‑judge parameters (sensitivities/specificities). Learned by EM (Dawid-Skene).
- Class‑independent Ising: judges’ joint distribution is an Ising model with pairwise couplings W that are shared across classes, but each class has its own local fields h⁽ʸ⁾. The Bayes rule remains linear but weights incorporate correlation corrections.
- Class‑dependent Ising: both fields and couplings differ by class, leading to a quadratic Bayes log‑odds.

Training uses unsupervised EM: the E‑step computes posterior label probabilities γᵢ = P(Yᵢ=1|Jᵢ) by evaluating class‑conditional densities; the M‑step maximizes the expected complete‑data log‑likelihood. For Ising models, exact likelihood is intractable, so pseudo‑likelihood (weighted logistic regressions) is employed. The paper evaluates on three datasets with six proprietary LLM judges (Claude, OpenAI, Llama, DeepSeek) under varying numbers of judges and training sizes.

## Key Results
- Class‑dependent Ising achieves test accuracies: relevance 0.90, toxicity 0.79, summarization 0.68.
- CI‑weighted majority vote baselines: relevance 0.82, toxicity 0.72, summarization 0.60.
- Theoretical separation: Under a class‑dependent Curie‑Weiss model, CI risk remains bounded away from Bayes risk as K→∞, while Bayes risk vanishes; under informative marginals, both risks vanish but at different rates.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Quadratic Bayes rule from class‑dependent Ising
**Claim:** Class‑dependent Ising models allow the Bayes‑optimal posterior log‑odds to be a quadratic function of the binary votes, enabling the classifier to exploit label‑specific correlation patterns.  
**Mechanism:** The joint class‑conditional distribution is  
$P(\mathbf{J}\mid Y=y) \propto \exp(\sum_{j} h^{(y)}_j J_j + \sum_{j<k} W^{(y)}_{jk} J_j J_k)$.  
Applying Bayes’ rule yields  
$\log\frac{P(Y=1\mid\mathbf{J})}{P(Y=0\mid\mathbf{J})} = \mathrm{const} + \sum_j \Delta h_j J_j + \sum_{j<k} \Delta W_{jk} J_j J_k$,  
with $\Delta h = h^{(1)}-h^{(0)}$ and $\Delta W = W^{(1)}-W^{(0)}$. Quadratic terms survive only when the couplings differ between classes.  
**Evidence:** Derived in Section 2.1 (Eq. 2); the abstract also states the quadratic decision result.

### Mechanism 2: Correlation‑adjusted linear rule from class‑independent Ising
**Claim:** Even if the pairwise dependence is shared across classes (class‑independent Ising), the Bayes‑optimal predictor remains linear but its weights are shifted by the correlation structure, correcting the over‑counting of agreement inherent in CI methods.  
**Mechanism:** With $W^{(0)}=W^{(1)}=W$, the quadratic terms cancel in the likelihood ratio. The posterior log‑odds becomes  
$\log\frac{P(Y=1\mid\mathbf{J})}{P(Y=0\mid\mathbf{J})} = \mathrm{const} + \sum_j c_j J_j$,  
where $c_j$ depends on the difference of class‑conditional fields $h^{(1)}_j-h^{(0)}_j$ and also on the partition function $Z(y;W)$, which encodes the effect of the coupling matrix. Thus the effective voting weight of judge $j$ is not solely its marginal accuracy; it is shrunk or expanded depending on its correlation strength with others.  
**Evidence:** Section 2.2 and equations (4)–(5) derive the linear form with correlation‑corrected parameters.

### Mechanism 3: Asymptotic separation between CI and Ising predictors
**Claim:** A predictor that naively assumes conditional independence can incur non‑vanishing excess risk even as the number of judges $K$ grows, whereas the Bayes‑optimal predictor under the true Ising model achieves vanishing risk.  
**Mechanism:** For certain dependent families (e.g., Curie‑Weiss), the joint distribution encodes class information in the sample‑wide magnetization $M_K = \frac{1}{K}\sum_j (2J_j-1)$. As $K\to\infty$, $M_K$ concentrates around distinct values under the two classes, enabling a simple quadratic rule to classify perfectly. However, the CI likelihood ratio collapses to a constant (if per‑judge marginals are identical) or to a different function that cannot exploit this dependence‑induced signal, leading to a constant error probability regardless of $K$.  
**Evidence:** Theorem 1 (Curie‑Weiss separation) and Theorem 2 (informative marginals) in Section 3; large‑deviation arguments in Appendix E.

## Foundational Learning
- **Conditional Independence in Crowdsourcing**  
  **Why needed:** Classical aggregation (Dawid‑Skene, weighted majority) assumes judges are independent given the true label. Understanding this assumption reveals why violations lead to over‑counting of agreement and motivates the Ising alternative.  
  **Quick check:** Given three binary judges and a binary true label, write the joint distribution under conditional independence and under a simple shared‑bias model where each judge’s vote depends on a common latent variable $Z$ that is independent of $Y$.

- **Ising Model (Pairwise Markov Random Field)**  
  **Why needed:** The proposed method models within‑item dependencies with an Ising energy $\sum_j h_j J_j + \sum_{j<k} W_{jk} J_j J_k$. This representation is central to deriving the quadratic/linear decision rules and to implementing the EM algorithm with pseudo‑likelihood.  
  **Quick check:** For three judges with a symmetric coupling matrix $W$ (zero diagonal), what does $W_{12}>0$ imply about the conditional probability $P(J_1=1 \mid J_2=1, Y)$ relative to $P(J_1=1 \mid Y)$? How would you update $W_{12}$ in a pseudo‑likelihood M‑step?

- **Expectation‑Maximization (EM) for Unsupervised Aggregation**  
  **Why needed:** The true labels are unobserved; EM alternates between computing posterior label probabilities (E‑step) and maximizing the expected complete‑data log‑likelihood (M‑step). This framework unifies classical CI methods (Dawid‑Skene) and the proposed Ising learners.  
  **Quick check:** In the CI EM (Dawid‑Skene) with asymmetric sensitivities/specificities, what closed‑form update is used for a judge’s false‑negative rate in the M‑step? How do responsibilities $\gamma_i$ enter into those updates?

- **Pseudo‑Likelihood for Ising Models**  
  **Why needed:** Exact Ising likelihoods involve the partition function $Z(y)$, which is intractable for large $K$. Pseudo‑likelihood replaces the joint by a product of conditionals that do not require normalization, providing a consistent objective for parameter estimation.  
  **Quick check:** Write the pseudo‑likelihood expression for a single item under a class‑dependent Ising model. How does the M‑step reduce to weighted logistic regressions when coupling parameters are estimated?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Data loader → Model registry (CI, ClassIndepIsing, ClassDepIsing, LatentFactor) → EM engine → Optimiser (L‑BFGS / coordinate descent) → Evaluator.

### Critical path
1. Load data; inspect judge patterns (pairwise agreement matrix, variance).  
2. Choose model complexity: start with CI; if residual correlations remain, escalate to class‑independent Ising; if further improvement possible, try class‑dependent Ising.  
3. Run EM with multiple restarts; monitor held‑out log‑pseudo‑likelihood or validation accuracy.  
4. Extract final predictions $\hat{Y}_i = \mathbb{1}\{\gamma_i \ge 0.5\}$.  
5. Validate on a small labeled subset; adjust regularization or model order if calibration error high.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Model capacity vs data:** CI uses $O(K)$ parameters; class‑independent Ising adds $\sim K^2/2$ couplings; class‑dependent doubles that. With limited $n$, use class‑independent or penalize couplings (ℓ1).  
- **Exact vs approximate inference:** Exact Ising likelihood feasible only for very small $K$. Pseudo‑likelihood scales linearly in $K$ and is easy but introduces bias; MCMC/loopy BP is slower but may improve performance for moderate $n$.  
- **Regularization:** Strong ℓ2 penalties shrink couplings toward zero, making the model approach CI; helps when $K$ is large relative to $n$ but may under‑fit true dependence. Use validation to set $\lambda$.

### Failure signatures
- **EM divergence** (log‑likelihood decreases or NaN): often due to over‑parameterization, poor initialization, or numerical instability. Remedy: reduce model order, add damping, increase MC samples.  
- **Calibration mismatch** (posterior means $\gamma_i$ do not match empirical error): indicates misspecification (e.g., missing class‑specific couplings) or distortion from pseudo‑likelihood. Try a more accurate E‑step or switch to class‑dependent Ising.  
- **Identifiability warnings** (high correlations among estimated couplings, near‑singular design): $n$ too small relative to number of pairwise parameters. Increase ℓ2 penalty or fall back to class‑independent Ising.  
- **Phase collapse** in Ising: Estimated couplings may force spins into a single phase, yielding uninformative $\gamma_i\approx0.5$. Monitor the largest eigenvalue of $W$; if it nears the critical value, the model is under‑determined.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Synthetic CI recovery:** Generate votes from a known CI model (heterogeneous β/α) with $n=200$, $K=6$. Run CI‑WMV (EM) and uniform MV. Verify that CI‑WMV recovers the true weights and outperforms uniform MV when judges are heterogeneous. Expect results consistent with Table 3 pattern.  
2. **Curie‑Weiss separation:** With $K\in\{50,100,200\}$, sample from class‑conditional Curie‑Weiss (β₀<1, β₁>1, equal marginals). Fit class‑dependent Ising via pseudo‑EM and compute test risk; also compute CI predictor risk using true marginals. Expect Ising risk to decay with $K$ while CI risk stays >0; plot to confirm asymptotic separation (replicating Figure 4).  
3. **Real‑world introspection (WikiQA):** Fit class‑dependent Ising to the relevance task with all six judges. Inspect the learned $\Delta W$ matrix: judges from the same family (e.g., Claude Sonnet vs Claude Haiku) should exhibit positive entries, indicating similar strengths. Also, evaluate calibration: hold out 200 labeled items, bin $\gamma_i$ into deciles, and compare average $\gamma_i$ with observed error rate in each bin. A well‑calibrated model will lie near the diagonal.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
Consistency of EM-based estimation of Ising parameters. The paper’s theory assumes a known true generative model, but practical EM with pseudo‑likelihood lacks guarantees. A proof of consistency for pseudo‑likelihood EM and finite‑sample excess‑risk bounds would be needed to show that these algorithms inherit the population‑level separation from CI baselines.

### Open Question 2
Statistical tests for conditional independence in LLM‑as‑judge aggregation. The authors suggest informal tests (residual correlation checks, pseudo‑likelihood comparisons) but provide no formal hypothesis testing framework with controlled type I error and power. Developing such tests would allow practitioners to decide whether to use CI or a richer dependence model in a principled way.

### Open Question 3
Principled model selection across the dependence hierarchy (CI, class‑independent Ising, class‑dependent Ising, latent‑factor). The paper offers manual diagnostics (estimated coupling strength, stability, calibration) but no automatic criterion. Determining a reliable method (e.g., BIC, cross‑validated likelihood) to choose the appropriate level from observed votes remains open.

## Limitations
- **Proprietary LLM judges block replication:** The six judges are closed models (Claude, OpenAI, etc.); without API access or model disclosures, independent reproduction of the empirical gains is impossible.
- **EM implementation details are underspecified:** The paper does not provide convergence thresholds, regularization strengths, or initialization strategies for pseudo‑likelihood optimization, which may lead to numerical instability or non‑reproducible results.
- **Theoretical separation relies on idealized generative models:** Guarantees depend on specific assumptions (e.g., Curie‑Weiss) that may not hold for real LLM dependencies, limiting the generalizability of the asymptotic results.

## Confidence
- **Ising Bayes rule derivations (quadratic/linear forms):** High – direct mathematical consequence.
- **Asymptotic risk separation (Theorems 1–2):** High – rigorously proved under specified models.
- **Empirical accuracy gains on three datasets:** Low – data and code unavailable; results cannot be verified independently.
