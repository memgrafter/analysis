# Divide and Learn: Multi-Objective Combinatorial Optimization at Scale

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.11346  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11346  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Achieves a regret bound of *O*(d√T log T) (d = subproblem size) and reaches 80%–98% of specialized solver performance, with 2–3 orders of magnitude improvement in sample and computational efficiency over Bayesian optimization.

## Executive Summary
Divide & Learn introduces a novel online learning framework for multi‑objective combinatorial optimization (MOCO) that overcomes exponential state spaces by decomposing decisions into overlapping subproblems. Each subproblem is solved via position‑wise bandit algorithms (UCB, EXP3, FTRL) coordinated through Lagrangian relaxation. This approach yields a regret bound polynomial in subproblem size rather than the combinatorial size, and empirical results show near‑specialized solver performance with drastically reduced computational cost. The method demonstrates strong scaling with problem size and objective count, making it suitable for expensive black‑box simulations like hardware‑software co‑design.

## Method Summary
The method reformulates MOCO as an online learning problem over a factored decision space. Given an instance with *n* decision variables, it partitions the positions into *K* overlapping subproblems of size *d = n/K*. Each subproblem independently employs a bandit algorithm to select actions for its positions; the full solution is assembled by resolving overlaps via a global consistency rule. A mixture of three experts—UCB/Thompson Sampling for optimism, EXP3 for adversarial robustness, and FTRL for regularization—allows robust learning from full‑bandit feedback. Coordination is achieved by introducing Lagrangian dual variables *λ*ᵢ that penalize disagreement on overlapping positions, updated via mirror descent based on a soft‑violation measure. Additionally, periodic local refinement (zeroth‑order search) exploits subproblem structure. The regret decomposes into a term *O*(d√T log T) per subproblem plus coordination overhead *O*(K²Q√T), where *Q* is the overlap multiplicity. This yields an exponential‑to‑polynomial reduction in effective dimensionality.

## Key Results
- **Regret bound:** *O*(d√T log T) where *d* is subproblem size, avoiding exponential dependence on the total number of variables.  
- **Empirical performance:** 80%–98% of specialized solver hypervolume on standard benchmarks; 2–3 orders of magnitude better sample and computational efficiency than Bayesian optimization.  
- **Scalability:** Advantages increase with problem size and number of objectives; demonstrated on real‑world hardware‑software co‑design with expensive simulations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
We incorporate three core mechanisms from the paper:

### Mechanism 1: Decomposition Reduces Effective Dimensionality
By partitioning the exponential decision space into *K* overlapping subproblems of size *d* (where *d = n/K*), the algorithm reduces the bandit learning regret from exponential in *n* to polynomial in *d*. The solution space *X* is decomposed into subproblems *S*₁,…,*S*ₖ. Each subproblem is optimized independently via bandit learners (UCB, EXP3, FTRL). The overlap regions allow information transfer. The regret bound in Corollary 12.3 scales as *O*(d√T log T) instead of *O*(√|X| T). **Core assumption:** Bounded Coupling (Assumption 12.2.3): modifying variables in subproblem *S*ₖ changes the objective by at most *L·δ(x,x′) + C·|S*ₖ ∩ *O*|, where *O* is the overlap set. **Evidence anchors:** Theorem 12.1 & Corollary 12.3; Section 4.2.1 & Definition 4.2; corpus neighbors. **Break condition:** If bounded coupling fails (dense global interactions), the coupling error can become linear in *T*.

### Mechanism 2: Multi‑Expert Bandit Coordination
Combining UCB/Thompson Sampling, EXP3, and FTRL balances optimism, adversarial robustness, and regularization, enabling effective learning under full‑bandit feedback. At each iteration, one expert (sampled by a mixing distribution) proposes actions for each position. All experts update their internal statistics using the same scalarized reward *r*ₜ via clipped importance weighting (Algorithm 5). This decouples action selection from credit assignment. **Core assumption:** Each expert is a no‑regret algorithm for its respective setting. **Evidence anchors:** Algorithm 2 & Section 4.2.2; Theorems 12.7, 12.14, 12.15; Theorem 12.2. **Break condition:** If the clipping threshold *C* in EXP3 is too large, bias dominates; if too small, variance explodes. Fixed weights may cause premature convergence.

### Mechanism 3: Lagrangian Coordination for Overlap
Dual variables *λ*ᵢ penalize soft coordination violations on overlapping positions, ensuring subproblems converge to consistent global solutions with only *O*(√T) extra cost. For each overlapping position *i*, a dual variable *λ*ᵢ ≥ 0 is updated via accelerated mirror descent (Algorithm 4) based on a soft violation measure *ξ*ᵢ⁽ᵗ⁾ that captures disagreement potential (variance in value estimates). The primal updates subtract *λ*ᵢ from scores of high‑uncertainty actions (Equation 48). **Core assumption:** *ξ*ᵢ⁽ᵗ⁾ is an upper bound on the expected disagreement probability across subproblems. **Evidence anchors:** Theorem 4.5 & Section 12.6.3; Algorithm 4; Appendix 12.8.4. **Break condition:** If *ξ*ᵢ⁽ᵗ⁾ is poorly calibrated or overlap *Q* very large, the *O*(K²Q) term can dominate.

## Foundational Learning
We outline key concepts underlying the approach:

**Combinatorial Bandits (Full‑Bandit Feedback)**  
*Why needed:* The problem uses full‑bandit feedback: only a scalar reward for the entire solution is observed, not per‑component rewards. This makes credit assignment hard; standard semi‑bandit or linear bandits don’t apply.  
*Quick check:* What is the key difference between semi‑bandit and full‑bandit feedback in combinatorial spaces, and why does full‑bandit make credit assignment harder?

**Decomposition for Regret Reduction**  
*Why needed:* To reduce effective dimensionality from *n* to *d*. Requires assumptions: bounded coupling, Lipschitz, decomposability.  
*Quick check:* If you decompose a TSP with *n*=100 cities into subproblems of size *d*=10, how does the effective action space per subproblem compare to the total number of tours? What assumption makes this valid?

**Online Convex Optimization & Mirror Descent**  
*Why needed:* The Lagrangian dual variables are updated via mirror descent with an entropic regularizer.  
*Quick check:* Why use mirror descent instead of projected gradient descent for the dual?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Decomposition Manager → Global State → Subproblem Optimizer → Expert Sampler → Coordinate Solver → Lagrangian Unit → Pareto Archive

**Critical path**  
1. Decompose current problem into subproblems.  
2. For each subproblem: sample an expert, select actions for its positions, optionally apply LocalRefine.  
3. Assemble full solution, enforce consistency on overlaps.  
4. Evaluate scalarized reward (weighted sum or Chebyshev).  
5. Update all experts using clipped importance‑weighted reward.  
6. Update dual variables *λ*ᵢ based on soft violations.  
7. Repeat for *T* iterations; add best‑found solutions to Pareto archive.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- **Subproblem size *d* vs. number *K*:** smaller *d* reduces per‑subproblem learning but increases coordination overhead *O*(K²Q√T). Optimal *K*⁎ = √(n/(QRmax)).  
- **Overlap schedule:** diminishing overlap (*e*ₜ = *o*₀·*t*⁻⁰·⁵) yields *O*(√T) cumulative coupling cost.  
- **Expert mixture:** UCB/TS 30%, EXP3 30%, FTRL 40% recommended; high EXP3 slows convergence, high UCB may trap in local minima.  
- **Clipping threshold *C* in EXP3:** smaller *C* reduces bias but increases variance; *C* = 0.01 recommended.

**Failure signatures**  
- Dual variables *λ*ᵢ diverge: soft‑violation measure mis‑calibrated or overlap too high.  
- Heterogeneous subproblem convergence: indicates poor decomposition or *d* mismatch.  
- Pareto front collapse: inadequate scalarization set or insufficient expert diversity.  
- Memory explosion: *O*(n·|A|) space may be large; ensure |A| moderate.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. **Baseline sanity check:** On tiny TSP (*n*=8), compare against exhaustive enumeration; verify regret decreases sublinearly.  
2. **Decomposition validation:** Compare (a) no decomposition, (b) correct decomposition (sliding window *d*=4), (c) wrong decomposition (random partitions). Expect (b) to outperform others.  
3. **Coordination necessity:** On crafted BiTSP‑20 with conflicting boundary objectives, run with and without Lagrangian updates. Track hypervolume, reward variance, cumulative disagreement; Lagrangian should improve HV and reduce variance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Adaptive expert mixing:** Can the fixed mixture weights be made adaptive to improve constants? The paper notes this as an open direction.  
2. **Sub‑Gaussian rewards:** Does the *O*(d√T log T) bound extend to sub‑Gaussian (unbounded) reward noise? The analysis relies on bounded rewards.  
3. **Non‑local interactions:** For problems with dense global coupling (large constant *C*), does decomposition still yield computational advantage, or does coordination overhead dominate?  
4. **Log factor tightness:** Is the logarithmic factor in the bound tight, or can the optimal *O*(d√T) rate be achieved?

## Limitations
- Decomposition parameters (*d*, overlap schedule) likely need problem‑specific tuning.  
- Theoretical analysis assumes bounded coupling; real‑world problems may have dense interactions violating this.  
- Empirical validation is limited to a small set of benchmarks; generalization to broader domains remains uncertain.

## Confidence
- Regret bound (Theorem 12.1): **High**  
- Empirical performance (hypervolume ratios, FLOP reduction): **Medium**  
- Generalization to new domains: **Low**
