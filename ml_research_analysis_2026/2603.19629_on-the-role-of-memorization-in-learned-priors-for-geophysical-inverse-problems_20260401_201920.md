# On the role of memorization in learned priors for geophysical inverse problems

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.19629
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19629
- **Reference count:** 6
- **Primary result:** Learned priors for geophysical inversion risk memorizing finite training datasets, reducing the posterior to a likelihood-weighted lookup among stored examples.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates memorization in learned priors for geophysical inverse problems, focusing on diffusion models trained via maximum likelihood. The authors show that when the prior memorizes its finite training dataset, the Bayesian posterior collapses to a likelihood-weighted empirical distribution—a discrete lookup over stored examples. For diffusion models, this corresponds to a Gaussian mixture prior that degenerates as the noise level vanishes. By linearizing the forward operator around each training example, they derive a closed-form Gaussian mixture posterior whose component means are shifted by the adjoint Jacobian and covariances balance prior and data constraints. Experiments on a stylized inverse problem and frequency-domain full waveform inversion (FWI) confirm that memorization, quantified via a nearest-neighbor ratio $r<0.5$, is severe for small training sets ($N=50$, memorization rate 58%) and leads to overconfident posteriors: high pointwise standard deviation but low error, and inaccurate mean recovery. Increasing the training set size to $N=1000$ eliminates memorization and yields well-calibrated posteriors. The study highlights the risk of using learned priors with limited data and provides theoretical and empirical evidence of the pitfalls.

## Method Summary
The study reproduces the analysis of memorization in diffusion posterior sampling (DPS) for frequency-domain full waveform inversion (FWI). The synthetic setup uses squared-slowness fields represented by 100 Karhunen–Loève coefficients, drawn from a Gaussian random field (smoothness $\alpha=3$, correlation $\tau=5$). Training sets of sizes $N=50, 200,$ and $1000$ are generated. The true model is constructed as the average of the three most memorized examples from the $N=50$ set. The forward operator solves the 6 Hz Helmholtz equation with 15 surface sources and 196 bottom receivers, with additive Gaussian noise at 5.5% relative level. Diffusion models (MLP-based) are trained on KL coefficients using denoising score matching for $\approx50$k steps. DPS is then used to draw 256 posterior samples given observed data from the true model. Memorization is quantified via the nearest-neighbor ratio $r = d_1 / \text{mean}(d_{2:k}) < 0.5$ in KL coefficient space, applied to both unconditional prior samples and posterior samples. The analysis compares memorization rates, posterior means, pointwise standard deviations, data misfit curves, and calibration (error vs. std) across training set sizes. Key unknowns include the exact diffusion architecture, training hyperparameters (learning rate, batch size, noise schedule), and DPS implementation details (step size, guidance scale). A minimal reproduction requires generating the datasets, training the diffusion models, running DPS, and computing the reported metrics, while ensuring the forward operator and noise match the described setup to avoid false negatives.

## Key Results
- Memorization causes the posterior to collapse to a likelihood-weighted empirical distribution (lookup table) over training examples.
- Quantified via nearest-neighbor ratio $r<0.5$: for $N=50$, DPS posterior memorization rate is 58% (vs. 0% for $N=1000$).
- Overconfident posteriors: high pointwise standard deviation but low error; posterior mean recovery inaccurate even when the true model lies near the training distribution.
- Increasing $N$ reduces memorization and improves posterior fidelity, as shown by calibration plots and KL-space scatter analysis.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Likelihood-weighted lookup
**Claim:** When a learned prior (trained via maximum likelihood) memorizes its finite training dataset, the Bayesian posterior reduces to a likelihood-weighted empirical distribution—a discrete lookup table over stored training examples.  
**Mechanism:** The maximum likelihood objective converges to the empirical distribution $\hat{p}_N$ when the model is expressive and data is finite. Substituting $p_\theta(x) = \hat{p}_N(x) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^N \delta(x-x_n)$ into Bayes' rule yields a posterior that assigns mass only to training points, weighted by their likelihood under the observed data.  
**Core assumption:** The generative model's training procedure (e.g., MLE) converges exactly to the empirical distribution of the training set.  
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "the posterior under such a memorized prior reduces to a reweighted empirical distribution—i.e., a likelihood-weighted lookup among the stored training examples." [section] Page 2, Equation 2 derivation: "Substituting this memorized prior into Equation 1 yields $p(x|y) = \sum_{n=1}^N w_n \delta(x-x_n)$."  
**Break condition:** The model generalizes to a smooth distribution beyond the training examples, which requires $N$ to scale exponentially with dimensionality (per Biroli et al., 2024).

### Mechanism 2: Diffusion prior as Gaussian mixture
**Claim:** For diffusion models, exact memorization implies the prior is a Gaussian mixture model (GMM) with components centered on training examples, which becomes a discrete empirical distribution as the noise level $\sigma \to 0$.  
**Mechanism:** The denoising score matching loss, when minimized exactly on a finite dataset, yields a score function corresponding to a GMM. The forward diffusion process creates overlapping Gaussians at noise level $\sigma(t)$; as $t \to 0$, $\sigma(t) \to 0$ and the mixture collapses to spikes at training data.  
**Core assumption:** The diffusion model's score matches the empirical score at all noise levels, which occurs with sufficient model capacity and training steps.  
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "For diffusion models specifically, memorization yields a Gaussian mixture prior in closed form." [section] Page 2, Equation 3: "the minimizer is the score of a Gaussian mixture: $p_N(x,t) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^N \mathcal{N}(x|m(t)x_n, \sigma^2(t)I)$."  
**Break condition:** The training set size exceeds the memorization threshold, which grows exponentially with data dimension (Biroli et al., 2024; Bonnaire et al., 2025).

### Mechanism 3: Linearized posterior
**Claim:** Under a memorized diffusion prior, linearizing the forward operator $F$ around each training example yields a closed-form Gaussian mixture posterior with component means shifted by the adjoint Jacobian and covariances balancing prior/data constraints.  
**Mechanism:** Each prior Gaussian component is narrow ($\sigma$-ball). Linearizing $F(x) \approx F(x_n) + J_n(x-x_n)$ around $x_n$ makes the likelihood Gaussian in $(x-x_n)$. Completing the square for each component gives a Gaussian posterior component with mean $x_n + \Sigma_n \gamma^{-2} J_n^\top (y-F(x_n))$ and covariance $\Sigma_n = (\sigma^{-2}I + \gamma^{-2} J_n^\top J_n)^{-1}$.  
**Core assumption:** The forward operator $F$ is smooth and its linearization is accurate over the $\sigma$-ball around each training example; i.e., higher-order terms are negligible compared to noise $\gamma$.  
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "linearizing the forward operator around each training example gives a closed-form Gaussian mixture posterior whose components have widths and shifts governed by the local Jacobian." [section] Page 2, Equation 5 derivation and description: "$\mu_n$ is the training example $x_n$ shifted toward better data fit by $\Sigma_n \gamma^{-2} J_n^\top (y-F(x_n))$."  
**Break condition:** The nonlinearity of $F$ or large $\sigma$ invalidates linearization; components overlap, breaking the discrete mixture assumption.

## Foundational Learning
- **Bayesian inverse problem formulation**  
  *Why needed:* The entire analysis rests on Bayes' rule $p(x|y) \propto p(y|x)p(x)$. Understanding prior/likelihood interplay is essential to see how a memorized prior distorts the posterior.  
  *Quick check:* If the prior is a sum of delta functions at training data, what is the mathematical form of the posterior? (Answer: Weighted sum of deltas, weights $\propto$ likelihood.)

- **Diffusion models and score matching**  
  *Why needed:* The paper's core derivation assumes a diffusion prior; knowing the forward noising process and score function training is necessary to derive the GMM prior.  
  *Quick check:* What distribution does the denoising score matching loss converge to when trained on a finite dataset? (Answer: Score of a Gaussian mixture centered on training data.)

- **Memorization in generative models**  
  *Why needed:* Memorization is the central failure mode; distinguishing it from generalization is key. Relates to training set size vs. model dimensionality.  
  *Quick check:* How is memorization quantified in the paper's experiments? (Answer: Nearest-neighbor ratio $r = d_1/d_{2:k} < 0.5$ in KL coefficient space.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Training dataset → Diffusion model → Memorization diagnosis → Linearized posterior theory → DPS algorithm → Validation.
- **Critical path:**  
  1. Generate training dataset and true model.  
  2. Train diffusion model on KL coefficients.  
  3. Diagnose memorization via nearest-neighbor ratio $r$.  
  4. If memorization detected, derive linearized posterior (Equation 5) using Jacobian.  
  5. Run DPS to draw posterior samples.  
  6. Validate via calibration plots, KL-space scatter, and posterior mean error.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Dataset size $N$: Larger $N$ reduces memorization but may be scarce; small $N$ risks lookup-table posterior.  
  - Noise level $\sigma_{\text{min}}$: Smaller $\sigma$ sharpens prior but increases memorization; linearization more accurate at small $\sigma$ but posterior collapses.  
  - Linearization: Valid for small $\sigma$ and smooth $F$; invalid for strong nonlinearity, leading to inaccurate posterior covariances/means.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - High memorization rate ($r<0.5$) in unconditional diffusion samples.  
  - DPS posterior samples remain close to training data (low $r$) despite likelihood fit.  
  - Overconfident posteriors: high pointwise standard deviation but low error vs. true model.  
  - Posterior mean fails to recover true model even if near training distribution.  
  - Calibration plot: error much lower than posterior std at many points.
- **First three experiments:**  
  1. Stylized 1D/2D inverse problem: Validate linearized posterior (Equation 5) against exact posterior as $\sigma$ varies; confirm collapse to lookup table at small $\sigma$.  
  2. Frequency-domain FWI: Train diffusion models on KL coefficients with $N=50,200,1000$; compute memorization rates for unconditional and DPS samples; visualize posterior means/stds for true model near but not in training set.  
  3. Calibration analysis: For $N=50$, plot pointwise error vs. posterior standard deviation in KL space; demonstrate overconfidence.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **What are the practical consequences of memorization when the true subsurface model is out-of-distribution relative to the training dataset?**  
   *Basis in paper:* [explicit] "Our analysis focuses on the setting where the true model lies within the support of the training distribution. Out-of-distribution models introduce an additional source of error—the score function itself becomes unreliable far from the training examples—compounding memorization with biased score gradients. ... Characterizing the practical consequences of memorization in these settings remain important open problems."  
   *Why unresolved:* The theoretical analysis assumes the true model lies within the training support; out-of-distribution cases involve unreliable score gradients and potentially invalid linearization, which are not addressed.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Theoretical extension of the memorized prior/posterior analysis to out-of-distribution regions, combined with numerical experiments on synthetic inverse problems where true models are deliberately placed outside the training manifold, measuring posterior accuracy, calibration, and memorization metrics (e.g., nearest-neighbor ratios).

2. **How does memorization affect posterior inference in conditional diffusion models and amortized Bayesian inference approaches for geophysical inverse problems?**  
   *Basis in paper:* [explicit] "The same memorization phenomenon arises in conditional score matching (Baptista et al., 2025), implying that amortized approaches based on conditional diffusion models (Baldassari et al., 2023; Orozco et al., 2025) face analogous risks. Characterizing the practical consequences of memorization in these settings remain important open problems."  
   *Why unresolved:* The paper studies unconditional diffusion priors; conditional models introduce conditioning on observed data, altering the memorization dynamics and posterior structure. The theoretical framework and empirical evidence for conditional settings are lacking.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Derivation of a linearized posterior for conditional diffusion models under memorization, and empirical validation using conditional diffusion posterior sampling (or amortized inference) on frequency-domain FWI with limited training data, comparing memorization rates and posterior fidelity against unconditional baselines.

3. **What are the tight theoretical conditions (in terms of training set size, forward operator properties, and noise level) that determine when a diffusion model will memorize its training data in the context of inverse problems?**  
   *Basis in paper:* [explicit] "Deriving tight conditions that predict its onset as a function of training set size, forward operator, and noise level remain important open problems."  
   *Why unresolved:* While prior work (Biroli et al., 2024) gives exponential scaling with dimensionality for standard generative modeling, the interplay with the forward operator (e.g., its condition number, nonlinearity) and observation noise is not characterized. The paper does not provide any such conditions.  
   *What evidence would resolve it:* Mathematical analysis yielding bounds (e.g., on the nearest-neighbor ratio) that incorporate training size $N$, forward operator Jacobian statistics, and noise variance $\gamma^2$. Empirical validation by systematically varying these parameters in controlled inverse problems (both stylized and FWI) and measuring memorization thresholds.

## Limitations
- Missing implementation details (diffusion architecture, training hyperparameters, DPS settings) impede faithful reproduction.
- Theoretical analysis assumes exact memorization and perfect linearization, which may not hold in practice for complex PDEs.
- Study limited to KL coefficients for a specific GRF and frequency-domain FWI; generalizability to other inverse problems is unknown.

## Confidence
- **Claim 1** (memorized prior → likelihood-weighted lookup): *Medium*. The derivation is sound under exact memorization and empirical evidence (high $r$ rates for $N=50$) supports it, but exact memorization is a limiting case rarely achieved.
- **Claim 2** (diffusion GMM + linearized posterior): *Medium*. Closed-form derivation is valid and confirmed in stylized experiments, but for complex nonlinear FWI the linearization assumption may be violated.
- **Claim 3** (increasing $N$ reduces memorization): *High*. Direct experimental evidence across $N=50,200,1000$ shows monotonic decrease in memorization and improved posterior fidelity.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-problem validation:** Apply the same memorization-linearization analysis to at least two other inverse problems (e.g., linear tomography and nonlinear traveltime tomography) to test robustness of findings.
2. **Capacity vs. memorization mapping:** Train diffusion models with varying network capacities and fixed $N$ to empirically determine the capacity threshold for memorization, and compare with theoretical sample complexity bounds (Biroli et al., 2024).
3. **Real-data calibration:** If real seismic data and corresponding true models are available, run DPS with a memorized prior (small $N$) and check posterior calibration (error vs. std) to confirm overconfidence phenomenon extends beyond synthetic data.
