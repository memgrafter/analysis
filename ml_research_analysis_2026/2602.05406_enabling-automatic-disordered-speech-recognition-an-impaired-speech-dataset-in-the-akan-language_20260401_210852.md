# Enabling Automatic Disordered Speech Recognition: An Impaired Speech Dataset in the Akan Language

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.05406
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05406
- **Reference count**: 0
- **Primary outcome**: Creation of the UGAkan-ImpairedSpeechData corpus: 50.01 hours of impaired speech audio from 162 native Akan speakers (stammering, cerebral palsy, cleft palate, stroke) with expert-validated transcriptions and metadata, enabling inclusive ADSR research.

## Executive Summary
This study tackles the scarcity of impaired speech data for low-resource languages by developing the first publicly available dataset for Akan. The authors modified an existing mobile app to enable flexible recording that preserves natural disfluencies and extended articulation, and built a custom desktop transcription tool with Akan-specific keyboard support to ensure orthographic accuracy. A dual-expert validation workflow with blind pairing and detailed transcription guidelines was used to produce high-quality transcriptions. The resulting UGAkan-ImpairedSpeechData corpus contains 50.01 hours of audio from 162 speakers across four impairment aetiologies, accompanied by rich metadata. This resource opens avenues for automatic disordered speech recognition research in Akan and similar linguistic contexts.

## Method Summary
The authors created the UGAkan-ImpairedSpeechData corpus through a multi-stage pipeline. First, they modified the UGSpeechData Android app to better accommodate impaired speech recording: pause restrictions were removed, maximum recording duration was extended to 60 seconds, a field for impairment aetiology was added, and in-app transcription was disabled. Participants, who were native Akan speakers with self-identified impairments (stammering, cerebral palsy, cleft palate, or stroke), used the app to describe 1,200 pre-selected images in quiet environments. Recordings were required to be in Akan, last 5–60 seconds, and avoid initial filler words to capture natural speech.

Audio files were transcribed using a custom Python desktop application (Tkinter, Pygame, Pillow) that integrated playback, image prompts, and a dedicated keyboard for Akan characters (e.g., ɔ, ɛ). Eight linguistic experts, all native speakers of Asante Twi, processed the recordings in batches of approximately 500 files each. A two-phase blind-pair validation workflow ensured consistency: one expert first transcribed and validated a file, after which a second independent expert repeated the process; any discrepancies were reassigned to a new pair for resolution. Detailed guidelines governed the handling of repetitions, disfluencies, stretched or split words, and uncertain segments (flagged with “Issue:”). Final transcriptions and metadata (speaker demographics, aetiology, recording environment, device) were exported as CSV files and organized by impairment type to form the complete dataset.

## Key Results
- **50.01 hours** of impaired speech audio from **162 native Akan speakers**.
- Coverage of **four impairment aetiologies**: stammering, cerebral palsy, cleft palate, and stroke.
- Each recording includes an expert-validated transcription and rich metadata; the dataset is publicly released as UGAkan-ImpairedSpeechData.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
#### Mechanism 1: Flexible Recording Setup
- **Claim**: Modifications to the mobile recording app enable capture of authentic impaired speech patterns by accommodating natural disfluencies and extended articulation.
- **Mechanism**: Removing pause restrictions, extending maximum recording duration to 60 seconds, and adding simpler image prompts reduce constraints that would otherwise truncate or alter impaired speech, allowing speakers to produce utterances at their natural pace with repetitions and hesitations.
- **Core Assumption**: Impaired speech inherently includes features like frequent pauses, slow articulation, and repetitions that are integral to the speaker’s communication and must be preserved for dataset validity.
- **Evidence Anchors**: The abstract mentions flexible recording modification. Section 2.1.1 details specific changes (pause restrictions removed, duration extended to 60s, 200 simpler images added). The corpus includes recordings with varied durations (average 12.46 seconds); direct evidence of preserved disfluencies is supported by the transcription guidelines in section 2.2.3.
- **Break Condition**: If recordings are made in noisy environments without app enforcement (the app prevents recording if too noisy, but this may fail), or if participants are pressured to speak “clearly,” authenticity degrades.

#### Mechanism 2: Custom Transcription Tool with Akan Keyboard
- **Claim**: A custom desktop transcription tool with Akan-specific keyboard support ensures accurate linguistic representation of impaired speech transcriptions by reducing orthographic errors.
- **Mechanism**: The tool provides a larger interface for long sessions, integrates audio playback with image prompts, and uses a custom keyboard for Akan characters (e.g., “ɔ”), minimizing misrepresentation that standard keyboards cause (e.g., using “)” instead).
- **Core Assumption**: Standard system keyboards cannot accurately input Akan orthography, leading to consistent transcription errors that compromise dataset quality for language-specific modeling.
- **Evidence Anchors**: The abstract notes the development of a custom desktop tool. Section 2.1.2 describes the Python-based transcription app (Tkinter, Pygame, Pillow) and the custom Akan keyboard. Transcriptions are stored in Excel files; accuracy is inferred from validation rules but not directly reflected in corpus metadata.
- **Break Condition**: If transcribers bypass the custom keyboard or if the tool crashes during sessions, orthographic consistency fails, especially for characters like “ɛ” or “ɔ”.

#### Mechanism 3: Dual-Expert Validation with Blind Pairing
- **Claim**: A dual-expert validation workflow with blind pairing and conflict resolution increases inter-rater reliability and minimizes subjective bias in transcribing ambiguous impaired speech.
- **Mechanism**: Eight linguistic experts process batches independently in pairs without awareness of duplication; discrepancies are resolved by a new pair, ensuring consensus and flagging uncertainties via predefined guidelines.
- **Core Assumption**: Multiple independent transcriptions can identify and correct individual interpreter biases, and structured guidelines standardize handling of impaired speech patterns.
- **Evidence Anchors**: The abstract states that transcription was guided by specific rules for impaired speech patterns. Section 2.2.3 outlines the blind-pair workflow: batch processing of ~500 files, independent transcription by two experts, and reassignment for conflicts; guidelines are provided in Tables 2–4.
- **Break Condition**: If expert training is insufficient or if impairment severity exceeds interpretability (e.g., highly slurred speech), agreement rates drop, leading to higher flagging or exclusion.

## Foundational Learning
Understanding the following concepts is essential for working with this dataset or extending the methodology:

- **Impaired Speech Disfluency Patterns**  
  *Why needed*: Transcribers must recognize and accurately represent patterns such as stammering repetitions, blocks, or motor-speech articulatory distortions without “correcting” them.  
  *Quick check*: According to the transcription guidelines, how should a word like “baaaanku” (stretched) or “Ani die” (split) be handled versus standard Akan?

- **Low-Resource Language Data Scarcity**  
  *Why needed*: The dataset addresses a dual scarcity—impaired speech and low-resource Akan—so grasping constraints like convenience sampling and limited speaker diversity is crucial for generalizing results.  
  *Quick check*: What are the main limitations of convenience sampling in this study, and how might they bias the dataset toward certain impairment severities?

- **Annotation Consistency via Prescriptive Guidelines**  
  *Why needed*: The transcription rules (Tables 2–4) are fundamental to dataset uniformity; without them, transcriptions would vary widely, harming ASR model training.  
  *Quick check*: What should a transcriber do if they encounter an inaudible segment or a word in an unknown language during transcription?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**:  
UGSpeechData Mobile App (modified) → Custom Desktop Transcription App → Validation Workflow Manager → Metadata CSV Files → Cloud/Storage (final UGAkan-ImpairedSpeechData corpus)

**Critical Path**:  
1. Participant recruitment and profile setup in mobile app.  
2. Audio recording via modified app with image prompts, adhering to guidelines (quiet environment, Akan language, 5–60 s).  
3. Batch validation and transcription by expert pairs using the desktop app, following detailed rules.  
4. Conflict resolution: discrepant files are reassigned to a new pair of experts.  
5. Final dataset assembly: audio files and metadata CSVs are organized by aetiology and packaged for release.

**Design Tradeoffs**:  
- *Separation of recording and transcription*: A mobile app for field recording and a desktop app for transcription increase workflow complexity but provide optimal interfaces for each task.  
- *Blind pairing without overlap*: Reduces bias at the cost of additional coordination time and expert effort.  
- *Image-based elicitation*: Promotes spontaneous speech and aids participants with cognitive impairments, yet may constrain lexical diversity compared to free conversation.

**Failure Signatures**:  
- High validation rejection rate (>20% flagged as inaudible or PII) indicates poor recording quality or guideline violations.  
- Low inter-rater agreement (many conflicts requiring third review) points to ambiguous guidelines or insufficient expert training.  
- Metadata inconsistencies (missing aetiology tags, extreme duration outliers) suggest app logging errors or human mistake.  
- Tool crashes during transcription (e.g., audio playback issues) halt progress; error logs should be monitored.

**First 3 Experiments**:  
1. *Reproduce transcription reliability*: Randomly select 100 audio files, have two new experts transcribe independently with the desktop app, compute word error rate between transcripts, and compare to the paper’s conflict-resolution workflow.  
2. *Analyze impairment distribution*: Load metadata CSVs, generate histograms for duration, speaker age, and aetiology counts, and correlate with recording environment to detect imbalances or biases.  
3. *Baseline ASR feasibility*: Fine-tune a lightweight ASR model (e.g., Wav2Vec2) on a small subset (e.g., 10 hours) from one aetiology, evaluate on a held-out set, and report word error rate to gauge data usability and identify challenges.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **To what extent does the convenience sampling strategy and resulting underrepresentation of certain impairment etiologies (e.g., stroke, cleft palate) and severity levels bias the performance of automatic disordered speech recognition (ADSR) models trained on this dataset?**  
   *Basis*: The limitations section states that convenience sampling restricted balanced representation, made recruitment of stroke and cleft palate participants difficult, and excluded speakers with extreme severe impairments.  
   *Why unresolved*: The paper creates the dataset but does not evaluate whether its demographic and severity skew limits the generalizability of ASR models to broader, more severely impaired populations.  
   *Resolving evidence*: Systematic evaluation of ASR model performance on held-out subsets representing undercollected groups and comparison with performance on majority groups.

2. **Can the impairment-specific transcription guidelines (e.g., for stretched words, split words, repetitions) developed for Asante Twi be reliably generalized and applied by linguists to impaired speech datasets in other low-resource languages with different phonological and morphological structures?**  
   *Basis*: The methodology details a collaborative, consensus-driven process to create detailed guidelines for Akan-specific patterns (Tables 3 & 4), presented as necessary for consistency but not addressed for cross-linguistic portability.  
   *Why unresolved*: The guidelines are deeply tied to Akan orthographic and phonetic conventions; their effectiveness for languages with different writing systems or phonotactics is an unstated assumption.  
   *Resolving evidence*: A replication study where linguistic experts from another low-resource language, using only translated guidelines, transcribe a common set of impaired speech utterances; measure inter-rater reliability and consistency against a gold standard.

3. **What are the specific acoustic-phonetic signatures (e.g., vowel duration, formant transitions, pause patterns) that most robustly differentiate between the four impairment aetiologies (stammering, cerebral palsy, cleft palate, stroke) in spontaneous Akan speech?**  
   *Basis*: The dataset is segmented by aetiology and transcription guidelines acknowledge distinct patterns (e.g., stammering and blocking). However, no acoustic analysis is provided.  
   *Why unresolved*: The dataset’s value for comparative clinical linguistics and multi-condition ASR modeling is stated, but the fundamental acoustic correlates of each condition in Akan remain unidentified.  
   *Resolving evidence*: A detailed acoustic analysis of the aligned audio-transcript pairs, measuring parameters like syllable duration, spectral characteristics of distorted phonemes, and disfluency frequency per aetiology, with statistical comparison.

4. **Does the use of static image-prompt elicitation, compared to free conversation, systematically constrain the lexical diversity, syntactic complexity, and natural disfluency patterns in impaired speech recordings, thereby affecting the ecological validity of the corpus for some impairment types?**  
   *Basis*: The methods describe using pre-selected images to elicit “spontaneous natural speech” and note simpler images for participants with cognitive disabilities; the paper assumes this yields valid data but does not question potential limiting effects.  
   *Why unresolved*: Image description tasks may pressure speakers toward specific vocabulary and shorter, more planned utterances, which could interact differently with various impairments; this potential source of methodological bias is not discussed.  
   *Resolving evidence*: A controlled study collecting both image-prompted and free-speech samples from a subset of speakers with the same impairment; compare lexical diversity metrics, syntactic complexity scores, and disfluency rates between the two speaking tasks.

## Limitations
- **Severity & Clinical Profiles**: Exact impairment severity scales and specific medical diagnoses are unreported, limiting analysis of within-aetiology speech pattern variability.  
- **Transcription Consistency Metrics**: The blind-pair workflow lacks quantitative inter-rater reliability statistics (e.g., Cohen’s κ), making objective quality assessment incomplete.  
- **Sampling Bias**: Convenience sampling leads to underrepresentation of certain impairment groups (e.g., stroke, cleft palate) and likely milder severity cases, restricting linguistic diversity and model generalizability.

## Confidence
- **Dataset Creation & Release**: High Confidence – Direct evidence confirms 50.01 hours of curated audio and transcriptions for 162 Akan speakers across four aetiologies, with tools and workflow documented.  
- **Transcription Methodology Validity**: Medium Confidence – The custom tool and guidelines are well-specified, but without reported IRR metrics or error audits, the achieved consistency remains inferred.  
- **Generalizability to ADSR Research**: Low Confidence – The dataset’s utility for training robust disordered ASR models is uncertain due to severe aetiology imbalances, unreported severity distributions, and lack of baseline performance results.

## Next Checks
1. *Compute Inter-Rater Reliability*: Sample 500 audio files (stratified by aetiology), have two new experts transcribe independently using the provided desktop app, and calculate word-level agreement (e.g., word error rate) and Cohen’s κ. Compare conflict rates to the paper’s described workflow to validate reported consistency.  
2. *Annotate Severity Metrics*: Recruit a speech-language pathologist to re-assess a subset (e.g., 20 speakers per aetiology) using standard severity scales (e.g., SSI-4 for stammering, consonant accuracy for CP). Correlate these scores with transcription confidence flags (Issue:) in the corpus to characterize severity bias.  
3. *Train Aetiology-Specific ASR Baselines*: Fine-tune a pre-trained multilingual model (e.g., XLS-R) on the data for each aetiology separately (using available hours). Report word error rate per aetiology and analyze error patterns (e.g., disfluency handling) to quantify usability and identify high-risk impairment types.
