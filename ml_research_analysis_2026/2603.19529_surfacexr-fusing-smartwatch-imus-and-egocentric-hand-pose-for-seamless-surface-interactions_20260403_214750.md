# SurfaceXR: Fusing Smartwatch IMUs and Egocentric Hand Pose for Seamless Surface Interactions

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.19529
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19529
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Achieved window-level touch detection F1-score of 91.2% and 8-class surface gesture recognition F1-score of 95.0%.

## Executive Summary
SurfaceXR addresses unreliable surface interactions in extended reality by fusing headset-based 3D hand pose with smartwatch IMU micro-vibrations using a gated multimodal neural network. This approach enables robust touch and gesture recognition without requiring explicit surface calibration. In a 21-participant study, the system significantly outperformed single-modality baselines across all metrics and maintained performance even when surface plane registration was omitted. The multimodal fusion leverages complementary cues: hand tracking provides spatial context while IMUs capture high-frequency contact signatures, and an adaptive gating mechanism dynamically balances their contributions.

## Method Summary
SurfaceXR processes two synchronized streams: hand tracking (60 Hz, 8 index/thumb joints with 3D position and computed acceleration) and smartwatch IMU (200 Hz, 6 channels decomposed into 4 frequency bands). Hand data are fed to a Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) to extract spatial‑temporal features; IMU data go through a 2D‑CNN. Both embeddings are projected to 256‑D and fused via a gated mechanism that learns modality‑specific weights. The fused representation feeds two heads: a frame‑level binary contact detector and a window‑level 9‑class gesture classifier (8 gestures + negative). Training uses a combined loss `L = L_G + 0.5*L_C` with Adam (lr=3e‑4) and class weighting. During inference, a sliding window (1 s) and hysteresis filter yield final contact decisions and gesture labels. Touch coordinates are obtained by projecting the index fingertip onto the estimated surface plane.

## Key Results
- **Touch detection:** F1‑score 91.2% (window‑level macro).
- **Gesture recognition:** F1‑score 95.0% on 8 surface gestures + negative class.
- **Spatial accuracy:** Cross‑hair targeting error 7.07 mm.
- **Latency:** Median contact onset latency 47 ms.
- **Robustness:** Without surface registration, multimodal degrades only 2.5% in gesture F1 vs. 4.4% for hand‑only.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Complementary Cues from Hand Tracking and IMU**  
Hand tracking gives precise 3D joint positions but suffers from noise and low temporal resolution (~60 Hz). Smartwatch IMUs capture high‑frequency micro‑vibrations (~200 Hz) during surface contact, providing a distinct temporal signature (e.g., z‑axis deceleration) that is insensitive to spatial inaccuracies. The physical contact generates a vibratory impulse at the wrist that correlates with touch events even when visual tracking fails. Evidence: Section 4.1.1 notes acceleration data captures contact signatures missed by position; prior work like WatchHand and Wrist2Finger explores wrist IMUs for hand inference, though not directly for surface touch fusion.

**Gated Multimodal Fusion**  
A gated fusion network dynamically weights contributions from hand and IMU streams based on context, outperforming simple concatenation. Separate TCN (hand) and 2D‑CNN (IMU) encoders produce 256‑D feature embeddings. A gating network (linear + sigmoid) learns a weight (0‑1) per feature before summation, allowing the model to rely more on IMU during tracking glitches. Evidence: Ablation shows multimodal (F1 = 95.0%) significantly exceeds hand‑only (92.6%) and IMU‑only (82.7%). Without surface registration, multimodal performance drops only 2.5% vs. 4.4% for hand‑only, indicating gating mitigates spatial misalignment.

**Multi‑Task Learning**  
Contact detection (frame‑level binary) and gesture classification (window‑level 9‑class) share a common multimodal backbone, forcing the network to learn representations useful for both tasks and leveraging their relationship (gestures imply contact). The loss `L = L_G + 0.5*L_C` balances the tasks. Evidence: The multimodal touch model (F1 = 91.2%) outperforms threshold (74.0%) and hand‑only (89.1%) baselines, demonstrating benefits of shared representation.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Hand stream (60 Hz, shape `[60,8,3,2]`) → Hand Encoder (TCN) → 64‑D feature  
IMU stream (200 Hz, shape `[200,6,4]`) → IMU Encoder (2D‑CNN) → 64‑D feature  
Both features → Linear projection to 256‑D → Gated Fusion (weighted sum) → 256‑D fused vector  
Fused vector → Contact Head (FC → Dropout → FC2, per frame) and Gesture Head (FC → Dropout → FC9, per window)

**Critical path:** Synchronized sliding window (1 s, stride 1) from hand+IMU streams → parallel TCN and 2D‑CNN encoders → gated fusion → two classification heads → post‑processing (hysteresis for contact, argmax for gesture).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Sampling rate mismatch* (60 Hz vs. 200 Hz): handled by different window lengths; IMU’s higher rate captures finer contact impulsions.  
- *Model size* (~670 k parameters) enables powerful fusion but requires GPU for real‑time (~6 ms inference); impractical for on‑watch deployment.  
- *Gated vs. concatenation*: Gated adds parameters but learns adaptive modality importance, yielding significant gains over static fusion (evidenced by ablation).

**Failure signatures:**  
- High false negatives on taps indicate missed IMU vibrations or over‑reliance on hand tracking during glitches.  
- Confusion between swipe and path tracing suggests insufficient temporal differentiation in the gesture head.  
- Without surface registration, if multimodal performance degrades substantially, the gating may fail to down‑weight misaligned hand features.

**First three experiments:**  
1. **Modality Ablation** – Train and evaluate hand‑only, IMU‑only, and multimodal models; confirm multimodal significantly outperforms (p < .001).  
2. **Surface Registration Robustness** – Evaluate models with and without surface plane registration; replicate smaller performance drop for multimodal (2.5% vs. 4.4%).  
3. **Real‑Time Pipeline Validation** – Measure end‑to‑end latency using 240 fps video; verify median contact onset latency ≈ 47 ms and gesture latencies within expected ranges.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The authors identify the following open questions:

1. **Multi‑touch interactions** – Can SurfaceXR generalize to two‑finger gestures (e.g., zoom, rotate) or simultaneous touches from multiple fingers? The current evaluation only considered single‑touch index finger events.
2. **Bi‑manual input without dual smartwatches** – Is it possible to support two‑handed interactions (e.g., typing) with only one smartwatch, and what are the performance trade‑offs?
3. **Surface variability** – How do different surface characteristics (material compliance, texture, curvature) affect touch detection and gesture recognition? The system was only tested on flat, rigid surfaces.
4. **Demographic diversity** – How does performance vary across handedness, age, and hand size? The study participants were all right‑handed young adults.

## Limitations
- **Hardware specificity** – Evaluated only on Meta Quest 3 and Google Pixel Watch 3; watch fit strongly influences IMU signal quality.
- **Surface material not evaluated** – Performance on soft, textured, or curved surfaces (which may damp vibrations) remains unknown.
- **Preprocessing underspecification** – Details of acceleration computation, Butterworth filter parameters, and surface registration procedure are missing, hindering exact replication.

## Confidence
- Complementary cues (hand tracking + IMU): Medium  
- Gated fusion mechanism: Medium  
- Multi‑task learning benefit: Medium  
- Robustness without surface registration: Medium  

## Next Checks
1. **Hardware portability test** – Replicate the study with other commercial headsets (e.g., Quest Pro, Apple Vision Pro) and smartwatches (e.g., Apple Watch, Samsung Galaxy Watch) to assess generality.
2. **Surface material challenge** – Collect data on diverse surfaces (carpet, wood, glass, foam) to quantify the break condition where vibrations are damped and measure resulting performance changes.
3. **Gating interpretability analysis** – Extract gating weight distributions, correlate with hand tracking quality metrics, and perform ablation with forced modality weights to demonstrate the adaptive benefit.
