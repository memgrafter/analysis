# Non-Euclidean Gradient Descent Operates at the Edge of Stability

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.05002  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05002  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Extends the Edge of Stability to non‑Euclidean gradient descent by introducing a norm‑agnostic directional smoothness measure, showing loss decrease requires it ≤ 2/η and that it empirically stabilizes near this bound across diverse models and optimizers.

## Executive Summary
This paper broadens the “Edge of Stability” (EoS) phenomenon from standard gradient descent to a wide spectrum of non‑Euclidean optimization methods. The authors define directional smoothness—a generalized sharpness measure valid for any norm—unifying the analysis of vanilla GD, preconditioned GD, ℓ∞‑descent, block coordinate descent, spectral GD, and Muon. Theoretically, they prove that a step size η permits loss decrease only when directional smoothness is at most 2/η; otherwise, the loss oscillates. Empirical evaluations on MLPs, CNNs, and Transformers reveal that during training the generalized sharpness rises (progressive sharpening) and then stabilizes at or slightly above 2/η, confirming EoS across these algorithms. For quadratic objectives, they establish a divergence condition when directional smoothness exceeds 2/η along a norm‑specific invariant direction. The work thus offers a single, geometry‑aware spectral measure that captures stability behavior across optimizers.

## Method Summary
The core contribution is the definition of generalized sharpness based on directional smoothness, which quantifies the maximum curvature of the loss along any direction under an arbitrary norm. This measure reduces to the classic sharpness (largest Hessian eigenvalue) for the Euclidean norm but extends seamlessly to non‑Euclidean settings (e.g., coordinate‑wise, preconditioned). The authors combine theoretical analysis—deriving bounds on one‑step loss change in terms of directional smoothness—with empirical studies. They compute the measure during training on various architectures and optimizers, employing Hessian‑vector products to avoid explicit Hessian formation, keeping computational cost manageable. Additionally, they analyze synthetic quadratic losses to prove precise divergence thresholds, linking the norm of the optimizer to an invariant direction that governs stability.

## Key Results
- Generalized sharpness (directional smoothness) dictates loss decrease: if it exceeds 2/η, the loss cannot drop in a single step; observed oscillations indicate the measure hovers around 2/η.
- Across MLPs, CNNs, and Transformers, progressive sharpening causes generalized sharpness to rise and then stabilize near or slightly above 2/η, consistent with an EoS regime.
- For quadratic objectives, divergence is proven when the directional smoothness (in the optimizer’s norm) exceeds 2/η along a specific invariant direction; this theoretical threshold aligns with the empirical stabilization point.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The mechanism hinges on the interplay between step size and curvature. Directional smoothness captures the worst‑case curvature along directions relevant to the optimizer’s norm. When this curvature is too high (> 2/η), a gradient step overshoots, causing the loss to increase or oscillate. The optimizer thus naturally drives the parameters toward a region where the curvature along the update direction is as large as possible without exceeding the stability bound—this is the Edge of Stability. In non‑Euclidean settings, the norm reshapes the notion of “direction”, but the same fundamental constraint applies: the effective smoothness must approach 2/η to maximize progress while avoiding divergence, leading to the observed stabilization.

## Foundational Learning
- **Edge of Stability (EoS):** Phenomenon where gradient descent drives the loss toward a state where the sharpness (largest eigenvalue of the Hessian) is just below the instability threshold 2/η, causing persistent loss oscillations. *Why needed:* Classic concept tying curvature to step size limits. *Quick check:* In a quadratic, if the Hessian eigenvalue λ > 2/η, a GD step diverges; EoS refers to training dynamics that push λ to ≈2/η.
- **Directional Smoothness:** Maximum ratio of quadratic form along a direction to squared norm of that direction, defined with respect to a given norm. *Why needed:* Generalizes sharpness beyond Euclidean norm to arbitrary update geometries. *Quick check:* For any direction u, compute (uᵀ H u)/(∥u∥²); directional smoothness is the supremum over u under the chosen norm.
- **Generalized Sharpness:** The value of directional smoothness for the optimizer’s norm; reduces to usual sharpness for Euclidean norm. *Why needed:* Provides a unified stability measure for diverse algorithms. *Quick check:* For preconditioned GD with preconditioner M⁻¹, the relevant norm is ∥M¹ᐟ²u∥; compute directional smoothness in this norm.
- **Non‑Euclidean Gradient Descent:** Optimization updates that are not simple Euclidean gradient steps; includes coordinate descent, mirror descent, or spectral methods. *Why needed:* Many practical optimizers (e.g., Adam, Muon) fall into this category and require norm‑specific analysis. *Quick check:* If the update direction is not exactly the negative gradient in the Euclidean sense, the method is non‑Euclidean.
- **Progressive Sharpening:** Training phase where the loss landscape’s sharpness (as measured by the relevant norm) gradually increases. *Why needed:* Explains how models reach the EoS regime during training. *Quick check:* Track generalized sharpness over epochs; a monotonic increase before plateau indicates progressive sharpening.
- **Invariant Direction (Quadratic):** A direction that remains unchanged (up to scaling) under the optimization update for a quadratic loss, determined by the dominant eigenvector of the transformed Hessian. *Why needed:* Central to proving divergence when smoothness > 2/η. *Quick check:* For a quadratic loss with Hessian H and update matrix T (encapsulating norm), solve Hv = λ T v; the corresponding v is invariant.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Model → Loss → Gradient → (Optional) Preconditioner/Transform → GD Step (η) → Parameter Update → New Loss.  
Parallel: Parameter vector → Hessian‑Vector Product → Directional Smoothness Estimate → Monitor vs. 2/η.

**Critical Path:** Training loop repeatedly computes loss and gradient, applies the optimizer‑specific update, and periodically evaluates the generalized sharpness. The EoS phenomenon emerges after many steps when the sharpness estimate converges near the stability threshold 2/η.

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- Estimating directional smoothness requires Hessian‑vector products, which are more expensive than gradient computations but scale linearly with the number of directional probes.  
- The choice of norm determines which directions are considered “sharp”; this must match the optimizer’s geometry to obtain a meaningful stability bound.  
- The theoretical bound (2/η) is exact for convex quadratics but only empirically observed for deep nets; relying on it for step‑size selection may be risky in non‑convex settings.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- No stabilization of generalized sharpness near 2/η; instead it keeps growing or remains far below, indicating either a mis‑aligned norm or insufficient training steps.  
- High variance in smoothness estimates across different random directions, suggesting numerical instability or inadequate probing.  
- Divergence during training when the observed sharpness clearly exceeds 2/η, especially with large η.

**First Experiments:**  
1. Train a two‑layer MLP on MNIST with vanilla gradient descent (Euclidean norm), compute generalized sharpness each epoch, and verify progressive sharpening culminating near 2/η.  
2. Train a ResNet‑18 on CIFAR‑10 using RMSProp (preconditioned GD), measure sharpness in the ℓ2‑preconditioned norm (i.e., using the reciprocal of the squared gradient moving averages as diagonal preconditioner), and check for EoS stabilization.  
3. Train a small Transformer on WikiText‑2 with the Muon optimizer (spectral‑norm‑based), evaluate directional smoothness in the operator norm induced by the spectral preconditioner, and confirm the sharpness threshold behavior.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper highlights several open questions for future research:
- How does directional smoothness behave for very large models (e.g., billion‑parameter transformers) where Hessian estimation is infeasible? Can cheaper proxies be developed?
- Does the EoS phenomenon generalize to optimizers with momentum or adaptive methods like Adam, which combine multiple norms or non‑stationary preconditioning?
- Can the theoretical 2/η stability bound be formally proven for non‑quadratic losses, perhaps under additional assumptions like smoothness or overparameterization?
- How does the choice of probing distribution for directional smoothness affect the measured value and its relation to stability?
- What is the relationship between the invariant direction in quadratics and the Hessian top eigenvectors in deep nets, and does it explain the observed stabilization just above 2/η?

## Limitations
- Generalized sharpness computation relies on Hessian‑vector products, which can be numerically unstable or computationally expensive for very high‑dimensional models (e.g., large language models); its practical tractability and sensitivity to implementation details (batch size, perturbation scale) are not fully explored.  
- Empirical validation covers only standard vision and NLP architectures on common datasets; it remains uncertain whether the EoS dynamics hold for more specialized models (e.g., diffusion models, graph networks) or under significant distribution shift.  
- Theoretical guarantees linking generalized sharpness to stability are restricted to quadratic losses; for deep, non‑convex networks the connection is empirical, and a formal proof is lacking.

## Confidence
- **Unification via Generalized Sharpness:** High  
- **Empirical Stabilization near 2/η:** Medium  
- **Divergence Condition (Sharpness > 2/η):** Medium for quadratics, Low for general deep nets
