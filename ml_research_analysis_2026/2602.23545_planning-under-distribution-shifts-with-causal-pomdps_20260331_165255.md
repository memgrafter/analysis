# Planning under Distribution Shifts with Causal POMDPs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.23545  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23545  
- **Reference count:** 11  
- **Primary result:** By modeling distribution shifts as stochastic interventions on a causal graph and maintaining a joint belief over latent states and the unknown intervention, the finite‑horizon value function remains piecewise linear and convex (PWLC) in the augmented belief space, preserving the tractable α‑vector representation for planning.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles planning in partially observable environments when the true transition dynamics differ from the learned model due to unknown distribution shifts. It introduces a causal POMDP framework where the state is factorized into causally related variables, and shifts are represented as stochastic interventions on the causal graph. The agent maintains a joint belief over latent states and the unknown intervention domain, updated via Bayes’ rule after each action and observation. The key theoretical contribution proves that, despite this augmentation, the finite‑horizon value function remains piecewise linear and convex in the joint belief. This guarantees that standard α‑vector‑based POMDP planning algorithms (e.g., PBVI) remain applicable, enabling the agent to evaluate policies under hypothesized changes and to actively infer which intervention occurred.

## Method Summary
The framework extends classical POMDPs by embedding a causal model into the state transition. The state vector is decomposed into variables following a directed acyclic graph; each variable’s conditional distribution given its parents is explicitly represented. A distribution shift is formalized as a stochastic intervention—a set of modifications to one or more of these conditional distributions, parameterized by an intervention symbol σ from a finite set Σ. The agent’s belief is a joint distribution b(s,σ) over full state s and intervention σ. After executing action a and receiving observation o, belief update proceeds via a Bayesian filter that uses the intervention‑adjusted transition model T(s′|s,a;σ). For planning, finite‑horizon value iteration is performed in this augmented belief space. Because the value function stays PWLC, it can be represented as the pointwise maximum of a finite set of α‑vectors, each now a function α(s,σ). Standard α‑vector backups (computing new α for each action–observation pair) and policy extraction (maximizing α·b) are used unchanged, except that expectations integrate over σ according to the current marginal b(σ).

## Key Results
- Distribution shifts are modeled as stochastic interventions on a causal graph, allowing a structured representation of unknown dynamics.
- The agent maintains a tractable joint belief over latent states and the unknown intervention, updated via a Bayesian filter.
- The finite‑horizon value function remains piecewise linear and convex (PWLC) in the augmented belief space, so standard α‑vector‑based POMDP planning algorithms continue to apply.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Modeling unknown distribution shifts as stochastic interventions on a causal graph allows the agent to maintain a tractable joint belief over latent states and the unknown intervention (domain).
- **Mechanism:** The causal factorization means an intervention on a variable only alters its specific conditional distribution (Equation 3). The joint belief b(s,σ) couples state and domain uncertainty. After each action and observation, the Bayesian filter (Proposition 2) updates this belief using the intervention‑adjusted transition model T(v_i|pa(V_i);σ). The intervention type σ is a single discrete (or finite‑set) variable, preventing the belief space from becoming intractably large.
- **Core assumption:** The causal graph structure (parent sets pa(V_i)) is known and fixed; only the conditional distributions (mechanisms) are subject to interventions. The set of candidate interventions Σ is finite or discretized.
- **Break condition:** If Σ is continuous or extremely high‑dimensional, the joint belief b(s,σ) may become too large to represent. The mechanism also assumes the causal graph is correct; a missed edge or wrong parent set invalidates the intervention model.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The finite‑horizon value function remains piecewise linear and convex (PWLC) in the augmented joint belief space, preserving the α‑vector representation.
- **Mechanism:** The proof (Lemma 1 & Theorem 1) shows by induction that V_n(b) can be expressed as the maximum over a finite set of linear functionals of b(s,σ). Linearity comes from the expectation over s and σ (integration in Equation 26). The induction step: the Bellman backup (combining immediate reward and discounted future value) produces new linear functionals α_n that remain linear in b because the belief update and transition model are linear in b(s,σ), and the max over actions/observations preserves the pointwise‑max‑of‑linear‑functions form.
- **Core assumption:** Σ is such that the integral ∫ α(s,σ) b(s,σ) dσ is well‑defined and finite. The transition functions T(v_i|pa(V_i);σ) are known functions of σ.
- **Break condition:** If the intervention σ affects the observation model O or reward R in a non‑linear way, linearity in b(s,σ) could be lost, breaking the PWLC guarantee.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The framework enables evaluating a policy’s performance under a specified distribution shift (hypothesized change) and supports active identification of the actual shift.
- **Mechanism:** For a known shift σ, the agent uses the modified transition model T(·;σ) and computes the standard POMDP value V^π(b_S; σ). For an unknown shift, the joint belief b(s,σ) evolves, and policy selection is based on the current belief. The belief update (Proposition 2) naturally performs Bayesian inference over σ: observations unlikely under a given σ downweight its probability in b(σ|s). Planning with b(s,σ) implicitly evaluates expected return across hypothesized shifts and can concentrate belief on the true σ over time.
- **Core assumption:** The true shift σ* is in the hypothesis set Σ. The observation model O(s′,o′) is invariant to the shift (or its change is also captured by the causal model/intervention). The prior over σ is accurate enough to eventually identify σ*.
- **Break condition:** If σ* ∉ Σ (model misspecification) or if different interventions produce identical transition and observation models (observational indistinguishability), identification fails.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Causal Graph & Transition Module -> Joint Belief Manager -> Planner (α‑vector Generator) -> Policy Executor & Shift Identifier

### Critical path
1. Initialize joint belief b(s,σ) (e.g., uniform).
2. For each planning step:
   a. Run PBVI backup using intervention‑adjusted transitions T(·;σ) inside the expectation over σ.
   b. Execute action a = argmax_π V(b) (i.e., choose α‑vector with highest α·b).
   c. Receive observation o′.
   d. Update belief b(s,σ) via Proposition 2.
   e. (Optional) Check convergence of marginal b(σ).

### Design tradeoffs
- *Belief representation:* Finite Σ is exact but explodes with |S|. Continuous Σ requires approximate inference (e.g., sampling over σ).
- *Intervention scope:* Restricting interventions to a small subset of state variables keeps Σ manageable and belief update simpler. Allowing interventions on many variables increases expressiveness but blurs identification.
- *Planning horizon:* The PWLC guarantee holds for finite horizons. Infinite‑horizon (discounted) requires additional assumptions about contraction; the paper focuses on finite‑h.

### Failure signatures
- *Non‑PWLC symptoms:* Value function surface over beliefs becomes non‑convex or non‑linear between sampled beliefs, causing α‑vector methods to fail/diverge. Check if backup operations produce new α‑vectors that are not linear in b(s,σ).
- *Identification failure:* Marginal belief b(σ) remains uniform or multi‑modal despite many steps, indicating Σ is too large, interventions are not distinguishable given O, or the prior is misleading.
- *Computational blowup:* Belief table size |S|×|Σ| becomes infeasible. Monitor memory and number of α‑vectors generated during PBVI.

### First 3 experiments
1. **PWLC Preservation Validation:** Construct a small causal POMDP (2–3 state variables). Define a finite set of stochastic interventions on one variable. Run finite‑horizon value iteration in the augmented belief space. Verify that the resulting value function, sampled over a grid of beliefs, is piecewise linear and convex (e.g., plot slices, check linear segments).
2. **Comparison with Ambiguous POMDP Baseline:** Implement a standard “discrete model uncertainty” POMDP where the transition model is a finite set of unknown MDPs (like ME‑POMDP). Compare solution quality and α‑vector count against the causal POMDP approach on the same set of shifts. Hypothesis: causal structure reduces α‑vector count by leveraging factorization.
3. **Shift Identification under Known Causal Graph:** Deploy an agent in a simulator where the true environment corresponds to a specific intervention σ*∈Σ. Run the online planner with the joint belief. Track the evolution of b(σ) over steps. Test with interventions of varying “strength” and observability. Check if b(σ*) converges to high probability and how many steps/observations are needed.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How to obtain the set of possible intervention models Σ? The paper uses a prior over Σ but does not describe how Σ is specified or learned. Without a proper Σ, the agent’s belief may exclude the true distribution shift.
- **Open Question 2:** Does the finite‑horizon PWLC result extend to infinite‑horizon planning? The proof only covers finite horizon; infinite‑horizon case is not considered but is needed for many practical problems.
- **Open Question 3:** Can the framework accommodate interventions on reward and observation probabilities? Shifts are currently modeled only on state transition variables; changes in R or O are not captured.
- **Open Question 4:** How does augmenting the belief with domain uncertainty affect the computational complexity of α‑vector methods? The PWLC guarantee ensures a finite α‑vector representation but provides no bounds; belief space grows with |Σ|, possibly worsening scalability.

## Limitations
- Requires a known and correct causal graph; misspecification breaks the model.
- Assumes a finite/discrete intervention set Σ; continuous or large Σ leads to intractable belief.
- Observations are assumed invariant; unmodeled observation shifts hinder identification.
- Theory limited to finite horizon; infinite‑horizon case remains open.
- No empirical validation; scalability and robustness are untested.

## Confidence
- **Modeling approach:** Medium – The Bayesian update is solid, but tractability relies on finite Σ and a correct graph.
- **PWLC preservation:** High – The proof (Theorem 1 & Lemma 1) is rigorous and aligns with classic POMDP theory.
- **Evaluation/identification:** Medium – Theory is sound, but depends on Σ covering the true shift and distinguishability; lacking experiments.
