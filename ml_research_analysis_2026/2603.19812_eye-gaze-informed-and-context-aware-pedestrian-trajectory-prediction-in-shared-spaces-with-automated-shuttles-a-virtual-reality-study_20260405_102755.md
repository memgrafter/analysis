# Eye Gaze-Informed and Context-Aware Pedestrian Trajectory Prediction in Shared Spaces with Automated Shuttles: A Virtual Reality Study

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.19812  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19812  
- **Reference Count:** 40  
- **Primary Result:** Incorporating continuous eye gaze orientation (e.g., eye vislet) and situational context into a multimodal LSTM model significantly improves long-term pedestrian trajectory prediction accuracy in shared spaces with automated shuttles, achieving super-additive gains over motion-only baselines.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates pedestrian trajectory prediction in unstructured shared spaces with automated shuttles—a challenging scenario due to complex human-vehicle interactions. Using a virtual reality experiment, the authors collected fine-grained eye gaze, full-body motion, and contextual data from 51 participants across 537 trials. They propose GazeX-LSTM, a model that fuses pedestrian motion, eye gaze dynamics, and situational factors (e.g., approach angle, traffic flow). The core finding is that continuous eye gaze orientation, particularly the "eye vislet" representation, provides a strong leading signal for long-term predictions. Crucially, combining gaze with contextual variables yields super-additive improvements, demonstrating that human attentional cues and environmental factors are complementary. The results advance human-centered behavior modeling for automated driving in shared environments.

## Method Summary
Data were collected in a VR simulation (Unreal Engine 5, HTC VIVE Pro Eye at 20 Hz) where participants crossed a shared space while interacting with automated shuttles under varied conditions (eHMI, yielding, approach angle 45°/90°/135°, traffic gaps). After preprocessing (Gaussian smoothing, missing gaze interpolation, semantic target extraction via I-VT), trajectories were split into past (40 steps) and future (40 steps) windows. The GazeX-LSTM model processes three parallel input streams—past motion/velocity, distance to shuttles, and eye gaze—through dedicated LSTM encoders. Their final hidden states, concatenated with static experimental variables (categorical approach angle, continuous traffic), are passed through dense layers to output a Gaussian distribution over future positions. Training uses negative log-likelihood loss, with hyperparameter tuning via Optuna and a participant-stratified train/val/test split (6:1:3).

## Key Results
- The best model (eye-in-walking + context) reduces average displacement error (ADE) to 6.62–6.81 cm vs. 7.09–6.90 cm for the mean baseline, and final displacement error (FDE) to 9.94–10.74 cm vs. 11.17–10.34 cm for min-20 stochastic prediction.
- Continuous gaze representations (eye vislet, eye-in-walking) significantly outperform head orientation and semantic gaze labels for long-term horizons.
- Adding situational context to the best gaze model yields super-additive error reductions, with approach angle and number of shuttles emerging as strong predictors via SHAP analysis.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1  
- **Claim:** Continuous eye gaze orientation provides predictive information about a pedestrian's long-term trajectory by reflecting the direction of intended travel and attentional focus.  
- **Mechanism:** Pedestrians typically orient their eyes toward their goal or path segments ahead; these gaze directions are correlated with future walking direction. The GazeX-LSTM model encodes gaze dynamics with an LSTM, allowing it to capture gaze shift patterns that precede trajectory adjustments, especially for predictions beyond 2 seconds.  
- **Core assumption:** Eye gaze is a leading indicator of future motion; the relationship holds particularly for longer horizons where current velocity alone is insufficient.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] "leveraging eye-tracking data to capture pedestrian attention" and "fine-grained eye gaze dynamics... significantly improves long-term trajectory prediction accuracy."  
  - [Section VI.A] Table III shows that eye vislet and eye-in-walking reduce ADE to 6.81–6.62 cm compared to baseline 7.09–6.90 cm.  
  - [corpus] The surrounding literature (e.g., Gaze-Informed Vision Transformers) highlights gaze utility in driving decisions, but the target paper does not directly reference these, so corpus evidence is tangential.  
- **Break condition:** The mechanism may fail if pedestrians are distracted (e.g., smartphone use), if gaze no longer correlates with intended path, or if eye-tracking data quality is poor (missing values, calibration drift).

### Mechanism 2  
- **Claim:** Situational context variables (approach angle, traffic flow, shuttle yielding, eHMI) independently influence pedestrian behavior and, when combined with gaze, produce super-additive gains in prediction accuracy.  
- **Mechanism:** Contextual factors alter the pedestrian's perception of risk and available options. For instance, a 45° approach angle induces more hesitation and lateral deviation than a perpendicular 90° angle. These effects are not fully captured by motion or gaze alone. The model appends these static variables to the fused latent representation, conditioning the output distribution.  
- **Core assumption:** Contextual factors provide information orthogonal to motion and gaze, thus serving as complementary signals.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] "Combining eye gaze with situational context yields super-additive improvements."  
  - [Section VI.B] SHAP analysis (Fig. 6) reveals approach angle and number of shuttles are strong predictors of trajectory adjustments; Table V shows that adding experimental variables lowers long-term ADE.  
  - [Section IV.F] Table II documents systematic behavioral differences across context conditions (e.g., waiting time, deviation, gaze time).  
- **Break condition:** If key context variables are omitted (e.g., presence of obstacles, group dynamics), or if the model cannot capture nonlinear interactions, the predictive benefit diminishes. Also, noisy or mis-specified context labels could mislead the predictor.

### Mechanism 3  
- **Claim:** The multimodal LSTM fusion architecture effectively integrates motion, gaze, and context by preserving modality-specific temporal dynamics and merging them before decoding.  
- **Mechanism:** Each input stream (motion, distance, gaze) is processed by a dedicated LSTM encoder. Their final hidden states, together with static context X, are concatenated and passed through dense layers to output a Gaussian distribution over future positions. This late fusion strategy allows the model to capture both sequential patterns within each modality and cross-modal interactions.  
- **Core assumption:** The concatenated latent representation is sufficiently expressive to encode interactions without deeper architectural complexity.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [Section III.B] The architecture diagram (Fig. 2) and equations explicitly define separate encoders and a fusion layer.  
  - [Section VI.B] Ablation studies (Tables III and V) confirm that the full model with eye-in-walking and context achieves the lowest errors, validating the fusion approach.  
  - [corpus] Related works (e.g., Polar Collision Grids) also employ multimodal fusion, indicating the design pattern's prevalence, though not a direct evidence source.  
- **Break condition:** If one modality dominates or if the LSTM capacity is inadequate, fusion may not yield improvements; gradient issues could also prevent proper training.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks  
  - **Why needed:** The core predictive engine uses LSTM encoders to model temporal dependencies in motion, gaze, and distance sequences. Understanding LSTM gating (input, forget, output) is essential to grasp how past information influences future predictions.  
  - **Quick check question:** "In an LSTM, what is the purpose of the forget gate and how does it help with long-term dependencies?"  

- **Concept:** Eye-tracking and gaze representations  
  - **Why needed:** The paper introduces several ways to encode eye gaze (e.g., eye-in-space, eye-in-walking, eye vislet) and compares them to head orientation. Knowing the differences between these representations is crucial for designing effective human-aware models.  
  - **Quick check question:** "Why might continuous gaze orientation (e.g., eye vislet) be more informative than semantic labels like 'looking at shuttle' for trajectory prediction?"  

- **Concept:** Context-aware modeling in shared spaces  
  - **Why needed:** The inclusion of situational variables (approach angle, traffic flow, yielding, eHMI) is a key contribution. Learners should understand how to incorporate static or slowly varying context into sequence models.  
  - **Quick check question:** "How can categorical variables like 'approach angle' be meaningfully integrated into a neural network that otherwise processes continuous time series data?"  

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Inputs (Y_past, V, D, E, X) -> LSTM Encoders (Motion, Distance, Gaze) -> Concatenation (hidden states + X) -> Dense Layers (ReLU) -> Output Layer (Gaussian parameters: μ_x, μ_y, Cholesky U)  
- **Critical path:**  
  1. Preprocess raw signals: smooth trajectories, interpolate missing eye data, extract semantic targets.  
  2. Slice sliding windows of length T_p (40 steps at 20 Hz) from each trial.  
  3. Feed each window through the three LSTM encoders in parallel.  
  4. Concatenate encoder outputs and context; pass through dense layers to get distribution parameters.  
  5. Compute negative log-likelihood loss against ground‑truth future trajectory T_f (40 steps).  
  6. Backpropagate and update weights.  
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Gaze representation:* Continuous angular (eye vislet, eye-in-walking) avoids wrap‑around issues and provides smooth supervision; semantic labels reduce dimensionality but lose fine‑grained dynamics.  
  - *Context inclusion:* Static variables improve long‑term accuracy but may slightly hurt short‑term (Table V); careful encoding (one‑hot or embedding) required.  
  - *Deterministic vs stochastic:* Predicting a Gaussian distribution allows multiple futures; mean prediction is used for deterministic evaluation.  
  - *Head vs eye:* Head orientation is easier to obtain in real‑world but delivers weaker performance; deployment must weigh data availability against accuracy.  
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Long‑term error spikes while short‑term errors are low: Model may not be capturing goal‑directed gaze cues or context may be ignored.  
  - Training loss diverges: Likely due to unnormalized inputs, too large learning rate, or LSTM gradient explosion.  
  - Poor generalization to unseen approach angles (e.g., 45° or 135°) suggests context variables are insufficient or interaction effects are not learned.  
  - Sensitivity to missing eye data: If interpolation is not robust, the gaze encoder may produce erratic hidden states; consider masking or dropout.  
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. *Baseline replication:* Train model with only motion (Y_past) and velocity (V) inputs, no gaze or context. Verify ADE and FDE match Table III baseline (ADE≈7.09 cm, FDE≈11.17 cm for min‑20).  
  2. *Gaze representation ablation:* Iterate over four gaze encodings (eye‑in‑space, eye‑in‑walking, eye vislet, eye+head) plus head‑only variants. For each, train with same hyperparameters and compare ADE/FDE. Expect eye vislet and eye‑in‑walking to outperform others.  
  3. *Context contribution isolation:* Starting from best gaze model (e.g., eye‑in‑walking), add context variables one at a time (approach angle, then number of shuttles, then yielding, then eHMI). Measure ADE/FDE changes and perform SHAP analysis to observe variable importance, replicating the super‑additive effect in Table V and Fig. 6.

## Limitations
- Hyperparameter ambiguity: Exact tuned values (LSTM hidden sizes, layer counts, dropout) are not reported, risking capacity mismatches.  
- Baseline implementation gaps: Details of how motion and velocity are combined (single vs. separate encoders) are unspecified.  
- Context encoding unclear: Whether categorical approach angles are one-hot, embedded, or normalized is not stated.  
- Data split and windowing: Need verification of train/val/test sample counts and participant isolation to avoid leakage.  
- Gaze preprocessing sensitivity: Missing data interpolation and smoothing parameters could affect results if deviated.  
- Generalizability concerns: Findings are based on VR; real-world distractors (smartphones, occlusion) may break gaze-direction assumptions.
