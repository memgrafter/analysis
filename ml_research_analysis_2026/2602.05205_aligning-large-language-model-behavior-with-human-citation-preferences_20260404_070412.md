# Aligning Large Language Model Behavior with Human Citation Preferences

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.05205
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05205
- **Reference count:** 7
- **Primary result:** LLMs significantly misalign with human citation preferences, but Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) on a small pairwise dataset can calibrate behavior, with smaller models showing the largest gains.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether large language models (LLMs) select sentences for citation in ways that match human judgment. The authors construct a dataset of 6,000 Wikipedia sentences across eight categories, with human annotators labeling 2,596 cross-category pairs to indicate which sentence more requires a citation. Evaluating 11 LLMs, they find systematic misalignment: models over-select sentences marked "Citation needed" by up to 27% relative to humans, while under-selecting numeric sentences (-22.6%) and sentences containing personal names (-20.1%). Peak agreement with humans caps around 62% for the largest models. Fine-tuning five models with Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) on the human preferences improves alignment, most notably for the smallest model (Llama 1B), which gains 11.8%. This demonstrates that model citation behavior can be steered toward human preferences using targeted preference data.

## Method Summary
The study uses a pairwise preference paradigm. A dataset of Wikipedia sentences is grouped into eight semantic categories (e.g., "Citation needed", numeric, person-name). Human annotators compare sentence pairs from different categories and label which one is more cite-worthy. This creates a preference dataset (chosen vs. rejected sentences). LLMs are evaluated by prompting them with the same sentence pairs and parsing their choice; agreement rate with human labels measures alignment. For fine-tuning, DPO is applied: the human preference pairs train the model to increase the likelihood of the chosen sentence relative to the rejected one, using a reference model (often the same model before training) to prevent excessive deviation. Training employs LoRA (low-rank adaptation) for parameter efficiency, targeting specific modules (query, key, value, output projections) with rank 16 and alpha 32. Hyperparameters include a per-device batch size of 1, gradient accumulation of 8, 20 epochs, learning rate 2e-5 with cosine decay, and DPO beta=0.1. Training uses 1,206 pairs, validation 100 pairs, and evaluation on a held-out test set of 1,288 pairs.

## Key Results
- LLMs over-select sentences explicitly tagged "Citation needed" by up to 27% relative to human annotators, indicating a bias toward template-like markers.
- LLMs under-select numeric sentences (-22.6%) and sentences with personal names (-20.1%) compared to humans, showing insensitivity to factual density or entity recognition.
- Alignment with human preferences peaks at approximately 62% for the largest models, suggesting a ceiling even for state-of-the-art LLMs.
- DPO fine-tuning improves alignment across models, with the smallest (Llama 1B) showing the greatest gain (+11.8%), while the largest (Llama 70B) actually degrades (-1.6%), highlighting inverse scaling in this preference learning task.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core mechanism is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), which fine-tunes the LLM using pairwise human judgments without requiring a separate reward model. DPO directly optimizes the model's policy to assign higher probability to the human-preferred (chosen) sentence and lower to the rejected one, regularized by a reference model to maintain overall language quality. This aligns the model's internal notion of "citation need" with demonstrated human preferences. The use of LoRA (low-rank adaptation) makes the process efficient by updating only a small set of adapter weights, preventing catastrophic forgetting and enabling application to large models with limited computational resources. The observed gains, especially in smaller models, suggest these models are more pliable to preference updates, whereas larger models may already have entrenched biases or overfit the limited preference data.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component Map:** Dataset (6k sentences, 2.6k pairs) -> Model (LLaMA/Mistral) + LoRA adapters -> DPO Loss (chosen vs. rejected) -> Optimizer (AdamW) -> Updated Model.
- **Critical Path:** 1. Prepare sentence pairs from categories. 2. Load base model and attach LoRA modules. 3. Compute log-probabilities for chosen/rejected under current policy and reference. 4. Backpropagate DPO loss. 5. Evaluate on held-out pairs.
- **Design Tradeoffs:** LoRA rank vs. expressiveness (rank 16 balances efficiency and capacity); DPO beta controls deviation from reference model (beta=0.1 prevents collapse); category-balanced pairs ensure broad coverage but may oversample rare types.
- **Failure Signatures:** Validation loss increase (overfitting); negative gain on large models (catastrophic interference or insufficient data); high variance in prompt-based evaluation (output parsing inconsistency); category-specific accuracy drop (failure to correct bias).

## Limitations
- The exact dataset (sentence-level labels, pairwise mappings) is not publicly released, preventing exact replication and category-balance verification.
- The evaluation prompt wording and output parsing logic are unspecified, introducing potential variance in reported alignment rates.
- Model versions and inference parameters (e.g., sampling strategy) are only partially documented, risking drift from reported baselines.
