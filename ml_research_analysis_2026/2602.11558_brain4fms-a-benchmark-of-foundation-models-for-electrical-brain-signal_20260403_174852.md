# Brain4FMs: A Benchmark of Foundation Models for Electrical Brain Signal

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.11558
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11558
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Brain4FMs establishes a standardized benchmark for evaluating Brain Foundation Models on EEG/iEEG classification tasks, revealing that generative pretraining (autoencoders) and multi-modality alignment yield superior cross-subject generalization.

## Executive Summary
Brain4FMs introduces the first comprehensive benchmark for evaluating Brain Foundation Models (BFMs) on electrical brain signals, integrating 15 BFMs, 18 public datasets, and 22 downstream classification tasks under a unified cross-subject finetuning protocol. The systematic analysis yields five key insights: (1) generative models (autoencoders) consistently outperform contrastive ones; (2) pretraining on mixed EEG/iEEG data enables robust cross-modality generalization; (3) spatial inductive biases are task-dependent, with explicit spatial models vulnerable to channel permutation; (4) frequency-domain reconstruction benefits disease diagnosis in a task-specific manner; and (5) hierarchical codebook discretization balances robustness and capacity. The benchmark facilitates fair, reproducible comparisons to guide future BFM development.

## Method Summary
The benchmark defines a standardized pipeline: raw EEG/iEEG recordings are preprocessed (filtering, downsampling, windowing, per-channel z-scoring) to match the input expectations of each BFM. For each downstream dataset, a leave-subjects-out cross-validation scheme is used (train/valid/test ≈ 3:1:1). A pretrained BFM serves as a backbone to extract features; a simple task-specific classifier (linear layer) is then finetuned with fixed learning rates (1e-5 for backbone, 1e-4 for classifier) and early stopping. Performance is aggregated across folds using metrics like AUROC, accuracy, F1, and Cohen’s κ. The codebase and preprocessing scripts ensure consistency, though some hyperparameters (e.g., optimizer variants) are model-specific and not fully documented in the paper.

## Key Results
- Generative BFMs (autoencoders) produce tighter within-class clusters and larger inter-class separation than contrastive models, leading to higher classification performance.
- Models pretrained on both EEG and iEEG (e.g., BrainWave) generalize robustly across modalities, while single-modality models suffer degradation on the other modality.
- Explicit spatial inductive biases (e.g., graph convolutions) improve performance on spatially dependent tasks (epilepsy) but cause significant drops under channel permutation; spatially weak models remain insensitive.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Generative pretraining (autoencoders) yields latent embeddings with class-discriminative geometry that generalizes better to downstream classification than contrastive pretraining.
- **Mechanism**: Autoencoders optimize reconstruction of the input, forcing the encoder to retain all information useful for signal recovery. This creates embeddings where same-class instances are tightly clustered and different classes are well-separated (high R_cls, low LMER). Contrastive methods optimize alignment of positive pairs but may discard information not needed for instance discrimination.
- **Core assumption**: Downstream classification performance correlates with geometric quality of the latent space.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract states generative models outperform contrastive ones with tighter clusters and larger separation; Section 3.3.2 and Figure 3a show higher R_cls and lower LMER for generative models.
- **Break condition**: If reconstruction target is misaligned (e.g., autoregressive next-step prediction models unidirectional context, which may not capture bidirectional features needed for global discrimination).

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Pretraining on a mixture of EEG and iEEG yields representations that generalize robustly across both modalities, whereas single-modality pretraining suffers modality-specific degradation.
- **Mechanism**: Joint pretraining exposes the model to both scalp EEG and intracranial EEG, encouraging it to learn features invariant to recording modality differences (spatial resolution, noise profiles) while retaining task-relevant information.
- **Core assumption**: There exists a latent representation largely agnostic to EEG vs. iEEG acquisition differences.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract notes models trained on both EEG and iEEG (e.g., BrainWave) demonstrate robust cross-modality generalization; Table 3 shows BrainWave maintains high AUROC on both CHBMIT (EEG) and MAYO/FNUSA (iEEG), while modality-specific models degrade on the other.
- **Break condition**: If the two modalities have fundamentally different neural generators or vastly different protocols, joint pretraining might cause interference.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: The benefit of explicit spatial inductive biases (e.g., graph convolutions, spatial positional encodings) is task-dependent; they improve performance on tasks with strong spatial topology dependence but confer no advantage (or even harm) on tasks where channel order is irrelevant.
- **Mechanism**: Spatially aware architectures incorporate the physical arrangement of electrodes, enabling modeling of spatial propagation patterns (e.g., seizure spread). Channel permutation disrupts these patterns, causing performance drops. Spatially weak models treat channels independently or use permutation-invariant operations, making them insensitive to channel order.
- **Core assumption**: Spatial topology is a meaningful inductive bias only if the downstream task relies on relative electrode positions.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract states spatially strong models (BrainWave, REVE) suffer significant performance drops under channel permutation on tasks like epilepsy, while spatially weak models (BrainBERT) show insensitivity; Section 3.3.3 and Appendix F report AUROC changes after permuting channels.
- **Break condition**: For tasks with weak spatial dependence (e.g., based on global spectral features), spatial biases increase model complexity without benefit and may overfit to arbitrary channel ordering if not permutation-equivariant.

- **Why needed**: The benchmark emphasizes cross-subject evaluation as a proxy for real-world clinical applicability. This core design choice affects how results are interpreted.
- **Quick check**: Why is leave-subjects-out validation more challenging than random split of trials, and what does it measure?

## Architecture Onboarding
The pipeline consists of the following components:
Data loader -> Backbone extractor -> Task head -> Training manager -> Evaluation aggregator

**Critical path**: For each downstream dataset: split subjects into folds → for each fold: load train/val/test subjects → preprocess all data → initialize BFM with pretrained weights → optionally finetune on training set → evaluate on test set → record metrics. The path is sequential per fold but can parallelize across folds.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- (a) Frozen vs. finetuned backbone: Frozen reduces overfitting and compute but may not adapt to new distribution; finetuning can improve performance but requires careful learning rate tuning.
- (b) Model selection: generative vs. contrastive, spatial vs. non-spatial, codebook vs. continuous; trade-offs in performance, robustness (e.g., channel permutation), and computational cost.
- (c) Preprocessing alignment: Must match the BFM's pretraining pipeline (sampling rate, montage) to avoid domain gap; otherwise adapt with minimal fine-tuning.

**Failure signatures**:
- (a) Large performance gap between training and validation (overfitting) suggests backbone not frozen properly or insufficient data.
- (b) Near-chance performance on all models may indicate preprocessing mismatch or task impossibility.
- (c) For spatially explicit models, a drastic drop after channel permutation indicates reliance on absolute channel order rather than relative topology; if permutation is expected (e.g., variable montage), this is a failure.
- (d) High variance across CV folds indicates data leakage or subject grouping issues.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. Reproduce baseline results: Run the full benchmark on a small subset (e.g., CHBMIT and SleepEDF) with at least three BFMs (one contrastive, one generative, one hybrid) to verify the pipeline matches reported numbers.
2. Modality alignment test: Using BrainWave, evaluate on both an EEG dataset (CHBMIT) and an iEEG dataset (MAYO) with and without modality-specific pretraining (if available) to confirm the cross-modality robustness.
3. Spatial robustness probe: For a spatially strong model (e.g., BrainWave) and a spatially weak model (e.g., BrainBERT), perform channel permutation on CHBMIT (epilepsy, spatially dependent) and ADFD (Alzheimer’s, spatially weak). Compare AUROC degradation to validate the permutation sensitivity pattern.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
How do pretraining data modality and composition influence downstream BFM performance? Results are correlational; performance differences may stem from other factors like architecture or SSL strategy. Systematic ablation varying only pretraining modality while keeping other factors constant is needed.

### Open Question 2
What is the independent effect of SSL strategy on cross-task BFM generalization? Models differ in multiple dimensions; no controlled experiments isolate SSL objective. Evidence: Train models with identical backbones and pretraining data using different SSL losses and evaluate on Brain4FMs.

### Open Question 3
Do BFMs learn task-relevant spatial structure, and is this learning robust to channel permutation? Unclear what spatial priors are encoded (absolute vs. relative) and how task-dependent sensitivity is; some spatially strong models remain stable. Evidence: Fine-grained spatial perturbations and probing with synthetic data.

### Open Question 4
How is frequency information represented across BFMs, and does band-specific encoding causally affect task performance? Only correlation shown; causality unknown. Evidence: Intervention studies manipulating frequency representation (e.g., frequency-domain pretraining objectives, adversarial band suppression) and measuring performance changes on tasks with known frequency biomarkers.

### Open Question 5
How do codebook discretization strategies impact BFM representation robustness, capacity, and task adaptability? Optimal codebook design (size, depth, fine-tuning) not determined; trade-offs underexplored. Evidence: Large-scale ablation of codebook parameters across tasks, measuring representation quality and downstream accuracy.

## Limitations
- The selected datasets and models may not fully represent the diversity of real-world EEG/iEEG recordings and future foundation model architectures, limiting generalizability of conclusions.
- Causal relationships (e.g., frequency-domain benefits, codebook effects) are not established; evidence is correlational.
- Incomplete documentation of preprocessing and finetuning hyperparameters introduces hidden variability and hampers reproducibility.

## Confidence
- Generative vs. Contrastive Superiority: High
- Modality Alignment Criticality: High
- Task-Dependent Spatial Inductive Bias: High
- Frequency-Domain Reconstruction Benefit: Medium
- Codebook Discretization Trade-offs: Medium

## Next Checks
1. **Independent Baseline Reproduction**: Execute the full pipeline on a minimal subset (e.g., CHBMIT and SleepEDF) with three representative BFMs (contrastive, generative, hybrid). Verify cross-subject AUROC/accuracy match reported values within ±0.02.
2. **Controlled Modality Transfer Experiment**: Compare BrainWave (EEG+iEEG) against a purely iEEG-pretrained model (e.g., Brant) on CHBMIT (EEG) and MAYO (iEEG) under identical finetuning. Measure AUROC and embedding similarity (CCA/RSA) to determine if cross-modality generalization stems from shared latent factors.
3. **Expanded Spatial Sensitivity Analysis**: Apply channel permutation to additional spatially dependent tasks (e.g., motor imagery, P300 detection) and models with diverse spatial biases (graph convolutions, separable CNNs, permutation-equivariant Transformers). Test whether the observed pattern (spatially explicit models vulnerable to permutation) generalizes beyond epilepsy and Alzheimer’s domains.
