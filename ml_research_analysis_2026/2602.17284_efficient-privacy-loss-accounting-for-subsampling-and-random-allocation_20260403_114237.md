# Efficient privacy loss accounting for subsampling and random allocation
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.17284
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17284
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Provides an efficient algorithm to compute the privacy loss distribution (PLD) for random allocation, yielding tight privacy bounds for DP-SGD.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses inefficiencies in privacy loss accounting for subsampling schemes, specifically random allocation where each user's data is used in exactly k randomly chosen steps out of t. Existing methods using hockey-stick or Rényi divergences produce loose bounds. The core contribution is an efficient algorithm to compute the privacy loss distribution (PLD) for the random allocation of any differentially private algorithm. The approach expresses the PLD of random allocation as a log-transform of sums of exponentiated PLDs, enabling computation via convolutions. The algorithm runs in O(log³(t)·log(t/β)/α²) time with approximation parameters α (privacy loss error) and β (probability slack). For the Gaussian mechanism, the resulting bounds are nearly tight, matching Monte Carlo estimates and improving upon prior analytical bounds. The privacy-utility tradeoff of random allocation is shown to be at least as good as Poisson subsampling, making it better for DP-SGD. The method also extends to general subsampling and composition, demonstrated by improved accounting for DP-SGD with PREAMBLE, achieving up to 20% lower noise requirements than RDP-based accounting.

## Method Summary
We compute privacy guarantees by directly manipulating Privacy Loss Distributions (PLDs). For a base DP mechanism, we obtain a PLD realization that dominates its true privacy loss. Random allocation (1-out-of-t) is then reduced to a transformation (Theorem 4.4): the PLD equals a scaled logarithm of a sum of exponentiated PLDs from the base and its dual. This transformation is computed efficiently via convolution using exponentiation by squaring on a discretized multiplicative grid. The algorithm outputs an (α,β)-approximate PLD that stochastically dominates the true PLD. Poisson subsampling can be applied directly on PLDs using separate transformations (Theorem 3.3). Composition of multiple steps uses standard convolution. This framework yields tight numerical privacy accounting for complex subsampling patterns and demonstrates improved privacy-utility tradeoffs.

## Key Results
- Provides an efficient algorithm to compute the PLD for random allocation, with runtime O(log³(t)·log(t/β)/α²).
- For the Gaussian mechanism, the bounds are nearly tight, matching Monte Carlo estimates and improving prior analytical bounds.
- Random allocation achieves at least as good a privacy‑utility tradeoff as Poisson subsampling, leading to improved DP‑SGD accounting (e.g., up to 20% lower noise with PREAMBLE).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** If the target algorithm is dominated by a randomizer with pair \((P,Q)\), then the PLD of its random allocation (1-out-of-\(t\)) equals a specific transformation of the PLDs of \((P,Q)\) and its dual.
- **Mechanism:** Theorem 4.4 expresses \(L_{\bar{P}_t, Q_t} = \ln\left(\frac{1}{t}\left(e^{L_{P,Q}} + \sum_{i=1}^{t-1} e^{-L_{Q,P}}\right)\right)\) and \(L_{Q_t, \bar{P}_t} = -\ln\left(\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=1}^t e^{-L_{Q,P}}\right)\), where \(L_{Q,P}\) is the dual of \(L_{P,Q}\).
- **Core assumption:** The algorithm is dominated by a randomizer (Defn. 2.6); standard add/remove adjacency with a distinguished null element \(\bot\).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "the privacy loss distribution (PLD) of random allocation applied to any differentially private algorithm can be computed efficiently."
  - [section 2.2] Claim 2.9 gives the dominating distributions \(\bar{P}_t, Q_t\) and reduction to randomizer.
  - [corpus] Prior work (Feldman & Shenfeld, 2025) derives analogous bounds via divergences; this identity enables PLD computation.
- **Break condition:** The transformation is exact for the dominating pair, but the overall bound may be loose if the dominating pair is not tight for the actual algorithm (e.g., adaptive steps with strong dependency). The symmetry argument fails if the dominating pair’s PLD depends on past views.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** There is an algorithm that takes a PLD realization \(L\) and produces a PLD realization \(L_t\) that \((\alpha,\beta)\)-tightly stochastically dominates the true PLD of random allocation, in time \(O(\log^3 t \cdot \log(t/\beta)/\alpha^2)\).
- **Mechanism:** Discretize \(L\) and its dual to grids of width \(\alpha' = \alpha/(2\lceil\log_2 t\rceil+1)\) and tail probability \(\beta' = \beta/t\). Then perform \((t-1)\) self‑convolutions on \(e^{-D(L)}\) via exponentiation by squaring, convolve once with \(e^L\), take the logarithm and divide by \(t\). Each convolution uses direct multiplication and re‑discretization to a geometrically‑spaced grid.
- **Core assumption:** The input \(L\) is a PLD realization that dominates the per‑step PLD. Accurate quantile computation is feasible. The interquartile range (IQR) is not astronomically larger than \(\alpha\).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4] Theorem 4.6 states the algorithm, its guarantees, and runtime.
  - [section 4] Algorithm 1 (remove direction) and 2 (add direction) implement the steps.
  - [corpus] The direct convolution approach contrasts with FFT‑based composition (Koskela et al., 2020), justified by multiplicative spacing requirements.
- **Break condition:** If the IQR of \(L\) or \(D(L)\) is huge, the number of bins becomes large and runtime may become prohibitive. Truncating the extreme \(\beta'\) quantiles introduces a probability mass \(\beta\) that may become non‑negligible for very small \(\beta\). Numerical underflow/overflow can occur for extreme values when exponentiating.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Poisson subsampling with probability \(\lambda\) can be applied directly on a PLD realization \(L\) to produce a new PLD realization that dominates the subsampled algorithm, avoiding mechanism‑specific closed forms.
- **Mechanism:** Theorem 3.3 gives transformations \(\vec{\phi}_\lambda(L)\) for remove direction and \(\check{\phi}_\lambda(L)\) for add direction, using \(\varphi_\lambda(l) = \ln(1+(e^l-1)/\lambda)\). For a PLD realization \(L\) dominating \(L_{P,Q}\), \(\vec{\phi}_\lambda(L)\) dominates the PLD of the Poisson‑subsampled mechanism (by convex combination \(P_\lambda = \lambda P + (1-\lambda)Q\)).
- **Core assumption:** The input PLD realization \(L\) dominates the original algorithm’s PLD in the relevant direction (remove or add). The transformations depend only on \(L\) and its dual \(D(L)\).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3] Theorem 3.3 provides the formulas.
  - [section 3] Lemma 3.2 (from Zhu et al., 2022) ensures domination is preserved under subsampling.
  - [corpus] Balle et al. (2018) derived analogous transformations for hockey‑stick divergence; this extends to PLD representations.
- **Break condition:** The transformation requires knowledge of the dual PLD, which may have infinite support. Truncation errors can accumulate if the tails are heavy. The transformation assumes independent subsampling; it does not directly apply to dependent schemes like without‑replacement sampling without further modification.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Privacy loss distribution (PLD)
  - **Why needed here:** The paper represents privacy guarantees as a PLD realization, enabling composition and subsampling via simple transformations rather than recalculating divergences.
  - **Quick check question:** For Gaussian mechanism with sensitivity \(c\) and noise \(\sigma\), the privacy loss is Gaussian; what are its mean and variance? (Answer: mean \(c^2/(2\sigma^2)\), variance \(c^2/\sigma^2\).)

- **Concept:** Dominating pair
  - **Why needed here:** It reduces the analysis of complex t‑step algorithms to a single pair \((P,Q)\); the transformations operate on the PLD of this pair, making the method independent of algorithm adaptivity.
  - **Quick check question:** What pair dominates the Gaussian mechanism? (Answer: \(\mathcal{N}(1,\sigma^2)\) and \(\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)\).)

- **Concept:** Stochastic domination (and approximate stochastic domination)
  - **Why needed here:** The algorithm outputs a random variable that stochastically dominates the true PLD; the \((\alpha,\beta)\) notion quantifies how tight the bound is, directly relating to final \((\varepsilon,\delta)\) errors.
  - **Quick check question:** If \(L_1 \preceq^{(\alpha,\beta)} L_2\), what happens to \(\delta(\varepsilon)\) computed from \(L_1\) vs \(L_2\)? (Answer: \(\delta_{L_1}(\varepsilon) \le \delta_{L_2}(\varepsilon-\alpha) + \beta\).)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** The system comprises: (a) a PLD realization class storing support values and probabilities (including atoms at \(\pm\infty\)), (b) core operations: `PLD-dual` (extract dual), `disc-dist` (discretize with truncation), `conv` (direct convolution with renormalization), `self-conv` (exponentiation by squaring), and `rand-alloc` (add/remove variants) that compose these, (c) utility functions for quantiles and range calculation.
- **Critical path:** 1. Obtain or construct a PLD realization \(L\) for the base mechanism (e.g., Gaussian). 2. For the desired directions, call `rand-alloc-remove` or `rand-alloc-add` with \(L\), number of allocations \(t\), and accuracy \((\alpha,\beta)\). 3. Use the returned PLD realization in composition or convert to \((\varepsilon,\delta)\) via filtering.
- **Design tradeoffs:** Direct convolution (used) places bins in multiplicative space, ensuring relative error is bounded but may waste bins on large values. FFT could be faster but would require additive spacing, leading to high resolution at large losses and poor at small losses; the paper notes FFT may be better for the remove direction in extreme regimes. Truncating tails (\(\beta/t\)) keeps support size small but introduces slack \(\beta\); choosing \(\alpha\) controls the discretization error per step.
- **Failure signatures:** Memory blowup if the number of bins after convolution grows; the algorithm controls this by re‑discretizing after each convolution. Numerical instability for very small probabilities (e.g., when \(\delta < 10^{-15}/t\)) due to floating‑point limits; mitigations include log‑space computations and higher precision. Runtime increases quadratically with \(1/\alpha\) and polylogarithmically with \(t\); if the IQR of the PLD is large relative to \(\alpha\), the grid size may become huge.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Compare `rand-alloc` against Monte Carlo estimates for Gaussian random allocation; verify that the obtained \((\varepsilon,\delta)\) matches MC within \(\alpha,\beta\) and improves prior analytical bounds.
  2. Compose `rand-alloc` with Poisson subsampling (using `PLD-subsam-*`) to reproduce the PREAMBLE DP‑FTRL experiment; confirm a ~20% noise reduction relative to RDP accounting.
  3. Stress test scaling: measure runtime as a function of \(t\) and \(1/\alpha\); confirm \(O(\log^3 t / \alpha^2)\) and that the IQR‑based bound on grid size holds in practice (e.g., for \(\sigma=1\), IQR ~ \(\sqrt{\ln(t/\beta)}/\sigma\)).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Does a tight, direct PLD computation exist for k‑out‑of‑t random allocation without the lossy reduction to composition of 1‑out‑of‑⌊t/k⌋ cases?
2. Can we develop an FFT‑based convolution method for random allocation with rigorous approximation guarantees and without the large dynamic range problem?
3. For which DP mechanisms beyond the Gaussian mechanism can we derive efficient PLD realizations (or tight approximations) to apply the random allocation accounting framework?
4. Does random allocation retain its privacy‑utility advantage over Poisson subsampling for noise distributions other than Gaussian, such as Laplace or heavy‑tailed distributions?

## Limitations
- The method depends on a dominating pair; if this pair is loose, the final PLD overestimates privacy loss.
- Discretization and truncation errors introduce approximation slack, and their cumulative effect on very small \(\delta\) is not fully analyzed.
- The reduction for \(k>1\) (multiple allocations per user) can be significantly lossy when \(\lfloor t/k \rfloor\) is small.
