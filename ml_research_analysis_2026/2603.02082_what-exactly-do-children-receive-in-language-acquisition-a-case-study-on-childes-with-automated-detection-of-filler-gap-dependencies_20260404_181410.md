# What Exactly do Children Receive in Language Acquisition? A Case Study on CHILDES with Automated Detection of Filler-Gap Dependencies

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.02082
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02082
- **Reference count:** 26
- **Primary result:** A hybrid automated detector for filler-gap dependencies achieves high accuracy and reveals frequency patterns in CHILDES, with child production mirroring adult input distributions.

## Executive Summary
This study addresses a central debate in language acquisition—whether children's acquisition of filler-gap dependencies (FGDs) relies on innate knowledge or statistical learning—by quantifying children's exposure to these structures at scale. The authors develop a hybrid automated detector that combines constituency and dependency parsing to identify three FGD constructions (matrix wh-questions, embedded wh-questions, relative clauses) and their extraction sites (subject, object, adjunct). Validated on human-annotated data, the detector achieves high F1 scores (>0.8). Applied to 57 English CHILDES corpora (2.8M utterances), it characterizes developmental trajectories: matrix questions are an order of magnitude more frequent than other FGDs; wh-questions show a consistent object-bias, while relative clauses are balanced; and children's production mirrors adult input distributions. A case study using filtered corpus training demonstrates that removing specific FGD constructions impairs language model performance on those constructions, suggesting piecemeal acquisition and limited cross-construction generalization. The work provides a scalable tool for future theoretical and computational modeling.

## Method Summary
The core method is a rule-based detector that processes English utterances from CHILDES using two pre-trained parsers: a constituency parser (Berkeley Neural) and a dependency parser (spaCy). For each target construction, the system applies heuristic rules to the constituency parse to identify the construction's structural skeleton (e.g., `NP -> NP SBAR` for relative clauses). It then uses the dependency parse to validate and subtype the extraction site (subject, object, adjunct) via relations like `relcl` and `nsubj`. The detector outputs a list of labels per utterance. It was validated against human-annotated data (Pearl & Sprouse, 2013), achieving high precision and recall. The system was then run on 2.8M utterances from 57 English CHILDES corpora to derive frequency statistics by speaker role (adult/child) and child age bin (normalized per 1,000 utterances). No machine learning model is trained for the detector itself.

## Key Results
- Hybrid detector achieves F1 scores >0.8 for most FGD categories on validation data.
- Matrix wh-questions are approximately 10 times more frequent than embedded questions or relative clauses in child-directed speech.
- Children's production of FGDs mirrors the distributional patterns of adult input, including extraction-site biases (object-bias in wh-questions, balance in relative clauses).
- Filtered corpus training (removing a specific FGD construction) significantly impairs language model performance on that construction, indicating limited cross-construction generalization.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** A hybrid parsing approach improves accuracy in identifying filler-gap constructions and extraction sites compared to using either parser alone.
**Mechanism:** Constituency parsing provides explicit clausal boundaries and phrase structure (e.g., `NP -> NP SBAR` for relative clauses), crucial for construction classification. Dependency parsing directly encodes head-dependent relations (e.g., `relcl`, `nsubj`), which are more robust for pinpointing extraction sites. The system uses constituency to detect the construction's skeleton, then dependency relations to validate and subtype the extraction site, cross-checking for consistency.
**Core assumption:** Constituency and dependency formalisms provide complementary, non-redundant syntactic signals; errors in one can be resolved by constraints from the other.
**Evidence anchors:** 
- Abstract: "Our approach combines constituency and dependency parsing, leveraging their complementary strengths..."
- Section 3.2: Examples where dependency alone fails to distinguish matrix vs. embedded questions, and constituency alone misidentifies extraction sites.
- Section 4: Evaluation shows F1 >0.8 for most categories, supporting the hybrid method's viability.
**Break condition:** If one parser systematically fails on noisy child-directed speech (e.g., constituency mis-parses fragments), the cross-checking could cause cascading errors, leading to under- or over-detection.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Applying the detector at corpus scale to CHILDES enables quantification of children's exposure to fine-grained FGD distributions, infeasible manually.
**Mechanism:** The detector processes millions of utterances, labeling each with FGD categories (e.g., `OMQ`, `SRC`). Aggregating labels over age bins and speaker roles yields frequency trajectories (e.g., rates per 1,000 utterances), transforming raw transcripts into quantifiable input distributions for testing acquisition theories.
**Core assumption:** CHILDES provides a representative sample of children's linguistic input, and the detector's accuracy is sufficient to capture stable statistical trends (e.g., the order-of-magnitude frequency difference).
**Evidence anchors:** 
- Abstract: "Applying the system to 57 English CHILDES corpora, we are able to characterize children’s filler-gap input..."
- Section 5: Presents descriptive statistics showing matrix questions are ~10x more frequent and child production mirrors adult input.
- Corpus: Analysis uses 2.8M utterances; validation against Pearl & Sprouse annotations anchors reliability.
**Break condition:** If detector errors are systematically correlated with speakers, ages, or transcript types, derived frequency profiles could be biased. The paper acknowledges limitations in parsing noisy child speech.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Filtered corpus training—removing specific FGD constructions from a language model's training data—impairs model performance on those constructions, suggesting piecemeal acquisition and limited cross-construction generalization.
**Mechanism:** The detector's labels allow precise removal of all sentences containing a target construction from training data. Models trained on filtered corpora are evaluated on synthetically generated minimal pairs for all three constructions. A significant drop in accuracy only on the filtered construction indicates the model does not generalize the dependency pattern from other constructions.
**Core assumption:** Language models trained on child-scale data (BabyLM's 10M words) share inductive biases with child learners, and the synthetic test set probes abstract FGD knowledge separate from lexical knowledge.
**Evidence anchors:** 
- Abstract: "A case study demonstrates utility... where removing specific FGD constructions... impairs model performance."
- Section 6.2: Experiment with Llama and GPT-2 shows filtering matrix questions hurts performance on matrix questions but not as much on others.
- Corpus: Uses CHILDES component of BabyLM Challenge (~360k sentences); synthetic test sets in Appendix F.
**Break condition:** If the synthetic test set confounds FGD knowledge with other linguistic factors (e.g., specific wh-words), or if filtering is incomplete due to detector recall limits, the effect could be due to lexical frequency shifts rather than loss of structural generalization.

## Foundational Learning
### Filler-Gap Dependency Syntax
**Why needed:** To understand what the system detects (wh-movement, relativization) and interpret extraction site labels (subject/object/adjunct). Without this, the engineering task is mere pattern matching without linguistic meaning.
**Quick check:** In "Which book did Mary read?", what is the filler and where is the gap? (Answer: Filler = "Which book"; Gap = object position after "read".)

### Constituency vs. Dependency Parsing
**Why needed:** The core algorithm's logic hinges on using output from both parser types. An engineer must understand what information each provides (phrase structure trees vs. head-dependent relations) to follow or modify the heuristics.
**Quick check:** What structural information is explicit in a constituency parse but only implicit in a dependency parse? (Answer: Clausal boundaries and coordination, e.g., distinguishing a matrix clause from an embedded complement.)

### Corpus Linguistics & Frequency Analysis
**Why needed:** The output is a dataset of frequencies and trajectories. An engineer must understand binning, normalization (per 1,000 utterances), and statistical comparison to correctly use the released data or extend the analysis.
**Quick check:** Why is it problematic to compare raw counts of FGDs from a 12-month-old’s transcript (100 utterances) to a 48-month-old’s transcript (5,000 utterances) without normalization? (Answer: The older child has more total speech, so raw counts reflect overall productivity, not relative use of FGD structures.)

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Data Ingestion -> Preprocessing Pipeline -> Construction Detectors -> Label Aggregator. Output feeds into Validation Module (for evaluation) and Analysis Module (for results).

### Critical Path
Raw text → Dual parse generation (constituency + dependency) → Heuristic matching (construction detection) → Heuristic matching (extraction site subtyping) → Dependency validation → Output label list. The dependency validation step is crucial for precision; skipping it increases false positives.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Precision vs. Recall:** Heuristics can be tuned. The paper prioritized high precision, likely at some cost to recall (e.g., missing reduced relatives). The break condition in Mechanism 1 relates here.
- **Parser choice:** Using spaCy’s off-the-shelf parsers favors robustness and speed over state-of-the-art accuracy. A more accurate parser could improve F1 but may not generalize as well to noisy child speech.
- **Granularity vs. Coverage:** The system targets 3 constructions. Adding more (clefts, topicalization) would increase utility but requires new heuristics and validation, risking parser-specific failure modes.

### Failure Signatures
- **Parsing failure cascades:** If either parser returns an error or malformed tree (common in child speech), the detector likely skips the utterance (low recall). Monitor parser error rates.
- **Heuristic brittleness:** A rule like "check for NP before VP inside S" fails for subject extractions with inserted adverbs, leading to systematic mis-subtyping (e.g., labeling SRC as ORC). Check for unbalanced error rates across extraction sites.
- **Cross-construction interference:** An embedded question inside a relative clause might be double-counted or mis-typed. The system outputs multiple labels, but complex sentences could confuse heuristics.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Do the fine-grained distributions of filler-gap dependencies (FGDs) in child-directed speech support poverty-of-stimulus arguments (innate knowledge) or statistical learning theories?
- **Basis in paper:** The abstract states the study aims to resolve debates about language acquisition by quantifying children's exposure to FGDs. The introduction frames the core debate: “Children’s acquisition of filler-gap dependencies has been argued by some to depend on innate grammatical knowledge, while others suggest that the distributional evidence available in child-directed speech suffices.”
- **Why unresolved:** The paper provides descriptive statistics of input and child production but does not directly test which theoretical account the data favors; it only equips future work with the tools to do so.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Longitudinal studies correlating specific distributional measures (e.g., frequency of particular extraction sites within constructions) with age of acquisition in children, or computational simulations that train learners on the quantified input and compare outcomes to child development.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** At what level of linguistic generalization (lexical items, constructions, or extraction-site subtypes) do frequency effects in child-directed speech most strongly predict the acquisition order and timing of FGDs?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 6.1: “Crucially, frequency effects are expected only when frequency is measured at the appropriate level of generalization (lexical items vs. constructions vs. subtype divisions; Ambridge et al., 2015).” The detector enables such fine-grained measurement.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper reports aggregate frequencies across constructions and sites but does not analyze which granularity best predicts developmental trajectories; it calls for future work to extract lexical distributions.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Regression or correlation analyses using children’s production accuracy/age as dependent variables and frequency measures at varying levels (e.g., overall FGD rate, construction-specific rates, subtype-specific rates) as predictors, controlling for lexical factors.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Why do extraction biases (subject vs. object) differ systematically across FGD constructions (e.g., strong object-bias in wh-questions vs. near-balance in relative clauses), and how are these biases reflected in children’s developing speech?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 5.3 and Figure 6 show consistent object-bias in matrix and embedded questions, but relative clauses are balanced. The paper states: “These results suggest that extraction biases are not uniform across filler-gap constructions.”
- **Why unresolved:** The paper documents the pattern but does not investigate its etiology—whether it arises from input distributions, processing constraints, or innate biases—nor how children’s production trajectories relate to it.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Cross-linguistic studies to test if the same biases hold in languages with different typological profiles; experimental training studies manipulating the frequencies of extraction sites in child-directed speech to see if production biases shift accordingly.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Does filtering out specific FGD constructions from training data impair language models’ performance on those constructions more than on others, and what does this reveal about the abstractness of their internal representations?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 6.2 poses: “to what extent do human-scale LMs generalize across related constructions, and how does input data affect such generalization?” The filtered corpus training experiment found that removing a construction hurt performance on that construction most (“suggesting piecemeal acquisition”).
- **Why unresolved:** The experiment is preliminary (small models, limited evaluations); it is unclear whether results generalize to larger architectures or reflect broader principles of representation learning.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Scaling up the filtered-training paradigm across diverse model sizes and architectures, with comprehensive evaluations for all FGD subtypes, and probing internal representations (e.g., via attention patterns or linear probes) to assess whether shared abstract structure emerges or learning remains construction-specific.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Can the automated detection approach be extended to other languages and other filler-gap phenomena (e.g., clefts, topicalization), and do the observed distributional patterns (e.g., extraction biases) generalize cross-linguistically?
- **Basis in paper:** The Limitations section states: “our detectors only identify some filler-gap phenomena and not others (e.g., cleftings and topicalization), and they are only defined over English. Future work could extend the approach to other phenomena and languages.”
- **Why unresolved:** The study is restricted to English and three constructions; cross-linguistic variation in FGDs is critical for evaluating universalist vs. language-specific acquisition theories but remains unexplored.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implementing comparable detectors for other languages using CHILDES or similar corpora, then comparing distributional patterns (frequencies, extraction biases) across languages to test for universals or language-specific effects.

## Limitations
- **Code/Heuristic Spec Incompleteness:** The exact rule implementation (e.g., handling reduced relatives) is not fully specified; reliance on the flowchart may miss critical edge cases. Parser choice and preprocessing details are under-documented.
- **Parser Performance on Noisy Child Speech:** Both parsers are trained on standard text; ungrammatical, fragmented child utterances may cause systematic misparses, risking cascading errors.
- **Potential Corpus Bias:** The 57 CHILDES corpora may over-represent certain interaction types or demographics, and the filtered training case study may conflate lexical frequency loss with structural knowledge loss.

## Confidence
- **Hybrid Detector Accuracy:** High (validated with F1 >0.8 on human-annotated data).
- **Corpus-Scale Frequency Trajectories:** Medium (order-of-magnitude difference is robust, but extraction-site biases could be skewed by non-random mis-subtyping).
- **Filtered Training Generalization Claim:** Low (preliminary experiment with small models, synthetic test sets, and possible confounds).
