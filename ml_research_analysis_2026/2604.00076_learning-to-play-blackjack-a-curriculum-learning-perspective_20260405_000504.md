# Learning to Play Blackjack: A Curriculum Learning Perspective

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.00076  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00076  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** LLM-guided action curriculum increased DQN agent's average win rate from 43.97% to 47.41%, reduced bust rate from 32.9% to 28.0%, and accelerated total training time by over 74%.

## Executive Summary
Reinforcement Learning (RL) agents often struggle with efficiency in environments having large action spaces. This study introduces an LLM-guided action curriculum for Blackjack, where a Large Language Model (Gemini 2.0 Flash) dynamically generates a multi-stage training path that progressively introduces actions based on complexity. Agents must achieve LLM-defined win rate thresholds to advance stages. In an 8-deck simulation, the curriculum boosted the DQN agent's win rate by over 3 percentage points, lowered its bust rate by nearly 5 points, and cut training time by more than 74%. The agent's full training completed faster than the baseline's evaluation phase alone, with peak performance at Stage 4 using the full basic strategy (Hit, Stand, Double, Split). The framework yields more structured, expert-like policies and demonstrates viability for discrete action spaces with separable complexities.

## Method Summary
The method employs a curriculum learning framework where an LLM generates a staged curriculum grouping Blackjack actions (Hit/Stand as level 1, Double/Split as level 2, Surrender/Insurance as level 3). At each stage, the environment masks all actions except the current subset, reducing the effective branching factor. Afterallocated episode budgets or earlier, a compact performance summary (win rate, bust rate, common errors) is fed to the LLM acting as a "coach," which decides whether the agent advances or continues training. Two RL agents—Tabular Q-learning and DQN—are trained with identical hyperparameters both with the LLM-guided curriculum and without (all actions available from start). The curriculum's adaptivity and action masking aim to accelerate learning by focusing on foundational decisions first, avoiding interference from complex, situational actions.

## Key Results
- DQN agent's average win rate increased from 43.97% (baseline) to 47.41% with the curriculum.
- Average bust rate decreased from 32.9% to 28.0%.
- Total training time reduced by over 74%; curriculum training completed faster than the baseline's evaluation phase alone.
- Peak performance occurred at Stage 4 with the full basic strategy (Hit, Stand, Double, Split).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Progressive Action Introduction
- **Claim:** Introducing actions progressively reduces initial exploration complexity, enabling the agent to master simpler strategic decisions before tackling compound tactical moves.
- **Mechanism:** The LLM groups actions by predefined complexity (e.g., Hit/Stand as level 1, Double/Split as level 2). The agent is trained in stages where only a subset of actions is available. This restricts the action space early on, reducing the effective branching factor of the MDP. The agent first learns a robust policy for the most frequent decisions (Hit/Stand) without the distraction of rarely applicable but high-impact actions like Split.
- **Core assumption:** Actions in Blackjack can be cleanly separated into pedagogical subsets where mastery of a simpler subset provides a beneficial prior for learning a more complex superset.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract]: "...progressively introduces actions based on complexity."; [section 3.1]: "The LLM receives structured prompts containing six available actions which are... manually defined complexity levels 1-3... Stand and Hit are considered as complexity level 1, Double Down and Split as level 2..."; [corpus]: Neighbors like "Multi-Agent Training for Pommerman" discuss curriculum learning for complex action spaces but do not provide direct comparative evidence for *this specific* complexity grouping.
- **Break condition:** The mechanism fails if the predefined complexity ordering is incorrect or if the separated action subsets are not meaningfully learnable in isolation (e.g., if "Double" fundamentally requires a prior "Split" policy).

### Mechanism 2: LLM-Driven Adaptive Coaching
- **Claim:** An LLM-driven, adaptive success threshold for each stage provides a more robust pacing mechanism than fixed episode budgets, preventing premature progression or stagnation.
- **Mechanism:** After completing a stage's allocated training episode budget (or earlier), a compact performance summary is fed back to the LLM. The LLM is prompted to act as a "coach" and decides if the agent has met the *spirit* of the stage's goal, potentially adjusting the win rate threshold or recommending focus areas. This moves beyond a simple binary "threshold met" check to a qualitative assessment of learning stability.
- **Core assumption:** An LLM can interpret compressed performance diagnostics and correlate them with underlying strategic competency well enough to judge readiness for more complex actions.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 3.2]: "The LLM is then prompted to act as a coach, deciding whether the agent is ready to advance or if it needs to continue training."; [section 3.2]: "This feedback mechanism allows the LLM to dynamically manage the agent’s progression."; [abstract]: "...with adaptive success thresholds driven by compact performance summaries."; [corpus]: Corpus evidence for this specific adaptive, LLM-as-coach loop is weak; neighboring papers use LLMs for curriculum *generation* but not for closed-loop, performance-summary-based adaptation.
- **Break condition:** The mechanism fails if the LLM's coaching decisions are inconsistent, biased, or misaligned with actual learning dynamics, potentially causing catastrophic forgetting when new actions are introduced.

### Mechanism 3: Environment-Enforced Action Masking
- **Claim:** Environment-enforced action masking during training, as dictated by the current curriculum stage, ensures clean credit assignment and prevents the agent from learning invalid or context-inappropriate associations for restricted actions.
- **Mechanism:** The Blackjack environment reads the current stage's allowed action list and masks (sets to invalid) any action not in that list. The agent's policy network is never queried for masked actions. This guarantees the agent cannot execute or receive reward signals for actions it is not yet meant to consider, forcing learning to converge on the permitted subset's optimal policy first.
- **Core assumption:** Isolating the action space is a sufficient condition for stable learning of a sub-strategy; the value of a permitted action in a given state is not fundamentally altered by the *future existence* of a restricted action.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 3.2]: "The curriculum is enforced by the environment, which masks any actions that are not part of the current stage."; [section 3.3]: "The environment also enforces all game rules... which prevents the agent from learning invalid policies and simplifies credit assignment."; [corpus]: The concept of environment-enforced curricula is common in RL but direct evidence for *action masking* specifically is not strong in the provided corpus.
- **Break condition:** The mechanism fails if the optimal policy for the full-action space requires early co-adaptation of all actions (e.g., if the value of "Hit" is conditional on eventual availability of "Split").

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Curriculum Learning**  
  - **Why needed here:** The core innovation applies curriculum learning to the *action space* of an RL problem, structuring learning from easy to hard actions to combat the curse of dimensionality.  
  - **Quick check question:** What is the primary hypothesized benefit of starting an RL agent on a simplified version of the task, and how does that relate to the "curse of dimensionality" mentioned in the introduction?

- **Concept: Q-Learning & Deep Q-Networks (DQN)**  
  - **Why needed here:** These are the specific RL algorithms used; understanding that Q-learning learns a state-action value function `Q(s,a)` is critical to see how restricting actions `a` during a curriculum stage directly shapes what is learned. The difference between tabular (explicit Q-table) and DQN (function approximation) explains the performance gap.  
  - **Quick check question:** In a tabular Q-learner, if the action "Split" is masked in early stages, what happens to the Q-values for state-action pairs `(s, Split)`? How might this differ in a DQN with a shared network?

- **Concept: Blackjack Basic Strategy**  
  - **Why needed here:** This is the expert, human-level benchmark (~42.4% win rate). Understanding the basic strategy chart (when to Hit, Stand, Double, Split) is necessary to interpret the strategy heatmaps and judge if the learned policy is "expert-like."  
  - **Quick check question:** According to basic strategy, what should you do with a pair of 8s against a dealer's upcard of 6? Why is this a non-trivial decision for an RL agent to learn?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
LLM Service (Gemini 2.0 Flash) -> Curriculum Orchestrator -> Blackjack Environment (with action masking) -> RL Agent (Tabular Q or DQN), with Data Loggers monitoring all components.

**Critical path:**  
1. Orchestrator prompts LLM for initial curriculum (stages with actions & thresholds).  
2. For Stage 1: Orchestrator tells Environment to mask all actions except `[Hit, Stand]`.  
3. Agent trains in masked environment for up to `stage_episode_budget` episodes.  
4. Orchestrator evaluates agent's win rate on a held-out set. If `win_rate >= threshold` or budget exhausted, it compiles a summary (`{win_rate, bust_rate, struggling_actions}`).  
5. Orchestrator sends summary to LLM with adaptation prompt. LLM responds with `advance_stage: true/false` and optional modifications.  
6. If advancing, Orchestrator increments stage index, loads new `available_actions`, and loops back to step 2. Else, continues training in current stage.  
7. After final stage (or max total episodes), training terminates.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **LLM Choice vs. Cost/Determinism:** Using a powerful LLM enables sophisticated coaching but introduces API cost, latency, and non-determinism; a fallback to a static curriculum trades adaptability for reproducibility.  
- **Monotonic vs. Permissive Curriculum:** The design enforces a strictly forward-moving curriculum; peak performance at Stage 4 suggests allowing regression or early termination might be better.  
- **Threshold Rigidity:** Success thresholds are LLM-generated but fixed per stage; adjusting based on *rate of improvement* could better handle plateaus.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **Stage Stall:** Agent repeatedly fails to meet a stage's threshold despite exhausting its budget.  
- **Performance Collapse on Advance:** Upon introducing a new action, win rate drops significantly and never recovers.  
- **LLM Output Malformation:** LLM returns invalid JSON, incorrect `stage_id`, or an action set violating environment rules.  
- **Masking Bug:** Environment fails to mask a restricted action, allowing the agent to "cheat" by using future-stage actions.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline Replication:** Train DQN and Tabular agents with *no curriculum* (all 6 actions always available) for 500k episodes in the 8-deck environment. Record final win rate, bust rate, and training time.  
2. **Curriculum Efficacy Run:** Execute the full LLM-guided curriculum (7 stages) for one DQN agent. Log: (a) win rate at the *end of each stage*, (b) the stage at which the agent's *all-time best win rate* occurs, and (c) total wall-clock time.  
3. **Strategy Validation via Heatmap:** After the agent completes Stage 3, generate a policy heatmap. Visually inspect if clear "Split" regions appear for strong candidate hands and if Hit/Stand boundaries are crisp; compare against baseline heatmap.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1:** Does the LLM-guided action curriculum generalize to other RL environments with discrete but non-separable action spaces, or is it limited to domains like Blackjack?  
  **Basis:** Authors state applicability to other domains is an open question; effectiveness may be limited in continuous action spaces or where actions are not easily isolated.  
  **Why unresolved:** Study evaluates only Blackjack, which has discrete action space with naturally separable complexity.  
  **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply the framework to at least two other distinct RL environments (e.g., robotic control, game with compound actions) and compare performance against standard training.

- **Question 2:** How does the choice of LLM affect the quality and effectiveness of the generated curriculum for RL agents?  
  **Basis:** Authors note reliance on Gemini-2.0-flash-001; different models may yield different curricula.  
  **Why unresolved:** Experiments use only one specific LLM; unclear whether others would produce similar ordering, difficulty progression, and thresholds.  
  **What evidence would resolve it:** Generate separate curricula using multiple LLMs (GPT-4, Claude, Llama), train agents with each, and compare win rates, bust rates, training time, and curriculum structure.

- **Question 3:** Can a non-monotonic curriculum (e.g., allowing regression to earlier stages) improve final outcomes compared to the strictly progressive curriculum?  
  **Basis:** Authors identify monotonic nature as a limitation; it does not determine if settling on a restricted action space indefinitely is better.  
  **Why unresolved:** Results show performance peaks at Stage 4 and declines when more situational actions are added; forced progression may harm final performance.  
  **What evidence would resolve it:** Design variants that either terminate at Stage 4 or allow LLM to recommend staying/returning to previous stages; compare final performance and stability.

- **Question 4:** How does incorporating betting strategies and bankroll management affect the benefits of the LLM-guided action curriculum in more realistic casino settings?  
  **Basis:** Authors state they do not model betting strategies or dynamic table limits, which influence expected value of actions like Insurance.  
  **Why unresolved:** Current reward structure evaluates per-hand outcomes without considering bet sizing, bankroll growth, or risk of ruin.  
  **What evidence would resolve it:** Extend environment to include bankroll, betting units, and optionally counting system; train agents with/without curriculum under these conditions and compare long-term growth rate, risk of ruin, and profitability.

- **Question 5:** Do common rule variations in Blackjack (e.g., dealer hits soft 17, 6:5 blackjack payout) alter the optimal curriculum design or the performance gains?  
  **Basis:** Authors mention using casino rules but note richer rule sets like "dealer hits soft 17" as future extensions.  
  **Why unresolved:** Study uses a specific rule set; variations change basic strategy and may affect relative complexity and importance of actions.  
  **What evidence would resolve it:** Run experiments with modified rules (e.g., dealer hits soft 17, 6:5 payout) and check if same curriculum still yields improvements, or if LLM adapts curriculum effectively.

## Limitations
- **LLM specifics unknown:** Exact Gemini 2.0 Flash API parameters (temperature, top_p) and potential model version drift post-experiment are unspecified, risking non-reproducible curriculum generation.
- **Performance summary underspecified:** Precise format, feature calculations, and "struggling actions" detection logic fed to the LLM coach are not detailed, making the adaptive loop a black box.
- **Monotonic curriculum assumption:** The framework enforces strictly forward progression; peak performance at Stage 4 with later declines suggests this rigidity may be suboptimal.

## Confidence
- **Primary outcome claims** (47.41% win rate, 74% time reduction): **Medium**  
  Results are statistically reported but depend on unverified LLM outputs and a specific, non-open-source model.
- **Mechanism 1** (Action separation): **Medium-High**  
  Masking concept is clearly described and aligns with curriculum theory, but predefined complexity ordering is not empirically validated as optimal.
- **Mechanism 2** (LLM coaching): **Low**  
  Core claim relies on unevaluated, proprietary LLM's qualitative judgment with minimal transparency on decision logic or consistency.
- **Mechanism 3** (Environment masking): **High**  
  Implementation is straightforward and described failure condition (improper masking) is a clear, testable bug.

## Next Checks
1. **LLM Coaching Audit:** For multiple independent runs, log every LLM coaching decision alongside exact performance summaries. Manually verify if the LLM's qualitative assessment is justified by quantitative metrics and observed policy volatility.
2. **Non-Monotonic Curriculum Test:** Implement a variant where the curriculum can regress to a prior stage if win rate drops significantly after an advancement. Compare final performance and stability to the original monotonic design.
3. **Ablate the Adaptive Summary:** Replace the LLM coach with a simple fixed threshold (e.g., 45% win rate for 10 consecutive evaluation batches) while keeping the same action curriculum. Isolate the contribution of the LLM's adaptive coaching from the mere benefit of staged action introduction.
