# Quantifying Normality: Convergence Rate to Gaussian Limit for Stochastic Approximation and Unadjusted OU Algorithm

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.13906
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13906
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Provides non-asymptotic Wasserstein‑1 error bounds for stochastic approximation under i.i.d. additive and multiplicative martingale noise, using a coupling with an Ornstein‑Uhlenbeck process and Stein’s method; rates depend on step‑size schedule and smoothness δ.

## Executive Summary
This paper establishes finite‑time Gaussian approximation guarantees for stochastic approximation (SA) algorithms. The core idea is to decompose the distributional error of the rescaled iterate into two parts: the Wasserstein distance between SA and a discrete Ornstein‑Uhlenbeck (DOUG) process, and the convergence of DOUG itself to its Gaussian limit. Through a careful coupling that shares additive noise and an adaptation of Stein’s method to matrix‑weighted sums, the authors derive explicit rates for constant, polynomially decaying, and linearly decaying step‑size schedules. The results also yield tail bounds on linear functionals of the iterates, quantifying how quickly the normalized process behaves like a Gaussian. This analysis advances the theoretical understanding of normality in SA and offers a template for non‑asymptotic distributional approximations.

## Method Summary
The proof architecture hinges on an auxiliary DOUG process that evolves with the same linearized drift as the SA iterates but uses only a simplified additive noise structure. A coupling is built where both SA and DOUG share the same i.i.d. additive noise stream, while the multiplicative martingale‑difference component of SA is treated separately. The error between the rescaled SA and DOUG satisfies a linear recursion whose expected squared norm contracts under a global quadratic Lyapunov function, yielding either exponential or polynomial decay. For DOUG itself, the authors adapt Stein’s method to handle matrix‑weighted sums of non‑Gaussian i.i.d. noise, overcoming the lack of symmetry in the drift matrix. The final Wasserstein‑1 distance is obtained by combining these two error components via the triangle inequality, resulting in sharp rates that expose the influence of the smoothness exponent δ and the step‑size parameter ξ.

## Key Results
- Constant step‑size (ξ = 0): total error = O(α^{δ/2}) + O(√α log(1/α)).
- Polynomial decay (0 < ξ < 1): error = O((k+K)^{-ξδ/2}) + O(log(k+K)/(k+K)^{ξ/2}).
- Linear decay (ξ = 1): error = O((k+K)^{-δ/2}) + O(log(k+K)/√(k+K)).
- DOUG converges to its Gaussian limit in O(√α_k) Wasserstein‑1 in all regimes.
- Tail bound: for a linear functional ⟨y_k,ζ⟩, deviation from Gaussian tail is O(1/((k+K)^{δ/4} a)).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1 – Error decomposition via coupling**  
The distributional error of the rescaled SA iterate $y_k$ is split into (a) the Wasserstein distance between $y_k$ and the DOUG process $\hat z_k$, and (b) the convergence of $\hat z_k$ to its Gaussian limit. The coupling shares the additive i.i.d. noise $b(w_k)$ across both processes, so that the difference $y_k-\hat z_k$ evolves according to a recursion driven only by the nonlinear remainder $R(x_k)$ and the multiplicative martingale difference $A(v_k,x_k)$. Under Assumption 3.1 (quadratic Lyapunov with negative drift) and Assumption 3.3 (linear growth of multiplicative noise), the squared norm of this error contracts exponentially (for constant step) or polynomially (for decaying step). This yields a tight bound on the first component.

**Mechanism 2 – Stein’s method for matrix‑weighted sums**  
For the DOUG process, which is a linear recursion with additive noise, one can express the iterate as a matrix‑weighted sum of the i.i.d. $b(w_i)$. The authors adapt Stein’s method: the Gaussian Stein operator $L f(x) = \langle J x, \nabla f(x) \rangle + \frac12 \operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma \nabla^2 f(x))$ is applied to test functions $f$. Bounding $\mathbb{E}[L f(\hat z_k)]$ requires controlling the residual between the finite Lyapunov sum and its infinite limit, and bounding derivatives of the Stein solution. Under a finite third moment of $b$ and Hurwitz $J$, the Wasserstein‑1 distance turns out to be $O(\sqrt{\alpha_k})$ regardless of the step‑size regime (Theorems A.1 and 4.2). This rate is optimal for Stein‑based CLTs and matches known results for martingale central limit theorems.

**Mechanism 3 – Handling multiplicative martingale differences**  
Because the multiplicative noise $A(v_k,x_k)$ is a martingale difference with zero conditional mean and second moment bounded by $c\|x_k-x^*\|^2$, it vanishes in expectation when coupled out. The squared error recursion contains a cross‑term that cancels exactly due to the martingale property, and the remaining quadratic term is controlled using the mean‑square bound $\mathbb{E}\|x_k-x^*\|^2 = O(\alpha_k)$ from Lemma B.1. This yields recurrence relations solved in Lemma F.1, producing polynomial or exponential decay of the coupling error depending on the step‑size schedule.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**  
DOUG process analysis (Stein + matrix‑weighted sum bounds) → Multiplicative noise coupling (error recursion and mean‑square bounds) → SA‑DOUG coupling (nonlinear remainder and step‑size artifacts) → Synthesis (triangle inequality in Wasserstein‑1).

**Critical path**  
1. Prove Theorem A.1 for DOUG convergence using Stein’s method and Lemma A.1.  
2. Extend to Lemma B.2: incorporate multiplicative martingale differences via coupling.  
3. Decompose $d_W(y_k,\Sigma_n^{1/2}Z)$ into $d_W(y_k,\hat z_k)+d_W(\hat z_k,z_k)+d_W(z_k,\Sigma_n^{1/2}Z)$.  
4. Combine the bounds from steps 2–3, optimize constants across the step‑size regimes, and prove Theorem 4.1.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Using a general Lyapunov norm $\|\cdot\|_V$ (induced by $V$ from the Lyapunov equation) yields slightly smaller constants than $\|\cdot\|_2$ when $J_F$ is asymmetric, but complicates calculations.  
- The analysis chooses Wasserstein‑1 because it directly gives first‑moment and tail bounds; higher‑order Wasserstein distances would require stronger moment assumptions and more complex couplings.  
- Step‑size schedules are restricted to $\alpha/(k+K)^\xi$ for tractability of the recurrence; more general schedules would need a different approach.

**Failure signatures**  
- If the smoothness exponent $\delta\notin(0,1]$ (Assumption 3.2), the remainder $R(x)$ cannot be bounded as $R_1\|x-x^*\|^{1+\delta}$ and the dominant term in Theorem 4.1 changes.  
- If the Lyapunov‑drift constant $\iota_V$ is too small relative to the growth of the multiplicative noise, the transient decay may be dominated, and the claimed rates may not hold.  
- Violation of the finite third‑moment condition ($B_3<\infty$) breaks the Stein derivative bounds (Lemma 5.2), invalidating the $O(\sqrt{\alpha_k})$ rate.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. *Scalar linear SA*: Implement $d=1$, $F(x)=-\lambda(x-x^*)$ with $\lambda=1$, $x^*=0$, and $M_k=b_k\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$. Simulate SA and DOUG with identical noise streams, compute Wasserstein‑1 between rescaled $y_k$ and $N(0,\Sigma_n)$, and verify the theoretical scaling (constant after transient for ξ=0; $W_1\propto (k+K)^{-\xi/2}/\log$ for ξ∈(0,1); $W_1\propto (k+K)^{-1/2}/\log$ for ξ=1).  
2. *Nonlinear δ test*: Use $F(x)=-x^{1+\delta}$ with δ∈(0,1). Check that $W_1$ scales like $(k+K)^{-\xi\delta/2}$; when δ=1, observe the transition to $O(\log(k+K)/(k+K)^{\xi/2})$.  
3. *Multiplicative robustness*: Add a martingale $A_k$ with $\mathbb{E}[A_k|\mathcal F_{k-1}]=0$ and $\mathbb{E}[A_k^2|\mathcal F_{k-1}] \le c\|x_k-x^*\|^2$. Vary $c$ and confirm that the total error stays within the predicted bound. Then break the linear growth (e.g., variance $\propto\|x_k-x^*\|^3$) and observe failure of the bound.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Handling Markov noise** – Current analysis assumes i.i.d. or martingale difference noise; extending to Markov‑dependent data (common in RL and SGD) would require new techniques (e.g., mixing‑time or coupling arguments) for the error recursion.
2. **Wasserstein‑p for p > 1** – Stein’s method currently yields Wasserstein‑1 rates; achieving higher‑p refinement needs stronger moment bounds and possibly higher‑order Stein equations.
3. **Improved tail bounds under sub‑Gaussian noise** – Present tail bounds decay as $O(1/a)$, but sub‑Gaussian noise should yield sub‑Gaussian concentration in the finite‑time regime; this likely requires Wasserstein‑p bounds or direct concentration inequalities.
4. **Relaxing global linearity of F** – The proof uses a global quadratic Lyapunov function and global linear approximation (Jacobian) of $F$; local linearity near the optimum might suffice, but handling the global nonlinearity remains open.
5. **Optimal rate for DOUG** – DOUG currently converges in $O(\sqrt{\alpha_k})$ Wasserstein‑1; improving this to $O(\alpha_k)$ (matching the continuous‑time OU or ULA with Gaussian noise) would tighten the overall bound and might be achievable with more refined analysis.

## Limitations
- **Strong structural assumptions**: global quadratic Lyapunov function, δ‑smoothness of the Jacobian, finite third‑moment of additive noise, and specific step‑size schedules.
- **Linear decay regime**: requires $\alpha > 2/\iota_V$, which can be quite large and may affect practical stability.
- **Tail behavior**: The derived tail bounds only achieve an algebraic $O(1/a)$ remainder, falling short of sub‑Gaussian decay even when the noise itself is sub‑Gaussian.
