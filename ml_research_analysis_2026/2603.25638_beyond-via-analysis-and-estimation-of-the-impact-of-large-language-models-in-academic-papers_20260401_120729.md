# Beyond Via: Analysis and Estimation of the Impact of Large Language Models in Academic Papers

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.25638
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25638
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** LLM adoption in academia leaves measurable word-frequency shifts with distinct model-specific fingerprints and convergent stylistic changes; the proposed constrained optimization recovers time‑varying LLM contributions.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how large language models (LLMs) influence academic writing by analyzing arXiv abstracts and titles. The authors simulate outputs of nine LLMs on pre‑2023 papers, fit linear trends to word frequencies from the pre‑LLM era (2015‑2021) to predict a counterfactual evolution, and compare observed frequencies to these predictions. A constrained optimization estimates the time‑varying proportion of each LLM’s style in the corpus. Classification experiments reveal that while distinguishing human from LLM text is easy (~90% binary accuracy), identifying the specific LLM source is much harder (~60% for seven classes), indicating stylistic convergence. The study uncovers model‑specific word preferences (e.g., increased use of “via” and “beyond” in titles, decreased function words in abstracts) and dynamics such as a peak in GPT‑3.5‑like usage around 2024. ROUGE scores rise with model releases but BERTScore does not, suggesting stylistic alignment without deeper semantic convergence.

## Method Summary
The authors processed the arXiv dataset (2.9 M papers) to extract abstracts and titles, tokenizing content and computing word frequencies per 10 000 tokens. They selected 2 000 pre‑2023 abstracts and generated rewritten versions using nine LLMs under two prompt conditions (short/long). For each word, a linear regression was fitted on data from 2015‑2021 (pre‑LLM period) to predict its frequency in later years. The ratio of observed to predicted frequency captures deviation from the baseline. Assuming LLM influence is a mixture of model‑specific biases (the ratio of simulated to baseline frequencies) weighted by time‑varying proportions, the authors solved a constrained linear system (Equation 6) with non‑negativity and sum‑to‑one constraints using SLSQP to estimate these proportions. Additionally, they fine‑tuned neural classifiers (BERT, GPT‑2, T5, LLM2Vec) on human and LLM‑generated text for binary (human vs. LLM) and multi‑class (human + six LLM families) detection tasks, and computed ROUGE and BERTScore between human and simulated abstracts.

## Key Results
- Distinct word usage shifts emerge: titles show rising frequency of “via” and “beyond” from 2025 onward; abstracts exhibit declining use of function words like “the” and “of”.
- Binary classifiers achieve ~90% accuracy in separating human from LLM text, but multi‑class accuracy drops sharply to ~60%, highlighting convergent styles across LLMs and increased difficulty of source attribution.
- The estimation method captures heterogeneous and dynamic LLM usage: the proportion of GPT‑3.5‑like style peaks around 2024 then declines; newer models tend to avoid words like “delve” while most prefer “furthermore”.
- ROUGE scores improve with newer model releases, but BERTScore remains stagnant, indicating stylistic similarity without deeper semantic alignment.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** LLM adoption introduces measurable, model‑specific shifts in word frequency that deviate from pre‑existing linear trends.
- **Mechanism:** For each word *w*, fit a linear model `f_w(t) = a_w + b_w*t` using only pre‑LLM data (e.g., ≤2021). This `f_pred_w(t)` estimates the counterfactual frequency absent LLM influence. The observed‑to‑predicted ratio `r_w(t)` captures deviation. LLM impact is modeled as a mixture of model‑specific biases `(f^m_w / f^0_w)` weighted by time‑varying proportions `η_m(t)`.
- **Core assumption:** Word frequency trends in academic writing are approximately linear in the absence of LLMs and can be extrapolated. Model‑specific word biases are stable and represent “fingerprints.”
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] Reports shifts like increased “via”/“beyond” in titles and decreased “the”/“of” in abstracts.
  - [section 3.1] Explicitly defines the linear baseline fitting period (2015–2021) and prediction for T₂.
  - [corpus] Neighbors (e.g., arXiv:2406.07016, arXiv:2404.08627) use similar word frequency deviation methodologies. *Evidence for fingerprint stability is weaker in the corpus.*
- **Break condition:** If baseline linguistic trends become nonlinear or exhibit structural breaks post‑2022, the linear extrapolation becomes invalid, contaminating estimates.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Current neural classifiers can distinguish human vs. LLM text in binary tasks (~90% accuracy) but fail to identify the specific LLM source in multi‑class settings (~60% for 7 classes), due to convergent stylistic outputs.
- **Mechanism:** Fine‑tune architectures (BERT, GPT‑2, T5, LLM2Vec) on labeled datasets of human‑written text and text generated by specific LLMs (with different prompts). Performance collapse from binary to multi‑class indicates that inter‑model variance is smaller than human‑model variance, and that model outputs have homogenized.
- **Core assumption:** Linguistic fingerprints exist between models but are subtle and erode over time/model iterations. Prompts and human post‑editing add noise that masks source identification.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] States high binary accuracy (~90%) but sharp drop in multi‑class (~60% for 7 classes).
  - [section 5.5] Provides detailed confusion matrices (Figures 7, 14‑19) showing misclassification patterns, especially between newer model families.
  - [corpus] Weak. Neighbors focus on detection feasibility (e.g., arXiv:2501.15654 on human detection ability) but not multi‑class collapse specifically.
- **Break condition:** If a new model family emerges with radically distinct stylistic signatures, multi‑class accuracy could temporarily improve.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The proposed constrained optimization on word frequencies can recover time‑varying, heterogeneous proportions of different LLM styles in real corpora, capturing dynamics like the rise and fall of GPT‑3.5‑like usage.
- **Mechanism:** Solve for `η_m,p(t)` in Equation 6, minimizing squared error between observed ratio `r_w(t)` and the mixture model `Σ η_m,p(t) * (f^m,p_w / f^0_w)`, subject to mixing proportions summing to 1 and bounds. This estimates the proportional contribution of each (model, prompt) pair to the corpus at time `t`.
- **Core assumption:** The set of words `W` is sufficient to span the stylistic differences between human text and each LLM’s output. Word frequency is the primary vector of LLM influence and additive in mixture.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] Claims the method captures heterogeneous/dynamic usage, e.g., GPT‑3.5‑like proportion peaking around 2024 then falling.
  - [section 3.2] Formulates the optimization problem with constraints and discusses parameter choices (e.g., bounds in Equation 11).
  - [section 5.6] Shows estimates (Figure 8) with plausible temporal dynamics (near‑zero pre‑2022, a peak, fluctuations).
  - [corpus] arXiv:2512.01560 (Gray) and arXiv:2504.12317 (Lin et al.) use word‑based estimation, supporting the approach’s validity.
- **Break condition:** If LLMs begin to internally re‑weight their token predictions to match observed human distributions for key indicator words, then the assumed constant biases become time‑varying and unmodeled, inducing systematic error.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Linear Trend Extrapolation & Residual Analysis**  
  - **Why needed here:** The entire estimation framework rests on establishing a credible “no‑LLM” baseline from pre‑2022 data to interpret post‑2022 deviations.  
  - **Quick check question:** Given a plot of word frequency vs. time with a linear fit on 2015‑2021 data, what would a residual of +0.5 in 2025 suggest about LLM influence for that word?

- **Concept: Constrained Linear Mixture Modeling**  
  - **Why needed here:** The core estimation (Equation 6) is a linear system with non‑negativity and sum‑to‑one constraints, analogous to a blind source separation problem.  
  - **Quick check question:** If for a single word `w`, `r_w(t)=1.05`, `f^m_w/f^0_w=1.2` for one model and `1.0` for human, what are the possible `(η_human, η_model)` pairs satisfying the mixture equation? How does the constraint `η_human + η_model = 1` narrow it?

- **Concept: Classification Performance Collapse as a Signal of Convergence**  
  - **Why needed here:** The drop from binary to multi‑class accuracy is the key empirical evidence that LLM outputs are becoming stylistically similar to each other and to human text in ways that defeat fine‑grained source attribution.  
  - **Quick check question:** If a 7‑class classifier has 60% accuracy but a binary classifier (LLM vs. Human) has 90% accuracy, what does this imply about the relative distances in the feature space between: (a) human and any LLM cluster, (b) between different LLM clusters?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Data Ingestion -> Simulation Engine -> (Baseline Trend Fitter, Similarity Scorer, Classifier Trainer) -> Impact Estimator -> Visualization
- **Critical path:** Data Prep → Simulation → (Parallel: Trend Fitting, Similarity, Classification) → Estimator → Synthesis.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Word set selection:* Top‑N Google Ngram words (2741) vs. stopwords (126) trades signal richness for stability; stopwords show larger fluctuations due to low model differentiation.  
  - *Linearity:* Assumes pre‑LLM trends are linear; a more flexible baseline (e.g., spline) could fit better but sacrifices interpretability and risks overfitting.  
  - *Prompt uniformity:* Using only two prompts contrasts with the vast diversity of real LLM use; estimates capture a “central tendency” of LLM assistance.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - **Trend fit failure:** Pre‑2022 residuals show clear nonlinear patterns (e.g., quadratic), invalidating linear extrapolation.  
  - **Estimation instability:** `η_m(t)` estimates swing wildly between adjacent time bins or produce infeasible values (e.g., >1) despite constraints, indicating insufficient word set or poor model biases.  
  - **Classifier ceiling:** Binary accuracy plateaus below 80% even for old models, suggesting poor simulation or already minimal stylistic differences.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Validate Baseline:** Fit linear trends for 100 high‑frequency words on 2015‑2021 data; compute 2022 prediction error (should be small). Large errors prompt re‑examination of data filtering or fitting period.  
  2. **Measure Model Divergence:** For the 2000 simulated abstracts, compute pairwise ROUGE‑1 and BERTScore between all (model, prompt) outputs and real abstracts; reproduce Figure 6’s trend of increasing ROUGE but stagnant BERTScore over release dates.  
  3. **Reproduce Classification Collapse:** Train a BERT classifier on the 7‑class task (human + 6 LLM groups) with the long prompt; confirm ~60% accuracy and examine the confusion matrix. Then run the binary task (human vs. all‑LLM) and confirm >85% accuracy.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Evolution over time:** How has the impact of LLMs on academic writing evolved, especially with newer model releases? The study’s linear baseline and coverage only up to early 2026 leave non‑linear dynamics and future impacts unclear. Resolution would require continuous tracking with flexible time‑series models and inclusion of post‑2025 LLMs.
2. **Geographic/demographic variation:** How does LLM impact vary across regions or author demographics? The analysis aggregates all arXiv papers without author affiliation data. Disaggregating by location/country and comparing regional trends in LLM‑associated word usage would provide evidence.
3. **Substantive content changes:** To what extent do LLMs alter facts, claims, and references versus style? The work focuses solely on word frequencies, ignoring factual integrity. Manual or automated auditing of LLM‑processed texts for consistency, distortion, and reference validity is needed.
4. **Hallucination prevalence:** How prevalent are hallucinations (e.g., fabricated references) in LLM‑assisted academic writing? The paper cautions but offers no data. Large‑scale detection of non‑existent or incorrect citations via verification tools would quantify the risk.

## Limitations
- **Linear baseline validity:** Assumes pre‑LLM word trends are linear and extrapolable; structural breaks from non‑LLM societal or disciplinary shifts could bias estimates.
- **Constant model biases:** Treats model‑specific frequency ratios as fixed over time; if LLMs adapt to evade detection (e.g., suppressing “fingerprint” words), estimated `η_m(t)` becomes misleading.
- **Word‑frequency sufficiency:** Relies on a selected vocabulary to capture all stylistic divergence; key signals in syntax, named entities, or longer‑range context may be missed.

## Confidence
- **Linear trend deviation & fingerprint detection:** *Medium* – strong in‑paper validation and alignment with neighboring work, but linear extrapolation assumption is not stress‑tested against alternative baselines.
- **Classifier multi‑class collapse:** *Medium* – robust in‑paper results demonstrate the effect, but external corroboration is weak; neighbors focus on binary detection.
- **Time‑varying mixture estimation:** *Low* – plausible temporal dynamics are shown, but identifiability depends entirely on word set and optimizer settings; no ground‑truth `η_m(t)` exists for validation.

## Next Checks
1. **Test baseline nonlinearity:** Re‑fit pre‑2022 trends using splines or piecewise regressions for a subset of words; compare 2022‑2023 prediction errors against the linear model. Significant improvement would invalidate the core extrapolation assumption.
2. **Probe classifier confusion on newest models:** Train the 7‑class detector including very latest LLMs (e.g., GPT‑4.5, Claude 4); a persistent ~60% accuracy with confusion concentrated among recent models would confirm ongoing convergence.
3. **Verify bias stability:** Simulate outputs from the same LLMs on *post‑2023* human abstracts and recompute `f^m_w / f^0_w`; substantial changes vs. the original biases would indicate temporal drift in model fingerprints.
