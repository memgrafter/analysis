# Governing AI Forgetting: Auditing for Machine Unlearning Compliance

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.14553
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14553
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** First economic auditing framework for machine unlearning compliance, revealing audit/transparency paradoxes and achieving up to 2549.30% payoff increase with disclosed auditing.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the enforcement gap in machine unlearning (MU) compliance under right‑to‑be‑forgotten regulations. It proposes an economic auditing framework that merges certified unlearning theory with regulatory game theory, capturing the strategic interplay between AI operators and auditors. The authors resolve MU‑specific nonlinearities by converting a bivariate fixed‑point problem into a univariate one, proving equilibrium existence and uniqueness. Their analysis uncovers two paradoxes: (1) an audit paradox where, under high fines, auditors optimally lower inspection intensity as deletion requests rise because unlearning weakens and violations become easier to spot; and (2) a transparency paradox where disclosing audit policies improves cost‑effectiveness despite the auditor’s informational disadvantage. Real‑data experiments confirm the theory, showing disclosed auditing boosts auditor payoff by up to 2549.30% and operator payoff by up to 74.60% over the state‑of‑the‑art baseline.

## Method Summary
The paper does not include dedicated reproduction notes. Its theoretical core transforms a complex bivariate fixed‑point problem into a tractable univariate formulation, enabling equilibrium analysis in a regulatory game. The method integrates certified unlearning guarantees with a game‑theoretic model of audit and unlearning decisions.

## Key Results
- **Audit paradox:** With sufficiently high non‑compliance fines, the auditor’s optimal inspection intensity decreases as the volume of deletion requests increases, because operator unlearning effort wanes, making violations more detectable.
- **Transparency paradox:** Disclosed auditing (where inspection policies are announced) yields higher regulatory cost‑effectiveness than undisclosed auditing, even though the auditor forfeits informational advantage.
- **Empirical validation:** Tests on real data reveal that disclosed auditing increases the auditor’s payoff by up to 2549.30% and the operator’s payoff by up to 74.60% compared to the state‑of‑the‑art benchmark.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The mechanism hinges on the functional form of the detection probability, which depends jointly on the operator’s unlearning effort \(e\) and the auditor’s inspection intensity \(\lambda\). By mathematically transforming the bivariate equilibrium conditions into a univariate fixed‑point problem, the authors obtain closed‑form expressions for best‑response functions and prove existence and uniqueness of Nash equilibrium. This transformation resolves the MU‑specific nonlinearities that previously made the analysis intractable. The equilibrium strategies then generate the paradoxical outcomes: as deletion requests increase, the operator’s marginal benefit from unlearning falls, reducing effort; with high fines, the auditor exploits the resulting higher detection likelihood by cutting inspection intensity to save costs. Disclosed auditing alters the timing of information, leading to a more efficient allocation of audit resources despite reduced asymmetry.

## Foundational Learning
The framework rests on several key concepts:

- **Machine Unlearning (MU):** Techniques for removing data influence from trained models. *Why needed:* To fulfill legal “right to be forgotten” obligations. *Quick check:* Verify that the unlearned model is indistinguishable from one trained without the deleted data (e.g., via statistical tests or certificates).
- **Regulatory Game Theory:** Models strategic interactions between a regulator (auditor) and a regulated entity (operator). *Why needed:* To predict compliance behavior under different audit regimes. *Quick check:* Identify players, strategy spaces, payoffs, and equilibrium concept (e.g., Nash).
- **Certified Unlearning:** Formal guarantees (e.g., cryptographic proofs) that deletion has occurred. *Why needed:* Enables auditors to verify compliance without re‑training from scratch. *Quick check:* Ensure certificates are efficiently verifiable and binding.
- **Detection Probability:** The likelihood that an audit discovers non‑compliance, typically a function \(p(e,\lambda)\). *Why needed:* Central to the auditor’s expected payoff optimization. *Quick check:* Model \(p\) as increasing in \(\lambda\) and decreasing in \(e\), respecting bounds [0,1].
- **Disclosed vs. Undisclosed Auditing:** Whether the auditor announces the inspection policy in advance. *Why needed:* Transparency affects operator incentives and audit efficiency. *Quick check:* Compare equilibrium outcomes under both regimes to assess welfare effects.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Operator -> e (unlearning effort) -> p(e,λ) <- λ (inspection intensity) <- Auditor -> Payoffs -> Nash Equilibrium.

**Critical path:** 
1. Define detection probability function \(p(e,\lambda)\).  
2. Formulate auditor’s expected payoff \(U_A(\lambda; e)\) incorporating audit cost and fine revenue.  
3. Optimize \(U_A\) to derive auditor’s best‑response \(\lambda^*(e)\).  
4. Derive operator’s best‑response \(e^*(\lambda)\) from its payoff function.  
5. Solve the bivariate fixed‑point \((\lambda^*, e^*)\) via the univariate transformation to obtain equilibrium.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Audit intensity vs. cost:** Higher \(\lambda\) improves detection but raises direct audit expenses.  
- **Fine magnitude vs. operator welfare:** Larger fines deter non‑compliance but may force excessive unlearning costs, reducing operator profit.  
- **Transparency:** Disclosed auditing reduces information asymmetry but may allow operators to precisely minimize effort, affecting detection probabilities.

**Failure signatures:**  
- No equilibrium exists if the detection probability function is too irregular (e.g., non‑monotonic) or if the auditor’s budget constraint is binding.  
- The model becomes intractable if the transformation to a univariate problem fails (e.g., due to parameter mis‑specification).  
- Insufficient fines lead to a corner solution where the operator never complies, rendering audits pointless.

**First experiments:**
1. Implement the theoretical equilibrium solver and simulate deletion request distributions (e.g., Poisson, heavy‑tailed) to observe how equilibrium \(\lambda^*, e^*\) respond.  
2. Run a head‑to‑head comparison of disclosed versus undisclosed auditing across a range of fine levels, measuring auditor and operator payoffs.  
3. Vary the shape of the detection probability function (e.g., linear vs. sigmoid) to stress‑test the robustness of the audit paradox.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can auditors adaptively learn unknown regulatory parameters (e.g., model characteristics G and deletion volume distributions) through repeated interactions with AI operators to dynamically optimize inspection strategies?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors explicitly state in the Conclusion (Section VIII) that "Future research could explore how auditors adaptively learn regulatory parameters (e.g., model characteristics and deletion volume distributions) through repeated interactions with the AI operator to optimize the inspection strategies."
- **Why unresolved:** The current framework assumes complete information about G and η and models a one-shot game, ignoring potential learning dynamics and parameter uncertainty that auditors face in practice.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Development of a repeated-game model with parameter estimation (e.g., using reinforcement learning); empirical analysis of auditors' historical data to identify learning patterns; simulation studies comparing static vs. adaptive audit policies over time.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How can the auditing framework be extended to account for heterogeneity among AI operators (e.g., varying model architectures, compliance costs) and incorporate more flexible detection rules (e.g., probabilistic compliance thresholds) and penalty schemes beyond the current zero‑tolerance and fine‑plus‑corrective‑unlearning structure?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors mention in the Conclusion that "Another promising direction is to develop richer auditing frameworks that accommodate heterogeneity among AI operators and incorporate more flexible detection rules and penalty schemes."
- **Why unresolved:** The model assumes a homogeneous operator population and a rigid zero‑tolerance compliance policy, which oversimplifies real‑world diversity in AI systems and regulatory practices.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Formulating a heterogeneous‑agent game with type‑dependent utilities; analyzing equilibria under alternative detection rules (e.g., allowing a small fraction of failed inspections); comparative case studies of different regulatory regimes (e.g., EU vs. China) to validate extended models.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How can practical MU verification techniques be implemented and empirically validated in large‑scale AI systems (e.g., deep neural networks with millions of parameters) to ensure the proposed economic auditing framework's applicability and reliability in real‑world settings?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The Conclusion states: "Finally, we plan to pursue empirical validations by implementing practical MU verification procedures in large‑scale AI systems, further strengthening the applicability and insights of our framework."
- **Why unresolved:** Experimental validation used a small logistic regression model on MNIST; scaling verification to large models introduces computational complexity and may violate theoretical assumptions (e.g., the detection probability bound).
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Empirical measurements of MU verification accuracy (e.g., via backdoor or reproduction methods) on large models like transformers; calibration of detection probability bounds with real verification tools; pilot implementations in regulatory sandboxes to assess feasibility.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How do incomplete information and imperfect verification (e.g., correlated inspection errors, bounded detection capabilities beyond the idealized lower bound) affect the equilibrium audit strategies and welfare outcomes compared to the complete‑information, independent‑inspections assumptions?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The model assumes complete information about G and η (Section III‑D) and independent inspections with ideal detection (Section III‑C2). These simplifications are noted in footnotes (e.g., Footnote 6 on correlated inspections) but not formally relaxed.
- **Why unresolved:** Relaxing these assumptions introduces information asymmetry and correlated signals, which could undermine key results like the unlearning audit paradox and the transparency paradox by altering strategic incentives.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Development of Bayesian game models with asymmetric information; analytical or numerical studies of correlated inspection structures; computational experiments comparing baseline outcomes with those under realistic noise and partial observability.

## Limitations
- The framework assumes static operator capabilities and perfectly rational actors, which may not hold in fast‑evolving ML environments or with bounded rationality.
- Empirical validation used a simplistic logistic regression on MNIST; scalability to large models and diverse data domains remains unproven.
- The analysis relies on specific functional forms for detection probability and fines; sensitivity to these parameters is not thoroughly explored, limiting policy robustness.

## Confidence
- **High:** Mathematical transformation of the bivariate fixed‑point problem and formal equilibrium proofs under the stated assumptions.
- **Medium:** The audit paradox (inspection intensity decreases with more deletion requests) depends on the fine magnitude and the shape of the detection probability function.
- **Low:** The superiority of disclosed auditing in practice, given institutional, legal, and implementation barriers not captured by the model.
