# mAceReason-Math: A Dataset of High-Quality Multilingual Math Problems Ready For RLVR

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.10767  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10767  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** mAceReason-Math provides over 10,000 high-quality multilingual math problems per language, a fully parallel train split of 7,620 samples per language, and a human-validated test set of 190 samples, enabling rigorous RLVR research.

## Executive Summary
mAceReason-Math tackles the shortage of large-scale, high-quality multilingual math datasets for Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR). Existing resources are often English-centric and too easy for modern models. The authors address this by cleaning the AceReason-Math corpus—removing ~4% of samples with critical issues and fixing ~11% with salvageable artifacts—and then translating the remaining problems into 14 languages using an iterative LLM-based pipeline with native speaker verification. The result is mAceReason-Math, a dataset with over 10,000 samples per language, a fully parallel train split of 7,620 samples per language, and a human-validated test set of 190 samples.

Evaluation of various models on the test set shows that performance scales with model size and that advanced reasoning models, particularly Qwen3, achieve strong cross-lingual results (averaging 88.6% accuracy across all 14 languages). This suggests that mathematical reasoning skills transfer effectively across languages at the instance level, validating the dataset's design for multilingual RLVR experiments.

## Method Summary
The dataset construction begins with the AceReason-Math English corpus. A hybrid cleaning stage applies rule-based filters (e.g., removing samples with unresolved links or image placeholders) and an LLM classifier to identify and remove ~4% of samples with critical corruption (e.g., solution mentioned in the question) and repair ~11% with salvageable artifacts (e.g., formatting errors). After cleaning, each problem is translated into 14 target languages (Chinese, German, Spanish, French, Italian, Portuguese, Russian, Japanese, Korean, Thai, Bengali, Telugu, Swahili) via an iterative LLM pipeline. An initial translation by Claude Sonnet 4 is graded by an LLM against criteria including mathematical accuracy, LaTeX consistency, and natural fluency. If issues are detected, a refinement prompt generates an improved version; this loop repeats up to five times until acceptance. The pipeline yields a parallel train set by intersecting translations available in all languages (7,620 samples per language) and independent full train sets exceeding 10,000 samples per language. A final test set of 190 samples per language undergoes native speaker validation (except for Swahili, Telugu, Bengali) to ensure quality. The paper uses an automatic answer verification system based on math-parse for evaluation.

## Key Results
- **Large-scale parallel dataset:** Over 10,000 problems per language, with a parallel train split of 7,620 samples per language and a human‑validated test set of 190 samples (Table 3).  
- **Strong cross‑lingual performance:** Qwen3 reasoning models average 88.6% accuracy across all 14 languages, with a small standard deviation (±5.2%), indicating effective transfer.  
- **Positive scaling and diversity:** Performance improves with model scale and varies across languages, demonstrating the dataset’s suitability for multilingual RLVR.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Filtering and Cleaning Secure Problem Quality
**Claim:** Removing critical and salvageable issues from AceReason‑Math ensures only high‑quality, solvable problems enter the translation pipeline, preventing corrupted data from propagating.  
**Mechanism:** A hybrid rule‑based (regex for links, image placeholders) and LLM‑based (prompted classification) process discards ~4% of samples with critical corruption and cleans ~11% with fixable artifacts.  
**Core assumption:** RLVR depends on unambiguous problems; low‑quality or unsolvable problems yield noisy or invalid reward signals, harming policy learning.  
**Evidence:** Section 3.1 and Table 4 detail the criteria and provide examples.

### Iterative LLM Translation with Grading and Refinement
**Claim:** The iterative LLM‑based translation pipeline produces faithful, natural translations that preserve mathematical notation and linguistic fluency.  
**Mechanism:** An initial translation by Claude Sonnet 4 is graded by an LLM against accuracy, LaTeX consistency, and naturalness. If issues are found, a refinement prompt generates an improved version, repeating up to 5 times. Most samples accept after 0‑1 iterations (Figure 1).  
**Core assumption:** Single‑pass translation can introduce errors in math notation or phrasing; iterative critique and correction align translations with strict conventions.  
**Evidence:** Section 3.2 describes the process; Appendix A provides prompts A.2, A.3, A.4; Figure 1 shows iteration distribution.

### Consistent High Performance Validates Cross‑Lingual Transfer
**Claim:** Strong cross‑lingual performance by advanced reasoning models (e.g., Qwen3 averaging 88.6% across 14 languages) indicates that challenging math problem‑solving capability transfers across languages at the instance level, confirming the dataset’s validity for multilingual RLVR.  
**Mechanism:** Models trained with RLVR on English data maintain high accuracy on translated problems, suggesting the reasoning skill is language‑agnostic and translations are semantically equivalent.  
**Core assumption:** If mathematical reasoning were highly language‑dependent, performance would correlate with linguistic similarity to English or drop significantly; the observed consistency suggests otherwise.  
**Evidence:** Section 4 and Table 2 show Qwen3 achieving >82% on all languages with small std (±5.2%); the authors interpret this as evidence of strong cross‑lingual transferability.

## Foundational Learning
- **Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)**  
  *Why needed:* RLVR uses programmatic reward functions (e.g., answer verification) instead of human feedback, making it scalable but requiring problems with unambiguous, automatically checkable solutions. mAceReason‑Math provides a large set of such problems.  
  *Quick check:* Why must RLVR problems have automatically verifiable answers, and how does the "goldilocks zone" of difficulty affect the learning signal?

- **Multilingual Data Curation Challenges**  
  *Why needed:* Existing multilingual math datasets (e.g., MGSM, mMMLU) are English‑centric, often too easy for modern models, and lack parallel structure, limiting their utility for RLVR with current large models.  
  *Quick check:* What specific limitations of MGSM (Shi et al., 2022) make it unsuitable for training state‑of‑the‑art models with RLVR?

- **Mathematical Reasoning Evaluation Protocol**  
  *Why needed:* Evaluating math problem solving across languages demands robust answer extraction and verification that can handle formatting variations (e.g., number separators, LaTeX). The dataset uses math‑parse to ensure consistent scoring.  
  *Quick check:* How does the evaluation system normalize different number formats (e.g., “12,5” vs “12.5”) and LaTeX expressions to avoid penalizing correct but differently formatted answers?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Source Corpus Loader → Cleaning & Filtering Module → Translation Orchestrator → Translation Module → Grading Module → Refinement Module → Validation & Splitting. The Verification Utility is applied during model evaluation (Appendix D.1).

**Critical path:**  
Raw AceReason‑Math data are cleaned; the clean English set enters a per‑language pipeline where each problem is iteratively translated, graded, and refined. After translation, the parallel train set is constructed by intersecting translations available in all 14 languages (7,620 samples per language). The remaining translations form full per‑language train sets. A human‑validated test set (190 samples per language, except Swahili/Telugu/Bengali) is held out.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Cost vs. Quality:** Full human translation is infeasible; using LLMs with human validation only on the test set scales production but risks residual errors in languages without native‑speaker checks (Swahili, Telugu, Bengali).  
- **Parallelism vs. Coverage:** Enforcing parallel intersection yields a perfectly comparable split (7,620 per language) but discards many good translations that are only available in a subset of languages.  
- **Iteration Depth:** Capping refinement at 5 attempts controls API costs but may limit improvement on particularly challenging translations.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Critical issue rate >5% in cleaning suggests source corpus corruption or overly aggressive filtering prompts.  
- Many samples (>10%) requiring >3 refinement loops indicate misaligned translation prompts or language‑specific conventions not captured.  
- Parallel split yield <60% of cleaned set points to poor translation coverage in one or more languages, requiring prompt tuning.  
- Post‑hoc keyword cleaning removing >1% of the test set implies initial filtering missed problematic patterns.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Sanity Check Cleaning:** Apply the cleaning pipeline to a random 500‑sample subset of AceReason‑Math; manually verify that the proportions of critical (~4%) and salvageable (~11%) issues match the paper.  
2. **Translation Quality Anchor:** For German (high‑resource) and Thai (low‑resource), translate 100 samples each and grade them using the provided rubric. Target >95% “Acceptable/Excellent” acceptance rate to align with the human review reported in the paper.  
3. **Parallel Split Consistency:** After full translation, compute the intersection size across all 14 languages. Confirm the parallel train split contains approximately 7,620 samples per language (as in Table 3). Investigate any language with <90% yield relative to its full‑train count.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Effect of RLVR training on mAceReason‑Math:** The paper only evaluates models without RLVR; the impact of using this dataset as RLVR training data, and how it compares to English‑only RLVR, remains unknown.  
2. **Cause of Qwen3’s cross‑lingual consistency:** Qwen3 reasoning models achieve exceptionally high and uniform performance across languages. The paper does not analyze whether this is due to training data, model architecture, or inherent multilingual capabilities.  
3. **Impact of translation quality variance on low‑validation languages:** Swahili, Telugu, and Bengali lack native‑speaker validation; it is unclear how translation errors in these languages affect model performance.  
4. **Necessity of cultural adaptation:** The pipeline includes guidelines for localizing cultural references, but the paper does not assess whether such adaptation changes problem difficulty or introduces bias compared to literal translations.

## Limitations
- No native‑speaker validation for Swahili, Telugu, and Bengali, leaving potential translation errors in these languages.  
- Language‑specific guidelines and grading thresholds are not fully disclosed, making exact replication difficult.  
- The parallel train split discards translations not present in all 14 languages, reducing per‑language data volume compared to independent splits.  
- Automatic answer verification may mishandle formatting variations, risking evaluation noise.  

## Confidence
- Filtering effectiveness: **High** (detailed criteria and examples provided).  
- Translation quality: **Medium** (iterative LLM pipeline with partial human validation; some languages lack validation).  
- Cross‑lingual transfer: **Medium** (consistent results but no ablations on translation fidelity or training data differences).
