# A Foundation Model Approach for Fetal Stress Prediction During Labor From cardiotocography (CTG) recordings

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.06149
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06149
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: AUC 0.83 on CTU-UHB test set (0.853 for uncomplicated vaginal deliveries), exceeding prior benchmarks (0.68–0.75).

## Executive Summary
Fetal monitoring via cardiotocography (CTG) during labor is essential but suffers from high inter‑observer variability and limited accuracy. This work introduces a foundation model that leverages self‑supervised masked pre‑training on 2,444 hours of unlabeled CTG recordings to learn robust representations of fetal heart rate (FHR) dynamics, using a PatchTST transformer with channel‑asymmetric masking (only FHR patches are masked while uterine contraction context is retained). The model is fine‑tuned on the 552‑recording CTU‑UHB benchmark for binary classification of fetal acidemia (umbilical artery pH < 7.15). It achieves an AUC of 0.83 on the full test set and 0.853 on uncomplicated vaginal deliveries, substantially outperforming previous results (0.68–0.75). Error analysis shows that false positives often align with CTG patterns deemed concerning by clinicians, suggesting clinical relevance. To support reproducibility, the authors release standardized dataset splits and model weights.

## Method Summary
The task is binary classification of fetal acidemia. The labeled dataset (CTU‑UHB) contains 552 recordings; pre‑training uses 2,444 hours of unlabeled CTG from three institutions (CTGDL). Preprocessing includes clipping FHR (50–210 bpm) and UC (0–100 mmHg), artefact/spike removal, linear interpolation, detection of flat UC segments (rolling std < 1e‑5 over 30 s), and per‑channel min‑max normalization to [0,1]. Signals are segmented into overlapping windows of 1800 samples (7.5 min at 4 Hz) and split into patches (length 48, stride 24).

Pre‑training employs a PatchTST transformer with channel‑asymmetric masking: 40% of FHR patches (contiguous blocks, excluding first/last) are masked, while all UC patches remain visible. The model reconstructs masked FHR patches via mean squared error, forcing it to use both local FHR context and aligned UC context. After pre‑training, the encoder is fine‑tuned on the CTU‑UHB train set; class imbalance is mitigated by augmenting training data with cesarean deliveries from SPaM (stage‑2 duration = 0). A linear classification head is trained with cross‑entropy loss, AdamW, and early stopping on validation AUC.

Inference slides windows across the full recording, producing a probability per window. Contiguous regions where probability > 0.5 are identified. Features of the largest region (duration, peak, cumulative score) are fed to a logistic regression model (trained on the fine‑tuning set) to produce a single recording‑level prediction.

## Key Results
- Achieved AUC 0.83 on full CTU‑UHB test set and 0.853 on uncomplicated vaginal deliveries, surpassing prior art (AUC 0.68–0.75).
- Error analysis: many false positives correspond to CTG patterns flagged as concerning by clinicians, indicating clinically meaningful alerts.
- Released standardized dataset splits and model weights for reproducible benchmarking.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Self‑supervised mask pre‑training learns transferable FHR representations
Pre‑training on massive unlabeled data drives the model to reconstruct masked FHR patches. To succeed, it must exploit (1) surrounding FHR context to capture temporal morphology (e.g., deceleration shape) and (2) the concurrently available UC signal to learn timing relationships (e.g., late decelerations relative to contraction peaks). This dual requirement embeds features useful for downstream acidemia detection. Evidence: the final model outperforms supervised baselines, attaining 0.83 AUC. Break condition: if reconstruction fails to correlate with pH or if UC noise outweighs its benefit.

### Mechanism 2: Channel‑asymmetric masking reinforces clinically central FHR‑UC coupling
By masking only FHR patches while leaving UC intact, the pre‑training task forces the network to use the UC channel as conditioning for FHR reconstruction. This mirrors the clinical principle of evaluating FHR responses *to* contractions, distinguishing pattern types (e.g., variable vs. late decelerations). The asymmetry thus biases the representation toward inter‑channel relationships most diagnostic of fetal stress. The model’s strong performance supports this design; a break condition would be if UC’s role is merely ancillary (e.g., artefact detection) or if standard channel mixing suffices.

### Mechanism 3: Sliding‑window inference with alert segmentation yields temporally coherent alerts
Real‑time monitoring requires decisions based on evolving signals, not static snapshots. The model processes overlapping 7.5‑minute windows and aggregates sustained high‑confidence regions. A two‑stage post‑processor—thresholding to find candidate alert segments followed by logistic regression on segment features—produces a final alert. This balances neural pattern recognition with interpretable, auditable rules, aligning with clinical workflow. Break condition: if optimal detection requires longer context (>7.5 min) or different feature summaries.

## Foundational Learning

### Self‑Supervised Learning via Masked Modeling
**Why needed:** Labeled CTG data is scarce (n=552), but large unlabeled recordings exist. Masked pre‑training enables learning general FHR dynamics without manual labels, overcoming the bottleneck.
**Quick check:** Does reconstruction loss decrease on unlabeled data, and do learned features cluster by known CTG morphologies (e.g., decelerations) on an annotated hold‑out like FHRMA?

### Patch‑Based Tokenization for Time Series (PatchTST)
**Why needed:** CTG patterns span tens to hundreds of seconds. Raw high‑frequency processing loses morphological context and inflates sequence length. Patches create semantically meaningful tokens and reduce sequence length for efficient attention.
**Quick check:** For fixed context, does larger patch size (e.g., 48 vs. 96 samples) improve AUC by better capturing shape, or degrade due to excessive coarseness?

### Handling Class and Data Source Imbalance
**Why needed:** Labeled set has 20.5% positives and is single‑center; pre‑training data comes from three institutions with potentially different monitors. The model must generalize across sources and learn from the minority class.
**Quick check:** Does augmenting with SPaM cesarean recordings improve recall without raising false positives on normal vaginal deliveries? Does performance drop on an external center not seen during pre‑training?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**
- Data Pipeline: raw FHR/UC → artefact/spike removal → linear interpolation → UC flat detection (rolling std < 1e‑5, 30 s) → per‑channel clipping & min‑max normalization → windowing (1800 samples)
- Pre‑training Stage: patchify → apply channel‑asymmetric mask (only FHR) → patch embedding + positional encoding → Transformer encoder → reconstruct masked FHR patches (MSE)
- Fine‑tuning Stage: load pre‑trained encoder → replace head with linear classifier → process full windows (no mask) → [CLS] pooling → linear layer → cross‑entropy
- Inference Engine: slide window over full recording → per‑window probability → threshold (0.5) to identify contiguous alert regions → extract features from the largest region (duration, peak, cumulative score) → logistic regression → final recording prediction

**Critical path:** Preprocessing → Pre‑training (large unlabeled corpus) → Fine‑tuning (labeled CTU‑UHB) → Sliding‑window inference → Alert segmentation & logistic regression.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Patch size/stride: smaller → finer detail but longer sequences; larger → better morphology but may miss nuances.
- Masking ratio & contiguity: higher ratio increases pre‑training difficulty (potentially better features) but risks instability; contiguous grouping prevents trivial interpolation.
- Context window length (1800 samples ≈ 7.5 min): longer windows capture more context but reduce number of training patches; shorter windows might miss delayed decelerations.
- Channel independence vs. mixing: independent encoders reduce parameters and force UC to act as context; mixing could learn complex interactions but risks noise.

**Failure signatures:**
1. Pre‑training loss plateaus high or diverges → masking ratio too high, patch size unsuited, or learning rate mis‑set.
2. Fine‑tuned AUC ≈ random or below supervised baseline → pre‑training task misaligned, channel‑asymmetric masking ineffective, or incorrect dataset split.
3. High false‑positive rate on normal vaginal deliveries → alert threshold too low, logistic regression over‑weights segment length, or model confused by benign patterns (e.g., early decelerations).
4. Performance varies dramatically across pre‑training sites → poor domain generalization; may require adversarial training or domain‑specific normalization.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Ablate channel‑asymmetric masking:** Pre‑train a model with symmetric random masking (both FHR and UC) and compare fine‑tuned AUC against the published asymmetric approach to isolate the effect of UC context.
2. **Vary masking ratio:** Pre‑train with masking ratios 0.2, 0.4, 0.6 and evaluate downstream AUC to understand the trade‑off between pre‑training difficulty and representation quality.
3. **Test sliding window duration:** During inference, use different window lengths (5, 7.5, 10 min) without altering the pre‑trained model to assess whether longer context improves detection of sustained patterns or increases false positives.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can clinical context (maternal risk factors, labor interventions) be effectively integrated into CTG analysis models when large labeled datasets with such metadata are scarce?
2. To what extent does the model's high performance on the CTU‑UHB benchmark generalize to diverse global populations, monitoring devices, and clinical practice settings?
3. Are umbilical artery pH thresholds (e.g., <7.15) an insufficient and potentially biased outcome for training models to detect clinically meaningful fetal compromise?
4. Is the proposed channel‑asymmetric masking strategy (masking only FHR, retaining UC) optimal for learning generalizable representations of fetal stress patterns, or could alternative masking schemes (e.g., symmetric, UC‑only, channel‑mixing) yield better downstream performance?
5. Can the sliding‑window inference and alert‑generation pipeline be validated in a real‑time, prospective clinical trial to assess its impact on false‑positive/negative rates and clinical decision‑making compared to standard care?

## Limitations
- **External generalizability:** Evaluation limited to the single‑center CTU‑UHB dataset; no validation on other hospitals or populations.
- **Outcome definition:** Binary pH threshold is an imperfect proxy for fetal compromise and may introduce bias; inter‑observer CTG variability not addressed.
- **Reproducibility gaps:** Missing details on inference stride, exact SPaM augmentation protocol, and logistic regression coefficients hinder exact replication.

## Confidence
- Self‑supervised pre‑training: Medium (single‑benchmark improvement shown, but lacks external validation)
- Channel‑asymmetric masking: Low‑Medium (conceptually justified but not empirically ablated)
- Sliding‑window alert segmentation: Medium (transparent design but not compared to alternatives)

## Next Checks
1. Conduct an ablation of channel‑asymmetric masking by pre‑training a symmetric model and comparing fine‑tuned AUC on CTU‑UHB.
2. Perform external multi‑site validation on an independent dataset from a different hospital/country to assess generalization.
3. Optimize alert segmentation by grid‑searching sliding window stride (e.g., 30 s, 60 s, 120 s) and post‑processing thresholds on the validation set; re‑evaluate test AUC and false‑positive rate.
