# A feature-stable and explainable machine learning framework for trustworthy decision-making under incomplete clinical data
## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.17364  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17364  
- **Reference count:** 0  
- **One-line primary result:** CACTUS achieves superior feature stability and comparable or better classification accuracy under up to 30% random missingness versus random forests and gradient boosting.

## Executive Summary
The reliability of machine learning models in healthcare is compromised when clinical data are incomplete, as feature importance rankings become unstable and erode clinician trust. The CACTUS framework addresses this by combining feature abstraction with systematic stability analysis to quantify how robustly important features hold as data completeness degrades. Evaluated on a 568‑patient bladder cancer cohort, CACTUS maintained significantly higher feature stability (lower average relative change and higher top‑10 overlap) under up to 30 % randomly introduced missingness compared to random forests and gradient boosting, while delivering equal or superior balanced accuracy and recall across the full cohort and sex‑stratified subgroups. This work demonstrates a practical path toward trustworthy AI‑assisted decision‑making in the presence of incomplete clinical data.

## Method Summary
CACTUS operates in two phases: first, it transforms raw clinical features into an abstract representation designed to mitigate the impact of missing values; second, it systematically evaluates feature stability by training predictive models on datasets with varying levels of artificially injected missingness and then measuring changes in feature importance rankings. Stability is quantified using two metrics: the average relative change in importance scores and the overlap of the top‑10 ranked features across missingness levels. The framework was benchmarked against random forests and gradient boosting on a bladder cancer dataset (n = 568) with up to 30 % randomly missing values. Performance was assessed via balanced accuracy and recall, and stability metrics were used to compare robustness across models.

## Key Results
- **Feature stability superiority:** CACTUS exhibited significantly lower average relative change and higher top‑10 feature overlap under up to 30 % random missingness relative to RF and GBM.  
- **Predictive performance:** Balanced accuracy and recall were equal to or better than those of RF and GBM across the overall cohort and male/female subgroups.  
- **Trustworthiness:** The framework’s explicit stability analysis provides transparent insights into feature robustness, supporting clinical adoption.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
CACTUS enhances stability through two synergistic mechanisms: (1) feature abstraction produces latent representations that are inherently less sensitive to missing values, thereby reducing variance in importance scores; (2) systematic stability analysis quantifies how feature rankings evolve as data completeness declines, enabling identification of consistently predictive features. This dual approach ensures that clinical decisions rely on robust, interpretable factors, which is critical for trust in AI‑driven healthcare.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Feature Importance Stability**  
   - *Why needed:* Trustworthy models must depend on features that remain consistent despite data incompleteness.  
   - *Quick check:* Compute Jaccard similarity between top‑k feature sets across different missingness levels; high overlap indicates stability.

2. **Missing Data Mechanisms (MCAR/MAR/MNAR)**  
   - *Why needed:* Real‑world clinical data exhibit diverse missingness patterns; understanding these mechanisms is essential for evaluating robustness.  
   - *Quick check:* Simulate different missingness types and compare stability metrics; sensitivity to mechanism reveals model limitations.

3. **Explainable AI in Clinical Contexts**  
   - *Why needed:* Clinicians require understandable rationales to adopt model predictions.  
   - *Quick check:* Generate SHAP/LIME explanations for a subset of predictions; align with stability findings to validate interpretability.

4. **Systematic Robustness Evaluation**  
   - *Why needed:* Accuracy alone is insufficient for assessing trust; stability metrics complement performance evaluation.  
   - *Quick check:* Plot stability metrics (e.g., top‑10 overlap) against missingness percentages; compare curves across models.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Data → Feature Abstraction → Model Training → Stability Analysis → Evaluation  

**Critical path:**  
- Load clinical dataset  
- Apply feature abstraction (e.g., encoding, transformation)  
- Train predictive models on datasets with incrementally increased random missingness  
- Extract feature importance from each trained model  
- Compute stability metrics (average relative change, top‑10 overlap)  
- Compare against baseline models and report results  

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Abstraction complexity vs. interpretability:* More intricate abstractions may improve stability but obscure clinical meaning.  
- *Model choice:* Simpler ensembles provide a stability baseline; CACTUS adds abstraction and systematic analysis, trading some simplicity for robustness.  
- *Stability vs. accuracy:* Emphasizing stability can modestly sacrifice accuracy; CACTUS aims to balance both.  

**Failure signatures:**  
- Sharp drop in top‑10 overlap (>30 %) with increasing missingness.  
- High variance in feature importance across cross‑validation folds.  
- Performance degradation (balanced accuracy >5 % drop) at low missingness (<10 %).  

**First experiments:**  
1. Baseline assessment on complete data: train CACTUS, RF, and GBM on the full dataset; record balanced accuracy, recall, and feature importance.  
2. Random missingness injection: introduce 10 %, 20 %, and 30 % missing values randomly; repeat training for all models; compute stability metrics and performance.  
3. Comparative validation: use paired statistical tests (e.g., t‑test) to compare CACTUS stability metrics against baselines across missingness levels; assess significance of improvements.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1:** How do biomarker measurements versus lifestyle/demographic factors independently contribute to the classification performance and feature stability in CACTUS?  
  - *Basis in paper:* [explicit] The authors propose future work to "divide the datasets into biomarker and lifestyle/demographic subsets" to clarify relative contributions (Conclusion, page 23).  
  - *Why unresolved:* The current analysis combines all features, making it unclear which type drives the results or if one overshadows the other.  
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Controlled experiments where models are trained separately on biomarker-only and lifestyle-only subsets, comparing performance and stability metrics.

- **Question 2:** Do the superior feature stability and performance of CACTUS generalize to larger and more diverse clinical cohorts beyond the HaBio bladder cancer dataset?  
  - *Basis in paper:* [explicit] The authors note the need for "larger datasets should be used to confirm our initial findings" (Conclusion, page 23).  
  - *Why unresolved:* Results are based on a single cohort (n=568), which may not capture broader variability in patient populations or disease presentations.  
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Multi-center validation studies using independent datasets from different regions, populations, and healthcare systems, applying the same CACTUS framework and stability analyses.

- **Question 3:** How can the feature thresholds derived by CACTUS for binary discretization be aligned or integrated with established clinical diagnostic thresholds to enhance trust and adoption?  
  - *Basis in paper:* [explicit] The authors distinguish that CACTUS thresholds "represent the best value that separates two classes" and "do not carry the same information as thresholds set by medical institutions" (Conclusion, page 22-23).  
  - *Why unresolved:* There's a conceptual and practical gap between data-driven thresholds and clinically validated ones, potentially hindering clinician trust and utility.  
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Studies comparing CACTUS-derived thresholds with known clinical cutoffs, evaluating concordance, and assessing whether incorporating both improves diagnostic accuracy or clinician confidence in real-world settings.

- **Question 4:** What are the underlying reasons for the sex-specific differences in both feature importance and stability observed in CACTUS analyses?  
  - *Basis in paper:* [explicit] The authors highlight that "biomarkers characteristic of bladder cancer development differ by sex" and note only two overlapping top features between males and females (Results, page 17; Conclusion, page 23).  
  - *Why unresolved:* While sex-stratified results are presented, the paper doesn't investigate why these differences exist—e.g., biological, methodological, or data-related factors.  
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* In-depth analysis of feature distributions by sex, interaction effects in models, and possibly mechanistic studies linking biomarkers to sex-specific pathophysiology.

- **Question 5:** Should feature stability metrics be formally adopted as a standard component alongside traditional performance metrics in evaluating biomedical machine learning models?  
  - *Basis in paper:* [explicit] The authors argue that "feature stability provides information complementary to conventional performance metrics and is essential for assessing trustworthiness" and should be "considered a standard component of pattern discovery pipelines" (Abstract, page 3; Conclusion, page 23).  
  - *Why unresolved:* Current practice in biomedical ML still prioritizes accuracy/sensitivity over stability, partly due to lack of consensus on metrics and their clinical relevance.  
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Community-driven benchmarking challenges that include stability metrics, studies correlating stability with outcomes like reproducibility or clinician trust, and development of standardized evaluation protocols incorporating stability.

## Limitations
- The evaluation relies solely on a single bladder cancer cohort (n=568), limiting generalizability to other diseases or populations.
- Missingness was introduced randomly; real-world patterns (e.g., missing not at random) were not considered.
- Benchmarks excluded widely used methods like XGBoost, CatBoost, and neural networks, restricting the scope of comparison.

## Confidence
- Feature stability superiority: Medium  
- Predictive performance (balanced accuracy/recall): Medium  
- Explainability and systematic stability analysis: Low  

## Next Checks
- **External cohort validation:** Apply CACTUS to at least two independent multi‑center datasets (>1,000 patients) across different clinical domains to assess generalizability of stability and performance.  
- **Real-world missingness scenarios:** Test robustness using missingness patterns derived from electronic health records, including missing not at random (MNAR) mechanisms, and compare against multiple imputation and robust learners.  
- **Broader benchmarking:** Compare against XGBoost, CatBoost, and deep learning models (with advanced imputation) using standardized stability metrics (e.g., Jaccard index of top features) and statistical significance testing.
