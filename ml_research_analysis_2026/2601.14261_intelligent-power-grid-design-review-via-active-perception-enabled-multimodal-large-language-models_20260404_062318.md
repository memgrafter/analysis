# Intelligent Power Grid Design Review via Active Perception-Enabled Multimodal Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.14261
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.14261
- Reference count: 22
- Primary result: Achieved F1-score of 0.77 for violation detection on 4K-resolution power grid drawings.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces an automated design review system for ultra-high-resolution power grid drawings, addressing challenges of computational inefficiency and semantic understanding. The proposed three-stage framework uses a Multimodal Large Language Model (MLLM) with prompt engineering to mimic expert review: first, global semantic understanding to propose domain-specific regions from a low-resolution overview; second, high-resolution fine-grained recognition within those regions with confidence scoring; third, confidence-aware decision-making to diagnose errors and assess reliability. Evaluated on a 4K test set, the system achieved an F1-score of 0.75 in precision and 0.80 in recall for violation detection, demonstrating improved accuracy and reliability over passive MLLM inference.

## Method Summary
The method employs a pre-trained MLLM (Qwen2.5-VL-7B) without fine-tuning, relying on prompt engineering across three stages. Stage 1 processes a downsampled image (1024×1024) with design rule prompts to output semantic region proposals (bounding boxes with labels). Stage 2 crops each proposed region at native resolution and extracts detailed elements (components, connections, OCR) with confidence scores. Stage 3 aggregates all extracted data, applies design rules, resolves conflicts using confidence weights, and outputs error descriptions with certainty levels. Coordinate transformations map local detections to global image space. The system uses leave-one-out cross-validation on a small set of 12 proprietary 4K substation drawings targeting CT secondary grounding violations.

## Key Results
- F1-score of 0.77 for violation detection (precision 0.75, recall 0.80) on 4K-resolution test set.
- Stage 1 region proposal metrics: precision ~0.70, recall ~0.65, IoU@0.5 ~0.60.
- Demonstrated improved defect discovery accuracy and reliability compared to passive MLLM inference.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Active Perception via Semantic Region Proposal
**Claim:** Active perception via domain-specific semantic region proposal enables efficient, focused analysis by identifying high-priority functional zones from a low-resolution overview, avoiding full-image processing and generic proposals.
**Mechanism:** The MLLM processes a low-resolution image with task and design rule prompts to output semantically meaningful regions (e.g., "220kV busbar areas"). These guide high-resolution cropping, reducing computational load and preserving critical details.
**Core assumption:** Global semantics can be understood from a downsampled image, and the MLLM can follow prompts to identify relevant regions.
**Evidence:** [abstract] "Mimicking the human expert review process, the first stage leverages an MLLM for global semantic understanding to intelligently propose domain-specific semantic regions from a low-resolution overview." [section III.B] "Unlike general active perception methods that propose generic bounding boxes [6], our system guides the MLLM, via advanced prompt engineering, to identify and propose domain-specific semantic regions."
**Break condition:** If the MLLM fails to infer relevant regions (due to poor prompts or limited domain knowledge), critical areas may be omitted, leading to low recall.

### Mechanism 2: Confidence-Aware Fine-Grained Extraction
**Claim:** Confidence-aware high-resolution information extraction within proposed regions yields precise element-level details with uncertainty estimates, enabling downstream reliability assessment.
**Mechanism:** Each proposed region is cropped at native resolution and processed by the MLLM, prompted to extract specific elements and assign confidence scores (0.0–1.0). Coordinates are transformed to global space.
**Core assumption:** The MLLM can provide meaningful confidence scores for fine-grained tasks when explicitly prompted, and these scores reflect true likelihood.
**Evidence:** [abstract] "The second stage then performs high-resolution, fine-grained recognition within these proposed regions, acquiring detailed information with associated confidence scores." [section III.C] "Crucially, this module outputs identification results with associated confidence scores or uncertainty measures for each acquired piece of information (e.g., OCR reading confidence, device type classification confidence)."
**Break condition:** If confidence scores are poorly calibrated (e.g., overconfident on OCR errors), the decision module may trust erroneous information, causing false negatives/positives.

### Mechanism 3: Confidence-Integrated Decision-Making
**Claim:** Confidence-aware comprehensive decision-making integrates multi-region information to diagnose logical inconsistencies and conflicts according to design rules, providing reliability assessments for each detected error.
**Mechanism:** The system aggregates Stage 2 outputs (elements with confidence and global bboxes). An MLLM processes global context and aggregated data with prompts to apply rules, identify inconsistencies, resolve conflicts by weighting confidence, and output errors with certainty levels (High/Medium/Low).
**Core assumption:** The MLLM can perform complex multi-hop reasoning over extracted elements and incorporate confidence weights, mimicking a human expert's holistic review.
**Evidence:** [abstract] "In the final stage, a comprehensive decision-making module integrates these confidence-aware results to accurately diagnose design errors and provide a reliability assessment." [section III.D] "The MLLM's reasoning is guided by prompts to: Identify Logical Inconsistencies; Resolve Information Conflicts (using confidence scores); Assess Judgment Reliability."
**Break condition:** If the MLLM lacks sufficient reasoning depth for the specific rule (e.g., cannot infer electrical topology), the module may miss violations or produce incorrect diagnoses even with accurate extraction.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs)
**Why needed:** The entire framework relies on a pre-trained MLLM to perform visual understanding, region proposal, element extraction, and reasoning. Understanding MLLM input/output formats, token limitations, and prompting strategies is essential.
**Quick check:** What are the typical maximum input resolution and token limits for open-source MLLMs like Qwen-VL, and how do these constraints influence the design of multi-stage processing?

### Concept: Active Perception (Active Vision)
**Why needed:** The core innovation is an active perception mechanism where the system decides where to look (region proposal) rather than passively processing all scales, shifting from fixed multi-scale extraction to task-driven semantic focusing.
**Quick check:** How does active perception reduce computational cost compared to processing an image at multiple fixed scales (e.g., using a Feature Pyramid Network)?

### Concept: Prompt Engineering for MLLMs
**Why needed:** The framework uses fixed prompt templates to guide the MLLM at each stage without fine-tuning. Effective prompts must convey role, task, output format, and reasoning steps.
**Quick check:** What elements should be included in a prompt to elicit structured JSON output from an MLLM, such as bounding boxes and confidence scores? Consider role assignment, output schema, and delimiters.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Input (high-res drawing) -> Stage 1 (MLLM Region Proposal on low-res) -> Stage 2 (MLLM Fine-grained Extraction on high-res crops with confidence) -> Stage 3 (MLLM Decision with Confidence Integration) -> Output (Review Report)

### Critical path
1. Stage 1 must output valid semantic regions (non-empty, correct format). If empty, system halts.
2. For each region, Stage 2 must produce elements; if a region yields no elements, it may indicate an erroneous proposal.
3. Stage 3 requires at least one element with sufficient confidence to form a judgment; otherwise, reports "inconclusive" for that rule.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Model size vs. capability:** 7B parameter MLLM keeps GPU memory low (~24GB) but may limit reasoning depth; larger models (e.g., 72B) could improve accuracy but require more resources.
- **Prompt-driven vs. fine-tuning:** Fixed prompts avoid costly fine-tuning but may not generalize to unseen rules; fine-tuning on domain data could improve performance at the cost of flexibility.
- **Region proposal granularity:** Overly broad regions include irrelevant details; too narrow may miss context. Prompt engineering and NMS (IoU 0.3 threshold) help.
- **Confidence usage:** Trusting high confidence blindly may propagate MLLM overconfidence; a calibration step using a validation set could help but is not implemented.

### Failure signatures
- **Stage 1:** Repeatedly returns empty or nonsensical labels (e.g., "text_box" instead of "busbar"). Indicates poor prompt clarity or limited domain knowledge.
- **Stage 2:** Consistent low confidence scores (<0.3) on all elements in a region may indicate the region is irrelevant or the MLLM cannot recognize details at that resolution.
- **Stage 3:** Many "Low" certainty errors or high "conflicts unresolved" ratio suggests poor extraction or that the rule is too complex for MLLM reasoning.
- **Systemic:** High variance across folds indicates instability, possibly due to small dataset or prompt sensitivity.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Stage 1 validation:** On 5 drawings with manual semantic region annotations, compute IoU@0.5 between proposed and ground-truth regions. Adjust prompt if recall <0.7.
2. **Confidence calibration:** For 100 sampled Stage 2 extractions, manually label correctness. Plot reliability diagram (confidence vs. accuracy). If poorly calibrated, consider post-hoc calibration or prompt adjustment.
3. **End-to-end rule check:** Run full pipeline on a held-out drawing with known violations. Verify detected violations match ground truth, reliability assessments are reasonable, and processing time is acceptable (<30 minutes). If recall low, check Stage 1 proposals; if precision low, check Stage 2 extraction or Stage 3 reasoning.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** Does the framework generalize to larger, more diverse power grid drawing datasets?
**Basis in paper:** The evaluation set is small (12 drawings) and authors state future work should expand to a larger public corpus to stabilize metrics and validate robustness.
**Why unresolved:** Results show high standard deviations (F1 ±0.09) and are based on a limited in-house collection, leaving generalizability uncertain.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Testing on a large, publicly available dataset covering a wide range of grid designs and rule violations, with consistent reporting of precision, recall, and F1 scores showing reduced variance.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** Can the framework detect other design rule violations (e.g., CT ratio consistency) beyond the CT grounding rule?
**Basis in paper:** Authors explicitly plan to apply the framework to additional design rules, implying a need to test generalizability across rule types.
**Why unresolved:** Current validation covers only a single rule; different rules may require distinct semantic regions, element types, and reasoning patterns, challenging the present prompt engineering and decision module.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Extending the same three-stage pipeline to at least one additional rule, adapting prompts and annotations as needed, and reporting comparable detection performance (e.g., F1 > 0.7) on a held-out set.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** How effective is semi-automated labeling for scaling dataset creation?
**Basis in paper:** Authors mention exploring semi-automated labeling to scale quantitative validation, acknowledging manual annotation as a bottleneck.
**Why unresolved:** Manual annotation of semantic regions and violation boxes is time-intensive; the potential of an MLLM-assisted labeling loop to reduce effort and maintain quality is unknown.
**What evidence would resolve it:** Implementing a semi-automated workflow (MLLM proposes labels, experts correct), measuring reduction in human labor (time, corrections), and comparing annotation quality (e.g., inter-annotator agreement) and downstream model performance against fully manual labels.

## Limitations
- **Exact prompt engineering details are unreported:** The specific wording, few-shot examples, and rule phrasing that drove performance are omitted, blocking accurate replication.
- **Confidence score generation mechanism is unclear:** It is unknown whether scores are derived from model logits, heuristic prompting, or post-processing, creating risk of poor calibration.
- **Evaluation on a tiny, proprietary dataset (n=12):** Results lack statistical robustness and generalizability to other grid designs or rules.

## Confidence
- **Performance claim (F1=0.77):** Medium. Supported by internal LOOCV on a very small, non-public set; high variance across folds suggests instability.
- **Mechanism 1 (Active perception region proposal):** Medium. Evidence is conceptual (prompt design) with no external corpus support; break condition (omitting critical areas) is plausible but unquantified.
- **Mechanism 2 (Confidence-aware fine-grained extraction):** Low-Medium. Novelty is noted, but the core assumption of well-calibrated MLLM confidence is unsupported and likely fragile without dedicated calibration.
- **Mechanism 3 (Confidence-integrated decision-making):** Medium. Relies on MLLM reasoning depth, which is not benchmarked against simpler rule engines; conflict resolution strategy is vaguely described.

## Next Checks
1. **Stage 1 proposal quality audit:** Manually annotate semantic regions (e.g., CT circuits) on a held-out drawing. Compute IoU@0.5 between proposed and ground-truth boxes. Target: proposal recall >0.7.
2. **Confidence calibration test:** For 100 randomly sampled Stage 2 element extractions, label correctness. Plot a reliability diagram (confidence vs. accuracy). Target: misalignment (e.g., 90% confidence but 60% accuracy) must be <0.1 ECE.
3. **End-to-end violation detection with ablation:** Run the full pipeline on a drawing with 3–5 known CT grounding violations. Ablate the confidence-aware module (use majority vote instead) to measure its contribution to precision/recall.
