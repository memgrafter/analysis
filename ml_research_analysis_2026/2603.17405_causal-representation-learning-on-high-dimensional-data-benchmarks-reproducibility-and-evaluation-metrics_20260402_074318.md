# Causal Representation Learning on High-Dimensional Data: Benchmarks, Reproducibility, and Evaluation Metrics

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.17405
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17405
- **Reference count:** 18
- **Primary result:** Proposed dataset guidelines and the Origami plot metric; benchmarked β‑VAE, Conditional VAE, and CausalVAE on Pendulum; β‑VAE achieved highest normalized Origami score (0.590); identified reproducibility challenges in CRL.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the lack of robust evaluation in causal representation learning (CRL). It analyzes limitations of existing synthetic and real‑world datasets and proposes four essential characteristics for future CRL benchmarks: inclusion of all three basic causal junctions (fork, chain, collider), sufficient number of variables, real‑world semantic alignment, and presence of confounders. To compare models across multiple evaluation dimensions (reconstruction, disentanglement, causal discovery, counterfactual reasoning), the authors introduce the order‑invariant Origami plot, a unified aggregate metric that consolidates diverse metrics into a single normalized score. They apply this framework to benchmark β‑VAE, Conditional VAE, and CausalVAE on the Pendulum dataset, finding that β‑VAE attains the top score (0.590). A reproducibility investigation reveals discrepancies in reported results (e.g., for CausalVAE) and widespread missing code and inconsistent dataset usage, highlighting the need for standardized practices.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates three CRL models—β‑VAE, Conditional VAE, and CausalVAE—using the synthetic Pendulum dataset, which includes four generative factors and a causal graph with fork and collider junctions but lacks chains and confounders. Performance is measured across eight metrics: reconstruction (1 – MAE), disentanglement (IRS, JEMMIG, MIC, TIC), and counterfactual realism (FID, IS, KID). Downward‑oriented metrics (FID, KID) are transformed to upward orientation via `1 – metric`. All metrics are min‑max normalized using observed minima and maxima (from Table 1) to [0,1]. The Origami plot aggregates these normalized values by arranging them on radial axes, computing the enclosed area above a reference value `h=0.25`, and normalizing by the maximum possible area `0.765`. The resulting score reflects holistic model capability. A reproducible protocol is outlined: (1) environment setup; (2) dataset generation from Yang et al. (2021); (3) model training with consistent hyperparameters; (4) metric computation on a fixed test set; (5) Origami area calculation. Unknowns blocking exact replication include missing dataset generation details, model hyperparameters, and full metric ranges; common failure modes involve metric implementation errors, dataset shift, and mishandling of variable types.

## Key Results
- **Dataset guidelines:** Identified four key characteristics for CRL benchmarks: all three causal junctions, sufficient variables, real‑world alignment, and confounders.
- **Unified metric:** Introduced the order‑invariant Origami plot to aggregate reconstruction, disentanglement, and counterfactual metrics into a single normalized score.
- **Benchmark outcome:** β‑VAE achieved the highest normalized Origami score (0.590) on the Pendulum dataset among the three tested models.
- **Reproducibility issues:** Discrepancies in reported CausalVAE results and widespread lack of code/dataset consistency across CRL papers impede verification.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The Origami plot works by converting heterogeneous evaluation metrics to a common scale where higher values indicate better performance. After transformation and normalization, each metric occupies a vertex on a regular polygon (e.g., an octagon for eight metrics). The polygon is drawn by connecting these vertices in a fixed circular order (e.g., reconstruction → IRS → JEMMIG → MIC → TIC → FID → IS → KID). The area between the polygon and a horizontal reference line at height `h=0.25` is computed; this area represents the aggregate performance above a minimal acceptable threshold. Dividing this area by the theoretical maximum `0.765` (the area of a unit‑radius polygon with all vertices at 1, above `h=0.25`) yields a normalized score between 0 and 1. Because the metric values are placed at predetermined angular positions, the contribution of each metric to the total area is fixed, making the final score independent of the order in which the metrics are listed (i.e., cyclic rotations give the same area) and ensuring a fair aggregation across evaluation dimensions. The reference value `h` penalizes uniformly low scores while rewarding balanced high performance across all metrics.

## Foundational Learning
- **Causal Representation Learning (CRL)**  
  *Why needed:* To discover representations that reflect the causal factors governing data, enabling robust generalization and counterfactual reasoning.  
  *Quick check:* After training, can we intervene on latent variables to produce predictable changes in observed outputs?

- **Disentanglement**  
  *Why needed:* Isolating individual generative factors into separate latent dimensions improves interpretability and facilitates causal interventions.  
  *Quick check:* Use metrics like IRS or JEMMIG; a high score indicates that latent dimensions align with true factors.

- **Causal Junctions (fork, chain, collider)**  
  *Why needed:* A comprehensive dataset should cover all three basic causal structures to test a model’s ability to learn diverse dependency patterns.  
  *Quick check:* Inspect the causal graph: fork (A→B←C), chain (A→B→C), collider (A→B←C) – ensure all are present.

- **Confounders**  
  *Why needed:* Confounders create spurious correlations; including them challenges models to disentangle true causal effects.  
  *Quick check:* Identify variables that are common causes of at least two observed variables in the graph.

- **Synthetic Datasets**  
  *Why needed:* Provide known ground‑truth factors and causal graphs, which are essential for objective evaluation of CRL methods.  
  *Quick check:* Verify that the dataset generation code exposes all latent factors and the causal structure.

- **Evaluation Metrics (IRS, JEMMIG, MIC, TIC, FID, IS, KID)**  
  *Why needed:* No single metric captures all aspects of CRL; this suite covers reconstruction, disentanglement, and counterfactual realism.  
  *Quick check:* Compute on a reference model; ensure values align with expected ranges (e.g., lower FID indicates better sample quality).

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Data Generation (Pendulum) → Model Training (β‑VAE, CVAE, CausalVAE) → Inference (reconstructions & counterfactuals) → Metric Computation (IRS, JEMMIG, MIC, TIC, FID, IS, KID) → Normalization (min‑max, invert downward metrics) → Origami Area Calculation → Final Score.

**Critical Path:**  
1. Generate or obtain the Pendulum dataset with specified factor ranges and causal graph.  
2. Train each CRL model on the training split, saving checkpoints.  
3. On a held‑out test set, produce reconstructions and generate counterfactuals via latent interventions.  
4. Compute all eight metrics using the ground‑truth factors and/or a pretrained Inception network.  
5. Transform downward metrics: e.g., `FID' = 1 – FID_norm`.  
6. Min‑max normalize each transformed metric to [0,1] using the minima and maxima observed across all models (or the theoretical min/max if available).  
7. Arrange the eight normalized values around a circle in any order; compute the polygon area above `h=0.25`.  
8. Normalize this area by `0.765` to obtain the final Origami score.

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- *Metric selection:* More metrics yield a finer‑grained view but may introduce correlated noise; the chosen eight cover main aspects but omit causal discovery accuracy.  
- *Normalization bounds:* Using minima/maxima from only three models risks truncation; a larger, more diverse model pool would produce robust bounds.  
- *Order‑invariance:* The claim that the score is independent of metric order relies on the fixed angular placement; however, extreme values placed adjacent can affect the polygon shape if the area calculation were order‑sensitive; the chosen method averages contributions via the reference line, mitigating this.  
- *Reference value `h=0.25`:* This threshold determines the minimal acceptable performance; different values would rescale scores and possibly change model rankings.  
- *Aggregation by area:* This emphasizes balanced performance (all metrics above threshold) but may mask failure in a critical metric if others compensate.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- Normalization errors: metrics not scaled to [0,1] before area computation.  
- Metric mis‑implementation: e.g., Hungarian matching incorrectly applied for MIC/TIC, or using a non‑standard Inception network for FID/IS/KID.  
- Dataset mismatch: different resolution or factor ranges than the original Pendulum set, leading to incomparable scores.  
- Forgetting to invert downward metrics (FID, KID), which would penalize good models.  
- Area miscalculation: incorrect polygon traversal, wrong reference line, or forgetting to divide by 0.765.

**First Three Experiments:**  
1. **Replication:** Train β‑VAE on Pendulum and compute the Origami score; target 0.590 ± 0.05 to confirm the reported result.  
2. **Order‑invariance test:** Permute the order of metrics around the circle; verify that the final score remains unchanged after correct area computation.  
3. **Cross‑dataset validation:** Apply the same pipeline to another synthetic dataset (e.g., Flow Noise) to check whether the model rankings are consistent and whether the dataset guidelines (junctions, confounders) hold.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Design of comprehensive CRL datasets** – How to create synthetic datasets that simultaneously include all three causal junctions and confounders while maintaining real‑world semantic alignment?  
2. **Adequacy of the Origami metric** – Does aggregating multiple evaluation dimensions into a single score obscure critical weaknesses and fail to reflect real‑world utility?  
3. **Counterfactual accuracy evaluation** – What protocols can assess the factual correctness of generated counterfactuals beyond distributional realism (FID, IS)?  
4. **Limited‑supervision CRL** – How can we achieve identifiability and effective learning with minimal labeled data, reducing the annotation bottleneck?

## Limitations
- **Incomplete dataset specifications:** The exact Pendulum generation parameters (resolution, factor ranges, train/test split) are not provided, hindering exact replication.  
- **Missing training details:** Hyperparameters, network architectures, and training epochs for the benchmarked models are absent, preventing faithful recreation of the reported scores.  
- **Potential normalization bias:** Min‑max normalization based on only three models may not capture true minima/maxima, distorting normalized scores, especially for new models outside the original range.  
- **Scarcity of public code:** The lack of official implementations makes verification and extension difficult, contributing to broader reproducibility challenges in CRL.

## Confidence
- Dataset guidelines: **Medium** – derived from analysis of existing datasets but not empirically validated across a wide range of benchmarks.  
- Origami plot as unified metric: **Medium** – conceptually sound; however, its robustness to metric choices and normalization remains to be tested extensively.  
- β‑VAE highest Origami score on Pendulum: **Low** – missing training details and potential normalization bias compromise confidence in this ranking.  
- Reproducibility issues (code scarcity, inconsistent usage): **High** – corroborated by our own attempts to locate code and by the authors’ investigation.

## Next Checks
