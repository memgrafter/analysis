# EDU-CIRCUIT-HW: Evaluating Multimodal Large Language Models on Real-World University-Level STEM Student Handwritten Solutions

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.00095
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.00095
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: LLM-as-a-judge detection of latent recognition errors and error-pattern-driven regrading achieve near-optimal grading robustness with minimal human intervention.

## Executive Summary
Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) are increasingly used for understanding handwritten documents, but their performance on complex STEM solutions remains under-evaluated. Existing benchmarks rely on downstream tasks like auto-grading, which can mask recognition failures that do not affect the final score. This paper introduces EDU-CIRCUIT-HW, a dataset of 1,334 authentic university-level circuit analysis handwritten solutions with expert-verified transcriptions and detailed grading rubrics. The authors build an LLM-as-a-judge pipeline to itemize and categorize recognition errors (Symbolic, Structural, Diagrammatic, Logical) and compute the Error Impact Rate (EIR)—the proportion of errors that alter grading outcomes. Evaluating six MLLMs reveals substantial latent errors; symbolic errors have the highest EIR (~20%). Finer-grained rubrics expose more errors, and a regrading workflow that flags suspicious transcription segments reduces human effort to 3.3–4.4% while improving type agreement by up to 6.7% and point agreement by up to 8.9% (GPT-5.1), achieving parity with perfect recognition.

## Method Summary
The study constructs two data splits: an observation set (513 handwritten solutions with expert transcriptions and grades) and a larger test set (821 solutions with grades only). Each solution is a circuit analysis response with a corresponding problem description and a fine-grained rubric. The core pipeline consists of: (1) an MLLM recognizer that converts handwritten images into Markdown text; (2) an LLM-as-a-judge that compares the MLLM transcription against the expert gold standard to produce an item-level list of recognition errors and assign each to one of four categories; (3) an LLM grader that receives the problem context and the transcribed solution to output a JSON score per rubric criterion. Primary metrics include SER/AEC for recognition quality, binary/type/point agreement for grading, and EIR to measure how often errors affect grading. The observation set also serves to derive error patterns—systematic weaknesses of the recognizer—which are then used by a regrading module to flag suspicious segments in new solutions. Low-confidence flags trigger human re-grading, while higher-confidence ones are re-graded by the LLM while ignoring the flagged content. The evaluation tests six MLLMs (e.g., Gemini-2.5-Pro, GPT-5.1) and demonstrates that the regrading workflow can recover grading accuracy with only a small fraction of human intervention.

## Key Results
- Evaluation across six MLLMs uncovered significant latent recognition errors; Symbolic errors had the highest Error Impact Rate (EIR) consistently around 20%, directly linking surface-level mistakes to grading penalties.
- Finer-grained rubrics revealed more recognition issues; grading agreement dropped notably as the evaluation criteria became more detailed, especially for weaker recognizers.
- A case study using error patterns to preemptively filter potential recognition errors reduced human intervention to 3.3–4.4% and improved grading agreement: type agreement up by 6.7% and point agreement up by 8.9% (GPT-5.1), matching the performance of a perfect recognizer.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Expert transcriptions disentangle visual recognition from task performance
By comparing MLLM-generated transcriptions against gold-standard human transcriptions at the item level, the pipeline isolates recognition errors independently of whether they affect downstream grading. This decoupling exposes failures that would otherwise remain latent in auto-grading outcomes. Evidence: [abstract] states the dataset provides expert-verified verbatim transcriptions; [section 3.1] describes the LLM-as-a-judge locating recognition errors; [section 4.2] notes that auto-grading masks many recognition failures. Core assumption: expert transcriptions accurately capture student intent and item-level discrepancy detection identifies meaningful errors. Break condition: if transcription errors or misclassification of benign variations occur.

### Mechanism 2: Error taxonomy and EIR reveal differential propagation
Categorizing item-level errors into Symbolic, Structural, Diagrammatic, and Logical types enables measurement of Error Impact Rate (EIR)—the proportion of errors that cause grading discrepancies. Symbolic errors show highest EIR (~20%), indicating that auto-grading pipelines heavily rely on exact symbolic matching. Diagrammatic and logical errors have lower EIR, suggesting they are less penalized by current rubrics. Evidence: [section 3.1.2] defines the four categories; [section 4.2] reports EIR trends, with symbolic errors consistently around 20%; Figure 4 visualizes error counts and EIR per model and category. Core assumption: the taxonomy captures distinct error modes with varying downstream sensitivity and the LLM-based categorizer maintains human-like consistency. Break condition: taxonomy conflation or unstable EIR across rubrics.

### Mechanism 3: Error patterns guide efficient regrading
Observations from the error analysis yield patterns that generalize to new solutions. The regrading module flags transcription segments that match these patterns; flagged content is either re-graded by an LLM (ignoring the flagged material) or, if confidence is low, routed to a human grader. This preemptive filtering reduces the proportion of solutions requiring human review to 3.3–4.4% while restoring grading agreement to levels near those obtained with perfect recognition. Evidence: [section 5] reports the intervention rate and agreement improvements; Table 6 shows performance gains; [section 4.2] indicates that stronger visual understanding correlates with higher EIR, supporting the pattern’s predictive power. Core assumption: error patterns are generalizable and the confidence assessment effectively distinguishes recognition errors from genuine student mistakes. Break condition: patterns overfit to observed problems/models or confidence misclassifies.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Handwritten image → MLLM Recognizer → Transcribed Markdown → LLM Grader → Score (JSON). Optional: Expert transcription (gold) → LLM-as-a-Judge → Error list & categories → EIR calculation. Regrading module: MLLM transcription + error patterns → flagged segments → (low confidence → human re-grade) OR (high confidence → LLM re-grade ignoring flagged content).

**Critical path**: The primary evaluation flow is recognition → grading → agreement metrics. For error analysis, the LLM-as-a-judge compares transcription to gold transcriptions to produce errors and compute EIR. The regrading workflow inserts after initial grading to refine scores.

**Design tradeoffs**: (1) Gold-standard expert transcriptions are costly, limiting the observation set size vs. scalability of annotation. (2) Fully automated grading risks unfair penalties from recognition errors; the proposed regrading module trades a small amount of human effort (3–4%) for robustness. (3) Error patterns derived from circuit analysis may not generalize to other STEM domains (e.g., geometry). (4) Reliance on a proprietary MLLM (Gemini-2.5-Pro) as the judge introduces potential bias and cost concerns.

**Failure signatures**: (a) High SER but low EIR indicates many recognition errors that do not affect grading (e.g., irrelevant diagram mislabels). (b) As rubrics become finer, type and point agreement drop more for weaker recognizers, revealing sensitivity to subtle errors. (c) The regrading detector may flag too many ambiguous cases, raising human intervention above the target 5%. (d) Error patterns extracted from one model (e.g., Gemini) may fail to detect errors from models with different architectures, reducing regrading gains.

**First 3 experiments**: (1) Recognition evaluation: run multiple MLLMs on the observation set; compute SER and AEC relative to expert transcriptions. (2) Validator check: sample 186 solutions; have human experts annotate recognition errors; compare LLM-as-a-judge output (precision, recall, F1). (3) Regrading case study: apply error patterns to test set; route low-confidence cases to human; measure changes in binary/type/point agreement compared to vanilla grading.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How generalizable are the recognition error patterns and their impact rates (EIR) to other STEM domains (e.g., physics derivations, mathematical proofs) that involve different diagram types (e.g., free-body diagrams, geometric figures)?
- **Basis in paper:** The dataset is centered on circuit-related analysis and the authors state that "other diagram types strongly associated with abstract mathematics (e.g., complex geometric graphs) are underrepresented," and they did not investigate other downstream tasks like VQA.
- **Why unresolved:** The findings are derived from a specific domain (circuit analysis), and the error taxonomy may not capture failure modes unique to other visual notations or reasoning styles.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replication studies on new, authentic datasets from other STEM fields (e.g., mechanics, calculus) using the same evaluation framework (LLM-as-a-judge error detection, EIR calculation) to compare error distributions and downstream impacts.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What is the causal relationship between specific *structural* and *textual/logical* recognition errors (which have lower observed EIRs) and performance on downstream tasks that require deeper reasoning, such as circuit-to-netlist generation or multi-step problem-solving?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper notes that diagrammatic and textual/logical errors had lower EIRs for auto-grading but may be "catastrophic for downstream STEM-specific tasks" like circuit-to-netlist generation, which they did not evaluate.
- **Why unresolved:** The study only measured impact on auto-grading. Lower EIR in grading does not imply these errors are harmless for tasks requiring holistic structural or logical comprehension.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Designing and evaluating MLLMs on targeted downstream benchmarks (e.g., netlist generation, VQA on solution logic) while controlling for the presence/absence of specific error categories from the taxonomy, to measure their actual sensitivity.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can the proposed human-in-the-loop regrading workflow, which uses error patterns from one set of models (Gemini-2.5-Pro) to detect errors from others (e.g., GPT-5.1), be generalized into a robust, model-agnostic error detection system without significant performance drop?
- **Basis in paper:** The case study used error patterns summarized from Gemini-2.5-Pro's errors on the observation set to flag issues in test set transcriptions from both Gemini-2.5-Pro and GPT-5.1. The paper does not analyze whether the patterns are model-specific or universal.
- **Why unresolved:** The error patterns were derived from a single model's failures. It is unclear if these patterns transfer to the error signatures of other MLLMs with different architectures and training.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An ablation study applying the error pattern filter derived from Model A's errors to detect errors in transcriptions from Model B, C, and D, measuring detection recall and precision compared to a model-specific filter.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** To what extent do subjective biases in the human-created grading rubrics and expert labels (the "ground truth") influence the measured grading performance gaps between MLLMs and human graders, and the calculated Error Impact Rate (EIR)?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors acknowledge "human evaluation and rubric-crafting in open-ended STEM problem-solving are inherently subjective to some extent" and that "potential subtle subjective biases may still exist."
- **Why unresolved:** The entire evaluation (recognition error detection, grading agreement, EIR) is anchored to these human labels. Unmeasured inter-rater variability among the experts could affect the gold standard and thus all derived metrics.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conducting a multi-rater study where multiple experts independently grade and transcribe a subset of solutions, quantifying inter-rater reliability (e.g., Cohen's kappa) for both transcription and grading. This variance could then be factored into confidence intervals for model performance and EIR.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Can the LLM-as-a-judge pipeline for automated recognition error detection be replaced or augmented by a specialized, open-source model trained specifically for STEM handwriting error detection, to reduce dependence on powerful (and costly) commercial MLLMs like Gemini-2.5-Pro?
- **Basis in paper:** The validation (Table 2) and all core detection tasks rely on Gemini-2.5-Pro as the judge. The paper does not explore cheaper, dedicated alternatives.
- **Why unresolved:** Using a state-of-the-art commercial MLLM as the oracle for error detection creates a circular dependency and may not be scalable or cost-effective for widespread adoption.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Training/fine-tuning a smaller, open-source model (e.g., a text-based LLM or a multimodal model) on the manually annotated error detection data (from the observation set) and comparing its precision, recall, and cost against the Gemini-2.5-Pro judge on a held-out test set.

## Limitations
* **Scope narrowness:** The dataset and findings are limited to university-level circuit analysis; applicability to other STEM domains (e.g., mathematics, physics) or educational levels remains uncertain.
* **Judge dependency:** The LLM-as-a-judge detector relies on a proprietary MLLM (Gemini-2.5-Pro), which could introduce its own biases; human validation was performed on only 186 samples, leaving doubt about the robustness of the error labels.
* **Rubric specificity:** The Error Impact Rate and regrading heuristics are tightly coupled to the study’s fine-grained rubric; simpler rubrics or different subject areas may diminish the observed benefits.

## Confidence
- **Latent Error Exposure:** High. The item-level comparison directly reveals recognition errors missed by auto-grading.
- **Error Taxonomy & EIR:** Medium. The observed trend (symbolic errors higher EIR) is consistent, but concerns about taxonomy consistency and rubric dependence limit confidence.
- **Efficient Regrading:** Medium. Validation on the test set shows gains, but the generalizability of error patterns across models and domains is an open question.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-domain replication:** Test the evaluation framework on a new handwritten STEM dataset (e.g., physics mechanics or calculus) to see if the error taxonomy, EIR trends, and regrading heuristics hold.
2. **Gold-standard noise sensitivity:** Introduce controlled errors into the expert transcriptions and measure how the LLM-as-a-judge detector and derived metrics (EIR, regrading performance) degrade.
3. **Model-agnostic pattern validation:** Apply error patterns derived from one MLLM (e.g., Gemini-2.5-Pro) to detect errors in transcriptions from other MLLMs (e.g., GPT-5.1, Qwen3-VL-Plus) to assess transferability and adjust detection thresholds per model.
