# Close to Reality: Interpretable and Feasible Data Augmentation for Imbalanced Learning
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.13927
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13927
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: DPG‑da generates constraint‑satisfying synthetic minority samples, achieving the highest macro F1‑score rank in a Nemenyi post‑hoc test across 27 datasets with zero constraint violations.

## Executive Summary
Imbalanced classification suffers from poor minority class representation, and common oversampling methods like SMOTE often produce unrealistic samples that violate domain constraints. This paper introduces DPG‑da, a framework that extracts interpretable decision predicates from a random forest surrogate to define class‑specific feasible regions. A genetic algorithm then synthesizes samples within these regions, optimizing for constraint adherence, diversity, and sparsity. The approach guarantees zero constraint violations while improving macro F1 scores compared to state‑of‑the‑art baselines, albeit at a substantially higher computational cost. DPG‑da’s key advantages are its interpretability via explicit predicate rules and traceable generation process.

## Method Summary
DPG‑da operates in three stages: First, a random forest surrogate model is trained on the imbalanced data. Second, a Decision Predicate Graph (DPG) is built from the forest’s decision trees, extracting all predicates (feature‑operator‑threshold) that lead exclusively to minority class leaves; these constitute hard constraints defining the feasible region for synthetic minority samples. Third, for each original minority instance, a genetic algorithm evolves a population of candidates, using a composite fitness function that combines classification validity, strict constraint satisfaction, Euclidean distance (for diversity), and sparsity (ℓ0 norm). The top‑scoring synthetic samples are added to the training set, yielding an augmented dataset that respects domain rules and enhances minority class representation.

## Key Results
- DPG‑da achieves the highest average rank in macro F1‑score among compared methods (SMOTE, TVAE, SMOTE‑SVM, etc.) on 27 benchmark datasets (Nemenyi post‑hoc test).
- The method guarantees zero constraint violations, unlike baselines that frequently produce invalid samples.
- On violation‑prone benchmarks, DPG‑da significantly outperforms alternatives, demonstrating its ability to generate realistic, feasible augmentations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** DPGs extract interpretable, class‑specific decision predicates from a surrogate model to define hard boundaries (feasible regions) that synthetic samples must obey, preventing semantically invalid outputs.
- **Mechanism:** A random forest (RF) surrogate is trained on the imbalanced data. From its ensemble of decision trees, predicates of the form (feature, operator, threshold) are extracted and aggregated into a weighted graph (DPG). Class‑bound predicates—conjunctions of predicates that consistently lead to a specific class leaf—are then derived. These define coordinate‑wise bounds (e.g., `Age ≥ 30`) that delineate the feasible region for that class.
- **Core assumption:** The decision logic captured by the RF surrogate meaningfully approximates the true, constraint‑compliant data manifold for the minority class. If the surrogate is poorly calibrated or the class boundaries are highly non‑linear and not well‑approximated by axis‑aligned splits, the extracted predicates may be misrepresentative.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  Abstract: “extracts interpretable decision predicates from trained models to capture domain rules and enforce them during sample generation.”  
  Section 4.1: Details the RF surrogate choice and predicate extraction, noting that DPGs provide “explicit descriptions of feasible regions.”  
  Corpus: Weak direct evidence; neighboring papers focus on SMOTE variants/VAEs without explicit rule extraction. Assumption: DPG‑based constraint extraction is novel in this context.
- **Break condition:** If the RF surrogate has low fidelity (e.g., high error on minority class), the derived predicates will be inaccurate, either overly restrictive (stifling diversity) or too permissive (allowing violations).

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** A genetic algorithm (GA) with a composite fitness function generates diverse, sparse, constraint‑adhering synthetic samples by optimizing within the DPG‑defined feasible region.
- **Mechanism:** Starting from a minority‑class query sample, the GA evolves a population of candidate synthetic samples. The fitness function combines: (1) **Classification validity** (candidate and query share predicted class), (2) **Constraint adherence** (satisfies all class‑bound predicates), (3) **Distance** (Euclidean distance from query, for diversity), and (4) **Sparsity** (ℓ0 norm, favoring small feature changes). Weighted sum guides evolution toward valid, diverse, and interpretably modified samples.
- **Core assumption:** These four objectives are sufficiently aligned with the goal of improving minority‑class representation without introducing noise or unrealistic records. Overweighting any single objective (e.g., distance over constraints) could degrade utility.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  Section 4.2: Presents the exact fitness function and stopping criterion, stating it “ensures that generated data points are both diverse and strictly confined to feasible regions.”  
  Appendix B: Ablation study shows performance is not hypersensitive to weight choices, supporting the robustness of the multi‑objective formulation.  
  Corpus: Neighbors use GAs/variational methods for generation but without explicit constraint terms; DPG‑da’s novelty is the constraint‑integrated fitness.
- **Break condition:** If the weight configuration is poorly chosen (e.g., constraints underweighted), the GA may produce invalid samples. If the feasible region is too small/empty due to over‑restrictive predicates, the GA cannot find viable candidates.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The augmentation process is interpretable and traceable, allowing users to understand how and why synthetic samples are generated and to validate their realism.
- **Mechanism:** (i) **Constraint transparency:** The extracted class‑bound predicates are human‑readable rules (e.g., `Glucose < 140`) that define the augmentation boundaries. (ii) **Evolution traceability:** The GA’s generational progression is logged, enabling visualization of feature‑wise deltas (∆) from the query sample to the final synthetic sample (e.g., Figure 8). This shows which features changed, by how much, and when convergence occurred.
- **Core assumption:** Users (e.g., domain experts) can meaningfully interpret simple threshold rules and feature change patterns to audit synthetic data realism. For highly complex or interacting constraints (e.g., `if A then B`), this interpretability degrades.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  Section 4.3: Explicitly defines interpretability as “traceability and interpretation of the synthetic data generation process,” and provides the Wisconsin/Pima/Ecoli examples with feature change visualizations.  
  Abstract: “offering clear, interpretable explanations of the over‑sampled data.”  
  Corpus: Neighbors use post‑hoc XAI (SHAP/LIME) *after* generation; DPG‑da’s novelty is inherent interpretability *during* generation via rules and traces.
- **Break condition:** If the DPG produces a large, complex set of predicates, the constraints become unwieldy for human comprehension. If the GA evolves via many small, distributed changes, the “story” of a single meaningful transformation is lost.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Decision Predicate Graphs (DPGs)
- **Why needed here:** DPGs are the core mechanism for extracting global, interpretable decision logic from the RF surrogate. They convert black‑box ensemble decisions into a structured graph of predicates that can be programmatically queried to define class‑specific feasible regions.
- **Quick check question:** How does a DPG differ from simply collecting all decision tree split rules from a random forest, and why is the graph structure important?

### Concept: Constraint Satisfaction in Data Generation
- **Why needed here:** The entire value proposition of DPG‑da is avoiding violation‑prone samples. Understanding what a “constraint” is in this context (a Boolean predicate over features), how it is evaluated, and why hard enforcement is critical for high‑stakes domains is foundational.
- **Quick check question:** Given a set of predicates like `x1 ≥ 5`, `x2 ≤ 10`, what is the feasible region, and how would you programmatically check if a sample violates any?

### Concept: Multi‑objective Heuristic Optimization (Genetic Algorithms)
- **Why needed here:** The GA is the workhorse that searches the feasible region for diverse, sparse samples. Understanding the basics of population initialization, selection, crossover, mutation, and fitness evaluation is necessary to grasp how constraints are enforced *during* search rather than as a post‑hoc filter.
- **Quick check question:** In the DPG‑da fitness function, what is the role of the `(1 - S(xa, xq))` term, and how does a GA use this scalar value to guide evolution?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  SurrogateRF -> DPGExtractor -> ConstraintExtractor -> FeasibilityChecker  
  For each query: GA (with FeasibilityChecker) -> synthetic_sample  
  AugmentationPipeline: original_minority ∪ synthetic_samples -> augmented_dataset
- **Critical path:** Training Data → SurrogateRF.fit() → DPGExtractor.build(RF) → ConstraintExtractor.get_class_predicates(class=minority) → **For each query sample:** GA.run(query, constraints=P_minority) → synthetic_sample → augmented_dataset = original_minority ∪ {synthetic_samples}.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - **Validity vs. Diversity:** Hard constraints guarantee no violations but can shrink the feasible region, potentially limiting diversity. Weight `w1` trades this off.  
  - **Interpretability vs. Automation:** The traceability is a feature, but the GA’s stochastic nature means the *reason* for a specific change isn’t causal—it’s just a high‑fitness mutation. True causal explanations would require more structure.  
  - **Computational Cost vs. Guarantee:** The GA is slow (~30 min on larger datasets). A simpler sampler (e.g., random perturbation + rejection) would be faster but might not efficiently explore the feasible region or balance multiple objectives.  
  - **Surrogate Fidelity vs. Complexity:** A deeper RF yields more predicates (potentially finer‑grained constraints) but risks overfitting and a more fragmented feasible region. Simpler RFs are faster but may miss important boundaries.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - **Runtime explosion:** GA does not converge within max generations → feasible region is extremely fragmented or weights cause endless exploration.  
  - **Zero valid samples:** `FeasibilityChecker` rejects 100% of GA candidates → `P_minority` is empty or contradictory (often from a poor RF).  
  - **Performance drop vs. baseline:** F1‑score after augmentation is lower than no augmentation → synthetic samples are either too similar to original (no new info) or violate implicit manifold structure even if predicates are satisfied.  
  - **High violation rate in *output*:** If post‑hoc validation on a held‑out set shows violations, the `ConstraintExtractor` likely missed some critical predicates (RF not capturing all rules).
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Constraint Extraction Validation:** Manually define a simple toy dataset with known valid ranges (e.g., `0 < age < 120`, `income ≥ 0`). Train a surrogate, extract DPG predicates, and verify they match or are subsets of the known constraints. This tests if the pipeline correctly captures basic rules.  
  2. **GA Feasibility Sanity Check:** On a dataset with a clearly defined minority class (e.g., Pima diabetes), run the GA with `w1` (constraint weight) set very high. Verify that 100% of generated samples satisfy all `P_minority` predicates (using `FeasibilityChecker`). This tests the constraint enforcement mechanism in isolation.  
  3. **End‑to‑End Augmentation Quality:** Compare a single run of DPG‑da (with default weights) against SMOTE on a small, violation‑prone dataset (e.g., Wisconsin). Visually inspect pairwise scatter plots of augmented samples (as in Figure 10) to confirm DPG‑da stays within valid ranges while SMOTE produces outliers. Also, train a simple classifier (e.g., Decision Tree) on both augmented sets and compare F1 on a held‑out test set.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper identifies four unresolved issues:

1. **Scalability & deep learning integration:** How can DPG‑da be scaled to handle very large datasets and integrated into deep learning pipelines for high‑stakes applications? The current GA and RF surrogate impose high computational overhead.
2. **Surrogate model choice:** To what extent does the choice of surrogate model for constraint extraction affect DPG‑da’s generated sample quality, classifier performance, and interpretability? The paper only experiments with RFs; alternatives like gradient‑boosted trees or rule‑based models may yield different constraints.
3. **Automated weight tuning:** Can the manually weighted fitness function be replaced by an automated multi‑objective optimization approach (e.g., NSGA‑II) that optimally balances constraint adherence, diversity, and sparsity without degrading performance? The current weights are empirically tuned and may not generalize.
4. **Complex constraint handling:** Beyond axis‑aligned feature thresholds, can DPG‑da’s predicate extraction and enforcement be extended to handle more complex domain constraints, such as multi‑feature inequalities or non‑linear relationships? The current method is limited to coordinate‑wise thresholds.

## Limitations
- **Surrogate fidelity risk:** Valid constraints depend on the RF accurately approximating the minority‑class manifold; poor surrogate calibration leads to inaccurate or overly restrictive predicates.
- **Weight sensitivity:** Although Appendix ablations suggest robustness, extreme or unbalanced weight choices may cause constraint neglect or stagnation.
- **Computational cost:** The GA runtime (e.g., ~30 minutes on larger datasets) may preclude use in time‑sensitive or large‑scale applications, despite the zero‑violation guarantee.
