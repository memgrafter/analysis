# On-Average Stability of Multipass Preconditioned SGD and Effective Dimension

## Quick Facts

- **arXiv ID**: 2603.11989  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11989  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: Excess risk bounds for multipass preconditioned SGD are derived via on‑average stability, yielding an effective dimension that combines the population Hessian, gradient noise covariance, and preconditioner; optimal rates achieved with \(P=H^{-1}\) and alignment measured by \(\kappa(PH)\).

## Executive Summary

This paper investigates the generalization behavior of multipass preconditioned stochastic gradient descent (PSGD) using on‑average algorithmic stability. The analysis explicitly accounts for correlations between iterates across multiple passes over the data, which is typical in practical training. A novel stability argument yields excess risk bounds expressed through an effective dimension that aggregates the population Hessian \(H\), gradient noise covariance \(\Sigma\), and preconditioner \(P\). For smooth strongly convex losses, the bound scales as \(\frac{1}{t}\operatorname{tr}(PHP\Sigma_S) + \operatorname{tr}(P\Sigma)(\frac{1}{\sqrt{nt}}+\frac{1}{n})\); choosing \(P=H^{-1}\) recovers the optimal rate and aligns with the lower bound. For non‑convex smooth losses satisfying a Polyak‑Łojasiewicz condition, the asymptotic excess risk depends only on \(\operatorname{tr}(H^{-1}\Sigma)\) and becomes independent of \(P\). Lower bounds confirm the optimality of these effective‑dimension dependencies and show that a poorly aligned preconditioner (large \(\kappa(PH)\)) can degrade the rate by a factor up to \(\kappa(PH)\). The results reveal a trade‑off: the preconditioner simultaneously influences optimization speed and statistical stability, with optimal alignment (\(\kappa(PH)\) small) delivering the best of both.

## Method Summary

The paper presents a theoretical analysis without accompanying empirical reproduction notes. Its core method is an on‑average stability framework adapted to multipass SGD with a fixed preconditioner. The analysis tracks the covariance of the gradient noise induced by data sampling and the dependence across passes, using martingale techniques and concentration inequalities. This yields a bound on the stability coefficient that incorporates the effective dimension \(\operatorname{tr}(PHP\Sigma_S)\) and an additional term \(\operatorname{tr}(P\Sigma)\). The stability bound is then translated into an excess risk bound under standard smoothness and (strongly) convexity or Polyak‑Łojasiewicz assumptions. The approach carefully handles the correlation between iterates, which is a key challenge in multipass analyses.

## Key Results

- Excess risk for smooth strongly convex losses: \(\frac{1}{t}\operatorname{tr}(PHP\Sigma_S) + \operatorname{tr}(P\Sigma)(\frac{1}{\sqrt{nt}}+\frac{1}{n})\).
- For non‑convex smooth losses satisfying the Polyak‑Łojasiewicz condition, asymptotic excess risk depends only on \(\operatorname{tr}(H^{-1}\Sigma)\) and is independent of \(P\).
- Lower bounds confirm the optimality of effective‑dimension dependencies and show that a large condition number \(\kappa(PH)\) can worsen the rate by up to a factor \(\kappa(PH)\).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

The stability analysis works by decomposing the change in the algorithm’s output when a single training example is replaced into a sum of martingale differences corresponding to each pass. By controlling the covariance of the gradient noise across these correlated passes, the authors derive a tight bound on the on‑average stability coefficient. This bound captures the interplay between the preconditioner, the population Hessian, and the noise covariance. Classical results then convert this stability measure into an excess risk bound. The key insight is that the covariance structure of the noise—shaped by the preconditioner and data geometry—directly governs the generalization ability, even when data is reused across multiple epochs.

## Foundational Learning

- **On‑average stability**: Quantifies the expected change in the learned predictor when a training point is altered; needed to bound generalization error via uniform stability theory. *Quick check*: Verify that the derived stability coefficient \(\beta(t)\) satisfies the claimed bound.
- **Effective dimension**: The scalar \(\operatorname{tr}(PHP\Sigma_S)\) summarizes problem complexity after preconditioning; needed to express excess risk compactly. *Quick check*: Compute effective dimension for given \(P, H, \Sigma_S\) and compare with empirical excess risk.
- **Preconditioning**: Scaling gradients by \(P\) to accelerate convergence; needed to improve both optimization and statistical properties. *Quick check*: Assess \(\kappa(PH)\) to evaluate alignment between \(P\) and \(H\).
- **Polyak‑Łojasiewicz (PL) condition**: A non‑convex analogue of strong convexity that ensures fast convergence; needed to extend results beyond convex losses. *Quick check*: Estimate the PL constant from the loss curvature.
- **Population Hessian \(H\) and noise covariance \(\Sigma\)**: Second‑order statistics of the loss; needed to characterize optimization dynamics and stochastic effects. *Quick check*: Estimate \(H\) and \(\Sigma\) from data via sample averages or use known values in synthetic settings.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**:  
  Initialize \(\theta_0\) → for each pass \(m=1,\dots,M\) and iteration \(t\): sample minibatch → compute gradient \(g_t\) → precondition: \(P g_t\) → update \(\theta_t = \theta_{t-1} - \eta P g_t\).
- **Critical path**: The iterative update loop is critical; each step depends on the previous parameters and the random minibatch, with dependencies across passes due to parameter reuse.
- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - Preconditioner choice: a well‑aligned \(P\) (small \(\kappa(PH)\)) speeds up optimization and yields optimal statistical rates, but computing \(H^{-1}\) is often infeasible.  
  - Poor alignment (large \(\kappa(PH)\)) can degrade both convergence and generalization by a factor up to \(\kappa(PH)\).  
  - Multiple passes improve optimization but introduce correlations that complicate stability analysis.
- **Failure signatures**:  
  - Excess risk bound becomes vacuous if data passes are highly correlated and mixing conditions are violated.  
  - Large \(\kappa(PH)\) inflates the bound, signalling poor generalization.  
  - Ignoring estimation error of \(H\) and \(\Sigma\) may lead to over‑optimistic predictions in practice.
- **First experiments** (3 exact):  
  1. Empirical validation of excess risk bound on synthetic quadratic loss with known \(H,\Sigma\), varying \(P\) (including \(H^{-1}\)), plotting excess risk vs. effective dimension.  
  2. Systematic variation of \(\kappa(PH)\) (by choosing differently aligned \(P\)) to measure impact on convergence speed and final generalization error.  
  3. Test asymptotic independence of \(P\) under a PL‑satisfying non‑convex loss (e.g., a shifted quadratic) to confirm theoretical prediction.

## Limitations

- Assumptions of smoothness, strong convexity, or Polyak‑Łojasiewicz are often unrealistic for modern non‑convex models like deep neural networks.
- The stability analysis requires strict control of data‑pass correlations; mixing conditions are left informal and may be hard to verify in practice.
- Bounds depend on unknown population quantities (\(H, \Sigma\)) and neglect estimation error; the fixed‑preconditioner setting excludes adaptive methods like Adam.
