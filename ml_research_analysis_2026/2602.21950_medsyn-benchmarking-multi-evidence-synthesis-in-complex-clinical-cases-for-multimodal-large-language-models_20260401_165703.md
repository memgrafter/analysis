# MEDSYN: Benchmarking Multi-EviDence SYNthesis in Complex Clinical Cases for Multimodal Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.21950
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21950
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Top MLLMs match/exceed human experts in generating differential diagnoses but show a ~20 percentage point larger performance gap to final diagnosis selection, indicating a failure in evidence synthesis.

## Executive Summary
Existing multimodal benchmarks are inadequate for evaluating large language models on complex, real-world clinical diagnosis, which requires synthesizing heterogeneous evidence. This paper introduces MEDSYN, a multilingual benchmark featuring up to seven distinct visual evidence types per case. It evaluates two critical tasks: differential diagnosis (DDx) generation and final diagnosis (FDx) selection. Results show state-of-the-art MLLMs achieve strong DDx coverage comparable to clinicians (e.g., GPT-5.2: 88.55%), but all exhibit a substantially larger DDx-to-FDx performance gap than human experts (~20 pp vs. ~5 pp). This reveals a fundamental shortcoming in synthesizing evidence to converge on a single diagnosis. Ablation studies identify overreliance on less discriminative textual evidence and a cross-modal utilization gap, quantified by a new Evidence Sensitivity metric. Targeted interventions like prompt refinement and supervised fine-tuning that narrow these gaps yield measurable improvements in FDx accuracy.

## Method Summary
MEDSYN is a curated benchmark of complex clinical cases sourced from NEJM and Chinese medical journals, provided in English and Chinese. Each case contains textual clinical evidence (e.g., history, physical exam) and multiple visual clinical evidence (CE) types (e.g., CT, MRI, microscopy, lab images)––up to seven per case. The benchmark defines two evaluation tasks: (i) **DDx generation**, where models produce an open-ended list of potential diagnoses, scored via GPT-5 for quality and coverage of the ground-truth final diagnosis; (ii) **FDx selection**, a multiple-choice task where models select the correct diagnosis from a set of distractors. Crucially, for FDx, each visual CE is presented *either* as a raw image *or* an expert-derived textual summary (never both), enabling analysis of modality-dependent evidence utilization. Models are evaluated zero-shot using a framework like VLMEvalKit. To diagnose failure modes, the paper introduces **Evidence Sensitivity**, a metric that quantifies how much a model's posterior belief over diagnoses changes when a specific piece of evidence is removed, computing this separately for when the evidence is provided as an image vs. text. The resulting difference, expressed as NMSE relative to the identity line, measures the "cross-modal CE utilization gap." Ablations involve removing or randomizing textual evidence and targeted fine-tuning.

## Key Results
- MLLMs achieve DDx coverage rates (e.g., GPT-5.2: 88.55%) matching or exceeding expert clinicians, demonstrating capability in hypothesis generation from multimodal inputs.
- All evaluated MLLMs exhibit a much larger DDx–FDx performance gap (~20 percentage points) compared to expert clinicians (~5 pp), indicating a failure in evidence synthesis to select a singular correct diagnosis.
- Ablations reveal overreliance on textual evidence (REMOVE-TEXT, RANDOM-TEXT improve FDx accuracy) and a systematic cross-modal utilization gap (large NMSE) where visual evidence is underutilized; narrowing this gap via prompt refinement or fine-tuning improves FDx accuracy by up to 1.51 pp.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Textual Evidence Overreliance
**Claim:** MLLMs over-rely on less discriminative textual clinical evidence (e.g., medical history) due to an inherent bias toward linguistic tokens, which miscalibrates evidence weighting and harms diagnostic accuracy.  
**Mechanism:** The model's attention mechanism disproportionately allocates processing to text tokens over image tokens. When coherent clinical text is present, it "distracts" the model from grounding its reasoning in more discriminative visual evidence (e.g., imaging, microscopy). Removing or randomizing this text attenuates the bias, forcing the model to attend more to image tokens and, counterintuitively, improving final diagnosis (FDx) accuracy despite less total evidence.  
**Core assumption:** The model's internal attention distribution is semantics-sensitive; coherent clinical narrative creates a stronger "pull" on attention than scrambled text, diverting capacity from visual feature extraction.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] "overreliance on less discriminative textual CE (e.g., medical history)"  
- [section 4.3, Finding 1] Ablation studies (REMOVE-TEXT, RANDOM-TEXT) show consistent FDx accuracy gains; RAPT analysis reveals attention shifts toward image tokens when text is randomized.  
**Break condition:** If removing textual evidence consistently *harms* FDx accuracy across multiple models and contexts, the core claim of "miscalibration due to overreliance" would be falsified.

### Mechanism 2: Cross-Modal Utilization Gap
**Claim:** Cross-modal misalignment—information loss or noise when mapping raw images to visual tokens—creates a systematic "cross-modal CE utilization gap," where the model assigns drastically different diagnostic importance to identical evidence depending on whether it is input as an image or a textual summary.  
**Mechanism:** The vision encoder fails to preserve all diagnostically relevant features from the raw pixel data. Consequently, the model's posterior belief update (measured via Jensen-Shannon divergence) is smaller for the same evidence provided as an image versus a perfect textual summary. This gap is quantified by Evidence Sensitivity (NMSE relative to the identity line S_image vs S_text) and correlates inversely with overall FDx accuracy.  
**Core assumption:** The model's internal representation of visual evidence is less information-rich than the expert-derived textual summary of that same evidence, leading to under-sensitivity.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] "cross-modal CE utilization gap... quantified via a new Evidence Sensitivity metric... a smaller gap correlates with higher diagnostic accuracy"  
- [section 4.3, Finding 2] Figure 6 shows L-shaped distributions (S_image << S_text) for microscopy; Table 5 shows lower NMSE correlates with higher accuracy.  
- [section B.4] Figures 13-14 extend this pattern to Laboratory, MRI, Clinical Photography, X-ray.  
**Break condition:** If NMSE values cluster tightly around zero (S_image ≈ S_text) for all CE types across models, or if no correlation exists between NMSE and accuracy, the claim of a pernicious, performance-hindering gap would be invalidated.

### Mechanism 3: Hypothesis Generation vs. Synthesis Failure
**Claim:** The primary failure mode in complex clinical diagnosis is not in generating a plausible differential diagnosis (DDx) but in the subsequent evidence synthesis step required to select the single correct final diagnosis (FDx), revealing a gap between hypothesis generation and evidence-weighted verification.  
**Mechanism:** MLLMs can access and recall relevant medical conditions from their training data when presented with heterogeneous multimodal inputs (hence good DDx coverage). However, the process of weighing conflicting evidence from multiple CE types, discounting less specific findings, and converging on a singular, most probable diagnosis—a core clinical reasoning skill—is not robustly learned. This manifests as a large DDx-FDx performance gap (~20 pp) versus experts (~5 pp).  
**Core assumption:** DDx generation and FDx selection test distinct cognitive operations; success on the former does not guarantee competence on the latter. The gap magnitude is a direct measure of synthesis failure.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] "all MLLMs exhibit a much larger DDx–FDx performance gap compared to expert clinicians"  
- [section 4.2, Table 2 & Figure 1b] Quantifies the gap across 18 models vs. physicians.  
- [section 4.2] "this suggests that while MLLMs can effectively enumerate potential diagnoses, they cannot yet... synthesize them into a singular, accurate diagnosis."  
**Break condition:** If models with high DDx coverage consistently achieve FDx accuracy within a few percentage points of their DDx score (gap ≤5 pp), similar to clinicians, the claimed synthesis failure mode is not universal.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Multimodal Fusion Bottlenecks**  
  **Why needed:** Understanding *how* image tokens and text tokens are combined in the model's Transformer layers is crucial. MEDSYN's findings imply fusion is superficial or biased, not allowing for deep, reciprocal evidence calibration.  
  **Quick check:** If you removed all text tokens from a case, how would you expect the model's FDx accuracy to change based on MEDSYN's findings, and why?
- **Concept: Evidence Calibration in Reasoning**  
  **Why needed:** The paper defines a failure as poor calibration—assigning diagnostic importance (sensitivity) not by objective discriminative power but by modality and format. This is a metacognitive skill for the model.  
  **Quick check:** What would an ideally "calibrated" model's Evidence Sensitivity plots (S_image vs. S_text) look like for all CE types?
- **Concept: Differential Diagnosis as a Scaffold**  
  **Why needed:** MEDSYN treats DDx generation not as the end goal but as an intermediate state. The architectural challenge is using the generated DDx set as a constraint or context for the subsequent synthesis to FDx.  
  **Quick check:** Why is evaluating FDx selection *from a model-generated DDx* a harder and more realistic test than standard multiple-choice with predefined distractors?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Raw CE Images/Text → Input Preprocessor → [ImageEncoder → Multimodal Projector] OR [TextEncoder] → LLM Backbone → Output Head
- **Critical path for FDx selection:**  
  `Raw CE Images/Text` → `Preprocessor` → `[ImageEncoder -> Projector] OR [TextEncoder]` → `LLM Backbone (cross-modal attention)` → `Attention-weighted evidence synthesis` → `FDx token prediction`.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - **Modality-specific vs. Shared Projectors:** Separate projectors for each CE type (e.g., radiology vs. pathology) might preserve domain-specific features better but increase parameters. A single shared projector risks generic, lossy representations.  
  - **Evidence Format at Inference:** Providing raw images vs. textual summaries trades realism for potentially clearer signal; MEDSYN highlights the cost of the image path.  
  - **Prompt Engineering vs. Model Weights:** Can the evidence utilization gap be closed solely by test-time prompts (as shown), or does it require costly SFT? The paper suggests both work, with SFT more durable.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - **High DDx Coverage + Low FDx Accuracy:** Classic signature of synthesis failure.  
  - **Large NMSEy=x:** Quantifies the cross-modal utilization gap.  
  - **RAPT Imbalance:** Disproportionate attention to text tokens vs. image tokens in mid-layers, which normalizes only when text is made uninformative (RANDOM-TEXT).  
  - **Specialty-specific dropouts:** e.g., poor performance in obstetrics/gynaecology may indicate missing or poorly fused CE types.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Baseline Replication:** Run the 18 models on MEDSYN's English FDx selection task (raw images) to reproduce the reported DDx-FDx gap and NMSE calculations.  
  2. **Ablation Chain:** For a single model (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL 7B), run the REMOVE-TEXT and RANDOM-TEXT ablations. Measure change in FDx accuracy and compute RAPT for text vs. image tokens across layers to confirm the text-distraction effect.  
  3. **Evidence Sensitivity Profiling:** For the same model, compute S(m)_image and S(m)_text for every CE type in the leave-one-out analysis. Calculate NMSE per CE type. Validate the hypothesis that Microscopy has the highest NMSE and that its NMSE correlates with accuracy drop.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **What are the root causes of the textual evidence bias in MLLMs (e.g., data distribution vs. architecture), and can it be reduced by changing training objectives or fusion mechanisms?**  
  *Basis:* The authors demonstrate that removing textual CE improves FDx accuracy and that text tokens receive disproportionate attention. The underlying cause is not investigated.  
  *Why unresolved:* Without knowing whether the bias stems from pretraining data composition or model design, effective mitigation remains speculative.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Comparative studies of models trained on balanced vs. text‑biased datasets, ablation of cross‑modal fusion layers, and probing attention patterns across architectures.
- **What explains the cross‑modal evidence utilization gap and can it be closed beyond prompt refinement through training or architecture changes?**  
  *Basis:* Evidence Sensitivity reveals a systematic L‑shaped distribution and correlates a smaller gap with higher accuracy. The paper narrows the gap via prompt refinement and targeted SFT but does not analyze its origins.  
  *Why unresolved:* The gap may arise from vision encoder limitations, projection‑layer issues, or insufficient multimodal alignment. Understanding this is key to robust improvements.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Experiments with alternative vision encoders, training objectives that explicitly align modalities, and layer‑wise analysis of cross‑modal interactions across model families.
- **How reliable are the reported performance gaps given the potential bias of using GPT‑5 as an evaluator, and what unbiased evaluation alternatives exist for medical reasoning?**  
  *Basis:* The Limitations section acknowledges that GPT‑5 as judge may introduce bias, yet the paper provides no quantification. Conclusions about evidence synthesis rely heavily on GPT‑5 scores.  
  *Why unresolved:* The magnitude and direction of evaluator bias are unknown; it could unfairly advantage certain models and invalidate the observed DDx‑FDx gap.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Human evaluation of a large, diverse sample of model outputs, correlation analyses between GPT‑5 and human judgments, and development of model‑agnostic metrics (e.g., expert‑curated answer keys or checklist‑based scoring).

## Limitations
- **Dataset accessibility:** MEDSYN is not yet public, blocking exact replication of preprocessing and case splits.
- **Evidence Sensitivity implementation:** The method to derive posterior p(y) from MLLMs (e.g., sampling strategy, candidate pooling) is unspecified, risking metric incompatibility.
- **Causal claims:** Mechanisms rely on correlational evidence (attention distributions, NMSE); direct proof of *why* attention shifts or representation loss occurs remains inferential.

## Confidence
- **DDx–FDx synthesis gap:** **High** – Consistently observed across 18 models vs. clinicians with clear quantitative metrics.
- **Textual evidence overreliance:** **Medium** – Ablations show performance gains when text is removed/randomized, but the assumed attention-semantics link is not directly validated.
- **Cross-modal utilization gap:** **Medium** – NMSE–accuracy correlation is demonstrated, yet the claim that vision encoder degradation *causes* the gap is not proven.

## Next Checks
1. **Dataset audit:** Upon release, verify case structure (CE type pairing, multilingual splits) and benchmark integrity (label correctness, image–summary alignment).
2. **Posterior extraction protocol:** Standardize Evidence Sensitivity calculation: define p(y) estimation (e.g., top-k logits, temperature sampling), candidate diagnosis set handling, and JSD numerical stability.
3. **Break condition test for Mechanism 1:** Replicate REMOVE-TEXT/RANDOM-TEXT ablations on at least three additional MLLMs (e.g., GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet); if FDx accuracy *decreases* with text removal, the overreliance claim is falsified.
