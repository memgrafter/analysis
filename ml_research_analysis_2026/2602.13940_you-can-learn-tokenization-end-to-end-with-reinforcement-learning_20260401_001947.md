# You Can Learn Tokenization End-to-End with Reinforcement Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.13940  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13940  
- **Reference count**: 27  
- **Primary result**: End-to-end RL tokenization yields 4% lower bits-per-byte (1.297 vs. ~1.36) and 2.4× absolute LAMBADA accuracy improvement (0.086 vs. 0.036) with <0.1% additional pretraining compute.

## Executive Summary
This paper replaces the standard hardcoded tokenization step in large language models with a learnable, stochastic boundary predictor. The method uses a reinforcement learning score function estimator (REINFORCE) with variance reduction (time discounting, batch-relative advantages) to train a Bernoulli policy that decides token boundaries autoregressively. A U‑net architecture operates at the byte level: a sliding‑window encoder produces representations, the policy samples boundaries, downsampling yields tokens, a full‑attention token‑level transformer processes them, and upsampling reconstructs byte predictions. At the 100‑million‑parameter scale, the approach discovers meaningful splits (e.g., at whitespace) without linguistic bias, significantly improving compression (bits‑per‑byte) and zero‑shot language understanding while incurring negligible extra compute.

## Method Summary
The core contribution is a policy gradient framework for token boundary learning. Byte sequences are encoded with a decoder‑only transformer using sliding‑window attention. At each position, a Bernoulli policy π_θ(a_i=1\|x_{≤i}, a_{<i}) samples whether to end the current token; its logits combine the current byte representation with a weighted sum of the last *w*=8 boundary decisions. Downsampling selects hidden states where boundaries occur, forming tokens processed by a full‑attention token transformer. Upsampling distributes token representations back to byte positions and adds residuals from the encoder. The loss combines: (1) next‑token cross-entropy (*L_auto*), (2) policy gradient with advantages derived from an early‑exit model’s reward (*L_π*), (3) a rate regularization (*L_target*) to keep the average downsample rate near a target (e.g., 1/5), and (4) a loss to train the early‑exit model (*L_early*). Advantages are computed per‑step as discounted cumulative rewards (γ=0.99) and centered within each batch. Training uses AdamW with moderate learning rates (1.5e‑3) and softcapping on logits.

## Key Results
- **Bits‑per‑byte on FineWeb Test**: 1.297, a 4% reduction compared to prior straight‑through methods (~1.36).  
- **LAMBADA zero‑shot accuracy**: 0.086, a 2.4× absolute gain over the previous maximum of 0.036.  
- **Compute overhead**: less than 0.1% additional FLOPs relative to standard pretraining.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The reinforcement learning approach tackles the non‑differentiability of token boundaries directly. The score‑function estimator provides unbiased gradients for the binary decisions. Variance is controlled via discounting and batch‑relative baselines, making training stable despite high‑dimensional sparse rewards. The early‑exit model offers a dense, per‑step reward: each boundary’s contribution to improving next‑byte prediction over a shallow model. This local supervision guides the policy toward splits that align with linguistic structure (e.g., whitespace) even without explicit bias. The U‑net design preserves fine‑grained byte information while allowing the token‑level transformer to capture higher‑order patterns, and the downsample‑rate regularization prevents collapse to degenerate policies. The combination yields better compression and downstream accuracy with minimal extra computation because boundary decisions are lightweight (small MLP on past contexts) and only a fraction of bytes become tokens.

## Foundational Learning

- **Reinforcement Learning (Policy Gradient)**: Optimizes stochastic policies via reward signals; enables learning discrete decisions through sampling. Quick check: Implement REINFORCE on a toy bandit problem and verify expected reward increases.
- **Score Function Estimator**: Provides gradient estimates for non‑differentiable sampling paths; e.g., REINFORCE uses likelihood ratio. Quick check: Derive gradient for Bernoulli policy and validate with numeric gradient check.
- **Variance Reduction (Baseline, Discounting)**: Baselines and discounting lower gradient variance for stable updates. Quick check: Measure gradient variance with/without baseline on a short MDP.

- **U‑net Architecture**: Downsamples to a coarser representation and upsamples back, preserving multi‑scale information. Common in vision and segmentation. Quick check: Build a tiny U‑net for an image autoencoder and inspect skip connections.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Input Bytes → Byte-Encoder (sliding window) → Boundary Policy (Bernoulli) → Downsample → Token-Transformer (full attention) → Upsample (distribute‑then‑add) → Byte-Decoder → Predictions

**Critical path**:  
Forward pass encodes bytes, policy samples boundaries (using past decisions), downsample selects tokens, token transformer processes them, upsample reconstructs byte‑level features, decoder outputs next‑byte probabilities. Losses are computed: *L_auto* from decoder, *L_π* from policy gradients using per‑step rewards from early‑exit model, *L_target* from boundary rate, and *L_early* from early‑exit predictions. Policy gradient uses detached advantages (discounted rewards minus batch mean).

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- Sliding window size balances compute vs. ability to capture long‑range byte patterns (w=8 used).  
- Downsample rate target (π_target) trades compression for model capacity; too high loses information, too low increases compute.  
- Early‑exit model adds parameters but provides a stable, learned baseline.  
- Bernoulli independence simplifies policy but ignores global constraints; the window provides limited context.

**Failure signatures**:  
- *L_π* oscillates or becomes NaN → reduce λ_π, increase batch size, verify advantage normalization.  
- Average downsample rate drifts far from target → adjust λ_target or scaling factor *D*.  
- Token boundaries appear random or uniform → early‑exit reward may be uninformative; check that early‑exit loss decreases.  
- Validation bits‑per‑byte does not improve despite training loss drop → possible overfitting to short sequences; check generalization on held‑out data.

**First experiments**:  
1. **Sanity check on tiny data**: Train on 1k sequences for 10 steps; confirm *L_π* has non‑zero gradients and that sampled boundary rates change (e.g., from 0.2 toward 0.2).  
2. **Boundary visualization**: Run forward pass on a simple English sentence (e.g., "The cat sat.") and plot π_θ(a_i). Expect peaks near whitespace/punctuation.  
3. **Ablate early‑exit baseline**: Train with λ_early=0 and compare validation bits‑per‑byte; significant degradation confirms its importance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Scaling to >10²¹ FLOP regimes**: Authors note U‑net superiority only emerges at extremely large scales, but experiments are at ~10¹⁸ FLOP (≈100M params). Does the method maintain benefits or become unstable at 10B+ scale?  
- **Adaptive downsample rate**: The target rate is fixed manually; could the model learn an optimal compression ratio automatically, e.g., via an auxiliary objective, to improve efficiency for different data or sizes?  
- **Advanced RL variance reduction**: Simpler techniques (discounting, batch baseline) are used; would actor‑critic methods (e.g., PPO) or a learned value head further stabilize training and sample efficiency?

## Limitations
- Dataset preprocessing details (filtering criteria, language/quality thresholds for FineWeb and CodeParrot) are unspecified, hindering exact replication.  
- Initialization of the early‑exit projection matrix *W_early* and several Gemma 2 component hyperparameters (RMSNorm ε, rotary base, GeGLU scaling) are unclear, requiring assumptions.  
- Evaluation protocols for zero‑shot benchmarks (prompt formats, few‑shot examples, normalization) and the exact compute baseline for the overhead claim are ambiguous.
