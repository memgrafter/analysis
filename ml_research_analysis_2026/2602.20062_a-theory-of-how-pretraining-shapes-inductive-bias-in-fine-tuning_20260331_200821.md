# A Theory of How Pretraining Shapes Inductive Bias in Fine-Tuning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.20062
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20062
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** The theory identifies four fine-tuning regimes and a trade-off between sparsity bias and pretraining dependence; scaling down relative scale (λPT) and absolute scale improves generalization on tasks that reuse a subset of pretrained features.

## Executive Summary
Pretraining followed by fine-tuning is widely used, but a theoretical understanding of how weight initialization influences feature reuse and refinement is lacking. This paper develops an analytical theory for the pretraining-fine-tuning pipeline in diagonal linear networks, deriving exact generalization error expressions as a function of initialization parameters (absolute scale cPT, relative scale λPT, readout scale γFT) and task statistics (sparsity, overlap). The theory introduces ℓ-order and pretraining dependence (PD) to characterize the inductive bias. It reveals four distinct fine-tuning regimes and a fundamental trade-off: lower λPT and cPT promote both sparsity bias and pretraining dependence, leading to superior performance when the target task relies on a sparse subset of pretrained features. Empirical validation on ResNet-18 trained on CIFAR-100 confirms that scaling early layers and reducing readout initialization improves fine-tuning, matching theoretical predictions.

## Method Summary
The authors construct a tractable model: a diagonal linear network, where weight matrices are diagonal, allowing exact decoupling of parameters. They first pretrain on a source task, then fine-tune on a target task. The initialization of the fine-tuning stage is controlled by three scales: cPT (absolute magnitude of pretrained weights), λPT (relative scaling between feature and readout), and γFT (readout initialization scale). Using stochastic gradient descent, they derive the implicit bias of the optimization, which leads to an effective regularizer on the feature norms. The ℓ-order measures the strength of this regularizer (e.g., ℓ2 vs ℓ1), while pretraining dependence (PD) quantifies how much the solution relies on the pretrained features. Generalization error is expressed analytically in terms of these quantities and task sparsity/overlap. The method yields precise predictions about which initialization parameters lead to which (ℓ-order, PD) regime.

## Key Results
- Four fine-tuning regimes are identified: (1) rich pretraining-independent (ℓ-order=1, PD=0), (2) lazy pretraining-dependent (ℓ-order=2, PD=−1), (3) lazy pretraining-independent (ℓ-order=2, PD=0), and (4) intermediate pretraining-dependent rich (ℓ-order<2, PD<0).
- A trade-off between ℓ-order and PD: decreasing λPT (making it negative) reduces ℓ-order (promoting sparsity) but makes PD more negative (increasing pretraining dependence); Proposition 5.1 shows the attainable region is a triangle bounded by ℓ-order ∈ [1,2] and PD ≥ -(2-ℓ).
- For target tasks that are sparse and share features with pretraining, smaller λPT and lower cPT yield lower generalization error, confirming that both feature reuse (negative PD) and refinement (ℓ-order<2) are beneficial.
- Empirical experiments on ResNet-18/CIFAR-100 show that scaling early layers (κ<1) and using a smaller readout initialization (γFT) improves fine-tuning performance, consistent with the theory’s prediction that lower absolute and readout scales favor better generalization.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The mechanism hinges on the implicit bias of gradient descent in diagonal linear networks. When the network is initialized with a large absolute scale cPT, the optimization effectively applies weak regularization (ℓ-order=2) and the solution remains close to the pretrained weights, yielding high pretraining dependence (PD negative). Conversely, a small cPT increases regularization strength, encouraging sparser solutions (ℓ-order<2) but also making the solution more influenced by pretraining because the pretrained weights provide a strong prior. The relative scale λPT controls the balance between feature and readout initialization; a negative λPT amplifies the effect of cPT on sparsity, pushing ℓ-order below 2 while maintaining negative PD. The readout scale γFT directly scales the output and acts as an additional regularizer on the readout layer. The derived exact generalization error shows that for sparse target tasks (high ρF T) with moderate feature overlap (ρshared F T), the combination of ℓ-order<2 and PD<0 minimizes error, explaining why scaling down cPT, λPT negative, and small γFT improves fine-tuning. This reveals a nuanced interplay: the same knobs that increase reliance on pretraining also promote sparsity, and the optimal point lies in the intermediate pretraining-dependent rich regime.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Diagonal Linear Networks**  
   *Why needed:* Provide a mathematically tractable setting where parameters decouple, enabling exact characterization of implicit bias and generalization.  
   *Quick check:* What property of diagonal matrices makes them analytically convenient? (Answer: Element-wise operations decouple, so gradient dynamics can be solved independently across dimensions.)

2. **Implicit Bias**  
   *Why needed:* Explains why gradient-based optimization, without explicit regularization, converges to solutions that generalize well; in this theory, initialization scales determine the implicit penalty.  
   *Quick check:* How does the scale of initialization affect the implicit bias in linear networks? (Answer: Larger scales lead to weaker implicit regularization, approaching the maximum margin solution with ℓ2 bias, while smaller scales strengthen implicit regularization, introducing sparsity.)

3. **ℓ-order**  
   *Why needed:* Quantifies the strength of the implicit regularizer on feature norms (e.g., ℓ1, ℓ2, interpolated); directly influences sparsity of learned features.  
   *Quick check:* What does ℓ-order = 1 imply about the solution’s feature support? (Answer: It implies an ℓ1-like bias that yields exactly sparse support.)

4. **Pretraining Dependence (PD)**  
   *Why needed:* Measures how much the fine-tuned solution relies on the pretrained feature subspace; negative PD indicates that pretraining helps.  
   *Quick check:* If PD = -1, what does it say about alignment with pretrained features? (Answer: The solution is fully aligned with the pretrained features, i.e., no refinement.)

5. **Task Statistics (Sparsity ρF T, Overlap ρshared F T)**  
   *Why needed:* The target task’s sparsity and overlap with pretrained features determine which (ℓ-order, PD) regime yields lowest generalization error.  
   *Quick check:* For a target task with high sparsity and high overlap, which regime is predicted to be optimal? (Answer: Intermediate pretraining-dependent rich (ℓ-order<2, PD<0) where both sparsity and feature reuse are balanced.)

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Input Data → Pretrained Diagonal Features (W_PT) → Fine‑tuned Diagonal Adjustment (scaled by λPT, γFT) → Predictions. In detail: after pretraining, the weight matrix W_PT (diagonal) encodes source features. During fine‑tuning, a second diagonal matrix is initialized as a scaled version of W_PT (scales: λPT for feature-readout balance) and the readout is scaled by γFT. Training then adjusts both matrices, but the initialization determines the implicit bias.

### Critical Path
1. Choose initialization (cPT, λPT, γFT).
2. Pretrain on source task → obtain W_PT with norm ~ cPT.
3. Initialize fine‑tuning matrix using λPT and γFT.
4. Perform gradient descent on target task; solution converges to a point governed by implicit bias (ℓ-order, PD).
5. Compute generalization error as function of ℓ-order, PD, ρF T, ρshared F T.
6. Compare predicted error with empirical performance to identify regime.

### Design Tradeoffs
The core trade‑off (Proposition 5.1) restricts the attainable (ℓ-order, PD) pairs: as ℓ-order decreases (more sparsity bias), PD becomes more negative (greater pretraining dependence). Extreme ℓ-order=1 forces PD=0 (no pretraining benefit), while ℓ-order=2 allows PD as low as -1 (full dependence) but with no sparsity bias. Thus, one cannot simultaneously achieve maximal sparsity and complete independence from pretraining; the intermediate region (ℓ-order<2, PD<0) offers a compromise where both feature reuse and refinement occur. Additionally, cPT and γFT affect the absolute scale of regularization, with lower values generally improving generalization for sparse tasks but risking underfitting if too small.

### Failure Signatures
- **Regime mismatch:** If the model ends in a lazy regime (ℓ-order=2) for a sparse target task, validation performance will be poor because the solution lacks sparsity.
- **Excessive pretraining dependence without sparsity:** PD strongly negative (≈-1) with ℓ-order=2 may cause over‑reliance on irrelevant pretrained features when overlap is low, leading to high error.
- **Scaling issues:** Too large cPT or γFT increases the ℓ-order and reduces PD, moving the model away from the favorable rich regime for sparse tasks. Empirically, this manifests as slower convergence and higher training loss.
- **Breakdown of theory:** In real networks, deviations from the linear diagonal model (e.g., nonlinearities, convolutional structure) may cause the measured ℓ-order/PD to fall outside the predicted triangular region, indicating model mismatch.

### First Experiments
1. **Regime mapping on synthetic diagonal networks:** Sweep λPT over a range (e.g., -1 to 1) while fixing cPT and γFT. Use low‑dimensional Gaussian data with known sparsity ρF T and overlap ρshared F T. After pretraining and fine‑tuning, estimate ℓ-order and PD via the implicit bias equations and measure generalization error. Validate the four regimes and the trade‑off curve.
2. **Impact of absolute and readout scales:** For a sparse target task (e.g., ρF T=0.2) with moderate overlap, vary cPT (e.g., 0.1, 0.5, 1, 5) and γFT (e.g., 0.1, 0.5, 1) in a factorial design. Plot generalization error as a heatmap; expect a valley of low error at lower scales, confirming the theoretical prediction that smaller cPT and γFT improve performance when ℓ-order<2 and PD<0.
3. **Extension to ResNet‑18 and layer‑wise scaling:** Replicate the empirical validation from the paper but with systematic hyperparameter search: vary early‑layer scaling factor κ (multiplicative factor applied to pretrained weights of early layers) and readout scale (by initializing the final linear layer with different norms). Train on CIFAR‑100 with multiple random seeds, compute mean and confidence intervals for fine‑tuning accuracy, and check that optimal κ<1 and smaller readout scale align with the regime predicted for the target task’s sparsity (CIFAR‑100 has 100 classes, so moderate sparsity). Also estimate effective ℓ-order/PD from the fine‑tuned weights using linear approximations.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Extension to nonlinear networks:** How can the theoretical framework for PT+FT in diagonal linear networks be extended to nonlinear networks? The paper notes that extending the framework to nonlinear networks represents an important next step. The theory relies on linearity to derive exact implicit bias expressions and decoupling results. Nonlinear activations, depth, and convolutional architectures break the tractability and may introduce new inductive biases. Resolution would require developing analogous mean‑field or other analytic theories for nonlinear PT+FT; deriving precise implicit bias characterizations for simple nonlinear models (e.g., two‑layer ReLU networks) and testing predictions on modern architectures like ResNets or Transformers.
2. **Measuring ℓ-order and pretraining dependence in complex architectures:** How can ℓ-order and pretraining dependence be measured in complex architectures (e.g., using influence functions), and what do these measures reveal about feature reuse and adaptation during PT+FT? The paper suggests that ℓ-order and PD are analytically defined in terms of the implicit penalty in a linear model; in nonlinear nets they are not directly accessible and require practical estimation methods. Resolution would involve implementing estimators for ℓ-order and PD (e.g., via influence functions or probing) and applying them across diverse models (e.g., ResNets, ViTs, LLMs) trained with different initializations; correlating measured values with fine‑tuning generalization to map out regime transitions.
3. **Optimal initialization given task statistics:** For a given fine‑tuning task characterized by sparsity ρF T and overlap with pretraining ρshared F T, what initialization parameters (cPT, λPT, γFT) minimize generalization error? The paper identifies four learning regimes and shows that different task statistics favor different regimes, but it does not provide an explicit formula mapping task parameters to optimal initialization. The characterization is regime‑based and requires solving fixed‑point equations numerically for each task. Resolution would involve deriving approximate closed‑form solutions for optimal (cPT, λPT, γFT) as functions of ρPT, ρF T, ρshared F T, and αF T; validating with comprehensive simulations on diagonal linear networks and, where possible, on ResNets.
4. **Persistence of the ℓ-order/PD trade‑off in nonlinear networks:** Does the fundamental trade‑off between ℓ-order and pretraining dependence (Proposition 5.1) persist in nonlinear networks, or does nonlinearity enable regimes beyond the triangular region [1,2] for (ℓ-order, PD)? Proposition 5.1 is proven for diagonal linear networks; the authors acknowledge that extension to nonlinear networks is future work. The trade‑off may be an artifact of linearity. Resolution would involve numerically estimating ℓ-order and PD in well‑controlled nonlinear networks (e.g., two‑layer networks with various activations) under PT+FT to see if the (ℓ-order, PD) region remains confined to the triangle; or developing new theoretical analyses that characterize the attainable region for nonlinear models.

## Limitations
- The theory is restricted to linear diagonal networks; its extension to nonlinear deep models is unproven.
- Empirical validation is limited to ResNet‑18 on CIFAR‑100, so broader applicability across architectures and datasets remains uncertain.
- Assumptions such as infinite width and Gaussian data distributions may not hold in practical settings, potentially affecting the accuracy of predictions.
