# Near-Optimal Primal-Dual Algorithm for Learning Linear Mixture CMDPs with Adversarial Rewards

## Quick Facts

* **arXiv ID:** 2603.27884  
* **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27884  
* **Reference count:** 40  
* **Primary result:** PD-POWERS achieves $\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{d^2 H^3 K})$ regret and constraint violation for finite‑horizon linear mixture CMDPs with adversarial rewards, unknown transitions, and full‑information feedback.

## Executive Summary

This paper tackles safe reinforcement learning in constrained Markov decision processes (CMDPs) where the transition dynamics are governed by a linear mixture model and the rewards are adversarial. The authors propose PD‑POWERS, a primal‑dual policy optimization algorithm that works under full‑information feedback and without knowledge of the transition kernel. The key innovation lies in a regularized dual update that restores a drift‑based analysis despite the failure of strong duality under non‑stationary rewards, and an extension of weighted ridge regression that incorporates Bernstein‑type bonuses to tighten confidence intervals. The resulting theoretical guarantees match the minimax lower bound for unconstrained linear mixture MDPs up to logarithmic factors, making PD‑POWERS the first provably efficient algorithm for this challenging setting.

## Method Summary

PD‑POWERS operates in a primal‑dual framework. At each episode, it first estimates the unknown transition model using weighted ridge regression, where weights are derived from the current policy’s state‑action visitation distribution. To account for estimation uncertainty, the algorithm adds Bernstein‑type bonuses that depend on both the variance of the value estimates and the model error. These bonuses yield tighter confidence intervals compared to standard Hoeffding‑based approaches. The policy is then updated by solving an optimistic planning problem that maximizes the penalized objective. Simultaneously, the dual variables are updated with a regularization term that prevents them from growing too large; this regularization is crucial for enabling a drift‑based analysis in the presence of adversarial rewards, where standard Lagrange multiplier methods would fail. The interplay between the optimistic bonuses and the regularized dual updates ensures low regret and low constraint violation.

## Key Results

* Regret and constraint violation bounds both scale as $\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{d^2 H^3 K})$, where $d$ is the feature dimension, $H$ the horizon, and $K$ the number of episodes.
* The bounds are near‑optimal, matching the minimax lower bound for unconstrained linear mixture MDPs up to logarithmic factors.
* This is the first algorithm with provable efficiency for linear mixture CMDPs under adversarial rewards.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

Adversarial rewards break strong duality in CMDPs, invalidating standard primal‑dual analyses that rely on stationary reward distributions. PD‑POWERS overcomes this via two tightly coupled innovations. First, a regularization term in the dual update (e.g., adding an ℓ₂ penalty on the multipliers) prevents their magnitude from growing uncontrollably. This regularization is essential to maintain a stable drift structure: it ensures that the expected change in a carefully chosen potential function can be expressed as a sum of estimation errors, which are then controlled by the bonuses. Second, the transition model is estimated using weighted ridge regression that incorporates Bernstein‑type bonuses. Unlike Hoeffding‑based bonuses, Bernstein bonuses scale with empirical variance of the value estimates, yielding tighter confidence intervals around the unknown transition probabilities. The accuracy of these model estimates directly reduces planning error. The synergy arises because the drift analysis requires bounds on cumulative estimation errors; the Bernstein bonuses provide such bounds, while the regularized dual updates keep the multipliers bounded so that the drift cancellation terms do not explode. Consequently, both regret and constraint violation grow sublinearly with the horizon, matching the minimax lower bound for unconstrained linear mixture MDPs up to logarithmic factors.

## Foundational Learning

* **Primal‑dual optimization:** Combines a primal policy update with dual multiplier adjustments to handle constraints. *Why needed*: Enables balancing objective maximization and constraint satisfaction. *Quick check*: Verify that the dual update includes regularization to avoid explosion.
* **Constrained MDPs (CMDPs):** MDPs with additional constraints on expected cumulative costs. *Why needed*: Formalizes safety requirements. *Quick check*: Ensure constraints are linear in the occupation measure.
* **Linear mixture models:** Transition lies in the span of known basis functions. *Why needed*: Allows efficient model estimation via linear regression. *Quick check*: Confirm that the feature map satisfies the mixture property.
* **Weighted ridge regression:** Least‑squares estimator with importance weighting and $\ell_2$ regularization. *Why needed*: Produces consistent model estimates under distribution shift. *Quick check*: Check that weights correspond to visitation probabilities of the current policy.
* **Drift analysis:** Technique bounding change in a potential function across episodes. *Why needed*: Central to proving sublinear regret in non‑stationary settings. *Quick check*: Derive the drift inequality and verify the cancellation terms.
* **Bernstein‑type bonuses:** Bonus terms based on an estimate of variance, not just range. *Why needed*: Yields tighter confidence intervals, leading to sharper sample complexity. *Quick check:* Ensure the bonus scales with empirical variance of the value estimate.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Reward & Constraint Feedback → Weighted Ridge Regression (visitation weights) → Transition Model Estimate → Bernstein Bonus Computation → Optimistic Planning → Policy Update → New Policy.  
Dual Update (regularized) runs in parallel, using constraint violation signals to adjust multipliers.

### Critical Path
1. **Estimation:** Weighted ridge regression produces a model estimate and its uncertainty.  
2. **Bonus:** Bernstein bonuses are computed from the uncertainty and variance.  
3. **Planning:** The policy is updated by solving an MDP with reward plus bonus.  
4. **Dual update:** Regularized gradient step on multipliers.

All steps must execute within an episode; the quality of the estimate directly affects the optimism and thus the planning.

### Design Tradeoffs
* **Regularization strength** in the dual update: too small may break the drift; too large slows constraint adaptation.  
* **Bonus inflation factor:** Larger bonuses encourage exploration but may degrade performance; Bernstein terms help but require accurate variance estimation.  
* **Weighting scheme:** Using current visitation probabilities introduces bias but enables tighter confidence; alternative weighting schemes could simplify analysis at the cost of sample efficiency.

### Failure Signatures
* Sudden spikes in constraint violation indicate insufficient regularization of dual variables or under‑estimated bonuses.  
* Growing cumulative model error (e.g., large loss in ridge regression) leads to optimism mismatch and high regret.  
* Divergence of dual variables suggests the regularization parameter is too small or the step size is inappropriate.

### First Three Experiments
1. Run PD‑POWERS on randomly generated linear mixture CMDPs with known ground truth to verify $\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{K})$ scaling.  
2. Vary the feature dimension $d$ and horizon $H$ to confirm the predicted dependence on $d^2 H^3$.  
3. Compare against a baseline unconstrained linear mixture MDP algorithm (e.g., RLSVI) to demonstrate the impact of constraint handling and to check that the regret matches unconstrained lower bounds up to factors.

## Limitations

* No empirical validation; computational cost and practical performance are unknown.  
* The analysis assumes full‑information feedback and known feature dimension; robustness to noise, function approximation errors, or partial observations is not addressed.  
* The claim of near‑optimality relies on matching the unconstrained lower bound; a matching lower bound for the constrained setting is still missing, and the “first provably efficient” claim may be contested by recent optimistic CMDP algorithms.
