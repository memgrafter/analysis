# Aesthetic Assessment of Chinese Handwritings Based on Vision Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.26768  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26768  
- **Reference count:** 6  
- **Primary result:** Vision-language models generate multi-level feedback for Chinese handwriting, achieving an F1-score of 0.76 on quality grading (Task 1) and ranking third in the CCL 2025 evaluation with a final score of 0.52 on descriptive comment generation (Task 2).

## Executive Summary
Automated Chinese handwriting assessment traditionally provides only a numeric score, which lacks actionable guidance for learners. This paper leverages vision‑language models (VLMs) to produce multi‑level feedback: a three‑grade classification (Excellent, Medium, Unqualified) and natural‑language comments on structure and strokes. The authors compare two strategies—LoRA fine‑tuning on open‑source VLMs and in‑context learning with a closed‑source VLM—and find that LoRA fine‑tuning excels at the grading task (F1 0.76), while in‑context learning yields the best descriptive feedback (final score 0.52, third in CCL 2025).

## Method Summary
The study addresses two subtasks on single Chinese character images. **Task 1** classifies handwriting into three quality grades. **Task 2** generates free‑form comments describing structural and stroke aesthetics. Two main approaches are explored. First, **LoRA fine‑tuning** on the open‑source Qwen2.5‑VL‑72B‑Instruct (and QVQ‑72B‑Preview for Task 2) adapts the VLM to the task using a dataset of ~1500 training images formatted as single‑turn dialogues (image → grade or comment). Second, **in‑context learning** with the closed‑source `qwen‑vl‑max‑2025‑01‑25` constructs prompts containing a few demonstration examples (retrieved via image‑embedding similarity for Task 1, randomly sampled for Task 2) followed by the test query. For Task 2, they also experiment with feeding the predicted grade from Task 1 as additional context. Evaluation uses F1‑score (Task 1) and ROUGE‑1/2/L (Task 2), with a weighted final score.

## Key Results
- LoRA fine‑tuned Qwen2.5‑VL on the task‑specific dataset achieved an F1‑score of **0.76** on Task 1; expanding the training data with the external CHAED dataset (which has misaligned score definitions) caused performance to drop to 0.61.
- For Task 2, in‑context learning with random demonstration selection attained the highest final score of **0.52** (ROUGE‑L 0.56, ROUGE‑2 0.34, ROUGE‑1 0.63), placing third in the CCL 2025 shared‑task evaluation.
- Providing the predicted grade as input to the Task 2 LoRA model did not yield consistent improvements over image‑only conditioning.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1 – VLM joint understanding of visual and linguistic cues.** VLMs process the raw handwritten image together with a textual prompt, leveraging pretrained knowledge of Chinese character structure and aesthetic principles to reason about spatial relationships, stroke consistency, and balance. This bypasses the need for handcrafted features and enables both categorical grading (mapping visual patterns to the three‑grade taxonomy) and descriptive comment generation targeting specific dimensions. Evidence: The paper contrasts VLMs with traditional CV methods and reports successful grading and comment generation. A break condition: the VLM’s pretraining must include sufficient examples of Chinese calligraphy aesthetics; otherwise output may be generic or misaligned.

**Mechanism 2 – LoRA fine‑tuning efficiently transfers domain knowledge.** Low‑rank adaptation freezes the base VLM weights and injects trainable low‑rank matrices into attention layers, allowing task‑specific adaptation with minimal compute. The grading task mainly requires aligning the VLM’s existing visual representations to the three‑grade output space rather than learning new visual features from scratch. Evidence: LoRA fine‑tuning on the task‑specific dataset alone achieved the best Task 1 F1 (0.76); adding misaligned CHAED data hurt performance, confirming that careful data alignment is crucial. Break condition: if training data is too small or unrepresentative, or if the grading criteria diverge from the model’s pretraining, fine‑tuning may fail to converge.

**Mechanism 3 – In‑context learning elicits descriptive feedback via few‑shot imitation.** For Task 2, the closed‑source VLM conditions its generation on a few demonstration (image, comment) pairs drawn from the training set. The demonstrations implicitly instruct the model on the desired style, structure, and content coverage. Random selection of diverse examples worked best, suggesting that for open‑ended generation a variety of feedback patterns is more beneficial than strict similarity to the test image. Evidence: In‑context learning outperformed all LoRA variants on Task 2, yielding the highest final score. Break condition: if demonstrations are irrelevant, noisy, or fail to cover all evaluation dimensions (e.g., only strokes but no structure), the generated comments may be incomplete or off‑topic.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Vision‑Language Models (VLMs)**  
  *Why needed:* VLMs are the core engine that ingests both the handwritten image and textual instructions to produce outputs, replacing traditional feature extraction pipelines.  
  *Quick check question:* How might a VLM’s pretraining on natural images affect its ability to evaluate artistic handwriting, and what adjustments are needed?

- **Concept: Low‑Rank Adaptation (LoRA)**  
  *Why needed:* LoRA enables parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning of large VLMs with limited labeled data, preserving pretrained knowledge while adapting to the grading taxonomy.  
  *Quick check question:* Why does updating only low‑rank matrices during fine‑tuning preserve pretrained knowledge while allowing task‑specific adaptation?

- **Concept: In‑Context Learning (ICL)**  
  *Why needed:* ICL provides a training‑free way to steer VLM generation by including example demonstrations in the prompt, ideal for tasks where fine‑tuning data is scarce or where style imitation is key.  
  *Quick check question:* What factors (e.g., example similarity, ordering, prompt clarity) most affect the quality of ICL for a nuanced generation task like handwriting feedback?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Handwritten Image -> (Option A: LoRA fine‑tuning pipeline) Dataset -> Formatted dialogues -> LoRA adapter injection -> Fine‑tuned VLM -> Grade/Comment  
Handwritten Image -> (Option B: In‑context learning pipeline) Image embedding -> Similarity search (ChromaDB) -> Retrieval of k demonstrations -> Prompt composition with system message -> Closed‑source VLM -> Grade/Comment

**Critical path:**  
- Task 1 (LoRA): Prepare dialogue‑format training set -> run LoRA fine‑tuning on Qwen2.5‑VL (3 epochs, LR 1e‑4) -> load adapter -> predict grades on test images.  
- Task 2 (ICL): For each test image, retrieve k similar training examples (using multimodal‑embedding‑v1) -> construct prompt with system message, demonstrations, and test query -> call `qwen‑vl‑max` -> extract comment.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- LoRA offers lower inference cost and stable outputs but requires labeled data and training time; ICL avoids training and is flexible to new criteria via prompt changes but incurs high token costs and sensitivity to example quality.  
- Using external CHAED data increased training set size but degraded Task 1 performance due to misaligned grade definitions—quantity vs. label consistency tradeoff.  
- For Task 1, similarity‑based demonstration ordering helped; for Task 2, random ordering worked better—suggests diversity outweighs strict similarity for open‑ended generation.

**Failure signatures:**  
- LoRA: Sudden F1 drop (e.g., with CHAED expansion) indicates data misalignment or distribution shift.  
- ICL: Coherent but irrelevant comments if demonstrations omit key dimensions (structure, strokes) or if VLM ignores the “generate comment” instruction and outputs a grade letter instead.  
- General: Poor performance if image preprocessing (resolution, background) differs between training and test, or if prompt translation loses nuance.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Task 1 LoRA data alignment:** Train Qwen‑VL LoRA on (a) task‑specific data only vs. (b) task‑specific + CHAED. Quantify F1 to test whether external aesthetic scores align with the three‑grade taxonomy.  
2. **Task 2 LoRA with grade conditioning:** Train LoRA on inputs of image + predicted grade (from Task 1 model) vs. image only. Measure ROUGE improvement to assess whether providing a grade anchor helps generate targeted feedback.  
3. **Task 2 ICL example selection:** Compare similarity‑based retrieval vs. random sampling for selecting k demonstrations on a held‑out dev set. Identify optimal strategy (random) and hypothesize why diversity trumps strict similarity for comment generation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:** How can reinforcement learning (RL) be effectively designed to train VLMs for nuanced aesthetic reasoning in Chinese handwriting, particularly to handle subjective evaluation criteria and mitigate data scarcity?  
*Basis:* The paper explicitly proposes future work using RL but provides no implementation details or reward design.  
*What would resolve it:* A detailed RL framework proposal and pilot experiments showing improvement over supervised fine‑tuning.

**Open Question 2:** What specific characteristics of in‑context examples (e.g., diversity, error types, structural vs. stroke focus) most improve a VLM's ability to generate actionable, descriptive feedback, and why did random selection outperform similarity‑based selection for Task 2?  
*Basis:* The result is presented without analysis of the selected examples’ content.  
*What would resolve it:* Ablation study varying example attributes and human evaluation of output quality to identify correlated properties.

**Open Question 3:** To what extent do current ROUGE‑based metrics capture the educational utility and actionability of generated handwriting feedback, and what complementary metrics or human evaluations are needed?  
*Basis:* Task 2 scores rely solely on ROUGE overlap, but “actionable guidance” is a subjective quality not directly measured by lexical overlap.  
*What would resolve it:* Human study where educators/learners rate feedback pairs for usability, or development of a rubric‑based metric assessing coverage of structure/stroke dimensions.

**Open Question 4:** How can external aesthetic scoring datasets (like CHAED) be aligned or adapted to specific task grading criteria to avoid performance degradation when used for fine‑tuning?  
*Basis:* The paper notes that CHAED expansion hurt Task 1 performance due to misaligned grade definitions but offers no solution.  
*What would resolve it:* Experiments applying score mapping, criterion‑based relabeling, or domain adaptation to integrate CHAED with improved F1.

**Open Question 5:** Does the single‑character assessment paradigm scale to multi‑character words or sentences, where inter‑character spatial relationships and overall composition introduce new aesthetic dimensions?  
*Basis:* The study focuses solely on isolated characters; real‑world handwriting involves continuous text.  
*What would resolve it:* Dataset extension with multi‑character samples, model adaptation (e.g., sequence modeling), and evaluation of transfer from single‑character fine‑tuning.

## Limitations
- **Reproducibility blocked:** Access to the CCL 2025 test dataset is unavailable, forcing reliance on misaligned CHAED data for Task 1 and preventing exact replication of Task 2 results.  
- **Proprietary dependencies:** The best Task 2 method uses the closed‑source `qwen‑vl‑max` API and a proprietary embedding model (`multimodal‑embedding‑v1`), hindering independent verification.  
- **Metric validity:** sole reliance on ROUGE scores for Task 2 may not reflect the educational value of the generated feedback.
