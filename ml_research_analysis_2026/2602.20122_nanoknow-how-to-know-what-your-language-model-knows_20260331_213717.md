# NanoKnow: How to Know What Your Language Model Knows

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.20122
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20122
- **Reference count**: 35
- **Primary result**: NanoKnow partitions NQ and SQuAD into "supported" and "unsupported" splits, showing that closed‑book QA accuracy increases strongly with answer frequency in pre‑training data; external evidence mitigates this bias, yet parametric and external knowledge remain complementary, while irrelevant distractors degrade performance.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the challenge of understanding how large language models (LLMs) acquire knowledge by using fully transparent nanochat models trained on the FineWeb‑Edu corpus. It introduces NanoKnow, a benchmark that classifies questions from Natural Questions (NQ) and SQuAD as "supported" (answers present in the pre‑training corpus) or "unsupported" via a BM25 retrieval, string matching, and LLM verification pipeline. Experiments across eight nanochat checkpoints reveal four key findings: (1) closed‑book QA accuracy correlates strongly with answer frequency in pre‑training data; (2) providing external evidence from retrieval reduces this frequency dependence; (3) even with external evidence, models are more accurate on supported questions, demonstrating that parametric and external knowledge are complementary; and (4) non‑relevant distractors consistently harm accuracy, with greater damage from more distractors and earlier positions (e.g., LLM‑Judge accuracy for SQuAD drops from 0.478 to 0.367 when four distractors are added). The work provides a rigorous, reproducible method to dissect LLM knowledge sources and highlights the interplay between memorization and retrieval.

## Method Summary
NanoKnow’s objective is to create high‑precision relevance judgments (qrels) linking NQ and SQuAD questions to FineWeb‑Edu documents. The pipeline has three stages: (1) BM25 retrieval of the top‑100 candidate documents per question using Anserini/Pyserini; (2) case‑insensitive, whitespace‑normalized substring matching against the provided answer strings; (3) LLM‑based verification with Qwen3‑8B (greedy decoding) on a 512‑word context window (256 words before and after the answer) using a strict prompt that classifies matches as `TRUE` or `COINCIDENTAL`. Questions with at least one `TRUE` judgment are labeled "supported"; otherwise they are "unsupported". The benchmark’s validity is checked by verifying that closed‑book QA accuracy on unsupported questions is near zero (~1.5% for SQuAD, ~0.8% for NQ), confirming that these questions indeed lack parametric support.

## Key Results
- Closed‑book QA accuracy increases strongly with the frequency of the answer in the pre‑training corpus.
- Supplying external evidence (retrieved context) reduces the reliance on frequency and boosts overall accuracy, but models still perform significantly better on supported questions, showing that parametric and external knowledge are complementary.
- Non‑relevant distractors in the context consistently lower accuracy; the harm escalates with the number of distractors and their position (e.g., LLM‑Judge accuracy for SQuAD fell from 0.478 to 0.367 when four distractors were added).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The NanoKnow pipeline’s three‑stage filtering ensures a high‑precision split between supported and unsupported questions. The strong frequency dependence observed in closed‑book QA indicates that models rely on memorized training data for answering. External evidence mitigates this bias because retrieval provides factual context even for rarely seen answers, yet the persistent advantage on supported questions suggests that exposure during pre‑training aids the model’s ability to incorporate and reason over retrieved information. The degradation caused by irrelevant distractors reveals that models struggle to ignore noise, likely due to limited contextual filtering capabilities. While the paper establishes these empirical patterns, the underlying cognitive or architectural reasons for the complementarity and distractor sensitivity remain open.

## Foundational Learning
**BM25 Retrieval**: Lexical information retrieval method using term frequency, inverse document frequency, and length normalization. *Why needed*: Retrieves candidate FineWeb‑Edu documents for each question, the first step in identifying supported answers. *Quick check*: What two main factors does BM25 score combine?

**Retrieval‑Augmented Generation (RAG)**: Paradigm that combines parametric model generation with external knowledge retrieval. *Why needed*: The paper evaluates how external evidence changes the frequency dependence of closed‑book QA. *Quick check*: How does RAG differ from pure parametric generation?

**Answer Frequency**: Count of how often a specific answer string appears in the pre‑training corpus. *Why needed*: Serves as a proxy for the model’s exposure to a fact during pre‑training, driving the observed accuracy trends. *Quick check*: If a fact appears 1000 times versus 5 times, which is expected to yield higher closed‑book accuracy?

**Parametric Knowledge**: Information encoded in the model’s weights from pre‑training. *Why needed*: Central to understanding why some questions are answerable without retrieval. *Quick check*: What term describes knowledge stored within the model itself?

**Distractors in QA**: Irrelevant context sentences that compete with the true answer. *Why needed*: The paper shows they systematically reduce accuracy, highlighting model susceptibility to noise. *Quick check*: What effect do multiple non‑relevant distractors have on retrieval‑augmented QA?

**Supported / Unsupported Splits**: Categorization of questions based on presence of their answer in the pre‑training corpus. *Why needed*: Enables controlled experiments isolating the impact of parametric versus external knowledge. *Quick check*: How does NanoKnow define a "supported" question?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**:  
NQ/SQuAD questions → BM25 Retriever → Top‑100 candidate documents → String Matcher → Answer matches → Context extraction (512 words) → Qwen3‑8B Verifier → Qrels (supported/unsupported)

**Critical Path**: For a question to be labeled supported, BM25 must retrieve at least one document containing the answer string and the LLM verifier must output `TRUE` on the extracted context. If either step fails, the question becomes unsupported.

**Design Tradeoffs**:  
- BM25 (lexical) vs. dense retrieval: BM25 is fast and interpretable but may miss paraphrased answers.  
- Verifier model size: Qwen3‑8B balances cost and accuracy; a larger model could reduce coincidental false positives but increase compute.  
- Context window: 512 words balances relevance and noise; larger windows risk introducing more distractors.  
- Pipeline precision: The three‑stage filter prioritizes high confidence supported samples, potentially at the expense of recall.

**Failure Signatures**:  
- Zero supported questions → likely corpus/index mismatch or retrieval failure.  
- High coincidental match rate (>15%) → verifier too permissive or prompt inadequate.  
- Missing or malformed document IDs → indexing inconsistency between FineWeb‑Edu and the provided ID format.

**First Three Experiments**:  
1. Corpus Consistency Check: Confirm that known supported documents (e.g., `shard_00151_20323`) exist in the downloaded `karpathy/fineweb-edu-100b-shuffle` corpus.  
2. Verifier Model Audit: Run Qwen3‑8B on a manually curated set of 100 true answer contexts; ensure it outputs `TRUE` for >90% of them.  
3. Baseline QA Evaluation: Compute closed‑book accuracy of a nanochat checkpoint on the unsupported split; expect ~1.5% (SQuAD) and ~0.8% (NQ) to validate the split’s integrity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1**: How does topical variation in pre‑training data impact LLM knowledge acquisition?  
*Basis*: Explicitly stated in the paper’s conclusion as an open direction.  
*Why unresolved*: Only a single homogeneous corpus (FineWeb‑Edu) was used; no manipulation of topic distribution.  
*What would resolve*: Train models on corpora with deliberately varied topic compositions and evaluate with NanoKnow.

**Open Question 2**: Can answer frequency information guide more effective data curation to boost accuracy on rare facts?  
*Basis*: Identified as future work in the paper.  
*Why unresolved*: The study is correlational; no experiments that actively upsample or downsample documents based on answer frequency were performed.  
*What would resolve*: Conduct pre‑training runs where documents containing rare answers are upsampled and measure the effect on rare‑question accuracy.

**Open Question 3**: Do the observed relationships (frequency dependence, RAG mitigation, distractor harm) generalize to other corpora and larger models?  
*Basis*: Inferred from the limited scope: experiments only on nanochat models with FineWeb‑Edu.  
*Why unresolved*: No cross‑corpus or cross‑model validation was attempted.  
*What would resolve*: Apply the NanoKnow methodology to other open datasets (e.g., The Pile) and test with diverse model families and scales.

**Open Question 4**: Why does parametric knowledge enhance performance even when relevant external context is provided?  
*Basis*: Finding (3) shows supported questions are easier with oracle context, but the mechanism is not studied.  
*Why unresolved*: No analysis of how prior exposure affects context processing or internal representations.  
*What would resolve*: Probing experiments comparing model internals (e.g., attention patterns, layer activations) on supported vs. unsupported questions given identical external contexts.

## Limitations
- Benchmark validity depends on the exact FineWeb‑Edu corpus shuffle; different shuffles could alter supported/unsupported splits.
- The Qwen3‑8B verifier model version and prompt details are unspecified, risking inconsistency in reproduction.
- Results are confined to nanochat models on NQ and SQuAD; broader LLM applicability and other QA datasets remain untested.

## Confidence
- Frequency dependence: High
- Evidence mitigation: High
- Parametric/external complementarity: Medium
- Distractor harm: High
