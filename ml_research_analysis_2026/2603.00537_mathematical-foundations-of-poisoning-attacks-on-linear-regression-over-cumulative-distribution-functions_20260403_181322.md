# Mathematical Foundations of Poisoning Attacks on Linear Regression over Cumulative Distribution Functions

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.00537  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00537  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** For poisoning attacks on learned indexes using linear regression over CDFs, placing poisons adjacent to legitimate keys is optimal for a single-point attack; for multi-point attacks, an optimal attack must be "connected" to legitimate keys, and a proposed Segment+Endpoint heuristic often matches or exceeds greedy performance while an efficient upper bound is tight in practice.

## Executive Summary
This paper theoretically analyzes poisoning attacks on linear regression models used within learned indexes for cumulative distribution functions. The attacker aims to maximize the mean squared error (MSE) of the fitted linear model by inserting malicious "poison" keys. For a single poison, the authors prove that optimal placement is always adjacent (immediately before or after) to a legitimate key in the sorted order, confirming a common empirical heuristic. For multiple poisons, they establish a structural theorem showing any optimal attack must have all poison keys either directly adjacent to a legitimate key or connected through other poisons (no isolated blocks). This rules out the possibility of an optimal attack placing poisons in an unconnected interior segment. They propose an efficient method to compute an upper bound on the maximum achievable MSE by relaxing constraints (allowing duplicate keys) and swapping optimization order; this bound is empirically very tight, with greedy attacks achieving on average 97% of the bound. They also introduce the Segment+Endpoint attack class and provide fast exact/heuristic algorithms that often match or exceed greedy performance. Experiments on synthetic and real datasets validate the theory and show poisoning can degrade lookup performance by up to 1.6×.

## Method Summary
The core method involves analyzing how inserting integer-valued poison keys into a sorted set of legitimate keys affects the closed-form solution of a linear regression predicting ranks. The loss is the MSE of the fit. For a single point, they derive the derivative of the loss with respect to the poison's position and prove its sign is monotonic within any interval between legitimate keys, implying the loss is unimodal and the maximum lies at an interval endpoint (adjacent to a legitimate key). For multiple points, they extend this to show moving an isolated block of poisons strictly improves the loss unless the block is adjacent to legitimate data, forcing any optimal solution to be "connected." To bound the maximum loss, they relax the problem: allow duplicate keys and permit poisons on legitimate key positions. In this relaxed setting, they prove the optimal budget allocation either concentrates on a single interior key or splits mass between the two extreme keys. By first minimizing over model parameters (slope and intercept) and then maximizing over this relaxed allocation, they obtain an upper bound via a max-min inequality. The Segment+Endpoint attack restricts attention to configurations where poisons occupy at most three positions: a block at the left endpoint, one interior segment, and/or a block at the right endpoint. In the relaxed setting, this class is conjectured to be optimal and can be solved efficiently via enumeration or cubic root solving.

## Key Results
- Optimal single-point attacks place poisons adjacent to legitimate keys (Theorem 1).
- Any optimal multi-point attack must have poisons directly or transitively adjacent to legitimate keys; isolated poison blocks are suboptimal (Theorem 2).
- A greedy iterative attack is not always optimal (Figure 3 counterexample).
- An efficient upper-bounding method based on relaxation is tight in practice: greedy achieves ~97% of the bound on average, and the bound is at most 1.25× the greedy loss (Section 6).
- The Segment+Endpoint attack class often matches or exceeds greedy performance and can be computed in O(nλ) time.
- Poisoning can increase learned index lookup time by up to 1.6× on real datasets.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The optimal single-point poisoning attack places the poison key immediately adjacent to a legitimate key.
- **Mechanism:** The change in MSE when inserting a single poison point is governed by the derivative of the loss function with respect to the poison key position. Lemma 1 proves this derivative's sign is monotonic within any interval between consecutive legitimate keys, implying the loss is unimodal within that interval. Therefore, the maximum loss must occur at an endpoint of an interval, i.e., adjacent to a legitimate key.
- **Core assumption:** The loss function `E(X)` is differentiable and its derivative's sign behaves monotonically within unoccupied integer intervals between sorted keys.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "For single-point attacks, they prove that placing poisons adjacent to legitimate keys is optimal, confirming prior empirical work."
  - [section 4] Theorem 1 and Lemma 1 provide the formal proof, with Lemma 1 establishing the monotonic derivative property.
- **Break condition:** If the monotonicity of `sign(dE/dx_i)` from Lemma 1 fails, e.g., for non-integer keys without a smooth mapping or if the regression model is non-linear, the adjacency optimality may not hold.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The greedy multi-point attack is not always optimal; any optimal multi-point attack must have all poison keys directly or transitively adjacent to legitimate keys (i.e., no "isolated" poison blocks).
- **Mechanism:** Lemma 2 extends the single-point derivative analysis to a block of consecutive poison keys. It shows that for any isolated block (with unoccupied integers on both sides), shifting the entire block left or right strictly increases the MSE unless the block is adjacent to a legitimate key. Therefore, in any optimal solution, no such isolated block can exist, forcing all poisons to be connected to the legitimate set.
- **Core assumption:** The loss function's response to shifting a consecutive block of poisons follows the same monotonic derivative principle as in Lemma 1.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "For multi-point attacks, they demonstrate that the existing greedy method is not always optimal and establish a structural theorem: any optimal attack must have poisons directly or transitively adjacent to legitimate keys."
  - [section 5.1] Theorem 2 states the structural property, and Figure 3 provides a concrete counterexample where greedy fails.
- **Break condition:** If the adversarial perturbation model changes such that poisons can be placed without the rank-shifting effect described in Definition 1, the adjacency property may break.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** An efficient upper bound on the maximum achievable MSE under multi-point attacks can be computed by (1) relaxing the problem to allow duplicate poisons and placement on legitimate keys, and (2) swapping the order of minimization over model parameters and maximization over poison configurations.
- **Mechanism:** The Relaxed Poisoning Problem (Definition 3) is easier because it allows duplicates, eliminating the combinatorial constraint of distinct integer positions. Theorem 4 shows the budget is always exhausted. Theorem 6 characterizes the optimal relaxed solution: all poison mass concentrates either on a single key or on the two extreme keys `k1` and `kn`. This reduces the search space drastically. The max-min inequality (Equation 14) then yields an upper bound by optimizing model parameters first.
- **Core assumption:** The relaxation allowing duplicates provides a valid upper bound, and the max-min inequality holds with the specific loss function `L(K ⊎ Q_K(d); w, b)`.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "They propose a method to compute an upper bound of the multi-point poisoning attack's impact... empirically demonstrate that the loss under the greedy approach is often close to this bound."
  - [section 5.2] Theorems 3, 4, and 6 formally derive the relaxed problem's structure and the upper-bounding procedure.
- **Break condition:** If the gap between the relaxed problem and the original (no duplicates, distinct poisons) becomes large, e.g., in extremely dense key distributions, the bound may be loose and less informative for practical attack assessment.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Linear Regression on Cumulative Distribution Functions (CDFs)
  - **Why needed:** The entire attack model is defined on fitting a linear model `w*x + b` to predict the rank `r` of sorted keys, minimizing MSE. This is the core primitive in many learned index leaf models.
  - **Quick check:** Given a sorted set of keys `K = {k1, k2, ..., kn}`, how does adding a poison key `p` affect the ranks of keys greater than `p`? How does this rank shift propagate into the MSE calculation?
- **Concept:** Poisoning Attack Optimization
  - **Why needed:** The goal is to maximize the post-attack MSE `E(K ∪ P)` by choosing `P`. This requires understanding how the closed-form solution for linear regression (Equation 4) changes when the dataset `X` is altered.
  - **Quick check:** From Equation 4, `E(X) = Var(R) - Cov(X,R)^2 / Var(X)`. Which terms change when a new key `p` is inserted, and what is the sign of `dE/dp`?
- **Concept:** Convexity and Unimodality in Discrete Settings
  - **Why needed:** The proofs for single-point (Lemma 1) and multi-point (Lemma 2) attacks rely on showing the derivative of the loss with respect to a poison position is monotonic, implying the loss is unimodal over integer intervals. This guarantees optimality at interval boundaries.
  - **Quick check:** If `sign(dE/dx_i)` is non-decreasing over an interval `(x_{i-1}, x_{i+1})`, what can you conclude about the shape of `E(X)` as a function of `x_i` within that interval? How does this extend to a block of consecutive `x_i` values?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Legitimate Keys (K) → Poison Set (P) → Regression Model (w*, b*) → Loss Function E(K ∪ P) → Greedy/Upper Bound/Seg+E Attack Modules
- **Critical path:**
  1. Input K and λ → compute single-point candidates (adjacent integers).
  2. For multi-point: theoretical analysis shows optimal P* must be "connected" to K (Theorem 2).
  3. Use this to bound search space or compute upper bound via relaxation (Definition 3, Theorem 6).
  4. Implement efficient Seg+E heuristic/algorithms (Section 5.3).
  5. Validate against greedy and optimal (small-scale) via experiments (Section 6).
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Optimality vs. Efficiency:** Exact optimal search is `O((n+λ) * binomial(2n-2+λ, λ))` (Theorem 2), often infeasible. Greedy is `O((n+λ)λ)` but suboptimal. Upper bound is `O((n+λ)log(n+λ))` and tight empirically. Seg+E heuristic is `O(nλ)` and near-optimal.
  - **Relaxation Tightness:** Allowing duplicates (relaxed setting) yields an upper bound. The bound tightness depends on key density; for sparse keys it's very tight (ratio ~1.0), for extremely dense keys it loosens (Section 6.5).
  - **Structural Assumptions:** Seg+E assumes optimal poisons concentrate at endpoints or one interior segment. This holds empirically but has rare counterexamples (Figure 16).
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Large gap between greedy MSE and upper bound (`MSE_G / MSE_UB` significantly < 1, e.g., < 0.8) indicates greedy is far from optimal and the bound may be loose or the attack structure is complex.
  - Relaxed optimal solution uses multiple interior distinct keys (not just one segment), violating Seg+E structure (Observation 4 corner cases).
  - For very small `k_n - k_1` (high density), the duplicate-allowing relaxation dominates the bound looseness (Section 6.6, `ρ_R` drops).
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Replicate Suboptimal Greedy Example:** Use the dataset from Figure 3 (e.g., `K = {2, 11, 13, 19, 32, 36, 39}` with `λ=2`). Run greedy attack (Algorithm 2) and compute MSE; verify it's lower than the optimal `{37, 38}`.
  2. **Upper Bound Tightness Sweep:** For synthetic Uniform distribution with `n=1000`, `R=100000`, vary `λ/n` from 2% to 20% over 100 seeds. Compute `MSE_G` and `MSE_UB` (using strict explicit method). Report ratio distribution; expect median >0.9 and max <1.25 per paper.
  3. **Seg+E vs. Greedy:** For real dataset (e.g., Amzn from SOSD) with `n=1000`, `λ=100` (10%). Run exact Seg+E (relaxed setting, `O(nλ)`) and greedy. Compare `MSE_Seg+E / MSE_G`; should be ≥1 (Seg+E never worse) and often >1.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Prove that the loss function E(K ∪ {p}) for single-point poisoning attacks is convex with respect to the poison location p.
2. How can we derive tighter upper bounds on the maximum possible MSE increase from multi-point poisoning attacks that avoid the two relaxations (allowing duplicate keys and swapping min_w and max_d)?
3. Does the Segment+Endpoint (Seg+E) attack class always achieve the optimal solution in the relaxed setting where duplicate keys are allowed?
4. Can the theoretical framework for poisoning attacks be extended to nonlinear regression models (e.g., polynomial, neural networks) used in learned indexes?

## Limitations
- Theoretical results rely on integer key domains and linear regression; extension to continuous keys or nonlinear models is non-trivial.
- The upper bound's tightness may degrade for extremely dense key distributions due to the duplicate-key relaxation.
- The Seg+E heuristic, while empirically strong, has known rare counterexamples and lacks a proof of optimality even in the relaxed setting.
- Exact optimal attack computation is only feasible for small `n, λ`; for larger scales reliance on heuristics and bounds is necessary.
- Experimental details for real datasets (SOSD extraction) and lookup timing are underspecified, hindering exact replication.

## Confidence
- **Single-point adjacency optimality:** High (Theorem 1, Lemma 1 provide rigorous proof).
- **Multi-point connectivity theorem:** High (Theorem 2, Lemma 2).
- **Upper bound correctness:** High (Theorems 3–6).
- **Upper bound tightness:** Medium (empirical across synthetic/real datasets but may weaken under high density).
- **Seg+E heuristic effectiveness:** Medium (extensive tests show near-optimality but rare failures exist; no proof).
- **Generalization beyond integer keys/linear model:** Low (theoretical analysis heavily relies on these assumptions).
