# Instruction-Tuned, but Not More Verifiable Instruction-Following: A Cross-Task Diagnosis for LoRA Adapters

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.22379  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22379  
- **Reference count:** 10  
- **Primary result:** Nominal “instruction‑tuned” LoRA adapters often degrade strict verifiable instruction‑following (IFEval PLA) while substantially improving numeric reasoning, revealing a recurrent “capability drift” that renders adapter labels unreliable proxies for realized capabilities.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether the label assigned to a LoRA adapter (e.g., “instruction‑tuned”) accurately reflects its performance on a strict, automatically verifiable instruction‑following benchmark (IFEval). The authors train adapters on different objectives (instruction tuning vs. numeric reasoning) and evaluate them cross‑task, comparing gains on the nominal target task against off‑target tasks. A diagnostic drift score quantifies the mismatch between target and off‑target improvements. The primary finding is a systematic “capability drift”: instruction‑tuned adapters consistently improve numeric reasoning but fail to improve—or even worsen—performance on IFEval’s prompt‑level accuracy (PLA). This pattern holds across multiple seeds, base models, and LoRA configurations, with only rare near‑zero exceptions. The work concludes that nominal adapter labels are poor predictors of specific realized capabilities and argues for routine cross‑task evaluation before deployment.

## Method Summary
The study reuses existing LoRA adapter artifacts trained on two nominal objectives: `instr` (instruction‑tuned) and `reason` (numeric‑reasoning‑tuned). These adapters are applied to frozen base models (e.g., Qwen3‑8B, Llama‑3.1‑8B‑Instruct). Each adapter is evaluated on two benchmarks: a numeric reasoning metric (numeric match, NM) and the verifiable instruction‑following benchmark IFEval (reporting both PLA and ILA). For a given adapter, the gain on the target task (e.g., IFEval PLA for the instruction adapter) is computed relative to the base model, as is the off‑target gain (e.g., NM). The **drift score** is defined as off‑target gain minus target gain. To assess robustness, the authors vary random seeds, base models, and LoRA hyperparameters (rank, targeted modules, dropout, learning rate). They also supplement with other instruction‑following benchmarks (IFBench, FollowBench) to probe benchmark dependence.

## Key Results
- Instruction‑tuned adapters improved numeric reasoning dramatically (NM base 0.133 → adapter 0.632) but degraded IFEval PLA (base 0.250 → adapter 0.143) in a canonical setting.
- Drift scores were consistently positive across most seeds, models, and LoRA configurations, indicating a recurrent pattern where off‑target gains exceed nominal target gains.
- Results on broader instruction‑following benchmarks were mixed: IFBench aligned with IFEval (no improvement), while FollowBench improved, highlighting that “instruction following” is operationalized differently across evaluations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The observed capability drift may stem from one or more of the following mechanisms:

- **Response‑prior shift:** Instruction tuning may alter the model’s output distribution (e.g., favoring numbers, adopting regular formatting) in ways that incidentally benefit benchmarks sensitive to surface features (like numeric match) without enhancing strict constraint adherence required by IFEval. Evidence: The `instr` adapter strongly boosts NM while harming PLA; the paper suggests this shift explains the asymmetry.
- **Non‑localized LoRA updates:** Low‑rank adapters may modify features across many layers and attention heads, leading to unpredictable interactions with downstream tasks. An adapter trained for instruction tuning could still significantly affect numeric reasoning pathways without explicit targeting. Evidence: Exploratory probing found only modest, non‑uniform overlap between instruction‑ and numeric‑tuned updates, with no single module explaining drift.
- **Benchmark misalignment:** The training objective for “instruction tuning” often optimizes for helpfulness or fluency rather than the precise, multi‑constraint compliance measured by IFEval. Consequently, an adapter may excel on benchmarks that reward general conversational quality (or coincidentally on numeric tasks) but fail on IFEval’s verifiable constraints. Evidence: IFBench and IFEval show no improvement, while FollowBench (a different operationalization) does.

## Foundational Learning
- **LoRA (Low‑Rank Adaptation):** Inserts trainable low‑rank matrices into attention and MLP layers while keeping the base model frozen. *Why needed:* It is the intervention used throughout the study. *Quick check:* If LoRA updates are low‑rank, why might they still cause large, unpredictable cross‑task effects?
- **Verifiable Instruction Following (IFEval):** Evaluates a model’s ability to exactly satisfy a set of automatically checkable constraints (e.g., format, keyword inclusion) within a single prompt. *Why needed:* Serves as the stringent target metric for strict constraint satisfaction. *Quick check:* How does IFEval’s PLA differ from more open‑ended instruction‑following evaluations in what it measures?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Base model (frozen) → LoRA modules (injected into attention/MLP) → Evaluation on IFEval & numeric NM → Drift score calculation  

**Critical path:**  
1. Train a LoRA adapter on a nominal objective (e.g., instruction tuning).  
2. Evaluate the adapter (and base model) on target task (IFEval PLA) and off‑target task (numeric NM).  
3. Compute `TargetGain = adapter_score - base_score` on IFEval and `OffTargetGain` on NM.  
4. Compute `DriftScore = OffTargetGain - TargetGain`.  
5. Repeat across seeds, base models, and LoRA hyperparameters to assess robustness.  

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Target metric choice:* PLA emphasizes strict end‑to‑end compliance; ILA provides per‑constraint view but dilutes signal. PLA is chosen for drift as the most stringent.  
- *Off‑target task choice:* Numeric reasoning (NM) offers a simple objective metric contrasting verifiable compliance; other off‑target tasks could reveal different patterns.  
- *Drift score vs. raw matrix:* Drift score provides a compact diagnostic but raw cross‑task numbers are the primary evidence.  

**Failure signatures:**  
- High positive drift (>0.4): Classic capability drift; nominal label unreliable.  
- Near‑zero or negative drift: Configuration‑sensitive case where nominal gains align or adapter harms off‑target.  
- Benchmark disagreement: IFEval shows no gain while a broader benchmark shows improvement → signals operationalization mismatch.  

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Main cross‑task table (Table 1):** Inspect raw base vs. `instr`/`reason` adapter scores on NM and IFEval (PLA/ILA). Does `instr` improve IFEval? Does it improve NM more?  
2. **Robustness sweep (Table 2, Appendix 6):** Vary seeds, models, LoRA ranks/modules; compute drift scores. Check if positive drift is recurrent and note sensitivity.  
3. **Supplementary benchmark check (Table 5):** Evaluate on IFBench (strict PLA) and FollowBench (rule‑based). Assess alignment: IFBench with IFEval (no improvement), FollowBench differs → confirms benchmark dependence.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **OQ1:** What neural or representational mechanisms cause an instruction‑tuned LoRA adapter to improve numeric reasoning while degrading strict instruction‑following (IFEval) and vice versa? The paper’s exploratory probing is non‑causal and inconclusive.  
- **OQ2:** How generally does capability drift occur across different model architectures, parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning methods, and a wider range of tasks beyond numeric reasoning and instruction following? The study is limited to LoRA on specific models and two tasks.  
- **OQ3:** Which adapter and training configuration variables (e.g., LoRA rank, targeted modules, dropout, learning rate) most strongly influence the magnitude and sign of capability drift? The paper varied some hyperparameters but did not isolate their effects systematically.  
- **OQ4:** What properties of instruction‑following benchmarks determine whether an instruction‑tuned adapter shows drift (i.e., no gain) versus apparent improvement? Is it strictness of constraints, instruction type, or evaluation methodology? The paper reports heterogeneity but does not analyze determinant features.

## Limitations
- **Generalizability:** Findings are tied to specific LoRA adapters,开源模型 (Qwen/Llama), and tasks (numeric NM, IFEval). Unclear if drift persists with full fine‑tuning, other architectures, or broader off‑target tasks.  
- **Mechanism ambiguity:** The paper disfavors simple localized explanations but cannot definitively identify whether drift stems from response‑prior shifts, distributed parameter changes, or benchmark mismatches; evidence is correlational.  
- **Benchmark dependency:** The negative IFEval result may partly reflect its narrow, constraint‑focused design. If “instruction following” is multi‑faceted, the conclusion about label unreliability could be task‑specific.

## Confidence
- Capability drift exists and is recurrent: **High**  
- Nominal labels are unreliable proxies for verifiable instruction‑following (as measured by IFEval): **High**  
- Drift is caused by response‑prior shifts or non‑localized LoRA effects: **Medium/Low**  
- Benchmark heterogeneity explains mixed results on other instruction‑following evaluations: **Medium**

## Next Checks
1. **Expand off‑target task diversity:** Replicate the diagnostic with additional off‑target tasks (e.g., code generation, summarization, multilingual QA) to test whether numeric NM is anomalous or part of a broader pattern.  
2. **Output‑level diagnosis for response priors:** Analyze generated texts from `instr` vs. `base` models on shared numeric prompts, quantifying superficial features (number density, formatting) vs. deep compliance on a simplified probe to directly test the response‑prior shift hypothesis.  
3. **Strict benchmark replication:** Evaluate the same adapters on newly released automatically verifiable instruction‑following benchmarks (e.g., GPQA’s instruction subset, TULU’s control tasks) to confirm whether IFEval’s negative result generalizes to the “verifiable compliance” operationalization.
