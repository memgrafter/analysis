# Optimal Lower Bounds for Online Multicalibration

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2601.05245
- **Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05245
- **References:** 40
- **Result:** Proves optimal Ω(T^{2/3}) lower bounds for online multicalibration, matching known upper bounds up to log factors and strictly separating multicalibration from marginal calibration.

## Executive Summary
This paper establishes optimal lower bounds for online multicalibration, demonstrating that any algorithm must suffer expected multicalibration error of at least Ω(T^{2/3}). This matches existing upper bounds up to logarithmic factors, thereby closing the gap and showing that multicalibration is fundamentally harder than marginal calibration, which admits O(T^{2/3−ε}) algorithms. The lower bounds are shown for both prediction‑dependent and prediction‑independent groups using tailored constructions, and an oracle lower bound reveals that any proper reduction to marginal calibration requires exponentially many oracle copies in the number of groups, highlighting a fundamental barrier.

## Method Summary
The paper develops three complementary lower bound techniques to characterize the minimax rate of online multicalibration. For prediction‑dependent groups, it uses a Bernoulli‑grid environment with three binary groups (overshoot, undershoot, η‑honest) to create a bias‑vs‑noise trade‑off. For prediction‑independent groups, it leverages orthonormal systems (Walsh and Hadamard) over a Θ(T)-sized family to enforce ℓ₁‑truthfulness and then applies an adaptive noise‑bucketing theorem. Additionally, it proves an oracle lower bound via a bit‑group construction showing that any proper reduction to a context‑blind marginal calibration oracle requires exponentially many copies. Each construction assumes an oblivious adversary and bounded martingale‑difference noise.

## Key Results
- For any online algorithm and any set of binary prediction‑dependent groups, the expected multicalibration error after T rounds is at least Ω(T^{2/3}).
- Even when groups are prediction‑independent, a carefully chosen family of Θ(T) binary groups forces expected multicalibration error ~Ω(T^{2/3}).
- Any proper black‑box reduction from multicalibration to marginal calibration requires exponentially many oracle copies in |G|; specifically, for |G| = Θ(log T) one needs m ≈ 2^{|G|} to avoid trivial error.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** For general prediction‑dependent groups, any online algorithm obtains expected multicalibration error Ω(T^{2/3}).
- **Mechanism:** Construct a Bernoulli‑grid environment with label means x_t on a grid of size m=Θ(T^{1/3}). Define three disjoint binary groups: g₁ activates on overshoots (p_t ≥ x_t+η), g₂ on undershoots (p_t ≤ x_t−η), g₃ on η‑honest rounds (|p_t−x_t|< η). The round partition forces a trade‑off: many overshoots/undershoots yield linear bias on g₁ or g₂; few such rounds force mostly η‑honest rounds where accumulated zero‑mean noise (x_t−y_t) on g₃ scales as Ω(√(mT)). Balancing η=Θ(√(m/T)) gives the Ω(T^{2/3}) lower bound.
- **Core assumption:** The adversary is oblivious; label means are revealed via x_t, and grid spacing is much finer than η so g₃’s η‑balls are disjoint. Noise Z_t=x_t−y_t is a bounded martingale difference with variance bounded away from zero, allowing martingale concentration to show noise accumulation whenever a constant fraction of occurrences of each context are honest.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [Section 3.1 (Defining D_{T,m} and G)] The grid distribution and group definitions are fixed explicitly.
  - [Section 3.3 (Proof of Theorem 1)] The case analysis (big deviations vs honest rounds) and Lemma 4 (noise vs bias on g₃) rely on martingale arguments.
- **Break condition:** The noise accumulation argument fails if the environment’s residuals Z_t lose nontrivial variance (e.g., deterministic labels) or if the groups are not prediction‑dependent (they could not separate overshoots from undershoots).

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Even for prediction‑independent binary groups, a family of size Θ(T) forces expected multicalibration error ~Ω(T^{2/3}).
- **Mechanism:** Use a fixed mean grid and augment context with time. Construct groups from orthonormal systems: (i) global Walsh groups on the mean grid, (ii) blockwise Hadamard groups on time blocks. Global Walsh groups enforce ℓ₁‑truthfulness E[∑|p_t−x_t|]≤O(log m)·MCerr via Walsh expansion and prefix‑sum bound. ℓ₁‑truthfulness forces diverse predictions (∑_v √n_v large). Blockwise Hadamard groups decompose calibration error into bias (≤O(√{n_{v,a}}E_a) on average) and noise. An adaptive “noise bucketing” theorem shows that for each Hadamard functional, the noise term ∑_v |N^{(a,j)}_v|≥c·E[N_a]/log L on average, even if the learner adaptively groups predictions. Summing blocks and using the large ∑_v √n_v lower bound from ℓ₁‑truthfulness yields the overall Ω̃(T^{2/3}) error.
- **Core assumption:** Groups are binary and prediction‑independent; the environment uses Rademacher noise y_t = x_t + ξ_t/4 so residuals are i.i.d. signed steps. The Walsh prefix‑sum property (Lemma 8) and orthonormality of Hadamard systems (Parseval) are essential for the ℓ₁ control and bias averaging.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [Section 4.2 (Hard group family)] Definitions of Walsh/ Hadamard groups.
  - [Section 4.3 (Walsh groups enforce ℓ₁‑truthfulness)] Lemma 10 and Lemma 11.
  - [Section 4.6 (Adaptive noise bucketing)] Theorem 3 and Corollary 2.
- **Break condition:** The Walsh prefix‑sum bound holds only for the Walsh system; using another orthogonal system without that property might not enforce ℓ₁‑truthfulness. The noise‑bucketing lower bound requires bounded i.i.d. increments; if noise were not symmetric or had zero variance on some contexts, the argument breaks.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Any proper black‑box reduction that uses m ≤ poly(|G|) copies of a context‑blind marginal calibration oracle cannot guarantee non‑trivial multicalibration error for a group family of size Θ(log T); indeed it requires m exponential in |G| to avoid Ω(T^{1−γ}) error.
- **Mechanism:** Consider a family of boolean “bit” groups on the context hypercube. The reduction runs m independent copies of a context‑blind marginal oracle, each outputting a distribution Q^i_t that depends only on its own state. Properness forces the final prediction P_t to lie in the convex hull of these Q^i_t. Show that if m < N/2 where N=2^k and |G|=k+1, then the expected mass that P_t puts on the correct interval J_{val(x_t)} is at most m/N. Missing the correct interval forces squared loss of order 1/N². For any algorithm that achieves small U‑calibration on the bit groups, its predictions must have small squared loss on average, contradicting the lower bound unless m is large (≈N). Hence there is an information‑theoretic barrier: reducing multicalibration to marginal calibration in a “proper” way incurs exponential dependence on |G|.
- **Core assumption:** The marginal oracle is context‑blind; the reduction is proper (predictions are convex combinations of oracle forecasts) and the environment has deterministic labels (y_t = μ(x_t) midpoint of an interval). The bit groups g_r(x)=x_r are linear in the binary representation; calibration on them forces the predictor to assign high mass to the correct interval for each context.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [Appendix B.2 (Hard instance)] Construction of context space and bit groups.
  - [Appendix B.4 (Proof of Theorem 5)] Lemmas 20–22 (mass bound, misses ⇒ squared loss, squared loss bounded by bit‑group calibration).
- **Break condition:** If the reduction uses non‑convex combinations or can condition on the context before querying the oracles, the lower bound may not hold. The argument relies on the inability of a small set of context‑blind distributions to cover the correct interval for every context.

## Foundational Learning
- **Online Adversarial Calibration Framework:** Formalization of rounds, predictions, outcomes, and definitions of calibration error and multicalibration error.  
  *Why needed:* Provides the precise setting and quantifies error.  
  *Quick check:* If you see a forecaster’s predictions p_t and outcomes y_t, how do you compute the multicalibration error for a group g?  
  (Answer: For each group g, compute the conditional expectation of (p_t - y_t) over rounds where g(x_t)=1, then take the absolute value.)
- **Martingale Theory and Concentration:** Martingale difference sequences, predictable quadratic variation, Burkholder‑Rosenthal inequality, martingale transform lower bounds.  
  *Why needed:* Both lower bounds use martingale arguments: Proposition 1 shows noise accumulates even under adaptive filtering (g₃ honest rounds and adaptive bucketing in the prediction‑independent case).  
  *Quick check:* What condition on the predictable indicators I_t ensures E[|∑ I_t Z_t|] is large?  
  (Answer: If ∑ I_t is large and Z_t are bounded martingale differences with variance lower bound, then the expectation of the absolute sum is at least c·∑ I_t.)
- **Orthonormal Systems (Walsh/Hadamard):** Properties: orthogonality (H₁), {±1}-values (H₂), Walsh prefix‑sum bound (H₃). Parseval identities.  
  *Why needed:* Used to enforce ℓ₁‑truthfulness via Walsh expansion and to average bias via Hadamard.  
  *Quick check:* What does the Walsh prefix‑sum bound say about ∑_{s<r} ψ_j^{Walsh}(s)?  
  (Answer: For Walsh functions, the absolute prefix sum is bounded by a constant independent of r, which is crucial for the ℓ₁ control.)

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
The architecture consists of:
- **Environment Generator:** Produces the context sequence (e.g., Bernoulli‑grid for prediction‑dependent; time‑augmented grid for prediction‑independent; hypercube for oracle experiments).
- **Predictor Module:** Implements the online algorithm whose multicalibration performance is being evaluated.
- **Group Evaluator:** For each group g in the family, maintains running sums B_T(v,g) over prediction values v.
- **Calibration Error Tracker:** Computes Err_T(g) = |(1/T)∑ I_t(g)(p_t − y_t)| and MCerr_T(G) = max_g Err_T(g).
- **Distributed Subalgorithm Wrapper:** For reduction experiments, manages multiple copies of a marginal calibration oracle, each with its own state, and combines their outputs properly (e.g., convex combination).

### Critical Path
1. On each round t: environment emits context x_t; predictor outputs p_t; environment reveals outcome y_t.
2. For each group g, the Group Evaluator updates B_T(v,g) for v = p_t whenever g(x_t)=1.
3. The Calibration Error Tracker updates Err_T(g) using the latest B_T values.
4. At the end of T rounds, MCerr_T(G) is reported.
   For reduction experiments: before step 1, the wrapper computes convex combination weights from the states of the m oracle copies and sets p_t accordingly.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Binary vs real‑valued groups:** The lower bounds hold for binary groups; real‑valued groups do not increase difficulty in these constructions.
- **Group size:** Constant‑size prediction‑independent families reduce to marginal calibration with rate O(T^{2/3−ε}), while Θ(T)‑sized families hit the ~Ω(T^{2/3}) barrier; intermediate sizes illustrate an open transition.
- **Convex combination vs selection:** The properness restriction (convex hull) is crucial for the oracle lower bound; allowing non‑convex or context‑dependent selection could break the exponential barrier.
- **Orthogonal system choice:** Walsh is needed for ℓ₁‑truthfulness due to its prefix‑sum property; any Hadamard system suffices for the blockwise averaging of bias in the prediction‑independent case.

### Failure Signatures
- Empirical MCerr_T scales sub‑T^{2/3} (e.g., o(T^{0.5})) in the hard instances, indicating either an implementation bug or violation of assumptions (e.g., groups not binary/prediction‑dependent).
- For the oracle reduction, using m ≪ 2^{|G|} copies still achieving small multicalibration error would contradict Theorem 5; check properness and context‑blindness of the oracle.
- In the adaptive noise‑bucketing test (Theorem 3), if the noise contribution ∑_v |B_v| is significantly below c·∑_v √n_v / log L, the adapted excursion‑length bound was misapplied.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Prediction‑dependent lower bound test:** Implement the Bernoulli‑grid environment with m = ⌊T^{1/3}⌋ and binary groups g₁, g₂, g₃. Run a state‑of‑the‑art online multicalibration algorithm (e.g., Noarov et al. [2025]) over increasing T. Verify that MCerr_T = Ω(T^{2/3}) and that error concentrates either on g₁/g₂ (bias) or g₃ (noise), as per the case analysis.
2. **Prediction‑independent orthogonal groups test:** Use the time‑augmented grid instance, global Walsh groups (ℓ = 1,…,m−1), and blockwise Hadamard groups with K = ⌈log T⌉ blocks. Run the same algorithm; check (a) ℓ₁ deviation A satisfies A ≤ O(log m)·MCerr (Lemma 11) and (b) MCerr_T = Ω̃(T^{2/3}). Also confirm the noise‑bucketing lower bound by measuring the average |∑ I_t Z_t| per Hadamard functional.
3. **Oracle reduction barrier test:** For k = ⌈γ log₂ T⌉, define bit‑groups G_bits on the hypercube context. Build a context‑blind marginal oracle that outputs a fixed distribution (e.g., uniform). Wrap it in a proper m‑copy reduction (e.g., average of m independent oracle forecasts). Vary m from 1 to 2^k and measure MCerr_T(G_bits). Expected error ≈ Ω(T/4^{k}) unless m is exponential in k (≈2^k/2), confirming the exponential dependence on |G| for proper reductions.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:**  
*Question:* For online multicalibration with prediction‑independent groups, what is the minimax expected error rate when the group family size |G| grows sublinearly with T, such as |G| = polylog(T) or |G| = T^α for α ∈ (0,1)? Does the rate remain ~Ω(T^{2/3}) as for |G| = Θ(T), or can it be strictly lower, e.g., o(T^{2/3})?  
*Basis in paper:* Explicit from the discussion section, where the authors ask: "What about for families of size |G| = polylog(T)?" after establishing tight bounds for constant and linear |G|.  
*Why unresolved:* The paper provides matching lower and upper bounds only for the extremes: constant |G| (reducing to marginal calibration with rate O(T^{2/3−ε})) and Θ(T) sizes (~Ω(T^{2/3})). For intermediate sizes, no direct lower bound exists, and the oracle lower bound for proper reductions suggests potential barriers but does not directly constrain the minimax rate.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* A lower bound construction using |G| = polylog(T) showing ~Ω(T^{2/3}) error, or an upper bound achieving o(T^{2/3}) with explicit dependence on |G|. Matching bounds for specific intermediate sizes (e.g., |G| = log T) would clarify the dependence.

**Open Question 2:**  
*Question:* As |G| for prediction‑independent groups increases from constant to linear in T, does the minimax multicalibration rate exhibit a sharp phase transition (e.g., from O(T^{2/3−ε}) to ~Ω(T^{2/3}) at a threshold |G| = f(T)), or does it interpolate smoothly (e.g., the lower bound gradually tightens with |G|)?  
*Basis in paper:* Explicitly asked in the discussion: "Is there a sharp threshold, or does the complexity interpolate smoothly?" following the presentation of extreme‑case results.  
*Why unresolved:* Only the endpoints of the spectrum are understood: constant |G| reduces to marginal calibration, while |G| = Θ(T) incurs ~Ω(T^{2/3}). No results exist for intermediate |G| to distinguish between a threshold and smooth behavior.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* A characterization theorem expressing the exact rate as a function of |G|, such as proving that for |G| = o(T^{1/3}) the rate is O(T^{2/3−δ}) for some δ>0, or that any ω(1) growing |G| forces ~Ω(T^{2/3}).

**Open Question 3:**  
*Question:* The paper shows that any proper black‑box reduction from multicalibration to marginal calibration requires exponentially many oracle copies (in |G|) for |G| = Θ(log T) to achieve non‑trivial rates. Can this obstruction be circumvented by considering improper reductions or direct algorithms that do not rely on such reductions? Specifically, does there exist an algorithm for online multicalibration with |G| = polylog(T) achieving o(T^{2/3}) error using only poly(|G|) resources?  
*Basis in paper:* Inferred from Appendix B’s oracle lower bound and the surrounding discussion. The authors note that the lower bound serves as an obstruction to refined reductions, and that upper bounds with logarithmic |G| dependence exist but are not reduction‑based.  
*Why unresolved:* The lower bound applies only to the natural class of "proper" reductions (where predictions are convex combinations of oracle outputs). It leaves open whether improper reductions (e.g., allowing non‑convex combination or more complex aggregation) or entirely different algorithmic approaches can avoid the exponential blow‑up.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Constructing a multicalibration algorithm for |G| = polylog(T) with sub‑T^{2/3} error that uses only poly(|G|) marginal calibration oracles or computations. Alternatively, proving that even for general algorithms the rate must be ~Ω(T^{2/3}) for such |G| would show the reduction barrier is fundamental.

## Limitations
- The lower bounds assume an oblivious adversary and bounded martingale‑difference noise; they may not extend to adaptive adversaries or alternative noise models.
- The constructions are tailored to binary groups and specific orthonormal systems (Walsh/Hadamard); it is unknown whether similar Ω(T^{2/3}) rates hold for real‑valued or overlapping groups.
- The oracle separation (Theorem 5) only rules out proper black‑box reductions with context‑blind oracles; improper or context‑aware reductions might achieve better dependence on |G|.
- The results are asymptotic and contain logarithmic gaps; tight constants and the exact role of log|G| vs log T remain open.

## Confidence
- **Claim 1 (Prediction‑dependent Ω(T^{2/3}) lower bound):** High – The mechanism is clear and uses standard martingale concentration; the proof is constructive and matches upper bounds up to logs.
- **Claim 2 (Prediction‑independent Ω̃(T^{2/3}) lower bound):** High – The orthonormal system approach is rigorous; the noise‑bucketing theorem is solid, albeit technical.
- **Claim 3 (Exponential barrier for proper reduction to marginal calibration):** Medium – The construction is clean but restricted to proper, context‑blind reductions; extensions beyond this model are open.
