# Certainty-Validity: A Diagnostic Framework for Discrete Commitment Systems
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.00070
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00070
- Reference count: 7
- Primary result: Standard accuracy metrics fail for discrete commitment systems; the Certainty-Validity (CVS) framework reveals a pathological UI→CI migration during benign overfitting and an 83% ambiguity ceiling on structurally ambiguous datasets.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces the Certainty-Validity (CVS) framework for diagnosing discrete commitment systems that output ternary decisions {-W, 0, +W}. It demonstrates that conventional accuracy metrics conflate appropriate uncertainty (UI) with dangerous overconfidence (CI), obscuring critical failure modes. Experiments on Fashion-MNIST, EMNIST, and IMDB show an “83% Ambiguity Ceiling” where the model correctly refuses to commit on ~17% of ambiguous samples. Prolonged training triggers a migration from Uncertain-Incorrect (UI) to Confident-Incorrect (CI) errors, degrading epistemic calibration while raw accuracy remains stable—a phenomenon termed benign overfitting. The CVS framework, by disentangling certainty from validity, identifies optimal model states that maximize both accuracy and calibration, and provides actionable diagnostics for discrete architectures.

## Method Summary
The CVS framework decomposes model outcomes into a 2×2 matrix: Confident/Uncertain versus Correct/Incorrect, yielding four quadrants—Confident-Correct (CC), Confident-Incorrect (CI), Uncertain-Correct (UC), Uncertain-Incorrect (UI). Key derived metrics include CommitAcc (accuracy on confident predictions), AppropUncert (rate of appropriate uncertainty among confident errors), Coverage (fraction of samples receiving a confident prediction), and CVS (product of CommitAcc and AppropUncert). The authors train a ternary commitment model using a ProbableCollapseLayer driven by Gumbel-Softmax (temperature τ ≈ 0.7–0.9) and a FractalOptimizer with multi‑scale learning rates. On ambiguous benchmarks (full Fashion‑MNIST, EMNIST, strong‑sentiment IMDB), the model naturally produces an “83% Ambiguity Ceiling”: it refuses to commit on structurally ambiguous examples (e.g., shirt vs. pullover), yielding ~83% test accuracy. Removing ambiguous classes raises accuracy to ~97%, confirming the ceiling is dataset‑driven. Training dynamics reveal a Platonic Spike where test accuracy exceeds train accuracy at Epoch 1, followed by a UI→CI migration (benign overfitting) that degrades CVS while accuracy stabilizes; early stopping at the CVS peak, not the accuracy peak, yields the most reliable model.

## Key Results
- Standard accuracy conflates UI (appropriate doubt) and CI (dangerous overconfidence); CVS isolates CI as the true failure mode.
- On ambiguous datasets, discrete commitment systems exhibit an “83% Ambiguity Ceiling”: they correctly refuse ~17% of structurally ambiguous samples, achieving ~83% accuracy; removing ambiguity lifts accuracy to ~97%.
- Benign overfitting manifests as a migration from UI to CI during prolonged training, causing CVS collapse while raw accuracy remains stable.
- The Gumbel‑Softmax temperature τ (0.7–0.9) controls the UI→CI migration; low τ forces premature commitment and accelerates CVS decay.
- The optimal model state maximizes CVS, not accuracy; early CVS peaks (Epoch 1–2) should be selected via CVS‑based early stopping.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1 – Benign Overfitting as UI→CI Migration**: During training, the model gradually reclassifies samples it previously flagged as appropriately uncertain (UI) into confidently incorrect predictions (CI). This increases coverage (more samples receive a confident commitment) but degrades both commitment accuracy and appropriate uncertainty rate. Standard accuracy remains insensitive because UI and CI are both counted as errors. Evidence: Table 10 shows UI decreasing from 2,082 to 1,441 while CI increases from 1,507 to 2,029 between Epochs 1 and 2, with test accuracy changing only from 82.11% to 82.70%. Break condition: CVS declines while test accuracy stays stable or increases; training must be halted at the CVS peak.

**Mechanism 2 – 83% Ambiguity Ceiling as Appropriate Refusal**: For samples where structural evidence alone is insufficient (e.g., shirt vs. pullover), the discrete model outputs 0 (uncertainty) instead of guessing. This lowers accuracy on ambiguous subsets but prevents confident hallucinations. Evidence: The model’s refusal aligns with the limit of topological discrimination; ablation of ambiguous classes raises accuracy to 97%, confirming the ceiling is dataset‑driven. Break condition: Introducing texture/context features could override the refusal, converting UI to CI and potentially surpassing the ceiling at the cost of reliability.

**Mechanism 3 – Temperature τ Controls Uncertainty Expression**: Gumbel‑Softmax temperature directly modulates the sharpness of commitments. Moderate τ (0.7–0.9) preserves the ability to express uncertainty; very low τ forces high coverage by eliminating the 0 state, artificially converting UI→CI and accelerating the CVS decay seen in overfitting. Evidence: τ‑sweep experiments show CVS peaks within 0.7–0.9, while τ=0.1 yields highest coverage but lowest CVS. Training dynamics (E1→E2 migration) mimic the effect of low τ. Break condition: Early annealing of τ too low can permanently destroy uncertainty expression, making CVS recovery impossible.

## Foundational Learning
- **Discrete vs. continuous commitment**: The CVS framework applies to architectures that explicitly output ternary states {-W, 0, +W}. The 0 output represents epistemic uncertainty, not a numerical value. *Quick check*: If a model outputs 0 on a sample with a ground‑truth label, is this always an error under CVS? Explain. (Answer: No; 0 on an ambiguous or uncertain sample is appropriate, counted as UI or UC depending on ground truth.)
- **Epistemic calibration**: CVS measures whether the model’s certainty aligns with actual correctness. High accuracy can mask low calibration if errors are confidently made. *Quick check*: Which is worse for a medical diagnosis system: high accuracy with low CVS, or moderate accuracy with high CVS? Justify using CI vs. UI. (Answer: Low CVS is worse because CI implies confident wrong diagnoses that could lead to harmful actions; UI implies appropriate uncertainty that might trigger human review.)
- **Structural vs. textural discrimination**: The 83% ceiling arises when discrimination requires texture or context beyond topology (e.g., shirt vs. pullover). Recognizing that some categories depend on such features explains why refusing commitment is rational. *Quick check*: Could a model achieve >95% accuracy on full Fashion‑MNIST by learning texture? Would this contradict the CVS framework’s conclusions? Why or why not? (Answer: Yes, by using texture it could surpass the ceiling, but this would involve converting ambiguous samples from UI to confident predictions, potentially increasing CI and reducing CVS, thus contradicting the claim that the discrete system’s refusal is appropriate. The CVS framework would then show lower calibration.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Input → Observers (score paths) → Gumbel‑Softmax (τ) → ProbableCollapse (threshold) → Ternary output {-W,0,+W} → Loss (cross‑entropy on committed outputs only) → FractalOptimizer (multi-scale updates). CVS Tracker logs CC, CI, UC, UI counts per epoch using a certainty threshold (e.g., 0.7).
- **Critical path**: The discrete selection happens via Gumbel‑Softmax for differentiable training, followed by a ProbableCollapseLayer that maps scores to {-W,0,+W} based on a confidence band. The FractalOptimizer applies distinct learning rates to coarse, triadic, and fine frequency bands (requires `batch_scale=4.0`). The CVS metrics are computed post‑epoch by comparing the certainty scores (before collapse) against the threshold and ground truth.
- **Design tradeoffs**: 
  - Coverage vs. CVS: Lower commitment threshold increases coverage but risks UI→CI migration; optimal threshold maximizes CVS, not accuracy.
  - τ vs. stability: Keep τ fixed in 0.7–0.9; annealing too low erases uncertainty expression.
  - Dataset curation: Removing ambiguous classes boosts accuracy but may create an unrealistically clean task; for realistic evaluation retain ambiguity and use CVS.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - No Platonic Spike (E1 gap ≤ 0): Dataset too ambiguous or observers poorly initialized.
  - CVS collapse with stable accuracy: Classic UI→CI migration; monitor CI count rising while AppropUncert falls.
  - E3/E7‑style catastrophes (accuracy ~50%): Complete representational instability; likely FractalOptimizer band interference or incorrect batch scaling.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Ablation by ambiguity**: Train on full Fashion‑MNIST and on the subset excluding shirt/pullover/coat. Plot accuracy and CVS (threshold 0.7). Expect 83% vs. 97% accuracy and higher AppropUncert on full set.
  2. **τ‑sweep**: Train on IMDB strong‑sentiment with τ ∈ {0.1,0.5,0.7,0.9}. Track CVS and coverage. Expect τ=0.1 gives highest coverage but lowest CVS; τ=0.7–0.9 peaks CVS.
  3. **Full trajectory with CVS early stopping**: On full IMDB, checkpoint every epoch, plot accuracy and CVS. Select model with max CVS; expect Epoch 1 or 2 has highest CVS despite lower later accuracy.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1 – Invariance of the 83% Ambiguity Ceiling**: Is the ceiling an invariant property of all discrete commitment architectures, or specific to the ProbableCollapseLayer/Eidos design? The paper tests dataset‑driven hypothesis (H2) by removing ambiguous classes, but not architectural hypothesis (H1). No alternative discrete commitment mechanisms were evaluated. *What evidence would resolve it*: Train at least two other discrete selection mechanisms on the same ambiguous benchmarks. If they also plateau near 83% with high UI, the ceiling may be fundamental; if they surpass it with different CVS profiles, it is architecture‑specific.

**Open Question 2 – Hierarchical Texture Augmentation**: Can a hierarchical system that first applies discrete structural commitment and then invokes a separate texture/context module for ambiguous samples preserve high CVS while breaking the 83% accuracy ceiling? The paper proposes augmenting with a texture module activated when structural confidence is low, but does not implement or evaluate it. *What evidence would resolve it*: Build a two‑branch system: (1) discrete commitment module (outputs {-W,0,+W}) for structural features; (2) continuous texture/context module that only processes samples where the first module outputs 0. Evaluate accuracy and CVS; success requires accuracy >83% *and* CVS comparable to the pure discrete model on clean subsets.

**Open Question 3 – Optimization Dynamics of UI→CI Migration**: What is the precise optimization dynamics (loss landscape geometry, gradient signals) that drive the pathological UI→CI migration during benign overfitting? The paper documents the migration (UI↓, CI↑) and links it to continued training, but does not explain *why* the model learns to be overconfident on ambiguous samples instead of learning to classify them correctly or maintaining uncertainty. *What evidence would resolve it*: Mechanistic analysis: track gradient norms and curvature around ambiguous samples across epochs; ablate FractalOptimizer’s frequency bands; visualize decision boundary evolution for ambiguous class pairs (e.g., shirt vs. pullover) using activation atlases. Test whether migration is an attractor of the loss landscape under standard cross‑entropy on ambiguous data.

**Open Question 4 – Generalization of the Excitability Phase Diagram**: Does the “Excitability Phase Diagram” pattern (early CVS peak followed by monotonic CVS decay despite stable accuracy) generalize to other discrete commitment systems trained on different ambiguous datasets? The pattern is shown for MNIST (clean) and implied for ambiguous sets, but only for the Eidos architecture. *What evidence would resolve it*: Replicate full training with CVS tracking on at least three diverse discrete architectures (different ternary activation schemes) across multiple ambiguous benchmarks (full Fashion‑MNIST, full IMDB, CIFAR‑10 with ambiguous class merges). Test if CVS consistently peaks early (within 1–5 epochs) and then degrades monotonically, regardless of architecture and ambiguity level.

**Open Question 5 – Predictive Power of CI/(CI+UI) for Player Experience**: Can the CVS “playtesting metric” CI/(CI+UI) quantitatively predict real‑world player behavior (refund rates, negative review likelihood) better than raw accuracy or traditional sentiment scores? The paper maps quadrants to player experience and proposes this ratio, but provides no empirical validation. *What evidence would resolve it*: Collect data from game launches where pre‑release surveys measure player expectations (certainty) and post‑release outcomes (satisfaction). Code outcomes into CC/CI/UC/UI based on expectation vs. experience. Test whether CI/(CI+UI) is a stronger predictor of negative reviews/refunds than aggregate satisfaction or raw expectation‑reality match.

## Limitations
- **Implementation gaps**: Core components (ProbableCollapseLayer, FractalOptimizer) are described but not open‑sourced; reverse‑engineering may introduce subtle deviations affecting CVS dynamics.
- **Hyperparameter transferability**: Vision task configurations (e.g., model size, observers) are unspecified; scaling from the provided NLP settings may not preserve ternary behavior or the ambiguity ceiling.
- **Threshold dependency**: The certainty threshold (0.7) is dataset‑specific; its robustness across modalities or distributions is unverified.

## Confidence
- **UI→CI migration as benign overfitting**: High (supported by epoch‑wise tabular evidence and logical coherence).
- **83% ambiguity ceiling as dataset‑driven**: Medium (ablation evidence strong but limited to specific class removals in Fashion‑MNIST; generalizability untested).
- **τ (temperature) controls uncertainty expression**: Medium (τ‑sweep results clear, but break condition of permanent UI loss is inferred, not directly demonstrated).
