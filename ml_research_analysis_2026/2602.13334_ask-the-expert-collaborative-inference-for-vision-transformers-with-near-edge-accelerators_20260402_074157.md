# Ask the Expert: Collaborative Inference for Vision Transformers with Near-Edge Accelerators

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.13334
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13334
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A collaborative inference framework combining a lightweight edge ViT with specialist near-edge experts, trained via progressive specialization and routed by edge Top‑k predictions, achieves notable accuracy gains and up to 45% latency reduction on CIFAR‑100.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the challenge of running Vision Transformers (ViTs) on resource-constrained edge devices. It proposes a two-tier inference system: a compact generalist ViT runs on the edge, while a library of medium-sized specialist ViTs resides on a more powerful near-edge accelerator. The edge model evaluates each input; if its confidence (max softmax probability) falls below a threshold, the input is offloaded. The offloading decision uses the edge model’s Top‑k predictions to select the appropriate specialist(s) based on a predefined partition of the class space. Specialists are trained with a progressive specialist strategy that encourages focused expertise while retaining general knowledge through distillation from a strong teacher. Evaluated on CIFAR‑100, the method improves overall accuracy by 2.76% over static experts, reduces latency by up to 45% compared to edge‑only execution, and saves up to 46% energy versus near‑edge‑only offload.

## Method Summary
The system operates in two phases. Offline, the class space is partitioned into S non‑overlapping superclass‑based subsets. For each combination of up to k subsets, a specialist expert is trained. Training starts from a large teacher ViT trained on the full dataset with a progressive loss weighting that increases the importance of samples belonging to the expert’s target subsets. The teacher is then pruned to a smaller architecture (e.g., DeiT‑4H) and distilled into a student using hard targets and the same progressive weighting. The edge generalist is similarly obtained by pruning and distilling a large teacher on the full data without progressive weighting. Online, the edge model processes an input; if its top‑1 confidence exceeds a threshold τ, the prediction is output immediately. Otherwise, the edge’s Top‑k class indices are mapped to their partition IDs, and the union of these IDs selects the corresponding specialist from the library. The specialist’s prediction becomes the final output.

## Key Results
- Progressive specialist training boosts expert specialization accuracy by 4.12% and overall accuracy by 2.76% over static experts.
- The collaborative framework reduces inference latency by up to 45% compared to edge‑only execution and saves up to 46% energy compared to near‑edge‑only offload.
- It improves accuracy by up to 1.7% relative to a generalist co‑inference baseline (edge + near‑edge with a single generalist near‑edge model).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The approach leverages the observation that a modestly sized edge model often has high Top‑1 confidence on many inputs, allowing fast local inference. For uncertain inputs where the edge is unsure (low confidence), the Top‑k set frequently includes the true class, providing a reliable signal to route the sample to a specialist that has been trained to excel on that subset of classes. Progressive weighting during specialist training ensures that the expert focuses on its target classes while still maintaining performance on others, preventing catastrophic forgetting. The use of distillation preserves the teacher’s knowledge in a compact student, and the partition‑based routing is simple and efficient. Together, these elements yield a system that dynamically allocates compute: easy samples stay on the edge, hard ones are offloaded to more capable but less frequently used near‑edge experts, optimizing the accuracy‑latency‑energy trade‑off.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Edge Generalist (DeiT‑3H) → Confidence Check → (if τ exceeded) Output; else → Top‑k Extraction → Partition Mapping (class → partition ID) → Union of partition IDs → Expert Lookup → Specialist Expert (DeiT‑4H/6H) → Output

**Critical path:**  
1. Input image resized to 224×224 and normalized.  
2. Edge model forward pass yields logits and softmax.  
3. Compute max softmax probability p_max.  
4. If p_max ≥ τ, return top‑1 class.  
5. Else, retrieve top‑k class indices, map each to its partition via predefined mapping S(·).  
6. Take union of partition IDs, find expert indexed by that combination (pre‑trained).  
7. Run expert forward pass, return its top‑1 prediction.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Number of partitions S:* Larger S yields more experts, enabling finer specialization and potentially higher accuracy, but increases memory footprint on the near‑edge device and may lead to under‑utilized experts.  
- *Threshold τ:* Lower τ reduces offload, favoring latency/energy but may hurt accuracy; higher τ increases offload, improving accuracy at the cost of more near‑edge computation.  
- *Edge model capacity:* A smaller edge model (e.g., DeiT‑3H) reduces edge latency but may have lower Top‑k recall, increasing misrouting. A larger edge could reduce offload but increase edge cost.  
- *Expert capacity:* Larger experts (DeiT‑6H) offer better accuracy but higher near‑edge latency/energy when invoked.

**Failure signatures:**  
- *Low Top‑k recall:* If the true class is rarely in the edge’s Top‑k, routing fails consistently; measured by Top‑k accuracy.  
- *Over‑specialization:* Experts perform well only on their target subsets but poorly on others; overall accuracy may drop if routing misassigns.  
- *High offload rate:* Unexpectedly large fraction of samples routed to near‑edge, leading to latency/energy worse than edge‑only.  
- *Missing expert combination:* If the union of partition IDs from Top‑k does not correspond to any trained expert, the system fails; must ensure all combinations up to size k are covered.

**Exactly 3 first experiments:**  
1. **Progressive vs static specialist training:** Train a single expert on a specific partition (e.g., partition 0) using progressive weighting and compare its target accuracy to a static expert trained only on that subset. Expect progressive to show higher specialization.  
2. **Edge Top‑k recall measurement:** Compute the edge model’s Top‑k (k=2) accuracy on CIFAR‑100 validation set. Verify it is sufficiently high (paper implies >~85%) to make routing effective.  
3. **End‑to‑end trade‑off profiling:** For a range of thresholds τ (e.g., 0.3 to 0.9), measure overall accuracy, average latency, and energy consumption on a fixed test set. Compare against edge‑only and near‑edge‑only baselines to reproduce the reported Pareto curves.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Scalability to complex datasets:** Does the framework yield similar gains on larger‑scale benchmarks like ImageNet or fine‑grained recognition datasets, where the recall gap may be larger but expert specialization complexity increases?  
2. **Optimizing edge training for Top‑k:** Can the edge model’s Top‑k accuracy be improved by using list‑wise loss functions or curriculum learning, thereby enhancing routing precision and overall system performance?  
3. **Impact of edge model compression:** How do aggressive compression techniques (quantization, pruning) affect the quality of Top‑k predictions and routing robustness? Can progressive specialist training compensate for such degradation?  
4. **Handling non‑stationary data:** How does the fixed expert library behave under data distribution shifts or class imbalance? Is online updating of experts or the routing policy necessary for dynamic environments?

## Limitations
- The exact pruning procedure to derive DeiT‑3/4/6H from DeiT‑Base is not fully described, hindering exact replication.  
- It is unclear whether strong augmentations (Mixup, Cutmix, label smoothing) are applied to the edge generalist, which could affect baseline accuracy comparisons.  
- All reported quantitative improvements (e.g., +4.12% specialization, 45% latency reduction) have only medium confidence due to the above ambiguities.
