# Why Some Models Resist Unlearning: A Linear Stability Perspective

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.02986
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02986
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Data coherence (normalized max eigenvalue of the cross-sample Hessian) governs gradient-based unlearning stability: above a derived threshold unlearning diverges, below it converges; lower SNR reduces coherence, easing forgetting.

## Executive Summary
Machine unlearning—removing the influence of specific data from a trained model—lacks rigorous theoretical foundations. This paper introduces an asymptotic linear stability framework to analyze gradient-based unlearning. The central metric is data coherence, defined as the normalized maximum eigenvalue of a matrix capturing cross-sample Hessian alignment among retain, forget, and cross sample pairs. The authors prove that if coherence exceeds a threshold (Theorem 3.6), unlearning diverges; if it falls below a matching lower bound (Theorem 3.7), unlearning converges.

Empirical analysis on a two-layer ReLU CNN trained on signal-plus-noise data reveals that lower signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), which promotes memorization of noise, weakens cross-sample alignment and reduces coherence, making forgetting easier. High SNR, conversely, strengthens alignment and creates resistance. Extensive experiments measuring Hessian spectra and CNN activation heatmaps confirm both the theoretical stability frontier and the memorization–forgetting relationship. This work provides the first theoretical conditions for unlearning stability and identifies data properties that influence unlearning difficulty.

## Method Summary
The authors develop a linear stability theory for iterative, gradient-based unlearning. Given a model trained on a full dataset, unlearning aims to erase the forget set while preserving retain set performance. To study stability, they linearize the gradient update around the post‑training parameters and examine the Jacobian of the update map. This yields a key object—the mix‑Hessian—which aggregates second‑order interactions (Hessians) across samples from retain, forget, and cross pairs. Coherence is then defined as the maximum eigenvalue of the mix‑Hessian, normalized by learning rate, batch size, and dataset sizes. Two main theorems provide a necessary condition for divergence (coherence larger than a bound) and a sufficient condition for convergence (coherence smaller than a bound). The asymptotic analysis assumes small perturbations and a quadratic local loss model.

Empirically, the authors use a two‑layer ReLU CNN trained on synthetic data comprising a signal and additive noise. By varying the SNR, they control the degree of memorization (low SNR yields more noise memorization). They compute coherence via cross‑sample Hessians and show that lower SNR leads to reduced coherence and thus easier unlearning. They also visualize Hessian spectra and activation heatmaps, which exhibit stronger cross‑sample alignment at high SNR, corroborating the theory.

## Key Results
- Coherence directly governs unlearning dynamics: if the maximum eigenvalue of the mix‑Hessian $D$ exceeds $\sqrt{2}\sigma/\eta\big((1-\alpha)n_f/\sqrt{n_r/B-1} + \alpha n_r/\sqrt{n_f/B-1}\big)$, gradient‑based unlearning diverges (Theorem 3.6); below a matching lower bound (Theorem 3.7) it converges.
- Analysis of a two‑layer ReLU CNN with signal‑plus‑noise data shows that lower SNR (more memorization) weakens cross‑sample alignment, reducing coherence and making forgetting easier, while high SNR yields strong alignment and resistance.
- Extensive experiments measuring Hessian spectra and CNN heatmaps verify the predicted stability frontier and the memorization–forgetting relationship.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The mechanism rests on linearizing the unlearning update and analyzing the resulting discrete‑time system. Gradient‑based unlearning subtracts updates computed on the forget set from the parameters while maintaining retain set performance. The mix‑Hessian arises from the second‑order expansion of the gradient residuals and captures how the gradient direction from one sample is altered by the presence of others. Coherence—the normalized spectral norm of this matrix—quantifies the worst‑case alignment between retain and forget objectives. When coherence is large, even small unlearning steps amplify interference because the gradient updates from forget and retain samples are nearly opposed, leading to divergence. When coherence is small, the objectives are more compatible and the update converges. The SNR effect occurs because high‑SNR data produces more correlated (aligned) Hessians across samples, increasing coherence; low‑SNR (noisy) data yields less alignment, lowering coherence and easing unlearning.

- **Data coherence**: The normalized maximum eigenvalue of the mix‑Hessian matrix that captures cross‑sample second‑order alignment. *Why needed*: It is the central predictive metric for unlearning stability. *Quick check*: Why is the max eigenvalue, rather than the full spectrum, used in the threshold?
- **Cross‑sample Hessian alignment**: The degree to which Hessian matrices (or directional curvatures) of individual samples point in similar directions. *Why needed*: Alignment between retain and forget samples causes interference in gradient updates, raising coherence. *Quick check*: How would perfect alignment across all samples affect coherence?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map** (plain text flow):
Synthetic Dataset (varying SNR) → Two‑Layer ReLU CNN Training → Compute Cross‑Sample Hessians → Mix‑Hessian Construction → Data Coherence Calculation → Compare to Theoretical Threshold → Predict Unlearning Stability → Empirical Validation (Hessian spectra, heatmaps, unlearning runs)

**Critical Path**:
1. Train a model on a full dataset.
2. Partition data into retain and forget sets.
3. Estimate cross‑sample Hessians (e.g., via Hessian‑vector products or direct computation if feasible).
4. Assemble the mix‑Hessian matrix and compute its maximum normalized eigenvalue (coherence).
5. Plug coherence and hyperparameters (learning rate, batch size, split sizes) into the theoretical bound to predict stability.
6. If performing empirical validation, run unlearning and measure retention/forgetting performance.

**Design Tradeoffs**:
- *Asymptotic vs. finite‑step*: The theory assumes infinitesimal steps and a perfect quadratic local model; in practice, finite learning rates and non‑convexity may shift the effective stability boundary.
- *Computation vs. accuracy*: Exact coherence requires $O(n^2)$ Hessian evaluations for $n$ samples; approximations (e.g., random projection or subsampling) reduce cost but introduce bias/variance.
- *Generality vs. rigor*: The analysis specializes to quadratic‑like local geometry and may not hold for deep, highly non‑convex models; yet it offers a clear predictor when assumptions approximately hold.

**Failure Signatures**:
- Coherence exceeds the theoretical divergence threshold.
- Unlearning loss on the forget set diverges or oscillates while retain performance collapses.
- Hessian spectra show a large outlier eigenvalue far beyond the bulk.
- Discrepancy between predicted convergence and observed divergence for coherence just below bound (indicating limitation of asymptotic approximation).

**First Three Experiments**:
1. **Synthetic quadratic verification**: Construct a simple convex quadratic model where the mix‑Hessian is known analytically. Compute coherence and run gradient‑based unlearning (e.g., gradient descent on a combined loss) to confirm that the predicted threshold separates converging and diverging regimes.
2. **SNR–coherence relationship**: Train the two‑layer ReLU CNN on synthetic datasets with a range of SNR values (e.g., 0.1 to 10). For each, compute coherence and verify that coherence monotonically increases with SNR, and that unlearning success rate decreases with coherence.
3. **Stability frontier mapping**: For a fixed CNN and SNR, systematically vary the forget set size $n_f$, retain set size $n_r$, learning rate $\eta$, and batch size $B$. For each configuration, compute the theoretical bound, determine whether coherence lies above or below, and compare to the empirical outcome of unlearning (e.g., after 100 steps, whether forget loss increases and retain loss remains low). This tests the tightness of the theorems and identifies regimes where the bound is conservative.

## Limitations
- Asymptotic linear analysis may not capture finite‑step, non‑convex unlearning dynamics in realistic deep networks.
- Coherence estimation via cross‑sample Hessians is computationally expensive; approximations for large models lack validation.
- Empirical evaluation is confined to a shallow CNN on synthetic data; generalizability to deep architectures and real‑world datasets remains uncertain.

## Confidence
- **Coherence predicts unlearning stability**: Medium (theory solid under assumptions, but real complexities may affect predictability).
- **Threshold conditions (divergence/convergence bounds)**: Medium (proofs correct but bounds appear conservative; empirical transition may be sharper).
- **Low SNR → easier forgetting**: Medium (synthetic evidence strong; real‑data verification pending).
- **Empirical validation (Hessian spectra/heatmaps)**: High (measurements align with predictions in experiments).

## Next Checks
1. **Generalization**: Test coherence on deeper/real models (ResNet‑50 on CIFAR‑10, BERT on GLUE) to see if the stability frontier holds for non‑synthetic settings.
2. **Bound tightness**: For a simple net (e.g., two‑layer CNN), sweep key hyperparameters ($\eta$, $B$, $\alpha$) to locate the empirical divergence boundary and compare it to the theoretical threshold.
3. **Real‑data link**: Correlate coherence with unlearning performance on datasets with varying memorization risk (e.g., data vulnerable to membership inference) to assess whether coherence explains practical unlearning difficulty.
