# Uncertainty in Federated Granger Causality: From Origins to Systemic Consequences

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.13004  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13004  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Derived closed-form recursions for uncertainty propagation in federated Granger causality, proving steady-state variances depend only on client data statistics, independent of initial priors.

## Executive Summary
Existing federated Granger causality methods provide only deterministic point estimates, ignoring uncertainty from data noise and model variability. This paper introduces the first rigorous uncertainty quantification framework for federated causal inference, explicitly separating aleatoric (data) and epistemic (model) uncertainties. By treating client and server parameters as random variables, the authors derive exact covariance recursions and identify four cross-covariance terms that couple uncertainties across the client-server loop. Under stationarity, the steady-state variances are shown to depend solely on client data moments, eliminating sensitivity to initial priors. Empirical validation on synthetic and industrial datasets (HAI, SWaT) confirms that tracking uncertainty improves estimation accuracy, convergence speed, and interpretability.

## Method Summary
The method extends the FedGC architecture by maintaining second-order statistics for all learnable quantities. Each client runs a local Kalman filter to compress high‑dimensional measurements \(y_t^m\) into a low‑dimensional state estimate \(\hat{h}_{t,c}^m\). This is augmented with a learnable matrix \(\theta_t^m\) to form \(\hat{h}_{t,a}^m = \hat{h}_{t,c}^m + \theta_t^m y_t^m\). The client updates \(\theta_t^m\) using a combination of a local loss \((L_m)_a = \|y_t^m - C_m \hat{h}_{t,a}^m\|^2\) and a gradient from the server \(\nabla_{\theta_m} L_s\). The server aggregates all clients’ \(\hat{h}_{t,a}^m\) to build predictions \(H_t^a\) and \(H_t^s\) (using current off‑diagonal blocks \(\hat{A}_{mn}\)), computes global loss \(L_s = \|H_t^a - H_t^s\|^2\), and updates each \(\hat{A}_{mn}\) via gradient descent.

The innovation lies in deriving closed‑form recursions for the covariances: \(\Sigma^{\theta_m} = \text{Var}(\theta_m)\), \(\Omega_m = \text{Cov}(\theta_m, y_m)\), \(\Lambda_m = \text{Cov}(\theta_m, \hat{h}_{m,a})\), \(\Sigma^{A_{mn}}\), \(\Gamma_{mn} = \text{Cov}(A_{mn}, \hat{h}_{m,a})\), \(\Psi_{mn} = \text{Cov}(A_{mn}, \theta_m)\), and \(\Sigma^{h_m} = \text{Var}(\hat{h}_{m,a})\). These recursions follow from vectorizing the affine update equations and expanding second moments. Key assumptions: weak stationarity of client data (constant mean \(\mu_{y_m}\) and covariance \(\Sigma_{y_m}\)), linear (affine) updates, additive noise, and independence across clients. Before tracking, data moments are estimated (e.g., from a pre‑run) to compute gain matrices \(D_n\), \(H_m\) that appear in the recursions; the spectral radii must satisfy \(\rho(D_n)<1\) and \(\rho(H_m)<1\) for convergence.

## Key Results
- First rigorous uncertainty quantification framework for federated Granger causality, distinguishing aleatoric and epistemic uncertainties.
- Derived closed‑form recursions for covariance propagation, uncovering four novel cross‑covariance terms that couple client and server uncertainties.
- Steady‑state variances depend exclusively on client data statistics, not on initial parameter priors, enhancing robustness.
- Empirical validation on synthetic and industrial datasets (HAI, SWaT) demonstrates faster convergence of covariance traces, lower estimation errors, and improved interpretability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Closed‑form uncertainty propagation
The authors treat all random variables (client parameters \(\theta_m\), server parameters \(A_{mn}\), augmented states \(\hat{h}_{m,a}\), data \(y_m\)) and derive exact second‑moment updates by vectorizing the affine client and server equations. This yields linear recursions for the variances and introduces four cross‑covariances: \(\Omega_m = \text{Cov}(\theta_m, y_m)\), \(\Lambda_m = \text{Cov}(\theta_m, \hat{h}_{m,a})\), \(\Gamma_{mn} = \text{Cov}(A_{mn}, \hat{h}_{m,a})\), and \(\Psi_{mn} = \text{Cov}(A_{mn}, \theta_m)\). These appear explicitly in the covariance recursions for \(\Sigma^{\theta_m}\) (Theorem 6.8) and \(\Sigma^{A_{mn}}\) (Theorem 6.7). The derivation relies on linearity of updates, stationarity, additive noise, and cross‑client independence; any violation would add extra terms.

### Mechanism 2: Steady‑state independence from priors
Under weak stationarity, the gain matrices \(D_n\) and \(H_m\) converge to fixed values, turning the covariance recursions into linear equations. The steady‑state solution, expressed via a Neumann series (Theorem 7.4–7.5), depends only on client data moments (\(\mu_{y_m}, \Sigma_{y_m}\)) and not on the initial covariances \(\Sigma^0_{\theta_m}\) or \(\Sigma^0_{A_{mn}}\). This is because the recursions are contractive (\(\rho(D_n)<1, \rho(H_m)<1\)) and the forcing terms (e.g., \(Q_{mn}(\cdot)\)) are purely data‑driven. Thus epistemic uncertainty from initialization vanishes, while aleatoric uncertainty from data persists.

### Mechanism 3: Non‑zero cross‑covariances
The feedback loop—\(\theta_m\) affects \(\hat{h}_{m,a}\), which affects \(A_{mn}\), which affects the server gradient sent back to \(\theta_m\)—creates dependencies that keep all four cross‑covariances generally non‑zero. They are essential for accurate propagation: e.g., \(\Sigma^{h_m}\) depends on \(\Omega_m\) and \(\Sigma^{\theta_m}\) (Lemma 6.5), and the variance of the communicated gradient depends on \(\Gamma_{mn}\) (Lemma 6.6). Empirical results (Figures 2–3) confirm that steady‑state variances are unaffected by initial priors, validating the coupling captured by these terms. Ignoring any cross‑covariance would break the closed loop and yield inaccurate uncertainty estimates.

## Foundational Learning
- **Granger Causality in State‑Space Models**: Why needed – Directed causal links between clients are encoded in the off‑diagonal blocks of the state transition matrix \(A\). Quick check – In a multi‑client LTI system, \(A_{mn}\neq0\) indicates client \(n\) Granger‑causes client \(m\).
- **Federated Learning (Client‑Server Paradigm)**: Why needed – Data remain local; the server aggregates updates without accessing raw data. Quick check – What does each client send to the server each round? Answer: the augmented state \(\hat{h}_{t,a}^m\).
- **Kalman Filtering for State Compression**: Why needed – Each client compresses high‑dimensional measurements \(y_t^m\) into a low‑dimensional state estimate \(\hat{h}_{t,c}^m\) using local dynamics. Quick check – What are the two steps of the Kalman filter? Prediction and update.
- **Aleatoric vs. Epistemic Uncertainty**: Why needed – Aleatoric (data noise) is irreducible; epistemic (model) uncertainty can be reduced with more data. Quick check – Which type diminishes as more data is collected? Answer: epistemic.
- **Stationarity in Time Series**: Why needed – Guarantees constant data moments so gain matrices converge. Quick check – What does weak stationarity require? Constant mean and covariance over time.
- **Spectral Radius and Convergence**: Why needed – Conditions \(\rho(D_n)<1\) and \(\rho(H_m)<1\) ensure covariance recursions contract. Quick check – If \(\rho(D)<1\), what happens to \(D^k\) as \(k\to\infty\)? It converges to zero.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Client \(m\):  
\(y_t^m\) → Kalman filter \((A_{mm}, C_{mm}, Q_{mm}, R_{mm})\) → \(\hat{h}_{t,c}^m\)  
\(\hat{h}_{t,c}^m + \theta_t^m y_t^m\) → \(\hat{h}_{t,a}^m\)  
Send \(\hat{h}_{t,a}^m\) to server; receive \(\nabla_{\hat{h}_{m,a}} L_s\)  
Update \(\theta_t^m\): \(\theta_{t+1}^m = \theta_t^m - \eta_1 \nabla_{\theta_m}(L_m)_a - \eta_2 \nabla_{\theta_m} L_s\)  
Track: \(\Sigma^{\theta_m}\), \(\Omega_m = \text{Cov}(\theta_m, y_m)\), \(\Lambda_m = \text{Cov}(\theta_m, \hat{h}_{m,a})\)  

Server:  
Receive all \(\hat{h}_{t,a}^m\)  
Build \(H_t^a = [A_{11}\hat{h}_{t-1,a}^1, \dots, A_{MM}\hat{h}_{t-1,a}^M]\) and \(H_t^s\) using current \(\hat{A}_{mn}\)  
Compute loss \(L_s = \|H_t^a - H_t^s\|^2\)  
Update each off‑diagonal block: \(\hat{A}_{t+1}^{mn} = \hat{A}_{t}^{mn} - \gamma \nabla_{A_{mn}} L_s\)  
Send \(\nabla_{\hat{h}_{m,a}} L_s\) to each client \(m\)  
Track: \(\Sigma^{A_{mn}}\), \(\Gamma_{mn} = \text{Cov}(A_{mn}, \hat{h}_{m,a})\), \(\Psi_{mn} = \text{Cov}(A_{mn}, \theta_m)\), \(\Sigma^{h_m}\)

### Critical path
1. **Client → server**: \(\hat{h}_{t,a}^m\) carries uncertainty from \(\theta_t^m\) and \(y_t^m\) (\(\Omega_m, \Sigma^{\theta_m}\)); \(\Sigma^{h_m}\) depends on these (Lemma 6.5).  
2. **Server update**: \(\hat{A}_{mn}\) updated using all \(\hat{h}_{t,a}^m\); \(\Sigma^{A_{mn}}\) recursion includes \(\Sigma^{h_m}\) and \(\Gamma_{mn}\) (Theorem 6.7).  
3. **Server → client**: \(\nabla_{\hat{h}_{m,a}} L_s\) sent; its variance depends on \(\Sigma^{A_{mn}}\) and \(\Gamma_{mn}\) (Lemma 6.6).  
4. **Client update**: \(\theta_m\) updated; \(\Sigma^{\theta_m}\) recursion depends on \(\Sigma^{h_m}\), \(\Sigma^{A_{mn}}\), \(\Lambda_m\), \(\Gamma_{mn}\), \(\Psi_{mn}\) (Theorem 6.8), closing the loop.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Linearity vs. realism**: Closed‑form recursions require affine updates; nonlinear models (e.g., EKF/GP) only yield approximations without exact recursions.
- **Stationarity vs. adaptability**: Steady‑state results assume constant data moments; non‑stationarity (e.g., drift) breaks gain convergence, motivating heuristic EWMA.
- **Complexity vs. completeness**: Tracking \(O(M^2)\) cross‑covariances may be costly for large \(M\); sparsity in the causal graph could reduce burden but is not exploited in the analysis.
- **Independence assumptions**: Cross‑client independence (A2–A3) simplifies recursions; correlated data (e.g., shared external shocks) would introduce additional cross‑terms.

### Failure signatures
- **Diverging covariances**: If \(\rho(D_n)\ge1\) or \(\rho(H_m)\ge1\) (e.g., from too large learning rates or unstable client dynamics), covariance traces explode.
- **Uncalibrated uncertainty under non‑stationarity**: Drifting data moments cause mismatch to steady‑state predictions, leading to over‑ or under‑confident estimates.
- **Missing cross‑covariance**: Setting \(\Omega_m\), \(\Lambda_m\), \(\Gamma_{mn}\), or \(\Psi_{mn}\) to zero breaks the coupling and yields inaccurate propagated uncertainty.
- **Violation of independence**: Correlated client data (\(\text{Cov}(y_m,y_n)\neq0\)) invalidates Lemma C.1, adding extra cross‑client terms and breaking recursions.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Synthetic aleatoric test**: Vary \(\Sigma_{y_m}\) and observe faster convergence of covariance traces for larger \(\Sigma_{y_m}\) (Figure 1).
2. **Synthetic epistemic test**: Vary initial \(\Sigma^0_{\theta_m}\), \(\Sigma^0_{A_{mn}}\); steady‑state variances unchanged (Figures 2–3).
3. **Real‑world test (HAI & SWaT)**: Apply to industrial datasets; uncertainty decreases over iterations and estimation error lower when tracking uncertainty (Figures 4, 14, 17, 18).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**1. Nonlinear extension:** How can the framework be extended to general nonlinear dynamical systems without relying on EKF or GP approximations? The paper only sketches linear extensions; a full theory for arbitrary nonlinearities is missing. Resolution would require deriving closed‑form recursions (e.g., via variational methods) and proving convergence on diverse synthetic nonlinear datasets.

**2. Process noise impact:** What is the effect of non‑negligible process noise on uncertainty propagation and steady‑state variances? The analysis assumes noiseless states (A5), ignoring an aleatoric source. Incorporating process noise would alter recursions and steady‑state solutions; empirical validation against Monte‑Carlo on noisy systems is needed.

**3. Non‑stationarity handling:** Do the convergence and steady‑state results hold under time‑varying data distributions, and how can uncertainty be tracked then? Stationarity (A4) is essential; non‑stationary data may invalidate gain convergence. The paper suggests EWMA but lacks guarantees. Evidence would require theoretical analysis of time‑varying moments or adaptive step‑size rules.

**4. Spectral‑radius conditions:** Are \(\rho(D_n)<1\) and \(\rho(H_m)<1\) both necessary and sufficient for convergence, and can adaptive learning rates relax them? These conditions restrict hyperparameters; stability boundaries are unknown. Resolution would involve spectral analysis of the joint client‑server system and development of adaptive rules that stabilize beyond the strict region.

## Limitations
- Strong assumptions (linearity, weak stationarity, cross‑client independence) may not hold in many real federated settings, potentially breaking the derived recursions.
- \(O(M^2)\) memory/computation overhead for cross‑covariance tracking could be prohibitive for large numbers of clients.
- Limited empirical validation (only two datasets) and incomplete specification of hyperparameters and preprocessing hinder reproducibility and generalizability.

## Confidence
