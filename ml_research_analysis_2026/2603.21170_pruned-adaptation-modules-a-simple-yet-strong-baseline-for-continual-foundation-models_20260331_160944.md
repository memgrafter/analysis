# Pruned Adaptation Modules: A Simple yet Strong Baseline for Continual Foundation Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.21170  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21170  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** PAM achieves state-of-the-art final accuracy on multiple class-incremental learning benchmarks while drastically reducing parameters (up to 5× fewer trainable parameters and up to 6× fewer total parameters than FM-based methods). For example, on CIFAR-100 B0 Inc5 with ResNet152, PAM reaches 93.79% accuracy using only 600K trainable parameters per task, compared to EASE’s 85.97% and 1.2M parameters.

## Executive Summary
Continual learning with foundation models (FMs) often overlooks strong, lightweight convolutional baselines. This paper introduces Pruned Adaptation Modules (PAM) to bridge that gap: it freezes all but the last layer of a pre-trained ResNet and applies structured pruning to create sparse, task-specific modules. PAM limits weight updates to a tiny fraction of the network, preserving transferable features and minimizing interference. Across four benchmarks (CIFAR-100, ImageNet-R, CUB-200, Cars-196), PAM consistently outperforms recent FM-based class-incremental learning methods in final accuracy while being far more parameter‑efficient.

The results challenge the notion that larger models are necessary for effective continual learning and demonstrate that a simple combination of frozen general features and early‑pruned specialized modules can achieve superior performance with minimal overhead.

## Method Summary
PAM starts from a ResNet pre‑trained on ImageNet‑1K. The first three residual stages are frozen into a shared extractor Φ. When a new task arrives, a new, initially dense convolutional layer γ_b is attached as the fourth stage and trained together with a unified linear classifier W⊤ for one epoch via cross‑entropy. After this first epoch, structured pruning based on the L1‑norm of output channels removes all but the top 4% of channels, converting γ_b into a sparse module S_b. Training continues on S_b and W⊤ for the remaining epochs; then S_b is frozen and stored. At inference, each S_b processes the frozen features from Φ; the module that produces the highest average maximum softmax probability over a batch is selected, and its associated W⊤ yields predictions over the cumulative class set. The unified classifier expands to incorporate new classes from each task.

## Key Results
- PAM reduces trainable parameters per task by up to 5× and total parameters by up to 6× compared to state‑of‑the‑art FM‑based CIL methods like CODA‑Prompt and EASE, while achieving higher final accuracy across all four benchmarks (CIFAR-100, ImageNet-R, CUB-200, and Cars-196).
- On CIFAR‑100 B0 Inc5 with ResNet152, PAM reaches 93.79% final accuracy compared to EASE’s 85.97%, using only 600K trainable parameters versus 1.2M.
- Ablation studies identify an optimal pruning ratio of 96% (keeping 4% of channels) when pruning after the first epoch, yielding the best accuracy‑efficiency trade‑off.
- The confidence‑based module selection mechanism reliably routes samples to the correct task‑specific module without requiring task identifiers, achieving near‑oracle assignment accuracy.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
1. **Frozen backbone preserves general features**  
   Keeping all layers except the last one of the pre‑trained ResNet fixed maintains a stable, transferable feature extractor (Φ). This prevents catastrophic overwriting of broadly useful low‑level and mid‑level representations. Evidence: Sec 3.2 explicitly states the shared extractor is frozen to retain transferable representations. Break condition: If the pre‑trained backbone’s features are mismatched to the target domain (e.g., natural images → medical scans), freezing may propagate non‑transferable features and harm performance.

2. **Early structured pruning yields sparse yet capable modules**  
   After the first training epoch on a new task, channels in the newly added last layer (γ_b) are ranked by L1‑norm and all but the top 4% are pruned. This creates a sparse module S_b with dramatically fewer parameters while retaining sufficient capacity because many early filters are redundant. Evidence: Ablation in Sec 5.2 shows pruning at epoch 1 yields the best results; extreme pruning (98%) harms accuracy. Break condition: Over‑pruning or pruning later may remove essential filters, limiting representational capacity.

3. **Confidence‑based selection reliably routes samples without task IDs**  
   At inference, each S_b processes features from Φ; the module whose output probabilities exhibit the highest average maximum softmax across the batch is selected. The assumption is that each S_b becomes highly confident on its own task and less confident on others. Evidence: Figure 7a shows a strong diagonal in the module selection confusion matrix, and confidence‑based routing outperforms distance‑based alternatives (Figure 4c). Break condition: For semantically very similar tasks, confidence scores may not cleanly separate, leading to misrouting.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Image → Φ (frozen ResNet stages 1‑3) → Sb (pruned task‑specific last layer) → W⊤ (unified classifier). Inference adds a confidence‑based selector that picks Sb.
- **Critical path:**  
  1. Initialize: load pre‑trained ResNet, define Φ as frozen, create empty module registry.  
  2. For each new task D_b:  
     a. Attach a new, dense γ_b (4th stage) to Φ.  
     b. Train γ_b and W⊤ on D_b for 1 epoch with cross‑entropy.  
     c. Prune γ_b: compute L1‑norm saliency per output channel, keep top 4%, remove others → Sb.  
     d. Continue training Sb and W⊤ for remaining epochs (e.g., total 25).  
     e. Freeze Sb, store Sb and its class set.  
  3. At inference on a batch x_test:  
     a. Extract Φ(x_test).  
     b. For each stored Sb, compute p_b = σ(W⊤Sb(Φ(x_test))).  
     c. Compute confidence: c_b = mean(max(p_b) over batch).  
     d. Select module with max c_b.  
     e. Output predictions from that module.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Pruning magnitude vs. capacity: Higher pruning (e.g., 98%) yields smaller modules but risks losing critical filters; paper finds 96% optimal.  
  - Module density vs. selection robustness: Sparse modules may produce less confident predictions, potentially weakening the router.  
  - Module reuse vs. forgetting risk: Reusing modules for similar tasks reduces total modules but hurts accuracy due to overspecialization.  
  - Backbone scale vs. efficiency: Larger ResNets (RN152) give best accuracy but increase frozen parameter count; smaller ones (RN18) are ultra‑efficient but lag on fine‑grained datasets.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Poor module selection confidence: Correct Sb’s confidence not significantly higher than others → misrouting and accuracy drops.  
  - Module collapse from over‑pruning: Too few channels remain after pruning, causing training loss saturation and low accuracy.  
  - Classifier saturation: Unified W⊤ grows; improper initialization/regularization may cause interference with old class predictions even with frozen Φ.  
  - Task similarity misclassification in adaptive reuse: Bad threshold τ leads to dissimilar tasks sharing a module, causing rapid forgetting.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. *Pruning schedule ablation*: Train with pruning at epoch 1, 5, and 10 on CIFAR‑100 B0 Inc5. Expect epoch 1 to yield highest final accuracy, confirming early pruning benefit.  
  2. *Confidence vs. distance router*: Implement both confidence‑based (Eq. 6) and centroid‑distance selection. Measure per‑task module assignment accuracy (via oracle) and final CIL performance; confidence should dominate.  
  3. *Module reuse stress test*: Apply adaptive reuse (Appendix A.1) on ImageNet‑R B0 Inc20 with β=0.75. Expect significant accuracy drop vs. per‑task modules, quantifying reuse‑accuracy tradeoff.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Can PAM’s core methodology—freezing early layers and applying structured pruning to task‑specific last layers—be effectively transposed to transformer‑based foundation models such as ViT, while maintaining comparable parameter efficiency and accuracy gains?
- How well does PAM generalize to other continual learning paradigms beyond class‑incremental learning (e.g., domain‑incremental or task‑incremental learning), and what adaptations are necessary for each scenario?
- Can dynamic pruning strategies (e.g., iterative pruning during training or task‑specific sparsity schedules) enhance PAM’s parameter‑accuracy trade‑off compared to the static one‑time pruning used in the paper?
- What is the optimal way to integrate PAM’s sparse task‑specific modules into larger foundation models (e.g., combining ResNet features with ViT) to achieve both parameter efficiency and the broad generalization capabilities of large FMs?

## Limitations
- The exact architecture of the task‑specific module γ_b/S_b (single convolutional layer vs. full residual block) is unspecified, hindering faithful reproduction.
- Details of the unified classifier W⊤ (initialization and dimensionality) remain ambiguous.
- The pruning implementation (e.g., channel reordering after pruning) is not fully described, which may lead to variations in results.
