# Functional Decomposition and Shapley Interactions for Interpreting Survival Models

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.16505  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16505  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Introduces Survival Functional Decomposition (SurvFD) and SurvSHAP‑IQ to interpret survival models by decomposing time‑dependent and time‑independent effects and estimating higher‑order Shapley interactions, achieving near‑perfect local accuracy and clinically relevant explanations.

## Executive Summary
Survival models predict hazard or survival probabilities over time, but their outputs are inherently non‑additive, limiting standard additive explanation methods. This paper proposes **Survival Functional Decomposition (SurvFD)**, a theoretical framework that separates higher‑order effects into time‑dependent and time‑independent components, and **SurvSHAP‑IQ**, a practical algorithm that extends Shapley interaction quantification to time‑indexed survival functions. Theoretically, SurvFD recovers ground‑truth interaction structures under feature independence and additive risk representations, though transformations to hazard or survival can introduce spurious interactions. Empirically, SurvSHAP‑IQ accurately estimates interactions in simulated and real‑world survival data (e.g., cancer outcomes with multi‑modal inputs), with a regression‑based approximator scaling efficiently to medium‑dimensional problems (≈70 features).

## Method Summary
The method centers on decomposing a survival model’s prediction function \(F(t|x)\) (e.g., survival probability \(S(t|x)\)) into contributions from feature subsets. SurvFD defines a functional decomposition where each term \(f_M(t|x_M)\) represents the effect of feature set \(M\), orthogonal under feature independence. To make this computable, the authors use Shapley interaction values (n‑Shapley) with a value function \(\nu(t|M) = \mathbb{E}[\overline{X}_M][F(t|X) \mid X_M = x_M] - \mathbb{E}[F(t|X)]\). Exact computation is exponential; approximations include KernelSHAP‑IQ, SVARM, Monte Carlo, and a regression‑based approach that fits a weighted least‑squares model to estimate Möbius coefficients. The regression‑based SurvSHAP‑IQ approximator is recommended for medium dimensions (\(p \leq 70\)) due to its balance of accuracy and scalability. Implementation requires selecting a reference distribution (marginal or conditional), a target scale (log‑hazard avoids transformation artifacts), and a discrete time grid.

## Key Results
- SurvFD theoretically recovers ground‑truth interactions under feature independence, but transformation to hazard/survival can cause downward/upward propagation of effects (Theorem 3.4).
- SurvSHAP‑IQ achieves near‑perfect local accuracy (decomposition error \(\sigma(t) < 0.015\)) and correctly identifies clinically relevant feature interactions (e.g., age × tumor size) in cancer survival models, including multi‑modal data with images and clinical features.
- The regression‑based SurvSHAP‑IQ approximator is most faithful for medium‑dimensional tasks (up to 70 features) among compared methods, with lower MAE than Monte Carlo, permutation, or SVARM approaches.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** SurvFD decomposes survival predictions into pure time‑dependent and time‑independent effects, revealing where standard additive explanations fail.  
**Mechanism:** It extends functional decomposition to time‑indexed functions by projecting the prediction onto subsets of features and recursively subtracting lower‑order contributions, separating components that vary with time from those that are constant.  
**Core assumption:** For the decomposition to recover the ground‑truth interaction structure (time‑dependent vs. time‑independent sets), features must be mutually independent and the ground‑truth risk score must have an additive representation in transformed effects.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract]: “SurvFD … decomposes higher‑order effects into time‑dependent and time‑independent components … explicitly characterizing when and why additive explanations fail.”  
- [section 3.1]: “By decomposing higher‑order effects into time‑dependent and time‑independent components, SurvFD offers a previously unrecognized perspective on survival explanations.”  
- [corpus]: Weak – neighboring papers discuss functional ANOVA and SHAP but not survival‑specific decomposition.  
**Break condition:** When features are dependent, marginal SurvFD may misrepresent interaction structure unless conditional FD is used; transformation to hazard/survival induces additional spurious interactions (downward/upward propagation, Thm. 3.4).

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** SurvSHAP‑IQ extends Shapley interaction quantification to time‑indexed survival functions, providing a practical estimator for higher‑order, time‑dependent interactions.  
**Mechanism:** It uses n‑Shapley values (Shapley interaction index) where contributions for each feature subset are computed via weighted discrete derivatives of a value function \(\nu(t|M)=\mathbb{E}[\overline{X}_M][F(t|X) \mid X_M=x_M]\). Exact computation is exponential; sampling‑based approximations (e.g., KernelSHAP‑IQ) are used for scalability.  
**Core assumption:** The value function correctly captures the conditional expectation of the chosen target (hazard, survival) given a feature subset at a fixed time; interventional (marginal) or observational (conditional) reference distribution is consistently chosen.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract]: “SurvSHAP‑IQ … extends Shapley interactions to time‑indexed functions, providing a practical estimator for higher‑order, time‑dependent interactions.”  
- [section 3.3]: “SurvSHAP‑IQ provides estimates of higher‑order interactions that can be visualized to interpret time‑dependent survival predictions.”  
- [corpus]: Neighbors cover Shapley for general functions but not survival‑specific extensions.  
**Break condition:** Exact computation becomes infeasible for \(p > 10\); approximations lose fidelity for high‑order interactions or if the sampling budget is too low (Figure 7).

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** The regression‑based SurvSHAP‑IQ approximator offers the best trade‑off between accuracy and scalability for medium‑dimensional survival tasks (\(p\) up to \(\sim 70\)).  
**Mechanism:** It fits a weighted least‑squares model to estimate Shapley interaction values from a limited set of sampled coalitions, leveraging the linearity of Shapley values in the Möbius transform and minimizing estimation variance.  
**Core assumption:** The true interaction values can be well approximated by a linear model in the basis functions (Möbius coefficients); the sample size (budget) is larger than the number of interaction terms of interest.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [section 4.2]: “The regression‑based SurvSHAP‑IQ approximator is shown to be most faithful for medium‑dimensional tasks (up to 70 features).”  
- [section 4.2, Figure 7]: Benchmark results where regression achieves lower MAE than Monte Carlo, permutation, or SVARM approaches on Bergamaschi (\(p=10\)) and similar tasks.  
- [corpus]: Weak – recent works on scalable SHAP approximation exist but survival context is unique.  
**Break condition:** For very high dimensions (\(p \gg 70\)) or high interaction orders (\(k\) large), the linear model may be underspecified, leading to instability (see Figure 7 at budget 26).

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Survival Analysis**  
  Why needed: The paper manipulates hazard and survival functions, which are non‑additive and time‑dependent. Understanding these quantities and their relationship \(S(t)=\exp(-\int_0^t h(u)du)\) is essential to grasp why decomposition is non‑trivial.  
  Quick check: If \(h(t|x) = \lambda \exp(\beta x)\), write \(S(t|x)\). (Answer: \(S(t|x)=\exp(-\lambda \exp(\beta x)\cdot t)\).)  

- **Concept: Functional Decomposition**  
  Why needed: FD is the mathematical tool for separating pure main and interaction effects. The paper builds on HD/ANOVA decomposition and extends it to survival functions.  
  Quick check: In FD \(F(x)=f_\emptyset + \sum_{j} f_{j}(x_j) + \sum_{j<k} f_{jk}(x_j,x_k)+\dots\), what property do the \(f_M\) satisfy? (Answer: They are orthogonal under feature independence and sum to \(F(x)\).)  

- **Concept: Shapley Values & Interaction Indices**  
  Why needed: Shapley values provide attributable, efficient explanations; interaction indices generalize them to quantify synergistic effects between features, which is central to SurvSHAP‑IQ.  
  Quick check: What does the “efficiency” axiom require for Shapley attributions \(\phi_j(t|x)\) when summing over all features? (Answer: \(\sum_j \phi_j(t|x) = F(t|x) - \mathbb{E}_X[F(t|X)]\).)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input -> SurvFD (theoretical) -> Value function \(\nu(t|M)\) -> SurvSHAP‑IQ (exact/approximate) -> Approximators (KernelSHAP‑IQ, SVARM, MC, permutation, regression) -> Evaluation (local accuracy \(\sigma(t)\))  

**Critical path:**  
1. Define the prediction target \(F(t|x)\) (e.g., survival probability) and select reference distribution (marginal for model‑centric, conditional for data‑centric).  
2. For \(p \leq 10\), use exact SurvSHAP‑IQ; for larger \(p\), choose an approximator and set a budget \(B\) that exceeds the number of expected interactions.  
3. Verify local accuracy on a validation set (\(\sigma(t)\) should be near 0).  
4. Visualize \(\phi_M(t|x)\) over time to separate time‑dependent vs. time‑independent effects (compare with SurvFD expectations).  
5. If using real data, validate with domain knowledge (e.g., known clinical interactions).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Exact vs. approximate: Exact provides gold‑standard but is \(O(2^p)\); approximate reduces cost but may introduce bias/variance.  
- Marginal vs. conditional: Marginal respects model structure but ignores feature dependencies; conditional reflects data distribution but may attribute effects to correlated non‑influential features (Thm. 3.6).  
- Target scale: Log‑hazard avoids transformation‑induced interactions; hazard/survival are clinically interpretable but contain spurious higher‑order effects.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High local accuracy error (e.g., \(\sigma > 0.1\)) suggests poor approximation or incorrect value function.  
- Time‑varying attributions for features known to have constant effects (e.g., in a linear CoxPH) indicate transformation artifacts.  
- Approximator MAE does not decrease with increasing budget \(B\), indicating potential implementation error or insufficient basis samples.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Simulated ground‑truth test: Use synthetic \(G(t|x)\) with known time‑dependent/interaction terms (e.g., scenarios in Fig. 2) to confirm that SurvSHAP‑IQ recovers exact \(f_M\) (time‑dependence matches ground truth). Check condition (i) of Thm. 3.2 and downward propagation (Thm. 3.3).  
2. Real‑world cancer survival: Apply to uveal melanoma or TCGA‑BRCA with a GBSA/DeepHit model; verify that attributed interactions align with clinical literature (e.g., age × tumor size).  
3. Scalability benchmark: For \(p=16\), compare MAE of different approximators (Monte Carlo, SVARM, permutation, regression) as budget increases (like Fig. 7) to justify regression use for \(p \leq 70\).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How can higher‑order Shapley interactions be efficiently estimated for survival models with many features and high interaction orders, avoiding exponential computational cost?**  
   Basis in paper: The conclusion states that "estimating higher‑order interactions remains an open research question due to exponential growth with interaction order \(k\)." Why unresolved: Exact computation is infeasible for high orders; existing approximations are validated only up to second‑order and medium dimensions. What evidence would resolve it: Development of scalable approximation algorithms that maintain fidelity for \(k>2\) in high‑dimensional settings.

2. **To what extent do the theoretical guarantees of SurvFD (e.g., recovery of ground‑truth interactions) hold for survival models that do not conform to the multiplicative hazard assumption, such as additive hazards or ensemble methods?**  
   Basis in paper: The conclusion notes the study is "theoretically confined to multiplicative hazards." Why unresolved: SurvFD theory relies on the multiplicative hazard structure; other models may yield different decomposition properties. What evidence would resolve it: Extension of SurvFD theorems to alternative hazard frameworks, with empirical validation on models with non‑multiplicative effects.

3. **How do feature dependencies in real‑world survival data impact the interpretability and validity of SurvSHAP‑IQ explanations, and what guidelines should practitioners follow when choosing between marginal and conditional functional decomposition?**  
   Basis in paper: Theorem 3.6 shows conditional SurvFD can attribute effects to non‑influential features when dependencies exist, but no practical decision criteria are offered. Why unresolved: Clinical datasets often contain correlated features; marginal vs. conditional trade‑offs for survival interpretation are unclear. What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies on synthetic data with controlled dependencies and real clinical datasets, assessing alignment with domain knowledge and robustness to correlation structures.

## Limitations
- Theoretical guarantees rely on feature independence and an additive risk representation; violations cause spurious interactions due to transformation‑induced artifacts.
- Exact SurvSHAP‑IQ is computationally infeasible for high dimensions or high interaction orders; approximations may lose fidelity for very high‑order effects.
- Real‑world applications involve ambiguous imputation strategies (e.g., for multi‑modal data) and lack ground‑truth interactions for validation, reducing confidence in clinical relevance.

## Confidence
- **Theoretical framework (SurvFD/SurvSHAP‑IQ definitions, axioms, recovery theorems):** High. Rigorous proofs under explicit assumptions.  
- **Empirical accuracy on synthetic data (local error <0.015, correct interaction recovery):** Medium. Simulation is well‑specified, but integration precision and minor implementation choices could affect exact error values; qualitative findings likely robust.  
- **Scalability of regression‑based approximator for medium dimensions (\(p \leq 70\)):** Medium. Benchmarks demonstrate advantage for \(p \leq 16\); extension to \(p = 70\) is claimed without detailed results in the provided summary.  
- **Clinical relevance in real‑world datasets:** Low. Ambiguous reference distribution (imputation) and absence of ground‑truth interactions make verification speculative.
