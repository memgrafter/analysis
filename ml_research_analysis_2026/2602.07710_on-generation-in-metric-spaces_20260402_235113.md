# On Generation in Metric Spaces

## Quick Facts

- **arXiv ID**: 2602.07710  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07710  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: The paper characterizes (ε, ε′)-generatability for binary hypothesis classes in separable metric spaces via the (ε, ε′)-closure dimension; uniform generatability is equivalent to finite closure dimension, non‑uniform generatability to a union of such classes, and doubling metric spaces yield scale‑invariant generatability while general spaces (e.g., ℓ²) exhibit abrupt parameter sensitivity.

## Executive Summary

This work extends the language generation framework from countable to separable metric instance spaces. Novelty is defined through metric separation with asymmetric parameters for the adversary (ε) and the generator (ε′). The central contribution is the (ε, ε′)-closure dimension, a scale-sensitive analogue of the classical closure dimension. The authors prove that a hypothesis class is uniformly generatable iff its (ε, ε′)-closure dimension is finite, and non‑uniformly generatable iff it can be expressed as a countable union of subclasses each with finite closure dimension. For generation in the limit, finite unions of uniformly generatable classes ensure generatability, though this condition is not necessary. In doubling metric spaces (encompassing all finite‑dimensional normed spaces), generatability is invariant under changes of scale and equivalent metrics. In contrast, for general spaces like ℓ², generatability can change abruptly as parameters vary, highlighting a delicate dependence on geometry.

## Method Summary

The paper adopts a combinatorial approach based on metric covering numbers. Core definitions include (ε, ε′)-generatability (the adversary presents ε‑separated examples, the generator must eventually output an ε′‑separated one) and the (ε, ε′)-closure dimension (the largest number of ε‑separated revealed points whose hypothesis closure still has an infinite ε′‑cover). Under the r‑UUS assumption (each hypothesis has unbounded r‑covering), the authors connect these notions: finite closure dimension is both necessary and sufficient for uniform generation, and a countable union decomposition characterizes non‑uniform generation. A constructive generator for uniform classes uses an empirical risk minimization (ERM) oracle. The analysis is complemented by scale‑invariance results in doubling spaces and counterexamples in ℓ² that demonstrate sensitivity to parameters and metrics.

## Key Results

- Uniform generatability ⇔ finite (ε, ε′)-closure dimension.
- Non‑uniform generatability ⇔ H is a countable union of subclasses each with finite (ε, ε′)-closure dimension.
- In doubling metric spaces, generatability is scale‑invariant and stable under equivalent metrics; in non‑doubling spaces (e.g., ℓ²), it can be highly sensitive to ε, ε′ and the choice of metric.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim**: Defining novelty via metric separation with asymmetric parameters ε (adversary) and ε′ (generator) generalizes language generation from countable to metric spaces while preserving combinatorial characterizations.  
**Mechanism**: The adversary reveals examples that are ε‑separated (each new example lies outside the ε‑ball of all previous ones). The generator must eventually output an example ε′‑separated from all observed ones. This replaces simple distinctness with geometric separation, enabling continuous space handling.  
**Core assumption**: The instance space is a separable metric space (X,ρ); the hypothesis class H satisfies the uniformly unbounded support (r‑UUS) property for some r>0, ensuring the adversary can always reveal novel points.  
**Evidence anchors**: [Abstract] "We extend the language generation framework … by defining novelty through metric separation and allowing asymmetric novelty parameters for the adversary and the generator." [Section 2.2] Formal definitions of (ε,ε′)-generatability, uniform, and non‑uniform generation. [Section 3] Characterization theorems build on these definitions.  
**Break condition**: If the adversary’s ε is too small relative to the generator’s ε′ (e.g., ε < ε′), even finite hypothesis classes may be non‑uniformly non‑generatable (Example B.3). In non‑doubling spaces, generatability can fail abruptly when parameters cross thresholds (Section 4.2).

### Mechanism 2
**Claim**: The (ε, ε′)-closure dimension, defined via metric covering numbers, provides necessary and sufficient combinatorial conditions for uniform and non‑uniform generation.  
**Mechanism**: The (ε,ε′)-closure dimension of H is the largest d such that there exists a sequence of examples with N(ε) = d but N(ε′) of its closure is finite. It measures the trade‑off: many ε‑separated revealed points yet the closure (hypotheses consistent with them) is still richly coverable at scale ε′.  
**Core assumption**: H satisfies r‑UUS for some r; covering numbers N(ε;·,ρ) are used in a separable metric space.  
**Evidence anchors**: [Section 3.1] Definition 3.1 introduces (ε,ε′)-closure dimension. [Theorem 3.1] Uniform generatability iff closure dimension finite. [Theorem 3.3] Non‑uniform generatability iff H is a union of subclasses each with finite closure dimension.  
**Break condition**: Infinite closure dimension precludes uniform generation (Lemma B.1). For non‑uniform generation, inability to decompose H into subclasses with finite closure dimension leads to failure (Theorem 3.3).

### Mechanism 3
**Claim**: In doubling metric spaces (including all finite‑dimensional normed spaces), generatability is scale‑invariant and robust to equivalent metrics; outside doubling, generatability becomes highly parameter‑sensitive.  
**Mechanism**: Doubling implies covering numbers at different scales are linearly related: N(δ;A) ≤ C·N(ε;A) for δ<ε. Thus (ε,ε′)-closure dimension is equivalent up to scaling, making uniform/non‑uniform generatability independent of the choice of ε,ε′. If two metrics are equivalent (mutually Lipschitz), they induce identical generatability properties.  
**Core assumption**: X is doubling (Definition 4.1); H satisfies UUS (which in doubling spaces is itself scale‑invariant by Theorem 4.1).  
**Evidence anchors**: [Abstract] "In doubling metric spaces … generatability is stable across novelty scales and invariant under equivalent metrics." [Section 4.1] Theorems 4.2 and 4.4 state scale‑ and metric‑invariance results. [Corollary 4.3] Finite‑dimensional normed spaces are doubling.  
**Break condition**: In non‑doubling spaces like ℓ², generatability can fail abruptly as ε or ε′ cross thresholds (Examples 4.8,4.9) and may depend on the chosen equivalent metric (Example 4.11).

## Foundational Learning

- **Separable metric spaces and covering numbers**:  
  *Why needed*: The framework assumes a separable metric instance space to apply combinatorial arguments with countable dense subsets. Covering numbers N(ε;A,ρ) quantify the size of ε‑nets and are central to the definition of (ε,ε′)-closure dimension.  
  *Quick check*: What does it mean for a metric space to be separable? Must every closed ball have a finite ε‑cover? (Not necessarily; total boundedness is separate. The UUS property allows infinite covering numbers.)

- **Closure dimension (countable case)**:  
  *Why needed*: The paper generalizes the classical closure dimension from countable spaces to metric spaces. Understanding the original (Definition A.5) clarifies why the new definition replaces "finite closure" with "finite covering at ε′".  
  *Quick check*: In a countable space, why does finite closure dimension characterize uniform generatability? (It bounds the number of hypotheses consistent with the observed examples; if finite, a generator can eventually exhaust the boundary.)

- **Doubling metric spaces**:  
  *Why needed*: Doubling ensures covering numbers at different scales are polynomially related, yielding scale‑invariance of generatability. Many practical spaces (ℝ^d) are doubling, so this captures a broad stable regime.  
  *Quick check*: Is ℓ² doubling? No. Can you prove ℝ^d is doubling? (Yes, via volume argument: a ball of radius R can be covered by (1+2R/ε)^d balls of radius ε.)

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map** (plain text):  
Separable metric space (X,ρ) and hypothesis class H ⊆ {0,1}^X → r‑UUS property and novelty parameters (ε,ε′) → Generator G: X^⋆→X and definition of (ε,ε′)-generatability → (ε,ε′)-closure dimension as combinatorial certificate ↔ Characterizations (uniform, non‑uniform) and sufficient conditions for generation in the limit ↔ Structural conditions (doubling, equivalent metrics) determining stability vs sensitivity.

**Critical path**:  
Proof development proceeds from extending definitions to metric spaces (Section 2), introducing (ε,ε′)-closure dimension (Section 3.1), proving uniform characterization (Theorem 3.1), deriving non‑uniform characterization via decomposition (Theorem 3.3), showing finite unions suffice for generation in limit (Theorem 3.5), then investigating scale‑sensitivity and metric‑dependence (Section 4): stability in doubling spaces (Theorems 4.2,4.4) and counterexamples in ℓ² (Examples 4.8–4.11).

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- Asymmetric ε vs ε′ increases expressiveness but complicates analysis; prior work often set ε=ε′ for simpler theory.  
- Using metric covering numbers (instead of cardinality) introduces scale‑dependence; the doubling property resolves this for a broad class.  
- The r‑UUS condition avoids trivial failures (e.g., finite supports). In doubling spaces UUS becomes scale‑invariant; otherwise it is parameter‑sensitive.  
- Generality vs robustness: Allowing arbitrary separable metrics yields rich phenomena (abrupt failures) but loses stability; restricting to doubling spaces recovers nice properties.

**Failure signatures**:  
- Uniform generatability fails when (ε,ε′)-closure dimension is infinite (Lemma B.1): existence of a sequence with arbitrarily many ε‑separated points but whose closure has small ε′-cover.  
- Non‑uniform generatability fails if H cannot be written as a countable union of classes with finite closure dimension (Theorem 3.3).  
- Generation in limit can fail even if both uniform and non‑uniform hold individually: finite union of uniformly generatable classes may not be generatable in limit (Theorem 3.6, construction from Hanneke et al. [2025]).  
- In ℓ², abrupt transitions: For a fixed H, generation may hold for all γ<ε but fail once γ≥ε (Example 4.8). Similarly, changing to an equivalent metric can collapse non‑uniform generatability while uniform still holds (Example 4.11).  
- When ε < ε′, finite classes can be non‑uniformly non‑generatable (Example B.3).

**First 3 experiments**:  
1. **Scale‑invariance in doubling spaces**: Take a finite H in ℝ^2 (e.g., two disjoint disks with sufficiency large r‑UUS). Compute (ε,ε′)-closure dimension for various ε,ε′ pairs with ε′ ≤ ε and ε′ > ε. Uniform/non‑uniform status should remain unchanged for ε,ε′ ≤ r. Also test equivalent metrics (ℓ¹ vs ℓ²) – no change.  
2. **Scale‑sensitivity in ℓ²**: Implement the construction from Example 4.8 using a finite‑dimensional truncation (first N coordinates). Vary ε and ε′ around a threshold (e.g., ε=1, ε′=0.5). Measure the generator’s ability to produce novel examples; expect success for γ<ε and γ′<ε′, failure when either γ≥ε or γ′≥ε′.  
3. **Metric‑dependence**: In ℓ², use the weighted metric ρ′ from Example 4.11 (weights δ_n alternating 1/4 and 1). Construct the hypothesis class H from that example. Show under standard ℓ² metric that C_τ^τ(H) is finite (uniform generatable) by verifying the covering argument. Under ρ′, demonstrate that H cannot be non‑uniformly generatable (e.g., by showing its closure dimension remains infinite or by applying Lemma C.1 with H₁ not generatable in limit).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
**Question**: How does the generation framework extend when outputs are considered equivalent under a group of symmetries (e.g., rigid motions in continuous spaces)?  
**Basis**: The authors state: "Several directions remain open, including studying generation when outputs are considered equivalent under natural symmetries" (Section 5).  
**Why unresolved**: The current model distinguishes every point; symmetries collapse points into equivalence classes, requiring new definitions of novelty and coverage, and the (ε,ε′)-closure dimension may not directly apply.  
**What evidence would resolve it**: A theory of generation on quotient spaces X/G, with characterizations analogous to Theorems 3.1 and 3.3, and constructive algorithms for common symmetry groups (e.g., translations in ℝ^d, rotations in 3D).

### Open Question 2
**Question**: What geometric properties of a metric space beyond doubling guarantee scale- and metric-invariance of generatability?  
**Basis**: The paper identifies doubling as a regime of stable behavior and asks to identify "geometric conditions beyond doubling that ensure stable behavior" (Section 5).  
**Why unresolved**: In non‑doubling spaces (e.g., ℓ²), generatability can be highly sensitive to ε, ε′, and the metric. Intermediate geometric conditions (e.g., finite Assouad dimension, Ricci curvature bounds) might yield partial robustness but are not explored.  
**What evidence would resolve it**: A theorem showing that if X has property P (e.g., finite Nagata dimension, or is a length space with curvature bounds), then for any H satisfying UUS, uniform/non‑uniform generatability is invariant under changes of ε, ε′ and equivalent metrics.

### Open Question 3
**Question**: Under what additional assumptions on H can generation in the limit be fully characterized, not just sufficient conditions?  
**Basis**: The authors seek to "strengthen our understanding of generation in the limit under additional structural assumptions" (Section 5). Theorem 3.6 shows the finite‑union condition is not necessary.  
**Why unresolved**: The sufficient condition from Theorem 3.5 does not capture all generatable classes. Structural conditions like finite VC dimension, being in a doubling space, or having finite metric entropy might yield a full characterization.  
**What evidence would resolve it**: A necessary and sufficient condition for generation in the limit when H is a class of binary classifiers with VC dimension d in a doubling metric space, or when H is a union of finitely many classes with finite (ε, ε′)-closure dimension for all scales.

### Open Question 4
**Question**: Does there exist an algorithm for generation in metric spaces that uses only the membership oracle (i.e., queries "is x in supp(h)?") and does not require an ERM oracle?  
**Basis**: Appendix E states: "the extension of Kleinberg and Mullainathan's algorithm to metric spaces remains an open problem." Their original algorithm uses only membership oracles; the paper's algorithm for uniform generation uses an ERM oracle.  
**Why unresolved**: The generator in Lemma B.2 relies on an ERM oracle to compute points in the closure ⟨x₁,...,xₙ⟩_H. It is unclear how to simulate this with only membership queries because the closure operation may require solving an optimization problem over H.  
**What evidence would resolve it**: An algorithm that, given a hypothesis class H (described by a finite set of binary classifiers on a separable metric space) and access only to h(x) for the target h, outputs a sequence of novel examples that eventually succeed for every uniformly generatable H. Alternatively, a proof that such an algorithm cannot exist in general (e.g., by reducing an undecidable problem).

## Limitations

- Dependence on the r‑UUS property and separability; classes with finite or totally bounded supports fall outside the theory.
- Algorithmic generation relies on an exact ERM oracle; for infinite H this may be undecidable or intractable.
- Infinite‑dimensional counterexamples (ℓ²) cannot be directly simulated; validation requires analytic proofs, limiting empirical verification.

## Confidence

- **High**: Core extension to metric spaces, (ε,ε′)-closure dimension characterizations (Theorems 3.1, 3.3), scale‑invariance in doubling spaces (Section 4.1). All results are rigorously proved.
- **Medium**: Practical implications for actual learning algorithms; the ERM‑based generator is non‑constructive for infinite classes. Stability claims hinge on doubling, which may not hold in many modern high‑dimensional settings.
- **Low**: Generalization to arbitrary metrics; the paper shows abrupt failures, but the extent of pathology beyond ℓ² remains an open question.
