# Towards a Science of AI Agent Reliability

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.16666  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16666  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Standard agent benchmarks that measure only mean task success hide critical reliability flaws; the paper introduces a multi-dimensional framework (consistency, robustness, predictability, safety) with 12 metrics and shows that reliability improvements do not keep pace with accuracy growth on 14 contemporary models.

## Executive Summary
Evaluating AI agents solely by their average task success rate is insufficient for real-world deployment because it conceals serious reliability issues such as inconsistent behavior, brittleness to perturbations, unpredictable failures, and potential for severe harm. Drawing from safety-critical engineering, the authors propose a four-dimensional reliability framework—consistency, robustness, predictability, and safety—and define 12 concrete metrics to quantify each dimension independently of raw accuracy. Applying this framework to 14 state-of-the-art language models on the GAIA and τ‑bench benchmarks reveals that reliability metrics do not scale uniformly with capability: outcome consistency remains low (median ~0.6), prompt robustness varies widely, and fault/environment robustness appear high but may be limited by mild perturbations. The proposed metrics provide a systematic way to assess and compare agent reliability, enabling more informed deployment decisions.

## Method Summary
The evaluation harnesses the Holistic Agent Leaderboard (HAL) to run agents on GAIA (165 tasks covering web navigation, coding, and file manipulation) and τ‑bench (26 curated customer-service tasks). For each model, the authors execute every task five times under nominal conditions and subject parallel runs to three perturbation types: prompt paraphrases (5 variants), simulated API faults (20% injection probability with task-specific recovery), and environment shifts (e.g., reordered fields, format changes). Agent trajectories, confidence scores (elicited post‑hoc), and resource usage are logged. An LLM judge analyzes traces for constraint violations and error severity to compute safety metrics. All 12 reliability metrics are calculated according to predefined formulas (Table 2), using normalization or ratio‑based designs to isolate reliability from raw task accuracy. Temperature is set to zero to minimize stochastic noise.

## Key Results
- Outcome consistency (`Cout`) is low, with a median of ~0.6 across models, indicating large run‑to‑run variability in success for the same task.
- Prompt robustness (`Rprompt`) spans a wide range (approximately 0.3 to 0.9), exposing substantial sensitivity to instruction phrasing.
- Fault and environment robustness (`Rfault`, `Renv`) are generally high, though this may reflect the limited intensity of the injected perturbations rather than genuine resilience.
- Reliability scores show weak correlation with accuracy; newer and more capable models do not consistently improve on reliability dimensions, highlighting a gap between capability and dependability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Decomposing reliability into orthogonal dimensions prevents obscuring critical failure modes.
**Claim:** A single accuracy metric averages away distinct behavioral flaws; decomposing reliability into consistency, robustness, predictability, and safety surfaces deployment‑relevant issues.  
**Mechanism:** By measuring variance across runs (consistency), sensitivity to perturbations (robustness), calibration of confidence (predictability), and consequence‑aware harm (safety), the framework reveals problems like unpredictable failures, brittle prompt handling, and uncalibrated trust signals that mean success cannot detect.  
**Core assumption:** Deployment risk depends on *how* an agent behaves, not just *whether* it succeeds; an 80% accurate agent can be riskier than a 70% one if its failures are catastrophic or unpredictable.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- Abstract: “compressing agent behavior into a single success metric obscures critical operational flaws… ignores whether agents behave consistently… withstand perturbations… fail predictably… have bounded error severity.”  
- Section 2: Cross‑domain survey shows safety‑critical engineering treats reliability as multi‑dimensional.  
- Corpus: Neighbor papers (e.g., Holistic Agent Leaderboard) highlight challenges in agent evaluation, supporting multi‑metric frameworks.  
**Break condition:** If all failures are uniform and benign (e.g., only minor formatting errors with no distributional variance), a single accuracy metric might suffice—an unrealistic scenario for consequential tasks.

### Mechanism 2: Normalization disentangles reliability from raw capability.
**Claim:** Ratio‑based and normalized metric designs allow comparison across agents with different accuracy levels by isolating stability, sensitivity, and harm severity from pure capability.  
**Mechanism:** Metrics like outcome consistency (`Cout`) normalize variance by `p(1−p)`, robustness uses accuracy ratios (`Acc_perturbed / Acc_baseline`), and safety conditions on violation occurrence. This ensures a highly capable agent can still be flagged as unreliable, and a less capable one can be reliable within its envelope.  
**Core assumption:** Capability and reliability are not perfectly correlated, especially in current models.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- Section 3.5.1: “Our metrics capture these distinctions through normalization… ratio‑based comparisons.”  
- Section 4.2: Results show reliability does not scale uniformly with accuracy (Figure 1, Figure 8).  
- Corpus: Neighbor papers (e.g., Real‑Time Trust Verification) separate trust/verification from task accuracy.  
**Break condition:** If capability and reliability become perfectly correlated (e.g., at 100% accuracy all dimensions trivially perfect), disentanglement is unnecessary, but current systems are far from that regime.

### Mechanism 3: Perturbation‑based evaluation exposes hidden flaws.
**Claim:** Evaluating agents under structured perturbations—prompt rephrasings, tool faults, environment shifts—reveals robustness and predictability properties that nominal single‑run tests miss.  
**Mechanism:** Systematic perturbations stress‑test agent behavior: prompt robustness checks invariance to semantically equivalent instructions; fault injection introduces API errors; environment perturbations change data formats or tool interfaces. This exposes brittleness and overconfidence that static benchmarks cannot.  
**Core assumption:** Real‑world deployments involve input variation, infrastructure noise, and dynamic conditions; agents failing under controlled perturbations will likely fail in production.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- Section 3.2: Defines fault, environment, and prompt robustness with explicit perturbation protocols.  
- Section 4.2: Results show prompt robustness varies widely while fault/environment robustness saturates (Figure 17), indicating some dimensions are more sensitive to evaluation design.  
- Corpus: Neighbor papers (e.g., Back to Basics: Revisiting ASR) emphasize that benchmarks fail to cover real‑world conditions.  
**Break condition:** If agents are always deployed in perfectly static, noise‑free, single‑prompt environments—highly unrealistic—perturbations would not reflect operational realities.

## Foundational Learning

### Multi‑metric evaluation design  
**Why needed:** The paper’s core argument is that a single metric (accuracy) is insufficient; understanding how to design and interpret a suite of orthogonal metrics is prerequisite to applying the framework.  
**Quick check:** If two agents have the same accuracy but one has higher outcome consistency, what does that imply for deployment in a CI/CD pipeline?

### Calibration and discrimination in confidence estimation  
**Why needed:** Predictability hinges on whether confidence scores match success rates (calibration) and separate correct/incorrect cases (discrimination). Misunderstanding these leads to flawed trust decisions.  
**Quick check:** Can an agent have perfect calibration but zero discrimination? What would its confidence scores look like?

### Normalization for capability‑disentanglement  
**Why needed:** Metrics like outcome consistency and robustness use normalization or ratios to isolate the reliability property from raw task difficulty. Without this, comparisons across tasks/models are confounded.  
**Quick check:** Why does outcome consistency normalize by `p(1−p)`? What would a raw variance metric confound?

### Safety as risk decomposition (violation probability × severity)  
**Why needed:** The safety score follows Kaplan & Garrick’s risk formula. Safety is not just “low violation rate” but “low expected harm given violation,” which is key to interpreting results.  
**Quick check:** If Agent A has 5% violations with high severity and Agent B has 10% violations with low severity, which might be preferable in a finance context?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Metric computation module → Execution harness → Perturbation engine → Confidence elicitation → Safety judge → Aggregation layer

### Critical path
1. **Baseline execution:** Run all tasks `K=5` times under nominal conditions to obtain `Acc0` and collect trajectories.  
2. **Perturbation runs:** For each perturbation type (prompt, fault, environment), re‑run tasks and compute `Acc_pert`.  
3. **Confidence & safety:** During/after runs, collect confidence scores and parse traces for constraint violations.  
4. **Metric calculation:** Apply formulas to aggregated data; ensure normalization corrects for capability (e.g., `Cout` uses Bernoulli variance).  
5. **Interpretation:** Compare dimension scores; note divergences (e.g., high robustness but low consistency).

### Design tradeoffs
- **Temperature setting:** Zero temperature reduces sampling noise but may not reflect production use (often non‑zero). The paper acknowledges this may overestimate reliability.  
- **Metric aggregation:** Equal weighting of dimensions is a default; practitioners may reweight based on deployment context (e.g., consistency critical for CI/CD, safety for autonomous systems).  
- **Safety aggregation:** Safety excluded from overall `R` to avoid masking tail risks—must be reported separately as hard constraints.  
- **Perturbation expressiveness:** Current perturbations (prompt paraphrases, medium environment changes) are limited; more aggressive shifts (API version upgrades, adversarial attacks) could reveal greater fragility.

### Failure signatures
- **Consistency:** Low `Cout` (<0.6) indicates stochastic outcomes; large gap between `Cd_traj` and `Cs_traj` shows action order instability.  
- **Robustness:** `Rprompt` ≪ 1 signals prompt sensitivity; `Rfault` or `Renv` ≪ 1 indicates brittleness to realistic disruptions.  
- **Predictability:** High `Pcal` error (ECE) means poor confidence calibration; `PAUROC` near 0.5 shows confidence provides no discrimination.  
- **Safety:** Non‑zero violation rate (`1−Scomp`) or any high‑severity `Sharm` entries flag tail risks.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Reproduce main result:** Run a subset of models (e.g., GPT‑4 Turbo, Claude 3.7 Sonnet) on GAIA validation set with `K=5`, compute all metrics. Verify outcome consistency is low (~0.6) and prompt robustness varies.  
2. **Benchmark error sensitivity:** Compare results on τ‑bench full vs. clean subset (as in Figure 6). Confirm metrics like calibration degrade when answer keys are flawed, testing robustness of reliability metrics to benchmark quality.  
3. **Perturbation intensity sweep:** For one model, vary prompt perturbation strength (mild→strong) and plot `Rprompt`. Test whether robustness claims hold under more aggressive rephrasings or if saturation occurs only at mild levels.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **What taxonomy of agent failure modes most effectively maps to the four reliability dimensions (consistency, robustness, predictability, safety) and enables targeted mitigation?**  
   The paper notes that current evaluations treat failures monolithically; a systematic decomposition would clarify whether, e.g., a consistency issue stems from planning instability versus resource variability, guiding architecture or training interventions. A large‑scale, cross‑benchmark audit of failure traces manually labeled by domain experts, with statistical analysis of co‑occurrence patterns and root causes, would provide needed evidence.

2. **Which specific metric formulations and aggregation strategies should be standardized to enable reliable comparison across agents, benchmarks, and deployment contexts?**  
   The metrics involve subjective choices (normalization factors, aggregation weights); without standards, reported reliability scores are not directly comparable. Community‑wide benchmarks and competitions that evaluate multiple metric variants on common agent/scaffold combinations, coupled with sensitivity analyses showing how conclusions change under different schemes, are required.

3. **Does improved reliability emerge inevitably from scaling model capability, or does it require explicit architectural designs, training objectives, or scaffolding interventions?**  
   The observed disconnect between accuracy and reliability gains suggests scaling alone is insufficient, but the paper cannot causally separate scale, architecture, and training effects from correlational data. Controlled experiments holding task and scaffold constant while varying model size, training data diversity, and alignment procedures—measuring all 12 metrics—are needed to isolate which factors improve which dimensions.

4. **What are the fundamental trade‑offs between the four reliability dimensions, and can they be jointly optimized, or are some combinations inherently conflicting?**  
   The paper treats dimensions as independent, but real deployments may require balancing, e.g., higher consistency (determinism) against higher robustness (adaptive behavior). Systematic multi‑objective optimization studies that tune agents (via prompting, RL, or architecture) to maximize one dimension while measuring side‑effects on others, across tasks and scaffolds, would map the Pareto frontier of achievable reliability profiles.

## Limitations
- Agent scaffolding and exact prompts are not released, risking implementation deviations.  
- Perturbation details (e.g., exact transformations, fault seeding) are described only at a high level, limiting precise replication.  
- Safety metrics rely on a single LLM judge with unknown consistency, potentially propagating judge‑specific biases.

## Confidence
- Decomposition into orthogonal dimensions: **High**  
- Normalization disentangles reliability from capability: **High**  
- Perturbation‑based evaluation exposes hidden flaws: **Medium**
