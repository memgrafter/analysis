# Cross-Granularity Representations for Biological Sequences: Insights from ESM and BiGCARP

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.20825
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20825
- **Reference count:** 29
- **Primary result:** Concatenating amino acid-level (ESM) and Pfam domain-level (BiGCARP) embeddings yields a Macro AUROC of 0.917 ±0.003 for Biosynthetic Gene Cluster (BGC) product classification, a statistically significant improvement over using either embedding alone.

## Executive Summary
This study examines how biological sequence representations from different granularities—amino acid-level embeddings from ESM and domain-level embeddings from BiGCARP—can be integrated to improve prediction tasks. It reveals that simply initializing BiGCARP with ESM embeddings fails because BiGCARP's early dilated convolutional layers rapidly transform the input, discarding fine-grained structural information as shown by Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) analysis. Instead, BiGCARP's last-layer embeddings, trained via masked language modeling on domain sequences, capture contextual, BGC-level information. Probe tasks demonstrate that ESM excels at fine-grained local tasks (e.g., A-domain substrate prediction), while BiGCARP encodes global context. Fusing these complementary embeddings via simple concatenation enhances performance on intermediate-scale tasks, notably achieving state-of-the-art BGC product classification and improved halogen prediction. The work provides a clear case for cross-granularity representation fusion in computational biology.

## Method Summary
The authors use pre-trained ESM (amino acid-level model) and BiGCARP (Pfam domain-level model with a dilated convolutional backbone). For BiGCARP, they consider two embedding sources: the static embedder-layer vectors (one per Pfam ID) and the contextual last-layer vectors (one per domain instance in a BGC sequence). These embeddings, alone or concatenated, are fed to a frozen two-layer MLP classifier (512→256 units) for three downstream probe tasks extracted from MIBiG v3.0: (1) multi-label BGC product classification (global), (2) binary A-domain substrate property prediction (fine-grained, using only Stachelhaus positions from ESM), and (3) binary halogen product prediction (intermediate). Performance is evaluated via Macro AUROC under stratified 5-fold CV (10 repeats) for product classification and LOOCV with bootstrap for halogen. The key methodological insight comes from analyzing layer-wise CKA to understand how representations evolve, particularly the impact of initializing BiGCARP with ESM embeddings.

## Key Results
- **Fine-grained strength:** ESM embeddings achieve AUROC >0.9 on key A-domain substrate properties (e.g., aromaticity, polarity), significantly outperforming BiGCARP embeddings.
- **Contextual benefit:** BiGCARP's last-layer embeddings consistently outperform its static embedder-layer embeddings on the global BGC product classification task, demonstrating the value of contextualization.
- **Fusion gains:** Concatenating ESM and BiGCARP last-layer embeddings yields Macro AUROC 0.917 ±0.003 for product classification—a significant improvement over ESM alone (p=0.046)—and AUROC 0.759 ±0.209 for halogen prediction, confirming complementary information.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Straightforward ESM-based initialization into BiGCARP fails because early dilated convolutional layers rapidly transform these embeddings, preventing retention of pre-trained fine-grained structural information.
**Mechanism:** The first dilated convolution layer applies strong local context aggregation, causing an abrupt shift in the embedding space geometry. This is evidenced by a sharp drop in Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) similarity between the embedder layer (Layer 0) and Layer 1, regardless of initialization scheme. The architectural inductive bias prioritizes domain-level co-occurrence patterns over amino-acid homology.
**Evidence anchors:**
- [abstract] "...straightforward ESM-based embedding initialization into BiGCARP fails because early dilated convolution layers rapidly transform these embeddings, as shown by Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) analysis..."
- [section IV.B.1] CKA heatmap shows a "sharp drop in similarity immediately after the embedding layer (Layer 0), suggesting that the model rapidly transforms the input embeddings in early stages."
**Break condition:** If CKA similarity between Layer 0 and early convolutional layers remained high (>0.8), the rapid transformation claim would be invalidated.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** BiGCARP's last-layer embeddings capture more contextual and task-relevant (global BGC-level) information than static embedder-layer embeddings.
**Mechanism:** Stacked dilated convolutions and the masked language modeling objective integrate long-range dependencies across the Pfam domain sequence. This creates representations where a domain's embedding is conditioned on the full BGC context, which is directly probed by the BGC product classification task.
**Evidence anchors:**
- [abstract] "...last-layer BiGCARP embeddings capture more contextual and task-relevant information."
- [section IV.C.1] "Across all configurations, last-layer embeddings from BiGCARP consistently outperform those from the embedder layer... indicating that the model’s learned knowledge is more faithfully captured in final representations."
**Break condition:** If embedder-layer embeddings matched or exceeded last-layer embeddings on BGC-level tasks, the value of deep contextualization would be disproven.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Representations from different granularities (amino acid vs. Pfam domain) encode complementary biological information, and their fusion improves performance on intermediate-scale prediction tasks.
**Mechanism:** ESM embeddings excel at capturing local, fine-grained sequence features (e.g., active site motifs), while BiGCARP last-layer embeddings encode global, coarser-grained functional context (e.g., pathway architecture). For tasks requiring integration of both (e.g., predicting a chemical property influenced by local active sites *and* genomic context), concatenation allows a simple classifier to leverage both information sources.
**Evidence anchors:**
- [abstract] "Probe tasks demonstrate complementary strengths: ESM excels at fine-grained local tasks... while BiGCARP encodes global Biosynthetic Gene Cluster context. Combining embeddings improves intermediate-level tasks..."
- [section IV.C.3] "concatenating ESM and BiGCARP embeddings achieves the highest AUROC, with a statistically significant improvement over ESM alone (p=0.046)... suggesting that the two models encode complementary information."
**Break condition:** If fusion never significantly outperformed the best single-granularity model across diverse intermediate tasks, the complementarity hypothesis would be weakened.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Hierarchical Granularity in Biology**
  - **Why needed:** Biological sequences (BGCs) can be represented at multiple, non-reversible levels (nucleotides → amino acids → protein domains → domain sequence). Each level prioritizes different functional information.
  - **Quick check:** When representing a BGC as a Pfam domain sequence, what information is lost compared to raw amino acids, and what is gained? (Lost: residue-level motifs; Gained: explicit functional module ordering.)
- **Concept: Static vs. Contextualized Embeddings**
  - **Why needed:** The paper contrasts static embedder-layer vectors (fixed per Pfam ID) with contextual last-layer vectors (instance-specific). This distinction is critical to understanding why initialization fails and why last-layer embeddings work.
  - **Quick check:** Why might the same Pfam ID (e.g., PF00501) have different embeddings in BiGCARP's last layer when appearing in two different BGCs?
- **Concept: Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA)**
  - **Why needed:** CKA diagnoses how representation geometries evolve across layers and between models, measuring if patterns of variation are preserved, not just overall similarity.
  - **Quick check:** What does high CKA between Layer 0 and Layer 5 imply about the transformation of ESM-initialized embeddings?
- **Concept: Dilated Convolutions for Long Sequences**
  - **Why needed:** BiGCARP uses dilated 1D convolutions to efficiently handle long BGC sequences (hundreds of domains). This architecture has a strong local inductive bias.
  - **Quick check:** How do dilated convolutions expand the receptive field without increasing parameters, and what trade-off does this introduce?
- **Concept: Masked Language Modeling (MLM) for Domains**
  - **Why needed:** BiGCARP is pre-trained to predict masked Pfam domains in a sequence, forcing it to learn contextual relationships between domains within a BGC.
  - **Quick check:** Why might MLM on domain sequences better capture BGC-level context than a model trained only on individual domain sequences?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Pfam Domain Sequence -> Embedder Layer (lookup table) -> Stacked Dilated 1D Convolutional Blocks -> Last-Layer Contextual Embeddings. Extraction points: (a) Embedder output (static), (b) Last-layer output (contextual).

**Critical path for the paper's key finding:**
1. Pre-train BiGCARP (random or ESM-initialized) on antiSMASH-DB BGCs via masked domain modeling.
2. For analysis, extract embeddings for all domain occurrences from a specific layer (Layer 0, last layer).
3. Compute CKA matrices between layers of the same model (to see transformation) and between final layers of different models (to see convergence).
4. For probe tasks: per BGC, extract embeddings (static or contextual), mean-pool, feed to frozen MLP classifier, evaluate Macro AUROC via CV.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Dilated Convolutions vs. Transformers:** Dilated convolutions offer computational efficiency for very long sequences but may have a stronger local bias that aggressively remaps inputs early. Transformers (ESM) have flexible global attention but higher memory cost.
- **Static vs. Contextual Extraction:** Embedder-layer embeddings are fast/deterministic but discard sequence-context knowledge. Last-layer embeddings capture context but require full forward pass and are position-specific.
- **Simple Concatenation vs. Learned Fusion:** Naive concatenation is parsimonious but may not optimally combine streams. A learned fusion (e.g., cross-attention) could extract more synergy but adds parameters and overfitting risk on small probe datasets.

**Failure signatures:**
- **CKA Pattern:** High CKA between Layer 0 and final layers would contradict the "rapid transformation" claim, suggesting deeper layers are not effectively utilized.
- **Probe Task Divergence:** Last-layer embeddings significantly underperforming embedder-layer on BGC-level tasks would indicate pre-training objective is not learning transferable representations.
- **Fusion Non-benefit:** If concatenation harms performance or shows no significant gain across intermediate tasks, the complementarity hypothesis is unsupported.

**First three experiments:**
1. **CKA Transformation Analysis:** For an ESM-initialized BiGCARP model, compute CKA between all layer pairs at initialization and during training. Expect a sharp Layer 0→1 drop and low similarity between initial and final layers.
2. **UMAP of Functional Clustering:** Project mean-pooled last-layer embeddings (from randomly initialized BiGCARP) for all Pfam domains; color by functional category (core, tailoring, etc.). Expect non-random clustering aligned with biology.
3. **A-domain Substrate Probe:** Compare ESM (mean-pool over Stachelhaus positions) vs. BiGCARP (mean-pool over entire BGC) for binary substrate properties. Expect ESM to dominate, demonstrating fine-grained strength.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. What specific architectural or dynamical properties of BiGCARP's early dilated convolutional layers cause the rapid transformation and loss of ESM-initialized structural information, as indicated by the sharp CKA drop?
2. Beyond simple concatenation, what sophisticated fusion architectures (e.g., attention-based, joint pre-training) optimally combine amino acid-level and domain-level representations to maximize performance on intermediate-granularity tasks?
3. How does the efficacy of cross-granularity integration scale with dataset size, and does the observed complementarity between ESM and BiGCARP generalize to other biological domains (e.g., nucleotide-level models) or different hierarchical granularities?

## Limitations
- **Reproducibility gaps:** Critical MLP hyperparameters (optimizer, LR, batch size) and exact CV splits are unreported, hindering exact replication, especially for the small halogen dataset (n=46).
- **Fusion simplicity:** Only naive concatenation is tested; more expressive fusion methods might yield different or larger gains, and the complementarity might be task-specific.
- **Data leakage risk:** Random stratified CV splits on BGCs may lead to leakage via homologous sequences or shared species, potentially inflating performance metrics.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (Rapid Early Transformation): High.** Directly supported by consistent, sharp CKA drop between Layer 0 and Layer 1 across models.
- **Mechanism 2 (Last-Layer Contextuality): High.** Probe tasks show robust last-layer > embedder-layer performance for BGC-level tasks.
- **Mechanism 3 (Complementary Fusion): Medium.** Statistically significant gain for product classification (p=0.046) but high variance on halogen task (±0.209 AUROC); simple concatenation may not be optimal.

## Next Checks
1. **Reproduce CKA robustness:** Recompute the Layer 0→1 CKA drop across multiple independent training runs with different seeds to confirm it is not an artifact.
2. **Test advanced fusion:** For intermediate tasks (e.g., product classification), replace concatenation with a learned fusion module (e.g., gated sum, cross-attention) and evaluate performance change.
3. **Conduct biologically-aware CV:** For the halogen prediction dataset, re-evaluate using a leave-cluster-out CV (by BiG-SCAPE family or species) to assess result stability against potential data leakage and confirm statistical significance.
