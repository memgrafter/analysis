# Forecast collapse of transformer-based models under squared loss in financial time series

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.00064
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00064
- **Reference count:** 6
- **Primary result:** Transformer models exhibit forecast collapse on financial time series under squared loss, with nearly three times higher average trajectory error than linear benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
The paper explains why Transformer models fail on financial time series when using squared loss. It shows that when the conditional mean of future prices or returns is effectively zero (martingale-like), the Bayes-optimal predictor is trivial (flat for prices, zero for returns). Highly expressive models trained by empirical risk minimization do not reduce bias but amplify noise through interpolation, increasing prediction variance. Numerical experiments on high-frequency EUR/USD data confirm this: a PatchTST Transformer achieves a risk ratio of 1.71 and incurs larger errors on 92% of forecasting windows compared to a linear benchmark.

## Method Summary
The study compares a PatchTST Transformer against a simple linear model on high-frequency EUR/USD data. Input windows of 451 past observations forecast 30 steps ahead. Models are trained via empirical risk minimization with squared loss. The linear model predicts a scaled version of the last observed value. The Transformer uses patch-based tokenization and self-attention. Evaluation uses trajectory-level squared error, computing average loss, risk ratios, and the proportion of test windows where Transformer error exceeds linear.

## Key Results
- Transformer average trajectory error is 1.71 times higher than the linear benchmark.
- The Transformer incurs larger errors on 92% of forecasting windows.
- Under squared loss, expressive models amplify noise rather than improve bias in weak-signal financial regimes.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Trivial Bayes-Optimal Predictor
When financial processes follow a martingale, the conditional mean E[Y_t^{(H)} | F_t] equals the last observed value (prices) or zero (returns), making any complex model's optimal forecast indistinguishable from a naive predictor. Evidence: Proposition 1 and Corollary 1 in the paper; empirical dominance of naive baselines in finance.

### Mechanism 2: Noise Amplification via Interpolation
Highly expressive models fit stochastic noise in the targets when the signal is weak. This "noise reuse" generates spurious fluctuations around the trivial predictor, raising variance while bias remains at the irreducible noise level. Evidence: Proposition 2 shows expected error 2Hσ² for interpolating vs. Hσ² for linear; Transformer instability noted in corpus.

### Mechanism 3: Strict Worsening with Expressivity
Since the trivial predictor already achieves minimum bias, added complexity only increases variance, leading to higher total risk for expressive models. Evidence: Theoretical comparison and empirical risk ratio >1; 92% of test windows show larger Transformer errors.

## Foundational Learning
- **Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM):** Ensures empirical minimizers converge to population minimizers within the hypothesis class.  
  *Why needed:* The paper analyzes predictors trained via ERM; consistency results justify the theoretical setup.  
  *Quick check:* What conditions guarantee that ERM over H yields a predictor whose population risk approaches the best-in-class risk in H?
- **Bias-Variance Trade-off:** Explains why complexity harms performance in weak-signal regimes—bias already minimized, so complexity increases variance.  
  *Why needed:* To understand why model complexity hurts when Bayes predictor is simple.  
  *Quick check:* In trajectory forecasting under squared loss, how does increasing model capacity affect bias and variance when the Bayes predictor is constant?
- **Martingale Property in Finance:** Underlies the theoretical collapse; if prices form a martingale, E[X_{t+h} | F_t] = X_t, making forecasting trivial under squared loss.  
  *Why needed:* Core assumption about financial processes enabling degenerate conditional means.  
  *Quick check:* If asset prices form a martingale, what is E[X_{t+h} | F_t] for h>0?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Input window -> Patch tokenizer (split into patches, linear embedding) -> Transformer encoder (self-attention layers with shared weights, positional encoding) -> Output head (projection to H-step forecast).  
**Critical path:** 1. Raw input sequence segmented into patches and embedded. 2. Embeddings processed through Transformer layers for temporal mixing. 3. Final layer output reshaped/projected to trajectory.  
**Design tradeoffs:** Patch size balances detail vs. computational cost; model capacity trades representation power vs. overfitting noise; attention enables long-range dependencies but may overfit to spurious correlations.  
**Failure signatures:** Forecasts collapse to flat/near-zero lines; high variance in predictions for similar inputs; training loss decreases but test error remains high or worsens with capacity.  
**First 3 experiments:** 1. Train PatchTST and linear model on EUR/USD data (L=451, H=30). 2. For each test window, compute squared trajectory error. 3. Compare distributions: plot CDFs of errors and log-ratios; compute risk ratio and fraction of windows where Transformer error exceeds linear.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Can diffusion-based or probabilistic models learn full conditional distributions while avoiding variance amplification under squared loss for point forecasts?
2. What is the precise signal-to-noise ratio threshold at which the conditional mean becomes "effectively trivial" causing expressive models to underperform?
3. What alternative objectives, targets, or problem formulations can circumvent limitations of conditional mean regression under squared loss in finance?

## Limitations
- Theoretical mechanism assumes degenerate conditional mean (martingale), which may not fully capture weak but non-zero predictability in real markets.
- Empirical results are specific to one dataset (high-frequency EUR/USD) and one Transformer variant (PatchTST) with unspecified hyperparameters.
- Generalizability to all financial forecasting tasks is low; collapse is tied to weak signal and squared loss regime.

## Confidence
- **Theoretical Mechanism (Bias-Variance Collapse):** Medium (sound under strong assumptions, but realism of assumptions uncertain)
- **Empirical Findings (Transformer vs. Linear):** Medium (specific to dataset and model config)
- **Generalizability to All Financial Forecasting:** Low (not proven universally)
