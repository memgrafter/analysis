# The Curse and Blessing of Mean Bias in FP4-Quantized LLM Training

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.10444
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10444
*   **Reference Count:** 15
*   **Primary Result:** FP4-quantized LLM training is destabilized by a coherent rank-one mean bias in activations; the proposed Averis method (source-level mean subtraction) restores full-precision performance with minimal overhead.

## Executive Summary
This paper identifies a fundamental instability in low-precision large language model (LLM) training, specifically for 4-bit floating-point (FP4). The root cause is a coherent, rank-one mean bias that emerges in activation tensors. This bias originates from frequency-weighted embeddings, is amplified by non-linearities and residual connections, and becomes the dominant source of outlier values that inflate quantization scales. The proposed solution, Averis, computationally separates this mean component from the residual activation at the source (before each linear layer), quantizing them independently. This simple operation, requiring only reductions and element-wise arithmetic, effectively stabilizes training. On Qwen3-0.6B, Averis narrows the loss gap to BF16 training and improves downstream task performance, demonstrating that mitigating this specific bias recovers most benefits of more expensive spectral correction methods.

## Method Summary
Averis is a training-time intervention applied at each matrix multiplication (GeMM) in a transformer. For an activation tensor `X` (reshaped to a token-by-feature matrix), it computes the column-wise mean vector `µ_X`. The activation is split into a rank-one mean matrix `1 * µ_X^⊤` and a residual matrix `X_R = X - 1 * µ_X^⊤`. These two components are quantized to FP4 separately using their own scales. In the forward pass, the quantized mean vector is broadcast and multiplied by the weights, and this result is added to the quantized residual's matrix product. The backward pass applies an analogous split to the output gradient `D` to compute gradients for the weights and the original activation. The method uses only standard reduction (mean) and element-wise operations, adding negligible overhead compared to the cost of a full singular value decomposition (SVD).

## Key Results
*   Averis stabilizes FP4 (W4A4G4) training on Qwen3-0.6B, reducing the final training loss gap relative to the BF16 baseline.
*   Downstream average task score improves from 0.4564 to 0.4661 at 10B tokens, effectively restoring full-precision performance.
*   The method achieves stabilization comparable to SVD-based spectral correction (e.g., Metis) but with far lower computational cost, as it requires only reduction operations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Origin of a Coherent Mean Bias**
*   **Claim:** A coherent, rank-one mean bias in activation matrices emerges from frequency-weighted embeddings and becomes a dominant component of spectral anisotropy.
*   **Mechanism:** Non-uniform token frequencies cause high-frequency words to disproportionately influence the expected embedding vector (µ_embed). This initial corpus-level mean shifts token representations in a common directional sense. Self-attention and non-odd nonlinearities (e.g., ReLU, GELU) regenerate and amplify this mean shift during forward passes.
*   **Core Assumption:** Language corpora exhibit Zipf-like frequency distributions, and model architectures use non-odd activation functions and residual connections.
*   **Evidence Anchors:**
    *   [abstract] "This mean component emerges systematically across layers and training stages... This mean component emerges systematically across layers and training stages and accounts for the majority of extreme activation magnitudes."
    *   [section 3] "Stage I: Statistical Origin via Frequency-Weighted Embedding Expectation... µ_embed = E_v∼p[E_v]... high-frequency tokens receive disproportionate updates and induce coherent embedding alignment."
    *   Corpus evidence is indirect; related work discusses frequency bias and anisotropy but does not directly confirm this precise causal chain.
*   **Break Condition:** If token frequencies were uniform or if all nonlinearities were odd functions, the coherent mean regeneration would not occur.

**Mechanism 2: Growth via Residual Accumulation**
*   **Claim:** Residual connections preserve and accumulate the coherent mean bias across network depth, causing its norm to scale with hidden dimension and dominate activation energy in later layers.
*   **Mechanism:** Each layer's output mean is the sum of the previous layer's mean and a new layer-induced mean shift (µ_{l+1} = µ_l + ∆µ_l). Additive residual pathways prevent cancellation. In high-dimensional hidden spaces, even small per-coordinate drifts aggregate into large vector norms, making the mean component a primary contributor to large-magnitude activations.
*   **Core Assumption:** Residual connections are used, and layer-induced mean shifts have a nonzero projection onto the existing mean direction.
*   **Evidence Anchors:**
    *   [abstract] "This bias arises from frequency-weighted embeddings, grows with depth via nonlinearities and residuals..."
    *   [section 3] "Stage III: Residual Preservation and Network-Wide Accumulation... µ^{l+1} = µ^l + ∆µ^l... ∥µ∥² ∼ √H·µ̄."
    *   Corpus evidence is absent for this specific accumulation dynamic.
*   **Break Condition:** If residuals were multiplicative (gating without strong additive bias) or if layer-induced mean shifts were consistently orthogonal to the accumulated mean direction, accumulation would not dominate.

**Mechanism 3: Outlier Dominance in Low-Bit Quantization**
*   **Claim:** The coherent mean bias is the principal driver of activation outliers (extreme elementwise values) that inflate quantization scales in low-bit training.
*   **Mechanism:** For a coordinate `j` with mean shift `µ_j`, the activation `X_{ij} = µ_j + Z_{ij}` exceeds a threshold `t` with constant probability if `|µ_j| > t`, yielding Θ(l) exceedances across `l` tokens. In contrast, variance-only extremes occur with exponentially small probability per token, yielding o(l) exceedances. Since blockwise quantization scales are set by the maximum absolute entry (L∞ norm), the dense mean-dominated extremes stretch the dynamic range.
*   **Core Assumption:** Quantization scales are determined per-block by the elementwise L∞ norm, and activations contain independent/sub-Gaussian noise around the mean.
*   **Evidence Anchors:**
    *   [abstract] "accounts for the majority of extreme activation magnitudes, making it the principal driver of dynamic-range inflation under low precision."
    *   [section 4] Elementwise mechanical decomposition and Theorem 2 contrast mean-dominated (Θ(l)) vs. variance-only (O(l e^{-t²/(2σ²)})) exceedance counts.
    *   Corpus work (SmoothQuant, SVDQuant) addresses outliers but does not confirm mean bias as the *dominant* source; the paper's own decomposition (Figure 4) is the primary evidence.
*   **Break Condition:** If quantization scales were determined by a norm less sensitive to outliers (e.g., L2) or if residuals alone produced denser extremes, mean removal would not significantly stabilize quantization.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Anisotropy in LLM Representations**
    *   *Why needed:* The premise rests on anisotropic activations (energy concentrated in few directions). This geometric property explains why low-bit quantization struggles and what "mean bias" means spectrally.
    *   *Quick check:* If you projected LLM activations onto their top-5 right singular vectors, what variance explained would you expect relative to a random isotropic baseline?
*   **Blockwise Quantization and Scale Determination**
    *   *Why needed:* The instability mechanism directly links mean bias to how quantization scales are computed per activation block. Knowing scales come from the maximum absolute entry (L∞) explains why outliers are catastrophic.
    *   *Quick check:* In a blockwise 4-bit quantizer, if one outlier value is 1000× larger than the median magnitude, what happens to the quantization step size for all other values in that block?
*   **Residual Connections and Accumulation**
    *   *Why needed:* The mean bias growth mechanism (Stage III) depends on residual additivity. Understanding residual pathways is key to seeing why bias can accumulate rather than cancel.
    *   *Quick check:* In a residual block `y = x + f(x)`, if the column mean of `f(x)` is nonzero, how does the column mean of `y` relate to the column mean of `x`?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Activation capture -> Mean extraction (compute µ_X) -> Residual formation (X_R = X - 1µ_X^⊤) -> Dual quantization (quantize µ_X and X_R separately to FP4) -> Reconstruction (forward: ˆY = 1(¯µ_X ¯W) + ¯X_R ¯W; backward: split gradient D similarly).

Critical path: Correctness hinges on (a) accurate mean computation per batch/sequence dimension (l=b*s), (b) precise residual subtraction with correct broadcasting, and (c) proper reconstruction and gradient splitting per Equation (12). Any dimension mismatch or incorrect averaging corrupts the signal.

Design tradeoffs:
*   Cost vs. stability: Mean subtraction adds two reductions + two elementwise subtractions per GeMM (~<0.5% overhead) vs. significant compute/memory for SVD-based methods.
*   Precision: Means are quantized too, potentially introducing bias; not quantizing increases memory. The paper implies separate scaling for the mean vector.
*   Placement: Applied at every GeMM (source-level). Could be selectively applied only to outlier-prone layers (e.g., deeper FFN outputs) for further optimization.
*   Failure signatures:
    *   Training divergence (loss spikes/NaN) if mean subtraction is omitted or broadcast incorrectly.
    *   Persistent outlier dominance if post-Averis histograms still show heavy tails (spike components remain).
    *   Performance regression if mean quantization is too aggressive or if the mean direction carries useful signal.
*   First 3 experiments:
    1.  Profile activation matrices (l×m) from a small pretrained LLM (e.g., 100M params). Compute column means µ_X, Frobenius norm ratio ∥µ_X∥²/∥X∥_F², and outlier attribution (mean vs. variance) across layers to confirm rank-one bias dominance (>50% of top-0.1% magnitudes in later layers/training stages).
    2.  Integrate Averis into a single linear layer's forward pass. Train with FP4 emulation for 500 steps. Compare loss curves and activation L∞ norms against vanilla FP4; verify that the broadcast addition (1(¯µ_X ¯W) + ¯X_R ¯W) matches a BF16 reference within FP4 tolerance.
    3.  Implement a top-1 SVD projection removal baseline on the same layer. Compare stabilization (loss variance, peak magnitude) and per-step compute time (wall clock) against Averis. Expect similar stabilization with ≥5× higher cost for SVD.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Open Question 1:** Does the coherent mean bias phenomenon and the effectiveness of Averis scale to larger LLMs (e.g., 10B+ parameters)? The paper tests Qwen3-0.6B; theory suggests ∥µ∥² ∼ √H, but empirical validation on larger models is absent.
*   **Open Question 2:** Is the phenomenon universal across different LLM architectures (decoder-only vs. encoder-decoder, different normalization/activations)? All experiments use Qwen3-0.6B; generalizability is uncertain.
*   **Open Question 3:** Since Averis recovers "most" but not all stability benefits compared to SVD-based methods like Metis, what component of activation instability remains after mean removal, and can it be efficiently addressed?
*   **Open Question 4:** Does mean removal via Averis introduce subtle trade-offs in model capabilities (generation diversity, reasoning depth, attention patterns) beyond aggregate benchmark scores? The mean direction might encode useful information.
*   **Open Question 5:** Can the growth of mean bias during training be predicted or controlled? Would earlier or more frequent mean removal prevent amplification through residuals more effectively? The paper applies Averis uniformly but does not analyze optimal timing.

## Limitations
*   The exact NVFP4 format specification (exponent bias, subnormal handling), quantization granularity (block size), and precise handling of FP4 gradients are unspecified, requiring assumptions for reproduction.
*   Evidence for the proposed mean-bias causal chain relies heavily on internal analysis and decomposition; external corpus validation is indirect (related work on frequency bias/anisotropy does not directly confirm this specific mechanism).
*   Results are validated only on a ~1B parameter model (Qwen3-0.6B); scalability to modern LLM scales (10B+) and generalizability across architectures remain unproven.

## Confidence
*   **High:** Foundational concepts (activation anisotropy, blockwise L∞ scaling, residual accumulation math) are well-established and correctly applied.
*   **Medium:** The proposed mechanisms (mean bias origin/growth/outlier dominance) are plausible and supported by internal evidence (theoretical derivation, decomposition figures), but lack strong external corroboration from the literature corpus.
*   **Low:** Full reproducibility of quantitative results without clarifying the unspecified implementation details (NVFP4 format, block size, gradient handling) and without testing on other architectures or larger scales. Performance claims depend on these choices.
