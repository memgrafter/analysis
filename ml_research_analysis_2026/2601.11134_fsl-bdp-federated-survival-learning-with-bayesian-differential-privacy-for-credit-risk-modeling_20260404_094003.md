# FSL-BDP: Federated Survival Learning with Bayesian Differential Privacy for Credit Risk Modeling

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.11134
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.11134
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Federated Bayesian differential privacy yields a +7.0% C-index gain over classical DP, nearly matching non-private performance in credit risk survival modeling.

## Executive Summary
Credit risk models typically treat default as a binary outcome, ignoring the timing of default which carries critical economic information. This paper introduces FSL-BDP, a federated survival learning framework that leverages Bayesian Differential Privacy (BDP) to model time-to-default across multiple financial institutions without sharing raw data. By framing the problem as discrete-time survival analysis and combining federated averaging with BDP's data-aware privacy accounting, the approach achieves near non-private predictive performance and significantly outperforms classical DP in federated settings—reversing the usual privacy‑utility trade‑off.

## Method Summary
FSL-BDP builds on a discrete‑time survival neural network that outputs hazard probabilities for fixed time intervals. The model is trained in a federated manner using FedAvg: clients perform local updates with BDP‑SGD, which adds noise calibrated via a Bayesian privacy account that estimates Rényi divergence between posterior distributions conditioned on neighboring datasets. This data‑dependent accounting yields tighter privacy guarantees than classical DP's worst‑case sensitivity. The framework handles censored observations naturally and aggregates updates without centralizing data. Experiments on three credit datasets (LendingClub, SBA, Bondora) partitioned by geography demonstrate that BDP benefits substantially from federation (+7.0% C‑index gain) while classical DP gains little (+1.4%).

## Key Results
- **Federated BDP surpasses classical DP:** BDP achieves a +7.0% C‑index improvement relative to the non‑private baseline, while classical DP only gains +1.4% in the same federated setting.
- **Near non‑private utility:** Federated BDP's performance approaches that of training without any privacy protection, and it outperforms classical DP on most individual clients.
- **Better calibration:** Federated BDP maintains lower Integrated Brier Score (IBS) and better alignment between predicted and Kaplan‑Meier survival curves compared to classical DP.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Discrete‑Time Survival Captures Timing Heterogeneity
- **Claim:** Framing credit risk as a discrete‑time survival problem captures default timing heterogeneity, enabling risk‑adjusted decisions that binary classification misses.
- **Mechanism:** The model predicts time‑to‑default via discrete hazard probabilities \( h_l(x) \) across intervals \( l=1,\dots,T \). The likelihood (Eq. 1) handles right‑censored observations and yields month‑by‑month survival curves \( S_l(x) \). Early defaulters produce steep survival declines; late defaulters yield flatter curves.
- **Core assumption:** Default timing carries economic information (e.g., early defaults imply higher loss given less principal repaid). Censoring is non‑informative.
- **Evidence:** The abstract states that binary classification “ignores default timing, treating early defaulters … equivalently to late defaulters” and that the model “predicts time‑to‑default trajectories.” Section 3.2 formalizes the discrete hazard outputs and likelihood. Adjacent work in health federated learning uses similar survival formulations.
- **Break condition:** If censoring is informative (borrowers drop out due to unobserved improvement) or the observation window is too short to capture timing variation, survival estimates may be biased.

### Mechanism 2: Bayesian Differential Privacy Benefits More from Federation
- **Claim:** Bayesian DP outperforms classical DP in federated survival learning because data‑dependent privacy accounting better preserves gradient information under heterogeneous client distributions.
- **Mechanism:** Classical DP uses worst‑case sensitivity (clipping + fixed Gaussian noise). BDP estimates privacy loss via Rényi divergence between posterior distributions of model parameters under data inclusion/exclusion (leave‑one‑out gradients), using data‑aware bounds (Eq. 9). Federation stabilizes gradients via averaging, amplifying BDP’s advantage.
- **Core assumption:** Local client data are i.i.d. draws from client‑specific distributions \( P_k \). The Bayesian prior \( \mu(x) \) approximates the true data distribution well enough for tighter bounds.
- **Evidence:** The abstract reports the reversal: “while classical DP performs better than Bayesian DP in centralized settings, the latter benefits substantially more from federation (+7.0% vs +1.4% in C‑index gain).” Section 3.5 explains BDP’s posterior‑based formulation and its natural alignment with federated survival learning.
- **Break condition:** If the prior \( \mu(x) \) is misspecified (e.g., heavy‑tailed client data not captured), BDP may underestimate privacy loss, violating \( (\epsilon_\mu,\delta_\mu) \).

### Mechanism 3: Non‑IID Client Distributions Enable BDP’s Advantage
- **Claim:** Non‑IID client distributions (natural geographic splits) enable practical federated survival learning without performance collapse, but they are the reason BDP’s data‑dependent noise calibration succeeds.
- **Mechanism:** Clients differ in default risk (Figs. 4–6). FedAvg aggregates heterogeneous updates, implicitly regularizing noise effects. BDP’s posterior‑based privacy scales better with heterogeneity because its sensitivity estimates adapt to local gradient distributions, whereas classical DP’s worst‑case clipping is overly conservative.
- **Core assumption:** Client heterogeneity is bounded and not pathological (e.g., no client has extremely low \( n_k \) causing high‑variance gradients that dominate aggregation).
- **Evidence:** Section 5.5 shows Bayesian DP outperforms Classical DP in 39 of 62 clients across datasets. Section 3.3 defines the local loss \( L_k(\theta) \) and notes \( P_k \neq P_j \).
- **Break condition:** Extreme non‑IID (e.g., one client with all high‑risk borrowers) could cause gradient divergence, making FedAvg unstable and negating BDP’s advantage.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**
- Client‑side: Local dataset \( D_k \), survival neural network (6‑layer, sigmoid outputs), BDP‑SGD (Algorithm 2) with gradient clipping (\( C=1.0 \)), noise multiplier \( \sigma \).
- Server: Aggregates model updates via weighted FedAvg, broadcasts global \( \theta^{(r)}_{\text{global}} \), tracks cumulative privacy per client \( (\epsilon_k, \delta_k) \).
- Privacy accounting: Rényi orders \( \Lambda \), Monte‑Carlo sub‑batches \( M=10 \), tail bound \( \epsilon_\mu(\lambda) \) (Eq. 9).

**Critical path**
1. Server broadcasts global model \( \theta^{(r)}_{\text{global}} \) to all clients.
2. Each client runs BDP‑SGD for \( E=5 \) local epochs on its data.
3. Clients send updated parameters \( \theta^{(r+1)}_k \) back to server.
4. Server performs weighted FedAvg to produce \( \theta^{(r+1)}_{\text{global}} \).
5. Repeat for \( R=10 \) communication rounds.

**Design tradeoffs**
- Privacy vs. utility: Lower \( \epsilon_\mu \) demands higher noise \( \sigma \), degrading C‑index; BDP shifts this tradeoff favorably under federation.
- Federation overhead: Assumes full client participation; partial participation would need asynchronous handling.
- Model complexity: 6‑layer network may overfit small clients; simpler Cox models could be more robust.

**Failure signatures**
- **Privacy budget exhaustion:** Client’s \( \epsilon_k \) exceeds target mid‑training → stop or reduce \( \sigma \).
- **Non‑IID collapse:** High variance in client C‑indices (e.g., some near 0.5) → check local epochs or learning rate.
- **Calibration drift:** IBS rises >0.1 from non‑private baseline → review Rényi order \( \Lambda \) or increase \( M \).

**First 3 experiments**
1. **Survival baseline:** Train centralized model on one client; verify survival curves align with Kaplan‑Meier and C‑index >0.6.
2. **DP accounting sanity check:** Run BDP‑SGD with \( M=0 \) (analytical) vs. \( M=10 \) on synthetic data; ensure \( \epsilon_\mu \) converges and \( \delta_\mu \approx \beta+\gamma \).
3. **Federation effect:** Compare centralized vs. federated (no DP) C‑index; expect <1% drop. Larger drop indicates client heterogeneity issues.

## Limitations
- **BDP hyperparameters unspecified:** Exact Rényi orders \( \Lambda \), failure probabilities \( \beta,\gamma \), and prior \( \mu(x) \) for leave‑one‑out sampling are not provided.
- **DP noise calibration missing:** Noise multipliers \( \sigma \) for target \( \epsilon \) budgets are not reported; interaction with Adam may invalidate standard accountants.
- **Preprocessing and client splits ambiguous:** Full list of leakage features excluded and precise geographic grouping to achieve 27/32 clients are unclear.

## Confidence
- Discrete‑time survival formulation captures timing information: **High**
- BDP outperforms classical DP in federated settings due to gradient stabilization: **Medium**
- Non‑IID client distributions enable BDP’s advantage: **Medium**

## Next Checks
1. **BDP hyperparameter ablation:** Vary \( \Lambda \), \( \beta \), \( \gamma \) on one dataset to measure sensitivity of privacy budget and C‑index.
2. **DP accounting consistency:** Compare classical DP \( \epsilon \) computed via Opacus (with Adam) against the paper’s moments accountant on synthetic data; verify agreement within ±0.1.
3. **Non‑IID stress test:** Artificially create extreme client splits (e.g., one client with 90% high‑risk loans) and evaluate whether BDP’s advantage persists.
