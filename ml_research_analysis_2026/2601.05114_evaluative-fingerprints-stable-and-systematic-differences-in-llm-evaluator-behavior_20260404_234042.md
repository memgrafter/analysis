# Evaluative Fingerprints: Stable and Systematic Differences in LLM Evaluator Behavior

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.05114
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05114
- **Reference count:** 3
- **Primary result:** LLM evaluators exhibit stable, systematic "evaluative fingerprints" despite near-zero inter-rater agreement (α=0.042), enabling high-accuracy identification of the judge from its outputs.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates whether multiple large language models (LLMs) used as judges share a common conception of quality when evaluating content with a rubric. Through systematic evaluation of nine frontier models on 3,240 instances, the authors discover a "reliability paradox": judges show extremely low inter-rater agreement (Krippendorff’s α = 0.042) yet maintain high within-judge stability across repeated runs (ICC up to 0.872). Moreover, each judge can be identified from its evaluation outputs with 89.9% accuracy using features like score distributions and citation behavior. These stable, model-specific differences—termed "evaluative fingerprints"—appear in harshness/leniency, dimension weighting, and evidence handling. The findings imply that aggregating scores from multiple LLM judges yields a synthetic verdict that does not correspond to any single judge’s actual values, raising concerns about the interpretability of aggregated LLM-as-judge evaluations.

## Method Summary
The study reuses precomputed evaluation outputs from two domains: 30 YouTube videos and 15 Wikipedia briefing packs. Nine LLM judges (e.g., GPT-4.1, Claude-Opus) evaluated each item using a 5-dimensional rubric, producing JSON responses containing numeric scores and textual receipts (citations). For each evaluation, features were extracted: raw scores, judge harshness (mean score), dimension emphasis (standard deviation), receipt validity (proportion of citations that are valid spans in the source), and semantic linkage between justification and citation via an NLI model. A Random Forest classifier with stratified group cross-validation (grouped by content item to prevent overfitting) was trained to predict the judge from these features. Robustness was assessed through input perturbations, temperature variation, and cross-domain transfer tests. The reproduction uses the authors’ provided artifacts and scripts, avoiding the need for fresh model inference.

## Key Results
- Near-zero inter-rater agreement (Krippendorff’s α = 0.042) but high within-judge stability (ICC up to 0.872).
- Judge attribution accuracy reaches 89.9% (9-way) and 99.6% for distinguishing GPT-4.1 vs. GPT-5.2.
- Fingerprints appear in harshness/leniency, dimension weighting, and evidence metrics (e.g., citation validity ranges 80.3%–98.5%, semantic linkage 15.4%–44.2%).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Each judge exhibits a consistent evaluative disposition (harshness/leniency, dimension weighting) due to model-specific biases ingrained during pretraining and fine-tuning.
- **Mechanism:** The model's parameters encode a mapping from input (item + rubric) to scores and evidence. This mapping is shaped by the training objective and data distribution, leading to systematic preferences that are stable because the model's weights are fixed during evaluation.
- **Core assumption:** Evaluation behavior is primarily determined by the model's internal parameters and is not substantially altered by the evaluation context (given a fixed prompt).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "judges exhibit near-zero inter-rater agreement (Krippendorff’s α = 0.042) yet high within-judge stability (ICC up to 0.872)" and "stable, systematic differences—termed 'evaluative fingerprints'—manifest in harshness/leniency, dimension weighting, and evidence behavior."
  - [section]: Section 5.2 shows ICC up to 0.872; section 5.3 shows non-overlapping harshness CIs; section 5.4 shows evidence behavior varies widely across judges.
  - [corpus]: Corpus neighbors include papers on LLM judge biases (e.g., "Beyond the Illusion of Consensus" [paper_id 267246]), but direct evidence for fingerprint stability is limited in the corpus.
- **Break condition:** If judges are fine-tuned on a common evaluation dataset with calibrated examples, their dispositions may converge, reducing fingerprint identifiability.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Judges apply different implicit definitions to rubric dimensions, leading to systematic disagreement that is consistent across items.
- **Mechanism:** Rubric terms (e.g., "Faithfulness & Receipts") are underspecified. Each model fills in the meaning based on its training, resulting in different scoring for the same content. Because the model's interpretation is fixed, the disagreement pattern is stable.
- **Core assumption:** Rubric dimensions are ambiguous enough to allow multiple plausible interpretations, and models adopt distinct interpretations.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "each judge implements a distinct, consistent theory of quality."
  - [section]: Section 5.1: Negative Krippendorff's α on Readability & Structure (-0.064) and SEO Mechanics (-0.047) indicates systematic disagreement worse than chance, implying incompatible criteria.
  - [corpus]: Corpus includes "Rubrics as an Attack Surface" [paper_id 259189] discussing preference drift, indicating rubrics can interact with model biases.
- **Break condition:** If rubrics are replaced with highly specific, example-anchored guidelines, interpretation divergence may decrease, reducing fingerprint distinctiveness.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Judges differ in how they generate and use citations (receipts), and these citation behaviors are stable and fingerprintable.
- **Mechanism:** The model's decoding and evidence selection process leads to characteristic patterns: citation volume, validity of cited spans, and semantic linkage between citation and justification. These reflect a stable "evidence policy" driven by model-specific heuristics.
- **Core assumption:** Citation behavior is not purely random but reflects a stable strategy that can be captured by metrics like presence validity and NLI linkage.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "evidence behavior (e.g., semantic linkage of citations)" is part of fingerprint.
  - [section]: Section 5.4: Presence validity ranges from 80.3% to 98.5%; NLI linkage from 15.4% to 44.2%; Section 5.5 shows disposition features contribute to 89.9% attribution accuracy.
  - [corpus]: Corpus includes "Learning an Efficient Multi-Turn Dialogue Evaluator" [paper_id 107001] addressing evaluation biases, including citation behavior.
- **Break condition:** If judges are constrained to extract exact spans without paraphrasing (e.g., via retrieval), citation behavior differences may shrink.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Content source → Judge pool → Evaluation harness → Feature extractor → Attribution classifier → Validation suite

**Critical path:**
1. Prepare dataset: select content items and generate packs.
2. Define rubric: 5 dimensions, scoring anchors, JSON output spec with receipts.
3. Run evaluations: for each judge, on each item, repeat multiple runs (e.g., 3).
4. Parse and validate: filter non-compliant outputs; extract scores and receipts.
5. Compute features: per evaluation row, derive scores, harshness, receipt statistics.
6. Train/test classifier: using grouped CV (by video/article) to avoid overfitting, measure accuracy.
7. Run robustness checks: perturb inputs, vary temperature, test on different domain.
8. Interpret fingerprints: analyze harshness ordering, evidence behavior profiles.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Judge selection: more diverse models yield richer fingerprints but may reduce within-group discriminability.
- Rubric design: more dimensions increase feature dimensionality but may introduce ambiguity; trade-off between richness and reliability.
- Number of runs: more runs improve ICC estimates but increase cost; 3 runs is minimal for stability.
- Domain coverage: testing across domains validates generality but requires broader data; may dilute signal if rubrics not adapted.
- Feature engineering: including raw scores vs. disposition-only; simpler features may be more interpretable but less discriminative.

**Failure signatures:**
- Low ICC (<0.2) for a judge indicates instability; check prompt parsing or judge stochasticity.
- Attribution accuracy near chance in LOVO suggests overfitting to content; ensure proper grouping.
- Harshness order reverses across domains; may indicate domain-specific calibration rather than stable fingerprint.
- Disposition features show extreme missingness (e.g., many evaluations with no receipts); check prompt compliance or model capability.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Reproduce main attribution: On the provided 3-video sample, run the full pipeline and confirm combined features yield >80% 9-way accuracy.
2. Harshness stability: Compute per-judge mean harshness on the sample; verify non-overlapping bootstrap CIs for at least some pairs.
3. Evidence behavior variation: Compute presence validity and NLI linkage for each judge; expect at least 20 percentage points spread between highest and lowest.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Do evaluative fingerprints generalize across diverse domains and rubrics beyond the SEO content and Wikipedia briefing pack contexts studied?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The limitations section states "additional domains and rubrics remain untested."
- **Why unresolved:** The study only tested two domains with similar rubrics; it is unknown if fingerprints are robust to domain and rubric variation.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct a multi-domain study evaluating the same judge set across numerous tasks (e.g., code, scientific papers, creative writing) and varied rubrics, measuring attribution accuracy in each.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Are evaluative dispositions stable over longer time periods, or do they drift due to model updates or environmental changes?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The limitations note "Long-term stability and temporal drift remain open questions."
- **Why unresolved:** Only three runs per judge over a short period were analyzed; long-term consistency is unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Re-evaluate the same set of items with the same judges at multiple future time points (e.g., monthly for a year) and compute correlation of disposition metrics over time.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Are evaluative fingerprints intrinsic to the model, or do they depend on the specific rubric used for evaluation?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The study held the rubric constant across domains; the assumption is that fingerprints are model-specific, but rubric constraints could shape behavior.
- **Why unresolved:** It is unclear whether the same model would show consistent attribution patterns across different rubrics evaluating identical content.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Evaluate a fixed set of items using multiple distinct rubrics (different dimensions, scales) with the same judges, and test whether judge attribution accuracy remains high.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How sensitive are fingerprint-based attribution results to the specific feature engineering choices and classifier architecture?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The limitations state "results may vary with different feature engineering choices."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper used a specific set of features (scores, disposition metrics) and Random Forest; alternative features (e.g., raw text, different NLI models) or classifiers might yield different accuracy.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematically vary feature definitions (e.g., exclude receipt features, use different NLI thresholds, include token counts) and classification algorithms, reporting attribution accuracy across conditions.

## Limitations
- Generalizability is constrained: only 9 frontier models, two domains (video briefs, Wikipedia), and a single rubric were tested; extension to open-weight models, other tasks, and varied rubrics is needed.
- Causal origins of fingerprints (e.g., training data, fine-tuning) remain speculative; the paper shows correlation but not mechanism.
- Cross-domain robustness was assessed with only one additional domain; further validation on entirely new domains is required.

## Confidence
- Fingerprint existence & within-judge stability: High
- Cross-domain persistence: Medium
- Each judge’s distinct theory of quality: Medium
- Aggregation yields synthetic verdict: High

## Next Checks
1. **Domain and judge diversity replication:** Re-run evaluation on at least three new domains (e.g., code generation, poetic writing, mathematical proofs) and include open-weight judges (LLaMA, Mistral). Expected: Fingerprint attribution accuracy remains high (>80%) and Krippendorff’s α stays near zero.
2. **Rubric specificity manipulation:** Replace the original rubric with a highly detailed, example-anchored version. Hypothesis: reduced inter-judge disagreement and lower attribution accuracy, indicating fingerprints are partly rubric-ambiguity-driven.
3. **Fine-tuning experiment:** Fine-tune a single base model (e.g., GPT-3.5-turbo or LLaMA-3-8B) on three distinct instruction datasets (helpfulness, harmlessness, coding). If distinct fingerprints emerge across fine-tuned variants, it points to fine-tuning data as a key cause.
