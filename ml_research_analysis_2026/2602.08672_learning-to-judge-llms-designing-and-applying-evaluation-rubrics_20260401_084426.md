# Learning to Judge: LLMs Designing and Applying Evaluation Rubrics

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.08672  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08672  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: LLMs can design and apply self-generated evaluation rubrics, achieving high internal consistency but variable human alignment and poor cross-model transfer on domain-specific tasks.

## Executive Summary
The paper presents GER-Eval, a framework where large language models generate and apply their own evaluation rubrics for natural language generation tasks. Tested on four datasets and five LLMs, the study finds that LLM-produced rubrics align with human rubrics over 80% of the time and maintain within-model scoring consistency of 70–90% across zero-shot and few-shot prompts. However, agreement with human judgments deteriorates for factual tasks: correlations are 0.8–0.9 for conversational/instruction tasks, drop to ~0.5 for general summarization, and fall below 0.3 for biomedical summarization. Closed-source models (e.g., GPT-4o) outperform open-weight models in both rubric agreement and cross-model transfer. Rubric transfer works robustly for conversational tasks (ICC 0.7–0.8) but fails for domain-specific tasks (ICC <0.2). The results indicate that LLM-based evaluation is a learned capability that is reliable within a model but fragmented across different models, motivating new hybrid human–LLM evaluation approaches.

## Method Summary
GER-Eval consists of three stages: (1) Rubric generation, where an LLM is prompted to produce a structured set of evaluation criteria for a specific NLG task; (2) Rubric application, where the same LLM scores model outputs according to the generated rubric; and (3) evaluation, where metrics such as rubric–human alignment, intra-model consistency, correlation with human scores, and rubric transferability are computed. Experiments span four tasks: two conversational quality datasets (USR and a second) and two summarization benchmarks (SummEval and SumPubMed). Five LLMs are tested, including GPT-4o and four open-weight models of varying scales. The findings show that LLMs create coherent, task‑aware rubrics (>80% human alignment) and apply them consistently (70–90% agreement between zero/few-shot settings). Yet scoring reliability as judged by humans varies drastically by task type: high for conversational/instruction tasks, moderate for general summarization, and low for biomedical summarization. GPT-4o yields the strongest results, and rubric transfer is only successful for conversational tasks.

## Key Results
- LLM-generated rubrics align >80% with human rubrics; within-model scoring consistency 70–90% across zero/few-shot settings.
- Human correlation: 0.8–0.9 for conversational/instruction tasks, ~0.5 for summarization, and <0.3 for biomedical summarization.
- Cross-model rubric transfer ICC ~0.7–0.8 for conversational tasks, but <0.2 for domain-specific tasks; closed-source models show higher agreement and better transfer than open-weight models.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
LLMs can produce evaluation rubrics because their extensive pre‑training on diverse text implicitly encodes human notions of quality such as coherence, fluency, and relevance. When prompted, they externalize these notions in a structured format. The high within‑model consistency reflects a stable internal mapping from rubric concepts to scoring decisions. The sharp drop in human correlation for factual tasks suggests that LLMs prioritize surface‑level linguistic features over deep factual correctness, likely because the latter is not consistently reinforced during training. The fragmentation across models points to distinct “evaluation dialects” shaped by architecture, scale, and alignment; these dialects diverge especially when specialized domain knowledge is needed, causing catastrophic transfer failures in biomedical summarization.

## Foundational Learning
### Rubric Alignment
- **Why needed**: To assess whether LLMs capture the same quality dimensions as human evaluators.  
- **Quick check**: Does high rubric alignment guarantee high correlation with human scores? (No; factual tasks show misaligned outcomes.)

### Evaluation Dialect
- **Why needed**: Explains why rubrics generated by one model are not reliably used by another.  
- **Quick check**: Can two models with similar rubrics still produce divergent scores? (Yes; dialects affect interpretation.)

### Within-Model Consistency
- **Why needed**: Indicates reliability of a single LLM as an evaluator.  
- **Quick check**: Is 70–90% agreement sufficient for practical evaluation? (Depends on application tolerance.)

### Domain-Specific Evaluation Gap
- **Why needed**: Highlights LLMs' limitations in knowledge‑intensive domains.  
- **Quick check**: Would domain‑specific fine‑tuning improve factual scoring? (Empirically testable.)

### Rubric Transferability
- **Why needed**: Determines if rubrics can be shared across model families.  
- **Quick check**: Does transfer succeed for all task types? (No, only conversational tasks.)

### Closed- vs. Open-Weight Models
- **Why needed**: Guides model selection for evaluation tasks.  
- **Quick check**: Are performance gaps due to scale, data, or alignment? (Requires ablation studies.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Task Specification -> Rubric Generator (LLM) -> Generated Rubric -> Rubric Applicator (LLM) -> Output Scores -> Alignment Evaluator -> Metrics (Alignment%, Consistency, Correlation, ICC)

**Critical path**: Generate rubric with LLM → Apply rubric to outputs → Compute intra‑model consistency, rubric–human alignment, correlation with human scores, and cross‑model ICC.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- Same LLM for generation and application ensures consistency but may amplify model‑specific biases.  
- LLM‑based alignment measurement introduces circularity; human judgments are gold standard but costly.  
- Zero‑shot prompts simplify the pipeline; few‑shot can enhance rubric quality at the expense of complexity.  
- Open‑weight models are accessible but may lag behind closed‑ource ones in reliability.

**Failure signatures**:  
- Alignment% <80% between generated rubric and human rubric.  
- Within‑model consistency <70% across zero/few‑shot prompts.  
- Human correlation <0.5 for non‑factual tasks or near zero for factual tasks.  
- Rubric transfer ICC <0.3, especially for domain‑specific tasks.  
- Rubric content omits key dimensions (e.g., factuality).

**First three experiments**:  
1. **Baseline Consistency**: For GPT‑4o on USR, generate a rubric, then score outputs in both zero‑shot and few‑shot modes; compute Cohen’s κ. Expect >0.7 agreement.  
2. **Rubric Content vs. Factual Accuracy**: Analyze rubrics from all models for mentions of “accuracy”, “factuality”, “correctness”; correlate presence with human correlation on SumPubMed.  
3. **Cross‑Model Transfer Gap**: Use a GPT‑4o‑generated rubric to score SumPubMed with Llama‑3‑70B‑Instruct; compare ICC and human correlation to GPT‑4o’s self‑scores. Expect ICC <0.2 and near‑zero human correlation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How can evaluation frameworks be designed to jointly model human and LLM evaluative language, thereby improving the reliability and interpretability of LLM-based assessment?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors explicitly call for "new methods that jointly model human and LLM evaluative language to improve reliability and interpretability" in the abstract and conclusion.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper demonstrates a gap between LLM-generated and human rubrics, especially in factual tasks, and notes that models interpret their own rubrics best. No integrated approach is proposed or tested.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Development and empirical validation of a hybrid framework where human and LLM rubrics are co‑created or dynamically aligned, with measured improvements in cross‑model agreement and human correlation across diverse tasks.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Why does rubric transfer—the application of a rubric generated by one model by another model—fail catastrophically in domain‑specific, knowledge‑intensive tasks like biomedical summarization?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The results show rubric transfer ICC < 0.2 and correlations near zero for SumPubMed, while robust for conversational tasks (USR). The paper identifies this breakdown but does not analyze its root causes.
- **Why unresolved**: The failure is documented but not examined. Possible factors include divergent domain knowledge, inconsistent interpretation of terminology, or model‑specific biases in factual reasoning, but these are not disentangled.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Error analysis of rubric components (e.g., “Accuracy,” “Terminology Use”) across models, probing for knowledge gaps or semantic mismatches; controlled experiments varying domain specificity to isolate the source of transfer failure.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Do LLMs possess stable, intrinsic “evaluation dialects” that evolve with model scaling, architecture, or alignment, and can these dialects be mapped or aligned across model families?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The discussion posits that models develop distinct “evaluation dialects” shaped by internal representations, leading to fragmentation across architectures. This is inferred from the low cross‑model rubric agreement.
- **Why unresolved**: The study is a static comparison of models at fixed points; it does not trace how evaluation behaviors change with model updates, nor does it attempt to characterize the linguistic or representational basis of these dialects.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Longitudinal studies tracking rubric generation/application across model versions; probing or representation analysis to identify features correlated with dialect differences; interventions to induce alignment (e.g., distillation of rubric understanding).

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: What architectural or training modifications can enable LLMs to generate and apply rubrics that reliably capture factual correctness and domain‑specific quality, moving beyond surface‑level linguistic features?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] Scoring reliability degrades in factual tasks (SummEval, SumPubMed), and LLM rubrics often omit or misrepresent factuality. The paper notes models prioritize fluency over factual accuracy.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper identifies the problem but does not explore solutions. It is unclear whether this stems from training objectives, lack of factual grounding, or prompt‑based limitations in rubric conception.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Experiments augmenting LLMs with retrieval, fact‑checking modules, or fine‑tuning on domain‑specific evaluation data; analysis of whether rubric content (e.g., inclusion of factuality dimensions) correlates with improved factual scoring.

### Open Question 5
- **Question**: To what extent do LLM‑generated evaluation rubrics reflect genuine task understanding versus spurious correlations or dataset‑specific artifacts, and how can rubric coherence be measured independently of human benchmarks?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The paper uses human rubric alignment as a proxy for coherence, but acknowledges that self‑generated rubrics are internally consistent yet misaligned with humans in some cases. The metric “Align.” relies on LLM‑based tagging, introducing circularity.
- **Why unresolved**: Coherence is assessed by mapping to human rubrics, which may not be the gold standard. There is no independent validation of whether generated rubrics are semantically meaningful or task‑appropriate beyond human correspondence.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Development of intrinsic rubric quality metrics (e.g., criterion independence, coverage of perturbation tests); expert evaluation of rubric validity without reference to human dataset rubrics; testing rubrics on adversarial or out‑of‑distribution outputs to check for robustness.

## Limitations
- Unknown why rubric alignment is high but human correlation drops drastically for factual tasks.  
- Rubric design is model‑driven; generated rubrics may contain implicit biases or lack universal criteria compared to human‑crafted ones.  
- Small model set (5) and dataset scope (4 NLG tasks) limit generalizability; no statistical testing of significance reported.

## Confidence
- LLM‑generated rubrics align >80% with human rubrics: **High**  
- Within‑model scoring consistency 70–90%: **High**  
- Human correlation low for factual tasks (≤0.5 summarization, <0.3 biomedical) and rubric transfer ICC <0.2 for domain‑specific tasks: **High**  
- GPT‑4o outperforms open‑weight models in agreement and transfer: **Medium**

## Next Checks
1. Error analysis on factual tasks: Audit LLM-applied scores vs. human scores on biomedical summarization to identify whether failures are due to content errors, rubric misinterpretation, or knowledge gaps.  
2. Scale open‑model experiments: Replicate with additional open‑weight models (e.g., Llama 3 variants) at larger scales to confirm performance gap with GPT‑4o is model‑size/architecture‑dependent, not incidental.  
3. Cross‑domain transfer test: Apply rubrics generated for conversational tasks to out‑of‑domain summarization (e.g., scientific vs. news) to isolate whether transfer failure is task‑type or domain‑specific.
