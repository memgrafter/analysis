# A Comparative Study in Surgical AI: Datasets, Foundation Models, and Barriers to Med-AGI

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv**: 2603.27341
- **Source**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27341
- **References**: 40
- **Primary result**: Zero-shot and fine-tuned VLMs underperform compared to specialized detectors like YOLO, indicating that surgical AI progress requires more than scaling generalist models.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates whether scaling vision-language models (VLMs) can solve surgical tool detection, a basic perception task. Evaluating 19 VLMs (2B–235B) on the SDSC-EEA neurosurgical dataset, the authors find that zero-shot performance never exceeds the 13.4% majority-class baseline. Fine-tuning Gemma 3 27B with LoRA adapters and a linear classification head yields 51.08% exact-match accuracy, but increasing LoRA rank leads to severe overfitting (training accuracy 98.6%, validation <40%). In contrast, YOLOv12-m, a specialized detector with only 26M parameters, achieves 54.73% exact-match, outperforming all VLM methods. Cross-dataset tests on CholecT50 confirm similar patterns: fine-tuned models (Gemma 83.02%, YOLO 81.37%) surpass frontier zero-shot VLMs (≤69.15%). The study concludes that surgical AI is bottlenecked by limited specialized data and may benefit from hybrid architectures that combine generalist VLMs with task-specific perception modules.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates surgical tool detection on two datasets: SDSC-EEA (67,634 frames, 31 tools, with bounding box annotations, split 53/13 by procedure) and CholecT50 (100,863 frames, 6 tools, split 80/20 by video). The task is multi-label classification at the frame level; bounding boxes are used only for the specialized detector. Primary evaluation metric is exact-match accuracy. Methods include zero-shot inference using 19 VLMs prompted to output JSON, fine-tuning of Gemma 3 27B via LoRA adapters (rank 1024) on attention layers plus a linear classification head trained with binary cross-entropy for 10 epochs at a learning rate of 5e-6, a LoRA rank scaling experiment (r=2–1024), and training YOLOv12-m on bounding boxes for 300 epochs. Cross-dataset generalization is examined by training on one dataset and evaluating on the other.

## Key Results
- Zero-shot VLMs (up to 235B parameters) fail to exceed the 13.4% majority-class baseline on SDSC-EEA.
- YOLOv12-m (26M parameters) outperforms fine-tuned 27B VLMs (51.08%) with 54.73% exact-match, demonstrating the superiority of specialized detectors despite far fewer parameters.
- Increasing LoRA rank causes overfitting: training accuracy rises to 98.6% while validation plateaus below 40%, indicating that model capacity alone cannot overcome procedure-level distribution shifts.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Scaling vision-language model (VLM) parameters alone does not yield functional surgical tool detection in zero-shot settings.
- **Mechanism**: Surgical tool detection requires fine-grained visual discrimination under domain-specific distribution shifts (e.g., lighting, anatomy, instrument appearance) that general multimodal pretraining does not capture. Zero-shot VLMs default to majority-class heuristics or hallucinate labels.
- **Core assumption**: Generalist VLMs lack the specialized visual representations needed for surgical perception tasks, even with billions of parameters.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] “Zero‑shot VLM performance never exceeds the 13.4% majority‑class baseline.”
  - [section 3.1] Across 19 VLMs (2B–235B), validation accuracy ranges from 0.11%–14.52%, with only one model marginally surpassing majority baseline.
  - [corpus] Neighbors (e.g., GP-VLS) show task-specific adaptation improves surgical vision, but no direct evidence that scaling alone solves detection.
- **Break condition**: If a zero-shot VLM with >50B parameters consistently exceeds 40% exact-match on held-out procedures, this mechanism weakens.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Task-specific fine-tuning improves VLM performance but hits a generalization ceiling due to procedure-level distribution shift.
- **Mechanism**: Fine-tuning adapts the model to training procedures, but because data are split by surgical case, the model overfits to instrument distributions in training procedures and fails on unseen cases. Scaling LoRA rank amplifies overfitting.
- **Core assumption**: The primary bottleneck is not model capacity but lack of diverse, procedurally varied training data.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 3.2–3.3] LoRA fine-tuning with classification head reaches 51.08% validation exact-match, but training–validation gap persists.
  - [section 3.4] LoRA rank sweep (2→1024) increases trainable parameters ∼1000×; training accuracy rises monotonically, validation remains <40%.
  - [section A] Per-tool train/validation counts show extreme imbalance (e.g., Aspirating dissector: 88 train vs. 2,319 val), confirming procedure-level split causes distribution shift.
- **Break condition**: If validation accuracy scales with LoRA rank or dataset size without plateauing, capacity or data quantity become limiting factors instead.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Specialized perception modules (e.g., YOLO) outperform generalist VLMs on narrow surgical tasks with orders of magnitude fewer parameters.
- **Mechanism**: Task-specific architectures directly optimize for spatial detection and benefit from inductive biases (e.g., anchor boxes, localization loss) that VLMs lack when trained on set-level labels. Efficiency gains come from discarding unnecessary multimodal reasoning.
- **Core assumption**: Surgical AI progress may rely on hybrid systems where VLMs delegate perception to specialized modules, not on scaling generalist models.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 3.5] YOLOv12-m (26M params) achieves 54.73% exact-match vs. best VLM (51.08%), using >1000× fewer parameters.
  - [section J] ResNet-50 trained on set-level labels (no bounding boxes) reaches 39.6%, still beating all zero-shot VLMs, indicating supervision signal matters.
  - [section 3.6] On CholecT50, fine-tuned Gemma (83.02%) and YOLO (81.37%) both beat frontier VLMs (≤69.15%).
- **Break condition**: If a 1B+ parameter VLM fine-tuned on the same data consistently surpasses specialized detectors in accuracy and efficiency, the necessity of specialized modules diminishes.

## Foundational Learning
- **Distribution shift in medical imaging**  
  *Why needed here*: Train/validation split by surgical procedure creates covariate shift where tools, lighting, and camera angles differ across hospitals/surgeons. This explains why in-sample accuracy diverges from held-out performance.  
  *Quick check*: If two procedures use different instrument sets, how would a model trained on one generalize to the other?

- **Parameter efficiency vs. task specificity**  
  *Why needed here*: YOLO (26M) outperforms VLMs (27B) on tool detection, contradicting “bigger is better” scaling assumptions and highlighting role of architectural inductive bias.  
  *Quick check*: Why might a model with 1000× fewer parameters generalize better on a narrow task?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:
Data pipeline: SDSC-EEA/CholecT50 → frame extraction → tool-set labels (YOLO format) → train/validation split by procedure/video.
Baseline models: Zero-shot VLMs (Gemma, Qwen) via JSON prompt; Specialized detector (YOLOv12-m) with bounding boxes.
Fine-tuning: VLM + LoRA adapters → classification head (sigmoid outputs) trained with binary cross-entropy.
Evaluation: Exact-match (set equality), Jaccard, per-tool precision/recall, top-1 accuracy.

Critical path:
1. Ensure procedure-level split prevents leakage (frames from same surgery in only one set).
2. For VLM fine-tuning: choose between JSON generation (autoregressive) vs. classification head (parallel logits).
3. When scaling LoRA, balance rank with GPU memory; monitor training vs. validation accuracy for overfitting.
4. Compare against YOLO: convert bounding boxes to tool sets (confidence ≥0.25) for fair exact-match.

Design tradeoffs:
- VLM (generalist) vs. YOLO (specialized): VLMs offer multimodal reasoning potential but require huge compute; YOLO is efficient but limited to detection, no language interface.
- LoRA rank: Low rank saves memory but may underfit; high rank risks overfitting to training procedures.
- Output format: JSON generation is flexible but slow; classification head is fast but requires threshold tuning.

Failure signatures:
- High training accuracy + low validation accuracy → overfitting to procedure-specific noise; check per-tool counts (Appendix A) for imbalance.
- Output validation failures in zero-shot → model not adhering to tool ontology; examine Appendix G for hallucination patterns.
- YOLO underperformance vs. VLM fine-tuned → check if YOLO’s confidence threshold is too aggressive; inspect per-tool recall (Table 4).

First 3 experiments:
1. Zero-shot VLM evaluation on validation set: confirm no model beats majority baseline (13.4%).
2. Fine-tune Gemma 3 27B with LoRA + classification head: target validation >40%.
3. Train YOLOv12-m from scratch: expect >50% validation exact-match with 26M parameters.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Is the limited performance of fine-tuned VLMs on surgical tool detection primarily due to insufficient quantities of specialized surgical data, or are there fundamental architectural limitations that prevent VLMs from generalizing under distribution shift even with adequate data?
2. How should hybrid AI systems be architecturally designed to effectively combine the generalist capabilities of VLMs with the high performance of specialized perception modules like YOLO for surgical applications?
3. Do vision-language models exhibit improved scaling laws—where performance increases predictably with model size, data, and compute—on higher-level surgical perception tasks (e.g., phase recognition, surgical activity recognition) compared to the basic tool detection task studied here?
4. What specific characteristics of surgical datasets (e.g., tool class distribution, inter-procedure variability, annotation consistency) most strongly influence the effectiveness of fine-tuning VLMs for tool detection, and can these be used to predict cross-dataset generalization?

## Limitations
- Data and model access: SDSC-EEA is not publicly available, and key models (Gemma 3 27B, YOLOv12) are unreleased, hindering exact replication.
- Comparison fairness: VLMs are evaluated with set-level labels while YOLO uses bounding box supervision, an unfair mismatch that likely favors the specialized detector.
- Hyperparameter and scope uncertainties: Training details (e.g., LoRA α, learning rate schedule) are unspecified, and findings are limited to two surgical domains, raising concerns about generalizability.

## Confidence
- Scaling zero-shot VLMs does not solve surgical tool detection: High.
- Fine‑tuning hits a generalization ceiling due to procedure‑level distribution shift: High.
- Specialized perception modules (e.g., YOLO) outperform generalist VLMs on narrow surgical tasks with orders of magnitude fewer parameters: Medium.

## Next Checks

2. Supervision‑equivalent ablation: Fine‑tune a VLM with a detection head (e.g., DETR) using bounding box loss and compare to YOLO and the classification‑head VLM.
