# Beyond Uniform Credit: Causal Credit Assignment for Policy Optimization

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.09331
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09331
- **Reference count:** 32
- **Primary result:** Counterfactual importance weighting upweights critical reasoning tokens, improving GSM8K accuracy by 0.8–1.1 pp over uniform DAPO.

## Executive Summary
Policy gradient methods for language model reasoning uniformly assign credit to all tokens, causing gradient dilution where critical calculations receive the same weight as filler text. This paper proposes counterfactual importance weighting: reasoning spans (e.g., arithmetic steps) are masked, and the drop in answer probability measures their causal importance. Tokens in high-importance spans are upweighted during policy gradient updates without auxiliary models or annotation. On GSM8K across three models (Qwen1.7B/3B, Llama3.2-3B), CF‑DAPO improved test accuracy by 0.8–1.1 percentage points over uniform baselines and accelerated convergence. Inverting the importance signal degraded performance, validating the causal signal. However, no benefit was observed on code generation (MBPP+), indicating the approach is effective primarily where correctness depends on a concentrated answer signal.

## Method Summary
The method extends DAPO, a token‑level policy gradient algorithm that uniformly assigns advantage to all tokens. For each generated completion, the system first detects reasoning spans (arithmetic expressions, intermediate calculations) using regex patterns. Then for each span, the tokens are replaced with a placeholder (e.g., pad token) in a counterfactual forward pass to compute the answer log‑probability under teacher forcing. The importance drop \(D(s_k) = \log P(a|x,r_{-s_k}) - \log P(a|x,r)\) is recorded; a more negative \(D\) indicates higher importance because masking damages the answer probability. After normalizing importance scores per completion to \([0,1]\), they are mapped to a weight range \([w_{\min}, w_{\max}] = [0.5,4.0]\). Tokens within a span receive this weight; non‑span tokens retain weight 1; final answer tokens are upweighted to 1.5. The weighted loss \(L_{\text{CF‑DAPO}}\) multiplies each token’s advantage by its weight before applying the DAPO update. Training uses LoRA (rank 32, alpha 32), group size \(G=8\), learning rate \(2.5\times10^{-5}\), and typical decoding hyperparameters (temp=0.6, top-p=0.95). The implementation builds on TRL with `loss_type="dapo"`.

## Key Results
- **Accuracy gain:** CF‑DAPO improves exact‑match accuracy on GSM8K by +0.8–1.1 percentage points over uniform DAPO across Qwen1.7B, Qwen3B, and Llama3.2‑3B.
- **Causal validation:** Inverting the importance weights yields performance worse than uniform DAPO, confirming that the signal captures genuine causal structure rather than noise.
- **Domain limitation:** No significant improvement is observed on MBPP+ code generation, suggesting the method is most effective when correctness hinges on a single concentrated answer signal.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The improvement arises from three interconnected mechanisms.

**1. Counterfactual importance estimation:** Causal importance of a reasoning span is estimated by masking it and measuring the drop in answer probability. Formally, for span \(s_k\), \(D(s_k) = \log P_\theta(a|x, r_{-s_k}) - \log P_\theta(a|x, r)\), where \(a\) is the final answer. A more negative \(D\) indicates higher importance because the span's removal harms the answer. This relies on answer probability as a success indicator and assumes that placeholder masking does not introduce confounding distribution shifts. Evidence: Section 3.2 defines Equation (3) for counterfactual drop; the abstract describes the masking approach. *Break condition:* If the placeholder token is out-of-distribution, log‑probabilities become noisy, compromising estimates.

**2. Gradient upweighting:** The CF‑DAPO loss scales each token’s advantage \(A_i\) by a weight \(w_t\) derived from the normalized importance \(\hat{I}(s_k)\) of its span (Eqs. 6–7). This concentrates gradient mass on critical calculations while preserving the intended gradient direction. Evidence: Section 3.3 defines the loss; Section 4.3 reports that high‑importance tokens receive 1.6× more gradient mass. *Break condition:* Very large weight ranges (>5×) may cause gradient explosion; the paper’s [0.5,4.0] range appears safe but might need adjustment for other tasks.

**3. Directional validation via inversion:** To verify that the importance signal captures true causal structure, the authors invert the weights (using \(w_t \propto 1 - \hat{I}(s_k)\)). The expected ordering is Counterfactual > Uniform ≥ Random > Inverted. Performance degradation when the signal is reversed confirms that both magnitude and sign are meaningful. Evidence: Section 4.2 shows Inverted underperforms Uniform and Counterfactual across models (e.g., 81.9 vs 83.4 on Qwen3‑1.7B). *Break condition:* Ambiguous tasks or extremely sparse rewards may yield a weak inversion effect, making it a less reliable diagnostic.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Span detector (regex) → Counterfactual estimator (masked forward passes) → Weight normalizer (Eqs. 6–7) → Loss wrapper (injects per‑token weight into DAPO loss) [Optional: cache for weight reuse]

**Critical path:** For each batch:  
1. Generate completions.  
2. Detect reasoning spans in each completion.  
3. For each span, perform a masked forward pass (no_grad) to compute answer log‑probability; compute drop \(D(s_k)\).  
4. Aggregate drops into per‑token weights \(w_t\) via normalization and mapping to \([w_{\min}, w_{\max}]\).  
5. Compute the weighted CF‑DAPO loss.  
6. Backward pass.  
Counterfactual forward passes dominate runtime and should be done after generation but before loss computation.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Span detection patterns: broader patterns increase recall but may include non‑reasoning text; precise patterns risk missing critical steps.  
- Weight range \([w_{\min}, w_{\max}]\) (default [0.5,4.0]): larger range focuses more on important tokens but risks gradient explosion.  
- Placeholder token (pad vs. EOS): using an out‑of‑distribution token can bias drop estimates; a neutral token may be safer but requires model compatibility.  
- Caching: saves recomputation across epochs at memory cost.  
- Batching masked passes improves GPU utilization but requires careful padding for variable span lengths.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Validation accuracy does not improve over DAPO after several steps.  
- Importance weights are nearly uniform (~1), indicating detection or drop computation failed.  
- High variance across random seeds.  
- Inverted baseline outperforms Counterfactual, suggesting a bug in inversion logic or weight application.  
Monitor weight distribution and gradient concentration ratio (Section 4.3) for early detection.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Transfer to code generation and other domains:** The paper reports no benefit on MBPP+ and hypothesizes that code correctness lacks a concentrated answer sink. It remains unknown whether domain‑specific span detection (e.g., AST nodes, control‑flow blocks) could make the method effective for code or other distributed‑correctness tasks. Evidence to resolve: implement code‑specific detectors and evaluate on HumanEval or MBPP+.

2. **Reducing computational overhead:** The counterfactual forward passes add 32–74% per‑step overhead. Proposed optimizations (batched CF passes, proxy models, sparse span selection, caching) are speculative or partially studied. A comprehensive ablation combining all optimizations is needed to quantify real‑world speed‑accuracy trade‑offs.

3. **Distractor spans:** The analysis identifies a small fraction (3.5%) of spans with positive counterfactual drops (masking improves answer probability), often verbose headers or redundant restatements. Uniform weighting would incorrectly reinforce these distractors. It is untested whether filtering them or downweighting them in training improves baseline uniform‑credit performance.

4. **Comparison with alternative credit assignment signals:** The paper positions counterfactual importance as a non‑parametric alternative to process reward models (PRMs), Shapley values, and intra‑trajectory advantage estimates. However, no empirical or theoretical comparison exists. Evidence to resolve: compute multiple signals on the same completions, measure pairwise correlation and predictive power for answer correctness, and test hybrid losses.

## Limitations
- Span detection regex patterns are not fully disclosed; different implementations may yield divergent importance signals.
- The method incurs substantial computational overhead (32–74% per‑step), which may limit scalability to large models or high‑throughput training.
- Benefits appear restricted to math reasoning; no improvement on MBPP+ suggests limited generalizability to tasks without a concentrated answer signal.

## Confidence
- **Causal importance estimation (via masked drop):** High (consistent gains across three model‑dataset pairs).
- **Gradient upweighting improves accuracy and convergence:** High (reproducible +0.8–1.1 pp over DAPO).
- **Inversion validation of directional signal:** Medium (effect present but modest; could be confounded by regularization).
- **Domain specificity (math reasoning benefit, code limited):** Medium (single negative result on MBPP+; needs broader task validation).

## Next Checks
1. **Span detection robustness:** Replace the paper’s regex with an LLM‑based annotator and alternative pattern sets; compute correlation of resulting importance scores with human‑annotated critical steps on a held sample.
2. **Generalization to other reasoning tasks:** Apply CF‑DAPO to TheoremQA, CommonsenseQA, or proof‑generation benchmarks to verify whether accuracy gains replicate outside arithmetic reasoning.
3. **Placeholder sensitivity:** Ablate the masking token (pad, EOS, random token, dropout) and measure variance of importance estimates and final accuracy; if variance is high, adopt a distribution‑matched placeholder strategy.
