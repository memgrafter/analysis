# iMiGUE-Speech: A Spontaneous Speech Dataset for Affective Analysis

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.21464
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21464
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Winning athletes show higher valence (0.536 vs. 0.503) and dominance (0.434 vs. 0.414), lower sadness (64.7% vs. 85.2%), and more positive transcript sentiment (95.4% vs. 87.1%) compared to losers.

## Executive Summary
iMiGUE-Speech extends the iMiGUE video corpus of tennis post-match interviews with automatically extracted audio, transcripts, speaker role separation, and word-level alignments. The pipeline uses speaker diarization, Whisper transcription, and Montreal Forced Aligner, identifying athlete segments via the heuristic of longest cumulative speaking time. Benchmark tasks on affective dimensions and categorical emotions reveal systematic differences between winners and losers, demonstrating the dataset's potential for studying spontaneous affective speech.

## Method Summary
The core processing pipeline begins with raw interview audio. Speaker diarization splits the audio into speaker-homogeneous segments. Whisper transcribes each segment, producing text and coarse timestamps. Montreal Forced Aligner then generates precise word-level alignments. Athlete speech is isolated by selecting the speaker with the longest cumulative duration, based on the assumption that athletes dominate post-match interviews. The resulting aligned speech-text segments are used to extract affective features for regression on valence/arousal/dominance and classification of categorical emotions, with labels derived from match outcomes.

## Key Results
- **Dimensional emotion:** Winners have higher valence (0.536) and dominance (0.434) than losers (0.503, 0.414).
- **Categorical emotion:** Winners exhibit a lower proportion of dominant sadness (64.7%) compared to losers (85.2%).
- **Transcript sentiment:** Winners' transcripts are more frequently positive (95.4%) than losers' (87.1%).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The pipeline effectively leverages state-of-the-art automatic speech processing to obtain high-quality, word-aligned speech from unscripted interviews. The heuristic that the athlete is the longest cumulative speaker holds in typical post-match contexts, where athletes are encouraged to reflect at length. This automatic role assignment, despite lacking manual verification, yields segments reliably linked to match outcomes, enabling affective contrast without costly annotation.

## Foundational Learning
- **Speaker diarization**  
  *Why needed:* Separates overlapping speech and assigns segments to distinct speakers, a prerequisite for role-specific analysis.  
  *Quick check:* Compute diarization error rate (DER) on a small labeled sample; low DER indicates reliable separation.
- **Forced alignment**  
  *Why needed:* Provides precise word-level timestamps, aligning audio with transcripts for fine-grained feature extraction and potential multimodal integration.  
  *Quick check:* Inspect alignment of a few words by listening; mismatches suggest poor model or audio quality.
- **Transfer learning with pre-trained models**  
  *Why needed:* Allows leveraging large corpora to train affective models even when target data is limited, as in iMiGUE-Speech.  
  *Quick check:* Evaluate a model fine-tuned on a source corpus on a held-out portion of iMiGUE-Speech to measure transfer effectiveness.
- **Heuristic role assignment**  
  *Why needed:* Eliminates need for manual speaker labeling, enabling large-scale processing.  
  *Quick check:* Manually annotate speaker roles for a random subset and verify that the longest speaker is indeed the athlete in most cases.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Audio input -> Speaker diarization -> Speech segments -> Whisper ASR -> Montreal Forced Aligner -> Word-level alignments -> Athlete selection (cumulative speaking time) -> Affective feature extraction -> Model training/evaluation.

**Critical path:** Diarization must correctly separate speakers; transcription and forced alignment must produce accurate text and timestamps; athlete selection heuristic must correctly identify the athlete. Failure at any step propagates to affective labels.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Off-the-shelf Whisper vs. domain-adapted ASR: better out-of-the-box performance but potential mismatch with spontaneous speech.  
- Heuristic athlete selection vs. manual annotation: scalable but introduces possible label noise if the assumption breaks.  
- Forced alignment vs. ASR timestamps: higher precision at cost of additional computation and potential alignment errors on noisy speech.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High diarization error rate (DER) resulting in mixed speaker segments.  
- High word error rate (WER) in transcripts degrading text-based features.  
- Athlete misidentified (e.g., when interviewer speaks longer).  
- Large jitter in word-level timestamps affecting multimodal alignment.

**Three first experiments:**  
1. Reproduce the benchmark results: train the reported baseline models (e.g., from MSP-Podcast) on iMiGUE-Speech and report win/lose classification accuracy.  
2. Ablate the athlete selection step: manually correct a small subset of athlete assignments and quantify the change in affective differences.  
3. Compare transfer learning: fine-tune pre-trained speech emotion models on iMiGUE-Speech versus training from scratch, measuring performance and sample efficiency.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How reliable is the heuristic of identifying the athlete as the longest cumulative speaker in iMiGUE-Speech, and what is the impact of potential misidentification on affective analysis?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The data processing pipeline assumes the athlete speaks the longest (Section 2.2), but this is not validated.
- **Why unresolved:** Errors in speaker diarization or atypical interview structures could lead to incorrect role assignment, affecting the ground-truth affective labels linked to match outcomes.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Manual verification of speaker roles on a validation set; simulating misidentification errors to measure sensitivity of win/lose classification accuracy.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Do pre-trained speech and language models transfer effectively to the spontaneous domain of iMiGUE-Speech, or is domain adaptation necessary for accurate affective recognition?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Benchmarks use models fine-tuned on MSP-Podcast and other corpora (Section 4.1, 4.2), implying transfer learning without testing in-domain training.
- **Why unresolved:** Spontaneous speech characteristics (disfluencies, noise) may cause a domain shift, reducing model performance; this is not investigated.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Compare transfer learning with training from scratch on iMiGUE-Speech; analyze performance drops and error patterns to identify domain-specific challenges.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How do speech-based affective signals correlate with micro-gestures in iMiGUE-Speech, and does multimodal fusion enhance emotion recognition compared to unimodal approaches?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The abstract and Section 3.3 mention the dataset can be paired with micro-gestures for multimodal study, but benchmarks only use speech and text.
- **Why unresolved:** The multimodal potential is stated but not empirically validated; the complementarity of speech and gesture in this spontaneous context is unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Extract gesture features from iMiGUE annotations, align with speech using word-level timings, train multimodal models, and evaluate on emotion recognition tasks; analyze temporal synchrony.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What are the temporal dynamics of affect within individual interviews, and how do intra-session fluctuations differ between winning and losing athletes?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper computes intra-variability (Table 2) but aggregates segments for session-level analysis, missing fine-grained temporal patterns.
- **Why unresolved:** Affective states may evolve during an interview due to questions or reflections; aggregated profiles might mask important micro-changes.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Model segment-level predictions over time per session; correlate with question types or interview structure; use sequence models to capture dynamics.

## Limitations
- Audio processing validation missing: diarization, transcription, and forced alignment errors are not quantified.
- Emotion annotation reliability unknown: likely using automatic models; no human validation or inter-rater agreement reported.
- Statistical inference absent: reported differences lack significance testing and confidence intervals.

## Confidence
- **Dataset pipeline reliability:** Medium (standard components but output quality unverified).
- **Emotional difference claims:** Low (descriptive statistics only, no inferential statistics).
- **Generalizability to other domains:** Low (dataset limited to tennis post-match interviews).
