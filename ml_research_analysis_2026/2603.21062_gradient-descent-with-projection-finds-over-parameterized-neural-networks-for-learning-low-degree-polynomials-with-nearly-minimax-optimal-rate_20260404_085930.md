# Gradient Descent with Projection Finds Over-Parameterized Neural Networks for Learning Low-Degree Polynomials with Nearly Minimax Optimal Rate

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.21062
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21062
- **Reference count:** 16
- **Primary result:** Gradient Descent with Projection (GDP) with an over‑parameterized two‑layer ReLU network (augmented) attains regression risk $\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\delta) \cdot d^{k_0}/n)$ with probability $1-\delta$, achieving nearly minimax optimal sample complexity $n = \Theta(\log(1/\delta) \cdot d^{k_0}/\varepsilon)$ and improving over prior bounds.

## Executive Summary
The paper studies non‑parametric regression of a target function that is a low‑degree spherical polynomial on $S^{d-1}$. It introduces Gradient Descent with Projection (GDP) for over‑parameterized two‑layer ReLU networks equipped with an augmented feature to guarantee a positive‑definite Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK). GDP projects gradient updates onto the subspace spanned by the top eigenvectors of the empirical NTK corresponding to the true polynomial degree $k_0$. Under suitable over‑parameterization and step‑size conditions, GDP achieves a risk bound $\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\delta) \cdot d^{k_0}/n)$ with high probability, matching the minimax lower bound up to a logarithmic factor and improving over previous results. An adaptive algorithm identifies $k_0$ when it is unknown and attains the same near‑optimal rate. The analysis reveals that the projection mechanism enables feature learning beyond the standard NTK regime.

## Method Summary
The method uses a two‑layer ReLU network with an augmented linear head (driven by the initial random binary mask) to ensure a strictly positive‑definite NTK. Training proceeds via Gradient Descent with Projection (GDP): at each step, the gradient is projected onto the top $r_0 = m_{k_0}$ eigenspace of the empirical NTK Gram matrix (where $m_{k_0} = \Theta(d^{k_0})$ is the number of spherical harmonics of degree $\le k_0$). The projection forces the learned function to lie in a low‑dimensional, signal‑capturing subspace of the RKHS. For unknown $k_0$, an adaptive procedure runs GDP for candidate degrees $\ell$ from a large $L$ downwards, computes the scaled empirical loss $\mathcal{E}_\ell / \mu_{\ell+1}$, and stops when it drops below a threshold, which theory shows occurs exactly at $\ell = k_0$ under a minimum coefficient condition. The theoretical guarantees require network width $m \gtrsim (n/d^{k_0})^{25/2} d^{5/2}$ and iteration count $T \asymp n/d^{k_0}$.

## Key Results
- Achieves risk bound $\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\delta) \cdot d^{k_0}/n)$ with probability $1-\delta$, yielding sample complexity $n = \Theta(\log(1/\delta) \cdot d^{k_0}/\varepsilon)$ to reach risk $\varepsilon$, which is nearly minimax optimal (up to a log factor) and improves over prior $\Theta(d^{k_0} \max\{\varepsilon^{-2}, \log d\})$.
- Provides an adaptive algorithm that recovers the true degree $k_0$ and attains the same near‑optimal rate without prior knowledge of $k_0$.
- Theoretical analysis reveals that the augmented network exhibits feature learning beyond the standard NTK regime due to the projection mechanism.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Projecting the gradient updates onto the top eigenvectors of the Empirical NTK, corresponding to the true polynomial degree's eigenspace, forces the learned function to live in a low‑dimensional, signal‑capturing subspace of the RKHS.
- **Mechanism:** During training with GDP (Algorithm 1), the weight update projects the gradient $Z_S(t) P(r_0) u(t)$ using $P(r_0)=U \Sigma^{(r_0)} U^T$, where $U$ contains the top $r_0$ eigenvectors of the NTK Gram matrix. This projection mathematically confines the evolving weight vector $\text{vec}(W(t))$ to a trajectory that, in function space, approximates the target $f^*$ within the $r_0$‑dimensional subspace $\mathcal{H}_K(B_h) \cap \mathcal{H}_{S,r_0}$ (Theorem 4.2). The subspace aligns with the eigenspaces containing the target's spherical harmonic components.
- **Core assumption:** The target function $f^*$ is a degree‑$k_0$ spherical polynomial, so its RKHS representation is supported entirely on the first $m_{k_0}$ (where $m_{k_0}= \Theta(d^{k_0})$) eigenspaces of the NTK operator (Theorem B.2).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "projection operator which ensures that the learned neural network function lies in a low‑dimensional subspace of the RKHS associated with the NTK for a nearly optimal regression risk bound"
  - [section 4.5] "The novel projection operator $P(r_0)$ in GDP ensures that the neural network function almost lies in a $r_0$‑dimensional subspace of the RKHS $\mathcal{H}_K(\gamma_0)$ with $r_0=\Theta(d^{k_0})$"
- **Break condition:** If the eigenvalue gap $\mu_{k_0} - \mu_{k_0+1}$ is too small (e.g., for large $d$ or near higher‑degree transitions), the top $r_0$ eigenvectors become numerically unstable or the subspace no longer efficiently captures the signal. The theoretical bound in Lemma C.11 relies on this gap.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** An adaptive procedure (Algorithm 2) can identify the unknown true degree $k_0$ by monitoring the decay of empirical training loss scaled by the inverse eigenvalue $\mu_{\ell+1}$, triggering when the loss projection indicates sufficient alignment with the $\ell$‑degree subspace.
- **Mechanism:** For a candidate degree $\ell$ (starting from a large $L$ downwards), Algorithm 2 runs GDP with projection dimension $r=m_\ell$. It compares the final training loss $\mathcal{E}_\ell$ (empirical risk) against the scaled quantity $\mathcal{E}_\ell / \mu_{\ell+1}$. Theoretical guarantees (Theorem 3.2) show that if $\ell < k_0$, the scaled loss remains *above* a threshold $\beta_0^2/4$ (indicating the target function's residue outside the $\ell$‑subspace is still large). At $\ell = k_0$, the scaled loss drops *below* $\beta_0^2/8$ (indicating the residue is small enough). This creates a reliable "kink" to detect $k_0$.
- **Core assumption:** The target coefficients satisfy a minimum magnitude condition $\min_{\ell,j} |a_{\ell j}|/\sqrt{\mu_\ell} \ge \beta_0 > 0$ (Section 3.2). This ensures the smallest significant component of $f^*$ is detectable above the noise floor after projection.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "novel and provable adaptive degree selection algorithm which identifies the true degree $k_0$"
  - [section 3.2] "If $\ell$ is the first integer such that $\mathcal{E}_{\ell-1}/\mu_\ell \ge \beta_0^2/4$ and $\mathcal{E}_\ell/\mu_{\ell+1} \le \beta_0^2/8$... then $\ell=k_0$"
- **Break condition:** If the smallest non‑zero coefficient $\min |a_{k_0,j}|$ is too small relative to the noise level $\sigma_0$, the scaled loss $\mathcal{E}_\ell / \mu_{\ell+1}$ may not produce a clear drop, causing missed detection or false positives.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The network's "augmented feature" (the extra linear head using the initialization binary mask) ensures the NTK $K$ is strictly positive definite, so all eigenvalues $\mu_\ell$ decay smoothly as $\Theta(d^{-\ell})$, avoiding the spectral gaps (odd‑degree zero eigenvalues) that cripple standard ReLU NTKs for odd‑degree polynomials.
- **Mechanism:** The NTK of a standard two‑layer ReLU network has zero eigenvalues for all odd degrees $\ge 3$ (Bietti & Mairal, 2019). The augmented feature $F(W^{(0)},x)$ adds a linear component that couples across all degrees in the Taylor expansion, filling these spectral gaps. This makes the NTK's integral operator have an eigenbasis of *all* spherical harmonics (Theorem C.18), with $\mu_\ell = \Theta(d^{-\ell})$ for small $\ell$.
- **Core assumption:** The augmented feature is constructed exactly as in (1) using the initial random binary mask $F(W^{(0)},x)$. The NTK analysis treats the second‑layer weights $a$ as constant.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 2.1] "augmented feature... ensures that the NTK... is a PSD kernel $K$, and all of its eigenvalues are strictly positive"
  - [section C.4] Theorem C.18 gives the explicit eigenvalue decay $\mu_k = \Theta(d^{-k})$.
- **Break condition:** The analysis relies on the random initialization $W^{(0)}$ and the symmetric pairing ($w_{2r}, w_{2r-1}$). If initialization is biased or the augmentation is altered, the spectral gap‑filling property may vanish, reverting to the problematic NTK spectrum.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Spherical Harmonics
- **Why needed here:** They form an orthogonal basis for polynomials on the sphere. The target $f^*$ and the NTK eigenfunctions $Y_{\ell j}$ live in this basis. Projection onto the $k_0$‑degree subspace means keeping coefficients for $\ell \le k_0$ in the harmonic expansion (Section B).
- **Quick check question:** Given a polynomial $p(x) = x_1^2 - x_2^2 + x_3$ on $S^2$, what are its spherical harmonic degrees? Answer: Degree‑2 (harmonic) plus degree‑1 (harmonic). $k_0=2$.

### Concept: Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)
- **Why needed here:** The NTK $K$ defines the RKHS $\mathcal{H}_K$ that contains the network's features in the infinite‑width limit. The projection in GDP manipulates the network's function to lie in a small *subspace* of this RKHS. The eigenvalue decay $\mu_\ell$ governs the "dimension" $r_0 = m_{k_0} = \Theta(d^{k_0})$ needed to represent $f^*$.
- **Quick check question:** If the NTK eigenvalues decay as $\mu_\ell \sim d^{-\ell}$, what is the effective rank $r$ to capture all polynomials of degree $\le k_0$? Answer: $r = \sum_{\ell=0}^{k_0} N(d,\ell) \sim d^{k_0}$.

### Concept: Rademacher Complexity & Localization
- **Why needed here:** Used to convert the decomposition $f = h + e$ (Theorem 4.2) into a sharp risk bound (Theorem 4.3). The local Rademacher complexity approach (Bartlett et al., 2005) tightens the bound by focusing on functions near the truth, yielding the $\sim\Theta(d^{k_0}/n)$ rate instead of a worse $\sqrt{d^{k_0}/n}$ or worse.
- **Quick check question:** How does local Rademacher complexity improve over standard uniform bounds for this setting? Answer: It exploits that the excess risk is concentrated on a small $r_0$‑dimensional subspace, giving a bound proportional to $\sqrt{r_0/n}$ not $\sqrt{(\text{total dim})/n}$.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
The system has four key interacting parts:
1. Two‑layer NN with Augmented Feature (Eq 1): First layer weights $W$ (trained), second layer $a$ (random $\pm1$ fixed), augmented linear head $w_{m+1}$ (trained from 0).
2. Gradient Descent with Projection (GDP) (Alg 1, Eq 8): GD step with learning rate $\eta$, but gradient projected via $P^{(r_0)}=U \Sigma^{(r_0)} U^T$ onto top $r_0$ NTK eigenspace.
3. NTK Eigendecomposition: Computing $U$ (top $r_0$ eigenvectors of $K_n$) requires full Gram matrix at each projection? Actually, $U$ is fixed from the *empirical* NTK $K_n$ on the training set. In practice, one would compute $K_n$ upfront, do eigendecomposition, then run GDP with fixed $P^{(r_0)}$.
4. Adaptive Degree Selector (Alg 2): Loop over candidate degrees $\ell$ from $L$ down, running GDP with $r=m_\ell$ and checking scaled training loss $\mathcal{E}_\ell/\mu_{\ell+1}$.

### Critical path
1. Data & NTK setup: Generate training data $(x_i, y_i)$. Compute empirical NTK $K_n$ on features $S$. Perform eigendecomposition $K_n = U \Sigma U^T$. Obtain $U^{(r_0)}$.
2. Network init: Randomly initialize $W^{(0)}$, $a$, set $w_{m+1}^{(0)}=0$.
3. GDP loop: For $t=1..T$:
   - Compute network outputs $f^{(t)}(x_i)$
   - Compute residual $u^{(t)} = f^{(t)}(S) - y$
   - Compute gradient $\Delta W = -\frac{\eta}{n} Z^{(t)} P^{(r_0)} u^{(t)}$ (requires forming $Z^{(t)}$ from neuron activations)
   - Update first‑layer weights $W$ and augmented feature weights $w_{m+1}$.
4. (For adaptive) After $T_\ell = n/d_\ell$ steps for candidate $\ell$, compute empirical loss $\mathcal{E}_\ell$. Compare $\mathcal{E}_\ell/\mu_{\ell+1}$ to thresholds to select $k_0^{\text{hat}}$.
5. Evaluation: Compute final risk $\mathbb{E}_P[(f_T - f^*)^2]$.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Projection dimension $r_0$ vs. $d, k_0$**: Must set $r_0 = m_{k_0}$ exactly to align with true degree. Too small $r_0$ loses signal; too large $r_0$ (e.g., using $k_0+1$) incorporates irrelevant higher‑degree subspace which hurts risk due to noise (Theorem 4.4's $\|P_{\text{Weaker}}(f^*)\|^2$ term). The adaptive algorithm searches over $\ell$.
- **Network width $m$ vs. $w$**: Theorem 4.2 requires $m \gtrsim T^{25/2} d^{5/2} / w^5$ to bound the approximation error $e_t$. Larger $m$ reduces $w$ (L∞ error), improving final risk, but increases computational cost.
- **Learning rate $\eta$**: Must be $\Theta(1)$ and small enough ($\eta<1$) to keep weights stable (Lemma C.4), but large enough for convergence $T \asymp n/d^{k_0}$. There's a stability‑optimization tradeoff captured by the constant $c_u$.
- **Early stopping $T$**: Must scale as $T \asymp n/d^{k_0}$ (Theorem 3.1). Under‑running leads to underfitting ($\gamma_0^2/(\eta T)$ term in Theorem 4.4 large); over‑running risks instability/overfitting? The projection helps stability, but excessive $T$ increases the bound on $\|e_t\|_\infty$.

### Failure signatures
1. **Projection misalignment**: If the empirical NTK $K_n$ has poor spectral gap ($\mu_{k_0}$ close to $\mu_{k_0+1}$), the top $r_0$ subspace might not well contain $f^*$. Symptom: $\|P_{U^{(-r_0)}}(f^*(S))\|_2^2$ is large (Lemma C.11), leading to risk plateauing above $\Theta(d^{k_0}/n)$.
2. **Insufficient width**: If $m$ is too small, $w$ from (60) remains large ($w \gtrsim d^{k_0}/n$), causing the $+w$ term in (36) to dominate, yielding risk $\Omega(1)$ or $\Omega(p(d^{k_0}/n))$.
3. **Adaptive algorithm failure**: If the minimum coefficient condition fails ($\min |a_{\ell j}|/\sqrt{\mu_\ell}$ is very small), the scaled loss $\mathcal{E}_\ell / \mu_{\ell+1}$ may never drop below $\beta_0^2/8$ at $\ell=k_0$, causing the loop to return $\ell=L$ incorrectly.
4. **Numerical instability in $P^{(r_0)}$**: For large $d$, computing the full eigendecomposition of $K_n$ ($n\times n$) is expensive. Approximating $U^{(r_0)}$ (e.g., via iterative methods) inaccurately can make $P^{(r_0)}$ not truly a projection onto the top eigenspace, breaking the theoretical guarantee.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Convergence of GDP with projection**: Train a fixed‑width network ($m$ large, $k_0$ known) on synthetic data for various $n$. Plot training loss and risk $\mathbb{E}_P[(f_T-f^*)^2]$ vs. $n$. Verify it decays as $\sim d^{k_0}/n$ (log‑$d$ factor), matching Theorem 3.1. Control: vanilla GD without projection should decay slower (e.g., $\sim \sqrt{d^{k_0}/n}$).
2. **Sensitivity to projection dimension $r_0$**: For fixed $n, d, k_0$, train with GDP using $r = m_\ell$ for $\ell = k_0-1, k_0, k_0+1$. Measure final risk. Expect optimal at $\ell=k_0$, worse (higher risk) for $\ell<k_0$ (missing signal) and $\ell>k_0$ (extra subspace adds noise).
3. **Adaptive algorithm validation**: Test Algorithm 2 on data with varying $k_0$ (e.g., $k_0=1,2,3$). Check if it correctly identifies $k_0$ with high probability for $n = \Theta(d^{k_0} \log(1/\delta))$. Vary $n$ to see phase transition in correct detection rate. Also, compare final risk of the selected $k_0^{\text{hat}}$ network vs. the oracle network using $r_0=m_{k_0}$—they should match (Theorem 3.2).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Is the minimum coefficient magnitude condition ($\min_{\ell,j} |a_{\ell j}|/\sqrt{\mu_\ell} \ge \beta_0$) necessary for the adaptive degree selection algorithm, or is it an artifact of the GDP approach?
- **Basis in paper:** Theorem 3.2 explicitly requires this condition for the adaptive algorithm to succeed; the authors note its similarity to conditions in compressive sensing but do not prove necessity.
- **Why unresolved:** No lower bound is provided to show that adaptive recovery is impossible without such a condition. The condition may be specific to the projected gradient method rather than fundamental.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Either a theoretical impossibility result demonstrating that any algorithm fails with some instance violating the condition, or an alternative algorithm achieving the same near‑optimal rate without requiring it.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can the logarithmic factor in the risk bound $\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\delta)\cdot d^{k_0}/n)$ be eliminated to achieve exact minimax optimality $\Theta(d^{k_0}/n)$?
- **Basis in paper:** Theorem 3.1 yields a “nearly minimax optimal” rate with a $\log(4/\delta)$ factor, while the cited minimax lower bound has no logarithmic term.
- **Why unresolved:** The log factor arises from the proof (e.g., Rademacher complexity and union bounds) and may be an artifact of the analysis. It is unclear whether it reflects a fundamental limitation.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A matching lower bound that demonstrates a logarithmic factor is necessary for any algorithm, or a refined analysis that removes the log factor while maintaining the same probability guarantee.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Does the GDP approach achieve the same near‑optimal rate under non‑uniform data distributions on the sphere (or on other manifolds)?
- **Basis in paper:** The entire analysis assumes that the training features are i.i.d. from the uniform distribution on the sphere; the harmonic analysis in Appendix B relies on this assumption.
- **Why unresolved:** For non‑uniform data the kernel eigenfunctions are not spherical harmonics, and the low‑dimensional subspace used by projection may no longer align with the target polynomial’s span. Generalizations are not studied.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A theoretical extension of the uniform convergence and RKHS decomposition to distributions with a density bounded above and below (e.g., strongly log‑concave), together with a proof that the relevant eigenspace still has dimension $\Theta(d^{k_0})$ and that GDP can identify it.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Can GDP be generalized to deep neural networks (more than two layers) while preserving the near‑minimax rate for learning low‑degree polynomials?
- **Basis in paper:** The results are restricted to two‑layer networks; deep networks have richer NTK structures and feature learning dynamics that might require a different projection design.
- **Why unresolved:** The projection operator in GDP is defined via the two‑layer NTK; for deep networks the kernel is more complex and the analysis techniques (e.g., spherical harmonics) may not apply directly.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A rigorous analysis of a projected gradient descent variant for $L$‑layer networks, showing that the learner attains risk $\mathcal{O}(d^{k_0}/n)$ (up to log factors) for low‑degree target polynomials.

## Limitations
- Over‑parameterization requirements are extreme: $m \gtrsim (n/d^{k_0})^{25/2} d^{5/2}$ and $T \asymp n/d^{k_0}$, likely an artifact of loose analysis rather than fundamental necessity.
- The positive‑definite NTK hinges on the precise augmented feature construction and symmetric initialization; any deviation may reintroduce zero eigenvalues and break the eigenvalue decay guarantee.
- Adaptive degree selection assumes a uniform lower bound on normalized harmonic coefficients, which is not automatic and may fail under moderate noise.
- The theory is confined to uniform spherical data and two‑layer networks; extensions to other distributions or deeper architectures remain open.

## Confidence
- **Near‑minimax rate for known $k_0$ (Theorem 3.1):** High
- **Adaptive degree selection and matching rate (Theorem 3.2):** Medium (relies on minimum‑coefficient condition)
- **Positive‑definite NTK with eigenvalue decay (Theorem C.18):** High
- **Projection confines learning to a low‑dimensional subspace (Theorem 4.2):** High
