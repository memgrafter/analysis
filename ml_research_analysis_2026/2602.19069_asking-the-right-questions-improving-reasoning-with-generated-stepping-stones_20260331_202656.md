# Asking the Right Questions: Improving Reasoning with Generated Stepping Stones

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.19069
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19069
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: ARQ framework improves LLM reasoning on challenging math problems by up to 13% using generated stepping stones.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces ARQ (Asking the Right Questions), a framework that enhances LLM reasoning by generating intermediate stepping stone questions. These subproblems are solved first, and their solutions provide contextual guidance for tackling the original complex problem. Experiments on AIME and BeyondAIME math benchmarks show that high-quality stepping stones can boost solver performance by up to 13%. The benefits transfer across different solver models, and post-training the generator on synthetic data yields additional gains (~3%). The work demonstrates that learning to generate useful questions is a viable path to improving test-time reasoning.

## Method Summary
ARQ operates in two phases: inference-time scaffolding and optional generator post-training. During inference, a generator φ produces a stepping stone z for target problem x; a solver π first solves z, then uses (z, solution yz) as in-context examples to solve x. Stone quality is estimated by Monte Carlo rollout: score S(z, x) = average correctness over multiple samples of yz and y. For post-training, synthetic data is curated from a strong generator/solver pair: for each x, 20 candidate stones are generated and scored; the best stone forms a SFT dataset, and DPO pairs are created from top-3 vs bottom-3 stones with a score gap ≥0.25. The generator is fine-tuned via SFT then DPO to maximize the probability of producing stones that lead to solver success. Multi-stone variants sequentially generate additional stones conditioned on previous ones.

## Key Results
- Best stepping stones improve solver accuracy by up to 13% on AIME 2024/2025 and BeyondAIME.
- Improvements are transferable across solvers of varying strengths (within GPT family).
- Post-training generators (Qwen3-8B, Qwen2.5-32B) yields average gains of ~3%; sequential multi-stone generation adds further improvement.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Generating intermediate stepping stone questions that break down hard problems improves solver performance.
- **Mechanism**: ARQ introduces a separate question generator ϕ that creates subproblems (stepping stones) z∼ϕ(x). These stones are solved first (yz∼π(z)), and their solutions become in-context examples for solving the original problem (y∼π(x;z,yz)). The stepping stones serve as knowledge-building exercises that prepare the solver by simplifying the problem structure, providing practice on key methods, or exploring special cases.
- **Core assumption**: Some subproblems exist that are both easier to solve than the target problem AND provide useful guidance when their solutions are used as context for the target.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - Abstract: "good stepping stone questions exist and are transferrable, meaning that good questions can be generated, and they substantially help LLMs of various capabilities"
  - Section 4.2: "The best stones from ARQ low improve the performance by 5% over the solver only baseline, while the ones from ARQ high improve it by 13%."
  - Figure 4/Figure 9: Demonstrates that individual stones vary widely in quality; some scoring below baseline (detrimental) and some substantially above.
- **Break condition**: When generated stones are irrelevant, unsolvable, or misleading, they contaminate context and reduce performance below baseline, as observed in average results for weaker generators.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Good stepping stones are transferable across solvers of varying capabilities; their helpfulness does not depend on overfitting to a specific solver's biases.
- **Mechanism**: The value of a stepping stone lies in its intrinsic intellectual structure—capturing a key insight, simplifying constraints, or isolating a sub-technique. This structural value is independent of which model generates or solves it. When a solver encounters a high-quality stepping stone and its correct solution, it can extract reusable knowledge applicable to the original problem.
- **Core assumption**: The benefit of stepping stones generalizes beyond the specific model that generated them or solved them.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - Section 4.3: "Performance remains roughly the same regardless of whether the best stones are selected by the current solver itself or by a reference solver that has a different size and reasoning capacity."
  - Figure 5: Shows transferred stones perform comparably to stones selected by the target solver itself across multiple solver strengths.
  - Corpus: Limited direct support; corpus papers focus on question generation but not transferability across solvers as studied here.
- **Break condition**: If stepping stones only exploit solver-specific idiosyncrasies (e.g., formatting preferences, known failure modes), transfer would fail. The mechanism breaks when stones become too tailored to a particular model's behavior patterns rather than conveying generalizable mathematical insight.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: The ability to generate useful stepping stones can be improved through post-training on synthetic data curated via Monte Carlo scoring and preference learning.
- **Mechanism**: Frame stepping stone generation as a reinforcement task where reward = expected solver success on target problem conditioned on the stone. Score stones via S(z,x)=E[yz∼π(z),y∼π(z,yx)R(x,y)]. Train generator first via SFT on highest-scoring stones, then via DPO on pairs of stones with large score differences. This aligns the generator to produce stones that empirically lead to higher solver success.
- **Core assumption**: The proxy reward (solver success conditioned on stone) correlates with intrinsic helpfulness, and synthetic data from a strong generator/solver can teach weaker models this skill.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - Section 3.2: Explicitly defines the scoring function and synthetic curation pipeline.
  - Section 4.4: Post-trained Qwen3-8B improved by 3.1% and Qwen2.5-32B by 2.9% in average performance, showing learning occurred.
  - Section C.1: Details data curation: 918 target problems, 20 stones each, scored with 20 rollouts, top-3 vs bottom-3 pairs for DPO.
- **Break condition**: If the scoring function is noisy or misaligned (e.g., rewards stones that exploit solver flaws rather than true simplification), post-training may optimize for the wrong objective. Also fails if the distribution of synthetic stones does not match the test distribution or if the generator capacity is insufficient to learn from the signal.

## Foundational Learning
### Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Reasoning
- **Why needed**: ARQ assumes solvers that produce chain-of-thought traces. Stepping stones themselves are CoT-style problems, and the solver's CoT on the target problem conditions on the stepping stone solution. Understanding CoT is essential to grasp how intermediate reasoning is structured and consumed.
- **Quick check**: How does a chain-of-thought prompt differ from a direct-answer prompt, and why might providing an example CoT as context help with a hard problem?

### Test-Time Scaling (Inference-Time Computation)
- **Why needed**: ARQ is an inference-time scaffold that adds computation (generating and solving stepping stones) before final answer. It exemplifies test-time scaling where additional inference steps improve accuracy without changing model weights.
- **Quick check**: What is the difference between test-time scaling and train-time scaling? Why might a framework like ARQ be complementary to both?

### Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards
- **Why needed**: The post-training stage uses DPO on synthetic data where rewards come from solver success on math problems with deterministic correctness (via MathVerify). This is a form of RL with verifiable rewards.
- **Quick check**: In mathematical reasoning, what makes a reward "verifiable"? Why is this property crucial for scaling RL post-training without human annotation?

### Monte Carlo Estimation for Stone Scoring
- **Why needed**: Stone quality score S(z,x) is estimated via expected reward over Monte Carlo rollouts: sample multiple yz∼π(z) and y∼π(z,yx,x) and average R(x,y). This noisy estimate drives synthetic data curation.
- **Quick check**: Why use k=20 rollouts per stone instead of a single rollout? What tradeoff does this introduce between score reliability and compute cost?

### Preference Learning (DPO)
- **Why needed**: After SFT on high-quality stones, DPO fine-tunes the generator on pairs of stones with large score differences, directly optimizing for stones that lead to higher solver performance.
- **Quick check**: How does DPO differ from standard PPO-based RLHF? Why might DPO be preferable for this synthetic, offline-curated setting?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
φ (generator) → π (solver) → Scoring module → Synthetic data pipeline → Post-training module (SFT/DPO) → updates φ

### Critical path
1. **Inference**: Generate z∼φ(x) → solve stone yz∼π(z) → solve target y∼π(x;z,yz).
2. **Scoring for data curation**: For each (x,z), run k rollouts of (yz,y) pairs, compute average correctness.
3. **Dataset curation**: For each x, keep stone with highest S(z,x) for SFT. Create DPO pairs by comparing top-3 vs bottom-3 stones with ΔS≥0.25.
4. **Post-training**: SFT on {(x,z)} pairs → DPO on {(x,z+,z−)} pairs.
5. **Multi-stone extension**: Sequential: generate z1∼φ(x), then z2∼φ(x,z1), etc. Recursive: zi∼φ(zi−1) with z0=x; solve in reverse order.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Single-stone vs multi-stone**: More stones can yield additional gains but increase context length and risk of contamination. Sequential generalizes beyond post-training distribution; recursive is more speculative but may create deeper curricula.
- **Generator strength**: Generator needs sufficient reasoning to produce meaningful stones. May need generator capability ≥ solver capability? Not necessarily, as post-trained Qwen3-8B (8B) helps GPT-120B low (much larger).
- **Scoring budget**: k=20 rollouts per stone in paper. More rollouts → more reliable S(z,x) but higher compute.
- **Solver anchoring**: Solver used for scoring should be representative of target deployment solver; stones optimized for one solver may transfer but optimal stones likely depend on solver capability distribution.

### Failure signatures
- **Average performance drops below baseline**: Indicates many stones are detrimental; generator not reliably producing useful stones. Observed for GPT-120B low generator.
- **High variance in stone scores**: Some stones help a lot, some hurt. Without post-training, selecting best stones requires oracle access; real inference uses random stones → inconsistent gains.
- **Post-training failure to improve**: Model may not follow formatting or may not learn to produce stones that improve solver success.
- **Context contamination**: Wrong yz from unsolvable stone confuses solver. Evidenced by stones scoring below baseline.
- **Multi-stone degradation**: Recursive approach often underperforms sequential; sequential can fail if later stones don't build meaningfully on earlier ones.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Baseline comparison (Section 4.1)**: Run ARQ with off-the-shelf LLMs (GPT-120B low/high) on AIME2024, AIME2025, BeyondAIME vs baselines (Analogical, Least-to-Most). Goal: Establish that ARQ can help on hard problems when generator is sufficiently capable.
2. **Stone quality analysis (Section 4.2)**: For each target problem, generate 20 stones from ARQ low/high and Rand. Score each stone via rollouts. Plot individual stone scores and best-stone performance. Goal: Prove good stones exist and quality correlates with generator reasoning capability; also show random stones don't help.
3. **Transferability test (Section 4.3)**: Take best stones selected by reference solver (GPT-120B low/medium/high) and evaluate them on new target solvers (GPT-20B low/medium/high). Compare to stones selected by each target solver itself. Goal: Demonstrate that helpfulness is not solver-specific, validating that post-training data from one solver can benefit others.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **What defines a high-quality stepping stone question that consistently improves downstream task performance across diverse reasoning scenarios?** The paper demonstrates large variance in stone quality and only modest gains after post-training, indicating no clear definition or understanding of what makes a stone beneficial. A dataset of stones labeled by human experts or systematic ablation studies correlating structural features with performance gains would help.
2. **How can we design more reliable reward functions for stepping stone generation that do not rely on potentially noisy solver samples?** The paper acknowledges that the scoring treats sampled solutions as always correct, introducing bias if the solver samples are wrong. A more robust reward could incorporate verifiers or majority vote.
3. **What causes the observed performance degradation when generating stepping stones recursively, while sequential generation yields improvements?** Recursion may cause drift: stones become less relevant to the original problem or exceed solver capability. Analyzing properties of recursively generated stones and interventions (e.g., depth limits) could identify failure modes.
4. **To what extent do the benefits of stepping stones transfer across different model families and domains beyond mathematics?** Transferability was tested only within GPT family on math benchmarks. Cross-family and cross-domain experiments are needed to gauge generality.

## Limitations
- Undisclosed GPT‑120B and BeyondAIME details hinder exact replication.
- Stone scoring uses a fixed 20 rollouts; optimal rollout count unknown and may affect data quality.
- Transferability only demonstrated within GPT family; cross‑family generalization unclear.
- Post‑training improvements are modest (~3%), raising cost‑benefit questions.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1** (quality stones exist & help): High – best‑stone selection shows up to +13% on hard math.
- **Mechanism 2** (transfer across solvers): Medium – strong within‑family results, but not tested on other architectures.
- **Mechanism 3** (post‑training improves generator): Medium – gains observed on two models; scalability unknown.
