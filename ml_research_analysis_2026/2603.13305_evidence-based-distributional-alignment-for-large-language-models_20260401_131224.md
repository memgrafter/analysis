# Evidence-based Distributional Alignment for Large Language Models

## Quick Facts

- **arXiv:** 2603.13305  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13305  
- **Reference count:** 18  
- **Primary result:** Evi-DA reduces Jensen-Shannon divergence to 0.16 (in-domain) and 0.23 (out-of-domain), achieving up to 44% relative improvement over strong baselines.

## Executive Summary

The paper addresses instability in large language model (LLM) predictions of answer distributions under cultural and domain shifts. It introduces Evi-DA, a framework that leverages Welzel’s human values as an intermediate representation: for a given multiple‑choice question and target country, it retrieves related World Values Survey (WVS) items, predicts coarse value signatures (low/medium/high across eight dimensions) for each answer option, and infers the country‑conditioned answer distribution in a structured JSON format. A two‑stage reinforcement learning pipeline (GRPO) optimizes survey‑derived rewards to ensure accurate value signatures, distribution fidelity, and schema validity. Evaluations across six LLM backbones and seven baselines show Evi-DA lowers Jensen‑Shannon divergence, with average scores of 0.16 in‑domain and 0.23 out‑of‑domain, representing up to 44% relative improvement. Ablation studies confirm that both the Welzel value mapping and evidence retrieval are essential for performance.

## Method Summary

Evi‑DA predicts country‑specific response distributions by combining evidence from the World Values Survey with a two‑stage LLM pipeline. First, it builds an evidence bank from WVS data: for each country and item, it stores empirical answer distributions and option‑level Welzel value signatures (coarse LMH vectors). At inference, given a new question and target country, the system retrieves the top‑K semantically related WVS items that meet a minimum response threshold using a BERT encoder. Stage A of the LLM predicts an LMH signature for each option, conditioned on the target country and the retrieved exemplars. Stage B then generates a normalized probability distribution over the options, additionally conditioned on the predicted signatures. Training uses Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with four reward components: exact‑match accuracy of LMH signatures (R_LMH), 1 − Jensen‑Shannon divergence between predicted and gold distributions (R_dist), and binary rewards for valid JSON schema in both stages (R_schA, R_schB). The pipeline is evaluated on in‑domain (GlobalOpinionQA) and out‑of‑domain (2025 Pew/YouGov) benchmarks across multiple LLM backbones.

## Key Results

- Average Jensen‑Shannon divergence: 0.16 in‑domain, 0.23 out‑of‑domain.  
- Up to 44% relative improvement over baselines (e.g., model log‑probabilities).  
- Ablation confirms both Welzel value mapping and evidence retrieval are necessary; removing either degrades performance substantially.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

Evi‑DA’s robustness emerges from three interrelated mechanisms.

**1. Intermediate Welzel Value Signatures.**  
Mapping each answer option to a coarse (low/medium/high) profile across eight Welzel dimensions decouples semantic interpretation from demographic calibration. This abstraction reduces sensitivity to surface‑form wording and enables cross‑domain generalization. The core assumption is that the eight dimensions capture the relevant cross‑cultural variation and that LLMs can reliably assign LMH labels. Evidence includes the framework described in the abstract and Section 2.2. The mechanism fails when the question domain is unrelated to values (e.g., factual technical topics) or when the LLM mispredicts LMH signatures.

**2. Retrieval of Survey Evidence.**  
The system retrieves related WVS items for the target country, providing exemplars of how specific value signatures map to real answer distributions. These exemplars ground the final prediction in population‑level data. The mechanism relies on the retrieved items being semantically relevant and having sufficient response counts; retrieval is detailed in Section 2.4. If no sufficiently related items exist, or if they have low support, the evidence may mislead or be unavailable, undermining performance.

**3. Multi‑faceted Reinforcement Learning (GRPO).**  
A two‑stage RL pipeline optimizes a weighted combination of rewards: exact‑match LMH accuracy (R_LMH), distribution fidelity (1 − JSD, R_dist), and JSON schema validity (R_schA, R_schB). This shapes the LLM to produce semantically correct, accurate, and well‑structured outputs. The reward design is described in Section 2.6. However, GRPO can degrade performance for smaller models (e.g., Qwen3‑8B, Llama3‑8B) or with mis‑specified weights, indicating capacity limits and susceptibility to reward‑hacking.

## Foundational Learning

### Distributional Alignment (Pluralistic Alignment)
- **Why needed:** Predicting a distribution over answers for a demographic group captures uncertainty and disagreement inherent in population‑level opinions, in contrast to a single “best” answer.  
- **Quick check:** What is the difference between predicting a single “best” answer and predicting a distribution over answers for a given demographic group?

### Welzel’s Human Values as a Latent Space
- **Why needed:** Welzel’s eight dimensions provide a compact, stable coordinate system that abstracts away question‑specific wording, enabling cross‑domain generalization.  
- **Quick check:** How does discretizing continuous value scores into low/medium/high help, and why are eight specific sub‑indexes chosen?

### Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
- **Why needed:** GRPO allows joint optimization of multiple objectives (value prediction accuracy, distribution fidelity, schema validity) using automatically computed rewards from survey data.  
- **Quick check:** What do the four reward components (R_LMH, R_dist, R_schA, R_schB) correspond to, and why combine them?

## Architecture Onboarding

Component map:  
Evidence Bank Construction → Retrieval Module → Stage A (LMH Signature Prediction) → Stage B (Distribution Prediction) → GRPO Trainer (optional)

Critical path:
1. Receive question, options, and target country.  
2. Query evidence bank with BERT encoder; retrieve top‑K related WVS items satisfying support filter.  
3. Run LLM with Stage A prompt; parse JSON to obtain LMH signatures for each option; validate schema.  
4. Combine Stage A output with retrieved evidence in Stage B prompt; generate distribution JSON; validate normalization.  
5. Return the predicted distribution.

Design tradeoffs:
- **Structured JSON vs. free text:** Enforcing JSON enables automatic reward calculation but may constrain expressiveness and demand careful prompting.  
- **Discretization of Welzel values:** LMH categories simplify learning but discard fine‑grained variation; thresholds τ1=0.33, τ2=0.67 are heuristic.  
- **Retrieval encoder:** BERT‑base is used; larger encoders yield negligible gains but increase cost.  
- **Two‑stage vs. single‑stage:** Decomposition adds latency but improves interpretability and stability; direct prediction is simpler but more brittle.

Failure signatures:
- Invalid JSON or schema mismatch in Stage A/B outputs → triggers schema reward penalty; persistent failures indicate poor instruction following.  
- Low retrieval relevance (high cosine similarity but unrelated items) → evidence may mislead; monitor via manual inspection or a reranker.  
- Distribution not normalized (sum ≠ 1) → violates R_schB; suggests Phase B instruction weakness.  
- Performance drop on non‑Western countries → may reflect evidence bank bias or value‑mapping bias.  
- GRPO degrades performance on smaller models (7B–8B) → indicates limited capacity to optimize multi‑component rewards.

First 3 experiments:
1. **Zero‑shot benchmark:** Run Evi‑DA (no training) on in‑domain (GlobalOpinionQA) and out‑of‑domain (2025 Pew/YouGov) test sets; compute Jensen‑Shannon divergence vs. baselines (Table 1, 2).  
2. **Component ablation:** Systematically remove Welzel value mapping (predict distribution directly with only retrieval) and evidence retrieval (predict LMH without WVS); measure JSD changes (Table 3).  
3. **RL impact analysis:** Compare Evi‑DA with and without GRPO across multiple model sizes (Tables 1, 2); inspect per‑country performance (Table 4); evaluate reward component ablation to isolate contributions.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
**Question:** What is the acceptable error magnitude for distributional alignment outputs from the perspective of social science practitioners, and how should uncertainty be quantified and communicated for such probabilistic, group‑level estimates?  
**Basis in paper:** The Limitations section states that acceptability magnitude has not been addressed due to lack of expert annotations. The Ethical Statement calls for stronger uncertainty quantification.  
**Why unresolved:** The paper evaluates using Jensen‑Shannon divergence but does not establish thresholds for real‑world utility. Without expert judgment, it is unclear whether scores like 0.16 in‑domain are sufficient.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** A user study with social scientists evaluating the utility of Evi‑DA’s predicted distributions for tasks like survey design or policy stress‑testing, alongside calibrated uncertainty intervals.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** How transferable is the Welzel human values framework as an intermediate representation compared to other cultural value frameworks (e.g., Hofstede’s, Schwartz’s), and could a learned or hybrid representation improve generalization to unseen domains?  
**Basis in paper:** The paper commits to Welzel’s framework as compact and interpretable, noting in Future Work: “Future work will extend Evi‑DA to broader human‑values frameworks.” The Limitations state that other types of human values and survey datasets are not utilized.  
**Why unresolved:** The design choice of Welzel’s 8‑dimensional discretized space is presented as effective but not compared against alternatives. It is an assumption that this taxonomy optimally bridges semantics and distributions across diverse shifts.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** A controlled ablation replacing the Welzel mapping with another framework (e.g., Hofstede’s dimensions) or a learned embedding, while keeping the rest of the pipeline identical, and comparing performance on the same benchmarks.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** For smaller or less reasoning‑capable LLMs, why does reinforcement learning with GRPO degrade out‑of‑domain performance, and how can training objectives be adapted to improve robustness across model scales?  
**Basis in paper:** The Out‑of‑domain Evaluation section notes: “for Qwen3‑8B and Llama3‑8B, reinforcement learning degrades performance. We attribute this to their weaker reasoning ability, which reduces accuracy in LMH signature prediction and makes it harder to use the retrieved evidence effectively.” This is an inference, not fully investigated.  
**Why unresolved:** No diagnostic analysis is provided; it is unclear whether degradation is due to multi‑task reward structure, overfitting to in‑distribution WVS episodes, or RL instability.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Analysis of training curves for different model sizes, examining the individual reward components (R_LMH, R_dist, R_schA, R_schB). Experiments with adaptive reward weighting or simpler objectives (e.g., only R_dist) for smaller models could test if the multi‑component GRPO is the bottleneck.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** How does Evi‑DA perform when the retrieval mechanism fails to find semantically or value‑wise relevant WVS items for a completely novel question domain, and can the system gracefully degrade or self‑identify such low‑evidence cases?  
**Basis in paper:** The Retrieval Sensitivity Analysis varies K and encoder but does not evaluate scenarios where retrieved items are irrelevant. The framework assumes at least partially related items exist.  
**Why unresolved:** The pipeline’s success hinges on Stage A’s LMH prediction being guided by retrieved evidence. If retrieval yields poor exemplars, the model may produce a plausible but uncalibrated signature, leading to a spurious distribution. The paper does not measure retrieval failure rates or correlate them with final JSD.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** An error analysis correlating retrieval similarity scores (or human topical relevance judgments) with final distribution performance. Introducing a “retrieval confidence” signal to down‑weight evidence when relevance is low could be tested.

### Open Question 5
**Question:** To what extent does the country‑level demographic conditioning in Evi‑DA obscure within‑country heterogeneity (e.g., regional, socioeconomic, ethnic subpopulations), and could finer‑grained conditioning improve distribution fidelity while managing privacy and bias risks?  
**Basis in paper:** The Ethical Statement acknowledges overfitting to survey operationalizations and compression of within‑country heterogeneity into a single country label, which can erase important variation.  
**Why unresolved:** The evaluation treats country as the sole demographic group, following the benchmark structure. Real populations are not monolithic. The ethical risks are noted but not measured in terms of predictive performance loss for subpopulations.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Using survey datasets with richer demographic metadata (WVS includes age, gender, education, region), evaluate Evi‑DA conditioned on country + one additional attribute versus country alone. This would quantify fidelity gains from finer conditioning and reveal any data sparsity issues for smaller subgroups.

## Limitations

- Welzel index construction details (especially Appendix A) are not fully described, hindering faithful reproduction.  
- Retrieval support threshold `N_min` and exact out‑of‑domain test set composition (2025 Pew/YouGov questions) are unreported, limiting external validation.  
- GRPO implementation specifics (e.g., reward normalization, λ scheduling, handling of per‑token vs. per‑output rewards) are sparse, making training reproducibility difficult.
