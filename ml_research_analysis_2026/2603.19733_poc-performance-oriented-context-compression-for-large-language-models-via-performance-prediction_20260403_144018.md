# PoC: Performance-oriented Context Compression for Large Language Models via Performance Prediction

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.19733  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19733  
- **Reference count:** 33  
- **Primary result:** PoC uses a lightweight performance predictor to automatically find the most aggressive compression ratio that satisfies a user‑specified performance floor, achieving lower prediction error and higher performance‑at‑ratio scores than context‑agnostic baselines, with negligible overhead (~1.53 ms).

## Executive Summary
Large language models (LLMs) often struggle with long contexts, prompting the use of context compression to reduce latency. Existing methods rely on fixed compression ratios, leading to unpredictable and sometimes severe performance drops. This paper introduces **Performance‑oriented Context Compression (PoC)**, where users specify an acceptable performance retention floor instead of a compression budget. PoC employs a lightweight performance predictor to estimate the performance‑compression curve for a given context and then selects the most aggressive ratio that meets the floor. Two predictor variants are evaluated: a context‑agnostic average curve and a context‑aware model that captures sample‑specific compressibility. Across seven QA and summarization benchmarks, the context‑aware predictor consistently reduces **Performance Prediction Error (PPE)** and improves **Performance at Ratio (P@R)** scores, with particularly large gains on QA tasks (e.g., +2.4 F1 on SearchQA, +18.3 F1 on TriviaQA). The predictor’s inference overhead is minimal (1.53 ms), enabling efficient deployment in real‑world systems.

## Method Summary
PoC operates in three stages. First, a performance predictor forecasts task performance at various compression ratios for a given input context. The predictor is trained on a dataset of (context, ratio, performance retention) triples, where performance retention normalizes the raw task score (F1 or ROUGE) by the uncompressed baseline. The context‑aware variant uses a pruned small language model (Llama‑3.2‑1B‑Instruct, embedding layer + two Transformer blocks) to encode the context and multiple candidate ratios in parallel; a linear‑sigmoid head outputs predictions for all ratios in a single forward pass. Second, a two‑stage search algorithm (coarse then fine uniform sampling, 18 ratios each) identifies the smallest ratio satisfying the user‑specified performance floor. Third, an off‑the‑shelf compressor (LLMLingua2) is applied at the selected ratio to produce the compressed context, which is fed to the target LLM (Llama‑3.1‑8B‑Instruct) for response generation. This design balances accuracy and speed: the parallel predictor minimizes overhead, while the adaptive ratio ensures predictable performance.

## Key Results
- The context‑aware predictor lowers **Performance Prediction Error (PPE)** compared to the context‑agnostic baseline, especially on QA tasks where performance curves exhibit high variance.
- PoC achieves higher **Performance at Ratio (P@R)** scores; on SearchQA and TriviaQA, P@R F1 improves by +2.4 and +18.3 points respectively.
- Predictor latency is very low (~1.53 ms per 512‑token chunk), making the overhead negligible relative to the latency savings achieved by compressing the context for the reader LLM.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Specifying a performance floor instead of a compression ratio mitigates unpredictable performance degradation.
- **Claim:** Letting users define an acceptable performance retention floor avoids the mismatch between a fixed ratio and the actual compressibility of a context.
- **Mechanism:** For a given context, PoC queries the predictor to estimate the performance‑compression curve, then selects the smallest ratio that meets or exceeds the floor. This replaces a static, potentially inappropriate ratio with a dynamically tailored one.
- **Core assumption:** The predictor can reliably estimate the performance‑compression relationship for the specific context.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 3.1 describe the optimization problem. The corpus identifies related adaptive compression work, highlighting PoC’s unique “performance floor” and “predictable outcome” focus.
- **Break condition:** High predictor error may cause the chosen ratio to violate the floor (too aggressive) or be overly conservative (missing efficiency).

### Mechanism 2: A context‑aware predictor that models sample‑specific compressibility yields more accurate performance estimates than a context‑agnostic average curve.
- **Claim:** Capturing per‑context features (e.g., information density, noise) improves curve prediction, especially on volatile tasks like QA.
- **Mechanism:** The context‑aware predictor uses a pruned small LM to encode the context. It processes a sequence containing the context and multiple ratio tokens in parallel; hidden states at the ratio tokens are projected to predict performance for all ratios simultaneously. This shared encoding captures sample‑specific nuances.
- **Core assumption:** The intrinsic compressibility of a context significantly affects the performance‑compression curve and can be learned from its representation.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions a “more sophisticated context‑aware one”. Section 3.2 details the parallel architecture. Section 5.1 visualizes large deviations of individual sample curves from the average, especially in QA.
- **Break condition:** The pruned LM’s capacity or training data diversity may be insufficient to model complex compressibility patterns.

### Mechanism 3: A two‑stage coarse‑to‑fine search efficiently identifies a near‑optimal compression ratio with minimal predictor overhead.
- **Claim:** Uniformly sampling 18 ratios in two successive intervals achieves high precision (~0.0028) with only two predictor invocations.
- **Mechanism:** Stage 1 samples 18 ratios uniformly across [0,1], uses the predictor’s parallel output to bracket a promising sub‑interval. Stage 2 samples 18 ratios within that sub‑interval and selects the best ratio satisfying the floor. The precision approximates $1/19^2$.
- **Core assumption:** The performance‑compression curve is relatively smooth, so a coarse sampling reliably locates the interval containing the optimal satisfying ratio.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 3.3 describes the algorithm. Section 4.2 notes the parallel prediction speeds up the process. Table 3 shows the predictor overhead is fixed and tiny compared to Reader LLM latency.
- **Break condition:** If the curve is highly non‑monotonic or has multiple local maxima, the coarse stage might miss the true optimum.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Reader LLM (Llama‑3.1‑8B‑Instruct) → Performance Predictor (pruned Llama‑3.2‑1B‑Instruct) → Search Algorithm (two‑stage sampler) → Off‑the‑shelf Compressor (LLMLingua2) → (compressed context) → Reader LLM  

### Critical Path
Input context is first sent to the Performance Predictor, which estimates the performance‑compression curve. The Search Algorithm uses these estimates to find the smallest ratio meeting the user’s performance floor. The Compressor then produces the compressed context at that ratio, which the Reader LLM consumes to generate the final response.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Predictor Capacity vs. Latency:** A pruned 1B LM balances expressive power for context‑aware modeling with low prediction latency (~1.5 ms).
- **Task‑Agnostic vs. Task‑Specific Predictor:** Training on normalized retention across QA and summarization sacrifices some task nuance for generalizability; a task‑specific predictor could be more accurate but would require per‑task training.
- **Fixed vs. Adaptive Chunking:** Contexts are split into 512‑token chunks for prediction/compression, simplifying input size but assuming chunk‑level independence, which may harm tasks needing cross‑chunk reasoning.

### Failure Signatures
- **High PPE on validation set:** Systematic bias or noise in predictor estimates leads to sub‑optimal ratios and possible performance violations.
- **Inconsistent P@R gains:** If context‑aware PoC does not significantly outperform the baseline on a benchmark, the task’s performance‑compression relationship may be very stable or not captured by the pruned LM.
- **Latency overrun:** If predictor+compressor overhead approaches the latency saved by compressing the context for the Reader LLM, net efficiency is lost (though paper shows overhead is negligible).

### First 3 Experiments
1. Train the context‑aware predictor on the collected dataset (98% train, 2% val) and compute Mean Squared Error (PPE) on validation; compare against context‑agnostic baseline.
2. Run the full PoC pipeline (predictor → search → compress → read) on test sets, sweep performance floors, plot the performance‑compression curve, compute its AUC (P@R), and compare the two PoC variants.
3. Measure latency of predictor (per chunk), compressor (per chunk at various ratios), and Reader LLM (full 32k context at various ratios); verify the combined overhead is a small fraction of the Reader LLM time saved.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Generalization to different context compressors:** The predictor is trained with LLMLingua2; it is unknown whether it can steer other compressors (e.g., soft or query‑aware ones) without retraining.
- **Understanding contexts that benefit from compression:** Some tasks (e.g., TriviaQA) show performance peaking at moderate compression; the features causing this non‑monotonic behaviour remain unidentified.
- **Optimality of the two‑stage search:** The paper uses fixed uniform sampling (18 ratios per stage); it is unclear whether this is the most efficient or accurate search strategy for arbitrary performance‑compression curves.

## Limitations
- Training data generation details (exact number of candidate ratios N and sampling distribution) are unspecified; current reproduction assumes uniform sampling with N=18.
- Sub‑interval selection logic in the two‑stage search is not fully described, leaving ambiguity in implementation.
- LLMLingua2 may not precisely achieve the requested compression ratio, causing a mismatch between predicted and actual performance.

## Confidence
- **Claim 1 (Performance floor mitigates degradation):** Medium – strong empirical results but dependent on predictor accuracy; high prediction error could still cause performance violations.
- **Claim 2 (Context‑aware predictor superiority):** Medium‑High – consistently lower PPE and higher P@R on QA; limited to a pruned 1B LM, so may not generalize to other architectures.
- **Claim 3 (Two‑stage search efficiency):** High – overhead is minimal (~1.53 ms) and precision is theoretically derived; no non‑monotonic curves were observed in experiments.

## Next Checks
1. **Exact training data generation:** Determine the true N and ratio distribution used during data collection; regenerate data and compare PPE against the paper’s reported values.
2. **Search algorithm fidelity:** For held‑out contexts, compute the true performance‑compression curve; verify that the two‑stage search reliably finds a ratio that meets the performance floor and is near‑optimal.
3. **Compressor ratio attainment:** Audit LLMLingua2 to measure actual compression ratios versus requested ones; quantify systematic bias and assess its impact on PoC’s performance guarantees.
