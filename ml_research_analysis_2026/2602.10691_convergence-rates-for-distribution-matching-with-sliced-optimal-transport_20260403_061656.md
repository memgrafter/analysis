# Convergence Rates for Distribution Matching with Sliced Optimal Transport

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.10691
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10691
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: The expected Sliced-Wasserstein loss decays as O(1/k^{2α-1}) for step sizes γ_k = 1/(k+1)^α with 1/2 < α < 1 under Gaussian source and isotropic Gaussian target using random orthonormal projection bases.

## Executive Summary
The paper studies distribution matching via sliced optimal transport, focusing on the slice-matching iterative scheme. This scheme can be viewed as stochastic gradient descent (SGD) on the Sliced-Wasserstein (SW) loss, where at each iteration a random orthonormal basis of projection directions is used to compute the gradient. The method aims to transform a source distribution to match a target distribution in the sliced Wasserstein sense.

The authors provide a theoretical convergence analysis for the case where both source and target are Gaussian, with the target being isotropic. They show that with step sizes γ_k = 1/(k+1)^α for α in (1/2,1), the expected loss E[F(σ_k)] converges at rate O(1/k^{2α-1}). The proof establishes a Polyak-Łojasiewicz (PL) inequality whose constant is controlled via a recursion on eigenvalues of the covariance matrix along the iterates. Numerical experiments corroborate the theory: the SW distance decreases, eigenvalues stabilize more quickly with orthonormal bases than with single-direction updates, and smaller α (even α ≤ 1/2) yields faster empirical convergence in higher dimensions and for Gaussian mixtures, though the latter setting is not covered by the current theory.

## Method Summary
The algorithm operates on Gaussian source μ ~ N(0, σ²I) and isotropic Gaussian target ν ~ N(0, I). The transport map is linear, parameterized by a covariance matrix Σ that evolves over iterations. At each step k, an orthonormal basis V_k ∈ ℝ^{d×d} is sampled uniformly. For each basis direction v_i, the 1D Wasserstein distance between the projected source and target (which are 1D Gaussians) is computed; this yields an estimate of the gradient of the SW loss with respect to Σ. The update takes a stochastic gradient step: Σ_{k+1} = Σ_k - γ_k G_k, where G_k is the aggregated gradient estimate (often a sum of rank‑1 matrices derived from the projections).

The analysis leverages the Gaussian setting to obtain closed‑form expressions for the SW loss and its gradient. The gradient can be written as a linear operator applied to (Σ - I) projected onto the random basis, which is an unbiased estimator with favorable variance properties. This structure leads to a stochastic approximation of the ODE dΣ/dt = -(Σ - I). A key step is proving a Polyak‑Łojasiewicz inequality: the expected squared gradient norm is at least a constant c times the loss suboptimality (loss minus minimum). Through a careful recursion on the eigenvalues of Σ and martingale arguments, the authors show that the PL constant c can be bounded uniformly away from zero along the trajectory. Combining this with a step‑size schedule satisfying ∑γ_k = ∞ and ∑γ_k² < ∞ yields the stated convergence rate.

## Key Results
- For α ∈ (1/2,1), the expected SW loss satisfies E[F(σ_k)] = O(1/k^{2α-1}).
- The proof hinges on a uniformly bounded Polyak‑Łojasiewicz constant, obtained by analyzing the eigenvalue evolution of the transport covariance via a martingale‑based recursion.
- Numerical experiments confirm the theory: SW distance consistently decreases; orthonormal bases stabilize eigenvalues faster than single‑direction updates; smaller α (e.g., 0.1) accelerates convergence in dimensions up to 100 and also for Gaussian mixtures (empirically), though the latter lies outside the current theory.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The slice‑matching scheme converges because it effectively performs stochastic gradient descent on a smooth loss that satisfies a Polyak‑Łojasiewicz (PL) inequality under the Gaussian assumption. The SW loss, when both distributions are Gaussian with isotropic target, can be expressed as a function of the eigenvalues of the transport covariance. The gradient with respect to this covariance, estimated via a random orthonormal basis, is an unbiased estimator whose variance is controlled. Moreover, the PL inequality ensures that the expected squared gradient norm is proportional to the suboptimality (loss minus minimum). This property, together with a step‑size schedule that satisfies ∑γ_k = ∞ and ∑γ_k² < ∞, guarantees that the iterates approach the optimum at rate O(1/k^{2α-1}). The eigenvalue recursion analysis demonstrates that the condition number of the covariance matrix remains bounded along the trajectory, preventing the PL constant from degenerating. Thus, the algorithm inherits the convergence guarantees of SGD for PL objectives.

## Foundational Learning
- **Sliced-Wasserstein distance**  
  *Why needed*: It defines the loss for distribution matching, reducing infinite‑dimensional OT to a tractable integral over 1D projections.  
  *Quick check*: For two 1D Gaussians N(μ₁,σ₁²) and N(μ₂,σ₂²), the Wasserstein distance is |μ₁‑μ₂|+|σ₁‑σ₂|; averaging over random directions approximates the full Wasserstein.

- **Polyak‑Łojasiewicz (PL) inequality**  
  *Why needed*: It provides a quadratic lower bound linking the gradient norm to the function suboptimality, which is crucial for proving convergence of gradient methods without strong convexity.  
  *Quick check*: For a quadratic f(x)=½xᵀAx‑bᵀx with A ⪰ 0, the PL constant equals the smallest eigenvalue of A.

- **Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD)**  
  *Why needed*: The slice‑matching scheme is an SGD because the gradient is estimated using random projection directions, introducing stochasticity.  
  *Quick check*: Simulate SGD on a simple function (e.g., f(x)=x²) with unbiased gradient estimates having bounded variance and observe convergence.

- **Eigenvalue dynamics for Gaussian covariances**  
  *Why needed*: Under Gaussian assumptions, the update of the transport covariance can be reduced to tracking its eigenvalues; bounding their ratios prevents the PL constant from vanishing.  
  *Quick check*: For a linear covariance update Σ' = Σ - γ(Σ - I)VVᵀ averaged over V, derive the expected change in the largest and smallest eigenvalues.

- **Martingale difference arguments**  
  *Why needed*: To handle the randomness of orthonormal bases and bound the variance term in SGD analysis, establishing that the sequence of gradient estimates is a martingale difference sequence.  
  *Quick check*: Show that for a random orthonormal basis V, the matrix (VVᵀ - I/d) has zero expectation and is independent of past iterates, making the gradient increments a martingale difference.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Source Gaussian μ ~ N(0, σ₀²I) → Initialize Σ = σ₀²I → Loop: sample orthonormal basis V ∈ ℝ^{d×d} → Compute gradient G = (1/d)∑_{i=1}^d (proj_i(Σ) - I) v_i v_iᵀ → Update Σ ← Σ - γ_k G → (After many iterations) Σ ≈ I.

**Critical path**: The core is the unbiased gradient estimation using random orthonormal bases. This requires computing 1D Wasserstein distances between projected Gaussians (which reduces to comparing standard deviations) and aggregating them into a sum of rank‑1 matrices. The step‑size schedule governs progress versus stability; orthonormality ensures collective coverage of the whole space, which is essential for the eigenvalue recursion and uniform PL constant.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Step‑size α: Larger α (close to 1) yields faster variance decay but may cause early instability; smaller α (e.g., 0.6) is safer but slower. Empirically, α ≤ 1/2 can still work but is outside the theoretical guarantee.
- Projection scheme: Using a full orthonormal basis per iteration (d directions) is more expensive than a single random direction but dramatically improves eigenvalue conditioning and reduces total iterations needed.
- Computational cost: Each iteration’s cost scales with the number of projections; the trade‑off is between per‑iteration work and total iterations to reach a target error.

**Failure signatures**:
- Exploding eigenvalues: if the effective step size is too large or the PL constant degrades, Σ can become ill‑conditioned, causing loss to increase or oscillate.
- Slow stabilization of eigenvalue range: when using single directions, the ratio λ_max/λ_min may drift, indicating violation of the boundedness assumption in the proof.
- Divergence for α ≤ 1/2: The analysis requires ∑γ_k² < ∞; for α ≤ 1/2 this sum diverges, so no convergence guarantee; in practice loss may still decrease but with occasional spikes.

**First experiments**:
1. Convergence rate reproduction: For d=10, run the algorithm with α ∈ {0.6,0.7,0.8,0.9} and plot loss vs iteration on a log‑log scale; fit a line to estimate exponent and compare to 2α‑1.
2. Orthonormal vs single‑direction: Use the same total number of projections (d per iteration vs 1 per iteration for d times as many iterations) and compare the evolution of λ_max/λ_min and final loss.
3. Beyond Gaussians: Apply the algorithm to a Gaussian mixture target (e.g., two isotropic Gaussians with different means) and check whether loss still decreases; test sensitivity to α including α=0.1.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: Extend convergence rates to non‑isotropic Gaussian targets and non‑elliptic distributions.
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The Conclusion states that extending the theory to general Gaussian targets and non‑elliptic distributions remains open.
- Why unresolved: The eigenvalue control along the trajectory crucially exploits isotropy of the target (Λ = Id) and ellipticity. For non‑isotropic or non‑elliptic distributions, the constants in the Polyak‑Łojasiewicz inequality cannot be uniformly bounded with the current arguments.
- What evidence would resolve it: Develop new spectral control techniques (e.g., matrix martingales, entropy‑regularized dynamics) that propagate bounds on covariance eigenvalues for arbitrary target covariances or more general density classes.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Can regularization (e.g., diffusive or entropic terms) maintain density boundedness and thus make the analysis tractable beyond Gaussians?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The Conclusion suggests regularization as a promising direction to help maintain regularity along the dynamics.
- Why unresolved: The primary obstacle in extending the theoretical results is controlling the density (or covariance) constants in the PL inequality for non‑elliptic distributions. Regularization may keep densities sufficiently smooth and uniformly bounded, enabling a similar analytical treatment.
- What evidence would resolve it: Prove convergence rates for a regularized version of the slice‑matching scheme (e.g., with entropic OT maps or a diffusion term) on a broader class of target distributions, showing that the relevant PL constants remain bounded along the iterates.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Provide theoretical convergence guarantees for step‑size schedules with small α (α ≤ 1/2), where faster empirical convergence is observed.
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The Conclusion notes that step‑size schedules γₖ = 1/(k+1)^α with small α (e.g., α = 0.1) yield faster empirical convergence, but this regime is not covered by the theorems.
- Why unresolved: The current non‑asymptotic analysis requires α > 1/2 to ensure both ∑γₖ = ∞ and ∑γₖ² < ∞, a condition needed for the martingale‑type arguments. For α ≤ 1/2, the sum of squared step sizes diverges, invalidating the descent recursion as used.
- What evidence would resolve it: Obtain a refined convergence analysis that accommodates α ≤ 1/2, possibly by employing stronger Lyapunov functions, adaptive step sizes, or connections to ergodic Markov chains, as hinted by the authors.

### Open Question 4
- Question: Does the slice‑matching scheme converge for discrete (empirical) source and target distributions, and what are the quantitative rates?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The Introduction and Section 5 report empirical convergence for discrete Gaussian mixtures but explicitly state that these settings are not covered by the theory; related works (Tanguy et al., 2025; Vauthier et al., 2025) identify technical issues.
- Why unresolved: The theoretical framework relies on absolutely continuous measures and uses the Polyak‑Łojasiewicz inequality derived for densities. Discrete measures lack a density, and the SW loss landscape known to have nontrivial critical points, so the gradient dynamics may not enjoy the same properties as in the continuous case.
- What evidence would resolve it: Extend the PL‑type analysis to discrete measures (e.g., via smoothing or by working with the space of signed measures) or prove that the discretization error remains negligible, establishing convergence rates that depend on sample size.

## Limitations
- Theory only covers Gaussian source and isotropic Gaussian target; extending to non‑Gaussian and anisotropic distributions remains open.
- The Polyak‑Łojasiewicz constant derivation relies on eigenvalue recursion under Gaussian assumptions; its behavior for other distributions or finite samples is unknown.
- The method employs a fixed step‑size schedule and random orthonormal bases; the impact of alternative hyperparameters and projection designs remains unexplored.

## Confidence
- Gaussian convergence rate O(1/k^{2α-1}): High
- Eigenvalue stabilization with orthonormal bases: Medium
- Applicability to Gaussian mixtures: Low
