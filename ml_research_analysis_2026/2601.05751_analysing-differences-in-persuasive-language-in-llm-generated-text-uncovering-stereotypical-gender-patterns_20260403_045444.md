# Analysing Differences in Persuasive Language in LLM-Generated Text: Uncovering Stereotypical Gender Patterns

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.05751  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05751  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: All 13 studied LLMs generate gender-stereotypical persuasive language: female-targeted messages are more emotional/communal, male-targeted more direct/agentic, across 16 languages.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how prompt attributes (recipient gender, user intent, output language) shape the persuasive language produced by large language models. Using a pairwise experimental design, the authors generate responses from 13 LLMs to prompts that differ only in the gender of the recipient (e.g., "male" vs. "female"). A comprehensive evaluation framework scores each response across 19 persuasive language categories (rhetorical appeals, Cialdini principles, agentic/communal traits, etc.) via an LLM-as-judge setup. Results reveal robust gender-stereotypical patterns: female-targeted responses score higher on emotional and communal dimensions (pathos, relational, polite, affectionate), while male-targeted responses are more direct and agentic (logos, instrumental, direct). These patterns persist across 16 languages and all model families, though the magnitude of the gender gap varies (e.g., larger in GPT-5 than GPT-4.1). Extensive verification – including human annotation, alternative judges, and input neutralization – confirms the reliability of the findings, which are not explained by output length differences.

## Method Summary
The study constructs 150 base prompts for interpersonal messages and 150 for political arguments, each automatically generated and manually checked for topic diversity. For each base prompt, a treatment pair is created by inserting either "female" or "male" as the recipient's gender, yielding 300 pairs. Thirteen LLMs are prompted to generate short persuasive responses (2–3 sentences). Each response pair is evaluated twice (with swapped order) by a judge model (GPT-4o) using a detailed function-calling rubric covering 19 persuasive language categories on a symmetric -3 to +3 scale. The mean directional difference per category (Dj) indicates the bias toward one gender; the overall Gender Gap (GT) aggregates absolute differences. Statistical significance is assessed via Wilcoxon signed-rank tests and bootstrap resampling. The findings are validated through human annotations, a different judge model (Qwen2.5-72B), and by neutralizing gender terms in the inputs.

## Key Results
- All 13 LLMs exhibit significant gender-stereotypical differences: female‑targeted responses score higher on communal/emotional categories (pathos, communal, relational, polite, affectionate), while male‑targeted responses score higher on agentic/direct categories (logos, agentic, instrumental, direct).
- The pattern is consistent across 16 languages and model families; the magnitude of the gender gap varies by model (e.g., GPT-5 > GPT-4.1).
- Human annotators confirm the same directional split, and results are robust to alternative judges and input neutralization; output length shows no correlation with the gender gap.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Pairwise Prompt Isolation
**Claim:** Pairwise prompts that differ only in the treatment attribute isolate the effect of that attribute on generated persuasive language.  
**Mechanism:** By holding the core persuasive request constant and varying only the gender identifier, any systematic difference in the LLM's output can be attributed to the model's internal associations with that identifier. The prompt template is semantically equivalent across treatments.  
**Core assumption:** The LLM does not significantly alter other latent aspects of the request when the gender term is swapped. The authors mitigate potential confounds by using consistent recipient types (e.g., always "coworker" or "friend") and manually verifying topic equivalence.  
**Evidence anchors:** Prompt construction details (Section 3.1, Tables 1 & 2) and example pairs (Figure 1).  
**Break condition:** If the gender identifier changes the request's meaning or implied context, the observed difference could be a confound. The consistent recipient types reduce this risk.

### Mechanism 2: LLM-as-Judge Reliability
**Claim:** An LLM-as-judge setup with a fine-grained scoring rubric can reliably detect stylistic differences between paired LLM outputs at scale.  
**Mechanism:** For each response pair, the judge model (GPT‑4o) is prompted with a detailed instruction to assign a symmetric score (-3 to +3) for 19 categories. The mean score per category across many pairs (Dj) indicates directional bias. The prompt includes function‑calling specifications and the evaluation is run twice with swapped text order to mitigate positional bias.  
**Core assumption:** The judge's scoring, while imperfect, is sufficiently consistent and unbiased to detect true signal.  
**Evidence anchors:** Section 3.2 formalizes the evaluation; robustness checks show high correlation with an alternative judge (ρ≈0.85) and with human annotations; positional consistency reaches 75–84% (Table 4).  
**Break condition:** The judge exhibits systematic bias that correlates with the treatment identifier. The neutralization check (replacing gender terms) yields ρ>0.98, supporting robustness.

### Mechanism 3: Systematic Verification
**Claim:** Multiple verification steps establish reliability and validity of the measured gender-stereotypical differences, distinguishing them from artifacts of the automated pipeline.  
**Mechanism:** Findings are cross‑validated via human perception (annotators), different model architectures (Qwen2.5‑72B judge), and input transformations (gender‑neutralized texts). Statistical tests (Wilcoxon signed‑rank on ϵ scores, bootstrap on GT) assess significance of aggregate directional shifts.  
**Core assumption:** Convergence across diverse verification methods increases confidence that the pattern reflects real model bias, not evaluation artifacts.  
**Evidence anchors:** Section 7 reports: human annotators agree with the LLM‑judge's communal+/agentic+ pattern; high correlation across judge variants; near‑perfect correlation after neutralization; no length‑GT correlation (Section 4.2).  
**Break condition:** If any major verification channel contradicted the primary finding (e.g., humans saw no difference), the conclusion of pervasive bias would be undermined.

## Foundational Learning
- **Pairwise Comparative Experimental Design**  
  *Why needed:* Isolates the effect of a single prompt attribute (e.g., gender) by comparing minimally different pairs. Without it, differences could be due to topic variance.  
  *Quick check:* In a pairwise design where prompts differ only in the word "brother" vs. "sister", what is the null hypothesis about the distribution of score differences (ϵ) if the LLM's output is unaffected by the recipient's gender?
- **LLM-as-Judge Methodology & Its Biases**  
  *Why needed:* Automated evaluation at scale must account for known judge biases (position, verbosity, inherent stereotypes).  
  *Quick check:* Why is it insufficient to run the LLM‑judge only once per pair, and what specific step in this paper's pipeline addresses that limitation?
- **Operationalizing Social Psychology Constructs (Agency/Communion)**  
  *Why needed:* The 19 evaluation categories are grounded in established theory (Aristotle, Cialdini, Bakan) to interpret the gendered pattern.  
  *Quick check:* If a text scores high on "agentic" and "direct" but low on "polite" and "affectionate" for male‑targeted responses, what societal stereotype from sociolinguistics does this pattern align with?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Prompt Generator → Treatment Pair Constructor → Response Generator → LLM-as-Judge Evaluator → Aggregator & Statistician → Verification Suite  
**Critical path:**  
1. Generate 150 diverse base prompts (messages/arguments) and manually annotate for topic variation.  
2. For each base prompt, create the treatment pair (e.g., female/male) and generate responses from all target LLMs; log refusals.  
3. Run the double‑swapped LLM‑as‑judge evaluation on every valid response pair.  
4. Compute directional differences Dj and overall Gender Gap GT per model; perform statistical tests (Wilcoxon, bootstrap).  
5. Run verification experiments on an anchor model (e.g., LLAMA3.3): human annotations, alternate judge, neutralization, cross‑lingual checks.  
**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Scale vs. Cost:* Evaluating 13 LLMs × 150 pairs × 19 categories × 2 order swaps ≈ 73,800 judge calls demands significant budget. Using a single powerful judge (GPT‑4o) balances consistency and cost.  
- *Generality vs. Depth:* The 19‑category rubric covers many persuasion theories but may miss culture‑specific strategies; the authors note its Western grounding as a limitation.  
- *Automation vs. Trust:* Full reliance on LLM‑as‑judge is efficient but risky. The paper’s extensive verification (human + multiple checks) greatly increases confidence but adds annotation labor and compute.  
**Failure signatures:**  
- High refusal rate on specific treatments (e.g., ignoble intent), leading to data sparsity.  
- Low positional consistency (<70% identical scores on swapped inputs) indicating judge instability.  
- Low correlation between LLM‑judge and human annotations (ρ < 0.6) or low per‑item agreement (Kα < 0.2); aggregate trend may still be valid.  
- Strong correlation between output length and GT would suggest an artifact; the paper finds none.  
**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Judge Baseline Consistency:** Run the LLM‑as‑judge on 50 random response pairs from an anchor model (e.g., LLAMA3.3). Swap order and calculate percentage of identical scores across the 19 categories. Target >75% consistency; lower indicates unstable judge outputs.  
2. **Treatment Effect Sanity Check:** Manually inspect a sample of the same 50 pairs to see if texts differ as expected (communal vs. agentic). Use keyword extraction to check alignment (e.g., “family, support” vs. “individual, lead”). Validates the judge’s face validity.  
3. **Human Annotation Pilot:** Recruit 2–3 annotators to rate 30 pairs using a collapsed “communal+” vs. “agentic+” question. Compute whether the mean human score direction matches the LLM‑judge’s Dj. A significant match (p<0.05) confirms convergent validity before full annotation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Cultural universality of persuasive categories:** The 19 categories are grounded in Western theory; it is unknown whether they capture strategies in non‑Western contexts. The cross‑lingual study only covered 16 languages with cultural proximity to the West.
- **Gender beyond binary:** How do LLMs generate persuasive language for non‑binary recipients? The study only examined male/female.
- **Training data origin of bias:** What mechanisms (pre‑training data, fine‑tuning, RLHF) cause the observed stereotyping? The paper does not investigate this.
- **Societal impact and mitigation:** What are the real‑world harms of such biased outputs, and what interventions can mitigate them? This is out of scope.

## Limitations
- Swapping gender terms may inadvertently change perceived relationship context (e.g., “brother” vs. “coworker”), potentially confounding the effect.
- The judge model (GPT‑4o) may carry its own biases that influence scoring, despite robustness checks.
- The framework relies on Western persuasion theories and short, artificial prompts, limiting generalizability to real‑world, multi‑turn interactions.

## Confidence
- Pervasive gender‑stereotyped differences: **High**
- Directional pattern (female → communal/emotional, male → agentic/direct): **High**
- Magnitude variation across models: **Medium**
- Causal attribution to model internals: **Medium**

## Next Checks
1. **Judge Positional Consistency Audit:** Re‑run the LLM‑as‑judge evaluation on a fixed subset with temperature = 0; check that >80% of scores are identical across order swaps.
2. **Prompt Context Control Experiment:** Construct prompts where the relationship is explicitly held constant (e.g., “my supervisor”) and only the gender pronoun changes; test whether the pattern persists.
3. **Cross‑Judge Convergence with a Stereotype‑Naïve Model:** Use a smaller model fine‑tuned only on the scoring rubric as an independent judge; compute Spearman correlation with GPT‑4o. Correlation <0.7 would suggest strong judge‑dependence.
