# Improving Lean4 Autoformalization via Cycle Consistency Fine-tuning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.24372
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24372
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** Reinforcement learning fine-tuning with a cycle consistency reward substantially improves Lean4 autoformalization compared to supervised fine-tuning, increasing mean cycle consistency by ~0.156 on FineLeanCorpus and ~0.139 on PutnamBench, with only a slight increase (0.011 nats) in cross-entropy loss.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a reinforcement learning (RL) approach for autoformalizing natural language mathematics into Lean4 code. The method fine‑tunes a Qwen3.5‑2B model using the GRPO algorithm, where the reward is the cosine similarity between the embedding of the original statement and that of its back‑translation via a frozen Lean‑to‑natural‑language model. On held‑out benchmarks (FineLeanCorpus and PutnamBench), RL significantly outperforms both supervised fine‑tuning variants, achieving higher semantic alignment while preserving syntactic quality. Ablations indicate that curriculum ordering during supervised training offers no measurable benefit over shuffled data.

## Method Summary
The training pipeline consists of two phases. First, two separate supervised fine‑tuning (SFT) models are trained: a forward model (nl2lean) that maps natural language to Lean4, and a backward model (lean2nl) that translates Lean4 back to natural language. Both use LoRA adaptations on the Qwen3.5‑2B base, with a learning rate of 2e‑4 and a cosine schedule. Second, the forward model is fine‑tuned with RL (GRPO) while the backward model remains frozen. For each prompt, the policy samples multiple Lean4 completions, which are back‑translated to natural language. The reward is the cosine similarity between the embedding of the original statement and the back‑translation, normalized within the group of samples. An KL penalty (β=0.04) relative to the SFT checkpoint prevents catastrophic forgetting. Training uses a learning rate of 1e‑5, gradient accumulation (batch size 1, accumulation steps 16), and runs for approximately one epoch over the RL prompt set.

## Key Results
- On FineLeanCorpus, mean cycle consistency improves from 0.513 (SFT shuffled) to 0.669 (RL), with cross‑entropy loss increasing only 0.011 nats.
- On PutnamBench, mean cycle consistency increases from 0.422 to 0.561 with the same RL setup.
- Curriculum‑ordered SFT shows no statistically significant advantage over shuffled SFT.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The improvement arises from directly optimizing semantic alignment through cycle consistency rather than token‑level likelihood. Three key mechanisms support this:

1. **Reward design**: The cycle‑consistency reward (cosine similarity between original and back‑translation) encourages the generated Lean4 to preserve the meaning of the input. Because the backward model is frozen, the forward policy cannot exploit it; it must produce statements that yield natural‑language back‑translations close to the source. This objective captures semantic equivalence more directly than maximum likelihood estimation, which only matches superficial token patterns.

2. **Group‑relative advantage normalization**: GRPO computes advantages by normalizing rewards within each prompt’s sampled group. This removes dependence on absolute reward scales and reduces difficulty‑related bias, making learning stable even when the underlying cycle‑consistency scores are noisy. Empirically, the RL gain persists across a range of difficulties, decreasing monotonically with difficulty but remaining positive.

3. **SFT warm start with KL regularization**: Starting RL from an already competent SFT checkpoint gives the policy a strong syntactic foundation. The KL penalty keeps the RL updates close to the SFT policy, preventing degradation of basic formalization ability while allowing semantic refinements. This is reflected in the minimal increase in cross‑entropy loss.

A potential break condition is reward hacking: if the backward model learns to echo the input or produce generic translations, cycle consistency may become detached from true semantics, leading to spurious high rewards. The paper identifies isolated cases of perfect scores, suggesting such risks exist.

## Architecture Onboarding
Data: FineLeanCorpus provides NL/Lean4 pairs; splits are created for SFT training, RL prompts, and held‑out evaluation. PutnamBench is a separate test set. Decontamination removes examples with high TF‑IDF similarity to PutnamBench.

Models: The base is Qwen3.5‑2B. Two LoRA‑adapted models are trained: `nl2lean` (forward) and `lean2nl` (backward). During RL, `lean2nl` is frozen.

Training: Phase 1: SFT separately on both directions. Phase 2: RL with GRPO on `nl2lean` initialized from SFT checkpoint.

Infrastructure: Two GPUs for RL (model on one, back‑translator on another); single GPU sufficient for SFT.

Critical path:
1. Curate data: deduplicate, remove compilation errors, decontaminate against PutnamBench.
2. SFT: train both models to convergence; validate token accuracy.
3. Freeze `lean2nl`, prepare RL prompt set (held‑out from SFT).
4. Initialize `nl2lean` from SFT checkpoint; run GRPO updates.
5. Evaluate on FLC and PutnamBench using greedy generation and compute cycle consistency.

Design tradeoffs:
- LoRA rank: 16 balances expressivity and overfitting.
- Reward choice: cycle consistency is fast but can be hacked; a compiler‑based reward would be slower but more accurate.
- SFT initialization: Used curriculum but found shuffled SFT equally effective; RL can start from either.
- GRPO hyperparameters: group size G=8 balances bias‑variance; KL coefficient β=0.04.
- Freezing vs joint training: Freezing `lean2nl` prevents collusion but limits adaptability.

Failure signatures:
- Reward fails to increase or fluctuates wildly during RL.
- Cross‑entropy loss on validation spikes (>0.05 nats), indicating forgetting.
- Back‑translator starts echoing input (high exact‑match rate).
- RL model excels on FLC but not on PutnamBench, suggesting overfitting to dataset patterns.
- Generated Lean4 frequently fails to compile (if checkable), yet cycle consistency may remain high.

First 3 experiments:
1. **SFT smoke test:** Train `nl2lean` on 1 k examples for 1 epoch. Confirm token accuracy improves and outputs contain Lean4 structure (imports, `theorem`, `by sorry`).
2. **Cycle consistency sanity:** Compute cycle scores on 50 held‑out examples using the small SFT models. Check for extreme values (near 1.0) indicating echo; ensure a reasonable spread (e.g., 0.2–0.7). Spot‑check back‑translations for semantic similarity.
3. **Minimal RL validation:** Run GRPO for 5 optimizer steps on 100 prompts with G=2. Record average reward before and after; expect an increase >0.02. If none, inspect gradient norms and advantage calculation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can Lean4 compiler feedback be incorporated as a hard reward signal alongside cycle consistency to improve semantic correctness and mitigate reward hacking?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Future work states: "The most impactful next step is incorporating Lean4 compiler feedback as a hard reward alongside the soft cycle consistency signal."
- **Why unresolved:** Cycle consistency is a proxy that can be exploited. A direct compilation/proof verification signal could provide a more reliable training objective.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Integrate a Lean4 compiler or type‑checker into the RL loop, define a binary or graded reward based on compilation success and/or proof verification, then compare the model's performance and robustness against the pure cycle‑consistency baseline.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Does scaling the RL fine‑tuning regime to larger model sizes (e.g., 9B parameters) yield further improvements, and how does it compare to scaling supervised fine‑tuning?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Future work includes "scaling RL to 9B" and the results show that 9B SFT nearly closes the PutnamBench gap with 2B RL, suggesting scale may substitute for the RL signal on hard problems.
- **Why unresolved:** Only 2B RL was evaluated; scaling could bring disproportionate gains or new training instabilities.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Train a 9B model with GRPO using the cycle‑consistency reward, evaluate on held‑out FLC and PutnamBench, and compare against both 9B SFT and the 2B RL model to assess scaling trends and any diminishing returns.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Would replacing the off‑the‑shelf sentence‑embedding similarity measure with a stronger semantic equivalence check improve the correlation between the reward and true formalization quality?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Future work proposes "replacing the sentence‑embedding metric with a stronger semantic equivalence check." The limitations section also notes that the current embedding can be gamed when the back‑translator echoes the original statement.
- **Why unresolved:** All‑MiniLM‑L6‑v2 may not capture deep mathematical meaning; back‑translation can produce high scores for syntactically plausible but semantically incorrect Lean4.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Design alternative metrics (e.g., an NLI model to judge if back‑translated NL entails the original, or a formal equivalence check in Lean4), retrain the RL policy with the new reward, and evaluate whether cycle consistency and downstream formalization quality increase, particularly on examples that previously suffered from reward hacking.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** To what extent does the improvement on FineLeanCorpus reflect genuine generalization versus exploitation of dataset‑specific patterns or structural redundancies?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Future work includes "Analysing FLC for structural redundancies and evaluating on more diverse held‑out sets would help clarify how much of the FLC improvement reflects genuine generalization." The analysis section also speculates about RL "exploiting structural regularities" in FLC.
- **Why unresolved:** FLC may contain many near‑duplicate or template‑based problems; RL could overfit to these patterns without learning robust formalization skills, as suggested by the high‑score tail on FLC that is absent on PutnamBench.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct a thorough analysis of FLC to identify template clusters and structural redundancies. Construct counterfactual test sets by perturbing statements (e.g., changing variable names, constants, or mathematical context) while preserving meaning. Evaluate RL’s performance drop relative to SFT to measure dependency on dataset artefacts.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Would evaluation on a more diverse collection of held‑out math formalization benchmarks alter the conclusions about the effectiveness of cycle‑consistency RL and the observed domain performance differences?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Future work states that "evaluating on more diverse held‑out sets would help clarify" the generalizability of the FLC improvement. The paper currently relies on two benchmarks with different characteristics (FLC vs. PutnamBench).
- **Why unresolved:** The domain breakdown shows consistent patterns (e.g., discrete domains outperforming continuous), but these could be an artefact of the limited evaluation corpora. Additional data from various mathematical fields and difficulty levels is needed to confirm robustness.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Curate or adopt additional formalized math datasets (e.g., from textbooks, other competition archives, or Lean's Mathlib). Run the same models on these new test sets, compare cycle‑consistency scores, and check whether the RL advantage and domain trends hold, change, or vanish.

## Limitations
- Cycle consistency relies on an off‑the‑shelf embedding model; it may not capture deep mathematical semantics and can be gamed by back‑translator echo.
- The frozen back‑translator limits the reward signal; if it is weak, RL gains are capped.
- Evaluation is restricted to two benchmarks (FineLeanCorpus and PutnamBench), leaving generalizability to broader mathematics unverified.

## Confidence
- **RL improves semantic alignment:** Medium (substantial gains but based on a proxy metric).
- **GRPO effectiveness:** Medium (consistent improvements, but no comparison to other RL algorithms).
- **SFT warm‑start preserves syntax:** High (CE loss increases only slightly).
- **Curriculum SFT provides no benefit:** High (no significant difference found).

## Next Checks
1. **Human evaluation of semantic equivalence:** Sample 200 NL–Lean4 pairs from the held‑out sets and obtain expert ratings (1–5) on whether the formalization correctly captures the original statement. Compute correlation with cycle consistency scores to validate the proxy.
2. **Back‑translator robustness and reward hacking analysis:** Train a stronger `lean2nl` (e.g., higher LoRA rank) and re‑run RL. If gains disappear, the original improvement was limited by back‑translator capacity. Monitor echo rate (exact match between original NL and back‑translated NL) during RL; a rising trend indicates reward hacking.
3. **Comparison with compiler‑based rewards:** On a 1k‑problem subset, define a binary reward: 1 if generated Lean4 compiles without `sorry` and type‑checks against the NL statement via a Lean REPL, else 0. Run GRPO with this reward and compare final cycle consistency and compilation success to the cycle‑consistency RL baseline.
