# LiTo: Surface Light Field Tokenization

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.11047
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11047
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** LiTo achieves state-of-the-art appearance reconstruction (e.g., PSNR 34.2 vs 31.1 on Toys4k) and significantly improves input view fidelity (FID 6.22 vs 12.84) in single-image-to-3D while maintaining consistent object orientation.

## Executive Summary
LiTo introduces a compact 3D latent representation that tokenizes a surface light field, unifying geometry and view-dependent appearance modeling. Given random subsamples from multiview RGB-D images, an encoder produces a set of latent tokens. A flow-matching geometry decoder reconstructs surface point clouds, while a view-dependent Gaussian decoder (with spherical harmonics up to degree 3) renders multiview images for appearance supervision. A diffusion transformer (DiT) generates these latents from a single input image, with training-time coordinate alignment ensuring the output respects the input view’s orientation. On benchmarks like Toys4k, GSO, and PBR-Objaverse, LiTo sets new records in appearance quality and demonstrates strong single-image-to-3D performance with high conditioning fidelity.

## Method Summary
LiTo’s pipeline has two stages. First, a tokenizer is trained on multiview RGB-D data: random samples (surface positions, view directions, colors) are encoded by a Perceiver IO with 3D patchification into 8192×32 latent tokens. This latent is simultaneously decoded by a flow-matching module that models a 3D surface distribution (yielding point clouds) and a Gaussian splatting decoder with spherical harmonics (degree 3) that renders multiview images. Both decoders are trained with geometry and radiance losses to embed appearance and geometry in the shared latent. Second, a DiT is trained to generate these latents from single-image embeddings. Critically, during generative training, each scene is rotated so the input camera pose becomes identity; thus, at inference, generated objects automatically align with the conditioning image without explicit pose estimation.

## Key Results
- **Reconstruction:** Outperforms SOTA in appearance on Toys4k (PSNR 34.2 vs 31.1, LPIPS 0.023 vs 0.034) and achieves competitive geometry (Chamfer distance 80.5 vs 92.2 with mesh decoder).
- **Single-image-to-3D:** Conditioning FID 6.22 vs 12.84, KID 0.009 vs 0.088—significant improvement in input view fidelity while preserving novel-view quality.
- **Orientation consistency:** Generated objects are consistently oriented with respect to the input view, a direct result of the coordinate alignment prior.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### 1. Tokenizing surface light fields for unified representation
- **Claim:** Encoding random subsamples of a surface light field into a compact latent token set enables unified modeling of both 3D geometry and view-dependent appearance.
- **How:** The encoder processes tuples (position, viewing direction, color) from multiview RGB-D back-projection. Using a Perceiver IO with 3D patchification (approximating geodesic grouping via k‑nearest neighbors), it compresses millions of samples into 8192 tokens of dimension 32. The latent implicitly represents the continuous 5D light field ℓ(x, d̂), allowing interpolation of unseen view‑dependent samples.
- **Core assumption:** A fixed‑size token set can capture high‑frequency variation of ℓ across space and angle despite sparse input samples.
- **Evidence:** [Section 3.3] details the encoder and 3D patchification. The abstract and Sec. 3.4 collectively show that this latent supports both geometry and view‑dependent rendering. Related work (e.g., VoD‑3DGS, LIRM) confirms the need to model view direction.
- **Break condition:** Extremely sparse input views (<10) or too few tokens (<1024) may fail to interpolate complex view‑dependent variations (e.g., sharp specular highlights), causing artifacts.

### 2. Joint supervision disentangles geometry and appearance
- **Claim:** Two independent decoders—flow‑matching for geometry and spherical harmonics (SH) Gaussians for appearance—reading from the same latent can reconstruct both without interference.
- **How:** The latent S is fed to (i) a velocity decoder (flow‑matching) that models a 3D distribution p(x|S) ≈ δ(x∈∂Ω) via score matching, outputting surface points; and (ii) a Gaussian decoder that converts S (with a coarse occupancy grid) into 3D Gaussians with SH coefficients up to degree 3, rendered by rasterization. Losses: L_geo enforces surface alignment, L_radiance enforces multiview image fidelity.
- **Core assumption:** Geometry and view‑dependent radiance can be separated into distinct decoders with indirect supervision sufficient to learn ℓ.
- **Evidence:** [Section 3.4] fully specifies both decoders and losses. Comparisons to end‑to‑end approaches (e.g., LIRM) highlight the benefits of the shared latent design. Ablations in Sec. 4.2 and Fig. S8–S9 show that SH degree 3 is critical for specularities.
- **Break condition:** Instability in flow‑matching training or limiting SH degree to 0 destroys view‑dependent effects, reducing realism in specular regions.

### 3. Coordinate alignment for consistent orientation
- **Claim:** Pre‑aligning world coordinates to the input view’s identity orientation during generative training ensures that latent generation yields 3D objects oriented consistently with the conditioning image.
- **How:** For each training sample, the 3D scene (and thus the surface light field samples) is rotated so the input camera pose becomes identity. The DiT learns to generate latents S in this aligned frame. At inference, without explicit pose estimation, outputs naturally align with the input because the coordinate system is fixed relative to it.
- **Core assumption:** Orientation misalignment is a primary failure mode in single‑image‑to‑3D and can be mitigated by baking alignment into the training distribution rather than requiring the model to infer camera geometry.
- **Evidence:** [Section 3.5] explicitly describes the rotation. Results in Table 2 and Fig. 5 show dramatically improved conditioning FID/KID and consistent orientation compared to baselines.
- **Break condition:** If input images lack reliable camera geometry (e.g., extreme distortion) or objects are not upright in the training distribution, the learned prior may still produce misoriented outputs, as the assumption relies on canonical object placement.

## Foundational Learning
- **Surface Light Field:** The 5D function ℓ(x, d̂) giving radiance at surface point x along viewing direction d̂. *Why needed?* It is the fundamental representation being tokenized; including viewing directions enables modeling view‑dependent effects like specular highlights and Fresnel reflections. *Quick check:* What does ℓ output, and how does sampling it from multiview RGB‑D provide both geometry (via x) and appearance (via c and d̂)?
- **Flow Matching:** A technique for modeling continuous probability distributions by learning a velocity field Vθ(x_t, t) that transforms noise into data samples. *Why needed?* The geometry decoder uses flow‑matching to model a distribution over surfaces, enabling stochastic point cloud sampling and zero‑shot normal estimation without explicit SDF/occupancy. *Quick check:* How does flow matching define Vθ to transform noise into surface points, and why is it more flexible than deterministic decoders?
- **Spherical Harmonics in 3D Gaussian Splatting:** SH basis functions up to degree 3 used to encode view‑dependent radiance per Gaussian. *Why needed?* SH degree 0 represents diffuse color; higher degrees capture complex lighting like specularities and Fresnel effects. *Quick check:* Why does degree 0 represent diffuse color, and what visual phenomena (e.g., sharp highlights) require degree ≥2?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**
Multiview RGB‑D images → random subsamples (x, d̂, c) → Encoder (Perceiver IO with 3D patchification) → latent S → [Training: Flow‑matching decoder → point cloud; Gaussian decoder (SH degree 3) → 3D Gaussians → rendered images] → losses. Generative stage: Single image → DiT (conditioned on image embeddings) → S → Decoders (Gaussians or mesh) → final 3D output.

**Critical path:**
1. Train tokenizer (encoder + both decoders) end‑to‑end on multiview RGB‑D data to establish a meaningful latent space.
2. Freeze tokenizer, extract latents S from training data, train DiT to generate S conditioned on single images.
3. At inference, use pretrained DiT to generate S from a single image, then decode to 3D Gaussians or mesh.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Latent size:** k=8192, d=32 balances compactness vs. capacity; larger k improves reconstruction but increases DiT complexity.
- **SH degree:** Degree 3 captures view‑dependence but may not handle extremely high‑frequency specularities; higher degrees unsupported by 3DGS implementation.
- **Coordinate alignment:** Simplifies generation by removing orientation inference but assumes canonical object placement; may fail on arbitrary poses.

**Failure signatures:**
- Low reconstruction PSNR/SSIM or high LPIPS: latent underfitting or insufficient SH degree.
- Misoriented generations: coordinate alignment assumption violated (e.g., input image not upright).
- Missing specular highlights: SH degree limited to 0 or inadequate multiview samples in training.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Reconstruction sanity check:** Train tokenizer on 100 Toys4k objects; evaluate geometry (Chamfer distance) and appearance (PSNR, LPIPS) on held‑out views. Expect PSNR > 30, LPIPS < 0.05 for basic success.
2. **SH degree ablation:** Train with SH degrees {0,1,2,3} on same subset; render novel views focusing on specular regions. Visualize and quantify LPIPS—degree 3 should clearly show view‑dependent highlights absent in degree 0.
3. **Generative alignment test:** Train DiT for 10k iterations (small scale); generate from single images and compare conditioning view rendering with input image using FID/KID. Expect FID < 15 to confirm orientation fidelity; also check novel view consistency.

## Limitations
- **Token capacity:** Fixed latent size (8192×32) may fail to capture extremely complex view‑dependent effects (e.g., sharp, high‑frequency specularities) if multiview coverage is sparse or the scene is highly intricate.
- **SH degree ceiling:** Using SH up to degree 3 may still insufficiently model materials with very narrow specular lobes or complex caustics, limiting photorealism for certain real‑world materials.
- **Alignment prior:** The method’s strength in consistent orientation relies on a training‑time coordinate alignment that assumes canonical object placement. Performance may degrade on images with unknown/extreme camera poses or non‑upright object orientations.

## Next Checks
1. **Sparse view & token budget ablation:** Systematically vary the number of input views (e.g., 3, 6, 12) and token count k (e.g., 1024, 4096, 8192) on a subset of Toys4k. Measure PSNR/LPIPS drop to quantify the breakpoints for specular highlight reconstruction.
2. **Extreme material SH ablation:** Train/evaluate on a curated subset of PBR‑Objaverse with known high‑frequency specular materials (e.g., chrome, mirrored surfaces). Compare SH degree 0 vs 2 vs 3 vs 4 (if implementation allows) on LPIPS and visual fidelity of sharp highlights.
3. **Pose robustness evaluation:** Generate from single‑image inputs that are deliberately rotated (e.g., +90°, −90° yaw) relative to the canonical upright pose. Measure orientation consistency error (e.g., angle between input view direction and principal object axis in output) to test the limits of the alignment prior.
