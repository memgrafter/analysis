# Random Forests as Statistical Procedures: Design, Variance, and Dependence

## Quick Facts
* **arXiv ID**: 2602.13104  
* **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13104  
* **Reference count**: 40  
* **Primary result**: A finite-sample variance decomposition for random forests reveals a strictly positive covariance floor; the Procedure‑Aligned Synthetic Resampling (PASR) estimator provides unbiased estimation of this floor and enables theoretically‑grounded uncertainty quantification for predictions.

## Executive Summary
Random forests are widely used but lack a finite‑sample theory for prediction uncertainty when trained on fixed data. This paper bridges that gap by introducing a design‑based variance decomposition that separates Monte Carlo error from an irreducible “covariance floor” \(C_T(x)\) arising from observation reuse and partition alignment. The floor persists even as the number of trees \(B\to\infty\). The authors propose the Procedure‑Aligned Synthetic Resampling (PASR) estimator, which generates synthetic outcomes from the fitted model, fits paired forests, and computes the covariance of their predictions. Theoretically, \(C_T(x)>0\) under minimal conditions, and PASR is unbiased for the floor under the fitted model at any aggregation level. For continuous outcomes the estimator is provably conservative; for binary outcomes it achieves asymptotic unbiasedness at rate \(O(n^{-2})\). Operational prediction intervals constructed from the PASR floor and within‑forest variance attain nominal coverage in extensive simulations, providing the first theoretically‑supported pointwise uncertainty quantification for predicted probabilities from classification forests.

## Method Summary
The method combines a characterization of the true covariance floor with a practical estimation procedure. In Stage 1, the true floor is estimated by simulating many independent responses \(Y^{(r)}\) from the data‑generating process at the observed covariates, fitting large random forests (\(B_{\text{true}}=8000\)) and computing the empirical variance of predictions across replications at points of interest. Stage 2 (PASR) works on a newly fitted forest (\(B_{\text{deploy}}\approx2000\)). For continuous outcomes, a cross‑fitted mean forest and a residual‑product variance forest estimate \(\hat{m}(X)\) and \(\hat{\sigma}(X)\); for binary outcomes, OOB probabilities provide \(\hat{p}(X)\). Synthetic datasets are drawn from these conditional models. For each synthetic dataset, two independent forests (each with \(B_{\text{mc}}=2000\) trees) are trained; their predictions at the target \(x\) are recorded. The PASR floor estimate \(\hat{C}_T(x)\) is the sample covariance across replicated pairs. The total variance for the original forest prediction combines the within‑forest variance \(\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}_T(x)/B_{\text{deploy}}\) (the Monte Carlo term) and \(\hat{C}_T(x)\); for continuous outcomes an additional \(\hat{\sigma}^2(x)\) term accounts for irreducible noise. Prediction/confidence intervals use this variance estimate.

## Key Results
- **Variance decomposition**: \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat{f}_B(x)\mid X) = \frac{1}{B}\,\sigma_T^2(x) + \frac{B-1}{B}\,C_T(x)\), where \(C_T(x)>0\) is the covariance floor.
- **PASR properties**: Under the fitted model, \(\hat{C}_T(x)\) is unbiased for the floor at any \(B_{\text{mc}}\); for continuous outcomes it is conservative, for binary outcomes it is asymptotically unbiased at rate \(O(n^{-2})\).
- **Coverage**: Operational intervals achieve nominal (e.g., 95%) coverage in simulations across diverse data‑generating processes, including high‑dimensional settings.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The theoretical and practical success hinge on three mechanisms.

**Mechanism 1: Variance decomposition separates finite‑aggregation noise from the covariance floor.**  
Conditional on the covariate configuration \(X\), the forest prediction is the average of \(B\) exchangeable random variables \(T_\theta(x)\). Theorem 1 yields the exact variance identity  
\[
\operatorname{Var}(\hat{f}_B(x)\mid X) = \frac{1}{B}\,\sigma_T^2(x) + \frac{B-1}{B}\,C_T(x),
\]  
where \(\sigma_T^2(x)\) is the average within‑tree variance and \(C_T(x)=\operatorname{Cov}(T_\theta,T_{\theta'}\mid X)\) is the covariance between any two distinct trees. The Monte Carlo term decays as \(1/B\), while \(C_T(x)\) persists as \(B\to\infty\) because trees are correlated.

**Mechanism 2: Observation reuse (shared outcomes) yields positive covariance.**  
Each tree computes a weighted average \(T_\theta(x)=\sum_{i=1}^n W_i(x;\theta)Y_i\). If a training observation appears (with positive weight) in two different trees, the outcome noise \(\varepsilon_i\) contributes to both predictions and induces positive covariance. Under the conditional independence of \(Y_i\) given \(X_i\), one can show  
\[
C_T(x) = \sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_i^2\,\mathbb{E}[W_i(x;\theta)W_i(x;\theta')\mid X],
\]  
where \(\sigma_i^2=\operatorname{Var}(Y_i\mid X_i)\). As soon as there exists an \(i\) with \(\sigma_i^2>0\) and \(\mathbb{P}(W_i(x;\theta)>0\mid X)>0\) (infinite‑sample conditions), the covariance floor is strictly positive (Theorem 2).

**Mechanism 3: Partition alignment (similar splits) creates covariance even without observation overlap.**  
Even if two trees are trained on disjoint sets of observations, they may use similar splits that route \(x\) to leaves averaging responses from the same covariate‑defined subpopulation. This “alignment” is captured by the conditional expectation \(Z_\theta(x)=\mathbb{E}[T_\theta(x)\mid I_\theta,X]\). The alignment component  
\[
C_{\text{align}}(x)=\operatorname{Cov}(Z_\theta(x),Z_{\theta'}(x)\mid X)
\]  
remains positive when the split decisions are adaptive to the data. Proposition 1 shows that increasing the candidate set size \(q\) raises the probability that two trees make identical split decisions along the path to \(x\), thereby increasing \(C_T(x)\). This mechanism explains the floor’s persistence even in honest forests that avoid reusing observations.

## Foundational Learning
- **Design‑based inference (conditioning on \(X\))**  
  *Why needed*: By fixing the realized covariate matrix, we isolate variability coming from the forest procedure itself (outcome noise \(Y|X\) and randomization \(\theta\)), rather than from sampling new covariates. This yields finite‑sample results applicable to any given dataset.  
  *Quick check*: What two random sources contribute to \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat{f}_B(x)\mid X)\)? (Answer: \(Y|X\) and the tree‑generating mechanism \(\theta\).)

- **Trees as weighted averages (\(T_\theta(x)=\sum_i W_i(x;\theta)Y_i\))**  
  *Why needed*: The weight representation makes explicit which observations influence a prediction and allows variance decompositions via the distribution of \(\mathbf{W}(x;\theta)\).  
  *Quick check*: For a fixed tree structure, what guarantee must the weights \(\{W_i\}\) satisfy? (Answer: \(W_i\ge0\) and \(\sum_i W_i=1\).)

- **Law of total covariance**  
  *Why needed*: Conditioning on the resampling indicators \(I_\theta\) (which observations enter a tree) separates observation‑reuse from partition alignment. The law of total covariance is the formal tool:  
  \(\operatorname{Cov}(T_\theta,T_{\theta'}\mid X)=\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Cov}(\cdot\mid I_\theta,I_{\theta'},X)]+\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbb{E}[\cdot\mid I_\theta,X],\mathbb{E}[\cdot\mid I_{\theta'},X])\).  
  *Quick check*: Write the decomposition above.

## Architecture Onboarding
The PASR estimator is built in a modular pipeline.

**Component map (plain‑text flow)**  
Deployed forest → Nuisance estimators (mean/variance or OOB probabilities) → Synthetic outcome generation → Pair of independent forests (each \(B_{\text{mc}}\)) → Covariance computation → PASR floor estimate.

**Critical path**  
a. Fit the deployed forest and store tree structures or an interface to compute weights.  
b. Estimate nuisance model(s): for continuous outcomes, cross‑fitted mean and residual‑product variance forests; for binary outcomes, OOB probabilities from the deployed forest.  
c. For \(r=1,\dots,R_{\text{syn}}\): draw synthetic \(Y^{(r)}\) from the fitted nuisance model, train two independent forests on \((X,Y^{(r)})\) with \(B_{\text{mc}}\) trees each, record predictions \(f_A^{(r)}(x)\), \(f_B^{(r)}(x)\).  
d. Compute \(\hat{C}_T(x)=\frac{1}{R_{\text{syn}}-1}\sum_r (f_A^{(r)}-\bar{f}_A)(f_B^{(r)}-\bar{f}_B)\).  
e. Compute within‑forest variance \(\hat{\sigma}_T^2(x)\) as the sample variance of the \(B_{\text{deploy}}\) tree predictions from the original forest.  
f. Assemble total variance: \(\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat{f}_B(x)\mid X)=\hat{\sigma}_T^2(x)/B_{\text{deploy}}+\hat{C}_T(x)\); for continuous outcomes add \(\hat{\sigma}^2(x)\).

**Design tradeoffs**  
- **\(R_{\text{syn}}\)**: Larger reduces Monte Carlo noise but increases runtime; typical \(R_{\text{syn}}=150\)–\(300\).  
- **\(B_{\text{mc}}\)**: Theorem 2 guarantees unbiasedness for any finite \(B_{\text{mc}}\); using \(B_{\text{mc}}=B_{\text{deploy}}\) avoids extra approximation error.  
- **Nuisance model complexity**: For continuous outcomes, saturated mean/variance forests (no subsampling, \(q=p\)) minimize nuisance bias, yielding conservative intervals (Proposition 3).  
- **High‑dimensional \(p\)**: Nuisance estimates become noisy; conservative bias for continuous outcomes increases and binary intervals may smooth; coverage remains nominal in stress tests (e.g., \(n=p=200\)).  
- **Candidate set size \(q\)**: PASR estimates the floor corresponding to the deployed forest’s \(q\); changing \(q\) for the nuisance forests would target a different floor.

**Failure signatures**  
- Continuous intervals undercover: Check that \(\hat{C}_T(x)\) is not underestimated (increase \(R_{\text{syn}}\) or \(B_{\text{mc}}\)), and that the conditional variance model \(\hat{\sigma}^2(x)\) is accurate.  
- Binary intervals undercover at low \(C_T\) quantiles: The PASR smoother pools low estimates upward; consider increasing \(R_{\text{syn}}\) or \(B_{\text{mc}}\).  
- Excessively wide intervals: May indicate over‑conservative nuisance estimates (continuous) or overly small leaf sizes; validate nuisance components out‑of‑sample.  
- High run‑to‑run variance of \(\hat{C}_T(x)\): Suggests insufficient \(R_{\text{syn}}\); increase synthetic replicates.  
- Negative \(\hat{C}_T(x)\): Monte Carlo noise; increase \(R_{\text{syn}}\) or \(B_{\text{mc}}\).  
- Numerical instability: Verify weight extraction and tree construction; the linear‑in‑\(Y\) representation guarantees Lipschitz continuity (Proposition 5).

**First 3 experiments (validation)**  
1. **Simple continuous DGM**: Linear model with Gaussian noise. Approximate true \(C_T(x)\) via many large‑forest replications. Run PASR (e.g., \(R_{\text{syn}}=150\)), verify that \(\hat{C}_T(x)\) is at least the true floor (conservative) and that the 95% prediction interval achieves ≥95% coverage on fresh responses.  
2. **Binary DGM with known \(p(x)\)**: Logistic model with interactions and centered cubic terms. Approximate true \(C_T(x)\). Run PASR; check bias of \(\hat{C}_T(x)\) near zero and that the 95% confidence interval for \(p(x)\) attains nominal coverage.  
3. **High‑dimensional stress test**: \(n=p=200\) with many noise predictors. Apply PASR for both outcome types; confirm continuous intervals remain conservative (>95% coverage) and binary intervals stay near nominal. Additionally, compute the single‑run standard deviation of \(\hat{C}_T(x)\) across 50 independent PASR runs to gauge stability.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **CLT for PASR estimator**: Deriving the asymptotic distribution of \(\hat{C}_T(x)\) would enable formal inference on the covariance floor itself.
- **Empirical‑quantile intervals and conformal prediction**: Using the distribution of synthetic predictions to construct empirical‑quantile intervals or linking with conformal methods is an unexplored direction.
- **Extensions to other forest procedures**: Developing PASR for causal forests, honest forests, survival forests, and quantile regression forests requires appropriate synthetic outcome models and coverage analysis.
- **Improving nuisance estimation for continuous outcomes**: Better estimators of the conditional mean and variance (e.g., regularized, different ML) could reduce the conservative bias in the PASR variance component, especially in high‑dimensional or small‑\(n\) regimes.

## Limitations
- **Implementation ambiguities**: The paper does not specify several practical details (e.g., minimum node size for deployed/nuisance forests, exact binary OOB aggregation), which complicates exact replication.
- **Computational burden**: PASR requires training \(2R_{\text{syn}}\) additional forests per analysis, limiting scalability to massive datasets or massive \(R_{\text{syn}}\) needed for very low Monte Carlo noise.
- **Theory reliance on assumptions**: Exchangeability of trees and conditional independence of observations may be violated in non‑standard ensemble methods (e.g., boosting, adaptive sampling).
