# Drop-In Perceptual Optimization for 3D Gaussian Splatting
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.23297
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23297
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Regularized Wasserstein Distortion (WD-R) as a drop-in loss improves perceptual quality: 2.3× human preference over original 3DGS, 1.5× over previous SOTA, and ~50% bitrate savings in compression.

## Executive Summary
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) often produces blurry renderings due to its reliance on ad-hoc pixel-level losses (L1/L2). This work systematically searches for perceptual distortion losses that better align with human perception, using a large-scale human study (39,320 ratings) to evaluate candidates. The regularized Wasserstein Distortion (WD-R) emerges as the top performer. WD-R can be directly substituted for the original loss in the 3DGS optimization pipeline without architectural changes. Extensive evaluations demonstrate that WD-R is strongly preferred by humans (2.3× over original 3DGS, 1.5× over prior SOTA) and achieves superior scores on LPIPS, DISTS, FID, and CMMD across multiple datasets. The improvement generalizes to other 3DGS frameworks (Mip-Splatting: 1.8× preference; Scaffold-GS: 3.6× preference) and yields approximately 50% bitrate savings in scene compression for comparable perceptual quality. However, the paper notes limitations including lack of transparency in the human study, unquantified computational overhead, and the need for a warm-up phase during training. Open questions remain regarding the cause of artifacts in WD and the potential of adaptive strategies.

## Method Summary
The authors replace the standard L1/L2 loss in 3D Gaussian Splatting with a regularized Wasserstein Distortion (WD-R) loss. This substitution is a drop-in change that requires no modifications to the 3DGS architecture. WD-R was selected through a systematic empirical search over perceptual distortion losses, with final validation via a large-scale human preference study (39,320 ratings). The loss computes a regularized Wasserstein distance between feature distributions of the rendered and ground-truth images, aiming to better capture perceptual similarity. Training employs a warm-up phase with the original loss to stabilize optimization before switching to WD-R. The approach is evaluated on multiple datasets and generalized to other 3DGS variants, showing consistent improvements in perceptual metrics and human preference.

## Key Results
- Human preference: WD-R is preferred 2.3× over original 3DGS loss and 1.5× over previous state-of-the-art.
- Perceptual metrics: WD-R achieves superior LPIPS, DISTS, FID, and CMMD scores across all tested datasets.
- Generalization: Improvements hold for Mip-Splatting (1.8× preference) and Scaffold-GS (3.6× preference) within comparable resource budgets.
- Compression: WD-R yields ~50% bitrate savings for similar perceptual quality in scene compression tasks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
WD-R improves perceptual quality by measuring distortion via a regularized Wasserstein distance between feature distributions, which better aligns with human perception than pixel-level losses. The regularization mitigates the "web-like artifacts" observed with unregularized WD when the number of splats is limited. However, the paper does not provide a deep theoretical analysis of why WD-R outperforms composite losses (e.g., L1+L2+MS-SSIM+LPIPS); it is identified empirically. The authors explicitly state that understanding the root cause of WD artifacts and developing a principled remedy remain open questions. Thus, while WD-R is effective, its mechanism is not fully elucidated.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Ground truth images + Gaussian parameters -> Renderer -> Rendered images -> WD-R loss -> Optimizer -> Gaussian parameters
- **Critical path**: Initialize Gaussians (e.g., from SfM) -> iterate: render, compute WD-R loss, backpropagate, update Gaussians, adaptively densify/prune -> convergence.
- **Design tradeoffs**: Requires a warm-up phase with original loss to avoid instability; may need more Gaussians to prevent artifacts; balances perceptual quality against memory/rendering speed.
- **Failure signatures**: Web-like artifacts when splats are too sparse; training spikes/NaNs if WD-R used without warm-up; over-smoothing if regularization too strong.
- **First experiments**: (1) Train WD-R from scratch without warm-up to test necessity; (2) Ablate regularization strength to find optimal trade-off; (3) Explore adaptive σ strategies for spatially varying pooling.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: What causes the "web-like artifacts" in Wasserstein Distortion (WD)–based 3DGS when splat count is constrained, and how can they be eliminated without adding pixel-level regularization?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The Discussion (Page 11) explicitly states that "understanding and addressing the root cause [of WD artifacts] and developing a more principled remedy remains an open question." Artifacts are visualized in Fig. 6 and only mitigated heuristically by combining WD with the original loss (WD-R).
- Why unresolved: The underlying mechanism is not analyzed; WD-R suppresses artifacts but offers no theoretical guarantee or insight into when and why they occur.
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments varying texture frequency and splat density to map failure modes; gradient analysis of WD in sparse regions; study of covariance matrix statistics during optimization; perhaps deriving stability conditions for WD under adaptive densification.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Is the warm-up phase (initial training with the original 3DGS loss) necessary for stable and high-quality WD-based 3DGS training?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Appendix A.3 notes that "a deeper investigation (e.g. eliminating warm-up or using smoother schedules) is a worthwhile direction for future work," highlighting the warm-up strategy as an open empirical question.
- Why unresolved: The authors use warm-up based on common practice in perceptual training but have not tested its necessity or compared it to alternative stabilization methods.
- What evidence would resolve it: Ablation studies training WD from scratch without warm-up across multiple datasets, monitoring training stability (loss spikes, NaNs), final perceptual metrics, and human preference; comparison to other stabilizers like gradient clipping or adaptive learning rates.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can spatially adaptive pooling kernel sizes (σ) in Wasserstein Distortion yield consistent and significant perceptual improvements over a fixed σ across diverse scenes and textures?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The Discussion (Page 11) and Appendix C.2 propose investigating "more effective spatially varying σ strategies" as future work, after a preliminary saliency-guided approach showed only occasional gains.
- Why unresolved: No adaptive σ method has been shown to reliably outperform the fixed σ=4 baseline; the potential for learned σ maps remains unexplored.
- What evidence would resolve it: Develop a learning-based σ predictor (e.g., a small CNN) conditioned on local image content; evaluate on textures with mixed spatial frequencies; conduct human preference studies and measure LPIPS/DISTS/FID to validate improvements; analyze per-region σ choices.

### Open Question 4
- Question: Why does the composite loss (weighted sum of L1, L2, MS-SSIM, LPIPS) require higher Gaussian counts yet underperform WD-R on perceptual metrics despite including a perceptual term (LPIPS)?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] Tab. 1 shows composite loss achieves worse LPIPS/DISTS/FID than WD-R with similar or higher splat counts. The authors describe tuning composite weights as "non-trivial and engineering-intensive" (Sec. 3.1).
- Why unresolved: There is no analysis of why combining multiple losses leads to inefficient resource usage; conflicting gradients or suboptimal weighting are suspected but unverified.
- What evidence would resolve it: Analyze gradient magnitudes and cosine similarities between loss components during training; perform a systematic grid search for optimal weights under a fixed splat budget; investigate whether composite loss overfits to pixel-level errors or suffers from gradient cancellation.

## Limitations
- Human study lacks transparency on rater demographics, viewing conditions, and potential biases.
- Computational overhead of WD-R (time, memory) is not quantified; claim of comparable resource budgets is unverified.
- Bitrate savings (~50%) shown only for specific compression settings; broader applicability uncertain.
- Generalization tested on only two other frameworks (Mip-Splatting, Scaffold-GS); may not hold for all 3DGS variants.
- No public code release, hindering independent replication and further analysis.

## Confidence
- **High**: Perceptual metric improvements (LPIPS, DISTS, FID, CMMD) – consistent across multiple datasets.
- **Medium**: Human preference results (2.3× over original, 1.5× over SOTA) – limited study metadata, possible sample bias.
- **Medium**: Generalization to other 3DGS frameworks – positive but only evaluated on two.
- **Low**: Bitrate savings – highly dependent on encoder and bitrate control choices.
