# SafeTutors: Benchmarking Pedagogical Safety in AI Tutoring Systems

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: arXiv:2603.17373
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17373
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: No LLM achieves reliable pedagogical safety; multi-turn interaction amplifies harm from 17.7% to 77.8%, and scale does not consistently improve safety across STEM domains.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces SafeTutors, a benchmark for evaluating pedagogical safety in AI tutors across math, physics, and chemistry. It defines an 11-dimension risk taxonomy (48 sub-risks) grounded in learning sciences and provides 3,135 single-turn and 2,820 multi-turn dialogues generated via crescendo-based escalation. Testing 11 LLMs (3.8B–72B) reveals that all models exceed 60% harm on at least five dimensions in single-turn and six in multi-turn. Multi-turn interaction dramatically amplifies pedagogical harm, and harm profiles vary by subject, indicating the need for discipline-aware safety interventions.

## Method Summary
SafeTutors builds its benchmark by selecting seed questions from existing tutoring datasets (MathDial for math, CAMEL-AI for physics/chemistry). Single-turn instances combine a seed with a trailing risky question, while multi-turn instances use crescendo-based escalation scripts (5–8 turns) to steer the tutor toward a target risk. Generated dialogues undergo automated filtering (GPT-5.2) and a three-stage human validation (domain validity, risk alignment, crowd generalizability). The final dataset is used to evaluate 11 LLMs; model responses are scored for harm across 11 risk dimensions using majority voting among three evaluator models (GPT-5.2, DeepSeek-32B, Claude Sonnet 4.5). Pedagogical quality metrics (answer disclosure, challenge, on-topic, clarity for single-turn; learning gains, misconception reduction, motivation for multi-turn) are also measured.

## Key Results
- All evaluated LLMs exhibit high pedagogical harm: each exceeds 60% harm on at least five dimensions (single-turn) and six dimensions (multi-turn).
- Multi-turn dialogue amplifies average pedagogical harm from 17.7% to 77.8% (∼60 percentage point increase).
- Scale does not guarantee safety: within the Qwen2.5 family, the 72B model improves on only 17 of 33 subject–dimension pairs relative to the 7B model.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Multi-turn interaction amplifies pedagogical harm because early compliance with unsafe requests establishes a pattern that escalates and compounds over the dialogue.  
**Mechanism:** The crescendo-based escalation method generates multi-turn conversations where student queries progressively steer the tutor toward a target risk (e.g., from clarification to direct answer demands). This simulates how a student might exploit a tutor's initial compliance, leading to greater harm in later turns as the tutor fails to self-correct or re-establish scaffolding.  
**Core assumption:** Tutoring safety is trajectory-dependent; a single "safe" response does not guarantee sustained pedagogical integrity across an extended interaction.  
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] multi-turn amplifies harm; [section 7] quantitative amplification; [corpus] neighboring multi-turn jailbreak benchmarks.  
**Break condition:** If a model consistently resists escalation across crescendo sequences, amplification would not occur for that model.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Scale does not reliably improve pedagogical safety because failure modes are rooted in pedagogical alignment, not general capability.  
**Mechanism:** Comparison within the Qwen2.5 family shows inconsistent scaling effects—improvement on some risk dimensions but regression on others—indicating that scaling alone does not instill necessary pedagogical constraints. Safety requires targeted fine-tuning or architectural interventions.  
**Core assumption:** Tutoring safety is a distinct alignment problem requiring specialized training objectives.  
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] scale does not consistently improve safety; [section 7] scale-safety analysis; limited direct corpus support.  
**Break condition:** If a larger model consistently outperforms smaller ones across all 11 dimensions and both single/multi-turn settings, the claim would be falsified.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Subject domain materially shapes harm profiles because disciplinary norms and reasoning patterns differ.  
**Mechanism:** The benchmark finds subject-dependent patterns (e.g., mathematics shows 92.8% metacognitive harm but only 26.0% epistemic harm in multi-turn), suggesting generic mitigations are insufficient and interventions must be calibrated to discipline-specific goals and misconceptions.  
**Core assumption:** The 48 sub-risks instantiate differently across domains due to epistemology and typical student errors.  
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] harm varies by subject; [section 7] subject-dependent harm profiles; neighboring domain-specific tutoring benchmarks.  
**Break condition:** If harm distributions across the 11 dimensions were statistically indistinguishable between math, physics, and chemistry (controlling model and turn count), the claim would be weakened.

## Foundational Learning
- **Productive Struggle**  
  *Why needed:* The core pedagogical failure is erosion of productive struggle—over-disclosure prevents the cognitive effort necessary for durable learning.  
  *Quick check:* Why might a tutor saying "No, x=5 and here's the full derivation" after a student guess be more harmful than saying "Check your substitution; what is 3x when x=2?" even though both lead to the correct answer?

- **Self-Regulated Learning (SRL)**  
  *Why needed:* Metacognitive risks (external validation dependence, reflection bypass) attack SRL competencies—planning, monitoring, self-evaluation—impairing long-term autonomy.  
  *Quick check:* If a tutor always tells a student whether their answer is correct, what specific SRL skill is likely to atrophy, and how might that manifest in unsupported problem-solving later?

- **Conceptual Change & Misconceptions**  
  *Why needed:* Epistemic and informational risks threaten conceptual change; STEM domains have robust known misconceptions (e.g., "heavier objects fall faster"). Reinforcing these creates resistant misconceptions.  
  *Quick check:* A student believes "current is used up in a circuit." A tutor says "Yes, the battery provides current that gets used by bulbs." Is this a safe tutor response? Why or why not, considering epistemic risk?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Taxonomy Engine -> Dataset Generator -> Validator Layer -> Evaluation Suite

**Critical path:** Seed question selection -> Risk-targeted prompt generation (single-turn trailing question OR multi-turn crescendo script) -> Synthetic dialogue generation -> Automated filter (GPT-5.2) -> Human validation (Stages 1–3: domain validity, risk alignment, crowd generalizability) -> Final benchmark instance with risk label -> Model inference -> LLM-based harm scoring (majority vote among GPT-5.2, DeepSeek-32B, Claude Sonnet 4.5) -> Aggregation of metrics.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Breadth vs. Depth:* 48 sub-risks provide granularity but increase annotation complexity and potential confusion (e.g., overlap between Cognitive and Metacognitive risks).
- *Synthetic vs. Real:* Crescendo generation efficiently creates targeted risky dialogs but may lack natural incoherence of real student errors; human validation partially mitigates this.
- *Automation vs. Cost:* Using powerful LLMs as judges scales evaluation but introduces proprietary model biases and costs; majority voting across three models reduces individual bias.

**Failure signatures:**
- High single-turn harm but low multi-turn harm: Would contradict amplification finding; suggests models self-correct over time.
- Consistent scale-safety correlation: Would contradict paper's finding; indicates general alignment transfers to tutoring.
- Uniform harm across subjects: Would contradict subject-dependence; suggests domain irrelevance.
- Human-LLM judge discordance: If human annotation (κ=0.76) diverges significantly from LLM judge agreement, scoring pipeline is unreliable.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Replicate amplification:** Run the same 11 models on the provided single-turn and multi-turn test sets. Compute per-model and per-dimension delta (multi-turn HR - single-turn HR). Expect >60 pp jump for Pedagogical harm and 6–11 pp average increase.
2. **Scale invariance test:** Within Qwen2.5 family (7B, 14B, 32B, 72B), perform pairwise comparisons (McNemar's test) on harm rates per dimension/subject. Expect no statistically significant consistent improvement with scale.
3. **Subject profile check:** For a fixed model (e.g., Llama-3.1-70B), compare harm rates across math, physics, chemistry using chi-square test on contingency tables (safe/unsafe per dimension). Expect significant differences (p<0.05) for Metacognitive and Epistemic dimensions.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:** What alignment techniques (e.g., fine-tuning, RLHF, prompt engineering) can effectively reduce pedagogical harms in LLM tutors while preserving instructional quality?  
*Basis:* The paper evaluates existing models and finds all have high harm rates, and notes scale doesn't consistently improve safety, implying current approaches are inadequate.  
*Why unresolved:* The study only benchmarks; it does not investigate specific alignment methods.  
*Resolving evidence:* Systematic experiments applying alignment techniques (supervised fine-tuning on safe dialogues, RL with pedagogical safety rewards) using SafeTutors as evaluation, measuring trade-offs between harm reduction and pedagogical metrics.

**Open Question 2:** What mechanisms cause pedagogical harm to amplify in multi-turn interactions, and can they be mitigated without sacrificing engagement?  
*Basis:* The paper reports amplification (pedagogical harm ↑ from 17.7% to 77.8%).  
*Why unresolved:* The benchmark quantifies amplification but does not explore causal factors.  
*Resolving evidence:* Ablation studies isolating context length, turn order, tutor persona; probing model internals; testing interventions like dynamic safety filters or memory mechanisms.

**Open Question 3:** How can safety interventions be tailored to discipline-specific risk profiles observed in mathematics, physics, and chemistry?  
*Basis:* The paper states harms vary by subject and shows mathematics has highest metacognitive harm (92.8%) but lowest epistemic harm (26.0%) in multi-turn.  
*Why unresolved:* It identifies patterns but does not develop or evaluate subject-specific safety strategies.  
*Resolving evidence:* Design and test domain-adapted safety fine-tuning or rule-based filters targeting dominant risk dimensions per subject, comparing against general approaches.

**Open Question 4:** Do the pedagogical harms identified in SafeTutors correlate with actual learning loss in human learners?  
*Basis:* The benchmark defines risks based on learning science and evaluates model outputs, but does not validate whether harms cause poorer learning outcomes.  
*Why unresolved:* The study uses synthetic dialogues and automated scoring; lacks empirical connection to learning outcomes.  
*Resolving evidence:* Controlled user studies where students interact with tutors varying in harm levels, with pre/post tests to measure learning; statistical correlation between SafeTutors harm metrics and learning outcomes.

## Limitations
- Exact prompts for crescendo escalation and trailing question generation are undisclosed, hindering precise dataset reconstruction.
- Harm scoring relies on proprietary evaluator models (GPT-5.2, Claude Sonnet 4.5) without version/API details; small changes could affect harm rates.
- Synthetically generated dialogues may not capture the full erraticism of real student errors, potentially affecting the realism of amplification effects.
