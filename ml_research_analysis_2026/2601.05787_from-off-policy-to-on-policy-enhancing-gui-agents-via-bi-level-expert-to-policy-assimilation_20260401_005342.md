# From Off-Policy to On-Policy: Enhancing GUI Agents via Bi-level Expert-to-Policy Assimilation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.05787
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05787
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** BEPA improves UITARS1.5-7B success on OSWorld-Verified from 22.87% to 32.13% and on a held-out split from 5.74% to 10.30%, with consistent gains on MMBench-GUI and Online-Mind2Web.

## Executive Summary
Training end-to-end GUI agents with only a small set of expert trajectories is challenging because directly mixing such off-policy data into reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) causes distribution mismatches. This paper introduces Bi-level Expert-to-Policy Assimilation (BEPA), a method that progressively bridges the gap between expert demonstrations and the learner’s policy. Level‑1 converts expert plans into policy‑reachable trajectories that respect the current policy’s action distribution, providing high‑quality on‑policy‑like training data. Level‑2 maintains a dynamic cache of successful rollouts and only invokes it when the policy exhibits total exploration failure (i.e., all actions have very low probability). On the OSWorld‑Verified benchmark, BEPA lifts the success rate of UITARS1.5‑7B from 22.87% to 32.13% and from 5.74% to 10.30% on a held‑out split, with similar improvements on MMBench‑GUI and Online‑Mind2Web. These results demonstrate that careful, progressive assimilation of expert knowledge yields substantial gains over naive baselines such as SFT+RL or static off‑policy mixing.

## Method Summary
BEPA operates in two stages. First, it uses existing expert high‑level plans to generate trajectories that are reachable by the current policy (Level‑1). This involves aligning the expert’s intended actions with the policy’s probability mass, effectively creating on‑policy demonstrations from off‑policy data. Second, during online reinforcement learning, the agent maintains a cache of trajectories that have led to successful task completion. When the policy’s action distribution indicates total exploration failure—defined by a confidence threshold—the system retrieves a suitable cached action to guide the agent. The cache is updated dynamically as new successes are encountered. This bi‑level design ensures that expert knowledge is introduced in a way that respects the learner’s evolving distribution, while the fallback mechanism prevents catastrophic failures during exploration.

## Key Results
- On OSWorld‑Verified, BEPA raises UITARS1.5‑7B success from 22.87% to 32.13%.
- On a held‑out OSWorld split, success improves from 5.74% to 10.30%.
- Consistent gains are observed on MMBench‑GUI and Online‑Mind2Web, outperforming baselines like SFT+RL and static off‑policy mixing.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
BEPA works because it systematically reduces the distribution shift between expert data and the learner’s policy. Level‑1 ensures that the training trajectories derived from experts are within the support of the current policy, providing a sensible initialization and avoiding the pitfalls of raw off‑policy learning. Level‑2 introduces a safety net: only when the policy is utterly uncertain does it borrow from a cache of proven successful behavior. This targeted intervention minimizes unnecessary corruption of the learning signal while guaranteeing that the agent can recover from dead‑ends. The progressive nature—starting with heavily guided, policy‑compatible data and gradually letting the policy rely more on its own decisions—stabilizes training and yields higher final performance, as evidenced by consistent improvements across multiple GUI benchmarks.

## Foundational Learning
**Policy‑Reachable Trajectory Generation**  
*Why needed:* To convert off‑policy expert plans into actions that the current policy could realistically take, thereby creating on‑policy‑like demonstrations that align with the learner’s distribution.  
*Quick check:* After conversion, verify that at least 90% of the actions lie within the policy’s top‑5 probability mass on a held‑out set of states.

**Dynamic Cache of Successful Rollouts**  
*Why needed:* Provides a reservoir of proven behavior that can be retrieved when the policy fails, preventing catastrophic mistakes and reinforcing effective strategies.  
*Quick check:* Following a successful episode, assert that its trajectory is stored in the cache and can be retrieved for a state that closely matches one encountered during that episode.

**Total Exploration Failure Detection**  
*Why needed:* Acts as a trigger for when to invoke the cache, ensuring that guidance is only used when the policy is maximally uncertain, thereby preserving exploratory autonomy.  
*Quick check:* During rollouts, log the maximum action probability; if it falls below the predefined threshold, confirm that the cache is consulted and an action is overridden.

**Two‑Level Assimilation Framework**  
*Why needed:* The bi‑level structure enables a smooth transition from expert‑dominated training to policy‑driven learning, which is crucial when expert data is scarce.  
*Quick check:* Ablate the two‑level design (e.g., remove Level‑1 or Level‑2) and measure performance degradation on a validation set.

**Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)**  
*Why needed:* Supplies the scalar feedback that drives policy improvement toward task success.  
*Quick check:* Ensure that the reward function correctly reflects task completion (e.g., final binary success signal) and is consistently provided at episode end.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Expert Planner -> Policy‑Reachable Converter -> Expert Adaptation Buffer -> RL Trainer -> Policy.  
Policy -> Environment.  
Environment ->> Cache (on successful episode).  
Failure Detector <- Policy; Failure Detector ->> Guided Action Selector (on total failure).  
Guided Action Selector ->> Policy (action override).

**Critical path**  
1. Observe state s_t.  
2. Policy computes distribution π(a|s_t).  
3. Failure Detector checks max_a π(a|s_t) < θ.  
4. If failure: retrieve cached action a_c from Cache for s_t (or nearest neighbor) and execute; else sample a ∼ π.  
5. Execute a, observe r_t, s_{t+1}.  
6. On episode success, store trajectory (s_{0:T}, a_{0:T}) in Cache.  
7. Periodically, RL Trainer updates Policy using trajectories from Expert Adaptation Buffer and online data.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- *Cache size*: Bigger cache improves coverage but costs memory and retrieval time; too small may lack appropriate fallback.  
- *Failure threshold θ*: Lower values mean rarer guidance (more exploration) but risk dead‑ends; higher values increase support but may shorten exploration.  
- *Policy‑reachable conversion strictness*: Strict conversion (e.g., only top‑k actions) ensures alignment but discards some expert intent; lenient conversion retains more expertise but risks introducing out‑of‑distribution actions.

**Failure signatures**  
- Frequent activation of Failure Detector across many episodes indicates the policy is not improving.  
- Cache miss during failure (no similar state) leads to random action choice and likely episode termination.  
- High variance in training returns suggests improper Level‑1 conversion or cache interference.

**First three experiments**  
1. *Level‑1 conversion sanity*: Generate policy‑reachable trajectories from expert plans; measure the proportion of actions within the policy’s top‑5 probability mass. Expected >90%.  
2. *Cache fallback test*: Force failure by masking policy output; check that cached actions successfully complete a validation task at least 80% of the time.  
3. *Baseline comparison*: Run BEPA and SFT+RL on 10 OSWorld‑Verified tasks; expect BEPA to achieve higher average success rate with statistical significance.

## Limitations
- Generalization gap: Evaluations on synthetic OSWorld tasks may not reflect real‑world GUI complexity (dynamic content, proprietary software).  
- Absolute performance: Success rates remain low (e.g., 32.13% on OSWorld‑Verified), limiting practical deployment.  
- Cache mechanism sensitivity: Robustness to cache size, update frequency, and failure‑threshold choices is unclear.

## Confidence
- **High**: Reported improvements on OSWorld‑Verified, MMBench‑GUI, and Online‑Mind2Web are empirically supported with specific metrics.  
- **Medium**: The claim that “progressive assimilation” is key is plausible but rests on comparisons against limited baselines; broader ablation is needed.  
- **Low**: Assertions about real‑world applicability are speculative without testing on production‑grade GUI environments.

## Next Checks
1. Cross‑dataset robustness: Test BEPA on at least two additional real‑world GUI benchmarks (e.g., WebArena‑Live, commercial software suites) to measure generalization beyond synthetic tasks.  
2. Cache mechanism analysis: Systematically vary cache capacity (e.g., 50–500 rollouts) and update triggers (e.g., failure rate thresholds) to identify sensitivity and optimal configuration per task domain.  
3. Comparison with advanced on‑policy methods: Replicate experiments using modern on‑policy RLVR or inverse RL techniques (e.g., from neighbor papers like “In‑Context Steered Policy Optimization”) to validate BEPA’s relative advantage under identical low‑expert‑data constraints.
