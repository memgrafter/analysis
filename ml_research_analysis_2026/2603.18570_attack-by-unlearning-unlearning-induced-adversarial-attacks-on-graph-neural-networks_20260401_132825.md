# Attack by Unlearning: Unlearning-Induced Adversarial Attacks on Graph Neural Networks

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.18570  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18570  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Injecting nodes and then requesting their deletion can amplify approximate unlearning degradation, causing up to ~59% accuracy drop on Pubmed while remaining stealthy.

## Executive Summary
Graph neural networks (GNNs) are increasingly deployed in applications requiring data deletion rights, such as privacy regulations. Machine unlearning aims to remove the influence of deleted data from a trained model. However, approximate unlearning methods, which avoid full retraining for efficiency, often suffer from subtle performance degradations. This paper introduces a novel adversarial attack that exploits these degradations: an attacker injects a small number of malicious nodes into the training graph and later requests their deletion, causing a severe accuracy collapse on the model's test nodes. The attack, termed OptimAttack, formulates a bi-level optimization problem where the attacker crafts the injected nodes' features and edges to maximize the post-unlearning accuracy drop. The inner loop approximates the unlearning operator via one gradient step on a surrogate GNN, while the outer loop optimizes the injection parameters using pseudo-labels from the surrogate. A regularization term ensures that unlearning benign nodes does not harm performance, maintaining stealth. Experiments on four datasets across three unlearning algorithms show that OptimAttack achieves up to ~59% accuracy drop on Pubmed, significantly outperforming existing baselines, while preserving pre-unlearning utility and transferring across GNN architectures.

## Method Summary
OptimAttack solves the unlearning corruption attack via a bi-level optimization. The attacker controls injected nodes (ΔV) and can modify their features (X_inj) and edges to/from them (A_inter, A_intra) within a budget (each injected node adds at most B edges). The attack objective, L_atk = loss_after_unlearning_ΔV - λ · loss_after_unlearning_V0, combines two terms: one maximizes the loss on unlabeled test nodes after the victim unlearns ΔV, and the other (λ‑weighted) minimizes the loss after unlearning a benign set V0 to preserve stealth. Because test labels are unavailable, the attacker trains a surrogate GNN on the injected graph and uses its predictions as pseudo‑labels. The unlearning process is approximated by a single gradient ascent step: θ_un = θ_inj - η_un ∇_θ g_γ, where g_γ is the retention loss on clean labeled nodes minus γ times the forgetting loss on the unlearned nodes (γ = 1). The attacker then differentiates through this update to obtain gradients w.r.t. the injection variables, performs T gradient ascent steps (with learning rates η_a for edges, η_x for features), and finally projects continuous edges to binary by keeping the top‑B connections per injected node. The resulting injected graph is fed to the victim, who trains a GNN and later applies an approximate unlearning method (e.g., GIF, CEU, GA) to ΔV. The attack is evaluated by measuring ΔAcc = accuracy_before_unlearning - accuracy_after_unlearning on the test set, while also checking that unlearning a benign set V0 does not degrade performance.

## Key Results
- OptimAttack achieves up to ~59% ΔAcc on Pubmed under GIF/CEU, far surpassing baselines like Random, Copy, NewCopy, TestCopy, TestLink, SBA, UGBA, and TDGIA.
- The attack maintains pre‑unlearning accuracy within ~1% of the clean model, satisfying the stealth requirement.
- The crafted injections demonstrate transferability across different GNN architectures (e.g., GCN, GraphSAGE) and unlearning algorithms (GIF, CEU, GA), indicating robustness.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
*Claim:* The attack amplifies the inevitable performance degradation from approximate graph unlearning by injecting nodes that appear benign during training but cause severe accuracy collapse upon their removal.
*Mechanism:* The attacker carefully crafts injected node features and edges so that the approximate unlearning operator (e.g., gradient ascent or influence function) fails to cleanly remove their influence. This results in a disproportionate disruption to the model’s predictions on the unlabeled target nodes. The injected nodes are optimized to maximize the loss on V_U after unlearning ΔV, while ensuring they do not noticeably harm pre‑unlearning performance.
*Core assumption:* Approximate unlearning is imperfect and its residual influence can be predictably shaped; the attacker can model the unlearning dynamics (e.g., via gradient approximation) to design destructive injections.
*Evidence anchors:*
- [abstract] “approximate unlearning often incurs subtle performance degradation, which may incur negative and unintended side effects. In this work, we show that such degradations can be amplified into adversarial attacks.”
- [section 4.1] “the model performs normally during training, but accuracy collapses only after unlearning is applied.”
- [corpus] The corpus paper “Machine Unlearning Fails to Remove Data Poisoning Attacks” (arxiv:2511.01377) reports that unlearning may not fully eliminate malicious data effects, supporting the existence of inherent weaknesses. However, direct evidence for our specific amplification strategy is limited in the corpus.
*Break condition:* If unlearning is exact (full retraining) or the injected nodes are so disruptive they are detected during training (violating G2), the attack fails.

### Mechanism 2
*Claim:* A bi‑level optimization framework solves the attack problem by simulating the black‑box unlearning process and overcoming label scarcity via a surrogate model with pseudo‑labels.
*Mechanism:* The upper level optimizes the continuous representations of injected edges and features. The lower level approximates the unlearning operator using a one‑step gradient update (Eq. 5) on a surrogate GNN. The surrogate’s predictions provide pseudo‑labels for the unlabeled nodes V_U, enabling the construction of the attack objective L_atk (Eq. 6) that balances post‑unlearning damage (first term) and stealth under benign unlearning (second term with λ).
*Core assumption:* The one‑step gradient approximation sufficiently captures the unlearning dynamics; the surrogate model’s behavior is close enough to the victim’s to guide optimization toward effective attack vectors.
*Evidence anchors:*
- [abstract] “We formulate this attack as a bi‑level optimization problem: to overcome the challenges of black‑box unlearning and label scarcity, we approximate the unlearning process via gradient‑based updates and employ a surrogate model to generate pseudo‑labels for the optimization.”
- [section 5.1] “we employ a one‑step approximation ... This approximation has two advantages. First, it requires only a single gradient step ... Second, since the formulation of Solve consists entirely of differentiable operations, we can compute gradients through the unlearning process via standard automatic differentiation.”
- [section 5.2] “Since the attacker lacks access to the ground‑truth labels ... we adopt a surrogate‑modeling approach, where the attacker trains a surrogate GNN model 𝑓 on the injected graph eG and uses its predictions to construct pseudo labels.”
- [corpus] The corpus paper “Exploiting Machine Unlearning for Backdoor Attacks in Deep Learning System” (arxiv:2512.18035) uses surrogate modeling for unlearning attacks in non‑graph domains, but the bi‑level formulation for graphs is not directly evidenced.
*Break condition:* The gradient approximation diverges from true unlearning; the surrogate model’s architecture or training differs too much from the victim, rendering the optimized injection ineffective when transferred.

### Mechanism 3
*Claim:* Stealthiness during both training and benign unlearning is enforced via explicit constraints and a regularization term that isolates damage to the targeted unlearning request.
*Mechanism:* The attack optimizes under an edge budget (Eq. 2) to limit injection detectability. More critically, it includes a constraint (Eq. 3) bounding the loss after unlearning a benign node set V₀ (excluding ΔV). In the surrogate objective (Eq. 6), a λ‑weighted term penalizes damage from benign unlearning, ensuring the corruption is specific to the attacker’s request.
*Core assumption:* The attacker can define a representative benign set V₀ that reflects normal unlearning traffic; the regularization weight λ can be tuned to satisfy G3 without sacrificing G1.
*Evidence anchors:*
- [abstract] “while maintaining pre‑unlearning utility and stealth under benign unlearning.”
- [section 4.2] “Eq. (3) upper bounds the loss on V_U after unlearning a benign node subset V₀ ... ensuring that benign‑only unlearning requests do not cause a significant performance drop (G3).”
- [section 5.2] “L_atk := ∑ L(after unlearning ΔV) - λ ∑ L(after unlearning V₀), where the second term discourages significant degradation under benign‑only unlearning requests.”
- [corpus] Evidence for this specific constraint design is not prominent in the corpus; related works focus on other aspects of unlearning security.
*Break condition:* If V₀ is not representative of real benign unlearning, the constraint may not generalize; if λ is mis‑set, either stealthiness or attack effectiveness is compromised.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Attacker (Injection module, Optimization module, Surrogate model) → Victim (Training pipeline, Unlearning service) → Data (Original graph G, injected graph eG, labeled set V_L, unlabeled set V_U, injected nodes ΔV, benign unlearning set V₀).

Critical path:
1. Attacker computes injection parameters using G and surrogate model.
2. Victim trains GNN on eG → θ_inj.
3. Attacker submits unlearning request for ΔV.
4. Victim applies unlearning → θ_un.
5. Performance on V_U is evaluated (ΔAcc = Acc_before - Acc_after).

Design tradeoffs:
- *Effectiveness vs Stealthiness:* Higher λ improves stealth (G3) but may reduce attack damage (G1); edge budget B limits detection risk.
- *Computational cost:* Full optimization over all possible connections is O(mn); scalability is achieved via neighborhood restriction (Section 5.4).
- *Surrogate fidelity:* Surrogate model architecture and training should mimic victim to ensure transferability; using a different unlearning algorithm for the surrogate (e.g., GA) than the victim (e.g., GIF) is a key assumption.

Failure signatures:
- *Low ΔAcc (<5%):* Attack ineffective; injection did not exploit unlearning dynamics.
- *High pre‑unlearning drop:* Original accuracy significantly below clean baseline → stealthiness violated.
- *Benign unlearning damage:* Unlearning V₀ causes large drop → constraint/regularization failed, may trigger detection.

First 3 experiments:
1. *Main comparison (Table 1):* Evaluate OptimAttack vs baselines across datasets (Cora, Citeseer, Pubmed, Flickr) and unlearning algorithms (GIF, CEU, GA). Metrics: Original accuracy, unlearned accuracy, ΔAcc.
2. *Ablation on stealthiness regularization (Table 2):* Remove λ term (λ=0) to show its necessity in preserving benign unlearning utility (measured by Benign F1).
3. *Budget sensitivity (Figure 2):* Vary edge budget B and unlearning ratio to demonstrate attack efficiency and stability; even small budgets (B=5, 5% nodes) achieve near‑peak ΔAcc.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Scaling to billion‑node graphs:** The paper mentions refining the approximation for scalability (Conclusion) but provides no solution; requires efficient optimization for huge graphs.
- **Robustness of one‑step gradient approximation:** No theoretical analysis; may fail for complex unlearning algorithms or large graphs.
- **Impact of surrogate model quality:** Attack success depends on surrogate fidelity; sensitivity to architecture, training data, and label noise is unexplored.
- **Extension to other unlearning types and tasks:** Only node unlearning for node classification is considered; edge/feature unlearning and other tasks (link prediction, graph classification) remain open.

## Limitations
- Exact data splits, training hyperparameters, and regularization weight λ are not disclosed, hindering exact replication and sensitivity analysis.
- The assumption that a one‑step gradient approximation on a surrogate model accurately captures victim unlearning dynamics is not rigorously validated across diverse unlearning algorithms and graph scales.
- The benign unlearning set V₀ used to enforce stealth may not represent real‑world unlearning request distributions, potentially causing the attack to be detectable in practice.
