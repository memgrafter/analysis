# Ensembles-based Feature Guided Analysis

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.19653
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19653
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** EFGA (TOP(10)) boosts recall by +25–31% with <1% precision loss on MNIST and LSC.

## Executive Summary
Ensembles-based Feature Guided Analysis (EFGA) extends Feature Guided Analysis (FGA) to significantly improve recall for feature detection in neural networks without sacrificing precision. FGA extracts decision rules from pure decision trees built on neuron activations; each rule covers only a small fraction of inputs, leading to low recall. EFGA aggregates multiple disjoint rules that predict the same feature into an ensemble, thereby summing their coverage. Under the assumption of disjoint preconditions (pure leaf nodes), Theorem 1 guarantees that ensemble recall equals the sum of individual recalls. Experiments on MNIST and a large histopathology dataset (LSC) show that with the TOP(10) criterion, EFGA increases train recall by 28.51% (MNIST) and 33.15% (LSC), and test recall by 25.76% and 30.81%, respectively, while reducing test precision by less than 1%. Aggregation criteria (TOP, REC, AVG) provide a Pareto front of precision–recall trade‑offs. Moreover, EFGA can compensate for weaker base DNNs, achieving performance comparable to FGA on a stronger network.

## Method Summary
EFGA operates as follows. Inputs include a trained DNN, a dataset, a set of layers, and binary feature definitions. First, the FGA extraction module computes neuron activations for the specified layers and dataset. For each feature, it trains a decision tree on these activations to predict the feature label; only pure leaf nodes are retained, each yielding a rule (a conjunction of neuron conditions pointing to the feature). The preconditions of these rules are mutually exclusive. EFGA then takes all rules for the same feature, sorts them by descending training recall, and applies an aggregation criterion: TOP(X) selects the top X rules, REC(X) adds rules until training recall reaches X%, and AVG includes rules above the average. The ensemble is the disjunction of selected rules. A separate evaluator measures precision and recall on distinct training and test sets. For each feature, the layer that gives the highest single‑rule recall is chosen. The primary comparison evaluates the best single rule from FGA against the TOP(10) ensemble from EFGA across multiple networks and datasets.

## Key Results
- Recall gains: EFGA (TOP(10)) vs. FGA – train recall +28.51% (MNIST) / +33.15% (LSC); test recall +25.76% / +30.81%.
- Precision cost: test precision reduced by only -0.89% (MNIST) and -0.69% (LSC).
- Trade‑offs: Aggregation criteria (TOP, REC, AVG) enable tuning of the precision–recall balance, with higher recall typically requiring longer rule ensembles.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** EFGA increases recall by aggregating multiple, disjoint FGA rules into a single ensemble; the ensemble’s recall equals the sum of its constituent rules’ recalls.
- **Mechanism:** FGA extracts rules (pre→post) from decision trees, where preconditions correspond to pure leaf node paths and are pairwise disjoint. EFGA aggregates rules sharing the same postcondition into an ensemble (pre₁→post) ∨ … ∨ (preₙ→post). An input triggers the ensemble if it satisfies any precondition; thus true positives are the sum of the rules’ true positives. Since the ground‑truth positive count P is constant, recall sums.
- **Core assumption:** Decision trees contain only pure leaf nodes, ensuring mutual exclusivity of preconditions.
- **Evidence:**
  - [abstract] reported recall gains: +28.51% train (MNIST), +25.76% test.
  - [section III] Theorem 1: Re(E) = Σ Re(Rᵢ) with proof based on disjoint preconditions.
  - [corpus] The replication study (Formica et al., 2025) confirms the pure‑leaf‑node assumption in their implementation.
- **Break condition:** If decision trees contain impure leaf nodes (spurious nodes not discarded), preconditions may overlap, violating disjointness and breaking the recall‑sum property.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The choice of aggregation criterion (TOP(X), REC(X), AVG) trades off recall, precision, and ensemble rule length.
- **Mechanism:** Criteria select which rules to include from the sorted list. TOP(X) picks the X rules with highest training recall, favoring simplicity. REC(X) adds rules until training recall exceeds X%, increasing coverage but also length. AVG includes rules above the mean recall, offering a middle ground. Adding more rules captures more true positives (higher recall) but also more false positives (lower precision) and increases total rule length (more clauses).
- **Core assumption:** Rules are sorted by descending training recall; later rules add coverage but are less precise.
- **Evidence:**
  - [abstract] “Different aggregation criteria provide varying precision‑recall trade‑offs.”
  - [section V‑A] Figures 4a‑c show Pareto fronts where moving right (higher recall) generally lowers precision and increases length (Figures 4d‑f).
  - [section V‑A] “REC(95) is the solution with the highest recall… its length is considerably higher (+759.92).”
- **Break condition:** If a high‑recall rule also has high precision (e.g., in L‑DNN2), adding more rules may not significantly harm precision, weakening the trade‑off.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** EFGA can achieve performance comparable to FGA applied to a better‑performing DNN, even when FGA on the original DNN has low recall.
- **Mechanism:** By aggregating many low‑recall rules from a weak DNN, EFGA compensates for individual rule sparsity, achieving coverage similar to a few high‑recall rules from a stronger DNN. The ensemble aggregates diverse “covers” of the feature space.
- **Core assumption:** Individual FGA rules, even if weak, collectively capture the feature when combined.
- **Evidence:**
  - [abstract] “Our results show that EFGA can produce ensembles with performances comparable to those obtained by FGA for other DNN architectures.”
  - [section V‑B‑Exp2] Table VI: EFGA on M‑DNN1 (avg train recall 86.79%) is comparable to FGA on M‑DNN3 (84.35%), despite M‑DNN3 being the best‑performing DNN in [18].
  - [section V‑B] “EFGA over M‑DNN1 produces (on average) results with a higher (+2.44%) train recall than FGA on M‑DNN3.”
- **Break condition:** If the FGA rules for a DNN are systematically inaccurate (low precision), aggregation could compound errors. The paper shows precision penalties are negligible, but this may not hold universally.

## Foundational Learning

#### Concept: Decision Tree Rule Extraction (FGA)
**Why needed:** EFGA extends FGA, which forms the basic rule units. Understanding how decision trees on neuron activations yield pure leaf paths as rules is prerequisite.
**Quick check:** Given a decision tree with pure leaves, how are rules `(conjunction of neuron conditions) → feature label` constructed, and why are their preconditions mutually exclusive?

#### Concept: Precision‑Recall for Rule‑Based Explanations
**Why needed:** EFGA’s objective is to boost recall while preserving precision; metrics define TP/FP/FN based on rule satisfaction vs. ground truth.
**Quick check:** For an ensemble rule, how is a false positive defined, and why does adding more disjoint rules increase recall but potentially increase false positives?

#### Concept: Ensemble Aggregation Criteria
**Why needed:** EFGA’s behavior depends on the criterion (TOP, REC, AVG) that selects rules; understanding each criterion’s logic is key to predicting impact.
**Quick check:** What is the difference between `TOP(10)` and `REC(90)` in terms of how many rules they include and what recall threshold they target?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
FGA Extraction Module → Rule Sorter → Ensemble Builder (with Criterion) → Evaluator. The system also includes a Criterion Tuner (for RQ1, iterating over criterion parameters) and a Comparator (for RQ2, against FGA baseline).

### Critical Path
Input (trained DNN, dataset D, layers L, features F, criterion C) → FGA extraction of per‑feature rule lists → Rule sorter orders rules by descending training recall → Ensemble builder applies criterion C to form ensembles → Evaluator computes precision/recall on train/test sets → output ensembles with metrics.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Recall vs. Precision vs. Length:** Higher recall typically lowers precision and increases rule length (sum of clauses).
- **Criterion Selection:** TOP(X) gives controlled complexity; REC(X) guarantees minimum recall; AVG is automatic but may include many rules.
- **Layer Selection:** Rules from different layers capture different abstractions; paper selects layer with best single‑rule recall.
- **Generalization:** Test recall is key; train recall improvements may not fully translate to test.

### Failure Signatures
- **Low recall despite aggregation:** If individual rules have very low train recall or are highly overlapping (contradicting Theorem 1), ensembles may not sum recall effectively.
- **Precision collapse:** Adding many rules with high false positive rates could reduce precision significantly (beyond the ~1% observed).
- **Overly long ensembles:** REC(95) on MNIST yields length ~780, possibly unusable for human interpretation.
- **Dataset‑specific failure:** On L‑DNN2, high baseline recall means EFGA adds little value; aggressive criteria may still harm precision.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Baseline comparison:** Run FGA and EFGA (with TOP(10)) on M‑DNN1/L‑DNN1; verify recall gain >25% and precision loss <1% as in Table III/IV.
2. **Criterion sensitivity sweep:** For one feature (e.g., “Line” on MNIST), compute ensembles for TOP(1/3/5/10), REC(80/85/90/95), AVG; plot recall vs. precision vs. length to reproduce Pareto front (Figure 4a,d).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How should practitioners select an aggregation criterion?**  
   The paper evaluated three criteria (TOP, REC, AVG) and found they offer alternative trade‑offs, but did not provide a decision framework. The choice is application‑specific. Without guidelines, selecting the best criterion for a given network or application remains unresolved. Resolution would require a systematic analysis correlating network properties with optimal criterion performance or a meta‑analysis across diverse networks establishing selection heuristics.

2. **Why do some neural network architectures produce FGA rules with inherently higher recall than others, and what properties predict the benefit from EFGA?**  
   Results show large differences (e.g., M‑DNN1 recall 58.28% vs. L‑DNN2 89.02%). The EFGA improvement is larger on lower‑baseline networks. However, the paper does not analyze which architectural characteristics (depth, activation patterns, decision tree structure) cause baseline recall variation or predict amenability to ensemble improvement. Resolution would require controlled experiments varying architectural elements or correlation studies linking network metrics to baseline recall and EFGA improvement.

3. **Does the ensemble‑based approach generalize beyond feedforward CNNs?**  
   The evaluation is limited to two image classification benchmarks using CNNs. EFGA relies on extracting neuron activations and building decision trees per layer, assuming consistent activation patterns across inputs. This may not hold for RNNs, transformers, or other architectures. No evidence beyond image CNNs is provided. Resolution would require replication studies on diverse architectures (e.g., Vision Transformers, BERT) and domains (time‑series, tabular data).

4. **What are the theoretical limits of recall improvement via rule ensemble aggregation, and under what conditions does precision degrade catastrophically?**  
   The paper shows precision degrades gradually as recall increases but provides no theoretical analysis of the precision‑recall trade‑off’s shape or thresholds. Without understanding fundamental limits, practitioners cannot predict acceptable recall levels for a given precision requirement. Resolution would involve analytical derivation of precision bounds given rule overlap, empirical measurement of trade‑off curves across many networks, and identification of common inflection points.

## Limitations
- The disjoint precondition assumption (pure leaves) may not always hold; impure leaves could cause overlap and break the recall‑sum guarantee.
- Generalization is limited: results shown only on two image datasets with CNNs; benefits may not extend to other architectures or domains.
- The negligible precision drop observed on MNIST/LSC may not persist on noisier or more complex datasets.

## Confidence
- **Recall improvement cluster:** High (large gains across datasets, statistically significant, supported by Theorem 1 under assumption).
- **Trade‑off control cluster:** High (clear empirical Pareto fronts demonstrating expected relationships).
- **Compensation cluster:** Medium (evidence from one DNN pair; broader validation needed).
