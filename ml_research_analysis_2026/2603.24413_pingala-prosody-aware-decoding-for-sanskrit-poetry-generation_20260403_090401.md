# PINGALA: Prosody-Aware Decoding for Sanskrit Poetry Generation
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.24413
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24413
- **Reference count:** 32
- **Primary result:** A decoding-time biasing approach (PINGALA) combined with SLP1 transliteration achieves near-perfect metrical compliance (e.g., 100% Full Anuṣṭubh) while maintaining strong semantic similarity in Sanskrit poetry generation, resolving the trade-off between form and meaning.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the challenge of generating Sanskrit poetry that is both semantically meaningful and metrically precise. Prior methods struggled with this trade-off, either producing semantically clear but metrically flawed verses or metrically correct but semantically degenerate ones. The proposed solution, PINGALA, introduces a decoding-time logits processor that biases token selection toward longer tokens at mandatory pāda (line) boundaries, operationalizing the *yati* principle to ensure structural stability. Additionally, using the phonetically-aware SLP1 transliteration scheme instead of Devanagari improves tokenization and phonemic regularity, boosting metrical alignment. For evaluation, a cross-encoder architecture replaces prior bi-encoders, providing a finer-grained, reference-free semantic similarity metric that correlates better with human judgment. The combined system yields verses with excellent meter and preserved meaning.

## Method Summary
The approach involves three main phases. First, data preparation uses the Chandomitra parallel corpus, with Sanskrit text transliterated into SLP1 for Phi-4 experiments to improve tokenization. Second, models (NLLB-dist-1.3B, Phi-4-14B) are fine-tuned on the poetic distribution using instruction-style prompts. Third, during inference, a custom `LogitsProcessor` implements PINGALA: it scans cached syllable counts, applies hard constraints to prune invalid prefixes, adds a fixed boost (β=5) to logits when a token completes a pāda boundary (syllable count 8, 16, 24, or 32), and controls EOS generation. A fallback heuristic ranks candidates if no perfect 32-syllable verse is found. Semantic quality is assessed via a cross-encoder (fine-tuned InfoxLM) that scores English-Sanskrit pairs directly.

## Key Results
- Segmenting verses as grouped-lines improves semantic coherence by 10% with comparable metrical adherence.
- Using SLP1 with instruction fine-tuned Phi-4 increases metrical alignment by 46%.
- The cross-encoder establishes an 88% probability gap between correct and incorrect translations versus ~22% for the prior bi-encoder, and shows higher correlation with human judgments.
- Combining fine-tuning with PINGALA yields near-perfect metrical compliance (100% Full Anuṣṭubh for NLLB, 95.92% for Phi-4 with SLP1) while maintaining strong semantic similarity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Biasing token selection toward longer candidates at metrical boundaries (pāda lines) during decoding improves structural stability without sacrificing semantic coherence.
- **Mechanism:** A sparse re-weighting function adds a boost (β) to logits only when a candidate token completes a pāda boundary (syllable counts 8, 16, 24, 32). This "shaping function" preferentially selects tokens that complete well-formed words at mandatory structural milestones, operationalizing the *yati* principle. Hard constraints prune invalid prefixes.
- **Core assumption:** Longer tokens are more likely to align with word boundaries and reduce syllable fragmentation at critical junctures, leading to valid meter completion.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Abstract states biasing toward longer tokens at pāda boundaries.
  - Section 3.2 formulates the re-weighting Δ(v; y₁:ₜ₋₁) and its application in decoding.
  - Ablation results show β too high (≥7) causes semantic distortion, confirming the balancing act.
- **Break condition:** If β is too high, the model over-prioritizes metrical form, degrading semantic quality (Table 10).

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Using SLP1 transliteration instead of Devanagari for training and inference significantly improves metrical alignment for instruction fine-tuned models.
- **Mechanism:** SLP1's ASCII-based, phonemic representation reduces vocabulary fragmentation and Unicode normalization issues. This creates a more regular, tokenizer-friendly input space where sound-level patterns (crucial for meter) are more directly learnable from subword statistics.
- **Core assumption:** A phoneme-level, normalized script exposes phonological regularities more effectively to a model pre-trained on predominantly Latin-script data, facilitating the acquisition of prosodic patterns.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Abstract reports a 46% metrical alignment increase with SLP1 for Phi-4.
  - Section 3.3 cites reduced tokenization mismatch, normalization stability, and exposed sound-level regularities.
  - The neighbor paper on linguistically-informed architectures suggests script choices impact morphology-sensitive tasks, supporting this indirectly.
- **Break condition:** Pre-trained models like NLLB, trained exclusively on Devanagari, may degrade if switched to SLP1 without continued pre-training (Limitation 3).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A cross-encoder architecture for reference-free evaluation provides a discriminative, high-precision signal for semantic fidelity by enabling fine-grained, token-level alignment between source and candidate.
- **Mechanism:** Unlike a bi-encoder that creates separate embeddings, the cross-encoder processes the concatenated source-candidate pair through full self-attention. This allows direct comparison of lexical items, syntactic structure, and semantic roles, producing a calibrated scalar score that better separates correct from incorrect translations.
- **Core assumption:** Direct cross-attention between English source and Sanskrit candidate captures translation quality (e.g., agreement, negation, word order) more faithfully than comparing positions in a shared embedding space.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Abstract notes an 88% vs. ~22% probability gap for cross-encoder vs. bi-encoder.
  - Section 2.2 explains joint encoding advantage and shows the improvement (Table 2).
  - The Mitrasamgraha dataset provides parallel pairs, but does not validate the mechanism itself.
- **Break condition:** Requires high-quality parallel data with hard negatives for training and is computationally more expensive than bi-encoders for large-scale inference.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Sanskrit Prosody (Anuṣṭubh Meter)**
  - *Why needed here:* The entire constraint system (PINGALA) is built on the rules of the Anuṣṭubh meter: a 32-syllable verse divided into four 8-syllable pādas, with specific laghu (light) / guru (heavy) patterns. Understanding pāda boundaries and syllable weight calculation is essential to follow the decoding logic.
  - *Quick check:* Given a Devanagari/SLP1 string, can you programmatically count syllables and map them to L/G patterns for a target pāda position?
- **Concept: Tokenization & Subword Fragmentation in Multilingual LLMs**
  - *Why needed here:* The choice of SLP1 vs. Devanagari hinges on how subword tokenizers handle non-Latin scripts. Fragmentation increases sequence length and disrupts phonemic continuity, harming both semantic learning and meter detection.
  - *Quick check:* How would the tokenization of a common Sanskrit compound differ between Devanagari and SLP1, and why might the latter be more favorable for a model with a Latin-script vocabulary?
- **Concept: Evaluation Metric Design for Low-Resource Translation**
  - *Why needed here:* The paper moves from a bi-encoder (Chandomitra) to a cross-encoder for evaluation. This requires understanding the limitations of embedding-based similarity (anisotropy, coarse matching) and the benefits of joint discriminative scoring for high-stakes tasks where precision matters.
  - *Quick check:* Why might a cosine similarity score between two separate sentence embeddings be a poor proxy for translation correctness in a morphologically rich, low-resource language pair?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Input Preprocessor -> Fine-tuning Module -> Decoding Engine -> Candidate Scorer/Ranker -> Semantic Evaluator
- **Critical path:** Data Preparation (SLP1 conversion) -> Model Fine-Tuning -> Inference with PINGALA LogitsProcessor -> Post-generation Filtering -> Semantic Evaluation (Cross-Encoder).
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **β (shaping weight):** Low β risks incomplete meter; high β harms semantics (Table 10). Optimal β≈5 found empirically on validation.
  - **SLP1 vs. Devanagari:** SLP1 boosts metrical results for fine-tuned Phi-4 but may not benefit models pre-trained only on Devanagari (NLLB case untested). Semantic evaluation uses Devanagari for best human correlation (Table 14).
  - **Search budget (top-k):** Smaller k is faster but risks pruning valid paths; adaptive expansion mitigates this at some cost.
- **Failure signatures:**
  1. **Metrical failure:** High `Partial %` but low `Full %` indicates decoding often terminates with wrong syllable count or invalid patterns.
  2. **Semantic collapse:** High `Full %` but very low semantic similarity score (`Sim`) suggests β too high or model not fine-tuned on poetic distribution.
  3. **SLP1 mismatch:** Using SLP1 with a Devanagari-pretrained model (like NLLB) causes tokenization issues and scanner parsing failures.
  4. **Poor cross-encoder calibration:** Leads to wrong candidate ranking; check probability gap and calibration curves.
  5. **Fine-tuning underfit:** Model fails to generate metrically valid output; monitor validation Full % during training.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Ablation of β on validation set:** For a fixed fine-tuned model, sweep β ∈ [0,10] and plot `Full %` and `Sim` to locate Pareto front (expected peak at β≈5).
  2. **Tokenization analysis:** Process 1000 Sanskrit verses in Devanagari vs. SLP1; compare average tokens per line, subword split frequency at morpheme boundaries, and UNK rate. Expect SLP1 to show lower fragmentation.
  3. **Cross-encoder calibration check:** On 200 (English, Sanskrit) pairs with human scores, compute Pearson/Spearman correlation for raw and isotonic-calibrated scores. Expect calibrated scores to correlate higher.

## Limitations
- Fine-tuning hyperparameters (LR, batch size, epochs) and prompt engineering details are not fully specified.
- Implementation specifics of the syllable scanner, sandhi handling in `C(·)`, and fallback heuristic parameters (α, γ) require inference.
- SLP1 benefits are demonstrated only with Phi-4; impact on Devanagari-pretrained models like NLLB is untested.
- Human evaluation is limited (N=50); cross-encoder correlation, while positive, may not fully capture poetic quality beyond semantics and meter.

## Confidence
- **PINGALA decoding mechanism:** High. Supported by clear formulation, ablation showing β breakpoint, and theoretical *yati* alignment.
- **SLP1 transliteration advantage:** Medium. Strong empirical gain (46%) for Phi-4, but mechanism inferred (tokenization regularity) and untested on non-SLP1-pretrained models.
- **Cross-encoder evaluation superiority:** Medium. Compelling 88% vs. 22% gap and human correlation, but confidence tempered by unknown training specifics (hard-negative pipeline, calibration).
- **Combined system results:** High. Direct, replicated empirical outcome across model families with clear metrics (Full Anuṣṭubh %, similarity scores).
