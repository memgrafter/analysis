# The Subjectivity of Respect in Police Traffic Stops: Modeling Community Perspectives in Body-Worn Camera Footage

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.10339
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10339
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Perspective-aware model with SFT and rubric-guided DPO improves rating MAE and rationale rubric-F1 across all annotator groups, with largest rationale F1 gains (+2.4–3.5%) for justice-system-impacted annotators.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the subjectivity of respect perceptions in police traffic stops by leveraging body-worn camera (BWC) footage. The authors construct a multi-perspective dataset from Los Angeles Police Department traffic stops, where annotators from diverse backgrounds (police-affiliated, justice-system-impacted, and non-affiliated residents) provide respect ratings (1–5) and free-text rationales for both officers and drivers. Grounded in procedural justice theory, they develop a structured rubric (Emotional, Professional, Communicative, Contextual) to evaluate respect and use it to synthesize preference data for aligning a language model.

The proposed framework first fine-tunes a language model (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) via supervised learning with annotator conditioning. It then employs Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) with rubric-grounded preference pairs, where chosen responses are high-quality rationales (including paraphrased variants) and rejected ones are lower-quality candidates. Across all annotator groups, the perspective-aware model reduces rating prediction error (MAE) and improves rationale quality (rubric-F1). Notably, justice-system-impacted annotators see the largest rationale quality gains (+2.4% to +3.5% F1 over SFT), underscoring the value of incorporating lived experience.

## Method Summary
The study uses transcribed BWC conversations from ~1,000 traffic stops, each annotated by multiple annotators with respect ratings and rationales. Annotator backgrounds include group identity, age, race, and gender. A respect rubric, derived from procedural justice theory and LAPD training, defines four dimensions with binary activations. An LLM-as-a-judge (LLaMA-3-70B) maps rationales to rubric vectors. The training pipeline has two stages: (1) Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) of Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct using LoRA, where prompts encode annotator descriptors and the model predicts both rating and rationale. (2) Rubric-guided preference synthesis: the SFT model generates candidate rationales; ground-truth rationales are paraphrased (Qwen3-Next-80B); all candidates are judged by the rubric LLM. Pairs are formed between high-F1 chosen (ground truth + high-quality paraphrases) and low-F1 rejected samples, including those with specific errors. DPO with LoRA aligns the model to these preferences. At inference, the model conditions on the target annotator’s background to produce personalized outputs.

## Key Results
- The perspective-aware model reduces rating MAE and increases rationale rubric-F1 compared to baselines across all three annotator groups.
- Rubric-guided DPO yields rationale quality improvements of up to +3.5% F1 for justice-system-impacted annotators over SFT alone.
- The largest gains are observed for the justice-system-impacted group, demonstrating the benefit of modeling lived experience.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Capturing subjective interpretations via annotator conditioning
The model conditions on annotator background (group and demographics) to capture subjectivity in respect judgments. Group identity is a significant predictor of ratings (mixed-effects analysis). By including annotator attributes in prompts, the model tailors outputs to the associated perspective, improving zero-shot performance and enabling personalized generation after fine-tuning. Evidence: abstract, section 4.1, and related work. Break condition: missing attributes or high within-group variance may lead to averaged outputs.

### Rubric-driven preference alignment enhances rationale specificity
The rubric provides structured criteria (Emotional, Professional, Communicative, Contextual). Preference pairs are built by contrasting rationales with high rubric-F1 (chosen) against those with low precision/recall or systematic errors (rejected). DPO rewards chosen and penalizes rejected, creating a dense signal that guides the model to reference key dimensions. Evidence: rubric grounded in theory and training materials (section 3.2), preference construction (section 3.3), and DPO improvements (section 5). Break condition: incomplete rubric or biased judge may optimize for metric manipulation.

### Paraphrase augmentation increases robustness to linguistic variation
High-quality paraphrases of ground-truth rationales (preserving meaning and rubric alignment) are added to the chosen pool, exposing the model to diverse expressions. The paraphrase model (Qwen3-Next-80B) generates variants filtered by rubric F1. This reduces overfitting to specific phrasings and improves generalization. Evidence: section 3.3 and appendix A.1. Break condition: paraphrasing that distorts meaning or drops rubric dimensions introduces noise.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: The pipeline begins with the LAPD BWC transcript dataset and annotator backgrounds. The rubric and LLM-as-a-judge are developed to evaluate rationales. The generator (base language model) produces candidate rationales. The augmenter paraphrases high-quality rationales. The preference builder constructs chosen/rejected pairs based on rubric scores. Training involves SFT (using dataset with annotator conditioning) followed by DPO (using preference pairs). The final model is evaluated on rating MAE and rubric-F1.

Critical path:  
1. Collect multi-perspective annotations.  
2. Develop rubric and validate judge.  
3. Synthesize preference data (generate candidates, judge, filter).  
4. Train SFT model with LoRA.  
5. Apply DPO using constructed pairs.  
6. Evaluate on test set.

Design tradeoffs:  
- Rubric completeness vs. complexity: detailed rubric improves interpretability but may miss nuances; coarse rubric loses granularity.  
- Paraphrase quality: stronger models preserve meaning but increase compute; cheaper models risk meaning drift.  
- Group conditioning granularity: coarse labels simplify but obscure individual differences; adding demographics helps but risks reification.  
- DPO vs. RL-based methods: DPO is stable and avoids reward modeling, but may be less sample-efficient; chosen/rejected threshold selection critically affects alignment.

Failure signatures:  
- Low rationale rubric-F1 (<60%) after DPO: preference pairs may have weak separation or judge inconsistency.  
- High MAE for specific groups: model not capturing group-specific patterns; check conditioning and data balance.  
- Generic or off-rubric rationales: SFT overfitted; DPO mis-specified (chosen pool not truly high-quality).  
- Paraphrase meaning shift: monitor paraphrase F1; if low, adjust paraphrase model or filters.

First 3 experiments (for a new engineer):  
1. Mixed-effects validation: Replicate section 4.1 by fitting a mixed-effects model on annotation data to confirm annotator group predicts respect ratings.  
2. Prompt sensitivity test: Run zero-shot inference with base model using four prompt configurations (Base, Base(g), Base(g+demo), Base(g+demo+ent)) to assess impact of conditioning without training.  
3. SFT baseline: Train LoRA fine-tuned model on training set (using g+demo conditioning) and evaluate on test set for rating MAE and rubric-F1. Compare to zero-shot to quantify SFT gains.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How do multimodal cues (prosody, facial expressions, body language) influence respect perceptions in police-civilian interactions, and can integrating these cues improve model alignment with community perspectives? Basis: Limitation 8. Why unresolved: study relies solely on transcripts. What evidence would resolve: collect and annotate multimodal BWC data with respect ratings from diverse annotators, then train and evaluate multimodal models against text-only baselines.
2. What individual-level factors (e.g., specific trauma history, recency of justice system contact) explain within-group heterogeneity in respect interpretations among justice-system-impacted or police-affiliated annotators? Basis: Ethical Statement and mixed-effects analysis showing group identity as primary but not sole predictor. Why unresolved: model conditions on coarse group labels, potentially missing individual nuances crucial for personalization. What evidence would resolve: conduct fine-grained analysis of within-group annotation variance, incorporate additional annotator attributes, and test individualized modeling approaches.
3. Can the traffic-stop respect rubric generalize to other police-civilian contexts (e.g., use-of-force, community patrols), and how do community perspectives on respect differ across these contexts? Basis: Limitation 8. Why unresolved: rubric tailored to traffic-stop communication; other contexts involve different dynamics and respect cues. What evidence would resolve: apply rubric development process to new contexts, compare dimension relevance, and evaluate model transferability.
4. How sensitive are the model's alignment gains to the specific thresholds and rejection criteria used in rubric-guided preference data synthesis? Basis: Section 3.3 and Appendix A.1: thresholds are empirically determined without systematic ablation. Why unresolved: construction of preference pairs critically depends on these thresholds, but their impact on performance is unexplored. What evidence would resolve: perform sensitivity analysis varying τ_ch, τ_rej, and rejection conditions, measuring effects on rubric-F1 and rating MAE.

## Limitations
- Original LAPD BWC transcripts are contractually restricted; reproductions may rely on synthetic or alternative data, risking distribution shift.
- Exact rubric thresholds (τ_ch, τ_rej) for preference pair construction are undisclosed, hindering faithful replication.
- The LLM-as-a-judge reliability is unreported; potential misalignment could misguide DPO.

## Confidence
- Subjectivity captured via group conditioning: Medium
- Rubric-driven DPO improves rationale quality: Medium-High

- Generalization beyond traffic stops: Low
