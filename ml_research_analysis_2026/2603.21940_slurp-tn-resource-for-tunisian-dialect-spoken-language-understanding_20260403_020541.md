# SLURP-TN : Resource for Tunisian Dialect Spoken Language Understanding

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.21940
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21940
- **Reference count**: 0
- **Primary result**: State-of-the-art models remain challenged on Tunisian dialect SLU, with w2v-BERT 2.0 achieving WERs of 30.4% (val) and 33.6% (test), and SENSE attaining CoERs of 45.1% and 44.1% and CVERs of 55.7% and 59.0%.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces SLURP-TN, the first publicly available Spoken Language Understanding (SLU) dataset for Tunisian Arabic, a low-resource dialect. It addresses a critical gap in resources for multilingual dialogue systems by adapting the high-resource English SLURP dataset to the Tunisian context. The resource consists of 4,165 utterances (~5 hours) recorded by 55 native speakers across 18 regions, covering six SLURP domains and three acoustic conditions (clean, noisy, headphone). The dataset preserves natural code-switching between Arabic and Latin scripts. Benchmarking with state-of-the-art speech models reveals substantial performance gaps, underscoring the challenge of dialectal, code-switched speech understanding.

## Method Summary
The core method involves manually translating 4,165 English utterances from six SLURP domains into Tunisian dialect, guided by cultural and linguistic appropriateness while retaining the original semantic tag schemas (intents and slots). Fifty-five native speakers then recorded these translations under three acoustic conditions. The annotation process transferred the English SLU tags to the Tunisian transcripts, preserving code-switched segments (e.g., French/English loanwords) in their original Latin script. The resulting dataset is split into train/validation/test with balanced gender representation and broad geographic coverage.

## Key Results
- Dataset release: 4,165 utterances (~5 hours), 55 speakers, 18 regions, three acoustic conditions; code-switching present in ~54% of training utterances.
- w2v-BERT 2.0 fine-tuned for ASR yields WER of 30.4% (validation) and 33.6% (test).
- SENSE model for SLU achieves concept error rates (CoER) of 45.1% (val) and 44.1% (test), and concept-value error rates (CVER) of 55.7% (val) and 59.0% (test).
- Encoder-only models (w2v-BERT) excel at ASR but lag in SLU compared to semantically-enhanced (SENSE) or encoder-decoder (Whisper) architectures, revealing a trade-off between linguistic and semantic accuracy.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Translating utterances from the high-resource SLURP dataset provides a semantic scaffold that preserves task structure while enabling localization.
- **Mechanism**: The original SLURP scenarios and semantic tag schemas are reused; human translators adapt utterances to Tunisian dialect and cultural context while retaining the underlying intent-slot structure. This transfers the rich annotation framework to a low-resource setting without requiring semantic annotation from scratch.
- **Core assumption**: The mapping between English SLURP semantics and Tunisian dialect expressions is sufficiently systematic for a single bilingual annotator to transfer tags accurately post-recording.
- **Evidence anchors**: The annotator was provided with the annotated source utterances in English and asked to transfer the original English annotation to the correct position in the target sentences. Direct tag transfer (e.g., `[time]`, `[place_name]`) is shown across languages in examples.
- **Break condition**: If the Tunisian translation process introduces semantic drift (e.g., reordering slots, changing intent) that the annotator fails to correct, the SLU annotation becomes misaligned with the utterance's actual meaning.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Recording across diverse acoustic conditions and broad geographic representation increases the dataset's ability to train robust models for real-world deployment.
- **Mechanism**: Multiple recording setups simulate variable user environments (e.g., quiet home vs. café), while speakers from 18 regions capture phonological variation in Tunisian Arabic. This exposes models to acoustic and dialectal diversity, reducing overfitting to a single condition or accent.
- **Core assumption**: Acoustic and regional variability are primary factors degrading SLU performance in deployed systems; thus, including them in training yields more generalizable models.
- **Evidence anchors**: The dataset captures three acoustic conditions and includes speakers from 18 Tunisian regions, with nearly balanced gender representation. Code-switching prevalence is quantified (~54% train).
- **Break condition**: If acoustic condition labels are noisy or region labels are self-reported without phonetic validation, models may learn spurious correlations rather than robust features.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: The inclusion of natural code-switching (Arabic-Latin script) forces models to handle mixed-language input, a hallmark of Tunisian dialect speech, thereby improving real-world applicability.
- **Mechanism**: Translators are instructed to retain loanwords and code-switched segments in original script (English/French). This results in utterances where Arabic script and Latin script co-occur, requiring models to process a hybrid orthography without script normalization.
- **Core assumption**: Code-switching is a consistent and frequent feature of Tunisian dialect; models trained on monolingual Arabic (MSA) or single-script data will fail on such mixed input.
- **Evidence anchors**: Translators were directed to preserve code-switching. The paper reports that CS exists in almost 54% of the recorded training sentences, with examples showing mixed script within sentences.
- **Break condition**: If code-switching patterns are not represented in the SLU tag set (e.g., a slot value is partly in Latin script but tagged as a single unit), models may struggle to align mixed-script spans with tags.

## Foundational Learning
- **Self-supervised learning (SSL) for speech**
  - **Why needed here**: All baseline models (w2v-BERT 2.0, SENSE, Whisper) are SSL-pretrained. Fine-tuning on SLURP-TN builds on these representations; understanding SSL objectives explains why pretraining helps low-resource adaptation.
  - **Quick check question**: How does pretraining on large unlabeled multilingual speech transfer to a dialectal SLU task with only ~5 hours of labeled data?

- **Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) loss**
  - **Why needed here**: ASR and SLU models (except Whisper decoder) use CTC for training. CTC aligns variable-length speech sequences to token sequences without forced alignment, crucial for sequence-to-sequence tasks with limited data.
  - **Quick check question**: Why is CTC preferred over seq2seq with attention for low-resource SLU in this work?

- **Code-switching linguistics**
  - **Why needed here**: Tunisian Arabic frequently alternates between Arabic and French/English. Translators preserve this; models must handle mixed scripts. Understanding CS typology informs preprocessing and error analysis.
  - **Quick check question**: What challenges does script-switching (Arabic/Latin) pose for a character-level tokenizer, as used in this paper?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: SLURP-TN dataset (audio, transcripts, SLU tags, metadata) -> w2v-BERT 2.0 (encoder-only, CTC) / SENSE (semantically enhanced encoder) / Whisper-large-v3 (encoder-decoder, NLL loss) -> SpeechBrain toolkit (training pipelines) -> SCLITE (evaluation) -> metrics (WER, CER, CoER, CVER).
- **Critical path**:
  1. Load dataset splits (train/val/test) and acoustic condition subsets.
  2. Prepare tokenizer: extend with SLU tags (`[time:...]`) as single characters.
  3. Choose model architecture (encoder-only for CTC, encoder-decoder for Whisper).
  4. Fine-tune pretrained checkpoint (TEDx-TN adapted or off-the-shelf) on SLURP-TN train.
  5. Evaluate on clean/headphone/noisy val sets; select best model.
  6. Report WER/CER (ASR), CoER/CVER (SLU) on test.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Encoder-only vs. encoder-decoder: Encoder-only (w2v-BERT) gives better ASR (lower WER) but worse SLU (higher CoER/CVER) vs. SENSE; Whisper encoder-decoder improves SLU tag prediction but struggles with value accuracy (high CVER) and CER.
  - Acoustic condition: Using all three conditions improves robustness but noisy data hurts performance; best results often on clean/headphone.
  - Tokenization: Character-level avoids OOV but increases sequence length; SLU tag tokens must be carefully integrated.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - ASR: High WER on noisy splits; systematic errors on code-switched words (mixing scripts).
  - SLU: High CVER (tag-value errors) even if CoER (tag detection) is moderate, indicating value extraction is harder.
  - Generalization: Models trained only on clean may fail on noisy; performance drop when regional/accents not in train (though coverage is broad).
  - Whisper-specific: Encoder-decoder shows inflated CER on SLU task due to extended tokenizer.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Replicate baseline**: Fine-tune w2v-BERT 2.0 on SLURP-TN train (all conditions) and evaluate on clean val/test. Target: WER ~30% (val) / 34% (test) as in Table 7.
  2. **Acoustic robustness test**: Train separate models on clean-only, then test on noisy. Expect WER increase ~5-7 points (Table 8/9). Analyze if SENSE's semantic enrichment mitigates noise impact (it does not significantly).
  3. **Code-switching ablation**: Filter train set to only non-CS utterances; fine-tune w2v-BERT 2.0. Compare WER/CVER on CS vs. non-CS test segments. Hypothesis: CS segments will have significantly higher error rates, confirming CS as a primary challenge.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: How can SLURP-TN be extended to cover more of the original SLURP domains to improve model generalization?  
  *Basis*: The dataset covers only six of eighteen SLURP domains, limiting generalization.  
  *Why unresolved*: Practical constraints in translation, speaker recruitment, and recording time limited initial scope.  
  *What evidence would resolve it*: Release of an expanded version with more domains, accompanied by benchmarks showing improved cross-domain SLU performance.

- **Open Question 2**: What architectural or training modifications can significantly improve ASR and SLU performance on code-switched Tunisian Arabic under noisy conditions?  
  *Basis*: Results show high error rates (WER >30%, CoER >45%) despite SOTA models, with code-switching present in over 50% of utterances and noise causing notable drops.  
  *Why unresolved*: Current models are primarily trained on high-resource languages or MSA; dialectal code-switching with orthographic variability and noise remain challenging.  
  *What evidence would resolve it*: Development of a model (e.g., incorporating script normalization, multilingual pretraining, or data augmentation) that achieves a relative >20% reduction in WER and CoER on the noisy test split, with ablation studies confirming contributions.

- **Open Question 3**: Is there a fundamental trade-off between ASR transcription accuracy and SLU semantic accuracy in low-resource dialectal speech, and can it be mitigated?  
  *Basis*: SENSE outperforms w2v-BERT on SLU metrics but lags on ASR metrics; the paper identifies this as a trade-off.  
  *Why unresolved*: The trade-off is noted but not investigated; no experiments explicitly balance or joint-train for both tasks.  
  *What evidence would resolve it*: An experiment training a single model with a multi-task objective (ASR + SLU) on SLURP-TN, showing whether joint optimization can close the performance gap, measured by changes in correlation between WER and CoER.

## Limitations
- Semantic transfer accuracy relies on a single annotator; no quantitative evaluation (e.g., inter-annotator agreement) confirms that intent and slot boundaries remain correctly aligned after translation.
- Acoustic and regional metadata lack granularity (e.g., precise SNR for noisy clips, phonetic validation of accents), limiting diagnostic analysis of failure modes.
- The SLU annotation schema does not explicitly tag script-switching, and the impact of code-switching on value extraction (CVER) is not isolated.

## Confidence
- **High Confidence**: The dataset exists, is publicly released, captures documented code-switching prevalence (~54%) and broad geographic/gender balance. Baseline benchmark numbers (WER, CoER, CVER) are reproducible.
- **Medium Confidence**: Claims that the translation method preserves task structure and that acoustic/region diversity increases robustness are plausible but not causally proven; evidence is descriptive, not experimental.
- **Low Confidence**: Generalizability beyond the tested model families and to truly unseen Tunisian speakers/accents. The paper does not evaluate *why* SLU performance (CVER ~55-59%) is low—whether due to ASR errors, semantic transfer, or dialectal variation.

## Next Checks
1. **Semantic Drift Audit**: Randomly sample 200–500 translated utterances per domain. Have two new bilingual annotators re-annotate intent/slots from Tunisian audio/transcripts without seeing English source tags. Compute agreement (Cohen's κ) and error rate against dataset gold tags.
2. **Condition & Region Ablation Study**: Retrain the best-performing model (e.g., w2v-BERT 2.0) on ablations: (a) only clean recordings, (b) recordings from only 12/18 regions. Evaluate on the full test set to quantify marginal contributions of acoustic and geographic diversity.
3. **Code-Switching Error Analysis**: For all test-set utterances containing code-switching, analyze: (a) compute ASR WER separately on Arabic vs. Latin script segments, (b) categorize SLU errors as due to (i) incorrect recognition of the CS word, (ii) incorrect boundary tagging around the CS word, or (iii) incorrect value extraction within a correctly tagged CS span.
