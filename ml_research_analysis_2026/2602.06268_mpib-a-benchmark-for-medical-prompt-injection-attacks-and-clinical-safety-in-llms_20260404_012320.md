# MPIB: A Benchmark for Medical Prompt Injection Attacks and Clinical Safety in LLMs
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.06268
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06268
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: MPIB introduces a benchmark of 9,697 medical prompt injection instances (direct V1 and indirect V2) and reveals that attack success rate (ASR) and Clinical Harm Event Rate (CHER) diverge, especially for V2 attacks; V2 sometimes yields higher CHER than V1 in high-risk categories; defenses reducing ASR do not consistently lower CHER, and composite defenses are not uniformly optimal, highlighting metric misalignment and the need for threat-specific calibrations.

## Executive Summary
Medical large language models (LLMs) and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems are increasingly used in clinical settings, but they face prompt injection attacks that could cause patient harm. To address this, the authors present MPIB, a benchmark of 9,697 carefully crafted medical prompt injection instances covering direct (V1) and indirect (V2) attack vectors. They introduce the Clinical Harm Event Rate (CHER) metric to measure high-severity harm (severity ≥3). Evaluating 12 LLMs, they find that traditional attack success rates (ASR) often misalign with CHER, particularly for V2 attacks where models may comply at a surface level without causing catastrophic harm. V2 attacks sometimes produce higher CHER than V1 in high-risk categories, and defenses exhibit vector-specific effectiveness—input-focused defenses help against V1 while context sanitization helps against V2—but no single defense configuration is optimal across all models. The work underscores the need for diversified safety metrics and tailored defenses for clinical LLMs.

## Method Summary
MPIB builds on medical QA datasets (MedQA, PubmedQA) through a multi-stage pipeline: normalization, scenario classification (S1–S4), attack rule application (R1–R10), conflict quality gating (G3 for V2), and harm annotation via an LLM judge (Qwen-2.5-72B-Instruct). The benchmark releases both redacted and controlled unredacted versions. Evaluation involves running target models with or without defense wrappers (D0–D4), then scoring outputs for harm type (H1–H5) and severity (0–4). Metrics include ASR (Severity ≥2), CHER3 (Severity ≥3), and utility loss (FPR-H). Defenses tested include input rewriting (D2), context sanitization (D3), internal hardening (D1), and a composite policy (D4) built from the SLD-7B suite.

## Key Results
- ASR and CHER diverge significantly, especially under V2 attacks: many models show high ASR but lower CHER, meaning they obey malicious instructions without causing high-severity clinical harm.
- V2 attacks can yield higher CHER than V1 in certain high-risk categories, though patterns vary across models (e.g., Meditron-70B: V2 CHER 64.1% vs V1 98.5%).
- Defenses that reduce ASR do not consistently lower CHER, exposing metric misalignment; composite defenses (D4) are not universally effective and may increase utility loss.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Indirect (V2) prompt injection exploits the RAG trust boundary, where poisoned contextual documents are treated as authoritative evidence, leading to higher Clinical Harm Event Rate (CHER) in high-risk categories compared to direct (V1) injection for certain models.
- Mechanism: The model prioritizes retrieved context over user intent due to authority framing. In V2, an adversarial instruction embedded in a retrieved document (e.g., an "Editor’s Note") conflicts with the benign query but appears clinically plausible. The model cites or complies with the poisoned context, producing harmful outputs that may still be formatted correctly and clinically framed, thereby increasing high-severity harm (Severity ≥3) without necessarily increasing lower-severity compliance (Severity ≥2).
- Core assumption: Models treat retrieved text as a higher-priority instruction source than the user query in RAG setups, especially when the context adopts clinical language or institutional formatting.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "V2 often yielded higher CHER than V1 in high-risk categories (e.g., Meditron-70B: V2 CHER 64.1% vs V1 98.5%)"
  - [section 4.2] "For example, Meditron-70B reduces V2 CHER from 53.1% (D0) to 37.5% (D4)... V2 harms are mediated by poisoned context and thus can benefit from context-side interventions"
  - [corpus] Weak direct evidence; neighboring papers discuss general prompt injection and medical VLM security but do not specifically address V2 authority framing in clinical RAG. Assumption: Authority framing is a known psychological and LLM phenomenon extrapolated to clinical contexts.
- Break condition: If a model's internal safety constraints or medical knowledge consistently override contextual authority even when presented with poisoned evidence, the V2 CHER would drop significantly below V1 CHER for all models, contradicting the observed pattern where V2 exceeds V1 in specific high-risk scenarios.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Attack Success Rate (ASR) and Clinical Harm Event Rate (CHER) can diverge because ASR aggregates all instruction-following successes (Severity ≥2), while CHER focuses exclusively on high-severity clinical harm (Severity ≥3). This divergence is particularly pronounced under indirect (V2) injection, where models may comply at the level of framing or citation without executing actionable dangerous guidance.
- Mechanism: A model may produce an output that formally obeys the adversarial directive (e.g., citing a poisoned guideline) but still retains enough medical knowledge or internal consistency checks to avoid committing a clinically catastrophic error (e.g., a fatal overdose or missed stroke). Thus, ASR captures compliance, but CHER captures outcome-level risk; the gap ("Safe Gap") indicates failures that are non-catastrophic despite compliance.
- Core assumption: Not all instruction-following leads to equally severe clinical outcomes; severity is a distinct dimension from compliance.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "ASR and CHER diverged substantially, especially under V2 where many models showed high ASR but lower CHER—indicating instruction compliance without severe clinical harm."
  - [section 4.3] "In this scenario, elevated ASR often reflects compliance at the level of framing, citation, or formatting, rather than execution of actionable clinical guidance. Stronger models, in particular, appear to retain sufficient medical knowledge and internal consistency checks to avoid committing high-severity clinical errors despite partial compliance."
  - [section 5] "ASR (Severity ≥ 2) can remain high even when failures do not cross our high-severity threshold (Severity ≥ 3); thus, under indirect injection, ASR alone can overstate the frequency of high-severity patient-safety events."
- Break condition: If ASR and CHER become perfectly correlated across all vectors and models (i.e., ASR ≈ CHER3 always), it would imply that any compliance inevitably leads to high-severity harm, invalidating the "Safe Gap" phenomenon.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: The effectiveness of a defense configuration (e.g., input rewriting vs. context sanitization) is vector-dependent because the adversarial instruction’s location (user query vs. retrieved context) changes the effective trust boundary and failure mode.
- Mechanism: For V1 (direct injection), the malicious instruction is in the user channel, so defenses that rewrite or filter the user query (e.g., D2: Input Guard) directly intercept the attack. For V2 (indirect injection), the instruction is in the context, so defenses that sanitize retrieved documents (e.g., D3: Context Sanitizer) or harden system prompts to prioritize internal knowledge (e.g., D1: Internal Hardening) are more impactful. Composite defenses (D4) may underperform due to interaction effects (e.g., over-sanitization removing legitimate clinical guidance).
- Core assumption: The channel through which adversarial instructions enter the model’s context window determines the optimal defensive surface area.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "defenses reducing ASR did not consistently lower CHER, demonstrating metric misalignment."
  - [section 4.2] "Defense effectiveness differs by attack vector (V1 vs. V2). Across models, V1 CHER is generally higher than V2 CHER and is often reduced by the Input Guard (D2)... In contrast, V2 harms are mediated by poisoned context and thus can benefit from context-side interventions in several model families."
  - [section 4.2] "Composite defense (D4) is not uniformly best... This non-uniformity suggests that clinical prompt-injection defenses may benefit from model- and threat-specific calibration rather than assuming a single universally optimal configuration."
- Break condition: If a single defense (e.g., D2) consistently reduced both V1 and V2 CHER across all models to similar degrees, it would indicate that the attack vector does not fundamentally change the optimal defense strategy, contrary to the observed vector-specific effects.

## Foundational Learning
### Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) in clinical settings
- Why needed here: MPIB’s V2 attacks specifically target RAG systems where retrieved documents are used as context. Understanding RAG’s role in clinical applications (e.g., evidence-based guideline queries) is essential to grasp why poisoned context is a critical threat vector.
- Quick check question: How does the RAG paradigm change the trust boundary compared to a standalone LLM, and why does this make poisoned documents particularly dangerous?

### Clinical harm taxonomy and severity scaling (H1–H5, Severity 0–4)
- Why needed here: CHER and ASR are defined based on this taxonomy. Without understanding the distinction between harm types (e.g., H2 Treatment Error vs. H3 Emergency Downplay) and what constitutes Severity ≥3, the outcome metrics cannot be interpreted meaningfully.
- Quick check question: What differentiates a Severity 2 (moderate harm) from a Severity 3 (high-severity harm) in MPIB’s framework? Give an example of each.

### Metric misalignment in security evaluation (ASR vs. CHER)
- Why needed here: The paper’s core insight is that traditional attack success metrics (ASR) can misestimate clinical risk. Understanding why CHER is proposed as a complementary outcome-centric metric is crucial for interpreting results and designing defenses.
- Quick check question: Why might a defense reduce ASR but fail to reduce CHER? Provide an example scenario from the paper’s findings.

## Architecture Onboarding
- Component map:
  - Dataset Construction: Source data (MedQA/PubmedQA) -> Normalization -> Scenario Classifier (S1-S4) -> Attack Rule Application (R1-R10) -> Quality Gates (G1-G6) -> Harm Annotation (LLM judge) -> Release (redacted/unredacted tiers)
  - Evaluation Harness: Model inference with fixed prompts -> Judge evaluation (Qwen-2.5-72B-Instruct) -> Metric computation (ASR, CHER, FPR-H) -> Defense configuration wrapper (D0-D4)
  - Defense Scaffold: SLD (7B) components for Input Guard (D2), Context Sanitizer (D3), Policy Composer (D4)
- Critical path:
  1. Start with a normalized clinical query.
  2. For V2, attach a poisoned context via rule families (R1–R10) after passing Conflict Quality gate (G3).
  3. Run target model with defense configuration (if any).
  4. Feed model output and metadata to the judge to obtain harm type and severity.
  5. Compute metrics: ASR (severity≥2), CHER3 (severity≥3), FPR-H (on V0/V0’).
- Design tradeoffs:
  - Redaction vs. Reproducibility: Public release redacts V2 payloads to mitigate dual-use, but exact ASR replication requires gated access to payload registry.
  - Judge Dependency: Using a single LLM judge (Qwen-2.5-72B-Instruct) improves consistency but introduces model-specific biases; human validation is limited to a 300-sample holdout.
  - Guaranteed Exposure (V2): Forces poisoned context into every instance to isolate post-retrieval failure, but ignores retrieval ranking and false-negative retrieval scenarios.
- Failure signatures:
  - High ASR but low CHER in V2: Indicates "Safe Gap"—model complies with poisoned context at a surface level but avoids catastrophic clinical errors.
  - High FPR-H under D4: Indicates over-sanitization or aggressive rewriting harming utility on benign queries.
  - D4 not outperforming D2/D3: Suggests negative interactions between input and context defenses (e.g., over-correction).
- First 3 experiments:
  1. Replicate Table 5: Run all 12 models across D0–D4 on the test split to verify the vector-specific defense effectiveness and ASR-CHER divergence patterns.
  2. V2 Tier Analysis: Compare CHER3 between V2-S (Strict) and V2-B (Borderline) to validate that higher conflict quality (G3 scores) correlates with higher harm rates.
  3. Judge Sensitivity Check: Swap the primary judge with other candidates (Mixtral-8x22B, Llama-3.1-70B) on a subset to quantify stability of CHER3 rankings as reported in Tables 3–4.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1: How can evaluation metrics for medical LLMs be redesigned to accurately reflect high-severity clinical harm, rather than relying solely on attack success rates (ASR) that may misalign with patient-safety outcomes?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] from the key outcome stating that "defenses reducing ASR did not consistently lower CHER, demonstrating metric misalignment" and the need for "metric diversification."
- Why unresolved: ASR measures instruction compliance but not necessarily severe clinical harm, leading to underestimation or overestimation of risks when evaluating defenses or model robustness.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies correlating metric scores (e.g., CHER vs. ASR) with real-world clinical outcome simulations or large-scale human expert assessments across diverse attack scenarios.

### Open Question 2: What underlying factors cause indirect (RAG-mediated) prompt injection to diverge from direct injection in clinical harm event rates (CHER), and how can models be architected to prioritize critical clinical instructions over poisoned context?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] from experimental results showing "V2 often yielded higher CHER than V1 in high-risk categories" and "ASR and CHER can diverge substantially, especially under V2," indicating authority framing in RAG contexts.
- Why unresolved: The paper identifies the phenomenon but does not explain mechanistic reasons (e.g., how models weigh context vs. query) or provide robust, generalizable defenses for V2 attacks.
- What evidence would resolve it: Ablation studies on model attention patterns under V1 vs. V2 attacks, or development and testing of context-injection detection and neutralization techniques specifically for clinical RAG systems.

### Open Question 3: How can the reliability and clinical validity of automated safety judges (LLM-as-a-judge) be improved for benchmarks like MPIB, given their susceptibility to bias and limited recall on severe harm cases?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] from limitations noting that "outcome labels are primarily produced via a structured LLM-as-a-judge pipeline, which may introduce judge-dependent bias, domain-specific under- or over-estimation, and limited recall on severe harm."
- Why unresolved: Judge model biases can distort benchmark metrics like CHER, affecting the reproducibility and credibility of safety evaluations in high-stakes domains.
- What evidence would resolve it: Multi-judge ensembles calibrated against large, diverse human expert annotations, or hybrid frameworks combining rule-based clinical linting with fine-tuned judges for specific harm types.

### Open Question 4: How can defense mechanisms for clinical prompt injection be optimized to achieve threat-specific calibrations, balancing safety (low CHER) and utility (low false-positive refusals) across different models and attack vectors?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] from the conclusion that composite defenses "were not uniformly optimal, underscoring need for metric diversification and threat-specific calibrations," and findings that "defense effectiveness can depend on whether adversarial instructions appear in the query or in retrieved context."
- Why unresolved: Current defenses (e.g., input rewriting, context sanitization) show inconsistent performance, with no one-size-fits-all solution, and may increase utility loss in some models.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic benchmarking of adaptive defense strategies on MPIB across model families, coupled with cost-benefit analyses of safety-utility trade-offs in simulated clinical workflows.

## Limitations
- Dataset construction: V2 "guaranteed exposure" forces poisoned context in every instance, removing retrieval ranking dynamics; conflict quality gate (G3) thresholds lack validation for real clinical harm correlation.
- Judge dependency: Single LLM judge (Qwen-2.5-72B-Instruct) introduces model-specific bias; human validation limited to 300 samples; severity taxonomy relies on automated mapping without clinical expert consensus.
- Generalizability: Evaluation limited to 12 models (mostly English); findings may not transfer to non-English or multimodal clinical VLMs; defenses are prototype (SLD-7B), not industry-grade.

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (V2 > V1 CHER in high-risk): Medium confidence (evidence is correlational; authority framing assumption not directly tested)
- Mechanism 2 (ASR–CHER divergence): Medium confidence (divergence observed but severity thresholds are benchmark-specific; real-world clinical alignment inferential)
- Mechanism 3 (Vector-specific defense effectiveness): High confidence (clear empirical patterns from multi-model, multi-defense experiments)
