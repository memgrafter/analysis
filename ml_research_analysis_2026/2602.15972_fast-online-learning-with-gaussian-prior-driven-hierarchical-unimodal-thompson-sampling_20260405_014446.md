# Fast Online Learning with Gaussian Prior-Driven Hierarchical Unimodal Thompson Sampling

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.15972
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15972
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Two hierarchical Thompson sampling algorithms (TSCG and UTSCG) achieve logarithmic regret in multi-armed bandits with clustered arms and Gaussian rewards, with TSCG’s regret independent of total arm count and UTSCG further reducing regret by exploiting unimodality within clusters.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles stochastic multi-armed bandit problems where arms are grouped into clusters and rewards follow Gaussian distributions. It introduces two algorithms: TSCG, which performs two-level Thompson sampling by first selecting a cluster then an arm within it, and UTSCG, which additionally restricts exploration within a cluster to the leader arm and its neighbors under a unimodal ordering. Under strong dominance between clusters and unimodality within the optimal cluster, the algorithms achieve regret bounds that are independent of the total number of arms and logarithmic in the time horizon. Empirical results on mmWave beam selection and portfolio management show both methods outperform standard Thompson sampling, UCB, and a two-level policy, with UTSCG showing particular advantage when clusters are large.

## Method Summary
The methods extend standard Thompson sampling to a hierarchical setting. For TSCG, the agent maintains separate Gaussian posterior distributions for each cluster (based on rewards from any arm in that cluster) and for each arm. At each round, it first samples a cluster from its posterior and selects the cluster with the highest sample. Then, within that cluster, it samples each arm from its posterior and selects the arm with the highest sample. After observing the reward, both the arm and its cluster posteriors are updated. UTSCG modifies the inner step: after selecting a cluster, it identifies the current leader (arm with highest empirical mean in that cluster) and restricts candidate arms to the leader and its immediate neighbors according to a predefined unimodal ordering of arms within the cluster. The algorithms assume Gaussian rewards with known unit variance and use Gaussian priors with mean zero and variance one, yielding conjugate posterior updates.

## Key Results
- TSCG achieves an expected regret bound that depends only on the number of clusters and inter-cluster gaps, not on the total number of arms.
- UTSCG further reduces the intra-cluster exploration cost by limiting pulls to the leader and its neighbors, achieving a lower bound when unimodality holds.
- Both algorithms outperform baselines (TSG, UCB, TLP) in cumulative regret and optimal arm selection rate in experiments, with UTSCG showing clear gains for larger clusters.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Hierarchical Clustering Regret Decoupling
TSCG reduces regret bounds by making them independent of total arm count *n* instead depending on cluster count *K* and cluster quality (the minimum inter-cluster gap ∆′_C). The agent maintains separate posteriors for clusters and arms. Selecting a cluster first prunes the search space from *n* arms to ~*|C|* arms. The strong dominance assumption (∀C≠C*, all arms in C* are better than all arms in C) ensures that mis-selected clusters incur bounded regret proportional to inter-cluster gaps ∆′_C, not intra-cluster gaps ∆_i. The expected number of pulls of a sub-optimal cluster *C* is bounded by *O*(log(T)/∆′_C²) (Lemma 2), decoupling it from *n*.  
*Core assumption:* Assumption 1 (Strong Dominance): min_{i∈C*} µ_i > max_{i∈C≠C*} µ_i.  
*Evidence anchors:* Abstract states lower regret via two-level structure; Theorem 2 formally shows regret sums over clusters and optimal cluster arms only, independent of *n*. Corpus evidence for hierarchical MAB exists but not for this specific Gaussian combination.  
*Break condition:* If strong dominance is violated (e.g., an arm in a sub-optimal cluster has mean higher than the worst arm in the optimal cluster), the cluster-level bounding fails and regret may scale with *n* again.

### Mechanism 2: Unimodal Neighbor Exploitation
UTSCG further lowers regret within the optimal cluster by restricting exploration to the leader arm and its immediate neighbors in the unimodal ordering. This converts an *O*(|C*|) sum over arms to a sum over only neighbors of the optimal arm, turning a logarithmic term into a constant (F term from prior work) for non-neighbor arms. After cluster selection, UTSCG identifies the current leader (highest empirical mean) and considers only that leader and its adjacent arms as candidates. The unimodal property guarantees that non-neighbor arms are significantly sub-optimal once the leader is near the optimum, so pulling them yields high instantaneous regret.  
*Core assumption:* Assumption 2 (Unimodality within each cluster): The expected reward µ_i as a function of arm index (ordered by a covariate) is unimodal with a single peak.  
*Evidence anchors:* Abstract and Section 5 (Theorem 3) show the second term changes from ∑_{i∈C*} to ∑_{i∈Neighbor(i*)} plus constant. Corpus evidence supports unimodal TS generally but not the hierarchical combination.  
*Break condition:* If the reward ordering within a cluster is not unimodal (e.g., multimodal or random), the neighbor restriction may exclude the optimal arm or include highly sub-optimal arms with small gaps, degrading performance to or below TSCG.

### Mechanism 3: Gaussian Prior-Weighted Posterior Sampling
Using a Gaussian prior (conjugate to Gaussian rewards) enables efficient closed-form posterior updates and regret bounds that scale logarithmically via sub-Gaussian concentration. For each arm/cluster, the posterior is Gaussian with mean equal to the empirical mean and variance 1/(N+1), where N is the number of pulls. Sampling from this posterior naturally balances exploration and exploitation. The analysis leverages that sample means are sub-Gaussian, allowing Chernoff-Hoeffding bounds to show an arm with gap ∆ is pulled *O*(log(T)/∆²) times (Lemma 1).  
*Core assumption:* Rewards are i.i.d. Gaussian with unknown mean but known, equal variance (σ²=1 in updates). The prior variance is fixed (precision 1).  
*Evidence anchors:* Section 3 and Lemma 1/Theorem 1 provide Gaussian-specific sub-Gaussian bound. Corpus strongly supports Gaussian TS.  
*Break condition:* If the reward variance is unknown and/or heterogeneous, the fixed-precision prior is mismatched; posterior updates become incorrect and concentration bounds may fail, increasing regret.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Thompson Sampling (TS)
*Why needed:* TSCG/UTSCG are built by applying TS at two hierarchical levels. Understanding the base TS algorithm (sample from posterior, pick max) is essential to see how the hierarchy modifies the action space.
*Quick check:* What happens to the posterior distribution of an arm's mean after each pull?  
Answer: It becomes Gaussian with updated mean (weighted average) and decreased variance (increased precision).

### Concept: Regret Decomposition in Bandits
*Why needed:* The theoretical analysis decomposes total expected regret into sums over sub-optimal arms/clusters, each bounded by *O*(log(T)/gap²). Grasping this decomposition (Eq. 22) is key to understanding how structural assumptions (clustering, unimodality) change which gaps (∆′_C vs. ∆_i) appear in the bound.
*Quick check:* In Eq. 22 for TSCG, what does the first double summation (over C≠C*, i∈C) represent?  
Answer: Regret from pulling arms in sub-optimal clusters.

### Concept: Unimodal Bandit Structure
*Why needed:* UTSCG’s advantage hinges on knowing arms have a single peak in expected reward when ordered correctly. This allows neighbor-limited search.
*Quick check:* In a unimodal cluster with ordered arms 1...m and optimal arm i*, is arm i*-2 guaranteed to be better than arm i*+3?  
Answer: No. Monotonicity is only guaranteed on each side of the mode. i*-2 is on the left side (decreasing as index increases towards mode), i*+3 is on the right side (decreasing as index increases away from mode). Their relative order isn't specified without knowing which side has higher values.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Cluster Posterior Manager -> Hierarchical Selector -> Arm Posterior Manager -> Observation Processor; also Arm Posterior Manager -> Hierarchical Selector (for leader identification in UTSCG); Observation Processor updates both managers.

### Critical Path
1. Cluster TS: sample η_C for each cluster, select C(t) = argmax η_C.
2. If UTSCG: find leader L(t) = argmax_{i∈C(t)} ˆµ_i.
3. Arm TS: sample η_i for candidate arms (all arms in C(t) for TSCG; L(t) ∪ neighbors for UTSCG), select i(t) = argmax η_i.
4. Observe reward X(t).
5. Update arm posterior for i(t) and cluster posterior for C(t).

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Regret vs. Assumption Strength:** UTSCG offers better regret if unimodality holds; otherwise TSCG is safer.
- **Computational Cost:** UTSCG is slightly cheaper per round than TSCG because the inner TS loop runs over fewer candidate arms (neighbors + leader vs. all arms in cluster). Overhead is finding the leader (O(|C|)).
- **Prior Variance Tuning:** Paper fixes prior precision=1. If true variance σ² differs, convergence slows. Adaptive prior could help but complicates analysis.

### Failure Signatures
1. **Strong Dominance Violation:** Cumulative regret for TSCG/UTSCG approaches or exceeds TSG/UCB when a sub-optimal cluster contains arms with means interleaved with optimal cluster’s means. Diagnose by comparing min_{i∈C*} µ_i vs. max_{i∈C≠C*} µ_i from offline data.
2. **Non-Unimodal Cluster:** UTSCG performance degrades to TSCG levels or worse if optimal arm is not centrally located among neighbors or cluster has multiple modes. Sign: optimal arm selection rate plateaus lower than TSCG.
3. **Gaussian Reward Mismatch:** If rewards are heavy-tailed or heteroscedastic, posterior concentration is poor. Sign: posterior variance remains high for well-pulled arms, or algorithm frequently re-explores known bad arms.

1. **Replicate mmWave Scenario (Section 6.1):** 3 clusters (frequencies), 3 arms each (beams). Strong dominance holds. Verify Fig. 4/5: UTSCG ≈ TSCG (small cluster size, unimodality benefit minimal).

Compare TSG, TSCG (treat whole set as one cluster → no gain), UTSCG (should match unimodal TS performance). Also test a non-unimodal cluster (e.g., multimodal means) to show UTSCG fails to improve over TSCG.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Can the TSCG algorithm and its theoretical analysis be extended to sub-Gaussian reward distributions, and what would be the corresponding regret bounds?**  
   The paper states this as an immediate upgrade but notes the current analysis is tailored to Gaussian rewards and may not directly apply.

2. **How can TSCG and UTSCG be adapted when the standard deviations of the Gaussian rewards are unknown, and what are the implications for the regret bound?**  
   The algorithms assume known variance; unknown variances would require more complex inference (e.g., Gaussian-inverse-gamma) and may alter regret bounds.

3. **Under what weaker conditions than the Strong Dominance assumption (Assumption 1) can the regret bounds for TSCG and UTSCG still hold?**  
   The portfolio experiment violates strong dominance yet algorithms perform well, suggesting the assumption may be unnecessarily strict. A new analysis relaxing it is needed.

4. **Can the hierarchical bandit framework be extended to learn the cluster structure online, rather than requiring it to be given a priori?**  
   Current algorithms assume predefined clusters; online clustering would significantly increase complexity and require joint inference.

## Limitations
- Strong dominance and unimodality assumptions are fragile and often not guaranteed in real-world data (e.g., portfolio assets).
- Fixed Gaussian prior with unit variance assumes known, homoscedastic noise; unknown or heterogeneous variance degrades performance.
- Theoretical bounds are asymptotic; finite-time sensitivity to assumption violations is not fully characterized.
- Predefined clustering and unimodal ordering are nontrivial prerequisites that may not be available.

## Confidence
- **TSCG regret decoupling:** Medium. Proof relies on strong dominance, a restrictive condition with limited empirical validation.
- **UTSCG intra-cluster gain:** Medium. Unimodal restriction is sound theoretically but highly assumption-dependent; external validation for hierarchical case is weak.
- **Gaussian prior-driven TS:** High. Conjugate updates and sub-Gaussian analysis are standard and well-supported.

## Next Checks
1. **Dominance Violation Gradient:** Synthesize bandits where the inter-cluster gap (min µ_i in C* vs max µ_j in other clusters) varies from large positive to negative. Track TSCG/TSG regret ratio to pinpoint breakdown.
2. **Non-Unimodal Robustness:** In a single large cluster, create multimodal reward means while preserving the given arm ordering. Compare UTSCG vs. TSCG optimal arm selection rates.
3. **Variance Robustness:** Simulate with σ² = 0.5, 1, 2, 4 and heteroscedastic per-cluster variances. Monitor posterior variance calibration and cumulative regret growth to assess robustness to variance misspecification.
