# LM-Lexicon: Improving Definition Modeling via Harmonizing Semantic Experts

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.14060
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14060
- **Reference Count**: 40
- **Primary Result**: +7% BLEU improvement over prior SOTA on five benchmarks; human evaluation yields 4.6/5 definition accuracy.

## Executive Summary
Definition modeling generates natural language definitions for a target term given its context, but faces challenges due to semantic heterogeneity and rare word senses across domains. LM-Lexicon tackles this by decomposing the task into specialized semantic domains. It clusters training data into semantically coherent shards, trains separate small language models as domain experts, and merges them into a single sparse mixture-of-experts (MoE) model using a novel semantic‑aware domain‑level routing mechanism. After fine‑tuning, the model leverages expert specialization while maintaining a unified inference architecture.

On five standard benchmarks, LM-Lexicon improves BLEU by 7% over the previous state‑of‑the‑art. The clustering strategy alone contributes nearly a 10% boost in definition quality, and domain‑level routing adds a 1% gain over token‑level routing. Human evaluation confirms superior accuracy (4.6/5) and context appropriateness compared to frontier LLMs. The approach scales effectively with more experts and additional test‑time compute, demonstrating the benefits of harmonizing semantic experts.

## Method Summary
The method comprises four steps:  
1. **Data partitioning**: Prompt strings ``<BOS>{{c}} WHAT IS THE DEFINITION OF {{t}}<EOS>`` are embedded with the `nvidia‑embed‑v2` model. Balanced k‑means (k=4) clusters the 1.3M‑sample 3D‑EX corpus into semantically distinct shards.  
2. **Expert training**: Each shard fine‑tunes a separate Llama‑3‑8B for three epochs, masking prompt tokens and computing loss only on definition tokens (NLL).  
3. **MoE merging**: Experts are combined by stacking each layer’s FFN into a sparse mixture‑of‑experts block; other parameters (MHA, embeddings) are averaged. A linear router per layer is initialized, and routing is performed at domain level: the input’s embedding is compared to pre‑computed cluster centroids via cosine similarity, and the expert with the highest similarity is selected.  
4. **Fine‑tuning**: The merged MoE is fine‑tuned on the full 3D‑EX training set for one epoch to adapt the router and coordinate the experts, using the same hyperparameters with a slightly lower learning rate.

## Key Results
- +7% BLEU improvement over prior SOTA across five benchmarks.
- Clustering strategy yields ~10% boost in definition quality; domain‑level routing adds +1% over token‑level routing.
- Human evaluation: definition accuracy 4.6/5 and superior context appropriateness versus frontier LLMs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Semantic heterogeneity is mitigated by clustering similar contexts into shards; each expert then learns a coherent sense distribution without interference. Domain‑level routing reduces noise by matching an input to a pre‑computed centroid, ensuring that the appropriate specialist is activated. The MoE merge preserves these specialized weights while producing a single deployable model, and the final fine‑tuning step coordinates the router and experts to avoid catastrophic interference. This harmonization yields more accurate and context‑appropriate definitions, especially for rare senses.

## Foundational Learning
- **Definition Modeling**  
  *Why needed*: Core task of generating human‑readable definitions; introduces challenges of polysemy and context sensitivity.  
  *Quick check*: Can a model produce distinct definitions for “bank” in “river bank” versus “bank account”?

- **Semantic Heterogeneity**  
  *Why needed*: Variations in meaning across domains cause standard models to conflate senses and degrade performance.  
  *Quick check*: Inspect definitions for a polysemous word across different corpora; note inconsistencies.

- **Clustering for Semantic Sharding**  
  *Why needed*: Unsupervised grouping creates data partitions that reflect underlying sense distributions, enabling expert specialization.  
  *Quick check*: Apply k‑means to sentence embeddings; compute average intra‑cluster cohesion and inter‑cluster separation.

- **Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE)**  
  *Why needed*: Allows scaling model capacity by sparsely activating subsets of parameters, balancing quality and efficiency.  
  *Quick check*: Implement a simple MoE layer with two experts and verify that different inputs route to different experts.

- **Domain‑Level Routing**  
  *Why needed*: Operates on coarse semantic features (centroids) rather than token‑level decisions, improving routing stability and expert utilization.  
  *Quick check*: For a given input embedding, compute cosine similarity to stored centroids and route based on the argmax; observe that inputs from the same semantic domain consistently select the same expert.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**: Data -> Prompt Formatting -> Embedding -> Clustering -> Semantic Shards -> Expert Training (4× Llama‑3‑8B) -> MoE Merge (stack FFN, avg others) -> Router Initialization -> Fine‑tuning -> Final Model.

**Critical Path**: Raw data must be clustered successfully; expert training must learn distinct representations; merging must correctly stack FFN layers; fine‑tuning must coordinate router and experts. Any break (poor clustering, dimension mismatch, omitted fine‑tuning) leads to failure.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- **Number of experts**: More experts increase specialization but also memory, routing overhead, and training cost.
- **Clustering method**: Balanced k‑means ensures equal shard sizes but may sacrifice semantic purity; standard k‑means may yield better separation at the cost of imbalance.
- **Router design**: Domain‑level routing is sparse and robust to collapse but relies on accurate centroids; token‑level routing offers finer granularity but risks instability.
- **Parameter efficiency**: Training full experts is compute‑heavy; techniques like LoRA could reduce cost but may limit the degree of specialization.
- **Hardware/parallelism**: DeepSpeed ZeRO‑3 enables large models on many GPUs; naive pipeline parallelism may be more accessible but less memory‑efficient.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Low cluster separation: average inter‑centroid cosine similarity >0.6; manual inspection shows overlapping terms across shards.
- Router collapse: routing distribution >90% to a single expert during fine‑tuning.
- Catastrophic interference: after merging, the MoE’s performance on individual shard test sets is significantly lower than the corresponding standalone expert.

**First Experiments**  
1. **Cluster fidelity**: Use `sklearn`'s `KMeans` (k=4) and a public `SentenceTransformer` to replicate clustering on 3D‑EX. Compare cohesion/separation metrics and top terms per cluster against paper values.  
2. **Single expert verification**: Train one Llama‑3‑8B on one shard for 1 epoch; evaluate BLEU on that shard’s test set to confirm learning.  
3. **Router fine‑tuning protocol**: Merge two experts and fine‑tune under two conditions: (a) router‑only (experts frozen) and (b) joint (all parameters). Monitor routing entropy and validation BLEU to determine the optimal strategy.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1**: How can the LM‑Lexicon framework be adapted to other languages and semantic‑intensive tasks beyond English definition modeling?  
- **Basis**: The paper explicitly states in its limitations that it focuses only on English definition modeling.  
- **Why unresolved**: No experiments on cross‑lingual or multi‑task generalization are provided.  
- **Evidence to resolve**: Conduct experiments on multilingual definition datasets (e.g., BabelNet) and related tasks like word sense disambiguation or semantic role labeling, comparing LM‑Lexicon against baselines to demonstrate transferability and identify task‑specific modifications.

**Open Question 2**: What parameter‑efficient training techniques can reduce the computational cost of LM‑Lexicon while preserving its performance gains from semantic expert specialization?  
- **Basis**: The paper acknowledges the high cost of training N×M expert LMs and encourages exploration of parameter‑efficient methods.  
- **Why unresolved**: No alternatives such as LoRA or adapters are explored, and their trade‑offs with expert specialization remain unclear.  
- **Evidence to resolve**: Implement parameter‑efficient variants (e.g., low‑rank adaptation on expert FFNs), evaluate on 3D‑EX and other benchmarks, and measure FLOPs, memory footprint, and BLEU to quantify efficiency‑performance trade‑offs.

**Open Question 3**: How can we design robust verification methods (verifiers) specifically for definition modeling to enhance test‑time compute scaling, such as in Best‑of‑N sampling?  
- **Basis**: The paper highlights the lack of strong verifiers for language generation and the limitations of their outcome reward model (ORM).  
- **Why unresolved**: Current verifiers are either reference‑based or simple ORMs; more general, semantic‑aware verifiers are needed.  
- **Evidence to resolve**: Develop reward models trained on human preferences or using semantic metrics (e.g., BERTScore), integrate them into Best‑of‑N pipelines, and demonstrate improved BLEU and human evaluation scores over existing ORM setups.

**Open Question 4**: What is the optimal number of semantic clusters for data partitioning in LM‑Lexicon, and how does it affect expert specialization, model performance, and training efficiency?  
- **Basis**: The paper fixes k=4 based on pre‑experiments but does not systematically vary the number of clusters.  
- **Why unresolved**: The trade‑off between finer specialization (more clusters) and increased complexity/possible under‑specialization (fewer clusters) is unexplored.  
- **Evidence to resolve**: Train LM‑Lexicon with varying cluster counts (e.g., 2, 4, 8, 16) on 3D‑EX and other benchmarks, measuring BLEU/ROUGE gains, expert utilization patterns, and training time to identify the optimal balance for different resource constraints.

## Limitations
- **Clustering replication**: The exact balanced k‑means implementation and the `nvidia‑embed‑v2` model are unspecified; using standard `sklearn` may produce semantically different shards.
- **Router fine‑tuning protocol**: Ambiguity remains about whether experts are frozen or jointly trained, the exact loss composition, and the meaning of “slightly” fine‑tuning (duration, early stopping).
- **Hardware dependency**: Training relied on 8×H100 with DeepSpeed ZeRO‑3 and naive pipeline parallelism; details for porting to smaller GPU configurations are lacking.
