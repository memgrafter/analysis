# Evaluating Large and Lightweight Vision Models for Irregular Component Segmentation in E-Waste Disassembly

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.27441
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27441
- **Reference Count:** 38
- **Primary Result:** A task-specific lightweight model (YOLOv8) achieves 98.8% mAP50 on e-waste component segmentation, vastly outperforming a general-purpose foundation model (SAM2, 8.4% mAP50) when trained on a domain-specific dataset.

## Executive Summary
This study addresses the challenge of precise pixel-level segmentation of irregular, densely packed electronic waste components for robotic disassembly. It compares a large, general-purpose vision foundation model (SAM2) with a lightweight, task-specific architecture (YOLOv8) on a newly collected dataset of 1,456 annotated images of laptop assemblies. After applying targeted data augmentation and fine-tuning, YOLOv8 achieves near-perfect instance segmentation accuracy (mAP50 = 98.8%, mAP50-95 = 85%) with real-time inference speeds. In contrast, SAM2 yields a very low mAP50 of 8.4%. The authors attribute this drastic difference to a fundamental mismatch: SAM2’s class-agnostic, multi-mask generation strategy conflicts with the class-aware instance segmentation metrics required for the task, not purely to an inability to segment object boundaries. The work demonstrates that for narrow industrial domains like e-waste, compact task-optimized models can surpass massive pre-trained foundation models in accuracy and efficiency, provided sufficient domain-specific data is available.

## Method Summary
The evaluation uses a custom dataset of 1,456 RGB images capturing disassembled laptop components (logic boards, heat sinks, IO boards, fans). The raw dataset of 448 images is expanded to 1,344 training images via augmentation (random ±15° rotation, horizontal/vertical flips, and cropping with 0–14% zoom). Models are trained and evaluated using COCO-style instance segmentation metrics (mAP50, mAP50-95). YOLOv8 (specifically the segmentation variant) is trained from COCO pre-initialization with its standard anchor-free, decoupled head and a hybrid loss (CIoU, DFL, BCE). SAM2 is fine-tuned from a pre-trained checkpoint with its image encoder frozen and only the mask decoder updated using AdamW. Training, validation, and test splits are 1,344, 56, and 56 images, respectively.

## Key Results
- **YOLOv8** achieves **mAP50=98.8%** and **mAP50-95=85%** with stable, real-time performance.
- **SAM2** achieves **mAP50=8.4%**, primarily due to generating class-agnostic masks that conflict with class-aware evaluation metrics.
- **Data augmentation** (rotation, flip, crop) is applied to all training images to improve robustness to real-world variations in orientation and layout.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** A task-specific, lightweight architecture (YOLOv8) can outperform a large, general-purpose foundation model (SAM2) on pixel-accurate segmentation of irregular, densely packed e-waste components **when trained on a domain-specific dataset**.  
**Mechanism:** YOLOv8’s anchor-free detection and decoupled segmentation head are trained end-to-end to directly predict class-labeled masks. Its C2f feature fusion module aggregates multi-scale features, helping capture both fine structural details (e.g., edges of small IO boards) and broader contextual relationships (e.g., spatial layout of a logic board adjacent to a heat sink). This, coupled with a hybrid loss function (CIoU, DFL, BCE), optimizes for precise boundary delineation and class separation in cluttered scenes.  
**Core assumption:** The visual characteristics of e-waste (irregular shapes, dense packing, low-contrast metallic/PCB surfaces) form a sufficiently narrow domain where a model can learn specialized feature representations from scratch, surpassing the benefit of SAM2’s broad, class-agnostic pre-training.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract]: "YOLOv8 achieved superior segmentation accuracy... SAM2... produced flexible but often overlapping and class-agnostic masks... large pre-trained models require substantial task-specific optimization."  
- [section 3.2]: Describes YOLOv8’s "anchor-free strategy" and "decoupled task-specific head" that "segregates detection confidence, classification, and localization," minimizing cross-task interference in cluttered scenes.  
- [corpus]: Neighboring papers focus on robotic disassembly systems but do not provide direct comparative evidence; this supports the paper’s niche relevance.  
**Break condition:** If the target e-waste stream introduces component geometries, materials, or spatial arrangements (e.g., novel connector types, extreme occlusion) not represented in the 1,456-image laptop dataset, YOLOv8’s performance would likely degrade without further dataset expansion or architectural adjustment.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** A large, pre-trained vision transformer (SAM2) fails to achieve high mAP under standard instance segmentation metrics for e-waste **primarily due to its class-agnostic, multi-mask generation strategy**, not purely boundary precision.  
**Mechanism:** SAM2 is designed to output multiple candidate masks per image without inherent class labels. In COCO-style evaluation, a predicted mask must match both the ground-truth mask (IoU) *and* the correct class label. SAM2’s masks, even if geometrically reasonable, are often not assigned the correct class label via the study’s post-processing, and redundant/overlapping masks are penalized as false positives. This creates a systematic mismatch between the model’s output and the evaluation protocol.  
**Core assumption:** The primary value of a foundation model like SAM2 for this task is in accelerating annotation or providing flexible initial proposals, not in direct, plug-and-play metric optimization for a fixed, small set of component classes.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [section 5.3]: "The large gap in quantitative scores does not indicate that SAM2 fails to segment e-waste components... The low mAP scores instead reflect a mismatch between SAM2’s class-agnostic mask generation strategy and the COCO evaluation protocol..."  
- [section 5.1]: Qualitative figures show SAM2 can delineate primary object contours after fine-tuning but fails in dense multi-object scenes and produces overlapping masks.  
- [corpus]: Weak; no neighboring paper directly analyzes this limitation in e-waste contexts. Evidence is internal.  
**Break condition:** If the evaluation framework is adapted to assess mask quality independent of class (e.g., boundary F-score or mask-level IoU without class matching), SAM2’s perceived performance would likely improve, revealing its geometric segmentation capability.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** **Targeted data augmentation (rotation ±15°, flipping, cropping)** is a necessary prerequisite for enabling both models to generalize across variable illumination, orientation, and component layout within the limited 1,456-image dataset.  
**Mechanism:** The e-waste images are captured under real-world disassembly conditions with varying lighting and component orientation. Augmentation synthetically expands the training distribution, teaching the model to treat rotated, flipped, or partially cropped instances as valid examples. This reduces overfitting to the specific poses and backgrounds in the raw images and improves robustness to the inherent variability.  
**Core assumption:** The underlying 3D variability of components can be sufficiently approximated by 2D affine transformations (±15° rotation) without requiring 3D reconstructions or vastly larger datasets.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract]: "Data augmentation techniques, such as random rotation, flipping, and cropping, were applied to improve model robustness."  
- [section 4]: "Each training image generated three variants, expanding the training set from 448 to 1,344 images. This augmentation enriches the variety of object appearances and strengthens a model’s learning ability."  
- [section 5.1]: Compares fine-tuning with low- versus high-resolution images and shows improvement with high-resolution, implying augmentation was part of the standard protocol.  
**Break condition:** If component appearance changes are non-rigid (e.g., flexing cables, deformed heatsinks) or involve photometric changes beyond affine geometry (e.g., specular highlight shifts, shadow movement), simple rotation/flip augmentation may be insufficient, requiring more advanced photometric augmentations or physically-based rendering.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Instance vs. Semantic Segmentation**  
  *Why needed here:* The task requires distinguishing *individual* component instances (e.g., two separate fans) within a densely packed assembly, not just labeling all fan pixels as "fan." The paper’s metrics (mAP) and YOLOv8’s design are for instance segmentation. SAM2, while capable of instance segmentation via prompts, was evaluated in a class-agnostic manner that blurred this distinction.  
  *Quick check question:* If your model merges the masks of two adjacent, identical components into one blob, which segmentation paradigm has failed, and what architectural feature typically addresses this?  
  *(Answer: Instance segmentation has failed; typically addressed by separate detection heads or mask separation algorithms like those in Mask R-CNN or YOLO’s decoupled head.)*

- **Concept: Non-Convex Geometry and Thin Structures in Vision**  
  *Why needed here:* E-waste components (e.g., CPU sockets, finned heatsinks) have complex, non-convex shapes and thin protrusions. Standard convolutional kernels may struggle to preserve these fine details through downsampling, leading to boundary erosion. The paper contrasts YOLOv8’s "fine spatial details" capture with SAM2’s "inconsistent contours."  
  *Quick check question:* Why might a U-Net style skip connection be more effective than a pure transformer encoder for preserving thin component edges in a segmentation decoder?  
  *(Answer: Skip connections fuse high-resolution features from early layers with deep semantic features, preserving spatial details and boundaries that can be lost in transformer-based hierarchical downsampling.)*

- **Concept: Class-Agnostic vs. Class-Specific Segmentation**  
  *Why needed here:* SAM2 is inherently class-agnostic; it segments "objectness" without knowing it’s a "logic board." YOLOv8 is trained to output "logic board" masks. This fundamental difference explains the mAP gap. Applying SAM2 to this task requires an additional step (prompting or post-processing) to assign class labels to its many candidate masks.  
  *Quick check question:* In a robotic disassembly context, why is a class-specific output (e.g., "this mask is a screw") more directly actionable than a set of class-agnostic masks?  
  *(Answer: Robotic actions are component-specific (e.g., unscrew, pry, lift). Class labels map directly to the required tool and motion; class-agnostic masks would require an extra classification step, adding latency and potential error.)*

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Input RGB image -> YOLOv8: Backbone (CSPDarknet53 with C2f) -> Neck (Feature Pyramid with concatenation/upsampling) -> Head (Decoupled, anchor-free detection & mask prediction). SAM2: Image Encoder (Hierarchical Vision Transformer) -> Mask Decoder (takes image embedding + optional prompts) -> Output (Multiple candidate masks).

**Critical path:**
1. Start with YOLOv8n (nano) or YOLOv8s (small) pre-trained on COCO.
2. Replace the segmentation head’s output classes with 4 (logic board, heat sink, IO board, fan).
3. Train on augmented dataset with specified loss (CIoU, DFL, BCE) until validation loss plateaus.
4. Evaluate on holdout 56-image test set using COCO-style mAP50 and mAP50-95.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **YOLOv8:** Speed (real-time capable) vs. Potential missed small components (though paper shows high mAP). Class-specific design limits flexibility to unseen component types.
- **SAM2:** Flexibility (segment any object) vs. Practical usability (requires prompting/post-processing for class assignment; computationally heavier; poor mAP under standard protocol).
- **Augmentation:** Rotation range (±15°) balances variability vs. risk of making components unrecognizable (e.g., a heatsink upside-down might be improbable on a fixed disassembly line).

**Failure signatures:**
- **YOLOv8:** (1) Missing small components (e.g., individual screws not in dataset), (2) merged masks for components in extremely close proximity, (3) poor boundaries on highly reflective or low-contrast areas if augmentation didn’t simulate those conditions.
- **SAM2:** (1) Multiple overlapping masks for a single component, (2) masks for non-component regions (background clutter), (3) consistent failure to assign correct class label without significant prompt engineering.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Baseline replication:** Train YOLOv8s from COCO pre-training on the full augmented dataset. Report mAP50/50-95 and inference FPS. Expect ~98.8% / 85% as in paper.
2. **Resolution sensitivity:** Train identical YOLOv8 models on inputs downscaled to 320px, 480px, 640px (original). Measure impact on mAP, especially for small IO boards. Expect performance drop with lower resolution.
3. **SAM2 prompting protocol:** Implement a simple post-processing for SAM2: for each test image, generate all masks, then use a small, manually labeled patch of each component class as a prompt to assign masks to classes. Calculate a *mask-only* IoU (ignoring class errors) to isolate SAM2’s boundary quality from its class-assignment problem. Expect mask IoU to be higher than 8.4% mAP but still below YOLOv8.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:** How well do the segmentation models generalize to e-waste components from laptop brands and device categories beyond the three Apple MacBook models in the dataset?  
*Basis:* The test set contains 56 images from three Apple MacBook models; authors note limited device diversity may contribute to YOLOv8’s high mAP and plan future work on cross-brand evaluation.  
*Why unresolved:* Dataset’s narrow brand/device focus means reported performance may not hold for the broader, more variable e-waste stream.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Multi-brand, multi-device evaluation on a held-out test set comprising various manufacturers and form factors, measuring performance degradation or consistency relative to the current dataset.

**Open Question 2:** For SAM2, what specific architectural or post-processing modifications are needed to effectively translate its class-agnostic, multi-mask output into the class-specific, non-overlapping instance masks required for pixel-precise robotic disassembly?  
*Basis:* Authors state SAM2’s primary limitation is lack of class-specific mask assignment and that foundation models require additional post-processing or prompt engineering.  
*Why unresolved:* SAM2’s core design generates numerous candidate masks without class labels, leading to high false positives under standard evaluation. The paper identifies the mismatch but does not investigate solutions.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* An ablation study comparing different post-processing pipelines (e.g., clustering masks by location/appearance, training a secondary classifier on mask crops, few-shot prompt tuning) applied to SAM2’s outputs, measuring resulting mAP50 and overlap reduction.

**Open Question 3:** Can more computationally efficient fine-tuning strategies (e.g., parameter-efficient methods like LoRA, or training with fewer high-quality samples) mitigate SAM2's performance gap with YOLOv8 without the prohibitive computational cost implied by the authors' constraints?  
*Basis:* Authors note "limited fine-tuning iterations for SAM2 due to computational constraints" as a limitation and that they froze SAM2’s encoder and only updated the decoder.  
*Why unresolved:* SAM2’s severe underperformance (8.4% vs 98.8%) suggests the chosen fine-tuning scope and dataset size were insufficient. It is unclear if a different adaptation approach could yield competitive accuracy at feasible compute cost.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* An experiment comparing various fine-tuning regimes for SAM2 (full fine-tuning, decoder-only, LoRA adapters) on this dataset, tracking performance vs. compute cost (GPU hours, parameters updated) to identify a practical Pareto frontier.

**Open Question 4:** How would incorporating multimodal sensory data (e.g., depth, infrared) as proposed, specifically improve the segmentation of low-contrast, reflective e-waste components where RGB cues are insufficient?  
*Basis:* Conclusion lists "incorporate multimodal sensing (depth and infrared)" as key future direction to address "metallic and PCB surfaces produce low-contrast visual appearances with reflective artifacts."  
*Why unresolved:* The paper identifies low contrast and reflections as a major challenge but only evaluates monocular RGB. The hypothesis that depth can disambiguate geometry and infrared might capture thermal signatures or penetrate certain materials is untested.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Collection and annotation of a synchronized RGB-D and/or RGB-IR subset of the dataset, followed by training and evaluating fusion models (early/late fusion) for both SAM2 and YOLOv8, measuring performance gains on subsets with known problematic regions.

**Open Question 5:** Do hybrid transformer-convolutional architectures offer a better accuracy-latency trade-off for this task than either pure CNN (YOLOv8) or pure transformer (SAM2) approaches, and if so, what is the optimal integration point?  
*Basis:* Conclusion proposes to "investigate hybrid transformer-convolutional architectures optimized for precise, real-time robotic disassembly."  
*Why unresolved:* The paper contrasts a large, slow transformer (SAM2) with a fast, accurate CNN (YOLOv8). Recent architectures like SegFormer show promise in combining CNN backbones with transformer heads for efficiency. The specific benefits and costs of hybridization for irregular, dense e-waste segmentation—especially regarding YOLOv8’s boundary precision issues and SAM2’s speed—have not been explored.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* A benchmark study substituting YOLOv8’s backbone or feature pyramid with transformer blocks (or vice-versa for SAM2) and measuring the three-way trade-off (mAP50-95, inference FPS, model size) on the e-waste dataset to identify optimal hybrid configurations.

## Limitations
- **Dataset Scope & Generalizability:** Results are based on 1,456 laptop images from three Apple MacBook models. Performance may degrade on other e-waste types (phones, printers) with different component geometries and clutter patterns.
- **Reproducibility of Training:** Critical YOLOv8 hyperparameters (learning rate, loss weights λ) and exact SAM2 fine-tuning configuration (learning rate, scheduler) are unspecified; reliance on default settings introduces variability.
- **SAM2 Evaluation Mismatch:** The dramatic SAM2 mAP deficit is attributed to its class-agnostic output conflicting with class-aware COCO metrics. This interpretation is plausible but not independently verified with an alternative, class-agnostic evaluation protocol.

## Confidence
- **YOLOv8 superiority on this dataset:** High. Supported by reported metrics and consistent qualitative evidence; direct replication is feasible.
- **SAM2’s failure is due to evaluation mismatch, not pure masking ability:** Medium. The paper’s reasoning is coherent and supported by examples, but requires a validation check using a class-agnostic metric.
- **Targeted augmentation is necessary for robustness:** Medium. The paper states its use and implies benefit, but no ablation study isolates augmentation’s specific contribution.
