# Explaining Grokking in Transformers through the Lens of Inductive Bias

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.06702
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06702
- **Reference count:** 20
- **Primary result:** Layer Normalization placement and optimization settings steer grokking by modulating inductive biases (MLP input scale sensitivity, attention entropy, value channel scale), with generalization emerging predictably as feature representations become more compressible.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the phenomenon of *grokking*—where models achieve near-zero training loss but only generalize after a prolonged delay—by examining how inductive biases from architecture and optimization govern the dynamics. Using one-layer transformers trained on modular addition, the authors systematically vary the placement of Layer Normalization (LN) and the readout scale parameter. They find that architectural choices strongly control three biases: the network's sensitivity to the scale of MLP inputs (influencing shortcut learning), the entropy of attention distributions (affecting composition), and the scale of value channels. Configurations that reduce MLP scale sensitivity yield the fastest generalization. Regarding optimization, the effect of readout scale is confounded by learning rate and weight decay when using AdamW; with a constant learning rate, higher readout scale accelerates generalization by acting as an inverse softmax temperature. Across all conditions, features evolve continuously throughout training, and generalization consistently correlates with increasing feature compressibility (measured via effective rank decay and power-law eigenspectrum), challenging a simple lazy-to-rich transition narrative.

## Method Summary
The study trains one-layer transformers on the modular addition task (p=113) with different Layer Normalization insertion configurations (e.g., before MLP, on Q/K/V channels) and varied readout scales. It measures grokking speed via test loss curves and analyzes internal representations to isolate three proposed inductive biases: MLP input scale sensitivity (via a "SR-train" metric that removes scale), attention entropy, and value channel scale effects. Additionally, it tracks the compressibility of pre-logit feature representations using effective rank and the power-law decay of the correlation matrix's eigenspectrum across all experimental conditions to establish a unified predictor of generalization.

## Key Results
- Architectural placement of Layer Normalization dramatically alters grokking speed by controlling three inductive biases: MLP input scale sensitivity (shortcut learning), attention entropy, and value channel scale.
- Configurations that most reduce MLP input scale sensitivity (e.g., LN before MLP) generalize fastest.
- Features evolve continuously from the start of training; generalization onset correlates universally with increased feature compressibility (decaying effective rank and heavy-tailed eigenspectrum), not with a discrete lazy-to-rich phase transition.
- The commonly interpreted "laziness" parameter (readout scale) is confounded by optimizer dynamics under AdamW; with constant learning rate, it instead modulates an inverse softmax temperature.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: MLP Input Scale Sensitivity & Shortcut Learning
- **Claim:** Architectural placement of Layer Normalization (LN) modulates grokking speed by altering the transformer's sensitivity to the *scale* of MLP input activations, thereby controlling the network's tendency toward memorizing shortcuts versus learning general features.
- **Mechanism:** Without LN on the MLP pathway, the network's training loss becomes highly sensitive to the norm (scale) of inputs to the MLP, not just their directional components. The MLP can thus fit the training data by exploiting this scale (a "shortcut"), leading to memorization. Adding LN before the MLP normalizes this scale, forcing the MLP to rely on feature *directions* and promoting the learning of general, periodic solutions. This is measured via "SR-train" (train loss when MLP input is scale-removed), which is high for configs without LN on the MLP and decreases as grokking occurs.
- **Core assumption:** The MLP block in a one-layer transformer is the primary locus for memorization in this task, and its reliance on activation scale is a proxy for learning non-generalizing shortcuts.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "LN position changes inductive bias ... MLP input scale sensitivity (shortcut learning) ... configurations that reduce scale sensitivity generalizing fastest."
  - [section 2.4] "SR-train is found to be significantly larger than default train loss... SR-train reduces as the delayed descent in test loss, i.e., grokking, occurs."
- **Break condition:** If SR-train (scale-removed loss) shows no systematic correlation with grokking speed across LN positions, or if removing MLP scale does not alter memorization behavior.

### Mechanism 2: Attention Entropy Modulation
- **Claim:** LN applied to the *query* and *key* channels within the attention module reduces the entropy of attention scores, which constrains the network's ability to express the trigonometric compositions necessary for solving modular addition, thus slowing generalization.
- **Mechanism:** The scale of token embeddings before projection into Q/K vectors influences the sharpness (low entropy) or diffuseness (high entropy) of the softmax attention distribution. High entropy allows the network to attend to multiple positions simultaneously, which is needed to combine two input tokens (a, b) via trigonometric identities. LN on Q/K inputs reduces this entropy by normalizing away the embedding scale degree of freedom, making the attention distribution more peaked and less expressive for composition.
- **Core assumption:** The correct general solution for modular addition requires the attention mechanism to compose features from both operand positions, which is facilitated by a higher-entropy (softer) attention distribution.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "attention entropy" is listed as one of the three key biases controlled by LN.
  - [section 2.4] "Aqk configuration does not generalize... falling behind No LN in both generalization and attention score entropy."
  - [section 2.4] "LN on query and key inputs reduces the entropy of the attention score softmax distribution... thereby hurting generalization."
- **Break condition:** If attention entropy does not differ systematically between fast-grokking (e.g., `M`) and slow-grokking (`A*`, `Aqk`) configurations, or if artificially increasing entropy in `Aqk` restores fast grokking.

### Mechanism 3: Value Channel Scale Control
- **Claim:** LN specifically on the *value* channel inputs accelerates grokking by mitigating the scale sensitivity of the MLP input pathway (the residual connection from attention output), but is less effective than full MLP-input LN because it only partially controls scale.
- **Mechanism:** The output of the attention module (`ex`) is added to the residual stream (`y`) before entering the MLP. This sum's scale is influenced by the raw scale of the value vectors. Applying LN only to the value channel inputs (`Av` configuration) normalizes the contribution from the attention path, reducing the overall scale variability seen by the MLP. This partially alleviates the MLP's shortcut-learning tendency, leading to faster grokking than `A*` (LN only on Q/K) but slower than `M` (LN on MLP input), which fully normalizes the MLP input.
- **Core assumption:** The scale of the residual addition (`y + ex`) is a dominant contributor to MLP input scale sensitivity in the absence of normalization.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "value channel scale" is listed as the third bias.
  - [section 2.4] "LN only at the input of the value channel leads to a strong acceleration in grokking... Av still groks slower than M because M fully removes scale from the MLP input."
  - [section 2.4 & Figure 3a] `Av` does not show increasing SR-train during memorization period, unlike `No LN`, but also does not reduce SR-train as strongly as `M`.
- **Break condition:** If `Av` (value-only LN) shows no difference in grokking speed compared to `No LN` or `A*`, or if SR-train profiles for `Av` are identical to `M`.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Inductive Bias**
  - **Why needed here:** The paper's central thesis is that grokking is not merely a phase transition but is *steered* by architectural and optimization choices that constitute inductive biases—dispositions that make the network prefer certain solution types (e.g., periodic, compressible) over others. Understanding this lens is essential to interpret *why* different LN positions or learning rates change grokking dynamics.
  - **Quick check question:** How does placing LN before the MLP vs. before Q/K channels change the inductive bias of the network toward general vs. memorizing solutions?
- **Concept: Feature Compressibility**
  - **Why needed here:** Section 4 establishes that generalization across all inductive bias modulators correlates with increasing *compressibility* of the pre-logit features (measured via effective rank decay and heavy-tailed eigenspectrum). This provides a unifying, setting-agnostic progress metric for grokking, moving beyond a binary lazy/rich view.
  - **Quick check question:** What two quantitative metrics of the feature correlation matrix's eigenspectrum indicate a "compressible," generalizable feature representation?
- **Concept: Lazy vs. Rich Learning Regimes**
  - **Why needed here:** Prior work (Kumar et al., 2023) interprets grokking as a transition from lazy (kernel-like, minimal feature change) to rich (feature-learning) dynamics. This paper challenges that simple dichotomy by showing features evolve *continuously* from the start. Understanding this debate is crucial to contextualize the paper's claim that inductive bias steers a continuous evolution toward compressible features.
  - **Quick check question:** Does the continuous evolution of effective rank and powerlaw fit across training support a lazy-to-rich transition or a continuous rich-regime learning process?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** One-layer transformer flow: Token Embeddings -> Positional Encoding (added) -> x -> Multi-Head Self-Attention (MHSA) -> ex -> y = x + ex (residual) -> MLP -> ey -> pre-logits = y + ey (residual) -> W_U -> Logits. LN can be inserted before MHSA (affecting Q/K/V), before MLP (affecting y), or both.
- **Critical path:** `Input Tokens → [W_E + ρ] → x → [MHSA(x)] → ex → [y = x + ex] → [MLP(y)] → ey → [pre-logits = y + ey] → [W_U] → Logits`.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Control vs. Capacity:** LN alters learning dynamics (inductive bias) without changing model capacity. Placing LN on MLP input (`M`) gives strongest control over scale-sensitive memorization but may also constrain other dynamics.
  - **Pathway-specificity:** LN on Q/K vs. V vs. MLP targets different computational pathways (entropy vs. value-scale vs. MLP-scale), offering a tool to isolate biases.
  - **Optimizer interaction:** The effect of "readout scale" (`α`) is not a pure "laziness" control; its interpretation is confounded by learning rate and weight decay scaling in AdamW.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **High SR-train:** Indicates strong MLP shortcut learning via scale; config will likely grok slowly (e.g., `No LN`, `A*`).
  - **Low attention entropy:** Measured on attention softmax distributions; predicts poor composition and slow grokking (e.g., `Aqk`).
  - **Slow compressibility convergence:** High effective rank or high powerlaw fit `α` in pre-logit correlation matrix `Z` during training correlates with delayed generalization.
  - **Confounded `α` effects:** If varying readout scale `α` requires scaling learning rate as `η0/α²` to compare laziness, but this leads to diverging update norms ([Figure 5b]), the observed grokking trend is likely due to unintended regularization changes, not pure laziness.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Baseline grokking & Fourier analysis:** Train `No LN` transformer on modular addition (p=113). Plot training/test loss over 10k epochs. Fourier transform `W_E` at end to confirm periodic structure emerges. Validates task setup and baseline grokking delay.
  2. **LN position ablation:** Train transformers with `A*`, `M`, `M+A*`, and `No LN`. Compare test loss curves ([Figure 2a]) and measure Fourier mode concentration `C_Fourier` over time ([Figure 2b,c]). Identifies which LN positions accelerate grokking.
  3. **Mechanism validation via interventions:** For `No LN` and `M` configs, compute SR-train (loss with `MLP(./||.||)`) at checkpoints. For `Aqk` vs. `No LN`, measure average attention entropy. For `Av`, plot SR-train alongside. Directly tests the three proposed mechanistic pathways.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: To what extent do the identified inductive bias mechanisms (e.g., effects of Layer Normalization placement on MLP input scale sensitivity, attention entropy, and value channel scale) generalize to deeper transformer architectures and other algorithmic tasks beyond modular addition?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The limitations state: "This work is particularly restricted to transformers learning modular addition under adaptive optimizers... our findings... may not apply to other settings." Appendices F and G show limited depth-3 results but note this is not exhaustive.
- Why unresolved: The study primarily analyzes one-layer transformers on a single task. Different depths, architectures, or tasks may involve distinct inductive biases or interactions that alter grokking dynamics.
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic experiments across a range of depths (e.g., 3–12 layers), diverse algorithmic tasks (e.g., parity, sequence reversal), and alternative attention mechanisms, measuring grokking speed and the three bias metrics to test consistency.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Under precisely which combinations of optimization settings (optimizer type, loss function, learning rate schedule) does grokking exhibit a clear lazy-to-rich transition versus continuous feature evolution, and how does readout scale α reliably indicate regime boundaries?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper challenges the lazy-to-rich interpretation by showing continuous feature evolution (Section 4) and that readout scale α's effect is confounded by learning rate/weight decay when using AdamW with cross-entropy (Section 3). However, it does not map the conditions where each characterization holds.
- Why unresolved: The study uses only AdamW with cross-entropy and specific hyperparameters. The interplay between optimizer choice (e.g., SGD vs. adaptive), loss curvature, and α remains unexplored, leaving unclear when grokking aligns with regime shifts.
- What evidence would resolve it: Comparative studies varying optimizer (SGD, Adam, AdamW), loss (MSE vs. cross-entropy), and learning rate schedules, tracking parameter norms, feature compressibility, and training dynamics to define operational criteria for lazy vs. rich phases.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Is feature compressibility (effective rank, powerlaw eigenspectrum decay) a causal driver of accelerated generalization in grokking, or merely a correlated symptom, and can it serve as a universal early-stopping metric across inductive bias modulators?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper proposes compressibility as a setting-agnostic progress measure (Conclusion, Limitations) and shows correlation with grokking speed across LN positions and weight decay (Section 4). However, it acknowledges this needs validation in broader settings.
- Why unresolved: The evidence is correlational within the studied configurations. It is unknown if compressibility directly causes faster generalization (e.g., via implicit regularization) or if other factors (e.g., optimization trajectory) are primary.
- What evidence would resolve it: Intervention experiments where feature compressibility is explicitly regularized (e.g., penalizing effective rank) during training, measuring impact on grokking onset. Additionally, testing the predictor across diverse tasks and architectures to assess universality.

## Limitations
- The study is confined to one-layer transformers on a single synthetic task (modular addition), limiting generalizability to deeper models or natural language tasks.
- The analysis of optimization effects (readout scale) is specific to AdamW with cross-entropy; findings may not hold for other optimizers or losses.
- The proposed causal mechanisms (e.g., MLP scale sensitivity as primary shortcut) are supported by strong correlations but not definitive ablation studies that fully rule out alternative pathways.

## Confidence
- **MLP Input Scale Sensitivity as Primary Shortcut Mechanism:** Medium. Strong internal correlation (SR-train profiles) across LN configurations, but the assumption that MLP is the primary memorization locus lacks definitive proof and external corpus support.
- **Attention Entropy Modulation via Q/K Normalization:** Medium. Evidence shows `Aqk` has lower entropy and slower grokking; causal link relies on unverified premise that the true solution requires diffuse attention. No direct intervention to restore entropy.
- **Feature Compressibility as Unifying Predictive Metric:** High. Continuous decay of effective rank and power-law fit correlates tightly with generalization onset across *all* modulators. Well-measured and consistently challenges lazy/rich dichotomy.
- **Confounding of Readout Scale α under AdamW:** High. Demonstrates that α's effect is entangled with learning rate/weight decay scaling, reinterpreting it as inverse temperature under constant LR. Evidence is clear within the experimental setup.

## Next Checks
1.  **Test Necessity of MLP Scale Control:** Train models with `M` (MLP-input LN) but with an explicit *additive* scale term reintroduced to the MLP input (bypassing LN). If grokking speed reverts to `No LN` levels, it causally confirms scale sensitivity as the key factor.
2.  **Isolate Attention Entropy Effect:** For the slow-grokking `Aqk` configuration, inject a learned, *non-normalized* bias term into the Q/K projections post-LN to artificially restore scale variation. Measure if this increases attention entropy and accelerates generalization, directly testing the entropy-constraint hypothesis.
3.  **Challenge Compressibility Generality:** Apply the same effective rank/power-law analysis to pre-logit features in a *different* grokking setup (e.g., a 2-layer transformer on a different algorithmic task like parity). If compressibility metrics still predict generalization timing, it strengthens the claim as a universal indicator.
