# Revisiting Real-Time Digging-In Effects: No Evidence from NP/Z Garden-Paths
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.23624
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23624
- **Reference count**: 12
- **Primary result**: Human sentence processing shows robust garden-path effects but no real-time digging-in; ambiguity-length interaction non-significant at nonfinal disambiguation (reverse trends) and only positive at sentence-final positions, where wrap-up effects likely confound the measure; LLMs consistently predict reverse digging-in, aligning with human nonfinal data.

## Executive Summary
The study investigates the "digging-in" effect—whether extending an ambiguous syntactic region increases processing difficulty upon disambiguation—in NP/Z garden-path sentences. Using Maze and self-paced reading (SPR) tasks with human participants and comparing results to large language model (LLM) surprisal predictions, the authors manipulate disambiguation position (sentence-final vs. nonfinal). Human data reveal robust garden-path effects but no significant digging-in in nonfinal positions, with slight reverse trends; positive digging-in appears only in final positions, which is attributed to wrap-up processes that contaminate terminal word measures. LLMs uniformly show reverse digging-in, aligning with human nonfinal patterns but not final ones. These findings cast doubt on the robustness of real-time digging-in, support surprisal-based processing accounts over self-organizing dynamical models, and underscore methodological concerns about relying on sentence-final reading times to infer incremental parsing.

## Method Summary
The experiments employed two behavioral paradigms: a Maze task (lexical decision on word pairs) and a self-paced reading task (word-by-word reveal). Stimuli were NP/Z garden-path sentences with two versions of the ambiguous region (short vs. long) and two disambiguation positions (final vs. nonfinal). Experiment 1 used a between-item design for length and finality; Experiment 2 used a within-item design for length but between-item for finality. Participants read filler sentences interspersed with critical items, and comprehension questions followed some trials. Reading times on the critical disambiguating verb were extracted and cleaned (error trials, outliers). Concurrently, several transformer LLMs were queried to compute token surprisal (negative log probability) for the same sentences. A linear mixed-effects model was fitted to filler data to predict human reading times from LLM-derived features (surprisal, log-frequency, word length, spillover). These fitted models generated predicted RTs for critical items. Bayesian mixed-effects models were then applied to both empirical and predicted RTs, testing fixed effects of ambiguity (garden-path vs. control), length, finality, and their interactions, with maximal random effects. Posterior distributions (pd) assessed the credibility of effects.

## Key Results
- In nonfinal disambiguation conditions, humans showed no significant digging-in; the ambiguity × length interaction was negative in both tasks (Maze: -18 ms, pd=76%; SPR: -9 ms, pd=83%), indicating reverse rather than positive digging-in.
- In sentence-final conditions, both tasks exhibited positive digging-in (SPR: +20 ms, pd=93%; Maze: +94 ms, pd=97%), but this effect is likely an artifact of wrap-up processes that add load to final words.
- LLMs consistently predicted reverse digging-in across conditions (Maze: -5 ms, pd=99%; SPR: nonfinal -0.22 bits, final -0.21 bits, both pd > 98%), aligning with human nonfinal data but diverging from human final data.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The authors interpret the pattern through three mechanisms:

1. **No real-time strengthening of incorrect parse**: Extending the ambiguous region does not increase difficulty at the disambiguating verb when it appears nonfinally; the slight reverse trend suggests that the parser does not become more committed to the initially chosen (incorrect) analysis over time during incremental processing. Evidence: Exp1 nonfinal (pd=76%) and Exp2 nonfinal (pd=83%) yield negative mean differences.
2. **LLM surprisal correctly predicts nonfinal reverse digging-in**: In LLMs, longer ambiguous regions provide more contextual constraints, lowering surprisal at the disambiguating verb because the model's expectations become sharper. This yields a negative correlation between ambiguity length and surprisal, matching the human nonfinal pattern and supporting surprisal theory as a cognitive model.
3. **Sentence-final positive digging-in is a wrap-up confound**: At the end of a sentence, reading times incorporate additional processes (e.g., discourse integration, plausibility checking, response preparation). Lengthening the ambiguous region likely increases cognitive load during these wrap-up stages, inflating times on the final word and producing a spurious digging-in effect. The dissociation between final and nonfinal results points to this artifact.

## Foundational Learning
- **Surprisal Theory**  
  *Why needed here*: It is the dominant alternative to self-organizing dynamical models of parsing. Surprisal posits that processing difficulty at a word is proportional to its negative log probability given the preceding context. The paper's findings align with surprisal predictions in nonfinal positions.  
  *Quick check*: If a word is more expected (higher probability) in context, surprisal theory predicts lower processing difficulty because the word's information content is lower. Lengthening an ambiguous region should not automatically increase surprisal at disambiguation; in fact, more context can sharpen expectations and potentially reduce surprisal.

- **Garden-Path Effect**  
  *Why needed here*: The core empirical phenomenon is the garden-path effect—a spike in difficulty when the parser encounters a word that forces reanalysis of an initially misparsed structure. The study examines how this effect interacts with ambiguity region length.  
  *Quick check*: In an NP/Z garden-path like "Before the recruiters could adapt the policy changed", the initial misinterpretation arises because the parser takes "the policy" as the direct object of "adapt" (NP analysis). The verb "changed" forces a reanalysis to a different structure where "the policy" is the subject of a reduced relative clause.

- **Wrap-Up Effects**  
  *Why needed here*: These are non-incremental processes that occur at or near sentence boundaries (e.g., integration into discourse, evaluation of plausibility, preparation for a response). They contaminate measures like final-word reading times, making it hard to isolate pure parsing difficulty.  
  *Quick check*: Reading time for the last word in a self-paced reading trial may reflect not just incremental syntactic processing but also efforts to integrate the sentence into the broader context, check for overall plausibility, and prepare for the upcoming comprehension question or button press.

## Architecture Onboarding
Stimulus Generator → Task Implementer → LLM Surprisal Engine → RT Prediction Modeler → Statistical Analysis Engine → Adequacy Checker.

- **Stimulus Generator**: Creates sentence pairs (short/long ambiguous region, final/nonfinal disambiguation) using controlled templates and a filler bank.
- **Task Implementer**: Deploys two parallel experiment backends: Maze (forced lexical decision on word pairs) and self-paced reading (non-cumulative word-by-word reveal). It logs response times and accuracy, and inserts comprehension questions.
- **LLM Surprisal Engine**: Queries multiple transformer models (e.g., GPT variants) via an API/library to obtain token-level probabilities, from which surprisal values are computed for all items using the same tokenization as human stimuli.
- **RT Prediction Modeler**: Fits a linear mixed-effects regression on filler-item data to map LLM features (surprisal, log-frequency, word length, spillover) to human reading times. The resulting model predicts RTs for critical items.
- **Statistical Analysis Engine**: Runs Bayesian mixed-effects models (e.g., with `brms`) on both empirical and predicted RTs, including fixed effects of ambiguity, length, finality, and their interactions, with maximal random slopes for participants/items (or LLM instances). Posterior probabilities (pd) quantify evidence for effects.
- **Adequacy Checker**: Compares predicted RTs against observed ones across conditions (unambiguous, ambiguous short/long, final/nonfinal) to diagnose where the surprisal-based model succeeds or fails.

**Critical path**: Stimulus design → Human experiment deployment → Data cleaning (exclusion of errors, outliers) → LLM surprisal extraction → Filler-based RT prediction model fitting → Prediction of critical-item RTs → Main statistical analysis (focus on ambiguity×length interaction moderated by finality) → Adequacy assessment (residual patterns).

**Design tradeoffs**:
- *Maze vs. SPR*: Maze minimizes spillover and forces lexical access but may disrupt natural parsing (e.g., comma disambiguation becomes noisy); SPR is more traditional but requires careful aggregation of spillover regions and exhibits higher variance.
- *Within-item vs. between-item*: Within-item design (Exp. 2) controls for lexical/syntactic variation but complicates the finality manipulation; between-item design (Exp. 1) risks item confounding but allows cleaner factorial crossing.
- *LLM ensemble vs. single model*: Ensemble averages out architecture-specific quirks but adds computational cost; single-model analysis may reveal specific biases but could overgeneralize.
- *Bayesian vs. frequentist*: Bayesian approach yields full posterior distributions (pd) that directly quantify evidence for null vs. alternative, but requires careful prior specification; frequentist tests may be faster but less informative for small effects.

**Failure signatures**:
- High between-item variance in garden-path effect sizes that correlates with finality or resolution type and persists after including random effects.
- Uniform underprediction of RTs in ambiguous critical words by LLMs across all conditions, suggesting missing factors like working memory load or reanalysis cost.
- No significant interaction between ambiguity×length and finality in human data; if digging-in is uniform across positions, the wrap-up confound hypothesis loses support.
- LLM-predicted RT patterns diverge strongly between Maze and SPR, indicating task-specific calibration problems in the RT prediction model.

**First 3 experiments to run**:
1. Replication with nonfinal-only items: Design a large set of NP/Z garden-paths where the disambiguating word is always followed by at least one unrelated word, eliminating wrap-up contamination. Test the ambiguity×length interaction with high statistical power (target N > 50 participants, posterior probability for null > 97.5%).
2. Cross-construction generalizability: Apply the same design (final vs. nonfinal disambiguation, short vs. long ambiguous region) to other garden-path constructions (e.g., NP/S ambiguities, reduced relative clauses) to determine whether the absence of real-time digging-in is specific to NP/Z or a broader property.
3. Fine-grained LLM analysis: Beyond token surprisal, compute measures of syntactic uncertainty (e.g., parse-state entropy in recurrent models, attention entropy in transformers) across the ambiguous region. Test whether any hidden representation correlates with the observed slight reverse digging-in in human nonfinal data.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Is digging-in a robust real-time phenomenon?** The paper notes that whether digging-in is a genuine effect of incremental parsing or an artifact of wrap-up processes/methodological confounds remains unclear. Humans showed no significant digging-in in nonfinal positions (cleaner measure), but positive trends only finally; LLMs showed reverse digging-in. Resolution requires systematic manipulation of disambiguation position across constructions with measures isolating incremental processing.
2. **Why does sentence-finality moderate the digging-in pattern?** The data show a clear split: positive digging-in only for final words, while nonfinal words show reverse or null effects. The authors attribute this to wrap-up confounds, but the cognitive mechanism behind this moderation is not fully explained.
3. **Why do LLMs underpredicted garden-path difficulty, and why doesn't this underprediction vary with length?** LLMs substantially underpredicted RTs at ambiguous critical words (e.g., ~469 ms residual in Maze), yet this offset was similar for short and long ambiguous regions, contrary to human nonfinal reverse trends. The paper does not explain why lengthening, which should affect surprisal, does not exacerbate the underprediction.
4. **Is the lack of real-time digging-in effects specific to NP/Z garden-paths?** The study only examined NP/Z constructions. It is possible that strongly difficult garden-paths like NP/Z disrupt the normal processes that would produce digging-in; the effect might occur in milder ambiguities. Generalizability to other syntactic ambiguities is unknown.

## Limitations
- **Statistical power for null effects**: Nonfinal ambiguity×length interactions have modest posterior probabilities (76-83%), falling short of conventional robustness; sample sizes may be underpowered to detect small positive digging-in if present.
- **Item design constraints**: The between-item manipulation in Experiment 1 risks lexical/syntactic confounding; while Experiment 2 used within-item, the finality factor remained between-item, limiting clean interpretation of the interaction.
- **Wrap-up operationalization**: The wrap-up hypothesis is inferred from the final/nonfinal dissociation, but no independent measure (e.g., eye-tracking during final words, neuroimaging) directly confirms that wrap-up processes cause the positive digging-in.

## Confidence
- Absence of real-time digging-in at nonfinal disambiguation: **Medium** confidence.
- LLM surprisal alignment with human nonfinal reverse digging-in: **Medium** confidence.
- Sentence-final positive digging-in as a wrap-up artifact: **Medium-High** confidence.

## Next Checks
1. **High-powered nonfinal-only replication**: Test NP/Z garden-paths with disambiguation always followed by ≥1 unrelated word, using a within-item design and larger sample (>50 participants). Aim to conclusively assess the ambiguity×length interaction with high posterior probability (target pd > 97.5% for the null).
2. **Cross-construction generality**: Replicate the design with other garden-path types (e.g., NP/S, reduced relative clauses) to determine if the lack of real-time digging-in is construction-specific or a universal feature of garden-path recovery.
3. **Fine-grained LLM analysis**: Compute parse-state uncertainty metrics (e.g., entropy of attention distributions or hidden states in intermediate layers) across the ambiguous region in transformer LLMs. Test whether these metrics, rather than token surprisal alone, better predict human nonfinal RTs and account for the slight reverse digging-in trend.
