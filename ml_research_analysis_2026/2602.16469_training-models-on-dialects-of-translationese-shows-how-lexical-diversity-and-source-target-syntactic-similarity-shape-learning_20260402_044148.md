# Training Models on Dialects of Translationese Shows How Lexical Diversity and Source-Target Syntactic Similarity Shape Learning
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.16469  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16469  
- **Reference count**: 13  
- **Primary result**: Training small English LMs on translationese from 24 source languages shows a scale-dependent shift: lexical diversity drives perplexity at 100MB, while syntactic similarity drives grammatical competence (BLiMP) at 1000MB, and typologically similar source languages yield better cross-generalization.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how the source language of machine-translated training data (translationese) influences the learning dynamics of small English language models. The authors train 125M-parameter Transformers on English translations derived from 24 typologically diverse source languages, controlling for data size (100MB vs. 1000MB). Results reveal a clear scale-dependent pattern: at small data scales, the lexical diversity (type-token ratio) of the translated corpus is the strongest predictor of perplexity on native English; at larger scales, syntactic similarity between source and target (measured via lang2vec) becomes the dominant predictor of grammatical competence on the BLiMP benchmark, particularly for agreement and long-distance dependencies. Moreover, cross-evaluation shows that models generalize better to each other’s translationese when their source languages are typologically similar. These findings suggest that translationese introduces systematic, source-language-specific biases that act like distinct “dialects” and that the factors shaping model learning shift as training data grows.

## Method Summary
The study uses a controlled training pipeline. Models are small GPT-style Transformers (125 million parameters, 512-token context) with a fixed English tokenizer (50 k vocabulary). Training data come from the FineTranslations corpus: English translations of source-language texts produced by a high-quality LLM translator. Two data budgets are enforced: 100 MB and 1000 MB of text, each drawn from translations of 24 diverse source languages. All models are trained with the same architecture and hyperparameters; only the source language of the translationese varies. Evaluation comprises three parts: (1) perplexity on native English corpora (FLORES and FineWeb) to assess general language modeling; (2) the BLiMP benchmark, which measures grammatical competence across 12 fine-grained categories; and (3) cross-perplexity, where each model is evaluated on the translationese corpora of other source languages to probe generalization. Corpus properties—lexical diversity (type-token ratio) and syntactic similarity to English (lang2vec, derived from WALS)—are then correlated with the evaluation scores.

## Key Results
- Lexical diversity (type-token ratio) of the translated training corpus correlates strongly negatively with perplexity on native English at the 100 MB scale (Pearson r ≈ -0.56), but this correlation vanishes at the 1000 MB scale (r ≈ +0.06).
- Syntactic similarity between source language and English (lang2vec cosine) shows a strong positive correlation with overall BLiMP accuracy at the 1000 MB scale (Pearson r = +0.61, p < 0.01), especially for agreement (r = +0.74) and long‑distance dependencies; at 100 MB, the correlation is weak (r ≈ +0.06).
- Cross‑evaluation reveals that models trained on translations from source language A achieve lower perplexity on translations from source language B when A and B are typologically similar; the correlation between cross‑perplexity and source‑language similarity is significantly negative (r ≈ -0.24 across scales).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Lexical Diversity Drives Perplexity at Small Data Scales
- **Claim**: At 100MB training scale, the type-token ratio (lexical diversity) of translated corpora negatively predicts perplexity on native English.
- **Mechanism**: Higher lexical diversity provides a richer token distribution, helping the model generalize to native text despite translation artifacts. Assumption: Lexical diversity correlates with variation in the underlying source corpus.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "...general language modeling performance (perplexity on native English) is initially driven by the lexical diversity of the translated corpus at small data scales (100MB)..."
  - [section 4.1.2] Correlation analysis: TTR Pearson r = -0.56 on FLORES (significant) for 100MB.
  - [corpus] Table 1 shows TTR varies from 1.98% (Kinyarwanda) to 4.77% (English baseline); Table 2 shows corresponding perplexity ranges.
- **Break condition**: At 1000MB, TTR correlation disappears (r ≈ +0.06), indicating scale dependence.

### Mechanism 2: Syntactic Similarity Drives Grammatical Competence at Larger Scales
- **Claim**: At 1000MB training scale, typological similarity to English (via WALS) positively predicts BLiMP accuracy, especially for agreement and long-distance dependencies.
- **Mechanism**: Source language syntax influences translationese structure; with more data, models internalize these syntactic patterns, affecting grammatical judgments. Assumption: Translation preserves source-language syntactic traces that shape model grammar.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "...at a larger scale (1000MB) becomes moderately correlated with syntactic similarity to English. In contrast, grammatical competence, measured by BLiMP accuracy, shows a strong, significant positive correlation with syntactic similarity at the larger scale (Pearson r=+0.61)..."
  - [section 4.2.2] Overall BLiMP correlation r=+0.61 (p<0.01) at 1000MB; agreement categories r=+0.74.
  - [corpus] Figure 1 provides lang2vec similarity scores; Table 4 shows BLiMP scores by language.
- **Break condition**: At 100MB, no significant correlation (r=+0.06); also weak for some categories like Quantifiers even at 1000MB.

### Mechanism 3: Source Language Similarity Predicts Cross-Evaluation Generalization
- **Claim**: Models trained on translations from language A generalize better to translations from language B when A and B are typologically similar.
- **Mechanism**: Translationese variants form "dialects" influenced by source language; similar source languages produce similar translationese, so models adapt better. Assumption: Cross-perplexity reflects similarity of translationese distributions.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "...Cross-evaluation reveals that models trained on typologically similar source languages generalize better to each other's translationese..."
  - [section 4.3] Consistent negative correlation between similarity and cross-perplexity (r=-0.251 at 100MB, r=-0.228 at 1000MB, both p<0.001).
  - [corpus] Figure 2 cross-perplexity matrices show lower values for similar language pairs; Figure 3 plots the correlation.
- **Break condition**: If translation system or domain shifts (e.g., Telugu outlier due to named entities), correlation may weaken.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Source texts → LLM translator → English translations (FineTranslations) → English tokenizer (50k) → Small Transformer (125M) → Training (100MB/1000MB) → Evaluation (perplexity on FLORES/FineWeb, BLiMP, cross-perplexity)
- **Critical path**: Data preparation → training → evaluation. Must ensure tokenizer consistency (English vocab) and data budget control.
- **Design tradeoffs**: Small models for controlled comparison vs. limited realism; translating into English (high quality) vs. low-resource scenarios; single translation system (LLM) limits diversity; fixed data budgets enable comparability but exclude natural data volume variations.
- **Failure signatures**: (1) Very high perplexity on FineWeb (domain mismatch), (2) Low BLiMP on complex phenomena (Island Effects, NPI), (3) Outliers like Telugu (named entity density) or Upper Sorbian (tiny corpus).
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Replicate scale interaction: Train at 100MB and 1000MB, confirm TTR→perplexity correlation vanishes at larger scale while lang2vec→BLiMP emerges.
  2. Test translation system dependency: Use a different MT system (e.g., older NMT) on same source languages, see if correlations hold.
  3. Add native English data: Mix 10% native English into translated corpora, check if grammatical competence improves and correlations weaken.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How can we disentangle the effects of structural similarity, lexical overlap, and domain coverage when training on translationese?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] Future work (Section 4.5) suggests using controlled synthetic corpora to disentangle these factors.
- **Why unresolved**: Current translationese datasets inherently vary across all these dimensions simultaneously, making it impossible to isolate their independent contributions.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Controlled experiments with synthetic corpora where structural similarity, lexical diversity, and domain alignment are systematically varied while other variables are held constant.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Which specific typological features (e.g., word order, agreement systems, case marking) most strongly influence transfer performance in translationese-trained models?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] Future work (Section 4.5) calls for identifying which specific typological features drive the strongest transfer effects.
- **Why unresolved**: The analysis collapsed typological similarity into a single cosine similarity score, obscuring the predictive power of individual linguistic features.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Fine-grained correlation studies using individual WALS features, or experimental manipulations that selectively vary specific features in synthetic data.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Do the observed patterns—such as the growing importance of syntactic similarity with increasing data scale—generalize to larger language models and much larger training datasets?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] Future work (Section 4.5) questions whether the observed patterns hold for larger models and datasets.
- **Why unresolved**: The experiments were conducted with small (125M parameter) models and at most 1000MB of data; scaling may change the dynamics of how translationese properties affect learning.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Replicate the study with modern large language models (e.g., 1B+ parameters) trained on orders of magnitude more data (e.g., tens or hundreds of gigabytes), then reevaluate the correlations.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Does augmenting translationese training data with a small amount of native English text or combining translations from multiple source languages improve grammatical competence and language modeling performance?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] Future work (Section 4.5) asks whether adding native English data or combining multiple source languages may already improve grammatical competence and language modelling performance.
- **Why unresolved**: The study only examined pure translationese corpora; the potential benefits of native data or multi-source mixtures remain unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Train models with varying proportions of native English mixed into translationese, or with datasets that combine translations from several source languages, and compare BLiMP accuracy and perplexity on native English benchmarks.

## Limitations
- **Translation system bias**: All data generated via a single, high-quality LLM translator; this may produce a uniform "modern LLM translationese" dialect, obscuring effects from varied translation systems or lower-quality MT.
- **Model scale constraints**: Findings are from small (125M) English-tokenizer models; scaling to larger models or multilingual tokenizers could alter lexical vs. syntactic learning dynamics.
- **Coarse typological proxy**: lang2vec/WALS similarity aggregates features into a single score, failing to isolate the influence of specific linguistic structures (e.g., word order, case marking).

## Confidence
- Scale-dependent shift (lexical → syntactic drivers): **High**
- Cross-evaluation generalization by typological similarity: **Medium**
- Mechanistic interpretation (translationese as "dialect" learning): **Low to Medium**

## Next Checks
1. **Translation system variation**: Re-train a subset of models using a different MT system (e.g., a traditional NMT model like MarianMT) for 2–3 typologically diverse source languages. Check if the same scale-dependent correlations (TTR→perplexity, lang2vec→BLiMP) hold.
2. **Native data interpolation**: For key languages (one high-similarity, one low-similarity), train models with mixed budgets (e.g., 90% translationese + 10% native English). Measure whether grammatical competence (BLiMP) improves *more* for low-similarity models, and if correlations with syntactic similarity weaken.
3. **Cross-lingual transfer to new target**: Train equivalent models translating into a non-English target language (e.g., German). Test if the same pattern emerges: lexical diversity drives initial perplexity on native German, while source-target syntactic similarity drives BLiMP-style German grammar probes at larger scales.
