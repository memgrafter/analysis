# On Strengths and Limitations of Single-Vector Embeddings

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.29519
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.29519
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Single-vector embeddings fail on LIMIT due to domain shift and misalignment, not dimensionality; finetuning causes catastrophic forgetting, while multi-vector embeddings succeed with minimal forgetting and are less prone to drowning effects.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates why single-vector embeddings perform poorly on the LIMIT dataset, challenging the notion that limited dimensionality is the primary cause. Through theoretical analysis, the authors show that low-dimensional embeddings (e.g., 5D for LIMIT) should theoretically suffice for the sparse relevance structure. Empirically, controlling for tokenization and linguistic similarity yields only modest gains. The main issues are domain shift and misalignment between pretrained embedding similarity and the combinatorial relevance function. Finetuning on LIMIT-like data substantially improves recall for single-vector models but leads to catastrophic forgetting on MS MARCO (>40% drop). In contrast, multi-vector embeddings achieve near-perfect recall with minimal forgetting. Additionally, single-vector models are more susceptible to the "drowning in documents" effect as corpus size grows, due to noisier similarity scores, highlighting fundamental limitations compared to multi-vector approaches.

## Method Summary
The authors combine theoretical analysis (using sign-rank theory and Johnson-Lindenstrauss constructions) with empirical experiments on the LIMIT dataset and MS MARCO. They compare a single-vector model (Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B) and a multi-vector model (GTE-ModernColBERT-v1). Baseline performance is measured, followed by fine-tuning on LIMIT training data using contrastive loss (2 positives, 30 negatives). The impact on MS MARCO is evaluated to assess catastrophic forgetting. Additional experiments control for tokenization and linguistic similarity variations. The drowning effect is analyzed by examining score distributions as corpus size scales, supported by theoretical toy models that derive scaling laws for the "goodness" metric.

## Key Results
- Theoretical analysis shows that for LIMIT's sparse relevance (k=2), embeddings as low as 5D suffice for top-2 retrieval, contradicting the dimensionality limitation hypothesis.
- Finetuning single-vector embeddings on LIMIT yields large gains (Recall@10 from ~1% to ~40%) but causes catastrophic forgetting on MS MARCO (>40% drop in Recall@100). Multi-vector fine-tuning achieves near-perfect LIMIT recall (~98%) with minimal forgetting (<1%).
- Single-vector embeddings exhibit a steeper performance decline as corpus size increases ("drowning" effect) due to higher variance in similarity scores; multi-vector embeddings remain robust.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Dimensionality sufficiency
- **Claim:** Low-dimensional single-vector embeddings can theoretically represent LIMIT-like relevance structures, so limited dimensionality is not the fundamental cause of poor retrieval performance.
- **Mechanism:** The paper uses sign-rank theory (Alon et al., 2016) to show that for a *k*-sparse relevance matrix (each query has ≤*k* relevant docs), embeddings of dimension *2k+1* suffice to separate relevant from irrelevant documents. For LIMIT (*k*=2), 5 dimensions are enough. A Johnson-Lindenstrauss construction also yields low dimensions based on query/document lengths and attribute universe size.
- **Core assumption:** The relevance matrix has structural properties (sparsity or set-system structure) that allow low-dimensional representation. This applies to a fixed, known query-document set, not necessarily to unseen general queries.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "...2k+1-dimensional vector embeddings suffice for top-*k* retrieval... dimensionality alone cannot explain the observed failures."
  - [section 2] Proposition 2.4 and Proposition 2.5 formalize the bounds.
- **Break condition:** If the relevance matrix is dense and has high sign-rank *and* the set-system structure is highly complex (e.g., arbitrary set intersections), the required dimensionality may grow, but this is not the case for LIMIT.

### Mechanism 2: Domain shift and misalignment
- **Claim:** Poor performance arises primarily from **domain shift** and **misalignment** between pretrained embedding similarity and the combinatorial relevance function; finetuning can mitigate this but causes **catastrophic forgetting** in single-vector models.
- **Mechanism:** Pretrained embeddings are optimized for natural document distributions (e.g., news, web pages), not for set-structured profiles like LIMIT. Finetuning on LIMIT-like data adjusts the embedding space to align cosine similarity with the `rel_set(Q, D)` function. However, gradient updates overwrite weights important for the original distribution (MS MARCO), leading to large performance drops.
- **Core assumption:** The pretrained model's representation space is not robust to this specific domain shift; multi-vector models' finer-grained representations (token-level vectors) are more inherently compatible with set overlaps and thus more robust to forgetting.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Finetuning mitigates these effects... catastrophic forgetting (>40% drop on MSMARCO). In contrast... forgetting for multi-vector models is minimal."
  - [section 3] Tables 4 & 5 show finetuning gains on LIMIT but large drops on MS MARCO for single-vector; Tables 7 & 8 show near-perfect LIMIT performance and minimal forgetting for multi-vector.
- **Break condition:** If the domain shift is small (e.g., similar data distribution) or if multi-vector representations are used, catastrophic forgetting may be negligible.

### Mechanism 3: Drowning-in-documents effect
- **Claim:** Single-vector models are more susceptible to the **"drowning in documents"** effect because their similarity scores have higher variance for irrelevant documents, making relevant documents more likely to be ranked lower as corpus size grows.
- **Mechanism:** In single-vector models, the cosine similarity for a negative pair (query, irrelevant doc) has mean ~0 and variance ~1/*d*. For a positive pair with overlap *K*, mean ~*K*√(*d*/(*mn*)). The **goodness metric** *G* = (µ₊ − µ₋)/σ₋ ≈ *K*√(*d*/(*mn*)) scales poorly with document set size *n*. Multi-vector models (using Chamfer similarity) have negative mean ~√(log*n*/*d*) and variance ~1/(*d·m·log*n*), yielding *G* ~ *K*√(*d*/(*m log n*)), which degrades much slower with *n*.
- **Core assumption:** Embeddings behave like random projections where relevant overlap induces a mean shift, and the variance of the max-overlap operation in multi-vector models reduces noise. This holds for large *n* relative to dimension *d*.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Single-vector models are more susceptible to drowning effects... scores... behave like noisy statistical proxies."
  - [section 3] Figure 1 shows score distributions; single-vector negatives have wider spread. Appendix A.3/A.4 derives the theoretical scaling.
- **Break condition:** If corpus size *n* is small (so log*n* is negligible) or if the relevance overlap *K* is very large, the drowning effect may be minimal even for single-vector models.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Sign-rank
- **Why needed here:** It mathematically connects the complexity of a binary relevance matrix to the minimum embedding dimension required to represent it via sign of inner products. The paper counters Weller et al. by showing LIMIT's sign-rank is low.
- **Quick check question:** If a relevance matrix has at most 2 relevant documents per query, what is the upper bound on its sign-rank?
  (Answer: From Proposition 2.4, for a *k*-sparse relevance matrix, sign-rank ≤ 2^{k+1} - 1; for k=2, that is 7.)

### Concept: Catastrophic forgetting
- **Why needed here:** Explains why finetuning single-vector models on LIMIT degrades performance on the original MS MARCO task, a key limitation versus multi-vector models.
- **Quick check question:** Does catastrophic forgetting imply the model has *unlearned* the original task, or just that its representations have shifted?
  (Answer: It is typically a shift in representations that leads to poor performance on the old task, not necessarily complete unlearning.)

### Concept: Chamfer distance (late interaction)
- **Why needed here:** The scoring function for multi-vector embeddings (e.g., ColBERT) that compares sets of token vectors via average best-match. It is more expressive for set-overlap tasks than single-vector cosine.
- **Quick check question:** How does Chamfer scoring treat a query token that matches multiple document tokens?
  (Answer: For each query token, it takes the maximum similarity over all document tokens; then averages over query tokens.)

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Encoder (e.g., transformer) → token-level hidden states → Pooling head (single-vector: mean/CLS pooling → one *d*-dim vector; multi-vector: no pooling → list of token vectors) → Scoring function (single-vector: cosine similarity; multi-vector: Chamfer similarity) → Retrieval engine (ANN index)

### Critical path
Input (query/doc text) → tokenization → encoder → pooling (if single-vector) → normalized embeddings → similarity scoring → top-*k* retrieval via ANN search.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Single-vector**: compact index, fast ANN, but limited expressiveness for combinatorial relevance; prone to forgetting when finetuned.
- **Multi-vector**: higher accuracy on set-structured tasks, robust to forgetting, but index size ~*seq_len* × larger; requires specialized late-interaction ANN (e.g., PLAID, MUVERA).

### Failure signatures
- Near-zero Recall@*k* on LIMIT without finetuning.
- >40% Recall@100 drop on MS MARCO after finetuning on LIMIT (single-vector).
- Recall degrading sharply as corpus size increases ("drowning").

### First 3 experiments
1. Reproduce LIMIT baseline: evaluate a pretrained single-vector (e.g., Qwen3-Embedding) and multi-vector (e.g., ColBERT) on LIMIT test queries; expect ~1% vs. ~40% Recall@10.
2. Test dimensionality sufficiency: train a linear probe or random projection to validate that 5D embeddings can separate relevant/irrelevant for LIMIT's fixed query-doc set.
3. Measure drowning effect: for a fixed query, add random irrelevant documents to the corpus and plot Recall@10 vs. corpus size for both model types; expect steeper drop for single-vector.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can catastrophic forgetting during finetuning of single-vector embeddings be prevented?
- **Basis in paper:** Finetuning single-vector models on LIMIT reduces MS MARCO Recall@100 by >40%, while multi-vector models forget <1%.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper identifies catastrophic forgetting as a key limitation but does not explore mitigation strategies or underlying causes of this disparity.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic evaluation of continual learning techniques (e.g., elastic weight consolidation, experience replay, parameter-efficient methods) on single-vector models, measuring both retention on old tasks and gains on new.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What inherent properties make single-vector models more vulnerable to the drowning-in-documents effect, and can training objectives be modified to improve robustness?
- **Basis in paper:** Theoretical and experimental analysis shows single-vector models have a lower goodness metric (µ+−µ−/σ−), leading to higher drowning probability as corpus size increases.
- **Why unresolved:** The analysis relies on simplified toy models; it remains unclear whether real-world single-vector architectures can be trained to achieve better score separation.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Measure goodness metrics for various single-vector architectures trained with different losses; correlate these metrics with retrieval performance across varying corpus sizes; adjust temperature or margin parameters to test improvements.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can single-vector embeddings learn set-structured similarity to rival multi-vector performance given sufficient data or model capacity, or is the gap fundamental?
- **Basis in paper:** After finetuning, single-vector recall on LIMIT plateaus around 40% versus multi-vector’s 98%, indicating a large residual gap even with identical training data.
- **Why unresolved:** It is unknown whether this gap stems from optimization challenges, insufficient model capacity in low dimensions, or inherent architectural constraints of single-vector scoring.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct scaling studies varying model dimension, training set size, and contrastive loss variants; compare against multi-vector upper bounds to see if single-vector can close the gap under any conditions.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Why do multi-vector embeddings develop more orthogonal attribute representations during training, and how does this orthogonality contribute to superior performance on set-based retrieval?
- **Basis in paper:** Table 3 shows multi-vector models consistently increase pairwise angles between attribute embeddings (to ~35°–45°) after finetuning, while single-vector models exhibit erratic angle changes.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper observes this pattern but does not explain why multi-vector training promotes orthogonality or how exactly it aids retrieval of set-structured queries.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Analyze training dynamics (e.g., gradient magnitudes, loss curvature) for multi-vector models; perform ablations where orthogonality is regularized or penalized; correlate angle metrics with retrieval accuracy on LIMIT-like tasks.

## Limitations
- **Hyperparameter ambiguity:** Fine-tuning details (learning rate, negative sampling, epochs) are unspecified, introducing variance in catastrophic forgetting magnitudes.
- **Dataset construction gaps:** Exact algorithms for Permuted/Fresh LIMIT variants lack implementation specifics, hindering exact replication.
- **Theoretical experiment specifics:** The "drowning" analysis relies on precise goodness metric computation and token-correlation modeling not fully detailed.
