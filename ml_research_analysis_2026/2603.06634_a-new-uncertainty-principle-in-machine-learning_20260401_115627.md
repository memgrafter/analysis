# A new Uncertainty Principle in Machine Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.06634
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06634
- **Reference count:** 23
- **Primary result:** Increased representational capacity (more parameters) creates more "canyons" (flat valleys) in the loss landscape when using two-layer Heaviside networks to fit polynomials, drastically slowing convergence.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates why machine learning struggles on exact science problems where the target is a unique polynomial function. Using two-layer neural networks with Heaviside step functions, any polynomial can be represented exactly, but this representation introduces a degeneracy: many distinct weight configurations produce identical outputs. This degeneracy manifests as "canyons"—extremely flat valleys in the loss landscape—that trap gradient-based optimization, causing severe slowdowns. The effect worsens with network capacity, framing a trade-off between representational power and training stability akin to an uncertainty principle. Stochastic minibatch updates (as in TensorFlow) help escape canyons, and analytical polynomial ansätze provide effective initializations.

## Method Summary
The core method builds two-layer networks with Heaviside (or sigmoid-smoothed) activations to exactly represent polynomial target functions. The degeneracy arises because the Heaviside-based construction yields a continuous manifold of equivalent parameters (like a gauge symmetry). Optimization experiments compare full-batch steepest descent (which slides slowly along canyon bottoms) with minibatch stochastic gradient descent (which can jump out via noisy updates). Key numerical tests include learning the identity function \(y=x\) and low-order determinants, varying domain size \(X\) and training set size \(N\), and testing different initializations (random vs. polynomial ansatz).

## Key Results
- For the identity function \(y=x\), the minimum loss along canyon bottoms scales as \(\sim X\) while typical loss values scale as \(\sim X^3\), demonstrating extreme slowdown due to flat directions.
- For a 3×3 determinant analog (\(M=6\) hidden units), with only 10 training samples the network weights remain near the analytically derived diagonal ansatz; with 40 samples, weights drift significantly, indicating instability from degeneracy.
- Smoothing Heavisides to sigmoids does not remove canyons but makes them smoother; initializing with the polynomial ansatz reduces training time by starting near favorable canyon intersections.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Degeneracy in Heaviside Representations
- **Claim:** Arbitrary polynomials can be exactly represented by a two-layer neural network using Heaviside step functions, but this representation is degenerate.
- **Mechanism:** The Heaviside function's idempotency (\(\theta(\theta(x)) = \theta(x)\)) and its ability to encode logical operations (AND/OR) allow constructing polynomial terms via nested sums/integrals over discrete indices. This yields a vast manifold of equivalent weight configurations \((W, w, b)\) that all produce the same polynomial output—a gauge-like degeneracy.
- **Core assumption:** The training data is drawn from a noiseless polynomial relationship \(y(\mathbf{x})\).
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 3.4] derives formula (25) showing any polynomial can be written as a two-layer Heaviside integral. [section 4.2.1] demonstrates degeneracy for \(y=x\): \(W \cdot w = 1\) gives identical outputs, forming a valley. [section 7.1.9] numerically shows for \(M=6\) determinant with 10 samples, weights stay near a diagonal ansatz; with 40 they drift.
- **Break condition:** The degeneracy vanishes only if the polynomial ansatz is uniquely constrained by a training set covering all relevant input regions, which is impractical for finite samples.

### Mechanism 2: Canyon Formation and Scaling
- **Claim:** The degeneracy manifests as "canyons"—extremely flat valleys—in the loss landscape, where gradient descent slows dramatically along the valley bottom but falls quickly into it.
- **Mechanism:** For non-zero biases (\(b \neq 0\)), the loss \(L\) scales as \(\sim X\) along canyon bottoms versus \(\sim X^3\) typical height (\(X\) = training domain size). The second derivative along the canyon is suppressed by \(X^2\), making motion along it parametrically slow. More parameters increase degeneracy, creating more canyons—forming the capacity-stability tradeoff.
- **Core assumption:** Optimization uses steepest descent on squared-error loss over a bounded domain.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 4.2.2] analytically derives for \(y=x\) with bias: \(L_{\text{min}} \sim b^2 X^3/(4(\dots))\), showing canyon depth scales with \(X\) while typical \(L \sim X^3\). [section 5.1.2] notes smoothing to sigmoids replaces discrete valleys with smooth canyons but not the slow manifold. [corpus] Neighbor papers on polynomial chaos do not report canyons, suggesting Heaviside-specificity.
- **Break condition:** If the polynomial degree is low and training samples densely cover the domain, the true minimum may be uniquely identified before canyon trapping occurs.

### Mechanism 3: Stochastic Escape via Minibatch Updates
- **Claim:** Stochastic batched updates (as in TensorFlow) escape canyons more effectively than pure steepest descent by discontinuously jumping between canyon intersections rather than sliding slowly along a single canyon.
- **Mechanism:** Each minibatch computes gradients on a random subset, producing a noisy update direction. This noise can push parameters out of a flat valley when the gradient from one batch conflicts with the canyon's direction, allowing exploration of intersection points where multiple canyons meet and the true minimum may reside.
- **Core assumption:** Training uses minibatch SGD with random shuffling, not full-batch deterministic gradient descent.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] states TensorFlow's batched updates escape canyons more effectively. [section 6.1.3] contrasts analytical steepest descent (trapped) with TensorFlow SGD (escape via random restarts/batch sampling). [section 7.2.2] shows polynomial ansatz initialization reduces training time by starting near canyon intersections.
- **Break condition:** If the canyon network is extremely complex (many flat directions) or batch size is too large, SGD may still follow a canyon; if too small, noise may prevent convergence.

## Foundational Learning
- **Heaviside idempotency and logical encoding**  
  *Why needed:* The Heavisidization method relies on \(\theta(\theta(x)) = \theta(x)\) to collapse nested layers and on \(\theta(a)+\theta(b)-1\) encoding AND. Without this, the two-layer representation would not be sufficient for all polynomials.  
  *Quick check:* Given \(\theta(x)\) as defined, show that \(\theta(\theta(x)+\theta(y)-1)=1\) iff \(x>0\) AND \(y>0\). How does this enable multiplication of positive integers via \(\sum_{i,j} \theta(\theta(x-i)+\theta(y-j)-1)\)?
- **Loss landscape degeneracy vs. flat valleys**  
  *Why needed:* Distinguishing a *valley* (continuous set of minima with identical loss) from a *canyon* (flat bottom with higher loss than the true minimum) is crucial. The canyon is a near-degeneracy where \(L\) is constant along one direction but not minimal.  
  *Quick check:* If \(L(W,w,b)=X^3(Ww-1)^2 + b^2 X^3/(4(X^2 w^2 - 3Xbw + 3b^2))\), for fixed \(w\), what is the shape of \(L\) as a function of \((W,b)\)? Identify the valley and canyon directions.
- **Polynomial ansatz as regularization**  
  *Why needed:* The analytical Heavisidization formulas provide a structured initialization that places parameters near canyon intersections or true minima, bypassing slow descent.  
  *Quick check:* For \(y=x^2\), the Heavisidization uses \(w_1\) matrix with pattern [[1,1,0,0],[1,0,1,0],[0,1,0,1],[0,0,1,1]]. Why is this structure (off-diagonals) necessary? What would happen if you initialized \(w_1\) as diagonal?

## Architecture Onboarding
Input -> Layer1 (Linear + Heaviside/Sigmoid) -> Layer2 (Linear + Heaviside/Sigmoid) -> Output (Linear)

- **Critical path:**
  1. Define problem: target polynomial \(y(\mathbf{x})\).
  2. Derive or guess Heavisidization ansatz weights \((w_0, b_0, w_1, b_1)\) from formulas like (80)-(81).
  3. Initialize network with ansatz values.
  4. Train with TensorFlow using small batches, monitoring loss and weight drift.
  5. Validate on test set to detect instability (drift indicates canyons not avoided).

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Capacity vs. Stability:* More hidden nodes increase representational power but introduce more degeneracy/canyons. "Optimal" \(M\) is given by polynomial degree formulas.
  - *Smoothing:* Using sigmoid (\(\Lambda>0\)) smooths canyons but does not eliminate slow manifolds; very large \(\Lambda\) approximates Heaviside but may cause numerical saturation.
  - *Discretization:* Continuous integrals become sums over \(i,j \in [1,M]\). \(M\) must be large enough to capture polynomial degree; too small causes truncation error, too large increases canyon complexity.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - Loss decreases very slowly after initial drop (stuck in canyon bottom).
  - Weights drift significantly from ansatz when training set size increases.
  - Training loss low but test loss high (overfitting to finite samples due to degeneracy).
  - Gradient norms become extremely small while loss is still above zero.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Identity function \(y=x\):** Train with \(M=6\), compare full-batch steepest descent (expected: slow crawl along canyon) vs. TensorFlow SGD (expected: escape after some epochs). Measure loss decay rate vs. domain size \(X\).
  2. **1×1 determinant (same as identity):** Initialize with ansatz (\(w_1\) diagonal, \(b=0\)). Verify training stays near ansatz for small training set (\(N \leq 10\)) but drifts for larger \(N\) (\(N \geq 40\)). Check if SGD with batch size 1 escapes faster.
  3. **Sigmoid smoothing:** For \(y=x\), vary \(\Lambda\) (sigmoid steepness) and \(\xi\) (center). Plot \(L(w,b)\) landscape. Confirm canyons persist but become smoother; very large \(\Lambda\) approximates Heaviside but may cause numerical saturation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Under what conditions is the two-layer Heaviside network representation (Equation 25) of a given polynomial canonical, and how does the choice of finite training dataset \(\alpha\) affect the stability and uniqueness of the resulting local minima in parameter space?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 3.5 explicitly questions whether representation (25) is canonical and notes different finite training datasets lead to different stable parameter states (Figs. 1-3). The paper asks whether a trajectory trained on an ever-expanding, evenly covered infinite dataset has an accumulation point.
- **Why unresolved:** Empirical instability (e.g., weight drift from analytical diagonal ansatz for the 3x3 determinant with 40 samples vs. 10 samples) is shown but no theoretical framework predicts or guarantees convergence to a specific solution from the degenerate minima continuum.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can the "canyon" phenomenon—severe slowdown in convergence along flat directions—be fundamentally eliminated by modifying architecture or activation function, or is it intrinsic to representing sharp, discontinuous targets (polynomials) with smooth, over-parameterized sigmoids?
- **Basis in paper:** Sections 4.2.2 and 5.1.2 show canyons arise from degeneracies lifted by non-zero parameters. Section 5.1 states smoothing Heavisides to sigmoids does not remove canyons, only makes them smoother. The paper asserts the canyon problem is "unavoidable" for broadly applicable ansätze.
- **Why unresolved:** TensorFlow's batched updates empirically escape (Section 6.1.3), but this is a heuristic trick, not a solution to the geometric problem. No exploration of alternative activation functions (ReLU, B-splines, wavelets) or architectural constraints (weight tying, normalization) to reduce degeneracy.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** For a polynomial of fixed degree and number of variables, what is the minimum number of nodes per layer in a two-layer Heaviside network required to exactly represent it without introducing unnecessary parameter redundancies that lead to canyons? Is the discrete ansatz from Equation 81 optimal?
- **Basis in paper:** Sections 3.4 and 7.2 provide the constructive Heavisidization formula (80,81) for monomials/polynomials, implying a specific \(M\) for a given polynomial. Section 7.2.2 shows initializing with this "determinant ansatz" leads to faster convergence and less drift than random initialization, suggesting a favorable region.
- **Why unresolved:** The ansatz is presented but not analyzed for optimality. It is unclear if it minimizes parameter count or canyon severity. No comparison to more compact representations or proof that fewer nodes fails for some polynomials.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How does the "ML uncertainty principle" (sharper minima correspond to smoother canyons) scale with polynomial degree and number of variables, and what are the precise quantitative relationships (e.g., canyon width as a function of polynomial degree and domain size \(X\))?
- **Basis in paper:** The principle is stated in the Abstract and Section 4.1. The identity example (Section 4.2.2) shows \(L_{\text{min}} \sim X\) at canyon bottom vs. typical \(L \sim X^3\). Section 4.2.4 contrasts with wavelet bases, implying but not generalizing scaling.
- **Why unresolved:** Analysis is limited to \(y=x\). Derivation for general polynomials is not provided. Eigenvalues of Hessian or canyon cross-section for higher-degree/multivariate polynomials are not computed; scaling law remains conjectural.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Can insights from Heaviside/canyon analysis for polynomial targets be extended to more complex, non-polynomial scientific models (e.g., involving transcendental functions, differential equations, or knot invariants) that are still fundamentally exact?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 6.3 speculatively mentions "non-linear algebra and knots" as test sites but provides no analysis. Introduction frames polynomials as simplest case study for "exact science" ML problems.
- **Why unresolved:** Mechanisms causing canyons arise from discrete sum/integral structure of Heavisidization; these may not transfer to representations of PDE solutions or knot invariants, which might require different architectures (e.g., PINNs) or generate different loss landscapes.

## Limitations
- The constructive Heavisidization method (Equation 25) lacks a general algorithm for deriving weights for arbitrary polynomials beyond given examples; the gauge degeneracy is proven only for \(y=x\), not generalized.
- Analytical canyon scaling (\(L \sim X\) vs. \(X^3\)) is derived solely for \(y=x\) with bias; no theoretical extension to higher-degree polynomials, and domain size \(X\) is inconsistently defined across experiments.
- The claim that minibatch SGD escapes canyons more effectively is not isolated from other TensorFlow defaults (momentum, adaptive learning rates); batch size, learning rate, and noise level need systematic ablation.
- The "uncertainty principle" remains qualitative without a formal theorem quantifying canyon dimension as a function of network width.
- The polynomial ansatz initialization requires knowing the target polynomial's Heavisidization a priori; for unknown exact-science problems this is not applicable, and the paper offers no automatic discovery method.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (Degenerate Representation):** Medium. Strong evidence for \(y=x\) (analytic + numerical), but generalization is speculative.
- **Mechanism 2 (Canyon Landscape & Scaling):** High for \(y=x\) (analytics + scaling plots). Low for broader polynomial class; results for \(M=6\) determinant are suggestive but not conclusive due to unclear \(M\) selection.
- **Mechanism 3 (SGD vs. GD Escape):** Medium. Empirical evidence clear, but causality (minibatch noise vs. other optimizer features) not isolated.
- **"Uncertainty Principle" Claim:** Low. Conceptually plausible and supported by observations, but lacks formal theoretical grounding or quantitative measure of "canyon count" vs. capacity.

Systematically vary *only* batch size (1, 10, full) while fixing learning rate and epochs. Train networks *without* ansatz using full-batch GD and SGD. For each polynomial, measure: (a) existence of canyon-like plateaus, (b) scaling of minimum loss with \(X\), (c) correlation between \(M\) and convergence time.

Compare training stability/speed when initializing with this approximate ansatz vs. random initialization for the \(M=6\) determinant task with \(N=10\) vs. \(N=40\) samples.
