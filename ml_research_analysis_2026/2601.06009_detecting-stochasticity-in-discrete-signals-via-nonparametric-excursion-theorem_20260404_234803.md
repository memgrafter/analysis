# Detecting Stochasticity in Discrete Signals via Nonparametric Excursion Theorem

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.06009
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06009
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A nonparametric test based on the ε⁻² scaling of excursion counts versus quadratic variation distinguishes diffusive stochastic processes from deterministic signals with near-perfect accuracy on synthetic data and detects stochasticity in real-world series like Bitcoin returns and environmental audio.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces a model-free method to determine whether a discrete time series originates from a continuous semimartingale (e.g., an Itô diffusion) or a deterministic system, using only a single observed path. The core insight is a rigorous scaling law: for any continuous semimartingale with finite quadratic variation, the number of excursions of amplitude at least ε scales as ε⁻², with the quadratic variation as the proportionality constant. By estimating quadratic variation from the data and comparing empirical excursion counts to the theoretical ε⁻² curve, one computes a log-log slope; values near –2 indicate diffusion-like stochasticity, while deterministic (or chaotic) systems deviate sharply. The approach is validated on canonical stochastic processes, applied to financial and environmental data, and shown to be robust to moderate noise.

## Method Summary
The method rests on a pathwise theorem (Theorem 2.1) stating that for a continuous semimartingale X on [0,T], lim_{ε→0} ε² N_ε(X) = [X]_T / 2 in L¹, where N_ε is the number of excursions of magnitude ≥ ε and [X]_T is quadratic variation. Given discrete observations, one first estimates the quadratic variation as d[X]_T = ∑ (X_{i+1} – X_i)². For a grid of ε values, the theoretical excursion count is N_theory(ε) = d[X]_T/(2ε²). The empirical count N_emp(ε) is obtained by detecting intervals where |X_t| exceeds ε. The ratio K(ε) = N_emp(ε) / N_theory(ε) should be approximately 1 over a range of ε for a diffusion. A log-log linear fit of N_emp versus ε within that range yields a slope s; the signal is classified as diffusive if s ∈ [–2.5, –1], otherwise non‑diffusive. The procedure is nonparametric and does not assume stationarity or a specific model.

## Key Results
- **Synthetic processes:** Near‑perfect classification accuracy for Ornstein‑Uhlenbeck and Cox‑Ingersoll‑Ross processes across parameters; deterministic periodic and chaotic systems are reliably identified as non‑diffusive unless dominated by additive noise.
- **Financial data:** Minute‑level Bitcoin returns and daily S&P 500 returns exhibit slopes ≈ –2.11 and –1.61, respectively, placing them within the diffusion band.
- **Environmental audio:** Sounds with broadband stochastic components (rain, waves) are predominantly classified as diffusive, while more periodic or transient sounds show lower diffusion rates.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The number of excursions of magnitude at least ε scales as ε⁻² with the quadratic variation for continuous semimartingales.
- **Mechanism:** Theorem 2.1 establishes that for any continuous semimartingale with finite quadratic variation, lim_{ε→0} ε² N_ε(X) = [X]_T / 2 in L¹. This creates a theoretical expectation N_theory(ε) = [X]_T/(2ε²). Deterministic systems (e.g., periodic or chaotic ODE solutions) have zero quadratic variation in the continuous limit and thus do not satisfy this scaling.
- **Core assumption:** The observed signal originates from a continuous semimartingale (specifically an Itô diffusion) with finite quadratic variation over the observation window.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - Abstract: "The scaling law holds universally for all continuous semimartingales with finite quadratic variation... but fails sharply for deterministic systems."
  - Section 2.3: States Theorem 2.1 and notes "Deterministic systems do not satisfy the ε⁻² law, as their quadratic variation is zero in the continuous limit."
- **Break condition:** The process is not a continuous semimartingale (e.g., has jumps/Lévy motion, infinite variation like fractional Brownian motion) or the deterministic component dominates so strongly that finite‑sample estimates of [X]_T are negligible.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Quadratic variation can be reliably estimated from discrete observations via the sum of squared increments.
- **Mechanism:** For a semimartingale, the realized variance ∑ (X_{t_{i+1}} – X_{t_i})² converges to the continuous quadratic variation [X]_T as the sampling interval Δt → 0. This estimate d[X]_T is then used to compute the theoretical excursion curve N_theory(ε).
- **Core assumption:** The discrete time series is a sampled path of a continuous semimartingale, and the sampling interval Δt is sufficiently fine relative to the process’ volatility timescale.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Abstract: Mentions the method relies on "estimated quadratic variation" and is "nonparametric, model‑free."
  - Section 3.1: Explicitly defines d[X]_T = ∑ (ΔX_i)² as the estimator.
- **Break condition:** Sampling is too coarse (large Δt), suppressing small excursions and causing underestimation of [X]_T; or the presence of measurement noise/outliers biases the sum of squares.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The log‑log slope of empirical excursion count N_emp(ε) versus ε being approximately –2 is a diagnostic signature of diffusive (Itô) dynamics.
- **Mechanism:** Compute the ratio K(ε) = N_emp(ε) / N_theory(ε) across a range of ε. For a diffusion, K(ε) ≈ 1 over a meaningful range of ε, and fitting log(N_emp) ~ s log(ε) yields s ≈ –2. Deviations (s > –1 or s < –2.5 per the paper’s heuristic thresholds) indicate non‑diffusive structure.
- **Core assumption:** There exists a non‑trivial range of ε where the theoretical prediction based on d[X]_T is valid and K(ε) stabilizes near 1 for diffusive processes.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Abstract: "The resulting ratio K(ε)... is then summarized by a log–log slope deviation measuring the ε⁻² law."
  - Section 3.2 & 4: Shows histograms of slope concentrated near –2 for Brownian motion (Fig. 1) and defines classification thresholds [–2.5, –1].
- **Break condition:** No stable ε‑range exists where K(ε) ≈ 1 due to nonstationarity, excessive noise, or that the process does not obey the scaling law (e.g., deterministic chaotic map with fine sampling may still show sub‑ε⁻² scaling).

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Continuous Semimartingales**
  - *Why needed:* Theorem 2.1 applies exclusively to this class. Understanding that Itô diffusions are semimartingales (local martingale + finite variation) clarifies why deterministic ODE solutions (pure finite variation) are excluded.
  - *Quick check:* "Does a solution to ẋ = –x with x(0)=1 have finite quadratic variation over [0,1]?" (Answer: No, it’s zero.)
- **Concept: Quadratic Variation**
  - *Why needed:* It is the central pathwise quantity linking the excursion count to the diffusion intensity. The entire test hinges on its accurate estimation.
  - *Quick check:* "Given discrete data points (t_i, x_i), how would you compute an estimate of [X]_T? What assumptions make this estimate converge?"
- **Concept: Excursions (Amplitude Thresholding)**
  - *Why needed:* The method counts "excursions of magnitude at least ε." This requires defining what constitutes an excursion (typically a continuous segment where |X_t| > ε after crossing a threshold) and implementing a robust counting algorithm on discrete data.
  - *Quick check:* "If you have a noisy sine wave, would increasing ε increase or decrease N_ε, and why?"

## Architecture Onboarding
Preprocessor -> Quadratic Variation Estimator -> Excursion Counter -> Slope Fitter -> Classifier
- **Critical path:** Accurate d[X]_T → plausible N_theory(ε) → reliable N_emp(ε) → valid K(ε) range → unbiased slope estimate. Failure at any stage cascades.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - ε‑grid resolution: Finer grid gives smoother K(ε) but increases variance in N_emp(ε) for small ε (where counts are high).
  - Range selection for slope fit: Heuristic (K(ε)≈1) avoids small‑ε discretization artifacts and large‑ε undersampling; subjective but necessary.
  - Thresholds [–2.5, –1]: Empirical from Brownian motion simulations (Fig. 1); may not generalize to all diffusions or sampling rates.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - d[X]_T ≈ 0 despite visible fluctuations → suggests either pure deterministic signal or Δt too large.
  - K(ε) monotonically decreasing/increasing with no plateau → indicates mis‑specified theoretical curve or non‑diffusive dynamics.
  - Slope estimate highly sensitive to ε‑range choice → implies nonstationarity or inadequate signal duration.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Sanity check:** Generate Brownian motion (Δt=0.01, T=100). Expect slope ≈ –2, d[X]_T > 0.
  2. **Deterministic negative control:** Integrate ẋ = sin(t). Expect slope > –1, d[X]_T ≈ 0.
  3. **Noise robustness:** Add Gaussian noise (SNR=10 dB) to the sine wave. Observe transition in slope toward –2 as noise dominates; note classification flip.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question:** How can the excursion‑scaling test be extended to apply to jump processes and Lévy‑type models with infinite‑activity jumps?
  - *Basis:* Explicitly stated: "The method does not apply to jump processes, impulsive dynamics, or Levy‑type models with infinite‑activity discontinuities..." (Section 5).
  - *Why unresolved:* Theorem 2.1 assumes continuous semimartingale with finite quadratic variation; jump processes have discontinuous paths and different quadratic variation behavior.
  - *Needed evidence:* Theoretical result linking excursion counts to an appropriate quantity for jump processes; algorithm for discrete estimation; validation on jump‑diffusion simulators.
- **Question:** Is there an analogous scaling law for fractional Brownian motion (fBm) or other Gaussian processes with long‑range dependence?
  - *Basis:* Explicitly: "Fractional Brownian motion also lacks the finite quadratic variation needed for correct scaling." (Section 5).
  - *Why unresolved:* fBm’s quadratic variation diverges or vanishes with Δt depending on H; excursion scaling likely involves H, but no law is given.
  - *Needed evidence:* Theoretical analysis of fBm excursion counts; identification of a scaling exponent dependent on H; modified test to estimate H and separate from Itô diffusions.
- **Question:** Can the classification thresholds for the log‑log slope (currently [–2.5, –1.0]) be derived from first principles or learned to guarantee statistical robustness?
  - *Basis:* Inferred: thresholds chosen from Brownian motion simulation histograms, lacking theoretical justification or distributional analysis.
  - *Why unresolved:* Without knowing the sampling distribution of the slope under both hypotheses, thresholds are heuristic and may not control false positive rates.
  - *Needed evidence:* Derivation of asymptotic distribution of the slope estimator; bootstrap or extreme‑value methods for confidence intervals; comparative studies showing improved accuracy.
- **Question:** How does the test behave under slow nonstationarity, and can we design a time‑localized version to detect regime changes?
  - *Basis:* Inferred: Method assumes a single underlying process; no analysis of time‑varying parameters or sliding‑window implementation.
  - *Why unresolved:* Nonstationarity could bias d[X]_T and N_emp, leading to misclassification; unknown sensitivity.
  - *Needed evidence:* Theoretical investigation under piecewise‑constant or slowly varying parameters; simulation with time‑varying SDEs and deterministic regimes; development of moving‑window approach; application to data with known regime shifts.

## Limitations
- Excursion counting algorithm details are omitted (only cited [13]); reproduction relies on guesswork.
- ε‑range selection criteria (tolerance band, minimum log‑span) are qualitative and may bias slope estimates.
- The method is theoretically restricted to continuous semimartingales; no validation on jump processes or fractional Brownian motion.
- Real‑data classifications lack ground truth; financial returns may have microstructure noise or long memory that could mimic diffusive scaling.

## Confidence
- Theoretical scaling law (ε⁻² for semimartingales): **High** (rigorous theorem).
- Quadratic variation estimation from discrete sums: **Medium** (standard but sensitive to sampling rate and noise).
- Slope diagnostic thresholds (≈ –2): **Medium** (empirically set from limited simulations; robustness uncertain).
- Real‑world stochasticity detection: **Low** (no independent validation; alternative dynamics might mimic scaling).
