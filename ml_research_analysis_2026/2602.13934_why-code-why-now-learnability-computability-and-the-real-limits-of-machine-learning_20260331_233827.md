# Why Code, Why Now: Learnability, Computability, and the Real Limits of Machine Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.13934
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13934
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Code generation scales reliably due to dense, local, verifiable feedback (Level 4), while reinforcement learning typically suffers from sparse/adversarial feedback (Levels 0–1), making learnability dependent on feedback structure rather than model size.

## Executive Summary
This paper argues that the divergent scaling behaviors of code generation and reinforcement learning (RL) are not due to model size or algorithmic differences but stem from fundamental properties of the tasks themselves. It introduces a formal five-level hierarchy of learnability based on information structure, distinguishing expressibility, computability, and learnability. The core finding is that code benefits from inherently dense, attributable, and verifiable feedback (e.g., compiler errors per token), placing it at the highest learnability level (Level 4). In contrast, most RL problems involve sparse, delayed, or adversarial feedback (Levels 0–1), which causes unstable learning and collapse under distribution shift. The analysis shows that learnability ceilings are set by feedback structure, and that excessive expressibility (e.g., infinite VC dimension) can destroy learnability by making finite evidence insufficient to constrain hypotheses.

## Method Summary
The paper formalizes learnability, computability, and expressibility using risk functionals and quantifier depth to define a hierarchy of five levels (0–4). It proves separations between these levels, demonstrating that higher levels (dense, verifiable feedback) enable predictable scaling while lower levels (sparse/adversarial feedback) lead to instability. Key theoretical tools include VC dimension analysis to show that infinite expressibility destroys distribution-free PAC learnability, and the concept of "epiplexity" (structural information extractable by a bounded observer) to explain why code is learnable. The framework isolates the role of feedback structure by assuming realizability (a perfect hypothesis exists in the class).

## Key Results
- Introduces a five-level hierarchy (0–4) of learnability based on information structure, with formal separations between levels.
- Code generation occupies Level 4 (dense, local, verifiable feedback) due to compilers/type checkers, enabling monotonic improvement and stable scaling.
- Reinforcement learning tasks typically reside at Levels 0–1 (sparse/adversarial feedback) due to reward sparsity, non-stationarity, and reflexive collapse, causing unstable learning.
- Expressibility (e.g., infinite VC dimension) does not guarantee learnability and can prevent it by invalidating uniform convergence.
- Learnability ceilings depend on feedback structure, not model capacity; scaling compute in low-level tasks amplifies collapse.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Feedback Density Determines Scaling
- **Claim:** The quality and structure of feedback (dense, local, verifiable vs. sparse/adversarial) is the primary determinant of predictable scaling, not model size or algorithm.
- **Mechanism:** Dense feedback (Level 4) provides immediate, attributable error signals at each decision point (e.g., type checking per token). This creates a high-signal training distribution where mistakes are precisely localized, enabling stable gradient descent and monotonic improvement. Sparse or adversarial feedback (Levels 0–1) creates a low-signal or shifting distribution where credit assignment fails, targets move, or errors blend with noise.
- **Core assumption:** Learning systems fundamentally depend on the information structure exposed by the task; no amount of compute can extract absent, delayed, or corrupted structure.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states code provides dense feedback while RL has sparse/adversarial feedback; Section 3.4 notes binary reward acts as a filter, creating substantial information density differences.
- **Break condition:** If a task's feedback cannot be made dense and attributable via scaffolding (e.g., intermediate rewards), scaling yields diminishing returns or collapse.

### Mechanism 2: The Expressibility Trap
- **Claim:** Excessive expressibility in the hypothesis class (e.g., infinite VC dimension) destroys distribution-free learnability by making finite evidence insufficient to constrain predictions.
- **Mechanism:** A class that can shatter arbitrarily large finite sets contains infinitely many hypotheses consistent with any finite training set. Uniform convergence fails, so no algorithm can guarantee low error on unseen data under all distributions. The learner cannot rule out "bad" functions that agree on training data but diverge elsewhere.
- **Core assumption:** Learnability requires hypothesis complexity to match information provided by finite samples; expressibility alone is insufficient and can be counterproductive.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 5.1 states any class with VC(H)=∞ is not PAC-learnable distribution-freely; the class of all computable functions has infinite VC dimension (Lemma 1). Increasing expressivity can destroy learnability guarantees.
- **Break condition:** If the hypothesis class cannot be constrained (by architecture, inductive bias, or data manifold) to finite VC dimension or equivalent, PAC-style guarantees fail and learning becomes fragile to distribution shift.

### Mechanism 3: RL's Structural Information Obstacles
- **Claim:** RL's poor scaling stems from three structural obstacles—misaligned reward, non-stationary data distribution, and reflexive target collapse—relegating most RL problems to Level 1.
- **Mechanism:** 1) **Misalignment:** Sparse/delayed reward does not attribute credit to specific actions, causing causal ambiguity. 2) **Non-stationarity:** The learner's policy changes the state distribution, violating i.i.d. assumptions and making past data misleading. 3) **Reflexive collapse:** Optimizing a proxy reward leads to Goodhart failure where the target shifts because it is being chased, turning feedback into noise (Level 0).
- **Core assumption:** RL's closed-loop interaction alters the data-generating process in ways that break standard learning theory assumptions; this is inherent to the problem structure, not a solvable engineering bug.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 5.3 details the threefold bottleneck; abstract notes RL collapses under distribution shift with structural differences. Corpus support includes [93324] on RL reasoning limits and [7] on supervised vs. RL generalization.
- **Break condition:** If reward can be redesigned to be dense, timely, and attributable (transforming to Level 4), and if the environment is made non-reactive or predictable, RL may become learnable. Otherwise, scaling compute amplifies collapse.

## Foundational Learning
- **Quantifier Depth and Adversarial Robustness**  
  *Why needed:* The hierarchy shows learnability properties differ by quantifier alternation depth (∃∀ vs. ∃∀∀∃). Deeper alternation means the mechanism must succeed under more universal adversarial choices (e.g., all distributions, all data orderings), explaining why learnability is harder than computability.  
  *Quick check:* Does your task require success for every possible data ordering (worst-case presentation)? If yes, you are at least at Level 3/4 complexity.

- **Epiplexity vs. Time-Bounded Entropy**  
  *Why needed:* Following Jiang et al. [13], epiplexity is structural information extractable by a bounded observer; time-bounded entropy is residual unpredictability. Code's learnability stems from high epiplexity: its constraints yield structure a bounded model can internalize. RL environments often have low epiplexity relative to the observer—too much entropy/unpredictability for the model budget.  
  *Quick check:* For your task, does a computationally bounded observer have a systematic way to extract reusable patterns from individual training examples, or is most information irreducible noise?

- **Realizable vs. Agnostic PAC Learnability**  
  *Why needed:* The paper's analysis assumes realizability (H⊇C) to isolate structural barriers. Agnostic learnability (no perfect hypothesis in H) is harder. This assumption focuses on whether finite samples can *find* the perfect hypothesis, not whether a good approximate one exists.  
  *Quick check:* Is there a hypothesis in your model class that can achieve zero error on the target task (realizable)? If not, you are in the agnostic setting, requiring stronger data/regularization.

- **VC Dimension and Uniform Convergence**  
  *Why needed:* Finite VC dimension is necessary for uniform convergence and distribution-free PAC learnability. The paper uses this to show that infinite VC dimension (common in over-expressive models) destroys learnability guarantees.  
  *Quick check:* Can your hypothesis class shatter arbitrarily large finite sets? If yes, uniform convergence fails and learnability is not guaranteed.

- **Distributional Shift and Non-Stationarity**  
  *Why needed:* RL's non-stationary data distribution (policy changes state distribution) violates PAC's i.i.d. assumption, causing past data to become misleading. This is a core reason RL tasks fall into low learnability levels.  
  *Quick check:* Does the data distribution change as the learner improves (e.g., policy affects states)? If yes, non-stationarity may impede learnability.

- **Goodhart's Law and Reflexive Collapse**  
  *Why needed:* Optimizing a proxy reward can cause the target to shift (reflexive collapse), turning feedback into noise (Level 0). This is a key obstacle in RL and other adversarial settings.  
  *Quick check:* Does optimizing your reward metric cause the behavior it measures to change fundamentally (e.g., reward hacking)? If yes, reflexive collapse may occur.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Task Analyzer -> Feedback Engineer -> Hypothesis Constructor -> Stability Monitor

**Critical path:**  
1. **Level Diagnosis:** Statically analyze the task to determine its inherent learnability level (0–4) based on feedback structure (e.g., code → Level 4; standard RL → Level 1).  
2. **Scaffold Design:** If level <4, design interventions to raise effective feedback level: add verifiers, decompose into sub-tasks with local correctness checks, introduce intermediate dense rewards.  
3. **Complexity Budgeting:** Constrain hypothesis class VC dimension relative to expected sample size; prefer architecture biases that restrict hypothesis space without sacrificing necessary expressibility.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Dense feedback vs. task integrity:** Over-scaffolding may trivialize the task (e.g., per-token compiler signals may not guarantee full program correctness). Balance local verifiability with holistic goal.  
- **Expressibility vs. learnability:** Larger models (infinite VC dimension) can represent more functions but risk unlearnability. Prefer architectures with implicit capacity control (e.g., attention limits, modular reuse) over unrestricted universal approximators.  
- **Simulated vs. real feedback:** Simulated dense feedback (e.g., learned critics) may not be truly verifiable and can reintroduce adversarial dynamics (Level 1). Prefer objective, deterministic verifiers.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **Level 0/1 symptoms:** Reward hacking without capability gain, training instability with increasing compute, performance collapse under distribution shift, "semantic drift" where outputs look plausible but fail ultimate verification.  
- **Level 2 limit symptom:** Asymptotic accuracy plateaus despite more data, with errors concentrated on rare, hard sub-distributions (finite VC dimension reached).  
- **Expressibility trap symptom:** Perfect in-distribution fit but catastrophic out-of-distribution generalization, with no amount of additional in-distribution data helping.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Feedback ablation:** Train identical models on a code task under three reward structures: (a) per-token compiler signals (Level 4), (b) final program pass/fail (Level 1), (c) noisified pass/fail. Compare sample efficiency and stability; expect (a) to scale predictably, (b)/(c) to show diminishing returns/collapse.  
2. **VC-dimension trap probe:** Train models of increasing capacity (width/depth) on a fixed code dataset until test error plateaus. Then introduce a small held-out "hard" sub-distribution (e.g., rare syntax). Does over-capacitated model overfit and fail more severely on the hard subset?  
3. **Non-stationarity stress test:** Take a trained code model and RL-fine-tune on a variant requiring a different coding style (e.g., functional vs. OOP). Measure performance on original vs. new style. Does improvement on new style degrade original performance (catastrophic interference)? Does output distribution shift cause reward collapse even on a Level 4 task?

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Hierarchy applicability:** Is the five-level hierarchy generalizable beyond code and RL to other modalities (e.g., vision–language tasks)?
- **Realizability assumption:** How does the analysis change in agnostic settings where no perfect hypothesis exists in the class?
- **Emergent dense feedback:** Could dynamically learned intermediate representations provide emergent dense feedback, altering a task's effective level?
- **Scaffolding limits:** What are the precise boundaries of safe scaffolding that raises feedback density without trivializing the task?

## Limitations
- Theoretical analysis assumes realizability; real-world tasks are often agnostic.
- Hierarchy is tested primarily on code vs. RL; generalizability to other domains is unproven.
- Lack of broad empirical validation: central thesis (feedback density as scaling factor) relies on conceptual arguments and limited examples.
- Does not account for potential emergent dense feedback from model internals during training.

## Confidence
- **Hierarchy & quantifier-depth separations:** High (rigorous proofs).
- **Infinite VC → non-learnable (expressibility trap):** High (standard PAC result).
- **Feedback density as primary scaling factor:** Medium (plausible mechanistic explanation, limited direct experimental evidence).
- **RL tasks are inherently Level 0–1:** Medium (strong theoretical argument, but possible counterexamples via dense reward shaping not ruled out).

## Next Checks
1. **Dense vs. sparse feedback ablation:** On a code benchmark, train identical models under (a) per-token compiler signals (dense), (b) final pass/fail (sparse), (c) noisy pass/fail. Compare sample efficiency and stability across compute budgets.
2. **VC-dimension trap experiment:** Train models of increasing capacity on a fixed code dataset; evaluate both in-distribution accuracy and performance on a held-out "hard" sub-distribution (rare syntax). Expect over-capacitated models to overfit severely on the hard subset.

Compare scaling behavior with original sparse-reward setting; a shift toward monotonic improvement would validate the Level-shift hypothesis.
