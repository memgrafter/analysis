# RADAR: Revealing Asymmetric Development of Abilities in MLLM Pre-training

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.12892
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12892
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Introduces Soft Discrimination Score (SDS) and Multi‑Modal Mixture Benchmark (M³‑Bench) for efficient, fine‑tuning‑free evaluation of pre‑trained MLLMs; reveals asymmetric scaling of perception (data‑driven) and reasoning (scale‑driven) abilities.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the lack of tools to evaluate pre‑trained multimodal large language models (MLLMs) without fine‑tuning. It proposes the Soft Discrimination Score (SDS), a metric that uses normalized token logits to measure a model's preference for correct answers over distractors, and M³‑Bench, a large‑scale (15,894 samples) multiple‑choice benchmark covering perception and reasoning tasks. Experiments show SDS correlates with fine‑tuned performance and uncovers that perception ability scales with training data volume and alignment‑style pre‑training, while reasoning benefits more from model scale and fully open pre‑training, showing diminishing returns from additional data. Controlled two‑stage pre‑training confirms that reasoning gains are not solely data‑driven.

## Method Summary
RADAR introduces two key components: the Soft Discrimination Score (SDS) and the Multi‑Modal Mixture Benchmark (M³‑Bench). SDS evaluates an MLLM's 0‑shot ability by computing the average token logit for each candidate answer, normalizing via a softmax across all candidates to produce a relative preference score for the correct answer (range 0–1). This avoids instability from binary accuracy, especially with near‑correct distractors. M³‑Bench collects or reformats existing datasets into a unified image‑question‑candidates format, ensuring each question has one correct answer and one distractor. It spans 7 categories (e.g., visual recognition, spatial reasoning, math) totaling 15,894 samples, all sharing the same input format (image + question + candidate answers) to enable direct logit scoring without instruction tuning. To use, one runs the pre‑trained model on each image+question, obtains logits for each candidate token, computes SDS per sample, and aggregates across tasks. The paper also implements controlled two‑stage pre‑training to isolate effects of added data.

## Key Results
- SDS correlates positively with fine‑tuned accuracy (Pearson r=0.47–0.58), validating it as a predictor of downstream performance.  
- Analysis of open‑source models (LLaVA‑OneVision, Qwen2‑VL, InternVL‑3.5) shows that the model with the most pre‑training data (Qwen2‑VL) leads on perception tasks, while the largest model (InternVL‑3.5‑14B) leads on reasoning tasks.  
- Perception improves with increased data and alignment‑style pre‑training; reasoning plateaus with added data and requires larger model scale and fully open pre‑training, as confirmed by two‑stage experiments where perception gains were steady but reasoning gains were minimal or inconsistent.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: SDS as a stable metric**  
SDS measures relative preference for correct answers by normalizing token‑level logits across candidates. It averages the logits for each candidate, applies a softmax, and takes the score assigned to the correct answer. This soft normalization reduces sensitivity to absolute logit scale and mitigates issues when distractors are nearly correct, providing smoother curves than binary accuracy. The core assumption is that average token logit reflects the model's internal preference, and the relative score is comparable across questions. Evidence includes equations (3–5) and Figure 2 showing smoother trajectories; neighboring work often uses raw accuracy, which SDS improves upon.

**Mechanism 2: M³‑Bench enables comprehensive 0‑shot evaluation**  
The benchmark reformats existing multiple‑choice datasets by replacing option letters with actual text to avoid ordering bias and generates distractors for free‑form questions. It adds new data from Wikipedia visual concepts and web‑sourced spatial reasoning scenes, reaching 15,894 samples across 7 tasks, all sharing image+question+candidates format. This allows direct logit scoring without instruction tuning. The core assumption is that pre‑trained MLLMs assign meaningful token logits to candidates when conditioned solely on an image and question. Evidence includes dataset statistics (Figure 5) and the fact that existing benchmarks are either small or require instruction tuning, which M³‑Bench avoids.

**Mechanism 3: Asymmetric development of perception and reasoning**  
Evaluation via SDS on M³‑Bench reveals that perception and reasoning scale differently. Perception benefits from increased training data and alignment‑style pre‑training (e.g., LLaVA’s projector‑only tuning), while reasoning scales primarily with model size and requires fully open pre‑training (all parameters trainable). Two‑stage experiments adding category‑specific data show steady perception gains but flat or noisy reasoning improvements, indicating reasoning bottlenecks are not merely data volume. The core assumption is that SDS validly isolates these abilities and compared models differ systematically in pre‑training strategies. Evidence includes scaling trends (Figures 7, 8) and two‑stage results.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** M³‑Bench dataset -> Evaluation harness -> SDS module -> Analysis scripts

**Critical path:**  
a) Prepare M³‑Bench in the model’s expected input format (image tiling, tokenization).  
b) For each sample, run the model on the image+question context to obtain initial hidden states; then for each candidate token, using KV‑caching, compute the token‑wise logit and average over candidate tokens.  
c) Normalize candidate scores via softmax to get the preference for the correct answer.  
d) Aggregate across all samples to compute mean SDS per task and overall; split by perception/reasoning.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Each candidate’s token averaging avoids bias from single‑token answers but may introduce length normalization; alternative methods (first‑token logit) exist.  
- Number of distractors: paper uses one; more would stabilize normalization but increase compute.  
- Sample size: larger benchmarks reduce noise; they chose ~2K per category.  
- Model input format must match pre‑training (resolution, patch size) for valid logits.

**Failure signatures:**  
- All SDS scores cluster around 0.5 → model not properly aligned or projector not trained.  
- SDS near 1.0 but random accuracy → bias toward a single candidate, indicating implementation error (e.g., scoring only one candidate).  
- Huge gap between SDS and accuracy for a subset → near‑correct distractors causing instability; trust SDS for subtle changes.  
- In two‑stage experiments, reasoning improves linearly with added data would contradict the claimed asymmetry.

**First experiments:**  
1. **Correlation replication:** Pre‑train a small MLLM (e.g., Qwen2‑0.5B + SigLIP) on 300K general data for 1,000 steps, checkpoint every 100 steps. Compute pre‑training SDS on M³‑Bench, then fine‑tune each checkpoint on LLaVA‑665K and compute accuracy. Verify positive Pearson correlation (≈0.5) as in Figure 6.  
2. **Open‑model validation:** Evaluate LLaVA‑OneVision‑0.5B, Qwen2‑VL‑2B, InternVL‑3.5‑1B on M³‑Bench; compute perception and reasoning SDS. Confirm that the model with most data (Qwen2) leads on perception while the largest model (InternVL‑14B) leads on reasoning, mirroring Table 1/2 and Figure 7 trends.  
3. **Two‑stage controlled training:** Start with LLaVA‑558K pre‑training for 550 steps; then add a category‑specific dataset (e.g., Wiki‑Plant, 50K) mixed with general (100K) for another 550 steps. Track SDS on plant identification (perception) and on MathVista (reasoning). Expect steady increase for perception, flat or noisy trend for reasoning, as shown in Figure 8.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:** What specific non‑data factors (e.g., model architecture, optimization strategies, data quality) are primarily responsible for the observed plateau in reasoning ability gains, given that increasing homogeneous data volume alone does not reliably improve reasoning?  
*Basis:* The controlled two‑stage pre‑training experiments (Sec. 4.3.2) show modest and inconsistent gains for reasoning tasks. The authors conclude the bottleneck "is not merely data‑volume" but do not isolate key factors.  
*Why unresolved:* No systematic ablation of non‑data factors; unclear which architectural or optimization changes are most critical.

**Open Question 2:** Why do certain multidimensional abilities (e.g., image quality assessment) show negligible development during pre‑training but significant improvement during supervised fine‑tuning, as indicated by the static pre‑training SDS near 0.38 that later rises?  
*Basis:* In Sec. 4.2, pre‑training scores for image quality remain static, but fine‑tuning improves them. This suggests a distributional shift between pre‑training and fine‑tuning data.  
*Why unresolved:* The paper does not analyze the specific content differences between the pre‑training and fine‑tuning datasets; the trigger for phase‑dependent learning is unknown.

**Open Question 3:** What operational definition distinguishes "fully open, large‑scale pre‑training" (Qwen2‑VL/InternVL) from "lightweight alignment pre‑training" (LLaVA‑style) in terms of trainable parameters, data diversity metrics, and pre‑training objectives, and which aspect(s) are most critical for balancing perception and reasoning development?  
*Basis:* Sec. 4.3.1 contrasts LLaVA‑OneVision (lightweight alignment on projector) with Qwen2‑VL and InternVL (fully open, large‑scale). The former yields notable perception gains but almost no reasoning improvement, while the latter improves both.  
*Why unresolved:* The comparison conflates multiple differences (parameter count, data volume, training stages); it is unclear which factor(s) drive the reasoning advantage.

**Open Question 4:** How should future pre‑training evaluation benchmarks be designed to optimally align with the objectives and capabilities of broad, general‑purpose pre‑training, avoiding the pitfalls of being either too simplistic (failing to discriminate model strengths) or too specialized (requiring instruction‑following or niche skills)?  
*Basis:* The authors critique existing benchmarks: general ones are too small, while domain‑specific ones yield random‑chance results for general pre‑trained MLLMs. M³‑Bench attempts to fill the gap but uses manually curated "fundamental" spatial reasoning scenes.  
*Why unresolved:* The design principles for an ideal benchmark (large‑scale, diverse, cognitively appropriate, discriminative) are not formally extracted; the boundary between foundational and specialized tasks remains qualitative.

## Limitations
- SDS assumes average token logits reflect preference; length bias and poor model calibration can distort scores.  
- M³‑Bench may overlap with pre‑training corpora, risking overestimation; 0‑shot logit scoring may be unstable for poorly aligned models.  
- Asymmetric scaling claims rely on a small set of open‑source models with confounding differences in training objectives and data preprocessing.

## Confidence
- **SDS as stable metric:** Medium (±0.5 correlation shows promise, but break conditions and independent replication needed).  
- **M³‑Bench coverage:** Medium (diverse tasks but potential contamination and dependence on candidate format).  
- **Asymmetric development:** Low (observational analysis with many uncontrolled factors; two‑stage evidence narrow).

## Next Checks
