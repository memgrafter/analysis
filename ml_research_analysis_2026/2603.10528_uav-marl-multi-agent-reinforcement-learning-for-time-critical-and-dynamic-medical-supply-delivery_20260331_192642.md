# UAV-MARL: Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning for Time-Critical and Dynamic Medical Supply Delivery
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.10528
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10528
- **Reference count:** 16
- **Primary result:** Synchronous PPO achieves 100% task success, reduces mission time from ~1400 s to ~800 s as fleet size increases, while asynchronous variants (APPO, IMPALA) fail to converge.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the coordination of UAV fleets for dynamic, time-critical medical supply delivery under stochastic task arrivals and heterogeneous urgency. The problem is modeled as a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP) to reflect realistic communication constraints, and solved with a multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) framework using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). Tailored reward shaping provides dense intermediate feedback, and decentralized execution allows each UAV to act on local observations. The synchronous on-policy PPO approach converges reliably, scales to fleets of 4–20 UAVs, and achieves perfect task success while significantly reducing mission duration. In contrast, asynchronous off-policy methods do not learn, highlighting the importance of stability in this cooperative, deadline-driven setting.

## Method Summary
The environment is a 30×30 grid representing part of Brussels, with two depots and six clinics. Tasks arrive stochastically (rate 0.2/step), each assigned a source depot, destination clinic, and urgency level (critical, urgent, standard) that determines its deadline. UAVs have a payload capacity of 5 and move via discrete actions (up, down, left, right, stay). At each timestep, every UAV receives a fixed-length observation vector containing its normalized position, payload, carrying status, positions of all other UAVs (regardless of range), information about the closest pending task and current task (if any), nearest depot and hospital, and global context (number of active tasks, pending tasks ratio, time step). The reward function combines a large sparse bonus for timely delivery (+50, with urgency bonuses) and several dense shaping terms: proximity to pending tasks, distance reduction toward target, progress movement, pickup success, early delivery, and small penalties for idling and deadline misses. Training uses Ray RLlib with a single shared policy (homogeneous agents) and 8 workers, each running 2 environments. The core algorithm is synchronous PPO; baselines include A2C, APPO, and IMPALA. Hyperparameters: learning rate 0.0003, discount 0.99, 2 million training steps.

## Key Results
- PPO converges from an average return of –600 to –200 and achieves a 100 % task success rate across fleet sizes 4–20.
- Mission time decreases from ~1400 s to ~800 s as fleet size grows, demonstrating efficient coordination.
- Asynchronous variants (APPO, IMPALA) fail to improve beyond initial performance.
- Training times range from 350–1200 s; evaluation latency is 0.5–1.2 s per episode, indicating practical deployability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: POMDP formulation for decentralized coordination
The problem is modeled as a partially observable Markov decision process. Each UAV agent receives a local observation that does not include the exact positions of other agents unless they are within a nominal communication range. This mirrors real-world constraints where UAVs cannot track the entire fleet, forcing the learned policy to rely on features like “closest pending task” and “global context” rather than full global state. The core assumption is that decentralized execution with partial observability is sufficient for learning effective coordination. Evidence comes from Section II.E and III.B, which describe the POMDP and the observation components. The approach would likely break down if full observability were available, because the need for stable on‑policy updates might diminish and asynchronous methods could then converge.

### Mechanism 2: Dense reward shaping
The natural reward (delivery completion) is sparse and delayed. The authors introduce dense shaping components (e.g., +0.3 for distance reduction toward target, +0.2 for proximity to a pending task) that provide step‑wise feedback. These intermediate rewards prevent the credit assignment problem and steer agents toward useful behaviors—such as moving toward depots or hospitals—before they complete a delivery. The shaping is designed to not encourage reward hacking (e.g., circling near a hospital to gather proximity bonuses) and to align with the overall goal of timely, high‑urgency deliveries. Section III.C explicitly lists the shaping terms and states they “help agents learn useful intermediate behaviors.” If the dense rewards were mis‑specified (e.g., over‑weighting proximity), agents might loiter without completing deliveries, causing divergence from the true objective.

### Mechanism 3: Synchronous on‑policy learning (PPO)
Synchronous PPO with its clipped objective yields stable policy updates, which are essential in this cooperative, constrained multi‑agent setting. The paper shows that PPO converges while asynchronous/off‑policy methods (APPO, IMPALA) do not. In PPO, all agents collect data in parallel but perform updates synchronously from a relatively recent, consistent snapshot of the joint policy. The clipping mechanism prevents destructive large updates, which is critical when multiple agents’ actions are coupled through shared resources (tasks, airspace) and strict deadlines. Section IV.C reports the experimental contrast and attributes PPO’s success to the stability of its clipped updates. The method would likely break down if the environment’s stochasticity were greatly reduced or if agents operated in fully independent subtasks, because then the need for such conservative updates would be lower.

## Foundational Learning
- **Multi‑Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) in Decentralized Execution**  
  *Why needed:* The system involves multiple UAVs making decisions based only on local information. Understanding centralized training with decentralized execution (CTDE) and fully decentralized paradigms is essential. This paper uses independent PPO policies with a shared observation that excludes full global state.  
  *Quick check:* In a POMDP MARL setting with limited communication, how does an agent’s observation space exclude global state, and what components compensate for this lack?

- **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)**  
  *Why needed:* PPO is the core algorithm; its on‑policy nature, importance sampling, and clipping are central to the reported success over asynchronous methods. An engineer must grasp why PPO’s update rule matters for cooperative dynamics.  
  *Quick check:* What is the purpose of the clipping parameter (ε) in PPO’s objective, and why might it be more critical in multi‑agent coordination than in single‑agent tasks?

- **Reward Shaping in Sparse‑Reward Domains**  
  *Why needed:* The task has a natural sparse reward (delivery completion). The dense shaping terms are vital for learning speed and final performance. Engineers must understand how to design shaping rewards that align with the ultimate goal.  
  *Quick check:* Given the paper’s reward table, what undesirable behavior might emerge if the “Distance reduction” reward were removed? What if it were weighted too highly?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map (plain text):**  
  Environment → Observation Encoder → Policy Network (MLP) → Action Selector → Environment.  
  Environment → Reward Computer → Learner (PPO) → Policy Network (parameter updates).  
  Learners (multiple workers) asynchronously gather trajectories for PPO’s synchronous update step.

- **Critical path (per timestep):**  
  1. Environment updates task deadlines, inventories, UAV positions.  
  2. Each UAV receives its local observation Oᵢ(t).  
  3. Policy network π(Oᵢ(t)) outputs action distribution.  
  4. Action aᵢ(t) is executed.  
  5. Environment computes next state and reward R(t).  
  6. For PPO/A2C, trajectory segments (O, a, R) are collected; after enough steps, a synchronous policy update is performed. For APPO/IMPALA, experiences are sent to a central learner asynchronously.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Synchronous (PPO) vs. Asynchronous (APPO/IMPALA):* PPO offers stable convergence but lower sample throughput; asynchronous methods aim for higher throughput but suffer from off‑policy staleness, which destabilizes learning.  
  - *Network architecture:* MLP vs. LSTM. LSTM intended to capture temporal dependencies (e.g., approaching deadlines) but performed worse, suggesting reactive decisions suffice.  
  - *Observation complexity:* Including “closest pending task” and “global context” vs. purely local info balances communication overhead with performance.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Algorithmic divergence: APPO/IMPALA returns stagnate near initial –600.  
  - Poor coordination: High mission time and low success rate (<100 %).  
  - LSTM degradation: Slower evaluation (~3 s/episode) and lower performance compared to MLP.

- **First three experiments:**  
  1. **Fleet scaling test:** Train PPO with fleet sizes {4,8,12,16}. Expect success rate to remain 100 % and mission time to decrease as fleet size grows (Fig 4a).  
  2. **Reward shaping ablation:** Remove “Urgent task claim” (+3) or “Distance reduction” rewards from Table II. Expect learning to slow or success rate to drop, especially for urgent tasks.  
  3. **Algorithm comparison replication:** Train A2C, PPO, APPO, IMPALA with same seed/environment. Expect only PPO to converge to return ~–200; APPO/IMPALA remain near –600; A2C may converge but less stably.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Battery and altitude effects:** The paper acknowledges (Section II.D) that a detailed analysis of battery consumption and altitude variations is future work. Current environment uses constant energy per movement, ignoring realistic physics; this may lead to infeasible policies. Resolving this requires integrating a physics‑based energy model, retraining, and comparing performance against the simplified baseline.
- **Failure of asynchronous MARL algorithms:** The paper observes (Section IV.C) that APPO and IMPALA fail to converge. The underlying cause (e.g., non‑stationarity from dynamic task assignments, instability in off‑policy correction) is not investigated. Evidence to resolve this would include training diagnostics (gradient norms, value estimates, KL divergences), ablations on V‑trace parameters, or testing hybrid on‑policy/off‑policy architectures.
- **Impact of communication range and reliability:** Although limited communication is highlighted as a challenge, the paper does not evaluate how communication degradation (packet loss, reduced range, limited bandwidth) affects coordination robustness. Evidence would come from simulating such degradation and measuring changes in task success, mission time, and policy robustness compared to the ideal baseline.
- **Discrete vs. continuous control:** The discrete action space (up/down/left/right/stay) simplifies planning but may be suboptimal for real UAVs that operate in continuous space. To assess trade‑offs, one could replace discrete actions with continuous velocity commands using PPO with Gaussian policies, then compare mission efficiency, training stability, and trajectory quality.

## Limitations
- Missing implementation details: exact depot/clinic coordinates, precise reward formulas (e.g., proximity threshold, urgent task claim trigger), handling of partial observability, and task assignment policy at depots.
- Simulation‑only evaluation; no real‑world validation.
- Asynchronous failures could be due to hyperparameter choices rather than fundamental incompatibility.
- Observations may include positions of all UAVs (full observability), undermining the claimed POMDP formulation.
- Results are limited to a homogeneous fleet and a single map; unknown how they generalize to heterogeneous UAVs or different urban layouts.

## Confidence
- **POMDP formulation and decentralized execution:** Medium (insufficient detail, weak external support).
- **Reward shaping effectiveness:** Medium (no ablation, reliance on dense terms unvalidated).
- **Synchronous PPO superiority over asynchronous variants:** High (clear experimental contrast).
- **Scalability and 100 % task success rate:** Medium (single map, specific distributions).
- **Practical deployability (training/evaluation latency):** High (concrete measurements).
