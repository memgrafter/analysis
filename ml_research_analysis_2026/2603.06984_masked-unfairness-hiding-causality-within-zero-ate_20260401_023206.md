# Masked Unfairness: Hiding Causality within Zero ATE

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.06984
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06984
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Fairness regulations based solely on average treatment effect (ATE) can be circumvented by "causal masking," where a zero ATE is maintained while still enacting unequal treatment across subgroups, making unfairness statistically hard to detect.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces the concept of causal masking, an optimization strategy that satisfies a zero average treatment effect (ATE) constraint with respect to a protected attribute while still permitting discriminatory treatment across subgroups. The authors formulate the problem as a linear program that maximizes an auxiliary objective (e.g., graduation rate, recidivism reduction) subject to a zero ATE constraint. They prove that when confounding or heterogeneous treatment effects exist, masked policies can strictly outperform fair policies that enforce equal treatment across all subgroups. Empirically, on the COMPAS dataset, masked policies evade detection by standard ATE tests about five times longer than unmasked unfair policies and accumulate greater cumulative unfairness. Moreover, tiny relaxations of the masking constraint yield disproportionately large utility gains compared to relaxing fairness constraints. The authors argue that these findings necessitate model-level regulation rather than relying solely on outcome-level fairness audits.

## Method Summary
The core method involves three types of decision policies: Exploit (unconstrained utility maximization), Fair (enforcing equal treatment rates across protected groups for every stratum), and Mask (enforcing zero ATE overall while allowing stratum-level disparities). Policies are defined by treatment rates α_{x,p} for each combination of stratum x and protected group p, with constraints on the overall selection rate ρ. The Mask policy is obtained via a linear program that maximizes utility (weighted sum of α_{x,p} with weights γ_{x,p}) subject to the zero ATE constraint ∑_x π_x (α_{x,1} - α_{x,0}) = 0, where π_x is the proportion of individuals in stratum x. Synthetic experiments sample random π and γ to compare utilities under varying constraint relaxations ε. For the COMPAS dataset, strata are formed by k-means clustering (k ∈ {5,...,30}) on selected features, and π, γ are estimated from data. Detection experiments simulate repeatedly from the empirical distribution, applying decisions according to a given policy, and record the smallest sample size at which hypothesis tests (ATE z-test vs. conditional CATE test via Fisher’s method) reject the null of no unfairness.

## Key Results
- Under confounding or heterogeneous effects, a generic performance gap exists: masked policies achieve strictly higher utility than any fair policy while maintaining zero ATE.
- In COMPAS simulations, masked unfairness remains undetected by ATE tests roughly five times longer than unmasked unfairness, and accumulates more cumulative disparity during that period.
- Relaxing the masking constraint by even a small ε yields much larger utility improvements than comparable relaxations of the fairness constraint, demonstrating a strong incentive to adopt masked policies.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Causal masking exploits the difference between marginal and conditional independence. A zero ATE (marginal) does not guarantee that treatment is fair within each subgroup (conditional). When treatment effects vary across subgroups (heterogeneity) or when the protected attribute confounds the relationship between features and outcome, an optimizer can assign treatment asymmetrically across subgroups in a way that cancels out in the aggregate. Specifically, the LP solution tends to allocate higher treatment rates to strata with higher positive outcomes for the privileged group and lower rates for the disadvantaged group, balanced so that the weighted average difference across protected groups is zero. This makes the overall ATE test insensitive to the within-stratum disparities, while conditional tests (e.g., testing CATE across strata) have lower power due to multiple comparisons and smaller effective sample sizes per stratum.

## Architecture Onboarding
The implementation pipeline consists of:
Data -> Stratification -> Parameter Estimation -> Policy Computation -> Simulation -> Hypothesis Tests.

Component map:
Data (COMPAS or synthetic) -> Stratification (k-means clustering) -> Parameter Estimation (π_x, γ_{x,p}) -> Policy Computation (Exploit/Fair/Mask LP) -> Simulation (sample n, assign decisions D) -> Hypothesis Tests (H_ATE, H_CATE) -> Detection Time.

Critical path: Stratification quality directly impacts parameter estimation and subsequent policy feasibility; the LP solver determines the mask policy; simulation randomness affects detection time estimation.

Design tradeoffs: Increasing number of strata k gives the optimizer more flexibility to mask (larger utility gap) but reduces power of conditional tests due to smaller per-stratum samples and multiple comparisons. The choice of solver (e.g., linear programming vs. quadratic) affects solution precision and computational time.

Failure signatures: (1) Exploit utility not strictly greater than Fair utility suggests zero heterogeneity/confounding, so masking should not improve. (2) ATE test rejection rate under true Fair policy > α indicates mis-calibration. (3) Mask LP infeasible when ρ > sum π_x or due to numerical issues.

First experiments:
1. **LP Solution Verification**: On synthetic data with k=2, compute Exploit, Fair, Mask policies analytically and via LP; verify utilities: Exploit ≥ Mask ≥ Fair, with strict inequalities when heterogeneity exists.
2. **Test Calibration**: Generate data from a known fair policy (α_{x,p} = α_x independent of p). Run both ATE and CATE tests over many repetitions; each should reject at nominal α (e.g., 5%).
3. **Replicate COMPAS Detection Delay**: For k=10, run the simulation pipeline with 500 repetitions; compute average first rejection n for ATE and CATE under Mask and unmasked Exploit; confirm that Mask evades ATE detection ~5x longer than Exploit.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How does causal masking generalize to non-binary or continuous protected attributes beyond the binary case studied?
- What are the theoretical properties of causal masking under fairness constraints defined by dependency measures other than ATE, such as global odds ratios or conditional mutual information?
- What practical mechanisms can enforce the authors’ advocated model-level fairness regulation, given the computational and data-intensive nature of conditional independence testing?
- How does the number of stratification levels (k) theoretically affect the performance gap between masked and fair policies, and the sample complexity of detecting masking?

## Limitations
- Implementation specifics (protected group binarization, stratification method, ATE test variance estimator, LP solver tolerance) are not fully detailed and may affect reproducibility.
- Results are confined to linear policy classes and binary protected attributes; masking may behave differently with more complex models or multi-valued P.
- Statistical power of conditional tests degrades rapidly with many strata, making detection in high-dimensional settings potentially infeasible.

## Confidence
- Theoretical performance gap under confounding/heterogeneity: **High** (rigorous proofs).
- Synthetic utility-relaxation findings (Figure 3): **Medium** (depends on sampling randomness and LP precision).
- COMPAS detection delay for masked policies (Figure 4): **Low-Medium** (sensitive to test calibration, data preprocessing, simulation variance).
