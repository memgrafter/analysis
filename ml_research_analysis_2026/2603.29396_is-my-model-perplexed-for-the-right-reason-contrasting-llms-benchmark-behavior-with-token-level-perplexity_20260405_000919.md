# Is my model perplexed for the right reason? Contrasting LLMs' Benchmark Behavior with Token-Level Perplexity
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.29396
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.29396
- Reference count: 6
- Primary result: LLMs can achieve high benchmark accuracy without relying on linguistically appropriate token-level cues, as shown by minimal-pair perplexity analysis.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces a token-level perplexity framework to test whether LLMs' correct performance on linguistic benchmarks stems from expected linguistic mechanisms. By comparing minimal sentence pairs and quantifying the contribution of pivotal tokens to perplexity differences, the study finds that pivotal tokens never fully explain the shift, even in tasks designed to elicit clear cues. Crucially, task accuracy is uncorrelated with proper token-level behavior, indicating that high performance often relies on alternative heuristics rather than the intended linguistic reasoning.

## Method Summary
The method uses minimal pairs—two prompts identical except for a few pivotal tokens—to isolate the effect of those tokens on perplexity. Token-level perplexity is computed for each position in both prompts, then aligned by token strings. The total absolute difference in perplexity (ΔPPL) is decomposed into the share contributed by pivotal tokens via plain proportion (raw share) and normalized proportion (baseline-corrected share). This allows hypothesis-driven testing: if the model uses the expected cue, pivotal tokens should explain a large, positive normalized proportion. The approach is applied across five tasks (BLiMP animacy/anaphor, DUST, stereotypes, nonsense) and four LLMs, with accuracy derived from sequence-level PPL comparisons.

## Key Results
- Pivotal tokens consistently contribute more than average tokens to perplexity differences but never fully explain the shift; normalized proportions are positive but well below 1 across all tasks and models.
- Task accuracy (correct prompt lower PPL) is high for most tasks (e.g., >90% on nonsense, BLiMP), yet normalized proportion is low (e.g., ~0.2–0.5), showing no correlation between accuracy and linguistically appropriate token reliance.
- The DUST task shows the highest normalized proportion (~0.6), but still not 1, suggesting even in referential ambiguity resolution, models use additional cues beyond the theoretically pivotal ones.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Mechanism 1: Token-level perplexity contrasts isolate cue reliance. By computing ΔPPL for minimal pairs, the contribution of pivotal tokens is quantified. If the model uses the expected linguistic feature, pivotal tokens should account for a large portion of the shift. Evidence from the abstract and Section 2. Break condition: if plain proportion ≈ 0 despite high accuracy, the mechanism fails.

Mechanism 2: Minimal pairs provide a clean contrast. The prompts share all tokens except pivotal ones, serving as their own controls. Evidence from abstract and Section 2. Break condition: if pairs aren't truly minimal or model is sensitive to framing, attribution confounded.

Mechanism 3: Normalized proportion robustly measures direction-sensitive influence. It averages pivotal token contributions minus the median token contribution. Positive indicates pivotal tokens drive the shift correctly. Evidence from Section 2. Break condition: if median is biased or distributions skewed.

## Foundational Learning
- Concept: Token-level perplexity. Why needed: It's the foundation; allows interpretation of differences at token level. Quick check: How do you compute token-level perplexity from conditional probability? (Answer: 1 / p(token | prefix).)
- Concept: Minimal pairs design. Why needed: Isolates effect of specific linguistic cues, enabling causal attribution. Quick check: In anaphor agreement minimal pairs, which tokens are pivotal? (Answer: Reflexive pronouns like "himself" vs. "herself".)
- Concept: Baseline-corrected normalization. Why needed: Raw proportions can be misleading; normalization adjusts for typical framing noise. Quick check: What does normalized proportion 0 signify? (Answer: Pivotal tokens contribute as much as a typical token.)

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
Open Question 1: To what extent do the findings that pivotal tokens never fully explain perplexity differences generalize across a broader range of linguistic tasks, model architectures, and scales? Basis in paper: [inferred] from the first limitation: “These results are restricted to a few datasets and models: their generalizability is still to be fully investigated and may be restricted to tasks and models that are akin to those studied here.” Why unresolved: The experiments covered only four models and five tasks; it remains unknown whether the pattern holds for other phenomena and for much larger or differently trained LLMs. What evidence would resolve it: Evaluate the method on a large, diverse set of linguistic benchmarks across many more LLMs; systematic generalization would be demonstrated if the same trend persists.

Open Question 2: How do alternative token‑scoring metrics (e.g., surprisal, normalized conditional probabilities, or context‑masked likelihoods) affect the conclusion that pivotal tokens only partially drive perplexity shifts? Basis in paper: [inferred] from the second limitation: “The results depend on the sequential definition of PPL and may not generalize if PPL is recalculated in another fashion; there are various papers showing the limitations of using PPL for understanding how good models are.” Why unresolved: The framework relies on raw token‑level perplexity; other metrics might distribute the ΔPPL differently. What evidence would resolve it: Replicate the pipeline using surprisal, length‑normalized perplexity, or layer‑wise relevance propagation; robustness would show similar partial attribution.

Open Question 3: What specific heuristic cues (e.g., surface statistics, lexical associations, positional patterns) do models exploit when their accurate benchmark performance is not matched by linguistically expected token‑level perplexity behavior? Basis in paper: [explicit] “We find that [...] models rely on heuristics other than the expected linguistic ones.” Why unresolved: The paper identifies alternative strategies but does not diagnose them. What evidence would resolve it: Combine analysis with targeted probing or controlled manipulations (e.g., masking non‑pivotal tokens) to isolate which features carry the unexplained ΔPPL; or perform post‑hoc attribution analysis to derive and test hypotheses.

Open Question 4: To what extent do superficial aspects of the prompt (e.g., sentence order, surrounding commentary, punctuation choices) influence token‑level perplexity distributions and the resulting attribution metrics? Basis in paper: [inferred] from the third limitation: “The sensitivity of the results to the prompts has been partly investigated—repeating experiments over multiple prompts—but a more extensive study on the effect of prompts is warranted, given the results concerning the period tokens.” Why unresolved: The study tested a few variations but found unexpected spikes (e.g., period tokens), suggesting incidental design choices may affect outcomes. What evidence would resolve it: Conduct a large‑scale prompt robustness experiment: generate multiple semantically equivalent prompts per pair, recompute metrics, and analyze variance; low variance would confirm robustness.

## Limitations
- Exact prompt templates and variations are unspecified, risking reproduction inconsistencies.
- Token alignment method for mismatched tokenizations is unclear, a critical failure point.
- The baseline for normalized proportion (median over all vs. non-pivotal tokens) is ambiguous, affecting metric interpretation.

## Confidence
- Core method conceptual validity: High
- Empirical finding that accuracy does not correlate with proper token behavior: Medium
- Metric interpretation (normalized proportion as direction-sensitive): Low
