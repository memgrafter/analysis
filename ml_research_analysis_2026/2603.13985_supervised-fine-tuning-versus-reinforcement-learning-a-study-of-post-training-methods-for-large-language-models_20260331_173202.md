# Supervised Fine-Tuning versus Reinforcement Learning: A Study of Post-Training Methods for Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.13985
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13985
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Hybrid SFT-RL pipelines consistently outperform standalone methods across domains, with their adoption rising from 20% in 2023 to 73.8% in 2024.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a unified theoretical framework demonstrating that supervised fine-tuning (SFT) is a special case of reinforcement learning (RL), enabling the combination of their objectives. Through a large-scale analysis of post-training research from 2023–2025, it reveals a rapid and dominant shift toward hybrid SFT-RL pipelines, which now constitute the majority of published methods. Empirical evidence across question answering, mathematical reasoning, agentic tasks, and code generation shows these hybrid approaches yield superior accuracy, reasoning, and generalization compared to using either method in isolation. The trend is linked to the rise of open-weight models, which facilitate scalable, cost-effective RL exploration.

## Method Summary
The study employs a literature survey and trend analysis of arXiv publications from 2023 to 2025. It collects papers via Google Cloud Public Datasets, filtering for SFT/RL relevance using an LLM-assisted prompt. Papers are assigned to application domains (QA, math, agentic, code) based on the frequency of mentions of 26 specific benchmark datasets, with a threshold of >5 mentions for classification. The primary training methodology (SFT-only, RL-only, hybrid) for each relevant paper is then determined, followed by manual verification. Yearly counts and methodological splits are tabulated to identify trends.

## Key Results
- Hybrid SFT-RL pipelines consistently outperform standalone SFT or RL across diverse domains (QA, math, agentic, code), improving accuracy, reasoning, and generalization.
- There is a rapid shift toward hybrid methods, with their share of publications jumping from 20% in 2023 to 73.8% in 2024 (projected 70.6% in 2025).
- The theoretical unification framing SFT as RL (via an indicator-function proxy reward) justifies combined loss formulations and enables algorithmic cross-pollination.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Complementary Strengths of Imitation and Exploration
- **Claim:** Hybrid SFT-RL pipelines consistently outperform standalone methods by combining stable imitation from expert demonstrations with reward-driven exploration.
- **Mechanism:** SFT establishes a reliable initial policy via behavior cloning on high-quality data, reducing the search space for RL. RL then refines this policy using reward signals to improve generalization, correct errors, and explore beyond the demonstration distribution. The complementary strengths mitigate SFT's distribution shift and RL's sample inefficiency.
- **Core assumption:** High-quality expert data and a well-shaped reward function are available; the reference policy for KL regularization is sufficiently close to the optimal policy.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract states hybrid approaches "consistently outperform standalone methods"; Section 5 reviews applications where hybrid methods show gains; neighboring papers like "UFT: Unifying Supervised and Reinforcement Fine-Tuning" directly integrate SFT and RL objectives.
- **Break condition:** If expert data is low-quality or the reward function is misspecified (e.g., reward hacking), hybrid training may degrade performance or fail to stabilize.

### Mechanism 2: Theoretical Unification
- **Claim:** Framing SFT as a special case of RL (via an indicator-function proxy reward) enables transfer of algorithmic techniques between paradigms and justifies hybrid loss formulations.
- **Mechanism:** The SFT gradient ∇θ L_SFT = E[−∇θ log πθ(y|x)] can be rewritten as E[− (I{y=y_i} / πθ(y|x)) ∇θ log πθ(y_i|x)], where the ratio acts as a reward. This reveals that SFT optimizes a pseudoreward that is 1 only on exact demonstration matches, making it an RL objective with a sparse, deterministic reward. Hence, RL techniques like importance sampling or online rollouts can enhance SFT, and vice versa.
- **Core assumption:** The reference policy π_ref used for KL regularization remains close to the training policy, ensuring the importance-weight ratio stays bounded.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 4.1 derives the unified gradient formulation and states SFT is a special case of RL; "On the Non-decoupling of Supervised Fine-tuning and Reinforcement Learning in Post-training" discusses this theoretical linkage.
- **Break condition:** When the policy drifts far from π_ref, the importance weights can become extreme, causing high variance or instability in gradient estimates.

### Mechanism 3: Scalability via Open-Weight Models
- **Claim:** The rapid shift toward hybrid post-training is driven by increased adoption of open-weight models and scalable, model-generated rollouts, which reduce the cost of RL exploration.
- **Mechanism:** Open-weight models enable cheap, on-demand generation of rollouts for RL without relying on expensive API calls. Hybrid pipelines can then use these rollouts alongside curated SFT data, creating a cost-effective loop where SFT provides a stable foundation and RL leverages abundant self-generated data for refinement.
- **Core assumption:** Open-weight models generate rollouts of sufficient quality to provide meaningful learning signals for RL; the computational cost of generating rollouts is lower than collecting human demonstrations.
- **Evidence anchors:** Figure 2 and Section B.4 show the correlation between rising open-weight model usage and hybrid method adoption; papers like "GRPO and Reflection Reward for Mathematical Reasoning" use open models for rollout generation.
- **Break condition:** If open-weight models cannot produce high-diversity or high-accuracy rollouts, the RL component may fail to improve upon SFT.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)**
  - **Why needed:** SFT is the baseline for imitation learning; understanding its objective (cross-entropy minimization) and limitations (distribution shift, compounding errors) explains why RL is introduced.
  - **Quick check:** What loss function does SFT optimize, and what is a key failure mode when the test distribution shifts?
- **Concept: Reinforcement Learning (RL) for LLMs**
  - **Why needed:** RL optimizes a reward signal rather than imitating static data, enabling exploration and correction of SFT's errors. Core to hybrid methods.
  - **Quick check:** How does an RL update differ from SFT in terms of data source and gradient formulation?
- **Concept: KL Divergence Regularization**
  - **Why needed:** The unified objective includes a KL term β KL(π_θ || π_ref) to prevent excessive deviation from a reference policy, which stabilizes both SFT and RL training and is critical in hybrid pipelines.
  - **Quick check:** Why is a reference policy (π_ref) used, and what happens if β is set too high or too low?
- **Concept: Policy Gradient Methods (e.g., PPO, GRPO)**
  - **Why needed:** These are the dominant algorithms for RL fine-tuning; understanding their update rule (expected reward gradient via log-probability) is necessary to grasp how RL optimizes language models.
  - **Quick check:** What does the REINFORCE gradient estimate, and how does GRPO simplify it?
- **Concept: Reward Modeling**
  - **Why needed:** In RLHF, a reward model provides scalar feedback; hybrid methods often reuse this reward or design proxies. This bridges human preferences and optimization.
  - **Quick check:** How is a reward model typically trained, and what are its limitations?
- **Concept: Behavior Cloning and Distribution Shift**
  - **Why needed:** SFT is a form of behavior cloning; recognizing its tendency to fail under distribution shift motivates the exploration component of RL.
  - **Quick check:** What is compounding error in behavior cloning, and how might RL mitigate it?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Pre-trained Model -> SFT Module (uses expert dataset D) -> Hybrid Loss Mixer (combines L_SFT and L_RL) -> Updated Policy π_θ; Reference Policy (π_ref) provides KL regularization; RL Module generates rollouts y ~ π_θ(·|x) and uses Reward Model (optional) for r(x,y).
- **Critical path:** Pre-trained model → (optional initial SFT) → Hybrid training loop: sample batch (mix of offline SFT data and online rollouts) → compute combined loss → update π_θ → periodically evaluate → repeat until convergence.
- **Design tradeoffs:** SFT-only is fast/stable but overfits; RL-only explores better but is sample-inefficient and unstable; hybrid balances both but adds hyperparameter complexity (loss weights, scheduling) and higher compute (needs both offline data and rollout generation).
- **Failure signatures:** Performance collapse on OOD tasks (overfitting from SFT); reward hacking (verbosity over correctness); entropy collapse (policy becomes deterministic); KL divergence explosion (policy drifts from π_ref).
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Baseline comparison:** On a small QA dataset (e.g., HotpotQA), train three models: SFT-only, RL-only (e.g., GRPO with a simple correctness reward), and hybrid (sequential SFT then RL). Compare accuracy on in-distribution and held-out question types.
  2. **Ablate KL weight (β):** In the hybrid objective, vary β ∈ {0.0, 0.1, 0.5, 1.0} and measure (a) stability (gradient norm, KL divergence trend), (b) final performance on a validation set. Expected: β=0 may lead to collapse; too high β stifles RL learning.
  3. **Vary expert data quality:** For hybrid, use high-quality SFT data (curated demonstrations) vs. low-quality SFT data (synthetic or noisy). Hypothesis: poor SFT data harms RL performance because the initial policy is misaligned. Measure final reward and accuracy.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** What fundamental algorithmic advances can enable hybrid SFT-RL training to be orders of magnitude more compute- and data-efficient?
  - **Basis:** Future Directions cites "sample- and compute-efficient methodologies" as a central open challenge.
  - **Why unresolved:** Current methods provide marginal gains; theoretical limits and general principles remain unknown.
  - **Evidence:** Sample complexity analyses for hybrid objectives; systematic efficiency-performance trade-off benchmarks across model scales and domains.
- **Open Question 2:** How can RL for LLMs be reliably applied under sparse, inconsistent, or indirectly specified rewards (e.g., user feedback, self-evaluation)?
  - **Basis:** Future Directions explicitly identifies "sparse or indirect reward signals" as an open problem, particularly in safety-critical settings.
  - **Why unresolved:** Standard RL assumes dense, accurate rewards; leveraging implicit supervision is largely unexplored and lacks robustness guarantees.
  - **Evidence:** Empirical studies demonstrating stable learning curves under varying feedback sparsity; algorithms with provable convergence for weak reward models.
- **Open Question 3:** Does the optimal integration strategy for SFT and RL (e.g., sequencing, weighting, interleaving) systematically vary with task characteristics (e.g., reasoning depth, horizon), and what are the transferable principles?
  - **Basis:** The paper reports hybrid methods dominate overall, but Section 5 treats each domain with distinct challenges and ad-hoc combinations, without comparative analysis of integration efficacy.
  - **Why unresolved:** Most works propose heuristic hybrids; no controlled cross-domain study isolates task factors to derive general guidelines.
  - **Evidence:** Ablations holding model/data constant, measuring performance sensitivity to SFT-RL mixing ratios across diverse tasks; theoretical model linking task properties to optimal balance.
- **Open Question 4:** What are the long-term consequences of the shift from API-based to open-weight model-generated data on dataset quality, model diversity, and legal/ethical compliance in post-training?
  - **Basis:** Trend analysis documents a sharp decline in API reliance and rise in open-weight data, but the paper does not assess risks like homogenization or licensing issues.
  - **Why unresolved:** The trend is observational; causal impact on model robustness, bias, and compliance remains unmeasured.
  - **Evidence:** Longitudinal comparisons of models trained on API vs. open-weight generated data across diversity metrics, error taxonomies, and license audits; analysis of feedback loops in synthetic data ecosystems.

## Limitations
- Classification methodology (keyword counts with arbitrary thresholds, LLM prompt) may misattribute domains if benchmarks are cited but not used, and may inconsistently label hybrid methods (e.g., DPO vs. PPO).
- The analysis is limited to arXiv publications, omitting industry reports and non-academic work; trends are based on publication counts, not quality-adjusted or performance-weighted metrics.
- No controlled experimental validation; conclusions are drawn from survey data without standardized cross-paper performance aggregation.

## Confidence
- **Hybrid superiority** (hybrid methods outperform standalone): **Medium**. Evidence is a consistent but non-quantitative survey; lack of standardized benchmarks prevents aggregation.
- **Rapid shift toward hybrid** (20% → 73.8%): **Medium**. The trend appears real but classification noise could inflate numbers.
- **SFT as special case of RL** (theoretical unification): **High**. The gradient equivalence holds under the indicator-function reward assumption.
- **Open-weight adoption drives hybrid shift**: **Low**. Only a correlational analysis; no causal dissection.
