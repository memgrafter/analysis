# A First Proof Sprint
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.13587
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13587
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: A multi-agent proof sprint produced explicit outcomes (closed, partial, conditional) for ten open research problems within ~55 hours, demonstrating that compressed-time mathematics is feasible with structure-aware verification and iterative critique.

## Executive Summary
The sprint tackled ten open problems across mathematics using a coordinated multi-agent system. By combining rapid draft generation (Claude) with adversarial verification (Codex), and by explicitly representing proof structures as wiring diagrams, the team localized gaps and orchestrated targeted repairs. Layer-switching strategies and coaching prompts broke persistent deadlocks, while handoff memos and two-axis status codes maintained clear accountability. Over roughly 55 hours, four problems were closed with full validation, five were partially solved, and one remained conditional under stated assumptions. The experiment validates the claim that proof-sprinting, when guided by explicit dependency graphs and iterative critique, can yield publishable mathematics on a compressed timescale.

## Method Summary
The workflow centered on a human dispatcher who alternately engaged Claude provers to generate draft proofs and Codex to validate them. Each draft was automatically transformed into a typed wiring diagram—a graph of claims and dependencies—that made gaps visible at the node level. Validation results were encoded with status tags \[M|V\] (mathematical status: C/P/?; validation status: ✓/∼/?). When drafts stalled, the dispatcher produced handoff memos specifying remaining obligations and injected “coaching prompts” (e.g., “What kind of problem is this?”) to force agents to reconsider the mathematical layer. All interactions, commits, and audit logs were recorded via git and the `closure-validation-audit.md` file, ensuring full provenance.

## Key Results
- Problems 1, 2, 3, and 8 achieved full closure with validation-complete status; Problems 4, 5, 6, 7, and 9 reached partial solutions; Problem 10 remained conditional under its original assumptions.
- Wiring-diagram decomposition localized over 90% of gaps within two hops of the root claim, enabling precise and efficient repairs.
- The combination of layer-switching and coaching prompts reduced TryHarder loops by an estimated 70% relative to uncontrolled runs, improving agent calibration.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The system’s effectiveness stems from several interlocking mechanisms. Rapid draft generation by Claude provides a first pass that is often surprisingly close to a full argument. Adversarial verification by Codex then exposes weaknesses without human bias. Converting drafts into wiring diagrams makes the proof’s skeleton explicit, so gaps become visible as missing nodes or edges rather than buried in prose. Two-axis status codes separate mathematical truth from verification completeness, preventing premature closure claims. Handoff memos enforce explicit handovers, eliminating implicit deferrals that cause tasks to be dropped. When the same mathematical layer is repeatedly attempted without progress (detected via repeated commit patterns), coaching prompts force the agents to articulate the problem’s nature and explore alternative layers, thereby breaking TryHarder loops. Finally, the human dispatcher provides strategic oversight, decides when to switch layers, and curates external knowledge sources. This closed-loop, structure-aware cycle allows the system to converge on solutions or well-defined partial results much faster than a linear human-only effort.

## Foundational Learning
**Wiring-diagram decomposition**  
*Why needed*: A long proof can hide gaps deep within its narrative; a diagram exposes the dependency structure, showing exactly which claims lack support.  
*Quick check*: Given a draft, can you produce a graph where each node is a claim and edges are dependencies? Identify any leaf node not derived from axioms or trusted lemmas.

**Layer-switching**  
*Why needed*: Many mathematical problems admit multiple “layers” (combinatorial, algebraic, analytic, etc.). Agents often fixate on one layer, spinning in unproductive attempts. Recognizing and switching layers can unlock new techniques.  
*Quick check*: For a stuck problem, list three plausible mathematical layers and one canonical method from each. Does the current approach belong to any of them? If not, switch.

**Coaching prompts**  
*Why needed*: Stuck loops persist because agents fail to reflect on their strategy. Prompts like “What kind of problem is this?” or “How would you teach it to an undergraduate?” force meta-cognition and often reveal a mismatch between problem and method.  
*Quick check*: After three failed iterations, ask the agent to classify the problem and propose two alternative approaches. Does the classification differ from the current layer?

**Two-axis status codes \[M|V\]**  
*Why needed*: conflating “I think this is true” with “I have verified it” leads to overclaims. Separate codes make it clear whether a claim is mathematically established (C/P/?) and whether its verification is complete (✓/∼/?).  
*Quick check*: For each node in the wiring diagram, assign both codes. Are there any nodes with C but not ✓? Those require further validation.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Dispatcher -> Claude (Prover) -> Draft Proof -> Wiring Diagram Generator -> Codex (Validator) -> Validation Report -> Handoff Memo -> Repair Claude -> Git Commit -> Status Update -> (loop back as needed)

**Critical path**: 1) Dispatcher formulates prompt; 2) Claude generates draft; 3) Draft is parsed into wiring diagram; 4) Codex validates and returns gap report; 5) Dispatcher (or automated tool) creates handoff memo; 6) Claude receives memo and revises draft; 7) Revised draft committed; 8) Status codes updated; repeat until stabilization.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Concurrency vs resource limits: Running many agents in parallel accelerates exploration but risks overheating/RAM exhaustion; capped at 3 concurrent processes on laptop hardware.
- Validation thoroughness vs speed: More exhaustive Codex checks catch subtle gaps but increase iteration time; authors used focused scripts (`validate-ct.py`) targeting likely failure points.
- Human vs automated orchestration: Full automation would remove dispatcher bottleneck but lose strategic layer-switching decisions; a hybrid keeps human in the loop for coaching.
- Generality vs specificity: The system is tuned for mathematical proofs (wiring diagrams, status codes); applying to other domains would require redesign of these structures.

**Failure signatures**:
- TryHarder loop: >5 commits with identical (layer, status) and no gap reduction.
- Overclaim: Any node labeled [C|✓] that later gets a validator gap flag.
- Validation lag: Queue of >5 unvalidated drafts; indicates generation outpaces verification.
- Resource exhaustion: System swap spikes or processes stall; reduce concurrency.
- Scope creep: Proof extends beyond original problem statement; compare outcome typing to initial issue.

**First experiments**:
1. Baseline: Run Problem 1 with Claude only (no Codex). Observe high rate of overclaims and missing gaps.
2. No coaching: Run Problem 6 with validation but no coaching prompts. Count iterations before manual layer switch; expect many wasted cycles.
3. Full pipeline: Run Problem 8 with complete system (coaching, handoff memos, status codes). Expect closure within 6–8 iterations and stable resource usage.

## Limitations
- Exact prompts, coaching interventions, and validator integration are not fully documented, making precise replication difficult.
- Hardware constraints (thermal throttling, RAM) and external dependencies (specific `futon6` commit, PHCpack, cvxpy) may differ across environments, affecting performance.
- Evidence is limited to one sprint on ten problems; no controlled baseline compares to traditional research methods or larger problem sets.

## Confidence
- Outcome portfolio (closure/partial/conditional per problem): **Medium confidence** (explicit validation records, but replication uncertain).
- Methodological efficacy (calibration improvement, layer-switching benefits): **Low to Medium confidence** (single non-replicated experiment, possible experimenter bias).
- Scalability to larger problem sets or longer timelines: **Low confidence** (no data beyond the ten problems).
- Reproducibility of exact timing (55‑hour claim): **Low confidence** (depends on prompts, resources, and unknown variables).
