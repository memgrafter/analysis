# Analyzing and Improving Cross-lingual Knowledge Transfer for Machine Translation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.04036
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04036
- **Reference count**: 0
- **Primary result**: The thesis introduces four techniques that significantly improve cross-lingual transfer in machine translation, achieving up to +3.6 BLEU for low-resource languages and preserving key capabilities in large language models.

## Executive Summary
This thesis addresses the challenge of extending machine translation to thousands of languages with limited parallel data by improving cross-lingual knowledge transfer. It presents four main contributions: (1) a representational metric, Representational Transfer Potential (RTP), that strongly predicts translation improvements, and an auxiliary similarity loss applied on multi-parallel data that increases representational invariance and yields up to +1.8 BLEU for low-resource languages; (2) Multilingual k-Nearest-Neighbor Machine Translation, where cross-lingual and multilingual datastores boost low-resource directions by up to +3.6 BLEU while language-group-specific datastores achieve comparable performance at 4× smaller size and 5.3× faster inference; (3) a study showing that fine-tuning large language models on parallel data degrades capabilities like formality steering and document-level context handling, and a mixed-data fine-tuning strategy that preserves these capabilities while improving translation quality; (4) evidence that scaling language diversity during fine-tuning (across 132–272 directions) consistently enhances performance for both seen and unseen language pairs, nearly eliminates off-target generations, and induces greater representational overlap in middle layers, explaining the transfer gains.

## Method Summary
The thesis proposes a series of methods to enhance cross-lingual knowledge transfer. First, it introduces Representational Transfer Potential (RTP), a metric that measures the similarity of decoder context vectors across languages, and an auxiliary cosine similarity loss applied on multi-parallel batches to align these representations. Second, it develops Multilingual k-Nearest-Neighbor MT, which augments a base model with retrieval from datastores built from a teacher model’s representations; cross-lingual and multilingual datastores provide richer context for low-resource languages, while language-group-specific datastores improve efficiency. Third, it investigates fine-tuning of large language models (LLaMA, Falcon) on parallel data, revealing degradation in formality control, idiom translation, and document context processing; blending parallel and monolingual examples (mixed-data fine-tuning) mitigates these issues. Finally, it demonstrates that increasing the number of language directions during fine-tuning (from 10 up to 272) improves translation for both seen and unseen pairs, reduces off-target errors, and increases representational overlap, with benefits plateauing beyond a certain scale.

## Key Results
- Representational Transfer Potential (RTP) correlates strongly with BLEU improvements (ρ=0.77), and the auxiliary similarity loss yields up to +1.8 BLEU for low-resource languages.
- Multilingual kNN-MT with cross-lingual datastores improves low-resource translation by up to +3.6 BLEU; language-group-specific datastores achieve similar performance at 4× smaller size and 5.3× faster inference.
- Mixed-data fine-tuning preserves formality steerability, idiom translation, and document-level context handling in LLMs while improving general quality; scaling fine‑training to 132–272 directions boosts both seen and unseen translation and nearly eliminates off‑target generations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The gains arise from three core mechanisms. (1) The auxiliary similarity loss increases representational invariance across languages, reducing the learning burden for low-resource languages and making the shared space more amenable to transfer. (2) kNN-MT leverages a large, multilingual memory of retrieved examples, providing direct lexical and phrasal evidence that bypasses limitations of the parametric model, especially effective when the datastore spans related languages. (3) Mixed-data fine-tuning maintains the distributional characteristics of the pretrained LLM, preventing catastrophic forgetting of general-purpose abilities (e.g., following style instructions, using context) while still adapting to translation. Scaling language diversity further encourages the model to develop language-agnostic representations, as evidenced by increased middle-layer overlap, which improves generalization to unseen pairs and suppresses off-target generation.

## Foundational Learning
- **Representational Transfer Potential (RTP)**: Metric quantifying similarity of language-conditioned representations (e.g., decoder context vectors). *Why needed*: To measure and optimize for cross-lingual transferability. *Quick check*: On multi‑parallel data, compute average cosine similarity of mean‑subtracted context vectors across source languages.
- **k-Nearest Neighbor Machine Translation (kNN-MT)**: Inference‑time augmentation using a precomputed datastore of source‑target pairs. *Why needed*: To exploit large parallel corpora without retraining and improve low‑resource translation via cross‑lingual retrieval. *Quick check*: Build a FAISS index over teacher model hidden states; retrieve neighbors for test source and interpolate probabilities.
- **Mixed-data Fine‑tuning**: Fine‑tuning a pretrained LLM on a mixture of parallel and monolingual examples. *Why needed*: To preserve pretrained capabilities (e.g., formality steering, document context) that degrade under parallel‑only fine‑tuning. *Quick check*: Compare formality accuracy and idiom scores between parallel‑only and mixed‑fine‑tuned models.
- **Language Diversity Scaling**: Increasing the number of language pairs in the fine‑tuning set. *Why needed*: To promote shared cross‑lingual representations and reduce off‑target generation. *Quick check*: Fine‑tune on varying numbers of directions (e.g., 30, 132, 272); measure unseen‑pair performance and off‑target rate.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map** (plain text):
Input sentences → Joint BPE (32K) → Many-to-many Transformer (shared encoder/decoder, language tags) → Training: cross‑entropy + [optional: auxiliary similarity loss on multi‑parallel batches] → Inference: standard decoding OR kNN‑MT (retrieve from FAISS datastore built from teacher model) → Translation output.  
Alternative path for LLMs: Pretrained decoder‑only LLM → Fine‑tuning (AdamW) on mixed parallel/monolingual data → Output.

**Critical path**: (1) Preprocess parallel/multi‑parallel corpora; (2) Train base model or load pretrained LLM; (3) If using kNN, construct datastores from teacher representations; (4) Tune hyperparameters (λ, T, k, data mix ratio) on dev set; (5) Evaluate on test sets (BLEU, COMET, capability metrics).

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Auxiliary loss λ: Higher values increase invariance but risk over‑flattening and harming high‑resource performance.
- Datastore type: Bilingual datastores are small but limited; cross‑lingual/multilingual offer broader coverage at the cost of index size and retrieval latency; language‑group‑specific datastores balance both.
- Mixed‑data ratio: More monolingual data helps preserve capabilities but too much dilutes parallel signal; optimal around 1:1.
- Number of training directions: More languages improve generalization but incurring diminishing returns and higher compute; an optimal range exists.

**Failure signatures**:
- Over‑regularization: Average cosine similarity → 1, BLEU drops on high‑resource languages.
- Retrieval failures: For typologically distant languages, retrieved neighbors are irrelevant → BLEU decrease; monitor retrieval precision.
- Mixed‑data imbalance: Loss curves for parallel vs. monolingual objectives diverge, translation quality degrades.
- Diversity plateau: Per‑language BLEU stops improving or declines when adding directions beyond a threshold.

**First experiments** (exactly 3):
1. **RTP correlation**: Train baseline many‑to‑many model without auxiliary loss; compute RTP on dev and correlate with per‑language BLEU on FLORES; expect ρ≈0.77.
2. **Auxiliary loss impact**: Train with multi‑parallel auxiliary loss (λ=1); compare RTP and low‑resource BLEU to baseline; expect increased invariance and up to +1.8 BLEU.
3. **kNN‑MT efficiency**: Build bilingual, cross‑lingual, and multilingual datastores from M2M100; apply to low‑resource pairs; measure BLEU gain, datastore size, and inference time; expect +3.6 BLEU and 4× size reduction with group‑specific datastores.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How does the amount of multi-parallel data and its linguistic diversity (e.g., across different scripts and families) quantitatively influence cross-lingual transfer in low-resource settings beyond pairwise relatedness, and can the Representational Transfer Potential (RTP) metric be reliably generalized to predict transfer for non-English-centric multilingual models or specific tasks like translation direction?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] Chapter 3 identifies multi‑parallel overlap as the most predictive dataset feature for representational similarity but experiments are limited to English‑centric directions and a fixed number of languages (52). The paper notes RTP was developed for English‑targets and many‑to‑one models, leaving its applicability to other setups unclear.
- **Why unresolved**: The study did not systematically vary multi‑parallel data size/coverage beyond existing datasets, nor test RTP’s predictive power for different target languages or non‑English‑centric systems. The interaction between multi‑parallel overlap and typological diversity (e.g., scripts, syntax) remains unexplored.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Controlled experiments with synthetically created multi‑parallel corpora of varying sizes across diverse language families/scripts, measuring RTP and translation gains for both English and non‑English targets in multilingual NMT models. Evaluating RTP’s correlation with performance in tasks beyond translation (e.g., cross‑lingual transfer in multilingual LLMs) would test its generality.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: To what extent can cross-lingual and multilingual k‑NN datastores benefit translation directions involving languages from typologically distant families or with dissimilar writing systems, and what architectural adaptations or alignment techniques are necessary to make cross-lingual retrieval effective in such cases?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] Chapter 4 shows cross‑lingual datastores improve low‑resource translation for linguistically similar languages (e.g., Slavic group), but performance degrades for distant languages (e.g., Georgian). The paper hints that linguistic similarity drives effective retrieval but does not test beyond a limited set of language groupings or script variations.
- **Why unresolved**: The experiments focused on a single mNMT model (M2M100) and specific language groups, leaving open whether results hold for languages with no shared script (e.g., Arabic‑Japanese) or for non‑English‑centric setups. The role of explicit representation alignment (e.g., linear mapping) across greater linguistic distances is not fully explored.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Building cross‑lingual/multilingual datastores for languages spanning diverse scripts (Latin, Cyrillic, Arabic, Chinese, etc.) and families, measuring retrieval effectiveness and translation gains. Testing alignment techniques (e.g., adversarial training, supervised projections) to bridge distant language spaces and evaluating their impact on low‑resource translation would clarify boundaries of cross‑lingual kNN‑MT.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: What underlying mechanisms in LLMs cause certain capabilities (e.g., formality steering, document-level translation) to degrade during fine‑tuning on parallel data, and why does mixed‑data fine‑tuning mitigate these effects—is it through preventing overfitting, maintaining pre‑training distribution memorization, or altering internal representations in specific layers?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] Chapter 5 documents capability degradation in fine‑tuned LLMs and shows mixed‑data fine‑tuning preserves abilities while improving translation quality. However, the paper explicitly states this trade‑off’s mechanistic understanding remains an open question.
- **Why unresolved**: The analysis relies on evaluation metrics (e.g., formality accuracy, COMET) without probing internal model states. The paper does not compare model activations or attention patterns between parallel‑only and mixed fine‑tuning to isolate why formality/context handling is preserved. It also remains unclear whether the mixed‑data effect generalizes to all LLM architectures (beyond LLaMA/Falcon) and all capability types.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Analyzing layer‑wise activations or representation similarity (e.g., using SVCCA) between base models, parallel‑fine‑tuned, and mixed‑fine‑tuned checkpoints during qualitative tasks (formality steering, document translation). Ablations varying monolingual data domains/quality and probing gradient dynamics during fine‑tuning could identify whether preservation stems from distributional matching or regularization effects.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: What is the optimal level of language diversity during fine‑tuning that maximizes cross‑lingual transfer for unseen languages without incurring diminishing returns for seen languages, and does this optimal point depend on model scale, data quality, or task (e.g., translation vs. other multilingual tasks)?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] Chapter 6 shows scaling language diversity to 132 directions improves both seen and unseen directions but benefits plateau or slightly decrease when expanding to 272 directions. The paper notes this threshold effects and suggests the existence of an optimal diversity level.
- **Why unresolved**: Experiments only covered 132 vs. 272 directions with a single model family (Tower/LLaMA) and dataset (NTREX). The interaction between diversity, model size (7B/13B only), and fine‑tuning data quality (high‑quality multi‑parallel vs. filtered web data) is not thoroughly investigated. It is also unclear whether the plateau is inherent or due to suboptimal data mixing strategies.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Systematic scaling experiments with more granular steps (e.g., 30, 60, 90, 120, 150, 200 directions) across diverse model architectures (e.g., encoder‑decoder vs. decoder‑only), data sources, and quality filters. Measuring performance curves for seen vs. unseen directions would locate the sweet spot and test if it shifts with model scale or data characteristics. Analysis of representational overlap across layers for varying diversity levels could explain the plateau.

## Limitations
- **Reproduction gaps**: Exact language lists, data splits, and full hyperparameter schedules (e.g., convergence criteria, LR schedule) are not provided, hindering exact replication.
- **Generalization scope**: Results are validated only on specific datasets (TED, FLORES, NTREX) and model families (small Transformers, M2M100, LLaMA/Falcon, Tower); performance on other architectures or domains remains unknown.
- **Causal inference**: While correlations (e.g., RTP ρ=0.77) are strong, the causal role of representational overlap in driving gains versus being a correlative side‑effect requires further ablation.

## Confidence
- **High**: Auxiliary similarity loss improves low‑resource BLEU (+1.8) and increases representational invariance; Multilingual kNN‑MT shows large gains (+3.6 BLEU) and efficiency benefits.
- **Medium**: Scaling language diversity (132‑272 directions) boosts performance and reduces off‑target on evaluated models; Mixed‑data fine‑tuning preserves capabilities for tested LLMs and parallel data sources.
- **Low**: Universality of RTP as a predictor across all model families and languages; Long‑term preservation of steerability and context handling under distribution shift.
