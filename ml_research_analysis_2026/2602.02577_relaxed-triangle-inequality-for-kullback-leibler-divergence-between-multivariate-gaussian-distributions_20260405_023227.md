# Relaxed Triangle Inequality for Kullback-Leibler Divergence Between Multivariate Gaussian Distributions

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2602.02577
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02577
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: The tight supremum of KL(N₁∥N₃) given fixed KL(N₁∥N₂)=Δ₁ and KL(N₂∥N₃)=Δ₂ is ½[w₂(2Δ₁)−1][w₂(2Δ₂)−1] + Δ₁ + Δ₂, with asymptotic Δ₁+Δ₂+2√(Δ₁Δ₂)+o(Δ₁)+o(Δ₂) for small divergences.

## Executive Summary
This paper derives the exact maximum possible Kullback‑Leibler (KL) divergence between two multivariate Gaussian distributions N₁ and N₃ when the divergences to an intermediate Gaussian N₂ are fixed at Δ₁ and Δ₂. The authors decompose the original optimization into separate mean and covariance subproblems, solve each using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and properties of the Lambert W function, and then combine the results. The resulting bound tightens previous triangle‑type inequalities and offers sharper guarantees for applications such as out‑of‑distribution detection and safe reinforcement learning. The supremum is attained under specific eigenvalue configurations and equal means across the three Gaussians.

## Method Summary
The method begins by introducing auxiliary variables \(x = -\log|\Sigma_1| + \operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma_1) - n\) and \(y = -\log|\Sigma_2^{-1}| + \operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma_2^{-1}) - n\). For each fixed \((x,y)\), the constraints and the objective split into two subproblems: \(P_\mu\) (optimizing the means and \(\Sigma_2\)) and \(P_\Sigma\) (optimizing \(\Sigma_1\) and \(\Sigma_2\)), which are coupled only through \(\Sigma_2\). In \(P_\mu\), the authors expand the mean vector in the eigenbasis of \(\Sigma_2^{-1}\) and apply Cauchy–Schwarz to bound a quadratic form, showing the maximum occurs when the mean difference aligns with the top eigenvector of \(\Sigma_2^{-1}\). This yields a function \(G(x,y;\Delta_1,\Delta_2)\) whose maximal eigenvalue \(\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_2^{-1})\) is given by the Lambert W function \(w_2(y)\). In \(P_\Sigma\), a matrix–analytic bound \(F(x,y)\) is derived, tight when \(\Sigma_1\) and \(\Sigma_2^{-1}\) share eigenvectors with eigenvalues \(w_2(x)\) and \(w_2(y)\), respectively. The combined objective \(H(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}(F+G)\) is then maximized over the compact domain \([0,2\Delta_1]\times[0,2\Delta_2]\). The authors prove \(H\) has no interior critical point and increases monotonically toward the corner \((2\Delta_1,2\Delta_2)\), which therefore yields the global supremum. Substituting these values produces the closed‑form expression. Finally, the attainment conditions and a small‑Δ asymptotic are obtained by expanding the Lambert W function.

## Key Results
- **Exact supremum**: ½[w₂(2Δ₁)−1][w₂(2Δ₂)−1] + Δ₁ + Δ₂, where \(w_2(t) = -W_{-1}(-e^{-(1+t)})\) is the –1 branch of the Lambert W function.
- **Asymptotic for small divergences**: For Δ₁,Δ₂ → 0, the supremum simplifies to Δ₁ + Δ₂ + 2√(Δ₁Δ₂) + o(Δ₁) + o(Δ₂).
- **Attainment conditions**: The supremum is attained if and only if μ₁=μ₂=μ₃ and the covariance matrices share a common eigenbasis, featuring a single non‑unit eigenvalue \(w_2(2\Delta_1)\) for \(\Sigma_1\) and \(w_2(2\Delta_2)\) for \(\Sigma_2^{-1}\) along the same direction.
- **Tightness**: This result improves upon earlier triangle‑type bounds for Gaussian KL divergence, enabling stronger theoretical guarantees in applications such as out‑of‑distribution detection and safe reinforcement learning.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Decomposition via auxiliary variables
The optimization over Gaussian parameters is transformed by introducing auxiliary variables \(x\) and \(y\) that capture specific combinations of determinant and trace for \(\Sigma_1\) and \(\Sigma_2^{-1}\). For each fixed pair \((x,y)\), the KL objective and the two constraints split into two subproblems: \(P_\mu\) (involving the means and \(\Sigma_2\)) and \(P_\Sigma\) (involving \(\Sigma_1\) and \(\Sigma_2\)), which are coupled only through \(\Sigma_2\). This decoupling isolates the mean and covariance contributions, making the problem tractable.

### Mechanism 2: Mean‑dependent term maximization
Expanding the mean difference in the eigenbasis of \(\Sigma_2^{-1}\) reduces the relevant quadratic term to \(\sum_i \lambda_i (y_i - x_i)^2\), where \(\sum_i x_i^2 = 2\Delta_1 - x\) and \(\sum_i \lambda_i y_i^2 = 2\Delta_2 - y\). By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, this sum is bounded by \(\bigl(\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}(2\Delta_1-x)} + \sqrt{2\Delta_2-y}\bigr)^2\). The maximum possible \(\lambda_{\max}\) under the constraint \(\sum_i(\lambda_i - \log\lambda_i)=n+y\) is given by the Lambert W function: \(\lambda_{\max}=w_2(y)\). This yields the supremum for the mean part, \(G(x,y;\Delta_1,\Delta_2)\).

### Mechanism 3: Covariance term bound and global maximization
The covariance‑only term \(-\log|\Sigma_2^{-1}\Sigma_1| + \operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma_2^{-1}\Sigma_1) - n\) is bounded by a function \(F(x,y)\) derived from matrix inequalities (Lemma B.1). Equality requires \(\Sigma_1\) and \(\Sigma_2^{-1}\) to be simultaneously diagonalizable with eigenvalues \(w_2(x)\) and \(w_2(y)\), respectively. Combining the mean and covariance parts gives \(H(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}(F+G)\). \(H\) is continuous on a compact domain, but first‑order conditions have no interior solution. A boundary analysis (Lemma C.1) shows \(H\) increases along all edges and attains its maximum only at \((x,y)=(2\Delta_1,2\Delta_2)\), yielding the global supremum.

## Foundational Learning
- **Kullback‑Leibler divergence (Gaussian closed form)**  
  *Why needed*: The entire analysis builds on the explicit formula separating mean and covariance effects.  
  *Quick check*: Write \(\operatorname{KL}(N(\mu_1,\Sigma_1)\,\|\,N(\mu_2,\Sigma_2))\) and identify the terms that involve only \(\mu\)’s and those that involve only \(\Sigma\)’s.

- **Lambert W function**  
  *Why needed*: The function \(w_2(t)=-W_{-1}(-e^{-(1+t)})\) solves \(x-\log x = 1+t\) and appears when maximizing eigenvalues under a trace‑like constraint.  
  *Quick check*: Define \(w_2(t)\) and explain why the problem \(\max\{x-\log x \mid x\ge 1\}\) with a linear constraint naturally leads to this branch.

- **Cauchy‑Schwarz inequality**  
  *Why needed*: Provides a tight bound on the mean quadratic form \(\sum_i \lambda_i (y_i-x_i)^2\) given constraints on \(\sum_i x_i^2\) and \(\sum_i \lambda_i y_i^2\).  
  *Quick check*: State the Cauchy‑Schwarz inequality for vectors and apply it to the described sum.

- **Simultaneous diagonalisation of matrices**  
  *Why needed*: Equality in the covariance bound requires \(\Sigma_1\) and \(\Sigma_2^{-1}\) to share eigenvectors, reducing matrix expressions to eigenvalue sums.  
  *Quick check*: If two symmetric matrices share eigenvectors, how do \(\operatorname{Tr}(AB)\) and \(\log|AB|\) relate to their eigenvalues?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Transformer -> Decomposer -> {MeanSolver, CovarianceSolver} -> Combiner -> Optimizer -> Verifier

**Critical path**: Transform → Decompose → (MeanSolver & CovarianceSolver) → Combiner → Optimizer → Verifier. Each step relies on the previous; the decomposition is key to tractability.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Decoupling simplifies the problem but requires proving compatibility of the equality conditions from the two subproblems.
- Lambert W yields a closed‑form solution but ties the result tightly to the Gaussian KL structure.
- Boundary optimisation avoids solving partial differential equations but depends on rigorously ruling out interior maxima.

**Failure signatures**:
- Incompatible equality conditions between the mean and covariance solvers.
- Discovery of an interior critical point for \(H(x,y)\) (contradicts Lemma C.2).
- Misapplication of Lemma B.3 leading to a non‑tight covariance bound.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Do other branches of the Lambert W function (e.g., \(w_1\)) yield different supremum values for \(\operatorname{KL}(N_1\|\!N_3)\) under the same fixed divergences, and under what conditions might they become relevant?
2. Can the derived relaxed triangle inequality and its tight supremum be generalized beyond multivariate Gaussians to other exponential family distributions (e.g., Gamma, Dirichlet) or mixture models?
3. What are the exact second‑order asymptotic terms (beyond \(o(\Delta_1)\) and \(o(\Delta_2)\)) in the small‑divergence supremum expression \(\Delta_1 + \Delta_2 + 2\sqrt{\Delta_1\Delta_2} + o(\Delta_1) + o(\Delta_2)\)?
4. In high dimensions (\(n\to\infty\)), does the attainment condition (\(\Sigma_1\) and \(\Sigma_3\) having exactly one eigenvalue differing from 1) remain necessary and sufficient for achieving the supremum, or do alternative eigenvalue configurations (e.g., multiple large eigenvalues) approach the supremum?

## Limitations
- The decomposition’s assumption that fixing \((x,y)\) fully decouples the mean and covariance parts may hide subtle dependencies that could affect the validity of the separation.
- The Lambert W eigenvalue bound (Lemma A.1) relies on selecting the –1 branch; for extreme parameter values this could become numerically unstable or theoretically questionable.
- The claim that \(H(x,y)\) attains its maximum only at the boundary point \((2\Delta_1,2\Delta_2)\) is delicate; an overlooked interior saddle point would invalidate the derived supremum.
