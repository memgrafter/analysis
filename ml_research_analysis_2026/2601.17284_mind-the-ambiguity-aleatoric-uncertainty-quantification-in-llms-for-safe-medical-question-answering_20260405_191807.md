# Mind the Ambiguity: Aleatoric Uncertainty Quantification in LLMs for Safe Medical Question Answering

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.17284
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17284
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: AU-Probe achieves AUROC of 0.9991 on CV-MedBench and improves medical QA accuracy by 9.48%

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces AU-Probe, a lightweight module for detecting ambiguous medical queries in large language models (LLMs) to improve safety in medical question answering. The method leverages the observation that aleatoric uncertainty (input ambiguity) is linearly separable in LLM hidden activations, enabling efficient uncertainty quantification without fine-tuning or multiple forward passes. Evaluated on a new benchmark CV-MedBench, AU-Probe demonstrates exceptional ambiguity detection performance and enables a "Clarify-Before-Answer" framework that significantly improves medical QA accuracy by proactively requesting clarification for ambiguous queries.

## Method Summary
AU-Probe is a linear classifier trained on residual-stream activations from the final prompt token of an LLM, mapping these to an aleatoric uncertainty (AU) score. The probe exploits the linear separability of clear vs. ambiguous medical queries in the hidden space, requiring only binary labels for training. In the "Clarify-Before-Answer" framework, AU-Probe computes an AU score for each incoming question; if the score exceeds a threshold, the system flags the input as ambiguous and requests user clarification before generating a response. This approach is model-agnostic, data-efficient, and achieves near-optimal performance with minimal training data.

## Key Results
- AU-Probe achieves an average AUROC of 0.9991 for ambiguity detection on CV-MedBench across four open LLMs
- The Clarify-Before-Answer framework improves medical QA accuracy by 9.48% over baselines
- AU-Probe demonstrates strong data efficiency, maintaining high performance with as little as 2% of training data
- The method is model-agnostic and can be integrated with any LLM without fine-tuning

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- Claim: AU-Probe can accurately detect input ambiguity by leveraging the linear separability of aleatoric uncertainty in LLM internal activations.
- Mechanism: The probe trains a linear classifier on the residual-stream activations of the final prompt token, mapping them to a scalar AU score. This exploits the observation that clear and ambiguous medical questions form linearly separable clusters in the hidden space.
- Core assumption: Aleatoric uncertainty is encoded along a dominant linear direction in the activation space, and this encoding is stable across layers and models.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Using this benchmark, we analyze AU from a representation engineering perspective, revealing that AU is linearly encoded in LLM’s internal activation patterns."
  - [section] "Our analysis reveals a striking pattern: activations from low-AU (clear) and high-AU (ambiguous) medical queries can be separated by a linear boundary."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence; related papers focus on general UQ methods but do not establish linear separability of aleatoric uncertainty in hidden states.
- Break condition: If the linear separability assumption fails (e.g., due to highly non-linear representations of ambiguity), the probe's accuracy would degrade.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The AU-guided "Clarify-Before-Answer" framework improves medical QA accuracy by proactively detecting ambiguous queries and requesting clarification before answer generation.
- Mechanism: AU-Probe computes an AU score for each incoming question. If the score exceeds a threshold, the system flags the input as ambiguous and requests user clarification. Once clarified, the LLM generates a response. This two-stage process prevents misinterpretation-induced errors.
- Core assumption: Early detection of input ambiguity allows for timely intervention, reducing the risk of unsafe or incorrect medical advice.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract] "Extensive experiments across four open LLMs demonstrate the effectiveness of our QA framework, with an average accuracy improvement of 9.48% over baselines."
  - [section] "Our framework uses the AU score as an early signal to proactively detect ambiguous questions and ensure necessary clarification is obtained before generating a final response."
  - [corpus] Moderate evidence; related work shows UQ methods degrade under ambiguity, supporting the need for explicit ambiguity detection.
- Break condition: If the AU threshold is poorly calibrated, the system may either miss ambiguous queries (false negatives) or over-request clarification (false positives), harming usability.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: AU-Probe achieves high data efficiency, requiring only a small fraction of labeled data to train a robust ambiguity detector.
- Mechanism: The probe uses binary labels (clear vs. ambiguous) instead of fine-grained AU scores, simplifying supervision and reducing label noise. It converges quickly, achieving near-optimal performance with as little as 5-10% of the training data.
- Core assumption: Binary supervision is sufficient to learn the underlying structure of aleatoric uncertainty, and the model's hidden representations generalize well across datasets.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section] "Even with as little as 2% of the data (64 samples), AU-Probe remains substantially stronger than the baseline methods in Table 1, indicating strong data efficiency and reliable performance in low-data settings."
  - [section] "We adopt binary labels (𝑢∈ {0, 1}) instead of manually assigned continuous AU scores for two reasons... Binary labels, conversely, provide a more stable training signal and substantially higher inter-annotator agreement in practice."
  - [corpus] Weak evidence; related papers do not discuss data efficiency of UQ methods in low-resource settings.
- Break condition: If the binary labeling scheme fails to capture the nuances of real-world ambiguity, the probe's discrimination may suffer in more complex or varied contexts.

## Architecture Onboarding

- Component map:
  Input medical question -> LLM hidden states -> AU-Probe -> AU score -> Threshold check -> (Clarification request if AU > τ) -> LLM answer generation -> Safe medical response

- Critical path:
  1. Input question → LLM hidden states
  2. Hidden states → AU-Probe → AU score
  3. AU score → Threshold check
  4. If AU > τ: request clarification; else: proceed to answer
  5. Answer generation (after clarification if needed)

- Design tradeoffs:
  - Accuracy vs. efficiency: Using a single forward pass keeps latency low but may miss subtle ambiguities.
  - Safety vs. usability: A lower threshold increases sensitivity but may annoy users with unnecessary clarification requests.
  - Binary vs. continuous labeling: Binary labels simplify training but may lose granularity in uncertainty estimation.

- Failure signatures:
  - False negatives: Ambiguous queries not flagged, leading to unsafe answers.
  - False positives: Clear queries flagged, reducing user experience.
  - Calibration drift: AU scores become unreliable over time or across datasets.

- First 3 experiments:
  1. Verify linear separability: Train a linear probe on CV-MedBench and measure AUROC across layers.
  2. Threshold tuning: Sweep τ values and measure ambiguity detection accuracy and QA improvement.
  3. Low-data regime: Train AU-Probe on 2%, 5%, 10%, and 30% of data and compare performance to full training.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- Question: How does AU-Probe's performance degrade when applied to out-of-domain medical specialties not represented in CV-MedBench?
- Basis in paper: Explicit - The paper mentions AU-Probe generalizes to MedExQA but notes this dataset focuses on underrepresented specialties like Biomedical Engineering and Occupational Therapy
- Why unresolved: The paper only tests OOD performance on one specific dataset (MedExQA) and doesn't explore how the probe handles completely novel medical domains
- What evidence would resolve it: Testing AU-Probe on medical QA datasets from entirely different specialties (e.g., dermatology, cardiology) that weren't represented in the training data

### Open Question 2
- Question: What is the optimal threshold for ambiguity detection across different medical QA systems and risk tolerance levels?
- Basis in paper: Explicit - The paper uses τ=0.5 as optimal but acknowledges this is tunable and explores different thresholds in Figure 11
- Why unresolved: The paper doesn't provide guidance on how to set thresholds for different clinical scenarios or risk preferences
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic analysis of false positive/negative trade-offs across different medical specialties and clinical risk levels

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can the linear encoding of aleatoric uncertainty observed in LLMs be leveraged for other uncertainty-related tasks beyond ambiguity detection?
- Basis in paper: Explicit - The paper discovers linear separability of clear vs ambiguous activations and notes this enables lightweight AU quantification
- Why unresolved: The paper only applies this insight to AU detection and doesn't explore whether the same linear structure could be used for epistemic uncertainty, hallucination detection, or other uncertainty types
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments testing whether the same linear probes can detect other forms of uncertainty or whether different linear directions exist for different uncertainty types

### Open Question 4
- Question: How would AU-Probe perform when integrated with retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems for medical QA?
- Basis in paper: Inferred - The paper focuses on standalone LLM performance but medical systems increasingly use RAG to access up-to-date clinical information
- Why unresolved: The paper doesn't explore how AU-Probe would interact with retrieved context or whether retrieval quality affects AU signal detection
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments comparing AU-Probe performance on RAG-enhanced vs standalone LLM systems for ambiguous medical queries

## Limitations
- Linear separability assumption: The core premise that aleatoric uncertainty is linearly separable in LLM hidden states is supported by internal CV-MedBench experiments but not yet validated across diverse medical QA datasets or domains.
- Threshold calibration dependency: The reported 9.48% accuracy improvement assumes an optimal AU threshold (τ=0.5) that was likely tuned on validation data and requires domain-specific calibration in real-world deployment.
- Generalizability to broader medical domains: While tested on four open LLMs, the framework's effectiveness across different medical specialties, language models, and ambiguity types remains uncertain.

## Confidence
- High confidence: The technical feasibility of AU-Probe as a lightweight uncertainty detector and the overall framework architecture are well-supported by the reported experimental results and established linear representation literature.
- Medium confidence: The claimed accuracy improvement from the Clarify-Before-Answer framework is credible given the strong ambiguity detection performance, but the practical impact depends on threshold calibration and user interaction design.
- Low confidence: The data efficiency claims are based on ablation studies on a single dataset with binary labels; generalization to continuous uncertainty estimation and more complex ambiguity scenarios requires further validation.

## Next Checks
1. Cross-domain validation: Test AU-Probe on medical QA datasets from different specialties (e.g., radiology reports, clinical notes, drug interactions) to assess generalizability of the linear separability assumption beyond CV-MedBench.

2. Threshold sensitivity analysis: Conduct a systematic evaluation of QA accuracy and user experience across a wide range of AU thresholds (τ ∈ [0.1, 0.9]) on real user query logs to identify optimal calibration strategies for deployment.

3. Continuous uncertainty estimation: Replace the binary classification objective with regression on continuous aleatoric uncertainty scores and evaluate whether this provides more nuanced detection and improved safety outcomes compared to the current binary approach.
