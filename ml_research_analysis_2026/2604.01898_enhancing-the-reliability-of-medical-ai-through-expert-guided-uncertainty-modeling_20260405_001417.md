# Enhancing the Reliability of Medical AI through Expert-guided Uncertainty Modeling

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.01898
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01898
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Expert-guided uncertainty modeling improves aleatoric uncertainty estimation quality by 9% to 50% (AAC metric) over state-of-the-art across four medical tasks.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses a critical limitation in medical AI: poor estimation of aleatoric uncertainty (AU), which represents irreducible data ambiguity. The authors propose leveraging disagreement among multiple human experts to generate soft labels that serve as targets for training a Confidence-Aware Ensemble (CAE). This CAE is used alongside a base ensemble to decompose total predictive variance into distinct epistemic (EU) and aleatoric (AU) components. Evaluated on four medical tasks—classification, segmentation, and question answering—the method significantly outperforms existing uncertainty estimation techniques, demonstrating that incorporating expert knowledge yields more reliable, risk-aware AI systems for healthcare.

## Method Summary
The approach involves two main training phases. First, a diverse base ensemble is trained on standard hard ground-truth labels to achieve high task accuracy and provide an estimate of epistemic uncertainty (EU) via the variance of its members' predictions. Second, a Confidence-Aware Ensemble (CAE) is created by initializing new models with the base ensemble's weights and fine-tuning them on soft labels. These soft labels are derived by aggregating the annotations of multiple human experts (e.g., averaging binary votes or probabilities), which empirically approximate the conditional label variance (aleatoric uncertainty). At inference, predictions from both ensembles are combined using the law of total variance: total uncertainty = EU (from base ensemble variance) + AU (from the mean per-model variance of the CAE). A lightweight variant trains a single ensemble with a mixed loss on both hard and soft labels.

## Key Results
- The proposed CAE-based total variance method reduces the Area Above the Rejection Curve (AAC) metric by 9% to 50% compared to state-of-the-art baselines across all four tasks, with up to an 83% improvement in segmentation.
- Incorporating expert disagreement signals during training (CAE) or inference (using raw expert votes) substantially improves the correlation between uncertainty scores and actual model error, as measured by AUROC for error detection.
- Even a computationally efficient single-ensemble model trained with a mixed loss (hard + soft labels) retains most of the performance gain, achieving ~37.7% improvement over a single-model baseline.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Expert disagreement provides a superior target for training models to estimate aleatoric uncertainty (AU) compared to using only hard binary labels.
**Mechanism:** The variance in multiple expert annotations for the same sample is treated as an empirical approximation of the conditional label variance `Var(y|x)` from the law of total variance. This variance directly captures irreducible data ambiguity and noise (aleatoric uncertainty). A model (Confidence-Aware Ensemble, CAE) is fine-tuned to predict these "soft" expert-aggregation targets, forcing it to internalize patterns of sample-level difficulty.
**Core assumption:** The aggregated expert responses (e.g., mean vote or distribution) are a valid, noise-robust proxy for the true underlying conditional probability `p(y|x)` that gives rise to aleatoric uncertainty.
**Evidence anchors:** 
- [abstract]: "leverages disagreement in expert responses to generate targets for training machine learning models."
- [section "Inconsistency in expert annotations as a measure of aleatoric uncertainty"]: "This inconsistency directly reflects the irreducible ambiguity in the labeling task – precisely what aleatoric uncertainty captures."
- [corpus]: Weak direct support; neighboring papers focus on LLM-based uncertainty or general surveys, not expert disagreement for AU modeling.
**Break condition:** If the expert pool exhibits systematic bias or consistently low inter-rater reliability (e.g., low Cohen's kappa), the soft label target may misrepresent true aleatoric uncertainty and degrade CAE performance.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Decomposing total predictive variance into epistemic (EU) and aleatoric (AU) components via separate model ensembles yields more accurate and clinically useful uncertainty estimates than computing variance from a single ensemble.
**Mechanism:** A base ensemble, trained on hard labels, estimates EU via the variance of its members' mean predictions (`Var_θ[E(y|x,θ)]`). A separate CAE, fine-tuned on soft expert labels, estimates AU via the mean of its members' per-model variances (`E_θ[Var(y|x,θ)]`). Summing these gives total variance. The separation ensures the AU estimator is not contaminated by model-specific epistemic variability.
**Core assumption:** The law of total variance `Var(y|x) = Var_θ[E(y|x,θ)] + E_θ[Var(y|x,θ)]` correctly maps to the EU/AU decomposition for classification tasks, and an ensemble trained for categorical accuracy is optimal for estimating EU.
**Evidence anchors:**
- [section "Variance-based approach to uncertainty estimation"]: Full derivation of equations (2) and (3) and their mapping to EU and AU.
- [section "Uncertainty estimation via a combination of ensembles"]: "To estimate the final uncertainty value per sample, we generate two sets of predictions... The first set... is used to evaluate epistemic uncertainty... and the second is substituted in (3) to evaluate the aleatoric uncertainty."
- [corpus]: Neighboring papers (e.g., "Deep Modeling of Non-Gaussian Aleatoric Uncertainty") discuss variance decomposition but do not combine it with expert-guided targets.
**Break condition:** If the base ensemble lacks sufficient diversity (e.g., all models converge to similar minima), its EU estimate becomes an underestimate. Similarly, if the CAE overfits to the soft labels, its AU estimate may reflect model bias rather than data ambiguity.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Integrating expert confidence signals during training (via CAE fine-tuning) or inference (using raw expert votes) significantly improves the quality of uncertainty estimates for risk-aware decision rejection, compared to standard ensemble variance.
**Mechanism:** Expert soft labels provide a richer training signal about sample difficulty than binary labels. The CAE learns to map input features to an uncertainty-aligned prediction variance. At inference, using CAE outputs for AU instead of the base ensemble's outputs yields uncertainty values that better correlate with error likelihood, as measured by Area Above the Curve (AAC) on rejection curves.
**Core assumption:** Uncertainty estimates that align with expert-perceived difficulty are more clinically valid for triaging cases (i.e., high expert disagreement ↔ high AU ↔ high model error probability).
**Evidence anchors:**
- [abstract]: "Results demonstrate that incorporating expert knowledge can enhance uncertainty estimation quality by 9% to 50%."
- [section "Results"]: Tables 1 & 2 show `Total variance, CAE` and `Total variance, experts` consistently achieve lower AAC (better) and higher AUROC than `Total variance, base` across all tasks.
- [section "One-ensemble mixed loss approach"]: Even a single ensemble trained with a mixed loss on hard and soft labels shows significant improvement over the base ensemble.
**Break condition:** If the expert annotation process itself is low-quality (e.g., few experts, noisy labels), the improvement may vanish. The paper notes the gap between model-based and expert-based uncertainty is larger for tasks with lower baseline accuracy (e.g., PubMedQA).

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Aleatoric vs. Epistemic Uncertainty**
  - *Why needed here:* The paper's core mechanism relies on disentangling these two sources. Understanding that AU is irreducible (data ambiguity) while EU is reducible (model knowledge) justifies why a separate CAE is needed for AU.
  - *Quick check question:* If you collect labels from three experts who all disagree on a specific medical image, is this primarily an aleatoric or epistemic uncertainty signal for a model? Why?
- **Concept: Law of Total Variance**
  - *Why needed here:* It provides the mathematical foundation for decomposing the total predictive variance into the two summands used in equations (2) and (3). Misunderstanding this leads to incorrect implementation.
  - *Quick check question:* In the equation `Var(y|x) = Var_θ[E(y|x,θ)] + E_θ[Var(y|x,θ)]`, which term corresponds to the variance between different model predictions, and which corresponds to the average per-model predictive confidence?
- **Concept: Ensemble-Based Uncertainty Estimation**
  - *Why needed here:* The paper's method is fundamentally an ensemble technique. The engineer must understand how diversity across models (different initializations, augmentations) is used to approximate a Bayesian posterior and compute variance terms.
  - *Quick check question:* Why might a deep ensemble with 10 identical models trained with the same random seed provide a misleadingly low uncertainty estimate?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Base Ensemble (trained on hard labels) -> EU estimation
  Soft Label Generator (expert aggregation) -> CAE training targets
  Confidence-Aware Ensemble (CAE, fine-tuned from base on soft labels) -> AU estimation
  Uncertainty Combiner (EU + AU) -> Final uncertainty score
- **Critical path:**
  1. Train a diverse base ensemble on task-specific hard labels until convergence.
  2. Generate soft labels for the training set using expert annotations or an LLM oracle.
  3. For each model in the base ensemble, initialize a CAE counterpart with identical weights.
  4. Fine-tune all CAEs on the soft labels (using MSE or cross-entropy) with a potentially lower learning rate.
  5. At inference, run both ensembles, compute EU from base ensemble variance (Eq. 2) and AU from CAE variance (Eq. 3), and sum for final uncertainty score.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Two-Ensemble (CAE) vs. Mixed-Loss Single Ensemble:** The two-ensemble method (`Total variance, CAE`) is theoretically purer (separate training objectives) and performs best (AAC 2.18e-3). The mixed-loss single ensemble is ~37.7% better than single-model baseline but worse than CAE (AAC 3.18e-3). It trades significant accuracy for major computational savings (1x training/inference cost vs. 2x).
  - **Expert Annotation Cost vs. Performance:** Using 3-4 top-performing experts (rather than all 6) for CAE training often yields near-peak performance (Fig. 4b). This reduces annotation burden but requires ranking expert accuracy a priori.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Poor Rejection Curve:** The rejection curve for `Total variance, CAE` lies close to or below the `Single model` baseline. This indicates the CAE failed to learn a meaningful AU signal from the soft labels.
  - **High CAE Training Loss, Low Base Loss:** If CAE loss on soft labels remains high while base ensemble loss on hard labels is low, the soft labels may be inconsistent or the CAE may be underfitting.
  - **AUROC Near 0.5:** For error detection (Table 2), an AUROC ~0.5 means uncertainty scores are no better than random at distinguishing errors. Check expert consensus (Cohen's kappa) and CAE ensemble diversity.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Baseline Replication:** Implement `Total variance, base` and compare its rejection curve/AAC to the `Single model` and `Prediction variance` baselines on a small dataset (e.g., a subset of BloodyWell). Verify the variance decomposition produces a meaningful curve.
  2. **CAE Sanity Check:** Train the CAE for a single task (e.g., binary classification) for a few epochs. Visualize the distribution of CAE's predicted per-sample variance. It should be positively correlated with the variance in expert votes for a held-out set.
  3. **Ablation: Expert Count:** On a dataset with many experts (BloodyWell), train CAEs using soft labels from the 1, 3, and 6 most accurate experts. Plot AAC vs. number of experts used (replicating Fig. 4b trend). Confirm that quality plateaus or slightly decreases beyond a small group of top experts.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** Can expert-informed uncertainty signals be integrated into single-model or low-parameter architectures to match the performance of ensemble-based methods while drastically reducing computational cost?
  - *Basis in paper:* [explicit] The authors state: "Despite various considered optimizations and sensitivity studies, the proposed approach remains computationally expensive as it involves training an entire ensemble of models... Single-model or low-parameter methods will always surpass ensemble solutions in terms of efficiency. Whether expert knowledge can be integrated into a more efficient paradigm remains an open question."
  - *Why unresolved:* The paper's core solution relies on two ensembles (base + CAE), which is computationally heavy for clinical deployment. A practical, efficient alternative is not provided or validated.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Development and rigorous evaluation of a non-ensemble model (e.g., a single network with a modified loss or architecture) that ingests expert soft labels and achieves uncertainty estimation quality (e.g., AAC) within a small margin (e.g., <5%) of the two-ensemble CAE method across multiple medical tasks.
- **Open Question 2:** What is the Optimal Mechanism for Hyperparameter Tuning when Training Confidence-Aware Ensembles (CAEs) on Limited or Variable Numbers of Expert Annotations?
  - *Basis in paper:* [explicit] The authors note: "Training appears to be highly dependent on hyperparameter selection, and each scenario requires preliminary hyperparameter tuning to achieve optimal results." They also found that a CAE trained for 150 epochs on fewer experts improved, while a 300-epoch CAE suffered unless all six experts were used.
  - *Why unresolved:* The paper demonstrates sensitivity but does not establish a tuning protocol, a fixed schedule, or a meta-learning approach. The optimal training duration, learning rate, or loss weighting likely depends on the quantity and quality of available expert soft labels, which is impractical for real-world use.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* A systematic study that derives a set of robust, task-agnostic hyperparameter heuristics (e.g., scaling epochs with number of experts, using a validation set of expert soft labels) or a automated tuning strategy that reliably yields near-optimal CAE performance given a small, variable budget of expert annotations.
- **Open Question 3:** What are the fundamental reasons for the Performance Gap between LLM-based "Oracle" Annotations and Human Experts in Uncertainty Estimation for Medical QA Tasks?
  - *Basis in paper:* [explicit] The paper tests an LLM oracle on PubMedQA and finds: "This method is denoted LLM oracle... generally performs the same or worse at lower rejection rates... LLM oracle outperforms Total variance, base with AAC value of 0.109. However, in practical scenarios better performance at lower rejection rates would be more important." The method also underperforms using real experts (AAC 0.066 vs. 0.114).
  - *Why unresolved:* The paper posits potential reasons (small training size, lack of variety in expert responses, model accuracy) but does not conduct ablation studies to isolate the cause. It's unclear if the gap is due to LLM calibration, domain mismatch, or the nature of LLM-generated "disagreement" versus true human expertise.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Controlled experiments where LLM outputs are post-hoc calibrated, where LLM disagreement is induced via prompt variations, or where LLM "confidence scores" are compared directly to human confidence on a per-sample basis to identify systematic biases (e.g., overconfidence on ambiguous questions).
- **Open Question 4:** How does the proposed expert-guided uncertainty decomposition (Total Variance with CAE) behave under Distribution Shift, and does it maintain its advantage over non-expert methods when applied to data from a different hospital or scanner?
  - *Basis in paper:* [inferred] The paper evaluates on held-out test sets from the same distribution as training. While it claims improved aleatoric uncertainty estimation, it does not evaluate performance on out-of-distribution (OOD) or shifted data. A core promise of uncertainty estimation is detecting OOD samples; the paper's method explicitly models aleatoric uncertainty (in-distribution ambiguity), which might behave differently under shift than epistemic uncertainty. The relative advantage over methods like MC Dropout or ensembles under shift is unknown.
  - *Why unresolved:* All experiments are on in-distribution test sets. The paper's method's sensitivity to covariate shift (e.g., different CT scanner models for LIDC-IDRI) or label shift is not investigated. It is an unstated assumption that the expert soft labels capture the *in-distribution* aleatoric uncertainty, which may not generalize.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* A dedicated experiment where models trained on one source (e.g., one hospital's retinal images for RIGA) are tested on data from another source, with rejection curves and AAC computed. The relative degradation of the CAE-based total variance vs. baseline ensemble methods under shift would indicate robustness and generalizability.
- **Open Question 5:** For multi-class segmentation, does the "one-versus-all" summation of per-class uncertainties (Equation 4) produce a mathematically coherent and clinically meaningful total uncertainty measure, or does it introduce artifacts?
  - *Basis in paper:* [explicit] The authors write: "Such aggregation may appear counterintuitive, as classes in multi-class segmentation are not independent of each other, and the summation of per-class uncertainties is not guaranteed to be mathematically meaningful. We provide a rationale for this approach in Supplementary Methods." The main text does not elaborate on the rationale's strength or validation.
  - *Why unresolved:* The paper uses this summation for multi-class segmentation results but does not validate if the resulting total uncertainty map correlates with human-identified ambiguous regions (e.g., boundaries between optic disc and cup). It is a methodological assumption that may not hold. The superior results on RIGA are intriguing, but it's unclear if they stem from better aleatoric modeling or an artifact of the summation.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* An analysis comparing the summed per-class uncertainty to alternative, theoretically grounded multi-class uncertainty measures (e.g., based on the entropy of the full softmax distribution). Clinically, this could involve having experts rank pixel-level ambiguity in segmentation maps and correlating those ranks with both the summed uncertainty and alternative measures.

## Limitations
- **Implementation details lacking:** Paper omits model architectures, hyperparameters (learning rate, batch size), augmentation policies, ensemble sizes, and specific aggregation methods for segmentation. This hampers replication.
- **Soft-label generation unclear:** For multi-class/segmentation tasks, the process for converting expert annotations into continuous targets (e.g., per-pixel averaging, handling abstentions) is not described. The LLM oracle setup for PubMedQA is also missing.
- **Expert reliance:** The method assumes availability of multiple high-quality expert annotations; performance when expert agreement is low or when only few experts exist is not thoroughly evaluated.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (Expert disagreement → AU target):** Medium. Supported by experiments, but depends on expert quality; no tests with biased/unreliable experts.
- **Mechanism 2 (Separate ensembles for decomposition):** High. Mathematically sound and consistently validated across tasks.
- **Mechanism 3 (Expert signals improve rejection):** High. Large gains in AAC/AUROC for both CAE and mixed-loss variants, with clear clinical relevance.
