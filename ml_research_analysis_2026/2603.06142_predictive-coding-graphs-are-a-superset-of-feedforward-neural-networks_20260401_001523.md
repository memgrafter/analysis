# Predictive Coding Graphs are a Superset of Feedforward Neural Networks

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.06142
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06142
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Predictive coding graphs (PCGs) are mathematically proven to be a superset of feedforward neural networks (FNNs), encompassing all FNN computations and supporting arbitrary topologies; the universal approximation theorem formally applies to predictive coding networks (PCNs).

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the mathematical relationship between predictive coding graphs (PCGs) and feedforward neural networks (FNNs). By providing formal proofs, the authors show that predictive coding networks (PCNs) are behaviorally equivalent to FNNs during inference. They further prove that PCNs are a special case of PCGs with a specific weight matrix structure, establishing PCGs as a superset of FNNs. This superset includes networks with loops, lateral connections, and non‑hierarchical topologies that are not natively supported by backpropagation‑trained FNNs. As a corollary, the universal approximation theorem extends to PCNs, guaranteeing their expressive power for hierarchical configurations. The work thus positions PCGs as a general framework for exploring neural architectures beyond traditional constraints.

## Method Summary
The authors employ formal mathematical proofs. First, they establish behavioral equivalence between PCNs and FNNs during inference by constructing a weight matrix with hierarchical structure and transpose symmetry, showing that the iterative predictive coding dynamics converge in one step to the same activations as a forward pass of the corresponding FNN. Second, they embed any FNN into a PCG by placing its weights in the forward direction and setting feedback/lateral components appropriately, proving that PCNs are a special case of PCGs. This embedding demonstrates that PCGs can represent all computations of FNNs, thereby proving the superset relationship.

## Key Results
- **Superset relationship**: PCGs strictly subsume FNNs; any feedforward network can be expressed as a predictive coding graph with an appropriate weight configuration.
- **Inference equivalence**: Under equilibrium, PCNs produce identical outputs to their FNN counterparts, ensuring functional equivalence during testing.
- **Arbitrary topologies**: PCGs allow cyclic, lateral, and skip connections, extending the space of trainable network structures beyond what backpropagation can handle.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The superset relationship arises from the weight matrix flexibility in PCGs. By imposing a hierarchical block structure with transpose symmetry (feedback weights equal to transposed feedforward weights), the iterative inference dynamics of the PCG reduce to a single forward pass—exactly the computation of an FNN. Conversely, any FNN can be embedded into a PCG by placing its weights in the forward direction and setting other components (feedback, lateral) to zero or to the transpose; with a suitable initialization, the resulting PCG behaves identically to the FNN. This mechanism works because predictive coding inference converges to a fixed point that minimizes prediction error, and under the hierarchical symmetry condition that fixed point coincides with the FNN activations. Moreover, because PCGs place no restriction on connectivity (beyond being a directed graph), they can represent non‑hierarchical topologies, thereby broadening the representational scope beyond backpropagation.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Input -> Error₁ -> Activity₁ -> Error₂ -> Activity₂ -> ... -> Error_L -> Activity_L (output).  
Each layer comprises an error unit (computes difference between activity and its prediction) and an activity unit (integrates predictions from higher‑order error units). Edges carry predictive signals; the graph may also include lateral or skip connections.

**Critical path**: Inference starts at the input, propagates predictions upward through error units, then activities downward via feedback connections, iterating until prediction errors fall below a threshold or a fixed step budget is exhausted.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- *Topology flexibility vs. inference cost*: Dense or cyclic graphs increase per‑iteration computation (O(N²)) and may need more iterations.
- *Weight symmetry vs. representational freedom*: Enforcing transpose symmetry limits the function family but guarantees convergence and equivalence; relaxing it permits arbitrary graphs but risks instability.
- *Inference budget vs. accuracy*: More steps yield a better equilibrium but increase latency; adaptive schemes can trade precision for speed.
- *Depth vs. initialization*: Deeper PCGs require careful initialization to avoid divergent dynamics.

**Failure signatures**:
- Diverging or oscillating prediction errors, indicating an unstable weight matrix (e.g., spectral radius > 1).
- Stagnant errors that do not decrease, often due to poor initialization or lack of symmetry.
- Training collapse when updates produce weights that break inference convergence.
- Empirical performance that significantly lags behind FNN baselines on standard tasks.

**Three first experiments**:
1. *Hierarchical equivalence validation*: Train a PCN on MNIST with a given feedforward architecture and compare test accuracy and inference steps to an identically sized FNN trained by backpropagation.
2. *Benefit of skip connections*: Add lateral or backward skip connections to a PCG and measure whether learning speed, final accuracy, or robustness improves relative to the strictly hierarchical PCN.
3. *Universal approximation for non‑hierarchical graphs*: Construct a fully connected PCG with random cycles and train it on a simple function approximation task (e.g., 2‑D sinc). Test whether low error can be achieved, probing the limits of the universal approximation theorem beyond hierarchical topologies.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Do PCGs with non‑hierarchical connections (e.g., backward, lateral, cyclic) satisfy a universal approximation theorem?**  
  *Basis*: The authors state: "Whether PCGs with additional connections can also approximate any function is left for future work."  
  *Why unresolved*: The UAT proof only applies to PCGs reduced to hierarchical structure.  
  *Resolution evidence*: A mathematical proof extending the UAT to general connectivity, or a constructive counterexample.

- **Do specific non‑feedforward connection types (backward/skip, lateral, self‑connections) in PCGs provide measurable benefits for learning, analogous to skip connections in ResNets?**  
  *Basis*: "Whether the remainder of ew – that is, backward (skip) connections, lateral connections and self‑connections – does, by analogy, also bring benefits. Future work should investigate this."  
  *Why unresolved*: Empirical studies have only tested all‑to‑all or hierarchical PCGs; isolated effects of structured non‑hierarchical connections remain unknown.  
  *Resolution evidence*: Controlled experiments comparing PCG variants with specific added connection types against hierarchical baselines on standard benchmarks, measuring accuracy, convergence, and robustness.

- **Can the computational overhead of iterative inference in PCGs (O(N²T) vs. O(LM) for FNNs) be mitigated without sacrificing the benefits of arbitrary topology?**  
  *Basis*: The paper notes the high time complexity but suggests it "could potentially be compensated by other favorable properties." No solution is provided.  
  *Why unresolved*: Practical implementations remain slow; it is unclear whether algorithmic improvements (e.g., adaptive inference steps, sparse dynamics) or hardware exploitation can close the gap.  
  *Resolution evidence*: Development of PCG training/inference algorithms that match or beat FNN wall‑clock time on real tasks while maintaining topological flexibility, demonstrated via complexity analysis and empirical scaling studies.

- **How should PCGs with cyclic or arbitrary topologies be initialized to ensure stable and efficient convergence during inference learning?**  
  *Basis*: Appendix C explains that the standard feedforward initialization scheme for PCNs depends on hierarchical structure and "is not obvious" for general PCGs.  
  *Why unresolved*: Initialization critically affects PCN performance, but no general method exists for graphs with loops or lateral connections; random or zero initialization may perform poorly.  
  *Resolution evidence*: Proposal and evaluation of initialization strategies (e.g., spectral methods, message‑passing warm‑starts, learned initializers) for non‑hierarchical PCGs, showing improved convergence speed and final performance across diverse topologies.

## Limitations
- The proofs assume specific weight matrix structures (e.g., symmetry, block‑diagonality) that may not generalize to fully arbitrary PCGs; the superset claim might implicitly require similar constraints.
- Behavioral equivalence is established only under equilibrium conditions; training dynamics and finite inference steps are not fully addressed, which could limit practical applicability.
- The universal approximation theorem extension relies on continuous, bounded activations and hierarchical connectivity; its validity for loopy or highly irregular topologies remains uncertain.

## Confidence
- **PCGs as a superset of FNNs**: High  
- **Universal approximation for PCNs**: Medium  
- **PCGs enable topologies beyond backpropagation**: Low
