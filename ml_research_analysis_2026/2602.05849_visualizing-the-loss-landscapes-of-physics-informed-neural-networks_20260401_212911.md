# Visualizing the loss landscapes of physics-informed neural networks

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.05849
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05849
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Both Deep Ritz Method (DRM) and strong‑form PINN losses exhibit smooth, convex‑like basins near solutions, singular Hessians with many zero eigenvalues due to linearly dependent basis functions, and low intrinsic dimensionalities (~10–50), with monotonic linear interpolation holding in most trials.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces loss landscape visualization techniques from traditional machine learning to physics‑informed neural networks (PINNs). It studies two formulations—the Deep Ritz Method (DRM) and strong‑form PINN—on a 1D elliptic PDE and a 2D hyperelasticity problem. Key findings reveal that both losses share properties common to classification tasks: smoothness, local convexity, singular Hessians, mode connectivity, and low effective dimensionality. Quantitatively, the Hessian nullspace correlates with linear dependence among the network’s final‑layer basis functions. The work provides a foundation for understanding optimization dynamics in PINNs and highlights open challenges in boundary enforcement, spectral bias, and scalability.

## Method Summary
The authors adapt standard loss landscape analysis tools—monotonic linear interpolation (MLI), Hessian eigenvalue spectra, random‑direction cross‑sections, and intrinsic dimensionality estimation—to compare DRM and PINN objectives. Networks are two‑layer tanh MLPs with hard‑encoded homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions via a multiplicative distance function. Losses are integrated deterministically (midpoint rule for 1D, polar grid for 2D) or stochastically (Monte Carlo). Optimization uses Adam (lr=1e‑3) up to 7500 epochs from Kaiming initialization until convergence (loss <1e‑6). For each trained model, the full Hessian is computed (or approximated) at the solution, basis function Gram matrices are analyzed, and trajectories are projected onto principal components. Experiments are repeated across random seeds and integration schemes to assess robustness.

## Key Results
- Both DRM and PINN losses are smooth, convex near minima, and satisfy monotonic linear interpolation in most trials (60/60 for 1D, 8/10 for 2D).
- Hessian eigenvalue spectra are singular; the majority of eigenvalues are zero, corresponding to a nullspace explained by linear dependence among the final‑layer basis functions (DRM rank 11/20, PINN rank 13/20).
- Effective optimization occurs in a low‑dimensional subspace (~10–50 dimensions out of hundreds), as shown by random subspace training and PCA of parameter trajectories.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Hessian singularity from basis dependence
- **Claim**: The Hessian’s nullspace near the solution arises partly because the network’s final‑layer neurons (basis functions satisfying boundary conditions) are linearly dependent.
- **Mechanism**: When the basis function matrix has a nontrivial null space, adjusting outer parameters within that null space leaves the discretized state field unchanged, creating flat directions in the loss landscape.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - Gram matrix rank of learned basis functions: DRM 11/20, PINN 13/20.
  - Hessian walks along eigenvectors with smallest |λ| show nearly constant loss over hundreds of steps.
  - Similar eigenvalue concentration observed in the 2D hyperelasticity case.
- **Break condition**: If basis functions become linearly independent (e.g., via orthogonal regularization), the flatness from this mechanism should diminish.

### Mechanism 2: Monotonic Linear Interpolation (MLI)
- **Claim**: Straight‑line paths between initial and final parameters usually show monotonic loss decrease, indicating smooth, well‑behaved basins.
- **Mechanism**: The optimizer remains within a convex‑like neighborhood of the solution; high barriers are avoided, possibly due to overparameterization and PDE structure.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - 1D elliptic: 60/60 trials (both DRM/PINN) show MLI under varied initial scales.
  - 2D hyperelasticity: 8/10 trials show MLI; the two failing PINN trials exhibit small barriers.
  - Random‑direction plots around solutions reveal smooth, convex contours.
- **Break condition**: MLI can fail with certain optimizers/hyperparameters or when escaping plateaus (e.g., SGD on DRM stagnated).

### Mechanism 3: Low intrinsic dimensionality
- **Claim**: Good solutions lie in a low‑dimensional effective subspace (intrinsic dimensionality ~10–50 out of hundreds of parameters).
- **Mechanism**: The optimizer’s trajectory is confined to a low‑rank subspace spanned by a few principal components; random projections of that dimension still achieve near‑full performance.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - 1D problem: 10‑dimensional random subspace matches full 480‑parameter performance.
  - PCA shows first two components capture >90% of trajectory variance for both DRM/PINN.
  - 2D problem: 50‑dimensional subspace (~775 total) matches full performance; first two PCs explain >90% variance.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept**: Loss Landscape Visualization Techniques (Hessian eigenvalues, random‑direction plots, mode connectivity).  
  *Why needed*: To interpret morphology of high‑dimensional, non‑convex surfaces defined by PDE residuals rather than data.  
  *Quick check*: How would you visualize a 1000‑dimensional loss surface with a single contour plot?
- **Concept**: PINN Formulations (Deep Ritz Method vs. strong‑form squared residual).  
  *Why needed*: Different PDE‑based objectives may yield different landscapes; DRM uses variational energy (lower differentiation) while PINN uses squared PDE residual.  
  *Quick check*: What practical differences in implementation and boundary handling exist between DRM and PINN?
- **Concept**: Neural Network Parameterization for PDEs (basis functions with distance transforms for boundary enforcement).  
  *Why needed*: Allows isolating effects of the PDE operator loss from boundary penalty terms; interprets layer weights as coefficients.  
  *Quick check*: How does multiplying the network output by a distance function affect gradient dynamics?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:  
  Input (spatial coordinates X) → Hidden MLP (tanh, 2 layers) → Last hidden layer multiplied elementwise by distance function φ(X) → Linear output combination (no bias) → u(X) → Loss integration (deterministic grid or MC) → Loss value.
- **Critical path**:
  1. Define manufactured solution u(X) to compute exact source term.
  2. Implement u(X;θ)=φ(X)·N(X;θ) with automatic differentiation.
  3. Choose integration scheme (fixed quadrature or MC sampling).
  4. Train with Adam/SGD to convergence (loss threshold or max epochs).
  5. Extract Hessian, compute basis Gram matrix, run PCA as needed.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - DRM vs. PINN: DRM has lower differentiation order, often more stable with MC integration; PINN is more general (no variational principle required).
  - Deterministic vs. stochastic integration: Deterministic yields smooth landscapes for visualization; stochastic introduces noise but reflects realistic training gradients.
  - Network width/depth: Wider/deeper networks increase expressivity but may worsen conditioning; paper finds modest widths (20–25) sufficient.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - DRM with stochastic integration: integrand variance 5 orders higher than PINN → noisy, unstructured landscape.
  - PINN/SGD on 2D DRM: stagnation at suboptimal minima.
  - Large parameter norms in hyperelasticity: cause material inversion (J≤0) → NaN loss.
  - Poor basis learning: rank << width indicates redundancy; may cause flat directions but also explainability.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **MLI Check**: For 1D elliptic, train DRM/PINN from 10 random initializations; plot loss along straight line between init and final; expect monotonic decrease in most trials.
  2. **Hessian Analysis**: At solution, compute full Hessian; plot log‑absolute eigenvalues; expect concentration near zero. Compute basis Gram matrix rank; correlate with nullspace dimension. Take eigenvector with smallest |λ| and walk along it; loss should remain constant.
  3. **Intrinsic Dimensionality**: Train in random affine subspaces of varying dimension (e.g., 5D to full); compare final loss and accuracy. Expect ~10D to suffice for 1D problem.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How do different boundary condition enforcement methods (penalty, hard constraints, augmented Lagrangian) affect loss landscape geometry?
- What loss landscape features explain the spectral bias of PINNs (low‑frequency preference)?
- Does mode connectivity hold for physics‑informed networks under highly nonlinear physics?
- How can we efficiently compute or approximate the Hessian of PINN losses with high‑order derivatives for curvature analysis?

## Limitations
- Basis function independence not causally verified: link between linear dependence and Hessian nullspace is correlational; no experiment forced full rank to test the break condition.
- 2D quadrature details unspecified: polar grid construction (origin inclusion, Jacobian weighting) is ambiguous, potentially affecting integrated loss and landscape morphology.
- Scalability untested: results restricted to modest network widths (20–25) and two PDEs; behavior for deeper/wider networks or more complex operators remains unknown.

## Confidence
- **Hessian singularity due to basis dependence**: High. Directly supported by Gram matrix rank measurements and Hessian walk experiments.
- **Monotonic Linear Interpolation prevalence**: Medium. Strong experimental support (68/70 trials total) but known to fail with certain optimizers/hyperparameters; mechanism is correlational.
- **Low intrinsic dimensionality**: Medium. Validated on two specific problems; generalizability to more complex PDEs is unverified.

## Next Checks
1. **Causal basis rank test**: Retrain with orthogonal regularization on last‑layer weights to enforce full‑rank basis; re‑measure Hessian nullspace dimension to confirm reduction.
2. **Quadrature sensitivity analysis**: Implement alternative deterministic integration for 2D (e.g., Gaussian quadrature on disk); compare Hessian spectra and MLI rates to assess robustness.
3. **Complexity stress test**: Apply the same visualization pipeline to a third, more challenging PDE (e.g., 3D Navier–Stokes or high‑frequency Helmholtz) to probe whether low intrinsic dimensionality and MLI persist.
