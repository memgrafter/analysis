# Synthesizing Realistic Test Data without Breaking Privacy
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.05833
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05833
- **Reference count:** 25
- **Primary result:** Grammar-based fuzzing guided by a discriminator generates synthetic data with utility comparable to original data while ensuring no exact matches, preserving privacy.

## Executive Summary
We need a way to generate synthetic test data that preserves the statistical utility of a private dataset without exposing individual records. This paper introduces a method that replaces the generator in a GAN-like setup with a grammar-based fuzzer (Fandango). A discriminator, trained on the original data and an initial synthetic batch, acts as an oracle: samples that fool it are labeled "good" and seed subsequent fuzzing generations. The discriminator is periodically retrained on the accumulating good samples, tightening the feedback loop. Evaluated on four datasets, models trained on the synthetic data achieve performance nearly identical to those trained on the original data (e.g., insurance R²: 0.82 vs 0.88; bank accuracy: 0.91 vs 0.90). Crucially, no generated sample exactly matches any original record, and moderate Wasserstein distances indicate statistical similarity without overfitting, offering a practical privacy-utility trade-off.

## Method Summary
The method requires a manually written Fandango specification (grammar and optional constraints) for the target dataset. First, Fandango generates an initial synthetic dataset of the same size as the original. A discriminator (e.g., decision tree) is trained to distinguish original (label 1) from this initial synthetic data (label 0). An iterative loop follows: Fandango generates a new batch using the current set of "good" samples as seeds; the discriminator classifies each sample; those predicted as original (class 1) are collected as good and added to the seed pool. Once enough good samples are gathered or discriminator accuracy degrades, a new discriminator is trained on the original data plus all collected good samples. The loop repeats until synthetic utility meets a threshold or stagnation occurs. The final synthetic dataset is evaluated for utility (downstream model performance), resemblance (Wasserstein distance), and privacy (zero exact matches). The generator never sees the original private data, and privacy hinges on the fuzzer's ability to generate diverse samples without direct copying.

## Key Results
- Models trained on synthetic data achieve comparable performance to original-data models: insurance R² 0.82 vs 0.88; bank accuracy 0.91 vs 0.90.
- Privacy is maintained with no exact matches between synthetic and original samples across all four datasets.
- Normalized Wasserstein distances range from 0.11 to 0.61, indicating statistical similarity without memorization.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Privacy via Indirect Generation
**Claim:** Privacy is preserved because the fuzzer (Fandango) generates all synthetic data without ever accessing the original private dataset.
**Mechanism:** The generator is replaced with a grammar-based fuzzer that produces samples solely from a specification (grammar + constraints). The original data is only used to train a discriminator, which acts as an external oracle; there is no direct data flow into the generator.
**Core Assumption:** A well-specified grammar can produce a sufficiently diverse set of valid inputs to cover the input space, and the evolutionary process can converge toward statistical properties without seeing the source data.
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract]: "with only indirectly leveraging the original data" and "the generator has not been trained on the original data". [section 3.1]: "Crucially, though, Fandango never sees the private data."
**Break condition:** If the grammar is incomplete or too restrictive, the fuzzer cannot generate samples that match necessary feature dependencies, leading to low utility regardless of discriminator feedback.

### Mechanism 2: Utility via Discriminator Feedback
**Claim:** Utility (statistical resemblance) is improved by using a discriminator, trained on original and initial synthetic data, to label samples as "good" if they fool it, thereby guiding fuzzer evolution.
**Mechanism:** In each iteration, the fuzzer generates a batch of samples. Those classified as original by the discriminator are added to the initial population for the next iteration. This evolutionary loop steers generation toward regions of the input space that the discriminator perceives as similar to the original data distribution.
**Core Assumption:** A discriminator trained on a mix of original and random synthetic data learns a decision boundary that approximates the original data's distribution; samples that fool it are statistically plausible approximations.
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract]: "a discriminator model determines how close we are to the original data" and "evolving samples and determining 'good samples' with the discriminator". [section 3.2]: "Every sample that fools the discriminator... is considered a 'good sample', and these good samples are added to Fandango’s initial population in subsequent iterations."
**Break condition:** If the discriminator overfits to the initial synthetic data's artifacts or underfits (e.g., shallow decision tree), its feedback becomes misleading, causing evolution toward irrelevant regions or stagnation.

### Mechanism 3: Iterative Refinement
**Claim:** Privacy-utility balance is refined through iterative retraining of the discriminator on accumulating "good" samples, progressively tightening the notion of "original-like" data.
**Mechanism:** After collecting enough "good" samples, a new discriminator is trained on original + all good samples. This more discerning discriminator is then used in the next generation cycle. Repeating this process aims to increase utility (model performance on synthetic data) while maintaining privacy (avoiding exact matches), as the synthetic distribution gradually aligns with the original without copying.
**Core Assumption:** Each retraining iteration increases the discriminative power regarding subtle distributional differences, pushing synthetic data to capture deeper dependencies without memorizing individual records.
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract]: "By evolving samples and determining 'good samples' with the discriminator, we can generate privacy-preserving data that follows the same statistical distributions". [section 3.3]: "This process of retraining a new discriminator is repeated until the synthetic data provides a satisfying utility." [section 4.3, RQ2]: Utility results show models trained on synthetic data achieve comparable performance to original-data models (e.g., insurance R²: 0.82 vs 0.88).
**Break condition:** If the Wasserstein distance drops too low (approaching zero) or exact matches appear in synthetic data, privacy fails. Conversely, if utility plateaus below acceptable thresholds, the loop may not converge to a useful distribution.

## Foundational Learning
- **Grammar-based fuzzing (Fandango):** Generates syntactically valid, diverse samples without seeing private data. Why needed: It is the generator that produces samples solely from a specification, ensuring no direct data leakage. Quick check: How does Fandango use constraints to guide evolution toward a target distribution, and what happens if constraints are too loose or too tight?
- **Discriminator as a soft constraint:** The discriminator implicitly encodes the original data distribution via classification. Why needed: It replaces explicit statistical constraints, providing feedback to the fuzzer. Quick check: Why might a discriminator trained on original + random synthetic data initially have high accuracy, and what does its decreasing accuracy over iterations signify?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Specification Author -> Initial Generator (Fandango) -> Discriminator Trainer -> Evolution Loop Controller -> Retraining Trigger -> Utility/Privacy Evaluator

**Critical path:** The workflow starts with a manually authored Fandango specification. The Initial Generator produces a synthetic dataset. The Discriminator Trainer builds a classifier on original (label 1) vs. initial synthetic (label 0). The Evolution Loop Controller then repeatedly: runs Fandango with current good samples as seeds; the discriminator classifies outputs; samples predicted as original (class 1) are collected as "good". When enough good samples are gathered or discriminator accuracy degrades, the Retraining Trigger builds a new discriminator using original + all collected good samples. The loop repeats until a utility threshold is met or stagnation occurs. Finally, the Utility/Privacy Evaluator measures model performance and checks for exact matches/Wasserstein distance.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Grammar expressiveness vs. generation speed: More complex grammars (with many constraints) produce more realistic data but slow down fuzzing.
- Discriminator complexity vs. privacy: Deeper models capture nuances better but risk overfitting to initial synthetic data, potentially reducing diversity.
- Retraining frequency vs. compute cost: More frequent retraining may improve convergence but increases overhead; too sparse retraining leads to feedback staleness.

**Failure signatures:**
- Exact matches in synthetic data: Immediate privacy failure; indicates generator is too deterministic or discriminator is overfitted.
- Low model utility (synthetic-trained model performs poorly on original test set): Synthetic data lacks key correlations; likely grammar is insufficient or evolution stalled.
- Discriminator accuracy remains high ~100%: Fuzzer cannot fool discriminator; indicates generation is stuck or grammar cannot produce plausible samples.
- Wasserstein distance near zero: Synthetic data is a near-copy; privacy compromised.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Baseline generation:** Run Fandango with specification only (no discriminator). Measure diversity and validity rate. Expect many invalid/implausible samples if grammar lacks constraints.
2. **Single-loop utility test:** Perform one full iteration (initial discriminator → collect goods → stop). Train a model on these goods, test on original. Compare to original-trained model. Expect moderate utility gap.
3. **Privacy audit:** After full iterative run, check for exact row matches between original and synthetic datasets. Also compute per-feature Wasserstein distances. Expect zero exact matches and moderate distances (as in paper: 0.11–0.61).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can the Fandango specification be automatically generated from raw data or natural language descriptions to eliminate manual effort? The current process relies on manually writing specifications, which is a barrier for adoption and may not scale to diverse datasets.
2. How does the utility-privacy trade-off of the fuzzing-based approach quantitatively compare to state-of-the-art GAN-based methods like CTGAN? The paper lacks direct benchmarks against GANs.
3. What are the formal privacy guarantees of the generated data against membership inference attacks, and how do they vary with discriminator configuration? The current assessment only checks for duplicate samples, which is insufficient for rigorous privacy.
4. Can dataset-level constraints (e.g., preserving marginal distributions or correlations) be integrated into Fandango to improve statistical fidelity, and how should they be designed? Current constraints are sample-level via the discriminator.

## Limitations
- Manual grammar specification is required and may not capture complex feature interactions, limiting scalability and utility.
- Privacy evaluation is limited to duplicate detection and Wasserstein distance; it does not include tests against membership inference or reconstruction attacks.
- The study evaluates only four datasets, raising questions about generalizability to other domains or high-cardinality data.

## Confidence
- **Privacy preservation:** Low – relies on absence of exact matches; vulnerability to inference attacks not tested.
- **Utility preservation:** Medium – comparable model performance on four datasets, but lacks statistical validation and broader task diversity.
- **Evolution mechanism efficacy:** Low – iterative process described but no ablation on convergence, stagnation, or discriminator overfitting.
