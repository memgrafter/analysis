# Can Generative Artificial Intelligence Survive Data Contamination? Theoretical Guarantees under Contaminated Recursive Training

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.16065
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16065
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Under minimal assumptions, contaminated recursive training (CRT) converges at rate \( \mathcal{O}(t^{-\min(p, \alpha)}) \); with biased real data (BCRT) the rate becomes \( \mathcal{O}(t^{-\min(p, \alpha, q)}) \), offering the first positive convergence guarantees without distributional assumptions.

## Executive Summary
Data contamination—mixing real and AI‑generated samples—can cause model collapse in recursive training. This paper provides theoretical guarantees that such training can still converge, provided enough fresh real data is used and/or bias in the real data decays sufficiently. The analysis makes almost no assumptions about the real data distribution, assumes only that the generator is a universal approximator and that the training objective uses a convex distance metric. The core finding is that the convergence rate of the distributional error is determined by the worst among three factors: the baseline learning rate of the generator, the fraction of real data used each round, and (for biased data) the decay rate of the bias. Simulations with kernel density estimation, Wasserstein GANs, and a diffusion model on MNIST corroborate the theory. The results suggest model collapse is not inevitable and can be avoided by maintaining a sufficient real‑data proportion or by systematically correcting sampling bias.

## Method Summary
The study examines *contaminated recursive training* (CRT) where, at iteration \( t \), a generative model \( \hat{P}_t \) is trained on the union of all real datasets \( X^1,\dots,X^t \) and all synthetic datasets \( Y^1,\dots,Y^{t-1} \) collected so far. The real data are i.i.d. from a fixed target distribution \( P_0 \) (or from a biased sequence \( P_t^{\text{bias}} \) in BCRT). The synthetic data are drawn from the previous generator \( \hat{P}_{t-1} \). Let \( \alpha \) be the fraction of real samples relative to the total new samples added each round. Under three assumptions—(A1) the baseline generator achieves a uniform polynomial convergence rate \( p \) when trained on any fixed distribution; (A2) the distance \( d \) between distributions is convex in its second argument; (A3) for BCRT, the bias decay obeys \( d(P_t^{\text{bias}}, P_0) \lesssim t^{-q} \)—the error \( d(\hat{P}_t, P_0) \) satisfies a recursion that captures the accumulation of past errors. Solving this recursion yields the closed‑form rates: \( \mathcal{O}(t^{-\min(p, \alpha)}) \) for CRT and \( \mathcal{O}(t^{-\min(p, \alpha, q)}) \) for BCRT. Experiments vary \( \alpha \) and \( q \) with simple generators (KDE, WGAN) and show a predicted phase transition at \( \alpha = p \); a diffusion model on MNIST illustrates convergence in a high‑dimensional setting even with \( \alpha < 1 \).

## Key Results
- **CRT rate:** \( d(\hat{P}_t, P_0) = \mathcal{O}(t^{-\min(p, \alpha)}) \).
- **BCRT rate:** With biased real data converging at rate \( q \), \( d(\hat{P}_t, P_0) = \mathcal{O}(t^{-\min(p, \alpha, q)}) \).
- **Phase transition:** If \( \alpha = p \) (or the minimum is achieved by \( \alpha \)), an additional logarithmic factor appears, slowing the rate to \( \mathcal{O}(t^{-\alpha} \log t) \).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1 – Rate governed by \( \min(p, \alpha) \)
The accumulated training distribution \( Q_t \) is a convex combination (weight \( \alpha \) on real data, \( 1-\alpha \) on past synthetic data). The total error decomposes into (i) the learning error \( d(\hat{P}_t, Q_t) \), bounded by the baseline rate \( M_t^{-p} \); and (ii) the distributional error \( d(Q_t, P_0) \). By convexity (Assumption A2), \( d(Q_t, P_0) \leq \alpha d(\bar{P}_t^{\text{real}}, P_0) + (1-\alpha) \sum_{j=1}^{t-1} \gamma_j d(\hat{P}_j, P_0) \) for some weights \( \gamma_j \). Since the real‑data average \( \bar{P}_t^{\text{real}} = P_0 \) exactly, the distributional error reduces to a weighted sum of past errors. This yields a linear recursion \( S_t \lesssim S_{t-1} + M_t^{-p} \) for the cumulative error sum, whose solution gives \( d(\hat{P}_t, P_0) = \mathcal{O}(t^{-\min(p, \alpha)}) \).

### Mechanism 2 – Incorporating bias decay (BCRT)
When real data are drawn from a biased sequence \( P_t^{\text{bias}} \) converging to \( P_0 \) with rate \( q \) (Assumption A3), the real‑data average inside \( Q_t \) becomes \( \bar{P}_t^{\text{bias}} = \frac{1}{t}\sum_{j=1}^t P_j^{\text{bias}} \). By a Cesàro averaging argument (Lemma B.1), \( d(\bar{P}_t^{\text{bias}}, P_0) \lesssim t^{-\min(q,1)} \). This additional error term enters the recursion alongside the learning error and the synthetic‑data accumulation term. The overall error is now bounded by the maximum of three competing rates, i.e., the slowest decaying one: \( \mathcal{O}(t^{-\min(p, \alpha, q)}) \).

### Mechanism 3 – Phase transition at \( \alpha = p \)
When \( p \neq \alpha \), solving the recursion (after Equation 5) yields a pure power law \( t^{-\min(p,\alpha)} \). When \( p = \alpha \), the discrete integration produces a harmonic series that introduces a logarithmic factor, resulting in \( \mathcal{O}(t^{-\alpha} \log t) \). This mirrors the convergence of the \( p \)-series at the critical exponent. The phase transition is confirmed analytically and in simulations.

## Foundational Learning

- **Convex Distance Metric (Assumption A2).**  
  *Why needed:* Convexity in the second argument allows the distributional error of the mixed training distribution \( Q_t \) to be expressed as a convex combination of past generator errors, closing the recursion.  
  *Quick check:* Does the triangle inequality plus convexity imply \( d(P, \lambda Q_1 + (1-\lambda) Q_2) \leq \lambda d(P,Q_1) + (1-\lambda)d(P,Q_2) \)? If not, what property is missing? *(Answer: We need the distance to be convex in its second argument; the triangle inequality alone is insufficient.)*

- **Uniform Polynomial Convergence Rate (Assumption A1).**  
  *Why needed:* The bound \( d(\hat{P}_t, Q_t) \lesssim M_t^{-p} \) must hold for every possible accumulated distribution \( Q_t \), regardless of its specific mixture of real and synthetic data. Uniformity ensures the learning error term can be bounded by the same exponent \( p \) at every iteration.  
  *Quick check:* For a KDE with bandwidth \( h \sim n^{-1/(2s+d)} \) under \( s \)-smooth density, does this rate apply uniformly over all \( s \)-smooth densities? *(Answer: Yes, under standard smoothness assumptions the minimax rate is uniform; for finite mixture model classes, uniform convergence may fail if the true distribution lies outside the class.)*

- **Cesàro Averaging and Bias Decay (Assumption A3 & Lemma B.1).**  
  *Why needed:* The real‑data stream in BCRT does not concentrate on \( P_0 \) but its Cesàro average \( \bar{P}_t^{\text{bias}} \) does. The rate of this averaging directly impacts the overall convergence.  
  *Quick check:* If \( d(P_t^{\text{bias}}, P_0) \propto t^{-0.7} \), the average \( \bar{P}_t^{\text{bias}} \) converges as \( t^{-0.7} \). If the decay is \( t^{-1.2} \), the average converges as \( t^{-1} \). (In general, the rate is \( t^{-\min(q,1)} \).)

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**  
Data Accumulation Buffer → Generator (trained on all accumulated data) → Synthetic samples for next iteration.  
Distance Metric Calculator → evaluation of Generator (optional).  
Bias‑Decay Controller (BCRT only) → real data sampling distribution.

**Critical path**  
1. Initialize: collect initial real batch \( X^0 \), train \( \hat{P}_0 \).  
2. For \( t = 1,\dots,T \):  
 a. Draw synthetic batch \( Y^t \sim \hat{P}_{t-1} \) (size \( m_2 \)).  
 b. Draw fresh real batch \( X^t \sim P_0 \) (or \( P_t^{\text{bias}} \)) (size \( m_1 \)).  
 c. Append \( X^t, Y^t \) to Data Accumulation Buffer.  
 d. Train new generator \( \hat{P}_t \) on the full buffer contents.  
 e. (Optional) Compute \( d(\hat{P}_t, P_0) \).

**Design tradeoffs**  
- **Memory vs. Freshness:** Full accumulation maximizes information but grows linearly with \( T \). Fixed‑size buffers reduce memory but violate the theoretical recursion.  
- **Real‑data fraction \( \alpha \):** Larger \( \alpha \) improves the convergence rate but reduces the amount of synthetic data that could be used for exploration. The theory suggests choosing \( \alpha \geq p \) to avoid slowdown, though \( p \) is often unknown.  
- **Bias correction schedule:** The decay rate \( q \) must exceed \( \min(p, \alpha) \) to prevent being the bottleneck. Early aggressive correction (high \( q \)) is easier than late correction.

**Failure signatures**  
- **Divergence:** If \( \alpha \) is too small (\( \alpha < p \)), the error curve plateaus or increases after a few iterations.  
- **Bias lock‑in:** If \( q \) is too small, the generator converges to \( P^{\text{bias}} \neq P_0 \); final distribution statistics will differ from the target.  
- **Phase transition miss:** When \( \alpha \approx p \), empirical rates may appear worse than either extreme due to the logarithmic factor; do not expect a clean power law.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. **CRT with KDE:** Use a 1‑D Gaussian mixture target. Vary \( \alpha \in \{0.1,0.3,0.5,0.7,0.9\} \). For each, run \( T=2000 \) iterations, repeat 50 times, compute W1 and MMD. Fit slope of \( \log d \) vs \( \log M_t \). Expect transition from \( \alpha \) (for \( \alpha < p=0.5 \)) to \( p \) (for \( \alpha > 0.5 \)), with a log‑factor slowdown at \( \alpha=0.5 \).  
2. **BCRT with KDE:** Same setup but real data drawn from \( P_t^{\text{bias}} = (1 - 0.2 (t+5)^{-q}) P_0 + 0.2 (t+5)^{-q} \mathcal{N}(3,1) \). Fix \( \alpha=0.5 \), test \( q \in \{0.25,0.5,0.75\} \). Compare empirical rate with \( \min(0.5, q) \). If \( q<0.5 \), the limit distribution should be biased.  
3. **Baseline rate sensitivity:** Train a WGAN on a 2‑D Gaussian mixture; theory suggests \( p \approx 0.25 \) for W1. Set \( \alpha=0.3 \) (≥ p? Actually 0.3 > 0.25, so should converge at rate \( p=0.25 \)). Compare empirical rate to the KDE baseline run. Confirm that the limiting rate equals \( p \), not \( \alpha \).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How do selective mechanisms (e.g., human filtering, malicious amplification) that cause non‑uniform sampling of AI‑generated content affect CRT convergence?
2. Can the guarantees be extended to loss functions that are not convex in the distribution argument (e.g., cross‑entropy, KL divergence)?
3. How do modern practices like RLHF, curated datasets, and reward optimization alter CRT dynamics?
4. In scenarios where synthetic data is deliberately introduced to augment scarce real data, how does the controlled proportion and quality of synthetic data affect convergence and utility?

## Limitations
- Theory assumes a convex distance metric (A2) and uniform polynomial convergence (A1); both may fail for non‑convex divergences like KL or for finite‑capacity models.
- The baseline rate \( p \) is often unknown in practice and empirical rates for complex models (WGAN, diffusion) can be far below theoretical bounds.
- High‑dimensional experiments (MNIST) remain qualitative due to the lack of reliable W1 estimators; quantitative validation is an open challenge.

## Confidence
- **Claim:** CRT convergence rate equals \( \min(p, \alpha) \). **Confidence:** High (rigorous proof + empirical validation).
- **Claim:** BCRT rate equals \( \min(p, \alpha, q) \). **Confidence:** Medium (sound Cesàro argument but limited empirical exploration).
- **Claim:** Phase transition at \( \alpha = p \) with logarithmic slowdown. **Confidence:** Medium (derived mathematically, observed in simple simulations; may be obscured by noise).
- **Claim:** Practical guidance “maintain \( \alpha \geq p \) to avoid collapse”. **Confidence:** Low (because \( p \) is typically unknown and architecture‑dependent).

## Next Checks
1. **Measure baseline rate \( p \) for modern generators:** Train models (KDE, WGAN, diffusion) on increasing dataset sizes and fit empirical W1/MMD convergence rates to inform safe \( \alpha \) choices.
2. **Test non‑convex metrics:** Repeat CRT using KL or JS divergence; if collapse occurs or rates deviate from min(\( p, \alpha \)), it confirms the necessity of Assumption A2.
3. **Large‑scale diffusion with quantitative metrics:** Use Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) on CIFAR‑10/ImageNet across varying \( \alpha \) and \( T \) to check whether the min(\( p, \alpha \)) trend holds in high dimensions (with approximations like fixed‑size buffers).
