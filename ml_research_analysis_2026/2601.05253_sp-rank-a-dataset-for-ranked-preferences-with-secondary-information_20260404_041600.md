# SP-Rank: A Dataset for Ranked Preferences with Secondary Information
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.05253
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05253
- **Reference Count:** 5
- **Primary Result:** SP-Voting, which jointly leverages first-order votes and second-order meta-predictions, achieves consistent and substantial gains in rank recovery across three tasks, nine elicitation formats, and three domains.

## Executive Summary
The SP-Rank paper introduces a novel dataset and method for ranking aggregation using second-order beliefs. The dataset comprises 12,384 human-generated datapoints from 1,152 participants, combining votes (first-order preferences) and meta-predictions (beliefs about others' votes) across three domains (geography, movies, paintings) and nine elicitation formats. The proposed SP-Voting method (implemented via the Partial-SP algorithm) integrates both signals to infer ground-truth rankings. Benchmarking against traditional vote-only aggregation rules (Borda, Copeland, Maximin) shows that incorporating second-order information consistently and substantially improves performance on full and subset-level rank recovery, as well as on probabilistic voter modeling tasks.

## Method Summary
SP-Rank provides a dataset where participants not only rank or approve a subset of items but also predict how others would vote on the same subset. The Partial-SP algorithm first computes a local ranking per subset by scaling vote frequencies by a prediction-normalized ratio that upweights voters whose choices diverge from the majority but align with a latent truth. These local rankings are then lifted into global pairwise tallies and aggregated using a standard rule (Borda, Copeland, Maximin) to produce a full ranking over all domain items. The approach is evaluated via Kendall Tau correlation against provided ground-truth rankings and by relative error in recovering parameters of Mallows and Plackett-Luce probabilistic models.

## Key Results
- **Substantial full rank recovery gains:** Across domains and formats, SP-Voting improves Kendall Tau over vote-only baselines; e.g., Copeland in App3-Rank increases from 0.08 to 0.85.
- **Consistent subset-level improvements:** Local SP rankings show similar gains, such as Borda rising from 0.02 to 0.86.
- **Superior probabilistic modeling:** SP-Voting yields lower relative errors in recovered dispersion and strength parameters for heterogeneous voter groups.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Expert Upweighting via Discrepancy Normalization**  
SP-Voting identifies expert voters as those whose votes are both correct and unpopular. It amplifies their signal by multiplying local vote frequency by a ratio of cross-prediction probabilities. This ratio normalizes for how voters who chose a particular ranking predicted others would vote. The core assumption is a minority of experts with >50% accuracy on shown subsets whose meta-predictions about the majority are systematically wrong (they anticipate majority error). Evidence includes abstract claims of accuracy improvement and large Tau gains in tables (e.g., Copeland +0.77). The method breaks if meta-predictions are random or aligned with the majority, amplifying noise instead.

**Mechanism 2: Partial-SP Lifting from Local to Global**  
Algorithm 1 constructs a full ranking by (a) estimating a local SP ranking for each subset, (b) converting all local rankings into pairwise win/loss counts to build a global support matrix, and (c) applying a classical aggregation rule to that matrix. This assumes the ground truth is globally consistent and that randomly sampled, non-overlapping subsets provide enough overlapping pairwise comparisons to reconstruct the global order. Evidence is the detailed algorithm description and the robust gains reported in Tables 1–2. The approach fails if subsets are too small or non-overlapping, yielding a sparse matrix and arbitrary tie-breaking.

**Mechanism 3: Robustness Across Elicitation Formats**  
Testing nine format pairs (e.g., Rank-Rank, App1-App1) isolates the value of second-order signals under varying first-order sparsity. SP-Voting leverages predictions to correct majority bias even when votes only indicate a single top choice. The assumption is that predicting others' choices is feasible and orthogonal to one's own vote precision. Evidence includes gains in low-information formats (e.g., App1-App1: Borda Tau 0.11→0.27) and statements about second-order strength. Benefits vanish if predictions are absent or too noisy.

## Foundational Learning
- **Ranking Aggregation Rules (Borda, Copeland, Maximin):** Needed as baselines. Borda sums positional scores; Copeland does pairwise tournament wins; Maximin picks the candidate with best worst pairwise margin. *Quick check:* How does Copeland transform individual rankings into a collective score?
- **Kendall Tau Correlation:** Primary evaluation metric; measures ordinal association between two rankings (higher = better recovery). *Quick check:* What does a Kendall Tau of 0.8 imply about two rankings?
- **Surprisingly Popular (SP) Framework & Second-Order Beliefs:** The theoretical foundation where a choice is "surprisingly popular" if it is selected more often than predicted by those who didn't select it. This identifies expert voters. *Quick check:* Why might an expert’s vote be ‘surprisingly popular’ relative to their own prediction?
- **Probabilistic Preference Models (Mallows, Plackett-Luce):** Used to model heterogeneous voter groups. Mallows has a dispersion parameter (ϕ; lower = more consensus); Plackett-Luce has strength parameters. *Quick check:* In a Mallows model, what does a low dispersion parameter ϕ indicate about a voter’s preferences?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Data layer (CSV/JSON with workerid, problem, treatment, vote, prediction, ground-truth) → Aggregation module (PartialSP function implementing Algorithm 1) → Evaluation suite (scripts for Kendall Tau, relative error computation).
- **Critical path:**  
  1. Load domain datapoints filtered by treatment and subset size.  
  2. Group by problem to get all voter responses per subset.  
  3. For each subset, compute local SP ranking using vote frequencies and prediction cross-probabilities.  
  4. Aggregate all local rankings into a global pairwise support matrix.  
  5. Apply final rule (Borda/Copeland/Maximin) to produce full ranking; evaluate against ground truth.
- **Design tradeoffs:** Higher elicitation cost for expressive formats (Rank-Rank) vs. information; computational overhead from building prediction matrices per subset (scalable for k~4–5); rule choice (Borda simple, Copeland/Maximin robust to noise but sensitive to ties).
- **Failure signatures:** SP-Voting underperforms if meta-predictions are degenerate or local SP rankings inconsistent; domain-specific failure (e.g., paintings) may stem from subjective ground truth; high relative error in probabilistic models indicates noisy non-expert predictions or model misspecification.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Replicate Table 1: For each treatment (e.g., Rank-Rank), run Partial-SP with Copeland on 4-alternative data; report full Kendall Tau vs. vote-only Copeland. Expect gains >0.4 points.  
  2. Replicate Table 3: Compute subset-level Kendall Tau by evaluating each local SP ranking against its subset’s ground truth; confirm consistent improvements.  
  3. Replicate Table 5: Fit a 2-group Mallows model to votes and predictions (e.g., Rank-Rank); compute relative error for dispersion/strength parameters; check expert parameters have lower error.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Generalizability beyond US MTurk and three domains:** Results may not hold for different cultural/demographic groups or other domains. Resolution requires replication with new domains (e.g., scientific literature) and diverse populations (e.g., global crowds, experts).
- **Utility when ground truth is ambiguous:** SP-Voting assumes a single objective truth. In subjective/ contested settings (art, policy), its value is unknown. Resolution: test against multiple plausible “ground truths” from different expert panels and compare stability/coherence with vote-only methods.
- **Dynamic preferences:** SP-Rank captures static snapshots. Real-world rankings change over time. Resolution: longitudinal studies tracking votes/predictions over time to see if second-order signals remain predictive and how SP-Voting adapts.
- **High errors in non-expert prediction parameters:** Probabilistic models show disproportionately higher errors for non-experts’ meta-predictions (e.g., ϕnep=0.49, snep=1.54 in Table 5). The cause (noise vs. bias) is unclear. Resolution: residual analysis correlating prediction error with participant accuracy, demographics, or format; simulation to test model assumptions.
- **Effect of task difficulty (rank proximity):** Dataset uses fixed 6-position gaps between alternatives. It’s unknown how closer ranks affect SP-Voting’s gains. Resolution: vary rank gaps (e.g., 1,3,6 positions) within the same global ranking and measure Tau gains across conditions.

## Limitations
- Missing experimental details (random seeds, tie-breaking, missing-data handling) block exact replication.
- Ground-truth sources (e.g., external databases) are undisclosed, limiting validation of objectivity.
- Weak external corpus support; results may be dataset-specific.
- Domain subjectivity (paintings) may destabilize ground truth and inflate variance.

## Confidence
- **Expert upweighting (Mechanism 1):** Medium; gains observed but the identifiability of minority experts is assumed, not directly tested.
- **Partial-SP lifting (Mechanism 2):** Medium; algorithm plausible but lacks theoretical guarantees; performance on sparse/large sets unknown.
- **Format generalization (Mechanism 3):** High; consistent gains across nine formats, including low-information cases.
