# Data Reconstruction: Identifiability and Optimization with Sample Splitting

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.08723
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08723
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** For two-layer polynomial networks (degree ≥3), margin samples are uniquely recoverable from KKT equations under a full-rank Gram matrix condition; sample splitting improves optimization of the nonconvex reconstruction problem and consistently boosts empirical reconstruction quality across multiple objectives.

## Executive Summary
Data reconstruction attacks aim to recover training samples from the parameters of a trained neural network. This paper tackles two core challenges: identifiability (when do the KKT conditions uniquely determine the training data?) and optimization (how to reliably solve the resulting nonconvex problem?). For two-layer networks with polynomial activations of degree at least three, the authors prove that if the neuron Gram matrix is full rank, then margin samples are uniquely recoverable from the KKT system. To address optimization, they propose sample splitting—a curvature‑aware refinement that splits samples along negative curvature directions to escape plateaus. The method is compatible with various reconstruction objectives (KKT‑based, multiclass, NTK‑based). Experiments on MNIST and CIFAR‑10 demonstrate consistent improvements in reconstruction quality (e.g., higher SSIM on CIFAR‑10, lower L2 on MNIST). Theoretical analysis shows convergence to an approximate second‑order stationary point.

## Method Summary
The attack formulates a reconstruction loss  
\[
L = \alpha_1 \bigl\| \theta - \sum_i \lambda_i f(\theta; x_i) \bigr\|^2 + \alpha_2 \sum_i \max(-\lambda_i,0) + \alpha_3 L_{\text{prior}},
\]  
where \(f(\theta; x_i)\) depends on the variant (e.g., for KKT‑based, \(f(\theta; x_i)=y_i \nabla_\theta \Phi(\theta; x_i)\)). Variables \(\{x_i\}, \{\lambda_i\}\) are optimized jointly via gradient descent. To improve optimization, sample splitting periodically computes a per‑sample splitting matrix  
\[
S(x_i) = -2\lambda_i \sum_p r_p \nabla_{x_i}^2 f_p(\theta; x_i),
\]  
approximates its minimum eigenvalue \(\lambda_{\min}(S(x_i))\) and eigenvector \(v_{\min}\) using Lanczos, and if \(\lambda_{\min} < \lambda_*\) (a negative threshold), replaces \(x_i\) with two offspring \(x_i \pm \eta v_{\min}\) and halves \(\lambda_i\). Line search chooses \(\eta\) to ensure loss decrease. The cycle repeats until the gradient norm \(\|\nabla_x L\| \le \epsilon\) and no sample exhibits negative curvature, yielding an approximate second‑order stationary point.

## Key Results
- **Identifiability theory:** For two‑layer networks with polynomial activations of degree \(\ge 3\), margin samples are uniquely recoverable from the KKT equations provided the neuron Gram matrix has full rank.
- **Optimization contribution:** Sample splitting provides a curvature‑aware mechanism to escape plateaus; the algorithm converges to an \(\epsilon\)-second‑order stationary point.
- **Empirical validation:** Across KKT‑based, multiclass, and NTK‑based reconstruction objectives, adding sample splitting consistently improves reconstruction quality (e.g., higher SSIM on CIFAR‑10, lower L2 on MNIST).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** For two‑layer networks with polynomial activations of degree ≥3, margin samples are uniquely recoverable from the KKT equations if the neuron Gram matrix is full rank.
- **Mechanism:** The KKT stationarity equations provide evaluations of a homogeneous degree‑\((\alpha-1)\) polynomial map \(f(w)\). Full‑rank Gram matrix \(K\) computed from neurons \((W_j)\) makes the interpolation problem for \(f\) well‑posed, uniquely determining \(f\) and the associated symmetric order‑α tensor \(T\). Standard tensor decomposition then reconstructs the active samples \(\{x_i,b_i\}\) almost uniquely up to permutation.
- **Core assumptions:** Activation degree \(\alpha\ge3\); active samples are independent and have unit norm; interpolation condition \(\operatorname{rank}(K)=N\) where \(N=\binom{d+\alpha-2}{\alpha-1}\).
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract]: Theorem statement and sample recovery claim.
  - [Section 4.2]: Detail of the interpolation argument, definition of \(K\) and \(f\).
  - [corpus]: Neighbors on KKT conditions in homogeneous networks support the underlying implicit‑bias theory (e.g., “Benign Overfitting in Linear Classifiers and Leaky ReLU Networks from KKT Conditions for Margin Maximization”).
- **Break condition:** If \(\operatorname{rank}(K)<N\) or if active samples are not independent, identifiability fails; reconstruction may be under‑determined or ambiguous.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Identifiability extends to non‑homogeneous polynomial activations (degree ≥3) under strong separability, via homogenization.
- **Mechanism:** For polynomial activation \(\sigma(t)=\sum_{k=0}^\alpha c_k t^k\), as gradient flow drives \(\|\theta_t\|\to\infty\), the direction converges to a KKT point of the max‑margin problem defined by the homogeneous top‑degree component \(\sigma_H(t)=c_\alpha t^\alpha\). This follows the homogenization argument of Cai et al. (2025) and reduces the setting to Mechanism 1.
- **Core assumptions:** Near‑homogeneity (Assumption 1), strong separability of the homogenized model (Assumption 2), width sufficiently large for the interpolation condition; gradient flow dynamics.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [Section 4.3]: Explicit connection to homogenization and citation of Cai et al. (2025).
  - [abstract]: States extension to non‑homogeneous activations.
  - [corpus]: Weak direct evidence; adjacent work on KKT‑informed networks (“KKT Condition‑Trained Neural Networks”) treats KKT conditions in training.
- **Break condition:** If the homogenized model cannot separate the data with positive margin or the interpolation condition fails, the limiting KKT system does not guarantee recovery; moreover, the homogenization limit may not apply if training does not drive \(\|\theta_t\|\to\infty\).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Sample splitting along negative curvature directions improves optimization of the nonconvex reconstruction objective by generating new descent directions and escaping plateaus.
- **Mechanism:** The algorithm maintains candidate samples \(\{x_i\}\) and weights \(\{\lambda_i\}\). In Phase II, for a sample with sufficiently negative minimum eigenvalue of the splitting matrix \(S(x_i)\) (a curvature proxy), it replaces \(x_i\) by two offspring \(x_i\pm\eta v_{\min}\) with halved weights. The second‑order decrease in reconstruction loss is proportional to \(\eta^2 \lambda_{\min}(S(x_i))\). Repeating this creates a finer representation of the data manifold.
- **Core assumptions:** Smoothness (Hessian‑Lipschitz bound \(\rho\), gradient Lipschitz bound \(\ell\)), accurate estimation of minimum eigenvalue via Lanczos, step‑size \(\eta\) chosen by line search; the splitting matrix captures relevant negative curvature directions of the full Hessian.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [Section 5.2]: Algorithm description, splitting rule, and objective form.
  - [Section 5.3]: Convergence proof to \(\epsilon\)-second‑order stationary point.
  - [Section 6]: Empirical improvements on MNIST/CIFAR across multiple reconstruction baselines.
  - [corpus]: No strong direct neighbor evidence; methods for escaping saddles in non‑convex optimization are common, but the specific sample‑splitting idea appears original.
- **Break condition:** If no sample exhibits \(\lambda_{\min}(S(x_i))<\lambda_*\) (no negative curvature detected), splitting never triggers and plateaus may persist; oversplitting can cause sample explosion and memory blowup if not capped.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Implicit bias of homogeneous networks**  
  - *Why needed here:* Reconstruction relies on the fact that gradient flow on homogeneous models converges to a KKT point of a max‑margin problem; this justifies using KKT equations for recovery.  
  - *Quick check question:* What max‑margin program corresponds to logistic loss on a homogeneous two‑layer network?
- **Concept: Tensor interpolation and orthogonal decomposition**  
  - *Why needed here:* Identifiability proof uses the fact that evaluations of a homogeneous polynomial on multiple points uniquely determine the polynomial (via the Gram matrix), and that the resulting high‑order tensor admits a unique orthogonal decomposition under generic conditions.  
  - *Quick check question:* When does a symmetric tensor admit a unique orthogonal decomposition?
- **Concept: Nonconvex escape via curvature splitting**  
  - *Why needed here:* Understanding why gradient descent stagnates and how negative curvature directions can be exploited requires basic second‑order optimization concepts.  
  - *Quick check question:* How does the eigenvalue of the Hessian relate to saddle points versus local minima?
- **Concept: Homogenization and near‑homogeneous functions**  
  - *Why needed here:* Extends identifiability beyond pure‑power activations to general polynomial activations; uses the limiting behavior of scaled networks.  
  - *Quick check question:* In a near‑homogeneous model, which component dominates the gradient dynamics at large \(\|\theta\|\)?
- **Concept: Unified reconstruction objectives**  
  - *Why needed here:* Sample splitting is designed to work with any reconstruction map \(f(\theta;x_i)\); mapping KKT, multiclass, and NTK attacks to this form clarifies applicability.  
  - *Quick check question:* For the KKT‑based method, what is \(f(\theta;x_i)\) in terms of model and label?

## Architecture Onboarding

Component map: Candidate manager -> Reconstruction core -> Optimizer -> Candidate manager; Splitting evaluator -> Candidate manager.

Critical path: 
1. Initialize candidates.
2. Phase I: gradient descent until \(\|\nabla_x L\| \le \epsilon\).
3. Compute eigenvalues of \(S(x_i)\) for all candidates.
4. If any \(\lambda_{\min} < \lambda_*\), for each such sample split: \(x_i \to x_i \pm \eta v_{\min}\), \(\lambda_i \to \lambda_i/2\).
5. Return to Phase I with enlarged candidate set.
6. Terminate when gradient norm \(\le \epsilon\) and no sample has negative curvature (i.e., \(\lambda_{\min} \ge \lambda_*\)).

Design tradeoffs:
- Splitting frequency vs stability: More frequent splitting speeds improvement but risks sample explosion; a cap on total samples (e.g., ≤50% growth) is essential.
- Eigenvalue approximation: Lanczos iterations trade accuracy vs cost; too few iterations may miss negative curvature.
- Step size \(\eta\): Large \(\eta\) may over‑perturb; too small yields negligible loss decrease; adaptive line search is recommended.
- Threshold \(\lambda_*\): Controls when to split; lower magnitude delays splitting but may get stuck.

Failure signatures:
- Loss stagnates for many iterations with no splitting trigger → eigenvalues likely miscomputed or threshold too conservative.
- Reconstruction quality degrades after splitting → splitting moved away from true solution; step size too large or splitting inappropriate for that sample.
- Candidate count grows exponentially → missing cap on splits per iteration or on total number.
- Phase I never reaches \(\|\nabla_x L\| \le \epsilon\) → optimization ill‑conditioned; reduce learning rate or check gradients.

First 3 experiments for a new engineer:
1. **Baseline replication:** Implement a standard KKT‑based reconstruction (Haim et al.) on MNIST binary classification with 200 samples, no splitting. Record final L2 distribution.
2. **Add splitting with default hyperparameters:** Enable splitting (trigger every 20000 iterations, \(\lambda_* = -0.1\), \(\eta_{\max} = 0.01\)). Compare per‑sample metric improvement fraction. Expect most samples improve or stay similar.
3. **Ablation on splitting step size:** Run with three \(\eta_{\max}\) values (0.001, 0.01, 0.1) and observe (a) final average metric, (b) sample count growth, (c) number of splits triggered. Identify sweet spot.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** For which classes of neural network architectures (beyond two‑layer homogeneous polynomial networks) and training dynamics do the KKT conditions uniquely determine the training data?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper’s identifiability analysis is restricted to two‑layer networks with polynomial activations of degree ≥3 under a neuron Gram matrix full‑rank condition (Theorem 4.1). The authors explicitly state in the discussion that extending identifiability results to deeper networks or more general activations is future work.
- **Why unresolved:** The identifiability proof critically relies on tensor decomposition properties and the structure of two‑layer networks. Deep networks introduce additional layers and nonlinearities that fundamentally alter the KKT system, making the existing analysis inapplicable without new theoretical tools.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A theoretical analysis showing that similar tensor‑based identifiability conditions hold for deep homogeneous networks, or conversely, a characterization of when identifiability fails in deeper settings (e.g., through counterexamples or necessary conditions).

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Under what conditions can approximate reconstruction (rather than exact recovery) be guaranteed when the KKT system is underdetermined or the neuron Gram matrix rank condition is violated?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper’s identifiability results assume an exact KKT system and a full‑rank Gram matrix. The discussion conjectures that when these conditions fail, samples associated with spectrally isolated eigenmodes might still be approximately reconstructible. This addresses partial or approximate reconstruction, which is not theoretically formalized.
- **Why unresolved:** The current theory provides only exact recovery guarantees under strong assumptions. Real‑world scenarios (e.g., noisy gradients, limited network width, or overparameterization mismatches) likely violate these conditions, but no framework exists for quantifying reconstruction quality in such regimes.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A robust identifiability theory that defines and analyzes approximate recovery (e.g., in terms of tensor eigenvalue separation or noise sensitivity) and provides explicit bounds on reconstruction error given deviations from the ideal assumptions.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Why does sample splitting consistently improve reconstruction quality across different objectives (KKT‑based, multiclass, NTK‑based), and what geometric properties of the reconstruction loss landscape does it exploit?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Sample splitting is introduced as a curvature‑aware refinement that creates additional descent directions to escape plateaus. The paper shows empirical improvements but only provides a local convergence analysis to approximate second‑order stationary points, without fully explaining the observed gains in reconstruction accuracy (e.g., higher SSIM, lower L2).
- **Why unresolved:** The convergence analysis guarantees that splitting helps escape directions of negative curvature but does not link this to improved recovery of the true training samples. It remains unclear how splitting interacts with the specific ill‑posedness of reconstruction (e.g., multiple equivalent solutions) or helps avoid spurious local minima.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Empirical studies correlating splitting‑induced curvature changes with reconstruction metrics, or theoretical analysis showing that splitting reduces the basin of attraction for incorrect solutions in the reconstruction objective.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How sensitive is the identifiability and reconstruction success to violations of the KKT conditions (e.g., due to finite training time, stochastic optimization, or early stopping)?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The entire reconstruction framework assumes the network parameters exactly satisfy KKT conditions for a maximum‑margin problem, which holds only in the limit of gradient flow for homogeneous networks. Real training uses finite steps, learning rates, and regularization, leading to approximate KKT points. The paper does not analyze robustness to such deviations.
- **Why unresolved:** Practical reconstruction attacks must deal with parameters that are near‑KKT but not exact. Small errors could dramatically affect the solvability of the reconstruction system, but no robustness analysis exists to guide how much deviation is tolerable.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Perturbation analysis of the KKT system: given a bound on the distance from exact KKT satisfaction, derive bounds on reconstruction error. Alternatively, empirical studies measuring reconstruction quality versus training duration or optimization noise.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Can sample splitting be theoretically connected to established optimization techniques for nonconvex problems (e.g., cubic regularization, trust‑region methods), and does it offer computational or practical advantages?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper relates splitting to escaping saddle points and mentions similarities to neural splitting methods. The convergence proof shows it achieves an approximate second‑order stationary point within \(O(\epsilon^{-2})\) iterations, comparable to cubic regularization. However, the splitting strategy is heuristic (based on per‑sample curvature matrices) and its global optimality is not established.
- **Why unresolved:** The analysis treats splitting as a black‑box refinement step and does not compare its theoretical complexity or convergence rates to standard second‑order methods. The practical advantage—such as lower per‑iteration cost or better scaling—is only demonstrated empirically on small datasets.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A rigorous comparison of splitting to cubic regularization or trust‑region methods in terms of iteration complexity, Hessian computation cost, and worst‑case convergence guarantees. Additionally, empirical scaling studies on larger networks and datasets to validate computational benefits.

## Limitations
- Identifiability assumptions (full‑rank Gram matrix, margin samples, polynomial degree ≥3) may not hold in practice, especially for non‑homogeneous activations.
- Sample splitting’s curvature approximation is heuristic and its convergence does not ensure reconstruction quality; hyperparameters are chosen heuristically.
- Experiments are limited to two‑layer networks on MNIST/CIFAR‑10; scalability to deeper architectures and computational overhead of Hessian/Lanczos remain unaddressed.

## Confidence
- Homogeneous identifiability: **High**
- Non‑homogeneous identifiability: **Medium**
- Sample splitting gains: **Medium**
