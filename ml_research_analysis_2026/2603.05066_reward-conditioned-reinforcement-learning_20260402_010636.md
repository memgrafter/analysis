# Reward-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.05066
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05066
- **Reference count:** 40
- **One-line primary result:** RCRL improves normalized performance across benchmarks, enables zero-shot adaptation (up to 40% of optimal), and speeds up finetuning (up to 90% after 250k steps) without extra environment interaction.

## Executive Summary
Standard RL agents with fixed reward functions are brittle when preferences change. Reward-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning (RCRL) solves this by conditioning both policy and value functions on sampled reward parameterizations during off‑policy updates. A single replay buffer collected under a nominal reward is reused across many reward objectives. Across 23 single‑task, 33 multi‑task, and 9 vision‑based benchmarks, RCRL outperforms SimbaV2, BRC, and DrQV2 baselines, delivers meaningful zero‑shot adaptation, and accelerates finetuning. The method provides scalable, steerable policies without additional environment interaction.

## Method Summary
RCRL collects experience using a nominal reward ψ\*. During training, each gradient update samples a batch of stored transitions and independently samples alternative reward parameterizations ψ ∼ P_Ψ. The scalar reward is recomputed from stored reward components. Both the policy π_θ(a|s, ψ) and critic Q_θ(s,a, ψ) take ψ as input, allowing off‑policy learning of multiple reward objectives from the same data. At test time, changing ψ steers behavior without further training. The approach requires no extra environment interaction and yields a single adaptable policy.

## Key Results
- Improves normalized performance versus SimbaV2, BRC, and DrQV2 across single‑task, multi‑task, and vision benchmarks.
- Enables zero‑shot adaptation to new rewards (up to 40% of optimal without extra training).
- Accelerates finetuning (up to 90% performance after 250k steps).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** RCRL improves sample efficiency by reusing single‑task interaction data to learn multiple reward‑specific behaviors.
- **Mechanism:** The agent collects experience under ψ\*. For each update, a replay batch is resampled with alternative ψ ∼ P_Ψ. The reward r_ψ is recomputed from stored components. Policy and critic are conditioned on ψ, enabling off‑policy learning across diverse reward objectives from the same buffer.
- **Core assumption:** The state‑action distribution induced by ψ\* provides sufficient coverage for learning value functions under alternative ψ (off‑policy stability despite distribution shift).
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “RCRL conditions the agent on reward parameterizations and learns multiple reward objectives from a shared replay data entirely off‑policy.”; [section 3] “All updates rely on replayed data generated under ψ\*, the procedure remains fully off‑policy.”
- **Break condition:** If alternative ψ induce optimal policies far outside the support of ψ\*’s state‑action distribution, learning may deteriorate due to insufficient data.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** RCRL enables zero‑shot behavioral adaptation to new reward specifications at deployment without additional training.
- **Mechanism:** Because networks are explicitly conditioned on ψ during training, they learn to map ψ to behavioral modes. At test time, simply changing the conditioning input ψ steers the agent to optimize the new reward, as experiments verify.
- **Core assumption:** The architecture and conditioning scheme are expressive enough to disentangle reward‑specific policies within a single network.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “enables zero‑shot adaptation (up to 40% of optimal without extra training).”; [section 4.3] “RCRL reliably adjusts its behavior to optimize the specified auxiliary reward… all such behaviors are learned fully off‑policy.”
- **Break condition:** Test‑time ψ outside the training range (e.g., extreme perturbations) may extrapolate poorly and yield low performance.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Auxiliary task conditioning leverages shared embodiment across tasks to improve learning, capturing benefits of multi‑task RL while maintaining single‑task data collection.
- **Mechanism:** For tasks sharing the same agent, rewards from all tasks are computed on transitions collected under the nominal task. The policy is conditioned on each task’s reward parameterization, effectively receiving N×(N−1) additional reward signals from N tasks without extra interaction.
- **Core assumption:** Tasks share underlying dynamics and state representations; trajectories from one task provide meaningful signals for others.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “improves performance under the nominal reward parameterization, but also enables efficient adaptation to new parameterizations.”; [section 3.1] “auxiliary task conditioning captures part of the benefit of multi‑task learning while keeping interaction constrained to the single task.”; [section 4.2] Multi‑task experiments show gains (e.g., BRC+RCRL reaches 75% performance faster).
- **Break condition:** If tasks are dissimilar (conflicting objectives or dynamics), auxiliary rewards may introduce noise or interference, potentially harming learning.

## Foundational Learning

### MDPs and Composite Rewards
- **Why needed here:** RCRL operates on MDPs where rewards are compositions r_ψ(s,a) = f(ψ, c₁,…,cₖ). Understanding ψ‑parameterization is key to grasping conditioning.
- **Quick check question:** Given a linear reward r = ψ₁·c₁ + ψ₂·c₂, how would you sample an alternative ψ by perturbing ψ*?

### Off‑Policy Reinforcement Learning
- **Why needed here:** RCRL reuses experience collected under one reward to train value functions for many rewards, requiring off‑policy methods that learn from data not generated by the current policy.
- **Quick check question:** Why might off‑policy learning with highly diverse reward scales cause instability, and how does the paper address it?

### Reward Conditioning in Neural Networks
- **Why needed here:** Policy and value networks must accept a reward parameterization ψ as input. The conditioning choice (e.g., concatenation vs learned embeddings) affects capacity and stability.
- **Quick check question:** When would you prefer embedding tables over direct concatenation of ψ?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Replay buffer -> Sample minibatch -> Conditioning sampler draws ψ -> Reward computation module computes r_ψ -> Form conditioned inputs [s, ψ] -> Actor‑critic networks (policy and Q‑value) -> Actions -> Environment -> Store new transitions in Replay buffer. (Optional: Embedding table provides ψ embeddings.)

### Critical path
1. Collect data: Execute π_θ(a|s,ψ*) in environment, store (s, a, s', c₁…cₖ).
2. Sample minibatch from replay buffer.
3. For each transition, independently sample ψ_i from P_Ψ = α δ_{ψ*} + (1−α)p_Ψ.
4. Compute r_ψ_i = f(ψ_i, c₁…cₖ) for that transition.
5. Form conditioned inputs z = [s, ψ_i], z' = [s', ψ_i].
6. Update critic: minimize TD error on (z, a, r_ψ_i, z').
7. Update actor: maximize Q_θ(z,a) or use policy gradient.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Parameterized vs. auxiliary conditioning:** Parameterized (continuous ψ) offers infinite variation but requires careful perturbation design; auxiliary (discrete tasks) is simpler but limited to predefined tasks.
- **Mixture weight α:** Higher α preserves nominal performance; lower α increases diversity but risks interference. Ablations show robustness around α=0.5.
- **Conditioning input representation:** Concatenation is simple but may not scale to large ψ spaces; embeddings add parameters but can learn compact representations.
- **Stabilization methods:** Large reward scale differences across ψ can cause gradient imbalance; countermeasures include reward normalization or distributional critics.

### Failure signatures
- **Ablation failure:** Removing ψ conditioning degrades performance by up to 40% (Figure 6 left).
- **Instability:** Extreme reward scale differences imbalance gradients; use normalization or distributional critics.
- **Poor transfer:** Zero‑shot adaptation fails if auxiliary ψ are not meaningfully different from ψ* or tasks are too dissimilar (e.g., some HumanoidBench pairs).
- **Data insufficiency:** Learning behaviors unsupported by ψ*’s state‑action distribution may require explicit exploration; RCRL does not alter the data distribution.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Conditioning necessity ablation:** Train RCRL with ψ input vs. without ψ input on a subset of HumanoidBench tasks; expect ~40% performance drop when conditioning is removed.
2. **Parameterized vs. auxiliary conditioning:** Compare both in single‑task (SimbaV2 on DMC) and multi‑task (BRC on HB) settings; expect comparable gains, with auxiliary excelling for shared dynamics.
3. **Mixture weight α sweep:** Vary α ∈ {0.1,0.3,0.5,0.7,0.9} on single‑task benchmarks; identify optimal near 0.3–0.5, with extremes reducing benefits.

## Limitations
- Off‑policy reuse assumes the nominal policy’s state‑action coverage is sufficient for alternative rewards; divergent ψ may be unsupported.
- Zero‑shot adaptation fails for ψ far outside training distribution; generalization boundary unclear.
- Auxiliary conditioning relies on task similarity; dissimilar tasks cause interference, and large reward scale differences may destabilize learning.

## Confidence
- Sample efficiency via shared data: Medium
- Zero‑shot adaptation: Medium
- Multi‑task benefit without extra interaction: Medium‑High
- Conditioning necessity (ablation): High
- Robustness to mixture proportion α: Medium

## Next Checks
1. **Coverage Stress Test:** Train RCRL with nominal ψ* and evaluate zero‑shot performance on rewards parameterized by ψ with increasing distance from ψ*. Identify the failure boundary to validate the coverage assumption.
2. **Task Dissimilarity Ablation:** Create multi‑task groups with controlled similarity (shared vs. distinct dynamics/reward components). Compare RCRL to independent policies and standard multi‑task RL to determine where auxiliary conditioning becomes detrimental.
3. **Stability under Extreme Reward Scales:** Apply extreme multiplicative scalings to alternative ψ components, monitor gradient norms/TD errors, and measure final performance. Test whether simple normalisation suffices or if distributional critics become necessary.
