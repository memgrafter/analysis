# A Boolean Function-Theoretic Framework for Expressivity in GNNs with Applications to Fair Graph Mining
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.12751
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.12751
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: The paper proposes Subpopulation Boolean Isomorphism (SBI) as a new expressivity invariant for GNNs, proves it strictly subsumes WL and biconnectivity, and introduces FairSBF—a training algorithm that traverses the Boolean circuit of an intersectional subpopulation to enforce fairness even for NC1-definable groups; on Pokec-z with an XOR-based subpopulation, FairSBF significantly reduces fairness gaps while preserving accuracy.

## Executive Summary
Current fairness‑aware GNNs rely on expressive power measures like the Weisfeiler–Lehman test or biconnectivity, which cannot differentiate subpopulations defined by high‑complexity Boolean functions (e.g., parity). This limitation causes such methods to fail on intersectional groups requiring non‑linear logical combinations of attributes. The paper addresses this gap by introducing Subpopulation Boolean Isomorphism (SBI), an invariant that models each subpopulation as a Boolean function and compares these functions across graphs. Theoretically, SBI is shown to strictly generalize WL, biconnectivity, and homomorphism‑based measures. The analysis also connects expressivity to Fourier degree, circuit complexity classes (AC0 vs. NC1), and influence. Based on this, the authors design FairSBF, an algorithm that, given a Boolean circuit representation of the target subpopulation, traverses all intermediate gates during training, computes the fairness loss for each corresponding subpopulation mask, and aggregates them into a single regularizer. This allows FairSBF to enforce fairness for any subpopulation definable by an NC1 circuit. Experiments on the Pokec‑z dataset, using an XOR‑based subpopulation, demonstrate that FairSBF reduces Demographic Parity Difference and Equalized Odds Difference markedly compared to baselines while maintaining higher classification accuracy.

## Method Summary
The FairSBF algorithm operates on a graph \(G\) with node features and a predefined Boolean circuit \(C_f\) that describes the sensitive subpopulation \(f\). The circuit is constructed from primitive attributes using AND, OR, NOT, and XOR gates; all intermediate gates (excluding leaves) define subpopulations via their Boolean outputs on each node. Training proceeds as follows: (1) Forward pass of a GNN (e.g., a two‑layer GCN) yields node embeddings and predictions \(\hat{y}\). (2) For each intermediate gate \(g\) in \(C_f\), the precomputed mask \(S_g\) identifies nodes where \(g\) evaluates to 1. If the mask is non‑trivial, the fairness gap \(\Delta_g = |\operatorname{mean}(\hat{y}[S_g]) - \operatorname{mean}(\hat{y})|\) is computed, and the sum over all gates forms the fairness loss \(L_{\text{fair}}\). (3) The total loss combines the standard cross‑entropy task loss and \(\lambda \cdot L_{\text{fair}}\). (4) Backpropagation updates the GNN parameters. The circuit traversal is agnostic to the specific gate set, enabling FairSBF to handle any NC1‑definable subpopulation. Hyperparameters (\(\lambda\), GNN depth, etc.) require tuning but the framework is flexible.

## Key Results
- SBI invariant strictly subsumes WL, biconnectivity, and homomorphism‑based expressivity measures, establishing a new theoretical foundation for GNN expressiveness.
- FairSBF theoretically supports any subpopulation expressible by an NC1 Boolean circuit; prior fair GNN methods are limited to AC0 or low‑degree functions.
- On Pokec‑z, with a XOR‑based intersectional subpopulation, FairSBF achieves significantly lower DDP and DEO while maintaining higher node classification accuracy compared to state‑of‑the‑art baselines.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core mechanism is the decomposition of a complex sensitive group into a hierarchy of simpler subpopulations via the Boolean circuit. By applying the fairness regularizer to each gate’s output, the GNN is forced to treat all intermediate subgroups equitably, which in turn ensures fairness for the final composed function. This traversal captures the logical dependencies among attributes, allowing the model to address parity‑like interactions that WL cannot distinguish because WL tests only local neighborhood structure, not the specific Boolean combination of attributes. Moreover, the use of an NC1‑complete circuit ensures that any subpopulation computable by a log‑depth threshold circuit can be represented, making FairSBF broadly applicable. The theoretical link between Fourier degree and expressibility indicates that low‑degree functions (e.g., AC0) are insufficient for parity, thus explaining why prior methods fail on such groups.

## Foundational Learning
- **GNN Expressivity (WL test, biconnectivity)**  
  *Why needed*: Baseline measures of what graph structures a GNN can distinguish; the paper shows these are insufficient for complex subpopulations.  
  *Quick check*: Can a 1‑WL‑equivalent GNN distinguish two graphs that differ in a parity‑defined node labeling? (Often no.)

- **Subpopulation Boolean Isomorphism (SBI)**  
  *Why needed*: Central invariant that captures whether a GNN can differentiate nodes based on a Boolean function of their attributes; generalizes existing measures.  
  *Quick check*: If two nodes yield different Boolean values under \(f\), does an SBI‑powerful GNN assign them different embeddings in the limit? (Yes, by definition.)

- **Circuit Complexity (AC0 vs. NC1)**  
  *Why needed*: Classifies the computational difficulty of subpopulation definitions; AC0 (constant‑depth circuits) cannot compute parity, while NC1 (log‑depth) can.  
  *Quick check*: Is the parity function in AC0? (No; it requires depth \(\Omega(\log n)\).)

- **Fourier Analysis on Boolean Functions**  
  *Why needed*: Relates the spectral degree of a Boolean function to its expressibility by GNNs; higher Fourier degree indicates greater complexity.  
  *Quick check*: The Fourier degree of the XOR of two variables is 2.

- **Influence (Boolean function)**  
  *Why needed*: Measures the expected change in output when flipping an input variable; serves as another barrier to expressibility.  
  *Quick check*: For a single attribute that fully determines the subpopulation, its influence is 1.

- **Fairness Metrics (DDP, DEO)**  
  *Why needed*: Quantify disparities in predictions across groups; the algorithm directly minimizes these gaps.  
  *Quick check*: DDP = 0 indicates equal predicted positive rates across sensitive groups.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Graph \(G\), node features \(X\) → **[GNN Encoder]** → node embeddings \(Z\) → **[Classifier]** → predictions \(\hat{y}\).  
Boolean circuit \(C_f\) (intermediate gates \(g\)) → **[Gate Traversal]** → subpopulation masks \(S_g\).  
\(\hat{y}\) + each \(S_g\) → **[Fairness Calculator]** → \(\Delta_g = |\operatorname{mean}(\hat{y}[S_g]) - \operatorname{mean}(\hat{y})|\).  
All \(\Delta_g\) summed → \(L_{\text{fair}}\).  
Task loss \(L_{\text{task}}\) (cross‑entropy) computed on labeled nodes.  
Total loss \(L_{\text{total}} = L_{\text{task}} + \lambda \cdot L_{\text{fair}}\) → **[Backprop]** → updates GNN parameters.

### Critical Path
The main training loop’s critical path is: (1) forward pass through the GNN to obtain \(\hat{y}\); (2) compute \(L_{\text{task}}\); (3) traverse the circuit to compute \(L_{\text{fair}}\) (depends on \(\hat{y}\)); (4) sum losses; (5) backpropagation. The circuit traversal is sequential over gates but typically inexpensive compared to the GNN forward.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **λ parameter**: Balances fairness vs. accuracy. Too high \(\lambda\) hurts accuracy; too low may not enforce fairness. Requires tuning on a validation set.
- **Circuit representation**: Depth and gate types influence which subpopulations are monitored. Including more intermediate gates yields finer‑grained fairness but may increase noise from trivial masks.
- **Handling trivial masks**: Gates with nearly constant outputs produce uninformative \(\Delta_g\); implementations often exclude masks smaller than 1% or larger than 99% of nodes, which can affect theoretical guarantees.
- **Computational overhead**: Traversing all gates each epoch adds overhead; however, for typical subpopulations (5–10 gates) this is negligible relative to GNN computation.

### Failure Signatures
- **Fairness gap does not decrease**: \(\lambda\) may be too low; verify masks are non‑trivial and circuit correctly represents the target subpopulation.
- **Accuracy drop below expected range**: \(\lambda\) may be too high; reduce \(\lambda\) or use a gradual schedule.
- **Unstable training (loss spikes)**: Learning rate too high; ensure masks are binary and correctly computed.
- **No improvement over baselines**: Subpopulation may be too easy (AC0) or circuit missing essential intermediate gates; revisit circuit design.

### First Experiments
1. **Replication and λ sweep**: Train FairSBF on Pokec‑z with the XOR‑based subpopulation for \(\lambda \in \{0.01, 0.1, 1, 10\}\). Record accuracy, DDP, DEO; identify best \(\lambda\).
2. **Comparison with an AC0 subpopulation**: Replace XOR with an AND of two sensitive attributes (AC0). Run FairSBF and prior fair GNN methods (if implemented) to see if all can achieve fairness; this validates the claim that prior methods fail on NC1.
3. **Ablation of intermediate gates**: Train a variant using only the final gate’s mask (i.e., \(S_f\)) for \(L_{\text{fair}}\). Compare to full FairSBF to assess the contribution of full circuit traversal.

## Limitations
- Hyperparameter choices (GNN depth, hidden size, \(\lambda\), learning rate, etc.) are not fixed in the paper, necessitating additional tuning that may affect reproducibility.
- Details of Boolean circuit construction (which gates are considered intermediate, inclusion of NOT gates, traversal order) are ambiguous, leaving room for variations that could change fairness loss behavior.
- Empirical evaluation is confined to a single dataset (Pokec‑z) and a single complex subpopulation, limiting confidence in the method’s generalizability across domains.
- The claim that prior methods cannot handle NC1 functions is primarily theoretical; comprehensive empirical benchmarking against those methods on diverse NC1 subpopulations is lacking.

## Confidence
- **Expressivity Claim (SBI subsumes WL, biconnectivity)**: High
- **FairSBF Effectiveness on XOR Subpopulation (Pokec‑z)**: Medium
- **FairSBF as the Only NC1‑Capable Fair GNN**: Medium
