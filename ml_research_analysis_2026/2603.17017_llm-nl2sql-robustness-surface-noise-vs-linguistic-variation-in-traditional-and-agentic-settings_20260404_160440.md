# LLM NL2SQL Robustness: Surface Noise vs. Linguistic Variation in Traditional and Agentic Settings
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.17017
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17017
- Reference count: 8
- Primary result: Surface noise (e.g., typos) degrades traditional NL2SQL pipelines more severely, while linguistic variation (e.g., back-translation) challenges agentic settings more; additional databases reduce agentic accuracy, especially when semantically similar.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates the robustness of large language model (LLM) based natural language to SQL (NL2SQL) systems against query perturbations. The authors introduce a benchmark with ten perturbation types, categorized as surface-level noise (e.g., character typos) and semantic-preserving linguistic variation (e.g., paraphrasing). Testing multiple LLMs (including GPT-5.2 and Claude-Opus-4.6) in both traditional single-pass and agentic multi-step settings reveals distinct failure modes: surface noise causes larger performance drops in traditional pipelines, while linguistic variation poses greater challenges for agentic systems. Moreover, adding extra databases, particularly semantically similar ones, further reduces agentic accuracy. The findings highlight persistent robustness gaps, especially in handling linguistic variability in interactive environments.

## Method Summary
The research constructs a robustness benchmark by applying ten query perturbations to samples from the Spider 1 (200 samples) and Spider 2.0-lite (135 samples) datasets. Perturbations include surface noise (ButterFinger, SwapCharacters, ChangeCharCase) and linguistic variation (back-translation, Chinese translation, tense inflection). Two evaluation paradigms are used: (1) traditional single-pass SQL generation with zero-shot prompting, and (2) agentic multi-step interaction via the Spider-Agent framework, which iteratively reasons and executes SQL. Execution Accuracy (EX) measures whether the query output matches the gold result. Experiments run on multiple LLMs (GPT-5.2, GPT-4.1, Claude-Opus-4.6, Gemini-3-Pro, Grok-4.1) with temperature 0, averaging over five runs. Agentic experiments also test the effect of adding extra databases, selected randomly or by similarity.

## Key Results
- Surface-level noise (ButterFinger, character swaps) causes the largest performance drops in traditional single-pass pipelines.
- Linguistic variation (back-translation, Chinese translation, tense changes) leads to more pronounced degradation in agentic multi-step settings.
- Adding extra databases, especially semantically similar ones, further reduces agentic accuracy, indicating sensitivity to schema selection noise.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Surface-level noise (e.g., typos) causes larger performance drops in traditional single-pass NL2SQL pipelines compared to agentic settings.
- **Mechanism:** Traditional pipelines generate a final SQL query in one step without execution feedback or iterative refinement. Character-level corruptions (ButterFinger, SwapCharacters) directly alter the lexical tokens the model must map to database schema elements, increasing the chance of mis-linking or syntax errors with no recovery path.
- **Core assumption:** The model's internal mapping from natural language tokens to schema/operators is brittle to non-semantic character changes when there is no external verification loop.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "...surface-level noise causes larger performance drops in traditional pipelines..."
  - [section 3.1]: "Across all models, performance drops the most under the ButterFinger... perturbations."
  - [corpus]: Neighbor papers (e.g., "Evaluating NL2SQL via SQL2NL") study linguistic variation but do not directly evidence this mechanistic split between settings.
- **Break condition:** If a traditional pipeline is augmented with a post-hoc validation step (e.g., simple syntax check or small-scale execution sandbox), the relative gap may narrow.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Linguistic variation (semantic-preserving lexical/syntactic changes) poses greater challenges in agentic settings than in traditional ones.
- **Mechanism:** Agentic systems perform multi-step reasoning with tool (database) interaction. Their intermediate steps (e.g., forming sub-queries, interpreting schema results) rely on consistent surface forms to maintain a coherent reasoning trace. Paraphrasing (back-translation, inflection changes) can subtly disrupt this trace, causing the agent to explore irrelevant schema paths or misinterpret intermediate feedback, leading to cascading errors.
- **Core assumption:** Agentic workflows are more sensitive to lexical consistency across turns than a single-pass generation, as they build stateful interpretations.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "...linguistic variation presents greater challenges in agentic settings."
  - [section 3.2]: "linguistic variations lead to noticeable performance degradation... Perturbations such as zh, bt, and infection changes cause larger drops."
  - [section 3.2, Figure 3]: "linguistic variations (zh, inflection, past, future) cause substantially larger performance drops" in the "all-perturbation" vs. "one-perturbation" comparison.
- **Break condition:** If agents use more robust, semantic-oriented intermediate representations (e.g., canonical intent trees) less dependent on surface form, the variation sensitivity may decrease.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Adding extra (especially semantically similar) databases in agentic settings reduces accuracy, introducing schema selection noise.
- **Mechanism:** Agentic systems must first identify the correct target database from a set. When extra databases are added, the schema selection component (often an LLM call) can be misled by name similarity, causing the agent to reason over an incorrect schema. This "schema clutter" noise is unique to interactive agentic environments where exploration is database-bound.
- **Core assumption:** The agent's initial database identification step lacks sufficient disambiguation robustness against semantically similar but irrelevant schemas.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 2.2]: "Adding unrelated databases reduces execution accuracy. For both models, similarity-based retrieval/selection is more adversely affected by database clutter than by random selection."
  - [section 2.2, Figure 4]: Shows accuracy drops as extra DBs are added, with sharper drops for "similarity" vs. "random".
  - [appendix C, Table 2]: Illustrates the "five most similar databases" used to create this clutter.
- **Break condition:** If the agent employs a more rigorous schema disambiguation step (e.g., probing queries to validate database choice) before full reasoning, the impact of extra DBs may be mitigated.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** NL2SQL Task & Evaluation Metric
  - **Why needed here:** Understanding the core task (translating natural language to executable SQL) and the primary metric (Execution Accuracy, EX) is essential to interpret why perturbations cause failures. EX measures if the *result* of running the generated SQL matches the gold, which is sensitive to both syntax and semantic correctness.
  - **Quick check question:** If a generated SQL is syntactically valid but joins the wrong table, will EX be 0 or 1? (Answer: 0, because the execution result differs).
- **Concept:** Perturbation Taxonomy: Surface Noise vs. Linguistic Variation
  - **Why needed here:** The paper's core analysis splits failures by perturbation type. Surface noise (typos, case changes) corrupts character sequences. Linguistic variation (back-translation, tense changes) preserves semantic intent but alters lexical/syntactic form. This distinction predicts different failure modes.
  - **Quick check question:** Is "ChangeCharCase" considered surface noise or linguistic variation? (Answer: Surface noise, as it alters form without guaranteed semantic preservation).
- **Concept:** Traditional (Single-Pass) vs. Agentic (Multi-Step) Paradigms
  - **Why needed here:** The key finding is that the *same* perturbation impacts the two paradigms differently. Traditional pipelines output a final query immediately. Agentic systems (like Spider-Agent using ReAct) iterate: generate query -> execute -> observe error/result -> refine. This fundamental architectural difference explains the divergent robustness profiles.
  - **Quick check question:** In an agentic setting, what is a "tool" typically used for? (Answer: The database executor, to run SQL and get results/feedback).

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  1.  **Input Processor:** Handles user query (applies perturbations in this benchmark).
  2.  **Schema Linker/Retriever:** (Traditional) Identifies relevant tables/columns. (Agentic) May also select the correct database from a set (especially with extra DBs).
  3.  **Core Generator:** LLM that produces SQL. In traditional: one-shot. In agentic: part of a loop, often alongside a "reasoning" step.
  4.  **Tool Interface:** Executes SQL on a database and returns results/errors. Unique to agentic setting.
  5.  **Controller/Orchestrator:** (Agentic only) Manages the loop: decides when to generate, execute, and refine based on tool feedback (e.g., ReAct framework).
- **Critical path:**
  - **Traditional:** Query -> Schema Linker -> (Query + Linked Schema) -> Generator -> Final SQL -> Execute (for evaluation).
  - **Agentic:** Query -> (Database Selection if multi-DB) -> Loop: [Reasoning/Generator -> Tool (Execute SQL) -> Observe Result] -> Final SQL. The loop repeats until success or max steps.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Noise Handling vs. Computational Cost:** Agentic systems can recover from some errors via iteration but are more costly and, per this paper, more sensitive to linguistic variation. Traditional systems are cheap but have zero recovery.
  - **Schema Selection vs. Reasoning Clarity:** Adding extra databases tests the agent's disambiguation capability but introduces noise that can derail reasoning. A cleaner schema focus (fewer DBs) may improve accuracy but reduces real-world complexity simulation.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Traditional + Surface Noise:** Sharp EX drop on ButterFinger/SwapCharacters. Failure likely in final SQL generation (syntax errors, wrong columns).
  - **Agentic + Linguistic Variation:** Gradual degradation across zh/bt/inflection. Failure manifests as getting stuck in wrong reasoning loops, generating SQL against irrelevant schema parts, or misinterpreting intermediate execution results.
  - **Agentic + Extra DBs (Similarity):** Accuracy drop correlates with number of added DBs. Failure signature: initial database selection step picks a wrong, similarly-named database, dooming all subsequent steps.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1.  **Reproduce Traditional Baseline:** Run the 200-sample R-NL2SQL-Tradition set with GPT-5.2 in zero-shot. Verify baseline EX ~90% (as implied) and the specific drop for ButterFinger and BackTranslation. This confirms the test harness and baseline numbers.
  2.  **Isolate Perturbation Impact (Agentic):** Run the 135-sample R-NL2SQL-Agentic set with Claude-Opus-4.6. Test *only* surface noise (ButterFinger, CharCase) vs. *only* linguistic variation (zh, bt, inflection). Quantify the delta between these two groups. This directly tests the core claim of differential sensitivity.
  3.  **Test Extra Database Noise:** Using the same agentic setup, run with the "similarity" extra-database strategy for 1, 2, and 5 added DBs. Plot accuracy vs. number of DBs. This validates the schema clutter failure mode and the paper's Figure 4.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can agentic NL2SQL systems be architected to maintain robustness against semantically-preserving linguistic variations (e.g., back-translation, inflection)?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "Future work could explore agentic configurations that mitigate this drop." (Limitations)
- **Why unresolved:** Agentic settings showed larger performance drops under linguistic variations (Figure 2), and current iterative reasoning/tool-use does not mitigate this sensitivity.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic A/B testing of novel agentic components (e.g., intermediate semantic normalization steps, robust prompt templates) on the linguistic variation subset of R-NL2SQL-Agentic, measuring EX differences.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can we develop an evaluation framework that enables true apples-to-apples comparison of robustness across traditional and agentic NL2SQL paradigms?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "An ideal analysis would provide an apples-to-apples comparison... the two benchmarks rely on different pipelines." (Limitations)
- **Why unresolved:** Findings are based on distinct datasets (Spider 1 vs. Spider 2), making it unclear whether observed differences (surface noise vs. linguistic variation) stem from paradigm or dataset difficulty.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Construct a single unified benchmark with controlled difficulty, then evaluate both paradigm implementations (traditional single-pass vs. agentic multi-step) under identical query perturbations.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Why does similarity-based selection of extra databases degrade agentic NL2SQL performance more severely than random database addition?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Figure 4 shows agentic accuracy drops more with "similarity" vs. "random" extra DBs, but the mechanism is not analyzed.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper introduces extra DBs as a robustness factor but does not investigate whether semantically similar databases create misleading schema-linking cues or increase search space complexity.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Analyze agent trajectories (tool calls, schema linking steps) in both conditions to identify failure patterns; test controlled ablation where similar DBs differ in specific structural properties.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What is the minimum viable perturbation suite to efficiently estimate NL2SQL robustness, given the observed variance and cost constraints?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Study averages over 5 runs due to cost and skipped comprehensive perturbation experiments for some models (e.g., GPT-4.1 agentic).
- **Why unresolved:** Full evaluation across all perturbations is expensive; the paper uses a subset but does not establish which perturbations are most predictive of real-world failure.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct a feature selection study correlating perturbation-specific accuracy drops with performance on a held-out set of real noisy queries; derive a reduced, high-signature perturbation set.

## Limitations
- The schema-linking method used in traditional pipelines is unspecified.
- Agentic configuration details (prompts, loop limits, tool descriptors) are lacking.
- Perturbation generation procedures (back-translation prompts, model versions) are not fully documented.

## Confidence
- Traditional pipelines more vulnerable to surface noise: Medium
- Agentic settings more vulnerable to linguistic variation: Medium
- Extra databases reduce agentic accuracy (similarity-based worse): Medium

## Next Checks
- Standardize and document the schema-linking component, then re-run traditional experiments to measure its contribution to robustness.
- Vary agentic loop parameters (max steps, retry policies) and prompt templates to assess configuration sensitivity.
- Implement perturbations with multiple methods (e.g., diverse back-translation models) and correlate perturbation severity metrics (e.g., character error rate, BLEU) with accuracy drops across settings.
