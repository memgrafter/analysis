# On the Learning Dynamics of RLVR at the Edge of Competence
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.14872
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14872
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Outcome‑based RLVR can train transformers for long‑horizon compositional reasoning with sparse rewards, but gains occur only near the model’s competence edge, exhibiting relay or grokking dynamics depending on task difficulty distribution.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the learning dynamics of outcome‑based reinforcement learning from verbal rewards (RLVR) when training transformers on compositional reasoning tasks with sparse terminal feedback. Using a minimal transformer with a frozen MLP pretrained to perform atomic group operations, the authors analyze REINFORCE on a synthetic task where the agent must compose a sequence of group actions to reach a target state. The difficulty is controlled by composition length \(L\). By applying Fourier analysis on finite groups, they derive explicit gradient expressions showing that the learning signal scales as \((\Delta_L)^L(1-\Delta_L)\), where \(\Delta_L\) is the margin between correct and incorrect token probabilities. This causes exponential flatness for long horizons unless shorter tasks first raise \(\Delta_L\). Two regimes emerge: with smooth difficulty spectra (small gaps between lengths) a relay effect emerges where easier tasks continuously boost harder ones; with abrupt gaps, learning experiences long plateaus followed by sudden phase transitions (grokking). The theory yields convergence bounds for plateau lengths and is validated on synthetic experiments. The findings explain RLVR’s edge‑of‑competence effectiveness and highlight data mixing as a key scalable lever.

## Method Summary
The study employs a theoretical–experimental approach. The model is a one‑layer transformer: inputs are \((position, symbol)\) pairs; symbols are embedded via an orthonormal map; a single attention layer (with query matrix \(Q\)) soft‑maxes over inner products between current answer position and prompt positions; the attended sum is fed to a frozen MLP with ReLU activations, pretrained to implement atomic skills (i.e., given \((state, action)\) it outputs high probability on the next state). Training REINFORCE from sampled rollouts with length‑normalized updates on \(Q\). The task distribution varies composition length \(L\); difficulties are mixed either as a single length or a set of lengths related by a ratio \(R\). The core of the analysis is to express the terminal reward as a high‑order convolution of per‑step action measures. Using Fourier analysis on finite groups, this convolution factorizes into a product of spectral operators, allowing a precise characterization of the gradient magnitude in terms of \(\Delta_L\) and \(\delta_L\) (noise margins). This yields two distinct dynamical regimes (relay vs. grokking) whose plateau lengths are bounded in terms of \(d\), \(C_B\) (atomic margin parameter), and \(R\). Synthetic experiments on group tasks confirm the predictions.

## Key Results
- Smooth difficulty spectrum leads to a relay effect: easier tasks generate persistent gradient signals that lift performance on harder tasks, resulting in steady continuous improvement across all lengths.
- Coarse difficulty spectrum causes grokking: after shorter tasks saturate, the gradient for the next length suddenly becomes effective, yielding a rapid jump to mastery after a prolonged plateau.
- Plateaus scale polynomially with model dimension \(d\): for abrupt gaps, \(T_{\text{plat}} \sim d^{C_B-1}\); for smooth mixtures, \(T_{\text{relay}} \sim d^{(1-C_B/(C_B+R))C_B-1}\), which is exponentially shorter for moderate \(R\).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Edge-of-Competence Relay Effect
**Claim:** RLVR improvement occurs when training tasks sit at the model’s competence edge, and the effect scales smoothly with a moderate difficulty ratio \(R\).  
**Mechanism:** Easier tasks produce gradients that amplify attention scores for correct transition tokens, strengthening the feature margin \(\Delta_L\) for those tasks. Because the policy is shared across horizons, a larger \(\Delta_{L_k}\) on shorter tasks \(\ell = L_k\) reduces the effective flatness \((\Delta_{L_{k+1}})^{L_{k+1}}\) for the next harder length, making its gradient non‑negligible even while the shorter tasks are still improving. This “relay” sustains continuous reward growth.  
**Core assumptions:** Smooth difficulty spectrum (\(L_{k+1}/L_k = O(1)\)), and auxiliary conditions \(p_{L,2}/p_{L,1} = o(1)\cdot(1-\Delta_L)\) and \(L^2\delta_L/\Delta_L = o(1)\cdot(1-\Delta_L)\).  
**Break condition:** The relay fails when the difficulty gap is too large (\(R = \omega(1)\)) because achieving the needed margin for the harder task requires \(\Delta_{L_k} \approx 1\) (saturation), making the gradient for \(L_{k+1}\) negligible during the plateau.

### Grokking via Exponentially Flat Gradient Barrier
**Claim:** With abrupt difficulty gaps, RLVR undergoes long plateaus followed by sudden phase transitions reminiscent of grokking.  
**Mechanism:** For a long‑horizon task, the policy gradient scales as \((\Delta_L)^L (1-\Delta_L)\). Near random performance, \(\Delta_L = O(1/d)\), so \((\Delta_L)^L = d^{-\Omega(L)}\) makes the gradient exponentially tiny. Even if occasional rollouts yield small reward, the signal is dominated by noise. Escape is only possible when \(\Delta_L\) grows enough that \((\Delta_L)^L\) ceases to be exponentially small—this typically happens after shorter tasks have saturated (\(\Delta_{L_k} \to 1\)), causing a rapid rise in reward.  
**Core assumptions:** Difficulty ratio \(R\) satisfies \(\omega(1) \le R \le L_{\max}/(2C_B)\); the fixed MLP provides atomic skills with margin \(B = C_B \log d\).  
**Break condition:** The plateau persists indefinitely if the gap exceeds what the atomic margin \(B\) can bridge; specifically, the required \(\Delta_{L_{k+1}}\) cannot be achieved until \(\Delta_{L_k}\) is exponentially close to 1.

### Fourier Analysis Long‑Horizon Decoupling
**Claim:** The compositionality of group actions lets the terminal reward be written as a high‑order convolution of per‑step action measures; in the Fourier domain this becomes a product of spectral operators, isolating the dominant aligned term \((\Delta_L)^L\) and making gradient estimation tractable.  
**Mechanism:** The trajectory condition \(u_L \circ \cdots \circ u_1 = G^*\) couples all steps. Fourier transform yields  
\[
(\mu_L * \cdots * \mu_1)(G^*) = \frac{1}{d} \sum_{\lambda \neq 1} d_\lambda \,\text{Tr}\!\left( \prod_{\ell=1}^L \hat{\mu}_\ell(\lambda) \,\lambda(G^*)^{-1} \right) + \text{constant}.
\]  
Each \(\hat{\mu}_\ell(\lambda) = \Delta_L \lambda(g_\ell) + \delta_L \sum_{g\neq g_\ell} \lambda(g) = \Delta_L \lambda(g_\ell) + \delta_L W_\ell(\lambda)\) splits into an aligned part and a residual. When \(\sigma_G \delta_L/\Delta_L \ll 1/L\) (with \(\sigma_G = \max_{\lambda\neq1} \max_\ell \|W_\ell(\lambda)\|_{\text{op}}\)), the product is dominated by \((\Delta_L)^L\) across all nontrivial irreps, and cross‑terms produce the \((1-\Delta_L)\) factor.  
**Core assumptions:** Group \(G\) is non‑abelian simple (ensuring spectral decay \(\gamma(G)<1\)); per‑step measures are identical due to symmetric initialization and data distribution.  
**Break condition:** For abelian or large‑center groups, \(\gamma(G)\approx1\) and residuals may dominate, violating \(\sigma_G \delta_L/\Delta_L \ll 1/L\); the clean \((\Delta_L)^L\) scaling then fails.

## Foundational Learning
- **Compositional Reasoning (Group‑Based State Tracking)**
  - Why needed: The core task requires composing \(L\) atomic group transitions to reach a final state; RLVR must learn this from terminal feedback alone. Hardness scales with \(L\).
  - Quick check: If the group action is simply transitive, how many distinct \(L\)-step trajectories lead to a given final state from a fixed initial state?
- **Policy Gradient (REINFORCE)**
  - Why needed: The paper studies REINFORCE with terminal reward; the gradient \(\nabla J = \mathbb{E}[r \cdot \nabla \log \pi]\) couples all steps because \(r\) depends on the full trajectory.
  - Quick check: What does the term \(\nabla \log \pi_\theta(by_L \mid y_0, G_L)\) decompose into over steps in autoregressive models?
- **Fourier Analysis on Finite Groups**
  - Why needed: It converts the high‑order convolution of per‑step action measures into a product of linear operators, enabling precise estimation of posterior probabilities. Without it, the combinatorial explosion would be intractable.
  - Quick check: For a function \(f: G \to \mathbb{C}\), what is the Fourier inversion formula that reconstructs \(f(g)\) from its Fourier coefficients \(\hat{f}(\lambda)\)?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Input \((position, symbol)\) → Embedding (orthonormal map to \(\mathbb{R}^{2d}\)) → Attention (single layer, query \(Q \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\), softmax over \(\langle Qx_a, x_p \rangle\)) → MLP (one hidden layer ReLU, fixed pretrained weights \(W\)) → Output (logits → softmax policy)
**Critical path:** Attention weights must peak on the aligned prompt token for each step so the MLP receives the correct \((g_\ell, by_{\ell-1})\) pair. If attention peaks elsewhere, the MLP’s large negative margins for other symbols suppress the correct next state. REINFORCE updates \(Q\) by \(\Delta Q \propto \sum_{\ell} \text{Attn}_{a,\ell-1\to p,\ell} \cdot \sum_{j,r} \sigma'(\Lambda_{j,r}) \cdot (\langle W_{j,r}, Z_{p,\ell}\rangle - \Lambda_{j,r})\) conditioned on successful rollouts.
**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Simplicity vs. realism: single attention layer and frozen MLP isolate composition dynamics but real LLMs are deeper and co‑adapt.  
- Group choice: non‑abelian simple group eliminates commutative shortcuts, analytically convenient but may not capture all reasoning patterns.  
- Pre‑train assumption: perfect atomic skills; in practice RLVR must also improve atomic abilities, adding complexity.
**Failure signatures:**  
- Uniform initialization (\(q \approx r\)) yields \(\text{Attn}_L \approx 1/L\), causing exponential flatness for \(L > 2C_B\).  
- Difficulty ratio too high: after mastering \(L_k\), gradient for \(L_{k+1}\) stays negligible until \(q\) reaches a threshold, creating long plateaus.  
- Mixture missing intermediate lengths: a jump from \(C_B\) to much larger lengths prevents the relay effect and may stall learning.
**First 3 experiments (from Section 7):**  
1. Fixed‑length RL (\(L=5,15,45\)): short horizons learn quickly, long horizons plateau, confirming the critical horizon barrier.  
2. Mixed‑length RL, \(R=3\) (\(L\) set \(=\{5,15,45\}\)): continuous reward improvement for all horizons demonstrates the relay effect with a smooth spectrum.  
3. Mixed‑length RL, \(R=7\) (\(L\) set \(=\{5,35\}\)): long horizon \(L=35\) remains near zero while \(L=5\) improves, illustrating the grokking plateau when difficulty gap is large.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: How do other dimensions of task difficulty (e.g., diversity of required atomic skills, semantic context shifts) affect RLVR’s effectiveness and edge‑of‑competence dynamics? The authors focused solely on composition length; real reasoning problems may vary in skill distribution and context. Unresolved because the theoretical model does not account for such variations. Evidence would require extending theory and experiments to multiple difficulty axes.  
- **Open Question 2**: Do the grokking and relay dynamics generalize to real‑world reasoning benchmarks, especially those involving non‑abelian group structures? Theory assumes non‑abelian simple groups, but experiments use abelian cyclic groups, raising generalizability concerns. Evidence: replicate with non‑abelian groups and test on benchmarks with inherent non‑commutative operations.  
- **Open Question 3**: How can the framework incorporate planning and search strategies? The current model assumes purely sequential deterministic composition without backtracking. Planning is a key missing reasoning pattern. Evidence: extend formalism to include search (e.g., Monte Carlo Tree Search) and test on tasks requiring deliberate search (puzzles, game trees).  
- **Open Question 4**: What are the optimal data mixing principles for RLVR training to maximize the relay effect? The authors identify data mixing as a scalable lever but leave optimal ratios, smoothness, or curricula open. Evidence: derive convergence times as a function of difficulty distribution smoothness and design adaptive sampling policies.

## Limitations
- Theoretical assumptions (large \(d\), non‑abelian simple group, exact feature separation) are unrealistic for real LLMs; simplifications include frozen MLP and single‑layer attention.
- Empirical validation is limited to synthetic group tasks, and the use of abelian cyclic groups contradicts the non‑abelian theory, weakening the theory‑experiment alignment.
- Break conditions (smoothness ratio \(R\), spectral factor \(\sigma_G\)) are asymptotic; their finite‑model thresholds and the effect of alternative RL algorithms remain unexplored.
