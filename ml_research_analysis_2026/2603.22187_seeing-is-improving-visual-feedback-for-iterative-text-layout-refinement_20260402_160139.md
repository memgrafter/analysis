# Seeing is Improving: Visual Feedback for Iterative Text Layout Refinement

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.22187
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22187
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: VFLM achieves state-of-the-art OCR Char-F1 (0.9376) and superior graphic metrics on three text layout benchmarks, outperforming advanced MLLMs, image editing models, and specialized layout models.

## Executive Summary
Existing layout generation methods follow a code-only paradigm, producing layout code without ever seeing the rendered visual result. This disconnect often yields unreadable or unaesthetic outputs. The Visual Feedback Layout Model (VFLM) closes this gap by establishing an iterative generate-render-reflect-refine loop: it produces SVG code, renders it to an image, uses a multimodal model to inspect the layout, and revises until satisfied. Training combines a two-stage pipeline—first, a cold-start supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on teacher-generated reflection trajectories to learn iterative reasoning; second, reinforcement learning with group-relative policy optimization (GRPO), using a reward model that evaluates final layout quality (OCR accuracy, graphic metrics, and GPT-4o scores). Outcome-only rewards, rather than complex process rewards, are shown to stimulate self-improvement. Across three text layout benchmarks, VFLM substantially outperforms state-of-the-art MLLMs (GPT-4o, Claude 3.7, Doubao-Seed-1.6, Qwen2.5-VL-72B), image editing models (GPT-4o edit, FLUX-Kontext), and specialized layout models (OpenCOLE, IGD). Notably, on TextLayout it achieves an OCR Char-F1 of 0.9376 vs the best prior result of 0.8672, and receives higher human ratings for text accuracy, harmony, quality, and meaning.

## Method Summary
VFLM tackles automated text layout generation by introducing a closed‑loop refinement process. The system takes a background image and target text as input and uses a policy model (Qwen2.5‑VL‑7B) to generate SVG code. This code is rendered to an image, which the model then inspects. If unsatisfied, it revises the code; the cycle repeats up to four times. Training proceeds in two stages: (1) Cold‑start SFT on 8 K multi‑round trajectories distilled from Doubao‑Seed‑1.6, teaching the model to reason about layout and produce valid SVG with a structured output format; (2) RL with GRPO, where a reward model (based on Qwen2.5‑VL‑3B) scores the final rendered layout using a composite of OCR accuracy, graphic metrics (Rali, Rove, Rcom, RM), and GPT‑4o ratings, plus a format bonus. Only the final outcome is rewarded, encouraging the policy to harness its visual understanding across multiple iterations. This two‑stage pipeline stabilizes RL and yields significant performance improvements over single‑pass and non‑visual approaches.

## Key Results
- VFLM achieves SOTA on three benchmarks (TextLayout, Crello, DESIGNERINTENTION), with OCR Char-F1 reaching 0.9376 on TextLayout compared to the best prior result of 0.8672.
- It consistently surpasses strong baselines: advanced MLLMs (GPT-4o, Claude 3.7, Doubao‑Seed‑1.6, Qwen2.5‑VL‑72B), image editing models (GPT-4o edit, FLUX‑Kontext), and specialized layout models (OpenCOLE, IGD) on graphic metrics (Rali, Rove, Rcom, RM) and human evaluation scores.
- The reward model attains 97.4% pairwise accuracy on the hierarchical preference dataset. Ablations confirm the necessity of visual feedback: removing iteration or relying on single‑round generation leads to substantial drops. Moreover, outcome‑only rewards outperform a carefully designed process reward, validating the training design.
- A human study corroborates automatic evaluations, finding VFLM’s outputs significantly better in text accuracy, visual harmony, and overall quality.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Closed‑Loop Visual Feedback
**Claim:** VFLM improves layout quality by iteratively generating, rendering, and reflecting on visual feedback.  
**Mechanism:** The policy model generates SVG code, which is rendered to an image. The same multimodal model then inspects the rendered layout, identifies flaws (e.g., text overlap, poor contrast), and revises the SVG accordingly. This cycle repeats up to a maximum of four iterations, enabling the model to correct initial errors.  
**Core assumption:** The MLLM can reliably perceive visual layout issues and translate them into effective SVG edits.  
**Evidence anchors:** The abstract and Section 3.1 formalize the loop (Algorithm 1); the success of VFLM in ablations (removing iteration hurts performance) supports the mechanism’s necessity.  
**Break condition:** If the model misperceives the rendered image (e.g., due to rendering artifacts), subsequent revisions may degrade the layout. Excessive iterations could also accumulate errors if the model fails to converge.

### Mechanism 2: Outcome‑Only Reinforcement Learning
**Claim:** Reinforcing only the final rendered layout successfully stimulates the model’s iterative and reflective generative capabilities.  
**Mechanism:** During RL, a reward model scores the final output of each complete generate‑render‑reflect cycle (after up to 4 iterations). The policy is updated via GRPO to maximize this scalar reward, forcing it to discover effective multi‑step refinement strategies without intermediate guidance.  
**Core assumption:** The policy’s inherent visual understanding can be harnessed by a clear final objective; complex process rewards might inadvertently constrain or mislead the learning.  
**Evidence anchors:** Section 4.5 and Table 4 show that outcome‑only RL yields higher OCR and graphic scores than process‑based RL, and avoids the instability seen with the latter. The abstract highlights this design choice.  
**Break condition:** If the reward model is misaligned (e.g., over‑emphasizing certain metrics), the policy may exploit spurious patterns. Early training instability is possible when initial generations are far from optimal, but the cold‑start SFT provides a stable baseline.

### Mechanism 3: Two‑Stage Training Pipeline
**Claim:** A warm‑start supervised fine‑tuning phase followed by RL provides an effective and sample‑efficient path to high‑quality iterative layout generation.  
**Mechanism:** Stage 1 distills multi‑round reflection trajectories from a teacher MLLM (Doubao‑Seed‑1.6) via SFT, teaching the model the iterative format and basic reasoning without requiring perfect initial layouts. Stage 2 then applies GRPO RL with the visual reward model to refine the policy’s layout generation quality. The SFT stage seeds the policy with a viable exploration space, making subsequent RL more stable and sample‑efficient.  
**Core assumption:** Starting from a model that already knows how to iterate (even if suboptimally) is easier for RL to improve than training a randomly initialized model from scratch.  
**Evidence anchors:** Section 3.2 describes cold‑start data collection; ablation results in Table 3 show that both SFT alone and “RL from pretrained” (no SFT) underperform the full pipeline, confirming the synergy.  
**Break condition:** Poor‑quality cold‑start data could imprint flawed reflection patterns that RL cannot correct; excessive KL regularization during RL might freeze improvement.

## Foundational Learning

**Concept 1: MLLMs with Tool Use and Structured Reasoning**  
- **Why needed:** The base model must understand both images and code, generate valid SVG, and produce coherent reasoning (including tool calls) within the iterative loop.  
- **Quick check:** Given a background image and target text, can the model output syntactically correct SVG that respects the image dimensions and embeds the text at the right positions?

**Concept 2: Reinforcement Learning with Outcome Rewards**  
- **Why needed:** RL optimizes the policy to maximize a learned reward that captures final layout quality. Understanding GRPO, advantages, and KL regularization is essential for stable training.  
- **Quick check:** How does GRPO differ from PPO, and why might group‑relative normalization improve stability when training multimodal policies with on‑policy rollouts?

**Concept 3: Visual Feedback Loops in Generative Design**  
- **Why needed:** Self‑correction relies on interpreting a rendered layout image—detecting overlap, contrast, alignment—and then generating appropriate SVG edits.  
- **Quick check:** What specific visual flaws should the model look for, and how might it articulate a corrective action (e.g., “reduce font size and shift left to avoid overlap”)?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Policy MLLM (Qwen2.5‑VL‑7B) generates SVG code and reflection thoughts; calls svg_to_image_tool to render → gets image; receives background image; decides to refine or output final answer. Reward model (Qwen2.5‑VL‑3B) scores tuples of (background, text, rendered image). GRPO trainer collects rollouts, computes group‑relative advantages, updates policy. Data pipeline: Teacher Doubao‑Seed‑1.6 creates cold‑start trajectories; hierarchical perturbations produce 1.2 M preference pairs for reward training.

### Critical Path
**Inference:**  
1. Input: background image I_b and target text T.  
2. Policy produces initial SVG S_0 and calls renderer → I_0.  
3. Policy inspects I_0, I_b; if unsatisfied, revises to S_1, repeats rendering and reflection (max 4 iterations).  
4. When satisfied, final SVG S_final is returned.

**Training:**  
1. Cold‑start SFT: finetune Qwen2.5‑VL‑7B on 8 K teacher‑generated multi‑round trajectories (2 epochs, LR=1e‑5).  
2. Reward model training: train Qwen2.5‑VL‑3B on hierarchical preference pairs (2100 steps, batch 512).  
3. RL with GRPO: initialize policy with cold‑start model; collect rollouts from 32 K queries; compute normalized advantages within rollout groups; update policy (batch 64, 8 rollouts per sample, LR=1e‑6, KL coeff=1e‑3).

### Design Tradeoffs
- Outcome‑only vs. process reward: Simplicity and effectiveness vs. risk of reward hacking if misaligned.  
- Shared vs. separate models: Using the same MLLM for generation and reflection simplifies the loop but may limit specialization; separate models allow independent tuning.  
- Reflection depth: More iterations (up to 4) improve quality but increase latency; the model learns to stop early when appropriate.  
- Cold‑start data size: Larger datasets improve coverage but increase distillation cost.  
- KL penalty coefficient: Balances reward maximization against staying close to the reference policy; too high can stall learning.

### Failure Signatures
- Reflection misses obvious flaws (no improvement after iterations).  
- RL training collapses or plateaus, especially with process rewards.  
- Reward model assigns high scores to severely degraded layouts, indicating misalignment.  
- SFT overfits to teacher patterns, causing brittle behavior on out‑distribution backgrounds.  
- Rendering inconsistencies (e.g., font fallback) produce visual feedback that does not match the intended SVG.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Main comparison**: Evaluate VFLM on three benchmarks (TextLayout, Crello, DESIGNERINTENTION) against MLLMs, image‑editing models, and layout models using OCR Char‑F1, graphic metrics (Rali, Rove, Rcom, RM), and GPT‑4o ratings. Expect VFLM to achieve state‑of‑the‑art results.  
2. **Ablation of visual feedback**: Remove iterative refinement (e.g., cold‑start only, single‑round RL, RL from pretrained without SFT, direct output). Expect significant drops in all metrics, confirming the necessity of the visual feedback loop.  
3. **Reward study**: Compare outcome‑only RL vs. process‑reward RL (using intermediate layout scores). Expect outcome‑only to achieve higher final quality and more stable training; also verify reward model accuracy (97.4% pairwise) and hierarchical consistency.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- **Visual feedback generality**: Is the iterative visual feedback loop universally necessary for graphic design tasks, or are there conditions where code‑only approaches suffice? The paper only evaluates text‑heavy layouts; broader task coverage is needed.
- **Reward model coverage**: What specific aspects of layout quality are captured by the current reward model? Possible missing dimensions include semantic coherence, emotional impact, or cultural context, which could affect human aesthetic judgment.
- **Outcome vs. process reward**: Why do outcome‑only rewards outperform complex process rewards in VFLM’s RL? The underlying reasons—model capacity, task difficulty, reward design—are not deeply explored, limiting generalizability.
- **Latency optimization**: How can the iterative nature of VFLM be optimized for real‑time applications? The paper does not address strategies to reduce computational overhead, leaving a gap for deployment in time‑sensitive scenarios.

## Limitations

- Dataset specifics (source, licensing, preprocessing) are not fully disclosed, hindering exact replication and bias analysis.
- The rendering tool implementation details (library, resolution, anti‑aliasing, font handling) are omitted; variations could alter visual feedback and OCR results.
- Reward model alignment is not thoroughly validated; despite high pairwise accuracy, its robustness to unseen distributions and potential overfitting to spurious features remain unclear.
- Computational cost and latency introduced by multiple iterations are not quantified, leaving real‑world deployment trade‑offs unexplored.

## Confidence

- **SOTA performance (beat GPT‑4o, Claude, etc.)**: High – extensive comparisons on three benchmarks with consistent gains.
- **Visual feedback essential**: High – ablations show dramatic quality drops when iteration or visual perception is removed.
- **Outcome‑only reward superiority**: Medium – supported by one process reward design; alternative formulations might behave differently.
- **Two‑stage pipeline (cold‑start SFT + RL) necessity**: Medium – ablations indicate benefit, but sensitivity to data size and hyperparameters not fully tested.

## Next Checks

1. *Reward model robustness evaluation*: Generate adversarial layouts with subtle flaws (e.g., minor text overlap, low contrast) and measure whether reward scores align with human perceptual quality.
2. *Cross‑renderer generalization test*: Replace the proprietary SVG renderer with an open‑source alternative (e.g., CairoSVG) and quantify performance changes to identify renderer‑specific biases.
3. *Reflection depth ablation*: Systematically vary the maximum number of iterations from 1 to 6; track OCR Char‑F1, graphic metrics, and inference time to determine the point of diminishing returns and potential over‑refinement.
