# Procedural Knowledge at Scale Improves Reasoning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2604.01348
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01348
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Reasoning Memory improves accuracy by up to 19.2% over no retrieval and up to 7.9% over compute-matched test‑time scaling across multiple benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the limitation of test‑time scaling in reasoning models, which typically discards procedural knowledge from previous trajectories. They introduce Reasoning Memory, a RAG‑based framework that extracts a large corpus of subquestion‑subroutine pairs from public reasoning traces. At inference, the model’s current subquestion is used to retrieve relevant subroutines, which are then injected as in‑context priors. By sampling diverse trajectories with these subroutines and filtering based on uncertainty, the system selects final answers. Evaluations on math, science, and coding benchmarks with open‑weight models show consistent and significant improvements over both no‑retrieval and compute‑matched baselines.

## Method Summary
Reasoning Memory constructs a datastore of 32 million self‑contained procedural units (subquestion‑subroutine pairs) by decomposing existing long reasoning trajectories. During inference, a “thought‑hijacking” prompt causes the model to verbalize its current subquestion, which serves as a query for a dense retriever to fetch relevant subroutines. These subroutines are provided as in‑context examples to guide the generation of subsequent reasoning steps. Multiple candidate trajectories are sampled under different retrieved subroutines. A length‑based uncertainty filter selects the final answer. The approach thus combines retrieval of procedural priors with test‑time scaling through diverse sampling.

## Key Results
- Up to 19.2% absolute accuracy gain over no‑retrieval baseline.
- Up to 7.9% gain over compute‑matched test‑time scaling (more sampling) baseline.
- Outperforms document‑level RAG, trajectory‑level RAG, and template RAG baselines across six benchmarks with three open‑weight models (8B–32B).
- Gains scale with larger datastores and inference budgets, particularly with diversity‑first budget allocation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Reasoning Memory improves performance by reusing successful procedural strategies captured in past trajectories. The decomposition into subquestion‑subroutine pairs creates a library of fine‑grained reasoning steps that can be retrieved and applied to new problems. This allows models to bypass the need to reinvent common techniques (e.g., problem reframing, verification) from scratch. The diversity‑first sampling ensures that multiple viewpoints are explored, while the uncertainty filter picks answers from trajectories that converge, reducing the risk of outliers. Essentially, the system proxies a form of episodic memory for reasoning, augmenting the model’s innate capacity with externalized procedural knowledge.

## Foundational Learning
*RAG (Retrieval‑Augmented Generation)*
- **Why needed**: Provides external knowledge to overcome model memorization limits and support up‑to‑date or domain‑specific information.
- **Quick check**: Can the system incorporate new data without retraining? Yes, by updating the retrieval corpus.

*Subquestion Decomposition*
- **Why needed**: Breaks complex problems into manageable, self‑contained units that can be solved independently and reused.
- **Quick check**: Are subproblems sufficiently decoupled? Check if solving one depends on solving another; ideally they are independent.

*Dense Retrieval*
- **Why needed**: Finds semantically relevant subroutines for the current subquestion using embeddings, enabling flexible matching beyond keyword overlap.
- **Quick check**: Does the retriever return relevant results for known subquestions? Evaluate with ground truth relevance judgments.

*In‑Context Learning*
- **Why needed**: Allows the model to condition on retrieved examples without parameter updates, leveraging few‑shot transfer.
- **Quick check**: Does providing retrieved subroutines in the prompt change model outputs? Compare with no‑context baseline.

*Test‑Time Scaling (Sampling Diversity)*
- **Why needed**: Increases the chance of finding a correct solution by exploring multiple reasoning paths.
- **Quick check**: Does increasing number of samples improve performance? Typically yes, but with diminishing returns.

*Uncertainty Estimation via Length Filter*
- **Why needed**: Identifies confident answers by selecting those from trajectories that terminate with consistent lengths, implying convergence.
- **Quick check**: Do shorter or consistent‑length trajectories correlate with higher accuracy? Can be validated by plotting length vs. correctness.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Problem -> Subquestion Extract (via thought‑hijacking prompt) -> Dense Retriever -> Retrieved Subroutines -> Generator (with in‑context subroutines) -> Candidate Trajectories -> Length‑Based Uncertainty Filter -> Final Answer

**Critical path:** The system’s core flow is: extract the current subquestion, retrieve relevant procedural units from the 32M datastore, generate multiple reasoning continuations using these units, and select the answer from the most confident trajectory.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Datastore size vs. retrieval latency: larger corpus improves coverage but slows retrieval; they use approximate nearest neighbor search.
- Diversity vs. relevance: sampling diverse subroutines can yield creative solutions but may also include noisy examples; balance via diversity‑first budget allocation.
- Subroutine granularity: finer decomposition increases reuse but risks missing context; coarser units may retain context but reduce flexibility.

**Failure signatures:**
- Irrelevant retrieval: retrieved subroutines do not match the current subquestion, leading to misguided reasoning.
- Incomplete subroutines: extracted units lack necessary steps or assume hidden context, causing breakdowns.
- Non‑convergent trajectories: length filter fails to identify correct answers, e.g., when correct paths vary in length.

**First three experiments:**
1. Build a small prototype datastore (e.g., 100k pairs) and verify that retrieval for a held‑out set returns plausible subroutines (by human inspection or automated metrics like embedding similarity to ground truth).
2. Compare single‑trajectory generation with and without retrieval on a subset of benchmarks to confirm the immediate impact of providing subroutines.
3. Test the diversity‑first budget allocation against random allocation to measure its effect on final accuracy with a fixed inference budget.

## Limitations
- Risk of data contamination from using public corpora that may overlap with test benchmarks, potentially inflating results.
- Quality of extracted subroutines is heuristic; incomplete or context‑dependent units may degrade performance, but this is not thoroughly analyzed.
- Generalization to domains beyond math/science coding is unproven, as is effectiveness with closed‑source models that have stronger innate reasoning.
- The system reuses past episodes rather than acquiring abstract skills, leaving unclear whether it leads to lasting capability improvement.

## Confidence
- Effectiveness of RAG‑based procedural retrieval: **High** (consistent empirical gains across models and benchmarks).
- Superiority over simple test‑time scaling (more sampling): **Medium** (depends on diversity‑first allocation and datastore quality).
- Generalizability of the “procedural unit” concept: **Low** (limited to evaluated benchmarks; transferability unverified).

## Next Checks
1. Conduct experiments with strictly holdout problems (e.g., newly released competition math problems) to assess contamination effects.
2. Perform a human or high‑accuracy model audit of retrieved subroutines, categorizing them by correctness and self‑containment, and correlate with success rates.
3. Evaluate on an out‑of‑domain task such as strategic games or narrative reasoning to test cross‑domain transfer.
