# Scaling Reasoning Efficiently via Relaxed On-Policy Distillation

## Quick Facts

- **arXiv ID**: 2603.11137  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11137  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: REOPOLD stabilizes on-policy distillation for reasoning models, achieving 6.7×–12× improved sample efficiency and enabling a 7B student to match a 32B teacher with 3.32× inference speedup.

## Executive Summary

On-policy distillation, which transfers knowledge from a teacher to a student by minimizing log-likelihood differences on student-generated data, suffers from instability and negative transfer—especially when scaling reasoning capabilities across model sizes. This paper recasts the problem as policy optimization, interpreting token-level teacher-student log-likelihood ratios as rewards. Three key relaxations are introduced: (1) mixture-based reward clipping to cap extreme negative rewards, (2) entropy-guided token-level dynamic sampling to focus learning on high-uncertainty tokens, and (3) a two-phase exploration‑to‑refinement training schedule that initially suppresses harsh penalties before applying entropy-based masking. Empirical evaluations across mathematical, visual, and agentic reasoning tasks show that REOPOLD dramatically improves sample efficiency—outperforming recent RL‑based methods by 6.7× to 12×—and enhances test‑time scaling, with a 7B student matching a 32B teacher’s accuracy while delivering a 3.32× speedup.

## Method Summary

The method, REOPOLD, is applied to three domains: mathematical reasoning (using students DeepSeek‑R1‑Distill‑Qwen‑1.5B/7B and teachers SkyWork‑OR1‑Math‑7B/SkyWork‑OR1‑32B‑Preview), visual reasoning (students Qwen2.5‑VL‑3B/7B, teacher Qwen2.5‑VL‑32B‑Instruct), and agentic reasoning (student Qwen2.5‑VL‑3B, teacher Pixel‑Reasoner‑7B). Training data comes from OpenR1‑Math‑46k (math), Geometry3K (visual), and PixelReasoner‑RL (agentic). The core algorithm (Algorithm 1) computes a token‑wise reward as the stop‑gradient log‑likelihood ratio between teacher and student. This reward is clipped to the range [log(λ/(1−λ)), log((1−λ)/λ)] with λ=0.3 to mitigate outliers. Then a token mask M is generated: during the initial exploration phase (first T_switch steps) tokens are masked if their reward exceeds a threshold, while later (refinement phase) masking is based on entropy—tokens with entropy below the β‑th percentile (β=0.2) are excluded. Student updates use group‑relative policy gradient (via Verl/VerlTool) with AdamW. Hyperparameters (learning rate, batch size, maximum length, etc.) are task‑specific and provided in Table 8 and Appendix C.

## Key Results

- Sample efficiency: REOPOLD outperforms recent RL‑based methods (e.g., GRPO) by **6.7×–12×** on reasoning benchmarks.  
- Test‑time scaling: A 7B student model attains the same accuracy as a 32B teacher on visual reasoning tasks while being **3.32×** faster at inference.  
- Stability: REOPOLD consistently surpasses vanilla on‑policy distillation (RKL), exhibiting less training variance and higher final performance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

REOPOLD’s stability and efficiency gains arise from three synergistic relaxations. **Mixture‑based reward clipping** prevents a few large negative rewards from dominating the policy gradient, which is a common cause of divergence in on‑policy distillation. **Entropy‑guided token‑level dynamic sampling** directs the update toward tokens where the student is most uncertain (high entropy), thereby increasing the signal‑to‑noise ratio of the gradient; low‑entropy tokens often carry negligible learning signal because the student is already confident and the reward magnitude is small. The **two‑phase exploration‑to‑refinement schedule** decouples early exploration from later refinement: in the exploration phase the mask is reward‑based, allowing the student to experience a broad range of token outcomes without harsh penalties; once a stable policy is established, the refinement phase uses entropy‑based masking to focus on fine‑grained improvements. Together these techniques mitigate negative transfer, stabilize training, and make more efficient use of limited student capacity.

## Foundational Learning

- **On‑policy distillation** – The student generates responses that are used as the context for computing the divergence to the teacher; needed to avoid distribution shift. Quick check: Student samples should match the distribution seen during training.  
- **Log‑likelihood ratio as reward** – Transforms the teacher‑student difference into a token‑level feedback signal for policy gradient. Quick check: Rewards should be zero‑mean and roughly symmetric.  
- **Reward clipping** – Caps extreme negative rewards to a bounded range defined by λ, preventing gradient explosion. Quick check: Monitor clipping fraction; adjust λ if >5 % of tokens are clipped.  
- **Entropy‑guided dynamic sampling** – Masks tokens below an entropy threshold, focusing learning on uncertain regions. Quick check: Track average entropy and masked fraction; ensure refinement fraction is neither too sparse nor too noisy.  
- **Two‑phase training schedule** – Separates exploration (reward‑based mask) from refinement (entropy‑based mask) via a switch point T_switch. Quick check: Plot validation accuracy vs. step; expect a smooth transition after T_switch.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Teacher Model -> Log‑likelihood computation -> Reward module (mix & clip) -> Entropy calculator -> Token mask generator -> Student Model -> Policy gradient update (Verl) -> Evaluation loop

### Critical path
1. Load student and teacher checkpoints.  
2. For each batch, generate student responses (on‑policy).  
3. Compute token‑wise teacher and student log‑likelihoods; form stop‑gradient ratio as reward.  
4. Apply mixture‑based clipping.  
5. Compute token entropy; generate mask M based on current phase (exploration vs. refinement).  
6. Apply mask to rewards and compute group‑relative advantage.  
7. Perform a policy gradient step on the student using the masked advantages.  
8. Repeat until T_switch, then switch to refinement masking.  
9. Periodically evaluate on held‑out benchmarks.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Reward clipping λ**: Larger λ reduces clipping but risks instability; smaller λ increases bias toward the teacher.  
- **Entropy threshold β**: Lower β yields more masked tokens (faster but potentially lossy); higher β retains noisy tokens.  
- **Two‑phase vs. single‑phase**: Two‑phase adds scheduling complexity but improves stability; a single adaptive phase may be simpler but less robust.

### Failure signatures
- Sudden loss spikes or gradient norms exploding (indicates insufficient clipping or masking).  
- Masked fraction >90 % for extended periods (over‑masking leads to underfitting).  
- Plateauing or declining validation accuracy after T_switch (entropy mask too aggressive).

### Three first experiments
1. Ablate reward clipping (set λ → ∞) and observe training stability degradation.  
2. Replace entropy‑guided sampling with uniform token masking and measure sample efficiency.  
3. Replicate REOPOLD with Llama‑2‑7B/13B on GSM8K/MATH and compare against GRPO to verify gains on open‑source models.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **Convergence guarantees** – Under what conditions does REOPOLD guarantee convergence? The paper establishes gradient equivalence but provides no convergence analysis.  
2. **Adaptive hyperparameters** – Can λ, β, T_switch be scheduled adaptively instead of using fixed values?  
3. **Generalization beyond reasoning** – Does REOPOLD generalize to standard instruction following or dialogue tasks?  
4. **Causal role of entropy** – What is the causal mechanism by which high‑entropy tokens improve learning efficiency? Could alternative uncertainty measures work better?

## Limitations

- **Access to teacher models and training data** – Teachers like SkyWork‑OR1‑32B‑Preview and datasets such as OpenR1‑Math‑46k may be restricted, hindering exact replication.  
- **Underspecified entropy calculation** – The exact scope (full vocabulary vs. sampled tokens) and padding handling are not detailed, which can affect refinement behavior.  
- **Task‑specific hyperparameters** – Values for λ, β, and T_switch might need manual tuning for each new domain, limiting out‑of‑the‑box applicability.
