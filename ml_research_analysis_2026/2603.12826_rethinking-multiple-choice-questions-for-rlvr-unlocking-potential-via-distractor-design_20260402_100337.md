# Rethinking Multiple-Choice Questions for RLVR: Unlocking Potential via Distractor Design
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.12826
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12826
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Iterative Distractor Curation (IDC) improves RLVR accuracy by ~3.2% on medical benchmarks and reduces spurious rewards, enabling effective training even with binary-choice MCQs.

## Executive Summary
Reward hacking in Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) occurs when models exploit superficial patterns in multiple‑choice questions (MCQs) instead of engaging in genuine reasoning. This work identifies two key problems: mismatches in the number of options between training and testing degrade performance, and weak distractors allow models to guess correctly despite flawed reasoning. To address these, the authors propose Iterative Distractor Curation (IDC), a framework that repeatedly strengthens distractors using the model’s own response statistics, thereby blocking elimination shortcuts. Evaluated on medical benchmarks, IDC yields consistent accuracy gains (~3.2%) for both Qwen2-7B and Llama-3.1-8B, significantly lowers spurious reward rates, and requires only self‑generated distractors—avoiding reliance on external stronger models.

## Method Summary
The method combines standard RLVR with a novel distractor‑curation pipeline. Models are trained using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with the RLOO advantage estimator, using fixed hyperparameters: learning rate 1e‑6, KL coefficient 0.001, entropy 0.001, batch size 128, 8 responses per question, sequence length 2048, and a warm‑up of 10 steps. The key innovation is IDC, which operates as follows: for each training MCQ, empirical distractor strength estimates are obtained by sampling 8 responses from the base model. Over T = 7 rounds, the algorithm either (a) fills missing slots by generating candidates with a generator model (Qwen2.5‑32B‑Instruct) that achieve strength > 0, or (b) replaces the weakest existing distractor with a candidate that has higher strength. A semantic equivalence check prevents degenerate distractors. Two training runs are then performed—one on the original MCQs and one on the IDC‑augmented set—and final checkpoints are evaluated on held‑out medical benchmarks with consistent option counts.

## Key Results
- Mismatches in option count between training and testing lead to monotonic performance degradation; accuracy peaks when counts align (Δ = 0) and declines as |Δ| grows.
- Strong distractors reduce spurious reward rates, making RLVR effective even for 2‑way questions.
- IDC improves average accuracy by ~3.2% on medical benchmarks (e.g., Qwen2‑7B: 39.39% → 42.62%; Llama‑3.1‑8B: 48.63% → 51.90%) and significantly lowers spurious reward rates, while self‑generated distractors match or surpass those from external stronger models.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Performance degrades when training and testing option counts differ because the policy overfits to the distribution of choice counts seen during training.  
**Mechanism:** The model learns implicit patterns tied to the number of choices (e.g., label biases, guessing strategies). At test time, a differing option count breaks these patterns, impairing decision‑making.  
**Core assumption:** The policy adapts its output distribution to the option space encountered during training, creating a distributional bias that does not transfer across counts.  
**Evidence:** Accuracy peaks at Δ=0 and declines monotonically as |Δ| increases; controlled experiments confirm the alignment effect.  
**Break condition:** Training on a mixture of option counts or using architectures invariant to option count (e.g., normalized embeddings) may eliminate the mismatch effect.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Strong distractors improve the quality of the RLVR reward signal by reducing spurious rewards, even in binary‑choice settings.  
**Mechanism:** Weak distractors are easily eliminated via superficial reasoning, allowing the model to select the correct answer despite flawed chains (high s(O−)). Strong distractors compete for probability mass under incorrect reasoning, causing flawed chains to more often produce wrong selections (lower s(O−)), which yields accurate negative feedback.  
**Core assumption:** The probability of spurious reward Pr[a=o∗|c∈C−] depends on distractor strength; lowering this probability yields cleaner training signals.  
**Evidence:** Removing strong distractors reduces accuracy below random‑baseline levels; adding them restores performance. Empirical tables show that increasing option count and applying IDC both reduce spurious reward rates.  
**Break condition:** If models develop reasoning robust to distractor quality (e.g., via verifier ensembles or formal verification), the marginal benefit of strong distractors may diminish.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Iterative Distractor Curation (IDC) enables models to self‑improve distractor quality without external supervision by iteratively replacing weak distractors with self‑generated ones that have higher empirical strength.  
**Mechanism:** IDC estimates distractor strength using the model’s own choice frequencies. It then iteratively (a) fills missing slots with candidates that achieve strength > 0 and (b) replaces the weakest distractor if a new candidate has higher strength. This rejection sampling in option space progressively increases the number of effective distractors, enhancing the contrastiveness of the reward signal.  
**Core assumption:** Self‑generated distractors better align with the model’s current error patterns and decision boundary than those from stronger external models, providing more targeted learning signals.  
**Evidence:** IDC yields +3% average accuracy; self‑generated distractors match or exceed those from models like DeepSeek‑V3.1 and GPT‑OSS‑120B on most benchmarks.  
**Break condition:** Semantic collapse (distractors becoming equivalent to the correct answer) or failure to increase difficulty beyond a certain model capability may stall or degrade the process.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Base LLM (policy) → GRPO trainer → Option expansion/curation pipeline (generator + evaluator + semantic check) → Cross‑option evaluation suite.  
Key models: policy (e.g., Llama‑3.1‑8B), generator (can be same or stronger like Qwen2.5‑32B), evaluator (policy itself for strength estimation).

### Critical Path
1. Preprocess MCQ dataset to desired option counts (including expansion if needed).  
2. Train policy with GRPO on raw or curated MCQs.  
3. For IDC: iterate distractor generation → strength evaluation → replacement/filling.  
4. Retrain policy on IDC‑curated data.  
5. Evaluate on held‑out benchmarks with consistent option counts.

### Design Tradeoffs
- *Self vs external generator:* Self reduces dependency and cost but may limit strength if model capability is low; external stronger models may produce out‑of‑distribution distractors.  
- *Iteration count T:* More rounds increase distractor strength but with diminishing returns; computational overhead scales linearly.  
- *Option‑count strategy:* Mixed‑option training offers robustness when test distribution is unknown but may dilute specialization.

### Failure Signatures
- High semantic collapse rate in generated distractors (detected via equivalence prompt).  
- No reduction in spurious reward rate after curation.  
- Overfitting to curated option set: strong performance on matched test but collapse on mixed/alternative counts.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Cross‑option generalization matrix:** Train on fixed option count (e.g., mcq2, mcq4, mcq10) and test on all counts to verify mismatch effect. Expected: diagonal dominance.  
2. **Distractor strength ablation:** For a fixed stem, choose distractors by lowest/highest strength estimates and compare RLVR performance. Expected: high‑strength distractors yield best accuracy.  
3. **Full IDC pipeline vs baselines:** Apply IDC to training data and compare against direct conversion, filtering, and model‑based rewriting. Expected: IDC consistently outperforms on in‑domain and OOD medical benchmarks.

## Limitations
- Evaluation is limited to medical benchmarks; findings may not generalize to other domains or question types.  
- Exact RLVR training schedule and MedQA split preprocessing are unspecified, preventing full replication.  
- The scalability of IDC beyond 7 iterations and to more capable models remains uncertain.

## Confidence
- Option‑count mismatch degradation: **Medium**  
- Strong distractors reducing spurious rewards: **Medium**  
- Iterative Distractor Curation (IDC) self‑improvement: **Medium‑High**

## Next Checks
1. **Cross‑domain replication:** Apply the IDC pipeline to non‑medical MCQ datasets (e.g., MMLU‑Pro subsets) with varying option counts to verify generalization of mismatch effects and accuracy gains.  
2. **Distractor strength calibration:** Validate the empirical strength estimator via human judgments or alternative metrics on a sample to confirm correlation with true deception.  
3. **Break‑condition probing:** Train with mixed‑option counts or use embedding normalization to test whether either eliminates the option‑count mismatch, directly challenging Mechanism 1.
