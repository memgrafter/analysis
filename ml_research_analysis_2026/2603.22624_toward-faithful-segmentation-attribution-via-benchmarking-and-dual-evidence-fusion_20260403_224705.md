# Toward Faithful Segmentation Attribution via Benchmarking and Dual-Evidence Fusion

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.22624
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22624
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** DEA improves Target Deletion Drop from 0.223 to 0.381 on SBD and 0.287 to 0.449 on VOC over gradient-only baselines while reducing off-target leakage and preserving robustness, at the cost of higher compute.

## Executive Summary
Segmentation attribution methods are often judged only by visual plausibility, lacking rigorous evaluation of causal faithfulness or off-target leakage. This paper introduces a reproducible benchmark that measures target deletion faithfulness, absolute off-target leakage, perturbation robustness, and runtime across Pascal VOC and SBD using three segmentation backbones. It proposes Dual-Evidence Attribution (DEA), which fuses fine-grained gradient evidence (EGA) with coarse but causally validated region intervention evidence (RIA) via agreement‑weighted multiplication and residual addition. Across all benchmark runs, DEA consistently improves deletion‑based faithfulness over gradient‑only baselines on both datasets—for instance, increasing Target Deletion Drop from 0.223 to 0.381 on SBD and from 0.287 to 0.449 on VOC—while reducing off‑target leakage and keeping robustness comparable. However, DEA incurs a significant computational overhead due to multiple intervention passes. The benchmark reveals a fundamental faithfulness‑stability tradeoff among attribution families that is invisible to visual inspection, positioning DEA as a practical correction that shifts gradient methods toward intervention‑aligned behavior.

## Method Summary
The authors define a standardized evaluation framework for pixel‑level attribution in semantic segmentation. The benchmark uses Pascal VOC 2012 and the Berkeley BDD (SBD) datasets; images are resized to 224 × 224. Three pretrained segmentation models serve as backbones: DeepLabV3‑ResNet50, FCN‑ResNet50, and LRASPP‑MobileNetV3. Four attribution methods are compared: Gradient‑Pooled Attribution (GPA), Elementwise Gradient Attribution (EGA), Region Intervention Attribution (RIA), and the proposed Dual‑Evidence Attribution (DEA). DEA combines EGA and RIA via the formula  
\[
A_f = \alpha A_g \odot (1 + \beta A_r) + (1-\alpha) A_r,
\]  
with default hyperparameters \(\alpha = 0.65\), \(\beta = 0.35\). RIA partitions the image into a \(14\times14\) grid, replaces each cell’s pixels with the dataset mean, rescales the image, and measures the drop in target confidence to assign importance; these grid values are interpolated to a full‑resolution heatmap. EGA backpropagates the target confidence to a feature layer and multiplies gradients by activations elementwise. Both heatmaps are normalized to \([0,1]\) before fusion. Primary metrics are: Target Deletion Drop (TDD) – the drop in confidence when the top‑\(k\) (\(k=0.2\)) attributed pixels within the target mask are occluded; Absolute Off‑target Leakage (LeakAbs) – a normalized combination of TDD and Off‑target Deletion Drop (ODD); insertion gain; perturbation robustness (average correlation under noise, brightness, contrast, blur, and flip corruptions); and wall‑clock runtime. Each configuration is run with two random seeds; no model retraining is performed.

## Key Results
- DEA substantially increases Target Deletion Drop compared to gradient‑only methods, e.g., on SBD from 0.223 to 0.381 and on VOC from 0.287 to 0.449.
- DEA reduces off‑target absolute leakage relative to EGA while maintaining robustness comparable to EGA.
- The benchmark uncovers a faithfulness‑stability tradeoff that is not apparent from visual inspection alone.
- DEA introduces a significant computational overhead (10–20× slower than EGA) due to multiple region interventions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

1. **Dual‑evidence fusion improves faithfulness while preserving robustness.**  
   DEA combines fine‑grained gradient evidence \(A_g\) and coarse intervention evidence \(A_r\) via  
   \(A_f = \alpha A_g \odot (1+\beta A_r) + (1-\alpha) A_r\).  
   The multiplicative term amplifies pixels where both sources agree, while the residual addition retains causally supported evidence even when gradient signals are unstable. The core assumption is that agreement between gradient and intervention signals indicates true causality, and that gradient‑only attributions are noisy and prone to feature co‑occurrence artifacts. Evidence includes the abstract’s description of agreement‑weighted boosting and Section 3.2’s equation. A break condition occurs if the intervention grid is too coarse (introducing blocky artifacts) or if gradient signals are uniformly weak (limiting amplification).

2. **Benchmark metrics expose a faithfulness‑stability tradeoff.**  
   TDD measures the confidence drop when the top‑k attributed pixels inside the target mask are occluded; ODD does the same for off‑target pixels; LeakAbs combines them into a normalized tradeoff score. These intervention‑based metrics directly test causal dependence, revealing that intervention methods (like RIA) are highly faithful but unstable, while gradient methods are stable but less faithful—a tradeoff invisible to visual plausibility evaluation. Evidence: abstract and Section 3.3 definitions. A break condition arises if the mean‑value occlusion operator alters global semantics (confounding causal effects with dataset statistics) or if noisy target masks degrade metric reliability.

3. **Complementarity of gradient and intervention evidence.**  
   EGA provides pixel‑level detail but suffers from instability and off‑target leakage due to feature co‑occurrence. RIA offers stable, causally validated but coarse maps. DEA’s fusion leverages EGA’s resolution and RIA’s causal signal, balancing the two. Evidence: abstract, Section 3.2, and Section 5.1’s tradeoff analysis. Break conditions: thin structures may be missed by a coarse grid; ambiguous pixels where only one evidence source is present may be mishandled depending on \(\alpha,\beta\).

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**
- EGA module: backpropagates target region confidence to a selected feature layer, multiplies gradients by activations elementwise, normalizes to [0,1] → \(A_g\).
- RIA module: divides input into grid cells (default 14×14), for each cell replaces pixels with dataset mean value, rescales image, recomputes target confidence, records drop; interpolates grid values → \(A_r\) (also normalized).
- Fusion module: applies \(A_f = \alpha A_g \odot (1+\beta A_r) + (1-\alpha) A_r\) with fixed \(\alpha=0.65\), \(\beta=0.35\).
- Evaluation: computes TDD, ODD, insertion, robustness (perturbation correlation), runtime.

**Critical path**
1. Forward pass → logits → per‑class softmax.
2. Compute target region confidence \(s_c(x,M)\).
3. EGA: backprop \(s_c\) to layer, compute \(A_g\).
4. RIA: loop over grid cells, mask, rescale, compute new \(s_c\), deltas → \(A_r\).
5. Normalize both maps to [0,1].
6. Fuse → \(A_f\).
7. For metrics: extract top‑k pixels from \(A_f\), apply occlusion, recompute \(s_c\), calculate drops; for robustness, perturb input and correlate heatmaps.

**Design tradeoffs**
- *Speed:* RIA/DEA cost O(grid cells) forward passes; DEA slower by ~10–20× vs EGA.
- *Resolution:* EGA gives pixel‑level detail; RIA resolution tied to grid; DEA inherits EGA’s resolution but multiplication may blur edges.
- *Stability:* EGA robust; RIA sensitive to co‑occurrence and grid alignment; DEA aims to smooth RIA’s instability while retaining its causal signal.
- *Grid size:* Smaller grid improves RIA fidelity but increases compute and may overfit noise; larger grid reduces compute and smooths but loses detail.

**Failure signatures**
- High off‑target absolute drop: Persistent leakage, often when gradient noise dominates or intervention grid misses thin structures.
- Low TDD: Attributed target pixels not causal, may indicate failed fusion or poor gradient signal.
- High variance across seeds: Unstable methods, especially RIA.
- Visual: Blobby or blocky maps, missing fine object boundaries, residual off‑target hotspots in cluttered scenes.

**First 3 experiments**
1. **Main benchmark comparison:** Run DEA, EGA, GPA, RIA on Pascal VOC and SBD with three backbones. Measure TDD, ODD, insertion, robustness, runtime. Expect DEA to significantly increase TDD over EGA/GPA (e.g., SBD 0.223→0.381) while keeping robustness comparable to EGA.
2. **Ablation over fusion parameters:** Sweep \(\alpha\) in [0.5,0.8] and \(\beta\) to verify TDD remains above EGA baseline; observe tradeoffs with leakage and robustness.
3. **Grid‑size sensitivity:** Compare DEA and RIA with grids 7×7, 14×14, 21×21 to quantify impact on faithfulness (TDD) and compute time; expect finer grids to improve RIA faithfulness but at higher cost, with DEA mitigating uneven gains.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Formal hypothesis testing:** How can statistical significance testing be integrated into the segmentation attribution benchmark? The authors note that current uncertainty reporting relies on run‑level variability, not formal tests.
- **Adaptive intervention strategies:** What adaptive partitioning methods can improve sensitivity to thin structures and fine boundaries? The paper identifies the fixed‑grid limitation and proposes adaptive schemes as future work.
- **Computational cost reduction:** How can the computational cost of region‑level interventions be reduced to enable real‑time or large‑scale use? The paper highlights DEA’s overhead and calls for faster intervention‑grounded attribution.

## Limitations
- Mean‑value occlusion may alter global semantics beyond local removal, confounding causal effects with dataset statistics.
- Fixed fusion parameters (\(\alpha=0.65, \beta=0.35\)) might not generalize across datasets, backbones, or tasks.
- Evaluation limited to Pascal VOC/SBD with ResNet50 backbones; coarse 14×14 grid in RIA/DEA can miss thin structures, limiting broader applicability.
