# Who Wrote the Book? Detecting and Attributing LLM Ghostwriters
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.28054
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.28054
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: TRACE (lightweight fingerprinting) achieves SOTA on multi-class LLM authorship attribution for long-form texts, with macro-F1 up to 0.58 on OOD settings, and perfect human vs. LLM separation.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the challenge of attributing long-form texts to specific large language models (LLMs), moving beyond binary AI detection and addressing the scarcity of suitable datasets. The authors introduce GhostWriteBench, a dataset comprising 325 books (each with over 50,000 words) generated by 10 frontier LLMs, with out-of-distribution splits across domains (unseen genres) and authors (unseen models). They also propose TRACE, a lightweight, interpretable method that creates a fingerprint by modeling token-level transition patterns (such as word rank or entropy) estimated by a small evaluator LM. TRACE operates post‑hoc, requiring no access to model internals, and therefore works for both open‑ and closed‑source LLMs. On GhostWriteBench, TRACE achieves state‑of‑the‑art performance, significantly outperforming baselines in OOD and low‑resource scenarios. For example, in a high‑resource OOD‑author setting, the entropy‑based TRACE variant reaches a macro‑F1 of 0.58, while N‑GRAM and DETECTIVE obtain 0.28 and 0.49 respectively. TRACE also shows perfect separation (F1=1.0) between human and LLM authors in an unseen human OOD test and demonstrates robustness across different evaluator LMs.

## Method Summary
TRACE is a post‑hoc, model‑agnostic fingerprinting technique. It processes a text with a lightweight evaluator LM (e.g., GPT‑2) to assign each token a score—either its rank (position in the evaluator’s probability‑sorted vocabulary) or its entropy (negative log‑likelihood). From the sequence of scores, TRACE builds a 2D transition histogram (counts of consecutive score pairs). This histogram is compressed into a small grid: rank scores are binned into equal‑probability clusters (using a power‑law distribution), while entropy scores are smoothed via kernel density estimation. The resulting fingerprint serves as a statistical signature. At attribution time, the test fingerprint is compared to a set of reference fingerprints (one per training book or aggregated per LLM) using Jensen–Shannon divergence (for both variants) or the norm‑mean distance (for entropy). A calibrated threshold determines whether the closest reference is accepted as the predicted author or the text is rejected as unknown. TRACE requires no fine‑tuning and only needs the text itself; it works for both open‑ and closed‑source LLMs. The GhostWriteBench dataset provides standardized train/test splits with OOD domain (unseen genres) and OOD author (unseen LLMs) partitions, enabling robust evaluation.

## Key Results
- On GhostWriteBench’s high‑resource OOD‑Author split, entropy‑TRACE achieves macro‑F1 0.58, beating N‑GRAM (0.28) and DETECTIVE (0.49).
- TRACE maintains robustness across multiple evaluator LMs (GPT‑2, OLMo, Gemma) with no major performance loss.
- In the unseen human OOD test, TRACE perfectly separates human from LLM authors (F1 1.0).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** TRACE attributes LLM authorship by capturing distinctive token‑level transition patterns (e.g., rank or entropy shifts) that reflect an LLM’s word‑selection preferences.
- **Mechanism:** For a given text, an evaluator LM (e.g., GPT‑2) assigns each token a score (rank or entropy). TRACE builds a 2D histogram of consecutive scores and compresses it (binning for rank, KDE for entropy). The compressed fingerprint is compared to reference fingerprints via Jensen‑Shanon distance or norm‑mean.
- **Core assumption:** Different LLMs exhibit characteristic transition patterns in their generated text that remain stable across domains and are robust to content variations.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [Abstract] “TRACE creates the fingerprint by capturing token‑level transition patterns (e.g., word rank) estimated by another lightweight language model.”
  - [Section 4] Formal definitions and Algorithms 1 and 2 detail the fingerprint construction for rank‑ and entropy‑based variants.
  - [corpus] The neighbor corpus lacks transition‑based fingerprinting for multi‑class OOD attribution; related work (e.g., GLTR, watermarking) uses single‑token metrics, underscoring the novelty of modeling transitions.
- **Break condition:** If target LLMs converge on similar decoding strategies or the evaluator LM’s scores correlate poorly with the target’s word choices, the transition patterns may lose discriminative power.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Using a lightweight, fixed evaluator LM normalizes token scores across diverse target LLMs, enabling black‑box attribution without requiring target model internals.
- **Mechanism:** The evaluator LM computes token‑level statistics (rank: position in sorted vocabulary by probability; entropy: negative log‑likelihood sum) solely from the text. These scores serve as a common reference frame for all candidate LLMs.
- **Core assumption:** The evaluator LM’s token distribution, while independent of the target LLM, yields scores that reflect the target’s stylistic preferences when aggregated over transitions.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [Abstract] “TRACE operates post‑hoc, and so it works for both open‑ and closed‑source LLMs.”
  - [Section 4.1] “For each token ti, we compute its rank ri under an evaluator language model L̂θ.”
  - [corpus] Prior methods (e.g., GLTR) also use token‑level metrics but typically for binary detection; TRACE extends their use to transition modeling and OOD settings.
- **Break condition:** If the evaluator LM’s tokenizer or training data is too dissimilar from the target LLMs (e.g., different vocabulary, domain), the computed scores may not align with the target’s selection biases.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The compressed 2D fingerprint representation and robust similarity metrics (JS distance, norm‑mean) preserve discriminative information while enabling effective matching even with limited training data.
- **Mechanism:** Raw transition counts are high‑dimensional and sparse; they are compressed into a small grid (e.g., 50×50). Fingerprint similarity is measured by Jensen‑Shanon divergence (for both variants) or norm‑mean (for entropy), which treats the fingerprint as a probability distribution or a surface.
- **Core assumption:** Compression retains the essential distributional characteristics of the transition patterns, and the chosen similarity functions capture stylistic similarity reliably.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [Section 4] “We compress it by binning ranks into equal probability clusters” and “use kernel density estimation” for entropy.
  - [Section 6.1] Entropy‑based TRACE (norm‑mean) reaches macro‑F1 0.58 on high‑resource OOD‑Author, substantially outperforming N‑GRAM (0.28) and DETECTIVE (0.49).
  - [corpus] Neighbor papers do not explore this specific combination of transition‑based fingerprinting and OOD evaluation.
- **Break condition:** If fingerprint resolution is too low, discriminative details are lost; if too high, sparsity harms matching. Also, inappropriate similarity metrics (e.g., L2 distance on sparse counts) could degrade performance.

## Foundational Learning
### Authorship Attribution (AA) as Multi‑Class Classification
- **Why needed here:** The paper frames the problem as attributing a text to one of many LLM authors, not merely detecting AI vs. human. This multi‑class setup is essential for accountability in ghostwriting scenarios and introduces challenges like open‑set generalization.
- **Quick check question:** “What are the key differences between binary AI detection and multi‑class authorship attribution, and why does OOD evaluation matter more for the latter?”

### Token‑Level Probability Metrics (Rank and Entropy)
- **Why needed here:** TRACE’s fingerprints are built from token ranks or entropy computed by an evaluator LM. Understanding these metrics is necessary to grasp how stylistic information is extracted without model internals.
- **Quick check question:** “How does token rank differ from entropy as a measure of an LLM’s word choice? What might each capture about the model’s behavior?”

### Open‑Set Recognition and OOD Generalization
- **Why needed here:** The paper emphasizes performance on unseen authors (OOD‑Author) and unseen domains (OOD‑Domain), which are open‑set scenarios where test authors may not appear in training. This reflects realistic deployment for ghostwriting detection.
- **Quick check question:** “What is open‑set recognition and why is it a more realistic setting for LLM attribution than closed‑set evaluation?”

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
1. **Data Generation Module** – Uses LLM APIs to produce long‑form books via an outline→segment pipeline (Appendix A.1).
2. **Cleaning & Splitting** – Removes boilerplate, splits data into train/test/dev with per‑author genre partitions for OOD‑Domain and author‑holdout for OOD‑Author.
3. **Fingerprint Construction** – For each text, uses an evaluator LM (e.g., GPT‑2) to compute token scores, builds a transition matrix, and compresses it (rank binning or entropy KDE).
4. **Reference Storage** – Stores one fingerprint per training text (or aggregated per LLM) for similarity lookup.
5. **Attribution Engine** – Computes test fingerprint, compares to all references using JS distance or norm‑mean, applies a calibrated threshold to predict the LLM or reject.
6. **Evaluation** – Computes macro‑F1, confusion matrices, and performs ablations.

### Critical Path
To reproduce core results:
- Obtain GhostWriteBench (download or regenerate).
- Choose a split (ID, OOD‑Domain, OOD‑Author) and resource setting (low/high).
- Train reference fingerprints from the training set.
- Evaluate on the test set by matching fingerprints and applying the threshold.
- Compare against baselines (N‑GRAM, DETECTIVE, etc.) and report macro‑F1.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Evaluator LM size vs. speed** – Larger evaluators may be more accurate but slower; paper shows 1B models (OLMo, Gemma) are robust.
- **Fingerprint resolution** – Higher grid/cluster size retains details but risks sparsity; paper uses K=50 (rank) and G=50 (entropy).
- **Similarity metric** – JS distance works for both variants; norm‑mean slightly better for entropy but less general.
- **Post‑hoc vs. end‑to‑end** – Post‑hoc enables black‑box use but may sacrifice some ID accuracy compared to supervised methods; however, it generalizes better OOD.

### Failure Signatures
- **Same‑family LLM confusion** – Error analysis (Figure 3) shows models from the same family (e.g., GPT‑4.1 vs GPT‑5.1) are often confused, especially by model‑based baselines.
- **Low‑resource extremes** – With very few training books (<3), TRACE’s performance may drop relative to high‑resource settings (Table 4).
- **Short texts** – The paper notes adaptation needed for texts <10K tokens; fingerprint patterns may not fully form.
- **Evaluator‑target mismatch** – If the evaluator LM is extremely poor (e.g., random tokenizer), the scores become uninformative.

### First 3 Experiments

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can TRACE be effectively extended to handle multi‑authorship scenarios, where a single text is collaboratively written by multiple LLMs or humans?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors explicitly state in the Limitations section: “We also focus only on a single‑authorship setup. A natural next step would be to study multi‑authorship (i.e., a piece of text written by multiple authors), which is more common in long‑form documents that typically involve collaborative writing.”
- **Why unresolved:** TRACE currently models a single, unified fingerprint per text assuming one author. Multi‑author texts would produce blended or competing transition patterns, requiring new aggregation or segmentation strategies to attribute portions or identify contributors.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Create a variant of GhostWriteBench with books co‑authored by known pairs/trios of LLMs (with controlled contribution ratios). Develop and evaluate new TRACE extensions (e.g., chunk‑level fingerprinting, mixture models) on this dataset to measure attribution accuracy per author and overall.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What is the optimal choice and configuration of the evaluator language model (L̂θ) for TRACE, and can its performance be improved by using a more powerful or domain‑matched evaluator?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper’s ablation study (Section 6.2 and Appendix Table 14) tests three 1B‑parameter LMs (GPT‑2, OLMo, Gemma) and finds “no major performance differences,” suggesting robustness. However, the study is limited to small models and does not explore whether a larger, more capable evaluator (e.g., a 7B or 70B model) or one specifically fine‑tuned for stylistic analysis could yield richer, more discriminative token scores (rank/entropy) and higher attribution accuracy.
- **Why unresolved:** The conclusion of robustness is based on a narrow set of evaluators. It is unknown if there is a theoretical or empirical upper bound linked to evaluator quality, or if different evaluators might excel in different OOD settings (e.g., domain shift).
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematically evaluate TRACE using a diverse suite of evaluator LMs (varying size, architecture, training data) across all GhostWriteBench OOD scenarios. Correlate evaluator capabilities (e.g., perplexity on diverse corpora, stylistic sensitivity) with TRACE performance to identify optimal characteristics.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Do the token‑level transition patterns captured by TRACE (rank or entropy) reflect deep, model‑specific stylistic choices, or are they primarily surface‑level artifacts susceptible to simple obfuscation or paraphrasing attacks?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] TRACE is presented as a lightweight, post‑hoc fingerprint that works without model internals. The authors claim it is “less prone to obfuscation attacks” because it is not supervised, but they do not test this. The fingerprint’s reliance on token statistics estimated by an external LM raises the question: are these patterns inherent to the generating LLM’s sampling distribution, or could they be easily manipulated by an adversary applying simple text transforms (e.g., synonym replacement, shuffling sentences) that preserve meaning but alter token distributions?
- **Why unresolved:** The paper demonstrates strong OOD generalization but does not conduct adversarial robustness experiments. The interpretability of the 2D signatures (Figures 10 & 11) is qualitative; it is unclear which specific linguistic features drive discrimination and how fragile they are.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Design and apply a battery of obfuscation attacks (e.g., back‑translation, lexical substitution, sentence reordering) to LLM‑generated books in GhostWriteBench. Measure TRACE’s performance decay under these attacks compared to baselines. Perform ablation by perturbing only the token ranks/entropy sequences (e.g., random swapping) to isolate the sensitivity of transition patterns.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Can the TRACE framework be adapted for shorter texts (<10K words) without significant performance loss, and what is the minimum text length required for reliable attribution?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The Limitations section states: “Finally, adapting TRACE for fewer (<10K) tokens remains an open problem.” The paper’s experiments use books averaging ~53K words. The ablation in Appendix Figure 6 shows performance trends but does not establish a clear threshold or analyze failure modes for short texts.
- **Why unresolved:** Short texts provide insufficient token transition events to form stable fingerprints. The density and sparsity characteristics of the 2D signature for short inputs are not analyzed. It is unknown if different fingerprint sizes (K, G) or aggregation strategies (e.g., merging multiple short texts from same source) could mitigate this.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct a controlled study varying text length (e.g., 1K, 5K, 10K, 20K, 50K words) on a subset of GhostWriteBench or a new short‑text dataset. Plot performance (macro‑F1) against length, identify the “knee” point, and test whether fingerprint smoothing or normalization techniques improve short‑text robustness.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Are there other meaningful OOD dimensions beyond domain and author (e.g., temporal shifts, human vs. LLM hybrid authorship, or different prompt conditions) where TRACE’s generalization holds, and where might it fail?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The Limitations mention: “there are other OOD dimensions, such as time, human‑authors, and others, which are natural extensions of GHOSTWRITEBENCH.” The paper focuses on two OOD axes (domain, author) and includes a binary human vs. LLM test (Table 5), but does not explore temporal drift (e.g., same LLM at different times), heterogeneous prompts, or texts edited by humans post‑generation.
- **Why unresolved:** The current OOD design is a specific split of genres and held‑out models. Real‑world deployment faces more complex distribution shifts (e.g., an LLM’s style may evolve with updates, or human authors may use LLMs as co‑writers). TRACE’s assumption of stable, intrinsic style fingerprints may not hold under such conditions.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Extend GhostWriteBench or create new test beds with temporal slices (same LLM, different dates), human‑edited LLM texts (with varying edit ratios), and texts generated under diverse prompting strategies (e.g., persona instructions, stylistic constraints). Evaluate TRACE’s performance decay across these dimensions to map its robustness boundaries.
