# dnaHNet: A Scalable and Hierarchical Foundation Model for Genomic Sequence Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.10603
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10603
- Reference count: 34
- Primary result: Introduces a tokenizer-free autoregressive model with dynamic chunking that achieves superior scaling efficiency (α=0.06) and state-of-the-art zero-shot performance on protein variant effect prediction (Spearman ρ=0.3266) and gene essentiality classification (AUROC=0.6050).

## Executive Summary
dnaHNet addresses the trade-off in genomic foundation models between fixed tokenizers that fragment biological motifs and nucleotide-level models that are computationally prohibitive. It introduces a differentiable dynamic chunking mechanism that recursively compresses raw nucleotides into latent tokens, balancing compression with accuracy. Pretrained on prokaryotic genomes, dnaHNet achieves superior scaling efficiency compared to StripedHyena2, yielding 3.89× lower inference FLOPs at 1 million nucleotides. It sets state-of-the-art zero-shot performance on protein variant effect prediction (Spearman ρ=0.3266) and gene essentiality classification (AUROC=0.6050). The model automatically discovers hierarchical biological structures, learning codon-periodicity in early stages and functional region awareness in later stages, all without supervision.

## Method Summary
dnaHNet employs a two-stage hierarchical architecture. Raw nucleotide sequences are processed by an Encoder consisting of a hybrid Mamba-Transformer backbone that produces hidden states. A boundary predictor computes transition probabilities via cosine similarity between consecutive states; high dissimilarity triggers a chunk boundary, adaptively grouping nucleotides into latent tokens without a fixed vocabulary. The compressed sequence is then fed to a Main Network (e.g., Transformer) which operates on the reduced length, dramatically lowering quadratic computational costs. Finally, a Decoder smooths and upsamples the latent representations back to nucleotide-level logits. The model is trained with a composite loss: next-nucleotide prediction (negative log-likelihood) plus a rate regularizer that encourages the chunking to achieve a target compression ratio, balanced by a coefficient α. This design allocates about 30% of parameters to the Encoder-Decoder pair to capture complex local genomic syntax, while the Main Network handles long-range dependencies efficiently.

## Key Results
- Scaling efficiency: Achieves α=0.06 compared to StripedHyena2's α=0.04, yielding 3.89× lower inference FLOPs at 1 million nucleotides.
- Zero-shot variant effect prediction: Spearman ρ=0.3266 (vs. StripedHyena2's 0.3110).
- Zero-shot gene essentiality classification: AUROC=0.6050 (vs. 0.5759).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** A differentiable, dynamic chunking mechanism replaces fixed tokenization, enabling context-dependent segmentation that preserves biological motifs.
- **Mechanism:** The Encoder uses a hybrid Mamba-Transformer backbone to process raw nucleotide embeddings. A boundary predictor computes transition probabilities via cosine similarity between consecutive hidden states (Equation 1). High dissimilarity triggers a chunk boundary, adaptively grouping nucleotides into latent tokens without a fixed vocabulary.
- **Core assumption:** Genomic functional elements (e.g., codons, promoters) manifest as contextual shifts in the learned representation space, detectable by representation dissimilarity metrics.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "differentiable dynamic chunking mechanism... compresses raw nucleotides into latent tokens adaptively"
  - [section 3.1] Details Equation 1 for boundary probability and the downsampling process.
  - [corpus] Corpus analysis shows related work (e.g., VQDNA, PatchDNA) exploring dynamic tokenization but not end-to-end hierarchical learning at this scale.
- **Break condition:** Without the auxiliary rate loss (L_rate), the boundary predictor collapses to degenerate solutions (all/zero boundaries), as noted in Section 3.3.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Recursive hierarchical compression yields quadratic reductions in computational cost for the main network by processing compressed latent sequences instead of raw nucleotides.
- **Mechanism:** Multi-stage architecture (e.g., 2-stage: R1=3, R2=2) sequentially compresses the sequence. The inner Main Network (M) operates on a sequence length reduced by the product of compression ratios (e.g., 6×), scaling its quadratic attention cost as Θ((L/R)^2) instead of Θ(L^2).
- **Core assumption:** Biological information in genomes is organized hierarchically (e.g., codons within genes, genes within operons), so multi-level abstractive representations retain predictive signal while reducing sequence length.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "recursive chunking yields quadratic FLOP reductions"
  - [section 4.3] Figure 2 shows inference FLOPs scaling; total FLOPs = FLOPs_enc(L) + FLOPs_main(L/R) + FLOPs_dec(L).
  - [section 3.2] "Effective compression ratio of 6... reducing FLOPs... by 36×" (note: 6^2=36 for quadratic cost).
- **Break condition:** Excessive compression (e.g., R1,R2 too high) discards predictive signal, harming perplexity and downstream task performance, as implied by the design choice of biologically motivated ratios.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Jointly optimizing next-nucleotide prediction with a target compression ratio regularizer balances predictive accuracy with efficient segmentation, avoiding degenerate or overly aggressive chunking.
- **Mechanism:** Total loss L = L_NLL + α Σ L_rate(s). L_NLL drives nucleotide-level accuracy. L_rate(s) (Equation 4) aligns discrete chunk selections with continuous boundary probabilities toward a target ratio R_s. The coefficient α trades off compression adherence vs. prediction fidelity.
- **Core assumption:** Standard autoregressive loss alone is insufficient to learn useful chunking; explicit regularization toward a desired downsampling factor is necessary to steer the routing mechanism away from trivial solutions.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "balancing compression with predictive accuracy"
  - [section 3.3] Details the composite loss and notes α≈0.01 for dnaHNet vs. α≥0.03 in text H-Net.
  - [section 3.2] "Minimizing L_NLL alone leads to degenerate segmentation."
- **Break condition:** An inappropriate α (too high) forces adherence to ratio at the cost of predictive loss; too low fails to prevent degenerate segmentation. The optimal α is task/architecture-dependent.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Autoregressive sequence modeling (next-token prediction).
  - *Why needed here:* dnaHNet's core objective is to model P(X) = ∏_t P(x_t | x_<t) over raw nucleotides, as stated in Section 3.1. All components (Encoder, M, Decoder) serve this objective.
  - *Quick check question:* How does the Decoder (Section 3.1) convert latent sequence representations back into a per-nucleotide probability distribution?
- **Concept:** Differentiable, non-parametric routing / dynamic tokenization.
  - *Why needed here:* Replaces fixed k-mer/BPE tokenizers. The boundary predictor (Equation 1) is a learnable, continuous mechanism producing discrete decisions via thresholding at inference (Section 3.3).
  - *Quick check question:* What specific architectural change did dnaHNet make to the original H-Net to accommodate genomic sequences (Section 3.2)?
- **Concept:** Scaling laws and compute-optimal training.
  - *Why needed here:* The paper's core contribution is superior efficiency (α=0.06). Understanding requires knowing how scaling exponents are fit (PPL = A·C^(-α)) and that optimal data-to-parameter ratios deviate from Chinchilla because compression reduces effective sequence length for M (Section 4.3).
  - *Quick check question:* Why does dnaHNet benefit from more training tokens (140B vs. 68B) than StripedHyena2 at the same compute budget?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** 
  Input: Raw nucleotide sequence (length L, dim=4 one-hot) → Encoder (E): Hybrid (4 Mamba + 1 Transformer) → hidden states h_{1:L} → boundary predictor (cos-sim + linear) → p_t → downsampling selects representations at boundaries → compressed sequence E (length L') → Main Network (M): Transformer or another H-Net module (in 2-stage, M's output is final latent representation) → Decoder (D): Smoothing (recurrent interpolation, Eq 2) → ¯E → upsampling (copy per chunk) → ˜X (length L) → final layers (4 Mamba + 1 Transformer) → nucleotide logits.
- **Critical path:** Raw nucleotides → E (chunking) → M (modeling on compressed) → D (upsample + refine) → logits. The hierarchical application means the output of one E-M-D trio becomes the input to the next, with compression at each stage.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Encoder vs. Main capacity: Allocate ~30% parameters to E-D (vs. 15% in text H-Net) to capture complex local genomic syntax.
  - Compression ratios (R1, R2): Biologically motivated (R1=3 for codons, R2=2 for codon pairs) vs. aggressive compression that loses signal.
  - Hierarchy depth: 2 stages optimal for context lengths; deeper stages introduce optimization instability and diminishing returns.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Degenerate boundaries: All p_t → 0 or 1 if α is too low or training unstable.
  - Poor scaling: If FLOP reduction doesn't materialize, check if M's quadratic cost is still on compressed length L'.
  - Task performance drop: If VEP/GE AUROC lags, likely causes are: (a) R1 not matching biological periodicity, (b) α too high suppressing useful chunks, (c) Encoder too weak to produce discriminative h_t.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Single-stage sanity check: Train a 1-stage dnaHNet on a small subset. Verify learned boundaries show some periodicity in coding regions and that perplexity improves over a nucleotide-level baseline with similar M parameters.
  2. Encoder-decoder parameter ratio sweep: Hold total params constant, vary E-D vs. M split (e.g., 20/80, 30/70, 40/60). Monitor training stability and downstream VEP. Expect 30/70 to be optimal.
  3. Compression ratio & α tuning: For a 2-stage model, sweep R1 ∈ {2,3,4} and α ∈ {0.005, 0.01, 0.02}. Use held-out perplexity and boundary periodicity metric. Target: R1=3 yields strongest triplet periodicity; α=0.01 balances compression with lowest perplexity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Does pretraining dnaHNet on eukaryotic genomes lead to the discovery of splice sites and enhancers through its dynamic chunking mechanism?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 5.2 explicitly proposes "extending pretraining to eukaryotic genomes to test whether dynamic chunking discovers structures such as splice sites and enhancers."
- **Why unresolved:** The current study is limited to prokaryotic genomes, which lack introns and complex long-range regulatory interactions, leaving the model's behavior on eukaryotic genomic architecture unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Train dnaHNet on a diverse corpus of eukaryotic genomes, extract chunk boundaries, and statistically evaluate enrichment for annotated splice sites, enhancers, and other regulatory elements using genome-wide annotation tracks.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can the interpretable chunk boundaries from dnaHNet be used to discover novel functional genomic elements?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 5.2 states: "The interpretability of learned boundaries suggests applications in discovering novel functional elements."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper only validates that boundaries align with known functional regions; it does not test whether boundaries can predict previously unannotated elements.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply dnaHNet to poorly annotated or newly assembled genomes, use boundary predictions to nominate candidate functional regions, and validate them experimentally via high-throughput assays (e.g., STARR-seq for enhancers or RNA-seq for splice sites).

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Does dnaHNet's superior scaling efficiency (α=0.06) persist when models are scaled beyond 1B parameters?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 5.1 acknowledges that scaling analyses "extend only to 1B parameters, leaving large-scale behavior undetermined," implying the measured exponent may not hold at larger scales.
- **Why unresolved:** The quadratic FLOP savings from recursive compression might face diminishing returns, optimization instability, or changed data requirements at trillion-parameter scales, which are common for future foundation models.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Train dnaHNet models at multiple scales (e.g., 10B, 50B, 100B) on appropriate compute budgets, recompute scaling exponents for perplexity and downstream task performance, and compare favorably to StripedHyena2 baselines at matched FLOPs.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Are the fixed compression ratios (R1=3, R2=2) optimal for all genomic contexts, especially non-coding and eukaryotic sequences?
- **Basis in paper:** Section 5.1 notes that "the fixed target compression ratios... may be suboptimal for non-coding or eukaryotic sequences," as they were chosen based on prokaryotic coding region statistics (codon triplet and codon pair bias).
- **Why unresolved:** The paper only tested these ratios on prokaryotes; non-coding regions have different nucleotide patterns and functional units (e.g., transcription factor motifs), while eukaryotic introns may require different granularity.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematically vary compression ratios (or make them learnable) across models trained on mixed genomic datasets (including non-coding and eukaryotic partitions), and evaluate downstream task accuracy and boundary interpretability to determine optimal ratios per context.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** How does fine-tuning dnaHNet on downstream tasks compare to its zero-shot performance, and does the hierarchical representation improve sample efficiency?
- **Basis in paper:** The evaluation is exclusively zero-shot (Sections 4.4, 4.5), and Section 5.1 identifies this as a key gap: "behavior under fine-tuning remains unexplored."
- **Why unresolved:** Zero-shot testing measures inherent knowledge, but most real-world applications involve fine-tuning. The adaptive chunking may offer better generalization with limited data or might be disrupted by task-specific supervision.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Fine-tune dnaHNet and representative baselines (StripedHyena2) on a suite of genomic tasks (e.g., promoter prediction, variant effect scoring) with varying labeled dataset sizes, comparing final accuracy, convergence speed, and whether chunk boundaries adapt meaningfully to task-specific patterns.

## Limitations
- Boundary predictor architecture (e.g., projections, normalization) is not fully specified, hindering exact replication.
- GTDB preprocessing steps (quality filters, dereplication, handling of short genomes) lack documentation, affecting data distribution.
- Zero-shot evaluation protocols (variant construction, gene annotation matching, context window) are underspecified, impeding reproducibility.
