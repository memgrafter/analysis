# Human Attribution of Causality to AI Across Agency, Misuse, and Misalignment
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.13236
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13236
- Reference Count: 37
- Primary Result: AI autonomy increases perceived causal responsibility and blame for harms, while humans remain blameworthy especially under low AI agency, challenging the "responsibility gap" hypothesis.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates how people assign causal responsibility in AI-caused harms. Across five experiments with vignettes varying AI autonomy (low, medium, high) and agent roles, participants rated causality, blame, foreseeability, and counterfactual contributions for each agent on 0-100 scales. Findings reveal that AI is judged more causal than humans when AI autonomy is moderate or high; conversely, humans are judged more causal when AI autonomy is low, despite temporal distance. A persistent human bias emerges: humans are rated more causal and blameworthy than AI even when performing identical actions. Adding a developer reduces attributions to the human user but not to the AI. Decomposing AI into a language model and agentic tool shows the autonomous execution component carries strong causality, and human ratings drop further. These results underscore the role of perceived autonomy in folk attribution and challenge the notion of a "responsibility gap" where humans are absolved when AI acts autonomously.

## Method Summary
The researchers conducted five online experiments with UK and US participants via Prolific, targeting English speakers with ≥95% approval. Vignettes depicted a bank-hacking scenario with manipulated variables: agent type (human/AI), role (instructor/executor), AI autonomy level (low: human sets goal and means; medium: AI sets means; high: AI sets goal and means), presence of a developer, and AI decomposition (unified vs. separate LLM and agentic tool). After reading each vignette, participants provided four 0-100 scale ratings for each relevant agent: perceived causality, blame, foreseeability, and counterfactual contribution. Data exclusions included failed attention checks and rapid completions. Analyses used paired and independent t-tests, mixed ANOVAs, with Holm correction, reporting effect sizes and 95% confidence intervals. Sample sizes ranged from 50 to 160 per study. The design allowed isolation of autonomy effects and testing of role-swapped scenarios to uncover attribution biases.

## Key Results
- AI autonomy drives attribution: When AI autonomy is moderate or high, the AI is judged more causal and blameworthy than the human user; at low autonomy, the human is judged more causal despite temporal distance.
- Human bias persists: In role-swapped conditions where AI and human perform identical actions, the human is consistently rated as more causal and blameworthy.
- Adding actors redistributes responsibility: Including a developer lowers attributions to the human user but not to the AI; decomposing the AI reduces ratings for both AI components and the human, though the agentic tool remains more causal than the planning component.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Perceived autonomy of an AI agent is a primary determinant in causal attribution, overriding simple temporal proximity in a causal chain.
- **Mechanism:** When an AI exhibits moderate or high autonomy (determining means or both goal and means), it is judged more causal, blameworthy, and foreseeable than a human counterpart. When autonomy is low (agentic execution of explicit human instructions), the human instigator is judged more responsible despite being temporally distal.
- **Core assumption:** Causal selection is sensitive to the *self-directedness* of an agent's decision-making, not just physical or normative violation proximity.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "When AI agency was moderate [...] or high, participants attributed greater causal responsibility to the AI. However, under low AI agency [...] participants assigned greater causal responsibility to the human despite their temporal distance."
  - [section 6] "In the low agency condition, participants selected the agent less proximate to the outcome as more causal."

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** A strong, default bias exists to attribute greater causality and blame to a human agent over an AI agent performing the *same causal role* (instructor or executor), regardless of the specific contribution.
- **Mechanism:** When the exact same decision or instruction is framed as coming from either a human or an AI, the human is consistently rated as more causal and blameworthy. This suggests an attribution heuristic favoring human agency independent of the action's nature or position in the chain.
- **Core assumption:** There is a fundamental folk belief that humans are more appropriate bearers of causal responsibility than artificial systems, a bias that persists even when controlling for specific actions.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "When we reversed roles between human and AI, participants consistently judged the human as more causal, even when both agents perform the same action."
  - [section 7] "In the role-swapped conditions, participants judged the human as more causal and blameworthy."

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Introducing additional *human* actors into the causal structure (e.g., a developer) reduces attributed causality to other *human* actors (the user) but not to the AI system. Decomposing the AI into sub-components also redistributes responsibility, lowering judgments for all actors, but the autonomous execution component retains strong causality.
- **Mechanism:** The "responsibility space" appears segmented by agent type. Causal attributions to AI are relatively stable when new human actors are added. However, the presence of another human (developer) or AI sub-component (agentic tool) leads to a cognitive *redistribution* of responsibility *among humans*, diminishing the perceived causality of the primary user.
- **Core assumption:** People use a form of *social differentiation* in responsibility allocation; AI is perceived as a monolithic causal contributor, while human contributions are seen as divisible and potentially substitutable.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Adding a developer reduced causal attributions to the human but not to the AI."
  - [section 8] "Analyses [...] revealed that while participants’ judgments of the AI remained largely unaffected, ratings for the human user decreased."
  - [section 9] "Decomposing the AI [...] reduced causal and foreseeability ratings for the system while simultaneously decreasing scores attributed to the human."

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Causal Chain & Cause Selection**
  - *Why needed here:* AI incident analyses involve multiple contributing factors. Understanding how people select a *singular* "cause" from a chain (e.g., developer → AI → user → outcome) is fundamental to predicting liability judgments.
  - *Quick check question:* In a chain A→B→C (A instructs B, B executes C), if B is an autonomous robot that disobeys A's instructions and causes harm, who is more likely judged as the "cause"? A or B?
- **Concept: Agency vs. Autonomy (Operationalized)**
  - *Why needed here:* The paper's core manipulation is *autonomy* (self-generation of goal/means) but labels it "AI agency." Distinguishing capability (agency) from self-directedness (autonomy) is critical for modeling system design and legal responsibility.
  - *Quick check question:* Does an AI system that *autonomously selects* a hacking tool to achieve a user's goal have higher autonomy than one that *executes a user-specified* hacking tool?
- **Concept: The "Responsibility Gap" & Its Folk Psychology Challenge**
  - *Why needed here:* Legal theory posits a "gap" when autonomous AI acts, potentially excusing human designers/deployers. This paper shows folk intuitions *do not* create such a gap; humans are still held responsible alongside AI.
  - *Quick check question:* If an AI with high autonomy causes harm, does the average person tend to (a) exculpate the human developer/user, or (b) assign responsibility to both?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map:
1.  **Scenario Engine:** Templates for causal chain vignettes. Key variables: `[agent_type: human/AI]`, `[role: instructor/executor]`, `[autonomy_level: low/medium/high]`, `[additional_actors: none/developer]`, `[AI_decomposition: unified/LLM+tool]`.
2.  **Measurement Layer:** 5-point scale (0-100) for each agent on: `causality`, `blame`, `foreseeability`, `counterfactual`.
3.  **Analysis Module:** Paired t-tests (within-agent comparisons) and ANOVAs (between-condition effects). Key comparisons: *Agent effect within Condition* and *Condition effect for same Agent*.

### Critical path:
1. Establish Baseline (Study 1): Single vignette, compare AI vs. Human on all measures.
2. Manipulate Autonomy (Study 2): Vary AI's goal/means setting (low/medium/high). Confirm pattern: AI > Human in medium/high; Human > AI in low.
3. Test Role Swap (Study 3): Invert roles in low/medium conditions. Confirm mechanism: strong human bias persists in low agency; executor-proximity bias appears in medium agency but is stronger for human executors.
4. Introduce Developer (Study 4): Add collider structure. Observe human ratings drop, AI/developer remain stable.
5. Decompose AI (Study 5): Split AI into LLM (planner) and Agentic Tool (executor). Agentic Tool > LLM in medium agency. Human ratings drop further.

### Design tradeoffs:
- **Control vs. Ecological Validity:** Vignettes isolate single variables (autonomy) but simplify real-world AI supply chains (e.g., no hardware manufacturers, regulators).
- **Between- vs. Within-Subjects:** Primarily between-subjects for conditions to avoid contrast effects, but within-subjects for agent ratings (compares human vs. AI in same scenario).
- **Sum-to-100 Constraint:** Not used; participants give independent 0-100 ratings, avoiding forced trade-offs that might obscure overall attribution levels.

### Failure signatures:
- **Scenario Misinterpretation:** Failure in attention check ("What type of AI system?") indicates vignette ambiguity.
- **Condition Dilution:** Non-significant differences between autonomy levels (e.g., Medium vs. High) suggest manipulation was too subtle or ceiling/floor effects.
- **Role-Swap Null Effect:** If role-swapped conditions yield identical judgments to originals, the hypothesized human/autonomy bias may be absent or the vignette change didn't alter perceived autonomy/role sufficiently.

### The first 3 experiments:
1.  **Study 1 (Baseline):** Medium-agency vignette only. Establishes the core pattern where AI is judged more causal/blameful than human user.
2.  **Study 2 (Autonomy Gradient):** Low/Medium/High AI agency conditions. Isolates autonomy as driver: AI wins only when it has autonomy in goal/means.
3.  **Study 3 (Role & Nature Interaction):** Low/Medium Human agency (roles swapped). Tests if human advantage holds regardless of causal role (instructor/executor) when AI is passive (low) but flips to executor-proximity when AI is active (medium).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How does the contextual ambiguity of an agent's intention (e.g., red-teaming exercise vs. malicious intent) influence causal attributions in AI-caused harms?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "in our analyses we assumed that the executor’s intention was to hack bank accounts; however, this intention may in fact be more ambiguous. A human giving such an instruction to an AI could be doing so as part of a red-teaming exercise... Making these contextual distinctions explicit in future scenarios could lead to different attribution patterns."
- **Why unresolved:** The current experiments fixed intentions as uniformly malicious for humans and autonomous for AI, but real-world AI interactions often involve ambiguous or benign contexts that could fundamentally alter folk perceptions of causality, blame, and foreseeability.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Experimental vignettes that systematically vary the perceived intention of both human and AI agents (e.g., user in security testing vs. criminal intent, AI operating in constrained environment vs. open-ended) while keeping other factors constant, measuring impacts on all four attribution scales.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Which specific dimensions of autonomy (e.g., goal-setting autonomy, means-selection autonomy, contextual adaptability) are most predictive of perceived causal responsibility in AI systems?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "future work could manipulate both the type of AI and specific dimensions of autonomy, given that autonomy is a multidimensional construct." The study manipulated overall agency (low/medium/high) based on goal and means control but did not disentangle individual autonomy facets.
- **Why unresolved:** The operationalization of agency conflated multiple autonomy dimensions, making it impossible to determine whether effects are driven by goal autonomy, means autonomy, or their interaction. This limits theoretical understanding of how autonomy drives attribution.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Factorial experiments independently varying goal autonomy (human vs. AI sets goal) and means autonomy (human vs. AI selects means), with supplementary ratings of perceived autonomy for each dimension to perform mediation analyses on attribution outcomes.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Why does the presence of a developer in the causal chain reduce attributions to human users but not to AI systems, and what does this imply for liability allocation in multi-actor AI incidents?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Study 4 results show adding a developer significantly lowered human user ratings in the medium-agency condition while AI ratings remained stable. The paper notes this challenges the "responsibility gap" but does not explain the asymmetric effect.
- **Why unresolved:** The authors speculate developers are seen as highly foreseeable and norm-violating, but why this selectively buffers the AI from reduced attribution is unknown. It may relate to perceived human controllability or different normative expectations, but these are untested.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Comparative studies with varied distal actors (e.g., developer, regulator, corporate executive) in similar collider structures, measuring perceptions of control, foreseeability, and normative violation to identify mediators. Include scenarios where the developer acts intentionally vs. negligently.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What cognitive mechanisms cause human causal attributions to decrease when an AI system is decomposed into modular components (e.g., LLM and agentic tool), and does this reflect diffusion of responsibility or altered causal strength perception?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] In Study 5, decomposing the AI in medium-agency conditions reduced human attributions across all measures, alongside lower AI component ratings. The authors suggest "cognitive redistribution" and "causal redundancy" but provide no direct evidence for the mechanism.
- **Why unresolved:** The reduction could stem from perceived diffusion of responsibility, increased system complexity diluting causality, or weakened perceived causal links between components. Without process data, the underlying psychological process remains speculative.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Experiments manipulating component interdependence (e.g., necessary vs. redundant parts) and measuring causal strength judgments between components. Incorporate real-time protocols (e.g., think-aloud) or eye-tracking to trace how participants integrate decomposed components into causal judgments.

## Limitations
- **Methodological ambiguities:** Exact attention check criteria, completion time thresholds, scale anchors, and question order randomization are unspecified, potentially introducing uncontrolled variance.
- **External validity constraints:** Findings rely on simplified bank-hacking vignettes with UK/US online participants; generalizability to other AI domains, real-world incidents, and diverse cultures is uncertain.
- **Construct measurement:** Autonomy operationalization via goal/means control may not capture all relevant dimensions; the precise meaning of "counterfactual" ratings is unclear.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (Autonomy Drives Attribution):** High
- **Mechanism 2 (Human Attribution Bias):** Medium
- **Mechanism 3 (Responsibility Redistribution):** Medium-Low
- **Foundational Concepts (Causal Chain, Agency):** High
