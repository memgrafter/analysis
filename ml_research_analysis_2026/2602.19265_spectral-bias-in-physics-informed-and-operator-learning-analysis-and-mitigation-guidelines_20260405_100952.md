# Spectral bias in physics-informed and operator learning: Analysis and mitigation guidelines

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.19265
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19265
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Spectral bias in neural PDE solvers is primarily an optimization issue; second‑order methods (SS‑Broyden) achieve up to 5 orders of magnitude lower high‑frequency error than Adam, and spectral‑aware losses (BSP) reduce operator spectral error by 3.1×–5.6×.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates spectral bias—the tendency of neural networks to prioritize low‑frequency components—in physics‑informed neural networks (PINNs), physics‑informed Kolmogorov‑Arnold networks (PIKANs), and neural operators for solving partial differential equations. Using frequency‑resolved metrics across KdV, wave, diffusion‑reaction, and turbulent jet PDEs, the authors demonstrate that spectral bias is largely an optimization problem. Second‑order optimizers like SS‑Broyden dramatically accelerate high‑frequency learning, yielding error reductions of up to five orders of magnitude compared to Adam. For neural operators, binning spectral power (BSP) in the loss function mitigates spectral bias without increasing inference cost, improving energy‑spectrum accuracy by factors of 3.1–5.6 for turbulent jet reconstruction. The study also finds that activation functions (e.g., SIREN) offer diminishing returns when paired with strong second‑order optimizers. These results yield actionable guidelines: employ curvature‑aware optimizers for physics‑informed learning and spectral regularization for operator learning when high‑frequency fidelity is critical.

## Method Summary
The authors employ quantitative frequency‑resolved evaluation metrics—including frequency‑specific error (eF,p) and Barron norm—to diagnose spectral bias across PINNs, PIKANs, and branch‑trunk neural operators (DeepONet/DeepOKAN). Their experimental suite covers representative PDEs: dispersive KdV, hyperbolic wave, elliptic diffusion‑reaction, and a turbulent jet with broad‑band spectra. They systematically compare optimizers (Adam vs. SS‑Broyden), loss functions (standard L2 vs. binned spectral power), and activation functions (Tanh vs. SIREN). For neural operators, they examine both non‑causal and causal training regimes. All experiments use full‑batch training where applicable (except standard mini‑batch baselines) and include careful hyperparameter tuning. The analysis combines empirical results with theoretical insights from neural tangent kernel (NTK) theory to explain observed behaviors.

## Key Results
- SS‑Broyden reduces high‑frequency error by up to 5 orders of magnitude relative to Adam for KdV and wave PDEs, nearly eliminating spectral bias in PINNs.
- Binned spectral power (BSP) loss decreases energy‑spectrum error by factors of 3.1–5.6 for turbulent jet reconstruction in neural operators, with no additional inference overhead.
- SIREN activation improves high‑frequency learning with Adam but provides only modest gains with SS‑Broyden, indicating optimizer‑driven curvature compensation can supersede architectural expressivity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Spectral bias arises because first‑order optimizers like Adam converge poorly for high‑frequency components due to an ill‑conditioned curvature landscape. SS‑Broyden approximates the Hessian or its inverse, providing a preconditioner that equalizes gradient scales across frequencies and thereby accelerates high‑frequency learning. For neural operators, the BSP loss directly penalizes errors in selected frequency bins, forcing the model to allocate capacity to under‑represented bands and thus circumventing the optimization dynamics issue. The diminishing returns of SIREN with SS‑Broyden suggest that SIREN’s initial benefit stems from a neural tangent kernel with faster high‑frequency decay, but strong second‑order optimization can flatten the convergence gap regardless of the NTK spectrum.

## Foundational Learning
- **Spectral Bias**  
  *Why needed*: Core phenomenon under investigation; explains why networks miss high frequencies.  
  *Quick check*: Train a small MLP on a high‑frequency target (e.g., sin(20πx)) and compare low‑ vs. high‑frequency error after a few epochs.
  
- **Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)**  
  *Why needed*: Theoretical framework linking architecture, optimizer, and frequency‑dependent convergence rates.  
  *Quick check*: Compute NTK eigenvalues for a two‑layer network with different activations and observe decay rates.
  
- **Physics‑Informed Neural Networks (PINNs)**  
  *Why needed*: Primary application domain; incorporate PDE residuals and boundary/initial conditions into the loss.  
  *Quick check*: Solve the 1D Poisson equation with a PINN and verify boundary conditions.
  
- **Neural Operators (e.g., DeepONet)**  
  *Why needed*: Learn mappings between function spaces; spectral bias manifests in the output function space.  
  *Quick check*: Train a DeepONet on an ODE family (e.g., y'' = -λy) and evaluate on unseen λ.
  
- **Binned Spectral Power (BSP) Loss**  
  *Why needed*: Mitigation technique for operator spectral bias; explicitly targets frequency bands.  
  *Quick check*: Add BSP to an operator training run, compute power spectra of target and prediction, and compare bin‑wise errors.
  
- **Second‑Order Optimizers (SS‑Broyden)**  
  *Why needed*: Provide curvature‑aware updates to combat spectral bias in PINNs.  
  *Quick check*: Implement a Broyden update for a small network and observe faster convergence on a high‑frequency component compared to Adam.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
PINNs: Input (x, t) → Fully‑Connected Network → Output (û) ; Loss = MSE( PDE residual ) + MSE( boundary/IC )  
Neural Operators: Branch (input function samples) → Trunk (query coordinates) → Output (û) ; Loss = MSE( field ) optionally + BSP

### Critical Path
- **PINNs**: forward pass (compute û) → compute PDE residual via automatic differentiation → assemble total loss → backpropagation → optimizer step.  
- **Operators**: sample input function discretization and query points → forward through branch and trunk → combine (typically inner product) → compute loss → backpropagation → optimizer step.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Full‑batch vs. mini‑batch**: Second‑order methods like SS‑Broyden require full batch for accurate curvature, limiting scalability; Adam can use mini‑batches.  
- **Optimizer choice**: SS‑Broyden yields superior high‑frequency accuracy but higher memory/computational cost per step; Adam is cheaper but may plateau.  
- **Spectral loss**: BSP adds computation (FFT per batch) but no inference cost; may conflict with causal training in operators.

### Failure Signatures
- High‑frequency error plateaus despite long training with Adam.  
- BSP loss causing instability or degradation in field L2 error, especially under causal training.  
- SS‑Broyden divergence without sufficient damping or line search.

### First Three Experiments
1. Baseline PINN on KdV with Adam: quantify spectral error across frequencies using eF,p; expect low‑frequency dominance.  
2. Same PINN with SS‑Broyden: measure convergence speed and final high‑frequency error reduction.  
3. DeepONet on turbulent jet: compare standard L2 loss vs. L2+BSP loss; report energy‑spectrum nRMSE improvement.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How do spectral bias mitigation strategies (second‑order optimization, BSP loss) generalize across a broader spectrum of PDE classes beyond the dispersive, hyperbolic, and elliptic equations studied?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper explicitly asks, "Can spectral bias be mitigated in the same manner for dispersive, hyperbolic, and elliptic equations?" and tests three representative types (KdV, wave, diffusion‑reaction). The conclusion notes extending to large‑scale 3D problems as future work.
- **Why unresolved:** Different PDEs possess distinct spectral decay properties (e.g., turbulent flows have broad‑band spectra) and multi‑scale couplings that may interact uniquely with optimizers or losses. The paper's results are promising but not exhaustive; unstudied PDE families (e.g., multiphysics, nonlinear dispersive systems) could reveal limitations.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic validation across a wider set of PDEs (e.g., Navier‑Stokes, magnetohydrodynamics) using consistent frequency‑resolved metrics (eF,p, Barron norm). If mitigation effectiveness varies, identify PDE‑specific characteristics (e.g., spectrum exponent, nonlinearity strength) that predict success.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What is the mechanistic explanation for why oscillatory activations (e.g., SIREN) provide diminishing returns in high‑frequency learning when paired with strong second‑order optimizers like SS‑Broyden?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper explicitly investigates activation functions in PINNs/PIKANs and shows SIREN substantially outperforms Tanh with Adam but only modestly helps with SS‑Broyden. Theoretical analysis links this to optimizer‑induced preconditioning.
- **Why unresolved:** The NTK‑based argument suggests second‑order methods flatten frequency‑dependent convergence, but the representational capacity of SIREN may still matter for extremely high frequencies or stiff problems. The precise interaction between activation‑induced kernel spectra and Hessian conditioning is not fully quantified.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Measure NTK eigenvalues and Hessian condition numbers for SIREN vs Tanh networks under first‑ and second‑order updates. Ablate frequency scaling in SIREN with SS‑Broyden on problems with extreme high‑frequency content to see if any expressivity gap remains.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can curvature‑aware second‑order optimizers (e.g., SS‑Broyden) be adapted to mini‑batch training for neural operators, preserving their high‑frequency fidelity advantages while scaling to large datasets?
- **Basis in paper:** The conclusion states SS‑Broyden cannot currently use mini‑batching, unlike SOAP, and this is a "concrete direction." The paper successfully uses SS‑Broyden for PINNs (full‑batch) but fails to apply it to neural operators, which are larger and typically require mini‑batches.
- **Why unresolved:** Full‑batch SS‑Broyden is computationally prohibitive for large operator datasets; stochastic curvature estimates may introduce noise that destabilizes the quasi‑Newton updates and negates accuracy gains. The paper provides no mini‑batch SS‑Broyden results for operators.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Develop and test mini‑batch variants of SS‑Broyden (e.g., using random subspace or low‑rank curvature approximations) on high‑frequency operator benchmarks (e.g., turbulent flow reconstruction). Compare spectral errors (energy‑spectrum nRMSE, Barron norm) and convergence stability against SOAP and Adam.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How does the causal training structure in branch‑trunk operators (DeepONet/DeepOKAN) create gradient conflict between field‑based (L2) and spectral (BSP) losses, and can architectural or loss modifications reconcile this conflict to enable effective BSP regularization?
- **Basis in paper:** The appendix reveals that causal per‑timestep L2 loss and full‑sequence BSP loss have strongly negative cosine similarity for DeepONet, causing BSP to degrade performance. Non‑causal training aligns gradients but fails to converge. This structural mismatch is not addressed in the main text.
- **Why unresolved:** Causality is essential for temporal prediction in many applications, but the paper shows BSP is ineffective under causal training for branch‑trunk architectures. No solution is proposed, leaving a gap for spectral regularization in causal operators.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Design causal‑compatible spectral losses (e.g., BSP computed on autoregressively generated sequences with proper weighting) or architectural tweaks (e.g., attention mechanisms that preserve gradient alignment). Validate on earthquake response dataset by measuring whether BSP then improves spectral fidelity without harming field accuracy.

## Limitations
- The study tests only a limited set of PDEs (KdV, wave, diffusion‑reaction, turbulent jet), leaving generalization to other classes (e.g., high‑dimensional, stochastic) uncertain.
- Second‑order optimizer SS‑Broyden is evaluated only in full‑batch mode; its scalability and mini‑batch adaptation for larger models (neural operators) remain unproven.
- The Binned Spectral Power (BSP) loss hyperparameters (number of bins, frequency spacing) are fixed without sensitivity analysis, so optimal configuration may vary across problems.

## Confidence
- Spectral bias is primarily an optimization issue: **High**
- SS‑Broyden dramatically improves high‑frequency learning in PINNs: **High**
- BSP loss reduces spectral error in neural operators: **Medium**
- Activation functions (SIREN) matter less with strong optimizers: **Medium**
- Generalization of these strategies to all PDE classes: **Low**
