# PRISM: Distribution-free Adaptive Computation of Matrix Functions for Accelerating Neural Network Training

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.22137
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22137
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: PRISM adaptively fits polynomial surrogates to the evolving matrix spectrum via randomized sketching, eliminating prior eigenvalue bounds. It accelerates Newton‑Schulz iterations with quadratic convergence, reducing per‑iteration overhead to \(O(n^2 \log n)\). Integrated into Shampoo and Muon optimizers, it achieves lower validation loss on GPT‑2 (4.9886) than PolarExpress (5.4523) and AdamW (6.8689).

## Executive Summary
PRISM introduces a distribution‑free framework for accelerating matrix function computations essential to deep learning optimizers. By dynamically fitting polynomial updates to the current spectrum of the residual matrix using a sketched least‑squares problem, it removes the need for explicit eigenvalue bounds. The approach combines adaptive polynomial approximation with randomized subspace embeddings, yielding theoretical guarantees (quadratic convergence) and empirical speedups.

The method is compatible with Newton‑Schulz iterations for polar decomposition and matrix square root, reducing computational cost from cubic to quadratic scaling. When plugged into Shampoo and Muon, PRISM accelerates training and improves model performance, as demonstrated on GPT‑2 and ResNet benchmarks.

## Method Summary
PRISM operates on a base iterative matrix algorithm (e.g., Newton‑Schulz) that updates an iterate \(X_k\) using a polynomial \(g_d\) of the residual \(R_k\). Instead of fixing the polynomial coefficients ahead of time, PRISM chooses at each iteration a scalar \(\alpha_k\) that minimizes a sketched approximation of the next residual squared Frobenius norm. Concretely, for degree \(d\), it sets \(g_d(\xi;\alpha)=f_{d-1}(\xi)+\alpha \xi^d\) and solves \(\tilde{\alpha}_k = \arg\min_{\alpha\in[\ell,u]} \|S_k (I - X_k^2 g_d(R_k;\alpha)^2)\|_F^2\), where \(S_k\) is a small random sketch (\(p \ll n\)). The sketch reduces the cost of computing the necessary trace terms from \(O(n^3)\) to \(O(n^2 p)\), yielding overall \(O(n^2 \log n)\) overhead. After obtaining \(\tilde{\alpha}_k\), the update \(X_{k+1}=X_k g_d(R_k;\tilde{\alpha}_k)\) is applied. The process repeats until convergence. PRISM can be integrated into optimizers by replacing their matrix square root or orthogonalization subroutines (e.g., in Shampoo’s preconditioner or Muon’s stochastic orthogonalization), thereby accelerating training without altering the outer optimization dynamics.

## Key Results
- PRISM speeds up training when integrated into Shampoo and Muon optimizers, outperforming both classical Newton‑Schulz (fixed Taylor) and PolarExpress (minimax polynomial).
- On GPT‑2 Large, PRISM with degree‑5 polynomials achieves a validation loss of **4.9886**, compared to **5.4523** for PolarExpress and **6.8689** for AdamW.
- The method delivers stable speedups across Gaussian and heavy‑tailed (HTMP) spectral distributions, while baselines sensitive to spectral bounds degrade.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Adaptive Fitting to Evolving Spectrum
PRISM replaces the fixed Taylor polynomial with a one‑parameter family \(g_d(\xi;\alpha)=f_{d-1}(\xi)+\alpha\xi^d\). At each iteration, it selects \(\alpha_k\) to minimize the squared Frobenius norm of the next residual, which (when \(R_k\) is symmetric) is equivalent to a least‑squares fit over the eigenvalues of \(R_k\). This spectrum‑adaptive choice reduces approximation error on the actual eigenvalues present, accelerating convergence. **Core assumption**: \(R_k\) remains symmetric throughout. **Evidence**: abstract and Section 4.1. **Break conditions**: if eigenvalues cluster near 0 or 1, the fit becomes less sensitive; if the interval \([\ell,u]\) is mis‑specified for the degree \(d\), gains may diminish or convergence may stall.

### Mechanism 2: Low‑Cost Fitting via Randomized Sketching
Exact coefficient evaluation requires \(O(n^3)\) matrix multiplications. PRISM instead draws a sketch \(S_k \in \mathbb{R}^{p\times n}\) (e.g., Gaussian) and solves \(\tilde{\alpha}_k = \arg\min_{\alpha} \|S_k (I - X_k^2 g_d(R_k;\alpha)^2)\|_F^2\). This replaces trace terms \(\operatorname{tr}(R_k^i)\) with \(\operatorname{tr}(S_k R_k^i S_k^T)\), each computable in \(O(n^2 p)\) time. An oblivious subspace embedding (OSE) ensures the sketched objective approximates the true objective up to \((1\pm\varepsilon)\) with high probability, preserving the optimal \(\alpha\). **Core assumption**: the low‑dimensional subspace spanned by the polynomial terms is well‑approximated by the sketch. **Evidence**: abstract and Section 4.2. **Break condition**: if \(p\) is too small relative to the effective rank of this subspace, the approximation error in \(\tilde{\alpha}_k\) may accumulate and degrade convergence.

### Mechanism 3: Preservation of Quadratic Convergence
The base Newton‑Schulz iteration achieves quadratic convergence: after a transient, \(\|R_{k+1}\|_2 \leq C \|R_k\|_2^2\). PRISM’s adaptive \(\alpha_k\) (exact minimization) for \(d=1\) and interval \([1/2,1]\) is proven to maintain this bound (Theorem 1). With sketched \(\tilde{\alpha}_k\), Theorem 2 shows that with high probability the same quadratic contraction holds after an additional transient iteration (\(k-3\) vs \(k-2\)). The constants depend on the interval bounds ensuring contractiveness over the eigenvalue range. **Core assumption**: the chosen \([\ell,u]\) guarantees \(|h(\lambda,\alpha)| \leq C \lambda^2\) for \(\lambda\in[0,1]\). **Evidence**: Theorems 1 and 2. **Break conditions**: if \([\ell,u]\) is mis‑specified for the degree \(d\) or if sketching error (small \(p\)) pushes \(\tilde{\alpha}_k\) outside the contractive region, the residual may not decay quadratically, leading to stagnation or divergence.

## Architecture Onboarding
#### Component map
Meta‑algorithm core -> Residual computation -> Sketching module -> Polynomial fitting solver -> Update executor -> Convergence monitor

#### Critical path
1. Initialize \(X_0\) (e.g., \(X_0 = A\)).
2. Compute residual \(R_k\) (e.g., \(I - X_k^T X_k\)).
3. Generate random sketch \(S_k\) (size \(p \times n\)).
4. Compute polynomial coefficients via traced terms of \(S_k\) and \(R_k\) (\(O(n^2 p)\)).
5. Solve for \(\tilde{\alpha}_k\) within interval \([\ell,u]\).
6. Update \(X_k \rightarrow X_{k+1} = X_k g_d(R_k; \tilde{\alpha}_k)\).
7. Repeat until \(\|R_k\|\) is small.  
The sketching and coefficient steps must be faster than a full matrix multiply (\(O(n^3)\)) to yield net speedup.

#### Design tradeoffs
- **Sketch size \(p\)**: Larger \(p\) reduces \(\tilde{\alpha}_k\) error but increases per‑iteration cost; theoretically \(p = \Omega(\log n)\) suffices, but empirically \(p\) as low as 5 works; extreme spectra may require larger \(p\).
- **Polynomial degree \(d\)**: Higher \(d\) (e.g., 2 for degree‑5) can reduce iterations but requires computing higher powers of \(R_k\) (up to \(4d+2\)), increasing cost per iteration.
- **Interval \([\ell,u]\)**: Must be chosen based on \(d\) to guarantee contractiveness (e.g., \([1/2,1]\) for \(d=1\), \([3/8,29/20]\) for \(d=2\)). Wrong bounds can break convergence.
- **Coupled vs. uncoupled updates**: For square root, coupled updates (both \(X\) and \(Y\)) are more stable but require matrix inverses; PRISM can accelerate both, but Newton‑Schulz variants avoid inverses and are GPU‑friendly.

#### Failure signatures
- **Divergence or stagnation**: \(\|R_k\|\) increases or plateaus. Likely causes: \(\tilde{\alpha}_k\) outside \([\ell,u]\) due to poor sketch (small \(p\)) or numerical issues; interval too loose.
- **No speedup vs. classical Newton‑Schulz**: Overhead of sketching outweighs iteration reduction. Check if \(p\) is too large or \(d\) too high for matrix size.
- **Numerical instability**: High‑degree polynomials or large \(\tilde{\alpha}_k\) may cause overflow/underflow. Monitor \(\alpha_k\) evolution; consider higher precision or capping.
- **Spectral mismatch**: If input matrix spectrum is very narrow/wide relative to assumed \([\ell,u]\), fitted \(\alpha_k\) may not improve fit; PRISM is designed to adapt, so failure suggests \([\ell,u]\) constraints are too restrictive.

#### First 3 experiments
1. **Synthetic Gaussian matrices**: Generate \(A\) with entries \(\sim N(0,1)\), scale \(\sigma_{\max}=1\), vary aspect ratio \(\gamma = n/m\). Run PRISM‑accelerated Newton‑Schulz (degree‑5) vs. classical and PolarExpress. Measure iterations and wall‑clock time to reach \(\|I - X^T X\|_F < 10^{-6}\). Confirm stable speedup across \(\gamma\).
2. **Integration into Shampoo**: Train ResNet on CIFAR using Shampoo with eigen‑decomposition, PolarExpress, and PRISM‑5 (5 iterations) for square root/inverse square roots. Compare validation accuracy vs. time. Expect PRISM to match eigen‑decomposition accuracy but faster.
3. **GPT‑2 training with Muon**: Train GPT‑2 Large with Muon using PolarExpress, PRISM‑3 (5 iterations), PRISM‑5 (3 iterations) for polar decomposition of gradients. Report final validation loss and training time. PRISM‑5 should achieve lowest loss (4.9886) and stable speedup over PolarExpress.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Can PRISM’s framework be extended to broader matrix functions?**  
   The paper applies PRISM only to primitive functions (square root, inverse root, orthogonalization) and does not provide a general recipe for other iterative algorithms (e.g., rational approximants, Padé methods) or domains like optimal transport. A resolution would derive a universal mapping and validate across diverse matrix functions.

2. **What are convergence rates for higher‑degree polynomials (\(d\ge3\))?**  
   Theoretical analysis is limited to \(d=1\) (Theorem 1) and \(d=2\) (empirical). For \(d\ge3\), convergence rates, stability conditions, and spectral dependence are unknown. A resolution would mathematically bound the residual decay \(\|R_{k+1}\|_2\) in terms of \(\|R_k\|_2\) for general \(d\) and validate empirically across spectra.

3. **Why does PRISM work with very small sketch dimensions (\(p=5\)) despite theory suggesting \(p=\Omega(\log n)\)?**  
   Theory based on generic Johnson‑Lindenstrauss arguments yields large \(p\); empirically \(p=5\) suffices. The discrepancy suggests the polynomial‑fitting structure admits a tighter sample complexity. A resolution would tighten the bound by exploiting the design matrix’s properties and identify a practical lower bound for \(p\).

4. **Can PRISM handle non‑symmetric residual matrices?**  
   The analysis assumes \(R_k\) symmetric (e.g., \(R_k = I - X_k^T X_k\)). For non‑symmetric cases (e.g., general matrix inverse), the Frobenius‑norm minimization may not control the spectral norm, breaking convergence guarantees. A resolution would extend the analysis to non‑symmetric \(R_k\) and test on functions like matrix logarithm.

5. **Does PRISM remain stable in long‑horizon, stochastic training?**  
   Experiments cover 50–200 epochs; however, adaptive \(\alpha_k\) choices in noisy, million‑step training could amplify gradient noise or drift. A resolution would require extensive long‑run experiments (billions of tokens) and theoretical analysis of error propagation under stochastic perturbations.

## Limitations
- **Specification gaps**: The heavy‑tailed Marchenko‑Pastur (HTMP) matrix generator is not fully described, preventing exact replication of spectral experiments. Muon/Shampoo integration details (e.g., preconditioner damping, update frequency) are also omitted, possibly affecting reported speedups.
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: PRISM’s convergence depends critically on the spectral interval \([\ell,u]\) and sketch size \(p\); mis‑specification can violate the quadratic contraction condition. The minimal robust \(p\) for extreme spectra is not established.
- **Empirical scope**: Validation is limited to GPT‑2 and ResNet models; applicability to larger architectures (e.g., LLMs with billions of parameters) and diverse matrix shapes remains unknown.

## Confidence
- **Adaptive acceleration without spectral bounds**: High (sound algorithmic design) / Medium (real‑world optimizer stability due to missing implementation details)
- **Theoretical convergence guarantees**: High for polar decomposition (Theorem 1) / Medium for square root and sketched case (Theorem 2 suggests slight constant degradation)
- **Empirical speedups in Shampoo/Muon**: Medium (promising results but reproducibility hampered by omitted hyperparameters)
- **Overhead reduction to \(O(n^2 \log n)\)**: High (follows from linear‑algebraic complexity analysis)
