# Weighted Temporal Decay Loss for Learning Wearable PPG Data with Sparse Clinical Labels

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.02917  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02917  
- **Reference count**: 0  
- **Primary result**: Introduces a weighted temporal decay loss that dynamically down-weights PPG segments by their time gap to the nearest lab draw, achieving average 0.712 AUROC and 0.715 AUPRC across 10 biomarkers—outperforming fine-tuned self-supervised (0.660/0.674) and Random Forest (0.599/0.626) baselines.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles a key challenge in training machine learning models on wearable photoplethysmography (PPG) data: clinical laboratory labels are sparse and temporally misaligned with continuous PPG streams, making them potentially unreliable as supervision. Their solution is a weighted temporal decay loss that reduces the contribution of PPG segments whose nearest corresponding lab draw is further in the past. This loss uses a biomarker-specific, learnable decay rate to model how quickly information from a given biomarker becomes stale. Evaluated on smartwatch PPG from 450 participants for 10 blood biomarkers, the method significantly improves performance over strong baselines, demonstrating that careful handling of label temporal misalignment unlocks more data for training.

## Method Summary
The core method modifies the standard training loss by weighting each PPG segment’s loss contribution based on its time difference (Δt) to the closest laboratory measurement for that biomarker. A decay function (e.g., linear, exponential) w(t) maps Δt to a weight in [0,1], where larger Δt yields lower weight. Crucially, the decay rate (slope) is a learnable parameter, trained separately for each biomarker to capture differing temporal dynamics. A fixed 30-day temporal window is used; segments with Δt > 30 days receive zero weight. The weighted losses from all segments in a subject are pooled to form the final optimization objective, allowing the model to prioritize more temporally proximate (and likely more reliable) supervisory signals while still utilizing all available data.

## Key Results
- Achieves **0.712 AUROC and 0.715 AUPRC** on average across 10 biomarkers in a subject-wise holdout setting.
- **Outperforms** a fine-tuned self-supervised PPG encoder (0.660 AUROC, 0.674 AUPRC) and a feature-based Random Forest (0.599 AUROC, 0.626 AUPRC).
- **Ablations confirm** that removing temporal weighting (all weights=1) causes a significant performance drop, and using a single shared decay rate across all biomarkers also harms performance, proving the necessity of both components.
- Among tested decay families (exponential, linear, cosine, inverse), **linear decay** yielded the most robust average performance, though optimal decay varied per biomarker.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The method mitigates **label noise induced by temporal misalignment**. Clinical lab draws are point-in-time measurements, while PPG is continuous. A lab value measured days or weeks before/after a PPG segment may not reflect the physiological state during that segment, especially for dynamic biomarkers. By down-weighting segments with large Δt, the loss function reduces the model's reliance on these potentially misaligned labels, preventing it from learning spurious correlations. The **learnable, biomarker-specific decay rate** allows the model to automatically infer how quickly a given biomarker's signal "ages" or becomes uninformative—e.g., a fast-changing marker like white blood cell count likely has a steeper decay than a stable lipid. This adaptive weighting enables the use of a larger pool of weakly labeled data while maintaining supervision quality.

## Foundational Learning
- **Temporal Label Noise**: Clinical labels collected at discrete times are used to supervise continuous signals, creating noise when the true underlying biomarker level drifts. *Why needed*: Without accounting for this, models learn from misaligned pairs. *Quick check*: Correlate prediction error with Δt; increasing error with larger Δt confirms the problem.
- **Learnable Loss Weighting**: Instead of hand-designing weights, parameters of the decay function are trained via gradient descent. *Why needed*: Optimal temporal sensitivity is biomarker-dependent and data-dependent. *Quick check*: Freeze decay rates after initialization; performance drop indicates learning is beneficial.
- **Self-Supervised PPG Representation**: The paper fine-tunes an encoder pretrained on unlabeled PPG via contrastive learning. *Why needed*: Provides a rich feature initialization for the limited labeled data. *Quick check*: Compare training from scratch vs. fine-tuning; pretraining should accelerate convergence.
- **Biomarker-Specific Modeling**: Each blood test target is predicted by a separate head with its own decay rate. *Why needed*: Different biomarkers have different half-lives and biological rhythms. *Quick check*: Shared decay rate across all biomarkers should underperform per-biomarker rates.
- **Clinical Workflow Integration**: The 30-day window reflects typical clinical lab ordering frequency. *Why needed*: Defines the maximum time lag considered acceptable for supervision. *Quick check*: Vary the window; performance likely peaks at a physiologically plausible range.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**:  
Raw PPG Segments -> Feature Encoder (pretrained) -> [For each biomarker: Temporal Decay Module (computes weight w(Δt) using biomarker-specific decay rate) -> Weighted Segment Pooling -> Biomarker-Specific Prediction Head]

**Critical Path**:  
1. For a given subject, all PPG segments within a 30-day window around a lab draw are identified.  
2. Each segment's feature vector from the encoder is multiplied by its time-decay weight w(Δt).  
3. Weighted features for all segments targeting the same biomarker are pooled (summed/averaged).  
4. The pooled vector is passed through the biomarker-specific head to produce a prediction.  
5. Loss (e.g., BCE) for that biomarker is computed and weighted by the number of contributing segments, then summed across biomarkers.

**Design Tradeoffs**:  
- **Decay Function Family**: Linear is simple and performed best on average, but exponential or cosine might better capture biological half-lives. Choice involves bias-variance tradeoff.  
- **Fixed vs. Learnable Window**: A hard 30-day cutoff is simple but may discard useful distant samples for slow-changing biomarkers. A soft, learnable truncation is more flexible but adds parameters and risk of overfitting.  
- **Per-Biomarker vs. Shared Parameters**: Separate decay rates capture heterogeneity but increase parameter count; shared rates reduce complexity at the cost of expressivity.

**Failure Signatures**:  
- Decay rates for some biomarkers collapse to 0 (ignore all data) or 1 (ignore temporal weighting), suggesting optimization instability or insufficient gradient signal.  
- Performance degrades sharply on biomarkers with known rapid fluctuations (e.g., glucose) if the chosen window or decay shape is inappropriate.  
- Model becomes overly reliant on a small number of very recent segments, indicated by extreme weight distributions (most weights near 0).

**First Three Experiments**:  
1. **Ablation - No Weighting**: Set all w(Δt)=1. Expect significant drop in AUROC/AUPRC, confirming the necessity of temporal correction.  
2. **Ablation - Shared Decay Rate**: Use a single decay rate for all 10 biomarkers. Expect intermediate performance between no weighting and full per-biomarker model.  
3. **Window Sensitivity**: Vary the maximum Δt (7, 30, 90 days). Expect a peak at 30 days; shorter windows lose data, longer windows add noise, especially for dynamic biomarkers.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: Should the temporal window length be learned jointly with the decay rate and tailored per biomarker?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] In the Limitations section, the authors state: "A natural extension is to learn the window length (or a soft truncation) jointly with the decay rate, allowing the model to choose how far back to look for each biomarker."
- **Why unresolved**: The study used a fixed 30-day window for all biomarkers, but different biomarkers have varying temporal sensitivities (e.g., fast-changing vs. stable). A fixed window may include overly noisy distant samples or exclude potentially informative ones for certain biomarkers.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Parameterize the window as a learnable, differentiable mask over time gaps, trained per biomarker. Compare performance against fixed windows and correlate learned window lengths with known biomarker dynamics (e.g., half-lives, circadian patterns).

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: How does the model generalize to external datasets with different wearable devices, demographics, or clinical workflows?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] In both Limitations and Conclusion, the authors note: "Our study uses data from a single device family and a single health system, so domain shift... may affect performance; external validation remains important" and "Future work should focus on validating this model on external datasets from diverse clinical environments."
- **Why unresolved**: All data came from Samsung Galaxy Watch 6 and one health system, limiting assessment of robustness to hardware variations, population differences, or lab measurement discrepancies that could degrade performance in real-world deployment.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Apply the trained model to independent datasets from varied wearables (e.g., Apple Watch, Fitbit), demographic groups (age, ethnicity), and clinical settings. Report AUROC/AUPRC shifts, perform subgroup analyses, and identify specific failure modes due to domain shift.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: What is the optimal decay function for each biomarker, and how does it relate to the biomarker's temporal dynamics?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] In Section 3.2, the authors observe: "decay selection should consider the temporal characteristics of the target biomarker and the empirical distribution of Δt." They found linear decay best on average but noted per-biomarker variations.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper compared four decay families but did not systematically optimize the decay type per biomarker. The decay shape may significantly impact performance depending on how quickly a biomarker's underlying physiology changes (e.g., WBC vs. lipids).
- **What evidence would resolve it**: For each biomarker, train models with all decay families (exponential, linear, cosine, inverse) using learned decay rates. Evaluate which decay maximizes performance per biomarker, then correlate the optimal decay and learned rate with external biological knowledge (e.g., diurnal variation, metabolic turnover).

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Can the model effectively predict biomarker levels across the full distribution, not just the extreme quantiles?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] In Section 3.1, the authors state: "We report a two-extreme protocol (top vs. bottom quartile) to test feasibility... Performance in the middle 50% is not assessed, and full-distribution modeling is left for future work."
- **Why unresolved**: The binary classification of only the top and bottom 25% of biomarker values does not assess model calibration or discrimination for intermediate or continuous ranges, which are crucial for clinical decision-making (e.g., borderline dyslipidemia).
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Extend the model to multi-class classification or regression using all available biomarker values (not just extremes). Evaluate metrics like mean squared error, quantile F1-scores, or calibration curves on the middle 50% of values, and compare against baselines to determine if temporal decay weighting benefits full-spectrum prediction.

## Limitations
- **Dataset Scope & Generalizability**: Results are from 450 smartwatch users using a single device (Samsung Galaxy Watch 6) within one health system. Population diversity (age, skin tone, activity patterns) and hardware heterogeneity are unassessed, limiting claims about real-world deployment across diverse settings.
- **Temporal Decay Assumption**: The model assumes label unreliability scales monotonically and predictably with Δt via a simple parametric decay. This may oversimplify complex physiological dynamics (e.g., circadian rhythms, chronic condition progression, medication effects). No validation against contemporaneous true biomarker measurements.
- **Baseline Selection**: Comparisons include a fine-tuned self-supervised model and Random Forest, but not recent large-scale PPG foundation models, which might set a higher bar and potentially subsume the benefits of explicit temporal weighting.

## Next Checks
1. **External Validation on Diverse Cohorts**: Replicate the study on an independent wearable PPG dataset (e.g., from Apple Watch, Fitbit, or MIMIC-III) with matched clinical labs across different demographics and healthcare systems. Measure shifts in AUROC/AUPRC and conduct subgroup analyses to identify domain-specific failure modes.
2. **Ground Truth Temporal Error Analysis**: In a subset of participants, collect paired PPG and *immediately contemporaneous* finger-stick or point-of-care biomarker measurements. Quantify the actual increase in label error (e.g., MAE) as Δt increases and compare to the model's learned decay rates to validate their alignment with empirical staleness.
3. **Comparison to Modern Foundation Models**: Integrate the weighted temporal decay loss into training a state-of-the-art PPG foundation model (e.g., pretrained on millions of unlabeled segments). Evaluate whether the temporal weighting provides an additional gain over foundation model pretraining alone, or if large-scale pretraining already implicitly learns to handle temporal misalignment.
