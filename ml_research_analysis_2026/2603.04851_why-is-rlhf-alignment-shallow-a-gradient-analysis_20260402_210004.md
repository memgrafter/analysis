# Why Is RLHF Alignment Shallow? A Gradient Analysis

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.04851
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04851
- **Reference count:** 32
- **Primary result:** RLHF alignment produces shallow changes concentrated in early tokens because gradients vanish beyond the harm horizon, making models vulnerable to prefilling attacks; a deep alignment objective with recovery penalties yields non-zero gradients at all positions and improves robustness to prefilling attacks.

## Executive Summary
Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) is known to align language models but often yields only superficial changes that affect the first few tokens. This paper theoretically explains this phenomenon by analyzing the gradients of a harm objective. Using a martingale decomposition of sequence-level harm, the authors prove that the gradient at position *t* equals the covariance between the conditional expected harm and the score function. Consequently, if harm is determined by a finite prefix (the harm horizon), tokens beyond that horizon receive zero gradient signal, making deep alignment impossible under standard RLHF. The equilibrium KL divergence between the aligned and base models scales with the newly defined harm information *I_t*, which explains the empirical observation that KL concentration occurs only on early tokens. To address this, the paper derives a deep alignment objective that adds recovery penalties encouraging the model to emit refusal tokens at any position, which creates a non-zero gradient signal throughout the sequence and provides a theoretical justification for data augmentation techniques that improve robustness against prefilling attacks.

## Method Summary
The authors develop a theoretical framework for understanding gradient propagation in RLHF for autoregressive language models. They decompose the harm of a full sequence into per-token innovations via a martingale, leading to the key identity: ∇θE[Harm] = Σ_t E_y≤t[Cov(h_t, ∇θ log Pθ(yt|y<t))], where h_t is the conditional expected harm given the prefix. This shows that alignment pressure is concentrated where the harm information *I_t* = E[Δ_t²] is nonzero. They prove that if harm is a deterministic function of a prefix of length *k* (the harm horizon), then for all *t* > *k*, *I_t* = 0 and thus gradients vanish. At equilibrium, the per-position KL divergence *D_KL^{(t)}* is bounded by *O(λ² I_t)*, confirming that alignment changes are confined to the harm horizon. To enable deep alignment, they propose augmenting the RLHF objective with a recovery penalty µ Σ_t γ^{t-1} E_{y<t∼Q}[1 - Pθ(yt ∈ R|y<t)] that promotes the use of a predefined set of recovery tokens (e.g., "Sorry", "I", "cannot") under adversarial prefixes from distribution *Q*. This introduces a non-zero gradient at every position, as the recovery term depends on all tokens.

## Key Results
- The gradient at position *t* is Cov(h_t, ∇θ log Pθ(yt|y<t)); if the harm horizon is *k*, then for *t* > *k* the covariance is zero.
- Equilibrium KL divergence scales quadratically with the penalty λ and linearly with the harm information *I_t*, causing KL to drop sharply beyond the harm horizon.
- The deep alignment objective creates a non-zero gradient signal at all positions, enabling changes beyond the harm horizon and improving robustness to prefilling attacks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The gradient of expected harm at each position equals the covariance between the conditional expected harm and the score function, causing alignment pressure to concentrate where harm information is nonzero.
- **Mechanism:** Using the autoregressive factorization and the martingale decomposition of harm, Theorem 8 shows ∇θE[Harm(y)] = Σ_t E_{y<t}[Cov( h_t(y≤t), ∇θ log Pθ(yt|y<t) )]. This covariance is nonzero only if the token *y_t* influences the conditional expected harm *h_t*, i.e., if *I_t* > 0.
- **Core assumption:** Harm is a deterministic function of the full output sequence and the model is trained via gradient-based minimization of the RLHF objective (2) with a KL penalty.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "The gradient at position t equals the covariance between the conditional expected harm and the score function." [section 5] Theorem 8 provides the full derivation. [corpus] Neighbors such as "A Common Pitfall of Margin-based Language Model Alignment: Gradient Entanglement" examine gradient pathologies but do not derive this covariance structure, indicating limited direct corroboration.
- **Break condition:** If harm depends on internal states not captured by output tokens, or if the score function estimator is biased, the covariance identity may fail. Also, if the reward model approximates harm poorly, gradients may not reflect true harm information.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Positions beyond the harm horizon receive exactly zero gradient signal, making deep alignment impossible under standard objectives.
- **Mechanism:** Theorem 9 defines the harm horizon *k* such that Harm(y) = g(y≤*k*) almost surely. For *t* > *k*, the innovation Δ_t = h_t - h_{t-1} = 0 (since h_t = g(y≤*k*) is constant given y<t). Hence *I_t* = E[Δ_t²] = 0 and Cov(h_t, score)=0 (Theorem 10). No gradient reaches these positions.
- **Core assumption:** Harm is determined by a finite prefix and the model's conditional distributions are smooth enough for the gradient identity.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "positions beyond the harm horizon ... receive zero gradient signal during training." [section 6] Theorem 10 and its proof. [corpus] "Safety Alignment Should Be Made More Than Just a Few Tokens Deep" (arxiv:2406.05946) empirically finds KL divergence decays after a few tokens, consistent with a short harm horizon and zero gradient beyond it.
- **Break condition:** If harm has no finite horizon (*I_t* > 0 for all *t*) or is stochastic/context-dependent such that h_t never becomes constant, gradient may propagate deeper. However, empirical evidence suggests many harmful outcomes are determined early.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** At equilibrium, the per-position KL divergence between aligned and base models scales as *O(λ² I_t)*, explaining the observed KL concentration on early tokens.
- **Mechanism:** Under exponential-family decoupling, first-order optimality gives θ* - θ_base ∝ λ G_t. Expanding D_KL^{(t)} = ½(θ*-θ_base)^T Ẽ_t (θ*-θ_base) yields D_KL^{(t)} ∝ λ² ∥G_t∥². Theorem 13 bounds ∥G_t∥² ≤ Ẽ·I_t, so D_KL^{(t)} = O(λ² I_t). Positions with *I_t* ≈ 0 thus have negligible KL (Corollary 15).
- **Core assumption:** Per-position parameters are decoupled (exponential family), KL penalty is quadratic near the base model, and λ is small.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "equilibrium KL divergence tracks this quantity" [I_t]. [section 8] Theorem 14 and Corollary 15. [corpus] "Safety Alignment Should Be Made More Than Just a Few Tokens Deep" reports that KL divergences decay rapidly after a prefix, matching the predicted shape if *I_t* decays.
- **Break condition:** With shared parameters (transformers), cross-position coupling creates incidental KL beyond the horizon (Appendix A), but this KL is uncorrelated with harm (Theorem 26). The core insight—that safety-relevant change is confined to the harm horizon—remains valid as long as *I_t* = 0.

## Foundational Learning
### Martingale (conditional expected harm)
- **Why needed here:** The martingale property of h_t(y≤t) = E[Harm(y) | y≤t] enables decomposition of harm into per-position innovations Δ_t, which define harm information I_t and are central to the gradient characterization.
- **Quick check question:** Show that the sequence h_t is a martingale and explain why Δ_t = h_t - h_{t-1} are orthogonal and sum to Harm(y) - E[Harm].

### Score function and policy gradient identity
- **Why needed here:** The identity ∇θE_Pθ[f(y)] = E_Pθ[f(y) ∇θ log Pθ(y)] is used to derive the gradient expression; the autoregressive factorization then isolates the contribution at each position.
- **Quick check question:** Derive Theorem 8 step-by-step: substitute the martingale decomposition into the policy gradient and argue that cross-terms vanish except when s = t.

### KL divergence quadratic expansion and Fisher information
- **Why needed here:** The equilibrium analysis relies on expanding D_KL(Pθ || P_base) around θ_base; the Hessian equals the Fisher information matrix, which links parameter shifts to KL cost and enables the O(λ² I_t) bound.
- **Quick check question:** Prove that at θ = θ_base, the Hessian of D_KL(Pθ || P_base) with respect to θ is the Fisher information matrix Ẽ_t.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Base language model P_base → Harm model (reward model) → Training objective (standard or deep) with recovery token set R and adversarial prefix distribution Q → Monitoring components (per-position KL, recovery probability, harm score)

### Critical path
1. Identify a nonempty set of recovery tokens R such that P_base(yt ∈ R | any prefix) > 0.
2. Construct a distribution Q over harmful prefixes (e.g., from attack datasets or high-harm continuations).
3. Implement the deep objective H_deep(θ) with hyperparameters λ, µ, γ.
4. Train the model using gradient-based optimization (RLHF or DPO) with batches containing prefixes from Q for the recovery term.
5. Evaluate recovery probability at increasing depths on held‑out harmful prefixes and perform prefilling attack tests.

### Design tradeoffs
- Depth vs. capability: Larger µ or γ = 1 increases total KL, risking loss of fluency/knowledge. Smaller γ reduces total KL but weakens pressure at deep positions.
- Recovery token selection: Tokens must have nonzero base probability; if p_min is tiny, required µ grows as log(1/p_min), which may be impractically large.
- Importance weighting: Q should upweight prefixes where the base model fails to recover; otherwise recovery penalties may be wasted on safe prefixes.
- λ vs. µ: Balancing harm avoidance and recovery; within the harm horizon these can conflict if recovery tokens increase h_t.

### Failure signatures
- KL profile still decays sharply after a few tokens → recovery penalties too weak or p_min too small.
- Recovery probability at deep positions remains close to base model → insufficient µ or γ.
- Model becomes overly refusal‑heavy or nonsensical → µ too large, causing over‑regularization.
- Training instability (gradient explosion) from the recovery term, especially if p_min is very small and µ is large.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Baseline characterization:** Train a standard RLHF/DPO model. Compute per‑position KL (aligned vs. base) and estimate I_t via a reward model. Verify that KL decays mirror I_t decay.
2. **Deep alignment proof‑of‑concept:** Implement H_deep with small µ, γ < 1, and a simple R = {"Sorry", "I", "cannot"}. Sample prefixes from a set of harmful completions. Measure recovery probability at depths t = 1, 3, 5, 10 and compare to the standard model.
3. **Prefilling attack robustness:** Perform a prefilling attack where the adversary supplies prefixes of increasing length (covering the harm horizon). Measure attack success rate (fraction of completions that are harmful) for standard vs. deep models. For the deep model, test how success rate drops as prefix length grows and how it varies with µ and γ.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1: Semantic-level harm information
What is the semantic-level analogue of harm information and how does token-level alignment depth relate to semantic harm determination? The paper notes that its formalism operates at the token level, but harm is a semantic property; a semantic-level theory would be more fundamental. Resolution would require defining a semantic harm horizon and proving analogous gradient-vanishing results or a mapping between semantic and token-level information.

### Open Question 2: Multi-turn conversations
How can the gradient analysis be extended to multi-turn conversations to handle inter-turn credit assignment for alignment depth? The current analysis covers single-turn generation; multi-turn interactions introduce dependencies across turns, and a theory of inter-turn credit assignment is needed. A hierarchical martingale decomposition across turns could be a direction.

### Open Question 3: Capacity of transformers
Can finite-capacity transformers approximate the Gibbs distribution prescribed by the deep alignment objective for all harmful prefixes, and what capacity is required? The deep objective defines a Gibbs distribution over sequences, but finite models may lack expressiveness, especially for rare or long harmful prefixes. Evidence could come from scaling studies or theoretical approximation results.

## Limitations
- Finite harm horizon assumption may not hold for all harmful outcomes; many harms are context-dependent with no fixed prefix length.
- Recovery token set R is heuristic; selecting tokens with sufficient base probability is nontrivial and may require impractically large penalty coefficients.
- Shared parameters in transformers cause incidental KL beyond the horizon, obscuring the interpretation of alignment change.

## Confidence
- Gradient covariance identity: High
- Zero gradient beyond harm horizon: High
- KL divergence scaling with I_t: Medium
- Deep alignment objective effectiveness: Medium
