# UI-Venus-1.5 Technical Report

## Quick Facts

- **arXiv ID**: 2602.09082
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09082
- **Reference count**: 21
- **Primary result**: UI-Venus-1.5 achieves state-of-the-art performance on GUI agent benchmarks via a four-stage pipeline, with top scores: ScreenSpot-Pro 69.6%, VenusBench-GD 75.0%, AndroidWorld 77.6%, WebVoyager 76.0%.

## Executive Summary

UI-Venus-1.5 tackles the challenge of building a single GUI agent that generalizes across multiple domains while maintaining strong performance. It introduces a unified end-to-end system constructed through a four-stage pipeline: (1) Mid-Training on over 10B tokens from 30+ datasets to learn foundational GUI semantics; (2) domain-specific Offline RL to train specialized agents for grounding, web, and mobile tasks; (3) scaled Online RL with full-trajectory rollouts using a Device-as-a-Service (DaaS) infrastructure; and (4) TIES-Model Merging to combine the specialized checkpoints into a single model with refusal capability and an expanded action set. Ablation studies confirm that each stage contributes measurable improvements, and the merging process effectively balances cross-task performance with minimal regression.

The resulting agent sets new state-of-the-art results on key benchmarks: ScreenSpot-Pro (69.6%), VenusBench-GD (75.0%), AndroidWorld (77.6%), and WebVoyager (76.0%). These gains demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed staged training and merging approach for achieving broad generality in GUI agents.

## Method Summary

UI-Venus-1.5 builds upon a Qwen3-VL multimodal backbone (2B/8B/30B-A3B). The training proceeds in four sequential stages. First, Mid-Training continues pretraining on a curated corpus of >10B GUI tokens from more than 30 datasets, employing four supervision objectives (navigation & grounding, sequential reasoning, GUI-VQA, fine-grained perception). A teacher model (Qwen3-VL-235B) iteratively scores and rewrites trajectories, while a DaaS-driven generation pipeline produces real-device interactions to boost data quality. Second, Offline RL applies Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) separately to three domains (grounding, web, mobile) with reward functions that combine format compliance, coordinate accuracy, and (for grounding) a refusal output for infeasible instructions. Third, Online RL leverages a large-scale DaaS infrastructure (thousands of physical devices) to perform full-trajectory rollouts. A composite reward (task completion, length decay, invalid action penalties) and dual verification (rule-based + MLLM judge) guide learning; GRPO with adaptive KL and annealed entropy ensures stability. Fourth, the three domain-optimized models are merged using TIES-Merge, which prunes low-magnitude updates and resolves sign conflicts to minimize interference, yielding a single unified agent.

## Key Results

- **Grounding benchmarks**: ScreenSpot-Pro 69.6%, VenusBench-GD 75.0%
- **Navigation benchmarks**: AndroidWorld 77.6%, WebVoyager 76.0%

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Mid-Training on a large corpus of GUI data establishes foundational visual-semantic representations that reduce reward sparsity and improve exploration during subsequent reinforcement learning.
- **Mechanism:** The Mid-Training stage aggregates >30 datasets spanning >10B tokens from sources like Mind2Web, ShowUI, and AITW. An iterative refinement loop uses a strong teacher model (Qwen3‑VL‑235B) to score and rewrite trajectories, raising high‑quality sample ratio from 69.7% to 89.7%. A self‑evolving generation pipeline via DaaS yields >30K real‑device trajectories, increasing task success from 17.9% to >70%.
- **Core assumption:** General‑purpose vision‑language models lack fine‑grained GUI priors; intensive supervised exposure to UI data can align representations with interaction semantics without forgetting general knowledge.
- **Evidence anchors:** [Abstract] "Mid‑Training on 10B tokens across 30+ datasets to establish foundational GUI semantics". [Section 2.1] "The Mid‑Training phase is designed to bridge the semantic gap between general visual perception and the fine‑grained structural understanding required by GUI Agents." [Section 3.3] Mid‑Training improves latent space clustering: Silhouette Score +34% (0.235→0.315), Intra‑class Consistency -11.6% (0.448→0.396), showing enhanced feature discriminativeness. [Corpus] Neighboring works (e.g., UI‑TARS‑2, MagicGUI) also emphasize large‑scale pretraining, but the specific iteration and DaaS‑augmented loop described here is not directly evidenced in the corpus.
- **Break condition:** If the corpus fails to cover target application domains (e.g., niche Chinese apps) or if teacher scoring introduces systematic biases, the learned representation may not transfer, causing RL to suffer from misaligned features and poor policy initialization.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Scaled Online RL with full‑trajectory rollouts on real devices via DaaS aligns training with long‑horizon success, directly addressing the step‑trace discrepancy of Offline‑RL and handling dynamic GUI environments.
- **Mechanism:** After domain‑specific Offline‑RL, the agent interacts with thousands of physical devices managed by a Device‑as‑a‑Service layer. Rollouts generate complete traces; a composite reward includes task completion, length decay, and invalid‑action penalties. Advantage estimation uses group‑relative normalization across sampled trajectories, and a dual verification (rule‑based + MLLM judge) determines success. Regularization via adaptive KL and annealed entropy stabilizes training.
- **Core assumption:** Only interactive experience can teach the agent to compose individual actions into successful multi‑step traces; a scalable device infrastructure exists to provide sufficient environmental diversity.
- **Evidence anchors:** [Abstract] "scaled Online Reinforcement Learning with full‑trajectory rollouts using a Device‑as‑a‑Service infrastructure". [Section 2.3.1] "Observing that step‑level success rates increase while trace‑level peaks and declines... Online RL serves as the most critical stage for enhancing autonomous navigation capabilities." [Table 7] Ablation: Online RL boosts AndroidWorld performance by up to +14.5% (2B model: 45.3% → 59.8%), confirming its significant contribution. [Corpus] Several neighbors (e.g., SWIRL, UIPro) explore online/interactive RL, but the full‑trajectory, DaaS‑scaled approach described is not directly supported by the provided corpus entries.
- **Break condition:** If device access is limited or rollout latency high, sample efficiency drops; extremely sparse or ambiguous task definitions can still lead to poor credit assignment despite full‑trace rewards.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** TIES‑Model Merging combines domain‑specialized checkpoints into a single unified agent with minimal regression, enabling practical end‑to‑end deployment across grounding, web, and mobile tasks.
- **Mechanism:** After obtaining three specialized models (grounding, mobile, web) via Offline‑/Online‑RL, their parameters are merged using TIES‑Merge. The method first prunes low‑magnitude updates from task vectors, then resolves sign conflicts by electing a dominant direction per parameter. Only updates aligned with that sign are aggregated, reducing interference.
- **Core assumption:** Fine‑tuned models share a common parameter space around the base; preserving only aligned changes mitigates conflicting information when combining skills.
- **Evidence anchors:** [Abstract] "TIES‑Model Merging to consolidate specialized checkpoints into a single model with refusal capability and an expanded action set." [Section 2.4] "TIES‑Merge reduces parameter interference... achieves markedly lower performance regression than simple interpolation." [Table 7] For UI‑Venus‑1.5‑30B‑A3B, Linear Merge loses 2.9% (ScreenSpot‑Pro) and 2.3% (AndroidWorld), while TIES loses only 1.4% on ScreenSpot‑Pro and actually improves AndroidWorld by +2.1%, demonstrating superior balance. [Corpus] Model merging is a known technique (e.g., in DeepResearch, Ling‑Team), but the specific application to GUI‑specialized models with TIES is not detailed in the provided neighbors.
- **Break condition:** If the specialized models have diverged significantly (e.g., due to high learning rates or contradictory objectives), sign consensus may be low, leading to excessive pruning and loss of essential capabilities.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**: Base model (Qwen3-VL 2B/8B/30B-A3B) → Mid-Training data pipeline (dataset aggregation, teacher refinement, DaaS generation) → Offline-RL modules (grounding, mobile, web) with reward calculators → Online-RL infrastructure (DaaS: GCGW + SDK, GRPO trainer, verification modules) → Model Merge component (TIES implementation) → Action space expander (Hover, DoubleClick, Hotkey, refusal handling).

**Critical path**:
1. Assemble Mid-Training corpus and run continued pretraining.
2. Perform Offline-RL on each domain to obtain three specialized checkpoints.
3. Initiate Online-RL on mobile/web using DaaS rollouts, starting from corresponding Offline checkpoints.
4. Merge the three final checkpoints with TIES-Merge to produce UI-Venus-1.5.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Mid-Training is compute‑heavy but yields robust features; skipping it leads to weaker RL exploration.
- Online RL costs device time and energy; scaling DaaS is necessary but introduces orchestration complexity.
- Merging simplifies deployment but may sacrifice peak domain performance; TIES reduces regression at the cost of additional merging hyperparameters.
- Refusal capability slightly harms benchmarks without negative examples but improves real‑world safety.

**Failure signatures**:
- Mid-Training: Low latent space separability (Silhouette <0.25) indicates insufficient GUI priors; check data diversity and teacher score distribution.
- Offline-RL: High format reward but low action accuracy suggests reward hacking; ensure coordinate/content rewards are properly weighted.
- Online-RL: Trace success stagnates or degrades while step accuracy rises; adjust KL coefficient or entropy annealing schedule.
- Merge: >2% drop on any domain after merging indicates over‑pruning; tune TIES magnitude threshold or sign consensus threshold.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. Mid-Training ablation: Train a baseline with only Offline-RL vs. adding Mid-Training; evaluate on a validation grounding set and compute Silhouette Score to confirm representation improvement.
2. Reward component test: Ablate the format reward and coordinate tolerance in Offline-RL; measure step vs. trace success on a mobile navigation subset to validate reward design.
3. Merge strategy comparison: Take three domain checkpoints and merge via Linear and TIES; compare cross-domain performance on ScreenSpot-Pro (grounding) and AndroidWorld (navigation) to quantify interference and benefits.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What is the fundamental reason behind the divergence between step-level and trace-level accuracy during Offline-RL training for GUI agents, and how does online RL with full-trajectory rollouts specifically resolve this credit assignment problem?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper explicitly observes and graphs this discrepancy in Figure 6, stating: "while step-level success rates increase steadily, trace-level success rates eventually peak and then decline." They attribute this to Offline-RL's limitation in optimizing for long-horizon composition and introduce Online-RL to "explicitly optimiz[e] for trace-level rewards."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper postulates the cause but does not provide a rigorous analysis (e.g., ablation on reward shaping, empirical study of failure modes in mid-trajectory steps). The claimed resolution via Online-RL is presented as effective but the precise mechanism (e.g., how group-relative advantages in GRPO differ from step-wise rewards in stabilizing long-term planning) is not deeply investigated.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A controlled experiment isolating the reward function (e.g., comparing step-wise vs. trajectory-only vs. hybrid rewards) and analyzing the learned policy's action distribution over time. Probing the internal representations to see if Online-RL fosters better state abstraction for multi-step planning would also help.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What are the precise parameter interference mechanisms that cause performance regression in domain-specific models after linear merging, and why does TIES-Merge mitigate this more effectively? Can the merging process be further improved to eliminate the small observed regressions (e.g., the -1.4% on ScreenSpot-Pro)?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper compares Linear Merge and TIES-Merge, showing TIES causes less performance drop. They state TIES works by "pruning noisy updates and resolving sign conflicts" but do not analyze *why* conflicts occur specifically in their GUI domain or quantify the magnitude/type of conflicting parameter directions.
- **Why unresolved:** The explanation is high-level. It doesn't specify if interference stems from opposing gradients for similar visual features (e.g., grounding vs. navigation), or if certain layers (e.g., early vision vs. late policy heads) are more susceptible. The small residual regression after TIES suggests interference isn't fully resolved.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An analysis of the "task vectors" (delta weights) for each specialized model, calculating pairwise cosine similarity or sign agreement ratios per parameter tensor. Identifying which modules (e.g., vision encoder, action head) exhibit highest conflict and developing a targeted merging strategy (e.g., layer-wise weighting) could test this.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What is the optimal composition and quality threshold for the Mid-Training corpus (10B tokens, 30+ datasets) to maximize downstream RL efficiency, and what is the minimal adequate scale?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper demonstrates Mid-Training's value via latent space metrics (Silhouette Score) and ablation gains (+6-7% on grounding, +6.5% on AndroidWorld after Offline-RL). However, they do not study data scaling curves (e.g., performance vs. tokens) or the contribution of specific dataset categories (GUI-VQA vs. Navigation vs. Grounding). The iterative refinement pipeline focuses on cleaning noisy traces but not on strategic data selection.
- **Why unresolved:** The 10B token scale is presented as a fixed, successful choice. It's unclear if this is near an optimum or overkill. The cost of Mid-Training is high; understanding marginal returns per dataset type or token is crucial for efficiency.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic ablations training models on graduated subsets of the Mid-Training corpus (e.g., 1B, 5B, 10B tokens) and measuring: (a) latent space metrics (like Table 6), (b) final performance after full pipeline, and (c) sample efficiency of subsequent RL stages. Analyzing per-category impact by holding out specific functional groups (Figure 4a) would identify essential data sources.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How does the architectural design and scale of the DaaS infrastructure (thousands of devices, millisecond scheduling) quantitatively impact the sample efficiency and final performance of the Online-RL stage, and what are the practical deployment costs versus accuracy trade-offs?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper dedicates a section (2.3.2) to describing DaaS's technical achievements: scale, throughput, and concurrency. They state it enabled "scaled Online RL" and cite improvements (e.g., +14.5% on AndroidWorld for 2B model). However, no direct correlation is drawn between specific infrastructure metrics (e.g., concurrent devices, latency) and training dynamics like convergence speed or final policy quality.
- **Why unresolved:** The description is of a successful engineering implementation, not a controlled study. The cost of such an infrastructure (hardware, maintenance, energy) is not discussed. It's unclear if smaller-scale deployments would yield diminishing returns or if there are bottlenecks beyond device count (e.g., network bandwidth, GCGW processing).
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Experimentally varying DaaS scale (e.g., 100, 500, 1000 devices) while measuring metrics: trajectories generated per day, average rollout latency, GRPO update frequency, and final task success rate. A cost-benefit analysis modeling accuracy gains per additional device/hour would inform practical deployment.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** For the refusal capability in grounding, what is the precise failure mode when the model outputs `[-1, -1]` incorrectly (false refusal) or fails to refuse when it should (missed refusal), and how can the reward design (Section 2.2.1) be refined to improve calibration without sacrificing accuracy on standard grounding tasks?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper introduces refusal samples and a fixed `[-1, -1]` output. They acknowledge a "marginal performance trade-off on benchmarks lacking refusal examples (such as ScreenSpot-Pro)" but claim superiority on benchmarks with refusal tasks (VenusBench-GD). They do not analyze error types or the tension between refusal and localization accuracy.
- **Why unresolved:** The reward function (Equation 1) combines format and point-in-box rewards. For refusal, presumably a different logic applies (e.g., a binary correct/incorrect reward). It's unclear how the model learns to balance the two modes or if the format reward inadvertently penalizes valid refusals. The trade-off is accepted but not optimized.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An error analysis on a mixed dataset containing both standard and infeasible grounding instructions, categorizing failures into: (a) false positive (refuses valid element), (b) false negative (fails to refuse invalid element), (c) malformed output. Tuning the weights (w1, w2) or adding a dedicated refusal reward term and measuring the Pareto frontier between overall accuracy and refusal accuracy would provide insights.
