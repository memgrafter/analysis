# How Much Do LLMs Hallucinate in Document Q&A Scenarios? A 172-Billion-Token Study Across Temperatures, Context Lengths, and Hardware Platforms

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2603.08274
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08274
- **Reference count**: 16
- **Primary result**: Even the best model fabricates answers at a non-trivial rate (1.19% at 32K context), with fabrication rising steeply as context length increases (nearly tripling at 128K and exceeding 10% for all models at 200K).

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces RIKER, a novel methodology for measuring hallucination in LLMs during document Q&A by generating documents from a known ground truth, enabling deterministic and scalable evaluation. Evaluating 35 models across 172 billion tokens, the study finds that even state-of-the-art models exhibit non-trivial fabrication rates that increase dramatically with context length—exceeding 10% for all models at 200K tokens. Model architecture and family play a larger role than size in fabrication resistance, and temperature sampling, while often reducing coherence loss, can also lower fabrication rates, challenging the conventional use of greedy decoding. Results are consistent across NVIDIA, AMD, and Intel hardware platforms.

## Method Summary
The RIKER paradigm inverts traditional benchmark design: first, a structured ground truth database of entities and relationships is defined; then, documents are generated via deterministic templates (without LLMs) to populate contexts, and questions are created with known answers. This allows automatic scoring by exact match against the ground truth, avoiding contamination and judge bias. The study evaluates 35 open-weight models across three context lengths (32K, 128K, 200K tokens) and four temperature settings (0.0, 0.4, 0.7, 1.0) on three hardware platforms (NVIDIA H200, AMD MI300X, Intel Gaudi3), using vLLM for serving. Metrics include Overall accuracy, Faithfulness, Grounding, Fabrication (probe questions about non-existent entities), and Aggregation, along with truncation rate to measure coherence loss. Each configuration is repeated 8 times to ensure statistical reliability.

## Key Results
- Even the best model fabricates at 1.19% at 32K tokens; fabrication spikes with longer contexts, exceeding 10% for all models at 200K.
- Fabrication resistance is primarily determined by model family rather than size, and is distinct from grounding ability.
- Temperature settings above 0.0 often reduce fabrication and greatly decrease coherence loss (infinite loops), while maintaining comparable accuracy; findings hold across NVIDIA, AMD, and Intel hardware.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
RIKER's ground-truth-first design enables reliable, scalable hallucination measurement by inverting the traditional pipeline: it first defines a structured ground truth database (entities, relationships), then generates documents via deterministic templates and creates questions with known answers. This eliminates data contamination and judge bias, allowing deterministic automated scoring. The core assumption is that template-based generation produces sufficiently diverse and realistic documents. Evidence includes the abstract and Section 3's Coherent Simulated Universe, and the motivation from related work (TruthfulQA, FActScore). A break condition: if synthetic documents lack linguistic or structural diversity, the evaluation may underestimate hallucination on real heterogeneous corpora.

### Mechanism 2
Fabrication rates increase steeply with context length because longer contexts may cause attention diffusion or loss of focus, leading models to overconfidently invent entities. The core assumption is that the degradation is caused by context length itself, not correlated factors like document count. Evidence: the abstract's observation and Table 8 showing fabrication climbing from 32K to 200K (e.g., GLM 4.6 from 7.04% to 71.62%), consistent with other studies (RULER, LongBench v2). Break condition: if models adopt more efficient attention mechanisms or better context utilization, the trend may flatten or reverse.

### Mechanism 3
Higher temperatures often reduce fabrication and dramatically decrease coherence loss (infinite loops) compared to greedy decoding (T=0.0). Greedy decoding can trap models in repetitive loops when long contexts create self-reinforcing attention patterns; non-zero temperatures introduce stochasticity that breaks loops. Some models also benefit from sampling diversity for better calibration on fabrication probes. The core assumption is that coherence loss is primarily due to deterministic repetition, and fabrication reduction at higher temperatures is not offset by other errors. Evidence: abstract's truncation rate data and Section 5.6 (e.g., GLM 4.7 at 200K: 2.59% at T=0.0 vs 0.05% at T=1.0). Break condition: if models incorporate repetition penalties or improved decoding, the temperature dependence may vanish.

## Foundational Learning
- **Hallucination taxonomy**: Distinguishing grounding failures (missing relevant info) from fabrication (inventing non-existent info) is crucial. The paper uses L11/L12 probe questions to isolate fabrication.
  - Why needed: To interpret metrics like Faithfulness, Grounding, Fabrication and understand that a model can be good at grounding yet bad at fabrication.
  - Quick check: "If a model scores 90% on grounding but 50% on fabrication, what does that imply about its reliability?"

- **RIKER methodology**: The ground-truth-first evaluation approach that generates documents from known facts.
  - Why needed: It is the core experimental framework; without understanding it, the results' validity is unclear.
  - Quick check: "How does RIKER avoid the data contamination problem that plagues static benchmarks?"

- **Context length effects**: Understanding that longer contexts can degrade model performance due to attention limitations.
  - Why needed: The paper shows fabrication increases with context; this concept explains why.
  - Quick check: "Why might a model that performs well at 32K tokens fail at 200K tokens?"

- **Temperature sampling**: The role of temperature in decoding and its impact on output diversity and coherence.
  - Why needed: The paper's key finding about T=0.0 not being optimal hinges on understanding temperature.
  - Quick check: "What is the primary risk of using T=0.0 with very long contexts?"

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Document Generator -> Question Generator -> Model Serving Layer -> Scoring Engine -> Metadata Tracker -> Analysis Pipeline
- **Critical path**:
  1. Define ground truth schema and generate synthetic database.
  2. For each context length (32K, 128K, 200K), pack documents into contexts.
  3. Generate question sets per difficulty level.
  4. For each model, temperature, hardware: run inference on all questions, capture outputs.
  5. Score outputs against ground truth, compute metrics.
  6. Aggregate across runs, compute statistics, generate reports.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Scale vs. cost: 172B tokens required massive compute; tradeoff between statistical confidence and resource limits.
  - Deterministic scoring vs. realism: Using synthetic data ensures clean labels but may not capture all real-world document characteristics.
  - Temperature granularity: Only 4 temperatures tested; finer granularity might reveal optimal points but would multiply runs.
  - Hardware diversity: Testing across 3 platforms increases generalizability but adds complexity.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Cross-hardware inconsistency: Large deltas (>1-2 pp) in metrics between H200 and MI300X for same model at same temperature may indicate serving issues.
  - Temperature monotonicity failure: For most models, coherence loss should decrease with higher temperature; violation suggests data or serving bug.
  - Truncation rate anomalies: High truncation (>5%) at 32K may indicate output length misconfiguration.
  - Fabrication rate outliers: A model's fabrication rate much higher than family average may indicate prompt or scoring error.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Smoke test: Run a single model (e.g., GLM 4.5) at 32K, T=0.0 on one hardware with a tiny question set (100 Qs) to verify end-to-end pipeline.
  2. Cross-platform sanity: Run the same model on two hardware platforms at 32K, T=0.0 and check that overall accuracy difference is <1 pp.
  3. Temperature monotonicity: For a model known to have coherence loss (e.g., Llama 3.1 8B at 128K), verify that truncation rate decreases from T=0.0 to T=1.0.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Open Question 1**  
   - **Question**: How do incremental temperature values between 0.0 and 0.4 affect the trade-off between overall accuracy, fabrication rate, and coherence loss in document Q&A scenarios?  
   - **Basis in paper**: [explicit] Section 6.4 (Future Work) calls for "finer sampling in the 0.0–0.4 range could identify optimal temperature sweet spots."  
   - **Why unresolved**: The study tested only four temperatures (0.0, 0.4, 0.7, 1.0); the largest observed spread occurs between 0.0 and 0.4, so optimal points may have been missed.  
   - **What evidence would resolve it**: Systematically evaluate a finer temperature grid (e.g., 0.0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4) across representative models and context lengths, measuring overall accuracy, fabrication rates, and coherence loss to identify precise optima.

2. **Open Question 2**  
   - **Question**: For models advertising context windows of 1M+ tokens, how do hallucination rates and performance degradation behave at context lengths beyond 200K, such as 400K or 1M tokens?  
   - **Basis in paper**: [explicit] Section 6.4 (Future Work) states that "extending RIKER evaluations to 400K and beyond would test whether the degradation trends we observe continue, plateau, or accelerate."  
   - **Why unresolved**: The study’s longest context was 200K; the trajectory at longer lengths—crucial for evaluating advertised windows—remains unknown.  
   - **What evidence would resolve it**: Apply RIKER at 400K and 1M token contexts for a subset of models, then compare fabrication rates, overall accuracy, and aggregation performance to the observed 32K/128K/200K trends to determine the degradation curve.

3. **Open Question 3**  
   - **Question**: Can repetition penalty parameters reduce coherence loss (infinite generation loops) at temperature 0.0 without the accuracy trade-offs associated with increasing temperature?  
   - **Basis in paper**: [explicit] Section 6.4 (Future Work) suggests "repetition penalty parameters may provide an alternative mechanism to prevent these loops without the accuracy trade-offs of higher temperature."  
   - **Why unresolved**: The current experiments varied only temperature; repetition penalty was not manipulated, leaving open whether it can independently mitigate coherence loss.  
   - **What evidence would resolve it**: Conduct experiments varying repetition penalty values (e.g., 1.0–1.5) while holding temperature at 0.0 for models showing high coherence loss, measuring truncation rates alongside accuracy and fabrication to identify penalty settings that preserve coherence without sacrificing fidelity.

4. **Open Question 4**  
   - **Question**: What specific training or alignment techniques cause certain model families (e.g., GLM, MiniMax) to achieve low fabrication rates while maintaining strong grounding, compared to high-fabrication families (e.g., Llama)?  
   - **Basis in paper**: [explicit] Section 6.4 (Future Work) notes that "investigating what training approaches produce the low fabrication rates seen in the GLM and MiniMax families could benefit the broader field." Section 5.7 also states that "fabrication resistance appears to be a separately trained capability."  
   - **Why unresolved**: The study identifies a correlation between model family and fabrication resistance but does not probe the underlying training differences that might explain it.  
   - **What evidence would resolve it**: Perform comparative analysis of training data composition, fine-tuning objectives, and post-training methods (e.g., RLHF, DPO) of low- versus high-fabrication models, ideally with controlled ablations (e.g., fine-tuning a high-fabrication model using techniques from low-fabrication families) to establish causal factors.

5. **Open Question 5**  
   - **Question**: Does fabrication resistance generalize across languages, or do hallucination rates and context degradation patterns differ significantly in non-English document Q&A scenarios?  
   - **Basis in paper**: [explicit] Section 6.3 (Limitations) acknowledges that "all generated documents and questions are in English" and "fabrication rates and context degradation patterns may differ for other languages." Section 6.4 (Future Work) then includes "Multilingual evaluation."  
   - **Why unresolved**: All evaluation was conducted on English corpora; multilingual models may exhibit different behavior due to varying training data distribution and language-specific characteristics.  
   - **What evidence would resolve it**: Extend RIKER to generate ground-truth databases and document corpora in multiple languages (e.g., Spanish, Chinese, Arabic), then evaluate a diverse set of models at various context lengths to compare fabrication rates and context length effects across languages.

## Limitations
- Synthetic document generation may not fully capture the noise, formatting diversity, and implicit knowledge of real-world corpora, limiting generalizability.
- Exact scoring rules (e.g., case sensitivity, punctuation) and prompt templates are undisclosed, affecting reproducibility of reported metrics.
- Hardware configuration specifics (vLLM versions, kernel settings) are not fully detailed, introducing potential variability in cross-platform comparisons.

## Confidence
- Context length effects (fabrication rise): High
- Model family > size for fabrication resistance: High
- Temperature effects (reduced fabrication/coherence loss): Medium
- Cross-hardware consistency: Low
