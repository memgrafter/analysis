# Semantic Novelty at Scale: Narrative Shape Taxonomy and Readership Prediction in 28,606 Books

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.20647
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20647
- **Reference count**: 10
- **Primary result**: Discovered eight canonical narrative shape archetypes and identified volume (novelty variance) as the strongest length-independent predictor of readership (partial ρ = 0.32).

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a novel quantitative approach to narrative structure by measuring semantic novelty—defined as the cosine distance between a paragraph's embedding and the running centroid of all preceding paragraphs—across 28,606 public-domain English books from the PG19 corpus. By constructing novelty curves for each book, reducing them to 16-segment Piecewise Aggregate Approximation (PAA), and clustering with Ward-linkage, the authors identify eight recurring narrative shape archetypes. They then compute shape metrics (volume, speed, circuitousness) and correlate them with readership proxy (download counts) while controlling for book length. Key findings reveal that volume (variance of the novelty trajectory) is the most robust predictor of readership independent of length, while circuitousness loses significance after length adjustment, indicating a strong confound. Genre exerts a dominant constraint on narrative shape, and books from 1840–1910 show a trend toward increased predictability. The study demonstrates that low-dimensional narrative shapes capture meaningful aspects of storytelling that relate to reader engagement.

## Method Summary
The analysis uses the PG19 corpus (books published 1810–1923). Each book is split into paragraphs; paragraph embeddings are generated using a pretrained model (details unspecified). For each paragraph, semantic novelty is computed as the cosine distance between its embedding and the centroid of all previous paragraphs' embeddings, yielding a novelty curve. To enable cross-book comparison, curves are normalized to 100 points and reduced via PAA to 16 segments. These 16-dimensional vectors are clustered using Ward's hierarchical method, producing eight archetypal shapes. Shape metrics are defined: volume (variance of the curve), speed (average absolute first difference), and circuitousness (total absolute change relative to straight-line distance). Readership is approximated by download counts from the PG19 metadata. Partial correlation assesses the relationship between each metric and readership while controlling for book length (number of paragraphs). Additional analyses examine genre effects via chi-square tests and temporal trends by binning books into decades.

## Key Results
- Identified eight canonical narrative shape archetypes through clustering of PAA-reduced novelty curves.
- Volume (variance of novelty trajectory) is the strongest length-independent predictor of readership (partial ρ = 0.32); raw correlations for other metrics are weaker or confounded by length.
- Circuitousness shows a raw correlation of ρ = 0.41 with readership but drops to partial ρ = 0.11 after length control, demonstrating a severe confound.
- Genre strongly constrains narrative shape (χ² = 2121.6, p < 10⁻²⁴²).
- Books from 1840–1910 exhibit increasing predictability (declining T/I ratio) with correlation r = -0.74, p=0.037.
- SAX analysis indicates 85% of books have a unique symbolic representation, highlighting high individuality within the corpus.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Semantic novelty captures the degree to which each paragraph introduces new semantic content relative to the narrative established so far. The trajectory of novelty across a book reflects the pacing and structural dynamics of the story: high volatility (volume) suggests frequent shifts in topic, tone, or events, which may sustain reader interest; low volatility indicates consistency, possibly leading to monotony. Speed (rate of change) relates to how rapidly the narrative evolves, while circuitousness measures the total "travel" of the novelty path, often correlated with length. The method works because it compresses the high-dimensional text into a low-dimensional shape that summarizes these dynamics. The correlation of volume with readership, even after controlling for length, suggests that readers prefer narratives with a certain level of variability—neither too erratic nor too static. The confound of circuitousness by length underscores the importance of disentangling shape from size. The strong genre effect indicates that different narrative conventions produce distinct shape profiles, and the trend toward predictability may reflect evolving literary styles or reader preferences in the 19th century.

## Foundational Learning
- **Semantic Novelty**: *Why needed*: Quantifies how much new information a paragraph introduces relative to prior context, enabling measurement of narrative progression. *Quick check*: Compute novelty for a short story; does the curve spike at plot twists?
- **Paragraph Embeddings**: *Why needed*: Convert text to dense vectors for computable similarity; essential for novelty calculation. *Quick check*: Compare embeddings of synonymous sentences; similarity should be high.
- **Piecewise Aggregate Approximation (PAA)**: *Why needed*: Reduces a time series (novelty curve) to fixed-length representation for clustering, handling variable-length books. *Quick check*: Apply PAA to a sine wave; does it preserve the wave's overall shape?
- **Ward-linkage Clustering**: *Why needed*: Hierarchical method that minimizes within-cluster variance, suitable for grouping similar curve shapes without pre-specifying k. *Quick check*: Cluster synthetic curves; do similar shapes merge early?
- **Partial Correlation**: *Why needed*: Isolates the relationship between two variables while controlling for a third (e.g., length), avoiding spurious associations. *Quick check*: Compute partial correlation between X and Y controlling for Z; does it differ from raw correlation?
- **SAX (Symbolic Aggregate approXimation)**: *Why needed*: Converts curves to strings for uniqueness analysis, enabling quantification of how many books share the same symbolic signature. *Quick check*: Transform two similar curves; do they produce identical or similar strings?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Raw Books -> Paragraph Splitter -> Embedding Model -> Novelty Calculator -> Running Centroid Updater -> Novelty Curve Builder -> PAA Transformer -> Feature Vector -> Ward Clusterer -> Archetype Labeler -> Metrics Calculator -> Partial Correlation Analyzer -> Genre/Trend Analyzer

### Critical Path
1. Split each book into paragraphs.
2. Generate embedding for each paragraph.
3. Initialize empty centroid; for each paragraph, compute novelty as cosine distance to current centroid, then update centroid with new embedding.
4. Collect novelty values into a curve; normalize length to 100 points.
5. Apply PAA to reduce curve to 16 segments.
6. Perform Ward hierarchical clustering on all 16-segment vectors; cut tree to obtain eight clusters.
7. Assign each book its cluster's archetype label.
8. Compute shape metrics (volume, speed, circuitousness) from the original (or PAA) curve.
9. Correlate each metric with download counts using partial correlation controlling for paragraph count.
10. Analyze genre distribution across archetypes (chi-square) and compute decade-wise trend in predictability.

### Design Tradeoffs
- Fixed PAA length (16) simplifies clustering but discards granularity; alternative segment counts could yield different archetypes.
- Cosine distance for novelty is intuitive but embedding-dependent; other distance metrics (e.g., Euclidean) may produce different curves.
- Ward clustering assumes convex, isotropic clusters; narrative shapes may not obey this, potentially oversimplifying.
- Download counts as readership proxy are readily available but reflect availability and age biases, not actual engagement.
- Partial correlation adjusts for linear length effects; nonlinear confounding might remain.

### Failure Signatures
- Novelty curves appear random or flat across books, indicating embedding or centroid update issues.
- PAA-reduced vectors show no clusterable structure (e.g., dendrogram heights near zero), suggesting insufficient signal.
- Archetype assignments are highly sensitive to small changes in parameters (e.g., PAA segments), indicating instability.
- Partial correlation fails to reduce length confound (e.g., circuitousness remains strongly correlated), suggesting nonlinear relationships.
- Genre chi-square test yields non-significant result, contradicting the reported massive statistic, implying metadata quality problems.

### First Experiments
1. Vary the number of PAA segments (e.g., 8, 16, 32) and evaluate archetype stability using adjusted Rand index; check if volume's predictive power holds.
2. Swap the embedding model (e.g., from SBERT to GloVe or a distilled LLM) and recompute novelty curves; assess changes in archetype composition and readership correlations.
3. Obtain a subset of books with alternative readership metrics (e.g., library circulation, Goodreads ratings) and test whether volume's partial correlation remains consistent.

## Limitations
- Corpus is limited to Western, public-domain works (1810–1923); download counts are a noisy proxy for actual readership engagement or completion.
- Methodological choices (unspecified paragraph embedding model, arbitrary 16-segment PAA, Ward clustering parameters, 85% SAX uniqueness threshold) may affect robustness and generalizability.
- Length adjustment via partial correlation may not fully disentangle shape from book size; genre labels from PG19 metadata may be inconsistent or sparse.
