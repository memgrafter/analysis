# WFR-MFM: One-Step Inference for Dynamic Unbalanced Optimal Transport

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.20606  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.20606  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: A mean‑flow framework enabling direct one‑step inference for dynamic unbalanced optimal transport, achieving 100–1000× speedups while matching or exceeding the accuracy of trajectory‑based baselines.

## Executive Summary
Dynamic unbalanced optimal transport (OT) is essential for modeling biological systems where mass varies over time, such as single‑cell RNA‑seq data capturing proliferation and death. Existing methods simulate full trajectories via ODE solvers, which becomes a bottleneck for large‑scale inference. This paper introduces WFR‑MFM, a simulation‑free approach built on the Wasserstein‑Fisher‑Rao (WFR) geometry. It parameterizes neural networks to predict the mean velocity and mass‑growth fields directly, conditioned on initial states, perturbation embeddings, and time intervals. Training uses a mean‑flow matching loss with targets derived from WFR geodesics and precomputed optimal entropy transport couplings. WFR‑MFM predicts arbitrary future distributions in a single network forward pass, avoiding costly trajectory integration. Evaluations on synthetic and real single‑cell datasets show speedups of 100–1000× over ODE baselines, with equal or better distributional accuracy (1‑Wasserstein distance) and mass preservation (Relative Mass Error). The method also demonstrates a controllable speed‑accuracy trade‑off and scales to thousands of perturbation conditions.

## Method Summary
WFR‑MFM models the evolution of a probability measure with mass changes using two conditional neural fields: a velocity field \(v_\theta(x,c,t,T)\) and a mass‑growth field \(h_\phi(x,c,t,T)\). Here \(x\) is a cell state, \(c\) a perturbation embedding, and \([t,T]\) the integration interval. The training data consist of discrete time‑point snapshots; between consecutive time points \(t_i\) and \(t_{i+1}\) an optimal entropy transport (OET) coupling \(\gamma_i\) is precomputed using entropically regularized OT (e.g., Sinkhorn). For any pair \((x_0,x_1)\) drawn from \(\gamma_i\), the WFR geodesic yields explicit mean velocity \(u\) and mass evolution \(m_t\). The network targets are derived from the identities:

\[
v_\text{target} = u + \operatorname{sg}\big((T-t)[\partial_t v_\theta + (\nabla_x v_\theta)u]\big),\quad
h_\text{target} = \frac{d}{dt}\ln m_t/(T-t),
\]

where \(\operatorname{sg}\) denotes stop‑gradient. Training samples include time pairs \((t,T)\); a proportion \(p_\text{diff}\) ensures cross‑time (\(t<T\)) versus instantaneous (\(t=T\)) supervision. The loss is a weighted mean‑square error, with weighting \(m_t\) to emphasize mass‑bearing regions and a hyperparameter \(\lambda\) balancing the two fields. At inference, given initial cells \((x_0,m_0)\) and a target interval \([0,\Delta]\), the next state is computed as:

\[
x_1 = x_0 + v_\theta(x_0,c,0,\Delta),\qquad
m_1 = m_0\exp\big(h_\phi(x_0,c,0,\Delta)\big).
\]

Multi‑step predictions can be obtained by partitioning the interval. This one‑step evaluation eliminates repeated ODE solves, yielding drastic speed improvements.

## Key Results
- **Speedup**: Inference is 100–1000× faster than ODE‑based baselines (e.g., WFR‑FM with RK23) across synthetic and real datasets.  
- **Accuracy**: W1 distances and Relative Mass Errors are equal to or better than those of trajectory‑based methods.  
- **Trade‑off**: The loss weight \(\lambda\) provides a controllable knob between transport and mass accuracy.  
- **Scalability**: The method infers 5000 perturbation conditions on 10,000 cells in 6.55 seconds, demonstrating feasibility for large‑scale applications.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core insight is that under the WFR geometry, the geodesic connecting two endpoint distributions has an explicit closed‑form mean velocity and exponential mass growth. These “mean‑flow” quantities depend only on the endpoints and the time fraction. By learning to approximate them directly with neural networks, WFR‑MFM sidesteps the need to integrate an ODE. The OET couplings provide ground‑truth endpoint pairs derived from observed data, anchoring the learned fields to real distributions. The mean‑flow matching loss ensures that the networks’ outputs satisfy the WFR geodesic equations at interpolated times, which implicitly enforces the correct dynamics. Because the networks are conditional on arbitrary time intervals \([t,T]\), they generalize to any future horizon after training—a one‑step inference suffices. This mechanism combines the physical fidelity of WFR geodesics with the flexibility of neural function approximation, yielding both speed and accuracy.

## Foundational Learning
- **Dynamic Unbalanced OT**: Extends classic OT to allow mass creation/destruction; necessary for modeling proliferation/apoptosis in single‑cell data.  
- **Wasserstein‑Fisher‑Rao Geometry**: A metric on measures that blends transport cost and mass change; admits explicit Dirac‑to‑Dirac geodesics.  
- **Mean‑Flow Matching**: Simulation‑free training objective; regress networks to instantaneous velocity and growth rates of a known path.  
- **Optimal Entropy Transport**: Entropy‑regularized OT; yields smooth couplings and efficient computation via Sinkhorn iterations.  
- **Conditional Neural Fields**: Networks that accept additional context (perturbation, time interval) as input; enable zero‑shot generalization to new conditions.  
- **Gaussian Time Interpolation**: During training, states are sampled along the geodesic with small Gaussian noise; stabilizes learning and approximates the continuous path.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Precomputed OET Couplings → Mini‑batch Sampler → WFR Mean‑Flow Target Calculator → vθ & hϕ Networks → Loss (MSE + weighting) → Backpropagation → Trained Networks → One‑Step Inference

**Critical path for reproduction**:  
1. Compute OET couplings between consecutive observed time points (using POT).  
2. Initialize the two neural networks (5‑layer MLP, 256 units).  
3. For each training iteration, sample a mini‑batch: (a) a time interval from the sequence, (b) pairs \((x_0,x_1)\) from the corresponding OET coupling.  
4. Compute mean‑flow quantities \(u, m_t, \dots\) via WFR formulas.  
5. Sample intermediate time \(t\) (and possibly \(T\)) and Gaussian‑perturbed point \(x\).  
6. Obtain network predictions \(v_\theta, h_\phi\) and form targets using the derivative identities.  
7. Compute the weighted loss, backpropagate, and update parameters.  
8. After convergence, store networks; inference is a single forward pass for each cell and perturbation.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- **λ**: Controls balance between transport and mass supervision; high λ improves mass accuracy but may hurt spatial alignment.  
- **p_diff**: Ratio of cross‑time to instantaneous samples; influences generalization to large intervals.  
- **OET entropy regularization**: Larger entropy yields smoother couplings but may blur fine‑grained structure.  
- **Network capacity**: More units/layers improve fit but increase memory and risk of overfitting.  
- **Batch size**: Larger batches speed up training but require more GPU memory; also affect OET coupling loading.

**Failure signatures**:  
- Training loss diverges or yields NaNs → reduce learning rate, increase Gaussian noise bandwidth σ, or adjust \(p_\text{diff}\).  
- High Relative Mass Error on validation → increase λ or adjust δ (mass‑variation trade‑off parameter).  
- Inference unexpectedly slow → ensure code does not integrate ODEs; profile the forward pass.  
- Out‑of‑memory → reduce batch size, network width, or use on‑the‑fly OET computation (Algorithm 2) instead of precomputed full couplings.

**First experiments**:  
1. **Synthetic sanity check**: Train on a 2‑D Gaussian dataset where the WFR geodesic is analytically tractable; compare network‑predicted \(v,h\) to closed‑form expressions.  
2. **EMT replication**: Use the hyperparameters \(\delta=2\), \(p_\text{diff}=0.05\), \(\lambda=20\) from the paper; train on the EMT dataset and report W1/RME on test timepoints; verify numbers align with Table 2.  
3. **Scalability benchmark**: On a fixed GPU, run inference for 10 000 cells and 5 000 perturbations; time the one‑step forward pass (excluding OET precomputation) and total wall‑clock; confirm the claimed 6.55 s runtime or identify bottlenecks.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: How do recent refinements to the mean‑flow framework (e.g., [44–46]) impact stability and inference quality?  
  *Basis*: The conclusion notes these refinements are not incorporated and may enhance performance.  
  *Unresolved*: Their effect on WFR‑MFM remains unknown.  
  *Resolution*: Integrate refinements from [44–46] and benchmark stability (variance of W1) and accuracy.

- **Open Question 2**: Can WFR‑MFM scale to real‑world perturbation datasets with thousands of conditions and generalize from limited training perturbations?  
  *Basis*: The conclusion identifies extension to large‑scale experimental data as a future direction.  
  *Unresolved*: Real data bring noise, batch effects, and biological variability not captured by synthetic tests.  
  *Resolution*: Apply WFR‑MFM to a public large‑scale perturbation dataset (e.g., GEARS) and compare W1, RME, and runtime against baselines.

- **Open Question 3**: Does WFR‑MFM’s fast inference translate to practical biological insights, such as identifying driver genes or predicting therapeutic responses?  
  *Basis*: The conclusion calls for validation of practical utility.  
  *Unresolved*: Current evaluations use distributional metrics only; no downstream biological validation.  
  *Resolution*: Collaborate with biologists to apply WFR‑MFM to a specific problem (e.g., drug combination effects) and compare predictions with wet‑lab experiments.

- **Open Question 4**: Can the mean‑flow framework be generalized to other unbalanced OT geometries (e.g., Hellinger–Kantorovich)?  
  *Basis*: The conclusion suggests extension to other formulations with well‑defined Dirac‑to‑Dirac flows.  
  *Unresolved*: Derivation and training for other geometries are non‑trivial.  
  *Resolution*: Derive mean‑flow equations for another unbalanced OT (e.g., unbalanced Gromov‑Wasserstein), implement, and validate on an appropriate dataset.

## Limitations
- **Missing hyperparameters**: Detailed hyperparameter settings for several real datasets (EB 50D/100D, CITE‑seq, Mouse hematopoiesis) are not provided, requiring manual tuning.  
- **Implementation gaps**: Training details (batch size, learning‑rate schedule, epochs) are only partially specified, which may lead to non‑reproducible results.  
- **No official code**: The absence of a reference implementation increases reliance on paper interpretation and may cause subtle discrepancies.
