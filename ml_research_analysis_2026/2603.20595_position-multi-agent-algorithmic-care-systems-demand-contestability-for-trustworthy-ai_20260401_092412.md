# Position: Multi-Agent Algorithmic Care Systems Demand Contestability for Trustworthy AI

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.20595
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20595
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Proposes the Contestable Adaptive Network-of-Experts (CANOE) framework to embed contestability in multi-agent healthcare AI, preserving human agency and clinical responsibility.

## Executive Summary
The paper argues that explainability alone is insufficient for multi-agent healthcare AI systems because it fails to enable care partners to challenge or correct decisions. It introduces contestability as a necessary design principle and presents the Contestable Adaptive Network-of-Experts (CANOE) framework, which combines structured argumentation with role-based human-in-the-loop contestation. Using quantitative bipolar argumentation semantics, CANOE allows arguments to be weighted and revised throughout the decision-making lifecycle, thereby preserving human agency and clinical responsibility. The approach is illustrated with an aging-in-place care planning scenario, but remains a conceptual contribution without empirical metrics or deployment.

## Method Summary
The method is a phased pipeline: patient data and case complexity drive recruitment of specialized AI agents representing clinical roles. Each agent generates support and challenge arguments for candidate care options, forming a bipolar argumentation graph. A quantitative bipolar argumentation framework (QBAF) iteratively computes argument degrees and aggregates them into option confidence scores. Human reviewers from matching roles contest the arguments via accept/reject/modify/add actions, producing a revised argument set. The QBAF re-scores the revised set; a care plan synthesizer then uses the scores, options, and evidence to produce the final care plan. This process enables systematic challenge and override, with human interventions propagating through the graph.

## Key Results
- Contestability is defined as a core requirement for trustworthy AI in multi-agent care systems, addressing the limitation of standard XAI.
- The CANOE framework operationalizes contestability via QBAF and role-based HITL, enabling structured challenge, review, and override.
- The approach preserves clinical responsibility by ensuring human agency at the collective decision level, as demonstrated conceptually in an aging-in-place scenario.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Explainability alone fails in multi-agent systems because it provides retrospective understanding without actionable recourse, leaving clinicians as passive observers unable to correct system-level decisions.
- **Mechanism:** In MAS, decisions emerge from agent interactions, not single models. Standard XAI explains individual agent outputs but does not expose the collective reasoning structure or provide interfaces for intervention. This creates a gap between understanding and control.
- **Core assumption:** Clinicians require the ability to challenge, override, or revise decisions at the system level, not just interpret them.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "explainability alone is insufficient in multi-agent settings, as it does not enable care partners to challenge or correct system outputs."
  - [section 2.2] "an explanation without recourse leaves care partners as passive observers of an AI’s decision."
  - [corpus] Related work (e.g., "Contestable AI needs Computational Argumentation") supports the insufficiency of XAI, but direct evidence for this specific mechanism is primarily from the paper's own argument; corpus shows conceptual alignment.
- **Break condition:** If the system does not provide structured points for human intervention (e.g., argument revision, override controls) at the collective decision stage, the mechanism fails and clinicians remain unable to contest outputs effectively.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Quantitative Bipolar Argumentation Frameworks (QBAFs) can aggregate diverse, conflicting expert inputs from multiple agents into a weighted, revisable decision structure that supports both automated scoring and human contestation.
- **Mechanism:** Agent arguments are tagged with `type` (support/challenge) and `role`. A QBAF defines support/attack relations (`R+`, `R-`) and iteratively computes argument degrees `f(x)` by combining intrinsic strength `τ(x)` with influences from related arguments. This yields option-level confidence scores `F(oi)` that reflect the debated consensus.
- **Core assumption:** Formal argumentation semantics can model the dialectical process among agents and integrate human edits while preserving coherence.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3, Phase 2] Detailed definition of QBAF with equations (2) and (3), and description of argument representation and relations.
  - [section 3, Phase 3] Human edits produce `ΓH`, and the system re-applies QBAF semantics to propagate changes.
  - [corpus] Weak direct support; neighboring papers discuss argumentation for contestability but not this specific QBAF implementation.
- **Break condition:** If the iterative update in Equation (2) fails to converge, or if human edits to arguments break the support/attack relation structure (e.g., invalidating dependency assumptions), the aggregated scores become meaningless and the framework collapses.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Role-based human-in-the-loop contestation preserves clinical responsibility by structuring human review around professional expertise and enabling documented interventions (accept, reject, modify, add) that are propagated through the argumentative graph.
- **Mechanism:** Human reviewers from specific clinical roles examine arguments (with `role(x)` tags) and their scores. Their actions revise the argument set `ΓH`. The QBAF re-computes degrees on `ΓH`, ensuring human corrections influence final option scores. A final human care planner approves.
- **Core assumption:** Tagging arguments by originating professional role allows reviewers to focus on their domain of expertise, making contestation efficient and clinically valid.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3, Phase 3] "Because every argument is tagged with its originating role, the system can group arguments by profession... team members may: (1) accept, (2) reject, (3) modify, or (4) add new arguments."
  - [section 3, Phase 3] "The system re-applies the quantitative bipolar semantics to ΓH, yielding updated degrees... guaranteeing that any human changes propagate."
  - [corpus] Neighbors like "Contestable AI needs Computational Argumentation" argue for structured human challenge, but this specific role-based propagation mechanism is paper-specific.
- **Break condition:** If role definitions are too coarse (e.g., one role for all nurses) or too fine (fragmenting oversight), reviewers may miss relevant arguments or duplicate effort, undermining efficiency and accountability. Also, if the final "human care planner" role lacks proper clinical authority, responsibility diffusion occurs.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Contestable AI (CAI)**
  - **Why needed here:** CAI is the core paradigm shift the paper advocates. Understanding CAI—as distinct from XAI and emphasizing *active challenge* throughout the lifecycle—is essential to grasp the paper's thesis and the design of CANOE.
  - **Quick check question:** How does CAI differ from XAI in terms of human agency during decision-making? (Answer: XAI provides explanations *after* the fact; CAI provides mechanisms for humans to intervene *during* the process to challenge, correct, or override.)

- **Concept: Bipolar Argumentation Frameworks**
  - **Why needed here:** The QBAF is the computational engine that turns agent debates into weighted, contestable scores. Engineers must understand support/attack relations and iterative degree computation to implement or evaluate CANOE's core logic.
  - **Quick check question:** In a QBAF, how does an attacking argument with a high degree `f(y)` affect the degree `f(x)` of its target argument `x`? (Answer: It decreases `f(x)` via the negative influence term in Equation (2), weighted by `βy,x`.)

- **Concept: Role-Based Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) Design**
  - **Why needed here:** This design pattern is how CANOE embeds human judgment without requiring a single expert to master all domains. It structures *who* can contest *what*, which is critical for workflow integration and accountability.
  - **Quick check question:** Why tag arguments with `role(x)` instead of just letting any human reviewer edit everything? (Answer: It enables efficient, expertise-matched review (a nurse focuses on nursing arguments), maintains professional accountability, and surfaces inter-role disagreements systematically.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  1. **Patient Analyzer & Team Recruiter:** Takes `P` (patient data) → outputs `A` (set of recruited role-agents) and `c` (complexity score).
  2. **Candidate Intervention Generator:** Uses `P` and retrieved documents `D` to propose `O`.
  3. **Argumentation Engine:** For each `oi ∈ O` and `aj ∈ A`, generates `Sj,i` (support) and `Cj,i` (challenge) arguments. Constructs `Γ`, `R+`, `R-`.
  4. **QBAF Scorer:** Computes `τ(x)` per argument, runs iterative update (Equation 2) to get `f(x)`, aggregates to `F(oi)`.
  5. **Contestation Interface:** Presents grouped arguments (by `role`) with scores to human reviewers → produces `ΓH`.
  6. **Plan Synthesizer (`Π`):** Uses `ΓV` (re-scored `ΓH`), `O`, `D` to generate final care plan.
  7. **MCP Booking Agent:** Translates plan into scheduling tasks (post-planning).

- **Critical path:** `P → (D, c) → A → (Γ, R+, R-) → f(x), F(oi) → ΓH → ΓV → Π → Care Plan`. The contestation step (`Γ → ΓH → ΓV`) is the pivotal deviation from a purely automated pipeline.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Argumentation depth vs. cognitive load:** More agent rounds/debate depth may improve reasoning but increases the argument graph size `|X|`, making human review harder. Tradeoff controlled by `c`-driven team size and possible argument pruning.
  - **Automated weighting vs. human override:** QBAF automates weighting (`τ(x)`, `α`, `β`), but heavy weighting reduces apparent human impact. Design must balance algorithmic consistency with meaningful human authority.
  - **Role granularity vs. accountability clarity:** Fine roles (e.g., "cardiology nurse" vs "geriatric nurse") yield more precise review but increase recruitment complexity and may split responsibility. Coarse roles simplify but blur professional lines.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - **QBAF non-convergence:** Iterative update (Equation 2) oscillates or diverges → `f(x)` values unstable → scores meaningless. Monitor iteration count and value deltas.
  - **"Edit cascade" instability:** A minor human edit to one argument causes large swings in distant `F(oi)` scores due to dense `R+/R-` connections → plan flips drastically. Indicates over-connected argument graph or extreme influence weights.
  - **Role-based review silence:** Certain `role(x)` groups have zero human reviews in `ΓH` → suggests interface not surfacing their arguments or workload misallocation. Log review actions per role.
  - **Persistent disagreement:** After contestation, `F(oi)` scores for competing options remain within a narrow band (e.g., 0.48 vs 0.52) → system cannot resolve, passes burden to final planner. May indicate insufficient evidence in `D` or overly balanced debates.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Convergence Stress Test:** Feed the system artificially crafted argument graphs with cyclical attack/support patterns (e.g., A attacks B, B supports C, C attacks A). Monitor if the update rule converges to a stable `f(x)` vector and within what iteration budget. *Purpose: Validate core computational mechanism robustness.*
  2. **Human Edit Propagation Fidelity:** Have domain experts perform a specific edit (e.g., weaken argument `x`'s `τ` from 0.8 to 0.3). Measure the delta in `F(oi)` for all affected options. Compare to a manual "what-if" expert prediction of impact. *Purpose: Ensure the propagation logic matches human intuition about influence.*
  3. **Role-Based Review Coverage Audit:** Run 10 simulated aging-in-place cases. For each case, track: (a) number of arguments per `role`, (b) number of human reviews per `role`, (c) time spent per review. Identify roles with high `(a)` but low `(b)` or high `(c)`. *Purpose: Detect workflow bottlenecks and mismatches between agent output volume and human review capacity.*

## Limitations
- **Empirical validation gap:** The proposal is conceptual; no user studies, clinical trials, or system deployments exist to confirm that contestability improves trust, accountability, or outcomes in practice.
- **Implementation ambiguity:** Critical components lack specification: exact agent prompts, RAG integration, intrinsic strength model τ(x), aggregation function *g* in Equation (3), and the contestation interface workflow.
- **Scalability & cognitive load:** The argument graph size and human review burden for complex cases are unknown; role granularity trade-offs are untested.

## Confidence
- Claim: "Explainability alone is insufficient; contestability is necessary" – *Medium*: Well-argued through conceptual critique of XAI, but lacks field evidence from multi-agent healthcare settings.
- Claim: "QBAF can aggregate conflicting inputs into a revisable decision structure" – *Medium*: Mathematically defined, but convergence, stability, and human-intuitive propagation are unverified.
- Claim: "Role-based HITL preserves clinical responsibility" – *Low*: Assumes role tagging enables efficient, accountable review, but workflow practicality and liability clarity are speculative.
