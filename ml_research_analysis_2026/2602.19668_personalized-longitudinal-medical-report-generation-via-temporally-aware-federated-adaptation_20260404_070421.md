# Personalized Longitudinal Medical Report Generation via Temporally-Aware Federated Adaptation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.19668
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19668
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: FedTAR achieves BLEU-4 of 12.40 vs 10.98 (FedAvg) and CIDEr of 42.80 vs 31.70, up to 11.5% relative gain on CIDEr.

## Executive Summary
Longitudinal medical report generation must handle evolving patient conditions while respecting data privacy. FedTAR, a federated framework, introduces Federated Temporal Adaptation (FTA) to combine demographic-driven personalization, hypernetwork-based LoRA adaptation, and meta-learned temporal residual aggregation. Evaluated on J-MID (1M CT exams) and MIMIC-CXR, it consistently outperforms six federated learning baselines, showing significant improvements in linguistic metrics (BLEU, CIDEr), convergence stability, and cross-site generalization, all without sharing raw patient data.

## Method Summary
FedTAR operates in a federated manner across multiple hospitals (clients). Each client processes a patient’s demographic vector (pseudonymized ID, normalized age, sex) through a Gaussian Mixture Model to obtain soft cluster assignments, which are projected to an embedding ϕ_p. A hypernetwork then generates patient-specific low-rank adaptation (LoRA) matrices A_p, B_p conditioned on ϕ_p. These adapters are merged with a shared LoRA component and applied to every transformer layer of a pre-trained image encoder (ViT) and text decoder (DistilGPT2). Training proceeds over a fixed number of sequential visits (time steps T). After local training on a time point, clients upload their updated LoRA parameters to the server. The server aggregates client updates via a weighted average ¯w(t) and updates the global model using a residual rule: w(t) = w(t-1) + α_t·(¯w(t) - w(t-1)). The coefficients α_t are produced by a meta-learner (a small MLP on time embeddings) and optimized through first-order MAML to minimize a held-out validation loss after T steps. This design enables personalization, efficient communication (only LoRA adapters are transmitted), and adaptation to temporal distribution shifts while preserving privacy.

## Key Results
- FedTAR reaches BLEU-4 of 12.40, surpassing FedAvg’s 10.98 (~13% relative gain).
- CIDEr improves to 42.80 from 31.70 (≈35% relative gain).
- It delivers consistent improvements over six FL baselines on two datasets, with up to 11.5% relative gain on CIDEr.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: GMM-based demographic embedding
*Claim*: Captures patient-specific heterogeneity via soft clustering, enabling personalized model adaptation.  
*Mechanism*: Each patient’s three-dimensional demographic vector (hashed ID, normalized age, sex) is processed by a Gaussian Mixture Model (16 components, diagonal covariance) to obtain a probability distribution over latent subgroups. This distribution is linearly projected to an embedding ϕ_p that conditions subsequent LoRA generation.  
*Core assumption*: Demographics are predictive of clinically relevant disease trajectories; similar subgroups yield similar report characteristics.  
*Evidence anchors*: The GMM fitting and projection are detailed in Algorithm 1 (Section 3.3). Neighborhood works on personalized FL and longitudinal imaging motivate the use of demographic and temporal cues.  
*Break condition*: If demographics are uninformative for the target disease, or the GMM fails to converge due to sparse intra-client data, embeddings become noisy and personalization degrades.

### Mechanism 2: Hypernetwork-generated LoRA adapters
*Claim*: Provides parameter‑efficient, patient‑specific adaptation with minimal communication overhead.  
*Mechanism*: A lightweight hypernetwork h_l takes the patient embedding ϕ_p and outputs low‑rank matrices A_l_p and B_l_p. These are added to shared LoRA factors (A_l, B_l) to form the adapted layer weight: W_l_p = W_client + A_l B_l^⊤ + A_l_p B_l_p^⊤.  
*Core assumption*: The mapping from demographic embeddings to adapter parameters is learnable and generalizes across patients; the chosen LoRA rank (typically 4) suffices to capture patient‑specific deviations.  
*Evidence anchors*: Hypernetwork architecture is described in Section 3.3. The approach aligns with parameter‑efficient personalization trends (e.g., pFedLoRA).  
*Break condition*: Under‑parameterized hypernetwork (too shallow/narrow) may underfit; uninformative patient embeddings yield no benefit over shared LoRA.

### Mechanism 3: Meta‑learned temporal residual aggregation
*Claim*: Stabilizes federated optimization under non‑stationary distributions by adaptively weighting updates from different time points.  
*Mechanism*: After local training at time t, the server computes residual Δ_t = ¯w(t) – w(t‑1) and updates w(t) = w(t‑1) + α_t·Δ_t. The coefficient α_t ∈ (0,1) is produced by a hypernetwork that maps time embeddings through a softmax. Meta‑parameters ψ are optimized via first-order MAML (hypergradient descent) to minimize a held‑out validation loss after T steps.  
*Core assumption*: The global model can be expressed as a convex combination of past aggregated snapshots; the time‑dependent weights α_t can be bilevel‑optimized to balance adaptivity and stability.  
*Evidence anchors*: Section 3.4 formalizes the residual rule and provides theoretical guarantees (Theorems 1–2). Algorithm 2 details the meta‑learning procedure.  
*Break condition*: Overfitting of meta‑parameters to the validation set may produce α_t that fail to generalize, causing oscillations or slow adaptation. Extreme distribution shifts could violate the convex‑combination assumption.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Client-side: Demographic Encoder (GMM + linear projection) → Hypernetwork → Patient‑specific LoRA adapters (A_p, B_p) → merged with Base LoRA → Base Model (ViT image encoder + DistilGPT2 text decoder).  
Server‑side: Aggregated client weights (¯w(t)) → Residual computation (w(t‑1) + α_t·(¯w(t)‑w(t‑1))) → Global model w(t).  
Meta‑learner: time embeddings → MLP → α_t (softmax); α_t’s parameters ψ updated via hypergradient using a held‑out validation set.

### Critical Path
1. Server initializes global model w_g.
2. For each communication round r:
   - Set w(0,r) = w(T,r‑1) (for r>1).
   - For t=1..T:
     - Broadcast w(t‑1,r) to all clients.
     - Each client locally trains on its t‑th visit data using patient‑specific LoRA, updating all client‑side parameters (base model, shared LoRA, hypernetwork, projection).
     - Clients upload adapted LoRA parameters.
     - Server computes weighted average ¯w(t) across clients (by local sample count).
     - Server updates global model: w(t) = w(t‑1) + α_t(ψ)·(¯w(t)‑w(t‑1)).
   - After T steps, evaluate validation loss L_val(w(T,r)).
   - Compute hypergradient ∇_ψ L_val (first‑order approximation) and update ψ.
   - Optionally set next round’s w_g = w(T,r).
3. Repeat until convergence.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **LoRA rank r**: Higher r increases personalization capacity but also communication overhead and overfitting risk (typical 4–8).
- **GMM components k**: More components model finer demographic subgroups but risk fragmenting data and increasing EM fitting time; choice depends on client population size.
- **Meta‑learning step size**: Too large destabilizes α_t; too slow delays adaptation; requires tuning.
- **Residual coefficient schedule**: Softmax ensures α_t ∈ (0,1) and sum=1; saturation to extreme values reduces adaptivity or stability.
- **Number of time steps T**: Larger T increases memory for residual chain; theoretical bounds assume bounded per‑step residual norm.

### Failure Signatures
- **Divergence**: Rapid deterioration of CIDEr or loss across rounds (α_t too large or unstable meta‑updates).
- **α_t collapse**: All coefficients near 0 (no adaptation) or one dominates (ignores other time points); meta‑loss plateau.
- **Poor per‑client generalization**: High global metrics but low performance on specific clients, indicating GMM embeddings fail to capture heterogeneity.
- **GMM numerical issues**: Singular covariance warnings, suggesting sparse intra‑client data; may need diagonal covariances or regularization.
- **Hypergradient explosion**: Very large hypergradient norms; remedy with gradient clipping or smaller meta‑step size.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Irregular longitudinal data**: How can FTA handle varying visit intervals or missing time points? The current implementation assumes exactly five sequential visits with regular spacing.
2. **Privacy risks**: What are the potential leakage risks from shared demographic embeddings or temporal coefficients? The paper claims privacy but lacks formal analysis or empirical attacks.
3. **GMM component sensitivity**: How does the number of GMM components affect the trade‑off between personalization and generalization? The study fixes 16 components without sensitivity analysis.
4. **Clinical utility**: Do radiologists find FedTAR‑generated reports clinically accurate and useful compared to human‑authored or centralized model outputs? Evaluation relies solely on automated linguistic metrics.

## Limitations
- Dataset details (client partition, multi‑image selection, subject filtering) are not provided, hindering faithful reproduction on MIMIC‑CXR.
- Hyperparameters (LoRA scaling factor α=128 interpretation, local epochs, batch size, GMM regularization) are unspecified.
- Validation protocol for meta‑learning (centralized vs. federated held‑out set, aggregation weights) is ambiguous, introducing uncertainty in the training procedure.
- Theoretical assumptions (convex residual chain, stable meta‑gradients) may not hold under real‑world distribution shifts, potentially affecting generalizability.
