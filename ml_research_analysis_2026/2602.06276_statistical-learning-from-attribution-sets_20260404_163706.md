# Statistical Learning from Attribution Sets

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.06276  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06276  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Constructs an unbiased estimator of population loss from attribution sets, enabling Empirical Risk Minimization with generalization guarantees that scale with the squared L2 norm of the prior (Σ) and are robust to prior estimation errors.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses training conversion prediction models for online advertising under privacy constraints where direct click-conversion links are unavailable and only attribution sets (coarse candidate click groups) are observed. It formalizes this as learning from attribution sets generated by an oblivious adversary with a known prior distribution π. The core method constructs an unbiased estimator of the population loss by decomposing the loss and using a combinatorial argument to relate inaccessible moments to observable signals within attribution sets. This enables applying Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM) with theoretical generalization guarantees that scale with Σ = ∥π∥₂² and are robust to prior estimation errors. Empirical evaluations on MNIST, CIFAR-10, and Higgs datasets show the unbiased approach significantly outperforms common industry heuristics like random or maximum-prior attribution, especially when attribution sets are large or overlapping, demonstrating practical effectiveness.

## Method Summary
The input consists of n i.i.d. click feature vectors X₁,…,Xₙ with unobserved binary labels Y. For each conversion (Y=1), an attribution set Aⱼ of size k is provided, comprising k consecutive click positions containing the true responsible click, with the true click’s offset drawn from a known prior π. The loss is ℓ(h(x),y)=f₁(h(x))+y f₂(h(x)). The unbiased estimator is built by: splitting data into two halves; estimating conversion rate p, E[f₁], E[f₂] from the first half; for each eligible attribution set (k ≤ j ≤ M−k), computing weights using β constants derived from binomial tails and π; and averaging over sets and positions. Training minimizes this estimator via gradient descent (Adam). Baselines hallucinate labels by assigning a single positive per set according to random or max-prior selection, then train a standard binary classifier.

## Key Results
- The unbiased estimator achieves zero bias and enables ERM with generalization bounds scaling as 1/Σ, where Σ = ∥π∥₂².
- Theoretical guarantees remain robust to prior misspecification, with additional bias bounded by terms involving ∥π−bπ∥₂.
- Empirical tests on MNIST, CIFAR-10, and Higgs show substantial accuracy improvements over heuristics, particularly for large or overlapping attribution sets.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Unbiased Estimator via Combinatorial Mapping
- **Claim:** The population loss L(h) can be expressed as an expectation over the observed attribution sets and their positions, using the known prior π.
- **Mechanism:** Decompose loss ℓ(h(x),y)=f₁(h(x))+y f₂(h(x)). The unobservable term E[Y f₂(h(X))] is mapped to Eπ[ f₂(h(Aⱼ[i])) · 1{j≤M−k} ] via constants β₁(j,i), β₀(j,i) derived from binomial tails and π. This relies on the adversary creating consecutive intervals where the true index iⱼ(S) appears at position r with probability π[r]. The unbiasedness follows from conditioning on the labels of the candidate indices.
- **Core assumption:** The adversary is oblivious and selects intervals of consecutive clicks of fixed size k with the true click’s offset following known π; the event delays are independent of features.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "we construct an unbiased estimator of the population loss from these coarse signals via a novel approach."; [section 3, Theorem 1] States the exact unbiasedness equality involving β₁, β₀; [corpus] Weak fingerprint; related work uses heuristics or assumes non-overlapping bags, while the combinatorial argument is novel.
- **Break condition:** If attribution sets are not consecutive intervals or the prior π is unknown and severely misspecified (beyond robustness bounds), bias may appear.

### Mechanism 2: Generalization with Priors via Sensitivity Control
- **Claim:** ERM on the unbiased estimator yields an excess risk Reg(bh) that scales with 1/∥π∥₂², the Rademacher complexity of H, and the pseudo-dimension.
- **Mechanism:** The estimator bℓM(h) is rewritten as a sum over individual click features with weights summing to O(p) per set. By bounding the sensitivity of bℓM(h) to any single click’s feature/label (Lemma 11), McDiarmid’s inequality and Rademacher analysis uniform-convergence bounds. The factor 1/Σ arises because each π[i]² appears in the weight aggregation.
- **Core assumption:** Loss is bounded and Lipschitz; k ≤ np/8; np = Ω(log(1/(p max_i 1/π[i]))); hypothesis class H has finite capacity; feature/label pairs are i.i.d.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "Empirical Risk Minimization achieves generalization guarantees that scale with the informativeness of the prior."; [section 4.1, Theorem 2] Explicit bound with terms L·Rn(H)/Σ + F₂/Σ · sqrt(log 1/δ / n + p·min{PDim(H),k}/n); [corpus] The analysis technique (sensitivity control, Rademacher) is standard but adapted to the attribution-dependent estimator.
- **Break condition:** If np is too small so binomial tails deviate significantly from 1, the β constants become poorly conditioned and the sensitivity bound fails; also if the loss is not Lipschitz or unbounded, the concentration may not hold.

### Mechanism 3: Robustness to Prior Misspecification
- **Claim:** Using an estimated prior bπ instead of π incurs an additional bias that is small when bπ is close to π in L2 norm, and degrades gracefully otherwise.
- **Mechanism:** Replace π with bπ in estimator definitions (β₁→bβ₁ etc.). Decompose error into bias (difference between true and estimated constants) and variance (randomness). By bounding the constants, show bias is O(pF₂(∥π-bπ∥₁/Σ + ∥π-bπ∥₂/Σ^{3/2})) if Σ ≥ 8∥π-bπ∥₂², and O(pkF₂∥π-bπ∥₂/Σ^{1/2}) otherwise.
- **Core assumption:** bπ is a fixed estimate; the same other conditions as Theorem 2; π fixed but unknown.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "robust against estimation errors in the prior."; [section 4.2, Theorem 4] Two-case bound with explicit bias term; [corpus] The robustness result is original for this setting; no direct prior work.
- **Break condition:** If Σ is small (prior nearly uniform) and ∥π-bπ∥₂² is large, the regret can suffer an explicit factor of k, indicating reliance on a highly accurate prior when the prior itself is uninformative. Also if bπ[i] = 0 while π[i] > 0, singularities may occur unless restricted.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Data loader (click features, attribution sets) -> Prior module (π, Σ, β constants) -> Loss estimator (computes bℓM) -> Model & optimizer (minimizes loss) -> Validation/eval (test set)

**Critical path:**  
1. Accurate calculation of β₁(j,i) and β₀(j,i): Requires correct binomial tail computation and handling of indices j where k ≤ j ≤ M−k. These constants must be numerically stable (avoid division by near-zero).  
2. Integration with training loop: The estimator must be computed efficiently; precomputing r(j,i) tables for fixed p and n can help.  
3. Prior input: The value of π must be provided; if estimated, need a reliable method (e.g., from a small labeled dataset or domain heuristic). The worst-case degradation when Σ is small demands precise prior.  
4. Monitoring: Track the magnitude of bℓM and gradients; large values may indicate β₁ too small.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Data efficiency vs. independence: Using all attribution sets (even overlapping) maximizes data usage but makes dependencies complex; skipping sets (e.g., every k-th) would simplify analysis but waste data. The analysis goes for full usage.  
- Prior knowledge: The algorithm requires π. In practice, one may approximate π (e.g., uniform or last-touch). If the true prior is concentrated, small errors in bπ are acceptable; if true prior is uniform, poor bπ can hurt more.  
- Computational cost: The estimator loops over each attribution set and each position (O(kM) per batch). For large k and M, this could dominate training time. Potential batching of attribution sets or vectorization needed.  
- Hyperparameter sensitivity: The learning rate for Adam may need adjustment because the unbiased estimator could have higher variance than supervised loss. Monitoring training stability is important.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Divergent or NaN losses: β₁(j,i) becomes zero or negative if binomial tails B_{n,p,j+k} vanish (under extreme sparsity) or if π[i] is extremely small. Solutions: ensure np is large enough; clip β₁ away from zero or restrict i to those with π[i] above threshold (per Remark 3).  
- No improvement over baselines: If the prior is severely misspecified or the attribution sets do not follow consecutive intervals, the estimator is biased. Check by comparing empirical bℓM vs. frontier of supervised upper bound.  
- Overfitting despite theory: If hypothesis class H is too rich relative to n, the Rademacher term may dominate. Monitor gap between training bℓM and test loss.  
- Sensitivity to batch choice: The estimator uses subsampling of attribution sets; if batch size is too small, variance may be high. Increase batch size or use full-batch if feasible.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Replicate MNIST binary classification: Compare UNBIASED vs. RANDOM and MAX PRIOR for both uniform and exponential priors across k = 2⁰…2⁸. Verify that UNBIASED accuracy degrades gracefully as k grows and significantly outperforms baselines, especially when p ≈ 0.1 (overlap regime).  
2. Prior robustness test: Train on CIFAR-10 with true prior π_exp (exponential) but supply bπ = uniform. Observe the drop in test accuracy relative to using true π; confirm the theoretical bias scaling with approximation error (compute ∥π-bπ∥₂² and compare to Σ).  
3. Extreme overlap scenario: Generate synthetic data with small p (e.g., 0.01) and large k (>> 1/p) causing massive overlapping. Run all algorithms; expect baselines to collapse to trivial behavior while UNBIASED still learns meaningfully, demonstrating the method’s ability to handle complex dependencies.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** Is the sample complexity scaling with the squared L2 norm of the prior (Σ) tight? I.e., are there matching lower bounds for the regret guarantee?  
  **Basis:** Future work: "establishing the tightness of our results by proving matching regret lower bounds."  
  **Why unresolved:** The paper provides generalization guarantees that depend on Σ, but optimality is not proven.

- **Open Question 2:** How can the framework be extended to multi‑touch attribution, where a single conversion may be caused by multiple contributing clicks rather than a single responsible click?  
  **Basis:** Future work: "extending the framework to more complex attribution logics, like multi‑touch attribution."  
  **Why unresolved:** The current model assumes each conversion originates from exactly one click; multi‑touch changes the label generation process and breaks the combinatorial argument.

- **Open Question 3:** Can the prior distribution π over attribution positions be learned jointly with the conversion prediction model from the same data stream?  
  **Basis:** Future work: "developing methods to jointly learn the prior distribution and the conversion model from the data stream itself."  
  **Why unresolved:** The theory currently requires π to be known; joint learning introduces identifiability and statistical challenges not addressed.

- **Open Question 4:** Does the approach remain valid if the adversary generating attribution sets is adaptive (i.e., depends on click features) rather than oblivious?  
  **Basis:** The paper repeatedly assumes an oblivious adversary: "The generation of attribution sets is independent of the feature values X."  
  **Why unresolved:** The unbiased estimator and concentration arguments rely on independence between attribution sets and the feature distribution; adaptivity could break these properties.

## Limitations
- β₁, β₀ definitions are ambiguous in the PDF, risking incorrect implementation and biased estimators.
- The method for estimating E[f₁] and E[f₂] during training (sampling source/frequency) is underspecified, potentially introducing hidden bias.
- Core assumptions—consecutive attribution intervals, known prior π with sufficient Σ=∥π∥₂², and np large—may not hold under strong privacy or sparse conversions.
- Computational/variance cost grows with k; robustness degrades if Σ is small (near-uniform prior).
- Theory assumes bounded Lipschitz loss and i.i.d. clicks; real ad data may violate these.

## Confidence
- **Unbiased estimator exists:** Medium (proof structure plausible but exact β formulas critical and unclear).
- **Generalization bound scales with 1/Σ:** High (standard Rademacher analysis with clear conditions).
- **Robustness to prior misspecification:** Medium (bound includes worst-case factor k when Σ small; empirical tightness uncertain).
- **Empirical superiority over heuristics:** High (consistent gains across three datasets, but replication depends on resolving unknowns).
