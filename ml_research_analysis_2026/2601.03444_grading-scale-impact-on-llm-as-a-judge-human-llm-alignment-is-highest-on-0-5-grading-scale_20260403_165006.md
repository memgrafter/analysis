# Grading Scale Impact on LLM-as-a-Judge: Human-LLM Alignment Is Highest on 0-5 Grading Scale
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.03444
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03444
- Reference count: 35
- Primary result: LLM-as-a-judge alignment with humans is highest on the 0-5 grading scale and lowest on 0-10 across six diverse benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
This study examines how the choice of numeric grading scale (0–5, 0–10, 0–100) influences the reliability of LLMs serving as automated judges and their agreement with human evaluators. Across six benchmarks spanning objective to highly subjective tasks, the authors find that the 0–5 scale consistently yields the strongest absolute agreement between LLMs and human consensus, while the 0–10 scale is the weakest. Moreover, aggregated reliability metrics conceal large variations in LLM performance: LLMs are highly reliable on objective-like tasks but show major drops on subjective open-ended ones. Subgroup analysis reveals that both male and female human raters achieve their highest alignment with LLMs on the 0–5 scale. The work establishes grading scale design as a critical, non-interchangeable parameter for LLM-as-a-judge protocols.

## Method Summary
The authors conducted a fully crossed design using six diverse benchmarks (STS-B, ToxiGen, MoralChoice, TruthfulQA, MT-Bench, and SummEval). Human ratings were collected from 12 graduate students (balanced by gender) who evaluated 150 pooled items (25 per benchmark) on three separate numeric scales (0–5, 0–10, 0–100) in randomized sessions. Six LLMs (GPT-4o, Gemini-2.5-flash, Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct, Qwen3-32B, DeepSeek-v3.2, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3) were queried with fixed, task-specific prompts that varied only in the defined score range. Reliability and agreement metrics included ICC(A,1) and ICC(A,k) for absolute agreement, and normalized mean absolute error (nMAE) for human–LLM disagreement. Analyses were performed at the pooled level, per benchmark, per model, and per rater gender, with temperature set to 1 for LLM inference.

## Key Results
- Across all tasks, the 0–5 scale produces the highest ICC and lowest nMAE between LLM ensembles and human consensus; 0–10 consistently shows the weakest alignment.
- LLM inter-scale agreement remains high on objective benchmarks but drops substantially on subjective open-ended tasks (MT-Bench and SummEval), indicating sensitivity to scale granularity in ambiguous contexts.
- Pooled reliability metrics mask significant benchmark heterogeneity, creating a “reliability illusion” where aggregate ICC conceals poor performance on subjective items.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Scale Calibration via Pretraining Priors**
- **Claim:** The numeric grading scale significantly shifts human-LLM alignment because LLMs’ internal scoring distributions are better calibrated to coarser scales (e.g., 0–5) that resemble common human evaluation practices.
- **Mechanism:** LLMs are trained on vast corpora containing human feedback and evaluations, many of which use coarse Likert-style scales (e.g., 1–5). This imprints a prior that maps rubric interpretations more naturally onto such ranges. When forced onto finer scales (0–10, 0–100), the model must artificially subdivide its latent quality dimensions, leading to calibration drift relative to human consensus.
- **Core assumption:** The distribution of evaluation scales in the pretraining data is skewed toward coarse intervals, shaping the model’s “default” scoring granularity.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - Abstract: “Aggregated across all tasks, the grading scale of 0–5 yields the strongest human–LLM alignment… while 0–10 is consistently the weakest.”
  - Section 5.2: “Aggregating across all tasks, both humans and LLMs form highly reliable panels on every scale. However, cross-population agreement is consistently lower and varies systematically with the grading scale. In particular, the 0–5 scale shows the strongest pooled human–LLM alignment.”
- **Break condition:** If future training data shifts toward finer-grained human evaluations (e.g., 0–100), LLM calibration may invert, making finer scales optimal.

**Mechanism 2: Fine-Grained Discriminability Loss on Subjective Tasks**
- **Claim:** On subjective open-ended tasks, finer grading scales amplify inconsistency in LLM judgments because the model’s latent evaluation space lacks stable fine-grained discriminability.
- **Mechanism:** For ambiguous rubrics, LLMs construct ad-hoc mappings from textual cues to numeric scores. Coarser scales allow the model to collapse similar-quality outputs into broader bands, reducing sensitivity to minor cues that vary across scales. Finer scales force artificial precision, exposing the model’s lack of a true continuous metric and causing inter-scale score divergence.
- **Core assumption:** LLMs do not possess an internal, scale-invariant representation of quality for highly subjective tasks; instead, they perform scale-dependent rubric instantiation.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Abstract: “LLM judgments are not perfectly consistent across scales on subjective benchmarks.”
  - Section 5.1: “On benchmarks with comparatively objective or mixed criteria… inter-scale agreement is high… In contrast, on more subjective, open-ended quality benchmarks (MT-Bench and SummEval), inter-scale agreement drops substantially, especially for smaller or less capable judges.”
- **Break condition:** If LLMs develop more robust internal representations of subjective quality (e.g., via specialized fine-tuning), inter-scale consistency may increase even on fine scales.

**Mechanism 3: Pooling Illusion**
- **Claim:** Pooled reliability metrics create an illusion of LLM judge competence because they are dominated by objective benchmarks where LLMs excel, masking severe reliability drops on subjective benchmarks.
- **Mechanism:** When aggregating across heterogeneous tasks, high ICC values from objective-like benchmarks inflate the overall reliability estimate, while subjective benchmarks show dramatically lower LLM panel ICC but get diluted in the average. Relying solely on pooled numbers leads to overestimation of LLM judge robustness.
- **Core assumption:** Task-type heterogeneity is a major moderator of LLM judge reliability, and pooling erases this structure.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Abstract: “we further demonstrate that pooled reliability can mask benchmark heterogeneity… LLM panels are highly reliable on objective-like tasks but show major drops on subjective ones.”
  - Section 5.2: “Aggregate ICCs can conceal sharp differences across benchmarks… This ‘reliability illusion’ emphasizes that LLM judges’ reliability should be interpreted at more granular levels (such as by benchmarks, judges, and genders) rather than only in aggregate.”
- **Break condition:** If future benchmarks are carefully balanced or if LLMs achieve uniformly high reliability across task types, the pooling illusion will disappear.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Data pipelines → Rater subsystem → Scale manager → Metric engine → Analysis module

**Critical path**:
1. Define item subsets per benchmark (full for inter-scale, 25 each for human-LLM).
2. Collect fully crossed human ratings on all scales (randomized block design).
3. Query LLMs with standardized prompts per scale.
4. Compute inter-scale ICC for LLMs.
5. Compute human-only and LLM-only panel ICCs.
6. Compute human-LLM agreement (ICC, nMAE) at pooled, per-benchmark, per-model, and per-gender levels.
7. Perform ablations (temperature) and error analysis.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Scale count (3) balances coverage vs. annotator fatigue.
- Model selection (6 diverse LLMs) offers breadth but may not capture all architectures.
- Human rater pool (grad students) ensures consistency but limits ecological validity.
- Fractional scoring allowed to isolate range effects but may introduce rater uncertainty.

**Failure signatures**:
- Low human reliability (ICC<0.7) on a benchmark invalidates human-LLM comparisons.
- Near-perfect LLM inter-scale ICC (>0.95) across all tasks would contradict the paper’s core finding.
- Discrepancy between nMAE and ICC signals variance mismatch (e.g., ICC low but nMAE moderate indicates systematic offset).

**First 3 experiments**:
1. Replicate with a more diverse human population (e.g., crowd workers) to test generalizability of the 0–5 advantage.
2. Test additional scales (e.g., 1–7, continuous) to map the optimal granularity curve.
3. Systematically vary prompting style (e.g., with/without examples) to see if scale effects interact with prompt design.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1**: Why does a coarser numeric grading scale (0-5) consistently achieve higher human-LLM alignment compared to finer scales (0-10, 0-100)?
- **Basis in paper**: The study reports that "aggregated across all tasks, the grading scale of 0-5 yields the strongest absolute agreement" and "0-10 is consistently the weakest."
- **Why unresolved**: The paper establishes the effect but does not explore the underlying mechanisms, such as LLM training biases, human cognitive scaling, or rubric interpretation differences across ranges.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Manipulating scale properties (e.g., labeling, anchors) in controlled experiments; analyzing LLM internal representations during scoring; cross-scale human annotation studies with think-aloud protocols.

**Open Question 2**: What specific characteristics of subjective open-ended benchmarks cause greater sensitivity to grading scale changes, and how can these be predicted or mitigated?
- **Basis in paper**: Results show "on subjective, open-ended quality benchmarks (MT-Bench and SummEval), inter-scale agreement drops substantially" and LLM reliability declines while human reliability remains high.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper identifies benchmark heterogeneity but does not disentangle which aspects of subjectivity (e.g., ambiguity, rubric complexity, context dependence) drive the scale sensitivity.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Feature analysis of benchmark items (e.g., ambiguity scores, length); controlled task designs varying subjectivity; correlation of scale effects with item-level metadata like response entropy.

**Open Question 3**: Why do gender differences emerge in human-LLM alignment across grading scales, with females aligning better on 0-10 and males on 0-5?
- **Basis in paper**: Subgroup analysis reveals "female raters are more aligned with LLM judges on the scale 0-10, whereas male raters are more aligned on 0-5" (Table 5).
- **Why unresolved**: This pattern is reported without explanation; potential sociocultural, cognitive, or annotation behavior factors are not investigated.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Larger-scale studies with diverse gender identities; surveys on scale perception and usage; qualitative analysis of annotation comments by gender to identify systematic differences.

**Open Question 4**: How do the observed scale effects generalize to more diverse human rater populations and real-world annotation scenarios beyond the graduate student sample?
- **Basis in paper**: The limitations note that "human raters are all graduate students and thus do not represent the broader populations that produce many large-scale annotations," raising external validity concerns.
- **Why unresolved**: The homogenous human sample may not capture calibration variations in typical crowdsourced or professional annotator pools, affecting applicability of findings.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Replication studies with crowd workers, domain experts, and cross-cultural groups; analysis of scale effects under different rater training protocols; comparison with large-scale public annotation datasets.

## Limitations
- Exact evaluation items (the 150 rated by humans) are not released, preventing exact replication of human-LLM alignment numbers.
- Prompt specifications are incomplete; full templates, JSON schemas, and handling of fractional outputs are missing, which could affect LLM behavior and parsing.
- Human rater pool is limited to graduate students, reducing generalizability to broader populations or typical annotators.

## Confidence
- 0–5 scale yields strongest human–LLM alignment: **High**
- Pooled reliability masks benchmark heterogeneity: **High**
- Mechanisms (training data prior, latent discriminability): **Medium–Low**
