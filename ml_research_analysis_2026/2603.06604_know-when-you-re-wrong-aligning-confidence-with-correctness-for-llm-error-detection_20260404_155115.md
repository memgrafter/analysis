# Know When You're Wrong: Aligning Confidence with Correctness for LLM Error Detection

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.06604  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06604  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Supervised fine-tuning yields well-calibrated confidence (AUROC 0.879, ECE 0.034) while RL and DPO cause overconfidence; calibrated confidence enables efficient adaptive retrieval using only 58% of queries to recover 95% accuracy gain on TriviaQA.

## Executive Summary
Large language models often produce incorrect answers with high confidence, posing risks in critical applications. This work introduces a normalized confidence score derived from output token probabilities for classification tasks and a self‑evaluation framework (Yes/No) for open‑ended generation. Through systematic experiments across seven benchmarks, the authors show that supervised fine‑tuning (SFT) produces well‑calibrated confidence, significantly improving error detection (AUROC from 0.806 to 0.879, ECE from 0.163 to 0.034 on Qwen3‑4B). In contrast, reinforcement learning methods (PPO, GRPO) and Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) induce overconfidence by exploiting reward signals. Post‑RL SFT with self‑distillation restores calibration. The calibrated confidence is applied to adaptive retrieval‑augmented generation, achieving the same accuracy as always retrieving while using only 58% of the retrieval operations on TriviaQA.

## Method Summary
The paper proposes a unified confidence estimation pipeline. For classification tasks, the raw confidence is the product of token probabilities over the answer span, normalized by the sum of probabilities across all valid label options. For generation, the model is prompted after answering with “Is this answer correct? Answer only Yes/No.” and the normalized probability of the “Yes” token serves as confidence. These scores are evaluated using AUROC (discrimination) and Expected Calibration Error (ECE). The authors fine‑tune identical base models (e.g., Qwen3‑4B) with three paradigms: SFT (maximum likelihood), GRPO (trust‑region RL), and DPO (preference learning). They find SFT maintains calibration while RL and DPO produce sharp, overconfident distributions. A subsequent SFT step with self‑distillation after RL can recover calibration. Finally, they implement adaptive retrieval‑augmented generation: a confidence threshold τ decides whether to invoke retrieval, demonstrating substantial reductions in retrieval calls without sacrificing accuracy.

## Key Results
- SFT on Qwen3‑4B improves error detection from AUROC 0.806 to 0.879 and reduces ECE from 0.163 to 0.034.
- RL (GRPO) and DPO cause severe overconfidence (sharp calibration curves) despite similar or higher task accuracy.
- Calibrated confidence enables adaptive RAG that uses only 58% of retrieval operations to recover 95% of the maximum accuracy gain on TriviaQA.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: SFT Produces Well‑Calibrated Confidence
**Claim:** Supervised fine‑tuning yields well‑calibrated confidence because its maximum‑likelihood objective aligns the model’s output distribution with the training data distribution.  
**Mechanism:** Minimizing cross‑entropy loss performs maximum‑likelihood estimation, equivalent to minimizing KL(P_data || π_θ). This forces token probabilities to reflect empirical frequencies in the training set, naturally aligning confidence with correctness.  
**Break condition:** If training data is biased or not i.i.d. from the true task distribution, calibration may still suffer.

### Mechanism 2: RL/GRPO Induces Overconfidence
**Claim:** Trust‑region reinforcement learning (PPO, GRPO) induces overconfidence and distribution sharpening via advantage‑weighted policy gradients.  
**Mechanism:** The RL objective `clip(π_θ/π_old * Â)` amplifies the probability of tokens with positive advantage (Â > 0). Iterative updates push these probabilities toward 1, collapsing the distribution and making the model overconfident even for small advantages.  
**Break condition:** Perfectly calibrated advantage estimates and reward functions could in principle avoid sharpening, but this is unrealistic.

### Mechanism 3: DPO Degrades Calibration
**Claim:** Direct Preference Optimization also degrades calibration because it optimizes relative preference probabilities, not absolute output likelihoods, encouraging overconfidence on chosen responses.  
**Mechanism:** DPO’s sigmoid loss saturates when the model is confident about preferences. To maximize the margin between chosen and rejected responses, gradients push π(y_w) up and π(y_l) down aggressively, yielding extreme probabilities and a sharp distribution similar to RL.  
**Break condition:** Adding a regularization term that constrains absolute probabilities (e.g., KL penalty to a reference distribution) could mitigate the effect.

## Foundational Learning
### Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)
**Why needed:** Forms the theoretical basis for why SFT yields calibrated models; explains the connection between training objective and probability calibration.  
**Quick check:** Why does minimizing cross‑entropy loss on a representative dataset lead to a model whose output probabilities match the data distribution?

**Why needed:** Key to understanding why RL methods (PPO, GRPO) distort confidence; the advantage multiplier directly causes probability mass to concentrate on high‑reward actions.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Confidence Extractor → Normalizer → Calibration Evaluator  
Training Modules (SFT, RL, DPO) ↔ Model  
Application Layer (Adaptive RAG) ← normalized confidence + threshold τ

**Critical path:** Confidence extraction → normalization → evaluation → (if miscalibrated) apply post‑RL SFT → deploy with threshold‑based adaptation.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- SFT preserves calibration but may not maximize reward; RL optimizes reward but breaks confidence reliability.  
- Self‑evaluation overhead: single extra forward pass with `max_tokens=1` adds minimal latency.  
- Threshold selection: well‑calibrated models provide a smoother confidence‑accuracy relationship, making τ more robust.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Sharp calibration curve: confidence bins near 0 or 1 over a wide accuracy range.  
- High ECE (>0.1) despite decent AUROC (>0.75), indicating systematic over/under‑confidence.  
- Adaptive RAG shows poor efficiency: retrieval rate constant across τ, indicating uninformative confidence scores.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline calibration sweep:** On a held‑out set, compute normalized confidence, bin by confidence, plot accuracy vs. average confidence per bin, and calculate AUROC and ECE.  
2. **Training paradigm comparison:** Fine‑tune identical base model (Qwen3‑4B‑Instruct) with SFT, GRPO, and DPO on the same data; re‑measure AUROC/ECE to isolate training effect.  
3. **Adaptive RAG efficiency test:** On TriviaQA, run adaptive retrieval with varying τ; plot retrieval % vs. accuracy gain relative to always‑retrieve baseline; compare efficiency between baseline and SFT models.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can reinforcement learning algorithms for LLMs be modified to preserve confidence calibration while maintaining competitive task performance?
2. Can inference‑time mechanisms decouple reward exploitation from confidence estimation in RL‑trained LLMs, allowing calibrated confidence to coexist with reward‑optimized behavior?
3. How can calibrated confidence estimates be generalized to diverse adaptive intervention strategies beyond retrieval‑augmented generation?

## Limitations
- **Base model confounding:** Qwen3‑4B‑Instruct already underwent GRPO RL, so observed SFT calibration gains may partly reflect reversal of prior misalignment rather than inherent MLE properties.
- **Self‑evaluation brittleness:** Yes/No self‑evaluation assumes the model can reliably assess its own correctness; prompt formatting, tokenization, and task‑specific biases may undermine it.
- **Generalizability gap:** Experiments cover only three model families and seven English‑centric benchmarks; results may not transfer to other architectures, domains, or languages.

## Next Checks
1. **Disentangle prior RL effects:** Train SFT, RL, and DPO from a truly un‑aligned pre‑trained checkpoint (e.g., Qwen3‑4B‑Base) and compare calibration to isolate each method’s intrinsic impact.
2. **Stress‑test self‑evaluation:** Vary self‑evaluation prompts (e.g., “How confident are you?” vs. “Is this correct?”), collect token‑level probabilities, and correlate with human uncertainty judgments across diverse generation tasks.
3. **Cross‑benchmark RAG evaluation:** Replicate adaptive RAG on at least two additional retrieval‑augmented datasets (e.g., Natural Questions, PopQA) with different retrievers (BM25, dense) to test if efficiency gains generalize.
