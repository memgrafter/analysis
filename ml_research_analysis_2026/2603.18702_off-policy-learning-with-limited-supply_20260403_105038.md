# Off-Policy Learning with Limited Supply
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.18702
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18702
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: OPLS is a simple, low-cost algorithm that selects items based on relative reward gaps, achieving up to 29–50% higher policy value than greedy under item scarcity while matching greedy when supply is abundant.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Off-Policy Learning with Supply (OPLS), an algorithm for making sequential recommendations under per-item inventory constraints. Unlike conventional greedy methods that choose the highest predicted reward for each user, OPLS allocates scarce items to users who value them most relative to the average user, conserving inventory for high-impact allocations. A theoretical analysis proves OPLS can strictly improve upon greedy when user preferences are aligned (i.e., share a common ranking). Experiments on synthetic and real coupon data confirm significant gains (up to 29% on a real dataset, 50% in synthetic scarcity) and show OPLS reverts to greedy performance when supply is abundant. The method adds no extra computational cost beyond standard model-based greedy selection.

## Method Summary
OPLS learns a reward model \( \hat{q}(x,a) \) from historical off-policy data. At decision time, for each available item \( a \), it computes a score: \( \text{score}(a) = \hat{q}(x,a) - \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \hat{q}(x_i,a) \), where the second term is the average predicted reward of item \( a \) across all training users. The item with the highest score is selected. An adaptive extension predicts which items will be sold out by the end of the horizon using a naive uniform‐demand approximation. For those scarce items it uses the relative-gap score; for abundant items it uses the absolute \( \hat{q} \). The final selection is the maximum \( \hat{q} \) between the two group winners. This rule matches greedy when all items are abundant.

## Key Results
- Up to **29% higher policy value** than greedy on a real-world coupon recommendation dataset.
- Up to **50% improvement** in synthetic settings with extreme scarcity.
- Performance **matches greedy** when supply is abundant; no additional computational overhead.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Relative Reward Gap Allocation for Scarce Items
Claim: For items with limited supply, OPLS improves over greedy by selecting the user with the highest *relative* expected reward (user-specific reward minus the item's average reward), not just the highest absolute reward.

Mechanism: OPLS score = \( \hat{q}(x,a) - \overline{q}(a) \), where \( \overline{q}(a) \) is the average predicted reward for item \( a \) over the user population. This prioritizes users who derive exceptionally high value from a specific item compared to the average user, conserving scarce inventory for high-impact allocations.

Core assumption: The average reward \( \overline{q}(a) \) approximates the opportunity cost of allocating item \( a \) to a user. Assigning \( a \) to a user with reward near this average yields less total value than assigning it to a user with reward significantly above average.

Evidence: 
- Abstract: "OPLS focuses on items with relatively higher expected rewards compared to the other users, enabling more efficient allocation of items with limited supply."
- Theorem 4.1: Improvement lower bound depends on \( q(x_j,a_k) - \mathbb{E}_{p(x)}[q(x,a_k)] \).
- Coupon example (Table 2): Greedy fails to achieve optimal value (540), while OPLS (with relative gap) does for all arrival orders.

Break condition: The relative gap mechanism only helps when at least one item is scarce relative to demand. If all items remain in stock (abundant supply), the relative term becomes irrelevant and OPLS defaults to greedy (Eq. 8).

### Mechanism 2: Adaptive Switching Between Relative and Absolute Rewards
Claim: OPLS extends to heterogeneous supply conditions by applying the relative-gap rule only to predicted sold-out items and the greedy rule to predicted unsold items.

Mechanism: The policy estimates the set \( A_{\text{sold}} \) of items that will be exhausted by the end of the horizon. For candidates in \( A_{\text{sold}} \), it uses the relative-gap score; for \( A_{\text{unsold}} \), it uses absolute \( \hat{q} \). The final choice is the maximum \( \hat{q} \) between the two group winners.

Core assumption: The naive inventory forecast \( s_a^T = s_a^0 - T \cdot \frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|} \sum_i \hat{q}_c(x_i,a) \) accurately identifies sold-out vs. unsold items.

Evidence: 
- Section 4: Description of the adaptive rule (Eq. 8).
- Experiments with "inverse proportional supply" and "random supply" (Appendix D) show adaptive OPLS outperforms greedy even with the naive estimation.

Break condition: If the \( A_{\text{sold}} \) estimation is too inaccurate (e.g., abundant items misclassified as scarce), the adaptive mechanism may apply suboptimal rules and degrade performance relative to pure greedy.

### Mechanism 3: Theoretical Guarantee of Non-Inferiority
Claim: There always exists a deterministic policy (including one derived from the relative-gap insight) that performs at least as well as greedy under any inventory-limited setting with aligned user preferences.

Mechanism: Theorem 4.1 provides a lower bound on the value difference \( V(\pi^*) - V(\pi_{\text{greedy}}) \). Setting \( k=1 \) yields a non-negative bound, proving \( V(\pi^*) \ge V(\pi_{\text{greedy}}) \). The bound is maximized by allocating item \( a_k \) to a user \( x_j \) maximizing \( q(x_j,a_k) - \mathbb{E}_{p(x)}[q(x,a_k)] \), which matches OPLS’s criterion.

Core assumption: The reward function has a common preference ordering across users: \( q(x_j, a_k) \ge q(x_j, a_{k+1}) \) for all \( j, k \). This implies a single popularity ranking of items.

Evidence: 
- Theorem 4.1 statement and proof (Appendix B.2/B.3).
- Coupon example: Greedy achieves 420, optimal 540, and OPLS achieves optimal.

Break condition: The guarantee requires aligned preferences. If preferences are fragmented (e.g., different user segments have different rankings), the theorem's lower bound may become negative, and a strictly superior policy is not guaranteed.

## Foundational Learning
### Contextual Bandit & Off-Policy Learning (OPL)
Why needed: The paper builds on the standard OPL framework where a policy \( \pi(a|x) \) is learned from logged data \( \{(x_i,a_i,r_i)\} \). Understanding the unconstrained greedy baseline \( \pi_{\text{greedy}}(x) = \arg\max_a \hat{q}(x,a) \) is prerequisite to seeing why it fails under supply limits.

### Inventory State Transition
Why needed: The limited-supply formulation redefines the policy as \( \pi(a|x,s_t) \) and the logged data to include inventory states and consumption signals \( (x,a,c,r,s) \). The deterministic update \( s_a^{t+1} = s_a^t - c_t \cdot \mathbf{1}\{a_t=a\} \) is central.
Quick check: How does the inclusion of the binary consumption signal \( c \) and inventory state \( s \) change the definition of \( V(\pi) \) compared to the unconstrained case?

### Opportunity Cost via Average Reward
Why needed: OPLS replaces absolute reward with \( q(x,a) - \mathbb{E}_{p(x)}[q(x,a)] \). This requires interpreting the item's average reward as the opportunity cost of allocating it to an average user.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Q-function Estimator -> Inventory Tracker -> Item Group Classifier -> Decision Rule Engine

- **Q-function Estimator**: Supervised model (e.g., regression, neural net) trained on logged data to predict \( \hat{q}(x,a) = \mathbb{E}[c r | x,a] \).
- **Inventory Tracker**: Maintains current inventory state vector \( s_t \). Updated after each recommendation based on observed consumption \( c_t \). Requires `c` at deployment.
- **Item Group Classifier**: Predicts sold-out items \( A_{\text{sold}} \) via naive approximation \( s_a^T = s_a^0 - T \cdot \frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|} \sum_i \hat{q}_c(x_i,a) \).
- **Decision Rule Engine**: 
  - Precomputes per-item averages \( \overline{q}(a) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_i \hat{q}(x_i,a) \) from training data.
  - Given current user \( x_t \) and available items \( A_{s_t} \):
    - Basic OPLS: compute \( \text{score}(a) = \hat{q}(x_t,a) - \overline{q}(a) \) for all \( a \in A_{s_t} \), pick \( \arg\max \text{score} \).
    - Adaptive OPLS: find \( a_{\text{sold}} = \arg\max_{a \in A_{\text{sold}} \cap A_{s_t}} (\hat{q}(x_t,a) - \overline{q}(a)) \), \( a_{\text{unsold}} = \arg\max_{a \in A_{\text{unsold}} \cap A_{s_t}} \hat{q}(x_t,a) \), then return \( \arg\max_{a \in \{a_{\text{sold}}, a_{\text{unsold}}\}} \hat{q}(x_t,a) \).

### Critical Path
1. Train \( \hat{q}(x,a) \) on historical logged data \( D \).
2. Precompute \( \overline{q}(a) \) for all items using training data.
3. At each time \( t \), receive user context \( x_t \) and current inventory state \( s_t \).
4. Determine set of available items \( A_{s_t} = \{ a : s_a^t > 0 \} \).
5. If using adaptive OPLS, estimate \( A_{\text{sold}} \) (items predicted to sell out by horizon \( T \)).
6. Apply decision rule to select item \( a_t \).
7. After receiving consumption signal \( c_t \), update inventory: \( s_a^{t+1} = s_a^t - c_t \cdot \mathbf{1}\{a_t=a\} \).

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Accuracy vs. computational cost in \( A_{\text{sold}} \) estimation**: The naive uniform approximation is cheap but may misclassify items. More accurate methods (e.g., Monte Carlo simulation of future allocations) could be computationally expensive or intractable for large horizons.
- **Robustness to \( \hat{q} \) estimation error**: Subtracting \( \overline{q}(a) \) amplifies noise if the historical data for some items is sparse. The paper shows robustness to moderate noise in Figure 4c.
- **Generality vs. simplicity**: The adaptive rule guarantees OPLS reduces to greedy when supply is abundant, providing a safe fallback. However, it assumes that items can be cleanly partitioned into sold-out/unsold groups based on a single horizon forecast.

### Failure Signatures
- **Misaligned preferences**: If users have highly heterogeneous rankings, the relative-gap strategy may allocate a scarce item to a user with high relative but low absolute reward, missing a user with moderately high relative but very high absolute reward. Monitor by comparing OPLS vs. greedy performance when supply is abundant (they should be equal).
- **Severe \( A_{\text{sold}} \) misclassification**: If many abundant items are incorrectly labeled scarce, OPLS will waste their high absolute reward potential by applying the relative-gap rule. Monitor large gaps between adaptive OPLS and an oracle with true \( A_{\text{sold}} \).
- **Non-stationary demand**: \( \overline{q}(a) \) is fixed from training data. Shifts in item popularity (e.g., viral trends) make this average a poor estimate of current opportunity cost. Monitor declining performance over time without model retraining.

1. **Replicate Table 2 (Coupon Example)**: Implement OPLS on the 3-user, 3-item example with given rewards. Verify OPLS achieves optimal value (540) for all arrival orders, while greedy varies.

Adaptive should match or exceed basic, especially if abundant items have high absolute rewards.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Global constraints**: How can OPLS be extended to handle global budget constraints across all items rather than per-item inventory limits?
2. **Fairness-aware objectives**: How can fairness-aware objectives be incorporated into OPLS to balance efficiency with equity among users?
3. **Accurate sold-out item estimation**: Can the uniform-selection approximation for predicting sold-out items be replaced with a more accurate and scalable method?
4. **Theoretical guarantees under heterogeneity**: Does OPLS retain performance guarantees (e.g., lower bound improvement) under multi-unit inventory and heterogeneous user preferences?

## Limitations
- The theoretical guarantee (Theorem 4.1) relies on the strong assumption of aligned user preferences (common ranking of items), which may not hold in real-world, diverse populations.
- The adaptive OPLS variant depends on a naive approximation for predicting sold-out items; inaccurate forecasts can lead to suboptimal allocation decisions.
- Empirical validation is limited to one real-world coupon dataset and synthetic scenarios; broader evaluation across diverse domains is lacking.

## Confidence
- **High**: The relative-reward-gap mechanism clearly improves allocation under scarcity in simple and synthetic experiments; OPLS incurs no extra computational cost.
- **Medium**: Adaptive switching and the non-inferiority proof rely on assumptions (aligned preferences, accurate `A_sold` estimates) that hold in controlled settings but may be fragile in practice.
- **Low**: Generalizability to heterogeneous preferences, arbitrary scarcity patterns, and non-stationary environments remains uncertain; theoretical extension is an open question.

## Next Checks
1. **Preference heterogeneity test**: Generate synthetic data with fragmented user preferences (e.g., distinct segments with different item rankings) and compare OPLS vs. greedy. Expect OPLS's advantage to diminish or vanish if aligned preference assumption breaks.
2. **Robustness to `A_sold` misclassification**: Introduce controlled noise into the sold-out item prediction (e.g., randomly flip 10–30% of labels) and measure adaptive OPLS's performance degradation relative to an oracle with true `A_sold`.
3. **Multi-dataset replication**: Apply OPLS to at least two additional real-world recommendation or coupon datasets with varying scarcity levels to confirm the consistency of reported gains and identify boundary conditions for effectiveness.
