# Rotation-Robust Regression with Convolutional Model Trees
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.04899
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04899
- **Reference count**: 1
- **Primary result**: CMT-Full achieves MAE 0.1165 on MNIST perimeter regression, outperforming Ridge (0.1389); Orientation Search improves robustness at ±60° but degrades at 0°.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents Convolutional Model Trees (CMTs), a method for rotation-robust regression on image inputs. CMTs integrate three geometry-aware inductive biases—convolutional smoothing, tilt dominance constraint, and importance-based pruning—into an ensemble of model trees. Additionally, a deployment-time orientation search (OS) selects the rotation that maximizes a forest-level confidence proxy without retraining. Evaluated on MNIST perimeter regression and digit recognition, CMT-Full reduces error relative to ridge regression, especially under large rotations, while OS further boosts robustness at extreme angles. However, OS harms performance near the canonical orientation, and training time increases dramatically. The work reveals a trade-off between rotational robustness and computational efficiency.

## Method Summary
Convolutional Model Trees (CMTs) extend traditional model trees by replacing axis-aligned splits with convolutional operations, allowing the model to capture spatial hierarchies. The method introduces three inductive biases: (1) convolutional smoothing aggregates information from local neighborhoods to reduce sensitivity to small misalignments, (2) tilt dominance constraint penalizes filters that are unstable under rotation, encouraging rotation-equivariant representations, and (3) importance-based pruning removes less informative filters to control complexity and prevent overfitting. At inference, orientation search (OS) rotates the input across a set of angles and selects the one yielding the most confident forest prediction (e.g., lowest variance among tree outputs). The approach is evaluated on MNIST perimeter regression and one-vs-rest digit classification, with ridge regression as a baseline.

## Key Results
- CMT-Full (all biases) attains average MAE 0.1165 on MNIST perimeter regression, beating Ridge (0.1389).
- OS reduces MAE at ±60° (0.1416 vs. 0.1595 without OS) but increases MAE at 0° (0.1114 vs. 0.0609).
- For digit recognition, CMT-Full-HG reaches 51.23% average accuracy; OS similarly helps at extreme rotations but hurts at 0°.
- Training time for CMT-Full is 37.3 seconds vs. 0.7 seconds for Ridge, highlighting a robustness-compute trade-off.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The rotation robustness of CMTs stems from the three inductive biases. Convolutional smoothing dilutes the effect of small pixel shifts by averaging over neighborhoods, making predictions less sensitive to misalignment. The tilt dominance constraint explicitly regularizes filters to have similar responses under slight rotations, forcing the model to learn orientation-stable features. Importance-based pruning eliminates filters that capture noise or orientation-specific artifacts, reducing overfitting. During inference, OS leverages the ensemble's disagreement as a confidence cue: the rotation producing the most consistent predictions across trees (low variance) is selected, which tends to align the input with the model's learned invariances. However, OS degrades near 0° because the confidence proxy (variance) may be miscalibrated when the input matches the training distribution's mode, possibly due to the forest being optimized for rotated data and thus exhibiting higher uncertainty on canonical orientations.

## Foundational Learning
- **Convolutional smoothing**: Applies local averaging to reduce sensitivity to small translations/rotations. *Why needed*: Mitigates pixel-level noise and misalignment. *Quick check*: Compare predictions on slightly shifted images with/without smoothing.
- **Tilt dominance constraint**: Regularization that penalizes filter responses that vary under small rotations. *Why needed*: Encourages rotation-equivariant features. *Quick check*: Rotate input slightly and measure filter output variance; lower variance indicates stronger effect.
- **Importance-based pruning**: Removes convolutional filters or tree branches with low validation contribution. *Why needed*: Controls complexity and prevents overfitting to orientation-specific patterns. *Quick check*: Systematically prune and evaluate on a held-out set.
- **Orientation search (OS)**: Post-hoc rotation selection based on forest confidence (e.g., prediction variance). *Why needed*: Adapts to unknown test-time rotations without retraining. *Quick check*: With known true angles, check if OS selects the correct rotation.
- **Model trees**: Decision trees with linear models at leaves, combining nonlinearity with smooth outputs. *Why needed*: Captures complex relationships while preserving regression continuity. *Quick check*: Compare against pure decision trees on a nonlinear regression task.
- **Ensemble of trees**: Aggregates predictions from multiple trees to reduce variance. *Why needed*: Improves robustness and calibration. *Quick check*: Measure prediction variance as ensemble size increases.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Input image -> Convolutional smoothing layer -> Tilt-regularized convolutional filters -> Ensemble of pruned model trees -> Forest predictions -> Orientation search module -> Final output.

**Critical path**:  
*Training*: (1) Apply smoothing to training images. (2) Learn tree structures and linear leaf models with tilt constraint and pruning. (3) Train ensemble.  
*Inference*: (1) For each candidate rotation, apply smoothing and convolutions. (2) Obtain forest predictions. (3) Compute confidence proxy (e.g., variance). (4) Select best rotation. (5) Output prediction.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- **Robustness vs. compute**: Tilt biases and pruning increase training time (37.3s vs 0.7s) but improve rotation robustness; OS adds inference overhead (multiple forward passes) but avoids retraining.  
- **Bias-variance**: Smoothing and pruning reduce variance but may increase bias if over-regularized.  
- **Confidence proxy**: Simple variance may not correlate with accuracy, especially near canonical rotation.

**Failure signatures**:  
- OS harms accuracy at 0°, indicating miscalibrated confidence proxy when input aligns with training distribution.  
- Over-pruning or excessive tilt constraint may cause underfitting, seen by higher error without OS.  
- Training time scales poorly with model size, limiting use on large images.

**First three experiments**:  
1. **Ablation study**: Train CMTs with each bias removed to quantify individual contributions to rotation robustness on MNIST.  
2. **Confidence calibration**: Plot OS-selected rotation vs. true rotation and prediction error to diagnose OS failure at 0°.  
3. **Generalization test**: Apply trained CMT-Full to rotated natural image regression datasets (e.g., aerial vehicle count, medical lesion size) to evaluate transfer beyond MNIST.

## Limitations
- Evaluation restricted to MNIST perimeter regression and digit recognition; generalizability to natural images or other regression tasks is unknown.
- OS degrades performance near 0° rotation; mechanistic explanation is missing.
- Training time increases significantly (37.3s vs. 0.7s); practical deployment costs not analyzed.

## Confidence
- **Medium**: CMT-Full outperforms Ridge on MNIST perimeter regression (specific task/dataset).
- **Medium**: OS improves robustness at ±60° but harms at 0° (empirical trend without causal analysis).
- **High**: Training time increase with tilt biases is directly measured.
- **Low**: Claims about broader applicability to image inputs (evaluation limited to MNIST).
