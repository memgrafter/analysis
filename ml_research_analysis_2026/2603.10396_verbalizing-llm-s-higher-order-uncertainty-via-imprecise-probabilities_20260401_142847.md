# Verbalizing LLM's Higher-order Uncertainty via Imprecise Probabilities

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2603.10396
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10396
- **Reference count**: 34
- **Primary result**: Imprecise probability-based prompting achieves state-of-the-art AUROC for correctness detection (e.g., ProbInt: 0.8617±0.0082 on MMLU-Pro) and ambiguity detection, with lower API costs than sampling baselines.

## Executive Summary
Traditional uncertainty elicitation methods for large language models (LLMs) often fail to capture higher-order uncertainty, which is essential for handling ambiguous questions, in-context learning, and self-reflection. This paper introduces a novel framework that uses imprecise probabilities—specifically probability intervals, credal sets, and possibility measures—to represent both first-order (aleatoric) and second-order (epistemic) uncertainties. The authors propose prompting strategies that elicit these representations and quantify second-order uncertainty via Maximum Mean Imprecision (MMI). Evaluation on synthetic tasks and real-world QA benchmarks demonstrates that the approach disentangles aleatoric noise from epistemic reduction, achieving state-of-the-art performance in correctness and ambiguity detection while maintaining low API costs.

On synthetic sequence transformation tasks, the methods show that first-order uncertainty increases with ambiguity noise, whereas second-order uncertainty remains flat under noise and decreases as more in-context examples are provided, confirming the disentanglement. In real-world ambiguous QA (AmbigQA, MAQA) and multiple-choice (MMLU-Pro), the IP-based methods (especially ProbInt and DeFinetti) achieve higher AUROC than baselines, exhibit stronger concordance with ground-truth uncertainty proxies, and align with maximin decision rules. The work highlights the potential of imprecise probabilities for more faithful uncertainty communication in LLMs.

## Method Summary
The core contribution is a set of prompting techniques that instruct LLMs to output imprecise probability representations rather than single probabilities. For first-order uncertainty, the authors use a DeFinetti-style prompt that asks the model to provide coherent betting odds, ensuring probabilities sum to one. To capture second-order uncertainty, three variants are introduced: (1) ProbIntPrompt asks for lower and upper probability bounds for each candidate answer; (2) CredalPrompt obtains probability vectors from multiple model samples (ensemble) and constructs a credal set via element-wise minima and maxima; (3) PosPrompt elicits possibility scores, including an explicit "none of the above" option, and normalizes them to derive a measure of imprecision. For open-ended QA, a CandidateAnswerPrompt generates a set of plausible answers. A verifier module checks the coherence of the elicited intervals (e.g., sum of lower bounds ≤ 1, non-negativity) and may trigger re-prompting on failure. Second-order uncertainty is quantified using Maximum Mean Imprecision (MMI), with different approximations depending on the representation (e.g., 1 − Σ lower bounds for interval/credal, second-largest normalized possibility for POS).

The framework is evaluated on two fronts. First, synthetic tasks involving a rotated and cyclically shifted string transformation allow controlled manipulation of aleatoric noise (random lowercasing) and epistemic uncertainty (number of in-context examples). This disentanglement experiment shows that IP-based methods correctly separate the two uncertainty types, whereas vanilla uncertainty measures conflate them. Second, on real-world datasets—AmbiguQA and MAQA for ambiguous open-ended QA, and MMLU-Pro for multiple-choice—the IP methods are assessed for ambiguity detection (AUROC for questions with multiple valid answers) and correctness detection (AUROC for predicting whether the model's answer is correct). Additional metrics include concordance with ground-truth uncertainty proxies and alignment with decision rules like maximin. API costs are also compared, showing that except for Credal (which requires multiple samples), the IP methods are cost-competitive with verbalized baselines and cheaper than sampling-based approaches like MI-Clarifications.

## Key Results
- IP-based methods (ProbInt, Credal, Pos) achieve state-of-the-art AUROC for correctness detection on MMLU-Pro (e.g., ProbInt: 0.8617±0.0082 with GPT-5) and ambiguity detection (DeFinetti highest AUROC).
- On synthetic tasks, they successfully disentangle first-order (aleatoric) and second-order (epistemic) uncertainty: first-order increases with noise, second-order decreases with more in-context examples, while vanilla methods fail to show the latter pattern.
- They exhibit the strongest concordance with ground-truth uncertainty proxies and best alignment with maximin decision rationality, while maintaining lower API costs than sampling baselines (except Credal) and cost parity with verbalized baselines.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Probability intervals (ProbInt) capture second‑order uncertainty by allowing the model to express lower and upper bounds, which improves ambiguity detection and tracks error reduction in ICL.
- **Mechanism**: The model is prompted to provide lower and upper probabilities for each candidate answer (Prompt 2). A verifier enforces coherence (e.g., sum of lowers ≤ 1). Second‑order uncertainty is quantified via Maximum Mean Imprecision (MMI), often approximated by 1 − ∑ p_lower. First‑order uncertainty can be derived from interval midpoints or DeFinetti entropy.
- **Core assumption**: LLMs can meaningfully articulate intervals that reflect their credal state; these intervals are approximately coherent and capture epistemic spread.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: The abstract explicitly mentions “probability intervals (ProbInt)” as a representation for eliciting first- and second-order uncertainties.
  - [section]: Section 3.2 details the ProbInt prompt, verifier, and use of MMI; Figure 6 demonstrates disentanglement of first‑order (sensitive to ambiguity noise p) and second‑order (flat under p) uncertainty.
  - [corpus]: The corpus includes work on imprecise probabilities (e.g., “Property Elicitation on Imprecise Probabilities”; “Truthful Elicitation of Imprecise Forecasts”), showing broader research context.
- **Break condition**: The mechanism fails if intervals collapse (p_lower = p_upper) on clearly ambiguous items, or if the verifier repeatedly rejects outputs (e.g., p_lower > p_upper or ∑ p_lower > 1), indicating the model cannot produce coherent intervals.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Credal sets constructed from an ensemble of LLM outputs quantify second‑order uncertainty via model disagreement, yielding better correctness detection in group settings.
- **Mechanism**: Multiple independent LLM samples or models produce precise probability vectors (Prompt 3). The credal set is the convex hull; per‑answer lower/upper bounds are the element‑wise min/max across the ensemble. MMI is derived from these bounds.
- **Core assumption**: Disagreement among ensemble members reflects epistemic uncertainty about the true predictive distribution; the ensemble is sufficiently diverse.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: “Credal sets” are listed as an IP representation suited for ensembles of LLMs.
  - [section]: Section 3.2 and Figure 7(b) show CREDAL‑EU achieving higher AUROC than baselines for correctness detection with an LLM ensemble.
  - [corpus]: Corpus entries such as “Accelerated Preference Elicitation with LLM-Based Proxies” and “Eliciting Behaviors in Multi‑Turn Conversations” illustrate multi-model elicitation trends.
- **Break condition**: Low ensemble diversity yields overly narrow intervals, underestimating second-order uncertainty; a very small ensemble size (< 3) increases estimation noise and may not span the credal set.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Possibility measures (POS) handle open‑ended QA by separately modeling “none of the above,” avoiding forced probability mass redistribution and improving robustness when the candidate set is incomplete.
- **Mechanism**: The model assigns possibility scores to each listed answer and an explicit “none of the above” (Prompt 4). After normalizing by the maximum score, MMI equals the second‑largest normalized value (or, for a specific answer, min / max between that answer and its complement).
- **Core assumption**: Possibility functions encode relative plausibility without additivity; the top two normalized scores adequately capture imprecision.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: “Possibility measures” are noted as an IP representation useful when the candidate set may be incomplete.
  - [section]: Appendix B.3 derives MMI for possibility functions; Section 5 (Figure 9) reports competitive ambiguity detection results.
  - [corpus]: “Truthful Elicitation of Imprecise Forecasts” aligns with using imprecise, non‑additive representations for uncertainty.
- **Break condition**: If both the top answer and “none of the above” receive high possibility, MMI becomes large even when listed answers are likely correct; missing the “none of the above” option forces mass redistribution and can bias MMI.

## Foundational Learning
### Aleatoric vs. Epistemic Uncertainty
- **Why needed**: The paper’s core distinction between first‑order (aleatoric, irreducible) and second‑order (epistemic, reducible) uncertainty underpins the disentanglement experiments and interpretation of MMI.
- **Quick check question**: Give an example of aleatoric uncertainty in QA and an example of epistemic uncertainty.

### Imprecise Probabilities (IP)
- **Why needed**: IP generalizes classical probability by allowing sets of distributions (intervals, credal sets, possibility). All proposed elicitation methods are instances of IP; understanding IP is essential to grasp the representation of higher-order uncertainty.
- **Quick check question**: How does a credal set differ from a single probability distribution?

### Maximum Mean Imprecision (MMI)
- **Why needed**: MMI is the scalar metric used throughout to quantify second-order uncertainty from IP representations. Approximations (upper‑bound, singleton exact) affect computational cost and precision.
- **Quick check question**: What does a larger MMI value indicate about the model’s epistemic uncertainty?

### De Finetti Coherence (Betting Interpretation)
- **Why needed**: The DeFinetti prompting enforces probability axioms via a betting rationale, producing well‑calibrated first-order probabilities. This serves as a baseline for comparison and influences some design choices.
- **Quick check question**: Why would probabilities that do not sum to 1 expose the model to a “Dutch book”?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Prompt templates -> LLM API client -> Verifier module -> MMI calculator -> Evaluation pipeline

### Critical path
per query:
1. If open‑ended, call CandidateAnswerPrompt to obtain candidate set ˆY.
2. For chosen IP method (ProbInt/Credal/POS):
   - Invoke appropriate prompt(s) to get raw output.
   - Pass to verifier; if invalid, re‑prompt (loop).
   - Compute first‑order uncertainty (e.g., entropy from DeFinetti or interval midpoint) and second‑order uncertainty (MMI).
   - For correctness detection, select answer via maximin (argmax of lower bounds) or other rule.
3. Store results; after all queries, aggregate metrics.

### Design tradeoffs
- *Efficiency vs. fidelity*: ProbInt is single‑call and low‑cost but may produce less expressive intervals; Credal uses multiple calls (ensemble size M) to capture disagreement at higher API cost; POS is single‑call but relies on proper normalization of top‑two scores.
- *Verifier strictness*: Overly strict constraints increase re‑prompt latency; too lenient risks incoherent intervals. A practical balance is to allow a small number of re‑prompts (e.g., 3).
- *Scalability*: Exact MMI over all events scales exponentially with |Y|; the paper uses the linear upper‑bound for dataset‑level EU and exact only for answer‑level singleton events. This approximation trades a small amount of precision for tractability.
- *Open‑ended handling*: Candidate generation (Prompt 5) adds cost and may miss the true answer if ˆY is too small; limiting ˆY size trades coverage for speed.

### Failure signatures
- High re‑prompt rate (> 20%) indicates LLM difficulty producing valid intervals; consider simplifying the prompt or using lower temperature.
- MMI scores near zero on clearly ambiguous questions suggest the model is not expressing imprecision; check POS normalization or interval width.
- AUROC below 0.5 for correctness detection means the uncertainty score is anti‑correlated with error; perhaps the score polarity is flipped or the scale is misaligned.
- In credal methods, if all ensemble members output identical probabilities, intervals collapse; verify ensemble diversity (e.g., variance > 0.01).
- API cost exceeds budget: reduce ensemble size M or number of sampling calls; consider switching from Credal to ProbInt.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Synthetic disentanglement** – Replicate Section 4 on rotation tasks with varying ambiguity noise p and ICL examples m. Verify: (a) ProbInt first‑order uncertainty increases with p, (b) second‑order uncertainty (MMI) is flat across p and decreases with m, (c) vanilla uncertainty fails pattern (b).
2. **MMLU‑Pro sanity check** – Run ProbInt on a random subset of N = 50 MMLU‑Pro examples; expected AUROC for correctness detection ≈ 0.86 ± 0.05. This validates implementation against reported numbers.
3. **Verifier robustness test** – Feed intentionally malformed outputs (e.g., p_lower > p_upper, ∑ p_lower > 1) and ensure the verifier detects them and triggers re‑prompting. Acceptable re‑prompt rate under 10% after a small prompt‑tuning.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Question**: To what extent do LLMs interpret and respond to IP-based prompts according to the formal rational semantics of imprecise probabilities (e.g., coherence via de Finetti’s betting interpretation), versus producing arbitrary or heuristic verbalizations?
   - **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors state: "First, we assume verbalized uncertainty is approximately rational; although allowing imprecision mitigates, this cannot be fully verified." They also design prompts based on de Finetti’s coherence (Prompt 1, Alg. 1) but note the assumption cannot be fully verified.
   - **Why unresolved**: The prompt design imposes a rational structure (e.g., coherence checks in Alg. 1), but it is unclear whether the LLM’s internal processing genuinely adheres to the IP semantics or merely produces superficially plausible numeric intervals. This affects the validity of interpreting elicited intervals as true lower/upper probabilities.
   - **What evidence would resolve it**: Controlled behavioral experiments where the LLM’s elicited intervals are tested against Dutch book scenarios or other coherence axioms derived from IP theory. Alternatively, fine-grained analysis of model activations/policy gradients during prompt generation could reveal whether interval outputs correlate with concepts of ignorance vs. indifference.

2. **Question**: How robust are IP-based uncertainty elicitation methods to variations in model architecture, scale, and fine-tuning (e.g., instruction-tuned vs. base models, open-source vs. closed-source), and do they generalize beyond multiple-choice QA to open-ended generation tasks like summarization or translation?
   - **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The conclusion notes: "we focus primarily on Q&A tasks; extending to settings such as translation or summarization remains future work." Experiments use GPT-5-Mini, GPT-5-Nano, and Gemini-2.5-Pro, but do not systematically vary architecture or explore non-QA tasks.
   - **Why unresolved**: The paper’s success is demonstrated on QA benchmarks with candidate options Y provided or approximated via Prompt 5. It is unknown whether the same prompting strategies (e.g., eliciting probability intervals, possibility scores) will be effective or meaningful for tasks with continuous or unstructured output spaces, or for models with different training objectives (e.g., purely autoregressive vs. RLHF-tuned).
   - **What evidence would resolve it**: Running the same IP prompting protocols on a diverse set of models (e.g., Llama, Mistral, Claude) across tasks like summarization (e.g., XSum) and machine translation (e.g., WMT), using appropriate evaluation metrics for uncertainty (e.g., calibration, AUROC for hallucination detection). Success would require that IP scores continue to track ambiguity and error reduction meaningfully.

3. **Question**: Does the imprecise probability framework proposed here accurately disentangle first-order (aleatoric) and second-order (epistemic) uncertainty in ICL settings, and is the interpretation of ICL as reducing second-order uncertainty via implicit Bayesian inference consistent with current non-Bayesian theories of ICL?
   - **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The paper frames ICL through an IP lens: additional examples reduce second-order uncertainty (epistemic). They cite Falck et al. [2024] arguing ICL is not strictly Bayesian, yet their framework assumes a Bayesian-like reduction in uncertainty via evidence. This creates a potential theoretical mismatch.
   - **Why unresolved**: The synthetic ICL experiments show uncertainty decreasing with more examples (Fig. 7), which they attribute to second-order denoising. However, if ICL operates via gradient-based matching or associative retrieval rather than Bayesian posterior updating, the meaning of "second-order uncertainty reduction" becomes unclear. The IP representation may still be empirically useful, but its normative interpretation may not align with the mechanism.
   - **What evidence would resolve it**: Comparative experiments contrasting IP-based uncertainty scores with alternative ICL uncertainty measures derived from non-Bayesian theories (e.g., based on attention patterns, gradient norms, or similarity to training examples). A formal analysis linking IP interval narrowing to specific ICL mechanisms would clarify whether the observed effect is epistemic reduction or something else.

4. **Question**: For ambiguous questions with multiple valid answers, how do IP-based methods compare to, and potentially integrate with, clarification-based or sampling-based uncertainty decomposition techniques (e.g., MI-Clarifications, semantic entropy) in terms of cost, faithfulness, and decision utility?
   - **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The paper directly compares to MI-Clarifications (a sampling-based decomposition method) and shows lower API cost and better AUROC on some benchmarks (Fig. 11, Table 1). However, they note their approach is verbalized vs. sampling-based.
   - **Why unresolved**: While IP methods are more cost-efficient than sampling-based baselines (except Credal), it is unknown whether they capture the same nuance as entropy-based decompositions that explicitly model output distributions. The paper combines DeFinetti (first-order) with IP (second-order) multiplicatively for correctness under ambiguity (Fig. 10), but the theoretical justification for this combination and its optimality in decision-making is not explored.
   - **What evidence would resolve it**: An ablation study comparing the multiplicative combination to other fusion strategies (e.g., additive, learned weighting) across decision-theoretic tasks (e.g., abstention, fallback activation). A formal analysis of how IP intervals relate to the mutual information used in MI-Clarifications would clarify whether they are complementary or redundant.

5. **Question**: How sensitive are the elicited probability intervals and possibility scores to the wording of the IP prompts (e.g., Prompt 1, Prompt 2, Prompt 4), and can we design more robust prompt templates that reduce variance across model runs while preserving the intended IP semantics?
   - **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The paper provides specific prompt templates (e.g., Prompt 2 for ProbInt) but does not analyze prompt sensitivity. The synthetic experiments use 5 random seeds per condition, suggesting awareness of variability, but no breakdown of how prompt phrasing affects interval width or MMI.
   - **Why unresolved**: Verbalized uncertainty is known to be sensitive to wording (e.g., Tian et al. 2023). IP prompts introduce new concepts (lower/upper probability, possibility) that may be interpreted inconsistently by the LLM across runs or models. This could manifest as high variance in MMI scores even for identical questions, undermining reliability.
   - **What evidence would resolve it**: A systematic sensitivity analysis where minor variations of the IP prompts (e.g., rephrasing "lower probability" as "minimum plausible probability") are tested on multiple models and datasets, measuring variance in elicited intervals and downstream performance (AUROC, concordance). Developing and testing invariant prompt designs (e.g., using few-shot exemplars) would improve robustness.

## Limitations
- **Model access**: The paper uses unreleased models (GPT‑5, Gemini‑2.5‑pro); reproduction must use public substitutes (e.g., GPT‑4o), which may affect performance rankings.
- **Synthetic task definition**: Ambiguity in "rotation (n=13), followed by cyclic shift (n=1)" could lead to inconsistent replications.
- **Candidate set construction**: The procedure for generating candidate answer sets `Ŷ` is not fully documented; incomplete `Ŷ` may bias ambiguity detection results.
