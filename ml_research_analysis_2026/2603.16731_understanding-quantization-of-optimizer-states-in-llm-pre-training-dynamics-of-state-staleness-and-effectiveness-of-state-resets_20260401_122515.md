# Understanding Quantization of Optimizer States in LLM Pre-training: Dynamics of State Staleness and Effectiveness of State Resets

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.16731
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16731
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Periodic resets of quantized optimizer states recover validation loss degraded by quantization (e.g., FP8 100M loss reduced from 3.33 to 3.22) while lowering optimizer-state memory by 50–87.5%.

## Executive Summary
Quantizing optimizer states (exponential moving averages) in LLM pre-training induces update stalling, where low-precision storage causes many nominal updates to round back to the same value, making the state persistently stale. This staleness slows adaptation beyond the intended decay. The paper analyzes this via an effective precision ratio and shows that after a quantized state is reset to zero, it remains responsive for a finite startup window before stalling dominates. By balancing accumulated staleness against the remaining statistical benefit of the EMA, the authors derive theory-guided reset periods. Experiments on LLaMA models (60M–350M) confirm that periodic resets recover losses otherwise degraded by quantization to BF16, FP8, and FP4, aligning with predicted periods. The work provides a principled approach to mitigate quantization-induced staleness while achieving significant memory savings.

## Method Summary
The study combines theoretical analysis with empirical validation on small LLaMA models. Theoretically, it models stalling as a gating process where a quantized EMA update produces no change if the high-precision increment is smaller than half the ulp of the stored value. This yields a closed-form transient stalling probability that depends on precision and decay. The startup window length is defined as the steps until stalling probability exceeds a threshold. Reset periods are selected by minimizing a cycle-averaged excess staleness measure against the normalized remaining statistical error of the EMA. Empirically, LLaMA models are trained on C4 with AdamW (β₁=0.9, β₂=0.999), storing states in BF16/FP8/FP4 with nearest rounding. Periodic resets are applied every *K* steps, and validation loss is compared. The methodology also measures stalled fractions to validate the transient model.

## Key Results
- Quantizing optimizer states to FP8/FP4 causes near‑certain stalling (steady‑state stalled fraction ≈1.0), degrading final validation loss (e.g., +0.11 for FP8 100M).
- Periodic resets (e.g., every ~1000 steps for BF16, ~300 for FP8, ~200 for FP4) recover most or all of the loss degradation, reducing memory by 50–87.5%.
- The heuristic reset periods predicted from theory align with empirically robust performance windows; both shorter and longer reset periods hurt convergence.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Quantization-induced update stalling
Low‑precision storage of optimizer EMAs causes many nominal updates to round back to the same stored value, creating persistent staleness. The high‑precision update increment Δ_t = (1‑β²)(g_t² − v_{t‑1}) is compared against half the ulp of the stored v_{t‑1}^q. If |Δ_t| < ulp/2, the quantized state does not change: v_t^q = v_{t‑1}^q. The effective precision ratio ρ̂ = ε/(2(1‑β²) \bar{m}) (with format‑dependent spacing ε and \bar{m}≈0.721) predicts stalling; when ρ̂ ≥ 1 the one‑step stalling probability approaches 1 (e.g., ~0.946 for BF16, ~1.0 for FP8/FP4). Evidence: Abstract and Section 3.1 formalize the condition; measurements show steady‑state stalled fractions matching predictions (Figure 2).

### Mechanism 2: Transient responsiveness (startup window)
After a reset (or initialization), a quantized EMA remains updateable for a finite number of steps before stalling dominates. The state grows from zero; early on its magnitude is small, so the ulp is small relative to typical increments, allowing updates. As the state approaches steady‑state scale, stalling probability rises. The startup window *j*⋆ is the first step where transient stalling probability P_NR^stall(j) ≈ F_{χ²₁}((1+ρ̂)(1‑β^{2j})) exceeds a threshold P₀. For β²=0.999, the window can be hundreds of steps in BF16 but near zero in FP4. Evidence: Proposition 4.1 and Definition 4.2 provide the closed‑form; Figure 2 shows empirically measured stalled fraction rising after reset.

### Mechanism 3: Reset period selection via cycle‑averaged staleness
Periodic resets restore responsiveness after the startup window, but the period must balance accumulated reset‑sensitive staleness against the remaining statistical benefit of averaging. After a reset, normalized stalling progress S(j) grows from 0 to 1. Define cycle‑averaged excess staleness \bar{S}_{s₀}(K) = (1/K) ∑_{j=1}^K [S(j)‑s₀]₊/(1‑s₀) with tolerance s₀ (e.g., 0.6). Normalized remaining statistical error of the bias‑corrected EMA after K steps is E(K)=2β^{2K}/(1+β^{2K}). The heuristic reset period K⋆ is the smallest K where \bar{S}_{s₀}(K) ≥ E(K). For β²=0.999 this gives ≈1116 for BF16, ≈320 for FP8, ≈224 for FP4. Evidence: Proposition 5.2 defines K⋆; Table 3 and Figure 4 show predicted vs. empirical minima aligning.

## Foundational Learning
- **Exponential Moving Averages (EMAs)**: Used for optimizer moments (e.g., Adam’s first/second moment). Understanding their memory τ=1/(1‑β²) and stochastic updates is key to seeing how quantization alters effective decay. *Quick check*: If an EMA update stalls with probability P_stall, effective decay β_eff ≈ 1 − (1‑β²)(1‑P_stall).
- **Floating‑point precision and ulp**: Stalling occurs when the update increment is smaller than half the ulp of the stored value; format properties (e.g., BF16: ε=2⁻⁷, FP8 E4M3: ε=2⁻³) determine ulp scaling. *Quick check*: For a normalized float x=m2^e, ulp(x) = 2^{e‑(mantissa_bits‑1)}.
- **Stochastic rounding vs. nearest rounding**: Nearest rounding can cause deterministic stalling; stochastic rounding adds unbiased noise that can reduce stalling probability but not eliminate it in very low precision. *Quick check*: In stochastic rounding, if the high‑precision value is exactly halfway, both adjacent representable values are chosen with equal probability (0.5 each).

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Training loop → backward pass → optimizer step: load quantized state buffers (first/second moment) → dequantize to FP32 → compute high‑precision update → quantize new state (nearest/stochastic rounding) → update parameters using bias‑corrected moments. Reset controller may overwrite state buffers with zero every *K* steps.
- **Critical path**: 1) Load quantized state; 2) Dequantize; 3) Compute update; 4) Quantize new state (stalling occurs here); 5) Parameter update; 6) Periodic reset (before/after step 4). Steps 3–5 are where quantization effects and resets intervene.
- **Design tradeoffs**: Memory vs. quality: smaller formats (FP4) save more memory but suffer higher stalling. Reset frequency: too frequent (< startup window) discards useful averaging; too infrequent leaves state stale. Rounding mode: stochastic rounding reduces stalling but adds variance. Scaling: per‑tensor scaling worsens startup; block‑wise scaling helps.
- **Failure signatures**: High steady‑state stalled fraction (~1 for FP8/FP4 second moment) leading to frozen updates; first‑moment stalling causing spikes and divergence. Resets applied too early (< 500 steps BF16) or too late (> 2000 steps BF16) degrade final loss. Adaptive reset rule failing to trigger (noisy monitor) yields poor performance.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Simulated stalling intervention: Skip moment updates with probability *p* after warmup to show second‑moment staleness degrades loss gradually, first‑moment staleness more severely.
  2. Measure stalling fraction across precisions: Quantize only optimizer states in LLaMA pre‑training; confirm transient startup window and precision‑dependent steady state (Figure 2).
  3. Reset period sweep: Vary reset period widely for BF16/FP8/FP4. Performance robust near predicted periods; both too short and too long hurt (Figure 4).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Does the stalling model and reset period prediction hold for models >1B parameters?
2. How do stalling and reset effectiveness extend to first‑moment EMAs and multi‑state optimizers like Shampoo?
3. How do quantization implementation choices (scaling strategy, format, rounding mode) quantitatively affect the startup window and optimal reset period?
4. Can we design native quantization schemes that inherently mitigate state‑update stalling, reducing or eliminating the need for periodic resets?

## Limitations
- Exact model configurations (hidden dimensions, FFN sizes) and some hyperparameters (weight decay, cosine decay end LR) are unspecified, hindering exact replication.
- Quantization details (per‑tensor vs. block scaling for BF16/FP8, FP4 zero‑point handling, potential custom kernels) rely on conventions; small deviations may alter stalling statistics.
- Theoretical analysis assumes stationary, independent gradient coordinates, which may not hold in practice, especially early in training or with non‑iid data; validation is limited to small LLaMA variants (60M–350M).

## Confidence
- **Quantization‑induced stalling degrades performance**: **High** – Direct measurements and intervention experiments confirm the causal link.
- **Periodic resets mitigate degradation**: **High** – Empirical sweeps show consistent improvement across precisions.
- **Theory predicts optimal reset periods**: **Medium** – Alignment between predicted and empirical minima is strong but may be sensitive to assumptions and scaling to larger models.
