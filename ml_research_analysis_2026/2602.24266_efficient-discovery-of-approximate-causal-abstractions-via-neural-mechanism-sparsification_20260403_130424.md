# Efficient Discovery of Approximate Causal Abstractions via Neural Mechanism Sparsification

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.24266
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.24266
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** The proposed method achieves higher interventional fidelity than variance-based pruning on MNIST, especially at aggressive compression, and remains perfectly robust to scaling reparameterizations where variance-based pruning fails.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces a framework for discovering approximate causal abstractions of neural networks by sparsifying mechanisms. The authors reformulate structured pruning as the construction of a reduced structural causal model (SCM), where individual units are replaced by constants or affine functions. Using a second-order Taylor expansion of the task loss, they derive per-unit importance scores and closed-form parameters for replacements, unifying prior variance-based pruning under a uniform curvature assumption. The resulting abstractions are compiled into smaller networks and assessed via interchange interventions, which measure how well the reduced model preserves causal behavior. Empirical evaluation on MNIST demonstrates superior interventional fidelity compared to variance-based pruning, particularly under strong compression, and shows invariance to scaling reparameterizations that break variance-based selection. This work bridges causal abstraction theory with practical neural network compression.

## Method Summary
The method begins by treating a trained neural network as a structural causal model (SCM) where each unit’s activation is a deterministic function of its inputs. To abstract the network, a target compression level is specified, and each unit is evaluated for replacement by a simpler mechanism (constant or affine function). The expected change in task loss upon replacement is approximated via a second-order Taylor expansion around the original parameters, yielding a score that decomposes into a first-order term (sensitive to gradient magnitude) and a second-order curvature term. Under uniform curvature across units, this reduces to variance-based pruning. For each unit, the optimal replacement parameters (e.g., constant value or affine weights) are derived in closed form by minimizing the approximated loss change. After selecting units to replace based on scores, the network is transformed into a reduced SCM by substituting the chosen mechanisms. Finally, the abstracted network is evaluated using interchange interventions—systematically perturbing inputs to internal variables and comparing outputs between original and abstracted models—to measure causal fidelity. The entire process is efficient and provides a principled trade-off between compression and interventional accuracy.

## Key Results
- On MNIST, the Logit-MSE score yields higher interventional fidelity than variance-based pruning at aggressive compression (keep=256: IIA 0.782±0.006 vs 0.776±0.010, KL 0.578±0.020 vs 0.609±0.026).
- The method is robust to exact scaling reparameterizations; it maintains perfect stability (Jaccard=1.0) while variance-based pruning degrades (Jaccard≈0.45).
- The second-order scoring unifies variance-based pruning as a special case under uniform curvature, providing theoretical justification.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core insight is that replacing a unit with a constant or affine function perturbs the network’s computational graph, and the impact on loss can be locally approximated. The second-order expansion captures both the unit’s sensitivity (first-order gradient) and the loss curvature (Hessian), providing a more nuanced importance measure than variance alone. Under uniform curvature, the second-order term becomes constant across units, collapsing the score to the variance of the unit’s activation, thus recovering known pruning heuristics. The closed-form optimal replacement parameters ensure that the abstracted SCM remains as faithful as possible given the constraint, minimizing the loss perturbation. This approach treats pruning as an intervention in the causal graph, and the resulting abstractions are evaluated with interchange interventions that directly test causal consistency. The mathematical formulation thus aligns the objective (preserving causal relationships) with the optimization (sparsifying mechanisms), leading to improved fidelity, especially when loss curvature varies across units.

## Architecture Onboarding
The method consists of the following components and workflow:

Component map:  
Trained Network -> Curvature Estimator -> Scoring Module -> Replacement Parameter Solver -> Compiler -> Abstracted Network -> Interchange Intervention Evaluator

Critical path:  
1. Estimate curvature (Hessian or diagonal approximation) for each unit.  
2. Compute per-unit scores using pre- and post-replacement gradients and curvature.  
3. Select units to replace based on scores (target compression).  
4. Solve closed-form replacement parameters for selected units.  
5. Compile the reduced SCM by substituting mechanisms.  
6. Evaluate interventional fidelity via interchange interventions.

Design tradeoffs:  
- Constant vs. affine replacements: constants offer higher compression but may lose linear information; affine functions preserve more expressivity at slightly higher cost.  
- Curvature estimation: full Hessian is expensive; diagonal or empirical approximations trade off accuracy for speed.  
- Closed-form replacement: guarantees optimal local update but may not capture global interactions if many units are replaced simultaneously.

Failure signatures:  
- Large gap between original and abstracted model in interchange intervention metrics (e.g., IIA near 0).  
- Jaccard index of selected units between scaling variants drops significantly, indicating instability.  
- Compression ratio does not translate into expected speedup due to overhead of curvature estimation.

First experiments:  
1. Reproduce MNIST results, varying keep ratio and comparing IIA/KL against variance-based pruning.  
2. Test robustness to scaling reparameterizations (e.g., multiplying incoming weights by a constant and dividing biases accordingly) and measure Jaccard overlap of selected units.  
3. Ablate over replacement types (constant vs. affine) and curvature estimation methods to isolate their contributions to fidelity and efficiency.

## Limitations
- Evaluation is confined to MNIST; performance on more complex datasets (CIFAR, ImageNet) or diverse architectures remains unknown.
- Only structured pruning via constant/affine replacements is considered; nonlinear abstraction mechanisms are unexplored.
- Theoretical analysis assumes a valid second-order Taylor expansion, which may break down on highly nonlinear loss landscapes or very deep networks.
- Computational overhead of curvature estimation is not reported; efficiency claims require empirical runtime validation.
