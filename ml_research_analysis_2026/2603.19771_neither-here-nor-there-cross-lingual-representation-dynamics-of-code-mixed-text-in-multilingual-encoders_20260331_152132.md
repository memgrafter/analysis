# Neither Here Nor There: Cross-Lingual Representation Dynamics of Code-Mixed Text in Multilingual Encoders

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.19771  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19771  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Trilingual post-training alignment improves Cross-Lingual Alignment Scores (CLAS) for mBERT from 14.20 to 50.97 and for XLM-R from 39.53 to 47.50, achieving balanced alignment across English, Hindi, and code-mixed text.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates how multilingual encoders internally represent Hindi-English code-mixed (Romanized) text and its relationship to monolingual English and Devanagari Hindi. Using a newly constructed trilingual parallel corpus, the authors find that standard multilingual models (e.g., mBERT, XLM-R) align English and Hindi representations strongly but leave code-mixed text weakly anchored to either. Continued pretraining on code-mixed data improves alignment between English and code-mixed text but degrades alignment between English and native Hindi, revealing a trade-off in representational space. Interpretation via token saliency shows that code-mixed inputs are processed primarily through an English-dominant semantic subspace, while native-script Hindi provides complementary uncertainty-reducing signals. Motivated by this, the authors introduce a trilingual post-training alignment objective that encourages parallel sentences across all three forms to occupy nearby embedding spaces. This yields substantial gains in Cross-Lingual Alignment Scores (CLAS) and improves cross-lingual consistency on downstream sentiment analysis and hate speech detection tasks, confirming that grounding code-mixed representations in their constituent languages benefits multilingual understanding.

## Method Summary
The authors assemble a parallel trilingual corpus (English, Devanagari Hindi, Romanized code-mixed) by merging existing datasets (CM-En Parallel, PHINC, LinCE) and using machine translation (Google Translate or IndicTrans2) to fill missing Hindi. For analysis, they compute cross-lingual similarities via layer-wise CKA/SVCCA, token saliency using Integrated Gradients, and entropy-based uncertainty reduction. To improve alignment, they apply a post-training phase that adds a trilingual alignment loss (mean cosine distance between ℓ₂-normalized [CLS] vectors of parallel sentences) to the base objective (MLM ± NSP). Training uses AdamW with tuned hyperparameters (λ=0.05, learning rate ~6e-6) and selects the best epoch via validation CLAS. Evaluation includes retrieval accuracy, CLAS, and downstream task consistency (Macro-F1 mean minus std across language variants).

## Key Results
- Code-mixed pretraining increases English–code-mixed alignment but decreases English–Hindi alignment, indicating a representational trade-off.
- Token saliency analysis reveals that multilingual encoders process code-mixed inputs through an English-dominant semantic subspace, while Hindi signals reduce uncertainty.
- The trilingual alignment objective dramatically improves CLAS (e.g., mBERT: 14.20→50.97) and yields more balanced alignment across all language pairs, with downstream sentiment and hate speech tasks showing improved cross-lingual consistency.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Trade-off from Code-Mixed Adaptation
Continued pretraining on code-mixed data reshapes the model's representation space, pulling English representations toward the code-mixed manifold because code-mixed sentences contain many English lexical items. This repositioning weakens structural similarity between English and pure Hindi representations, which remain anchored in the Devanagari script space. Evidence: After Hinglish adaptation, EN↔CM accuracy increases while EN↔HI decreases; layer-averaged scores show ordering reversal from EN↔HI > EN↔CM to EN↔CM > EN↔HI (Section 4.1, Figures 2,4).

### Mechanism 2: English-Dominant Subspace for Code-Mixing
Gradient-based attribution (Integrated Gradients) reveals that English tokens contribute more to the final [CLS] representation than Hindi tokens in code-mixed inputs. The model thus relies more on the English portion to construct meaning. Evidence: All evaluated models assign substantially higher Relative Importance (RI) scores to English tokens than Hindi tokens across layers (Section 4.2, Figure 6).

### Mechanism 3: Trilingual Alignment Loss Balances Manifolds
The alignment loss directly minimizes divergence among the three representational manifolds (EN, HI, CM) by encouraging parallel sentences to occupy nearby embedding spaces. Joint supervision prevents overfitting to any single pair, preserving EN↔HI alignment while strengthening EN↔CM and HI↔CM. Evidence: CLAS improves dramatically (e.g., mBERT 14.20→50.97, XLM-R 39.53→47.50); layer-wise CKA shows more uniform alignment (Section 4.3, Table 3).

- **Cross-lingual representation alignment:** Needed to interpret how well multilingual models unify different languages’ meanings. Quick check: What does a high CKA score between English and Hindi sentences imply about the model’s shared subspace?
- **Code-mixing and romanization:** Understanding the orthographic and lexical properties of Romanized Hindi-English mixed text is essential. Quick check: Why might a model that aligns English and Hindi well still fail to align code-mixed text with either language?
- **Representation similarity metrics (CKA, SVCCA):** These tools probe internal dynamics of neural networks. Quick check: How does CKA differ from SVCCA in terms of what aspects of the representation they compare?
- **Token-level interpretability (Integrated Gradients):** Used to attribute prediction influence to input tokens. Quick check: Why might gradient-based saliency scores be noisy for subword tokenized inputs?
- **Entropy and uncertainty in representations:** Quantifies how much a representation’s uncertainty reduces given another language’s signal. Quick check: How does linear-Gaussian regression estimate uncertainty reduction between representations?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Data ingestion -> Model loader -> Evaluation suite -> Training loop

Critical path:
1. Build the trilingual corpus (sentence triples) and split into train/test.
2. Evaluate baseline models to quantify EN–HI, EN–CM, HI–CM alignment (CKA, retrieval, entropy, RI).
3. If studying adaptation, continue pretraining on code-mixed data (Hing models) and re-evaluate.
4. Apply the trilingual alignment stage; tune λ; pick best epoch via validation CLAS.
5. Report final alignment scores (CLAS) and downstream task consistency (sentiment, hate speech).

Design tradeoffs:
- Retrieval: dot-product vs. cosine similarity; 10 vs. 100 negatives; random vs. length-matched vs. FAISS hard negatives.
- Alignment loss: weighting λ (0.05–0.15); using only parallel pairs vs. adding NSP negatives.
- Corpus size: 21K triples is modest; larger data may give stronger gains but cost more compute.
- Script handling: Romanized vs. Devanagari Hindi affects HI↔CM alignment; mixed-script pretraining preserves Devanagari link.
- Translation quality: Using machine translation for missing Hindi introduces noise; human verification is costly.

Failure signatures:
- Validation CLAS does not increase after alignment training, or EN↔HI alignment drops further.
- Downstream task consistency (mean Macro-F1 – std) worsens, especially when training on one language and testing on another.
- Perplexity on CM test set rises significantly (e.g., >50% increase), indicating language modeling degradation.
- Training loss becomes unstable or diverges when λ is too high or objectives clash.

First 3 experiments:
1. **Baseline alignment quantification:** Compute directional retrieval accuracies (EN→HI, HI→EN, EN→CM, etc.) and layer-wise CKA to establish the initial EN↔HI > EN↔CM > HI↔CM pattern.
2. **Interpretability analysis:** Produce token-level RI plots (English vs. Hindi) and entropy-reduction curves to demonstrate English-dominance and complementary Hindi signals.
3. **Trilingual alignment training:** Train with the combined objective; compare CLAS before/after; evaluate downstream tasks on CM/EN/HI test sets to confirm improved cross-lingual consistency.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Generalization to other language pairs:** The study only examines Hindi-English; other code-mixed pairs may have different mixing patterns and scripts, affecting alignment.
- **Scaling corpus size:** The current corpus is modest (21k triples); larger and more diverse code-mixed corpora may lead to stronger and more stable gains, but this is uncertain.
- **Applicability to decoder-based LLMs:** Extending the alignment approach to decoder-based multilingual LLMs (e.g., mT5, BLOOM) remains open due to different architectures and objectives.
- **Impact of automatic translations:** Machine translation may not preserve linguistic nuances (especially for sarcasm, idioms), potentially biasing alignment measurements and downstream evaluations.

## Limitations
- The translation system used for the trilingual corpus (Google Translate vs. IndicTrans2) is unspecified, risking inconsistent data quality.
- Negative sampling details for retrieval evaluation (FAISS index parameters, pooling strategy) are vague, potentially affecting CLAS reproducibility.
- Hyperparameters for alignment training (selected learning rate, validation split) are omitted, hindering exact replication.
- The parallel corpus size (21k triples) is modest, raising concerns about overfitting and generalizability.
- Token language labels for code-mixed text rely on automatic sentence alignment, which may be noisy.
- No perplexity results after alignment leave open the possibility of degraded language modeling.

## Confidence
- Code-mixed pretraining trade-off (EN↔CM ↑, EN↔HI ↓): **High** – consistent patterns across models and layers.
- English-dominant subspace for code-mixing (token saliency): **Medium** – dependent on potentially noisy token labeling from the parallel corpus.
- Trilingual alignment objective (CLAS gains, balanced alignment): **Medium** – large improvements are promising but hinge on unverified data and evaluation choices; risk of overfitting remains.

## Next Checks
1. Reconstruct the trilingual corpus using a single, well-documented translation system (e.g., IndicTrans2) and perform human evaluation on a random sample to quantify alignment accuracy and translation drift.
2. Implement the retrieval benchmark with explicit FAISS settings (metric=cosine, nprobe=10) and compare results across length-matched and hard-negative sampling to assess robustness of CLAS improvements.
3. Conduct an ablation on alignment loss weight (λ ∈ {0.01, 0.05, 0.1}) and learning rate, reporting validation CLAS curves and test perplexity, to confirm stability and absence of language modeling degradation.
