# RAIGen: Rare Attribute Identification in Text-to-Image Generative Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.06806  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06806  
- **Reference count:** 39  
- **Primary result:** Discovers and amplifies semantically rare, underrepresented attributes encoded in diffusion models beyond predefined fairness categories, validated by low Attribute Presence scores and reduced distribution shift.

## Executive Summary
RAIGen addresses a gap in bias auditing for text-to-image models: existing methods focus on majority attributes or predefined fairness categories, missing minority features that are internally encoded but rarely manifested. The core method uses Matryoshka Sparse Autoencoders (MSAE) to decompose model activations into interpretable neurons. A Minority Score ranks neurons by activation rarity and semantic distinctiveness (measured via CLIP embeddings). Discovered attributes are validated through low Attribute Presence scores and a user study. Prompt revision amplifies these attributes, reducing distribution deviation while maintaining alignment. The approach scales across architectures (Stable Diffusion, FLUX) and enables cross-model auditing.

## Method Summary
RAIGen operates in three phases. First, for a target prompt (e.g., "a photo of a doctor"), it generates 5000 images using a diffusion model and extracts intermediate bottleneck representations (e.g., 1280×8×8 tensors) at a late denoising step (t=49). Second, it trains a Matryoshka Sparse Autoencoder on these flattened vectors with two sparsity budgets (coarse k₁=2048, fine up to 20480) for 5 epochs, using BatchTopK sparsity and an auxiliary penalty. Third, on a held-out generation set, it computes per-neuron activation frequency νᵢ and CLIP-based centroid distinctiveness dᵢ, combines them into s(z)=d⊙(1−ν), prunes redundant neurons (cosine similarity threshold 0.003), and selects the top 90th percentile. An LLM (e.g., GPT-5.2) annotates the selected neurons via a structured JSON prompt. The discovered attributes are evaluated using Attribute Presence (fraction of images where the attribute appears) and amplified via prompt revision.

## Key Results
- RAIGen uncovers rare attributes beyond standard fairness categories (e.g., specific clothing, accessories, or environmental features) that are encoded but underrepresented.
- Validation shows low Attribute Presence: for Stable Diffusion v1.4, scores are 0.205 on WinoBias and 0.220 on COCO; for SDXL, 0.194 and 0.199 respectively, confirming rarity.
- Prompt revision amplifies discovered attributes, reducing distribution deviation (e.g., 0.22 vs. 0.502) while maintaining CLIP alignment (score ↑). A user study confirms human-observed rarity (<3/10 images contain the attribute).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
RAIGen works because it targets the *encoding* of attributes rather than their *presence* in outputs. MSAE decomposes high-dimensional activations into sparse, interpretable neurons that each capture a specific visual or semantic feature. The Minority Score explicitly balances two signals: activation frequency (rarity in generated outputs) and semantic distinctiveness (deviation from the average CLIP embedding of the prompt's concept). This combination surfaces attributes that the model has learned (hence encoded) but suppresses during generation due to dataset biases or safety filters. Amplification then adjusts the prompt to increase the influence of these rare neurons, reducing the suppression without straying from the original intent (measured by CLIP alignment).

## Foundational Learning
- **Matryoshka Sparse Autoencoder (MSAE):** Why needed: To interpret dense, high-dimensional bottleneck activations from diffusion models by learning a sparse, hierarchical feature decomposition. Quick check: Train on a small dataset and verify that top-activating images for a neuron show a coherent pattern (e.g., all contain a specific object).
- **Activation Frequency (νᵢ):** Why needed: To quantify how often a neuron fires across generated samples; low ν indicates rarity. Quick check: Compute a histogram of activations over 1000 samples; a neuron with ν≈0.1 fires in ~10% of samples.
- **CLIP Centroid Distinctiveness (dᵢ):** Why needed: To measure how semantically unusual a neuron's activation pattern is compared to the average concept (using CLIP embeddings). Quick check: Compute the centroid of CLIP embeddings for all images; a high dᵢ neuron's top-activating images should lie far from this centroid.
- **Minority Score (s(z)=d⊙(1−ν)):** Why needed: To jointly prioritize neurons that are both rare and semantically distinctive, avoiding noisy or dead units. Quick check: Rank neurons by s(z) and by its components separately; the combined score should yield a higher proportion of interpretable attributes.
- **Attribute Presence:** Why needed: To validate that discovered attributes are indeed underrepresented in generations. Quick check: Use a VQA model (e.g., Llama 4-Scout) to query a sample of generated images for the annotated attribute; the fraction should match the low scores reported.
- **Bottleneck Representations:** Why needed: Intermediate activations in diffusion U-Nets or transformers contain rich, distributed features before they are collapsed into final pixels. Quick check: Extract from a known layer (e.g., UNet mid-block) and visualize with dimensionality reduction (PCA/t-SNE) to see cluster structure.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Prompt -> Text-to-Image Model (SD/SDXL/FLUX) -> Bottleneck Extraction (layer-specific) -> MSAE Training (coarse+fine sparsity) -> Score Computation (νᵢ, dᵢ) -> Pruning & Ranking -> LLM Annotation -> Discovered Attributes.

**Critical path:** Image generation → activation extraction → MSAE decomposition → scoring → annotation. Any break (e.g., unstable MSAE training) halts discovery.

**Design tradeoffs:** 
- MSAE sparsity: Coarse (k₁=2048) captures holistic concepts but may miss subtle minorities; fine levels risk fragmentation.
- Annotation LLM: GPT-5.2 yields high-quality labels but is unavailable; substitutes may reduce accuracy.
- Bottleneck layer: U-Net spatial bottlenecks are well-behaved; transformer blocks (FLUX) are less interpretable.

**Failure signatures:** 
- MSAE training loss plateaus or reconstruction error high → architecture mismatch or insufficient data.
- Top-ranked neurons show incoherent or noisy top-activating images → scoring overemphasizes frequency; increase weight on distinctiveness.
- Attribute Presence scores unexpectedly high → generated images not diverse enough, or MSAE trained on mismatched distribution.

**First experiments:**
1. **Layer probing:** Extract bottlenecks from multiple candidate blocks (e.g., UNet mid, output of attention layers) for 100 images. Visualize top-activating neurons; select the layer yielding most semantically coherent patterns.
2. **Score component ablation:** Compute rankings using (1−ν) only, dᵢ only, and combined s(z). Compare the proportion of neurons that a human annotator judges as interpretable (target >70%).
3. **Small-scale stability test:** Train MSAE on 1000 vectors for 1 epoch. Check that reconstruction MSE decreases and that top neurons activate on meaningful features. If unstable, reduce learning rate or increase sparsity budget.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can RAIGen identify entirely absent social groups (e.g., non-binary people) rather than just underrepresented ones? The method only captures encoded attributes; groups missing from the representation space will not be surfaced. Hybrid approaches with external knowledge (LLMs, taxonomies) are needed.
2. Can LLM-based and representation-based bias audits be unified into a single framework? Preliminary complementarity is shown (e.g., OpenBias finds near-zero presence attributes, RAIGen finds ~0.2), but no integration methodology exists. A hybrid pipeline where LLM-generated candidates guide RAIGen's neuron selection could improve coverage.
3. How does MSAE granularity affect minority attribute discovery, and can optimal sparsity be automated? The paper uses coarse-level neurons (k₁=2048), arguing finer levels fragment concepts, but this trade-off is not systematically analyzed. Evidence from evaluating across sparsity depths with human interpretability scores is required.
4. Why are transformer-based diffusion models (e.g., FLUX.1-schnell) harder to interpret via SAE than U-Net models, and can better hook points improve interpretability? The hypothesis is that transformer activations are less spatially grounded, but this is speculative. Probing all transformer blocks and MLP streams across models is needed to derive design principles.

## Limitations
- **Code dependency:** MSAE training relies on Bussmann et al.'s (2025) implementation, which may be unavailable or incompatible.
- **Metric sensitivity:** Minority Score thresholds (cosine 0.003, 90th percentile) and annotation LLM (GPT-5.2) are not ablated; substitutions may alter discovered attributes.
- **Implementation ambiguity:** Bottleneck extraction details (exact UNet block, spatial resolution) are inferred, not explicitly specified per model variant.

## Next Checks
1. **MSAE Training Stability Check:** Train on a small subset (1000 vectors) and visualize top-activating images for coarse neurons. Expect coherent, spatially consistent patterns; fragmented activations indicate instability.
2. **Distinctiveness Metric Ablation:** Recompute rankings using only frequency (1−ν) vs. only distinctiveness (dᵢ). Compare interpretability rates; if frequency-only yields high uninterpretable rate, distinctiveness is essential.
3. **Bottleneck Layer Verification:** For SD v1.4, extract activations from multiple candidate blocks (e.g., mid-block, attention outputs). The layer producing the most diverse and semantically meaningful neuron activations (by manual inspection) is likely the intended bottleneck.
