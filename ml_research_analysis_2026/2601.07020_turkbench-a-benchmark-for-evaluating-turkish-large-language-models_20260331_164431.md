# TurkBench: A Benchmark for Evaluating Turkish Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.07020
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.07020
- Reference count: 37
- Primary result: Introduces TurkBench with 8,151 curated Turkish samples across 21 tasks; leaderboard evaluation of 27 models reveals all struggle significantly on culturally specific tasks.

## Executive Summary
TurkBench addresses the lack of comprehensive, culturally-aware benchmarks for Turkish large language models. The benchmark comprises 8,151 samples spanning 21 tasks in six categories, sourced from authentic Turkish materials (national exams, academic texts) and validated by human experts for correctness, grammar, and cultural sensitivity. Evaluation uses exact-match accuracy for multiple-choice tasks and an LLM-as-a-Judge approach for open-ended tasks. Results from 27 models show that while larger models generally outperform smaller ones, all exhibit significant weaknesses on culturally specific tasks such as Turkish General Knowledge and idioms. Systematic prompt optimization in Turkish yields substantial gains (e.g., +13.71 points on Turkish General Knowledge), and difficulty calibration underscores the importance of balanced sampling for reliable assessment.

## Method Summary
TurkBench was constructed by collecting authentic Turkish materials—including national exams, academic texts, and native literature—avoiding translation or synthetic data. Each sample underwent human validation across six criteria: answer correctness, grammatical correctness, cohesion/coherence, comprehensibility, technical term usage, and cultural common-sense, with domain experts ensuring linguistic and cultural appropriateness. For evaluation, multiple-choice tasks use exact-match to gold labels, while open-ended tasks employ GPT-4o-mini as an LLM-as-a-Judge. The study also implemented metaprompting for prompt optimization and difficulty calibration experiments. Data, code, and a public leaderboard are hosted on HuggingFace, with automated evaluation via LM-EVALUATIONHARNESS and DeepEval.

## Key Results
- Larger models generally outperform smaller ones, but all evaluated models struggle significantly on culturally specific tasks (Turkish General Knowledge, idioms).
- Systematic Turkish prompt optimization yields notable gains, e.g., +13.71 points on Turkish General Knowledge.
- Balanced difficulty calibration is critical for reliable model ranking and assessment.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Culturally Grounded Data Collection
Claim: Culturally grounded data collection significantly improves benchmark relevance for Turkish LLMs.
Mechanism: By sourcing directly from Turkish national exams, academic texts, and native literature, tasks reflect authentic linguistic structures and cultural knowledge that translated benchmarks cannot capture.
Core assumption: Models trained on predominantly English data lack exposure to Turkish-specific phenomena; a culturally aligned evaluation exposes these gaps.
Evidence: The dataset is constructed entirely from Turkish sources using expert-curated material, explicitly avoiding adapted or synthetic data.

### Mechanism 2: Multi-criteria Human Validation
Claim: Multi-criteria human validation guarantees high data quality.
Mechanism: A structured pipeline applies six criteria (answer correctness, grammatical correctness, cohesion/coherence, comprehensibility, technical term usage, cultural common-sense) via domain experts, ensuring each sample is linguistically sound and culturally appropriate.
Core assumption: Automated checks cannot reliably assess nuanced cultural and linguistic aspects; human judgment is indispensable.
Evidence: All data are validated by human experts, with each task curated by relevant specialists (e.g., Sociology experts, Turkish Literature experts).

### Mechanism 3: Tailored Evaluation Methods
Claim: Tailored evaluation methods (exact-match for multiple-choice, LLM-as-a-Judge for open-ended tasks) yield reliable, scalable assessment.
Mechanism: Objective tasks use exact match to gold labels; subjective tasks employ GPT-4o-mini as a judge model to compare outputs against references, avoiding extensive human evaluation.
Core assumption: LLM-as-a-Judge can approximate human judgments for Turkish open-ended tasks; using a consistent judge reduces bias.
Evidence: The evaluation adopts an LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm with GPT-4o-mini as the judge model, following established practices from prior work.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Data Collection Module → Validation Pipeline → Prompt Engineering Module → Evaluation Engine (Exact-Match Scorer, LLM-as-a-Judge, DeepEval metrics) → Leaderboard Backend (Gradio UI, automated GPU allocation, results storage)

**Critical path:**
1. Define task categories and source materials.
2. Collect and preprocess raw data.
3. Conduct human validation.
4. Develop and select prompts via metaprompting.
5. Implement evaluation scripts for each metric.
6. Deploy leaderboard infrastructure.
7. Run baseline evaluations and calibrate difficulty.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Scale vs. Cultural Depth: Opted for native curation over large-scale translated datasets, yielding deeper cultural coverage but fewer tasks.
- Automatic vs. Human Evaluation: Used LLM-as-a-Judge for scalability, accepting potential judge bias.
- Open Submissions vs. Standardization: Enabled online submissions but enforced uniform inference conditions to reduce variance.

**Failure signatures:**
- Sudden universal drop on culturally specific tasks: possible data staleness or leakage.
- High variance in LLM-as-a-Judge scores for the same model: judge instability.
- Low inter-annotator agreement during validation: quality inconsistency.
- Repeated leaderboard submission failures: GPU allocation or environment mismatches.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Baseline evaluation across diverse model sizes to establish performance spreads.
2. Prompt optimization via metaprompting (e.g., test variants on Gemma-3-1B, select best Turkish prompts).
3. Difficulty calibration (e.g., bin Turkish General Knowledge by difficulty, assess impact on model ranking).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: Why do Turkish LLMs exhibit significantly lower performance on culturally specific tasks such as Turkish General Knowledge and idioms compared to other benchmark categories?  
  Basis in paper: The results section notes that "all struggle significantly on culturally specific tasks" and Table 5 shows consistently low scores on Turkish General Knowledge and Metaphors & Idioms across models, but no causal analysis is provided.  
  Why unresolved: The paper documents the performance gap but does not investigate underlying reasons, such as deficiencies in pretraining data cultural diversity, model architecture constraints, or task design issues.  
  What evidence would resolve it: Ablation studies varying the cultural content of training data, probing experiments to identify representational gaps, or detailed error analysis of model failures on culturally rooted items.

- **Open Question 2**: How does the choice of GPT-4o-mini as the LLM-as-a-Judge impact the reliability and cultural sensitivity of evaluations for Turkish open-ended tasks?  
  Basis in paper: The limitations state that the judge model "may have its own inherent biases, stylistic preferences, or gaps in understanding deep cultural nuances specific to Turkish," but no validation is performed.  
  Why unresolved: The study relies on GPT-4o-mini without assessing its alignment with Turkish cultural norms or comparing its judgments with human evaluators, leaving evaluation trustworthiness uncertain.  
  What evidence would resolve it: Correlation studies between GPT-4o-mini and human judgments on Turkish tasks, testing alternative judge models (including Turkish-native ones), and bias audits of the judge's outputs.

- **Open Question 3**: To what extent can Turkish LLMs handle informal language registers, regional dialects, and social media slang that are absent from TurkBench's formal data sources?  
  Basis in paper: The limitations explicitly state that the benchmark's data "may not fully capture a model’s proficiency in handling informal language, regional dialects, or the dynamic slang observed on social media" due to its focus on formal contexts.  
  Why unresolved: TurkBench does not assess performance on everyday, evolving language use, which is critical for real-world applications but remains unevaluated.  
  What evidence would resolve it: Construction of supplementary benchmark subsets targeting informal/dialectal Turkish, systematic evaluation on social media corpora, or user-based testing with native speakers.

- **Open Question 4**: What culturally specific biases and toxicities persist in Turkish LLMs, and how can future benchmarks more effectively capture these nuances?  
  Basis in paper: The conclusion calls for "the development of similar benchmarks in the ethical evaluation of large language models in low-resource settings," implying current bias and toxicity tasks may be insufficiently tailored to Turkish culture.  
  Why unresolved: Existing tasks like Bias Detection and Toxicity Detection are expert-curated but may not fully represent the spectrum of Turkish societal norms, stereotypes, and emerging harmful patterns.  
  What evidence would resolve it: Iterative benchmark expansion with diverse Turkish sociologists and community input, longitudinal tracking of bias/toxicity trends, and comparative studies across different cultural framing of the same concepts.

## Limitations
- Open-ended task scores rely on withheld GPT-4o-mini judge prompts, preventing full reproduction of results.
- Evaluation environment details (GPU specs, exact model revision hashes) are undocumented, introducing potential variance in replication attempts.
- No inter-annotator agreement metrics are reported for the human validation pipeline, raising concerns about data quality consistency.
