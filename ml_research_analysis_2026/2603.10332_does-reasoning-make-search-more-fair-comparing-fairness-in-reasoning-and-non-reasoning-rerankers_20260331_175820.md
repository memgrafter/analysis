# Does Reasoning Make Search More Fair? Comparing Fairness in Reasoning and Non-Reasoning Rerankers

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.10332  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10332  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: Reasoning rerankers neither enhance nor harm fairness; AWRF@10 remains stable (~0.33–0.35) across models, while relevance (nDCG@10) varies independently.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether reasoning-capable rerankers (e.g., those generating chain-of-thought justifications) improve fairness in search results compared to traditional non-reasoning rerankers. Using the TREC 2022 Fair Ranking dataset, the authors evaluate six rerankers (three reasoning, three non-reasoning) on fairness (AWRF@10) and relevance (nDCG@10) across eight demographic attributes. The results show that reasoning does not affect fairness: all rerankers achieve similar AWRF scores regardless of their reasoning capability. Fairness is constrained by the demographic diversity of the initial retrieval candidate pool, and certain attributes (e.g., subject geography) consistently underperform due to lack of explicit textual information. Query rewriting to natural language improves relevance but not fairness. The study concludes that current rerankers, reasoning or not, are limited in addressing fairness issues and require explicit fairness-aware training or retrieval strategies.

## Method Summary
The study uses the TREC 2022 Fair Ranking Track dataset, which consists of English Wikipedia articles with relevance labels and demographic annotations for eight attributes (e.g., gender, language, subject geography). For each query (both original keyword and rewritten natural language versions), initial retrievals are generated via BM25, dense retrieval with Qwen3-Embedding-8B, and their RRF fusion. The top 500 documents from each initial ranking are then reranked using six off-the-shelf rerankers: three reasoning models (Rank1, Qwen3-Reranker, ReasonRank) and three non-reasoning models (MonoT5, RankZephyr, RankLLaMA). The primary evaluation metrics are nDCG@10 for relevance and AWRF@10 (Average Woolley-Fairness) for group fairness, which measures how well the exposure distribution in the top-10 matches a target combining relevance and population statistics. Statistical significance is assessed via paired t-tests and equivalence tests (TOST) with Holm-Bonferroni correction. Additionally, an oracle ranking with near-perfect relevance is used to isolate fairness behavior.

## Key Results
- **Reasoning does not improve fairness:** All six rerankers (reasoning and non-reasoning) achieve statistically equivalent AWRF@10 scores (within 0.33–0.35) across all retrieval settings.
- **Fairness is attribute-dependent:** Subject geography consistently yields the lowest AWRF (around 0.82–0.83), while languages and alphabetic attributes achieve higher fairness (~0.94–0.96).
- **Query rewriting boosts relevance but not fairness:** Natural language queries significantly increase nDCG@10 (p<0.05) but leave AWRF@10 unchanged.
- **Rerankers preserve input pool fairness:** Oracle experiments show rerankers maintain the fairness profile of the initial candidate pool and cannot overcome representation gaps even with perfect relevance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Rerankers are constrained by initial candidate pool diversity
Rerankers only reorder the top N documents (e.g., 500) from an initial retrieval; they do not retrieve new documents. Therefore, the fairness of the reranked list is fundamentally limited by the demographic diversity present in that initial pool. If certain groups are underrepresented in the candidate set, the reranker cannot introduce them—it can only expose or hide what is already exists. Evidence: The paper finds that all rerankers demonstrate equivalent AWRF@10 to the initial retrieval (section 4.1), and oracle experiments show they preserve the oracle ranking's fairness profile (section 4.2). Break condition: If the reranker also performed retrieval or if the initial retrieval were optimized for diverse candidate pools, this constraint might be alleviated.

### Mechanism 2: Reasoning rerankers lack fairness-oriented training
Current reasoning rerankers are trained solely on relevance judgments (e.g., MS MARCO) without any fairness-specific loss function, prompt instruction, or reinforcement signal. Their "reasoning" steps (chain-of-thought generation) are merely a stylistic output format and do not inherently encode social or fairness considerations. Thus, they optimize for relevance matching, not equitable exposure. Evidence: The paper explicitly states that none of the rerankers were trained to optimize for fairness (section 4.2). Break condition: Introduction of fairness-aware training (e.g., multi-task loss with fairness regularization) or prompts that explicitly ask the model to consider demographic balance could engage the reasoning process toward fairness goals.

### Mechanism 3: Missing textual information for certain attributes
Demographic attributes like subject geography are often not explicitly mentioned or easily inferable from document text. Rerankers process only the provided query and document text; if the attribute is absent or ambiguous, the model cannot condition its ranking on it. This representational gap is a data limitation, not a model architectural one. Evidence: The paper reports consistently lower AWRF for subject geography across all models (Table 4) and notes that this is likely due to the unavailability of the information in text (section 4.2). Break condition: Providing explicit demographic metadata (e.g., Wikidata location tags) as part of the input could make the signal accessible to rerankers.

## Foundational Learning
- **Exposure-based group fairness (AWRF):** AWRF (Average Woolley-Fairness) measures how well the exposure distribution across demographic groups in a ranking matches a target distribution (often combining relevance and population statistics). It is position-weighted, emphasizing top positions. *Why needed:* It is the primary fairness metric used in the paper; scores near 1.0 indicate perfect alignment with the target, while lower scores indicate disparity. *Quick check:* If a ranking perfectly matches the target group distribution in its top positions, what would its AWRF be? (Answer: 1.0)
- **Candidate pool in reranking:** The set of documents (e.g., top 500) from an initial retrieval that a reranker reorders. *Why needed:* The paper's core insight is that reranker fairness is bounded by the diversity of this pool. *Quick check:* What two factors limit a reranker's ability to improve fairness? (Answer: 1) Diversity of the initial candidate pool, 2) Model's own ranking logic.)
- **Reasoning vs. non-reasoning reranker architectures:** Reasoning rerankers generate intermediate natural language steps (chain-of-thought) before producing a relevance score; non-reasoning rerankers directly output a score or ranking without intermediate textual output. *Why needed:* The paper compares these two types to see if reasoning inherently improves fairness. *Quick check:* What is a concrete example of a "reasoning step" in a reranker like Rank1? (Answer: A generated textual justification explaining why a document is relevant before outputting a score.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Initial Retriever (BM25, Qwen3-Embedding-8B, RRF fusion) -> Reranker (Rank1, Qwen3-Reranker, ReasonRank, MonoT5, RankZephyr, RankLLaMA) -> Evaluation (nDCG@10, AWRF@10) -> Statistical Engine (paired t-test, TOST, Holm-Bonferroni).

**Critical path:** For each query setting (keywords or rewritten), generate initial rankings using one of the retrieval methods. Take the top 500 documents and feed them to each reranker to produce a new top-10 ranking. Compute nDCG@10 and AWRF@10 for each query. Aggregate across queries and run statistical tests between models. Repeat with an oracle initial ranking (nDCG≈0.9) to isolate fairness behavior.

**Design tradeoffs:** 
- Candidate pool size (500) balances compute and coverage; deeper diverse docs might be missed.
- AWRF@10 focuses on top exposure; fairness deeper in the ranking is ignored.
- Query rewriting improves relevance but requires an additional LLM call and prompt engineering.

**Failure signatures:** 
- High variance in AWRF across attributes for a single model (e.g., >0.1 gap) indicates attribute-specific insensitivity.
- Consistently low subject geography AWRF (<0.83 even in oracle) signals a fundamental representational gap in the corpus.
- Non-significant AWRF differences between reasoning and non-reasoning models despite large effect sizes may indicate insufficient statistical power.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Reproduce Table 2 (RQ1): Run all six rerankers on BM25 with keywords vs. rewritten queries. Verify that nDCG@10 increases significantly (paired t-test, p<0.05) while AWRF@10 shows no significant change (TOST equivalence, δ=0.05).
2. Oracle fairness isolation (RQ2): Use the provided oracle initial ranking (nDCG@10≈0.886). Rerank with all models. Confirm that all rerankers achieve near-perfect nDCG@10 (≥0.923) and that AWRF@10 remains below the oracle's 0.352 with no significant reasoning advantage.
3. Attribute breakdown (RQ3): Compute per-attribute M1 (≈AWRF when nDCG≈1) from Table 4. Identify that subject geography is the worst across all models (e.g., 0.820 for ReasonRank, 0.827 for MonoT5), while languages/alphabetic are best (>0.90). This confirms attribute-specific fairness gaps independent of reasoning.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How does query formulation (keyword vs. natural language rewrites) impact fairness (AWRF) and relevance (nDCG) in reasoning and non-reasoning rerankers? The paper shows rewritten queries boost relevance without affecting fairness but does not explain why.
- **Open Question 2:** Do reasoning rerankers exhibit inherently different fairness characteristics compared to non-reasoning rerankers when controlling for relevance? Oracle experiments show minimal AWRF differences, but the results are inconclusive due to limited model diversity and potential confounders.
- **Open Question 3:** Why do certain demographic attributes (e.g., subject geography) consistently achieve lower fairness scores across all rerankers? The paper attributes this to underrepresentation in text but does not empirically isolate whether the issue is data sparsity, metadata absence, or model incapacity to leverage implicit cues.
- **Open Question 4:** Can reasoning rerankers be explicitly steered (via training or prompting) to improve fairness without sacrificing relevance? Current models are relevance-only trained and show no spontaneous fairness gains; this remains an unexplored direction.

## Limitations
- **Dataset scope:** Experiments are limited to TREC 2022 Fair Ranking (English Wikipedia), which may not generalize to other domains or languages.
- **Fixed candidate pool:** All rerankers re-rank the top-500 documents; fairness improvements might be possible with a more diverse initial pool.
- **Query rewrite details:** The exact prompt and random keyword selection for query rewriting are unspecified, hindering exact replication.
- **Model availability:** Some reranker checkpoints (e.g., Rank1, ReasonRank) may not be publicly released, limiting reproducibility.
- **Metric coverage:** AWRF@10 only measures top-10 exposure; fairness deeper in the ranking is unexamined.

## Confidence
- **C1 (Reasoning rerankers neither improve nor harm fairness):** High. Consistent AWRF across six models and retrieval settings, supported by equivalence tests.
- **C2 (Reranker fairness bounded by initial candidate pool diversity):** Medium. Oracle and preservation results imply the constraint but lack causal manipulation.
- **C3 (Missing text information for attributes like geography causes fairness gaps):** Medium. Geography consistently shows lower AWRF; the hypothesis is plausible but not experimentally isolated.
- **C4 (Query rewriting improves relevance but not fairness):** High. Significant nDCG gains (p<0.05) with stable AWRF.
- **C5 (Rerankers preserve the fairness profile of the input ranking):** Medium. Oracle experiment demonstrates preservation; needs testing across varied input fairness levels.

## Next Checks
1. **Exact replication of query rewriting:** Implement the “title + 4 random keywords” prompt with a fixed random seed; confirm that nDCG@10 and AWRF@10 match Table 2.
2. **Checkpoint verification:** For each reranker, run inference on a shared set of topics (e.g., 50); compare nDCG and AWRF to reported values to ensure reproducibility.
3. **Candidate‑pool diversity ablation:** Create initial rankings with controlled demographic diversity (e.g., via biased retrieval or query expansion). Test whether rerankers’ AWRF changes with pool fairness, directly probing Mechanism 1.
