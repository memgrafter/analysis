# Task-Conditioned Routing Signatures in Sparse Mixture-of-Experts Transformers
## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.11114
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11114
- **Reference count:** 1
- **Primary result:** MoE routing patterns encode statistically discriminative task information, with routing signatures enabling 92.5% ± 6.1% four-way task classification.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether expert routing in sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) transformers is sensitive to the category of the input task. It introduces "routing signatures"—concatenated, layer-normalized expert activation distributions—as a compact representation for comparing prompts. Using the OLMoE-1B-7B model on 80 prompts across code, math, story, and factual tasks, it finds that within-category routing similarity is significantly higher than across-category similarity. A logistic regression classifier on these signatures alone achieves high accuracy in predicting the task category, demonstrating that MoE routing contains measurable, task-sensitive structure beyond what is needed for load balancing.

## Method Summary
The authors collect routing traces by running 80 curated prompts (32 tokens each) through the OLMoE-1B-7B model, logging which experts are activated at each layer and token position. These traces are aggregated into per-layer expert activation count vectors, which are normalized to sum to 1 per layer. The normalized vectors across all layers are concatenated to form a high-dimensional "routing signature" for each prompt. Similarity between signatures is computed as the mean layer-wise cosine similarity. To probe task separability, they train a logistic regression classifier on the signature vectors to predict task labels using stratified 5-fold cross-validation. Two baselines are used for interpretation: a permutation baseline that randomly shuffles expert indices within each layer (destroying structure but preserving sparsity), and a load-balancing baseline that simulates uniform random expert selection while preserving per-layer total activation counts.

## Key Results
- Within-category routing similarity (0.8435 ± 0.0879) significantly exceeds across-category similarity (0.6225 ± 0.1687), with a large effect size (Cohen's d = 1.44).
- A logistic regression classifier on routing signatures alone achieves 92.5% ± 6.1% cross-validated accuracy on four-way task classification.
- Task separation, measured by Cohen's d, strengthens in deeper layers, peaking around layer 13, suggesting that routing specialization emerges gradually through the network.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Task-conditioned differences in hidden-state distributions lead to distinguishable expert activation patterns (routing signatures).
- **Mechanism:** The router computes expert probabilities as a function of token hidden states (e.g., `p(e|h) = softmax(W_rh)`). If prompts from different tasks produce systematically different distributions of hidden states across tokens/layers, the resulting softmax outputs and subsequent top-k selections will differ in aggregate. Over many tokens, this yield distinct normalized activation distributions (signatures) per task.
- **Core assumption:** Task category influences the distribution of internal representations sufficiently to affect router decisions. (Assumption: The router is sensitive enough to translate these distributional shifts into activation differences.)
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "routing patterns contain measurable, statistically discriminative task information."
  - [section 3.5] Proposition 1: "If hidden-state distributions differ across tasks, then routing distributions differ across tasks."
  - [corpus] Neighbor papers (e.g., "Input Domain Aware MoE") explore routing sensitivity to input domains, providing some external corroboration, though not direct evidence for this specific model/task setup.
- **Break condition:** If hidden-state distributions for different tasks are statistically indistinguishable (e.g., via centrality or distributional tests), the mechanism fails.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Task-specific routing specialization strengthens in deeper layers as representations become more abstract and semantically differentiated.
- **Mechanism:** Early layers process local, lexical features that may be通用 across tasks, leading to similar routing. As the transformer processes context through successive layers, hidden states increasingly encapsulate task-specific reasoning patterns (e.g., code syntax vs. narrative flow). The router, operating on these deeper states, activates different expert subsets in a way that amplifies task separation.
- **Core assumption:** Deeper layers in transformers develop more task-specific representations; the router’s sensitivity to these representations does not saturate or invert.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "task structure becomes increasingly apparent in deeper layers."
  - [section 5.3] Figure 3: Cohen’s d increases from early to late layers, peaking ~layer 13.
  - [corpus] Weak direct evidence; neighboring work on MoE interpretability often focuses on overall specialization rather than layer-wise dynamics.
- **Break condition:** If layer-wise similarity analysis shows no monotonic increase in task separation, or if early layers already show maximum separation.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Routing signatures, as a compressed summary of expert usage across layers, constitute a linearly separable feature space for task identity.
- **Mechanism:** The concatenated, layer-normalized activation counts form a high-dimensional vector. The empirical covariance structure of these vectors across prompts is such that prompts from the same task occupy a connected subspace that is well-separated from subspaces of other tasks. A simple linear classifier (logistic regression) can exploit this separation because the signal is strong and not overly entangled.
- **Core assumption:** The mapping from (task category → routing signature distribution) is approximately linear or yields linearly separable clusters in the signature space. (Assumption: Task effects dominate other sources of variation like prompt length or style.)
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "logistic regression classifier on routing signatures alone achieves 92.5% ± 6.1% cross-validated accuracy."
  - [section 5.5] Classification results with macro F1 0.93.
  - [section 5.4] PCA projection shows distinct clusters.
  - [corpus] Limited; related work on MoE classifier probes is sparse. Neighbors focus on routing optimization, not linear separability of task from routing traces.
- **Break condition:** If a linear classifier fails to achieve above-chance accuracy (e.g., <30% for 4-way) on held-out prompts, the claimed linear separability is false.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) Routing**
  - Why needed here: The entire analysis depends on understanding how tokens are assigned to experts via a learned router (top-k selection), and how activation traces are collected.
  - Quick check question: What does the router compute to select experts for a token at a given layer?
- **Concept: Conditional Computation**
  - Why needed here: MoE routing is a form of conditional computation; the paper’s thesis is that the *condition* (task) shapes the routing pattern.
  - Quick check question: Why does conditional computation improve parameter efficiency compared to dense models?
- **Concept: Normalization and Similarity Metrics**
  - Why needed here: Routing signatures require per-layer normalization (to create distributions) and a metric (cosine similarity) to compare them meaningfully, controlling for prompt length and absolute activation counts.
  - Quick check question: Why is cosine similarity used for comparing layer-wise expert distributions instead of Euclidean distance on raw counts?
- **Concept: Statistical Baselines for Interpretation**
  - Why needed here: To claim task structure exists, one must rule out that observed similarity patterns arise from sparsity or load-balancing constraints alone. The permutation and load-balancing baselines serve this purpose.
  - Quick check question: What does the "load-balancing baseline" simulate, and why is it necessary?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  1. **OLMoE Model**: 16-layer transformer with 64 experts per MoE layer, top-k=8 routing.
  2. **Router Module**: Linear layer + softmax per token to produce expert probabilities.
  3. **Trace Recorder**: Instrumentation to log `(layer, token position, expert index)` during forward pass.
  4. **Signature Engine**: Post-processor that aggregates traces into per-layer expert distribution vectors, concatenates, and normalizes.
  5. **Similarity Engine**: Computes mean layer-wise cosine similarity between two signatures.
  6. **Classifier**: Logistic regression (or other linear model) trained on signature vectors to predict task label.
- **Critical path:** Prompt input → model forward pass with trace logging → for each prompt: aggregate traces by (layer, expert) → normalize per layer → concatenate into signature vector → for analysis: compute pairwise similarities or feed into classifier.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Sparsity (k) vs. Signal Strength*: Lower k (higher sparsity) may increase potential for task specialization but increases noise; higher k may blur distinctions.
  - *Signature dimensionality*: Using all layers (L*E) preserves info but may be high-dimensional; PCA/t-SNE used for visualization but classification used full vectors.
  - *Baseline design*: Permutation baseline destroys structure but keeps sparsity; load-balancing baseline keeps expert totals but randomizes assignments—both needed to isolate task effect.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Within-category similarity ≈ across-category similarity (no diagonal dominance in heatmap).
  - Classification accuracy near chance (25% for 4-way) despite sufficient data.
  - No clear layer-wise trend (Cohen’s d flat or decreasing with depth).
  - Load-balancing baseline similarity matches empirical within-category similarity (suggesting effect is just balancing).
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Trace Collection & Signature Sanity Check**: Run a small set of prompts (2 per category), collect traces, compute signatures, and manually verify that signatures are normalized (sum to 1 per layer) and that different prompts produce distinct vectors.
  2. **Baseline Comparison**: Compute empirical within- and across-category similarities. Then generate permutation and load-balancing baseline distributions (via resampling/simulation). Confirm empirical within > load-balance > across (as in Figure 2). If ordering fails, re-examine trace collection or category definitions.
  3. **Linear Separability Probe**: With the full 80-prompt set, perform a single train/test split (e.g., 70/30) of a logistic regression classifier on signatures. Check accuracy and coefficients. If accuracy is low (<70%), check for data leakage in normalization (ensure fit on train only), class imbalance, or insufficient signal.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Does the observed task-conditioned routing structure generalize across different MoE architectures (e.g., Switch, Expert Choice) and model scales, or is it specific to OLMoE?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The Limitations section states: "It evaluates only one model and one prompt dataset."
- **Why unresolved:** Without replication on other models, it is unclear whether the clustering effect is a universal property of sparse MoE routing or an artifact of OLMoE’s specific design, router implementation, or training regime.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Repeating the routing signature analysis on multiple open MoE models of varying sizes and routing strategies using a consistent prompt set; consistent within-category similarity across models would confirm generality.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Are routing signatures causally involved in task performance, or merely correlated? Can targeted manipulation of expert selection patterns alter model behavior on specific tasks?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The Future Work section lists "causal intervention on experts" as a next step.
- **Why unresolved:** The current study demonstrates correlation between routing and task categories but does not establish whether routing patterns play a functional role or are epiphenomenal.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablation experiments where specific experts are silenced or biased, measuring changes in both routing signatures and task accuracy; a causal link would be indicated if perturbations reliably degrade or alter performance on associated tasks.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What underlying mechanisms cause task-separation in routing to strengthen in deeper layers? Is this driven by accumulating semantic differentiation in hidden states, layer-specific router adaptation, or interactions with non-MoE components?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Section 5.3 reports that "task separation... peaks around layer 13" and "suggests that routing specialization emerges gradually," implying but not specifying the cause.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper correlates depth with signal strength but does not probe whether the router’s inputs (hidden states) become more task-distinct, whether later routers are more specialized, or whether other architectural factors are responsible.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Joint analysis of per-layer hidden-state distributions (e.g., via linear probes) alongside routing; or comparing router weights/gradients across layers to identify where and how task-sensitive routing emerges.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What is the quantitative mutual information between routing signatures and task identity, and how does it compare to the information contained in final-layer representations or output distributions?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 3.6 notes: "We do not estimate mutual information directly... but our classification results provide strong evidence that routing signatures encode statistically extractable task information."
- **Why unresolved:** Classification accuracy implies high I(S;T), but the exact information quantity and its relationship to other representational levels remain unknown, limiting theoretical understanding of routing as a conditional computation signal.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Direct estimation of mutual information bounds (e.g., using MIME or k-nearest neighbor methods) for routing signatures versus task labels, alongside similar estimates for final hidden states; comparing these values would clarify routing’s relative informational role.

## Limitations
- Exact prompt texts are unknown; reconstruction may alter results.
- Crucial implementation details (load-balancing baseline algorithm, logistic regression hyperparameters) are missing.
- Model-specific configuration (number of MoE layers, expert count) must be inferred; errors affect signature dimensionality.
- Small sample size (80 prompts) relative to high dimensionality (1024) risks overfitting; reported CV accuracy could be optimistic.

## Confidence
- **Task-conditioned routing similarity (within > across):** Medium (statistically strong but susceptible to prompt/baseline discrepancies).
- **Linear separability of signatures (92.5% accuracy):** Medium (small‑N CV results may be unstable; unknown regularization).
- **Deepening task separation across layers:** High (direct empirical trend, robust to minor implementation variations).
- **Baselines ruling out load-balancing confound:** Medium (ordering supports claim, but baseline specs unspecified).
