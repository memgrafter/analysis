# Fast Catch-Up, Late Switching: Optimal Batch Size Scheduling via Functional Scaling Laws
**Quick Facts**
- arXiv ID: 2602.14208
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14208
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: For hard tasks (s ≤ 1 – 1/β), optimal training uses a small batch for most steps and switches late to a large batch, achieving lower final loss via the fast catch-up effect.

**Executive Summary**
This paper addresses the critical but theoretically under‑explored problem of batch size scheduling (BSS) in large‑scale deep learning. It introduces the Functional Scaling Law (FSL) framework to analytically characterize the expected excess risk of one‑pass SGD with a fixed learning rate. The analysis reveals that the optimal BSS under a given data budget hinges on task difficulty, quantified by two exponents: the source exponent *s* and the capacity exponent *β*. For easy tasks (*s* > 1 – 1/β), the optimal schedule increases batch size monotonically. For hard tasks (*s* ≤ 1 – 1/β), the optimal policy keeps the batch minimal throughout most of training and switches to a large batch only near the end—a strategy made viable by the **fast catch‑up effect**, whereby after switching the loss rapidly aligns with the constant large‑batch trajectory. These predictions are validated across extensive LLM pretraining experiments, including models up to 1.1B parameters and 1T tokens, where late‑switch schedules consistently outperform constant‑batch and early‑switch baselines.

**Method Summary**
The authors evaluate their theory in both small‑ and large‑scale settings. Small‑scale experiments employ LLaMA‑style dense models (50M–492M parameters) trained on the C4 dataset with a T5 tokenizer (~32k vocab). Large‑scale experiments use Mixture‑of‑Experts models (1B, 1.1B) on a private dataset. Training uses AdamW (β₁=0.9, β₂=0.95, weight decay=0.1, gradient clipping=1.0) with a constant learning rate (e.g., 1e‑3) and sequence length 256; small‑scale runs additionally include a 1000‑step linear warmup. Four batch schedules are compared: constant small (B₁), constant large (B₂), early‑switch (large from an early stage), and late‑switch (small for the majority then switch). All runs process exactly a predetermined total token budget *D*, and validation loss is monitored throughout. The late‑switch schedule achieves the lowest final loss, and after switching its loss curve quickly merges with that of the constant large‑batch run, confirming the fast catch‑up effect.

**Key Results**
- The optimal batch size schedule structure depends sharply on task difficulty (exponents *s* and *β*): monotonically increasing for easy tasks (*s* > 1 – 1/β), late‑switch for hard tasks (*s* ≤ 1 – 1/β).
- For hard tasks, maintaining a minimal batch for most of training before switching to a large batch avoids data waste and leverages the fast catch‑up effect, where loss rapidly aligns with the constant large‑batch trajectory.
- Extensive LLM pretraining experiments (up to 1.1B parameters, 1T tokens) confirm that late‑switch schedules achieve lower validation loss than constant‑batch and early‑switch baselines.

**Why This Works (Mechanism)**
The optimal batch size schedule derived from the Functional Scaling Law hinges on two distinct regimes determined by the exponents *s* and *β*.

For **hard tasks** (*s* ≤ 1 – 1/β), the optimal schedule maintains the smallest batch *B*_min for the majority of training and only switches to a large batch *B*_max near the end. This seemingly counterintuitive choice is justified by the **fast catch‑up effect**: after the switch, the loss rapidly approaches the trajectory of a constant large‑batch run. The underlying mechanism is that the noise accumulated during the small‑batch phase decays according to a forgetting kernel *K*(*t*) ∝ *t*^{-(2-1/β)}. Because the signal term decays as *t*^{-*s*}, when *s* < 1 – 1/β there is a clear separation of timescales: the forgetting of noise (fast) occurs much sooner than the reduction of the signal (slow). Hence, postponing the large batch does not sacrifice final performance, while saving data that would otherwise be consumed by the small‑batch’s high noise.

For **easy tasks** (*s* > 1 – 1/β), the signal term diminishes quickly relative to noise. Consequently, the optimal schedule monotonically increases the batch size according to *b*(*t*) ∝ (*T*–*t*+1)^{1/(2β)–1}, which optimally balances noise reduction against the dwindling number of remaining iterations.

The shape of the forgetting kernel itself is a direct consequence of the FSL’s noise accumulation term ∫₀ᵗ*K*(*t*–*τ*)/*b*(*τ*) d*τ*. Under the assumption that the noise covariance Σ(*θ*) satisfies Σ(*θ*) ∝ *R*(*θ*)*H*, the kernel takes the explicit form *K*(*t*) = (*t*+1)^{-(2-1/β)}. This power‑law decay ensures that past gradient noise becomes irrelevant after a finite period, making late switching feasible.

**Foundational Learning**
- **Functional Scaling Laws (FSL)**: Framework modeling expected excess risk of one‑pass SGD with constant learning rate as a signal term *t*^{-*s*} plus a noise term ∫*K*(*t*–*τ*)/*b*(*τ*) d*τ*. *Why needed*: FSL maps batch size schedules to expected risk, enabling analytical optimization and explaining fast catch‑up. *Quick check*: For constant batch *B*, how does the noise accumulation term scale with *t*? (∝ *t*^{-(1-1/β)}).
- **Source (*s*) and Capacity (*β*) Exponents**: Parameters characterizing the learning problem: *s* measures target alignment (smaller = harder), *β* reflects model capacity (larger = more capacity). *Why needed*: They quantify the trade‑off between signal learning and noise accumulation, dictating optimal BSS structure. *Quick check*: For a harder task (smaller *s*), do you expect optimal training to use more or fewer total steps under fixed data? *Answer*: More steps, by staying at small batch longer.
- **One‑pass SGD with Constant Learning Rate**: Regime where each data point is seen exactly once with a fixed learning rate. *Why needed*: This isolation yields a clean variational problem and minimax‑optimal rates. *Quick check*: In the hard‑task regime, why does a constant learning rate with optimal BSS achieve the same data‑efficiency rate *D*^{-*s*} as learning‑rate schedules? *Answer*: Because optimal BSS attains the minimax optimal rate for one‑pass SGD.

**Architecture Onboarding**
- **Component map**: Data Pipeline → Batch Scheduler → Optimizer (SGD/AdamW) → Validation Loss
- **Critical path**: Data ingestion → Batch size scheduler → Gradient computation → Parameter update
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Exploration vs. exploitation: Small batches provide gradient diversity; large batches improve gradient accuracy. Late switching postpones exploitation.
  - Compute efficiency vs. data efficiency: Larger batches improve hardware utilization but reduce steps per token; late‑switching maximizes data efficiency for hard tasks while still achieving compute efficiency at the end.
  - Schedule granularity: Theory suggests a smooth monotonic increase; in practice, piecewise‑constant stages (2–4) are used. Switch points must be chosen; the scaling law *D* – *P*_⋆ ∼ *D*^γ guides extrapolation from pilot runs.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Early‑switch (large batch from start) on a hard task yields higher final loss because the total steps become too small to learn the signal.
  - Constant small‑batch schedules often plateau above the constant large‑batch baseline due to excessive noise accumulation.
  - If after switching the loss does not merge with the large‑batch curve within predicted δ_ε, the noise covariance may deviate from the H‑dominance assumption (e.g., with AdamW).

  2. Test fast catch‑up quantitatively: Train a 50M‑parameter LLaMA on C4 with a two‑stage schedule (small then large). Record the loss gap after switching, fit to δ^{-(1-1/β)} using estimated *β*, and verify the gap falls below (1+ε) baseline within predicted δ_ε.
  3. Extrapolate optimal switch point scaling: Train the 50M model at three data scales (2B, 4B, 8B tokens). For each, grid‑search optimal small→large switch point *P*_⋆. Plot log(*D*–*P*_⋆) vs. log(*D*) and check for a linear relationship with slope γ ≈ (1–1/β–*s*)/(2–1/β).

**Open Questions the Paper Calls Out**
1. **Extension to adaptive optimizers** – The FSL framework is derived under standard SGD, whereas modern LLM pretraining predominantly relies on AdamW. Adaptive optimizers introduce per‑parameter scaling and momentum that likely alter the noise covariance structure. Extending the analysis to such methods is an important open problem; evidence would come from deriving a continuous‑time SDE for an adaptive optimizer and validating predicted batch‑size schedules at scale.
2. **Interaction with learning rate decay** – The analysis assumes a constant learning rate. In practice, learning rate decay schedules (e.g., cosine) are ubiquitous. Understanding how learning rate decay influences the fast catch‑up effect and the optimal late‑switch timing remains unresolved. Evidence would require extending the FSL to time‑varying learning rates and performing controlled experiments with various decay schedules.
3. **Theoretical justification for Transformers** – The FSL was originally derived for linear/kernel regression, but the paper applies it to Transformer models without a rigorous derivation showing that Transformer dynamics obey the same functional form. Proving this under appropriate scaling limits (e.g., NTK, mean‑field) would strengthen the foundation.

**Limitations**
- The FSL assumptions (one‑pass SGD, constant learning rate, specific noise covariance Σ(*θ*) ∝ *R*(*θ*)*H*) may not hold for modern training pipelines that use AdamW and multiple epochs.
- Accurate estimation of the difficulty exponents *s* and *β* is non‑trivial; misclassification could lead to suboptimal schedule choices.
- Large‑scale experiments rely on a private dataset, limiting external reproducibility and verification.
