# Translation Invariance of Neural Operators for the FitzHugh-Nagumo Model

## Quick Facts
* **arXiv ID:** 2603.17523  
* **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17523  
* **Reference count:** 39  
* **Primary result:** Convolutional Neural Operators (CNOs) are the only architecture that reliably generalizes to translated test dynamics, achieving a median relative L2 test error of 0.0941.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates seven Neural Operator (NO) architectures on learning the solution operator of the FitzHugh-Nagumo model, with a focus on translation invariance—the property that solutions remain identical under time shifts of the applied current. A novel training strategy reduces dataset generation costs by using stimuli at a fixed time with varying spatial positions and intensities, while test cases apply stimuli translated in both time and space. Results reveal a clear trade-off: CNOs are the only models that generalize to the translated dynamics, but they require significantly longer training times (~10 hours). In contrast, Fourier Neural Operators (FNOs) achieve very low training errors but fail catastrophically on translated tests, while Deep Operator Networks (DONs) and their variants are computationally efficient at inference but do not generalize. The work highlights that capturing translation invariance for this stiff PDE problem is uniquely achieved by CNOs, albeit at higher training computational cost.

## Method Summary
The authors design a cost-effective dataset generation protocol where training data uses current stimuli applied at a fixed reference time across various spatial positions and intensities. Test data deliberately applies the same stimuli but translated to different times (and possibly spaces), thereby challenging the learned operator to handle time shifts without explicit training on such shifts. Seven neural operator architectures are trained to map the input current (as a function of space-time) to the resulting voltage solution. Performance is measured via relative L2 error on the translated test set, comparing generalization capability across architectures.

## Key Results
* CNOs achieve a median relative L2 test error of 0.0941 on the translated dynamics, reliably generalizing where all other architectures fail.
* FNOs attain the lowest training error (0.0081) but exhibit a high median test error of 1.4655 on translated cases and have the highest inference latency.
* DONs and their variants are computationally efficient at inference but produce large errors on the translated test set, indicating a failure to capture translation invariance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper does not provide a detailed mechanistic explanation for why CNOs succeed where others fail. However, the likely reason is that convolutional layers are inherently translation-equivariant, allowing CNOs to preserve the time-shift symmetry of the solution operator during learning. In contrast, FNOs rely on global Fourier transforms which may struggle with non-periodic time shifts, and DONs use fully-connected layers that break translation equivariance by treating spatial and temporal coordinates as independent dimensions.

## Architecture Onboarding
*Component Map* (for CNO):  
Input discretization -> Stacked convolutional layers (with padding to manage boundaries) -> Output discretization  

*Critical Path:*  
Convolutional layers preserve translation equivariance at each step, enabling the network to internally represent time-shifted solutions without explicit training examples. This equivariance property propagates through the network, making the final output naturally invariant to time shifts of the input stimulus.

*Design Tradeoffs:*  
CNOs use local operations (convolutions) that respect spatial/temporal translation but require deeper networks and longer training to capture complex dynamics. FNOs use global spectral operations for efficient training on periodic domains but lose invariance under non-periodic shifts. DONs use pointwise MLPs for fast inference but treat coordinates as independent, breaking any symmetry.

*Failure Signatures:*  
Non-CNO architectures show very high errors (order 1) on translated test cases, indicating they have learned a mapping tied to absolute time rather than the relative time structure. FNOs also exhibit high inference latency due to global transforms.

*First Experiments:*  
1. Train CNO, FNO, and DON on the fixed-time dataset; evaluate on both fixed-time and translated test sets to quantify generalization gap.  
2. Ablate CNO by replacing convolutional layers with fully-connected layers; measure the resulting test error on translated dynamics.  
3. Test CNO on a different translation-invariant PDE (e.g., linear advection) to check if the advantage persists across problem families.

## Limitations
* Evaluation is restricted to a single PDE (FitzHugh-Nagumo) and a specific stimulus type; findings may not generalize to other equations or input forms.  
* No statistical analysis across multiple random seeds or hyperparameter sweeps; reported performance may not be robust.  
* Hardware specifications for training and inference times are missing, making computational comparisons anecdotal rather than quantitative.  
* The claimed cost reduction from the training strategy is not validated against a baseline dataset generation approach.  
* No ablation studies isolate which component of CNOs (e.g., depth, kernel size) is responsible for capturing translation invariance.

## Confidence
* **CNO generalization to translated dynamics:** Medium – the large error gap is compelling but lacks multi-seed statistical confirmation.  
* **Computational trade-offs (training time vs. inference efficiency):** Medium – ordering is plausible but not quantified with controlled hardware measurements.  
* **Dataset cost reduction due to training strategy:** Low – asserted without empirical baseline comparison.

## Next Checks
1. **Statistical robustness:** Train each architecture with at least five random seeds and multiple learning rates; report error distributions and perform ANOVA to assess significance of differences.  
2. **Cross-PDE generalizability:** Apply the same training and evaluation pipeline to at least two other translation-invariant PDEs (e.g., advection equation, wave equation) to test whether CNOs' advantage holds broadly.  
3. **Ablation of CNO’s convolutional layers:** Replace CNO’s convolutional layers with fully-connected layers of comparable parameter count; measure the change in median relative L2 error on translated test cases.
