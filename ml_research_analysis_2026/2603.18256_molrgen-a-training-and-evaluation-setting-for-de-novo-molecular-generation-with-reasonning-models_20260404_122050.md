# MolRGen: A Training and Evaluation Setting for De Novo Molecular Generation with Reasonning Models
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.18256
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18256
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A 24B LLM (Mistral-Small) trained with GRPO achieves state-of-the-art top-1 performance (0.64) but suffers severe diversity collapse, yielding low top-k and diversity-aware scores.

## Executive Summary
MolRGen introduces a benchmark and dataset for training and evaluating reasoning-based large language models on de novo molecular generation, property prediction, and retro-synthesis without relying on ground-truth molecule labels. The core method employs Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), where rewards are computed at generation time from docking scores and molecular properties. A new diversity-aware top-k metric jointly evaluates quality and chemical exploration. Results show a 24B LLM (Mistral-Small) fine-tuned with GRPO reaches state-of-the-art top-1 performance (0.64) but suffers from severe diversity collapse. Pretrained reasoning models (MiniMax-M2, Qwen3) achieve strong top-k scores, while a chemically specialized model (ChemDFM-R) excels under tight diversity constraints. Property prediction remains a challenge, with best normalized Spearman correlations around 0.5, underscoring the need for better balancing of exploitation and diversity in RL-driven molecule generation.

## Method Summary
MolRGen comprises three tasks: de novo molecular generation, molecular property prediction, and reaction-based retro-synthesis. The de novo task uses 4,500 protein pockets (SAIR and SIU datasets) and 12 standard RDKit properties; each prompt specifies one docking target and up to two additional property objectives. The dataset contains 49,000 training, 1,000 validation, and 1,000 test prompts, with protein IDs disjoint across splits. Property prediction draws from 27 ADMET benchmarks (~52,000 prompts, 79% regression, 21% classification). Retrosynthesis tasks are built from 115 reaction SMARTS and Enamine building blocks (50,000 prompts) but are not yet evaluated. All tasks follow a unified text-to-text formulation where the LLM outputs either a SMILES string or a numeric value.

Training uses Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). For each prompt, the policy generates G=8 completions. Rewards are computed on-the-fly: valid SMILES undergo 3D conformer generation, docking (e.g., QuickVina), and RDKit property calculation. Per-property rewards are normalized to [0,1] and aggregated via geometric mean; invalid SMILES yield zero reward. The GRPO loss combines a clipped PPO surrogate with a KL penalty to the old policy. Models are evaluated with top-k (average reward of best unique molecules) and the proposed diversity-aware top-k score, which selects high-reward molecules under a Tanimoto similarity threshold using ECFP4 fingerprints.

## Key Results
- Mistral-Small (24B) fine-tuned with GRPO achieves top-1 score 0.64 but exhibits severe diversity collapse, leading to low top-k and diversity-aware scores.
- Pretrained reasoning LLMs (MiniMax-M2, Qwen3) show strong top-k performance without RL fine-tuning.
- Chemically specialized model (ChemDFM-R) excels under tight diversity constraints, while all models have limited property prediction capability (best normalized Spearman ~0.5).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The success of GRPO in boosting top-1 performance stems from its ability to rapidly amplify the advantage of high-reward completions; however, this same mechanism causes diversity collapse. The loss uses exponentiated advantages relative to the group mean, which concentrates probability mass on the few best-performing molecules for a given prompt. As a result, the policy generates nearly identical outputs, as observed for RL-Mistral, where most rollouts are the same high-reward molecule. This collapse is evidenced by a steep drop in top-k and diversity-aware scores despite a high top-1 score.

The diversity-aware top-k metric provides an effective way to measure both quality and exploration. It greedily selects up to k molecules with the highest rewards while enforcing a pairwise Tanimoto similarity threshold (s_max). This combinatorial selection penalizes redundant chemotypes and highlights models that can produce distinct high-reward candidates. The metric’s sensitivity to the threshold s_max and fingerprint choice (ECFP4 vs. MACCS) is considered; rankings remain stable across reasonable variations, validating its utility for comparing models under diversity constraints.

Chemically specialized pretraining, as in ChemDFM-R, appears to align the model's internal representations with the chemical similarity spaces used in docking and property calculations. This domain-specific knowledge enables the model to generate a broader set of distinct high-reward chemotypes, rather than exploiting a narrow reward peak. Consequently, ChemDFM-R outperforms general reasoning models when the diversity constraint is tight, suggesting that in-domain pretraining is a viable path to improve exploration without sacrificing too much exploitation.

## Foundational Learning
- **Reinforcement Learning from Environment Rewards (GRPO):** Enables the model to optimize for non-differentiable rewards (docking scores, properties) computed at generation time. *Quick check:* If GRPO updates concentrate probability mass on one high-reward molecule for a given prompt, what effect does this have on the policy's entropy and on the diversity-aware top-k score?
- **Molecular Representations (SMILES, Fingerprints):** LLMs generate SMILES strings, while diversity metrics rely on fingerprint-based Tanimoto similarity; understanding validity, parsing, and similarity is crucial. *Quick check:* Why might a syntactically valid SMILES string still represent an invalid or unstable molecule? How does this affect reward computation?
- **Multi-Objective Optimization Trade-offs:** De novo generation optimizes multiple properties simultaneously; geometric mean aggregation forces all properties to be non-zero. *Quick check:* If a prompt asks to maximize docking score and minimize molecular weight, what happens if the optimal docking scaffold is inherently heavy? How does the reward aggregation resolve this tension?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Prompt Generator → LLM Policy (π_θ) → Reward Computer (docking + RDKit) → Diversity-aware Top-k Metric → GRPO Trainer.

**Critical path:** The sequence from receiving a prompt to policy update: generate prompt → sample G completions from LLM → extract SMILES/numeric answers → compute rewards via docking/property calculators → compute advantages (reward minus group mean) → apply GRPO loss (clipped surrogate + KL penalty) → update policy.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Reward aggregation:* Geometric mean avoids degenerate solutions where some properties are zero but may over-penalize borderline failures; arithmetic mean would allow some properties to be ignored.
- *Similarity metric:* Choice of fingerprint (ECFP4 vs MACCS) affects diversity measurement; ECFP4 captures substructure patterns, while MACCS keys are a fixed set of binary features.
- *Exploration incentive:* Token-level entropy bonuses are computationally cheap but may not prevent molecular collapse; cluster-level regularization (e.g., uniform cluster masses) aligns better with diversity-aware top-k but requires clustering per prompt, adding overhead.

**Failure signatures:**
- High top-1 score combined with rapidly decaying top-k and diversity-aware scores indicates collapse.
- High rate of invalid SMILES (>20%) suggests the model does not respect output format; may require constrained decoding.
- Low normalized Spearman (~0.5) in property prediction tasks reveals limited chemical reasoning capability.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. *Validate reward pipeline:* For a few sampled prompts, manually compute docking score and QED for a known high-reward molecule; ensure normalized rewards fall within [0,1] and geometric mean matches expectation.
2. *Test diversity threshold sensitivity:* Run a small model (e.g., ChemDFM-R) on 50 prompts; compute diversity-aware top-k for s_max values {0.2, 0.5, 0.8}. Expect scores to increase with s_max and cluster counts to stay non-zero at low thresholds.
3. *GRPO collapse debug:* Train Mistral-Small for 10 GRPO steps on 1,000 prompts; log per-prompt rollout rewards and pairwise Tanimoto similarities. Confirm that for many prompts, the highest reward molecule dominates probability mass and the remaining rollouts are near-identical.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Can reasoning-based LLMs achieve effective de novo molecular generation through RL?** The paper tests this by training Mistral-Small with GRPO, achieving high top-1 but suffering collapse, while pretrained reasoning models excel without RL. The interplay between reasoning ability, RL fine-tuning, and generation quality remains unclear; systematic comparisons and ablations would be needed.
2. **How can RL objectives balance exploitation and exploration?** The paper identifies diversity collapse as a key limitation and proposes a diversity-aware top-k metric and a theoretical exploration bonus, but no practical solution is implemented. Evidence: implementing and evaluating the bonus (or alternatives) within GRPO to improve diversity-aware scores is required.
3. **Why do LLMs underperform in molecular property prediction?** All models achieve normalized Spearman ~0.5, far below specialized ML. Root causes are not analyzed; potential factors include insufficient chemical knowledge, prompting deficiencies, or reward design. Error analysis and probing internal representations could clarify.
4. **How well do computational rewards correlate with experimental viability?** The benchmark uses only computational proxies (docking scores, predicted properties) without experimental validation. It is unclear whether optimizing these leads to real-world success; experimental testing of top-ranked molecules and correlation studies are needed.

## Limitations
- Missing critical implementation details: docking software, grid parameters, reward normalization, prompt templates, GRPO hyperparameters, and binary threshold settings.
- Retrosynthesis task relies on proprietary Enamine building blocks and unreleased reaction SMARTS, preventing full replication.
- Benchmark relies solely on computational proxies (docking scores, predicted properties), which may not correlate with experimental viability.
