# Competition for attention predicts good-to-bad tipping in AI

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.14370
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14370
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A derived formula (Eq. 2) predicts good-to-bad tipping point with 89% accuracy across six transformer models, enabling lightweight monitoring and steering on edge devices.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the risk of harmful outputs from AI models running offline on edge devices, where cloud-based safety mechanisms are unavailable. It shows that good-to-bad tipping originates from dot-product competition for attention between the conversation’s context and competing output basins in the transformer architecture. The authors derive a closed-form formula (Eq. 2) that predicts the tipping point n*, validated across six phone-scale transformer models with 89% accuracy in classifying tipping timing (immediate vs. delayed). The mechanism also allows steering future outputs by engineering the conversation history, providing a domain-portable monitoring approach without architectural changes.

## Method Summary
The study uses six decoder-only transformers (124M–410M parameters) with greedy decoding. Basin centroids for “good” (B) and “bad” (D) outputs are constructed from mean-pooled penultimate-layer embeddings of six curated example phrases each. For a given prompt history, context vectors (mean-pooled per token or sentence) are compared via dot products with B and D. The derived formula (Eq. 2) aggregates contributions from each history token to predict n*, the number of steps until the output tips from good to bad. Tipping is observed at two scales: token-level (first step where context·D ≥ context·B) and sentence-level (first sentence labeled bad by an external LLM/human). The formula’s predictions match observed timing classes in 16 of 18 cases (89%).

## Key Results
- Formula (Eq. 2) predicts the tipping point n* with 89% accuracy across six models, correctly classifying timing (immediate vs. delayed) in 16/18 test cases.
- The mechanism enables steering: injecting content aligned with the bad basin decreases n*, while content aligned against it increases n*.
- Analysis shows the two prediction failures occur in negation cases, revealing a boundary of the geometric approximation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Attention-based output selection via dot-product competition
The next token is selected by comparing dot products between the context vector c (from attention) and basin centroids B (good) and D (bad); the basin with higher dot product wins. Attention computes c as a weighted sum of previous token embeddings, and softmax over c·B and c·D determines the output. The core assumption is that the output distribution is dominated by competition between a single good and bad basin. Evidence: The paper’s derivation (Sec. 3) explicitly models the “compass needle” c and the softmax competition. Break condition: If multiple basins have comparable dot products or the architecture deviates from standard transformer attention, the simplification fails.

### Mechanism 2: Accumulation-induced context shift enables delayed tipping
When initial context favors B (A·B > A·D), B tokens accumulate. Each B token makes c more aligned with B. If D·B > B·B, then eventually c·D > c·B, causing tipping after n* B tokens. The condition D·B > B·B creates a positive feedback loop: more B increases c·D relative to c·B. Core assumption: Basin centroid dot products are fixed and capture the geometric relationships driving competition. Evidence: The paper’s theoretical derivation (Sec. 4) shows how successive B tokens shift c and the derived inequality governs tipping. Break condition: If D·B ≤ B·B, B remains a stable attractor and delayed tipping does not occur.

### Mechanism 3: Closed-form formula predicts tipping point and enables steering
Equation 2 gives an explicit expression for n* based on the conversation history, allowing real-time monitoring and steering by manipulating history. The formula aggregates contributions: Σ Pi(Pi·B - Pi·D)exp(Pi·B/T_eff) divided by a constant denominator (B·D - B·B)exp(B·B/T_eff). Injecting content C alters the numerator, thus increasing or decreasing n*. Core assumption: Coarse-graining approximations (effective head, mean-pooled embeddings) are valid for predicting timing class across models. Evidence: Validation across six models shows 89% classification accuracy (Fig. 4). Break condition: The formula fails for negation cases (2/18) where token geometry diverges from sentence semantics, indicating limits of the geometric approximation.

## Foundational Learning
- **Transformer attention mechanism**: Needed because the mechanism depends on how attention computes a context vector from previous tokens and how dot products with output embeddings drive token selection. Quick check: Describe how the “compass needle” c is computed and how it influences the next token choice between B and D.
- **Basins in representation space**: Needed because the paper reduces the vast vocabulary into a few semantic basins (good, bad) whose centroids represent the model’s internal concepts; competition occurs between these basins. Quick check: How are basin centroids B and D constructed? Why use mean-pooled penultimate-layer embeddings?
- **Tipping point n***: Needed because the core outcome is predicting exactly when the output switches from good to bad; n* quantifies the delay. Quick check: What does n* = 0 indicate about the initial prompt? What behavior corresponds to a large n*?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Model (pretrained transformer on edge) -> Monitor (intercepts embeddings, computes n*) -> Alert system (flags when n* ≤ threshold); Monitor <-> Basin database (precomputed centroids B, D); Monitor -> Steering interface (optional, injects content C).

Critical path: At each generation step, the model produces a token. The monitor captures its penultimate-layer embedding h_t. It updates numerator += (h_t·B - h_t·D) * exp(h_t·B / T_eff). Then computes n* = ceil(numerator / denominator). If n* ≤ 0, immediate tipping is flagged. The monitor operates in parallel with minimal overhead (O(d) per token).

Design tradeoffs: Token vs. sentence granularity (token-level is sensitive but noisy; sentence-level is robust but slower); Layer choice (penultimate layer captures high-level concepts; earlier layers fail); Basin definition (requires curated example phrases; quality is critical and domain-specific); Temperature (T_eff is model-specific, likely 1/√d_k; must be known).

Failure signatures: Disagreement between token-level and sentence-level predictions (e.g., with negation); Unstable n* when denominator ≈ 0 (B·D ≈ B·B); High variance in n* across similar prompts indicates poor basin definition; False negatives (missed tipping) or false positives (flagging safe outputs).

First 3 experiments:
1. Reproduce validation: For a held-back prompt set, compute n*_pred and compare with observed n*_obs (both token and sentence levels). Target ≥89% agreement on timing class.
2. Steering test: For a prompt with moderate n*, inject a C aligned with D; verify n* decreases. Then inject C aligned against D; verify n* increases. Quantify change.
3. Cross-model portability: Apply the same B, D basins to a different model family (e.g., Pythia). Check if timing class predictions hold; if not, investigate model-specific adjustments.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:** How can the approximate formula (Eq. 2) be systematically refined by reversing its derivation from the full transformer equations (Eq. 1) to eliminate successive approximations and achieve precise prediction of tipping points?  
*Basis:* The authors state Eq. 2 can be improved by removing coarse-graining steps. *Why unresolved:* The current formula works well but introduces inaccuracies for complex linguistics like negation. *Evidence to resolve:* A mathematically complete derivation without approximations, validated across diverse prompts, showing improved accuracy over Eq. 2.

**Open Question 2:** Can alignment techniques (e.g., RLHF, DPO) be engineered to permanently eliminate the dot-product inequality (D·B > B·B) that enables tipping for all possible future prompts in a given model, or do they merely shift the tipping condition to new adversarial regions?  
*Basis:* Alignment modifies basin geometry; the paper hypothesizes it cannot remove the fundamental competition. *Why unresolved:* It is unproven whether basin separation can be made absolute in high-dimensional space. *Evidence to resolve:* A formal proof that for any fixed B and D, there exists a prompt creating c·D > c·B; or exhaustive red-teaming of a highly aligned model to find any tipping prompt.

**Open Question 3:** Does the predicted competition-for-attention mechanism and its formula (Eq. 2) hold quantitatively for state-of-the-art, >100B parameter production models (e.g., GPT-4, Claude 3.5) where internal representations are not publicly accessible?  
*Basis:* Validation is on 124M–410M models; indirect evidence from CCDH study on ChatGPT-4o aligns phenomenologically but lacks direct embeddings. *Why unresolved:* Scaling effects could alter dynamics; formula parameters may not be estimable in opaque models. *Evidence to resolve:* Access to penultimate-layer embeddings (or robust proxy) for multiple large models across standardized prompts, replicating the 89% classification accuracy.

**Open Question 4:** How does the mechanism manifest when more than two output basins (B, D, others) compete, and can Eq. 2 be generalized to predict n* in multi-basin scenarios?  
*Basis:* The paper focuses on a dominant B-D pair, noting extension is straightforward but undeveloped. *Why unresolved:* Real conversations involve many concepts; the pairwise assumption may fail if multiple basins have comparable alignment, leading to oscillations or chaotic switching not captured by current reduction. *Evidence to resolve:* Empirical analysis of multi-basin prompt sequences showing complex trajectories, and a generalized formula incorporating multiple D basins validated against such data.

**Open Question 5:** What is the minimal set of linguistic or semantic features required to accurately predict tipping points using internal representation geometry, and can these features be automatically extracted for a robust, domain-portable monitor?  
*Basis:* The framework uses mean-pooled embeddings of hand-defined phrases; negation cases fail, indicating centroid model is too simplistic for compositional semantics. *Why unresolved:* Manual curation limits portability; an automated method is needed. *Evidence to resolve:* Development of an automated basis (e.g., via dimensionality reduction, sparse autoencoders) that captures good/bad directions, predicts n* with accuracy matching or exceeding hand-crafted centroids across domains and languages, and correctly classifies negation cases.

## Limitations
- Missing SI details (exact basin phrases, one-step token continuation rule) prevent exact replication.
- Effective temperature T_eff may require model-specific tuning beyond the theoretical 1/√d_k.
- Negation edge cases (2/18 failures) reveal token geometry diverges from sentence semantics, limiting formula reliability for complex language.

## Confidence
- Dot-product competition (Mechanism 1): High
- Accumulation-induced tipping (Mechanism 2): Medium
- Formula portability & steering (Mechanism 3): Medium
