# Scale Dependent Data Duplication

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.06603
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06603
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** As model scale increases, semantically equivalent documents (e.g., translations) become increasingly redundant due to gradient alignment; this induces a sharp acceleration in nearest-neighbor collisions and scale-dependent loss degradation that breaks naive scaling laws, but can be corrected with a uniqueness-aware scaling term.

## Executive Summary
This paper demonstrates that data duplication effects are not static but emerge and intensify with model scale. The authors show that larger models develop "semantic sensitivity," causing gradients for semantically equivalent documents to align. This reduces the effective uniqueness of a corpus in a scale-dependent way. Analysis of 190M documents reveals that nearest-neighbor similarity follows a power law only up to a threshold, beyond which collisions accelerate. Controlled pretraining experiments confirm that limited semantic uniqueness harms larger models disproportionately, invalidating standard compute scaling extrapolations. The authors derive a correction term—a scaling law of the form Δ(C, K) ≈ a C^β K^{-γ}—that restores predictivity and show how to estimate effective uniqueness K from nearest-neighbor statistics.

## Method Summary
The study combines empirical measurement and controlled training. First, it computes gradient cosine similarity between documents and their semantic-preserving transformations (e.g., translations) across model scales and training steps to measure signal redundancy. Second, it embeds 190M documents and samples subsets of varying sizes to track how mean nearest-neighbor cosine similarity scales, looking for deviations from isotropic power-law expectations. Third, it runs controlled pretraining "ladders" where training streams are sampled with replacement from pools of K unique documents (using exact repeats as a proxy for semantic duplicates) across multiple model sizes, matching total compute. The resulting loss degradation Δ(C,K) is fitted to a power-law correction. Finally, it validates that estimating effective uniqueness K_eff from the measured mean nearest-neighbor cosine similarity (via a calibrated model) recovers the same scaling correction.

## Key Results
- Gradient cosine similarity (Z-score, AUC) between a document and its translation increases monotonically with model size and training progress, indicating that larger models treat semantic equivalents as increasingly redundant.
- Nearest-neighbor cosine similarity in a fixed embedding space follows a power law for moderate corpus sizes but steepens sharply beyond a threshold (~10M documents for real data), indicating accelerated semantic collisions; synthetic data collapses earlier.
- Controlled training with limited uniqueness (K) shows negligible loss degradation for small models but rapidly increasing penalties for larger models, breaking infinite-uniqueness scaling predictions; a corrected plane law Δ(C,K) ≈ a C^β K^{-γ} restores predictability, and K_eff can be estimated from mean nearest-neighbor cosine.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Emergent Gradient Alignment for Semantic Duplicates
- **Claim:** As model capability increases, cross-entropy loss gradients for semantically equivalent documents (e.g., translations) become more aligned, making them act as effective duplicates.
- **Mechanism:** Larger models learn deeper latent invariances (e.g., semantics independent of language or superficial phrasing). Their gradients therefore reflect these deeper latents rather than surface features, causing gradients for different surface realizations of the same meaning to point in similar directions. Aligned gradients imply redundant weight updates.
- **Evidence anchors:** Gradient cosine similarity (Z-score, AUC) between original documents and translations increases with model size (e.g., from 34M to 1B parameters) and training steps; this trend does not hold for unrelated document pairs.
- **Break condition:** If a model does not develop semantic sensitivity (due to architectural limits, insufficient scale, or training), gradient alignment for semantic duplicates will not emerge, and duplication effects will remain mild.

### Mechanism 2: Accelerated Semantic Collisions at Large Corpus Scales
- **Claim:** The rate of semantic collisions (nearest-neighbor similarity) accelerates beyond a threshold, meaning the pool of semantically unique documents shrinks faster than power-law extrapolation predicts.
- **Mechanism:** For moderate corpus sizes, nearest-neighbor similarity in an isotropic high-dimensional embedding space follows a power law due to random geometry. Beyond a critical size, the real semantic distribution's structure causes a steep increase in collisions—more documents start sharing similar embeddings because the corpus exhausts the space of distinct semantic concepts. This "collision acceleration" indicates a drop in effective uniqueness.
- **Evidence anchors:** Embedding 190M FineWeb-Edu-Dedup documents with EmbeddingGemma-300m shows mean nearest-neighbor cosine rising with corpus size N; a power-law fit works only for N < ~10^6, after which the curve deviates sharply. Synthetic corpora (Recycling-the-Web) show deviation an order of magnitude earlier.
- **Break condition:** If the embedding model does not capture the true semantic structure relevant to the training model, nearest-neighbor statistics may not reflect actual collision probability under training.

### Mechanism 3: Scale-Dependent Loss Degradation and Uniqueness-Aware Scaling Law
- **Claim:** Limited semantic uniqueness (finite effective K) causes loss degradation that grows super-linearly with compute C for larger models, breaking naive scaling; a corrected law Δ(C,K) ≈ a C^β K^{-γ} restores predictivity.
- **Mechanism:** When training on a stream sampled with replacement from K unique documents, the effective number of independent gradient updates is reduced. The degradation depends on (i) total documents processed n ∝ C, (ii) the model's semantic sensitivity ρ (which itself grows with C), and (iii) K. The effective reuse ratio is ρn/K. For small C (small models), degradation is mild even with small K; for large C (large models), degradation becomes severe because both n and ρ are large. The derived power-law correction captures this interaction.
- **Evidence anchors:** Controlled training on K-pools (K = 10^5 to 10^8) across model ladders (34M–344M) shows that eval loss vs. compute curves diverge from the infinite-uniqueness baseline increasingly for larger models. Fitting Δ(C,K) yields a plane (in log-log) that accurately predicts loss. Using mean NN cosine to estimate K_eff and plugging into the law recovers the same predictivity.
- **Break condition:** If semantic sensitivity ρ does not grow with compute (v=0), the degradation simplifies to a ratio-only law Δ ∝ C^α K^{-1}, which under-predicts the observed catastrophic degradation for large models.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Gradient-based influence and signal alignment**
  - *Why needed here:* The core mechanism compares per-document gradients to measure redundancy. Understanding that cosine similarity between gradients indicates how similarly two documents update the model's weights is essential.
  - *Quick check question:* If two documents yield gradients with cosine similarity 0.9, what does this imply about the direction of the weight update when training on each document separately, and why might training on both be less efficient than on two independent documents?

- **Concept: Compositional hierarchies and latent structure (e.g., Random Hierarchy Model)**
  - *Why needed here:* The paper models semantics as a hierarchy of latents (z⁰ → zᴸ → x). Deeper latents correspond to finer semantic invariances (e.g., translation invariance). A model's progression to learning deeper latents explains why semantic duplicates become redundant only at sufficient scale.
  - *Quick check question:* In a hierarchical latent model, why would a model that only learns shallow latents treat a translation as distinct from the original, while a model that learns deeper latents collapse them into the same learning signal?

- **Concept: Nearest-neighbor statistics and effective uniqueness estimation**
  - *Why needed here:* Practitioners cannot directly observe the true number of semantic classes K. The paper shows how to estimate effective K from the mean nearest-neighbor cosine similarity in a high-quality embedding space, assuming a two-component collision model (background vs. same-latent neighbors).
  - *Quick check question:* Given a measured mean nearest-neighbor cosine M_N for corpus size N, a calibrated background mean m⁻, and a calibrated collision similarity m⁺, how would you compute the estimated probability q_N that a random document has a same-latent neighbor, and how would you then invert q_N ≈ 1 - exp(-N/K) to obtain K_eff?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Gradient similarity module -> Embedding collision analyzer -> Controlled pretraining ladder -> Scaling law fitter -> K_eff estimator

- **Critical path:**
  1. Establish that gradient alignment for semantic transformations increases with model scale and training (Mechanism 1).
  2. Measure nearest-neighbor cosine scaling on the target corpus to detect acceleration (Mechanism 2).
  3. Run controlled K-pool ladders to observe scale-dependent degradation and fit the plane law parameters (Mechanism 3).
  4. Validate that K_eff estimated from mean NN cosine (from a fixed subsample) restores scaling predictivity.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Gradient computation is expensive; authors truncated documents to 2000 tokens and used a negative baseline to reduce variance.
  - Embedding model choice (EmbeddingGemma-300m) may not perfectly align with the training model's semantics; using a better or co-trained embedding could improve K_eff estimation.
  - Controlled training used exact repeats as a proxy for semantic duplicates, likely overestimating degradation compared to real semantic duplicates with m⁺ < 1.
  - The plane law (3 parameters) offers a good fit; a simpler ratio-only law (γ=1) fails to capture the super-linear scaling of degradation with C for large models.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - Gradient alignment (Z-score/AUC) remains flat or decreases with model size; semantic sensitivity does not emerge.
  - Nearest-neighbor similarity follows a pure power law even at massive scales (N > 10^8); no collision acceleration observed.
  - The fitted plane law fails to predict eval loss on new (C, K) points; residuals show systematic bias.
  - Estimated K_eff from mean NN cosine is highly unstable across different subsamples or correlates poorly with true K in controlled experiments.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. *Gradient alignment sweep:* For multiple model families (e.g., Pythia, Qwen) at various training steps, compute gradient cosine between documents and their transformations (character swap, translation) vs. unrelated pairs. Plot Z-score/AUC against model size and step.
  2. *NN scaling curve:* Embed 190M FineWeb-Edu-Dedup docs with EmbeddingGemma-300m. For subset sizes N from 10^4 to 10^8, compute mean nearest-neighbor cosine using FAISS. Fit a power law to N < 10^6 data and check for deviation at larger N; repeat with a synthetic corpus.
  3. *K-pool ladder:* For each K in {10^5, 10^6, 10^7, 10^8}, sample a training stream with replacement from K unique docs. Train Chinchilla-optimal ladders (34M, 100M, 344M) at matched FLOPs. Also train an infinite baseline (without replacement). Plot eval loss vs. compute; compute Δ; fit Δ ≈ a C^β K^{-γ}.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Validate scaling degradation in multi‑billion‑parameter models using true semantic deduplication.
- **Basis in paper:** Explicitly noted as future work: “Future work could validate our findings on large models by training on semantically deduplicated datasets.”
- **Why unresolved:** The study used exact repeats as a proxy due to compute limits; it is unclear whether semantic duplicates (with m⁺ < 1) behave similarly at large scales.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Train large models (>1B parameters) on corpora with varying semantic uniqueness (achieved via semantic deduplication) and compare loss scaling to the proposed law.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How do embedding model properties (e.g., isotropy, dimensionality) affect the reliability of mean nearest‑neighbor cosine for estimating effective uniqueness?
- **Basis in paper:** Explicitly noted: EmbeddingGemma‑300m “does not produce perfectly isotropic or representative embeddings” and suggests training better embeddings to improve confidence.
- **Why unresolved:** The K_eff estimator assumes ideal embedding geometry; systematic deviations may bias estimates and distort scaling law predictions.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** On controlled datasets with known ground‑truth duplicate structures, compute K_eff using diverse embedding models and correlate the estimates with actual loss degradation.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What causes the sharp deviation from the isotropic power law in nearest‑neighbor cosine similarity at large corpus sizes, and can it be predicted a priori?
- **Basis in paper:** Inferred from Section 3.2: the deviation is reported and attributed to “accelerated semantic collisions” but no predictive mechanism is given.
- **Why unresolved:** The precise scale of deviation governs when datasets become semantically saturated; current theory offers no way to anticipate this threshold.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Derive a generative model of semantic latent distributions that quantitatively predicts the deviation scale; validate against multiple real corpora with embeddings of increasing size.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How can synthetic data generation be improved to maintain semantic diversity and avoid the earlier collapse of scaling laws?
- **Basis in paper:** Explicitly noted: Synthetic data shows collision‑scaling collapse “an order of magnitude earlier” (Fig. 5); the authors warn about insufficient semantic diversity.
- **Why unresolved:** Synthetic pretraining data often lacks the semantic variety of real text, potentially limiting scalability; methods to enhance diversity remain unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Evaluate semantic diversity (e.g., embedding spread) of synthetic datasets produced by varied generation strategies (diverse prompts, multi‑model mixtures); correlate these metrics with model scaling law adherence.

## Limitations
- Gradient alignment evidence relies on a small fixed set of 1000 documents; scaling to the full corpus is indirect.
- Nearest‑neighbor analysis depends on a single embedding model (EmbeddingGemma-300m); alignment with the training model’s semantics is not guaranteed.
- Controlled training uses exact repeats as a proxy for semantic duplicates, likely overestimating degradation.
- Scaling law parameters were fitted on a narrow compute range; extrapolation to larger scales is untested.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (gradient alignment growth):** Medium – supported by multi-size experiments but limited document sampling.
- **Mechanism 2 (collision acceleration):** High – strong empirical trend across 190M documents, though embedding dependent.
- **Mechanism 3 (scale‑dependent degradation and corrected scaling law):** Medium – rigorous controlled experiments, but proxy duplication and limited scale range.

## Next Checks
1. Expand gradient similarity analysis to thousands of document pairs across at least three model families and multiple training checkpoints, and correlate with actual training loss impact.
2. Perform the nearest‑neighbor scaling experiment with multiple off-the-shelf embedding models (e.g., OpenAI text-embedding-ada-002, BGE) to test robustness of the power‑law deviation.
3. Replace exact repeats in the K‑pool ladders with realistic semantic duplicates (e.g., machine translations, paraphrases) and remeasure degradation; compare resulting scaling‑law parameters to the exact‑repeat case.
