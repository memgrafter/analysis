# Chain-of-Trajectories: Unlocking the Intrinsic Generative Optimality of Diffusion Models via Graph-Theoretic Planning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.14704  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14704  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: CoTj achieves higher fidelity with fewer steps (e.g., GenEval +0.15 on Qwen-Image at 10 steps, MSE >60% reduction) via graph-based trajectory planning.

## Executive Summary
Diffusion models typically employ fixed sampling schedules, which lead to computational waste by misallocating steps across the denoising process. This paper introduces Chain-of-Trajectories (CoTj), a train-free framework that reformulates sampling as graph planning using a low-dimensional difficulty predictor called Diffusion DNA. By predicting per-timestep reconstruction error from the conditioning input, CoTj constructs a directed acyclic graph where edges represent correction costs, and finds the shortest path from noise to data. This dynamic allocation concentrates computation on difficult phases, achieving System 2-like deliberation. Experiments on image and video generation demonstrate significant gains: on Qwen-Image, CoTj improves GenEval from 0.70 to 0.85 at 10 steps; on Z-Image-Turbo it achieves a 0.19 absolute gain with only 2 steps; it reduces MSE by over 60% and improves PSNR by more than 6 dB compared to Euler; for video, it enhances imaging quality and motion smoothness under extreme step budgets. CoTj thus attains higher fidelity with dramatically fewer function evaluations.

## Method Summary
CoTj comprises three components: (1) a lightweight MLP (0.96M parameters) that maps condition embeddings (from the base model's text encoder) to a 100-dimensional Diffusion DNA vector representing per-timestep reconstruction difficulty; (2) a graph planner that builds a Super-DAG with nodes as timesteps and edge weights derived from DNA (e.g., \(W(t,k) = s(t,k) \cdot C(t)\) with \(s(t,k) = ((t-k)/t)^2\) for linear flow); and (3) an execution solver that runs the base diffusion model's sampler along the planned timestep sequence. The planning solves a shortest-path problem (e.g., via dynamic programming) to minimize cumulative cost. An adaptive-length regime optionally stops sampling once the explained gain ratio \(\rho(n)\) reaches a threshold (e.g., 0.99), automatically determining minimal steps. The framework is train-free except for the predictor, which is trained on a dataset of prompt embeddings and reference DNA computed from high-step denoising.

## Key Results
- CoTj improves GenEval by +0.15 (Qwen-Image, 10 steps) and +0.19 (Z-Image-Turbo, 2 steps) over uniform Euler.
- It reduces MSE by >60% and increases PSNR by >6 dB compared to Euler at 10 steps; with only 5 steps and a 1st-order solver, CoTj (0.626) surpasses baseline with a high-order solver (0.528).
- For video (Wan2.2), CoTj enhances imaging quality (60.29 vs 59.81) and motion smoothness (0.9784 vs 0.9769) at low step budgets.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
CoTj's effectiveness stems from three key innovations:

1. **Diffusion DNA as a low-dimensional difficulty surrogate.** Fixed sampling schedules misallocate compute because explicit trajectory planning in high-dimensional state spaces is intractable. CoTj introduces Diffusion DNA—a predicted per-timestep reconstruction error profile—that collapses the planning problem to tractable optimization over timestep indices. The core assumption is that DNA is an upper bound for global denoising difficulty. If predictor accuracy is low (cosine similarity <0.9), planning fails.

2. **Global shortest-path planning on a Super-DAG.** The denoising process is reformulated as a directed acyclic graph where nodes are timesteps and edges encode correction costs derived from DNA. The optimal trajectory minimizes cumulative cost, dynamically concentrating steps on high-difficulty phases. This relies on the on-manifold correction assumption: an ideal correction step exists that is superior to off-manifold continuation. Inaccurate DNA leads to suboptimal paths.

3. **Adaptive-length termination for efficient deliberation.** Instead of fixed steps, CoTj stops once the explained gain ratio \(\rho(n)\) reaches a threshold (e.g., 0.99), capturing 99% of attainable denoising gain. This yields phase-dependent step allocation: simple prompts use fewer steps, complex ones more. The gain curve must exhibit a super-linear region near the threshold; if not (e.g., for distilled models), the threshold may need adjustment.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Condition embedding → Diffusion DNA predictor (MLP) → Graph planner (DAG construction & shortest-path) → Execution solver (base diffusion sampler).

**Critical path:** Extract condition embedding from prompt → Predict DNA via MLP → Construct Super-DAG using DNA → Compute shortest path (or adaptive termination) → Obtain planned timestep sequence → Run denoising with base solver.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Predictor accuracy vs. latency: The MLP is tiny (~0.073ms) but low accuracy (cosine similarity <0.95) nullifies planning gains; may need larger model or more training data.  
- Graph density vs. planning time: DAG includes all possible jumps (\(O(T^2)\) edges). For \(T=1000\), planning may be slow; prune edges with high cost.  
- Adaptive termination sensitivity: Threshold \(\rho_{th}=0.99\) derived from Qwen-Image; other models (e.g., SD3.5) have different gain curves and may require tuning.

**Failure signatures:**  
- DNA predictor collapse: Predicted DNA has low cosine similarity (<0.8) to ground-truth; planned trajectories resemble uniform schedules.  
- Non-monotonic gain in DNA: Oscillatory or negative step-wise gain (as in SD3.5) may cause planner to choose regressive steps; post-process DNA to enforce monotonic decay.  
- Trajectory infeasibility: Planned jumps exceed solver stability (e.g., too large a step for Euler); constrain edge creation to solver's stability limits.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Validate DNA predictability: Train MLP on a subset of prompts; compute median cosine similarity on held-out prompts between predicted and ground-truth DNA (from 50-step reference). Target >0.95.  
2. Fixed-step ablation: Run base model (e.g., Qwen-Image) with Euler at 10 steps. Run CoTj with same 10 steps but using DNA-planned timesteps. Compare GenEval; expect CoTj to surpass baseline by ≥0.15 absolute.  
3. Adaptive termination curve: For 100 diverse prompts, run adaptive CoTj and record steps taken vs. \(\rho(n)\). Plot mean/variance; verify super-linear rise near \(\rho=0.99\) and that simple prompts terminate early (<10 steps) while matching uniform 10-step baseline quality.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Extending DNA to video dynamics:** The current video extension uses one-shot DNA from a reference prompt, lacking a generalizable temporal modeling component. Future work should develop video-specific DNA descriptors capturing frame coherence or motion entropy, validated on video datasets with motion complexity metrics.
2. **Integrating online feedback:** The Predict-Plan-Execute paradigm is open-loop; runtime errors or manifold drifts are not corrected. A closed-loop system that monitors intermediate reconstruction error and dynamically replans could improve robustness, especially under noise or model imperfections.
3. **Unsupervised Diffusion DNA discovery:** The predictor is supervised on prompt-DNA pairs from PickScore, which may not generalize to modalities without textual descriptions (e.g., audio, 3D). A self-supervised method extracting DNA from internal model states across unlabeled data would enable cross-modal transfer.
4. **Predictor transfer to video:** The video experiments bypass the image-trained predictor, using direct one-shot planning. It is unclear if the same MLP architecture trained on video prompt embeddings would generalize, given new temporal dependencies. Evidence: train the MLP on video prompts and compare predicted DNA against reference.
5. **Robustness to non-monotonic DNA:** Distilled models (e.g., Z-Image-Turbo) exhibit negative initial gain and persistent high gain late in diffusion, violating the monotonic decay assumption. This may lead to suboptimal trajectories. Applying CoTj to a broader set of distilled models and possibly modifying the cost function to handle non-monotonic patterns is needed.

## Limitations
- The exact training protocol for the Diffusion DNA predictor (hyperparameters, preprocessing) is omitted, hindering independent replication.
- Adaptive termination threshold (\(\rho_{th}=0.99\)) is model-specific; other models (e.g., SD3.5) show different gain patterns, requiring per-model tuning.
- Compatibility of planned non-uniform timesteps with high-order solvers (e.g., DPM-Solver) is unvalidated; current tests use Euler/UCGM only.

## Confidence
- **Claim:** Diffusion DNA predictor can achieve high accuracy (cosine similarity >0.95) with the described MLP. **Confidence:** Medium (training details missing, but reported results suggest feasibility).
- **Claim:** Graph-based trajectory planning yields optimal sampling paths for a given DNA. **Confidence:** High (theoretically grounded and empirically validated on image models).
- **Claim:** Adaptive termination at \(\rho=0.99\) universally balances speed and quality across diffusion models. **Confidence:** Low (only Qwen-Image evaluated; other models exhibit varying gain curves).
- **Claim:** CoTj generalizes to video generation without a learned predictor. **Confidence:** Medium (one-shot approach works but lacks a general video-DNA predictor).
