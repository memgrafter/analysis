# When Should We Introduce Safety Interventions During Pretraining?

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.07087
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.07087
- **Reference count:** 32
- **Primary result:** Intervention timing critically affects safety: a mid-phase start (applying safety data after 20–60% of pretraining) yields the greatest robustness under standard decoding, while starting from the very beginning (0%) maximizes steerability under safety-aware inference like SafeBeam.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates when to introduce safety interventions—such as rephrasing harmful content, refusal training, and metadata tagging—during the pretraining of language models, using a fixed underlying dataset. Experiments with a 1.7B model and a 600B token budget show that the optimal timing depends on the deployment scenario. For standard top‑k decoding, beginning safety interventions after an initial "safe‑only" phase (starting at 20%–60% of training) produces models with the lowest attack success rates and the strongest resistance to erosion after benign finetuning. In contrast, when using a safety‑aware inference method (SafeBeam), starting interventions from the very beginning (0%) yields the highest steerability toward safe outputs and the best post‑finetuning robustness. These timing choices do not cause meaningful capability degradation. The findings reveal timing as a crucial, previously overlooked curriculum decision for building safer models.

## Method Summary
The study trained 1.7B parameter models on a 600 billion token corpus derived from SmolLM2 (FineWeb‑Edu, code, math). Safety interventions were applied to a subset of harmful examples via (a) contextualized rephrasing, (b) refusal training pairs, and (c) insertion of a metadata token (e.g., `<potentially unsafe content>`). A curriculum controller varied the *start point* for mixing this safety‑augmented data into the main training stream: 0%, 20%, 60%, or 100% of the total token budget. After pretraining, models were evaluated directly (base) and after instruction tuning. Safety was assessed using HarmBench and AdvBench under standard top‑k sampling and the SafeBeam steering algorithm. Benign finetuning robustness was tested with one epoch on GSM8K. Internal representations were analyzed via linear probes trained to separate safe versus harmful examples.

## Key Results
- **Standard decoding (top‑k):** Introducing safety interventions after an initial safe‑only phase (20%–60% start) minimized attack success rates in base and instruction‑tuned models and provided the strongest robustness after benign finetuning on GSM8K.
- **Safety‑aware inference (SafeBeam):** Models with interventions starting from the beginning (0%) achieved the lowest attack success rates and the highest steerability, as measured by SafeBeam’s ability to suppress unsafe generations.
- **Representation analysis:** Earlier intervention start times increased the linear‑probe AUC separating safe and harmful representations, with the effect being most pronounced when metadata tokens were included.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Early introduction of metadata‑tagged safety interventions creates strong, well‑calibrated associations between the harmful content token and genuinely unsafe material, which improves the efficacy of safety‑aware inference like SafeBeam.
- **Mechanism:** The metadata token (e.g., `<potentially unsafe content>`) is inserted randomly into harmful examples during pretraining. Starting this process from the very beginning means the model learns this correlation throughout its entire developmental trajectory, leading to more reliable probability estimates for the token. SafeBeam then leverages these estimates to steer generation away from unsafe paths.
- **Core assumption:** The strength and reliability of the metadata token’s signal are proportional to the duration and consistency of its exposure during pretraining.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract notes that earlier interventions reshape internal representations, with linear probes more cleanly separating safe vs harmful examples, especially when metadata was included, and that for safety‑aware inference, interventions starting from the beginning maximized steerability. Section 5.5 shows that when metadata is included, the effect is more pronounced: introducing interventions from the start yields the highest linear‑probe AUC.
- **Break condition:** If the metadata token is introduced too late (e.g., 60% or 100%), its association remains weak, and SafeBeam’s beam filtering becomes less effective, as seen in Figure 2.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Introducing safety interventions after an initial phase of "safe‑only" pretraining (e.g., 20%–60%) yields models with more robust refusal behavior under standard decoding and after benign finetuning, because the model first acquires stable, general linguistic and world knowledge representations.
- **Mechanism:** An initial period on standard, high‑quality data (like FineWeb‑Edu) allows the model to build robust foundational representations of language, facts, and reasoning. Safety interventions introduced later then integrate into these already‑formed structures, creating a deeper, more entrenched behavioral pattern that is harder to overwrite during subsequent, non‑adversarial finetuning on tasks like GSM8K.
- **Core assumption:** Safety signals introduced into a "mature" representational space are integrated more meaningfully and are less susceptible to catastrophic forgetting than signals introduced into a nascent space that is still rapidly forming.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract states that for standard top‑k decoding, introducing interventions after some initial safe‑only pretraining (after 20%–60%) yields the safest base models and the clearest effects emerge after a benign finetune. Section 5.1 details this result, and the Discussion hypothesizes that delayed interventions may succeed because the model benefits from first acquiring general linguistic competence and world knowledge before safety signals are introduced.
- **Break condition:** If interventions are introduced too early (0%), they may interfere with the learning of basic linguistic patterns, leading to more superficial alignment. If introduced too late (100%), the opportunity to deeply integrate safety into the core model dynamics is missed, and the model remains highly vulnerable to finetuning erosion.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Early and consistent exposure to safety interventions (especially rephrasing/refusal training) reduces the model’s tendency to learn and exploit "shallow heuristics" for safety, leading to lower overrefusal rates on benign queries without degrading capability.
- **Mechanism:** Continuous exposure to nuanced, contextualized safety examples (where the model learns *why* something is harmful and sees refusals in conversational contexts) prevents the model from developing a brittle, token‑level shortcut (e.g., refusing any request containing a keyword). This results in better‑calibrated refusal behavior that maintains compliance on harmless prompts.
- **Core assumption:** Overrefusal is often a symptom of mis‑calibrated or oversimplified safety rules. A curriculum that weaves safety considerations throughout training promotes a more sophisticated, context‑dependent understanding of harm.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract notes that earlier interventions resulted in the lowest attack success rates after benign finetuning and no meaningful degradation. Section 5.3 shows that under standard top‑k sampling, incorporating interventions earlier (0% or 20%) yields a slight improvement in compliance rates on benign Alpaca queries, suggesting reduced overrefusal. The Discussion frames the absence of early intervention as a risk for shallow heuristics, implying early intervention combats this.
- **Break condition:** If interventions are too sparse or introduced too late, the model may still default to keyword‑based refusal patterns learned from post‑hoc alignment data or the final finetuning stage, leading to higher overrefusal.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Curriculum Learning**
  - **Why needed here:** The core hypothesis is that *timing* (the order of data exposure) matters for safety, directly borrowing from curriculum learning theory. Understanding that non‑i.i.d. training orders can induce qualitatively different model behaviors is prerequisite.
  - **Quick check:** What is the key difference between a "safe‑only" curriculum (100% start) and a "mixed" curriculum with a 20% safe‑only phase, in terms of the model’s representational state when safety data arrives?
- **Concept: Metadata‑Annotated Pretraining & Inference‑Time Steering**
  - **Why needed here:** The SafeBeam algorithm and its dependence on metadata tokens are central to one of the key findings (early start optimal). One must understand how a simple text token during pretraining can be repurposed for steering at inference.
  - **Quick check:** Explain how the `<potentially unsafe content>` token serves a dual purpose: as a normal training token and as a steering signal for SafeBeam. What does the model need to have learned about this token for SafeBeam to work effectively?
- **Concept: Linear Separability as a Probe for Internal Representations**
  - **Why needed here:** The paper uses linear probe AUC between safe/unsafe representations as a diagnostic tool to "see" the effect of timing. This connects abstract timing effects to measurable changes in the model’s internal geometry.
  - **Quick check:** If an earlier intervention start time leads to higher linear‑probe AUC for safe vs. unsafe examples in the final layer, what does this imply about the model’s internal organization of these concepts?

## Architecture Onboarding
Pretraining Corpus Mixture -> Safety Intervention Pipeline -> Curriculum Controller -> Pretraining Engine -> Evaluation Suite -> Inference Algorithms

Critical path:
1. Corpus Curation: Assemble base datasets (FineWeb‑Edu, code, math) and safety‑augmented versions (SafeWeb, RefuseWeb, MoralEducation).
2. Intervention Application: Process harmful examples into contextualized rephrasing, refusal pairs, and metadata‑annotated versions.
3. Curriculum Timing Definition: Set the start point (0%, 20%, 60%, 100%) for mixing safety data into the training stream.
4. Pretraining Run: Train model with standard autoregressive objective (LitGPT, FlashAttention‑2).
5. Base Model Evaluation: Assess safety (HarmBench, AdvBench) and capability (ARC, GSM8K) under top‑k and SafeBeam decoding.
6. Optional Instruction Tuning: Apply SFT to produce instruct‑tuned models; re‑evaluate.
7. Optional Benign Finetuning: Perform single‑epoch SFT on GSM8K; re‑evaluate safety to test robustness.
8. Representation Analysis: Train linear probes on final‑layer activations for safe/unsafe examples; compute AUC.

Design tradeoffs:
- **Early (0%) vs. Delayed (20–60%) Start:** Early start maximizes steerability (SafeBeam) and representation separability but may risk interference with foundational learning (though not observed here). Delayed start maximizes robustness under standard decoding and after benign finetuning, but sacrifices SafeBeam steerability.
- **Metadata Inclusion:** Enables powerful inference‑time steering (SafeBeam) but requires the specific curriculum timing to be effective. Without metadata, the timing effect is less pronounced.
- **Evaluation Context:** The "optimal" timing is conditional on deployment scenario (standard vs. safety‑aware inference) and post‑training pressures (benign finetuning).

Failure signatures:
- **High Attack Success Rate** (>30%) on base or instruct‑tuned models under standard top‑k decoding when using a 100% (fully safe‑only) or 0% (full early intervention) curriculum.
- **Severe Capability Drop** (>5% absolute) on benchmarks like GSM8K or ARC‑Challenge for any curriculum timing, indicating interventions are destructively interfering.
- **Extreme Overrefusal** (>40% full/partial refusal) on benign Alpaca queries for any timing under standard decoding, indicating poor calibration.
- **Collapse of SafeBeam:** For models without metadata (100% or no‑metadata variants), SafeBeam is inapplicable. For models with metadata started too late (60%+), SafeBeam shows no improvement over top‑k.

First 3 experiments:
1. **Base Model Safety Evaluation:** Run HarmBench/AdvBench with standard top‑k and SafeBeam across all 4 timing variants (0%, 20%, 60%, 100%) with metadata. Verify the U‑shaped curve where 20%/60% are best for top‑k and 0% is best for SafeBeam (Figures 1 & 3).
2. **Benign Finetuning Robustness Test:** Take each base model, perform a single epoch of SFT on GSM8K, then re‑evaluate safety (HarmBench etc.) with top‑k. Confirm the robustness benefit of the 20% timing persists post‑finetuning (Figure 2).
3. **Representation Separability Probe:** Train logistic regression probes on final‑layer representations for safe vs. unsafe examples from FineWeb. Measure AUC for all timing variants with and without metadata. Confirm the monotonic increase in AUC with earlier timing, especially with metadata (Figure 6).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What mechanisms cause intervention timing to differentially affect safety under standard decoding versus safety‑aware inference?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state in Discussion that "understanding the underlying mechanisms remains an open question" and hypothesize that delayed interventions help general competence while early metadata exposure aids SafeBeam associations.
- **Why unresolved:** No direct evidence links timing to these mechanisms; representation separability correlations do not imply causation.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Track representation changes during training; causally intervene on metadata associations; compare learning dynamics of general vs. safety concepts across timing.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Why does introducing interventions after 20–60% of pretraining maximize robustness to benign finetuning under top‑k decoding?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Figures 2 and 3 show middle‑phase starts achieve lowest attack success rates after GSM8K finetuning. The authors hypothesize this timing allows stable representations to form before safety integration.
- **Why unresolved:** Alternative explanations (e.g., data distribution) are not ruled out; the hypothesis is not tested.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Measure how finetuning gradients impact safety‑related weights for different timings; control for pre‑intervention learning by matching general capability levels at intervention start.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Do the timing effects scale to larger models and diverse pretraining corpora?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Experiments used only 1.7B models on SmolLM2 data. Scaling laws and curriculum learning suggest timing may interact with model size and data characteristics.
- **Why unresolved:** Computational limits prevented testing larger scales; generalizability is unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Repeat experiments with 3B/13B models and varied data mixtures (different harm rates, domains) to map optimal timing across conditions.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Is there a unified optimal timing across different safety interventions (rephrasing, refusal training, metadata), or does each require distinct timing?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The study combined interventions and had a metadata‑absent variant, but did not isolate timing per intervention. Non‑monotonic outcomes suggest interactions.
- **Why unresolved:** It is unclear whether timing sensitivity is universal or intervention‑specific; combined interventions confound effects.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Factorial experiments varying start time independently for each intervention type, evaluating safety metrics and representation separability for each.

## Limitations
- **Scale:** Experiments limited to a 1.7B model and 600B token budget; results may not hold for larger models or longer pretraining.
- **Intervention scope:** Only three safety approaches (rephrasing, refusal, metadata) were tested; other strategies (RLHF, DPO) were not examined.
- **Evaluation breadth:** Safety assessment focused on HarmBench/AdvBench and specific downstream tasks (GSM8K); broader capability and safety benchmarks were not used.
- **Representation probe assumptions:** Linear separability is used as a proxy for safety understanding; deeper mechanistic interpretability is lacking.
- **Statistical reliability:** Single‑seed training; variance across random seeds was not reported.

## Confidence
- **Timing effects on safety under standard decoding (mid‑phase optimal):** High (empirically robust across multiple eval sets, but limited to one model scale).
- **Timing effects on steerability with SafeBeam (early optimal):** Medium (mechanism relies on metadata token reliability; supported by linear probe but not fully disentangled).
- **No meaningful capability degradation from early interventions:** Medium (tested on standard benchmarks, but may not generalize to all tasks or larger models).

## Next Checks
1. **Scale study:** Pretrain models at 3B–13B scale with the same curriculum timings to verify whether timing effects persist, diminish, or amplify.
2. **Intervention diversity:** Include additional safety approaches (e.g., RLHF, DPO) within the curriculum to assess generalizability of timing findings beyond rephrasing/refusal/metadata.
3. **Statistical reliability:** Repeat key experiments with at least three random seeds to quantify result variance and confirm significance of timing differences, especially for SafeBeam steerability.
