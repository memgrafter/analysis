# Can LLM Agents Be CFOs? A Benchmark for Resource Allocation in Dynamic Enterprise Environments

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.23638
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23638
- **Reference count:** 28
- **Primary result:** Only 16% of evaluated LLM runs survived a 132-month enterprise simulation, while human experts achieved 100% survival; performance did not correlate with model scale.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces EnterpriseArena, a benchmark simulating a 132-month enterprise environment to test whether LLM agents can perform long-horizon resource allocation—a core CFO responsibility. Agents must balance monthly trade-offs between reconciling financial data (to improve situational awareness) and fundraising (to maintain liquidity) under partial observability and stochastic dynamics. Evaluations of 11 advanced LLMs revealed a significant capability gap: only 16% of runs survived the full horizon, and a 9B model (Qwen3.5-9B) outperformed its 397B variant. Human experts achieved perfect survival and nearly double the best LLM’s terminal valuation, indicating fundamental limitations in strategic anticipation and state management.

## Method Summary
The study builds EnterpriseArena, a closed-loop simulator modeling an enterprise with 16 data streams (financials, macro indicators, industry metrics) over 132 months. The agent operates under a constrained ReAct framework: each month it may use up to 20 tool calls to gather noisy, delayed observations, then must select exactly one action—`book_closing` (reconcile state), `fund_raising_request` (debt/equity), or `pass`. The environment applies stochastic internal noise and a deterministic exogenous economic trajectory (expansion → neutral → recession). Fundraising outcomes depend on market conditions and firm health, with settlement delays of 1–6 months. Survival requires cash never dropping below zero; performance is measured by a terminal valuation score.

## Key Results
- LLM agents achieved a survival rate of only 16% across >500 runs, compared to 100% for human experts.
- Performance was inverted relative to model scale: Qwen3.5-9B (80% survival) dramatically outperformed Qwen3.5-397B (20% survival).
- Failures clustered around months 40–60, when exogenous economic conditions shift, suggesting inadequate anticipation of regime changes.
- Humans used `book_closing` ~94% of the time and built substantial cash buffers early; LLMs under-utilized reconciliation and failed to prepare for downturns.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** LLM agents fail at the core CFO trade-off between improving situational awareness (via `book_closing`) and preserving liquidity (via fundraising).
- **Mechanism:** The environment imposes a binding constraint of one action per month. `book_closing` consumes the slot to generate an accurate financial snapshot, improving future decision quality. `fund_raising_request` consumes the slot to secure cash but risks acting on stale or incomplete internal data. Agents must explicitly choose between these competing needs each period.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract: "forcing agents to trade off information acquisition against conserving scarce resources." Section 3.3: "reconciling gives the agent a clearer picture... while raising capital improves survival odds but may be poorly timed."
- **Break condition:** An agent that consistently prioritizes `book_closing` or dynamically increases reconciliation frequency before major external shifts (e.g., before month 40–60 downturn) would demonstrate mastery.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** LLM agents inadequately anticipate distributional shifts, particularly exogenous economic regime changes, leading to clustered failures.
- **Mechanism:** The external economic layer follows a deterministic but non-stationary 132-month trajectory (expansion → neutral → recession). Fundraising success, cost, and settlement delays (1–6 months) are contingent on conditions at settlement, not request. Agents must build cash buffers *in advance* of deteriorating conditions.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 3.1: "Economic and industry indicators follow a fixed trajectory... requiring the agent to adapt." Section 4.2: "most failures cluster around month 40–60... human baseline anticipates this transition by building cash aggressively."
- **Break condition:** An agent that systematically builds a cash buffer (e.g., peak cash >$100M) before month 40 and survives the adverse period (40–60) shows effective anticipation.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Performance in this long-horizon allocation task is not monotonically related to model scale, pointing to a distinct, unsolved reasoning pattern.
- **Mechanism:** The task requires bounded rationality: maintaining a working memory of complex evolving state; planning under non-commutative action effects (sequence matters); managing exploration (tool use) vs. exploitation (action) costs. These may not scale with parametric size or general pretraining.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract: "Performance did not correlate with model scale; a 9B model... outperformed its 397B variant." Table 2: Qwen3.5-9B (78.8M score, 80% survival) >> Qwen3.5-397B (16.0M, 20%).
- **Break condition:** A consistent performance ranking across model families that correlates with a measure of *planning depth* or *state-tracking fidelity*, not model size.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP)**
  - *Why needed:* The agent never observes the true state vector. It only gets noisy, delayed, or partial views via tools; `book_closing` is the only way to reset observation to ground truth. Maintaining a belief state is critical.
  - *Quick check:* If you pass for 6 months without a `book_closing`, how confident can you be about your current cash balance and why?

- **Concept: Constrained Sequential Resource Allocation**
  - *Why needed:* The agent has two scarce resources: (1) monthly action slots (1/month), (2) a monthly tool budget (20 calls). Actions have irreversible, long-term consequences (e.g., debt increases permanent outflows). Requires budgeting these slots over 132 months.
  - *Quick check:* What is the opportunity cost of using a tool call to `review_financial_records` in a month where your cash is critically low?

- **Concept: Stochastic Dynamic Programming with Delayed Rewards**
  - *Why needed:* The reward (terminal valuation) is only realized at T=132. Actions like `fund_raising_request` have delayed settlement (1–6 months) and long-term cost implications (debt service). The optimal policy must value early information gathering that enables better future decisions.
  - *Quick check:* When is the optimal month to start a debt fundraising if you anticipate a cash shortfall in month 50, considering a stochastic 1–6 month delay?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:**
  State Tracker -> Tool Executor -> Memory Module -> Action Selector -> Environment Interface

- **Critical path (one monthly cycle):**
  1. Receive raw observations (current month, basic signals).
  2. (Optional) Use up to 20 tool calls to gather more data; tool output quality depends on `months_since_last_book_closing`.
  3. (Optional) Update internal belief state using `recall_notes` and tool outputs; save new inferences with `save_note`.
  4. **Decide and execute one action** (`book_closing`, `fund_raising_request`, or `pass`).
  5. Environment advances one month: internal indicators += noise, external indicators move along trajectory, true state updates.
  6. If action was `fund_raising_request` and successful, schedule delayed cash addition and, for debt, future interest obligations.
  7. If cash < 0 at any point post-update, **episode terminates with score 0**.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Tool budget vs. Information Value: Using 20 tools/month provides clarity but delays action; the real cost is the *action slot* spent on `book_closing` vs. fundraising.
  - Equity vs. Debt: Equity has lower success probability that decays with each round but no ongoing cost. Debt has higher success probability but creates a permanent monthly cash outflow.
  - Immediate Liquidity vs. Long-Term Flexibility: Raising capital now solves today’s gap but may be expensive (high rates during recession) or weaken the balance sheet.
  - Exploration (Tools) vs. Exploitation (Actions): Extensive tool use without action wastes the monthly slot; acting without `book_closing` risks decisions on stale data.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - Pattern A (Disengagement): Very high `pass%` (>90%) and low total actions; agent never engages. (e.g., GPT-5.4: 99.1% pass).
  - Pattern B (Analysis Paralysis): Very high tool usage/month but near-zero `book_closing%` and late, desperate fundraising. (e.g., Qwen3.5-397B: 0% book closing).
  - Pattern C (Liquidity Ignorance): Moderate action rate but fails to build cash buffer before known regime shift (month 40–60); cash trajectory flat near $15M.
  - Pattern D (Debt spiral): Uses debt heavily early, leading to unsustainable interest obligations causing later failure.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Random & Rule-Based Baselines:** Implement a random agent and a simple heuristic (e.g., "reconcile every 3 months, raise debt if cash < $5M"). Establish minimum survival bar; heuristic likely beats all LLMs.
  2. **Ablation: Force `book_closing` Schedule:** Run best LLM (Qwen3.5-9B) but force `book_closing` every N months (N=1,3,6). If survival jumps dramatically, confirms under-using reconciliation is primary bottleneck.
  3. **Sensitivity: Vary Tool Budget:** Run Qwen3.5-9B with tool budgets {5, 20, 50}. If performance degrades with lower budget, suggests tools inform *which* fundraising action to take, not just *trigger* reconciliation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- **Open Question 1:** How robust are LLM agents to extreme, low-probability "black swan" events that are not represented in the simulated environment?
- **Open Question 2:** To what extent does multi-agent collaboration (e.g., CFO with department heads) improve resource allocation performance compared to single-agent decision-making?
- **Open Question 3:** What specific cognitive or planning capabilities distinguish high-performing LLM agents (like Qwen3.5-9B) from larger but lower-performing models in long-horizon resource allocation?
- **Open Question 4:** How well do LLM agents trained or optimized on EnterpriseArena generalize to other dynamic, partially observable resource allocation domains (e.g., supply chain management, project portfolio selection)?

## Limitations
- Exact simulator mechanics (fundraising probability formulas, volatility calibrations) and the 132-month exogenous trajectories are not publicly released, forcing approximate reproduction.
- The curated dataset (16 anonymized data types, PayPal-derived initialization) is unavailable, blocking exact state replication.
- Failure mode analysis relies on aggregated metrics, not per-step decision logs, limiting diagnostic precision.

## Confidence
- **High:** Reported survival rates (16% LLM vs. 100% human) and performance inversion (Qwen 9B > 397B) are directly evidenced in results.
- **Medium:** Mechanistic claims (info/liquidity trade-off, anticipation of regime shifts) are consistent with observed failure clustering but not causally isolated via ablation.
- **Low:** Generalizability to other resource-allocation domains is speculative; the task's specific constraints may not represent broader CFO work.

## Next Checks
1. **Reconciliation schedule ablation:** Force the top-performing LLM (Qwen 9B) to execute `book_closing` at fixed intervals (e.g., every 1, 3, 6 months). A substantial survival increase would confirm that under-using information gathering is the primary bottleneck.
2. **Tool budget sensitivity:** Run the same LLM with varied monthly tool limits {5, 20, 50}. Performance degradation at lower budgets would indicate that tools are used to *inform action selection*, not merely to *trigger* reconciliation.
3. **Heuristic baseline benchmark:** Implement a simple rule-based agent (e.g., "reconcile quarterly, raise debt if cash < $10M"). If it exceeds all LLMs, this establishes a minimal competence floor and isolates a fundamental reasoning gap beyond mere engagement or tool-use deficiencies.
