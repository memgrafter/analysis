# On the Robustness of Knowledge Editing for Detoxification
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.10504
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10504
- Reference count: 21
- Primary result: Knowledge editing for detoxification frequently fails via pseudo-detoxification (degenerate generation) and exhibits poor robustness across optimisation, compositional, and cross-lingual settings.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the robustness of knowledge editing (KE) methods for detoxifying large language models. The authors introduce a comprehensive evaluation framework that assesses optimisation robustness (sensitivity to hyperparameters), compositional robustness (editing multiple unsafe behaviours), and cross-lingual robustness (transfer across languages). Using the multilingual mSAFEEDIT dataset and a degeneration detector alongside a safety classifier, they find that detoxification success is often illusory, with pseudo-detoxification—where models generate repetitive, meaningless outputs that evade toxicity classifiers—being a common failure mode. Effectiveness varies sharply with hyperparameters, degrades when multiple behaviours are jointly edited, and shows limited transfer beyond the training language. Only specific model-method combinations (e.g., Qwen2-7B with DINM) demonstrate stable robustness, while most configurations suffer from optimisation degeneration, objective interference, or language-specific editing limitations.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates two knowledge editing techniques—DINM (which identifies toxic regions via contrastive internal representations) and FT-M (a gradient-based fine-tuning variant)—across six LLMs (Llama2-7B, Llama3-8B, Mistral-7B, Ministral-8B, Qwen2-7B, Qwen3-8B). Central to the evaluation is mSAFEEDIT, a parallel dataset of 100 adversarial prompts with unsafe and safe responses translated into nine languages. The primary metric is the failure count, defined as the sum of unsafe responses (as classified by gemini-2.0-flash) and degenerate repetitive outputs detected by a custom token-level repetition detector. The framework systematically varies editing hyperparameters (learning rate, steps) to probe optimisation robustness, applies sequential edits for increasing numbers of unsafe behaviours to test compositional scaling, and compares monolingual versus cross-lingual editing scenarios. Hyperparameters are tuned on a held-out 50-item subset to minimise failures, providing a baseline for assessing robustness under distribution shift.

## Key Results
- Detoxification effectiveness is highly sensitive to editing hyperparameters; aggressive settings reduce unsafe outputs but simultaneously increase degenerate repetition, revealing a trade-off between safety and generation quality.
- Jointly editing multiple unsafe behaviours leads to objective interference, causing failure counts to rise rather than decrease monotonically for most model-method pairs (only Llama2-7B showed consistent improvement).
- Cross-lingual transfer of detoxification edits is severely limited; editing in English and evaluating in other languages yields minimal safety gains except for Qwen2-7B with DINM.
- Pseudo-detoxification via repetitive generation is a pervasive artefact, masking true detoxification failure when relying solely on toxicity classifiers.
- Robust detoxification is only observed for specific model-method combinations (e.g., Qwen2-7B with DINM), while other combinations exhibit high failure rates across robustness axes.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Optimisation-Induced Pseudo-Detoxification via Generation Degeneration
**Claim:** Apparent detoxification success can arise from optimisation-driven generation degeneration (e.g., repetition) that avoids triggering toxicity classifiers, rather than genuine suppression of harmful content.
**Mechanism:** Knowledge editing modifies model parameters to reduce toxic outputs on a training set. Over-aggressive editing (high learning rate, many steps) can collapse the generative distribution into low-entropy, repetitive patterns that are semantically neutral, thus classified as "safe" despite being degenerate failures.
**Core assumption:** Toxicity classifiers are blind to superficial linguistic safety achieved through non-meaningful, repetitive generation.
**Evidence anchors:**
- [abstract] "pseudo-detoxification as a common failure mode, where apparent toxicity reductions arise from degenerate generation behaviours rather than meaningful suppression"
- [section 5.1 & 5.2] Shows correlation between increased editing strength (LR/steps) and reduced unsafe responses alongside increased repetition; identifies trade-off.
- [corpus] Neighbors (e.g., "Model Surgery", "Detoxifying via Autoregressive Reward Guided Representation Editing") also note degeneration risks in parameter editing, but this paper provides direct systematic evidence via dedicated detector.
**Break condition:** Degeneration detector flags output as repetitive; detoxification effect vanishes when evaluating with degeneration-aware metrics (failure counts).

### Mechanism 2: Objective Interference in Compositional Editing
**Claim:** Jointly editing multiple unsafe behaviours causes interference between editing objectives, degrading overall detoxification robustness and often inducing degeneration.
**Mechanism:** Knowledge editing targets specific parameters or representations associated with a given unsafe behaviour. When multiple edits are applied sequentially to the same model instance, the parameter updates for earlier edits may be overwritten or conflict with later ones, destabilising the learned "safe" behaviour and amplifying optimisation instability.
**Core assumption:** Unsafe behaviours share overlapping or competing parameter subspaces; editing is not perfectly modular.
**Evidence anchors:**
- [abstract] "detoxification effectiveness degrades when multiple unsafe behaviours are edited jointly"
- [section 6.1] Figure 3 shows failure counts (unsafe + repetitive) do not decrease monotonically with added edits for most model-method pairs; e.g., Llama3-8B, Mistral-7B show rising repetition.
- [corpus] Neighbors on compositional/sequential editing (e.g., "Breaking mBad! Supervised Fine-tuning for Cross-Lingual Detoxification") suggest multi-objective challenges, but this paper quantifies the failure modes in KE context.
**Break condition:** Adding a new editing objective increases total failure count compared to editing a single objective.

### Mechanism 3: Language-Dependent Knowledge Editing Limits Cross-Lingual Transfer
**Claim:** Detoxification effects learned via KE in a source language (e.g., English) exhibit poor transfer to other languages, due to language-specific parameterisation of toxic representations.
**Mechanism:** Knowledge editing operations (e.g., locating toxic neurons, applying parameter updates) are likely performed in the linguistic-embedding space of the editing language. If toxic associations are encoded differently across languages (e.g., in separate neuron subsets or embedding subspaces), an edit in English may not affect the corresponding representations in, say, Bengali or Thai.
**Core assumption:** LLMs store language-specific "knowledge" in partly disjoint parameter subspaces; detoxification knowledge is not inherently shared across languages.
**Evidence anchors:**
- [abstract] "shows limited cross-lingual transfer"
- [section 6.3] Figure 5: Cross-lingual detoxification (edit in English, test in other languages) for Qwen2-7B+DINM shows reduced effectiveness vs. monolingual; other models degrade severely.
- [corpus] Neighbors like "Cross-lingual knowledge editing in large language models" hypothesise language-dependency; this paper provides empirical detox-specific evidence using mSAFEEDIT.
**Break condition:** Defence success rate in target language drops significantly (e.g., >20% relative) compared to monolingual editing in that language.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Knowledge Editing (KE) Methods**
  - **Why needed here:** The paper evaluates specific KE techniques (DINM, FT-M) for detoxification. Understanding their basic mechanisms—DINM locates toxic regions via contrastive representations; FT-M performs gradient-based fine-tuning on adversarial prompts—is essential to interpret why hyperparameter changes cause instability.
  - **Quick check question:** How does DINM's toxic-location identification differ from FT-M's approach, and why might this lead to different optimisation robustness?
- **Concept: Toxicity Classifier Limitations**
  - **Why needed here:** The core critique is that standard classifier-based evaluation masks pseudo-detoxification. One must understand that classifiers measure surface-level lexical safety, not behavioural meaningfulness.
  - **Quick check question:** Why would a perfectly safe but degenerate output like "Car. Car. Car." still be classified as safe by a toxicity classifier?
- **Concept: Multilingual Model Representations**
  - **Why needed here:** Cross-lingual robustness hinges on whether toxic knowledge is stored in shared or language-specific ways. Foundational knowledge of how LLMs encode multilingual semantics is key.
  - **Quick check question:** What evidence from multilingual probing studies suggests that factual or behavioural knowledge might be language-dependent in LLMs?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**
Models (Llama2-7B, Llama3-8B, Mistral-7B, Ministral-8B, Qwen2-7B, Qwen3-8B) + KE Methods (DINM, FT-M) + Data (mSAFEEDIT) -> Generation -> Evaluators (gemini-2.0-flash safety classifier, repetition detector) -> Metrics (Failure counts, OOD generalisation).

**Critical path:**
1. Prepare mSAFEEDIT: translate attacks/harmful questions/safe responses into target languages via gemini-2.0-flash; verify quality.
2. For each (model, method, language) pair: perform KE using selected hyperparameters (from prior tuning).
3. Generate responses for all test attacks.
4. Run safety classifier and degeneration detector; compute failure counts and OOD generalisation.
5. Compare pre/post-KE; analyse failure modes.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Classifier vs. Degeneration Detection:** Relying solely on toxicity classifiers risks missing pseudo-detoxification. Adding a lightweight, conservative repetition detector is crucial but may miss non-repetitive degeneration.
- **Computational Cost vs. Coverage:** KE edits one instance at a time; limiting to 100 test items is necessary for feasibility but may reduce statistical power. Multilingual evaluation multiplies cost.
- **Hyperparameter Tuning Scope:** Tuning for minimal failures on a held-out set (50 items) is used to select settings for main experiments, but effectiveness can still vary across datasets.

**Failure signatures:**
- **Pseudo-detoxification:** Low unsafe count + high repetition count. Model outputs semantically empty loops.
- **Optimisation failure:** Unsafe count does not decrease with increased LR/steps; or decreases only with accompanying rise in repetition.
- **Compositional failure:** After sequential multi-objective editing, total failures increase instead of decreasing monotonically.
- **Cross-lingual failure:** Post-English-edit, evaluation in non-English languages shows unsafe count nearly unchanged vs. pre-edit.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Optimisation Robustness Sweep:** For a fixed model-method, vary learning rate (1e-5 to 1e-3) and editing steps (1-15) on a 50-item English subset. Plot unsafe count and repetition count; identify trade-off curve.
2. **Compositional Scaling:** For a model-method, start with model edited on 1 unsafe behaviour, then sequentially add 2nd, 3rd,..., 10th behaviour (each edit applied to current model). After each addition, evaluate on full 10-item set. Plot total failures vs. number of edits.
3. **Cross-lingual Transfer:** For Qwen2-7B+DINM, edit on English 100-item set. Evaluate on the same 100 attacks translated into each target language (es, fr, bn, hi, zh, th, ms, vi). Compare failure counts to monolingual editing for each target.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What underlying mechanisms cause pseudo-detoxification, and can we develop more comprehensive degeneration detectors that capture failure modes beyond simple repetition?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper identifies pseudo-detoxification as a failure mode where detoxification appears successful due to degenerate generation (e.g., repetition) rather than genuine behavioural suppression. Their detector focuses on repetition but notes other forms of degeneration exist.
- **Why unresolved:** The current detector is intentionally conservative and targets only extreme repetition. The paper acknowledges that other degeneration types (e.g., incoherence, loss of general capability) are possible but not systematically measured. Without a fuller picture, it is unclear how pervasive pseudo-detoxification truly is across different models and editing methods.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A detailed analysis correlating various degeneration metrics (e.g., perplexity, semantic coherence, task performance drops) with safety classifier scores across multiple editing configurations and models. Human evaluations of output quality alongside safety judgments would also help calibrate the true scope of pseudo-detoxification.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can knowledge editing methods be made optimisation-robust, achieving stable detoxification without sensitive dependence on hyperparameters that trigger degeneration?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper shows a clear trade-off: increasing learning rate/editing steps reduces toxic outputs but increases repetition, indicating detoxification success is often optimisation artefact. Performance is highly sensitive to hyperparameters.
- **Why unresolved:** The study tunes hyperparameters per model/method to minimise failures, but this sensitivity itself is the problem—robustness requires stable outcomes under a range of reasonable settings. The fundamental reason for this instability (e.g., overfitting to editing data, loss landscape issues) is not diagnosed, nor are general strategies to decouple detoxification from degeneration proposed.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An ablation study isolating the contribution of different optimisation components (e.g., loss function design, constraint regularisation) to both toxicity reduction and degeneration. Theoretical or empirical analysis linking editing magnitude to changes in the model’s generation dynamics. Development of objective functions that explicitly penalise degeneration.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Why does compositional robustness fail for most model-method combinations when editing multiple unsafe behaviours, and can editing techniques be designed to scale to many objectives without interference?
- **Basis in paper:** Joint editing of increasing numbers of unsafe behaviours leads to rising failure counts for 4 out of 5 models with DINM. Only Llama2-7B showed monotonic improvement. The paper states edits "interfere" but does not explain why.
- **Why unresolved:** The interference could stem from overlapping or conflicting internal representations for different toxic behaviours, cumulative parameter updates destabilising the model, or compounding degeneration risks. The paper tests sequential editing on a single set of 10 items, but the exact failure mechanism (catastrophic interference, localisation limits, etc.) remains speculative.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Probing the internal representations (e.g., activation differences) of models after single vs. multiple edits to see if toxic features for different behaviours overlap or diverge. Testing if performance degrades linearly, sublinearly, or catastrophically with the number of edit instances. Comparing different editing algorithms (e.g., those with stronger locality guarantees) on compositional scaling.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What model or methodological factors determine cross-lingual robustness in knowledge editing for detoxification, and can we achieve reliable cross-lingual transfer without per-language editing?
- **Basis in paper:** Cross-lingual detoxification (editing in English, evaluating in other languages) is largely ineffective except for Qwen2-7B with DINM. The paper suggests language-dependent editing as a hypothesis but does not analyse why Qwen2-7B is an outlier.
- **Why unresolved:** The study covers diverse languages and models but leaves the causal factors unexplored. Is Qwen2-7B’s architecture or pre-training data more language-agnostic? Does DINM’s toxic location identification inherently find more language-independent features? Without ablation or analysis, this remains an observed pattern without explanation.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Comparative analysis of internal representations (e.g., probing multilingual neuron activations) for models that show vs. do not show cross-lingual transfer. Evaluating if editing in a high-resource language (English) versus the target language changes outcomes for different models. Testing if multilingual models with explicit shared-parameter training exhibit better transfer.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Do other knowledge editing methods (beyond DINM and FT-M) exhibit better robustness along these three dimensions, and what are the trade-offs between editing paradigms (factual vs. behavioural)?
- **Basis in paper:** The evaluation is limited to DINM and FT-M due to practical constraints and compatibility. Other KE methods (e.g., MEND, Ext-Sub) were excluded because they rely on auxiliary networks or retrieval that are less compatible with the multilingual setting.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper’s conclusions about KE-based detoxification’s fragility are based on only two methods. It is unknown if alternative KE approaches (e.g., those with better locality, different optimisation schemes, or memory-based updates) might avoid the observed trade-offs. The inherent suitability of factual editing techniques (like ROME/MEMIT) for behavioural detoxification remains an open design question.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A follow-up study integrating a broader set of KE methods into the same robustness framework. This would test if the observed fragility is universal or method-specific. Comparative analysis of how different editing logics (e.g., direct parameter modification vs. retrieval augmentation vs. in-context learning) handle optimisation, composition, and cross-lingual challenges.

## Limitations
- mSAFEEDIT dataset is not publicly released; reconstructed translations may diverge from the original used in experiments, affecting reproducibility of classifier and detector outcomes.
- Key methods (DINM, FT-M) lack full implementation details, risking reimplementation drift that could alter hyperparameter sensitivities and robustness conclusions.
- Evaluation relies on a single safety classifier (gemini-2.0-flash) and a simple repetition detector, potentially missing other degeneration forms (e.g., incoherence, capability loss).
- Experiments use small test sets (100 items per language) and 7–8B parameter models, limiting generalisability to larger or multilingual-optimised LLMs.

## Confidence
- Optimisation-induced pseudo-detoxification: **High** – systematic hyperparameter sweeps with a dedicated degeneration detector directly establish the trade-off between safety reduction and repetition.
- Compositional interference: **Medium** – sequential editing experiments show rising failures for most models, but direct mechanistic evidence (e.g., parameter overlap analyses) is absent.
- Cross-lingual transfer limits: **Medium** – consistent trends across nine languages, yet small per-language sample size and lack of probing analyses temper certainty.

## Next Checks
1. Expand evaluation to additional KE methods (e.g., ROME, MEMIT) and larger models (≥13B) to test if fragility is universal or method/model-specific.
2. Conduct ablations on editing order and parameter overlap (e.g., using CKA similarity) to diagnose the root cause of compositional interference.
3. Scale multilingual evaluation to >20 languages and use multiple toxicity classifiers (Detoxify, Perspective API) to validate cross-lingual robustness deficits and classifier dependence.
