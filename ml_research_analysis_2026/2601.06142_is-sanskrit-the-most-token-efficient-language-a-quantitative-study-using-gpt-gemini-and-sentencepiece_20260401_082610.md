# Is Sanskrit the most token-efficient language? A quantitative study using GPT, Gemini, and SentencePiece

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.06142
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06142
- **Reference Count:** 1
- **Primary Result:** Under an unbiased morphology-aware tokenizer (SentencePiece), Sanskrit achieves ~5.07 characters per token, ~50% more compact than English, but production tokenizers (GPT cl100k base, Gemini) fragment Sanskrit, imposing a severe "token tax."

## Executive Summary
This study quantifies the token efficiency of Sanskrit relative to English and Hindi using a parallel corpus of 701 verses from the Bhagavad Gita. It finds that when tokenized with a language-specific, morphology-agnostic SentencePiece model, Sanskrit demonstrates significantly higher information density (~5.07 characters per token) compared to English and Hindi. However, production tokenizers from GPT and Gemini exhibit a systemic bias, fragmenting Sanskrit text into many more tokens, making it appear less efficient. Translating Sanskrit into English or Hindi also results in a dramatic "semantic expansion," requiring ~20 times more tokens to express equivalent meaning due to fundamental grammatical differences.

## Method Summary
The study uses a parallel corpus of Sanskrit (Devanagari and transliteration), English, and Hindi text from the Bhagavad Gita, including both verses and commentaries. Tokenization is performed using four tokenizers: separate SentencePiece BPE models (8k vocab, whitespace-agnostic) for each language, GPT's `cl100k_base` and `o200k_base` encodings, and the Gemini Pro tokenizer. Key metrics computed are tokens per verse/segment, characters per token (CpT), tokens per character (TpC), and a "semantic expansion factor" for commentaries. The SentencePiece models serve as an unbiased baseline against which the bias of production tokenizers is measured.

## Key Results
- Under SentencePiece, Sanskrit achieves a mean of 5.07 characters per token and requires ~21.67 tokens per verse, approximately 50% fewer than English (41.96 tokens), confirming its high compactness.
- GPT's `cl100k_base` tokenizer severely inverts efficiency, assigning Sanskrit only 1.13 CpT and 111.46 tokens per verse—making it appear 2.41x *less* compact than English.
- English and Hindi commentaries require approximately 20x more tokens than the original Sanskrit verse, demonstrating a massive semantic expansion effect.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Production Tokenizer Bias
- **Claim:** Production tokenizers (GPT, Gemini) are systemically biased against Sanskrit because their subword vocabularies are optimized for English-dominant training corpora.
- **Mechanism:** Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) merges frequent byte sequences from its training data. With English-heavy data, the vocabulary saturates with English n-grams. Sanskrit's regular, concatenative morphology produces character sequences statistically infrequent in English corpora, causing fragmentation into many small, inefficient subword units.
- **Evidence:** The abstract notes tokenizer behavior varies across languages, and section 4 uses whitespace-agnostic SPM as an unbiased baseline. The stark contrast between SPM and `cl100k_base` results (5.07 vs. 1.13 CpT) is the primary evidence.
- **Break condition:** Bias diminishes if tokenizers are trained with representational parity for Sanskrit or incorporate explicit morphological priors.

### Mechanism 2: Sanskrit's Inherent Compactness
- **Claim:** Sanskrit's grammatical structure (Pāṇini's *Aṣṭādhyāyī*) inherently packs more semantic information per written character.
- **Mechanism:** As a synthetic language, Sanskrit uses agglutination and inflection (*Vibhakti* case system) to merge subject, object, verb, tense, etc., into single words. A morphology-aware tokenizer can represent this compressed package in fewer tokens than analytical languages.
- **Evidence:** The abstract states Sanskrit is "hypothesized to express more meaning per token." Section 7 details *Vibhakti* and deterministic *Sandhi* rules as the basis for compression. The high CpT for Sanskrit under SPM supports this.
- **Break condition:** If Sanskrit translations required similar token counts as originals under an ideal tokenizer, the claim would be falsified. The large expansion factors (~20x) contradict this.

### Mechanism 3: Semantic Expansion in Translation
- **Claim:** Translating dense Sanskrit into analytical languages like English or Hindi causes a dramatic token count increase.
- **Mechanism:** The single-token semantic package in Sanskrit (e.g., a conjugated verb) must be rendered as a multi-word phrase in analytical languages due to grammatical typology differences (synthetic vs. analytic).
- **Evidence:** The abstract notes ~20x token expansion for commentaries. Section 6.2 defines the "semantic expansion factor" and shows consistent results under the SPM baseline.
- **Break condition:** The expansion factor may be lower for different Sanskrit genres (e.g., modern prose) or target languages with more synthetic structures.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Parallel Corpus (701 verses x 4 languages) -> Tokenizer Engine (SPM/OpenAI/Google) -> Metric Calculator (token count, char count, CpT/TpC) -> Analysis Module (aggregation, comparison, summary)
- **Critical path:** Data integrity is the first dependency. Truly parallel, semantically equivalent segments are essential. Next is consistent tokenizer invocation (same version, settings, no chat formatting) across all inputs.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Vocabulary Size vs. Per-Language Performance:** A single shared vocabulary (production tokenizers) favors high-resource languages. Separate per-language SPM models remove bias but aren't deployable in a single multilingual model.
  - **Script Handling:** Tokenizers splitting on whitespace are disadvantaged for languages without explicit boundaries (e.g., Sanskrit Devanagari). SPM's whitespace-agnostic training is key for fairness.
  - **Baseline Selection:** Language-specific SPM models are a clean baseline but isolate raw segmentation logic from real-world multilingual compression trade-offs.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Symptom:** A language shows dramatically higher TpC (tokens per character) than others under a tokenizer.
  - **Likely cause:** The tokenizer's vocabulary lacks common subwords for that language's character combinations, causing fragmentation.
  - **Validation:** Compare vocabulary histogram for that language's corpus; expect a long-tail distribution with many low-frequency tokens.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Replicate Core Finding:** For the dataset, compute mean CpT for Sanskrit vs. English under SPM (expect ~5.0 vs ~2.5) and under `cl100k_base` (expect ~1.1 vs ~2.0).
  2. **Vocabulary Size Sensitivity:** Retrain SPM models with smaller (4k) and larger (16k) vocabularies. Does the Sanskrit advantage persist, and does the gap with `o200k_base` change?
  3. **Script Variant Test:** Tokenize the same Sanskrit content in Devanagari, IAST transliteration (Latin), and a spaced version. Isolates impact of script and word-boundary handling.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1:** Can a morphology-aware tokenizer that integrates Panini's grammatical rules (e.g., Sandhi and Vibhakti) achieve higher token efficiency for Sanskrit than subword tokenizers like SentencePiece?
  - **Basis:** Future Work proposes a "grammar and morphology-aware tokenizer" leveraging Panini's sutras.
  - **Why unresolved:** No existing tokenizer incorporates such explicit linguistic knowledge; unknown if gains are realizable without prohibitive overhead.
  - **Resolution evidence:** Implement and evaluate such a tokenizer on a large, diverse Sanskrit corpus, comparing token counts, CpT, and computational cost against SPM and production tokenizers.
- **Question 2:** Does training a large language model exclusively on Sanskrit text yield comparable or better performance on NLP tasks while requiring fewer parameters and lower inference costs than models trained on English or multilingual data?
  - **Basis:** Authors suggest exploring "Native Sanskrit Foundation Models" to test if density leads to more compact models.
  - **Why unresolved:** Based solely on tokenization metrics; unclear if compactness translates to model efficiency or limits capability due to data scarcity.
  - **Resolution evidence:** Train LLMs of similar architectures on massive Sanskrit corpora, measure parameter efficiency, training compute, inference latency, and task performance versus English/multilingual baselines.
- **Question 3:** How generalizable are the reported token efficiency advantages of Sanskrit to other domains and tokenizer configurations beyond the Bhagavad Gita?
  - **Basis:** Study uses a single dataset (Bhagavad Gita), acknowledged as a limitation. Tokenizer hyperparameters may also affect results.
  - **Why unresolved:** The Gita is a specific classical poetic/religious genre; Sanskrit usage varies widely. Tokenizer bias may differ with training data and vocab size.
  - **Resolution evidence:** Extend experiments to a large, multi-domain Sanskrit corpus (including contemporary texts) with a range of tokenizers and controlled vocabulary sizes, then compare consistency of the efficiency gap.

## Limitations
- Exact SentencePiece training hyperparameters (normalization rule, byte fallback) are unspecified; deviations could alter efficiency.
- Precise Gemini tokenizer version (gemini-pro vs. gemini-1.5-pro) and API access method are ambiguous, potentially affecting bias measurement.
- Parallelism of the 701 Bhagavad Gita verses is assumed but not independently verified; translation variance could influence expansion factors.

## Confidence
- **Sanskrit's Inherent Compactness** (higher CpT under SPM): **Medium**. Supported by SPM results, but confidence reduced by unspecified SPM training choices.
- **Systemic Production Tokenizer Bias** (GPT/Gemini fragment Sanskrit): **High** for `cl100k_base` (reproducible). **Medium** for `o200k_base` and Gemini due to version/API opacity.
- **Semantic Expansion Factor** (~20× tokens for commentary vs. verse): **Medium**. Consistent under SPM but contingent on corpus quality; unlikely to generalize to all text types.
