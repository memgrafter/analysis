# Learning to Stay Safe: Adaptive Regularization Against Safety Degradation during Fine-Tuning
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.17546
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17546
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Adaptive regularization reduces attack success rate from ~97% to 1–9% across five models while preserving task utility.

## Executive Summary
Instruction-following LLMs suffer severe safety degradation during standard supervised fine-tuning (SFT), with attack success rates exceeding 96% on harmful benchmarks. This work introduces adaptive regularization, a dynamic framework that modulates the balance between task loss and a KL penalty to a safe reference policy based on a training-time risk signal from a critic. Two critics are instantiated: an activation-based linear probe and a judge-based LLM scorer. Across five models, the method reduces attack success rates to 1–9% on HEx-PHI—matching initial safety—while maintaining competitive task performance on mixed datasets. The approach is robust to learning rates and adds negligible computational overhead.

## Method Summary
Adaptive regularization replaces static regularization weights with dynamic ones. During SFT, a risk estimator (critic) predicts harmfulness at each step from either hidden activations (via a linear probe) or generated outputs (via an external LLM judge). The risk score \( s_t \) determines weights \( \alpha_t \) (for negative log-likelihood task loss) and \( \beta_t \) (for KL divergence to the safe reference policy). High risk strengthens KL regularization, constraining updates away from harmful directions; low risk emphasizes task adaptation. This allows the model to stay safe on dangerous examples without sacrificing utility on benign ones.

## Key Results
- Reduces attack success rate on HEx-PHI from ~97% (SFT) to 1–9% across Phi-3.5-mini, Llama-3.1-8B, Llama-3.2-3B, Qwen2.5-7B/3B.
- Maintains task utility on mixed datasets (harmful ratios 1–9%) with Alpaca win rates 0.53–0.80, comparable to SFT.
- Both activation-based and judge-based critics are effective; method robust to learning rates and adds negligible overhead.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Adaptive regularization works by coupling safety enforcement to real-time risk perception during fine-tuning. The critic acts as an early warning system, detecting tendencies toward harmful outputs before they fully manifest. By increasing KL regularization when risk is high, the method prevents the model from straying too far from the safe reference policy on dangerous examples, while allowing normal task adaptation on benign data. This dynamic balance avoids the fixed trade-off of static regularization, which either over-penalizes and hurts utility or under-penalizes and compromises safety.

## Foundational Learning
- **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)**: Needed to adapt the model to downstream tasks. Quick check: Training loss decreases steadily.
- **KL Divergence Regularization**: Needed to penalize deviation from a reference (safe) policy. Quick check: KL term in loss; typical values around 0.1–0.5.
- **Risk Estimation via Critics**: Needed to detect harmful intent during training. Quick check: AUROC >0.9 for activation-based critic on validation set.
- **Adaptive Weight Modulation**: Needed to balance task loss and safety regularization dynamically. Quick check: αt/βt ratios correlate with risk scores; higher risk yields lower αt/βt.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Input batch -> Model (with reference policy) -> Critic (activation-based or judge-based) -> Risk score s_t -> Weight computation (α_t, β_t) -> Combined loss L = α_t * NLL + β_t * KL -> Gradient update -> Model parameters.
Critical path: For each training step: (1) Forward pass to get hidden states or outputs; (2) Critic computes s_t; (3) Compute α_t, β_t from s_t; (4) Compute NLL and KL; (5) Form combined loss; (6) Backpropagate and update.
Design tradeoffs: Activation-based critic is fast and model-integrated but may require probe training; judge-based critic is flexible but depends on external LLM and adds latency. Risk thresholding parameters affect safety-utility trade-off.
Failure signatures: Persistent high ASR indicates poor risk estimation (critic not detecting harm); drop in win rates suggests over-regularization (α_t too low on benign data).

## Limitations
- Scope limited to SFT; applicability to RLHF or preference tuning is untested.
- Dependence on a safe reference policy; performance with a misaligned starting model is unknown.
- Evaluation confined to curated benchmarks and synthetic mixtures; real-world noisy harmful data lacking.

## Confidence
- SFT causes severe safety degradation (ASR >96%): High
- Adaptive regularization preserves safety (ASR 1–9%) and utility (win rates 0.53–0.80): High
- Activation-based critic predicts harmful intent with >0.9 AUROC: Medium
- Method robust to learning rates and adds negligible overhead: Medium
