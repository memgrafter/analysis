# Marked Pedagogies: Examining Linguistic Biases in Personalized Automated Writing Feedback

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.12471
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12471
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** LLMs exhibit systematic, stereotype-aligned lexical shifts in personalized writing feedback, termed "Marked Pedagogies," including over-praise and feedback withholding for marginalized student groups.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates linguistic biases in personalized automated writing feedback generated by four large language models (GPT-4o, GPT-3.5-turbo, Llama-3.3-70B, Llama-3.1-8B). Using 600 identical eighth-grade essays from the PERSUADE dataset, the researchers varied student descriptors (e.g., race, learning needs, motivation) in prompts and analyzed the resulting feedback with a "Marked Words" framework based on log-odds analysis. They found that specifying a Black student increased the concentration of associated marked words by 180% (p<0.001), while specifying low achievement increased it by 22% (p<0.001). Even baseline feedback (no attribute) exhibited significant marked language (e.g., 10.6% concentration for low achievement), indicating that default pedagogical assumptions embed bias. The findings reveal that LLMs adapt feedback in ways that reinforce stereotypes, such as over-praising and withholding substantive critique for students from marginalized groups.

## Method Summary
The study employed a controlled experiment with identical essays and systematically varied prompts. For each of six attribute families (e.g., race, achievement), three prompt conditions were used: baseline (no descriptor), marked (e.g., "The student is Black"), and comparative (e.g., "The student is White"). Feedback was generated from four LLMs using zero-shot prompting (temperature=1.0). To identify biased language, the authors computed log-odds ratios of word frequencies between marked and comparative corpora, extracting top distinctive words as "Marked Words" (|z|>1.96, frequency ≥30). They defined a concentration metric (percentage of feedback tokens belonging to each marked word set) and used linear regression to estimate the effect of prompt condition on concentration, controlling for essay quality and true student demographics.

## Key Results
- Specifying a Black student increased the concentration of associated marked words by 180% (p<0.001).
- Specifying low achievement increased its marked words by 22% (p<0.001).
- Baseline feedback exhibited significant marked language (e.g., 10.6% concentration for low achievement), reflecting inherent "unmarked" pedagogical biases.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Varying prompt conditions with student attribute descriptors (e.g., "Black", "Low Achievement") triggers LLMs to retrieve and amplify stereotypical lexical associations from training data.
- **Mechanism:** The prompt acts as a conditioning signal that activates latent representational patterns in the model. When a socially marked attribute is specified, the model's next-token predictions shift toward words statistically co-occurring with that group in its training corpus, resulting in systematic differences in feedback language.
- **Core assumption:** LLMs encode and reproduce societal stereotypes as probabilistic word associations, which can be surface-activated by identity-related prompts.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "systematic, stereotype-aligned lexical shifts" and "feedback for students marked by race, language, or disability often exhibited positive feedback bias and feedback withholding bias."
  - [section 3.1] "We systematically varied the inclusion of student descriptors" and "For each essay, we generated feedback using all three prompt conditions—baseline, marked, and comparative—holding essay content constant."
  - [corpus] Evidence is indirect; neighbor corpus papers study automated feedback but not specifically stereotype activation via prompting. Direct evidence is the paper's own log-odds analysis.
- **Break condition:** If the LLM's internal representations are successfully debiased at a associative level, or if the prompting strategy explicitly instructs neutral, attribute-agnostic feedback.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Baseline feedback (no attribute specified) already contains embedded "Marked Pedagogies" because default prompt assumptions align with institutional norms (e.g., Whiteness, high achievement, maleness as unmarked).
- **Mechanism:** The baseline prompt ("You are a middle school English Language Arts teacher") invokes a default pedagogical stance that implicitly caters to a normative student profile, reflected in lexical concentrations (e.g., 10.6% for LowAchievement words). This reveals that even "neutral" personalization carries a marked orientation.
- **Core assumption:** Sociolinguistic markedness operates at the level of prompt design and model priors; the unmarked condition is itself a specific pedagogical mode.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Even the baseline (no attribute) feedback exhibited significant concentrations of marked language, such as a 10.6% concentration for the 'low achievement' pedagogy."
  - [section 4.4] Table 6 shows baseline mean concentrations for each marked word set (𝑀𝑠), e.g., 𝑀𝐿𝑜𝑤𝐴𝑐ℎ𝑖𝑒𝑣𝑒𝑚𝑒𝑛𝑡 = 10.609%.
  - [corpus] Weak; neighbor corpus does not address baseline bias in LLM feedback.
- **Break condition:** If the baseline prompt is re-engineered to explicitly mandate equitable, multi-level feedback for an unknown student, or if the model is fine-tuned to suppress default stereotype-aligned language.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Lexical concentration metrics (𝐶𝑠(𝐹)) reliably quantify the degree to which feedback embodies a specific Marked Pedagogy, and increases in concentration directly correspond to the model's perceived "markedness" of the student.
- **Mechanism:** By defining 𝑀𝑠 as the top 20 distinctive words for a marked condition, 𝐶𝑠(𝐹) measures the proportional reliance on that stereotypical lexicon. Regression analysis shows that explicit marked prompts significantly increase concentration relative to baseline and comparative prompts, isolating the prompting effect.
- **Core assumption:** A small set of high-log-odds words adequately captures a coherent pedagogical stance; concentration is a valid proxy for bias intensity.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.4] "We defined a simple concentration metric... Higher values indicate that feedback relies more heavily on words that most strongly distinguish marked from comparative conditions."
  - [section 4.4] Table 6 regression estimates: e.g., Black marked prompt +1.872pp (p<0.001) in 𝑀𝐵𝑙𝑎𝑐𝑘 concentration vs. baseline.
  - [corpus] Not applicable; this is the paper's own metric.
- **Break condition:** If the top 20 words are not thematically consistent or if concentration changes do not correlate with human judgments of bias.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Markedness (Sociolinguistics)
  - **Why needed here:** The entire framework rests on the idea that dominant identities/attributes are unmarked (default) while others are marked (deviant from norm). This theoretical lens explains why feedback for "White" or "High Achievement" serves as a comparative baseline and why marked attributes trigger compensatory language.
  - **Quick check question:** If "male" is the unmarked gender in a prompt, what lexical shifts might you expect when prompting for "female" compared to "male"?
- **Concept:** Log-odds ratio with informative Dirichlet prior (Monroe et al. 2008)
  - **Why needed here:** This is the core statistical method for identifying "Marked Words." It robustly determines which words are significantly over- or under-represented in one corpus (e.g., feedback for Black students) versus another (e.g., for White students), controlling for word frequency variance.
  - **Quick check question:** What does a positive z-score for a word in the Low Achievement vs. High Achievement comparison imply about that word's association?
- **Concept:** Feedback withholding and positive feedback bias (from teacher bias literature)
  - **Why needed here:** The paper links observed LLM patterns (over-praise, less critique for marked groups) to documented human teacher biases. This connection frames the findings as reproducing harmful pedagogical practices, not just arbitrary lexical differences.
  - **Quick check question:** How might "feedback withholding bias" manifest in the lexical choices for a student labeled with a learning disability?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** PERSUADE essays + prompts → LLM feedback generation → Log-odds analysis & word filtering → Concentration calculation → Linear regression
- **Critical path:** Essay sampling → Prompt generation (6 attribute families) → LLM feedback generation → Corpus assembly per condition → Log-odds analysis → Word list curation → Concentration calculation → Regression analysis.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Word list size (top 20):** Balances interpretability and coverage; larger lists may include noise, smaller lists may miss thematic signals.
  - **Pooling across prompts/models:** Increases power but may obscure prompt- or model-specific patterns; analysis by subset (Section 3.5.2) used for stability checks.
  - **Controlling for true student characteristics:** Necessary to isolate prompt effect, but true attributes are latent (inferred from essay) and may confound if imperfectly measured.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Low word overlap (<50%) between models for the same attribute, indicating unstable Marked Pedagogy.
  - Baseline concentration for a marked word set is high (e.g., >5%), suggesting the "unmarked" prompt itself is stereotyped.
  - Comparative prompt (e.g., "White") increases concentration for the marked word set (e.g., 𝑀𝐵𝑙𝑎𝑐𝑘), indicating race salience effects beyond simple stereotyping.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Sample Size Sensitivity Replication:** Run the log-odds pipeline on incremental essay subsets (n=100, 200, 300, 600) to confirm signal stability for your target attribute; expect plateau in significant word proportion.
  2. **Intersectionality Stress Test:** Prompt with combined attributes (e.g., "Black + Low Achievement") and analyze whether Marked Pedagogy words combine additively, synergistically, or antagonistically.
  3. **Name-Only Probe:** Generate feedback using only stereotypical names (e.g., "Lakisha" vs. "Emily") without explicit attribute descriptors; measure if concentration in 𝑀𝑠 increases, testing the minimal cue needed to elicit bias.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** To what extent do the identified "Marked Pedagogies" generalize beyond the specific middle school persuasive essays and U.S. stereotype context studied?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "Our analyses drew on two writing assignments from a single dataset of middle school persuasive essays... Future work should examine whether these findings generalize to other genres, grade levels, and assignments... Our attribute set was necessarily selective... Numerous additional student attributes and intersectional combinations remain to be explored."
- **Why unresolved:** The study's external validity is limited by its narrow focus on two prompts from one dataset (PERSUADE), a single country's (U.S.) stereotypes, and a specific age group (eighth grade). The pedagogical implications for different writing genres (e.g., narrative, poetry), educational levels (elementary, high school, college), or cultural contexts are unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A multi-site, multi-genre replication study using analogous methodologies across diverse student populations, grade levels, and writing tasks, coupled with qualitative validation from educators in those contexts.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How do fundamental architectural or training differences between LLM families (e.g., decoder-only vs. other architectures, reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) vs. supervised fine-tuning) systematically influence the expression and magnitude of Marked Pedagogies?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "We evaluated only four large language models... future work should quantify model-level differences." The paper notes some lexical variation but consistent thematic patterns across the four models tested (GPT-4o, GPT-3.5-turbo, Llama-3.3-70B, Llama-3.1-8B). However, these models share broad similarities (e.g., all are decoder-only transformers heavily RLHF-tuned).
- **Why unresolved:** The study demonstrates consistency across a small, non-diverse set of contemporary, large-scale models but does not probe the mechanistic origins of the bias. It is unknown if the patterns are inherent to next-token prediction, exacerbated by specific fine-tuning objectives, or vary with model scale or data curation.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A controlled ablation study comparing base models (pre-RLHF) to their RLHF-tuned counterparts, or models with identical architectures but trained on differently curated datasets (e.g., with/without educational text), measuring Marked Pedagogy concentration metrics.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How do Marked Pedagogies manifest and interact when student attributes are presented intersectionally (e.g., a "Black," "low-achieving" student with a "learning disability"), rather than in the singular, isolated manner tested?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "We examined singular attributes... even though these attributes are intersectional in real-world settings." The experimental design isolated each attribute to identify its primary lexical signal, which is a strength for causal attribution but a limitation for ecological validity.
- **Why unresolved:** Real student identities are multi-faceted. The compounded or mitigating effects of multiple marked attributes on feedback language are unknown. For instance, does specifying both "Black" and "low achievement" produce additive, synergistic, or contradictory marked language?
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Generating feedback with prompts containing combinations of two or more marked attributes (e.g., "Black & low achievement," "ELL & female") and applying the marked words analysis to both main effects and interaction terms. Qualitative analysis would be needed to interpret compound stereotypes.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What is the causal impact of receiving LLM feedback exhibiting specific Marked Pedagogies (e.g., feedback withholding for Black students) on students' writing development, self-efficacy, and sense of belonging?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper establishes the *existence* and *nature* of bias in feedback text ("feedback withholding bias... overuse of praise") and discusses potential harms by analogy to teacher bias literature (e.g., "lowered expectations... can directly impact learning under stereotype threat"). However, it does not measure any student outcomes.
- **Why unresolved:** The study analyzes model outputs only, not human responses. The pedagogical harms are inferred from content analysis and parallels to human-teacher bias research, but the actual effect of such feedback on learners remains an open empirical question. Could some students find certain "marked" praise motivating? Without experimental studies with students, these remain speculative concerns.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A randomized controlled trial where students receive either bias-affected feedback (from marked prompts) or neutral/baseline feedback on identical essays, with pre- and post-measures of writing performance, revision quality, self-efficacy, and affective responses (e.g., motivation, sense of teacher expectations).

## Limitations
- **Metric validity:** The core "Marked Words" (top 20 log-odds) and "concentration" metric lack external validation; it is unclear if these lexical clusters coherently represent harmful bias or merely correlate with stylistic adjustments (e.g., complexity adjustments for perceived skill level).
- **Prompt sensitivity:** Findings depend critically on the unstated baseline prompt wording and exact phrasing of attribute descriptors; minor variations could alter the elicited "Marked Pedagogy."
- **Generalizability:** Results are derived from one dataset (PERSUADE, 8th grade) and four models; transfer to other grades, subjects, or newer models is unknown.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (Prompt-triggered stereotype activation):** Medium
- **Mechanism 2 (Baseline as marked):** Low
- **Mechanism 3 (Concentration metric as bias proxy):** Low

## Next Checks
1. **Metric Human Validation:** Conduct a blinded study where educators rate feedback instances (from marked vs. baseline conditions) for perceived bias, constructiveness, and expected student impact. Correlate these human judgments with the automated concentration scores (𝐶𝑠(𝐹)).
2. **Prompt Template Audit:** Systematically vary the baseline prompt’s phrasing (e.g., neutral vs. "differentiated instruction" framing) and the specificity of student descriptors (e.g., "a student with dyslexia" vs. "a student with a learning disability"). Measure resulting changes in marked word concentration to identify the minimal cue and most bias-prone formulations.
3. **Model & Dataset Stress Test:** Replicate the analysis on a different writing corpus (e.g., high school or ELL texts) and with newer model versions (e.g., GPT-4.5, Claude 3.7). Specifically test if the observed ranked effect sizes (e.g., Black > LowAchievement > Female) hold invariant, which would strengthen the claim of a systemic "Marked Pedagogy."
