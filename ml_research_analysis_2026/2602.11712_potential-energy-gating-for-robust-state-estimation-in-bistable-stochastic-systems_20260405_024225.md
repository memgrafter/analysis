# Potential-energy gating for robust state estimation in bistable stochastic systems

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.11712
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11712
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Potential-energy gating reduces RMSE by 57–80% compared to standard EKF in outlier-contaminated bistable systems.

## Executive Summary
Robust state estimation in bistable stochastic systems with outlier-contaminated observations is addressed by introducing potential-energy gating. This method leverages a known or assumed double-well potential energy function to modulate the observation noise covariance of a Bayesian filter, thereby down-weighting measurements taken in high-energy barrier regions where outliers are most harmful. A regularization term based on the potential is also added to the state update to attract the estimate toward metastable states. On synthetic Ginzburg-Landau benchmarks with 10% outliers, the approach achieves an RMSE reduction of 57–80% over the standard Extended Kalman Filter (p < 10⁻¹⁵). It surpasses a naive baseline that only uses well positions, demonstrating the additional value of the continuous energy profile. The method remains robust to 50% misspecification of potential parameters (>47% improvement) and retains 68% improvement under spontaneous Kramers transitions, while the naive baseline degrades to 30%. Application to NGRIP ice-core data yields an asymmetry parameter γ = –0.109 (95% CI [–0.220, –0.011]) and shows that the outlier fraction explains 91% of the variance in filter improvement, indicating real-world utility.

## Method Summary
Potential-energy gating modifies a Bayesian filter (e.g., EKF, UKF, PF) in two complementary ways. First, the observation noise covariance becomes state‑dependent: \( R_{\text{eff}}(x) = R_0 (1 + g V(x)) \), where \( V(x) \) is the known double‑well potential and \( g \) controls gating sensitivity. This inflates uncertainty when the state estimate lies near the energy barrier, effectively downweighting outliers. Second, the state update is obtained by minimizing a penalized cost: \( H(x) = (x - \hat{x}_-)^\top P_-^{-1} (x - \hat{x}_-) + (y - x)^\top R_{\text{eff}}(x)^{-1} (y - x) + \lambda V(x) \), where \( \lambda \) attracts the estimate toward potential minima. The posterior mode \( \hat{x}_+ \) and covariance \( P_+ \) (from the Hessian of \( H \)) are then fed back into the base filter’s predict step. This yields a filter that robustly rejects outlier‑contaminated measurements while preserving sensitivity to genuine transitions.

## Key Results
- On synthetic Ginzburg‑Landau systems with 10% outliers, potential‑gated filters reduce RMSE by 57–80% relative to standard EKF (p < 10⁻¹⁵).
- The continuous energy profile adds ~21 percentage points over a naive well‑position baseline; the method remains robust to 50% potential parameter errors (>47% improvement) and to spontaneous Kramers transitions (68% improvement vs 30% for the naive baseline).
- Applied to NGRIP ice‑core data, the framework estimates asymmetry \( \gamma = -0.109 \) (95% CI [–0.220, –0.011]) and explains 91% of the improvement variance by outlier fraction.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Gating via Observation Covariance Modulation
**Claim:** The gating mechanism reduces the influence of outlier‑contaminated observations in high‑energy barrier regions by dynamically inflating the observation noise covariance.  
**Mechanism:** The effective observation noise covariance is modulated by the local potential energy: \( R_{\text{eff}}(x) = R_0 \cdot (1 + g V(x)) \). When the state estimate \( \hat{x} \) is near a potential minimum (\( V \approx 0 \)), \( R_{\text{eff}} \approx R_0 \) and observations are trusted. As \( \hat{x} \) approaches the barrier (\( V \to V_{\text{max}} \)), \( R_{\text{eff}} \) increases, down‑weighting the observation innovation in the Kalman update.  
**Core assumption:** The potential energy function \( V(x) \) is known or can be accurately approximated, and the system dynamics are governed by this landscape.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] “modulates the observation noise covariance of a Bayesian filter using a known or assumed double‑well potential energy function, down‑weighting measurements in high‑energy barrier regions.”  
- [section III.A] “This motivates replacing the constant observation noise covariance \( R_0 \) with a state‑dependent covariance… \( R(x) = R_0 \cdot (1 + g V(x)) \)”  
**Break condition:** If the potential is not double‑well (e.g., monostable or has more than two wells) or if the barrier region is misidentified, the gating may discount observations incorrectly, leading to loss of information during genuine transitions.

### Mechanism 2: Potential‑Energy Regularization of State Update
**Claim:** Adding a potential‑energy regularization term \( \lambda V(x) \) to the state update cost function attracts the estimate toward well minima, mitigating drift caused by outliers.  
**Mechanism:** The state update minimizes a penalized cost \( H(x) = (x-\hat{x}_-)^\top P_-^{-1} (x-\hat{x}_-) + (y-x)^\top R(x)^{-1} (y-x) + \lambda V(x) \). The \( \lambda V(x) \) term acts as a prior that penalizes high‑energy states, pulling the update toward the nearest well when the observation is untrustworthy.  
**Core assumption:** The potential energy function correctly identifies the locations of metastable states (well minima) as low‑energy regions.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [section III.B] “We augment this cost with a physics‑informed penalty term: \( H(x) = \dots + \lambda V(x) \)”  
- [section III.B] “This interpretation is approximate… the third is a Boltzmann‑inspired prior that penalizes states in high‑energy regions.”  
**Break condition:** Excessive \( \lambda \) causes over‑regularization, ignoring observations even when they are reliable, and may create bias. In particle filters, it can lead to weight degeneracy on clean data (Sec. VE).

### Mechanism 3: Robustness to Potential Misspecification via Topological Preservation
**Claim:** The method is robust to moderate misspecification of potential parameters because the gating mechanism depends primarily on the topological structure (two wells separated by a barrier) rather than precise parameter values.  
**Mechanism:** Even if the assumed potential parameters deviate from truth by up to 50%, the Ginzburg‑Landau form still encodes a bistable topology. The gating inflates \( R \) in the barrier region and keeps it low near the wells, which is the essential functionality.  
**Core assumption:** The true system is bistabile with a barrier, and the assumed potential form preserves this bistability despite parameter errors.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [section IV.D] “The improvement never falls below 47% across the entire grid… because the GL potential preserves the correct topology (two wells separated by a barrier)”  
- [abstract] “The method is robust to 50% potential parameter misspecification (improvement >47%)”  
**Break condition:** If misspecification is severe enough to alter the topology (e.g., making the potential monostable or creating additional wells), the gating mechanism fails as it no longer matches the true energy landscape.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Prior state and covariance \( (\hat{x}_-, P_-) \) → Potential evaluation (computes \( V(\hat{x}_-) \)) → Gating (computes \( R_{\text{eff}} = R_0 (1 + g V(\hat{x}_-)) \)) → Cost construction \( H(x) \) (adds \( \lambda V(x) \)) → Optimizer (minimizes \( H \) to obtain \( \hat{x}_+ \)) → Hessian evaluation \( H''(\hat{x}_+) = P_-^{-1} + R_{\text{eff}}^{-1} + \lambda V''(\hat{x}_+) \) → Covariance \( P_+ = (H'' + \epsilon I)^{-1} \) → Feedback to base filter (EKF/UKF/PF) for next predict step. Hyperparameters \( g, \lambda \) modulate gating and regularization.

### Critical path
1. **Predict:** Using the base filter, compute prior \( \hat{x}_- = f(\hat{x}_{k-1}) \) and \( P_- = F P_{k-1} F^\top + Q \) (or equivalent for other filters).
2. For each measurement \( y_k \):
   a. Evaluate \( V(\hat{x}_-) \).
   b. Compute \( R_{\text{eff}} = R_0 (1 + g V(\hat{x}_-)) \).
   c. Form penalized cost \( H(x) = (x-\hat{x}_-)^\top P_-^{-1} (x-\hat{x}_-) + (y_k-x)^\top R_{\text{eff}}^{-1} (y_k-x) + \lambda V(x) \).
   d. Minimize \( H(x) \) (e.g., L‑BFGS‑B, initialized from standard Kalman update) to obtain \( \hat{x}_+ \).
   e. Compute Hessian \( H''(\hat{x}_+) = P_-^{-1} + R_{\text{eff}}^{-1} + \lambda V''(\hat{x}_+) \) and set \( P_+ = (H'' + \epsilon I)^{-1} \) (with small \( \epsilon > 0 \) for flooring).
3. Loop to next time step.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Gating sensitivity \( g \):** Larger values inflate \( R_{\text{eff}} \) more aggressively, improving outlier rejection but risking over‑discounting during genuine transitions. The paper finds a broad optimal range \( g \in [5, 50] \) for the tested topologies.
- **Regularization strength \( \lambda \):** Controls pull toward well minima. Too small: negligible effect; too large: over‑regularization, especially problematic for particle filters on clean data. Typical \( \lambda \in [0.01, 1.0] \).
- **Optimizer choice:** L‑BFGS‑B is efficient for low‑dimensional states. For higher dimensions, consider gradient‑based methods with line search. Ensure gradients of \( V \) and \( V'' \) are available.
- **Covariance estimation:** Hessian‑based \( P_+ \) may be over‑confident. Consider multiplicative inflation if posterior calibration is required.
- **Filter architecture:** EKF is most stable (no sigma‑point issues). UKF may suffer when sigma points fall in the barrier region (\( V'' < 0 \)) causing ill‑conditioned Hessian. Particle filters can degrade on clean data due to weight bias.

### Failure signatures
- **No improvement in RMSE** under outlier contamination: Check potential specification (topology), hyperparameter tuning (\( g, \lambda \)), and implementation of \( R_{\text{eff}} \).
- **Over‑confident covariances:** Posterior covariance too small, leading to filter divergence. Diagnose via residual autocorrelation (Sec. VI.C). Remedy: inflate \( P_+ \) or adjust \( \lambda \).
- **Particle filter weight collapse** on clean data: Indicates over‑regularization. Reduce \( \lambda \) or apply only when outlier fraction >5% (Sec. VE).
- **Numerical instability in UKF:** When sigma points fall in barrier region, \( V'' < 0 \) makes Hessian non‑positive definite. Use fallback to standard update or switch to EKF version.

### First experiments
1. **Baseline comparison on synthetic data:** Generate 100 replications of the Ginzburg‑Landau system with 10% outliers. Compare PG‑EKF (default \( g=10, \lambda=0.1 \)) against standard EKF. Expect ~78% RMSE reduction (p < 1e‑15).
2. **Misspecification robustness:** Deliberately use wrong potential parameters (e.g., set \( \alpha_{\text{assumed}}=1.5, \beta_{\text{assumed}}=0.5 \) while true \( \alpha=1, \beta=1 \)). Verify improvement remains >47% across a grid of misspecifications.
3. **Transition type robustness:** Test on spontaneous Kramers transitions (lower barrier ratio). Compare PG‑EKF vs. naive topological baseline (NT‑EKF). Expect PG‑EKF to retain ~68% improvement while NT‑EKF drops to ~30%.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** How can the potential‑energy gating framework be generalized to multidimensional state spaces while preserving its robustness properties?  
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper states in Section VI.C that “the present formulation is restricted to scalar state variables. Extension to multivariate systems is conceptually straightforward—replace \( V(x) \) with a multivariate potential and \( R \) with a state‑dependent matrix—but raises computational cost questions and requires meaningful definitions of multivariate potential landscapes.”  
**Why unresolved:** The paper does not provide a concrete multivariate implementation or analyze its computational complexity and stability. The proposed gating mechanism relies on a scalar potential value; in higher dimensions, defining a single scalar potential that captures the bistable topology is nontrivial.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Development and empirical validation of a multivariate gating scheme, perhaps using a scalar “energy” defined from a Lyapunov function or a collective coordinate, along with benchmark comparisons showing preserved robustness against outliers.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** Can the parameters of the double‑well potential (α, β, γ) be estimated online jointly with the state, and what estimation strategies (e.g., joint Bayesian filtering, expectation‑maximization) would maintain filter stability under outlier contamination?  
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] In Section VII (Conclusion) the authors mention “online estimation of potential parameters” as a natural extension. Section V.B notes that they estimated γ from the NGRIP data offline, but the method assumes known parameters during filtering.  
**Why unresolved:** The paper does not address the identifiability of potential parameters from a single contaminated trajectory, nor the interplay between parameter learning and the gating mechanism, which itself depends on those parameters. Outliers could severely bias parameter estimates if not handled properly.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** A joint state‑parameter filter that incorporates the gating and demonstrates accurate parameter recovery in Monte Carlo experiments with outlier contamination, possibly using a hierarchical Bayesian approach or an adaptive scheme that accounts for the observation noise inflated by gating.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** Why does the posterior covariance from the Hessian‑based update exhibit significant autocorrelation, and how can it be corrected to ensure reliable uncertainty quantification without sacrificing estimation accuracy?  
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section VI.C (limitation c) reports “The Hessian‑based covariance … produces residuals with significant autocorrelation at lags 1–6, more pronounced than in the standard EKF. This indicates that the posterior is slightly over‑confident.” They suggest inflating \( P_+ \) as a remedy but do not test it.  
**Why unresolved:** Overconfident uncertainties undermine applications that rely on proper confidence intervals (e.g., risk assessment). The paper does not quantify the miscalibration or propose a theoretically grounded correction method.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Statistical tests (e.g., Ljung‑Box) on filter residuals demonstrating calibration after applying a covariance inflation factor, along with a sensitivity analysis to determine appropriate inflation values and trade‑offs with estimation error.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** Under what conditions does the potential‑gated particle filter (PG‑PF) degrade on clean data, and how can the particle weight update be modified to avoid over‑regularization while retaining robustness to outliers?  
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section V.E describes that “the PG‑PF shows a −23.4% degradation relative to the standard PF” on clean NGRIP data, worsening with shorter records. The degradation is attributed to “the potential‑energy gating introduces a systematic bias in the particle weights: particles near well minima receive disproportionately high weight even when no outliers are present.”  
**Why unresolved:** The PG‑PF is otherwise the top performer under outlier contamination, but its failure on clean data limits its applicability. The paper does not propose a solution or analyze the weight distribution dynamics that cause this over‑regularization.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** A modified weight update that normalizes or adapts the gating influence based on an estimate of the outlier fraction, perhaps via a data‑driven scaling of \( g \) or \( \lambda \), validated on synthetic and real data across a range of outlier levels and sample sizes.

## Limitations
- Outlier injection specifics (temporal distribution, magnitude) are not fully described, making synthetic benchmark replication and performance benchmarking ambiguous.
- NGRIP ice‑core preprocessing (bimodal normalization, event window selection) is insufficiently detailed, hindering exact reproduction of the real‑data analysis.
- The method assumes a known double‑well potential; if the true system deviates (e.g., multiple wells or monostable shape), gating may mis‑weight observations and degrade performance.
