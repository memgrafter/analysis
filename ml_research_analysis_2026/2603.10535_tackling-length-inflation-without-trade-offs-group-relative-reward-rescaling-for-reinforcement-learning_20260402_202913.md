# Tackling Length Inflation Without Trade-offs: Group Relative Reward Rescaling for Reinforcement Learning

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2603.10535  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10535  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: GR³ reduces token consumption by over 40% while maintaining or improving performance on challenging benchmarks (e.g., AIME-25 length drops from 14,032 to 8,582 tokens for 7B models, Avg@32 on AIME-24 increases from 52.4 to 60.1).

## Executive Summary
Length inflation in reinforcement learning (RL) for large language models (LLMs) causes models to generate unnecessarily long responses, harming inference efficiency without improving solution quality. This paper introduces **Group Relative Reward Rescaling (GR³)**, a multiplicative alternative to additive length penalties. GR³ rescales the task reward by a factor `1/(1+α·ℓ/¯ℓ)`, where `ℓ` is the response length and `¯ℓ` the mean length within a group of sampled responses. The coupling to task reward eliminates the optimization shortcut that plagues additive penalties, while group-relative normalization adapts to prompt difficulty. Advantage-aware calibration selects `α` to preserve the learning signal. Across RLVR and RLHF tasks, GR³ cuts token usage by more than 40% and often boosts accuracy, shifting the performance–cost Pareto frontier. The work demonstrates that verbosity is not a necessary trade‑off for gains in RL-trained LLMs.

## Method Summary
GR³ modifies the standard Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) framework by replacing additive length regularization with multiplicative reward rescaling. For each prompt, the algorithm samples a group of `G` responses from the current policy. It computes the task reward `R` (from a verifier or reward model) and response length `ℓ`. The group mean length `¯ℓ` is then used to rescale each reward: `ˆR = R / (1+α·ℓ/¯ℓ)`. Group-relative advantages are calculated using the mean `µ_R` and standard deviation `σ_R` of the (original) rewards within the group, exactly as in GRPO. The penalty coefficient `α` is determined in a short calibration phase where the largest `α` satisfying a high constraint satisfaction rate (CSR) – i.e., a representative high‑quality trajectory retains non‑negative advantage – is selected. This advantage‑aware calibration maximizes regularization strength while preserving the optimization signal. The method is applied to both RLVR (sparse verifier rewards) and RLHF (reward model with optional reference‑based shaping) settings, with `α` ≈ 0.33 for math/code and ≈ 0.00133 for chat. The overall training loop follows a PPO‑style clipped objective using the rescaled rewards.

## Key Results
- **Token reduction**: Over 40% decrease in average output length on math benchmarks (e.g., AIME-25: 14,032 → 8,582 tokens for 7B models).
- **Performance improvement**: Avg@32 on AIME-24 increased from 52.4 (GRPO) to 60.1 (GR³) for 7B models; similar gains on MATH500, AMC‑23, and RLHF benchmarks (Arena‑Hard‑Auto, AlpacaEval‑LC).
- **Stronger Pareto frontier**: GR³ consistently outperforms both standard GRPO and additive length‑regularized baselines, demonstrating that efficiency and capability can be improved simultaneously.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Multiplicative Rescaling Eliminates Additive Shortcut**  
Additive length shaping (`R - λℓ`) introduces an independent optimization objective: the policy can maximize the composite reward by reducing `ℓ` alone, even without improving `R`. GR³ uses multiplicative rescaling (`ˆR = R·S` with `S = 1/(1+α·ℓ/¯ℓ)`). The length signal becomes `R·(S-µ_S)`, weighted by `R`. Consequently, length reduction only contributes positively when the task reward is high, coupling the two objectives. Proposition 3.2 and Figure 3 show that additive shaping degrades task reward across all `λ`, while multiplicative maintains improvement.

**Mechanism 2: Group-Relative Normalization Adapts to Difficulty**  
The scaling factor uses the within‑group mean length `¯ℓ` instead of a fixed global threshold. This dynamic normalization adapts to the on‑policy distribution of each prompt: harder prompts typically have longer `¯ℓ`, yielding a more lenient length budget; easier prompts have shorter `¯ℓ` and a tighter penalty. It avoids arbitrary thresholds and improves robustness to prompt difficulty (Table 2: fixed truncation harms AIME‑24 accuracy, while GR³ maintains performance).

**Mechanism 3: Advantage-Aware Calibration Preserves Learning Signal**  
The penalty coefficient `α` is selected via constraint satisfaction rate (CSR) based on the condition `R_max/(1+α) ≥ µ_ˆR`. This ensures that a representative high‑reward trajectory (max reward, average length) retains a non‑negative advantage in expectation. The largest `α` achieving a high CSR (≥99.9%) is chosen, maximizing regularization strength while preserving the optimization signal. Calibration (Figure 4) picks `α=0.33` for math/code, yielding CSR ~100% and a positive reward gap over GRPO.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**  
Prompt → Group Sampling (G responses) → Compute R and ℓ → Compute ¯ℓ (group mean length) and α (from calibration) → Apply GR³ rescaling (ˆR = R/(1+α·ℓ/¯ℓ)) → Compute group‑relative advantages (using µ_R, σ_R) → PPO‑style clipped objective update.

**Critical path**  
The key steps are: sample a group per prompt, compute per‑group statistics (`¯ℓ`, `µ_R`, `σ_R`), apply multiplicative scaling, and perform a PPO update. Calibration of `α` must precede or be tightly coupled with the first major training loop to set the regularization strength.

**Design tradeoffs**
- *Alpha selection*: Too high → over‑penalization and capability loss; too low → insufficient length control. Use the CSR curve to pick the largest `α` that still meets the constraint.
- *Group size `G`*: Larger `G` stabilizes `¯ℓ` and advantage estimates but increases memory/throughput cost. Paper uses 8–16.
- *Filtering all‑R_max groups*: Groups where every trajectory attains the maximum reward are filtered online to avoid violating the advantage‑preservation condition; this reduces available training data and must be monitored.
- *Reward shaping for RLHF*: The reference‑based sigmoid (Eq. 17) stabilizes the reward signal but couples with GR³; careful hyper‑parameter tuning is required.

**Failure signatures**
- *Length collapse*: Extremely short outputs with stagnant or declining task reward → `α` too high or accidental additive shaping.
- *Diverging capabilities*: Significant drop on hard benchmarks (AIME‑24/25) → penalty overturns advantages for good trajectories.
- *Instability in `¯ℓ`*: High variance across training steps for the same prompt distribution → group size too small or prompt batch not diverse enough.
- *CSR collapse*: The chosen `α`’s satisfaction rate drops below ~95% during training → constraint too tight for the evolving policy; re‑calibrate or reduce `α`.

**First 3 experiments**
1. **Reproduce main result on small scale**: Train DeepSeek‑R1‑Distill‑1.5B on 500 prompts from DeepScaleR for 100 steps. Compare GR³ vs. standard GRPO. Expect ~30–40% length reduction with comparable or superior reward.
2. **Ablation on `α`**: Sweep `α ∈ {0.1, 0.33, 0.5, 1.0}` while keeping other settings fixed. Verify trade‑off: decreasing `α` increases length; reward plateaus after optimal `α`. Track CSR during training.
3. **Failure mode injection**: Intentionally set `α` very high (e.g., 2.0). Observe severe performance degradation on math problems alongside drastic length collapse. Also implement an additive shaping baseline (`R - α·ℓ/¯ℓ`) to confirm the rapid reward collapse described in Figure 3.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- **Open Question 1**: Does multiplicative rescaling preserve the unbiasedness of the policy gradient estimator from standard GRPO? The paper provides theoretical decompositions but no formal analysis of bias under group normalization and scaling. Resolution would require a proof or empirical gradient alignment study.
- **Open Question 2**: Can the penalty coefficient `α` be adaptively scheduled during training? The current method fixes `α` after an initial calibration, but the optimal regularization strength likely shifts as the policy improves. An adaptive control scheme could further improve the efficiency‑performance trade‑off.
- **Open Question 3**: Does GR³ remain effective when rewards are dense or stepwise (e.g., multi‑turn dialogue, summarization with ROUGE)? The evaluation covers sparse final‑answer rewards; the interaction with dense feedback may differ and could cause instability or over‑penalization of informative tokens.

## Limitations
- The alpha calibration protocol (number of rollouts, PPO steps, CSR tolerance) is not fully specified, making exact reproduction difficult.
- Ambiguity whether the group variance `σ_R` is computed on original rewards `R` or rescaled rewards `ˆR` affects advantage scaling.
- For RLHF, the reference response `y_ref` and whether the reference model updates during training are unspecified, potentially impacting reward stability.
- Small group sizes may lead to noisy `¯ℓ` estimates and unstable penalties.
- Filtering groups with all `R_max` reduces training data; its impact on rare/hard prompts is unknown.
- Generalizability across different reward models or verifiers has not been thoroughly validated.

2. **σ_R source ablation**: On MATH500, train two GR³ variants – one computing `σ_R` from original `R`, another from rescaled `ˆR`. Compare length reduction, reward trends, and final accuracy to infer the intended design.
3. **Sparse reward robustness test**: Construct a synthetic task with binary (0/1) reward and high failure rate. Evaluate whether GR³ still regularizes length when many rewards are near zero, as predicted by the break condition; compare against an additive shaping baseline.
