# From Latent to Observable Position-Based Click Models in Carousel Interfaces

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.16541
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16541
- **Reference count:** 39
- **Primary result:** The Observed Examination Position-Based Model (OEPBM) using eye‑tracking achieves the highest click prediction performance (test log‑likelihood up to –0.2264) and produces examination patterns most aligned with actual user eye movements.

## Executive Summary
Existing click models are designed for single ranked‑list interfaces and fail to capture the complexities of carousel recommender systems. This paper proposes three novel position‑based click models tailored for carousels, notably the Observed Examination Position‑Based Model (OEPBM) which incorporates eye‑tracking data to make user examination observable rather than latent. The models are trained using gradient ascent, expectation‑maximization, and maximum likelihood estimation and evaluated on the RecGaze dataset. Gradient‑based optimization consistently outperforms classical methods in click log‑likelihood. OEPBM not only attains the best click prediction accuracy but also yields examination patterns that closely match eye‑tracked user behavior. However, the study reveals that optimizing for click likelihood alone does not guarantee realistic examination, underscoring the importance of additional behavioral signals such as eye tracking for modeling user attention in complex interfaces.

## Method Summary
The authors introduce three carousel‑specific position‑based click models: a baseline Position‑Based Model (PBM) adapted for carousels, a latent examination variant that infers attention from clicks (similar to traditional click models), and the proposed Observed Examination Position‑Based Model (OEPBM) which treats examination as an observable variable using eye‑tracking data. Each model defines click probability as the product of an examination probability (depending on absolute position within the carousel) and an attractiveness probability (depending on item relevance or user preference). Parameter estimation is explored via three approaches: gradient ascent on the log‑likelihood, expectation‑maximization (EM) with a Bernoulli mixture for latent examination, and maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) as a simple baseline. All models are trained and tested on the RecGaze dataset, which contains click logs and synchronized eye‑tracking recordings from users interacting with a carousel interface.

## Key Results
- Gradient ascent yields higher click log‑likelihoods than EM and MLE across all three carousel models.
- OEPBM achieves the strongest click prediction performance, with a test log‑likelihood of up to –0.2264.
- OEPBM’s predicted examination probabilities are most correlated with actual eye‑tracked gaze, but the experiments show that optimizing solely for click likelihood does not guarantee realistic examination patterns, highlighting a limitation of click‑only models.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
OEPBM’s superior performance stems from directly incorporating eye‑tracking measurements, which convert the latent examination variable into an observable signal. This eliminates the uncertainty inherent in inferring attention solely from clicks, where many examined items go unclicked and clicks can occur without examination. As a result, the model can learn more accurate position‑based examination probabilities and better disentangle the effects of position bias and item attractiveness. Gradient‑based optimization further improves fit by allowing fine‑grained updates to all parameters simultaneously, avoiding some of the approximation limitations of EM’s alternating approach. However, the mismatch between click‑only likelihood and real examination suggests that clicks alone are a noisy and sparse indicator of attention, reinforcing the need for supplementary behavioral data.

## Foundational Learning
- **Position‑Based Click Model (PBM)**  
  *Why needed:* Provides a simple yet effective framework where click probability factorizes into examination (position‑dependent) and attractiveness (item‑dependent). This decomposition is essential for modeling position bias in ranked lists and carousels.  
  *Quick check:* Do earlier positions receive higher examination probabilities in the learned model?

- **Latent Examination**  
  *Why needed:* In traditional click models, user examination of a result is hidden and must be inferred from observed clicks. This introduces complexity due to the partial observability of user attention.  
  *Quick check:* Is the examination variable treated as a hidden state that is marginalized during the E‑step of EM?

- **Eye‑Tracking as Observable Attention**  
  *Why needed:* Offers a direct, ground‑truth measurement of user gaze, transforming examination into an observed variable and thus removing a major source of inference uncertainty.  
  *Quick check:* Does the model use eye‑tracking data (e.g., fixation within an item) as an input to the examination probability?

- **Expectation‑Maximization (EM)**  
  *Why needed:* An iterative algorithm for maximum likelihood estimation when latent variables are present; alternates between computing expected latent states (E‑step) and updating parameters (M‑step).  
  *Quick check:* Does the training procedure include an E‑step that computes the expected value of the latent examination indicator?

- **Gradient Ascent for Log‑Likelihood**  
  *Why needed:* Directly optimizes the log‑likelihood of the observed clicks; can scale to larger parameter sets and often converges faster than EM.  
  *Quick check:* Are the derivatives of the log‑likelihood with respect to model parameters computed and used to update the parameters?

- **Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)**  
  *Why needed:* Serves as a simple baseline where parameters are chosen to maximize the probability of the observed clicks under the model, without latent variable handling.  
  *Quick check:* Are parameters set to values that directly maximize the likelihood of the click data (e.g., via closed‑form solution or simple numerical optimization)?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Data Collection (clicks, eye‑tracking) → Preprocessing (session segmentation, feature extraction) → Model Specification (choose PBM variant) → Parameter Estimation (gradient ascent / EM / MLE) → Evaluation (test log‑likelihood, eye‑tracking alignment).

**Critical path:** The most vital flow is from Preprocessing through Parameter Estimation to Evaluation; the choice of estimation method (particularly gradient ascent) directly determines the model’s ability to fit the click data and, in OEPBM’s case, to align with eye‑tracking measurements. Any flaw in preprocessing or estimation propagates to all downstream results.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
1. Gradient ascent vs EM: gradient ascent offers faster convergence and higher final likelihood but may overfit without careful regularization; EM handles latent variables naturally but can be slower and may converge to inferior local optima.  
2. Eye‑tracking dependence: OEPBM’s realism comes at the cost of requiring specialized hardware, which limits dataset size and may introduce noise due to calibration errors; models without eye‑tracking are more practical but less accurate.  
3. Model complexity: Adding more position‑specific parameters can capture nuanced carousel effects but risks overfitting, especially with limited eye‑tracking data.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Gradient likelihood decreases during training → learning rate too high or poor initialization.  
- Large gap between training and test log‑likelihood → overfitting; consider simplifying the model or adding regularization.  
- Predicted examination probabilities show no correlation with eye‑tracking → model may have collapsed to a latent‑only solution; check eye‑tracking integration or switch to a more robust estimation method.

**Three first experiments:**  
1. Compare test log‑likelihoods of all three models (baseline PBM, latent PBM, OEPBM) when trained with gradient ascent, EM, and MLE.  
2. Quantify the correlation between OEPBM’s predicted examination probabilities and actual eye‑tracking fixations across positions; compare against the latent model’s inferred examination.  
3. Ablate the eye‑tracking component in OEPBM by replacing it with a heuristic (e.g., dwell time) to measure the drop in both prediction accuracy and eye‑tracking alignment.

## Limitations
- Evaluation is restricted to the RecGaze dataset; generalizability to other carousel domains or user populations remains uncertain.
- OEPBM relies on costly eye‑tracking equipment, which may be unavailable in production environments and serves only as an imperfect proxy for attention.
- Optimizing click log‑likelihood does not necessarily improve online user satisfaction or business metrics; the study lacks A/B testing or long‑term validation.
