# TowerMind: A Tower Defence Game Learning Environment and Benchmark for LLM as Agents

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.05899  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05899  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** LLMs (e.g., Claude 3.7 Sonnet) achieve only 38–42% of human scores and 82–87% of human valid action rates on TowerMind.

## Executive Summary
TowerMind introduces a lightweight, multimodal tower defense environment for evaluating LLM agents. It addresses the high computational cost and limited text support of prior RTS benchmarks like SC2LE by requiring only 0.15 GB of resources and running on CPUs. The environment provides pixel, textual, and structured observations, incorporates fog‑of‑war, and measures hallucination via invalid action rates across five levels of increasing difficulty. Evaluation of commercial and open‑source LLMs shows a large performance gap to human experts: top models (e.g., Claude 3.7 Sonnet) obtained 38–42% of human normalized scores and 82–87% of human valid action rates. Vision inputs generally improved results, while open‑source models exhibited higher hallucination that worsened with difficulty. Reinforcement‑learning baselines (Ape‑X DQN, PPO) solved easier levels but remained far below humans, confirming TowerMind’s challenge for future agent research.

## Method Summary
TowerMind is built on Unity and wrapped with ML‑Agents Toolkit to expose a Gym‑compatible interface. The agent acts every 16 game steps, selecting from a hybrid action space: continuous (x, y) coordinates in [–3.0, 3.0] and a discrete command (12 types such as build, upgrade, sell, deploy, hero movement/skill). Observations come in three synchronized forms: a 512×512 RGB pixel image, a JSON text dump of the game state, and a flattened 759‑dimensional numerical array. The environment validates each action against current resources, map geometry, and unit status; invalid actions are rejected and not executed. The reward is –1 per enemy reaching the base. Five benchmark levels vary enemy types, wave compositions, road layouts, and tower‑point density. For LLM evaluation, a zero‑shot prompt supplies fixed configuration tables (towers, enemies, hero), the game rules, and the last three observation‑action pairs. LLMs output JSON with coordinates and action. Metrics include normalized score (relative to human baseline and minimum) and valid action rate. RL baselines (Ape‑X DQN, PPO) are trained for 100M steps with downsampled 128×128 pixels and a discretized 10×10×12 action space, plus a small step penalty.

## Key Results
- LLMs significantly underperform humans: best model (Claude 3.7 Sonnet) achieved 38–42% of human normalized score.
- Vision‑language inputs boost performance for most LLMs; open‑source models suffer higher hallucination (invalid action rates), which escalates with level difficulty.
- RL algorithms solve the easiest levels but remain far below humans, establishing TowerMind as a difficult benchmark for RTS‑style planning.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
- **Mechanism 1: Lightweight design lowers adoption barriers.** TowerMind’s minimal resource usage (0.15 GB disk/RAM, CPU‑only) removes the hardware constraints that plague benchmarks like SC2LE, making it accessible for large‑scale LLM evaluation. Evidence: Abstract and Section 2 contrast with SC2LE’s 30 GB disk, 2 GB RAM, GPU requirement. If oversimplification sacrifices essential RTS complexity, long‑term relevance could suffer.
- **Mechanism 2: Multimodal observations enable flexible evaluation and reveal visual contributions.** The three synchronized representations (pixel, text, structured) allow testing language‑only vs. vision‑language models on identical tasks, isolating the effect of visual information. Most LLMs improve with vision input, indicating visual cues aid understanding. Outliers (e.g., Llama 3.2 90B) suggest model‑specific limitations in visual processing. Evidence: Section 3.2 defines observation types; Section 4.3 shows performance gains with vision.
- **Mechanism 3: Invalid action rate isolates hallucination from strategic effectiveness.** Actions must obey game rules (resources, geometry, unit status). Violations are counted as invalid and not executed, providing a direct measure of hallucination (outputs inconsistent with the observable state). This complements score, which measures strategic outcome. High invalid action rates, especially in open‑source LLMs and on harder levels, indicate hallucination challenges. Evidence: Abstract and Section 3.2 define the metric; Section 4.3 reports higher hallucination in open‑source models.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Real‑time strategy (RTS) core principles (macro vs. micro management).**  
  Why needed: TowerMind’s gameplay requires agents to allocate resources for tower placement (macro) while managing unit‑level actions like hero movement (micro). Understanding this split clarifies the cognitive demands placed on agents.  
  Quick check: What distinguishes macro‑management (e.g., choosing tower types and positions) from micro‑management (e.g., timing hero skill usage) in TowerMind?
- **Concept: Tower defense genre conventions (fixed enemy paths, wave‑based attacks, predefined tower points).**  
  Why needed: These conventions define the rules of the game and constrain the action space. Violating them (e.g., building off the road) leads to invalid actions or suboptimal strategies.  
  Quick check: Why might building a tower on a valid tower point but far from any road be a suboptimal or invalid strategic move?
- **Concept: LLM agent evaluation metrics (score vs. valid action rate vs. hallucination).**  
  Why needed: Score measures outcome (enemies reaching base), valid action rate measures correctness (executability), and their gap highlights agents that act correctly but ineffectively. The invalid action rate specifically quantifies hallucination.  
  Quick check: What does a high valid action rate but low score suggest about an LLM’s decision‑making?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Unity core (physics, rendering) -> ML‑Agents adapter -> Observation modules (pixel renderer, JSON state extractor, structured array encoder) -> Action validator -> Fog‑of‑war system. Supporting: Config system (JSON files) and Level editor GUI.
- **Critical path:**  
  1. `env.reset()` loads level config, initializes game state, returns initial observation.  
  2. Agent selects action `(x, y, c)`.  
  3. `env.step(action)` validates action; if valid, runs 16 game steps, accumulates reward (–1 per enemy reaching base), and returns next observation, done flag.  
  4. Repeat until episode termination (base health 0 or all waves cleared).
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Lightweight vs. expressiveness:* Simplified graphics and deterministic mechanics speed up training/evaluation but may omit nuances of full RTS (e.g., no opponent).  
  - *Multimodal vs. complexity:* Multiple observation types increase flexibility but require careful prompt engineering and may strain context windows.  
  - *Deterministic seeds:* Guarantees reproducibility but limits trajectory diversity unless seeds are varied.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - High invalid action rate → agent misunderstands action space or state constraints.  
  - Performance drop with vision input → model cannot process pixel data effectively (e.g., Llama 3.2 90B).  
  - Large gap between score and valid action rate → agent produces valid but strategically poor actions (lack of multifinality).
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Baseline replication:** Evaluate all LLMs on five levels with language‑only and vision‑language prompts; compute normalized score and valid action rate vs. human baseline.  
  2. **Modality ablation:** Test top model (Claude 3.7 Sonnet) with pixel, textual, and structured observations separately to isolate visual contribution.  
  3. **Hallucination stress test:** Raise difficulty (more enemy types, denser fog‑of‑war) and measure change in invalid action rate to assess scalability.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. What are the root causes of inadequate planning validation in LLMs when playing TowerMind, and how can LLMs be improved to better validate their long‑term strategies? The paper’s qualitative analysis notes “inadequate planning validation” (e.g., building on misleading tower points) but does not investigate why validation fails.
2. How does fog‑of‑war and partial observability affect LLM hallucination rates, and what mitigation strategies exist? Hallucination increases with difficulty, but the specific impact of environmental features like fog‑of‑war is not isolated.
3. Why do vision‑language models show varied performance gains from pixel inputs, and what architectural changes ensure consistent multimodal benefit? Most VLMs improve with vision, but Llama 3.2 degrades; reasons (training data mismatch, vision encoder quality, scaling) are unexplored.
4. What limits classical RL algorithms from achieving human‑level performance in TowerMind, and how can RL be adapted for RTS‑style hybrid action spaces? RL baselines solve easy levels but remain far below humans; the paper does not analyze why (e.g., credit assignment, exploration) nor propose adaptive algorithms.

## Limitations
- Transfer to full RTS: Simplified mechanics (no opponent, deterministic) may limit relevance for adversarial planning scenarios.
- Reproducibility gaps: Random seeds, exact prompt/config versions, and image encoding details are undisclosed, hindering exact replication.
- RL baseline sensitivity: Hyperparameter tuning was limited, possibly understating RL potential.

## Confidence
- Lightweight design: Medium
- Multimodal benefit: Medium
- Invalid actions as hallucination: Medium
- Benchmark difficulty: High

## Next Checks
- Seed robustness: Evaluate all models on 10+ seeds per level and report variance to confirm statistical significance of observed gaps.
- Modality ablation: Control for prompt wording and test pixel/text/structured observations separately to isolate vision’s contribution.
- Invalid action taxonomy: Enumerate all possible invalidities and verify the validator captures them completely, ensuring the metric’s completeness.
