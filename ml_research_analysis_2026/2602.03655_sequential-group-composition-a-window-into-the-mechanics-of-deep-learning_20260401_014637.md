# Sequential Group Composition: A Window into the Mechanics of Deep Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.03655
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03655
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Introduces sequential group composition; shows two-layer MLPs require exponential width but learn irreps sequentially based on Fourier statistics, while deeper architectures (RNN, MLP) exploit associativity to achieve constant or logarithmic depth with polynomial width.

## Executive Summary
This paper studies how neural networks acquire structured sequential operations through a new synthetic task: sequential group composition. Given a sequence of group elements from a finite group G, the network must compute their product. For a two-layer MLP with polynomial activation of degree matching the sequence length, training dynamics follow an alternating gradient flow that causes the network to learn irreducible representations of the group one at a time, in an order dictated by the Fourier power of the input encoding. Theoretical analysis proves that perfect performance requires hidden width scaling exponentially with sequence length k, and experiments confirm a sharp phase transition when width exceeds the derived bound. In contrast, deeper architectures (RNNs, multilayer MLPs) exploit the associativity of group operations to achieve constant (in k) width or logarithmic depth. The work thus illuminates a clear computational advantage of depth for algebraic tasks and offers a mechanistic view of feature learning in neural networks.

## Method Summary
The authors introduce the sequential group composition task, where a network processes a length-k sequence of group elements (from a finite group G) encoded as a vector and outputs the product. They analyze two-layer MLPs with polynomial activation σ(z)=z^k under vanishing initialization, proving that training follows an alternating gradient flow that induces a greedy acquisition of irreducible representations (irreps) in descending order of Fourier power. They derive a necessary condition on hidden width H: H must be at least (k+1)^2 k Σ_{ρ∈I(G)} n_ρ^{k+1} (or for Abelian groups H ≥ (k+1)^2 k-1 |G|) to achieve zero loss. They then construct RNN and deep MLP solutions that use associativity to break the k-ary composition into binary steps, achieving constant width O(|G|^{3/2}) independent of k. Experiments with small groups and sequence lengths confirm the theoretical width bound and the efficiency of deep architectures.

## Key Results
- Two-layer MLP learns the task by sequentially acquiring irreducible representations ordered by Fourier power of the encoding.
- Perfect performance for two-layer nets requires hidden width scaling exponentially with sequence length k; empirical phase transition matches the theoretical bound.
- RNNs and deep MLPs reduce width to O(|G|^{3/2}) independent of k by decomposing composition into binary steps; RNNs use sequential steps, MLPs use parallel tree.
- Transformers may achieve logarithmic depth; constant-depth shortcuts exist for solvable groups.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
- **Irrep Acquisition via Alternating Gradient Flow:** Under vanishing initialization, two-layer MLPs with polynomial activation learn irreducible representations of the group sequentially. Dormant neurons align with Fourier directions of highest utility, and then active neurons coordinate to implement a sigma-pi-sigma network for that irrep. The order is determined by the Fourier statistics of the input encoding.
- **Exponential Width Requirement:** Exact learning of the k-ary composition requires the hidden width to scale exponentially with k because the polynomial expansion must cancel extraneous terms, which necessitates a Waring decomposition of the square-free monomial z_1...z_k. This leads to the bound H ≥ (k+1)^2 k Σ_{ρ∈I(G)} n_ρ^{k+1} (or H ≥ (k+1)^2 k-1 |G| for Abelian groups).
- **Depth Exploits Associativity:** Associativity of group composition allows deeper architectures to avoid the exponential width blowup. An RNN can sequentially apply a pre-trained binary composition module in k steps, requiring only O(k) time but constant width H = O(|G|^{3/2}). A multilayer MLP uses a balanced tree of binary compositions to achieve logarithmic depth, also with width O(|G|^{3/2}). Transformers may similarly achieve logarithmic depth or even constant-depth shortcuts for solvable groups.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
- Two-layer MLP: Input (k|G|) → Hidden (H) with polynomial σ(z)=z^k → Output (|G|).  
- RNN: Input embeddings → Hidden state updated by W_mix (pre-trained binary composition) → Output; uses quadratic activation for pairwise composition.  
- Multilayer MLP: Geometric reduction in hidden widths per layer; each layer applies σ to merge pairs in a balanced tree.  
- Transformer: Token embeddings + attention; hypothesized to achieve logarithmic depth via learned attention patterns.

**Critical path**  
1. Define sequential group composition task and input encoding.  
2. Analyze two-layer MLP: derive alternating gradient flow dynamics, prove irrep acquisition order, and establish width bound.  
3. Construct deep solutions (RNN, MLP tree) using binary composition building blocks with favorable scaling.  
4. Empirically validate width scaling and depth efficiency through controlled experiments.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- *Two-layer MLP:* Simple and interpretable but requires exponential width in sequence length; training dynamics are well-understood via AGF.  
- *RNN:* Constant width (independent of k) and sequential processing; inference time scales linearly with k; robust to varying sequence lengths.  
- *Multilayer MLP:* Achieves logarithmic depth via parallel binary compositions; requires balanced tree structure and precise weight initialization; may be less flexible to non-power-of-two sequence lengths.  
- *Transformer:* Potential for constant-depth shortcuts for solvable groups; empirical training dynamics not fully explained by theory.

**Failure signatures**  
- For two-layer MLPs, if hidden width H is below the theoretical bound, training loss plateaus and the network may only achieve partial solutions.  
- If the binary composition modules in deep architectures are not learned (e.g., due to poor Fourier statistics or insufficient width), depth provides no advantage; performance may degrade.  
- For non-associative tasks (e.g., semiautomata), the associativity-based constructions fail entirely.  
- Degenerate input encodings (with zero Fourier power for some irreps) can disrupt the predicted irrep acquisition order in two-layer nets.

## Limitations
- The theoretical analysis assumes vanishing initialization, exact polynomial activation degree equal to sequence length, and non-degenerate Fourier coefficients; real-world networks (e.g., with ReLU) may not follow the predicted dynamics.
- The exponential width bound is proven for exact zero loss; it is unclear whether networks can achieve low error with sub-bound width or whether the bound is tight for practical settings.
- Deep architecture constructions depend on the availability of efficiently learnable binary composition modules; the conditions for their successful learning and the robustness to encoding variations are not fully characterized.
- Claims regarding Transformers (logarithmic depth, constant-depth shortcuts) are speculative and lack rigorous proof or empirical validation.

## Confidence
- Two-layer MLP AGF dynamics and irrep acquisition: Medium
- Exponential width requirement: Medium
- Depth advantage (RNN/MLP) via associativity: High
- Transformer logarithmic depth and shortcuts: Low
