# Reasoning as Compression: Unifying Budget Forcing via the Conditional Information Bottleneck

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.08462
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08462
- **Reference count:** 39
- **Primary result:** CIB achieves up to 41% token reduction with ≤1.5% accuracy drop across math benchmarks, outperforms length‑penalty baselines, and offers a fine‑grained accuracy‑compression trade‑off via the β parameter.

## Executive Summary
Chain‑of‑Thought (CoT) reasoning improves the accuracy of large language models but does so at the cost of generating long, verbose traces. Existing budget‑forcing methods apply uniform length penalties, which indiscriminately suppress both essential reasoning steps and redundant filler, harming performance. This paper recasts efficient reasoning as a lossy compression problem under the Conditional Information Bottleneck (CIB) principle. The reasoning trace is treated as a latent bridge that should encode only the information about the response that cannot be inferred directly from the prompt. A semantic cost based on token surprisal under a frozen language‑model prior replaces uniform penalties, and the objective is optimized via Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). Across multiple mathematical reasoning benchmarks, CIB reduces token usage by up to 41% while limiting accuracy loss to at most 1.5%, achieving Pareto improvements over length‑penalty baselines. The β parameter gives precise control over the trade‑off, and a larger prior model (7B) yields stronger compression.

## Method Summary
The CIB framework models CoT generation with a latent variable Z (the reasoning trace) that is sufficient for predicting the answer Y given prompt X, yet minimally informative about X itself. The information‑theoretic objective is max_Z [ I(Y;Z|X) − β I(X;Z) ]. Sufficiency is enforced by an accuracy reward r_acc = 1( by(X,Z)=Y ), where by(X,Z) extracts the final answer from the generated trace via a deterministic parser. Minimality is approximated by a semantic token cost r_min = Σ_t −log Q_ϕ(z_t|z_<t ), where Q_ϕ is a frozen pre‑trained base language model (not instruction‑tuned). The total reward R = r_acc + β·r_min is maximized using GRPO, which samples multiple traces per prompt, computes group‑relative advantages within each set, and updates the policy π_θ with a KL penalty toward the reference model. Training uses the DeepScaleR dataset filtered to exclude prompts with zero group‑reward variance. Hyperparameters include AdamW (lr=1e−6), batch size 128, 16 generations per prompt, temperature 0.8, KL penalty 5e−4, and 150 steps. At evaluation, vLLM inference (T=0.6, top_p=0.95) with 16 generations yields pass@1 scores. The approach provides a smooth accuracy‑compression curve; larger priors (7B) improve the quality of the surprisal cost and enable greater compression at similar accuracy levels.

## Key Results
- Token reduction up to 41% with ≤1.5% accuracy drop on challenging math benchmarks (MATH500, AIME24, AIME25, OlympiadBench).
- Outperforms length‑penalty baselines (e.g., L3L1) by achieving higher accuracy at comparable compression levels, establishing a superior Pareto frontier.
- The trade‑off is controlled by a single β parameter; a larger frozen prior (7B) further enhances compression capability without sacrificing accuracy when properly tuned.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
Standard Information Bottleneck (IB) assumes a Markov chain Y↔X↔Z, meaning the reasoning trace Z must contain all information about the answer Y that is not in the prompt X. However, in transformer decoders, the prediction of Y attends to both X and Z simultaneously, forming a collider structure (X,Z)→Y. This violates the Markov property, causing inefficiency: Z may retain redundant information about X because Y is directly accessible from X during decoding. This theoretical incompatibility is termed the "Attention Paradox."

### Mechanism 2
Conditional Information Bottleneck (CIB) aligns the reasoning objective to the transformer architecture by conditioning on the prompt X. CIB maximizes I(Y;Z|X) (sufficiency of Z for Y given X) while minimizing I(X;Z) (redundancy of Z about X). This ensures Z only supplements missing information, resolving the Attention Paradox by treating X as always‑available side information. Under CIB, if the prompt already contains all necessary information for Y, an optimal Z would be empty.

### Mechanism 3
Instead of uniform length penalties, CIB employs a semantic cost derived from token surprisal under a frozen prior Q_ϕ. The per‑token cost −log Q_ϕ(z_t|z_<t) is high for surprising, information‑rich tokens and low for predictable, redundant ones. The RL reward R = r_acc + β·r_min thus **penalizes** verbose, low‑surprisal fillers more than concise, high‑surprisal reasoning steps. This selective pruning preserves fluency and logic while compressing the trace. A stronger prior (larger model) gives a more accurate redundancy signal and yields better compression.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map** (plain text flow):  
Policy πθ (LLM) → generates reasoning trace Z|X and final answer Y|X,Z  
Frozen prior Q_ϕ (base LM) → computes token‑wise surprisal −log Q_ϕ(z_t|z_<t) for Z, summed as r_min  
Verifier Q_ρ (accuracy checker) → compares extracted answer by(X,Z) to ground truth Y, yielding r_acc (binary, possibly ε‑smoothed)  
Trainer (GRPO) → samples multiple Z per X, computes group‑relative advantages from R = r_acc + β r_min, performs policy gradient with KL penalty  
Training dataset PD(X,Y) → provides prompts and answers

**Critical path**
1. For each prompt x in a batch, sample k=16 traces z_j ~ π_θ(.|x).
2. For each (x, z_j):
   - Extract answer by(x,z_j) via deterministic parser.
   - Compute r_acc = 1(by == y).
   - Compute r_min = Σ_t −log Q_ϕ(z_j,t | z_j,<t).
   - R_j = r_acc + β * r_min.
3. Within group of 16 traces for the same x, compute mean μ_x and std σ_x of R_j; advantage A_j = (R_j − μ_x) / (σ_x + ε).
4. Update π_θ using GRPO with KL divergence penalty toward reference policy (usually the initial model), learning rate 1e−6.
5. Repeat for N steps (150). Q_ϕ and Q_ρ remain frozen.

**Design tradeoffs**
- Prior strength: Larger Q_ϕ (7B) gives more accurate redundancy estimates, enabling stronger compression but may require retuning β; smaller priors are cheaper but less precise.
- β selection: Controls trade‑off; low β (e.g., 5e−5) yields conservative compression, high β (e.g., 1.5e−4) yields aggressive compression but risks over‑compression and logic collapse.
- Verifier: Binary ε‑smoothed reward is simple; a learned reward model could provide granular feedback but adds training complexity.
- KL penalty (β_KL): Prevents policy from deviating too far; too low → collapse to degenerate short traces, too high → hinders learning.

**Failure signatures**
- Over‑compression: Sharp accuracy drop on hard benchmarks (e.g., AIME) while lengths plateau. Likely β too high or prior Q_ϕ mismatched.
- Under‑compression: Length distribution close to baseline, r_min not decreasing with larger β. Possibly Q_ϕ is instruction‑tuned or too weak, or β too low.
- Degraded logic/fluency: Traces become incoherent or skip necessary steps. Inspect examples; may indicate over‑penalization of high‑surprisal tokens or insufficient training.
- KL collapse: Policy emits very short or repetitive traces; monitor KL divergence and adjust β_KL or add entropy bonus.

**First 3 experiments**
1. **Validate minimality reward correlation.** Plot r_min versus sequence length for a sample of baseline generations (as in Figure 2). Expect a strong negative correlation; if the correlation is flat, the prior Q_ϕ is invalid.
2. **Compare against length‑penalty baseline.** Train a model with β = 0 (accuracy only) and with a uniform length penalty (equivalent to using a uniform prior, per Proposition 4.1). Compare length distribution and accuracy; CIB should shift the distribution left without catastrophic drop.
3. **Ablate prior size and β.** Sweep β ∈ {5e−5, 1.5e−4} and prior size (1.5B, 7B) on a held‑out set (e.g., MATH500). Plot accuracy vs. average length; expect a Pareto frontier shift, with the larger prior enabling more compression at the same accuracy.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **β scaling with prior capacity:** How does the optimal β scale with the capacity of the reference prior, and can independent tuning for each prior size recover the slight accuracy degradation observed when scaling from 1.5B to 7B? The authors report a ~1.4% accuracy drop when using a 7B prior with β tuned for a 1.5B prior, speculating that hyperparameter re‑tuning would close the gap but could not test due to resource limits.
2. **Generalization beyond mathematics:** Does CIB’s semantic compression generalize to reasoning domains where verbosity correlates with correctness (e.g., creative writing, planning), or does it risk over‑compression that harms performance? All experiments are on mathematical reasoning benchmarks where concise traces are assumed beneficial; it is unclear whether gains transfer or if CIB might suppress useful scaffolding in other contexts.
3. **Unifying other heuristics:** Can other budget‑forcing heuristics (e.g., difficulty‑aware token allocation, reference‑guided budgeting) be formulated as specific prior choices within the CIB framework, and do these instantiated methods match or surpass the performance of the original heuristics? The paper unifies uniform length penalties via a uniform prior but does not extend the analysis to the wide range of heuristic methods discussed in Related Work; the framework’s claimed generality remains undemonstrated.

## Limitations
- The exact answer extraction logic from CoT traces is unspecified, which critically determines the accuracy reward signal.
- The training data filtering criterion (“remove prompts with zero group reward standard deviation”) lacks implementation details, including how the initial group reward is computed.
- The handling of the ε‑smoothing in the verifier is ambiguous; the paper claims equivalence to a binary reward but does not state whether ε = 0 or a small positive value.

## Confidence
- **Attention Paradox & CIB theoretical foundation:** High – Derivation follows from transformer causal attention and information theory.
- **Semantic cost via surprisal prior:** Medium – Empirically validated but sensitive to prior choice and domain match.
- **Empirical compression‑accuracy trade‑off:** Medium – Results are self‑reported; missing implementation details hinder independent verification.
- **Scalability with larger prior (7B):** Medium – Plausible but not thoroughly explored; may not generalize beyond tested benchmarks.
