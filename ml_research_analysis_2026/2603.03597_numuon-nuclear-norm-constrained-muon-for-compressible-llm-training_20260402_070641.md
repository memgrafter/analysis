# NuMuon: Nuclear-Norm-Constrained Muon for Compressible LLM Training

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.03597
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03597
- **Reference count**: 0
- **Primary result**: NuMuon improves SVD-based LLM compression, reducing validation perplexity by up to 99.7% at 40% compression and boosting downstream task accuracy by up to 55.8% over Muon, especially at extreme compression (60–80%).

## Executive Summary
NuMuon modifies the Muon optimizer by adding a nuclear-norm constraint to the update direction, explicitly promoting low-rank weight structure that enhances compressibility. While Muon already exhibits implicit low-rank characteristics, NuMuon directly controls the rank of each update via a top-k singular vector step and a gradual rank scheduler. Evaluations on decoder-only models from 0.6B to 1.8B parameters confirm that NuMuon maintains Muon’s convergence speed while substantially improving quality after aggressive SVD-based compression, with particularly strong gains at high compression ratios (60–80%). The method thus offers a practical path to deploying smaller, efficient LLMs without sacrificing performance.

## Method Summary
NuMuon builds on Muon’s orthogonal update rule, which uses the polar decomposition of the momentum matrix to produce a full-rank direction. The key innovation replaces this full-rank outer product \(U_t V_t^\top\) with a low-rank approximation using only the top‑k singular vectors of the momentum matrix. The rank \(k\) is bounded by a nuclear‑norm budget \(\tau\), derived from solving a linear minimization oracle over a spectral‑norm ball extended with a nuclear‑norm constraint; the solution is a greedy top‑k step. A rank scheduler gradually reduces the per‑layer rank fraction \(r(t)\) from 1.0 to a target (e.g., 0.25) during training, allowing high‑rank exploration early and low‑rank refinement later. The top‑k subspace is approximated via randomized Block Krylov SVD (2 iterations, block size 8) for efficiency. Training otherwise follows Muon’s protocol (WSD learning rate, weight decay, etc.). The result is models with lower normalized stable rank that compress far better under SVD pipelines.

## Key Results
- NuMuon achieves significantly lower normalized stable rank than Muon across all weight matrices in 0.6B–1.8B models.
- Under SVD compression (ASVD, SVD‑LLM, Dobi‑SVD), NuMuon reduces validation perplexity by up to 99.7% at 40% compression and improves average downstream task accuracy by up to 55.8% vs. Muon, with pronounced gains at extreme rates (60–80%).
- NuMuon preserves Muon’s favorable convergence behavior while substantially enhancing compressibility.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Implicit low-rank induction by Muon**  
Muon’s full-rank orthogonal updates interact with the loss landscape and gradient noise to concentrate weight matrices into lower‑dimensional subspaces, as reflected by a stable rank of ~0.2–0.4 during training. This emergent low‑rank structure makes Muon‑trained models naturally compressible, although it degrades under aggressive compression limits.

**Mechanism 2: Explicit rank control via nuclear‑norm constraint**  
NuMuon augments Muon’s linear minimization oracle with a nuclear‑norm constraint. The LMO reduces to a linear program over singular values, yielding a closed‑form top‑k solution \(\Delta W^* = -\rho U_k V_k^\top\) with \(k \le \lceil \tau/\rho \rceil\). Each update is forced low‑rank, and under conditional‑gradient updates the iterates contract toward the low‑rank feasible set, directly shaping the singular value profile of the weights.

**Mechanism 3: Rank scheduler for convergence‑compressibility trade‑off**  
Early training needs high‑rank updates to escape poor basins; a schedule (e.g., cosine decay) starts with full rank and anneals to a low target rank before learning‑rate decay. This balances exploration (ensuring convergence) and exploitation (building a compact weight structure) to maximize final compressibility without harming base performance.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Gradient → Momentum \(M_t\) → (Rank scheduler → \(k_t\)) → Top‑k SVD of \(M_t\) (\(U_k,V_k\)) → Low‑rank update \(\Delta W = -\gamma U_k V_k^\top\) → Weight update \(W \leftarrow W + \Delta W\).

**Critical path**  
1. Compute gradient \(G_t = \nabla_W f(W_t)\).  
2. Update momentum \(M_t = \beta M_{t-1} + (1-\beta) G_t\).  
3. Determine current rank \(k_t = \lceil r_t \cdot \min(d_{\text{in}}, d_{\text{out}}) \rceil\) from scheduler.  
4. Approximate top‑k SVD of \(M_t\) → \(U_k, V_k\) via Block Krylov (L=2, b=8).  
5. Apply update \(W_{t+1} = W_t - \gamma U_k V_k^\top\).

**Design tradeoffs**  
- *Rank budget vs. convergence*: Lower final rank improves compressibility but risks underfitting; sweet spot often \(r_{\text{end}} \in [0.25,0.5]\).  
- *Scheduler choice*: Fixed rank is simple but may harm base quality; cosine/piecewise adds minor overhead but yields better trade‑offs.  
- *SVD approximation accuracy*: More Krylov iterations or larger block size improves top‑k fidelity but increases cost.

**Failure signatures**  
- Early divergence/plateau: \(k_t\) too small during warmup → increase initial rank or warmup duration.  
- Poor high‑rate compression: final stable rank too high → lower \(r_{\text{end}}\) or anneal faster.  
- Excessive per‑step time: high \(k_t\) or too many iterations → reduce initial rank or iteration count.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. Train Qwen3‑0.6B with Muon and NuMuon (cosine \(r_{\text{end}}=0.25\)); track normalized stable rank. Expect NuMuon ≥20% lower.  
2. Apply ASVD/SVD‑LLM at 60% and 80% compression to both checkpoints; measure WikiText2 PPL delta. Expect NuMuon to degrade ≤50% relative to Muon.  
3. Ablate rank schedulers (cosine vs. piecewise vs. fixed) on validation PPL and 80% Dobi‑SVD PPL with fixed \(\tau\). Expect cosine best trade‑off.

## Limitations
- Exact details of the rank scheduler timing (decay start/end within WSD phases) are unspecified, potentially affecting the rank trajectory and results.
- Block Krylov SVD configuration (block size vs. oversample, iteration count) and Muon’s Newton‑Schulz iteration count are not given, introducing variability in replication.
- The assumed bound on gradient tail energy (Assumption 3.5) has only been validated on a single small model; its generality across architectures and datasets remains uncertain.
