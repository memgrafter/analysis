# The Aftermath of DrawEduMath: Vision Language Models Underperform with Struggling Students and Misdiagnose Errors
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.00925
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00925
- **Reference count:** 25
- **Primary result:** All 11 evaluated VLMs significantly underperform on erroneous student handwritten math responses, with a persistent bias toward assuming error‑free solutions, especially on content description questions.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents the first large‑scale audit of vision‑language models (VLMs) on real student handwritten math responses, using the DrawEduMath benchmark. The core finding is that VLMs systematically fail on erroneous work across all 11 tested models, with performance gaps that remain even after controlling for problem difficulty and reducing image noise. The models exhibit a "default‑to‑correct" bias, frequently producing answers that match the gold responses for non‑erroneous versions of the same problem. Correctness and error diagnosis questions remain particularly challenging, often performing near random chance. These results reveal fundamental weaknesses in VLM handling of student mistakes, challenging their readiness for high‑stakes educational applications like automated feedback.

## Method Summary
The authors evaluate 11 VLMs on DrawEduMath, a benchmark of 2,030 real student handwritten images from ASSISTments, each with teacher‑annotated captions and question‑answer (QA) pairs. QA are categorized into three types: image creation/medium, correctness & errors, and content description. Responses are labeled as erroneous or non‑erroneous via teacher annotations and a validated GPT‑5‑mini classifier. Model outputs are scored by an ensemble of three LLMs (Claude Sonnet 4.5, Gemini 2.5 Pro, GPT‑4o) on a 1‑4 scale, then binarized. Primary analysis compares accuracy between erroneous and non‑erroneous responses using fixed‑effects regression controlling for the math problem. Follow‑up experiments include digitally redrawing a stratified sample (336 images) to reduce noise, injecting gold textual descriptions, and analyzing patterns of model errors.

## Key Results
- VLMs underperform on erroneous responses across all question types; the gap is largest for content description QA (effect sizes β₁=0.058–0.101, p<0.001).
- 29–35% of incorrect model answers on erroneous responses match the true answers for the corresponding non‑erroneous responses, evidencing a bias toward assuming correct solutions.
- The performance gap persists after digitally redrawing images to reduce noise, though it narrows for some models.
- Correctness & error QA remains difficult even with gold natural language descriptions; binary correctness judgments for several models hover near chance (~0.5).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** VLMs exhibit a bias toward error‑free mathematical content due to training distribution.
- **Mechanism:** Models are primarily trained on correct math solutions, learning to expect valid notations and answers. When faced with erroneous student work, they misrecognize elements or hallucinate correct parts, leading to underperformance on content description for erroneous responses.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract]: "VLMs may be optimized to be math problem solving experts" and "models’ weaknesses concentrate on a core component of math education: student error." [Section 6]: "We find that some models’ wrongly predicted answers for erroneous student work are similar to gold answers for non‑erroneous ones" and Table 3 shows 29–35% of incorrect model answers matched non‑erroneous gold answers.
- **Break condition:** If VLMs are fine‑tuned on large‑scale erroneous student responses, the performance gap on erroneous inputs narrows significantly.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Image noise from handwritten responses compounds difficulty but is not the primary cause of the performance gap.
- **Mechanism:** Handwritten submissions introduce visual noise that obscures notation. However, the redrawing experiment cleaned images while preserving student intent, and the gap persisted, indicating that mathematical errors themselves—not just visual noise—are the key challenge.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract]: "underperforming on erroneous versus non‑erroneous student work across question types, controlling for problem difficulty and image noise." [Section 5]: "On redrawn images, VLMs’ performance shift in the expected direction... models’ performance gap between erroneous and non‑erroneous student images remains in redrawn images" (Table 2).
- **Break condition:** If the performance gap disappeared entirely after redrawing, noise would be the main cause; since the gap remains, the break condition is not met.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Correctness & error QA requires evaluative reasoning that VLMs lack, and textual support only partially mitigates this.
- **Mechanism:** These questions ask models to judge correctness or diagnose errors, demanding comparison to ground truth and reasoning about deviations. VLMs are typically trained to generate/describe content, not evaluate. Providing gold descriptions offloads visual perception but leaves evaluative reasoning, which remains difficult.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract]: "across all QA, they struggle the most on questions related to assessing student error." [Section 7]: "models do improve in assessing student correctness when provided gold natural language descriptions... however, their performance... still lags behind their out‑of‑the‑box performance on other question types." [Section 8]: "some VLMs’ binary QA scores hover closely around a random baseline of 0.5."
- **Break condition:** If VLMs achieve near‑human accuracy on correctness & error QA without textual support, the evaluative reasoning gap would be disproven.

## Foundational Learning
- **Vision‑Language Models (VLMs):** Integrate visual and textual information, pretrained on image‑text corpora with objectives like contrastive learning, captioning, and VQA. *Why needed:* The paper evaluates VLMs on multimodal educational data; understanding their pretraining helps explain strengths and weaknesses. *Quick check:* How might objectives like image‑text contrast vs. captioning affect a model’s ability to describe student work vs. evaluate its correctness?
- **Mathematics Education – Error Analysis:** The pedagogical practice of examining student mistakes to inform instruction and provide formative feedback. *Why needed:* The paper’s focus on student errors is central to math education; errors are opportunities for learning, and automated diagnosis is a key potential application. *Quick check:* Why is identifying the nature of a student’s error (e.g., misconception vs. arithmetic slip) more valuable than just flagging an answer as wrong?
- **Benchmark Evaluation in AI:** A standardized dataset and protocol for measuring and comparing model capabilities. *Why needed:* DrawEduMath acts as a benchmark to audit VLM performance on a specific, real‑world task, enabling tracking of progress and weaknesses. *Quick check:* What makes a benchmark "good" for evaluating educational AI (e.g., authenticity, diversity, clear metrics), and how might DrawEduMath meet or fall short of these criteria?
- **Fixed‑Effects Regression:** A statistical model that controls for unobserved heterogeneity (e.g., specific math problems) by including fixed effects for each problem. *Why needed:* The paper uses this to isolate the effect of response erroneous‑ness on VLM accuracy while accounting for problem difficulty. *Quick check:* Why is it important to control for the specific math problem when comparing performance on erroneous vs. non‑erroneous responses?
- **LLM‑as‑a‑Judge:** Using large language models to evaluate the quality of other models’ outputs, often via scoring against a gold standard. *Why needed:* The study relies on an LLM judge ensemble to score VLM answers at scale, avoiding costly human annotation. *Quick check:* What are the risks of using LLMs as judges (e.g., inheriting model biases, inconsistencies) and how might the paper’s triple‑vote scheme mitigate them?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** DrawEduMath Dataset (images, teacher captions, QA) -> VLM Inference (11 model APIs) -> LLM Judge Scoring (ensemble of 3 models) -> Statistical Analysis (fixed‑effects regression, significance testing) -> Auxiliary Experiments (image redrawing pipeline, textual description injection).
- **Critical path:** (a) Preprocess dataset: label erroneous/non‑erroneous via teacher annotations and GPT‑5‑mini classifier. (b) For each VLM, generate answers for all QA inputs. (c) Score answers using LLM judge and binarize. (d) Aggregate accuracy by response and QA type. (e) Compare erroneous vs. non‑erroneous performance using regression controlling for problem. (f) Run follow‑up experiments: redrawing, textual support, default answer analysis, binary vs open‑ended splits.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - LLM judge vs. human: Using an LLM ensemble scales evaluation but may inherit biases; mitigated by high human correlation (ρ=0.808) and majority voting.
  - Teacher‑written QA quality: Reflects real educator concerns but is limited in number; synthetic QA augments volume but may introduce noise.
  - Redrawing sample size: Full dataset redrawing is ideal but labor‑intensive; authors used a stratified sample of 336 images, trading comprehensiveness for feasibility.
  - Binary accuracy: Simplifies interpretation but discards partial‑credit nuances; original dataset used a 1–4 scale.
  - Model selection: 11 models from 2025 provide a snapshot but may not capture earliest/latest models; focuses on recent, relevant architectures.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Low inter‑annotator agreement between LLM judge and humans (below ~0.7) would question scoring reliability.
  - If the GPT‑5‑mini error classifier had poor agreement with human labels (<0.9), the erroneous/non‑erroneous split would be noisy.
  - If redrawn images inadvertently changed mathematical meaning (checked via teacher captions), the noise manipulation would be invalid.
  - Dataset skew: >85% of images from Title I schools may limit generalizability, though acknowledged.
  - If newer models (post‑2025) resolve the gap, findings may be time‑bound; snapshot remains valid for its period.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Main performance disparity: Compute average accuracy for each VLM on erroneous vs. non‑erroneous responses across question types; show consistent underperformance on erroneous work (Figure 2, Table 1).
  2. Image noise impact: Redraw a stratified sample of erroneous and non‑erroneous responses digitally, re‑evaluate VLMs, and test if the performance gap persists (Table 2, Figure 5).
  3. Textual support for correctness QA: Add gold teacher captions to prompts for correctness & error questions and measure accuracy improvement relative to baseline (Figure 7).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Does the performance gap between erroneous and non‑erroneous student responses generalize to other educational math benchmarks, languages, and diverse student demographics beyond the primarily Title I U.S. schools in ASSISTments?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The study is limited to one English benchmark (DrawEduMath) from a single online learning platform, with 85% of images from Title I schools, raising representativeness concerns.
- **Why unresolved:** Findings may not hold in other cultural, linguistic, or socioeconomic contexts; the composition of student errors and image characteristics could differ.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicate the analysis on additional multimodal math benchmarks with varied student populations, error types, and question designs, comparing effect sizes and model behaviors.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How can VLMs be adaptively trained or fine‑tuned to improve their understanding of erroneous mathematical reasoning without degrading performance on correct solutions?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper suggests models are biased toward error‑free math due to training on "high quality" correct content, and they default to assuming correct solutions when presented with errors.
- **Why unresolved:** No prior work addresses curricular integration of erroneous student data into VLM training; it is unknown if doing so would help models recognize errors or introduce new biases.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Train VLMs on curated datasets with balanced erroneous/non‑erroneous samples, measuring changes in error detection accuracy and robustness across question types.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Why do gold natural language descriptions of student work improve VLMs’ correctness assessment but not to the level of their performance on other question types (e.g., content description)?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 7 shows textual support boosts correctness & error QA yet still lags behind captionless performance on other QAs; the authors note "challenging for VLMs to make higher‑level inferences."
- **Why unresolved:** It is unclear whether the residual gap stems from missing visual details in descriptions, insufficient multimodal reasoning in models, or inherent difficulty of correctness judgments.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct error analyses on model outputs with/without descriptions, systematically vary description completeness, and probe internal representations for correctness‑related signals.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Why do some VLMs’ binary correctness judgments hover near chance (~0.5), and what factors (e.g., question phrasing, model calibration) drive this inconsistent performance?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 8 reports that binary correctness QA scores for several models are "barely better than chance," with differing patterns across model families and versions.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not investigate whether poor binary performance is due to ambiguous question wording, models’ inability to handle binary logic, or lack of calibration for uncertainty.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Reframe binary questions to reduce ambiguity, analyze model confidence distributions, and evaluate whether fine‑tuning on similar binary tasks improves accuracy.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Does the performance gap on erroneous responses persist when image noise is uniformly reduced across the entire dataset, or is it primarily driven by noisier images from struggling students?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The redrawing experiment (Section 5) shows the gap remains after cleaning a small sample (336 images), but the authors note the sample size is limited.
- **Why unresolved:** The small‑scale redrawing cannot definitively isolate the effect of error from noise; it remains possible that broader noise reduction would narrow the gap.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply automated or human redrawing to all images, or use controlled synthetic degradation experiments, comparing performance deltas across error conditions and noise levels.

## Limitations
- Dataset composition: ≥85% of images from Title I schools, limiting demographic generalizability.
- Redrawing experiment: only 336 images were redrawn; full‑scale replication would increase statistical power.
- Binary accuracy metric discards partial‑credit nuances and may oversimplify performance.
- LLM judge ensemble details (prompt templates, exact model versions) are not fully reported, risking scoring inconsistencies.
- Model selection restricted to 11 VLMs from 2025; newer architectures could behave differently.
- Error labels derived from a GPT‑5‑mini classifier; although validated, automated labeling may introduce noise.
- Synthetic QA may not fully capture real educator priorities compared to teacher‑written questions.

## Confidence
- **VLMs systematically underperform on erroneous student work** (effect size β₁=0.058–0.101, p<0.001 across 11 models): **High**.
- **Visual noise is not the primary cause; semantic errors drive the gap** (gap persists after redrawing): **Medium** (redrawing sample small).
- **VLMs lack evaluative reasoning for correctness & error QA and show “default‑to‑correct” bias**: **Medium** (binary metric and LLM‑judge reliability uncertain).

## Next Checks
1. **Full redrawing:** Digitally redraw all 2,030 images and re‑evaluate to conclusively isolate the effect of visual noise.
2. **Human judge validation:** Conduct a human evaluation on a stratified subset of model outputs for correctness & error QA to verify LLM‑judge reliability and detect systematic biases.
3. **Fine‑tuning study:** Fine‑tune a representative VLM on a large corpus of erroneous student responses (e.g., from ASSISTments) and test whether the performance gap narrows, directly testing the bias hypothesis.
