# Set-based v.s. Distribution-based Representations of Epistemic Uncertainty: A Comparative Study

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.22747
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22747
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** Neither set-based nor distribution-based epistemic uncertainty representations is uniformly superior; performance depends critically on the interaction between the representation and the adopted uncertainty measure, with OOD detection tasks more clearly distinguishing the two.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a controlled empirical comparison between set-based (credal) and distribution-based representations of epistemic uncertainty in neural network classifiers. By constructing both representations from the same finite set of predictive distributions produced by shared second-order predictors (Bayesian neural networks, deep ensembles, and variants), the study isolates the effect of the representation itself from differences in predictive accuracy. Across a extensive evaluation involving three uncertainty measures per representation, eight benchmark tasks (selective prediction and out-of-distribution detection), six predictive models, and ten random seeds, the results reveal no single representation dominates. The choice of uncertainty measure interacts strongly with the representation, and OOD detection tasks provide clearer discrimination between them than selective prediction. For distribution-based uncertainty, Wasserstein distance consistently outperforms mutual information and label-wise variance. For set-based uncertainty, the Generalized Hartley measure is strongest, while entropy difference performs poorly.

## Method Summary
The core methodology constructs both uncertainty representations from an identical finite set of M predictive probability distributions \(\{p_m\}_{m=1}^M\) generated by a shared second-order predictor (e.g., a Bayesian neural network via SVI, an MCDO model, or a deep ensemble). The distribution-based representation treats this set as a uniform mixture distribution. The set-based representation constructs a credal set \(K\) as the class-wise intervals \([p_k^L, p_k^U]\) defined by the minimum and maximum probability assigned to each class \(k\) across all members of the set. This credal set compactly captures the range of plausible predictions. Three uncertainty measures are computed for each representation: Mutual Information and Label-wise Variance for distribution-based, and Entropy Difference, Generalized Hartley Measure, and Maximum Mean Imprecision for set-based. These measures are evaluated on downstream tasks: selective prediction (rejecting uncertain predictions, measured via AUARC) and OOD detection (using uncertainty as an OOD score, measured via AUROC). Experiments span CIFAR-10 (with SVHN, FMNIST as OOD), Camelyon17 (medical tiles, domain shift), and SeaShip (ship classification, with SeaShip-C and SeaShip-O as OOD).

## Key Results
- The efficacy of uncertainty quantification is jointly determined by the choice of representation (set vs. distribution) and the specific uncertainty measure; neither representation is universally superior.
- OOD detection tasks provide a clearer and more consistent signal to differentiate between representations compared to selective prediction tasks.
- For distribution-based methods, Wasserstein distance is the most robust and consistently high-performing uncertainty measure.
- For set-based methods, the Generalized Hartley measure achieves the strongest results, while the Entropy Difference measure performs poorly across the board.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The study's design successfully isolates representational effects by deriving both the set-based credal set and the distribution-based mixture from the *exact same* finite set of model predictions \(\{p_m\}\). This ensures any performance difference in downstream tasks stems solely from how uncertainty is quantified from this set (the representation and measure), not from variations in the underlying classifier's accuracy or diversity. The credal set construction uses element-wise min/max to form intervals, guaranteeing all \(\{p_m\}\) are contained within \(K\). The distribution-based mixture averages them. This controlled setup allows a clean, apples-to-apples comparison that prior work often confounded by comparing different model families.

## Foundational Learning
- **Epistemic Uncertainty:** Uncertainty due to limited training data, reflecting model ignorance. *Why needed:* The paper's core objective is to represent this type of uncertainty. *Quick check:* Should decrease with more training data or model capacity.
- **Bayesian Neural Networks (BNNs):** Models that place a prior over weights and infer a posterior, yielding a distribution over functions. *Why needed:* A primary source of the predictive distributions \(\{p_m\}\) via methods like SVI. *Quick check:* Posterior predictive variance should capture model uncertainty.
- **Deep Ensembles:** A set of independently trained neural networks. *Why needed:* Another main source of \(\{p_m\}\), providing diverse predictions. *Quick check:* Ensemble member predictions should disagree on ambiguous inputs.
- **Credal Set:** A set of probability distributions, used here as a set-based representation. *Why needed:* The set-based representation explicitly models imprecision via intervals. *Quick check:* For a given input, all \(\{p_m\}\) must lie within the credal set class-wise.
- **Wasserstein Distance:** A metric between probability distributions. *Why needed:* The top-performing distribution-based measure, computing the "cost" to transform one predictive distribution into another. *Quick check:* Should be zero if all \(\{p_m\}\) are identical.
- **Generalized Hartley Measure:** A set-based uncertainty measure based on the volume of the credal set. *Why needed:* The top-performing set-based measure, quantifying total imprecision. *Quick check:* Zero if all class intervals collapse to a single distribution.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component Map:
Input Data -> Second-Order Predictor (BNN/Ensemble) -> Finite Set of Predictions {p_m} -> Split Path: (A) Distribution-based Mixture -> Uncertainty Measure (MI/LV/WD) -> Score; (B) Set-based Credal Set (Intervals) -> Uncertainty Measure (HDiff/GH/MMI) -> Score -> Downstream Task (AUROC/AUARC)

Critical Path:
1. Train a second-order predictor to obtain a set of \(M\) predictive distributions for each input.
2. For each input, construct *both* representations from the *same* \(\{p_m\}\).
3. Compute all three uncertainty measures for each representation.
4. Evaluate each measure's score on the downstream task (OOD detection or selective prediction) across the entire test set.
5. Aggregate results (AUROC for OOD, AUARC for selective) and compare across the 3x3 grid of (representation × measure).

Design Tradeoffs:
- The credal set (min/max intervals) is extremely simple and efficient to compute but may be overly conservative, ignoring the distribution of predictions within the interval.
- The distribution-based mixture is a full probability distribution, capturing the central tendency but potentially under-representing prediction spread if the set is multi-modal.
- Wasserstein distance is computationally heavier (requires solving an optimal transport problem) but aligns better with human judgment of distributional dissimilarity than information-theoretic metrics like MI.
- Generalized Hartley measure is a closed-form volume calculation for intervals, making it efficient and interpretable.

Failure Signatures:
- **Credal Set Mismatch:** The set-based intervals do not contain all \(\{p_m\}\). *Diagnostic:* For a sample input, check \(p_k^L \leq p_m,k \leq p_k^U\) for all \(m,k\).
- **Wasserstein Solver Error:** Multi-class Wasserstein (Eq. 8) implementation produces NaNs or values not matching the binary closed-form (Eq. 9) on binary tasks. *Diagnostic:* Test on a binary problem with known distributions.
- **Evaluation Leak:** Using different model checkpoints for ID and OOD evaluation, or not sorting uncertainty ascending for selective prediction. *Diagnostic:* Ensure a single set of predictions per input feeds all measures; check ARC curve monotonicity.
- **Dataset Misalignment:** Using incorrect Camelyon17 centers or SeaShip corruption types. *Diagnostic:* Verify dataset statistics and OOD definitions against Table A.1 and cited sources.

First Three Experiments:
1. **Binary Verification:** On a binary dataset (e.g., CIFAR-10 binary subset), compute WD and MMI for a hand-crafted set \(\{p_m\}\). Verify outputs match Equations 9 and 17 exactly.
2. **Representation Isolation Test:** For a fixed ensemble, compute all six measures on the same validation set. Confirm that the distribution-based and set-based scores are not perfectly correlated, proving they capture different aspects.
3. **Downstream Sanity Check:** On CIFAR-10/SVHN, pick the best measure from each representation (WD and GH). Compute AUROC for OOD detection. Expect WD and GH to both perform well (>0.90 AUROC), but one may be slightly higher, illustrating the interaction effect.

## Limitations
- **Hyperparameter Gaps:** Critical training details for SVI (prior variance), MCDO (dropout rate), and ensembles (weight decay, scheduler) are omitted, preventing exact replication of the underlying predictors.
- **OOD Dataset Ambiguity:** Specifics of the SeaShip OOD datasets (corruption types in SeaShip-C, cropping/downsampling ratios in SeaShip-O) and checkpoint alignment across ID/OOD evaluations are unclear.
- **Implementation Underspecification:** The method for solving the multi-class Wasserstein barycenter (Eq. 8) and the convex optimization for multi-class Hdiff (Eq. 11) are not described (solver, tolerance), risking non-comparable implementations.
- **Resource Intensity:** Training 10 runs of multiple ensemble models on large datasets (e.g., SeaShip) is computationally demanding, and resource requirements are not quantified.

## Confidence
- **Interaction effect (representation × measure) dominates performance:** Medium. Supported by the controlled experimental design, but relies on correct multi-class implementations of WD and Hdiff which are underspecified.
- **OOD detection more discriminative than selective prediction:** Medium. Trend holds across benchmarks, but SeaShip OOD details are ambiguous.
- **Wasserstein distance is best distribution-based measure:** Medium. Consistent trend reported, but multi-class solver choice is an unknown variable.
- **Generalized Hartley is best set-based measure; Entropy Difference is worst:** Low. GH algorithm depends on unstated parameters; Hdiff is highly sensitive to entropy solver and numerical stability.
