# ARGUS: Seeing the Influence of Narrative Features on Persuasion in Argumentative Texts

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.24109  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.24109  
- **Reference count**: 29  
- **Primary result**: ARGUS shows that narrativity, when modeled as a continuous score, positively predicts persuasion in online arguments, with Curiosity and Suspense being the most influential narrative features; fine-tuned encoder models significantly outperform zero-shot large language models for detection tasks.

## Executive Summary
ARGUS investigates how narrative features affect persuasive success in online argumentative discussions, using the ChangeMyView subreddit as a testbed. The authors introduce a new corpus of 620 comments annotated with binary story labels and six fine-grained narrative features (Agency, Event Sequencing, World Making, Suspense, Curiosity, Surprise) based on Herman and Sternberg's frameworks. Using soft labels that preserve annotator rating distributions, they fine‑tune encoder models (DistilBERT, RoBERTa) to detect these features. Applied to nearly one million unlabeled comments, scalar narrativity scores predict higher persuasion (β=0.45, OR≈1.57), with Curiosity (β=0.60, OR≈1.82) and Suspense (β=0.15, OR≈1.16) emerging as key drivers, while Surprise has a negative effect (β=-0.11, OR≈0.89). The study demonstrates the value of scalar modeling, supervised fine‑tuning over zero‑shot LLMs, and opens avenues for deeper rhetorical analysis of persuasive writing.

## Method Summary
The study proceeds in several stages. First, the authors sample 620 ChangeMyView comments and have seven annotators rate each for binary story presence and six narrative features on a 5‑point Likert scale. Inter‑annotator reliability is assessed using Fleiss’ κ, ADI, and ICC, and soft labels are constructed from the label distributions. Second, multiple encoder models are fine‑tuned on these soft labels, with calibration via temperature scaling; nested cross‑validation selects the best model per feature. Third, the best scalar models are applied to a large corpus of 963,253 ChangeMyView comments to obtain predicted narrativity and feature scores. Finally, mixed‑effects logistic regressions model the relationship between these scores and the binary persuasion outcome (Δ award), including random intercepts for comment authors and original posters and controlling for comment length.

## Key Results
- Scalar narrativity score positively predicts persuasion (β=0.45, OR≈1.57).
- Among narrative features, Curiosity (β=0.60, OR≈1.82) and Suspense (β=0.15, OR≈1.16) show the strongest positive effects; Surprise has a significant negative effect (β=-0.11, OR≈0.89).
- Fine‑tuned encoder models (e.g., DistilBERT, RoBERTa) substantially outperform zero‑shot Llama 3.1 on both binary and scalar detection tasks (e.g., F1=0.842 for story scalar detection).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Representing narrativity as a scalar (graded) property rather than a binary label improves both model calibration and the accuracy of downstream persuasion analysis.
- **Mechanism**: Soft labels based on annotator rating distributions preserve inter‑annotator disagreement as meaningful signal about the strength or ambiguity of narrative features. Models trained on these distributions learn to predict a continuous strength score, which better captures the nuanced reality of subjective perception. When applied to persuasion prediction, this scalar score provides a more precise and less lossy predictor than a hard cutoff.
- **Core assumption**: Annotation disagreement for subjective tasks like narrative perception reflects stable perspectivist variation rather than mere noise; modeling this variation is beneficial.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "Treating narrativity as a scalar (soft labels) preserves annotation variability and improves downstream analysis accuracy compared to binary labels."
  - [section 3.2.2 & 3.4] Analysis of annotator clustering and rank correlation shows disagreement is systematic and consistent, not random.
  - [section 5.3.1] Binary narrativity discards information; persuasive comments show a normal distribution of scalar narrativity scores, while non‑persuasive cluster at low scores, indicating a graded relationship.
- **Break condition**: If the underlying annotator disagreement is found to be primarily random noise (e.g., through test‑retest reliability studies showing low individual consistency), the scalar approach may not provide benefit over a well‑defined binary schema.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Specific narrative features, particularly those related to reader‑response (Curiosity, Suspense), drive the positive effect of narrativity on persuasion more than structural features (Agency, Event Sequencing).
- **Mechanism**: Reader‑response features directly engage the audience's cognitive and emotional state—creating gaps in knowledge (Curiosity) or anticipation (Suspense)—which facilitates cognitive transportation and persuasive impact. Structural features provide a coherent narrative but contribute less to the persuasive "pull."
- **Core assumption**: The effect of narrativity on persuasion is not monolithic; different dimensions (text‑oriented vs. reader‑oriented) have distinct and measurable impacts on persuasive success.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "Feature‑level analysis reveals Curiosity (β=0.60, OR≈1.82) and Suspense (β=0.15, OR≈1.16) as key drivers, while Surprise has a negative effect (β=-0.11, OR≈0.89). Structural features (Agency, Event Sequencing) contribute modestly."
  - [section 5.2] Model 7 (scalar predictors) shows Curiosity has the largest β, Suspense is second, while Agency and Event Sequencing have small positive β. Surprise has a significant negative β.
  - [section 5.3.2] "Persuasive success is driven more by how a comment engages the reader by managing how information is disclosed."
- **Break condition**: If interaction effects between structural and reader‑response features are discovered to be synergistic (e.g., high Suspense only works with high Event Sequencing), the additive model used may be misspecified. The current analysis (Model 7) tests features separately, not in combination.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Fine‑tuned, smaller encoder‑based models (DistilBERT, RoBERTa) significantly outperform much larger zero‑shot LLMs (Llama 3.1) at both detecting narrative presence and rating the strength of fine‑grained narrative features.
- **Mechanism**: Encoder‑based models are explicitly optimized for the target task through supervised learning on a domain‑specific corpus, learning subtle textual patterns associated with narrative features. Zero‑shot LLMs, despite their scale, lack this task‑specific fine‑tuning and are prone to overgeneralization or misinterpreting nuanced narrative definitions in a prompt‑based setup.
- **Core assumption**: Task‑specific supervised fine‑tuning on a moderately‑sized, high‑quality annotated dataset provides a stronger inductive bias for detecting nuanced, theory‑grounded narrative features than the general world knowledge of a large zero‑shot LLM.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "Fine‑tuned encoders significantly outperform zero‑shot Llama 3.1 models in both binary and scalar tasks."
  - [section 4.2 & Table 6] Test set results show encoder models achieve F1 up to 0.842 (Story scalar) and strong RMSE; LLM results in Appendix A.3 show much lower F1 and higher RMSE.
  - [section A.3] "For all features, the fine‑tuned encoder‑based models outperform both Llama 3.1 Instruct 8B and 70B... The performance gap is especially large for the narrative features."
- **Break condition**: If advanced prompting strategies (e.g., chain‑of‑thought, few‑shot with tailored examples) or model architectures specifically designed for narrative understanding close the performance gap, the superiority of fine‑tuned encoders may be context‑dependent. The evidence is limited to zero‑shot Llama 3.1.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept**: Narrativity as a multidimensional, scalar construct (Herman & Sternberg frameworks).  
  Why needed here: The entire study rests on operationalizing narrativity not as a binary "story/non‑story" but as a spectrum composed of textual (Agency, Event Sequencing, World Making) and reader‑response (Suspense, Curiosity, Surprise) dimensions. Misunderstanding this leads to misinterpreting the core variable.  
  Quick check question: "How would you design an annotation guideline to capture 'Curiosity' as a continuous strength, not just a yes/no label? What is a minimal example text that scores a 1 vs. a 5?"

- **Concept**: Perspectivist annotation and soft labels for subjective NLP tasks.  
  Why needed here: The paper's key methodological innovation is using the distribution of annotator ratings (soft labels) instead of majority vote (hard labels). This is central to their claim about scalar narrativity and improved model performance.  
  Quick check question: "Given three annotators rating 'Suspense' as {2, 3, 5} for a text, what is the soft label representation? How does this differ from the binary (majority) label?"

- **Concept**: Mixed‑effects logistic regression for nested data.  
  Why needed here: The persuasion analysis (Section 5) uses mixed‑effects models with random intercepts for authors and OPs to account for repeated contributions in the large‑scale ChangeMyView corpus. Ignoring this hierarchy would violate independence assumptions.  
  Quick check question: "Why is a standard logistic regression inadequate for modeling `Delta` (persuasion) in the ChangeMyView corpus with 963k comments from many repeated users?"

- **Concept**: Model calibration for probabilistic predictions.  
  Why needed here: The downstream persuasion analysis relies on the *strength* of predicted narrativity scores. A poorly calibrated model (e.g., overconfident or poorly scaled probabilities) would distort the estimated β coefficients in the regression. Temperature scaling is used for calibration.  
  Quick check question: "If your model predicts a narrativity score of 0.9 for a comment but the true average annotator score is 0.6, what is the likely impact on the persuasion analysis that uses this score as a predictor?"

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:
  1.  **Data & Annotation Layer**: Raw ChangeMyView threads → Sampling (StorySeeker model) → Seven annotators → Binary "Story" label + 6x 5‑point Likert feature ratings → Inter‑annotator reliability metrics (Fleiss' κ, ADI, ICC) → Soft label distributions.
  2.  **Modeling Layer**: Encoder‑based models (DistilBERT, BERT, RoBERTa, ModernBERT) trained on soft labels (regression/classification) & binary "Story" model → Temperature scaling calibration → Best model per feature selected via nested CV.
  3.  **Application Layer**: Best scalar models (chosen via nested CV) applied to 963k unlabeled ChangeMyView comments → Predictions (narrativity score, feature strengths) → Dataset for persuasion analysis.
  4.  **Analysis Layer**: Mixed‑effects logistic regression (Δ ~ predictor + text length + (1|author) + (1|OP_author)) → Coefficients (β), Odds Ratios (OR), p‑values.
- **Critical path**: Annotation schema definition → Human annotation & reliability analysis → Soft label creation → Model selection & training (nested CV) → Calibration → Large‑scale prediction → Regression analysis with random effects. Failure at the annotation/soft‑label stage propagates to all downstream results.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - **Scalar vs. Binary Narrativity**: Scalar preserves information and improves calibration/analysis but is more complex to model and interpret. Binary is simpler but lossy.
  - **Encoder vs. LLM**: Fine‑tuned encoders are task‑specific and superior here but require labeled data and retraining. Zero‑shot LLMs are flexible but underperform on nuanced tasks.
  - **Feature Set**: Including all six features vs. composites (Structural/Response) vs. individual. Composites simplify analysis but mask individual effects (e.g., Surprise's negative effect).
- **Failure signatures**:
  - **Poor Model Calibration**: Low Brier score / high Wasserstein distance on validation set. Check: Are predicted distributions matching annotated distributions? Revisit loss function (MSE vs. distribution matching) and calibration step.
  - **Domain Mismatch**: Model performance drops on external datasets (StorySeeker, NarrDetect). Check: Training data domain (CMV) vs. target domain. May need in‑domain fine‑tuning.
  - **Feature Collinearity**: High correlation between narrative feature predictions. Check: Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) in regression models (5.2). Could obscure individual feature effects.
  - **Random Effects Overfitting**: Covariance matrix issues in mixed‑effects model. Check: Model convergence warnings; might need to simplify random effects structure if data is sparse for some authors/OPs.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1.  **Reproduce Annotation Reliability**: Compute Fleiss' κ for "Story" and ADI/ICC for features on the provided ARGUS corpus. Verify the reported fair‑to‑moderate agreement and cluster analysis. If results differ drastically, there is a problem with the provided labels or your computation.
  2.  **Benchmark Model Pipeline**: Train a scalar model for `Curiosity` (the highest effect feature) using the provided code/data. Achieve test‑set F1 (from binarized prediction) ~0.87 and RMSE ~0.56? If not, debug data loading, soft‑label format, or model selection.
  3.  **Validate Scalar Persuasion Effect**: Replicate Model 7 (Table 10) using the large‑scale predictions (or a subset). Do you recover β_Curiosity ≈ 0.60 (p<0.001) and β_Surprise ≈ -0.11 (p=0.005)? A significant deviation suggests issues in the prediction pipeline or regression implementation (e.g., incorrect standardization, missing random effects).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How do different discussion topics (e.g., moral/political vs. technical/scientific) influence the prevalence and persuasive impact of specific narrative features (e.g., Agency, Curiosity)?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors state: "Future work could also explore how narrativity varies across discussion topics and whether specific narrative features are employed differently. For example, topics related to values or social issues may rely more on agency and personal stories than technical or scientific topics and might make more use of emotionally engaging features like Surprise, Suspense, or Curiosity."
- **Why unresolved**: The large‑scale analysis pooled all topics, preventing investigation into topic‑specific narrative strategies. The dataset's breadth lacks the granular topic annotation needed for such comparisons.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A follow‑up study applying ARGUS models to a corpus with fine‑grained topic labels (e.g., via manual coding or topic modeling) and running interaction analyses between topic categories and narrative feature coefficients in persuasion models.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Do users who consistently achieve high persuasion success (multiple Δ awards) exhibit systematic, learnable patterns in their use of narrativity and its features compared to less successful persuaders?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors suggest: "Further analysis could also study whether successful persuaders in ChangeMyView, measured by the number of Deltas awarded, show a systematic pattern in their use of narrativity in comparison to other users, and whether narrativity use increases as users gain more experience contributing to the forum."
- **Why unresolved**: The study treats comments as independent samples with random effects for authors, but does not longitudinally track individual users' narrative strategies over time or correlate specific feature usage with a user's persistent persuasiveness.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: An analysis of user‑level aggregates (e.g., average feature scores per user) correlated with user‑specific delta rates, coupled with a temporal analysis of feature usage for users with long posting histories to detect skill development.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Can other large language model families (e.g., GPT, Claude) or fine‑tuned generative models outperform the fine‑tuned encoder‑based models (DistilBERT, RoBERTa) for scalar and binary narrative feature detection, especially with few‑shot prompting?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The paper compares only zero‑shot Llama 3.1 models and notes: "Our comparison with LLMs is limited to models from the Llama family and to a zero‑shot setting. These results may not generalize to other model families or to different prompting strategies." The strong performance of supervised encoders leaves open whether advanced prompting or fine‑tuning could close the gap.
- **Why unresolved**: The evaluation scope is restricted, providing no data on other state‑of‑the‑art LLMs or on whether few‑shot/fine‑tuned generative models could better capture the nuanced, subjective annotations.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A benchmark study evaluating multiple LLM families (GPT‑4, Claude, etc.) on the ARGUS test set using various prompting strategies (zero‑shot, few‑shot) and possibly fine‑tuning on the training data, with results compared to the published encoder baselines.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: What is the cognitive or rhetorical mechanism by which moderate levels of *Surprise* enhance persuasion (per the binary model) but higher degrees of *Surprise* reduce persuasive success (per the scalar model)?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The analysis shows a significant negative effect of *Surprise* as a scalar predictor (β = -0.11, OR ≈ 0.89), but its binary presence shows a large positive effect (OR ≈ 1.51). The authors speculate: "introducing unexpected elements may disrupt the engagement and reduce the persuasive effect," but they do not test this hypothesis or explain the nonlinear relationship.
- **Why unresolved**: The regression models show correlation but cannot reveal the psychological process. The paper lacks any user study or qualitative analysis to explain why too much *Surprise* might backfire in argumentative contexts (e.g., causing mistrust, breaking narrative flow, or perceived manipulation).
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A controlled experiment where participants read argumentative comments with systematically varied levels of *Surprise* (while holding other features constant), measuring perceived credibility, engagement, and persuasion, or a qualitative analysis of high‑*Surprise* low‑delta comments to identify disruptive patterns.

## Limitations
- **Scalar threshold arbitrary**: The 2.5 cutoff for binarizing feature strength (from 1‑5 Likert) is not theoretically justified and may affect binary model performance and the operationalization of "presence."
- **Feature collinearity unaddressed**: The six narrative features are conceptually related and likely correlated. The regression models test them additively without reporting variance inflation factors; independent β estimates may be confounded.
- **LLM comparison incomplete**: The claim of encoder superiority is based solely on zero‑shot Llama 3.1; advanced prompting or fine‑tuning of other LLM families could close the performance gap.
- **Persuasion metric indirect**: Using Δ (award difference) as a binary persuasion signal is noisy and may be influenced by unmeasured confounders like topic, OP baseline bias, or comment timing.
