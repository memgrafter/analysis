# Are Multimodal LLMs Ready for Surveillance? A Reality Check on Zero-Shot Anomaly Detection in the Wild

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.04727  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04727  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Multimodal LLMs show a strong conservative bias in zero-shot video anomaly detection, defaulting to "normal" predictions with high precision but often below 10% recall; class-specific prompting significantly improves recall but remains a critical bottleneck across datasets.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates multimodal large language models (MLLMs) on zero-shot video anomaly detection (VAD) for surveillance, reformulating the task as prompt-guided binary classification of short video clips. Using the Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite model on ShanghaiTech and CHAD datasets, the authors find a pervasive conservative bias: models nearly always predict "normal," yielding high precision but severely collapsed recall. Adding class-specific instructions to prompts shifts the decision boundary, substantially boosting F1-score on ShanghaiTech (from 0.09 to 0.64) and recall (from ~5% to ~50%). However, recall remains low on both datasets, and longer temporal windows (up to 3 seconds) help on ShanghaiTech but not on the more challenging CHAD, indicating that increased context does not universally overcome the underlying reasoning gap for real-world anomalies.

## Method Summary
The task is framed as zero-shot binary classification of non-overlapping video clips (1s, 2s, 3s) derived from ShanghaiTech and CHAD test splits. Clip-level labels are assigned via an "any-anomaly" rule: a clip is anomalous if any frame within it is annotated as anomalous. No model training is performed; the native video-understanding Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite model is queried via API with textual prompts instructing a binary output (`1` for anomalous, `0` for normal). Four prompt variants are tested: a base human-authored prompt, and three LLM-generated prompts of varying specificity, each with and without an appended class-specific instruction enumerating anomalous behaviors. The model's textual response is parsed deterministically to a binary label using a function `g(·)`. Metrics (Precision, Recall, F1) are computed at the clip level and aggregated to video-level reports.

## Key Results
- Conservative bias dominates: without class-specific instructions, models almost always predict `0`, achieving >90% precision but recall often below 10%.
- Class-specific instructions shift the decision boundary, raising peak F1 on ShanghaiTech from 0.09 to 0.64 and recall from ~5% to ~50%; on CHAD, peak F1 reaches 0.48 but recall stays critically low.
- Longer temporal windows (3s vs 1s) yield marginal F1 gains on ShanghaiTech for many prompts but show no consistent improvement on CHAD, suggesting dataset-specific interactions with temporal context.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The conservative bias likely stems from the model's pretraining distribution, which over-represents normal scenes and under-represents rare anomalies, causing a default "normal" prediction to minimize loss. Class-specific instructions work by making anomaly classes salient in the prompt, effectively adjusting the internal decision boundary to increase sensitivity to those concepts. However, the persistent recall collapse indicates that MLLMs lack robust spatiotemporal reasoning for subtle or complex anomalies, and their token-level textual processing may not fully align with video-level anomaly semantics. The limited benefit from longer contexts on CHAD suggests that mere temporal extension does not resolve deeper representational gaps, possibly due to CHAD's higher anomaly complexity, resolution, or background clutter.

## Foundational Learning
- **Zero-shot learning:** Why needed: Enables deployment without task-specific finetuning, using pretrained MLLMs directly. Quick check: Verify model can follow binary classification instructions on a held-out video without examples.
- **Binary classification reformulation for VAD:** Why needed: Simplifies continuous anomaly scoring into discrete decisions for probing model biases. Quick check: Convert frame-level annotations to clip-level labels using any-anomaly rule and confirm label distribution.
- **Conservative bias in video models:** Why needed: Common outcome of data imbalance; models default to majority class. Quick check: Calculate prediction distribution on a balanced sample; if >95% are normal, bias is present.
- **Prompt engineering for decision boundary shifting:** Why needed: Textual instructions can re-weight model's internal concepts without weight changes. Quick check: Compare predictions with/without appended class descriptors.
- **Temporal context in video understanding:** Why needed: Anomalies may require multi-second evidence; longer clips provide more frames. Quick check: Systematically vary clip duration and measure metric changes.
- **Clip-level vs. video-level evaluation:** Why needed: Real-world systems operate on video streams; clip-level metrics may misrepresent operational performance. Quick check: Aggregate clip predictions per video using majority vote or anomaly proportion and compare to standard VAD metrics.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Video Clip -> Prompt Engineering -> Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite API -> Text Response -> Deterministic Parsing (g·) -> Binary Label  
Critical path: The inference pipeline relies on successful API call and correct parsing; failures at parsing or conservative outputs break detection.  
Design tradeoffs: Short clips (low latency, less context) vs. long clips (more context, higher compute); generic prompts (broad applicability) vs. class-specific (higher recall but dataset-dependent).  
Failure signatures: (1) Prediction distribution overwhelmingly normal (>95% `0`); (2) Parsing errors when model output is verbose or non-binary despite instructions; (3) No recall improvement with longer clips on complex datasets.  
First experiments: (1) Run all four prompt variants with and without class instructions on ShanghaiTech 1s clips to confirm baseline bias; (2) Compare 1s vs 3s clips using the best prompt from (1) to measure temporal effect; (3) Sample 100 raw model responses to validate `g(·)` parsing logic and identify common deviation patterns.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** What prompting techniques beyond adding class-specific context can effectively mitigate the conservative bias and improve recall in zero-shot MLLM anomaly detection?  
  *Basis:* The abstract and conclusion identify "recall-oriented prompting" as a key future direction, and the paper shows that while class-specific instructions shift the decision boundary, recall remains critically low (e.g., below 50% on ShanghaiTech).  
  *Why unresolved:* The study only tested a limited set of manually or LLM-generated prompts varying in length and specificity; it did not explore other strategies such as dynamic prompting, inclusion of negative examples, or hierarchical task decomposition that could further reduce the bias toward "normal."  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* A systematic evaluation of diverse prompt designs—such as few-shot exemplars, context-aware instructions, or confidence-eliciting phrasing—across multiple datasets, measuring recall gains while monitoring false positive rates to ensure precision does not degrade excessively.

- **Open Question 2:** How can MLLMs be better calibrated to produce reliable confidence scores for anomaly decisions, addressing their tendency to output high-confidence "normal" predictions?  
  *Basis:* The conclusion calls for improved "model calibration" to enhance reliability under open-world uncertainty, noting that models exhibit high confidence despite severe recall collapse.  
  *Why unresolved:* The paper reports performance metrics but does not analyze or apply calibration methods (e.g., temperature scaling, ensembles, or Bayesian approaches) to align model confidence with true correctness, leaving the underlying cause of overconfidence unaddressed.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Apply standard calibration techniques to the MLLM's binary outputs, evaluate reliability diagrams and expected calibration error (ECE) on VAD benchmarks, and correlate calibration improvements with operational metrics like detection latency or alert trustworthiness.

- **Open Question 3:** Why does increasing temporal context (from 1s to 3s) improve F1-score on ShanghaiTech but not on CHAD, and how do dataset-specific characteristics (e.g., resolution, anomaly subtlety) interact with MLLM temporal reasoning?  
  *Basis:* Table V shows consistent positive ΔF1 on ShanghaiTech for many prompts, while CHAD exhibits marginal or negative changes; the paper notes CHAD's higher complexity but does not explain this divergent temporal effect.  
  *Why unresolved:* The study limited temporal windows to ≤3s and did not analyze properties like anomaly duration, motion coherence, or background clutter that might make longer contexts less useful in CHAD, so the interaction remains speculative.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Extend experiments to longer windows (e.g., 5s, 10s) on both datasets, and correlate performance changes with fine-grained dataset statistics (anomaly length distribution, object density, camera motion) to identify which factors limit temporal gains.

## Limitations
- Unspecified Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite API parameters (version, temperature, safety settings) and the deterministic parsing function `g(·)` block faithful reproduction and may explain performance variance.
- Findings are confined to two datasets and a single model family; generalizability to other MLLMs or diverse surveillance environments is uncertain.
- Evaluation uses clip-level metrics; real-world utility likely differs when aggregating to video-level decisions, which are standard in VAD but not analyzed.

## Confidence
- Conservative default bias exists → **High** (consistently observed across prompts and datasets in reported results).  
- Class-specific instructions lift recall/F1 → **Medium** (effect direction plausible but magnitude depends on unspecified parsing rules and prompt design).  
- Longer temporal windows universally improve performance → **Low** (only tested on two datasets with unsystematic scaling; CHAD shows no benefit).
