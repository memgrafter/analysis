# What If Consensus Lies? Selective-Complementary Reinforcement Learning at Test Time

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.19880
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19880
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: SCRL improves pass@1 over TTRL on math reasoning benchmarks, with gains up to +27.1% on Minerva for Qwen2.5-Math-7B.

## Executive Summary
Test-Time Reinforcement Learning (TTRL) uses majority voting over sampled rollouts to generate pseudo-rewards, but this can amplify noise when consensus is weak. This paper introduces Selective-Complementary Reinforcement Learning (SCRL), which addresses this by three innovations: Selective Positive Pseudo-Labeling that only incorporates rollouts with strong consensus, Entropy-Gated Negative Pseudo-Labeling that penalizes high-uncertainty answers, and Dynamic Reward Shaping that scales rewards by consensus strength and adds an entropy penalty. Evaluations on math reasoning benchmarks with Qwen2.5 and Llama models show consistent improvements in pass@1, with notable gains on challenging datasets like AIME25 and Minerva. SCRL maintains higher label accuracy and training stability under limited rollout budgets.

## Method Summary
SCRL modifies the standard TTRL pipeline. First, for each problem, multiple rollout answers are sampled. Positive pseudo-labels are assigned only to answers that exceed a consensus threshold (τ_pos), filtering out noisy proposals. Additionally, answers with high entropy (dispersed probability mass) are assigned negative pseudo-labels, providing corrective supervision that TTRL lacks. Finally, rewards are dynamically shaped: the magnitude of the reward scales with the strength of consensus (e.g., majority margin) and includes an entropy penalty to discourage uncertain outputs. This combination ensures that the policy gradient updates are driven by high-confidence signals and that uncertain behaviors are explicitly penalized, mitigating the noise amplification problem of vanilla TTRL.

## Key Results
- Qwen2.5-3B (32 rollouts): average pass@1 improvement of +1.6%; AIME25 gain +5.8% (2.6% → 8.4%).
- Qwen2.5-Math-7B: average +7.7%; Minerva +27.1% (14.5% → 41.6%), avoiding training collapse.
- Llama-3.1-8B: 29.0% average pass@1, demonstrating generalization across model families.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
SCRL works by ensuring that reinforcement signals are derived only from high-confidence consensus for positive updates, thus avoiding reinforcement of incorrect trajectories that majority voting might accept under weak agreement. The entropy-gated negative labeling introduces explicit penalties for uncertain outputs, which often correlate with incorrect answers, providing a corrective signal that TTRL lacks. Dynamic reward shaping further refines updates by weighting samples proportionally to their consensus strength, so more certain samples have larger impact. Together, these mechanisms maintain training stability and improve sample efficiency, especially under limited rollout budgets where noise would otherwise dominate.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:  
Rollout Sampling -> Consensus Computation -> Selective Positive Labeling (τ_pos) -> Entropy Calculation -> Negative Labeling (entropy gate) -> Reward Shaping (consensus scaling + entropy penalty) -> Policy Gradient Update

Critical path: The process starts by sampling multiple responses per problem using the current policy. These responses are aggregated to compute a consensus distribution. Answers that exceed the positive threshold (e.g., majority margin > τ_pos) receive positive labels. Simultaneously, entropy over the distribution is computed; answers with entropy above a threshold are labeled negative. Rewards are then constructed: positive labels get a reward proportional to consensus strength, negatives get a penalty scaled by entropy, and others receive zero. Finally, a policy gradient update uses these reward-weighted responses.

Design tradeoffs:
- Strict positive thresholds reduce noise but risk label starvation, especially for weaker models or hard domains.
- Entropy gating adds valuable negative supervision but assumes entropy correlates with incorrectness; this may fail under miscalibrated models or distribution shift.
- Dynamic scaling balances contributions but introduces extra hyperparameters (thresholds, scaling factors).

Failure signatures:
- Performance collapse on knowledge-intensive tasks (e.g., Minerva with 3B model) when positive labeling becomes too sparse.
- If entropy does not distinguish correct from incorrect answers (e.g., in multiple-choice with uniform guessing), negative labeling may prune correct samples.
- Over-penalizing entropy could suppress exploration and lead to premature convergence.

First experiments:
1. Ablate each SCRL component (positive selection, negative gating, dynamic shaping) to measure individual impact.
2. Test SCRL on non-math tasks (e.g., code generation, commonsense QA) to probe domain generalization.
3. Analyze correlation between trajectory entropy and answer correctness across model scales and tasks.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: Can the consensus thresholds (τ<sub>pos</sub>, τ<sub>marg</sub>, τ<sub>neg</sub>) be dynamically adapted during training or across tasks instead of using static values?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The authors set thresholds empirically (τ<sub>pos</sub> = 0.375, τ<sub>marg</sub> = 0.125, τ<sub>neg</sub> = 0.125) and conduct sensitivity analyses (Tables 5, 9), but provide no mechanism for automatic adjustment. The failure on Minerva with a 3B model (Appendix F) suggests thresholds may need to vary with model capacity or domain.
- Why unresolved: Static thresholds may not generalize to new benchmarks, model scales, or distribution shifts. The reliance on manual tuning limits SCRL’s practicality.
- What evidence would resolve it: Experiments with adaptive schemes (e.g., threshold scheduling based on training dynamics, dataset-specific validation, or meta-learning) showing robust performance without manual re-tuning.

### Open Question 2
- Question: Why does SCRL underperform TTRL on Minerva with smaller models, and is this due to over-conservative labeling or fundamental limitations in using entropy-based uncertainty for knowledge-intensive domains?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Appendix F attributes the gap to the 3B model’s limited domain knowledge causing dispersed answer distributions, leading SCRL’s selective positive labeling to abstain frequently and starve the model of positive supervision. TTRL’s noisy updates inadvertently reinforce partial progress.
- Why unresolved: It is unclear whether this failure is inherent to SCRL’s design (e.g., entropy gate) or fixable by adjusting components (e.g., relaxing thresholds, incorporating external knowledge). The interaction between model size, domain specificity, and labeling strictness lacks a theoretical framework.
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments varying model scale, domain knowledge (e.g., fine-tuning on physics texts), and threshold configurations on Minerva; analysis of pruned answer correctness rates.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How reliably does trajectory-level entropy distinguish correct from incorrect answers across diverse model architectures, tasks, and distribution shifts?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Entropy-gated negative labeling assumes high uncertainty correlates with incorrectness. Table 8 shows a consistent entropy gap for Qwen2.5-3B on math benchmarks, but no tests on other models (e.g., Llama) or non-math tasks.
- Why unresolved: Entropy depends on model calibration and output distribution. Under distribution shift (e.g., new problem types or miscalibrated models), the assumed correlation may break, leading to false negative pruning or missed errors.
- What evidence would resolve it: Broad evaluations across model families (Decoder-only, Encoder-Decoder), tasks (commonsense, multi-modal), and perturbed models (e.g., temperature-scrambled outputs) measuring the precision/recall of entropy-based negative labeling against ground-truth labels.

## Limitations
- Evaluation confined to math reasoning benchmarks; generalizability to other domains (e.g., code, commonsense) remains unproven.
- No ablation study isolating the contributions of SCRL’s three components, making it hard to pinpoint sources of improvement.
- Hyperparameters (consensus thresholds, entropy gates) are set empirically and their sensitivity is unexplored, hindering reproducibility.

## Confidence
- TTRL amplifies noise under weak consensus: Medium
