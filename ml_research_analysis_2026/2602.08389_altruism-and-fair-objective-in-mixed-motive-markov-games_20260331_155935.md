# Altruism and Fair Objective in Mixed-Motive Markov games

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.08389
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08389
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: The proportional fairness approach based on log-payoff utilities dramatically improves both efficiency (~120 vs ~40 apples) and fairness (Gini ~0.1 vs ~0.8) in the CleanUp social dilemma.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the problem of inefficient and inequitable cooperation in multi-agent social dilemmas arising from standard utilitarian objectives. It introduces a proportional fairness framework that redefines individual utilities as the logarithm of payoffs plus a weighted sum of others' logarithms. This fair altruistic objective is extended to sequential Markov games, with theoretical policy gradient derivations and practical Actor‑Critic algorithms (Fair MAA2C and Fair MAPPO). Empirical evaluation in the CleanUp environment shows that proportional fairness yields far higher collective efficiency (approximately 120 apples harvested versus 40 under utilitarian welfare) and drastically lower inequality (Gini coefficient around 0.1 versus 0.8). A moderate altruism level α ≈ 0.7 achieves the best performance, illustrating that balanced fairness encourages sustainable cooperation without relying on specialized, inequitable roles.

## Method Summary
The authors propose replacing the utilitarian sum of rewards with a proportional fairness objective defined in log-payoff space: for each agent i, the transformed utility is u_i(s) = (1−α) log(p_i(s)−m_p) + α·SW(s), where SW(s) is the sum of all agents' log-payoffs (i.e., the social welfare in log space). In two‑player settings this simplifies to R'_i = log(R_i) + α log(R_j). This transformation makes mutual cooperation an equilibrium at a threshold α_G when (1+α) log(R) ≥ log(T) + α log(S). The framework is extended to Markov games via a Fair Advantage function: A_F,i(s,a,s₀) = (A_i(s,a)/V_i(s₀)) + α Σ_{j≠i} (A_j(s,a)/V_j(s₀)), where A_j are per‑agent TD advantages and V_j(s₀) the value estimate of the initial state. Agents are trained with independent Actor‑Critic networks using either Monte‑Carlo policy gradient (MAA2C) or PPO (MAPPO). Experiments are run in the CleanUp domain (SocialJax) with 7 agents, partial observability (11×11 window), apple regeneration rate 0.05, episode length 100, and 3×10⁵ total timesteps. Performance is measured by total apples harvested (efficiency) and the Gini index of per‑agent harvests (fairness). Baselines use the standard utilitarian reward (global sum) with conventional advantage.

## Key Results
- Efficiency: proportional fairness harvests ~120 apples, while utilitarian welfare yields ~40.
- Fairness: Gini index drops from ~0.8 (utilitarian) to ~0.1 (fairness).
- Optimal altruism level is α ≈ 0.7 (or α ≈ 0.5 for MAA2C), indicating that moderate fairness promotes sustainable cooperation without extreme specialization.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Transforming individual utilities to the log-payoff space with a weighted sum of others' logs makes cooperation a Nash equilibrium in social dilemmas at a threshold altruism level α.
- **Mechanism:** The fair altruistic utility `u_i(s) = (1-α)log(p_i(s) - m_p) + α * SW(s)` rescales payoffs. For 2-player games, this becomes `R'_i = log(R_i) + α log(R_j)`. This changes the payoff comparison: mutual cooperation (C,C) becomes an equilibrium when `(1+α)log(R) ≥ log(T) + α log(S)`, solvable for α.
- **Core assumption:** All original payoffs are strictly positive (`R_i(s) > 0`) to apply the logarithm, and the minimum payoff `m_p` exists to shift values.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "redefining individual utilities via the log of payoffs plus weighted sum of others' logs"
  - [section 3.2] "𝑅′₁ = log 𝑅₁ + 𝛼 log 𝑅₂" and derivation of `𝛼_G = (log T - log R)/(log R - log S)` for `T > R`
  - [corpus] Weak anchor; neighboring papers discuss altruism in mixed-motive games but not this specific log-transform mechanism

## Foundational Learning
- **Log-payoff transform**: Turns cooperation into a Nash equilibrium in social dilemmas via logarithms.
  - *Why needed*: Enables proportional fairness and changes strategic incentives.
  - *Quick check*: Verify that α exceeds α_G threshold for equilibrium in 2-player games.
- **SocialJAX / CleanUp**: Multi-agent environment where agents harvest apples while avoiding pollution.
  - *Why needed*: Provides a testbed for cooperation under partial observability.
  - *Quick check*: Confirm 7 agents, 11×11 observation window, regeneration rate 0.05.
- **Independent Actor‑Critic**: MARL baseline using per‑agent policies and critics.
  - *Why needed*: Scalable to many agents and Markov games.
  - *Quick check*: Each agent has its own actor and critic networks.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Trajectory collection → Compute TD advantages A_j → Compute initial state values V_j(s₀) → Form Fair Advantage A_F,i = A_i/V_i(s₀) + α Σ_{j≠i} A_j/V_j(s₀) → Update actor (π_i) and critic (V_i).
- **Critical path**: 1. Collect trajectories; 2. Compute advantages; 3. Normalize by initial state values; 4. Apply α‑weighting; 5. Update actors and critics.
- **Design tradeoffs**: Using per‑agent critics allows decentralized execution but may cause variance; normalizing by V_i(s₀) stabilizes but risks instability if V_i(s₀)≈0.
- **Failure signatures**: Actor loss diverging, vanishing policy gradients, extreme Gini indices, or performance collapse at α>0.5.
- **First experiments**: 1. Verify Fair Advantage computation against manual calculation on a mini‑batch. 2. Compare per‑agent reward statistics (means, variances) across α values. 3. Run a small grid search over learning rates to ensure stability.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Convergence to equilibrium**: Does the proposed Fair Actor‑Critic algorithm converge to an equilibrium (e.g., Nash, Pareto) in fair altruistic Markov games? The authors state there is no convergence proof.
- **Monotonic improvement with TRPO**: Can the Fair Advantage be integrated into TRPO with provable monotonic improvement? They note the current formulation is inconsistent with TRPO theory.
- **Scalability to diverse environments**: How does proportional fairness perform in other social dilemmas and under extreme resource scarcity? They suggest testing more environments and varying agent counts and regeneration rates.
- **Integration with advanced MARL algorithms**: Would incorporating fairness into state‑of‑the‑art methods like HAPPO or HAA2C improve stability and performance? They recommend this as future work.

## Limitations
- Missing implementation details (exact network architectures, PPO hyperparameters, GAE λ, optimization settings) hinder exact replication.
- The log‑transform requires strictly positive payoffs; the sensitivity to the minimum shift parameter m_p is unexplored.
- Experiments are confined to the CleanUp environment; generalizability to other social dilemmas is uncertain.

## Confidence
- Theoretical equilibrium condition (static 2‑player): **High**
- Fair policy gradient extension to sequential games: **Medium**
- Empirical superiority of proportional fairness in CleanUp: **Medium**
- Optimal altruism level α ≈ 0.7: **Medium**

## Next Checks
1. **Full replication** of CleanUp experiments using precisely the reported architectures, PPO settings, GAE λ, and initialization to confirm the observed efficiency and fairness gains and the α‑optimum.
2. **Cross‑environment validation** on at least two other multi‑agent social dilemmas (e.g., CoinGame, Level‑Based Foraging) with identical hyperparameters, measuring whether proportional fairness consistently improves both welfare and fairness.
3. **Robustness to payoff scaling and shift**: Ablate the minimum shift m_p (including no shift) and introduce constant offsets to create non‑positive payoffs; assess training stability, fairness metrics, and performance.
