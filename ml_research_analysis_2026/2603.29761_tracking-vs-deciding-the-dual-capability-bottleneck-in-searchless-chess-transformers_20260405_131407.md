# Tracking vs. Deciding: The Dual-Capability Bottleneck in Searchless Chess Transformers

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.29761
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.29761
- **Reference count:** 3
- **Primary result:** Combining 120M parameter scaling with Elo-weighted training yields a searchless chess Transformer that achieves Lichess bullet 2570 (top 1%) and 55.2% top-1 human move accuracy, exceeding prior work Maia-2.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates building a human-like chess engine solely from move sequences, revealing a fundamental "dual-capability bottleneck": performance is limited by the weaker of two interdependent skills—state tracking (reconstructing the board from a move history) and decision quality (selecting strong moves). These skills require contradictory training data: tracking benefits from diverse, low-rated games with unconventional moves, while decision quality requires high-rated, sound moves. Hard filtering of low-rated data catastrophically degrades tracking. The solution marries two interventions: scaling model size to 120M parameters (which improves tracking capacity) and applying an Elo-weighted loss that downweights but does not remove low-rated games (which improves decision quality while preserving diversity). This combination is superadditive, producing a model that achieves grandmaster-level bullet ratings and state-of-the-art human-likeness among sequence-based approaches. The analysis also characterizes a "coverage-decay" phenomenon where intra-game reliability declines exponentially as the game progresses, a problem mitigated by stronger models.

## Method Summary
The method trains decoder-only Transformers (28M and 120M parameters) via next-token prediction on UCI move sequences from the Lichess open database. Inputs include metadata tokens for time control, average player Elo, and side to move. The core intervention is sample reweighting based on game Elo: a linear weighting scheme (target reweighting factor r≈20) downweights low-rated games without removing them, in contrast to prior work that filtered them out. A 2×2 factorial ablation crosses two scales (28M, 120M) and two weighting schemes (uniform, linear) to isolate effects on state tracking (measured by illegal-move rate and linear probe accuracy) and decision quality (measured by human move prediction accuracy, centipawn loss, and online Lichess bullet Elo). The final model uses the 120M scale with linear weighting. Analysis of intra-game errors defines a "degeneration point" per self-play game and derives a coverage-decay formula linking finite training data to exponential state-space growth to predict reliability cliffs.

## Key Results
- Performance is bottlenecked by the weaker of state tracking (T) and decision quality (Q); filtering low-rated data collapses T (illegal moves double) and overall strength, even if average move quality improves.
- Scaling to 120M parameters significantly improves tracking (illegal rate drops 5×) but yields modest Elo gains, indicating Q remains the bottleneck.
- Elo-weighted training (linear r≈20) improves decision quality (Top-1 accuracy rises, head-to-head dominance) while only slightly increasing illegal moves; over-weighting (exponential r≈200) recreates the tracking failure.
- The combined 120M+linear model achieves Lichess bullet 2570, 55.2% Top-1 accuracy, and delays intra-game degeneration (median degeneration move shifts from 26 to 31).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The model's playing strength is fundamentally limited by the weaker of two capabilities: state tracking (reconstructing the board from move history) and decision quality (selecting good moves). This creates a "dual-capability bottleneck" where performance cannot exceed the lower of the two.
- **Mechanism:** Tracking requires diverse positional exposure—found abundantly in low-rated games with unconventional moves—to learn robust board reconstruction. Decision quality requires high-quality move examples—found in high-rated games—to learn sound positional and tactical choices. These data requirements conflict: low-rated games provide diversity but poor decision signals, while high-rated games provide quality signals but narrow diversity.
- **Core assumption:** Tracking failures (illegal moves) are catastrophically costly, outweighing many suboptimal-but-legal moves; thus the weakest capability determines overall strength.
- **Evidence anchors:** Filtering low-rated data (V1.1 vs V1.0) doubles illegal-move rate (1.06% → 1.82%) and collapses middlegame/endgame accuracy (−3.6pp, −6.4pp). 2×2 ablation shows scaling fixes tracking and weighting improves decisions, with their combination superadditive.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Elo-weighted training—downweighting low-rated games without removing them—improves decision quality while preserving enough diversity for tracking, thus raising both capabilities toward a new balance.
- **Mechanism:** By reducing the gradient contribution of low-rated games, the model learns to prioritize high-quality move patterns from high-rated data. However, because low-rated games remain in the training set (albeit with reduced weight), their diverse positions still contribute to state tracking training. This soft modulation avoids the irreversible diversity loss caused by hard filtering.
- **Core assumption:** There exists a "sweet spot" in weighting intensity where the gain in decision quality outweighs the small loss in tracking fidelity.
- **Evidence anchors:** V2.2 (120M, linear r≈20) achieves 2570 Lichess bullet, dominating V2.0 (unweighted) 41–24 in head-to-head, while only increasing illegal rate slightly (0.22% → 0.26%). Over-weighting (V2.3, exponential r=200) reduces validation loss but degrades Lichess Elo, indicating the bottleneck shifts back to tracking.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Intra-game reliability decays because the effective training support for reachable board states decreases exponentially with game length, leading to a "degeneration point" after which catastrophic errors become more frequent.
- **Mechanism:** As a game progresses, the number of distinct board states reachable grows roughly as b^t (effective branching factor). With a finite training set of N games, average support per state is N / b^t. When support falls below a critical threshold k_crit, tracking becomes unreliable and error rates spike. The formula t* = log(N/k_crit) / log b estimates a characteristic reliability horizon.
- **Core assumption:** State tracking robustness depends on the amount of training data covering a given state; states with insufficient support are more likely to be mis-reconstructed.
- **Evidence anchors:** 63.2% of baseline games exhibit a degeneration point (median move 26), vs. 19.5% for final model (median move 31). Aligning games by t_deg reveals a sharp spike in blunder probability at t_deg. Stronger models delay the cliff, consistent with larger effective N in the formula.

  - **Quick check question:** Why does removing low-rated games reduce playing strength despite improving average move quality?

  - **Why needed here:** Provides a method to reconcile contradictory data needs (diversity vs. quality) without discarding data.

  - **Why needed here:** Used to measure whether the model's internal activations implicitly encode board state, evidencing tracking capability.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  - Lichess raw games → PGN parser → UCI move sequences → tokenization (vocabulary ~1900) → sequences with BOS and metadata tokens ([time_control], [elo], [color]) → padded/truncated to 170 tokens → Transformer (Pre-RMSNorm, RoPE, SwiGLU) → output logits → cross-entropy loss (with optional Elo-based sample weighting) → AdamW updates → checkpoints.
  - Evaluation: checkpoints → human move accuracy (held-out positions), illegal-move rate, linear probes on board state, self-play → Lichess bot (bullet) → Elo rating.
- **Critical path:**
  1. Download and preprocess Lichess database: extract Bullet/Blitz games, convert to UCI, attach time control and average Elo metadata, tokenize.
  2. Initialize Transformer (28M or 120M) and optimizer (AdamW).
  3. For each batch: compute weighted cross-entropy loss if weighting enabled; backpropagate with gradient clipping; update parameters.
  4. Periodically evaluate on validation sets (illegal rate, Top-1 accuracy) and via self-play to compute degeneration statistics.
  5. Select best checkpoint based on a composite metric (e.g., Top-1 accuracy + head-to-head win rate against baseline).
  6. Deploy final model to Lichess bot for online bullet match play to estimate Elo.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Sequence vs. FEN input:** Sequences encode full history, enabling trajectory-dependent decisions but requiring implicit state tracking; FEN provides explicit state but discards history. The paper shows sequence models can achieve higher human-likeness at the cost of tracking fragility.
  - **No search:** Allows fast, human-like move times and pure pattern learning, but limits absolute playing strength relative to search-augmented engines.
  - **No hand-crafted features:** Pure data-driven learning increases flexibility but demands sufficient model capacity (scaling to 120M) to learn robust tracking.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **High illegal rate (>0.5%):** Tracking failure; likely due to insufficient capacity or too aggressive weighting/filtering.
  - **Low Top-1 accuracy (<45%):** Decision quality failure; likely due to uniform weighting on all Elo data, drowning high-quality signals.
  - **Late-game accuracy drop (middlegame/endgame):** Coverage decay; indicates inadequate training data diversity or model capacity for long-horizon states.
  - **Weighting sensitivity:** Validation loss decreases but Lichess Elo drops with over-weighting (exponential r=200); indicates bottleneck shift from Q to T.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Filtering experiment (V1.0 vs V1.1):** Remove low-rated data → illegal rate doubles, middlegame/endgame accuracy collapses. Diagnoses tracking as the fragile capability.
  2. **Scaling experiment (V1.0 vs V2.0):** Increase parameters 28M → 120M (uniform weighting) → illegal rate drops 5×, Top-1 rises, but head-to-head gain modest. Shows scaling repairs tracking, leaving Q as bottleneck.
  3. **Weighting experiment (V2.0 vs V2.2):** Add linear Elo weighting (r≈20) to 120M → Top-1 improves (50.85% → 51.2%), head-to-head dominates (41–24). Shows weighting improves decisions while larger model absorbs tracking cost.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1:** Does the dual-capability bottleneck (P ≤ min(T, Q)) hold for other sequential games like Othello, Go, or Shogi, where state tracking and decision quality require contradictory training data?
  - **Basis in paper:** Section 8.2 states: "We offer a testable prediction: in any sequential game where a model is trained on move sequences spanning skill levels, filtering low-skill data will degrade state tracking. The most accessible validation would use Othello..."
  - **Why unresolved:** The paper only demonstrates the bottleneck in chess; generalizability to other games remains an untested prediction.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Train autoregressive sequence models on move sequences from other games (e.g., Othello), compare filtered vs. weighted training, and measure state tracking (via linear probes) and decision quality (move accuracy, CPL) to see if performance is limited by the weaker capability.
- **Question 2:** What is the precise functional relationship between weighting intensity r and performance P, and how does the optimal r scale with model capacity?
  - **Basis in paper:** Sections 5.2 and 5.3 model T(r) and Q(r) as linear in log-space and identify a sweet spot at r≈20, but note that "finer-grained sweeps" are needed (Section 5.7, limitations).
  - **Why unresolved:** The paper shows that both under- and over-weighting are suboptimal and that scaling shifts the sweet spot, but the exact shape of P(r) and its capacity dependence are not fully mapped.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct systematic experiments with a dense grid of r values (e.g., 1 to 200) across multiple model scales (e.g., 50M, 120M, 200M), fit T(r) and Q(r) parameters, and validate the predicted crossover r* and its scaling law.
- **Question 3:** What causes the discrepancy where supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on individual style data degrades a 28M model but improves a 120M model, and how can controlled experiments isolate the capacity vs. data specificity hypotheses?
  - **Basis in paper:** Section 8.1 states: "Designing the appropriate cross-controlled experiments is left for future work," and proposes two hypotheses (capacity vs. data specificity).
  - **Why unresolved:** Preliminary SFT results are contradictory without controlled comparisons; the underlying mechanism—whether model capacity or data distribution—is unknown.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Perform cross-controlled SFT: fine-tune models of varying capacities on individual player datasets (e.g., Hikaru Nakamura) and aggregated high-Elo data, measuring changes in tracking (illegal rate) and decision quality (CPL, accuracy) to test which hypothesis explains the discrepancy.
- **Question 4:** To what extent does sequence input confer human-likeness advantages over position-based input (e.g., FEN), independently of model architecture and training?
  - **Basis in paper:** Section 7.1 includes a fairness caveat: "part of V2.2’s advantage may stem from the representation rather than the model," and Section 8.2 contrasts sequence vs. position models for trajectory-dependent behavior.
  - **Why unresolved:** The move prediction comparison with Maia-2 confounds input format with model quality; sequence input provides more information (game history), so the isolated contribution of representation is unclear.
  - **What evidence would resolve it:** Train a position-based model (e.g., Maia-2 architecture) on move sequences or constrain a sequence model to use only current position, then compare human-move prediction and history-dependent tasks under matched conditions to isolate representation effects.

## Limitations
- Dataset specifics (exact Lichess months, preprocessing scripts, train/validation splits) and linear probe training details (probe architecture, sampling) are not fully described, hindering faithful replication.
- The coverage-decay formula parameters (N, k_crit, b) and degeneration point detection algorithm are not provided, leaving the quantitative validation incomplete.
- Evaluation is restricted to bullet time controls; generalizability to longer time controls (blitz/rapid) is unknown, and time-control confounding may affect the observed results.

## Confidence
- **Dual-capability bottleneck:** High. The 2×2 ablation cleanly isolates tracking vs. decision dimensions; scaling fixes tracking, weighting fixes decision, and their combination is superadditive.
- **Elo-weighted training:** Medium. Linear weighting (r≈20) helps, but the narrow sweet spot and over-weighting failure indicate fragility; finer-grained sweeps are needed.
- **Coverage-decay:** Medium. The theoretical alignment with data is plausible and supported by qualitative shifts in degeneration points, but lacks quantitative fitting; remains a scaffold rather than a precise predictor.

## Next Checks
1. **FEN-input baseline:** Train a model with explicit board state (FEN) on the same data. Higher strength without tracking issues would confirm the bottleneck and isolate the cost of sequence-based tracking.
2. **Longer time controls:** Evaluate the final model on Lichess blitz/rapid. A later degeneration point would validate the coverage-decay scaling prediction.
3. **Phase-specific fitting:** Estimate branching factors separately for opening, middlegame, and endgame phases and fit decay parameters; test whether phase-dependent predictions align with observed error patterns.
