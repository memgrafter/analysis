# Benchmarking Tabular Foundation Models for Conditional Density Estimation in Regression

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.26611
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26611
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Tabular foundation models (TabPFN-2.5, RealTabPFN-2.5, TabICL-Quantiles) achieve the best conditional density estimation accuracy (CDE loss, log-likelihood, CRPS) on the large majority of 39 real-world datasets across diverse training sizes.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents a comprehensive benchmark of conditional density estimation (CDE) methods for tabular regression, focusing on three emerging tabular foundation models that employ in-context learning. Evaluated against a broad suite of parametric, tree-based, and neural CDE methods on 39 real-world datasets with sample sizes ranging from 50 to 500,000, the foundation models consistently deliver superior predictive distribution accuracy. Their performance is particularly notable in data-scarce regimes, demonstrating a significant sample efficiency advantage. However, calibration performance is competitive at small sample sizes but lags behind some tuned neural baselines as training data grows, and the models face practical context-length and memory limitations on very large or high-dimensional datasets.

## Method Summary
The study evaluates conditional density estimation—predicting a full distribution f(y|x) rather than a point estimate—across 39 real-world regression tasks. Three tabular foundation models (TabPFN-2.5, RealTabPFN-2.5, TabICL-Quantiles) are compared against 14 baseline methods, including parametric distributions, quantile regression trees, basis-expansion techniques (FlexCode), neural flows and mixture density networks (MDN), and Bayesian additive regression trees (BART). All methods are assessed using six proper scoring and calibration metrics: CDE loss (primary), log-likelihood, CRPS, PIT Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic, 90% interval coverage, and wall-clock time. The foundation models are used in their pretrained, zero-fine-tuning form, processing the training set as context via a transformer. Baselines are tuned via random search or cross-validation. Evaluation uses multiple independent train/test splits to ensure robust rank comparisons, with significance testing via one-sided Welch tests and Holm–Bonferferroni correction.

## Key Results
- Foundation models achieve the best CDE loss, log-likelihood, and CRPS on the majority of datasets across all evaluated training set sizes (50 to 20,000).
- Calibration (PIT uniformity) is competitive with the best methods at small sample sizes (n=50) but becomes less calibrated than some neural baselines (e.g., MDN) at larger n.
- In a large-scale photometric redshift case study (500,000 galaxies), TabPFN-2.5 trained on just 50,000 galaxies outperforms all baseline methods trained on the full dataset, demonstrating a tenfold data efficiency advantage.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Pre-training on synthetic supervised tasks induces a highly effective prior for real-world conditional distributions.
- **Mechanism:** Models are meta-trained via the prior-data fitted network (PFN) paradigm: a transformer learns to solve many diverse synthetic regression tasks in-context. This encodes inductive biases (e.g., smoothness, heteroscedasticity) into model parameters, enabling accurate distribution estimation from a new dataset without gradient updates.
- **Break condition:** Performance degrades if the real dataset's conditional structure (e.g., multimodality type, discrete support) is underrepresented in the synthetic pre-training distribution, as seen on quasi-discrete (BlackFriday) and well-matched parametric (VideoTranscoding) datasets.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** In-context learning allows dynamic, dataset-aware adaptation of density estimates.
- **Mechanism:** The entire training set (X_train, Y_train) is tokenized and processed as context. The transformer attends over these examples to generate density parameters (e.g., bin probabilities, quantiles) for new queries, implicitly performing a localized nonparametric fit based on context similarity.
- **Break condition:** Fails when context length is exceeded (n > 50k for TabPFN, n > 10k for TabICL) or high dimensionality (d) causes memory issues (e.g., CTSlices OOM at n=20k, d=384).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Implicit regularization from synthetic pre-training prevents overfitting in small-sample regimes.
- **Mechanism:** Pre-training on a vast array of synthetic functions instills a "prior" favoring smooth, plausible densities, which generalizes better than the inductive biases of purpose-built CDE methods when training data is very limited.
- **Break condition:** The prior becomes a liability when the true conditional distribution aligns with a simple, well-specified parametric form or discrete support, where specialized models (e.g., LogNormal, CatMLP) can excel with fewer data.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Conditional Density Estimation (CDE)**
  - *Why needed:* The core task is predicting a full distribution f(y|x). Understanding CDE is essential to grasp the metrics (CDE loss, log-likelihood, CRPS) and conversions (e.g., bar→density).
  - *Quick check:* What does the Probability Integral Transform (PIT) measure, and why is its uniformity a sign of calibration?
- **Concept: In-Context Learning (ICL) for Tabular Foundation Models**
  - *Why needed:* The core mechanism is processing the training set as context without gradient updates. This dictates sample efficiency, context-length limits, and hardware requirements.
  - *Quick check:* How does the transformer's attention mechanism over (x_i, y_i) pairs enable conditional density estimation for a new x?
- **Concept: Proper Scoring Rules for Distributional Evaluation**
  - *Why needed:* Metrics like CDE loss, log-likelihood, and CRPS are proper scoring rules that evaluate the entire predictive distribution. Their strict propriety (minimized by the true distribution) is key to interpreting "best" claims.
  - *Quick check:* Why might a method score well on CRPS but worse on CDE loss after density conversion (as with TabICL-Quantiles)?

## Architecture Onboarding
Input -> Foundation Model Inference (TabPFN-2.5/RealTabPFN-2.5: bar distributions; TabICL-Quantiles: quantiles) -> Distribution Conversion (Bar→Density: mass/bin width, interpolation, renormalize; Quantiles→CDF→Density: interpolation, numerical gradient, clamp) -> Baseline Models (Parametric, tree-based, neural CDE with own architectures) -> Evaluation Engine (Computes CDE loss, log-likelihood, CRPS, PIT KS, coverage, wall-clock time).

Critical path: Data loading -> context construction -> foundation model forward pass -> density conversion -> metric computation. For baselines: hyperparameter tuning (CV) -> full training -> prediction -> metric computation.

Design tradeoffs:
- Accuracy vs. Calibration: Foundation models win density accuracy but may lag in calibration at large n.
- Sample efficiency vs. Context limit: Foundation models excel with limited data but hit hard context-length caps (n ≤ 50k) and OOM risks with high d.
- Generality vs. Specialization: Foundation models are strong off-the-shelf but can be beaten by models with exact structural match (e.g., CatMLP on discrete outcomes).

Failure signatures:
- Out-of-memory errors when n * d exceeds GPU memory (e.g., CTSlices: d=384, n=20k).
- Calibration degradation at larger n (PIT KS ranks drop vs. MDN).
- Density conversion artifacts for TabICL-Quantiles: roughness from numerical differentiation hurts CDE loss/log-lik but not CRPS.
- Parametric mismatches: LogNormal/Gamma fail if response is not positive; shifting adds heuristic bias.

First 3 experiments:
1. Replicate synthetic bimodal illustration (Figure 1): Train TabPFN-2.5, Flow-Spline, FlexCode-RF on n=200 from provided DGP. Visualize estimated densities vs. true for three test x. Confirm TabPFN captures bimodality earlier.
2. Digits dataset calibration check: Train all foundation models and CatMLP on Digits (n=50). Compute PIT histograms and KS statistic. Expect CatMLP near-perfect uniformity; foundation models may show systematic deviations.
3. SDSS scaling pilot: Run TabPFN-2.5 and best non-foundation competitor (e.g., Flow-Spline) on SDSS at n=10k, 50k, 100k. Verify TabPFN at 50k surpasses Flow-Spline at 500k in CDE loss. Record fit time to confirm speed advantage.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can post-hoc recalibration methods be effectively combined with tabular foundation models to improve probabilistic calibration for conditional density estimation?
- **Basis:** Future directions mention combining with recalibration (e.g., Dey et al. 2025) to address calibration gaps.
- **Why unresolved:** Foundation models are competitive at small n but lag some neural baselines at large n. Suitable techniques and extent of improvement are unclear.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic study of recalibration methods (histogram binning, isotonic regression, conformal) applied to foundation model outputs across datasets, using PIT KS and coverage.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How do tabular foundation models generalize to multivariate conditional density estimation and structured output tasks?
- **Basis:** Future directions note extension to multivariate CDE and structured outputs as a natural next step.
- **Why unresolved:** Evaluation is restricted to univariate responses; pretraining and architecture may not directly handle multivariate dependencies or structured outputs.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Adaptation of foundation models to multivariate CDE (e.g., via copulas or autoregressive factorizations) and evaluation on multi-output datasets with multivariate proper scoring rules.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can context-length and memory limitations be overcome for very large samples (n > 50,000) and high-dimensional covariates (d > 384) without subsampling?
- **Basis:** Paper reports OOM on CTSlices (d=384, n=20k) and context-length caps (n ≤ 50k) as constraints.
- **Why unresolved:** These constraints limit applicability to large-scale, high-dimensional problems. Efficiency of architectural changes (e.g., sparse attention) is unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Development and benchmarking of memory-efficient inference algorithms or model variants that successfully process n > 50k, d > 384 with performance comparable to current models on smaller subsets.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** For which data-generating mechanisms (e.g., discrete/quasi-discrete responses, well-specified parametric families) do specialized CDE methods consistently outperform tabular foundation models?
- **Basis:** Counterexamples identified: CatMLP beats foundations on discrete (Digits, BlackFriday); LogNormal beats foundations on VideoTranscoding.
- **Why unresolved:** Foundation models rely on a general pretraining prior; it's unclear if they can incorporate biases for specific response structures or if such regimes will always favor specialized methods.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments on synthetic datasets with varying response types (discrete, parametric) to map performance boundaries, and exploration of modifications (e.g., mixture density heads) to close gaps.

## Limitations
- Dataset provenance and preprocessing details (exact OpenML IDs, SDSS feature engineering) are unspecified, risking mismatched splits/features.
- Precise conversion implementations from model outputs to densities (bin widths, interpolation grids) are described but not coded, risking metric drift.
- Hardware and software environment specifics (GPU model, library versions) influence runtime and memory thresholds; reported OOM limits may not generalize.
