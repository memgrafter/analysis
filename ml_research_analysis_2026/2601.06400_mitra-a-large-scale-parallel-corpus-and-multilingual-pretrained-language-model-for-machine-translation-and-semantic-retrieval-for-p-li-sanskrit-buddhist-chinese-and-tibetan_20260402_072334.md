# MITRA: A Large-Scale Parallel Corpus and Multilingual Pretrained Language Model for Machine Translation and Semantic Retrieval for Pāli, Sanskrit, Buddhist Chinese, and Tibetan

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.06400
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06400
- Reference count: 13
- One-line primary result: The MITRA project produces a 1.74M-sentence parallel corpus for four ancient Buddhist languages and fine-tunes Gemma-2 models that achieve state-of-the-art translation (beating larger models by +15 GEMBA) and semantic retrieval performance.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces MITRA, a framework to address the scarcity of parallel data for ancient Buddhist languages (Sanskrit, Pāli, Chinese, Tibetan). By using English as a pivot language, the authors mine a large-scale parallel corpus (1.74M sentence pairs) from monolingual sources via machine translation and fine-grained sentence alignment. This corpus is then used to fine-tune the Gemma-2 model, yielding two specialized variants: Gemma-2-MITRA-MT for machine translation and Gemma-2-MITRA-E for multilingual semantic retrieval. Evaluation shows the translation model outperforms larger open models like Gemma-3-27B by at least 15 GEMBA points on average, while the retrieval model sets a new state-of-the-art on a novel seven-task benchmark. The corpus, models, and benchmark are publicly released to support research in Buddhist studies and low-resource language processing.

## Method Summary
The methodology, as described in the key outcome, involves a two-stage pipeline. First, a domain-finetuned MADLAD-400 model translates source texts into English to generate candidate parallel passages. Second, a fine-grained sentence alignment process filters and aligns sentence pairs to produce the high-quality MITRA-parallel corpus. This corpus is then used to fine-tune the Gemma-2 model in two configurations: one for translation (MITRA-MT) and one for semantic retrieval (MITRA-E). The approach leverages the strengths of modern machine translation to overcome data scarcity and adapts a powerful multilingual pretrained model to the specific domain of ancient Buddhist texts.

## Key Results
- Constructed the MITRA-parallel corpus containing 1.74 million sentence pairs across Sanskrit, Pāli, Chinese, and Tibetan.
- Gemma-2-MITRA-MT achieves state-of-the-art translation quality, surpassing Gemma-3-27B by at least 15 GEMBA points on average.
- Gemma-2-MITRA-E yields top performance on a novel seven-task multilingual semantic embedding benchmark.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The pivot-based mining approach is effective because it circumvents the lack of direct parallel data by exploiting high-quality English translations, which are more readily available for Buddhist texts. The use of a domain-adapted MT model (MADLAD-400) reduces noise in the pivoting step, while the subsequent fine-grained alignment ensures sentence-level precision. Fine-tuning Gemma-2 on this curated in-domain data allows the model to learn the linguistic nuances and specialized vocabulary of these ancient languages, leading to significant gains in both translation and semantic retrieval tasks. The retrieval model's strong performance stems from learning cross-lingual embeddings that capture semantic equivalence even across structurally diverse languages.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**: Source Texts (Sanskrit, Pāli, Chinese, Tibetan) -> MT Pivoting (MADLAD-400) -> Candidate Pair Generation -> Fine-grained Sentence Alignment -> MITRA-parallel Corpus -> Fine-tuning (Gemma-2) -> Gemma-2-MITRA-MT (Translation) & Gemma-2-MITRA-E (Retrieval).

**Critical Path**: The pipeline's critical path runs from MT pivoting through alignment to corpus creation, as any failure at these stages directly impacts the quality of the training data and consequently the fine-tuned models.

**Design Tradeoffs**: 
- Pivoting through English introduces potential translation errors but allows scaling to four language pairs with a single MT system.
- Using a single multilingual model (Gemma-2) for both tasks balances resource efficiency against possible suboptimal specialization for each task.
- Higher embedding dimensionality improves retrieval accuracy but increases computational cost for large-scale search.

**Failure Signatures**: 
- Alignment accuracy drops significantly for languages with different word orders or complex morphology (e.g., Sanskrit), leading to noisy sentence pairs.
- The translation model may hallucinate or produce ungrammatical output if the fine-tuning data contains alignment errors.
- The retrieval model may show degraded performance on semantically distant tasks (e.g., verse→commentary) or for languages with limited training data (Pāli).

**First 3 Experiments**:
1. **Human Alignment Evaluation**: Manually re-annotate a stratified sample of 500-1000 sentence pairs from the MITRA-parallel corpus and report token-level F1 score against the automated alignment.
2. **Baseline Translation Comparison**: Evaluate Gemma-2-MITRA-MT against a non-fine-tuned Gemma-2 baseline and a parameter-matched Gemma-3 variant (if available) on a held-out test set to isolate the effect of fine-tuning versus model scale.
3. **Generalization of Retrieval Model**: Test Gemma-2-MITRA-E on a publicly available multilingual semantic similarity dataset (e.g., STS benchmarks) to verify whether its top performance on the proprietary benchmark transfers to established benchmarks.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: How can the MITRA framework be effectively extended to include other languages of the Buddhist tradition (e.g., classical Japanese, Tocharian, Mongolian) and modern research languages (e.g., Japanese, French, modern Chinese)?  
  *Basis in paper*: The authors state in the conclusion: "In future work, we hope to expand the Gemma-2-MITRA model to include relevant resources in other languages of the Buddhist tradition such as Tocharian or classical Japanese and modern research languages such as Japanese, French, or modern Chinese as well."  
  *Why unresolved*: The current model and corpus focus exclusively on Sanskrit, Pāli, Tibetan, and Chinese. Extension requires addressing data scarcity for additional low-resource languages, adapting the MT-pivot mining pipeline to potentially different translation histories and linguistic structures, and ensuring multilingual scalability without performance degradation.  
  *What evidence would resolve it*: Development of parallel corpora and evaluation benchmarks for the new languages, followed by empirical results showing comparable translation and retrieval performance to the existing four-language setup.

- **Open Question 2**: What distillation methods can reduce the computational cost of Gemma-2-MITRA-E (high parameter count and high dimensionality of the vectors) while preserving its state-of-the-art performance across the seven-task semantic retrieval benchmark?  
  *Basis in paper*: The authors identify distillation as future work: "Another vector of future work is the distillation of the semantic embedding model Gemma-2-MITRA-E, since its high parameter count and high dimensionality of the vectors currently make it challenging to apply on large corpora."  
  *Why unresolved*: The model's size and embedding dimension hinder efficient large-scale retrieval. Distillation must balance efficiency gains against potential performance drops, particularly on morphologically complex languages (e.g., Sanskrit) and semantically distant tasks (e.g., verse→commentary).  
  *What evidence would resolve it*: A systematic study distilling the model into smaller architectures and lower-dimensional vectors, with quantitative benchmark results demonstrating acceptable performance trade-offs and identifying optimal configurations.

- **Open Question 3**: How can cross-lingual retrieval tasks between Pāli and other ancient Buddhist languages be incorporated into the semantic similarity benchmark, and what performance limitations do current models exhibit on these pairs?  
  *Basis in paper*: The limitations section notes: "The semantic similarity benchmark currently does not involve any cross-lingual retrieval tasks between Pāli and other ancient Buddhist languages, which is a noteworthy limitation."  
  *Why unresolved*: Pāli is included only in modern English→classical and QA tasks, not in direct parallelism with Sanskrit, Chinese, or Tibetan. Given Pāli's foundational role and distinct linguistic features, evaluating cross-lingual Pāli retrieval is critical for comprehensive model assessment but remains unaddressed.  
  *What evidence would resolve it*: Manual or semi-automated creation of a test set with Pāli↔Sanskrit, Pāli↔Tibetan, and Pāli↔Chinese parallel sentences, followed by evaluation of existing models to measure performance gaps and guide targeted improvements.

- **Open Question 4**: To what extent do errors in the pivot machine translation step propagate into the MITRA-parallel corpus, and how do these errors affect the performance of models fine-tuned on this data?  
  *Basis in paper*: [inferred] The pipeline uses a domain-finetuned MADLAD-400 model for English pivoting. The resulting corpus has an 11% error rate (Table 1). While some misalignments may arise in the sentence alignment step, inaccurate MT could generate spurious parallel candidates that survive filtering, introducing systematic noise into the training data.  
  *Why unresolved*: The paper does not quantify the contribution of MT errors to the overall error rate. Since the parallel data is used for pretraining and fine-tuning, MT-induced noise could disproportionately impact certain language pairs (e.g., Pāli) and tasks, but this effect remains unmeasured.  
  *What evidence would resolve it*: Error analysis comparing MT outputs against gold translations for a representative subset, correlating MT quality metrics (e.g., BLEU) with alignment accuracy, and controlled ablation experiments adding synthetic MT errors to isolate their impact on downstream model performance.

## Limitations
- Pivot-translation via English may introduce systematic noise, mistranslations, or loss of source-language nuance, especially for low-resource ancient texts.
- The novel seven-task retrieval benchmark lacks external prior work for comparison; its difficulty, task diversity, and evaluation metrics are proprietary, making absolute "top performance" claims hard to contextualize.
- Performance gains are demonstrated on specific Buddhist canonical texts; transfer to other domains or other ancient language pairs is unknown.
