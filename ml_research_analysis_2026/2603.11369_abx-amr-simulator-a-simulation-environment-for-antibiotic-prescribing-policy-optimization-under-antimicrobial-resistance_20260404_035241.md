# abx_amr_simulator: A simulation environment for antibiotic prescribing policy optimization under antimicrobial resistance

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.11369
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11369
- **Reference count:** 4
- **Primary result:** Release of a configurable, extensible Python framework for AMR policy optimization; no experimental agent performance metrics reported.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents `abx_amr_simulator`, a Python-based simulation environment designed to study antibiotic prescribing policies under antimicrobial resistance (AMR). The simulator models the interaction between prescribing decisions and population-level resistance dynamics as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) or Partially Observable MDP (POMDP). Key features include a "leaky-balloon" abstraction for resistance accumulation and decay, modular components for synthetic patient generation, and a tunable reward function that balances immediate clinical benefit against long-term community resistance. It supports partial observability through configurable noise, bias, and delay in observations, and integrates with the Gymnasium reinforcement learning API to enable training and benchmarking of RL agents. The work focuses on software design and potential applications rather than reporting empirical results from agent training.

## Method Summary
The simulator constructs a synthetic patient cohort at each timestep using a `PatientGenerator`, which defines latent infection statuses and patient attributes. Community resistance for each antibiotic is tracked via `AMR_LeakyBalloon`, a continuous accumulator that increases with prescribing and decays over time; observable resistance is derived through a sigmoid transform. A `RewardCalculator` produces a scalar reward as a linear combination of an individual clinical benefit term and a community AMR penalty, weighted by a user-specified λ. These components are orchestrated by the `ABXAMREnv` class, a Gymnasium-compatible environment that yields observations (possibly corrupted by noise, bias, or delay) and rewards. Agents interact with the environment by selecting antibiotic prescriptions per patient. Configuration is driven by YAML files, allowing customization of population demographics, resistance dynamics (including cross-resistance), reward terms, and observation degradation. The framework supports standard RL algorithms (e.g., PPO) and hyperparameter tuning via Optuna.

## Key Results
- The `abx_amr_simulator` package is released as an open-source, modular tool for AMR stewardship research.
- It introduces a novel "leaky-balloon" model for population-level resistance dynamics and flexible partial observability settings to mimic real-world information constraints.
- No quantitative agent performance metrics (e.g., success rates, cumulative reward) are presented; the paper's contribution is the framework itself and its potential for investigating information degradation effects.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The simulator translates complex antimicrobial resistance (AMR) dynamics into a computationally tractable form using a "leaky-balloon" abstraction, enabling simulation of resistance accumulation from prescribing and decay over time.
- **Mechanism:** Each antibiotic's community resistance level is modeled as a latent pressure variable that increases with prescribing (like inflating a balloon) and decays spontaneously (like a leak). An observable resistance probability is derived via a sigmoid function of this pressure.
- **Core assumption:** AMR prevalence at the population level can be effectively modeled as a continuous, soft-bounded accumulator responding to aggregate antibiotic selection pressure, without tracking individual bacterial genetics.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "resistance dynamics modeled via a leaky-balloon abstraction"
  - [section 2.1.3] "AMR_LeakyBalloon... implements a soft-bounded accumulator with decay dynamics... Observable AMR levels (σa) are derived from latent resistance pressure via a sigmoid function"
  - [corpus] Corpus neighbors focus on data-driven prediction or EHR datasets; none describe this specific accumulator abstraction. Evidence for mechanism's novelty is weak in the provided corpus.
- **Break condition:** If cross-resistance effects are not properly parameterized, the model may produce unrealistic resistance correlations between unrelated antibiotic classes, violating known microbiological principles.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The simulator makes the trade-off between immediate patient benefit and long-term community resistance manageable for optimization algorithms by encoding it into a single, tunable scalar reward function.
- **Mechanism:** At each time step, the reward is a weighted average (`λ`) of an individual clinical benefit term and a community AMR penalty term. This allows an agent to navigate the Pareto frontier by adjusting `λ`.
- **Core assumption:** The conflict between individual and population-level outcomes in antibiotic stewardship can be adequately represented by a linear combination of two scalar metrics, sidestepping more complex ethical or multidimensional utility formulations.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "reward functions that balance immediate clinical benefit against long-term resistance management"
  - [section 2.1.4] "Roverall(t) = (1−λ)·⟨˜Rindividual(t)⟩+λ·⟨Rcommunity(t)⟩"
  - [corpus] Neighbors discuss stewardship frameworks and decision support, but not the specific mathematical parameterization of this trade-off for RL. Evidence is indirect.
- **Break condition:** If the individual reward component does not sufficiently penalize treatment failure (e.g., low `ϕf,i`), agents may learn reckless prescribing that optimizes the community term by ignoring patient outcomes.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The simulator enables study of how real-world information limitations (e.g., outdated lab data, noisy assessments) affect optimal policy by structuring partial observability as configurable distortions of the true state.
- **Mechanism:** The true state (patient infection status, exact resistance levels) is hidden. Instead, the observation vector presented to the agent can have injected noise, systematic bias, or temporal delay, controlled via the `PatientGenerator` configuration.
- **Core assumption:** Clinical uncertainty in AMR stewardship can be decomposed into independent, parametric noise channels applied to latent state variables, rather than requiring a full model of observation process evolution.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "supports partial observability through noisy, biased, or delayed observations"
  - [section 2.1.1] "The simulator is structured as a configurable MDP/POMDP-style environment, with explicit control over observability (noise, bias, and delay)"
  - [section 2.1.2] "These attributes can be configured... to introduce noise or bias to observed values, enabling exploration of partial observability."
  - [corpus] Neighbors highlight data deficiencies and prediction challenges in AMR (e.g., "incomplete information" in one title), but do not specify a simulation framework for this. Evidence is thematic, not mechanistic.
- **Break condition:** If observation delay is set longer than the resistance decay `leak` parameter, the agent may perceive resistance as permanently high even after a prescribing holiday, leading to permanently suboptimal, overly conservative policies.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept:** Markov Decision Process (MDP) / Partially Observable MDP (POMDP)  
**Why needed:** The entire simulator is an instantiation of these frameworks. The user must understand states, actions, rewards, transitions, and the critical distinction between full observability (MDP) and belief-state management (POMDP) to interpret agent behavior and design valid experiments.  
**Quick check:** "If an agent's observation lacks the true current resistance level but includes a delayed history, does the problem become an MDP or POMDP? Why?"

**Concept:** The "Leaky-Balloon" Resistance Abstraction  
**Why needed:** This is the core dynamic model. Misunderstanding its parameters (`flatness`, `leak`, cross-resistance links) will lead to misinterpreting simulation outputs or specifying biologically implausible scenarios.  
**Quick check:** "How would increasing the `leak` parameter affect the optimal long-term prescribing strategy for a given antibiotic?"

**Concept:** Multi-Objective Reward Shaping  
**Why needed:** The paper's primary scientific insight hinges on the `λ` trade-off. Users must grasp that this scalarization is a design choice with major implications for what "optimal" means, and that naive reward specification can produce degenerate policies.  
**Quick check:** "If `λ` is set to 0.9, what is the agent's primary optimization target? What clinical or public health outcome might this over- or under-emphasize?"

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
PatientGenerator → AMR_LeakyBalloon → RewardCalculator → ABXAMREnv → Agent  
(PatientGenerator provides synthetic patient data; AMR_LeakyBalloon tracks resistance pressure; RewardCalculator computes reward; ABXAMREnv orchestrates and interfaces via Gymnasium; external RL Agent selects actions.)

**Critical path:**  
1. Initialize subcomponents from YAML configs (PatientGenerator, AMR_LeakyBalloon, RewardCalculator).  
2. Construct ABXAMREnv with these components.  
3. Call `env.reset()` to start an episode.  
4. Loop: Agent receives `observation` from `env.step(action)`, outputs new `action`.  
5. Optionally log or render intermediate states.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Modularity vs. Realism:* Clean separation aids customization but omits epidemiological couplings like patient-to-patient transmission.  
- *Configurability vs. Usability:* YAML system allows deep tweaks but risks invalid parameter combinations (e.g., `flatness` and `initial_pressure` causing saturation).  
- *Gymnasium Compatibility:* Enables broad ML tool use but imposes episodic, fixed-horizon design that may not map to continuous clinical practice.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **State collapse:** All `AMR_LeakyBalloon`s saturate to `σa ≈ 1.0` due to high `initial_pressure` or low `leak`; no learning signal.  
- **Reward hacking:** Agent exploits a poorly constrained `R_individual` (e.g., always prescribing a "benefit" antibiotic with no failure penalty) to maximize reward while ignoring `R_community`.  
- **Observational confusion:** Extreme observation delay or noise makes the POMDP unsolvable; agent performance plateaus at random, indicating experimental mis-specification.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Sanity Check - MDP Homogeneous:** Use default configs, set `λ=0.5`, no observation noise. Train a simple RL agent (e.g., PPO). Verify it prescribes the most effective antibiotic when `R_community` is negligible and diversifies when `λ` is high.  
2. **Partial Observability Stress Test:** Take Exp 1 config, set `observation_bias` to overestimate infection probability (`π_i`). Compare policy and performance: does the agent over-treat? Does performance drop more for high-`λ` regimens?  
3. **Cross-Resistance Scenario:** Modify `AMR_LeakyBalloon` config to add strong cross-resistance from Antibiotic A to B. Train with `λ=0.7`. Inspect prescribing patterns: does the agent avoid A to preserve B? Plot resistance curves for A and B to see if correlation matches configured matrix.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Question:** How do specific clinical constraints (e.g., limited diagnostic tests) and informational degradations (e.g., delayed antibiograms) quantitatively alter optimal antibiotic prescribing strategies?  
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state the simulator enables investigation of "how different clinical or informational constraints shape prescribing strategies" and how "data deficiencies impede antibiotic stewardship efforts."  
**Why unresolved:** The paper presents the simulation framework but does not report empirical results from RL agent training under varying constraint scenarios.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic experimental results comparing agent performance (e.g., cumulative reward, resistance levels) across systematically varied configurations of observation noise, bias, and delay parameters.

**Question:** What is the quantitative benefit (e.g., in reduced AMR prevalence vs. clinical outcome trade-off) of specific stewardship interventions, such as increasing antibiogram update frequency?  
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors pose the question: "What are the quantitative benefits of interventions like more frequent antibiogram updates or improved patient risk assessments?"  
**Why unresolved:** The simulator is released as a tool, but no comparative analysis of intervention efficacy is performed.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Experimental runs simulating different "intervention" configurations (e.g., varying update frequency of observable resistance levels) and measuring the differential impact on long-term community resistance and patient outcomes.

**Question:** How should the individual-community reward trade-off parameter (λ) be calibrated in practice to reflect real-world public health priorities, and are there robust values across diverse population/resistance dynamics?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper introduces λ as a user-configurable parameter but does not provide guidance on its selection, implying this is an open practical question for applying the framework to real policy goals.  
**Why unresolved:** The framework allows arbitrary λ but offers no empirical evaluation of how λ choice affects learned policies or which values align with societal cost-benefit trade-offs.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** A sensitivity analysis across a range of λ values in multiple simulated scenarios (e.g., high vs. low infection risk populations) to identify stable versus fragile policy outcomes and map λ to measurable health economic metrics.

**Question:** How can optimal prescribing policies be learned or remain robust when key drivers of AMR (e.g., agricultural antibiotic use, environmental reservoirs) are unobserved latent factors?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper notes that "antibiotic exposure is influenced by unmeasured factors" and models partial observability via noise/bias/delay in *observed* patient/resistance data, but does not account for truly exogenous, latent pressure sources.  
**Why unresolved:** The current POMDP abstraction only degrades observation of included state variables; it does not incorporate missing major state variables that drive true dynamics.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Extending the simulator to include a latent "background resistance pressure" variable and evaluating whether RL agents can infer its effects from correlations or if policy performance degrades severely.

**Question:** How do nonstationary dynamics—such as evolving patient demographics, seasonal infection patterns, or irreversible resistance shifts—affect the durability and adaptation requirements of learned prescribing policies?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors identify "nonstationary dynamics" as a planned future extension, acknowledging current models are stationary, which is a major limitation for real-world applicability.  
**Why unresolved:** The current simulator assumes static population and resistance parameters; the impact of time-varying ground truth on policy stability is unknown.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Implementing nonstationarity (e.g., slowly shifting patient risk distributions, step-changes in resistance decay rates) and evaluating how often policies must be retrained, or whether certain architectures (e.g., meta-RL) can adapt online without catastrophic forgetting.

## Limitations
- The simulator's core mechanisms (leaky-balloon dynamics, scalar reward, partial observability) are design-specified but lack empirical validation against real-world AMR surveillance or clinical data.
- It oversimplifies key epidemiological factors: no patient-to-patient transmission, linear scalarization of multi-objective trade-offs may induce reward hacking, and cross-resistance parameterization could yield unrealistic correlations.
- Default configuration values (e.g., `leak` rate, `initial_pressure`) are undocumented, hindering exact replication and sensitivity analysis.
