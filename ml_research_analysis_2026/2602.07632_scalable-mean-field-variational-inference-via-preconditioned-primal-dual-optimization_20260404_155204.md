# Scalable Mean-Field Variational Inference via Preconditioned Primal-Dual Optimization

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.07632  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07632  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** PD-VI and P²D-VI introduce a mini‑batch primal‑dual augmented Lagrangian framework with constant step sizes and block‑preconditioning, enabling scalable mean‑field VI for large models without diminishing stepsizes or bounded‑variance assumptions.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the scalability challenges of mean‑field variational inference (MFVI) for large Bayesian models with many local variables and heterogeneous parameter curvature. The authors reformulate MFVI as a constrained finite‑sum problem with consensus constraints on global parameters, then propose PD-VI—a mini‑batch primal‑dual algorithm based on an augmented Lagrangian that updates global and local variational parameters with a constant step size. A block‑preconditioned extension, P²D‑VI, assigns separate step sizes to parameter blocks to adapt to varying curvature. Theoretical analysis guarantees O(1/T) convergence to a stationary point without diminishing stepsizes or bounded‑variance assumptions, and linear convergence under strong convexity. Empirical results on a synthetic 100 k‑point Gaussian mixture model and a real spatial transcriptomics dataset (~150 k locations, ~3 M parameters) show faster convergence, better solution quality (lower Wasserstein distance, higher adjusted rand index), and sharper posterior approximations compared to stochastic variational inference baselines.

## Method Summary
The core innovation is a constrained reformulation of the MFVI objective: the global variational parameters (λ) are copied for each data point, and consensus constraints λᵢ = λ₀ are imposed. This allows mini‑batch processing while preserving global coupling. An augmented Lagrangian (AL) is constructed with dual variables μᵢ and a penalty parameter η. PD‑VI iteratively (1) samples a mini‑batch, (2) for each sample, performs coordinate descent to (approximately) minimize the AL subproblem (updating local variables ϕᵢ and local λᵢ), (3) updates dual variables μᵢ via μᵢ ← μᵢ + (1/η)(λᵢ − λ₀), and (4) aggregates local λᵢ to update the global λ₀ (including a bias‑correction term hₜ). P²D‑VI extends this by replacing the scalar η with a block‑diagonal preconditioning matrix D_η, giving each parameter block its own step size ηⱼ to better handle heterogeneous curvature. The algorithms require only a single pass over the data per iteration and avoid diminishing stepsizes.

## Key Results
- On a synthetic 100 k‑point Gaussian mixture model, PD‑VI and P²D‑VI achieve lower Wasserstein distance to the true posterior and converge faster than SVI, especially under cluster‑imbalanced mini‑batches where SVI with diminishing step size fails.
- On a real spatial transcriptomics dataset (~150 k locations, ~3 M parameters), P²D‑VI yields higher adjusted rand index for spatial domain detection, sharper anatomical boundaries, and a steeper decay in gradient norm compared to SVI, SGD, RMSProp, Adam, and control‑variates baselines.
- Theoretical guarantees: O(1/T) convergence for non‑convex/convex settings and linear convergence under strong convexity, all with constant step size and without bounded‑variance assumptions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Constrained finite‑sum reformulation enables mini‑batch primal‑dual updates
The MFVI objective is separable over local variables but shares a global λ. By introducing copies λᵢ and enforcing λᵢ = λ₀ via consensus constraints, the problem becomes a constrained finite‑sum. The augmented Lagrangian (AL) converts this into unconstrained subproblems that depend on λ₀ only through a quadratic term. PD‑VI then processes mini‑batches: for each sampled i, it minimizes the AL over (ϕᵢ, λᵢ) (primal step) and updates μᵢ (dual step). The global λ₀ update follows from the first‑order optimality condition of the AL, yielding an averaged combination of local λᵢ corrected by hₜ. This structure allows constant step size because the dual variables μᵢ absorb sample‑specific gradient variations, stabilizing updates even with biased mini‑batches.  
*Break condition:* If the AL subproblems are solved poorly (e.g., early stopping in non‑convex updates), error terms in the convergence proofs may grow, degrading the O(1/T) guarantee.

### Mechanism 2: Block‑preconditioning adapts to heterogeneous curvature
P²D‑VI replaces the scalar penalty η in the AL with a block‑diagonal matrix D_η, where each block j (grouping parameters with similar curvature) has its own ηⱼ. This effectively rescales the proximal term per block, akin to pre‑whitening. The theoretical step‑size condition involves ∑ⱼ ηⱼ² Lⱼ² (with Lⱼ the smoothness constant for block j), allowing larger ηⱼ for well‑conditioned blocks. The block‑wise updates in Algorithm 3 separately handle each block’s proximal minimizations, making the algorithm robust to parameter heterogeneity.  
*Break condition:* A poor block partition mixing high‑ and low‑curvature directions can cause the effective step size to be dominated by the worst block, negating benefits. Mis‑specified ηⱼ that violate ∑ ηⱼ² Lⱼ² < constant also risk divergence.

### Mechanism 3: Constant step size with dual updates avoids diminishing stepsizes and handles non‑i.i.d. mini‑batches
In stochastic optimization, diminishing stepsizes are typically needed to average out gradient noise. Here, the dual updates μᵢ ← μᵢ + (1/η)(λᵢ − λ₀) act as an adaptive correction that tracks sample‑specific gradient differences. This stabilizes the algorithm even when mini‑batches are biased (e.g., cluster‑imbalanced in the GMM experiment). The analysis uses virtual iterates (exact AL minimizers) to control errors, proving O(1/T) rates with constant η without requiring bounded variance. Empirically, SVI with diminishing step size deviates on non‑i.i.d. batches, while P²D‑VI maintains steady progress.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept: Constrained Optimization & Augmented Lagrangian Method**  
*Why needed:* The mean‑field VI objective is transformed into a constrained finite‑sum problem (consensus on global λ) to handle shared parameters across data points. The AL (3) converts the constrained problem into unconstrained subproblems amenable to mini‑batch processing.  
*Quick check:* How does the λ₀ update in Algorithm 1 derive from the AL’s first‑order optimality condition? (Answer: ∂L/∂λ₀ = 0 ⇒ λ₀ = (1/n)∑(λᵢ + η μᵢ), leading to the averaging step plus bias‑correction hₜ.)

**Concept: Mean‑Field Variational Inference (MFVI)**  
*Why needed:* The entire setup assumes a factorized variational family q(ζ)q(β) with local ζᵢ and global β. The ELBO decomposes into a sum of per‑sample terms fi(ϕᵢ, λ) that depend on global λ, motivating the consensus constraint.  
*Quick check:* Why does the ELBO in (1) have the form (1/n)∑ fi(ϕᵢ, λ) even though q(β) appears only once? (Answer: The prior term on β is replicated n times in the derivation but scaled by 1/n to balance per‑observation terms.)

**Concept: Preconditioning & Block Diagonal Scaling**  
*Why needed:* Different parameter blocks (e.g., means vs. variances) have vastly different curvatures. A single step size wastes progress on slow directions. Diagonal block preconditioning rescales coordinates to have comparable Lipschitz constants, enabling larger effective steps.  
*Quick check:* What is a practical heuristic for setting ηⱼ? (Answer: Group parameters by estimated Lipschitz constant scale (e.g., via Hessian diagonals or gradient differences) and set ηⱼ ∝ 1/Lⱼ to balance steps.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Problem Reformulation -> Augmented Lagrangian Constructor -> Oracle Subroutine -> Aggregation Update -> Step Size Controller

**Critical path:** For each iteration t: sample mini‑batch Sₜ → for each i ∈ Sₜ run Oracle (I or II) → aggregate λᵢ to update global λₜ₀ → update running sum hₜ. Efficiency hinges on Oracle cost per sample and mini‑batch size.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Mini‑batch size |Sₜ|: larger reduces stochastic variance but increases per‑iteration cost.  
- Block partition granularity: finer blocks adapt better but increase hyperparameter tuning complexity.  
- Oracle solver accuracy: theory assumes exact AL minimization; early stopping introduces error that may slow convergence.  
- Penalty parameter η (or ηⱼ): too large weakens consensus penalty; too small slows dual updates. Must satisfy η ≤ O(1/L) and ∑ ηⱼ² Lⱼ² < constant.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Diverging objective / NaNs: η too large (violates ηL < 1/200) or unstable Oracle.  
- Stagnation at suboptimal value: poor block partition, insufficient Oracle iterations, or too small mini‑batch size.  
- Fragmented clusters in spatial domains: batch partitioning breaks spatial coherence (e.g., non‑contiguous patches).  
- Slow gradient norm decay: possibly due to mis‑specified step sizes or unbalanced blocks.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Synthetic GMM reproduction:** Implement PD‑VI/P²D‑VI for a 5‑cluster GMM with 100 k points, ensuring cluster‑imbalanced mini‑batches. Verify lower Wasserstein distance vs. SVI with diminishing step size.  
2. **Step size sensitivity:** On a strong convex quadratic (Appendix C.3), sweep η (and ηⱼ for P²D‑VI). Validate theoretical bound η ≤ 1/(200L) and linear convergence within range; test unstable ∑ ηⱼ² Lⱼ² violations.  
3. **Block partition impact on MOSTA:** Compare (a) one block (PD‑VI), (b) partition by parameter type (e.g., means vs. variances), (c) heuristic by Lipschitz scale. Measure convergence speed (gradient norm decay) and final ARI; expect (c) to match paper’s P²D‑VI results.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Automating block step sizes:** How can one set block‑specific ηⱼ without prior knowledge of Lipschitz constants? The paper suggests grouping by curvature scale but provides no practical procedure.
2. **Assumption 4.5 robustness:** How generally does ϕ‑strong convexity with respect to local parameters hold for non‑conjugate MFVI? Violation might slow convergence or break guarantees.
3. **Extension to structured mean‑field:** Can the primal‑dual constrained formulation handle non‑factorized variational families that induce dependencies among subsets of latent variables?
4. **Batch partitioning sensitivity:** Does performance depend critically on the fixed batch partitioning (e.g., 30 batches) used in experiments? Could adaptive batch sampling improve speed and accuracy?

## Limitations
- Theoretical analysis assumes exact AL subproblem minimization; practical early stopping may degrade convergence rates.
- Block partitioning and step size selection rely on heuristic curvature estimates, which may be non‑trivial for complex models.
- Empirical validation is limited to one synthetic and one real dataset; broader benchmarking is needed.
- Memory overhead for storing dual variables may become prohibitive for extremely large datasets, though mini‑batching mitigates this.
- Behavior in highly non‑convex settings (e.g., deep Bayesian neural networks) remains unexplored.

## Confidence
- **Reformulation and algorithmic framework:** High – mathematically sound and clearly derived.
- **Theoretical convergence guarantees (O(1/T) without diminishing stepsizes):** Medium – proofs rely on standard assumptions and exact subproblem solutions; practical step‑size sensitivity may affect applicability.
- **Block preconditioning benefits:** Medium – theory supports adaptive step sizes, but empirical evidence is sparse and depends on correct block heuristics.
- **Empirical superiority on tested tasks:** Medium – promising results on GMM and spatial transcriptomics, but based on limited datasets; risk of overfitting to specific setups.

## Next Checks
1. **Assumption verification across models:** Systematically estimate smoothness constants and Hessian spectra for common VI models (e.g., logistic regression, topic models) to assess realism of theoretical assumptions.
2. **Ablation on block partitioning:** Compare fine‑grained (per‑parameter) vs. coarse blocks, and heuristic vs. data‑driven selection, measuring impact on convergence and final ELBO.
3. **Robustness to mini‑batch bias:** Conduct controlled experiments with increasingly imbalanced mini‑batches (e.g., cluster‑skewed GMM) to validate constant step size dual updates vs. SVI diminishing stepsizes.
