# Mitigating Homophily Disparity in Graph Anomaly Detection: A Scalable and Adaptive Approach

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.08137
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08137
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** SAGAD achieves average AUPRC gains of 5.0% (up to 12.8% on T-Social) and uses only ~10% of GPU memory compared to baselines on million-node graphs.

## Executive Summary
Graph anomaly detection (GAD) suffers from homophily disparity—normal nodes are homophilic while anomalies are heterophilic—and from poor scalability of existing graph neural networks. This paper introduces SAGAD, a scalable and adaptive framework that precomputes multi‑hop embeddings and applies reparameterized Chebyshev filters to extract both low‑ and high‑frequency structural information. An anomaly context‑aware adaptive fusion merges these embeddings using node‑specific coefficients derived from Rayleigh Quotient‑guided subgraphs, while a frequency preference guidance loss enforces class‑level spectral separation. SAGAD supports mini‑batch training with linear time and memory complexity, yielding significant performance improvements over state‑of‑the‑art methods on ten benchmark datasets.

## Method Summary
SAGAD consists of four main stages. First, preprocessing caches multi‑hop Chebyshev basis embeddings \(T_k(\hat{L})X\) for \(k=0..K\) via sparse operations. For each node, a Rayleigh Quotient‑guided subgraph (1‑hop) is sampled and its features are aggregated to form an anomaly context vector. Second, two sets of reparameterized Chebyshev coefficients produce low‑pass (\(\mathbf{Z}^L\)) and high‑pass (\(\mathbf{Z}^H\)) embeddings. Third, an MLP takes the node features and the context vector to output a fusion coefficient matrix \(\mathbf{C}\in(0,1)\), which is applied element‑wise: \(\mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{C} \odot \mathbf{Z}^L + (1-\mathbf{C}) \odot \mathbf{Z}^H\). Fourth, an MLP classifier predicts anomaly scores using \(\mathbf{Z}\). The training objective combines binary cross‑entropy with a Frequency Preference Guidance Loss that encourages anomalies to use more high‑frequency information (larger \((1-\mathbf{C})\)). Training is mini‑batch with Adam, using hyperparameter search over learning rate, hidden dimensions, dropout, etc., and supports linear scalability.

## Key Results
- **Performance:** SAGAD outperforms SOTA on 10 datasets, with average AUPRC improvement of 5.0% and peaks of 12.8% on T‑Social; also achieves higher AUROC and Rec@K.
- **Scalability:** On the million‑node T‑Social graph, SAGAD consumes ~1.5 GB GPU memory (~10% of baselines) and runs with linear time/memory complexity.
- **Ablation:** Each component (dual‑filter, RQ‑guided subgraphs, frequency loss) contributes to the gains, with the full model yielding the best results.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Dual‑Pass Chebyshev Filtering
- **Claim:** Captures both homophilic (low‑frequency) and heterophilic (high‑frequency) structural patterns.
- **Mechanism:** Precomputed multi‑hop embeddings are reweighted by two sets of reparameterized Chebyshev coefficients with monotonic constraints, yielding separate low‑pass \(\mathbf{Z}^L\) and high‑pass \(\mathbf{Z}^H\) representations without iterative message passing.
- **Core assumption:** Anomalous nodes exhibit more heterophilic connections (higher‑frequency signals) than normal nodes, which are more homophilic (lower‑frequency).
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 4.1 detail the reparameterization (Eqs. 4–6) and monotonic constraints; related works (BWGNN, AMNet) use spectral filtering but lack the explicit dual‑pass precomputed design.
- **Break condition:** If anomalies do not show significantly higher average frequency content than normals, or if spectral separation between classes is minimal.

### Anomaly Context‑Aware Adaptive Fusion (ACAF)
- **Claim:** Mitigates node‑level homophily disparity by assigning personalized fusion coefficients based on Rayleigh Quotient‑guided subgraphs.
- **Mechanism:** For each node, a 1‑hop subgraph is sampled to maximize the Rayleigh Quotient (RQ), which measures accumulated spectral energy and proxies anomaly “camouflage”. An MLP fuses node features and aggregated subgraph features to produce node‑ and dimension‑specific coefficients \(\mathbf{C}\) for blending \(\mathbf{Z}^L\) and \(\mathbf{Z}^H\).
- **Core assumption:** The subgraph with the highest RQ for a node is most informative of its anomalous structural context and is discriminative for determining frequency preference.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states the adaptive fusion; Section 4.2 derives the RQ‑anomaly degree link (Lemma 1) and describes MRQSampler + MLP (Eqs. 7–9). The concept of RQ‑guided subgraphs is novel; prior works use different spectral heuristics.
- **Break condition:** If the RQ‑guided subgraph fails to capture relevant anomaly cues (e.g., due to noise or extreme sparsity) or if node features are uninformative for the MLP.

### Frequency Preference Guidance Loss (\(\mathcal{L}_{fpg}\))
- **Claim:** Enforces class‑level spectral separation by regularizing fusion coefficients to align with distinct frequency biases (anomalies → high‑frequency, normals → low‑frequency).
- **Mechanism:** A binary cross‑entropy loss penalizes deviation of the mean low‑frequency coefficient \(c_i\) from target values \(p_a\) (anomalies) and \(p_n\) (normals), with \(p_a \le p_n\).
- **Core assumption:** There exists a systematic difference in spectral energy distribution between anomalous and normal nodes at the class level, which can be encoded via fusion coefficient regularization.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions encouraging anomalies to preserve high‑frequency information; Section 4.3 defines \(\mathcal{L}_{fpg}\) (Eq. 10) and the targets. The assumption is supported by related spectral analyses (e.g., BWGNN’s “right‑shift phenomenon”).
- **Break condition:** If class‑level homophily disparity is negligible (\(h_a \approx h_n\)) or if the fusion mechanism cannot effectively modulate frequency preference through \(\mathbf{C}\).

## Foundational Learning

- **Graph Spectral Filtering (Chebyshev Polynomials)**  
  *Why needed:* SAGAD’s dual‑pass filter approximates arbitrary spectral filters (low/high‑pass) via Chebyshev polynomials, avoiding costly eigendecomposition and enabling precomputation.  
  *Quick check:* How does reparameterization of Chebyshev coefficients (Eq. 4–5) enforce monotonic filter shapes for low‑ versus high‑pass?

- **Node/Class Homophily Metrics**  
  *Why needed:* The paper identifies and targets two levels of disparity: class‑level (\(h_a \ll h_n\)) and node‑level (wide variance in \(h_i\)). These metrics define the problem.  
  *Quick check:* Given edge homophily \(h=0.8\), class homophily \(h_a=0.2, h_n=0.9\), what does this imply about anomaly detection difficulty?

- **Contextual Stochastic Block Model (CSBM)**  
  *Why needed:* Theoretical analysis (Theorem 1) uses a modified CSBM with node‑wise regime (homophilic/heterophilic) to prove linear separability under node‑adaptive filtering.  
  *Quick check:* In the CSBM definition (Def. 1), what do the parameters \((p_1,q_1)\) and \((p_2,q_2)\) represent, and how do they model homophily disparity?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map** (data flow):  
Preprocessing → Dual‑pass Filter → Adaptive Fusion → Fusion → Classification & Loss

**Critical path** during training:  
Precomputation (once) → for each batch: fetch cached embeddings/samples → dual‑filter lookup (no gradient) → subgraph aggregation (parameter‑free) → fusion MLP (trainable) → classification head.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Precomputation memory vs. training speed:* Caching \(K\)-hop embeddings uses RAM but enables linear training complexity.  
- *RQ subgraph size:* Larger \(k\) may capture more context but introduces noise; paper uses \(k=1\) for efficiency.  
- *Targets \(p_a, p_n\):* Must satisfy \(p_a \le p_n\); extreme values may over‑regularize.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High variance in performance across homophily quartiles (Q1–Q4 gap) → ACAF or \(\mathcal{L}_{fpg}\) ineffective.  
- Out‑of‑memory on large graphs → failed to decouple; entire‑graph operations remain.  
- \(\mathbf{C}\) values near 0 or 1 for all nodes → fusion collapsed; MLP not learning node‑specific contexts.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Ablation study** (Table 4): Remove \(\mathbf{Z}^L\), \(\mathbf{Z}^H\), \(\mathcal{G}_{RQ}\), or \(\mathcal{L}_{fpg}\) to quantify each component’s contribution.  
2. **Homophily quartile analysis** (Table 2, Figures 1/3): Compare AUPRC gaps between Q1 (high homophily) and Q4 (low homophily) across models; SAGAD should show smallest gap.  
3. **Scalability test** (Table 3): Measure GPU memory/time on T‑Social (5.78M nodes); SAGAD should use ~10% memory of baselines.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1:** What constitutes a suitable subgraph for graph anomaly detection?  
*basis:* [explicit] In Section 4.2 the authors ask this and propose Rayleigh Quotient‑guided subgraphs via MRQSampler.  
*Why unresolved:* The paper evaluates only Rayleigh Quotient without comparing to alternatives (centrality‑based, random‑walk) or establishing general principles.  
*What evidence would resolve:* Comprehensive empirical studies contrasting diverse subgraph extraction methods, with theoretical analysis of subgraph properties (size, spectral content) on GAD performance.

**Open Question 2:** Can SAGAD be efficiently extended to dynamic or evolving graphs?  
*basis:* [inferred] SAGAD relies on precomputed multi‑hop embeddings from a static adjacency matrix (Sections 4.1, 4.5), which is impractical for dynamic graphs.  
*Why unresolved:* The paper tests only static graphs and does not address temporal scalability for streaming or time‑sensitive anomaly detection.  
*What evidence would resolve:* Development of incremental algorithms for updating embeddings and fusion coefficients as the graph evolves, validated on temporal graph benchmarks with performance and efficiency metrics.

**Open Question 3:** How can the frequency preference guidance loss parameters (\(p_a, p_n\)) be set automatically in unsupervised or semi‑supervised settings?  
*basis:* [inferred] The loss requires predefined targets tuned via grid search using validation labels (Section 5.3), which may be unknown in practice with scarce supervision.  
*Why unresolved:* The method lacks a mechanism to estimate these preferences from unlabeled data or graph structure alone.  
*What evidence would resolve:* Introduction of a self‑supervised approach to infer \(p_a, p_n\) (e.g., clustering embeddings or optimizing a proxy objective), evaluated under varying label rates to demonstrate robustness.

## Limitations
- **Hyperparameter specifics undisclosed:** Exact values (learning rate, hidden size, dropout, \(p_a, p_n\), \(K\)) per dataset are not provided, requiring reliance on repository configs.
- **MRQSampler underspecified:** Subgraph size, number of hops beyond 1‑hop, and sampling strategy are vague, hindering exact replication.
- **Implementation details missing:** Laplacian scaling (\(\lambda_{\text{max}}\)), batch size, and training tricks (gradient clipping, warm‑up) are not documented, leading to potential reproducibility gaps.

## Confidence
- **Dual‑pass Chebyshev filtering:** High (solid theoretical basis, strong ablation, spectral GNN alignment).
- **ACAF with RQ subgraphs:** Medium (novel but depends on underspecified MRQSampler; limited external validation).
- **Frequency Preference Guidance Loss:** Medium (conceptually sound but coefficient regularization is new; needs testing on graphs with minimal homophily disparity).
- **Scalability claim:** High (linear complexity proven, million‑node experiments show ~10% memory usage vs. baselines).
