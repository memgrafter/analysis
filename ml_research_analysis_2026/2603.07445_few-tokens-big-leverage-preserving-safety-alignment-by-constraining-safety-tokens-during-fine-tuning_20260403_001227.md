# Few Tokens, Big Leverage: Preserving Safety Alignment by Constraining Safety Tokens during Fine-tuning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.07445
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07445
- Reference count: 39
- Primary result: PACT reduces ASR from 94.50% (SFT) to 13.50–29.50% while preserving task accuracy on Qwen2.5-7B with 10% harmful data, and achieves 0–14.5% ASR across Llama and Gemma families.

## Executive Summary
Safety-aligned large language models often drift toward harmful behavior when fine-tuned on downstream tasks, especially if the data contains even a small fraction of adversarial examples. PACT addresses this by constraining only a small, automatically identified set of “safety tokens” rather than applying broad parameter restrictions. It uses a weighted KL regularization with an adaptive calibration that mixes two reference distributions to avoid prefix contamination. Experiments on Qwen2.5, Llama, and Gemma models show drastic reductions in attack success rates (e.g., from 94.50% to as low as 0%) while maintaining downstream task performance.

The method is token‑efficient (≈50 safety tokens), outperforms existing defenses like SafeLoRA and AsFT, and remains robust across varying proportions of harmful data and model sizes. PACT offers a practical way to preserve alignment without sacrificing fine‑tuning flexibility.

## Method Summary
PACT operates in two phases. First, it identifies safety tokens by comparing the aligned model to the base model on safe responses to harmful prompts. For each token \(v\), the discrepancy score \(d(v) = \mathbb{E}_{t}[p_{\text{safe}}(v|t) - p_{\text{base}}(v|t)]\) is averaged over prompts and positions; the top \(K\) tokens form the safety set \(S_{\text{safety}}\) with weights \(V_{\text{safety}}(v)=d(v)\).

During LoRA fine‑tuning, for each batch the method computes two reference logits from the frozen aligned model: one conditioned on the full prompt plus response prefix (full‑context) and one conditioned only on the assistant header plus the prefix (no‑prompt). For every response position \(t\), it measures lexical dispersion \(I(t)\) – the smallest fraction of \(S_{\text{safety}}\) needed to cover 90% of the probability mass – for both the current model (\(I_{\text{ft}}\)) and the no‑prompt reference (\(I_{\text{post}}\)). A gating coefficient \(c_t = \sigma(I_{\text{ft}}(t) - I_{\text{post}}(t))\) is derived and decayed for later positions (\(\tilde{c}_t\)). The logits are then mixed: \(z_{\text{ref}}^{\text{mix}} = (1-\tilde{c}_t)z_{\text{full}} + \tilde{c}_t z_{\text{post}}\), converted to probabilities \(p_{\text{ref}}^{\text{mix}}\), and weighted by \(V_{\text{safety}}\) to obtain the reference distribution over \(S_{\text{safety}}\) (normalized). The fine‑tuned model’s distribution over \(S_{\text{safety}}\) is similarly normalized. The KL loss \(L_{\text{KL}}^{\text{safety}} = \text{mean}_t \text{KL}(p_{\text{ref}}^{\text{safety}} \| p_{\text{ft}}^{\text{safety}})\) is added to the standard cross‑entropy loss \(L_{\text{CE}}\) with weight \(\lambda_{\text{KL}}=3\). Only LoRA parameters are updated.

## Key Results
- On Qwen2.5-7B with 10% harmful data, PACT achieves HarmBench ASR of 13.50–29.50% vs SFT's 94.50%, while task accuracy remains 89–92%.
- Across Llama-3.1-8B and Gemma-2-9B, ASR falls to 0–14.5%, outperforming SafeLoRA, AsFT, and a full‑vocab KL baseline.
- The method is robust to harmful data ratios from 1% to 100% and scales to both 1B and 9B models.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
PACT exploits the concentration of safety alignment within a compact set of tokens that typically signal refusal or hedging. These tokens are discovered through discrepancy scores where the aligned model confidently diverges from the base. By regularizing only this subset, the model’s ability to decline harmful requests is preserved while it remains free to adapt other parts of the distribution to new tasks. The adaptive mixing of full‑context and no‑prompt references mitigates “prefix contamination” – a phenomenon where the fine‑tuning data biases early decoding steps toward unsafe continuations – by falling back on a no‑prompt distribution that is unaffected by the adversarial prefix. The sigmoid gating, driven by a dispersion metric, dynamically decides how much to rely on each reference, and the exponential decay ensures the calibration is strongest at the start of the response where safety matters most. This targeted, context‑aware regularization prevents safety drift without sacrificing utility.

## Foundational Learning
- **LoRA (Low‑Rank Adaptation)**  
  Why needed: Enables parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning by updating low‑rank matrices, drastically reducing memory and compute compared to full model updates.  
  Quick check: Confirm LoRA rank (set to 8 in PACT) and target modules (e.g., q_proj, v_proj) are correctly configured in the PEFT wrapper.

- **KL Divergence Regularization**  
  Why needed: Aligns the fine‑tuned model’s distribution over safety tokens with a safe reference, preventing drift in refusal behavior.  
  Quick check: Compute average KL loss on a validation batch; it should be positive and numerically stable.

- **Safety Token Identification via Discrepancy Scores**  
  Why needed: Isolates the subset of tokens that carry the safety signal, allowing precise constraints without hindering general adaptation.  
  Quick check: Inspect top‑20 tokens from \(d(v)\); they should include common refusal words like “sorry”, “cannot”, “assist”, “Apologize”, etc.

- **Token Dispersion Metric (I(t))**  
  Why needed: Quantifies how concentrated the model’s probability mass is on safety tokens; a low value indicates that a small fraction of \(S_{\text{safety}}\) already covers 90% of the mass, reflecting strong safety behavior.  
  Quick check: For a safe reference, \(I(t)\) should be low (e.g., <0.2) at early positions; for an unsafe model it should be higher.

- **Adaptive Calibration (Sigmoid Gating + Exponential Decay)**  
  Why needed: Mixes two reference distributions to counter prefix contamination while preserving early safety signals; the gating is controlled by the difference in dispersion scores.  
  Quick check: Log \(c_t\) across positions; it should start near 1 (favoring full‑context) and decay as positions increase, potentially approaching 0 if needed.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Safety Token ID → Reference Model (Full & No‑Prompt) → Dispersion Computation → Gating & Mixing → KL Loss Computation → Total Loss → Backpropagation

**Critical path**  
1. Precompute \(S_{\text{safety}}\) and \(V_{\text{safety}}\).  
2. During training, run a forward pass of the LoRA‑augmented model to obtain logits.  
3. In parallel, perform two forward passes on the frozen aligned model: full‑context (prompt + prefix) and no‑prompt (assistant header + prefix).  
4. For each response position in \(S_{\text{safety}}\), compute \(I_{\text{ft}}(t)\) and \(I_{\text{post}}(t)\).  
5. Compute \(c_t = \sigma(I_{\text{ft}} - I_{\text{post}})\) and apply exponential decay to get \(\tilde{c}_t\).  
6. Mix reference logits: \(z_{\text{ref}}^{\text{mix}} = (1-\tilde{c}_t)z_{\text{full}} + \tilde{c}_t z_{\text{post}}\); softmax → \(p_{\text{ref}}^{\text{mix}}\).  
7. Weight \(p_{\text{ref}}^{\text{mix}}\) by \(V_{\text{safety}}\) over \(S_{\text{safety}}\), normalize to obtain \(p_{\text{ref}}^{\text{safety}}\).  
8. Restrict the fine‑tuned model’s distribution to \(S_{\text{safety}}\) and normalize → \(p_{\text{ft}}^{\text{safety}}\).  
9. Calculate \(L_{\text{KL}}^{\text{safety}} = \text{mean}_t \text{KL}(p_{\text{ref}}^{\text{safety}} \| p_{\text{ft}}^{\text{safety}})\).  
10. Combine with cross‑entropy: \(L = L_{\text{CE}} + \lambda_{\text{KL}} L_{\text{KL}}^{\text{safety}}\).  
11. Backpropagate and update only LoRA parameters.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- **Token set size (\(K\))**: A small \(K\) may miss crucial safety tokens; too large risks over‑constraining. The paper finds a sweet spot around 50.  
- **Calibration complexity vs. speed**: Adaptive mixing adds two extra forward passes and dispersion calculations, increasing cost versus a simpler full‑vocab KL but improves safety‑utility trade‑off.  
- **Reference model robustness**: PACT assumes the frozen aligned model is safe; a flawed reference could propagate weaknesses.  
- **Position‑wise decay**: Hyperparameters (\(N\), \(\tau\)) require tuning; inappropriate values may under‑ or over‑correct.

**Failure signatures**  
- Safety token list lacks obvious refusal words → identification set too small or mis‑configured.  
- Gating \(c_t \approx 0\) or \(1\) for all positions → dispersion calculation error or mis‑formatted no‑prompt.  
- KL loss dominates, causing task accuracy drop → \(\lambda_{\text{KL}}\) too high or mismatch in token set normalization.  
- No‑prompt reference logits differ unexpectedly at early positions → incorrect assistant header string for the model’s chat template.  
- Out‑of‑memory errors due to two reference forwards → use gradient checkpointing or reduce batch size.

**First experiments**  
1. **Token Identification Sanity** – Run identification on 500 WildJailbreak prompts. Verify the top‑20 tokens include typical refusal phrases (e.g., “sorry”, “cannot”, “assist”). If not, increase the number of identification prompts.  
2. **Gating Coefficient Behavior** – After one training step, log \(c_t\) and \(\tilde{c}_t\) for a batch. Expect \(c_t\) to increase with position before decay and stay within \([0,1]\). Constant values indicate a dispersion or threshold issue.  
3. **No‑Prompt Reference Format** – On a known safe prompt, compare \(p_{\text{ref}}^{\text{full}}\) and \(p_{\text{ref}}^{\text{post}}\). The no‑prompt distribution should have higher probability on safety tokens at early positions. If the pattern is absent, the assistant header string likely does not match the model’s chat template.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What is the principled basis for the specific calibration mechanism (sigmoid gating and exponential decay) used to mix full‑context and no‑prompt references, and could alternative calibration strategies yield better safety‑utility trade‑offs?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors design a gating coefficient based on a confidence proxy and apply position‑wise decay, stating these are intuitive choices to mitigate harmful prefix contamination. However, they do not justify why a sigmoid function or specific decay form is optimal, nor do they systematically compare to other possible calibration functions.
- **Why unresolved:** The calibration design is presented as a heuristic solution to a identified failure mode (prefix contamination). Without an ablation of the calibration function itself or theoretical analysis of its properties, it is unclear if this is the most effective or robust approach.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An extensive study comparing different functional forms for the gating coefficient (e.g., linear, threshold‑based, learned) and decay schedules (e.g., different \(\tau\), step‑based) on a diverse set of models and harmful data distributions. Performance should be measured by both safety preservation (ASR) and stability of the calibration coefficient during training.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How does the number of constrained safety tokens *\(K\)* fundamentally relate to the model's architecture, scale, and the nature of its safety training, and can *\(K\)* be determined automatically rather than through grid search?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper fixes *\(K\)*=50 for analysis and main experiments after initial exploration, noting in Section 4.3 (Hyperparameter sensitivity) that performance is "optimal" at *\(K\)*=50 and stable up to *\(K\)*=1600. They state this provides "empirical support" for safety concentration but do not explain the plateau or the sharp drop if *\(K\)* is too small (e.g., *\(K\)*=5).
- **Why unresolved:** The choice of *\(K\)* is critical as it controls the granularity of constraints. The observation of a "remarkably concentrated" safety signal is intriguing, but the lack of analysis on *why* 50 tokens works across different models (Llama‑3.2‑1B vs. Gemma‑2‑9B) and what those tokens represent (e.g., function words vs. content) leaves the generalizability of this finding unclear.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A deeper analysis correlating *\(K\)* with model size, pretraining data, and alignment method (e.g., SFT vs. RLHF). An automated metric for selecting *\(K\)* (e.g., based on the distribution of discrepancy scores *d(v)*, such as an elbow method or a threshold on score variance) should be proposed and validated to see if it matches or surpasses the manually tuned *\(K\)*=50.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** To what extent does PACT's effectiveness depend on the initial safety‑aligned reference model being itself robust, and how does it perform when the reference model has latent safety weaknesses or has been subtly jailbroken?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] PACT assumes a frozen, safely‑aligned reference model (*M_safe*) provides a reliable signal. All experiments use standard instruct models (Qwen2.5‑7B‑Instruct, etc.). The paper notes in Section 4.4 that PACT can "mitigate latent safety risks" even with *P*=0% harmful data, implying the reference model is not perfect. However, the scenario where the reference model itself is vulnerable (e.g., due to poor base safety or its own fine‑tuning artifacts) is not explored.
- **Why unresolved:** PACT's core mechanism is to regularize toward a reference. If that reference is flawed, the regularization could cement undesirable behaviors or fail to correct them. This is a critical dependency not addressed in the current evaluation setup, which uses widely accepted "safe" instruct checkpoints.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Experiments where the reference *M_safe* is itself fine‑tuned on a small, subtly harmful dataset or is a weaker base model. Measure whether PACT can still prevent drift or if it propagates the reference's flaws. Additionally, analyzing the overlap between tokens identified as "safety tokens" from different reference models of varying robustness would clarify the signal's stability.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What are the computational and optimization overheads of PACT compared to simpler baselines (e.g., vanilla SFT) and other defense methods (e.g., AsFT), and do the token‑level operations introduce significant training slowdown or memory cost?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper focuses on safety and utility metrics (ASR, Accuracy) and states PACT uses LoRA with rank 8 and a learning rate of 3e‑5, like baselines. It does not report training time, memory usage, or per‑step computational cost.
- **Why unresolved:** The introduction positions PACT as a "fine‑grained" alternative to "model‑wide interventions" which can degrade performance. However, the proposed method involves extra forward passes for the reference model, computation of token‑level weights *V_safety*, and the adaptive calibration logic. The net efficiency impact relative to simply adding a full‑vocab KL penalty (which they ablate) is unknown. This is crucial for practical adoption.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A systematic benchmark reporting training throughput (samples/sec), GPU memory footprint, and wall‑clock time for PACT versus SFT, SafeLoRA, and AsFT under identical hardware and batch sizes. An analysis of how the cost scales with vocabulary size and the number of safety tokens *\(K\)*.

## Limitations
- Core hyperparameters \(N\) and \(\tau\) are not disclosed; the paper uses inferred values from ablations, which may not generalize to all settings.
- The method requires correct chat‑template formatting for the no‑prompt reference; variations across models can break the calibration.
- The stability of the identified safety token set with respect to the quantity and source of harmful prompts is unexplored, risking inconsistent \(S_{\text{safety}}\).

## Next Checks
1. **Token set sanity** – Compare the top‑50 safety tokens obtained from different numbers of identification prompts (e.g., 500 vs. 2000). A Jaccard similarity below 0.7 indicates instability and suggests using a larger, standardized set.
2. **Gating sensitivity sweep** – For fixed \(\lambda_{\text{KL}}=3\), vary \(N \in \{5,10,15\}\) and \(\tau \in \{5,10,20\}\) and plot ASR/accuracy heatmaps. If no robust plateau exists, PACT may be hyperparameter‑fragile.
3. **Header artifact test** – With Qwen2.5, measure ASR when using the correct `" Assistant:"` header versus a generic `"<assistant>"` or empty string. A >10% ASR difference confirms sensitivity to chat‑template formatting.
