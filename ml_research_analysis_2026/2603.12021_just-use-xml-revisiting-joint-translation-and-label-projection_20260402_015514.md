# Just Use XML: Revisiting Joint Translation and Label Projection

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.12021
- Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12021
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Joint translation and label projection using XML tags achieves high label projection accuracy (average Label Match F1 79.2–79.9 across 11 languages), improves translation quality (COMET) in most of 203 languages, and yields substantial downstream cross-lingual transfer gains (up to +39.9 F1 on NER) without extra inference cost.

## Executive Summary
The paper challenges the notion that joint translation and label projection degrades translation quality. It introduces LabelPigeon, a method that performs both tasks simultaneously by fine-tuning a multilingual translation model (NLLB-200) on an XML-tagged corpus. XML tags are used to mark source spans, and the model learns to preserve these tags in the translation, enabling direct extraction of target spans. Evaluation on multiple languages and tasks shows that LabelPigeon outperforms alignment and marker-based baselines in label projection accuracy while maintaining or improving translation quality. The approach yields significant gains in downstream cross-lingual transfer, such as NER, without additional inference overhead, demonstrating the viability of joint training.

## Method Summary
LabelPigeon builds on the NLLB-200 3.3B model, fine-tuned on a modified version of the Salesforce Localization XML MT dataset. The dataset’s original markup is replaced with generic XML tags (e.g., <a>, <b>) to encourage generalizability. During training, source sentences contain XML tags around annotated spans; the model learns to generate translations that preserve these tags. At inference, the same tag-insertion is applied to new source text, and the model’s output is parsed as XML to obtain the projected spans. This joint formulation eliminates separate label projection steps and their associated errors. The training uses standard sequence-to-sequence objectives with a modest number of language pairs (optimal at three high-resource languages) to avoid catastrophic forgetting.

## Key Results
- Label projection accuracy (Label Match F1) averages 79.2–79.9 across 11 languages, surpassing alignment and marker-based baselines.
- Translation quality, measured by COMET, improves over the base NLLB model in the majority of 203 evaluated languages.
- Downstream cross-lingual transfer (e.g., NER) sees substantial gains, up to +39.9 F1 across 27 languages, with no additional inference cost compared to the base model.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: XML Tag Structural Integrity
- Claim: XML tags preserve direct span correspondence during translation better than square brackets.
- Mechanism: XML's balanced opening/closing tags create unambiguous boundaries that the model learns to maintain across language boundaries, whereas square brackets require post-hoc fuzzy matching to re-align spans.
- Core assumption: Neural models can internalize structural markup patterns when trained on sufficient parallel XML-annotated data.
- Evidence anchors:
  - Abstract: "XML tags provide a direct correspondence between the source spans and translation spans. They can also handle nesting and overlapping spans gracefully."
  - Section 3.1: Technical comparison showing square brackets lack intrinsic pairing, forcing reliance on error-prone fuzzy string matching; XML tags encode structural relationships natively.
  - Section 4: Training on Salesforce dataset with natural XML markup enables learning this preservation.
- Break condition: If training data lacks sufficient examples of complex/nested structures (training max: 6 unique tags; evaluation max: 24 tags), generalization to highly complex annotation patterns may fail.

### Mechanism 2: Beneficial Fine-tuning Transfer
- Claim: Additional fine-tuning on XML-tagged corpora improves base translation quality while adding label projection capability.
- Mechanism: Fine-tuning provides general multilingual representation improvement (cross-lingual transfer) even for unseen languages, while simultaneously learning tag preservation as an auxiliary task. The XML markup serves as structured regularization.
- Core assumption: Benefits of extra task-specific training outweigh catastrophic forgetting risks, particularly with high-resource language pairs.
- Evidence anchors:
  - Abstract: "Translation quality (COMET) is improved over the base model in most of 203 languages."
  - Section 6 (Table 2): All fine-tuned models (NF, EProj, LP) outperform baseline on "No Markers" condition; NF (unmarked fine-tuning) shows pure fine-tuning benefits.
  - Appendix A.1: Ablation shows 3-language fine-tuning optimal; 7-language training degrades, suggesting forgetting threshold.
- Break condition: When fine-tuning data is too linguistically diverse (catastrophic forgetting) or dominated by low-resource language pairs that don't transfer broadly.

### Mechanism 3: Joint Training Synergy
- Claim: Joint label-aware translation avoids quality degradation reported in prior work by eliminating the projection step's error propagation.
- Mechanism: Training the model to produce translations with preserved spans incentivizes output fluency that maintains annotation coherence. Separate projection (alignment or marker-based) risks misalignment errors; joint approach internalizes span preservation as part of translation objective.
- Core assumption: Single model can effectively perform both tasks when trained on appropriate data, avoiding pipeline complexity.
- Evidence anchors:
  - Abstract: "prior work that combines the two reports degraded translation quality. We re-evaluate this claim... can be improved while simultaneously transferring labeled spans."
  - Section 5.2: LabelPigeon improves COMET over baseline in 10/11 languages; EasyProject degrades translation quality across all configurations.
  - Section 7: Downstream gains across 27 languages without "additional inference cost" vs multi-stage baselines.
- Break condition: For tasks with extremely dense or nested spans (e.g., coreference), even joint approach shows limitations (Table 3: low absolute scores despite relative gains).

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:
  - Base model: NLLB-200 3.3B (pre-trained multilingual encoder-decoder)
  - Training data: Modified Salesforce Localization XML MT dataset (XML tags simplified to <a>, <b>, etc.; ~150k samples)
  - Fine-tuning: Standard sequence-to-sequence with XML-tagged source sentences
  - Inference pipeline: Source text → insert XML tags around spans → model translation → XML parser extracts spans
  - Evaluation modules: Direct label projection (Label Match F1 on XQuAD/MLQA), translation quality (COMET/BLEU/chrF++), downstream (UNER/CorefUD/MLQA)

- **Critical path**:
  1. Data preparation: Download Salesforce XML dataset; replace original tags with alphabetical tags; filter tagless examples; split train/valid (25k/1.3k per language).
  2. Model fine-tuning: Load NLLB-200 3.3B; train 1 epoch on 3 language pairs (en-de, en-ru, en-zh) with hyperparameters in Table 4; save checkpoint.
  3. Label projection inference: For annotated source text, wrap spans in <a>, <b>... tags; translate; parse output XML to extract target spans.
  4. Validation: Run direct evaluation on XQuAD/MLQA; check COMET scores; compare against baselines.

- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - XML vs square brackets: XML gives direct correspondence & nesting support but requires balanced tags; square brackets simpler but need expensive fuzzy matching post-hoc.
  - Training scope: More languages → broader transfer but risk catastrophic forgetting; paper finds 3 high-resource pairs optimal.
  - Tag simplification: Original UI/SGML tags replaced with generic <a>, <b> to encourage generalizability, but loses semantic information.
  - Direct vs downstream: Direct evaluation (Label Match F1) gives precise projection accuracy; downstream measures real utility but conflates projection errors with model limitations.

- **Failure signatures**:
  - Label projection: Low F1 on XQuAD/MLQA; tags missing, split, or misaligned in output; particularly poor on nested spans (seen in CorefUD: EasyProject fails completely on 12/21 languages).
  - Translation degradation: COMET/BLEU below baseline, especially with Complex synthetic markers.
  - Generalization gaps: Performance drop on languages far from training distribution (e.g., low-resource, typologically distinct).
  - Overfitting: NF model performs best, but LabelPigeon with many/Complex markers shows quality drop, indicating limit to tag-handling capacity.

- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Direct projection validation: Replicate Table 1 on XQuAD/MLQA; verify LabelPigeon > EasyProject in Label Match F1; check translation quality doesn't degrade.
  2. Marker complexity test: Synthetically insert Single/Simple/Complex tags on FLORES-200; confirm LabelPigeon maintains projection rate and translation quality better than EasyProject under complexity.
  3. Downstream NER benchmark: Project UNER English training data to target languages; train XLM-R; evaluate on test sets; expect +10+ F1 gain over EasyProject for low-resource languages.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How does LabelPigeon compare to CODEC and CLaP in accuracy and efficiency across languages and tasks? The authors did not fully evaluate due to computational expense; a preliminary experiment on one language pair showed LabelPigeon outperforming CODEC. Full-scale evaluation was prohibitively expensive and implementation-challenging.
2. Does translationese in evaluation datasets bias label projection results, and how robust is LabelPigeon when evaluated on natural parallel text? The authors note that evaluations on XQuAD and FLORES-200 may be affected by translationese, as these are translated datasets. All evaluation data are translated from English; no experiments control for translationese effects.
3. What training data composition (language pairs, domains, tag types) maximizes LabelPigeon's transfer to low-resource and typologically diverse languages? The authors found training on three high-resource languages (German, Russian, Chinese) worked best, while training on all seven caused catastrophic forgetting. They used UI localization data with tags swapped. The impact of training data characteristics on cross-lingual transfer is not systematically studied.
4. Can LabelPigeon be adapted for tasks with high-frequency and nested span annotations, such as coreference resolution? Downstream coreference resolution results were low for both LabelPigeon and EasyProject. The authors hypothesize this is due to the frequency and nesting of coreference spans, noting that training data had a maximum of 6 unique tags per example while evaluation had up to 24. The method may not generalize to high tag density and nesting; no explicit adaptation was tested.

## Limitations
- Generalization to complex/unseen structures: The model trains on simplified tags (max 6 unique) but evaluation includes up to 24 tags; performance on highly nested/overlapping spans (e.g., coreference) remains low.
- Fine-tuning mechanism nuance: The narrow optimal fine-tuning scope (3 languages) suggests a risk of catastrophic forgetting; the relative contributions of general multilingual improvement vs. tag-specific learning are not fully disentangled.
- Downstream task confounders: Downstream gains could stem from better label projection or from the fine-tuned model's broader knowledge; these effects are conflated.

## Confidence
- XML Tag Structural Integrity Mechanism: Medium
- Beneficial Fine-tuning Transfer (empirical claim): High; (mechanism): Medium
- Joint Training Synergy & No Degradation: High
- Downstream Cross-lingual Transfer Gains (NER): High; (CorefUD): Medium

## Next Checks
1. Stress-Test XML Generalization: Synthetically generate test sets with increasingly complex nested/overlapping spans (beyond XQuAD/MLQA distribution) and evaluate Label Match F1 for LabelPigeon vs. baselines.
2. Isolate Fine-tuning Effects: Run controlled experiment: (a) fine-tune NLLB on plain Salesforce data (no tags), (b) fine-tune on synthetically tagged data with random span markers, (c) use LabelPigeon. Compare COMET scores and projection accuracy.
3. Downstream Task Breadth Verification: Replicate downstream transfer pipeline for at least two additional token-level tasks (e.g., POS tagging, dependency parsing) and one span-level task (e.g., semantic role labeling) using same projected data.
