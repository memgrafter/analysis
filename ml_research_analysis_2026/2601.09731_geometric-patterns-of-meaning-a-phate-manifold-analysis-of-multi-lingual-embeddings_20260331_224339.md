# Geometric Patterns of Meaning: A PHATE Manifold Analysis of Multi-lingual Embeddings

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2601.09731  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.09731  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** PHATE manifold learning reveals universal geometric patterns (clustering-branching for content words, spiral trajectories for numerals) and critical failures (radical collapse) in multilingual embedding spaces, providing a robust diagnostic for model quality beyond downstream task performance.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces a systematic framework for evaluating the geometric structure of multilingual embedding spaces, addressing a gap in tools that move beyond downstream task metrics. Using PHATE-based manifold learning via the Semanscope tool, it analyzes embeddings across linguistic levels—from Chinese radicals to Arabic numerals. Key findings include: (1) a geometric collapse of purely structural Chinese radicals; (2) a universal clustering-branching pattern for content words across English, Chinese, and German; and (3) a distinct spiral organization for Arabic numerals, which violates standard clustering assumptions. PHATE is validated as superior among 12 dimensionality reduction methods for preserving both local and global semantic structure. The work establishes geometric visualization as a critical diagnostic, with model comparisons showing that smaller parameter counts do not preclude superior geometric balance (e.g., Qwen3-0.6B vs. Gemma-300M).

## Method Summary
The methodology centers on a multi-level analysis pipeline applied to curated word lists across four linguistic strata: (1) sub-character components (Chinese radicals), (2) writing system characters, (3) semantic domain words (including morphological families) in English, Chinese, and German, and (4) Arabic numerals and mathematical terms. For each target model (accessed via HuggingFace, OpenRouter, or Ollama), vectors are extracted for the word lists. Dimensionality reduction is performed using PHATE with fixed hyperparameters (`k=10` neighbors, `alpha=10` decay, `t=20` diffusion steps) to produce 2D/3D coordinates, which are visualized interactively. The resulting geometric patterns (e.g., clustering, branching, spirals) are qualitatively assessed. Models are evaluated "out-of-the-box" without fine-tuning, and PHATE's superiority is benchmarked against 12 alternative methods.

## Key Results
- Geometric collapse of purely structural Chinese radicals indicates models fail to distinguish structural from semantic elements.
- Universal clustering-branching patterns for content words are observed across English, Chinese, and German, reflecting similarity-based clustering and derivational branching.
- Arabic numerals organize into spiral trajectories rather than clusters, violating standard distributional semantics assumptions and highlighting the impact of compositional density.
- PHATE outperforms 12 dimensionality reduction methods in preserving both local clustering and global branching.
- Qwen3-0.6B achieves optimal geometric balance, while Gemma-300M exhibits catastrophic collapse, demonstrating that parameter count does not correlate with geometric quality.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** PHATE manifold learning uniquely balances preservation of local clustering and global branching patterns, making it superior for semantic geometry analysis.
- **Mechanism:** PHATE applies a heat kernel to compute affinities, then models diffusion as a Markov process; the resulting diffusion distances capture both tight local neighborhoods and long-range transitional structure.
- **Core assumption:** Semantic relationships in embedding spaces lie on a manifold recoverable through diffusion-based distance metrics.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Abstract: “PHATE is validated as superior among 12 dimensionality reduction methods for preserving both local clustering and global branching.”  
  - Section 3.2 details PHATE parameters (k=10, α=10, t=20); Appendix A.5 (Table 2, Figure 9) shows cross-method comparison where PHATE outperforms t‑SNE, UMAP, TriMap, etc.  
  - Corpus: Neighbor papers (e.g., arXiv 2510.01230 on Chinese character embeddings, arXiv 2510.09790 on geometric rotation) independently use manifold learning for semantic structure.
- **Break condition:** Excessive diffusion time (t) or unsuitable neighbor count (k) can oversmooth the space; if embeddings are not manifold-like (e.g., isotropic noise), PHATE offers no advantage.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The universal clustering‑branching pattern across English, Chinese, and German arises from two competing semantic forces: similarity‑based attraction (clustering) and derivational directionality (branching).
- **Mechanism:** During training, words with high co‑occurrence or semantic similarity are pulled into proximate regions (clusters). Morphologically derived forms inherit directional offsets from base words, creating branching trajectories. Their interplay yields a stable geometry: dense clusters for content words, extended branches for functional/derivational families.
- **Core assumption:** Embedding models encode both semantic similarity and morphological compositionality; these properties are reflected geometrically.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Abstract: “content words form clustering‑branching patterns across 20 semantic domains in English, Chinese, and German.”  
  - Section 4.2 (Figures 2‑3) demonstrates the pattern across three typologically diverse languages; Appendix A.2 validates Gärdenfors’ theoretical prediction.
  - Corpus: Related work on Chinese character embeddings (arXiv 2510.01230) observes analogous clustering‑branching for content words.
- **Break condition:** Models that fail to capture semantics (e.g., Gemma‑300M) lose clustering and branching; datasets lacking morphological variation diminish branching visibility.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Arabic numerals organize into spiral trajectories because the compositional density of the numeral system forces clustering and branching to operate simultaneously along an ordinal axis.
- **Mechanism:** Magnitude progression (1 → 10 → 100) imposes branching directionality. Numbers of the same order of magnitude (e.g., all ’20‑s’) form local clusters. The combination yields a spiral winding inward/outward as magnitude increases, preserving both local group coherence and global magnitude order. This violates the pure clustering assumption of distributional semantics.
- **Core assumption:** Numerical semantics are compositional (every digit combination yields a valid number) and ordinal (magnitude introduces a strong directional bias).
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Abstract: “Arabic numbers organize through spiral trajectories rather than clustering, violating standard distributional semantics assumptions.”  
  - Section 4.3 (Figure 4) contrasts the spiral of Arabic digits with clustering of mathematical terminology; text links pattern to compositional density.  
  - Corpus: Neighbor papers on geometric rotation (arXiv 2510.09790) and manifold‑constrained embeddings (arXiv 2505.00014) explore how relational structure shapes geometry.
- **Break condition:** If a model cannot encode magnitude relationships (e.g., numeric embeddings collapse), the spiral degenerates; numeral systems with low compositional productivity would not generate the same spiral.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: PHATE Manifold Learning
- **Why needed:** PHATE is the core analytical engine that transforms high‑dimensional embeddings into interpretable 2‑D/3‑D visualizations while retaining both local and global semantic structure. Without it, intricate patterns (clustering‑branching, spirals) would be obscured by traditional methods like t‑SNE.
- **Quick check:** How does PHATE’s use of diffusion distances differ from t‑SNE’s pairwise probabilities in handling global structure? (Answer: PHATE models transitions across the manifold via Markov chains, preserving relationships between distant but semantically linked points; t‑SNE focuses almost entirely on local neighborhoods.)

### Concept: Clustering‑Branching Pattern
- **Why needed:** This pattern is the signature geometric organization for content words across languages; recognizing it allows quick assessment of whether an embedding model has captured semantic and morphological relationships.
- **Quick check:** What embeddings cause a single word (e.g., “work”) to generate a branching structure? (Answer: Derivational affixes and compositional extensions create systematic directional offsets.)

### Concept: Spiral Organization
- **Why needed:** Spirals reveal a distinct organization for ordinal, compositionally dense concepts (like Arabic numerals). Identifying this pattern warns against relying solely on clustering metrics and informs model diagnostics for numeric reasoning.
- **Quick check:** Why don’t Arabic numerals form simple linear clusters along magnitude? (Answer: Because clustering by same‑order magnitude and branching by magnitude increase interact to produce a spiral, reflecting both proximity and direction.)

### Concept: Geometric Collapse
- **Why needed:** Collapse (e.g., of Chinese radicals) signals a model’s inability to distinguish structural from semantic elements—a critical failure mode for logographic systems. Detecting collapse guides model selection and training improvements.
- **Quick check:** What does it mean if purely structural radicals all map to a single point or a tight line? (Answer: The model fails to treat them as distinct non‑semantic components.)

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Data pipeline -> Embedding service -> Dimensionality reduction -> Visualization frontend -> Evaluation module

### Critical path
1. **Ingest** word lists for target language/level.
2. **Fetch embeddings** for each word using the chosen model and access venue.
3. **Preprocess** (normalize, optionally average sub‑word tokens).
4. **Apply PHATE** (fit on the embedding matrix, output 2‑D/3‑D coordinates).
5. **Render** interactive plot in Streamlit with domain‑based coloring.
6. **Compare** across models/methods by re‑running steps 2‑5 with different configurations.

### Design tradeoffs
- **PHATE parameters:** Small k emphasizes local neighborhoods; large k enhances global continuity. α decay controls smoothness; t governs diffusion steps – higher t gives a more global view but risks over‑smoothing.
- **Model access:** HuggingFace requires GPU resources but offers full control; OpenRouter is API‑based with latency and cost; Ollama enables local CPU/GPU without internet.
- **2D vs 3D:** 2D is faster and simpler for static reports; 3D (Plotly) allows rotation to reveal spiral depth but demands more compute and user interaction.
- **Speed vs fidelity:** For large vocabularies, subsampling or PCA pre‑reduction can speed up PHATE at the cost of minor detail loss.

### Failure signatures
- **Catastrophic collapse:** All points collapse to a single blob or line (observed for Gemma‑300M). Indicates fundamental representational failure.
- **Radical collapse:** Chinese structural radicals map to a tiny set of points, showing the model treats semantic and non‑semantic components identically.
- **Branching loss:** Derivational families (e.g., work → worker) appear as tight clusters instead of extended sequences; suggests weak morphological encoding.
- **Modality disconnect:** Emoji and their text translations occupy separate regions instead of overlapping, indicating poor multimodal alignment.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Radical collapse replication:** Load Chinese radical dataset, compute embeddings with Sentence‑BERT, visualize via PHATE. Confirm 2‑point compression; then remove purely structural radicals and show the emergence of a 3‑D manifold structure.
2. **Cross‑lingual clustering‑branching verification:** Use the core word‑v2 dataset for English, Chinese, German; generate PHATE plots with Qwen3‑0.6B. Verify that content words form dense clusters (e.g., animals, family) while derivational families display clear branching.
3. **Arabic numeral spiral test:** Input numeric tokens 0‑1000 plus mathematical terms; produce PHATE 2‑D plot. Confirm the spiral trajectory for the digits and contrast with the clustering of mathematical vocabulary. Optional: alter t parameter to demonstrate breakdown of the spiral.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Why do embedding models exhibit catastrophic geometric collapse for purely structural components (e.g., Chinese radicals) while maintaining semantic clustering for content words?
- **Basis:** [explicit] The paper identifies "sub-character geometric collapse" as a critical limitation (Section 5) and demonstrates it in Appendix A.1, where purely structural radicals lose all spatial structure.
- **Why unresolved:** The collapse is documented but its cause is unknown—whether due to training objectives that ignore structural information, data sparsity, or architectural biases toward semantic over form.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablation studies comparing models trained with/without explicit structural supervision (e.g., radical-level losses), along with layer-wise probing of radical representations, could isolate the failure mode.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Do universal clustering-branching patterns in word-level semantics generalize beyond Germanic and Sino-Tibetan families to other language families (e.g., Romance, Semitic, Bantu)?
- **Basis:** [explicit] The authors explicitly limit their claims in Section 6: "our analysis covers only two language families... broader claims require validation across additional families (Romance, Semitic, Bantu, Austronesian)."
- **Why unresolved:** Patterns were validated only on English, Chinese, and German with ~400 core words; cross-linguistic universals remain speculative without broader sampling.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply the PHATE-based Semanscope framework to large-scale parallel semantic datasets (e.g., WordNet equivalents) in 10+ phylogenetically diverse languages, testing for consistent clustering-branching signatures.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Why does geometric embedding quality not scale monotonically with model parameters, as seen when Qwen3-0.6B outperforms larger variants like Qwen3-4B despite fewer parameters?
- **Basis:** [explicit] Table 3 and Section 4.2 explicitly state "parameter count ≠ geometric quality" and document non-monotonic scaling across Qwen3 models.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper notes the phenomenon but does not investigate architectural or training factors (e.g., optimization, data mixtures) that could explain why intermediate-scale models achieve better geometric balance.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic comparison of models with matched architectures but different parameter counts, controlling for training data and objectives, combined with fine-grained analysis of gradient flow and representation entropy during training.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Can next-generation multilingual embedding architectures incorporate specialized components to handle distinct linguistic levels (structural, semantic, numerical) without the geometric degradations observed in current unified models?
- **Basis:** [explicit] The conclusion advocates for "specialized components for different linguistic levels rather than assuming single embedding spaces," but no implementation is provided.
- **Why unresolved:** Current models use a single embedding space; it is unknown whether modular designs (e.g., separate encoders for radicals, words, numerals) can be trained jointly and retain global coherence.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Prototype models with level-specific modules (e.g., radical feature extractors, numeral trajectory networks) evaluated via PHATE across all four linguistic levels, measuring geometric consistency and downstream task trade-offs.

## Limitations
- **Dataset opacity:** The exact lexicons for the 20 semantic domains, Chinese radicals, and morphological families are not public, hindering exact replication.
- **Parameter dependency:** PHATE's geometric patterns (especially branching/spirals) are highly sensitive to `t` (diffusion time); the paper's fixed parameters may not generalize to all embedding spaces.
- **Generalizability:** Claims of "universal" clustering-branching are based on only three languages (English, Chinese, German) and specific semantic domains; scalability to other language families or scripts is unverified.

## Confidence
- **PHATE superiority for semantic geometry:** High. Strong evidence from 12-method benchmark (Table 2, Figure 9) with clear qualitative advantage in preserving branching.
- **Clustering-branching as universal pattern:** Medium. Solid multi-language demonstration, but limited language sample and potential dataset bias.
- **Arabic numeral spiral as violation of clustering assumptions:** High. Visually striking pattern in Figure 4 with mechanistic explanation tied to compositional density; easily falsifiable.
- **Geometric collapse as diagnostic for radical handling:** Medium. Clear demonstration for Chinese, but not tested on other logographic systems (e.g., Japanese kanji).
