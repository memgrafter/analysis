# Chain-of-Look Spatial Reasoning for Dense Surgical Instrument Counting

## Quick Facts
- ArXiv ID: 2602.11024
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11024
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: CoLSR counts densely packed surgical instrument handles with MAE 0.88 and RMSE 1.27 on SurgCount-HD, reducing error by >68% vs prior best (REC MAE 2.82) and >95% vs LLMs (Qwen MAE 17.06); inference 0.28 s per image (>100× faster than manual 25.12 s).

## Executive Summary
Accurate counting of densely packed surgical instruments is critical for patient safety but extremely challenging due to severe visual clutter and occlusion. Existing object counting methods treat instances independently, ignoring the sequential spatial reasoning humans employ. We introduce Chain-of-Look Spatial Reasoning (CoLSR), a framework that explicitly models the visual scanning path humans follow. CoLSR generates a spatial visual chain via a CountGD-based generator and enforces chain coherence with a novel neighboring loss that penalizes deviations from ground-truth adjacent instrument distances. Class-specific learnable prompts further improve handle boundary precision. Evaluated on our new SurgCount-HD dataset (1,464 high-density images), CoLSR achieves state-of-the-art performance with MAE 0.88 and RMSE 1.27, reducing error by over 68% versus the best prior counting method (REC MAE 2.82) and by >95% versus multimodal LLMs like Qwen (MAE 17.06). Inference runs in 0.28 seconds per image, over 100× faster than manual counting (25.12 seconds).

## Method Summary
CoLSR extends CountGD, a detection-based counting architecture. The model uses a Swin-B image encoder and a BERT text encoder. A feature enhancer (six transformer blocks) fuses image and text features, enhanced by class-specific learnable (CSL) prompts—64 tokens prepended to the text tokens and initialized with a text string plus Gaussian noise. A cross-modality decoder (six blocks) produces queries, from which the top k=900 are selected. During training, Hungarian matching pairs predictions with ground-truth bounding boxes using a cost combining L1 center distance and classification score. The total loss is a weighted sum: λ_loc·L_loc (L1 distance), λ_neigh·L_neigh (neighboring loss), λ_cls·L_cls (focal loss) with λ_loc=10, λ_neigh=100, λ_cls=1. L_neigh orders matched centers along the dominant spatial axis (the axis with larger coordinate spread) and penalizes squared differences between consecutive predicted and ground-truth center distances. Training uses Adam (lr=1e-4, weight decay=1e-4, batch size=4) for 30 epochs, with learning rate reduced by factor 10 after epoch 10. At inference, only the image is fed forward. Predictions with confidence below σ=0.26 are discarded, and duplicate points are removed by sorting along the dominant orientation and discarding any neighbor within a distance threshold τ. The final count is the number of remaining detections.

## Key Results
- On the SurgCount-HD test set (228 images), CoLSR achieves MAE 0.88 and RMSE 1.27.
- This surpasses the previous best method REC (MAE 2.82) by >68% error reduction and outperforms multimodal LLMs (e.g., Qwen MAE 17.06) by >95%.
- Inference time is 0.28 seconds per image, more than 100 times faster than manual counting (25.12 seconds).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
CoLSR succeeds by explicitly modeling the sequential visual scanning that humans use when counting densely packed objects. The visual chain generator produces an ordered set of points, mimicking a look path through the scene. The neighboring loss enforces that distances between consecutive points match the ground-truth spatial relationships, encouraging spatially coherent arrangements and reducing double-counting and missed instances. Class-specific learnable prompts fine-tune the text encoder’s representation to better capture the appearance of circular instrument handles, improving localization accuracy. Together, these components overcome the clutter and occlusion challenges that hinder independent instance detectors.

## Foundational Learning
- **Dominant Orientation Axis**: Why needed: To define a consistent one-dimensional ordering for the visual chain, simplifying the neighboring loss computation. Quick check: For a sample batch, compute max−min along x and y; the axis with larger spread should be used for ordering.
- **Hungarian Matching**: Why needed: To establish one-to-one correspondences between predictions and ground truths, enabling the use of L1 and neighboring losses on matched pairs. Quick check: Ensure the matching cost (center L1 + classification) decreases during early training epochs.
- **Neighboring Loss**: Why needed: To enforce spatial coherence between consecutive objects in the visual chain, preventing erratic placements and duplicate detections in dense regions. Quick check: Plot distribution of adjacent center distances before and after training; the variance relative to ground truth should shrink.
- **Class-Specific Learnable (CSL) Prompts**: Why needed: To adapt the text encoder’s embeddings to the specific visual features of surgical handles, improving handle boundary precision. Quick check: Compare IoU of predicted boxes with and without CSL prompts on a validation set; CSL should yield higher IoU.
- **Query Selection (k=900)**: Why needed: To reduce computational load by focusing the decoder on the most relevant queries, while retaining enough capacity for high-density scenes. Quick check: Verify that the number of selected queries remains constant and that the selection is based on attention scores.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Input image I → Swin-B encoder → Feature map.  
Class-specific learnable tokens T_C (64) → BERT text encoder → Text features.  
Text features with prepended CSL tokens + image features → Feature Enhancer (6 blocks) → Fused features.  
Fused features → Cross-modality Decoder (6 blocks) → Decoder queries.  
Query Selection (top-k=900) → Selected queries.  
Prediction heads → Output: centers, classification scores.  
Training: Hungarian matching (based on L1 center + classification cost) → Matched pairs → Compute L_loc (L1), L_neigh (adjacent distance diff), L_cls (focal) → Weighted sum.  
Inference: Confidence threshold σ=0.26 → Duplicate removal (sort along dominant orientation, discard neighbors with distance < τ) → Final count.

**Critical path**:  
During inference, the critical path is image encoding → feature enhancement (with CSL prompts) → cross-modality decoding → query selection → point prediction → thresholding → duplicate removal. Each step must preserve spatial details; any degradation (e.g., loss of fine-grained features in encoder) directly impacts counting accuracy.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- The weight λ_neigh=100 heavily favors spatial coherence, which is beneficial for dense, roughly linear arrangements but may over-constrain scenes with multiple separated clusters, causing under-counting.  
- Ordering along a single dominant axis simplifies L_neigh but fails for complex 2D packings where instruments are not aligned.  
- CSL prompts introduce additional parameters that require sufficient training data; with limited data they may overfit to the training distribution.  
- Fixed thresholds σ=0.26 and τ (unspecified) are dataset-specific; changing image resolution or density may require retuning.

**Failure signatures**:  
- Over‑counting: many predictions clustered closely, especially at high‑density junctions, indicating duplicate removal τ is too low or L_neigh is not suppressing redundant points.  
- Under‑counting: missing entire groups of instruments in scenes with multiple clusters, suggesting the visual chain is forced to connect across gaps.  
- Poor localization: low IoU or high center L2 distance when CSL prompts are disabled or improperly initialized.  
- Training instability: loss spikes if λ_neigh is too large relative to L_loc, requiring careful weight tuning.

**Three first experiments**:  
1. **Ablation of loss components**: Train three models with (a) only L_loc, (b) L_loc+L_neigh, (c) full loss. Compare MAE to confirm the contribution of L_neigh. Expect full loss to perform significantly better.  
2. **CSL prompt effectiveness**: Remove CSL tokens from the feature enhancer and retrain with identical settings. Measure change in box IoU and center L2 distance on validation; a drop would validate the prompts’ role.  
3. **Duplicate‑removal threshold sensitivity**: On a fixed validation set, vary τ (e.g., 5–20 pixels) and plot precision‑recall. Identify the τ that yields the best F1; this also reveals whether the unreported value in the paper is near-optimal and how sensitive results are to this hyperparameter.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Multi‑view or depth integration**: How can multi‑view video sequences or depth maps be effectively integrated into the CoLSR framework to resolve severe occlusions that disrupt visual chain continuity? The current implementation processes only single 2D images without temporal or 3D structural information, limiting its ability to disambiguate heavily overlapping instruments. Evidence from a modified architecture that aggregates visual chains across multiple views or depth slices, validated on a new multi-view surgical dataset, would be needed.  
- **Non‑linear spatial arrangements**: Does the enforced linear visual chain with single‑axis neighboring loss become a liability for instrument arrangements exhibiting complex 2D spatial patterns or multiple disconnected clusters? The need for a divide‑and‑conquer inference strategy indicates the base model assumes a single continuous chain, which may not generalize to arbitrary layouts. Controlled experiments with synthetic datasets having known non‑linear optimal counting orders could resolve this.  
- **Cross‑domain generalization**: To what extent does the Chain‑of‑Look spatial reasoning paradigm generalize beyond surgical instruments to other dense object counting domains (e.g., crowds, cells, retail inventory) with different visual characteristics? SurgCount‑HD contains only one class (circular handles) with handles oriented toward the camera; class‑specific prompts are tuned for this pattern. Direct application of CoLSR to standard benchmarks from other fields, measuring performance relative to domain‑specific SOTA, would quantify transferability limits and necessary modifications.

## Limitations
- Exact initialization of CSL prompts (text string and Gaussian noise scale) is undisclosed, hindering exact replication.  
- The duplicate‑removal distance threshold τ in post‑processing is not provided; it is a critical hyperparameter that influences precision/recall trade‑off.  
- The visual chain may incorrectly connect points across separate dense clusters, requiring a heuristic divide‑and‑conquer strategy that is only briefly described.

## Confidence
- **Reported SOTA performance (MAE 0.88, RMSE 1.27)**: High (clear experimental design, large margin over baselines).  
- **Neighboring loss enforcing spatial coherence**: Medium (described mathematically but practical impact depends on correct ordering and matching, which require code verification).  
- **Generalization to other dense counting tasks**: Low (evaluation only on SurgCount‑HD; no cross‑domain validation).  
- **Exact replication using provided details**: Medium (most hyperparameters are specified, but missing τ and CSL string leave gaps).
