# Information Routing in Atomistic Foundation Models: How Task Alignment and Equivariance Shape Linear Disentanglement

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.03155
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03155
- Reference count: 17
- Primary result: Task alignment is the dominant factor determining linear disentanglement of geometric information in atomistic representations; equivariant channel routing and pretraining diversity offer secondary gains.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Compositional Probe Decomposition (CPD) to isolate geometric information in molecular representations by projecting out linear composition via ordinary least squares and probing the residual with linear models. Evaluated on QM9 across ten models, the authors find that models trained on geometry‑sensitive targets (HOMO‑LUMO gap) achieve high linear geometric accessibility (R²_geom up to 0.53), while those trained on energy (compositional) show much lower values (down to 0.08). For equivariant architectures with persistent irreducible representation channels (e.g., MACE), information routes by symmetry: scalar channels encode the gap, vector channels encode the dipole moment. Nonlinear probes are shown to be misleading, and a structural isomer benchmark confirms that geometric residuals truly capture geometry (94.6% accuracy) whereas composition components are at chance (52.5%). Pretraining on diverse data (MPTraj) partially compensates for misaligned objectives but does not surpass task alignment.

## Method Summary
CPD consists of three main steps:  
1. Extract mean‑pooled final‑layer representations X for a fixed set of 2 000 QM9 molecules from each model. Construct a composition feature matrix Z (element fractions plus standardized atom count).  
2. Perform 5‑fold cross‑validation; within each training fold, fit an OLS regressor β from Z to X, then compute the residual X_geom = X – Zβ to remove linearly predictable composition.  
3. Train a Ridge regressor with cross‑validation on X_geom,train to predict the target property; compute test R² to obtain R²_geom.  
The process is repeated over 30 seeds for stability. Additional validation includes isomer pairwise ordering accuracy, linear vs. nonlinear probe comparisons, and cross‑architecture ablations.

## Key Results
- Task‑aligned models (trained on HOMO‑LUMO gap) achieve R²_geom 0.44–0.53, while energy‑trained models drop by ~0.25 R² (e.g., PaiNN: 0.53 → 0.31; MACE: 0.44 → 0.08).
- In MACE, L=0 (scalar) channels encode HOMO‑LUMO gap (R²=0.76), L=1 (vector) channels encode dipole (R²=0.59); this symmetry‑based routing is absent in ViSNet.
- Nonlinear gradient‑boosted trees on residualized representations recover R² 0.68–0.95 for purely compositional targets (e.g., average atomic mass), demonstrating that linear probing is necessary for faithful disentanglement.
- Structural isomer validation: geometric residuals achieve 94.6% pairwise accuracy in ordering isomers by HOMO‑LUMO gap, whereas compositional components perform at chance (52.5%).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Three factors explain the observed gradient in linear geometric accessibility:

1. **Task alignment dominates** – The training objective imposes selective pressure: geometry‑sensitive targets force the model to encode structural distinctions in linearly accessible directions; composition‑dominated targets do not. This is solidified by within‑architecture ablations showing >0.25 R²_geom swings when swapping objectives.

2. **Equivariance‑based channel routing** – In architectures with persistent irreducible representation channels (e.g., MACE), symmetry tags (L) survive to the output. Optimisation aligns property symmetry with channel type: scalar losses populate L=0, vector losses populate L=1. This yields a clean linear decomposition without mixing.

3. **Data diversity compensation** – Pretraining on a broad chemical dataset (MPTraj) captures general geometric primitives, partially offsetting a misaligned downstream objective. MACE pretrained on MPTraj reaches R²_geom 0.36 despite QM9 energy training, outperforming models trained solely on QM9 energy. Nonetheless, task alignment remains the strongest lever.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept: Linear Probing for Representational Analysis**  
- Why needed: CPD’s core metric (R²_geom) relies on linear probes to measure the linearly accessible geometric signal after composition removal. Nonlinear probes can artificially inflate scores by exploiting residual nonlinearities.  
- Quick check: If a gradient‑boosted tree attains R²=0.90 on a residualized representation for a target perfectly projected out (average atomic mass), what does this imply?  
  Answer: The OLS projection left nonlinear compositional traces; the representation still contains compositional information that the nonlinear probe can exploit.

**Concept: Compositional Confound in Molecular Properties**  
- Why needed: Many molecular properties are strongly correlated with atomic composition (e.g., number of electrons). Without explicit removal, probing cannot separate geometry encoding from composition memorisation. CPD’s OLS projection controls this confound.  
- Quick check: Why would a model trained on total energy exhibit lower R²_geom for HOMO‑LUMO gap than one trained directly on HOMO‑LUMo gap, even with identical architectures?  
  Answer: Energy is largely determined by composition, so the representation may compress or ignore geometric variations that are irrelevant to the energy objective, leaving little linearly accessible geometry.

**Concept: Equivariant Representations and Irreducible Representations (irreps)**  
- Why needed: To interpret symmetry‑based routing, we must understand that rotations decompose representations into irreps (scalars L=0, vectors L=1). When an architecture preserves these tags as separate channels, optimisation can specialise them by property symmetry.  
- Quick check: In a model with distinct L=0 and L=1 channels, what would you expect if probing L=1 channels with a scalar property (HOMO‑LUMO gap)? Conversely, probing L=0 with a vector property (dipole)?  
  Answer: L=1 probe on scalar property should yield low R² (ideally near zero); L=0 probe on vector property should also yield low R², as the information resides in mismatched symmetry channels.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Frozen representation extraction → Composition feature construction → Fold‑wise OLS projection → Geometric residual → RidgeCV probing → R²_geom calculation → Validation (isomers, nonlinear control)

**Critical path:**  
1. Extract mean‑pooled final‑layer representations X for all molecules in the fixed probe set.  
2. For each 5‑fold split, fit OLS β on training Z (composition) and X; compute X_geom = X – Zβ for both train and test.  
3. Train RidgeCV (20 alphas, log‑spaced 10⁻³–10⁶, intercept) on X_geom,train; predict on X_geom,test; record R².  
4. Repeat across 30 random seeds; average R² to obtain R²_geom for each model.  
5. Validate: run structural isomer pairwise logistic regression on X_geom and X_comp; compare Ridge vs GBT on average atomic mass; check model rankings.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Fold‑wise vs global OLS:** Fold‑wise avoids leakage, gives slightly higher R²_geom; global is more aggressive but risks over‑removal. Rankings unchanged.  
- **Composition feature spec:** Raw counts, fractions, or binary presence alter absolute R² but preserve model ordering.  
- **Linear vs nonlinear probes:** Linear (Ridge) is necessary for faithful disentanglement; nonlinear (GBT) inflates scores by reconstructing projected‑out compositional signal.  
- **Probe target selection:** Properties with low compositional ceiling (e.g., HOMO‑LUMO gap) exhibit the widest performance gradient; those with high ceiling (e.g., polarizability) show compressed differences.

**Failure signatures:**  
- GBT probe on average atomic mass yields R²_geom > 0.5 → residual composition remains (likely due to global OLS or incorrect Z).  
- Isomer benchmark accuracy near chance for X_geom → CPD failed or model encodes no geometry.  
- Rankings flip across Z specifications → CPD instability, perhaps from high Z correlations or numerical errors.  
- LEACE and CPD rankings disagree → likely because the true composition subspace is nonlinear, though unlikely for low‑k Z in high‑d X.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Replicate CPD on a 2‑model subset** (e.g., PaiNN‑HL vs SchNet) for HOMO‑LUMO gap; confirm the expected ~0.25 R²_geom difference.  
2. **Isomer control** for these models: extract X_geom and X_comp on 50 isomer pairs; train logistic regression to predict which isomer has the higher gap; expect X_comp accuracy ≈50% and X_geom >80% for PaiNN, lower for SchNet.  
3. **GBT inflation check** on average atomic mass: compare Ridge (≈0) vs GBT (>0.7); a high GBT R² indicates residual composition or flawed projection.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Generalisation across domains** – Does the three‑factor explanation (task alignment, equivariance, data diversity) hold for molecular systems beyond QM9 and Materials Project (e.g., larger organics, surfaces, protein‑ligand complexes)? The current evaluation is limited to two datasets.  
2. **Invariant architecture on geometry‑sensitive tasks** – How would an invariant model like SchNet trained on HOMO‑LUMO gap perform in linear geometric accessibility relative to equivariant models? This would isolate the contribution of equivariance.  
3. **Theoretical lower bounds on R²_geom** – What is the minimum attainable linear accessibility for a given property? A formal theory linking gradient width, architecture, and data distribution to a lower bound is missing.  
4. **Scaling to full QM9** – Does the model ranking based on linear accessibility remain stable when using the entire QM9 dataset (∼134k molecules) instead of the 2 000‑molecule probe set? The authors note stability down to N=500 but not at larger scales.  
5. **Effect of fine‑tuning** – If models with low linear accessibility are fine‑tuned on a geometry‑related downstream task, does their geometric accessibility improve? A model might encode geometry nonlinearly, making it accessible only after adaptation (e.g., ANI‑2x).

## Limitations
- **Checkpoint accessibility** – The required ten pretrained checkpoints (e.g., MACE‑HL, MACE‑97ep) may not be publicly available; their lack forces retraining with incomplete hyperparameter specifications.  
- **Representation layer selection** – Optimal extraction layers are architecture‑dependent; the paper’s choices (e.g., `descriptors_final` for MACE) might not generalise (e.g., DimeNet++’s “dead” output blocks).  
- **CPD linearity assumption** – OLS projection assumes linear composition→representation mapping; if composition is encoded nonlinearly, residual composition may bias R²_geom upward despite isomer controls.

## Confidence
- Task alignment dominance: **High** (within‑architecture ablations show robust >0.25 R²_geom swaps).  
- Symmetry‑based routing in MACE: **Medium** (clear for MACE but not yet generalised to other tensor‑product architectures).  
- Data diversity compensation: **Medium‑Low** (confounded by pretraining task and dataset size; the pure diversity effect remains inferential).

## Next Checks
1. **Within‑architecture ablation replication** – Obtain or retrain PaiNN and MACE on both HOMO‑LUMO gap and total energy with identical hyperparameters; confirm the ~0.25 R²_geom gap.  
2. **Symmetry routing generalisation** – Test another tensor‑product equivariant model (e.g., NequIP) with separate L=0 and L=1 channels; after training on scalar and vector targets, verify channel specialisation via CPD.  
3. **Isomer benchmark expansion** – Extend the isomer pairwise evaluation to formulas containing halogens (Cl, Br) and metals to ascertain whether geometric disentanglement holds beyond the C/H/N/O/F subset.
