# Task--Specificity Score: Measuring How Much Instructions Really Matter for Supervision

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.03103
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03103
- **Reference count:** 10
- **Primary result:** Task-specificity score (TSS/TSS++) enables effective instruction data selection, yielding consistent downstream improvements especially under tight token budgets, often outperforming perplexity and IFD filters.

## Executive Summary
Instruction tuning datasets often contain weakly specified examples where the same output could satisfy multiple plausible instructions for a given input. This work introduces the Task–Specificity Score (TSS) to measure how uniquely an output is determined by its true instruction. TSS contrasts the output's log-likelihood under the true instruction with its average likelihood under alternative instructions generated from the input alone. TSS++ improves upon TSS by using a contrastive (InfoNCE-style) objective with hard negative alternatives and adding an explicit output quality term. Across three instruction datasets (Alpaca, Dolly-15k, NI-20) and three open LLMs (Gemma, Llama, Qwen), selecting high-specificity examples for training under strict token budgets (e.g., 5% or 15% retention) consistently improved downstream performance compared to random selection and frequently outperformed quality-based filters like perplexity (PPL) and instruction-following difficulty (IFD). At 5% retention, TSS++ was the best method in 6 of 9 model-dataset settings. Notably, for several model-dataset pairs, the optimal performance was achieved at an intermediate retention fraction (e.g., 45%), surpassing full-dataset fine-tuning (e.g., +7.9 SUM for Llama on Alpaca). Gains were distributed across all four evaluation benchmarks (ARC-C, MMLU, HellaSwag, TruthfulQA), indicating broad generalization improvement. The results demonstrate that task specificity is a complementary and effective signal for instruction data selection, particularly valuable in compute-constrained regimes.

## Method Summary
TSS approximates the conditional mutual information \(I(I;Y|X)\) to quantify how much the instruction constrains the output beyond the input. For each example \((I, X, Y)\), compute the log-likelihood \(s_{\text{true}} = \log p_\theta(Y|I,X)\). Generate \(K\) alternative instructions \(\{I_k\}\) from \(X\) alone (without \(Y\)) using a separate language model. Compute \(s_k = \log p_\theta(Y|I_k,X)\). TSS is defined as \(s_{\text{true}} - \log\left(\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K \exp(s_k)\right)\). TSS++ enhances this by (i) normalizing likelihoods by output length, (ii) constructing a candidate set \(C(X)\) of alternative instructions (generated or retrieved), selecting the \(m\) hardest negatives based on normalized likelihood, and computing an InfoNCE-style contrastive term \(\text{TSS}_{\text{NCE}} = \log \frac{\exp(s^+/\tau)}{\exp(s^+/\tau) + \sum_{i^- \in H} \exp(s^-/\tau)}\), and (iii) adding a scaled quality term \(\alpha \cdot s^+\). For data selection, either retain the top-\(\rho\) fraction of examples by score (filtering) or use importance weights \(w = \exp(\lambda \cdot \text{score})\) during fine-tuning. The curated subset (or weighted data) is then used to fine-tune the target model, which is evaluated on held‑out benchmarks.

## Key Results
- At 5% retention, TSS++ achieved the highest downstream SUM score in 6 out of 9 model-dataset combinations, frequently surpassing perplexity- and instruction-following-difficulty-based selection.
- For several model-dataset pairs, peak performance occurred at an intermediate retention fraction (e.g., 45%), even exceeding full‑dataset fine‑tuning (e.g., +7.9 SUM for Llama on Alpaca).
- Gains were observed across all four evaluation benchmarks (ARC‑C, MMLU, HellaSwag, TruthfulQA), indicating broad generalization improvement from specificity‑driven data selection.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
TSS selects examples where the target output is uniquely compatible with the true instruction, because such examples provide non-redundant supervision about how instructions constrain behavior. The score compares \(s_{\text{true}} = \log p_\theta(Y|I,X)\) to the average of \(s_k = \log p_\theta(Y|I_k,X)\) over \(K\) alternative instructions generated from \(X\). A high score means \(Y\) is far more probable under \(I\) than under plausible alternatives, i.e., the instruction meaningfully specifies the task. This approximates the conditional mutual information \(I(I;Y|X)\). The core assumption is that the alternative instructions sampled from \(p(\cdot|\text{Prompt}(X))\) form a reasonable local approximation to the distribution over other tasks that could be asked about \(X\). If the alternatives are implausible, off‑distribution, or stylistically mismatched, the denominator becomes a poor proxy for \(p(Y|X)\) and the score is misestimated.

### Mechanism 2
TSS++ improves robustness by replacing random alternatives with hard negatives (instructions that also yield high likelihood for \(Y\)) and adding an explicit output quality term, preventing degenerate filtering and ensuring retained examples are both specific and fluent. Instead of random alternatives, a candidate set \(C(X)\) (e.g., generated or retrieved instructions) is built and the top‑\(m\) hardest alternatives \(H(X)\) are selected based on length‑normalized log‑likelihood. A contrastive (InfoNCE‑style) score is computed: \(\text{TSS}_{\text{NCE}} = \log\frac{\exp(s^+/\tau)}{\exp(s^+/\tau) + \sum_{I^- \in H} \exp(s^-/\tau)}\). Then a scaled quality term \(\alpha \cdot s^+\) is added. This forces discrimination only against instructions that are *confusingly* compatible with \(Y\), while rewarding high raw likelihood. The core assumption is that the most informative negatives are those that are *too* compatible with the output, and a small injection of output quality prevents discarding examples that are specific but marginally lower‑fluency. If hard‑negative mining systematically misses ambiguous instructions or \(\alpha\) is mis‑specified, the combined score may over‑ or under‑emphasize specificity.

### Mechanism 3
Task‑specificity selection works because it reduces *supervision redundancy* within the instruction mixture. Many \((I,X,Y)\) triples are "weakly specified" – \(Y\) is a plausible response to multiple instructions about \(X\). Training on such examples teaches the model general response heuristics but does not strengthen instruction adherence. Filtering for high TSS removes these ambiguous cases, leaving a dataset where each example requires its specific instruction to recover \(Y\). This sharpens the model's sensitivity to instruction nuances. The core assumption is that the pretrained model's likelihood function \(p_\theta(Y|I,X)\) already captures baseline task‑agnostic response quality; the incremental value of an example for instruction tuning lies in how much the instruction adds constraint beyond what the input alone suggests. If evaluation benchmarks primarily test generic knowledge or reasoning not contingent on fine‑grained instruction following, removing weakly‑specified examples might not yield gains, or could even hurt if those examples cover important task distributions.

## Foundational Learning
- **Conditional Mutual Information \(I(I;Y|X)\)**: *Why needed*: TSS is presented as a Monte Carlo approximation to this information‑theoretic quantity, measuring how much the instruction reduces uncertainty about the output given the input. *Quick check*: Why is the true \(p(Y|X)\) intractable for LLMs, and how does averaging over alternative instructions provide a practical estimate?
- **Contrastive Learning (InfoNCE)**: *Why needed*: TSS++ adopts an InfoNCE‑style loss for its specificity term. Knowing that this framework learns by differentiating a positive (true instruction) from negatives (alternatives) explains why hard negatives are crucial and how temperature \(\tau\) controls discrimination sharpness. *Quick check*: What happens to the \(\text{TSS}_{\text{NCE}}\) score if the hardest negative has \(s(I^-,X,Y)\) very close to \(s(I,X,Y)\)? What if all negatives are much worse?
- **Importance Weighting in Stochastic Optimization**: *Why needed*: The paper uses \(w = \exp(\lambda\cdot\text{TSS})\) to weight examples instead of hard filtering. This requires understanding that weighted training can smooth the optimization landscape but may increase variance or require careful \(\lambda\) tuning to avoid unstable gradients. *Quick check*: Under what circumstances might exponential weighting with high \(\lambda\) cause training instability compared to top‑\(\rho\) filtering?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Raw Dataset -> Alternative Instruction Generator -> Scoring Model (frozen pθ) -> Score Calculator -> Data Curator -> Fine-tuner

### Critical Path
For each example: (1) Generate/retrieve alternative instructions from X alone → (2) Compute log‑likelihoods of Y under true and alternative instructions using the scoring model → (3) Calculate TSS or TSS++ score → (4) Rank all examples → (5) Select top‑ρ subset or compute importance weights → (6) Fine‑tune target model on curated data → (7) Evaluate on benchmarks.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Number of alternatives \(K\)**: More alternatives improve \(p(Y|X)\) estimation but linearly increase scoring compute. Paper uses \(K=4\).
- **Hard‑negative mining**: Better discrimination but requires building/maintaining a candidate corpus \(C(X)\) and extra ranking passes. TSS++ trades compute for robustness against easy negatives.
- **Filtering vs. weighting**: Filtering is simpler and deterministic at a given \(\rho\); weighting uses full data but introduces hyperparameter \(\lambda\) and may require gradient clipping. Both can be competitive depending on budget/dataset.
- **Scoring model choice**: Using a larger, more capable model for \(p_\theta\) may yield better alternative instructions and likelihood estimates, but increases scoring cost. Paper uses 12B instruct variants for scoring 1B targets.

### Failure Signatures
- **Poor downstream gain despite high average TSS**: Likely due to low‑quality alternative instructions (e.g., repetitive, generic) making denominator artificially low → inflated scores.
- **High variance in wins across (model, dataset) pairs**: Suggests sensitivity to the scoring model's biases or to domain match between scoring model and target fine‑tuning distribution.
- **Degenerate weighting (e.g., NaN gradients)**: Typically from extreme \(\lambda\) causing saturated \(\exp(\lambda\cdot\text{TSS})\) values; mitigated by clipping weights or using smaller \(\lambda\).
- **No sweet spot (full data always best)**: May indicate the dataset has minimal weakly‑specified examples, or that evaluation benchmarks are insensitive to instruction specificity.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Correlation sanity check**: Compute TSS for a small manually annotated subset where human judges rate "how uniquely the output fits the instruction." Verify that TSS correlates with human specificity ratings (expected: moderate positive correlation). This validates the core assumption that the score captures perceived task specificity.
2. **Ablation of hard negatives**: Compare TSS++ (with hard negatives from \(C(X)\)) vs. TSS++ with only random alternatives vs. vanilla TSS. Hypothesis: Hard‑negative variant should show more stable wins across budgets, especially at mid‑range \(\rho\) where easy negatives might otherwise inflate scores.
3. **Filtering vs. weighting head‑to‑head**: At fixed small budgets (5%, 15%), train with (a) top‑\(\rho\) filtered subset by TSS++ and (b) full dataset with weights \(w = \exp(\lambda\cdot\text{TSS}++)\) where \(\lambda\) is tuned to match average weight of filtered set. Compare SUM. This isolates whether the observed gains come from subset selection or from the weighting distribution itself.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How stable and transferable are TSS/TSS++ scores across different scoring models and architectures?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors note in Limitations (Section 6): "TSS and TSS++ are model‑dependent: scores are computed using a frozen LM, so the ranking can change with the choice of scoring model, its calibration, and its prompt/template." They evaluated across three models but do not claim invariance.
- **Why unresolved:** If TSS rankings are highly sensitive to the scoring model, its utility for practical data selection (where a fixed "scoring model" is used to curate data for a different "target model") becomes questionable. It is unclear whether a scoring model from one family (e.g., Gemma) provides a robust ranking for fine‑tuning a different architecture (e.g., Qwen), or whether model‑specific tuning of the scoring pipeline is required.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A systematic study measuring rank correlation (e.g., Spearman's \(\rho\)) between TSS scores computed using a panel of diverse scoring models (different sizes, architectures, instruction‑tuned vs. base) on the same dataset. Follow‑up experiments would then test if using a "mismatched" scoring model (e.g., scoring with Llama for a Gemma target) degrades downstream performance compared to a "matched" setup.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What is the optimal design for generating the set of alternative instructions \(\{I_k\}\) to ensure TSS accurately reflects task specificity without being gamed by easy or implausible negatives?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors state in Limitations: "TSS relies on generating or retrieving plausible alternative instructions. If the alternative set is low‑quality... TSS may overestimate specificity; conversely, if alternatives are too strong or off‑distribution, it may underestimate specificity." TSS++ attempts to mitigate this with hard negatives via a candidate dataset \(C(X)\), but the pipeline's components (generation prompt, retrieval corpus, embedding model) are hyperparameters not fully analyzed.
- **Why unresolved:** The core operationalization of "plausible alternative instructions" is underspecified. The quality and diversity of the alternative set directly determine the denominator in the TSS formula. There is no principled guidance on how many alternatives are needed, what diversity means in this space, or whether generated negatives introduce subtle biases (e.g., favoring certain phrasings or task types). The "hard negative" mining in TSS++ adds complexity without a clear ablation of its design choices.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablation studies varying: (i) the source of alternatives (e.g., LLM‑generated vs. retrieved from a large corpus of human‑written instructions vs. a mixture), (ii) the number \(K\) of alternatives, (iii) the diversity metric used to select "hard" negatives (e.g., likelihood vs. embedding distance vs. task taxonomy overlap). The effect on downstream performance, correlation with human judgments of specificity, and robustness to different input domains would be measured.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Why does discarding a substantial fraction of instruction data (e.g., 45–55%) sometimes improve performance over full‑dataset fine‑tuning, and what does this reveal about the nature of "low-specificity" examples?
- **Basis in paper:** Results show multiple cases where the peak SUM occurs at an intermediate retention fraction \(\rho < 1.0\) (e.g., Llama on Alpaca peaks at \(\rho=0.45\), +7.9 SUM over full data). The authors note this indicates "a substantial fraction of redundant or low‑utility instances." However, the precise mechanism is not investigated.
- **Why unresolved:** Is the benefit due to (a) reducing noise from examples where the instruction is meaningless (purely random output‑inp pairs), (b) shifting the task distribution to focus on more "core" or well‑specified tasks, (c) reducing memorization/overfitting on repetitive patterns, or (d) a combination? Understanding this is crucial for interpreting what TSS is actually selecting for and whether the "low‑specificity" examples are actively harmful or just uninformative.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A fine‑grained analysis of the examples *removed* at the optimal \(\rho\) vs. the full dataset. Metrics could include: diversity of task types (via automatic task classifiers or embeddings), output length/entropy distributions, perplexity under a held‑out model, and human evaluation for instruction‑answer coherence. Training dynamics (loss curves, gradient norms) could be compared between full and optimal‑subset training to see if the subset leads to smoother optimization or better generalization.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What is the computational vs. performance trade‑off curve for TSS/TSS++ scoring, and can approximate scoring (e.g., using a smaller model, cached embeddings, fewer alternatives) maintain most of the benefit at significantly reduced cost?
- **Basis in paper:** In Limitations: "scoring is computationally non‑trivial: computing per‑example likelihoods for K alternatives... can be expensive for large corpora... This cost may limit applicability in very large‑scale instruction pools without additional approximation." The paper uses a 1B model for scoring but does not analyze cost‑benefit or propose approximations.
- **Why unresolved:** For TSS to be practical for massive datasets (millions of examples), the scoring overhead must be manageable. The paper does not report scoring time or explore approximations (e.g., scoring with a 100M model, using embeddings for alternative retrieval instead of generation, reducing \(K\), or using a single‑pass "summary" model). It is unknown how much performance degrades with cheaper scoring.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Benchmark the wall‑clock time and monetary cost (in GPU hours) of computing TSS/TSS++ scores at scale for a large dataset (e.g., >1M examples) using the proposed pipeline. Then conduct a systematic study of approximation strategies (e.g., smaller scoring models, fewer alternatives, different hard‑negative mining budgets) and measure the correlation between approximate and "full" TSS rankings, and crucially, the downstream performance when using the approximate scores for filtering.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Does TSS selection inadvertently amplify biases (e.g., stylistic, demographic, topic) present in the scoring model or the alternative generation process, and how can this be audited and mitigated?
- **Basis in paper:** In Ethical Considerations: "Because our approach can amplify certain subsets of data, it may also amplify biases present in the underlying instruction pools or the scoring model. For example, if the frozen LM assigns systematically higher likelihood to particular dialects, writing styles, or demographic topics, TSS‑based selection could disproportionately retain such examples."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper provides no analysis of the demographic or stylistic distribution of examples selected by TSS vs. other methods (PPL, Random). Since TSS relies on a model's likelihood judgments, and LLMs are known to have biases, TSS could systematically favor outputs and instructions that align with the scoring model's "preferred" (majority, high‑resource) styles, potentially reducing diversity and fairness in the fine‑tuned model.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Measure distributional shifts in selected data: analyze selected examples by (i) topic (using topic models), (ii) dialect/formality (using linguistic property taggers), (iii) demographic proxies (e.g., named entities, gendered language). Compare distributions between TSS‑selected subsets and Random/PPL baselines. Then, evaluate the fine‑tuned models on benchmarks designed to probe bias (e.g., BBQ, HellaSwag for stereotyping) to see if TSS selection leads to increased bias.

### Open Question 6
- **Question:** Can TSS be meaningfully extended or adapted to non‑instruction‑tuning settings (e.g., standard supervised fine‑tuning on single‑task data, preference learning, or multimodal tasks)?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper frames everything around instruction tuning, where the core concern is whether the instruction uniquely determines the output. In standard supervised learning (e.g., image classification), the "instruction" is often implicit or fixed (the label set). The paper's motivation and method assume a space of multiple plausible "instructions" for the same input, which may not exist in single‑task datasets. For preference learning (DPO), the supervision is a relative preference, not an instruction‑output pair.
- **Why unresolved:** The conceptual foundation of TSS—contrasting the true instruction with alternatives—relies on a multi‑task, instruction‑oriented data structure. It is unclear whether this signal is relevant or computable in other paradigms. For instance, in multimodal tasks, what constitutes an "alternative instruction" for a given image‑text pair? Could the idea be repurposed to measure output specificity relative to *different captions* or *different questions* about the same image?
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Attempt to define analogous "specificity" scores for other data types: (i) For single‑task classification: generate "counterfactual labels" (e.g., other classes) and measure how uniquely the true label is specified by the input. (ii) For preference data: compare the likelihood of the chosen response under the "chosen" prompt vs. alternative "rejected" or neutral prompts. (iii) For multimodal: use a vision‑language model to generate alternative questions about an image and score. Then, evaluate if such scores are useful for data selection in those respective training regimes.

### Open Question 7
- **Question:** What is the theoretical relationship between TSS and other data quality/importance metrics (e.g., perplexity, IFD, gradient‑based influence), and can they be formally unified or combined optimally?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper positions TSS as complementary to quality‑based signals (PPL, IFD) and shows it often wins in low‑budget regimes while they catch up at higher budgets. However, there is no theoretical analysis of the connections. TSS approximates \(I(I;Y|X)\), PPL relates to \(\log p(Y|I,X)\) but not the counterfactual, and IFD is \(\log p(Y|I,X) - \log p(Y|X)\) (approximating \(I(Y;I|X)\) via a proxy). The paper's InfoNCE‑style TSS++ also relates to contrastive learning objectives.
- **Why unresolved:** Without a formal understanding of how these metrics correlate or diverge empirically and theoretically, practitioners are left with heuristics ("use TSS for low budget, PPL for high budget") without knowing if a simple linear combination could dominate all. The "winner map" (Figure 2) shows different methods dominate in different regions, suggesting non‑linear interactions.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Compute all scores (TSS, TSS++, PPL, IFD, gradient norm, etc.) on multiple datasets and model pairs. Perform correlation analysis (pairwise scatterplots, PCA) to see if they occupy orthogonal dimensions. Then, train a simple meta‑model (e.g., linear regression, small network) to predict downstream performance from the suite of scores, identifying the optimal combination. Test if the combined score consistently outperforms any single heuristic across budgets and datasets.

### Open Question 8
- **Question:** Does the observed benefit of TSS selection generalize to larger models (e.g., 7B, 70B parameters) and to more diverse or challenging instruction datasets (e.g., synthetic data, multi‑turn dialogues, tool‑use instructions)?
- **Basis in paper:** In Limitations: "our experiments focus on three instruction pools (Alpaca, Dolly‑15K, NI‑20), three small open models, and four downstream benchmarks. The conclusions may not fully transfer to larger models, other domains (e.g., multimodal or tool‑use data), or alternative evaluation suites."
- **Why unresolved:** All experiments are on 1B‑parameter models. The nature of the "weakly specified" examples might change with scale: larger models might be more robust to noise or might require more specificity to learn. Datasets like Alpaca are relatively clean and short; it is unknown how TSS performs on noisier, longer, or more complex instruction data (e.g., ShareGPT multi‑turn dialogues). The evaluation is on standard academic benchmarks; gains might not hold for open‑ended generation or agent tasks.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicate the core budgeted selection experiments with at least one larger model family (e.g., Llama 3.2 3B/8B, Qwen 2.5 7B) and on at least one additional dataset type (e.g., OpenAssistant, ToolBench, synthetic data from GPT‑4). Evaluate on a broader set of downstream tasks, including generative benchmarks (e.g., MT‑Bench) and practical skill evaluations (e.g., coding, agent tasks). This would test the scalability and domain robustness of the TSS signal.

## Limitations
- Alternative instruction generation quality/diversity is critical but poorly documented; degenerate alternatives invalidate TSS.
- Hard‑negative mining details (candidate set \(C(X)\), selection size \(m\)) are vague, hindering reproducibility.
- Training specifics (epochs, LR, batch size, optimizer, possible LoRA) are missing, affecting result fidelity.

## Confidence
- **TSS approximates conditional mutual information**: Medium
- **TSS++ robustness via hard negatives**: Medium
- **Downstream gains from specificity selection**: High
- **Broad generalization**: High

## Next Checks
1. **Human correlation check**: Sample a diverse subset, collect human specificity ratings (how uniquely output fits instruction), compute correlation with TSS/TSS++ scores.
2. **Hard‑negative ablation**: Vary candidate set \(C(X)\) (generated vs. retrieved) and \(m\) (number of hard negatives) to measure sensitivity of TSS++'s downstream performance.
3. **Filtering vs. weighting comparison**: At fixed small budget (e.g., 5%), compare top‑\(\rho\) filtering by TSS++ to full‑data training with importance weights \(w = \exp(\lambda\cdot\text{score})\). Tune \(\lambda\) so the effective weight sum matches the filtered set size.
