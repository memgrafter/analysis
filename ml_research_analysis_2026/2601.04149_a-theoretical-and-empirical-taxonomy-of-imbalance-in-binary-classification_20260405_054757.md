# A Theoretical and Empirical Taxonomy of Imbalance in Binary Classification

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.04149
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04149
- Reference count: 9
- Primary result: Proposes a model-agnostic triplet (η, κ, Δ) characterizing imbalance effects with a four-regime taxonomy validated empirically.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces a unified theoretical framework for class imbalance in binary classification, defining three fundamental scales: the imbalance ratio η, the sample-dimension ratio κ, and the intrinsic separability Δ. Under a Gaussian Bayes classifier with shared covariance, these parameters yield closed-form Bayes errors and reveal that imbalance shifts the discriminant boundary by log(η). This shift defines a deterioration slope that categorizes imbalance into four regimes (Normal, Mild, Extreme, Catastrophic) based on sensitivity. Controlled experiments on a high-dimensional genomic dataset, varying only η while holding κ and Δ constant, confirm theoretical predictions: minority Recall collapses when log(η) exceeds Δ√κ, Precision rises asymmetrically, and F1-score and PR-AUC degrade according to regime transitions. The alignment of empirical degradation across diverse parametric and non-parametric models demonstrates that the triplet offers a model-agnostic, geometric account of imbalance.

## Method Summary
The authors validate the triplet framework through controlled experiments on a balanced high-dimensional genomic dataset. They fix the sample-dimension ratio κ (by maintaining a constant total training size n and feature dimension p) and the separability Δ (by using the same feature set and class distributions), then vary only the imbalance ratio η by subsampling the minority class. For each η, multiple classifiers (logistic regression, LDA, QDA, Naive Bayes, random forest, k-NN, SVM-RBF, XGBoost) are trained on the resampled training sets and evaluated on a fixed balanced test set. Metrics such as minority Recall, Precision, F1-score, and PR-AUC are recorded and compared against theoretical curves derived from the Gaussian Bayes model. The collapse point for Recall and the deterioration slope for each model are computed to assess alignment with the predicted regime boundaries and the slope ratio ρ_m.

## Key Results
- The deterioration slope S(η) is convex and bell-shaped, leading to a four-regime taxonomy (Normal, Mild, Extreme, Catastrophic) with thresholds based on its magnitude relative to the peak and a catastrophic collapse threshold η_max = exp(Δ²κ/2).
- Minority Recall collapses precisely when log(η) > Δ√κ, while Precision increases asymmetrically as the classifier becomes conservative toward minority predictions; F1-score and PR-AUC decline sharply across regime transitions.
- Empirical degradation patterns across a wide range of classifiers closely follow theoretical predictions, confirming the model-agnostic nature of the triplet framework; parametric models degrade earlier than non-parametric ones, captured by the slope ratio ρ_m.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The triplet (η, κ, Δ) orthogonally governs Bayes risk and imbalance degradation, providing a complete theoretical characterization.
- **Mechanism:** In the Gaussian Bayes model with common covariance, the decision boundary shifts by log(η), and high-dimensional effective separation becomes Δ√κ, yielding closed-form Bayes errors e₀(η, κ, Δ) and e₁(η, κ, Δ) that depend solely on these parameters.
- **Core assumption:** Class-conditional distributions are Gaussian with shared covariance; the asymptotic high-dimensional regime where κ = n/p is small and determines estimation noise.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract introduces the triplet as fundamental scales; section 3.2 defines η, κ, Δ and asserts their orthogonality; section 4.2 derives explicit Bayes error expressions; the corpus lacks direct validation of this specific triplet.
- **Break condition:** If class covariances are heteroscedastic or distributions deviate severely from Gaussian, the closed-form errors may not capture true Bayes risk; κ alone may not fully describe dimensionality effects in non-asymptotic regimes.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The deterioration slope S(η, κ, Δ) = ∂R*/∂logη defines four distinct imbalance regimes based on its magnitude relative to the peak and the catastrophic threshold η_max.
- **Mechanism:** Bayes risk R* is convex in log(η) due to Gaussian CDFs, making S(η) bell-shaped; the catastrophic collapse occurs when η > η_max = exp(Δ²κ/2), where the minority posterior is always below 0.5.
- **Core assumption:** Convexity of R* in log(η) holds for Gaussian class-conditional models; the slope captures instantaneous sensitivity to imbalance.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 4.3 defines the slope, proves convexity (Theorem 1), and introduces regime taxonomy; Theorem 2 derives η_max; Figure 2 visualizes S(η) across κ and Δ.
- **Break condition:** For non-convex risk landscapes (e.g., non-Gaussian overlaps or complex models), S(η) may not be bell-shaped, blurring regime boundaries; η_max may not indicate complete collapse if decision boundaries are non-linear.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Practical classifiers, regardless of parametric or non-parametric family, exhibit empirical degradation patterns that align with theoretical predictions when only η varies, confirming the model-agnostic nature of the triplet framework.
- **Mechanism:** By holding κ and Δ constant in controlled experiments, minority Recall collapses when log(η) exceeds Δ√κ, Precision increases asymmetrically due to conservative minority predictions, and metrics degrade according to regime transitions.
- **Core assumption:** The genomic dataset maintains fixed κ and Δ across η variations; classifiers approximate the Bayes discriminant behavior, with deviations captured by slope ratio ρ_m.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract states empirical degradation "closely follows theoretical predictions"; section 5 presents experiments showing Recall collapse near log(η) > Δ√κ, asymmetric Precision, and model-dependent slopes; Figures 5–8 illustrate metric trends.
- **Break condition:** If κ or Δ inadvertently vary with η (e.g., due to subsampling affecting n or feature distribution), or if models have extreme inductive biases (e.g., deep nets with overparameterization), alignment may weaken; the theory assumes asymptotic high-κ, which may not hold in low-κ settings.

## Foundational Learning
- **Bayes optimal classifier**: Provides the algorithm-independent baseline for minimum risk; all theoretical degradation is measured relative to it. Quick check: Under Gaussian class conditionals with equal covariance, how does the Bayes decision boundary shift when the class prior ratio η increases?
- **Mahalanobis distance (Δ)**: Quantifies intrinsic class separability scaled by covariance; directly determines effective margin Δ√κ and Bayes error. Quick check: In the Gaussian model, what does Δ² represent, and how does it affect the overlap between class distributions?
- **Sample-dimension ratio (κ)**: Captures high-dimensional geometry; κ rescales Δ to effective margin Δ√κ, governing estimation stability and regime thresholds. Quick check: Why is κ critical in high-dimensional classification, and what classifier behavior is expected when κ < 1?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Inputs: η (class prior ratio), κ (n/p), Δ (Mahalanobis distance) -> Theoretical outputs: Bayes risk R*, deterioration slope S(η), regime thresholds (τ₁, τ₂, η_max). Empirical outputs: model metrics (Recall, Precision, F1, PR-AUC), slope ratio ρ_m.
- **Critical path**: 1. Estimate η from training class frequencies. 2. Compute κ = n/p using training sample size and feature dimension. 3. Estimate Δ from class means and pooled covariance. 4. Calculate effective margin Δ√κ. 5. Compare log(η) to Δ√κ to predict regime entry (e.g., if log(η) > Δ√κ, expect Recall collapse). 6. For a given model, fit on imbalanced data, compute empirical slope S_m, and compare to theoretical S* via ρ_m.
- **Design tradeoffs**: Gaussian assumption simplifies analysis but may not hold for non-elliptical data; using closed-form Bayes errors requires true parameters, but in practice they are estimated, adding variance; regime thresholds τ₁, τ₂ are empirically set but could be application-specific; η_max is exact only under model assumptions.
- **Failure signatures**: κ too small (e.g., n < p) causes η_max to be tiny, triggering premature catastrophic collapse in theory; if Δ is overestimated (e.g., from noisy estimates), predicted regimes may be overly optimistic; model-agnostic claim fails if classifier has built-in imbalance handling (e.g., weighted loss) that alters slope independently of triplet.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Controlled imbalance sweep**: On a balanced high-dimensional dataset with known Δ and κ, subsample minority to create η ∈ {1,2,…,100}, plot minority Recall vs. log(η), and verify collapse near log(η) ≈ Δ√κ.
  2. **Regime transition validation**: For fixed (κ, Δ), compute theoretical S(η) and identify peak S_max; label regimes using τ₁=0.1S_max, τ₂=0.5S_max, then check if empirical F1-score transitions align with these log(η) thresholds.
  3. **Model-agnostic slope comparison**: Fit parametric (LDA) and non-parametric (Random Forest) models on same imbalanced data; compute empirical slope S_m from metric curves; calculate ρ_m = S_m / S*; verify ρ_m < 1 for non-parametric models indicating slower deterioration.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How can κ and Δ be reliably estimated from real-world high-dimensional data where the true distributions are unknown or non-Gaussian?**
   - Basis: The conclusion states future work will explore estimation of (κ,Δ) in complex data domains.
   - Why unresolved: The theoretical framework depends on these parameters to predict imbalance regimes, but no estimation methods are provided for settings beyond the ideal Gaussian case, limiting practical applicability.
   - Evidence needed: Development and validation of robust estimators for κ and Δ that perform well under distributional misspecification, tested via simulations with diverse data-generating processes and real datasets from various domains.

2. **To what extent does the proposed imbalance regime taxonomy remain valid when the homoscedastic Gaussian assumption is relaxed?**
   - Basis: The conclusion identifies "relax Gaussian assumptions" as a key direction for future work.
   - Why unresolved: All closed-form Bayes errors and regime thresholds are derived under Gaussian class-conditional densities with equal covariance; non-Gaussian or heteroscedastic data may alter the geometric relationships and deterioration dynamics.
   - Evidence needed: Theoretical generalization of the Bayes risk expressions to broader distribution families (e.g., elliptical or heavy-tailed) and empirical studies showing whether the critical condition log(η) > Δ√κ still predicts regime transitions across varied covariance and distributional settings.

3. **Can the triplet (η, κ, Δ) framework be extended to multi-class imbalanced classification, and what would be the analogous regime taxonomy?**
   - Basis: The paper exclusively addresses binary classification; multi-class imbalance, common in practice, is not considered.
   - Why unresolved: The deterioration slope and regime definitions rely on binary posterior shifts and discriminant boundaries. Multi-class scenarios involve multiple imbalances and interdependent decision boundaries, potentially requiring new fundamental scales or different geometric interpretations.
   - Evidence needed: Derivation of multi-class Bayes risk under generalized triplet parameters, identification of class-specific deterioration metrics, and empirical validation on multi-class imbalanced datasets to define and test new regime thresholds.

4. **What inherent properties of machine learning models (e.g., linearity, kernel choice, ensemble diversity) systematically determine their deterioration slope ratio ρ_m relative to the Bayes optimal slope?**
   - Basis: The paper introduces ρ_m as a robustness measure but does not analyze its theoretical determinants beyond empirical observations that parametric models degrade faster than non-parametric ones.
   - Why unresolved: Without a theoretical understanding of ρ_m, model selection or design for imbalance-prone tasks remains heuristic; linking model characteristics to ρ_m could enable principled choices.
   - Evidence needed: Theoretical analysis deriving bounds on ρ_m for specific model families based on approximation theory or optimization dynamics, coupled with systematic experiments varying model hyperparameters to isolate factors affecting ρ_m across diverse classifiers.

## Limitations
- The closed-form Bayes errors and regime thresholds rely on the homoscedastic Gaussian assumption, which may not hold for real-world data such as genomics.
- The theoretical analysis assumes an asymptotic high-dimensional regime (κ ≪ 1), whereas many applications have moderate κ, limiting direct applicability.
- Empirical validation is confined to a single genomic dataset; generalizability across diverse domains and classifiers is not demonstrated.

## Confidence
- Triplet completeness (η, κ, Δ govern Bayes risk): High
- Deterioration slope taxonomy (four regimes): Medium
- Model-agnostic empirical alignment: Medium-Low
