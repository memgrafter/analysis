# Separable neural architectures as a primitive for unified predictive and generative intelligence

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.12244  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12244  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Introduces the separable neural architecture (SNA) that factorizes high-dimensional mappings into low‑arity learnable components, achieving 94–98% parameter reduction and state‑of‑the‑art accuracy across aerodynamic prediction, generative material design, and chaotic turbulence modeling, while enabling unified predictive‑generative capabilities.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces the separable neural architecture (SNA), a novel neural primitive that constructs high‑dimensional functions from low‑arity atoms governed by a low‑rank interaction tensor. This factorization yields extreme parameter efficiency and smooth, neighbor‑preserving embeddings. SNA serves as a unified component: a standalone predictor‑generator (KHRONOS), a variational trial space for PDEs (VSNA), or a module within larger systems (SPAN, Janus, Leviathan). Results demonstrate dramatic compression (94–98% fewer parameters) with state‑of‑the‑art accuracy on multi‑fidelity aerodynamics, generative inversion of thermal histories to material properties, and long‑horizon chaotic flow modeling that avoids nonphysical drift. Theoretical guarantees for the variational form and universal approximation for the function classes underpin the approach.

## Method Summary
Separable neural architectures define a function class where a mapping \(f(x)\) is expressed as a sum over low‑arity interaction terms: \(f(x) = \sum_{j=1}^{r} C_j\bigl(\phi_{S_j}(x)\bigr)\). Here \(\phi_{S_j}\) are atom functions acting on subsets of coordinates, and the interaction tensor (or its factorised representation) controls the rank \(r\) and the interaction order \(k\). This structure imposes a strong inductive bias toward latent factorizability, drastically reducing parameters compared to dense layers while retaining universal approximation under suitable conditions. Instantiations include: KHRONOS (stand‑alone predictor‑generator), VSNA (variational solver for PDEs via Galerkin projection), and modular integrations into SPAN (autonomous control), Janus (generative material design), and Leviathan (distributional sequence modeling of turbulence). Training employs alternating least squares (ALS) for tensor decomposition or gradient‑based optimisation when embedded in larger networks.

## Key Results
- KHRONOS matches SOTA on multi‑fidelity aerodynamic prediction with 94–98% fewer parameters and inverts thermal histories to mechanical properties (\(R^2\) 0.76/0.70) in milliseconds.  
- Janus generates multiscale metamaterials with <3.5% local stiffness error and 0.7% global compliance error via MAP optimisation in a smooth latent space.  
- Leviathan models turbulent flow as a distributional sequence, preserving inertial‑range statistics (enstrophy, spectral slope, vorticity PDFs) over 20‑step rollouts without catastrophic drift, outperforming deterministic operators.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** SNAs achieve extreme parameter efficiency by factorising high‑dimensional mappings into low‑arity, learnable components (atoms) whose interactions are governed by a low‑rank tensor.  
- **Mechanism:** An interaction tensor with controllable rank \(r\) and interaction order \(k\) selects and combines univariate or low‑arity sub‑atoms. This structure imposes a strong inductive bias that exploits latent factorisable structure without forfeiting expressivity (density in \(C([0,1]^d)\)).  
- **Core assumption:** The target system's input‑output relationship is either inherently separable or can be expressed in a separable coordinate system.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract]: "constructs high‑dimensional mappings from low‑arity learnable components (atoms) governed by an interaction tensor with controllable rank and interaction order."  
  - [PAGE 3, Eq 1]: Formal CP‑class definition with product of univariate sub‑atoms.  
  - [PAGE 19‑20, Theorem 1 & Corollary 2]: Proof of universal approximation for CP and TD classes.  
- **Break condition:** If the true underlying mapping requires high‑order interactions that cannot be efficiently captured by the chosen rank \(r\) and order \(k\), or if a separable coordinate representation is not discoverable.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** SNAs enable neighbour‑preserving continuous embeddings, which is critical for modelling physical states and mitigating nonphysical drift in chaotic systems.  
- **Mechanism:** By using spline‑based or other smooth basis functions per coordinate, the SNA's latent space topology directly reflects adjacency in the original physical state space. Adjacent physical inputs map to adjacent points in the learned representation manifold.  
- **Core assumption:** Smoothness of the underlying physical manifold is a useful inductive bias; physical laws are continuous functions of their arguments.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract]: "Adjacent physical states are thus adjacent in representation... By treating continuous physical states as smooth, separable embeddings, SNAs enable distributional modelling of chaotic systems."  
  - [PAGE 9‑10, Fig 5a‑c]: PCA of Leviathan's embedding space shows a smooth, low‑dimensional manifold vs. isotropic clouds from dense/naive tokenisation.  
  - [PAGE 10, Fig 5f & 6]: Leviathan maintains vorticity PDFs and coherent structures over rollout, unlike deterministic operators which collapse to a mean state.  
- **Break condition:** The system's state space is fundamentally discrete or the chosen tokenisation scheme (e.g., arbitrary integer indexing) destroys neighbourhood relations, as shown in [PAGE 10, Fig 5c] for the `o200k` base tokeniser.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** SNAs provide a unified primitive for both deterministic prediction/generation and distributional modelling by treating sequential evolution (physical or linguistic) as modelling a conditional distribution over states in a smooth embedding space.  
- **Mechanism:** The SNA acts as a smooth, invertible interpolant. For chaotic systems, instead of a pointwise deterministic operator \(u(t+1)=F(u(t))\), it learns \(p(u(t+1)\mid u(t))\) in the embedding space. The smooth embedding ensures samples remain on the attractor manifold. For generative inversion, the smooth, cheaply differentiable mapping allows efficient gradient‑based search in the latent space.  
- **Core assumption:** Effective long‑horizon prediction of chaotic dynamics requires modelling a distribution of plausible futures, not a single point trajectory, due to sensitivity to initial conditions and finite‑precision effects.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract]: "This approach mitigates the nonphysical drift characteristics of deterministic operators whilst remaining applicable to discrete sequences."  
  - [PAGE 2]: "chaotic spatiotemporal dynamics and linguistic autoregression become structurally analogous."  
  - [PAGE 7‑8, Fig 4e‑f]: Janus's generative inversion via MAP in a smooth latent space yields valid microstructures with <3.5% local error.  
  - [PAGE 10‑11, Fig 5d‑f & 6]: Leviathan (distributional) preserves enstrophy, spectral slope, and vorticity PDFs over 20‑step rollouts; deterministic operators drift to mean states.  
  - [PAGE 4, Fig 2c]: KHRONOS's rapid generative inversion of thermal histories.  
- **Break condition:** The distributional assumption fails if the system is truly deterministic and low‑noise with a need for exact pointwise replication (e.g., certain control tasks), or if the embedding manifold is not sufficiently learned to represent the true attractor.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Tensor Decomposition (CP/Tucker/TT)**  
  *Why needed here:* The SNA's core mechanism is the tensor decomposition of the interaction object. Understanding CP factorisation \(\psi_j(x) = \prod_i \psi_i^{(j)}(x_i)\) is essential to grasp how parameter count scales with rank \(r\) vs. dimension \(d\), and why it avoids the curse of dimensionality.  
  *Quick check:* If you have a 5‑dimensional input and use a CP‑SNA with rank \(r=10\), how many univariate sub‑atom functions \(\psi_i^{(j)}\) do you need to define?
- **Concept: Variational Principles & Galerkin Methods**  
  *Why needed here:* The VSNA extension reinterprets the SNA as a trial space for solving PDEs. This requires understanding how a function approximation (the SNA) is used inside a weak formulation \(a(u,v)=l(v)\) and what guarantees (well‑posedness, quasi‑optimality) arise from the separable structure.  
  *Quick check:* In the Galerkin context, what role do the univariate sub‑atoms \(\psi_i^{(j)}\) play relative to the Hilbert space \(V = \otimes V_i\)?
- **Concept: Autoregressive Sequence Modelling & Tokenisation**  
  *Why needed here:* The Leviathan application extends SNAs to sequence data (turbulence as a "language"). This requires understanding how continuous physical states are quantised into tokens and how the SNA's embedding layer preserves neighbourhood relations, which is absent in standard lookup embeddings.  
  *Quick check:* Why does using an "unstructured" tokeniser like `o200k` suppress the neighbourhood structure that SNA embeddings rely on?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Atoms (\(\phi^{(S)}\)) → Interaction Tensor/Object (\(C\) or \(T\)) → Activation (\(\rho\)) → Wrapper/Module (e.g., KHRONOS, VSNA head, SPAN head, Janus property predictor, Leviathan embedding layer).  
- **Critical path:**  
  1. Start with the Canonical Polyadic (CP) class (\(k=d\), atoms are products of univariate sub‑atoms). This is the mathematically simplest and empirically validated instantiation.  
  2. Choose basis functions for sub‑atoms (e.g., B‑splines, Fourier, Chebyshev) based on domain smoothness and boundary conditions.  
  3. Set interaction rank \(r\) as the primary capacity knob. Start low (e.g., \(r=4-32\)) and increase until validation error plateaus. The "efficient frontier" is found by jointly increasing \(r\) and basis resolution \(C\).  
  4. For compositional use (SPAN, Janus, Leviathan), the SNA typically replaces a dense linear layer or is inserted as an embedding/head. The surrounding architecture (encoder, Transformer backbone) remains standard.  
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Rank \(r\) vs. Expressivity/Compute: Higher \(r\) increases capacity and accuracy but linearly increases parameter count and ALS iteration cost. The scaling is \(O(r^2 \cdot C \cdot d)\) for tensor‑native solves, far better than \(O(C^d)\).  
  - Basis resolution \(C\) vs. Accuracy: Higher \(C\) (more spline knots) increases fidelity to smooth functions, typically with error \(\sim C^{-p}\) where \(p\) is spline order.  
  - Tokenisation (for sequences): Continuous or dense quantisation (e.g., uint16) is required to preserve neighbourhood structure. Arbitrary categorical tokenisation destroys the inductive bias.  
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Catastrophic drift in chaotic rollouts: Indicates the model was trained as a deterministic operator, not a distributional predictor. Solution: Switch to Leviathan's tokenisation+distributional training scheme.  
  - Poor inversion/gradient hallucination: The latent space is not smooth or convex enough. Manifested as high physical errors despite low predictive loss. Solution: Ensure SNA head has sufficient rank \(r\) and use MAP inversion with ensemble validation (Janus).  
  - Underfitting on high‑dimensional PDEs: Rank \(r\) is too low for the solution's complexity. The error plateau in [PAGE 6, Fig 3c] will be reached at a high error level.  
  - Saturation in supervised regression: The problem is not separable in the chosen coordinates. For the 20D Sobol‑G (which *is* separable), only the SNA reached \(R^2=0.999\); others saturated below 0.88 [PAGE 24, Table 3].  
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Borehole Function (8D):** Verify CP‑SNA (KHRONOS) achieves \(R^2 \ge 0.999\) with <1k parameters on a standard benchmark [PAGE 22, Table 2]. Tests basic high‑dimensional regression capability.  
  2. **Sobol‑G Function (20D):** Test on a *known separable* function with added noise. Expect \(R^2 \sim 0.999\) with ~1.5k params while RF/XGBoost/MLP fail (\(R^2<0.88\)) [PAGE 24, Table 3]. Validates the core premise: the SNA exploits structure others miss.  
  3. **1D Inviscid Burgers' Equation:** Solve a nonlinear PDE (outside bilinear theory) with a VSNA [PAGE 23‑24, Fig 7]. Tests the variational formulation and tensor‑native ALS optimisation in a shock‑forming regime. Check absolute error <0.02 and pre‑shock accuracy.

## Limitations
- **Latent factorizability requirement:** SNAs assume the target mapping is either inherently separable or can be transformed into a separable coordinate system; generalisability to arbitrary high‑dimensional, non‑separable problems remains unproven.
- **Tokenisation sensitivity:** The neighbour‑preserving inductive bias for sequence data requires continuous quantisation; standard categorical tokenisation destroys it, limiting scalability and modality generalisation.
- **Computational cost of ALS:** Wall‑clock performance versus GPU‑optimised dense layers is not thoroughly benchmarked; the overhead of tensor‑native ALS may offset parameter‑count advantages in practice.

## Next Checks
1. **Benchmark Against Non‑Separable Functions:** Systematically evaluate a CP‑SNA (KHRONOS) on standard high‑dimensional non‑separable regression benchmarks (e.g., Hartmann family, Ackley function in >10D). *Pass criteria:* SNA should fail to match the accuracy of a sufficiently wide MLP with comparable parameter count, confirming the necessity of separability. Expected outcome: accuracy saturation at \(R^2 < 0.9\) despite increasing rank.
2. **Tokenization Robustness Ablation for Sequence Modeling:** For a chaotic system (e.g., 2D Kolmogorov flow), replicate the Leviathan setup using multiple continuous quantization strategies (uniform bins, k‑means, adaptive binning) and one "structured" categorical tokenizer (e.g., discretising a low‑dimensional latent code from an autoencoder). *Pass criteria:* Only schemes preserving local neighbourhood topology (measured by comparing input‑space vs. token‑space neighbor Jaccard scores) should yield low drift and preserved statistics; standard categorical tokenization must fail.
3. **Computational Scaling Analysis vs. Dense Baselines:** Measure wall‑clock training time, memory footprint, and per‑iteration cost for a CP‑SNA versus a dense MLP as a function of input dimension \(d\) (e.g., \(d = [10, 50, 100, 200]\)) on a synthetic separable task, matching validation error. *Pass criteria:* While SNA parameters scale better, the per‑iteration ALS solve should show a clear crossover point where its overhead surpasses a dense layer's matmul, quantifying the practical efficiency frontier.
