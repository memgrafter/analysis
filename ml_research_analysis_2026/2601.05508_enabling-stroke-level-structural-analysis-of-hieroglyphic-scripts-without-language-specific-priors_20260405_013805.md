# Enabling Stroke-Level Structural Analysis of Hieroglyphic Scripts without Language-Specific Priors

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.05508
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05508
- **Reference count:** 38

## Executive Summary
Current large language and multimodal models process hieroglyphic characters as tokens or pixel grids, missing crucial stroke-level structural information. Existing structural analysis methods are often script-specific and require labor-intensive annotations. This paper introduces Hieroglyphic Stroke Analyzer (HieroSA), a reinforcement learning framework that transforms character bitmaps into explicit line-segment representations in a normalized coordinate space, without any language-specific priors or annotated data. HieroSA uses a reward based on pixel coverage and stroke validity to train an MLLM to generate stroke parses. Evaluated on Chinese, Japanese, and Oracle Bone Script, HieroSA significantly outperforms state-of-the-art models like GPT-5, Claude, and Qwen3-VL on stroke parsing metrics. The derived stroke representations facilitate structure-guided character exploration, retrieving semantically related glyphs across scripts, and improve OCR accuracy when incorporated as additional features.

## Method Summary
HieroSA is a framework for unsupervised stroke decomposition of hieroglyphic characters. It employs a reinforcement learning (GRPO) approach to finetune a multimodal LLM (Qwen3-VL-4B) to output a set of line segments defined by normalized endpoint coordinates. The reward function combines a stroke coverage term, which measures the fraction of black pixels covered by polygonal regions generated by extending each line segment normal to its axis within the bitmap, and a penalty for invalid strokes that fall outside the character region. A format reward ensures well-formed output. The model is trained solely on binarized character bitmaps, with no stroke annotations or script-specific rules. The normalized coordinate space and optional coordinate grid overlay promote cross-script generalization. This unified training procedure works across multiple logographic scripts, producing interpretable stroke parses that can be used for downstream tasks.

## Key Results
- **Stroke parsing:** HieroSA achieves an average reward of 0.65 vs. 0.14 for baselines, coverage 63.3% vs. 3.0%, and invalid strokes 14.5% vs. 91.0% on a combined test set of Chinese, Japanese, and Oracle Bone Script.
- **Structure-guided exploration:** Using stroke-based masking and CLIP matching, the method retrieves semantically related glyphs across Chinese, Dongba, and Egyptian hieroglyphs.
- **OCR improvement:** Incorporating stroke representations into an OCR model boosts accuracy from 82.4% (without strokes) to 83.5% on a held-out set.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
HieroSA learns to decompose character bitmaps into stroke-like line segments via a reinforcement learning reward that maximizes pixel coverage while penalizing invalid strokes. The RL policy (a finetuned MLLM) generates a set of line segments. A geometric estimator computes a polygonal coverage region for each segment by extending along tangential/normal directions within black-pixel boundaries. The aggregated reward (`r_s`) is the fraction of black pixels covered by valid strokes, scaled by `(1 - α * N_invalid)`. The core assumption is that the spatial extent of a stroke can be approximated by extending a line segment normal to its axis until hitting the black-white boundary, and strokes that collectively cover the foreground without excessive redundancy correspond to a plausible structural decomposition. Evidence: The reward computation is detailed in Section 2.2; coverage-based supervision is central. Break condition: If stroke boundaries are highly irregular or overlapping such that normal extension consistently intrudes into adjacent strokes, the polygonal coverage estimate becomes inaccurate, leading to misguiding rewards.

### Mechanism 2
The coordinate-based line-segment representation enables cross-script generalization by avoiding language-specific stroke inventories or decomposition rules. Strokes are represented solely by normalized endpoint coordinates `(p_s, p_e) ∈ [0,1]^2`. The model learns directly from bitmaps without predefined stroke labels or topology constraints, allowing it to adapt to varying structural complexities (e.g., curved strokes via multi-segment approximations). The core assumption is that a flexible, unconstrained set of line segments can effectively approximate the visual essence of a hieroglyphic character’s stroke structure. Evidence: The abstract and Section 2.1 emphasize script-agnostic learning. Break condition: For scripts where strokes are fundamentally non-linear (e.g., fully curved flourishes), the two-point line-segment model may fail to capture essential structure without an impractically large number of segments.

### Mechanism 3
Stroke representations facilitate structure-guided character exploration by masking stroke regions and matching based on shared residual structure, surfacing semantically related but visually distinct glyphs. Given a query character, HieroSA’s strokes are used to generate perturbed images via stochastic masking (discarding strokes with probability based on distance to a sampled center). Matching is performed on the masked images using CLIP embeddings, biasing results toward characters sharing un-masked stroke configurations. The core assumption is that perturbing a character by masking a localized subset of its strokes reveals its latent structural skeleton; characters sharing this skeleton will have higher embedding similarity. Evidence: Section 3.3.1 describes the masking procedure; Table 4 shows qualitative retrievals across scripts. Break condition: If the stroke decomposition is noisy or includes many invalid strokes, masking may remove either too much or too little structure, degrading exploration quality.

## Foundational Learning
- **Reinforcement Learning for Structured Output (GRPO):** Needed because HieroSA must generate variable-length sets of line segments, and the reward (pixel coverage) is non-differentiable. GRPO enables policy gradient updates based on this reward and a format reward. *Quick check:* How does the reward function `r = r_s + β r_f` balance learning accurate stroke geometry (`r_s`) with producing parseable output (`r_f`), and what happens if `β` is too high or too low?
- **Geometric Stroke Coverage Estimation:** Needed to compute the supervision signal: mapping a thin line segment to a 2D region approximating the stroke’s inked area. This involves normal/tangential extension until hitting image boundaries. *Quick check:* Why is tangential extension applied only to endpoints, and how might this fail for strokes that naturally taper or have blobby terminations?
- **Normalized Coordinate Space for Generalization:** Needed to represent strokes in `[0,1]^2` rather than absolute pixels, removing scale and font-specific biases and enabling script-agnostic learning. *Quick check:* If the input character image is not centered or has excessive whitespace padding, how does the normalized coordinate system maintain consistency, and what role does the overlaid coordinate grid play?

## Architecture Onboarding
Input Preprocessor → Policy Network (Qwen3-VL-4B) → Reward Calculator → GRPO Trainer

**Critical path:** Binarized character bitmap → Policy forward pass generates `<stroke>` tags with normalized coordinates → Reward Calculator estimates coverage and invalid strokes → GRPO update → repeat.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Two-point vs. multi-point stroke representation: Two points simplify learning but force complex shapes into many segments; more points increase output dimensionality and instability.
- Coordinate system overlay: Adding a visible grid improves coordinate prediction accuracy but may introduce visual artifacts if misaligned.
- Penalty coefficient `α`: Balances coverage vs. stroke validity; too low → many invalid strokes, too high → under-generation.
- Font diversity in training: Increases structural variability but may add noise from atypical glyph designs.

**Failure signatures:**
- High invalid stroke rate (>50%): Likely due to `α` too small, poor coverage estimation for curved strokes, or insufficient training.
- Low coverage (<20%): `α` too large, model overly conservative; or reward misestimation for dense glyphs.
- Over-generation (thousands of strokes): Missing format reward (`β=0`) or broken output parsing.
- Poor cross-script transfer: Training data lacks diversity (e.g., only single font, simple glyphs).

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Baseline comparison: Train HieroSA per script and evaluate against GPT-5/Claude/Qwen3-VL on RE/CO/IS. Success: >0.6 reward, >60% coverage, <20% invalid strokes.
2. Ablation - penalty coefficient: Vary `α` on Chinese training set to find optimal balance (~0.1).
3. Downstream OCR utility: Finetune OCR model with/without stroke representations; expect >1% absolute accuracy gain.

## Limitations
- The polygonal coverage estimation via normal/tangential extension may misrepresent stroke boundaries for complex, curved, or overlapping glyphs.
- The fixed two-point line-segment representation may be insufficient for scripts with inherently non-linear or highly variable stroke forms.
- Performance is highly dependent on training font diversity and bitmap quality; generalization to severely degraded historical manuscripts is uncertain.

## Confidence
- **Cross-script generalization:** Medium (evaluated on three logographic scripts, but unknown for other writing systems)
- **Stroke parsing gains:** High (clear outperformance of baselines on automated metrics)
- **Structure-guided exploration:** Medium (qualitative examples show coherence but not fully quantified robustness)

## Next Checks
1. Apply HieroSA to scripts with predominantly curved strokes (e.g., Malayalam, Thai) and measure coverage/invalid-stroke rates against human-annotated stroke maps.
2. Remove the geometric coverage heuristic and replace with a learned stroke-width estimator to isolate the impact of the reward assumption on parsing accuracy.
3. Re-train and evaluate without the overlaid coordinate grid on highly variable font sets to quantify its contribution to normalized coordinate stability.
