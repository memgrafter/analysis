# REAL: Regression-Aware Reinforcement Learning for LLM-as-a-Judge
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.17145
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17145
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: REAL consistently outperforms regression-aware SFT and standard RL on LLM-as-a-Judge tasks, achieving up to +8.40 Pearson and +7.20 Spearman over SFT on Qwen3-32B, and +18.30 Pearson / +11.20 Spearman over the base model.

## Executive Summary
REAL (Regression-Aware Reinforcement Learning) is a framework for training LLM judges that produce numeric scores. It addresses the limitation of standard RL which uses binary rewards that ignore the ordinal nature of scores. REAL optimizes a policy-dependent squared error reward, which is theoretically optimal for Pearson correlation. The training objective decomposes into two complementary terms: one explores chain-of-thought (CoT) trajectories via a policy gradient (RLOO), and the other refines the final score prediction by directly minimizing the squared error through the RAIL predictor. Experiments across model sizes (8B to 32B) and multiple benchmarks show REAL consistently improves correlation metrics, outperforming both regression-aware supervised fine-tuning (e.g., TRACT) and conventional RL approaches. The gains generalize to out-of-domain evaluation sets.

## Method Summary
REAL formulates the LLM-as-a-Judge problem as a reinforcement learning task where the policy πθ generates a CoT followed by a numeric score. The reward is defined as r = -(ŷθ(x,c) - y*)² + λ log πθ(y*|x,c), with ŷθ the expected score computed by RAIL (summing digit token probabilities). Since the reward depends on the policy parameters, the standard policy gradient is generalized to include a direct term ∇θr. The final gradient is an average over K sampled CoTs: ∇θL = (1/K) Σ [Ã_i ∇θ log πθ(c_i|x) + β (-2(ŷ_i - y*)∇θ ŷ_i + λ ∇θ log πθ(y*|x,c_i))]. The first term uses RLOO advantages to encourage high-reward reasoning paths; the second term directly adjusts the score prediction. At inference, RAIL aggregates token probabilities to produce a calibrated score estimate, often yielding better correlation than greedy decoding.

## Key Results
- REAL on Qwen3-32B achieves +8.40 Pearson and +7.20 Spearman over the SFT baseline, and +18.30 Pearson / +11.20 Spearman over the base model.
- Gains are consistent across 8B and 32B model scales and robust across in-domain and out-of-domain benchmarks (Feedback Bench, FLASK, Vicuna Bench, MT Bench).
- REAL outperforms both regression-aware SFT methods (RAFT, TRACT) and standard RL with binary rewards, with the RAIL predictor providing a "free lunch" boost at inference.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- Claim: Policy-dependent squared error reward optimizes for Pearson/Spearman correlation in numeric scoring tasks.
- Mechanism: The reward r = -(ŷθ(x,c) - y*)² + λ log πθ(y*|x,c) provides a fine-grained gradient that respects ordinal structure, unlike binary accuracy.
- Core assumption: Lemma 3.1 proves that minimizing expected squared error recovers the Bayes estimator E[y*|x,c], which also maximizes Pearson correlation (up to positive affine transformation).
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "optimizes policy-dependent squared error rewards, theoretically proven optimal for Pearson correlation"; [section 3] Lemma 3.1 and proof; [corpus] TRACT (Chiang et al., 2025) uses similar regression-aware SFT but lacks RL exploration.
- Break condition: Task requires discrete categories without ordering, or tokenizer cannot represent numeric digits individually.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: Generalized policy gradient decomposition enables valid optimization despite policy-dependent rewards, separating reasoning exploration from prediction refinement.
- Mechanism: The gradient ∇θL = E[r ∇θ log πθ(c|x) + ∇θr] splits into: (Term 1) CoT update via RLOO advantages; (Term 2) direct backpropagation of the reward gradient -2(ŷ-y*)∇θ ŷ + λ ∇θ log πθ(y*|x,c).
- Core assumption: CoT c and final answer y can be separated; the reward is differentiable w.r.t. θ via ŷ.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "generalized policy gradient decomposes training into two complementary components"; [section 4.2] Lemma 4.1 and Eq.6; [corpus] JEPO (Tang et al., 2025) uses Jensen-bound but lacks regression-aware Term 2.
- Break condition: CoT generation not token-separable from answer, or reward not differentiable (e.g., non-differentiable verifier).

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: RAIL predictor at inference provides "free-lunch" correlation improvements by aggregating digit token probabilities.
- Mechanism: At evaluation, compute ŷ = Σ_{d∈{0,...,9}} d·πθ(token_d | x,c) instead of greedy decoding. This leverages the model's calibrated probability distribution, approximating the Bayes decision rule for squared error.
- Core assumption: The policy after REAL training yields well-calibrated digit probabilities.
- Evidence anchors: [abstract] "regression-aware refinement of final score predictions"; [section 2.2] Algorithm 1 RAIL predictor; [corpus] RAFT (Lukasik et al., 2025) and TRACT (Chiang et al., 2025) demonstrate RAIL gains.
- Break condition: Tokenizer represents multi-digit numbers as single tokens, or digit probabilities are miscalibrated.

## Foundational Learning
- **Policy Gradient Methods (REINFORCE, RLOO)**: Needed for REAL's Term 1 which reinforces CoT reasoning. RLOO provides variance reduction without a learned value function. Quick check: Why does RLOO use a leave-one-out baseline instead of a learned value function? It avoids bias from value function approximation but may have higher variance compared to a learned baseline.
- **Regression vs Classification Loss**: REAL treats numeric scoring as regression (squared error) rather than classification (cross-entropy), preserving ordinal distances. Quick check: Show that minimizing E[(ŷ-y*)²] yields the conditional mean E[y*|x,c] while cross-entropy over discrete categories does not necessarily respect magnitude differences.
- **LLM-as-a-Judge Setup**: The problem context: models evaluate responses using rubrics, producing integer scores 1–5 with chain-of-thought. Primary metrics are correlation (Pearson, Spearman) because they reflect ranking consistency with ground truth. Quick check: Why is Spearman often reported alongside Pearson? Pearson measures linear correlation of scores; Spearman measures rank correlation, capturing monotonic consistency without assuming linearity.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Dataset loader (x,y*) -> Policy network πθ (generates CoT c) -> RAIL module (computes ŷ from digit token probabilities) -> Reward calculator (computes r) -> RLOO estimator (computes advantages A_i) -> Gradient aggregator (combines ∇_θ L terms) -> Optimizer (AdamW) -> Inference wrapper (uses RAIL predictor).
- **Critical path**: 
  1. Initialize πθ from an SFT checkpoint (recommended TRACT for Qwen, base+warmup for Mistral).
  2. For each batch of (x,y*), sample K CoTs c_i from πθ(c|x) with temperature 1.
  3. For each c_i, compute ŷ_i via RAIL, then reward r_i = -(ŷ_i - y*)² + λ log πθ(y*|x,c_i).
  4. Compute RLOO advantage A_i = r_i - mean_{j≠i} r_j, standardize and clip to [-1,1].
  5. Compute ∇θ term: ∇θL = (1/K) Σ [Ã_i ∇θ log πθ(c_i|x) + β (-2(ŷ_i-y*)∇θ ŷ_i + λ ∇θ log πθ(y*|x,c_i))].
  6. Update parameters with AdamW.
  7. During evaluation, generate one CoT and compute ŷ with RAIL.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Group size K: Larger K reduces variance but increases memory (Tab.4 shows gain from 4→16).
  - Log-likelihood weight λ: λ=0 already beats TRACT; λ>0 stabilizes but excess overfits answer format.
  - Prediction weight β: Eq.6 suggests β=1, but Tab.8 shows β=0.01 optimal; balances exploration vs exploitation.
  - Temperature: T=1 default; lower T reduces exploration and may hurt generalization.
  - Initialization: Starting from SFT yields higher scores but REAL improves from base as well.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Correlation collapse during training (common with binary reward or miscomputed advantages).
  - Diverging loss if β too large or learning rate too high.
  - Format degeneration (loss of digit tokens or CoT); monitor digit token probability mass.
  - High variance if K too small.
  - Poor out-of-domain generalization if training data lacks rubric diversity.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Ablation: Group size K on Qwen3-8B (K=4,8,16); expect Pearson to increase with K, confirming RLOO variance reduction.
  2. Ablation: λ weight (λ∈{0,1,5,10}); expect λ=0 to already beat TRACT, with small λ possibly further improving.
  3. Checkpoint initialization: Compare base vs SFT (TRACT) init; expect SFT init to yield higher final correlation, but both show Δ over init.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1: Extension to pairwise preference learning.** The authors state that REAL focuses on pointwise regression and does not directly address pairwise tasks. Resolving this would require designing appropriate rewards for comparisons (e.g., ranking losses) and integrating them with the regression-aware objective, plus validation on pairwise benchmarks like Chatbot Arena.
- **Open Question 2: Impact of biases in self-generated CoT reasoning.** REAL relies on self-generated CoT which may inherit biases from the base model. Evidence needed: analysis of CoT distributions for systematic biases and development of mitigation strategies (e.g., adversarial filtering, diverse reasoning paths) evaluated on bias-annotated benchmarks.
- **Open Question 3: Alternative policy gradient estimators.** The paper uses RLOO for stability, suggesting that PPO or actor-critic methods might improve sample efficiency. Evidence would be implementations of REAL with such estimators, comparing convergence speed, gradient variance, and final correlation across runs.
- **Open Question 4: Theoretical guarantee for Spearman/Kendall.** Lemma 3.1 proves optimality for Pearson correlation only, though REAL empirically improves Spearman and Kendall. Extending the theory to show when squared error minimization maximizes these rank-based metrics, or designing alternative losses specifically for them, would resolve this.

## Limitations
- The exact prompt templates and answer formatting instructions are not provided, making exact replication difficult.
- The method assumes the tokenizer represents numeric scores as single-digit tokens; many tokenizers do not, and using multi-digit tokens would break RAIL.
- Critical training details (number of steps, batch size, learning rate schedule) are missing, and the best results depend on specialized SFT checkpoints (TRACT/RAFT) whose training procedures are not fully disclosed.
