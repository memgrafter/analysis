# Resilience Meets Autonomy: Governing Embodied AI in Critical Infrastructure
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.15885  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15885  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Proposes a hybrid governance architecture with four bounded autonomy oversight modes to allocate decision authority between AI and humans in critical infrastructure, aiming to achieve resilience during systemic crises.

## Executive Summary
Embodied AI (EAI) in critical infrastructure faces heightened vulnerability to systemic crises and cascading failures that exceed its training on statistically representable uncertainty. This paper introduces a hybrid governance framework based on four bounded autonomy oversight modes—Fully Automated, Human-on-the-Loop, Human-in-the-Loop, Human-in-the-Command—and maps them to specific EAI applications using task complexity, risk level, and consequence severity. The structured allocation of decision authority between AI and humans is argued to be essential for resilience, rather than pursuing full automation. The conceptual model aligns with regulatory (EU AI Act) and standardization (ISO) requirements for controllability and human oversight in high-risk settings but lacks quantitative performance metrics.

## Method Summary
The method is a qualitative, criteria-based assessment for mapping EAI applications in critical infrastructure to one of four oversight modes. The mapping relies on three explicit criteria: task complexity, risk level, and consequence severity, supplemented by the paper’s illustrative tables (I, II) and vulnerability pattern discussions. The objective is a defensible conceptual allocation, not empirical validation. A minimum reproduction requires: (1) formalizing the criteria into a rubric for a chosen domain; (2) selecting an EAI application from Table II and evaluating it against the rubric; (3) assigning an oversight mode with justification referencing the paper’s rationale and vulnerability context.

## Key Results
- Developed a hybrid governance architecture with four bounded autonomy oversight modes to balance AI capability and human judgment.
- Defined mapping criteria (task complexity, risk level, consequence severity) to assign modes to EAI applications across critical infrastructure sectors.
- Produced a conceptual framework that meets regulatory (EU AI Act) and standardization (ISO) demands for human oversight and controllability in high-risk AI deployments.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Resilience is achieved by matching the oversight mode (Fully Automated, HOTL, HITL, HIC) to the specific operational context of an EAI application based on task complexity, risk level, and consequence severity.
- **Mechanism:** A structured allocation of decision authority ensures AI handles routine, time-critical, or low-consequence tasks within safety-constrained boundaries, while humans retain authority for high-consequence, strategic, or novel situations where AI's training on statistically representable uncertainty fails.
- **Core assumption:** The boundaries between "routine" and "non-routine," "low-consequence" and "high-consequence" can be meaningfully defined *ex ante* for a given infrastructure sector and EAI function.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "...mapping them to critical infrastructure sectors based on task complexity, risk level, and consequence severity."
  - [section] Table I and II explicitly map modes to applications and domains using these criteria.
- **Break condition:** If the criteria for assigning modes are poorly defined, inconsistently applied, or fail to account for dynamically evolving crisis states, the governance architecture becomes brittle and may misallocate authority during systemic surprise.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Resilience depends on a *hybrid*, dynamic system where oversight modes are complementary and can switch or operate concurrently, rather than a single, static mode per system.
- **Mechanism:** An EAI system's governance controller can transition between modes (e.g., from HOTL to HITL to HIC) as operational conditions shift from steady-state to crisis, leveraging AI speed for stabilization and human contextual judgment for strategic adaptation.
- **Core assumption:** A real-time governance layer can reliably detect threshold crossings (e.g., rising anomaly rates, cascade indicators) that mandate a mode switch and execute a safe transfer of control.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section] "In practice, these modes should be treated as complementary elements of a hybrid system rather than as fixed design choices... may therefore need to switch between modes, or operate several of them concurrently, depending on function."
  - [abstract] "...structured combination of machine capability and human contextual judgement."
- **Break condition:** Mode-switching logic is flawed, delayed, or incontrollable during high-stress transitions, leading to "cliff effects" where authority lapses or conflicts during critical handovers.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Human judgment is indispensable for resilience during "systemic surprise" (cascading failures, unknown unknowns) because humans retain advantages in contextual interpretation, normative trade-offs, and improvisational problem-framing that AI lacks.
- **Mechanism:** When cascading failures or exogenous shocks exceed an AI's representational capacity and pre-programmed routines, human operators in HITL or HIC modes can engage in sensemaking, re-prioritize goals, and make ethical or political trade-offs outside the AI's optimization function.
- **Core assumption:** Human operators subject to stress and cognitive overload can still perform superior contextual judgment and improvisation compared to a brittle AI in truly novel crisis scenarios.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section] "Under such conditions, neither AI nor human judgement is sufficient on its own... humans retain advantages in contextual interpretation, normative judgement, and adaptive problem framing... expressed through organisational improvisation."
  - [abstract] "...resilience depends on bounded autonomy within a hybrid governance architecture... structured combination of machine capability and human judgement."
- **Break condition:** Human operators are inadequately trained for crisis improvisation, suffer catastrophic cognitive overload from AI-generated alerts, or are excluded from the decision loop by poorly designed HIC constraints, nullifying the human advantage.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Bounded Autonomy**  
  *Why needed here:* It is the paper's core thesis—AI in CI must not be fully autonomous or fully subordinate, but operate within pre-defined, auditable limits that can be overridden or reconfigured by humans.  
  *Quick check:* What is a concrete example of a "safety constraint that the system cannot override itself" in a fully automated power grid stabilizer?

- **Concept: Systemic Surprise & Cascading Failure**  
  *Why needed here:* This defines the problem space the governance architecture is designed to address. It explains *why* full AI autonomy fails in CI.  
  *Quick check:* Describe a cascading failure scenario in a water treatment plant where an AI anomaly detection system might miss the root cause because it was trained only on historical sensor data.

- **Concept: Oversight Modes (HOTL vs. HITL vs. HIC)**  
  *Why needed here:* These are the operational primitives of the proposed governance model. Correctly distinguishing between passive supervision (HOTL) and mandatory human approval (HITL) is critical for implementation.  
  *Quick check:* When would a pipeline inspection drone be in HOTL versus HITL mode? What specific action triggers the difference?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  EAI -> Oversight Controller -> Human Interface  
  Oversight Controller -> EAI  
  Human Interface -> Oversight Controller  
  EAI -> Infrastructure Interface  
  Safety Guardrails -> EAI

- **Critical path:**  
  1. EAI operates in assigned mode (e.g., HOTL).  
  2. Oversight Controller continuously assesses `task_metrics` and `context_metrics`.  
  3. If `context_metrics` breach mode-specific thresholds, Oversight Controller initiates **mode transition protocol**.  
  4. Protocol: (a) Alert human at new mode's level, (b) Safely pause EAI action if moving to HITL/HIC, (c) Transfer control/authority, (d) Log transition.  
  5. Human in new mode issues commands/approvals; EAI executes within new constraints.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - **Speed vs. Safety:** Fully Automated maximizes response speed but removes human veto; HITL maximizes safety but introduces latency.  
  - **Cognitive Load vs. Situational Awareness:** HOTL risks alert fatigue; HIC provides strategic clarity but may lose fine-grained tactical awareness.  
  - **Automation Bias vs. Automation refusal:** Over-trust in AI leads to missed errors; under-trust leads to dismissal of valid AI insights.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - **Cascading Failure from AI Error:** EAI misinterprets data in Fully Automated mode, takes harmful action, no human stop in time.  
  - **Mode Confusion/Stuck Transition:** Oversight Controller detects need for HIC but human unavailable; system remains in inadequate HOTL.  
  - **Cognitive Overload in HOTL:** Continuous false positives cause operator to ignore critical warning, leading to missed intervention.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Mode-Transition Logic Test:** Inject simulated cascade indicators into a digital twin of a power grid with inspection drones. Measure latency and correctness of mode switches (HOTL -> HITL -> HIC).  
  2. **HITL Efficacy Comparison:** For a high-consequence task (e.g., remote valve operation), compare operator accuracy and time between AI recommendation only vs. AI + mandatory HITL approval under time pressure and partial information.  
  3. **Cognitive Load Assessment:** During extended HOTL supervision of multiple robots, measure operator workload (NASA-TLX), alert dismissal rates, and time-to-detect a seeded critical anomaly.

## Limitations
- No weighting or thresholding for the three mapping criteria (task complexity, risk, consequence), leaving assignments to subjective interpretation.
- No empirical validation that proposed mode allocations are correct or optimal in real-world operations.
- Tension between the framework's emphasis on *dynamic* mode switching and the *static* mode assignments presented in Table II; lack of fail-safe design specifications for mode transitions under cascade stress.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (Criteria-based mode matching):** Medium confidence. Logic is clear and aligns with regulatory intent, but evidence is descriptive, not empirical.  
- **Mechanism 2 (Hybrid, dynamic mode switching):** Low confidence. Core assumption of reliable, real-time governance detection of cascade thresholds is untested.  
- **Mechanism 3 (Human advantage in systemic surprise):** Low confidence. Relies on contested assumptions about human cognitive performance under AI-generated stress; no crisis-specific validation.
