# MemReward: Graph-Based Experience Memory for LLM Reward Prediction with Limited Labels

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.19310  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19310  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: MemReward achieves 97.3% of Oracle performance with only 20% labeled data on Qwen2.5-3B, and 96.6% on 1.5B; performance scales to 99.4% at 70% labels.

## Executive Summary
MemReward tackles the scarcity of reward labels for RL fine‑tuning of large language models. It constructs a heterogeneous graph from initial policy rollouts, where queries, thinking processes, and answers form nodes linked by similarity and structural edges. A graph neural network trained on the limited labeled subset propagates reward predictions to unlabeled rollouts during online GRPO training. On Qwen2.5‑3B and 1.5B across mathematics, QA, and code tasks, using just 20% ground‑truth labels, MemReward achieves 97.3% and 96.6% of fully‑supervised Oracle performance, respectively, outperforming partial‑label baselines. It even exceeds Oracle on out‑of‑domain tasks, and performance scales smoothly, reaching 99.4% of Oracle at 70% labels, demonstrating effective label‑efficient RL fine‑tuning.

## Method Summary
MemReward operates in two phases. In the warmup phase, rollouts are generated using an initial policy; each rollout’s query, thinking process, and answer are encoded with a frozen sentence encoder (all‑MiniLM‑L6‑v2). A heterogeneous graph is built: nodes for queries, thinking, and answers; edges include query–query (top‑k cosine similarity, k=7), query–thinking, and thinking–answer. A two‑layer heterogeneous GNN (GATv2) is trained with binary cross‑entropy to predict reward scores from the limited labeled subset.

During online GRPO, for each batch the system uses ground‑truth rewards for labeled queries. For unlabeled queries, it dynamically connects them to the warmup graph by finding their top‑k similar query nodes, runs the frozen GNN to obtain a reward score, and thresholds it (>0.5) to a binary reward. These mixed rewards are then used in the GRPO objective (with standard advantage normalization and KL penalty) to update the policy. The GNN remains fixed throughout online training, providing a stable reward signal despite limited labels.

## Key Results
- On Qwen2.5‑3B and 1.5B across mathematics, QA, and code tasks, using only 20% ground‑truth labels, MemReward achieves 97.3% and 96.6% of Oracle performance, respectively, surpassing partial‑label baselines by 1.35 and 5.38 points.  
- It even exceeds Oracle on out‑of‑domain benchmarks (e.g., NuminaMath, SIQA, PIQA).  
- Performance scales smoothly, reaching 99.4% of Oracle at 70% labels.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: The heterogeneous graph structure captures exploitable inter‑query reward dependencies that independent sample processing misses.  
- **Mechanism**: Query‑query edges (built via top‑k cosine similarity) connect semantically related problems, enabling the GNN to aggregate reward signals across similar queries. This propagates labels from the limited supervised set to unlabeled rollouts by assuming “nearby” queries share reward patterns.  
- **Core assumption**: Semantically similar queries (as measured by embedding similarity) have correlated correctness outcomes.  
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] “queries, thinking processes, and answers form nodes in a heterogeneous graph with similarity and structural edges; a GNN trained on labeled nodes propagates rewards to unlabeled rollouts.”; [section 3.1] “query‑query edges are established using the top‑k cosine similarity between query embeddings.”; [section 4.4] Ablation shows the MLP baseline (no graph) drops 3.6% on QA vs. full model; query‑neighbor correctness alignment is 66–86% on math/QA.  
- **Break condition**: If the similarity metric fails to connect queries with truly correlated rewards (e.g., surface similarity masks deep structural differences), propagation introduces noise. Evidenced by performance drop when query‑query edges are removed or randomized.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: The heterogeneous node/edge design preserves multi‑step reasoning structure (query → thinking → answer), which is critical for accurate reward prediction beyond shallow similarity.  
- **Mechanism**: Type‑specific message passing (query‑thinking, thinking‑answer edges) allows the GNN to model distinct relational dynamics: query‑to‑thinking links capture strategy relevance, while thinking‑answer links encode solution correctness. This hierarchical signal is more granular than query‑query alone.  
- **Core assumption**: The quality of a rollout’s answer is determined by the validity of its intermediate thinking process, and this structure is consistent across tasks.  
- **Evidence anchors**: [section 3.1] Defines three node types and three edge types with separate weight matrices (W_qq, W_qt, W_ta).; [section 4.4] Ablating thinking nodes causes largest drop on Code (5.0% on 3B) and Math (2.5% on 3B), showing structural nodes carry essential signal.; [section C.4] GNN score separation is highest for math (0.60), where thinking steps are most diagnostic.  
- **Break condition**: If thinking traces are noisy, uninformative, or not systematically correlated with answer correctness (e.g., for tasks where final answer is independent of process), the thinking node design provides diminishing returns. Out‑of‑domain gains suggest some robustness.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Offline GNN warmup on a static graph of initial rollouts provides a reusable reward predictor that generalizes to online‑generated rollouts via top‑k similarity linking.  
- **Mechanism**: The GNN is trained once on labeled rollouts from the initial policy. During online GRPO, new (unlabeled) queries are dynamically connected to the warmup graph based on embedding similarity, and rewards are predicted without updating the GNN. This decouples reward prediction from policy updates, avoiding instability.  
- **Core assumption**: The reward function is relatively stable across policy iterations; similarity‑based linking is sufficient to ground new queries in the existing reward manifold.  
- **Evidence anchors**: [section 3.2] “During online policy optimization, unlabeled rollouts are connected to labeled rollouts via cosine similarity… rewards are predicted by aggregating information from labeled neighbors.”; [section 4.3] Out‑of‑domain generalization (e.g., NuminaMath) succeeds without domain‑specific fine‑tuning of the GNN, supporting transferability.; [section 4.5] Performance scales smoothly from 20% to 70% labels, indicating the warmup model remains effective as policy improves.  
- **Break condition**: If policy iterations induce distribution shift so severe that new rollouts are dissimilar to the warmup graph (e.g., novel reasoning strategies), top‑k linking fails, and predicted rewards become misaligned. Could manifest as plateauing or degradation beyond certain training steps.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:
1. Graph Builder: Takes initial policy rollouts, encodes nodes (query/thinking/answer via frozen LLM/SBERT), builds query‑query similarity edges (top‑k cosine, k=7) and structural edges (query‑thinking, thinking‑answer).
2. GNN Trainer: Uses labeled subset to train heterogeneous GNN (2‑layer GATv2, hidden 512, 4 heads, dropout 0.1) with BCE loss; outputs per‑rollout reward scores.
3. Online Reward Integrator: During GRPO, for each batch: if query labeled → use ground truth; else → connect to warmup graph via top‑k similarity, run frozen GNN, threshold score (>0.5) to binary reward.
4. GRPO Trainer: Uses mixed rewards to update policy, with standard advantage normalization and KL penalty.

Critical path: Warmup graph construction → GNN training → Online GRPO loop where unlabeled queries link to graph → GNN inference → reward assignment → policy gradient step. The GNN is frozen after warmup.

Design tradeoffs:
- Graph dynamism vs. stability: Static warmup graph avoids catastrophic forgetting but may become stale as policy improves; dynamic graph updating was not explored.
- Edge density: top‑k=7 balances connectivity and noise; higher k risks irrelevant neighbors, lower k risks isolation.
- Node representation: Using frozen sentence embeddings (all‑MiniLM‑L6‑v2) is efficient but may not capture task‑specific nuances; fine‑tuning encoders could help but adds complexity.

Failure signatures:
- Low average neighbor correctness (<50% in top‑k) → GNN predictions become noisy (check validation ROC‑AUC).
- Large gap between GNN‑predicted score means for correct/incorrect (<0.3 separation) → thresholding yields high error.
- Out‑of‑domain performance collapse → indicates graph topology doesn’t transfer; may need domain adaptation.

First 3 experiments:
1. GNN validation ROC‑AUC: Train GNN on 20% labeled data, compute ROC‑AUC on held‑out 20%. Target >0.85 (paper reports 0.917). If <<0.7, graph construction or representation is inadequate.
2. Ablation: remove thinking nodes: Retrain and evaluate on a small validation set. Expect >2% drop on math/code tasks. Larger drop confirms structural nodes are critical.
3. Out‑of‑domain probe: Test GNN predictions on a held‑out domain (e.g., NuminaMath) without any in‑domain fine‑tuning. If accuracy is near random (<55%), the graph’s similarity metric does not transfer; reconsider encoder or edge construction.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: Does MemReward maintain its label efficiency when applied to large language models (e.g., >10B parameters) and diverse architectures beyond Qwen2.5?  
- **Basis in paper**: Experiments were conducted only on Qwen2.5‑1.5B and 3B; no larger models or other families were evaluated.  
- **Why unresolved**: The graph construction and GNN training might scale differently with model size and embedding quality, limiting generalizability to state‑of‑the‑art LLMs.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Repeating the core experiments on models such as Llama 2 7B/13B/70B and Mistral across the same benchmarks to measure the percentage of Oracle performance achieved with limited labels.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Can MemReward be adapted to tasks that do not naturally separate a “thinking process” from a final answer, such as open‑ended generation or dialogue?  
- **Basis in paper**: The conclusion identifies extending MemReward to “more complex reasoning tasks” as future work, implying the current graph design may not directly apply.  
- **Why unresolved**: The heterogeneous graph relies on distinct thinking and answer nodes; tasks without an explicit reasoning trace lack this structure, requiring novel node/edge definitions or alternative memory representations.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Designing and evaluating modified graph schemas for non‑CoT tasks (e.g., utterance‑level nodes in dialogue) and demonstrating similar label efficiency on such benchmarks.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: What causes MemReward to sometimes achieve higher performance than fully‑supervised Oracle on out‑of‑domain benchmarks?  
- **Basis in paper**: Section 4.3 reports that MemReward exceeds Oracle on average for both model scales on out‑of‑domain tasks, but offers no explanation.  
- **Why unresolved**: The underlying mechanism—whether due to graph‑based regularization, aggregation of diverse reasoning patterns, or other factors—is unclear, hindering theoretical understanding and further improvement.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Ablation studies on out‑of‑domain data, e.g., removing query‑query edges, varying graph connectivity, or comparing with other regularization methods, to isolate the key factor driving the observed generalization boost.

## Limitations
- Exact implementation of dynamic graph linking during online GRPO (e.g., nearest‑neighbor search) is unspecified.  
- Calibration of the binary reward threshold (0.5) and score distributions are not detailed.  
- The static warmup graph may become stale as the policy improves, reducing neighbor relevance over time.

## Confidence
- Label‑efficient RL fine‑tuning: High.
- Heterogeneous graph design: Medium.
- Offline warmup generalizability: Medium.

## Next Checks
1. Compute top‑k neighbor reward agreement: For a validation set, measure the percentage of top‑7 similar queries sharing the same binary reward. If below 70%, the similarity‑based propagation assumption is weak.
2. Compare frozen vs. periodically updated GNN: During online GRPO, fine‑tune the GNN on newly labeled rollouts (if available) and evaluate policy performance difference.
3. Replace the frozen encoder with a task‑tuned version (e.g., fine‑tune all‑MiniLM‑L6‑v2 on in‑domain data) and assess changes in GNN ROC‑AUC and final RL accuracy, especially on out‑of‑domain tasks.
