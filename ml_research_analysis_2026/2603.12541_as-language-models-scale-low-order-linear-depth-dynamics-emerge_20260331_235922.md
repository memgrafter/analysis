# As Language Models Scale, Low-order Linear Depth Dynamics Emerge
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.12541
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12541
- **Reference count**: 28
- **Primary result**: A 32‑dimensional linear surrogate (LLV) approximates transformer depth dynamics with near‑perfect correlation (Spearman ρ ~ 0.99–1.00) on GPT‑2‑large, and surrogate fidelity improves monotonically with model scale.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a novel systems‑level analysis of transformer‑based language models by treating their depth as a discrete‑time dynamical system. Within a frozen context, the layer‑to‑layer transition map is linearized around the operating trajectory, and the high‑dimensional hidden state is projected onto a compact 32‑dimensional basis that includes the concept direction and a reachability‑informed Krylov complement. The resulting Linear Layer Variant (LLV) model accurately predicts how interventions at individual layers influence a final output concept. Across ten binary natural language tasks and three GPT‑2 model sizes (124M, 355M, 774M), the LLV reproduces the full model’s layerwise sensitivity profile with Spearman correlations rising from ~0.77 to ~0.995 as scale increases. Moreover, because the LLV is analytically tractable, it yields multi‑layer control policies that achieve a target concept shift with significantly less intervention energy than uniform or last‑layer‑only baselines. This work therefore provides a new lens into large language models—offering both interpretability through a low‑dimensional linear surrogate and practical benefits for efficient steering.

## Method Summary
The identification of the LLV surrogate proceeds in several stages. First, the dataset is partitioned into three balanced splits: a concept set used to estimate a layer‑wise “concept direction” vℓ (the normalized difference between class‑conditional mean activations of the last token), an operating set to estimate Jacobians of the frozen‑context last‑token map fℓ, and an evaluation set for validation. For each layer ℓ and each operating prompt p, the Jacobian Aℓ(p) is computed via finite differences or Jacobian‑vector products. A reduced basis Pℓ is constructed with first column vℓ; the remaining 31 columns form a Krylov subspace generated by repeatedly applying the mean Jacobian to the seed Aℓvℓ, while orthogonalizing against subsequent concept directions. The LLV dynamics in reduced coordinates rℓ are rℓ₊₁ ≈ Ãℓ rℓ + B̃ℓ uℓ, where Ãℓ = Pℓ₊₁ᵀ Aℓ Pℓ and B̃ℓ = Pℓ₊₁ᵀ Aℓ vℓ. The predicted gain for an intervention at layer k is g_predₖ(p) = C Φ(k+1, L) B̃ₖ, with Φ the state transition matrix; gains are averaged over the operating prompts. Empirical gains g_empₖ are obtained by injecting ±ϵvℓ before block k on held‑out prompts (ϵ = 0.1) and measuring the central difference of the final concept score. The correlation between predicted and empirical gains validates the surrogate. For control, the system’s impulse response h (the sum of step responses) is used to design a minimum‑energy input u* ∝ h that yields a desired output shift Δy; the required scale is found via bisection in the full model. The authors evaluate this pipeline on GPT‑2, GPT‑2‑medium, and GPT‑2‑large across ten binary tasks, finding monotonic improvement in surrogate fidelity with scale and significant energy savings for multi‑layer steering.

## Key Results
- The 32‑dimensional LLV reproduces GPT‑2‑large’s layerwise steering sensitivity profile with near‑perfect agreement (Spearman ρ ~ 0.99–1.00).
- For the fixed order d=32, agreement improves monotonically with model size: mean Spearman rises from ~0.77 (GPT‑2) to ~0.81 (GPT‑2‑medium) to ~0.995 (GPT‑2‑large) across ten tasks.
- The LLV‑optimal multi‑layer intervention achieves a target concept shift with significantly less ℓ2 injection energy than uniform‑all or last‑layer‑only baseline schedules.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1**  
*Claim*: Frozen‑context transformer depth dynamics are locally linear and low‑dimensional.  
*Mechanism*: For a prompt p, fix all non‑last tokens, defining a map fℓ from last‑token state xℓ to xℓ₊₁. Compute the Jacobian Aℓ(p) at the operating trajectory. Project onto a reduced basis Pℓ (concept direction + Krylov complement) to obtain the LLV surrogate: rℓ₊₁ ≈ Ãℓ rℓ + B̃ℓ uℓ.  
*Core assumption*: Small perturbations yield linear effects, and the steering‑relevant subspace is ~32‑dimensional.  
*Evidence anchors*: Abstract states “a 32‑dimensional linear surrogate reproduces the layerwise sensitivity profile… agreement improves monotonically with model scale.” Section 2 (page 5) details frozen‑context map, linearization, and projection to LLV.  
*Break condition*: Large perturbations, highly nonlinear operating regions, or poor basis choice degrade accuracy.

**Mechanism 2**  
*Claim*: A reachability‑informed Krylov subspace captures steering‑relevant dynamics better than a random complement.  
*Mechanism*: Construct Pℓ with first column equal to the concept direction vℓ. The complement is a Krylov space: start from seed Aℓvℓ, iteratively apply mean Jacobians, orthogonalize against later vℓ′, and orthonormalize. This sliding product‑Krylov privileges directions actually excited by steering.  
*Core assumption*: The mean Jacobian actions approximate a prompt‑invariant reachable subspace; the Krylov span is effective.  
*Evidence anchors*: Abstract mentions “remaining columns are chosen by a reachability‑informed Krylov construction.” Page 6 states that the sliding product‑Krylov basis “privileges directions actually excited… gives a more faithful reduced model.”  
*Break condition*: High variance of Jacobians across prompts; a weak concept direction leads to divergence from the true reachable subspace.

**Mechanism 3**  
*Claim*: As model size increases (within the GPT‑2 family), the local depth dynamics become more compressible, and a fixed‑order LLV surrogate matches the full model more accurately (monotonic scaling).  
*Mechanism*: Larger width and redundancy likely stabilize Jacobian responses and align concept directions with a few dominant modes, improving low‑order fidelity.  
*Core assumption*: Scale systematically enhances linear approximability without altering the local regime.  
*Evidence anchors*: Abstract: “agreement improves monotonically with model size across the GPT‑2 family.” Figure 3 and page 10 show mean Spearman rising from ~0.77 (GPT‑2) to ~0.995 (GPT‑2‑large); page 11 offers the width/redundancy intuition.  
*Break condition*: The trend may not hold for other architectures or at extreme scales if nonlinearities dominate.

## Foundational Learning
- **Local linearization of nonlinear systems**  
  *Why needed*: The LLV surrogate relies on the Jacobian to approximate the transformer’s discrete‑time dynamics near an operating point.

  *Why needed*: The high‑dimensional hidden state is projected onto a low‑dimensional basis to form a tractable surrogate; understanding projection is essential to grasp how the LLV is built.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Data pipeline → Concept direction estimation → Jacobian estimation → Krylov basis construction → Reduced model assembly → Gain prediction → Control design → Validation  

**Critical path**:  
1. Prepare data splits and compute concept directions vℓ.  
2. Compute Jacobians Aℓ(p) for a batch of operating prompts.  
3. Build Krylov bases Pℓ using mean Jacobians.  
4. Form reduced matrices and predicted gains.  
5. Check agreement with empirical gains; proceed if high.  
6. Compute LLV‑optimal control policy and evaluate its energy.  

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- Reduced order d: larger d improves accuracy but increases computation; d=32 saturates performance.  
- Krylov vs. random complement: Krylov yields better prediction, especially when concept direction is not dominant.  
- Perturbation magnitude ϵ: must stay in linear regime; paper uses ϵ=0.1 robustly across {0.01–0.5}.  
- Operating prompt batch size: larger batches improve mean Jacobian estimate at higher cost.  
- Finite‑difference vs. JVP: central differences more accurate but double evaluations; JVPs efficient if available.  

**Failure signatures**:  
- Low prediction correlation (Spearman < 0.8).  
- LLV‑optimal control using more energy than uniform baseline.  
- High variance of Jacobian estimates across prompts.  
- Krylov basis ill‑conditioned or not spanning relevant directions.  
- Absence of monotonic scaling trend across model sizes.  

**First 3 experiments**:  
1. Replicate gain‑prediction results on GPT‑2‑large (all ten datasets) achieving Spearman ρ ≥ 0.99.  
2. Conduct scaling analysis on GPT‑2, GPT‑2‑medium, GPT‑2‑large with d=32; verify mean Spearman rises from ~0.77 to ~0.995.  
3. Compare minimal‑energy steering: for each dataset and target Δy, confirm LLV‑optimal policy requires less energy than uniform‑all and last‑layer‑only baselines.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **What mechanistic factors cause identifiability of low‑order linear surrogates to improve with transformer model scale?**  
   The paper observes the scaling trend but only speculates on mechanisms like width and redundancy; controlled experiments varying only width or depth are needed to isolate causal factors.

2. **Does the emergence of accurate low‑order linear depth dynamics generalize to transformer architectures beyond the GPT‑2 family?**  
   The study is limited to three GPT‑2 models; other architectures (e.g., LLaMA, Mistral, T5) may exhibit different depth‑wise computation patterns, so extension is required.

3. **To what extent are the identified local linear surrogates specific to the prompt context used for identification, and can they generalize to new prompts or tasks without re‑identification?**  
   The surrogate is deliberately local to the prompt distribution; cross‑context or cross‑task transferability is not explored.

4. **Is the fixed reduced order d=32 optimal across all model scales and tasks, or does the required order for high‑fidelity surrogates systematically vary with model size or concept complexity?**  
   The paper holds d constant; the saturation point of correlation versus d might change with scale, which remains untested.

## Limitations
- Evaluation restricted to GPT‑2 models on ten binary English tasks; generalization to other architectures (e.g., LLaMA, T5) or multilingual/multimodal settings is unknown.  
- The linearity assumption may not hold for large perturbations or prompts drawn from out‑of‑distribution contexts; robustness beyond the studied regime is unclear.  
- Details of the Krylov basis construction are underspecified, which may hinder exact replication.

## Confidence
- *Local linearity and low order (d=32)*: High for GPT‑2‑large (ρ ≥ 0.99), Medium for smaller models (ρ ~ 0.77–0.81).  
- *Krylov basis superiority*: Medium (limited ablation evidence).  
- *Monotonic scaling*: Medium (only three GPT‑2 sizes tested).

## Next Checks
1. **Cross‑architecture test**: Apply the LLV pipeline to LLaMA‑7B and report gain prediction correlation on the same ten tasks.  
2. **Krylov ablation**: Compare Krylov complement against random, PCA, and fixed bases over multiple random seeds; quantify correlation improvements.
