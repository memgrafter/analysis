# "Newspaper Eat" Means "Not Tasty": A Taxonomy and Benchmark for Coded Languages in Real-World Chinese Online Reviews

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.19932  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.19932  
- **Reference count:** 17  
- **Primary result:** Introduces CODEDLANG (7,744 Chinese reviews, 900 coded) with a 7-class taxonomy; LLMs show detection F1 0.56–0.78, classification F1 0.08–0.84, and rating prediction error rises with phonetic distortion (Spearman ρ=0.80).

## Executive Summary
Coded language—slang, obfuscation, or playful substitutions—is pervasive in Chinese online reviews but poorly understood by current language models. This paper presents CODEDLANG, a dataset of 7,744 real-world Google Maps reviews with span-level annotations for 900 reviews containing coded expressions, organized under a novel seven-class taxonomy. Benchmarking state-of-the-art LLMs reveals systematic failures: detection and classification are highly variable across encoding strategies, and rating prediction error increases dramatically for coded and masked reviews. A phonetic analysis further shows that greater pronunciation distortion between coded and decoded forms correlates with higher rating prediction error, underscoring the challenges posed by phonological opacity.

## Method Summary
CODEDLANG was built via an iterative bootstrapping process: starting from a small seed dictionary of known coded spans, the authors performed string matching across a large corpus, followed by human validation and span extraction to expand the dictionary. This human-in-the-loop approach yielded 184 unique coded spans from a screening pool of ~118k reviews. The dataset includes precise span boundaries and decoded meanings, and a seven-class taxonomy (e.g., ambiguous homophone, cross-lingual phonetic encoding). The authors evaluated LLMs on three tasks: binary detection of coded reviews, multi-label classification of encoding strategies (with required evidence spans), and rating prediction (1–5 stars) under five conditions: non-coded, coded, character-masked, span-masked, and decoded. Phonetic distortion was quantified via Character Error Rate (CER) between coded and decoded spans using Pinyin/IPA conversions, and correlated with rating prediction error.

## Key Results
- Detection F1 across models: 0.56–0.78.  
- Classification F1 per class: 0.08 (cross‑lingual phonetic) to 0.84 (cipher).  
- Rating prediction MSE increases for coded and masked reviews; decoded reviews often yield higher error than masks for intermediate ratings.  
- Greater phonetic distortion (higher CER) correlates with higher rating prediction error (Spearman ρ=0.80).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** A bootstrapping process using a small seed dictionary of known coded spans can effectively surface a substantial portion of real-world coded language instances from a large corpus.
- **Mechanism:** An initial manually‑annotated seed set of coded reviews is used to extract specific annotated text spans into a dictionary. This dictionary is then used for string matching across a massive corpus of reviews. Validated matches are re‑annotated to extract *new* spans, which are added to the dictionary for the next iteration. This iterative loop expands the dictionary's coverage and surfaces more coded reviews until convergence.
- **Core assumption:** While coded language is rare (~0.35% in pilot study), a non‑trivial subset of expressions are reused sufficiently often that a dictionary‑based approach can capture them. The phenomenon is not entirely amorphous or invented de novo for each review.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “Progress has been limited by the lack of real‑world datasets… We introduce CODEDLANG… using an iterative, human‑in‑the‑loop mining process.”
  - [section 3.2] Full description of the “Iterative Bootstraping” process, seed dictionary construction from pilot annotations, and use of an external homophone dictionary.
  - [corpus] Final dataset: 900 coded reviews discovered from a screening pool of ~118k Chinese reviews. The 23‑seed set expanded to 184 unique coded spans.
- **Break condition:** The process would fail to capture highly novel, one‑off, or personalized codes that do not share surface forms with the seed dictionary or external lexicons. The paper explicitly notes this as a key limitation: “If a coded expression never appeared in the seed dictionaries… it was missed.”

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Phonetic distortion—quantified via Character Error Rate (CER) between coded and decoded forms—is conditionally associated with increased error in downstream tasks like rating prediction for language models.
- **Mechanism:** Models process the literal surface text of the coded review. Greater phonetic divergence between this surface form and the intended meaning (high CER) means the model’s internal representations (often trained on standard, literal text) are more misaligned with the true semantic and sentiment signal. This misalignment manifests as higher prediction error (MSE) for the user’s star rating.
- **Core assumption:** Current LLMs primarily rely on lexical and surface‑level pattern matching from their training data rather than performing explicit, robust phonological decoding to recover intended meaning from non‑standard phonetic substitutions.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 5] “We found that greater phonetic distortion… was associated with higher rating prediction error… The Spearman’s ρ between MSE and average CER reached 0.80.”
  - [section 4.3] Table 4 and Figure 3 show consistent MSE increases from non‑coded → coded → masked reviews, with especially high error for cross‑lingual phonetic encoding (highest CER).
  - [corpus] The analysis in Section 5.1 shows classes with high CER (Cross‑Lingual: 1.23/2.46, Phonetic Substitution: 0.61/0.68) also show among the highest rating prediction MSE (0.86, 0.56/0.51).
- **Break condition:** The correlation (ρ=0.80) is noted as “modest” and non‑significant (p=0.104). The mechanism breaks down if models develop stronger intrinsic phonetic reasoning capabilities or if the dataset’s specific phonetic patterns are not generalizable. The paper also notes the Cipher class, which is not phonetic, still hurts rating prediction.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A clear, mutually‑intentioned taxonomy of encoding strategies (e.g., homophone vs. cipher) provides a stable target for multi‑label classification, enabling the measurement of model failure modes per strategy.
- **Mechanism:** The taxonomy defines discrete, annotator‑agreeable categories based on the primary encoding mechanism (e.g., homophonic, orthographic). Span‑level annotations link specific text to a primary mechanism. Models are prompted to output a list of classes and evidence spans. Performance variance across classes (Table 3) is interpretable as a function of each strategy’s inherent opacity and prevalence, rather than task ambiguity.
- **Core assumption:** The encoding mechanisms are conceptually distinct enough that human annotators can consistently assign a single primary label per span, and that this label reflects a genuine cognitive/linguistic distinction relevant to model processing.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “a seven‑class taxonomy… Benchmarking… shows significant failure: detection F1… classification F1 varies widely by category (0.08 for cross‑lingual phonetic… to 0.84 for cipher).”
  - [section 3.3] Presents the final converged taxonomy with clear definitions and examples, noting it was “inductively refined” through annotator discussion.
  - [section 4.2] Reports strong inter‑annotator agreement (Cohen’s kappa for review‑level detection = 0.99, 98% span overlap), supporting the taxonomy’s operational stability.
  - [corpus] Figure 2 shows the distribution across the 7 classes. Table 3 shows the resulting per‑class F1 scores for DeepSeek‑V3.2, demonstrating the variance the taxonomy was designed to expose.
- **Break condition:** Overlap or ambiguity between categories (e.g., a phrase that is both a non‑lexical homophone *and* an orthographic substitution) would cause inconsistent annotation and muddy performance interpretation. The multi‑label setup partially addresses this but the primary label assignment remains a potential source of noise.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Coded Language Taxonomy (7‑class)**  
  *Why needed here:* Provides the target schema for the classification task. Understanding the distinctions (e.g., *Ambiguous Homophone* vs. *Non‑Lexical Homophone*) is essential to interpret *why* a model fails on specific strategies.  
  *Quick check question:* What is the primary difference between “炒鸡→超级” (Ambiguous Homophone) and “灰常→非常” (Non‑Lexical Homophone)?  
  **Answer:** The former’s surface form is a *valid lexical phrase*; the latter’s is *not*.

- **Concept: Span‑Level Annotation**  
  *Why needed here:* Coded language is often a phrase‑internal phenomenon. Detection and classification require identifying the exact disrupted text unit, not just the whole review. This is critical for evaluation precision and for training/analysis.  
  *Quick check question:* In the review “这家餐厅真的`虾`到爆”, where is the coded span and what is its class?  
  **Answer:** Span=”虾”, Class=Orthographic Substitution, as it stands for “瞎”.

- **Concept: Phonetic Error Rate (CER) for Analysis**  
  *Why needed here:* To move beyond intuition and quantify *how much* phonetic distortion exists between coded form and intended meaning. This allows for correlating a continuous measure of “encoding strength” with model performance (rating prediction error).  
  *Quick check question:* If a coded span “tm” maps to “他妈”, a Pinyin representation might be [“t”, “m”] vs [“t”, “a”, “m”, “a”]. What is the likely CER direction (higher/lower) and what does it imply?  
  **Answer:** Higher CER due to deletions of vowels; implies greater phonetic distortion.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:**  
  Corpus Ingestion → Seed Dictionary → Bootstrapping Engine → Taxonomy Module → Annotation Interface/Process → Benchmark Suite → Phonetic Analysis Pipeline

- **Critical path:** The most resource‑intensive and foundational step is **Bootstrapping & Annotation** (Seed Dictionary → Bootstrapping Engine → Taxonomy Module → Annotation Interface). The quality and coverage of the final `CODEDLANG` dataset (7,744 reviews, 900 coded, 184 spans) are entirely dependent on this human‑in‑the‑loop process. All downstream benchmarks and analyses are only as valid as this curated dataset.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Precision vs. Coverage in Bootstrapping:** Strict string matching yields high‑precision candidates but misses novel forms. The paper expands coverage by incorporating an external homophone dictionary and allowing annotator intuition to find “seed” new patterns, trading some recall for manageable annotation effort.
  - **Taxonomy Granularity:** The 7‑class system is interpretable but may force spans into a single primary label even when multiple encoding strategies overlap (e.g., a phrase that is both a homophone *and* visually similar). The multi‑label allowance for a review helps, but span‑level single‑label is a simplification.
  - **Rating Prediction Setup:** Comparing MSE across *different* text transformations (coded vs. masked vs. decoded) isolates the effect of coding but confounds it with text length/perturbation. The “Decoded” condition assumes a perfect, context‑insensitive dictionary lookup, which may not reflect how a human decoder would work.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Dataset Construction:** Low agreement on *new* coded forms not resembling the seed dictionary. Rapid saturation (no new spans in an iteration) indicating the dictionary has hit its coverage ceiling for the corpus.
  - **Detection Task:** High recall but low precision (Gemini‑2.5‑Flash) indicates over‑triggering on noise (maybe dialect, misspellings). High precision but low recall (Qwen2.5‑7B) indicates being overly conservative.
  - **Classification Task:** Abysmal F1 (<0.20) on low‑resource classes like *Cross‑Lingual Phonetic Encoding* (N=7) signals the model cannot learn the pattern from so few examples. The wide performance spread (0.08 to 0.84) confirms the taxonomy captures meaningful difficulty differences.
  - **Rating Prediction:** *Lower* MSE on `[CODE]`‑masked reviews vs. *decoded* reviews for intermediate ratings (Figure 3) is a counter‑intuitive failure signature. It suggests that literal decoding can introduce misleading cues or that models are better at recognizing “missing signal” (mask) than interpreting a potentially incorrect decoded string.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Reproduce Detection & Classification Benchmarks:** Run the exact prompts (Appendix A.4.1) on the provided `CODEDLANG` test set for the listed models. Verify the reported F1 ranges (Detect: 0.56–0.78; Class: 0.08–0.84). This establishes a baseline.
  2. **Replicate Phonetic Analysis:** For the ~800 coded spans (excluding Cipher/Orthographic), compute CER using the same Pinyin/IPA tools cited. Re‑calculate the Spearman correlation between per‑review average CER and GPT‑5‑mini’s rating MSE. Does ρ≈0.80 hold? This validates the core phonetic distortion claim.
  3. **Category‑Ablation Study:** Take the best‑performing model on *Cipher* (F1=0.84) and worst on *Cross‑Lingual* (F1=0.08). Manually inspect 20 error pairs for each. For *Cipher*, are errors due to misclassifying non‑cipher gibberish as cipher? For *Cross‑Lingual*, are errors due to failing to recognize tiny borrowed words (e.g., “古德”) or misinterpreting them as typos? This probes *why* the taxonomy differences manifest.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can the coverage of CODEDLANG be expanded to systematically capture rare, highly novel, or rapidly evolving coded expressions that fall outside the current bootstrapping dictionary?
- **Basis in paper:** “our data curation relied on an iterative bootstrapping process that was inherently constrained by what LLMs and existing lexicons could surface. If a coded expression never appeared in the seed dictionaries… it was missed. This bias was difficult to avoid when constructing datasets for low‑frequency linguistic phenomena and fundamentally limited coverage of rare, creative, or highly novel forms.”
- **Why unresolved:** The current data collection method is fundamentally reactive and dependent on initial seeds and model capabilities, creating a coverage gap for emergent or idiosyncratic coding strategies that do not resemble known patterns.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A new dataset construction methodology that combines crowdsourced or community‑based reporting (e.g., from social media platforms where codes are shared) with active learning or unsupervised anomaly detection to surface low‑frequency patterns, validated by a diverse panel of annotators with different community knowledge bases.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** To what extent do different annotator groups, with varying linguistic backgrounds or community insidership, disagree on the boundaries of the seven‑class taxonomy and the identification of coded spans, and how does this disagreement impact model performance benchmarks?
- **Basis in paper:** “annotation and interpretation were inevitably shaped by the annotators’ linguistic backgrounds and community knowledge… it remained possible that a different group of researchers… might have made different judgments about which cases to include or exclude.” Inter‑annotator agreement was high (kappa 0.99 for review‑level detection, 98% span overlap) but measured between two trained native‑speaking linguists.
- **Why unresolved:** The reported agreement may not generalize to annotations by non‑linguists or speakers from different Chinese dialect communities (e.g., Cantonese vs. Mandarin). Taxonomic boundaries (e.g., ambiguous homophone vs. non‑lexical homophone) are inherently fuzzy and culturally situated.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A multi‑round annotation study involving multiple annotator cohorts (e.g., native speakers from different regions, non‑native speakers, domain experts vs. crowd workers) on a common subset of reviews. Measuring both inter‑annotator agreement and the variance in model performance when trained/evaluated on annotations from each cohort.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Why do character‑masked or span‑masked reviews sometimes yield lower rating prediction error than fully decoded reviews for intermediate ratings (2–4 stars), and what does this imply about the sufficiency of literal semantic decoding for sentiment understanding in coded language?
- **Basis in paper:** “masked reviews have lower error than decoded and coded reviews” for intermediate ratings, and notes that “literal decoding does not always clarify intent.” This is counterintuitive if decoding is assumed to always clarify meaning.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper speculates that “coded expressions can introduce misleading surface cues,” but does not investigate *why* the decoded text might be more confusing or ambiguous than the mask. Possibilities include: (a) decoded forms might be nonsensical or out‑of‑context without the original coded framing; (b) the act of masking forces the model to focus on remaining context; (c) decoded forms may inadvertently introduce incorrect sentiment polarity if the decoding dictionary is imperfect or context‑dependent.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A fine‑grained error analysis comparing the decoded reviews to the original coded reviews and masks, identifying cases where decoding produces grammatically odd, contextually inappropriate, or sentiment‑reversed phrases. Also, human evaluation of decoded review intelligibility and sentiment compared to coded and masked versions.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Do models struggle with *phonetic distortion* (measured by CER) because of an inherent inability to perform phonological reasoning, or because they lack the cross‑lingual/socio‑cultural knowledge required to map distorted phonetic forms to their conventional decoded meanings?
- **Basis in paper:** “greater pronunciation distortion… correlates with higher rating prediction error (Spearman’s ρ=0.80)” and poor classification on cross‑lingual and phonetic substitution classes. However, the correlation is not statistically significant (p=0.104) due to small sample sizes, especially for cross‑lingual encoding.
- **Why unresolved:** The analysis conflates *phonetic distortion* with *decoding difficulty*. A high CER could indicate a complex code that requires specific cultural knowledge (e.g., a transliteration from Japanese “kawaii” → “卡哇伊”) rather than pure phonological processing. Without isolating the factors of phonological complexity vs. knowledge required, we cannot pinpoint the core model deficiency.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An ablation study where models are tested on (a) phonetically distorted but culturally familiar codes vs. (b) phonetically simple but culturally obscure codes, controlling for distortion level. Additionally, probing internal model representations to see if they activate phonological or lexical/semantic pathways when processing coded spans.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** How does the trade‑off between expressiveness and interpretability in coded language manifest as codes evolve from niche, opaque forms to widely shared memes, and how do model performance trajectories differ across this lifecycle?
- **Basis in paper:** “Codes that are easy to recognize, humorous, or representative are more likely to be widely adopted… but their obfuscatory… function weakens as they spread. In contrast, niche or opaque codes preserve stronger hiding effects but require greater interpretive effort.” The dataset shows a long‑tail distribution of coded spans.
- **Why unresolved:** CODEDLANG is a static snapshot. It captures some frequently used codes (e.g., “滴”) and many rare ones, but does not track how a specific code’s usage frequency, form, or interpretability changes over time. The relationship between popularity (e.g., frequency, spread across platforms) and model accuracy (detection, decoding) is not analyzed.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A longitudinal study tracking a set of emerging coded expressions across multiple time points and platforms (e.g., from initial niche use on a forum to mainstream use on Google Reviews). Measuring both human interpretability (via crowdsourcing) and model performance at each stage to see if popular codes become easier for models (due to more training data) but lose their “code” function.

## Limitations
- Dataset coverage is limited by the bootstrapping process; rare, novel, or highly personalized codes are likely missed.
- The correlation between phonetic distortion (CER) and rating prediction error is modest (ρ=0.80) and statistically non‑significant (p=0.104), weakening causal claims.
- The single‑label span annotation oversimplifies cases where multiple encoding strategies overlap, potentially adding noise to classification metrics.
