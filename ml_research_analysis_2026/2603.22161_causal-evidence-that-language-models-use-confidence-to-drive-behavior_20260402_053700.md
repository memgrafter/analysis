# Causal Evidence that Language Models use Confidence to Drive Behavior
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.22161
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22161
- Reference count: 38
- Primary result: Large language models employ internal confidence signals metacognitively to decide when to abstain, applying an implicit threshold; confidence is the strongest predictor (∼10× effect size of alternatives), causal manipulations confirm its central role, and threshold instructions systematically shift abstention.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether large language models (LLMs) use internal confidence as a metacognitive signal to decide when to answer or abstain from multiple-choice questions. Through a four-phase paradigm—measuring natural abstention, analyzing predictors, causally steering confidence, and modulating explicit thresholds—the authors demonstrate that LLMs rely on an implicit confidence threshold: confidence outweighs other factors (question difficulty, retrieval scores, semantic features) by an order of magnitude; boosting or suppressing confidence via activation steering changes abstention rates in a mediated manner (67.1% of effect explained by confidence redistribution); and instructing specific confidence thresholds systematically adjusts abstention behavior. Together, these findings reveal a two-stage metacognitive control architecture in LLMs, analogous to biological systems.

## Method Summary
The study employs a dataset of 1,000 four-option multiple-choice questions (SimpleQA) and several LLMs (GPT-4o, DeepSeek, Qwen, Gemma 3 27B). Phase 1 collects model answers without an abstention option and extracts logits; confidence scores are calibrated via temperature scaling on a separate set. Phase 2 introduces an abstention token (“5”) and records binary abstention outcomes. Logistic regression of abstention on calibrated confidence and alternative predictors (question difficulty, RAG similarity, PCA sentence embeddings) quantifies each factor's contribution. Phase 3 (Gemma 3 27B only) uses activation steering: high- and low-confidence steering vectors are derived from phase-2 activations, then injected at a selected layer with scaling factor α; resulting abstention and confidence changes are measured, and mediation analysis decomposes the effect. Phase 4 instructs the model to first produce an internal confidence rating (0–100%) and then answer if the rating exceeds a specified threshold T; varying T yields a mapping from instructed threshold to actual abstention, which is fit to a logistic model to recover policy parameters (scale, shift, temperature).

## Key Results
- Confidence is the dominant predictor of abstention; its standardized effect size is approximately ten times larger than that of question difficulty, RAG scores, or semantic features.
- Activation steering provides causal evidence: enhancing confidence reduces abstention, suppressing increases it; mediation analysis attributes 67.1% of the total effect to confidence redistribution and 26.2% to policy shifts, with a residual direct effect.
- Explicitly instructing confidence thresholds systematically modulates abstention rates, confirming that LLMs implement a threshold-based policy that can be externally shifted.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The results reveal a two-stage metacognitive architecture. Stage 1 generates an internal confidence signal from the model’s forward pass (e.g., max probability of the chosen option after calibration). Stage 2 applies an implicit threshold: if confidence exceeds the threshold, the model proceeds to answer; otherwise it abstains. The overwhelming predictive power of confidence, the directional changes induced by steering, and the systematic shifts with instructed thresholds all converge on this mechanism. Moreover, the policy appears adaptable: the weight given to confidence varies with instructions (scale and shift parameters), suggesting the threshold is not fixed but can be re-calibrated. This architecture mirrors biological systems where confidence monitors guide decisions under uncertainty.

## Foundational Learning
- **Confidence Calibration**  
  *Why needed:* Raw model probabilities are often overconfident; calibration aligns them with empirical accuracy to make confidence a reliable signal.  
  *Quick check:* After temperature scaling, plot binned confidence vs. error rate; the curve should be near-diagonal with low Expected Calibration Error (ECE).
- **Activation Steering**  
  *Why needed:* To test causal sufficiency of confidence by directly manipulating internal representations without changing weights.  
  *Quick check:* Inject high-confidence vector at the target layer; observe decreased abstention and increased measured confidence compared to baseline.
- **Logistic Regression for Behavioral Analysis**  
  *Why needed:* To quantify and compare the influence of multiple predictors on a binary outcome (abstention).  
  *Quick check:* Fit `abstain ~ confidence + difficulty + RAG + embeddings`; the standardized coefficient for confidence should be an order of magnitude larger than others.
- **Temperature Scaling**  
  *Why needed:* A simple post-hoc calibration method that multiplies logits by a learned temperature τ to reduce overconfidence.  
  *Quick check:* Optimize τ on a validation set to minimize ECE; τ > 1 typically softens the distribution.
- **Mediation Analysis**  
  *Why needed:* To decompose the effect of an intervention (steering) into the portion carried by changes in confidence (redistribution) and the portion altering the decision policy.  
  *Quick check:* In a parallel mediator model, the proportion mediated by confidence should be substantial (∼67% as reported).

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input (question + options) -> Token embedding -> Transformer layers (with optional steering injection at a specific layer) -> LM head (logits for tokens) -> Softmax -> Calibrated confidence (max probability after temperature scaling) -> Threshold comparison (implicit during generation or explicit post rating) -> Output (answer token or abstention token "5").

**Critical path:**  
The path from the initial input tokens through the transformer blocks to the LM head determines the output distribution. The confidence signal is extracted from that distribution and compared against a threshold; this comparison decides whether the model outputs an answer or abstains. Activation steering modifies the hidden states along this path, altering the confidence distribution and thus the final decision.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Calibration overhead:* Temperature scaling requires a held-out set but adds negligible runtime cost.  
- *Steering layer choice:* Earlier layers affect broader contextual representations; later layers more directly influence the final logits but may interfere with answer correctness.  
- *Prompt sensitivity:* Some models need specific phrasing to exhibit abstention; overly vague prompts yield near-zero abstention.  
- *Abstention coverage vs. accuracy:* A low implicit threshold yields high coverage but more errors; a high threshold ensures accuracy but leaves many questions unanswered. The policy parameters (scale, shift) balance this trade-off.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Phase 2 abstention rate near 0% or 100% indicates the prompt failed to elicit meaningful abstention behavior.  
- Poor calibration (high ECE) weakens the confidence-abstention link; binned confidence vs. error will show a jagged curve.  
- Steering produces no effect or opposite direction: likely the steering vectors were constructed from mismatched trials (e.g., not controlling for answer option or correctness).  
- In Phase 4, confidence ratings correlate strongly with the instructed threshold (“bandness index” shows horizontal bands), suggesting ratings are post-decisional rather than genuine pre-abstention confidence.

**Three first experiments:**  
1. **Natural abstention correlation:** Run Phase 2 (with abstention token) and compute Pearson’s r between calibrated confidence and binary abstention. Expect a strong negative correlation (r < -0.9).  
2. **Steering effect test:** For Gemma 3 27B, use the reported high-confidence steering vector and layer (e.g., layer 20). Inject with α = 1.0 and measure abstention rate. It should be significantly lower than baseline; vary α to confirm monotonic decrease.  
3. **Threshold modulation:** Execute Phase 4 with thresholds from 0% to 100% in 10% increments. Plot abstention rate versus threshold; the curve should be sigmoidal. Fit the logistic model to recover the policy’s scale and shift parameters and verify they match reported values.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1**  
*Question:* What is the precise computational mechanism within transformer architectures that generates the internal confidence signals used to guide abstention decisions?  
*Basis in paper:* The authors note that characterizing the exact computational architecture of confidence generation remains an important direction for future work.  
*Why unresolved:* While confidence signals are shown to exist and be used, the specific layers, attention heads, or pathways responsible for computing them are not identified. Steering vectors contrast activations but do not reveal the algorithmic process.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Targeted interpretability studies (e.g., activation patching, probing, circuit analysis) that trace information flow from input to confidence-reporting logits, and controlled ablations of candidate mechanisms (evidence accumulation vs. post-hoc monitoring).

**Open Question 2**  
*Question:* What are the specific factors (e.g., training data, optimization objectives, model scale, or inherent cost asymmetries) that cause the observed quantitative variations in abstention policies across different LLMs?  
*Basis in paper:* The paper documents substantial cross-model differences in implicit thresholds and scale parameters, and states that disentangling these contributions remains an open question.  
*Why unresolved:* The study does not systematically manipulate or test the origins of these quantitative policy differences; variations are only correlational across models of different sizes, training regimes, and architectures.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* A controlled training study where models with identical architectures are trained with varying objectives (e.g., different losses on abstention, RL rewards for accuracy vs. coverage), or a larger correlational study across a diverse model set linking policy parameters to model characteristics.

**Open Question 3**  
*Question:* What accounts for the residual direct effect of confidence steering on abstention behavior that remains after accounting for confidence redistribution and policy shifts in the mediation analysis?  
*Basis in paper:* The mediation analysis shows confidence redistribution explains 67.1% and policy shifts 26.2% of the total effect, leaving a significant direct effect (c' = -0.32). The authors speculate it may reflect nonlinear interactions or dynamic changes not captured.  
*Why unresolved:* The parallel mediator model assumes independence between confidence and policy pathways, but the residual indicates unmeasured mechanisms. The study did not test whether steering alters other aspects (e.g., entropy, attention patterns) or temporal dynamics.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* More fine-grained mediation models incorporating additional mediators (e.g., output entropy, layer-specific effects) or dynamic analyses tracking confidence and decision across layers. Experiments that isolate steering at early vs. late layers could clarify.

**Open Question 4**  
*Question:* Does the two-stage metacognitive architecture (confidence formation followed by threshold-based policy) generalize to all LLMs, or is it contingent on specific architectures, training procedures, or prompt formats that successfully elicit abstention?  
*Basis in paper:* The paradigm required prompt engineering for Gemma 3 27B, while other models responded to a standard prompt; some models (e.g., Llama 70B) rarely abstained. The authors note that appropriate prompt framing is critical for eliciting reliable abstention.  
*Why unresolved:* The paradigm was not uniformly effective across all tested models without modification, raising questions about whether the architecture is universal or an artifact of instruction-following capabilities.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Replicate the full four-phase paradigm across a broader range of architectures, sizes, and training paradigms using both standardized and model-specific prompts. If the two-stage pattern holds universally, it supports generality; otherwise it may depend on specific conditions.

## Limitations
- Incomplete reporting for Gemma’s Phase 4 prompts and activation steering parameters (exact layer matching criteria, α optimization) hampers full replication.  
- Temperature scaling calibration details (binning strategy, optimization method, validation procedure) are not fully specified, which could affect the calibrated confidence values and downstream analyses.  
- The study restricts evaluation to a single dataset (SimpleQA) and a multiple-choice format; it remains unknown whether the findings generalize to other tasks or open-ended generation.

## Confidence
- Implicit confidence threshold policy: **Medium** – supported by consistent regressions and threshold modulation, but prompt sensitivity and calibration dependence introduce uncertainty.  
- Confidence dominance over alternatives: **High** – effect sizes an order of magnitude larger across multiple models and analyses.  
- Causal role of confidence via steering: **Medium** – strong evidence from one model (Gemma 3 27B) but lacks replication across other models.  
- Threshold instruction modulation: **Medium** – systematic effects seen, but Phase 4 confidence may be post-decisional, questioning pre-decisional assumption.

## Next Checks
1. Replicate Phase 4 for all models (including Gemma) using the standardized prompt template, verify the logistic fit, and assess pre-decisional confidence with the bandness index.
2. Systematically map activation steering across layers and α values to identify the optimal layer; re-run mediation with stricter trial matching to confirm the 67.1% proportion.
3. Evaluate calibration robustness by applying multiple calibration methods (e.g., Platt scaling, isotonic regression) and checking stability of the confidence-abstention relationship.
