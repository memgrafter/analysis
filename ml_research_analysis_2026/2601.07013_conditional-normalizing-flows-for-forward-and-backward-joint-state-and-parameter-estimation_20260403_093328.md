# Conditional Normalizing Flows for Forward and Backward Joint State and Parameter Estimation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.07013
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.07013
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Conditional normalizing flows with an optimal-transport kinetic loss improve joint state and parameter estimation for non‑Gaussian nonlinear systems, reducing KL divergence by up to 0.5 on autonomous driving data and achieving near‑zero negative log‑likelihood for COVID‑19 infected trajectories.

## Executive Summary
Traditional state estimation filters such as the Kalman filter and particle filters struggle with nonlinear systems that exhibit non‑Gaussian and multi‑modal uncertainties. This paper introduces a deep generative approach based on conditional normalizing flows (CNF) to perform joint inference over latent states and model parameters. The flow transformations are conditioned on the observation history using either a transformer or a Mamba‑SSM backbone, enabling the model to capture complex temporal dependencies. To mitigate over‑parameterization and improve training stability, the authors augment the maximum likelihood objective with an optimal‑transport kinetic loss term.

Experiments on three distinct applications—autonomous driving, SIR epidemiology, and real‑world COVID‑19 forecasting—demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method. On autonomous driving data, incorporating the kinetic loss yields up to a 0.5 reduction in mean KL divergence (e.g., backward before‑bifurcation: 3.65 → 2.78). In the SIR setting, the Mamba‑SSM variant achieves the lowest KL for the susceptible compartment. For COVID‑19, the method transfers from synthetic training to real data, producing accurate forward and backward forecasts with negative log‑likelihoods around -0.035 for infected trajectories.

## Method Summary
The core method is a conditional normalizing flow that models the posterior distribution \(p(x_{1:T}, \theta \mid y_{1:T})\) over latent states \(x\) and parameters \(\theta\) given observations \(y\). An invertible transformation \(f_\theta\) maps a simple base density (e.g., standard Gaussian) to the complex posterior. The parameters of \(f\) are generated by a sequence model (transformer or Mamba‑SSM) that processes the observation sequence and produces a context vector \(c\). This context is used to parameterize coupling layers within the flow, making the transformation conditional on the data. The training objective combines the exact log‑likelihood of the observed latents (or equivalently the negative log‑likelihood of the observations under the model) with a kinetic loss term derived from optimal transport. The kinetic loss penalizes the squared norm of the velocity field of the flow, encouraging the transport map to be as close as possible to an optimal (minimal‑energy) map. This regularization mitigates over‑parameterization, improves identifiability, and stabilizes training.

The approach supports both forward filtering (predicting future states) and backward smoothing (inferring past states) in a unified manner, as the flow is trained on full trajectories. Joint state‑parameter inference is naturally handled by augmenting the latent variable with the unknown parameters. The method is evaluated in several domains with non‑Gaussian, multi‑modal uncertainties.

## Key Results
- Kinetic loss reduces mean KL divergence by up to 0.5 on autonomous driving data (e.g., backward before‑bifurcation: 3.65 → 2.78).
- Mamba‑SSM achieves the lowest KL for the susceptible compartment in SIR epidemiology experiments.
- On real‑world COVID‑19 data, the method yields accurate forward/backward forecasts with negative log‑likelihood ≈ -0.035 for infected trajectories.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Conditional normalizing flows provide a flexible, tractable way to represent complex, multi‑modal posterior distributions over states and parameters. By conditioning the flow on a learned representation of the observations (via transformer or Mamba‑SSM), the model captures the dependence of the posterior on the data history. The optimal‑transport kinetic loss acts as a regularizer that encourages the flow to follow a straight, low‑energy path in the transport space, preventing collapse to trivial solutions and mitigating over‑parameterization. This yields a smoother, more identifiable mapping that improves training stability and generalization, as evidenced by the reduction in KL divergence and accurate predictions on challenging real‑world tasks.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Observations \(y_{1:T}\) → Encoder (Transformer or Mamba‑SSM) → Context \(c\) → Flow parameters \(\theta(c)\) → Invertible transformation \(f_\theta\) → Latent variable \(z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)\) → Posterior sample \(x = f_\theta^{-1}(z)\). For joint state‑parameter estimation, \(z\) concatenates both state and parameter components.

**Critical path**:  
1. Input observations are normalized and possibly embedded.  
2. Encoder processes the sequence to produce a context vector (or per‑time‑step context).  
3. Context is fed to a network that generates coupling parameters for each flow layer.  
4. A base Gaussian sample \(z\) is drawn and passed through the inverse flow to obtain a posterior sample.  
5. The log‑likelihood of the sample under the flow is computed; the kinetic loss is computed from the velocity field along the transformation.  
6. Total loss (negative log‑likelihood + \(\lambda \cdot\) kinetic) is backpropagated to update encoder and flow parameters.  
7. After training, the model can be used for filtering (conditioning on observations up to time \(t\)) or smoothing (conditioning on full sequence) by sampling from the flow.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- **Transformer vs Mamba‑SSM**: Transformers provide strong global context via attention but have \(O(n^2)\) complexity; Mamba‑SSM scales linearly and is more memory‑efficient, which matters for long sequences. However, Mamba‑SSM may be less expressive for certain non‑Markovian patterns.  
- **Flow depth and architecture**: Deeper flows increase capacity to model multi‑modal posteriors but risk training instability and higher computational cost. Fewer layers may underfit.  
- **Kinetic loss weight \(\lambda\)**: Too small → insufficient regularization; too large → over‑smoothing and loss of detail. Requires tuning.  
- **Conditioning mechanism**: Using a context vector vs. per‑time‑step conditioning affects granularity and applicability to online filtering.

**Failure signatures**:  
- **Mode collapse**: Posterior collapses to a single mode despite known multi‑modality; manifested as low entropy but high KL when compared to ground truth.  
- **Kinetic loss mis‑tuning**: \(\lambda=0\) yields over‑parameterization and poor generalization; \(\lambda\) too high leads to degenerate flow (e.g., near‑identity) and underfitting, seen as negligible NLL improvements.  
- **Gradient issues**: Invertible layers can produce NaNs if not implemented with sufficient numerical stability.  
- **Encoder failure**: If the transformer/Mamba‑SSM fails to capture relevant observations, the flow conditioning becomes uninformative, leading to high error and flat posteriors.

**First experiments**:  
1. **Toy bimodal tracking**: Construct a 1‑D nonlinear state‑space model with Gaussian mixture noise; verify that the flow posterior captures both modes and that KL matches analytical solution.  
2. **Transformer vs Mamba‑SSM ablation**: Train both encoders on a synthetic dataset with sequence length 100; compare NLL, KL, and training time per epoch to assess trade‑offs.  
3. **Kinetic loss sensitivity**: Train on autonomous driving data with \(\lambda \in \{0, 0.1, 1, 10\}\); report mean KL and compute mode‑coverage metrics (e.g., precision/recall against held‑out samples) to determine optimal regularization.

## Limitations
- The evaluation is restricted to three specific applications; broader generalizability remains unproven.
- The primary metric is KL divergence, which does not fully assess uncertainty calibration or multi‑modal fidelity.
- Real‑world COVID‑19 forecasts rely on synthetic training data, raising concerns about model mismatch and potential overfitting (NLL near zero may indicate overconfidence).
- Computational complexity of the proposed architecture is not analyzed, limiting scalability assessment.

## Confidence
- Joint state‑parameter estimation in non‑Gaussian settings via conditional normalizing flows: **Medium**  
- Optimal‑transport kinetic loss reduces overparameterization and improves KL: **Medium**  
- Mamba‑SSM achieves best performance on SIR epidemiology: **Low**  
- Synthetic‑to‑real transfer yields accurate COVID‑19 forecasts (NLL ≈ -0.035): **Low**
