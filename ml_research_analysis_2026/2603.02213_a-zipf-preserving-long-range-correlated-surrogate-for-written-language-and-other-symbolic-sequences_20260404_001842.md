# A Zipf-preserving, long-range correlated surrogate for written language and other symbolic sequences

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.02213
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02213
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Generates symbolic surrogates that simultaneously preserve the exact empirical Zipf rank–frequency distribution and the detrended fluctuation analysis (DFA) scaling exponent of the original sequence.

## Executive Summary
Existing surrogate models for symbolic sequences (e.g., text, DNA) are limited to preserving either first-order statistics (like Zipf’s law) or long-range correlation structure (like the DFA exponent), but not both. This paper introduces a method that combines fractional Gaussian noise (FGN) with a deterministic, frequency-preserving rank assignment. Continuous FGN values are sorted and mapped to symbols according to their global frequencies, ensuring exact Zipf preservation. An iterative bisection search adjusts the FGN Hurst exponent to compensate for correlation loss during discretization, matching the target DFA exponent. The resulting surrogates isolate linear long-memory effects while randomizing short-range dependencies, providing a principled null model for analyzing symbolic systems.

## Method Summary
The algorithm takes a symbolic sequence T (e.g., a tokenized text). First, it computes the empirical symbol frequencies and the target DFA exponent α from the Zipf-rank encoded sequence. It then enters a bisection loop to find a suitable Hurst exponent α₀ for generating fractional Gaussian noise (FGN). For each candidate α₀, it generates an FGN realization Z of length N, sorts its values, and constructs a rank list R by repeating symbol indices in descending frequency order. The surrogate S is formed by mapping the sorted Z values to symbols via R, which enforces exact frequency matching. The DFA exponent α_S of S is measured. Bisection adjusts α₀ until α_S converges to the target α within tolerance. This process decouples marginal frequencies from temporal order, allowing independent control of long-range correlations.

## Key Results
- Surrogates exactly match the original symbol frequencies and Zipf distribution for English and Latin texts.
- After bisection adjustment, surrogates replicate the original DFA scaling exponent (e.g., ~0.65–0.75 for language, 0.65 for Drosophila DNA).
- The method consistently reproduces long-range correlations while randomizing short-range dependencies such as word bigrams or dinucleotide biases.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Claim 1: Exact preservation of Zipf’s law
The method uses **Zipf-rank encoding**: symbols are assigned ranks based on global frequency. A continuous-valued FGN signal is sorted, and its values are deterministically assigned to symbols in blocks proportional to their frequencies. The most frequent symbol receives the smallest FGN values, the next most frequent the next block, and so on. This rank-based assignment ensures the marginal symbol counts in the surrogate match the original exactly, independent of the FGN’s temporal structure.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “Our method generates surrogates… by mapping fractional Gaussian noise (FGN) onto the empirical histogram… match the original in first-order statistics.” [section 3.1] describes rank ordering and assignment.
- **Break condition:** Unreliable frequency estimation (e.g., very short sequences) prevents exact matching.

### Claim 2: Reproduction of long-range correlation structure (DFA exponent)
The surrogate inherits long-range memory from the underlying **fractional Gaussian noise (FGN)**, a stationary Gaussian process with prescribed Hurst exponent H (where DFA exponent α ≈ H). Because the rank assignment is monotonic—similar FGN values at nearby times lead to similar or adjacent ranks—the power-law dependence in FGN translates into persistent correlations in the symbolic sequence.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “…reproduces its long-range correlation structure, quantified by the detrended fluctuation analysis (DFA) exponent.” [section 2] discusses long-range correlations in language. [section 5] confirms matching of long-range scaling.
- **Break condition:** If the original sequence exhibits multifractal or non-linear dependencies, a monofractal FGN cannot fully replicate its scaling spectrum.

### Claim 3: Bisection search compensates for discretization loss
Discretizing continuous FGN values into a finite alphabet reduces effective long-range dependence, causing the surrogate’s DFA exponent α_S to be lower than the input FGN exponent α₀. The algorithm iteratively adjusts α₀ via bisection: it generates a surrogate, measures α_S, and updates the bracket until α_S matches the target α. This works under the assumptions that the mapping α₀ → α_S is monotonic and consistent for given N and symbol frequencies.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 5] “due to the discretisation… the DFA exponent α_S… is typically lower.” [section 5.1] details the bisection procedure.
- **Break condition:** Non-monotonic or high-variance α₀→α_S mapping (e.g., with small N, extreme α, or skewed frequencies) can cause bisection to fail or converge incorrectly.

## Foundational Learning
- **Zipf’s Law:** Why needed: The surrogate’s core constraint is preserving the empirical rank–frequency distribution (f(r) ∝ 1/r^γ). This is a static, first-order property separate from temporal order. Quick check: Shuffling a text at the word level preserves Zipf’s law but collapses the DFA exponent to ~0.5.
- **Detrended Fluctuation Analysis (DFA):** Why needed: DFA exponent α quantifies long-range correlations; the surrogate must match this value. It relates to Hurst exponent H and power-law autocorrelation. Quick check: What does α = 0.7 indicate? Persistent long-range correlations; H = 0.7, β = 0.4.
- **Fractional Gaussian Noise (FGN):** Why needed: FGN is the continuous-valued source process whose correlation structure is transferred to the surrogate. Its stationarity, Gaussianity, and parameterization by H explain why it serves as a linear null model. Quick check: How is H related to DFA exponent for FGN? For FGN, α = H.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input Processor -> FGN Generator -> Rank-based Discretizer -> DFA Appraiser -> Bisection Controller
- **Critical path:** The bisection loop (FGN generation -> discretization -> DFA measurement -> exponent adjustment) is the core runtime path. Each iteration is O(N log N) due to sorting.
- **Design tradeoffs:** 
  - Accuracy vs. Speed: More bisection iterations improve exponent matching but increase runtime; tolerance ε trades precision for speed.
  - Generality vs. Simplicity: Works for any discrete alphabet but assumes monofractal scaling; cannot replicate multifractal spectra.
  - Frequency Fidelity vs. Correlation: Symbol frequencies are matched exactly; correlation matching is asymptotic via bisection. Short-range structure is deliberately randomized.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - DFA mismatch: α_S fails to converge to α within tolerance after max iterations. Likely cause: non-monotonic α₀→α_S mapping (small N, extreme α, skewed frequencies).
  - Frequency distortion: Surrogate symbol counts differ from original. Cause: bug in rank list construction R or permutation application.
  - Non-stationarity artifacts: DFA curve shows scale-dependent exponent. Could indicate insufficient FGN length or inappropriate scaling range.
  - Short-range autocorrelation: Significant ACF at small lags. May indicate FGN generation artifacts or overcompensation in bisection.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Sanity check - FGN properties: Generate FGN with known H (e.g., 0.7), apply rank-discretization with uniform symbol frequencies. Measure resulting α_S; should be systematically lower than H but consistent, establishing baseline discretization loss.
  2. Zipf preservation test: Apply full method to a short English text (~10k words). Plot rank–frequency curves for original and surrogate; they should overlay exactly. Compute KL divergence between frequency distributions; should be zero.
  3. DFA matching test: For the same text, run bisection to match α (original ~0.65–0.75). Plot F(L) vs. L for original and final surrogate; scaling exponents and linear fits in log–log space should be indistinguishable over the scaling range (e.g., 10² to 10⁴ words).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
How can the surrogate model be extended to replicate multifractal scaling properties, such as a spectrum of Hurst exponents, beyond the monofractal DFA exponent?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The surrogates are monofractal by design due to construction from FGN; discrepancies in multifractal spectra can reveal higher-order nonlinear or hierarchical mechanisms.
- **Why unresolved:** FGN has a single H and cannot generate a distribution of scaling exponents required for multifractality. Extending would require integrating a multifractal process (e.g., multifractal fBm) or a more complex mapping that preserves both Zipf frequencies and a multifractal spectrum.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Development of a surrogate based on a multifractal stochastic process that also respects empirical symbol frequencies, validated on texts/genomes with significant multifractal spectra under MFDFA. Success would match both the monofractal DFA exponent and the multifractal scaling function.

### Open Question 2
Which specific short-range dependencies (e.g., syntactic structures in language, dinucleotide biases or palindromic motifs in DNA) are eliminated by the surrogate, and what is their quantitative contribution to the long-range correlations observed in the original sequences?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The surrogate randomizes short-range dependencies and does not preserve higher-order statistics such as dinucleotide frequencies or syntactic/semantic patterns; it retains only first-order symbol frequencies and long-range second-order correlations.
- **Why unresolved:** While the surrogate intentionally removes local structure to isolate linear long-memory effects, the extent to which these omitted features contribute to the overall DFA exponent (or multifractal properties) remains unmeasured. This limits interpretation of what drives scaling beyond the preserved constraints.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Comparative analyses using measures of local structure (e.g., syntactic parse tree complexity, k-mer enrichment scores) applied to original and surrogate sequences. Correlating deviations in these measures with differences in DFA scaling or multifractal spectra would quantify the contribution of short-range dependencies.

### Open Question 3
To what extent does the assumption of stationary symbol frequencies limit the applicability of the surrogate to non-stationary real-world symbolic sequences, such as texts with evolving vocabulary or genomic regions with variable base composition?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The method relies on a globally computed empirical frequency distribution for the entire sequence, an assumption not discussed. Real-world symbolic streams often exhibit non-stationarity (e.g., topic shifts, isochores), which could violate the frequency-preserving mapping and distort correlation structure.
- **Why unresolved:** The surrogate partitions the real line based on fixed, sequence-wide frequencies. Local frequency variations are ignored, potentially leading to mismatches in correlation structure when applied to non-stationary data. The paper does not address robustness to such violations.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Testing the surrogate on synthetic sequences with controlled frequency drift (e.g., gradual vocabulary change) and on naturally non-stationary segments of real genomes. Adapting the method to use sliding-window frequency estimates and measuring the impact on DFA exponent replication would clarify limitations and possible extensions.

## Limitations
- **DFA scaling region selection:** The replication of target α critically depends on the unspecified procedure for choosing window size range [L_min, L_max] and fitting the scaling exponent. Different valid choices can yield different α_target values.
- **FGN generation invariance:** The theoretical mapping from input Hurst exponent α₀ to output surrogate exponent α_S is assumed monotonic and consistent. This is not proven and may vary with sequence length N, alphabet size |A|, and the specific FGN generation algorithm, affecting bisection convergence.
- **Monofractal limitation:** The method produces a monofractal surrogate (single α). It cannot replicate a multifractal spectrum, which may be present in natural language or genomic sequences. Thus, it is a valid null model only for sequences whose long-range correlations are adequately captured by a single DFA exponent.

## Confidence
- **Exact Zipf preservation:** High. The rank-based deterministic assignment directly and provably enforces exact symbol frequency matching independent of the underlying continuous process.
- **DFA exponent matching via bisection:** Medium. The iterative adjustment is logically sound if the α₀→α_S mapping is monotonic. Empirical evidence from the paper’s tests supports this for their specific corpora and parameters, but the underlying assumption is not formally guaranteed for all inputs.
- **Principled linear null model:** Low-to-Medium. The approach rightly isolates the contribution of long-range memory from frequency statistics. However, its validity as a linear null model rests on the unverified assumption that the original sequence’s long-range structure is itself linearly correlative (i.e., FGN-like after encoding). Non-linear dependencies would not be correctly nulled.

## Next Checks
1. **FGN Generator Validation:** For fixed N and |A|, generate multiple FGN realizations with known α₀ (e.g., 0.6, 0.7, 0.8). Apply only the Zipf-rank discretization with a uniform frequency distribution. Measure resulting α_S. Plot α_S vs. α₀ to empirically characterize the discretization loss curve and test for monotonicity and consistency across runs.
2. **Frequency Fidelity Stress Test:** Apply the full method to a short sequence (e.g., N=1000) with a highly skewed Zipf distribution (e.g., 90% of tokens from 10% of vocabulary). After bisection convergence, perform a direct count of all symbols in the surrogate. Verify zero deviation from the original histogram. Any failure indicates a bug in rank-list construction R or permutation application.
3. **Monotonicity and Convergence Robustness:** Systematically vary sequence length N (e.g., 5k, 20k, 100k tokens) and alphabet size |A| (e.g., 50, 500, 5000 unique words). For each combination, characterize the α₀↔α_S mapping (as in Check 1) and run the full bisection to match a fixed α_target (e.g., 0.7). Document the number of iterations required and whether convergence fails. This tests the algorithm’s robustness to the fundamental parameters of the symbolic sequence.
