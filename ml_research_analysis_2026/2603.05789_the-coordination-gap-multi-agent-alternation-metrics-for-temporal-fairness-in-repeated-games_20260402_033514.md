# The Coordination Gap: Multi-Agent Alternation Metrics for Temporal Fairness in Repeated Games

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.05789
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05789
- Reference count: 14
- Primary result: Learned policies achieve high outcome fairness (often >0.9) but perform up to 81% worse than random baselines under novel temporally-sensitive Alternation metrics, revealing a severe coordination gap.

## Executive Summary
This paper identifies a critical flaw in conventional fairness evaluation for multi-agent repeated games: standard outcome-based metrics (e.g., Reward Fairness, Gini coefficient) fail to capture the temporal structure of coordination, such as turn-taking. The authors formalize a multi-agent Battle of the Exes (MBoE) Markov game and introduce Perfect Alternation (PA) as a normative benchmark for fair rotation. They propose six Alternation (ALT) metrics that directly evaluate the sequence of wins across episodes. Through experiments with independent tabular Q-learning agents, they demonstrate that while traditional metrics suggest fair and efficient outcomes, ALT metrics reveal profound coordination failures—learned policies significantly underperform even random policies in temporal fairness. The findings underscore that high aggregate payoffs can mask a "coordination gap" and that random policy baselines are essential to expose such hidden deficits.

## Method Summary
The study uses a deterministic, symmetric MBoE environment where n agents compete in episodic games with exclusive high rewards. Independent tabular Q-learners are trained without communication or opponent modeling. Performance is evaluated using both conventional metrics (Efficiency, Reward Fairness) and the novel ALT metrics (e.g., CALT, EALT, AALT) that score sliding windows of consecutive episodes for exclusive, non-monopolistic win patterns. Random-policy trajectories serve as a null baseline for comparison. Experiments span agent counts n = 2 to 10, and results are aggregated via Coordination Score (relative change vs. random) and PA-equivalent estimates.

## Key Results
- Conventional metrics are deceptively high: Reward Fairness often exceeds 0.9, yet learned policies achieve ALT scores up to 81% lower than random baselines.
- Coordination quality degrades non-linearly with agent count: PA-equivalence drops from ~57% at n=2 to ~22% at n=10.
- Random policies themselves yield surprisingly high outcome fairness (≥0.816) but low ALT, demonstrating that stochastic equality can mimic fair outcomes without any temporal coordination.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: ALT Metrics Encode Temporal Structure
The ALT metrics evaluate batches of consecutive episodes, rewarding patterns where each agent wins exactly once per n-episode cycle (Perfect Alternation) and penalizing monopolization or ties. Unlike cumulative metrics, they explicitly track the order of wins, making them sensitive to turn-taking. The core assumption is that PA represents a Pareto-optimal, fair rotation. Evidence: ALT definitions in Section 4 show boundedness and PA=1, and degrade under manipulated sequences (e.g., one agent winning early episodes). If ALT remained high under monopolization, the metric would be flawed, but the paper shows degradation.

### Mechanism 2: Random Baselines Expose Deceptive Fairness
Uniform random action selection produces a null process where wins distribute by chance. This yields high min/max payoff ratios and efficiency due to stochastic equality, but low ALT due to random temporal ordering. The core assumption is that "no temporal coordination" is meaningfully defined by independent random draws. Evidence: Random baselines achieve Fairness ≥0.816 and Efficiency ≥0.220 across n, yet CALT declines from 0.486 to 0.111 as n increases. If random policies ever achieved ALT=1, the environment or metric would be degenerate, which is not observed.

### Mechanism 3: Independent Q-learning Fails Credit Assignment for Reciprocation
Tabular Q-learning treats episodes as independent, valuing immediate high rewards over future reciprocation (allowing others to win). Non-stationary opponent learning disrupts convergence to turn-taking equilibria, yielding Nash-like but temporally myopic outcomes. Core assumption: without opponent modeling or communication, Q-updates cannot internalize delayed collective benefits of alternating. Evidence: Section 6.1 describes the credit assignment problem; all Coordination Scores are negative, and Q-learning CALT is lower than random for all n. If coordination scores became positive or traditional metrics collapsed while ALT improved, the failure mode would shift—this is not observed except a small positive qEALT gain at n=10.

## Foundational Learning
- **Temporal Fairness vs. Outcome Fairness**  
  *Why needed:* Outcome metrics are distributional and time-averaged, so they cannot distinguish between fair turn-taking, monopolization, or random access that yields similar cumulative payoffs.  
  *Quick check:* Why would a monopolist (one agent wins all early episodes) and a perfect alternator have the same Reward Fairness if payoffs are equalized over time?

- **Perfect Alternation (PA) as Reference Regime**  
  *Why needed:* PA provides a concrete, Pareto-optimal benchmark for turn-taking that maps to temporal fair division, allowing ALT metrics to be calibrated (e.g., PA=1).  
  *Quick check:* How does PA generalize beyond 2-agent Battle of the Exes to n-agent periodic rotation?

- **Random Baseline as Null Hypothesis**  
  *Why needed:* Without a chance-level reference, high traditional metrics create an illusion of coordination. Random baselines expose that temporal structure may be absent even when outcomes look fair.  
  *Quick check:* What would you conclude if Q-learning achieved Reward Fairness=0.99 but random policy had Reward Fairness=0.95? What does that gap (or lack thereof) imply?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** MBoE Environment Configuration -> Independent Tabular Q-learning Agents -> Metrics Module (Traditional & ALT) & Baseline Module (Random) -> Analysis Module (Coordination Score, PA-equivalent)
- **Critical path:** 1. Define MBoE parameters (n, state type, rewards). 2. Generate random baseline trajectories and compute baseline metrics. 3. Train Q-learning agents for fixed episodes. 4. Compute learned policy metrics. 5. Compare via relative change and PA-equivalent, repeat across n=2–10.
- **Design tradeoffs:** Tabular Q-learning ensures interpretability but limits scalability; adding minimal memory (previous winner) helps slightly but does not resolve fundamental credit assignment. Metric choice tradeoff: CALT is balanced primary, AALT provides strict upper bound, FALT is too lenient.
- **Failure signatures:** Traditional metrics high (Reward Fairness >0.9, Efficiency >0.5) but ALT low (CALT <0.3) and Coordination Score negative; PA-equivalent below ~30% for n≥3, plateauing near 20% for n≥8; negative Relative Change across all ALT variants (except occasional small positive at n=10).
- **First 3 experiments:** 1. Random baseline sweep: run 10k episodes for n∈{2,3,5,8,10}, verify CALT declines with n while traditional metrics remain high. 2. Q-learning training curve: plot CALT and Efficiency over training episodes to show CALT decreases as ε decays. 3. ALT vs. traditional correlation: scatter CALT against Reward Fairness across runs; expect no correlation or inverse relationship.

## Limitations
- Findings are confined to the deterministic, symmetric MBoE game with independent tabular Q-learners; generalizability to stochastic environments, heterogeneous agents, communication, or deep MARL is unknown.
- The normative reliance on Perfect Alternation may not encompass all valid turn-taking conventions in multi-agent systems.
- The random-policy null hypothesis, while crucial, is a specific stochastic process; its dominance over learned policies might not hold for alternative null definitions (e.g., memory-based).

## Confidence
- **High:** Empirical demonstration that within the MBoE/tabular-Q setup, conventional metrics can be high while ALT metrics are low and coordination scores negative—the measurement failure is robustly shown.
- **Medium:** Claim that this represents a general class of "coordination gaps" in multi-agent temporal fairness; supported by theory but not yet validated in broader MARL settings.
- **Low:** Extrapolation to real-world multi-agent systems or advanced LLM-based agents; the paper's environment and learning algorithm are minimal, so failure modes in complex systems could differ fundamentally.

## Next Checks
1. **Metric Sanity Stress-Test:** Generate synthetic win sequences with known temporal properties (e.g., monopolization first half, perfect alternation second half) to confirm ALT metrics degrade/increase as designed while traditional metrics remain constant.
2. **Baseline Null Variation:** Replace uniform random baselines with alternative nulls (e.g., round-robin with random starting agent, memory-1 stochastic policies) to test whether the "deceptive high fairness" of random persists or is an artifact of uniform independence.
3. **Agent/Environment Sweep:** Replicate core experiments with independent DQN agents or with simple opponent modeling, and in a stochastic variant of MBoE (e.g., random action failures). Goal: determine if the coordination gap shrinks with function approximation or disappears under stochasticity.
