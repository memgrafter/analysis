# AzeroS: Extending LLM to Speech with Self-Generated Instruction-Free Tuning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.06086  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06086  
- Reference count: 13  
- Primary result: AzeroS achieves state-of-the-art performance, scoring 73.13 on VoiceBench (semantic) and 77.25 average (8.28 chat) on AIR-Bench (Speech) (paralinguistic), approaching the text-only semantic ceiling with only ~47.6M trainable parameters.

## Executive Summary
Current speech-LLMs rely on large-scale, manually curated instruction-tuning data, which is inefficient and leads to poor generalization. AzeroS introduces Self-Generated Instruction-Free Tuning (SIFT), where a frozen LLM generates supervision targets from textual speech representations (transcripts/metadata) without any explicit instruction-response pairs. The model freezes a 7B Instruct LLM (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) and two audio encoders (semantic and paralinguistic), training only two lightweight projection modules (23.8M parameters each) on ~25k hours of public speech-text data. A two-stage training process first aligns semantics, then paralinguistics, yielding a model that approaches the text-only semantic ceiling and substantially outperforms prior speech-LLMs on paralinguistic understanding.

## Method Summary
SIFT constructs training targets automatically: for each speech sample, an oracle textual representation containing a transcript (and optionally paralinguistic metadata such as emotion or gender) is fed to the frozen LLM with no instruction; the LLM’s generated response becomes the supervision target. The audio encoders project raw audio features into the LLM’s embedding space; the model is trained to make the LLM output match the automatically generated target. Training occurs in two stages: Stage 1 uses a semantic‑focused encoder on transcript‑only data to establish a strong text‑aligned foundation; Stage 2 freezes this semantic branch and trains a second, paralinguistic encoder on transcript‑plus‑metadata data. At inference, both projected streams are concatenated and fed to the LLM, which can then follow arbitrary user instructions. Only the two projectors are updated (total 47.6M parameters), avoiding the need for large instruction‑tuning datasets.

## Key Results
- VoiceBench overall score: 73.13 (SOTA, nearing text‑only ceiling 77.52)  
- AIR‑Bench (Speech) average score: 77.25, chat score: 8.28  
- Substantial gains over prior cascaded and end‑to‑end speech‑LLMs on paralinguistic understanding.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Instruction‑free training prevents feature collapse and overfitting
**Claim:** Training without explicit instructions (SIFT) yields stronger generalization to unseen tasks than instruction tuning by preventing instruction‑induced feature collapse and instruction overfitting.  
**Mechanism:** Removing the instruction variable (i=∅) forces the audio encoder to fully align with the oracle textual representation across all information dimensions, rather than only those relevant to a specific instruction. The frozen LLM acts as a universal decoder that, given perfectly aligned input, can execute any test‑time instruction.  
**Core assumption:** The frozen LLM already possesses strong general instruction‑following and reasoning capabilities; the only limitation is the modality alignment quality.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract, Section 3.4 (gradient flows entirely through encoder), related work.  
**Break condition:** If the LLM does not “self‑elicit” a response covering all information in the oracle when given no instruction, SIFT targets become sparse and the encoder learns an incomplete mapping (Section 5.7).

### Mechanism 2: Two‑stage training with separate encoders balances semantic and paralinguistic alignment
**Claim:** A two‑stage process optimally balances semantic and paralinguistic alignment without catastrophic forgetting.  
**Mechanism:** Stage 1 uses a semantic encoder on ASR data under SIFT to establish a strong text‑aligned foundation; Stage 2 freezes this branch and trains a second, paralinguistic encoder on metadata‑augmented data under SIFT. This prevents interference between potentially conflicting alignment objectives.  
**Core assumption:** Semantic and paralinguistic information have distinct optimal representations; joint training risks degrading one or both.  
**Evidence anchors:** Section 4.2, Section 5.4 (SIFTsp improves paralinguistic scores with slight semantic trade‑off), Table 6 (two‑stage slightly outperforms one‑stage).  
**Break condition:** If the LLM’s embedding space is sufficiently rich for both feature types, a single encoder might suffice; break condition would be evidence that joint training causes no degradation.

### Mechanism 3: Effectiveness hinges on the LLM’s self‑elicit capability
**Claim:** The effectiveness of SIFT hinges on the “Self‑Elicit Capability” of the frozen LLM—its intrinsic tendency to produce detailed outputs from input without prompting.  
**Mechanism:** For SIFT to create dense targets reflecting all oracle information, the LLM’s default next‑token prediction given only the oracle must elaborate on all aspects (semantic + paralinguistic). If the model is biased toward conciseness (e.g., dialog‑optimized), self‑elicit fails, targets become sparse, and alignment is incomplete.  
**Core assumption:** The LLM’s base behavior (without system prompt) is detail‑oriented for textual summaries of speech (e.g., Qwen2.5‑Instruct).  
**Evidence anchors:** Section 3.3 (Goal 2), Section 5.7 case study (injecting a “natural conversation” system prompt degrades extraction tasks).  
**Break condition:** If the base LLM is inherently concise, SIFT alone fails; must be combined with explicit prompting (SIT) to force verbosity.

## Foundational Learning
- **Universal Decoder Assumption**  
  *Why needed:* SIFT rests on the idea that the frozen LLM is a universal decoder; alignment is the sole bottleneck.  
  *Quick check:* If you perfectly projected any audio into the LLM’s text embedding space, should the LLM be able to answer any question about that audio? Why or why not?
- **Instruction‑Induced Feature Collapse & Overfitting**  
  *Why needed:* These are the harms SIFT avoids; they explain why TSIT underperforms.  
  *Quick check:* In a standard ASR instruction‑tuning setup, what specific features might the audio encoder learn to ignore, and how would that hurt performance on an emotion recognition task at test time?
- **Oracle Textual Representation  h̃(x)**  
  *Why needed:* The quality of the oracle (transcript + metadata) bounds what SIFT can learn.  
  *Quick check:* If you only had ASR transcripts (no emotion labels) as  h̃(x), what capabilities could you never hope to achieve with SIFT, regardless of model architecture?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Frozen LLM (Qwen2.5‑7B‑Instruct) <- Trainable Projectors (2× MLP, 23.8M each) <- Frozen Audio Encoders (TTA semantic, Auden‑Voice paralinguistic) <- Audio input. At inference, both projector outputs are concatenated before LLM input.

### Critical path
- Stage 1 (Semantic): Audio → TTA Encoder → Semantic Projector (trainable) → LLM input → Loss updates only Semantic Projector (∼22k hours transcript‑only).  
- Stage 2 (Paralinguistic): Audio → Both encoders → Both projectors → LLM input → Loss updates only Paralinguistic Projector (∼3.5k hours transcript+metadata); semantic branch frozen.  
- Inference: Both projectors active; concatenated embeddings fed to frozen LLM; free to use any user instruction.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Frozen vs. Trainable:** Freezing encoders and LLM reduces compute dramatically but limits adaptation; trade‑off: efficiency vs. potential upper bound.  
- **Dual vs. Single Encoder:** Dual encoder explicitly separates modalities, likely preventing interference but adding parameters/inference cost; single encoder (e.g., Whisper) simpler but may not capture paralinguistics as well (Table 6).  
- **Two‑stage vs. One‑stage:** Two‑stage ensures clean semantic foundation first but requires separate data pools and more complex training; one‑stage is simpler but risks interference (ablation shows two‑stage slightly better).

### Failure signatures
1. **Poor VoiceBench + Good AIR‑Bench:** Encoder overfit to paralinguistics, lost semantic fidelity. Likely insufficient Stage 1 data or aggressive Stage 2.  
2. **Good VoiceBench + Poor AIR‑Bench:** Encoder failed to capture paralinguistics. Check Stage 2 data quality (metadata accuracy) and ensure paralinguistic projector is actually being trained.  
3. **Overall low performance:** Check projector initialization and alignment; common failure is poor initialization causing misalignment that gradients cannot quickly fix.  
4. **SIFT‑specific collapse (Sec 5.7):** If using a non‑self‑elicit LLM (e.g., dialog‑optimized) without mixing SIT data, expect poor scores on extraction‑heavy tasks (AdvBench, OBQA, age/emotion recognition).

### First 3 experiments
1. **Replicate SIFT_s baseline:** Train *only* the semantic projector on a small subset (e.g., 100 hrs) of transcript‑only data using SIFT (no instruction). Expect dramatic improvement over TSIT_s on VoiceBench semantic tasks, confirming instruction‑free benefit.  
2. **Test Self‑Elicit property:** With Qwen2.5‑7B‑Instruct, take a sample oracle (transcript+metadata). Generate output with prompt `""` (null) and with prompt `"Describe all information."` Compare output density/detail. If null is sparse, you must use SIT fallback.  
3. **Dual‑encoder sanity check:** Freeze everything, randomly initialize projectors. Train for 1 step on 1 batch. Check if projected vectors have reasonable norm (similar to LLM embeddings) and are not NaN. A common failure is projector output variance mismatch with LLM embedding space.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Can the SIFT paradigm be effectively extended to vision‑language models (VLMs) to align visual representations with LLMs without explicit instruction‑tuning data?  
2. How can we systematically measure and ensure that a frozen LLM possesses the “self‑elicit capability” required for optimal SIFT performance?  
3. Is the dual‑encoder design necessary for AzeroS’s performance, or could a single multimodal encoder with SIFT achieve comparable results?  
4. What is the data scaling behavior of SIFT, and does performance saturate at the current ∼25k hour scale for both semantic and paralinguistic tasks?

## Limitations
- Exact semantic and paralinguistic encoders (TTA, Auden‑Voice) are unreleased; using Whisper as a proxy may fail to capture paralinguistics, limiting fair performance replication.  
- Hyperparameters and training details (learning rate, batch size, epochs, projector architecture specifics) are undisclosed; proposed defaults may not achieve reported results.  
- Oracle text formatting and LLM generation parameters (temperature, top‑p) are unspecified, affecting target quality and thus training signal.

## Confidence
- **SIFT yields superior generalization vs. instruction tuning.** Confidence: High (strong empirical evidence, supported by ablation).  
- **Two‑stage dual‑encoder training optimally balances semantic/paralinguistic alignment.** Confidence: Medium (ablation supports but other architectures untested).  
- **Frozen LLM acts as a universal decoder; alignment is the sole bottleneck.** Confidence: Medium‑Low (plausible but unverified; LLM biases are a known breaking condition).  
- **Self‑elicit capability of the base LLM is essential for SIFT.** Confidence: Medium (directly evidenced by Section 5.7 case study, but model‑dependent).

## Next Checks
1. **Replicate SIFT_s baseline with public proxy:** Train semantic projector with Whisper‑large‑v3 and Qwen2.5‑7B‑Instruct on 100 h LibriSpeech. Validate: SIFT_s should significantly outperform a TSIT_s baseline (same data but with a fixed ASR instruction) on a held‑out semantic task (e.g., question answering on LibriSpeech test‑clean).  
2. **Quantify Self‑Elicit property of the frozen LLM:** For 100 random samples from joint metadata data, generate two targets from Qwen2.5‑7B‑Instruct: (a) `""` (no instruction) and (b) `"Provide a comprehensive summary including all speaker traits and emotions."` Validate: Measure output length and explicit mention of metadata tags (e.g., "emotion: happy"). A significant gap (shorter, sparser outputs for (a)) confirms the self‑elicit dependency and necessitates SIT fallback mixing.  
3. **Test dual‑encoder necessity vs. single encoder:** Ablate the two‑stage design. Train a single‑projector model (using Whisper) on a mixed dataset of semantic (22k h) and joint (3.5k h) data under a **one‑stage SIFT** regime (no freezing). Validate: Compare VoiceBench and AIR‑Bench scores to the reported two‑stage dual‑encoder model. If single‑encoder performance is statistically inferior (>2 pt gap), it supports the claim of conflicting alignment objectives; if comparable, it challenges the necessity of the complex dual‑branch design.
