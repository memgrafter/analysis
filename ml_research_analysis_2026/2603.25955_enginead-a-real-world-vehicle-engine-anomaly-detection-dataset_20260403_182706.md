# EngineAD: A Real-World Vehicle Engine Anomaly Detection Dataset
## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.25955
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25955
- **Reference count:** 22
- **Primary result:** A real-world, expert-annotated engine telemetry dataset from 25 commercial vehicles; benchmark shows simple classical methods often match or exceed deep learning performance on segment-based anomaly detection (avg F1 ~0.65), with high cross-vehicle performance variability.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces EngineAD, a novel dataset addressing the scarcity of real-world benchmarks for anomaly detection in transportation systems. It comprises six months of high-resolution (1Hz) sensor telemetry from 25 commercial vehicles, with expert annotations identifying segments indicative of incipient engine faults. The data is preprocessed into 300-timestep (5-minute) windows of 8 principal components derived via fleet-wide PCA. Benchmarking nine one-class models reveals two key findings: first, classical methods like K-Means and One-Class SVM frequently perform as well as or better than deep learning approaches; second, there is substantial performance disparity across vehicles, with F1-scores ranging from 0.21 to 0.93, highlighting significant domain shift challenges.

## Method Summary
Raw CAN bus data undergoes a standardized preprocessing pipeline: initial cleaning, identification of continuous engine-on periods (using RPM with a 30-second gap threshold), resampling to a uniform 1Hz, and segmentation into non-overlapping 300-timestep blocks. A fleet-wide Principal Component Analysis (explained variance ratio 0.95) is fitted on all vehicles' normalized sensor data to reduce the 13 raw sensors to 8 principal components, ensuring a consistent, anonymized feature space. For each vehicle, normal segments are split 70/30 for training and testing; all anomalous segments are reserved for the test set. Models are trained on the normal training segments, and an anomaly threshold is set at the 95th percentile of the training anomaly scores. Performance is evaluated on the test set using segment-level F1-score.

## Key Results
- EngineAD provides 6 months of authentic operational telemetry from 25 commercial vehicles, with expert labels distinguishing normal states from subtle precursors to engine failure.
- Benchmark evaluation across nine one-class models (K-Means, OCSVM, IF, LOF, AE, DeepSVDD, etc.) yields an average segment-based F1-score of approximately 0.65, with classical methods often superior to deep learning.
- Cross-vehicle performance is extremely variable, with F1-scores per vehicle ranging from 0.21 to 0.93, indicating a major challenge of domain shift for deployment.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Authentic, real-world operational data with expert annotations captures the subtle, context-dependent nature of true engine anomalies better than synthetic or injected datasets.
- **Mechanism:** Continuous high-resolution telemetry (1Hz) from 25 commercial vehicles over six months is collected via CAN bus logging. Technicians analyze sensor traces and maintenance reports to label segments as "normal" or "abnormal" based on incipient fault indicators. This process grounds the dataset in naturally occurring failure patterns, including gradual drifts and transient spikes.
- **Core assumption:** Human expert analysis of combined sensor traces and maintenance history can reliably distinguish between normal operational variance and early fault signatures.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Unlike synthetic datasets, EngineAD features authentic operational data labeled with expert annotations, distinguishing normal states from subtle indicators of incipient engine faults."
  - [section 3.1] "The recording company employed a team of technicians who systematically analyzed both the sensor traces and maintenance reports. Through this process, they annotated operational states, distinguishing between normal conditions and patterns indicative of potential defects that may precede engine failure."
- **Break condition:** If expert labeling is inconsistent, biased, or misses key failure modes, the "authentic" label becomes unreliable, potentially embedding human error into the benchmark.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Fleet-wide Principal Component Analysis (PCA) provides a consistent, anonymized feature space that preserves dominant variance patterns across all vehicles while preventing vehicle-specific reconstruction.
- **Mechanism:** PCA is fitted on the entire fleet's normalized sensor data (`n_components=0.95`), reducing 13 sensors to 8 principal components. This creates a single, shared representation of "normal" variation, removes vehicle-identifying information, and ensures all vehicles' data resides in the same latent space for fair model comparison.
- **Core assumption:** The dominant sources of variation (principal components) across the entire fleet are meaningful for characterizing normal engine operation and that anomalies will manifest as deviations in this shared space.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.3] "To ensure a consistent and objective transformation, both data normalization and the PCA model were fitted across the entire vehicle fleet... This approach is preferable to training separate models for each vehicle, as it establishes a single, fleet-wide baseline... The PCA model was configured with `n_components = 0.95`... after running PCA... 8 principal components were saved."
- **Break condition:** If vehicle-to-vehicle physiological differences (e.g., wear, calibration) are the primary source of variation, fleet-wide PCA could obscure vehicle-specific normal patterns, making anomalies harder to detect or creating artificial "anomalies" due to the projection.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Segmenting time series into fixed-length, non-overlapping windows (300 timesteps = 5 minutes) and flattening them transforms the temporal AD problem into a static, high-dimensional outlier detection task, enabling the application of a wide range of classical and deep one-class models.
- **Mechanism:** Continuous engine-on periods are split into 5-minute blocks. Each block's 8 PC × 300 timestep data is flattened into a 2400-dimensional vector. Models are trained on these vectors using a one-class protocol (train on normal only). This bypasses sequential model complexity but discards fine-grained temporal ordering within the window.
- **Core assumption:** The relevant anomaly signature for "incipient engine faults" is sufficiently captured by the aggregate statistical and spectral properties within a 5-minute window, not requiring explicit modeling of long-range dependencies across windows.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.2] "The time series were divided into non-overlapping segments of length L= 300 (five minutes)... This approach ensures that the resulting dataset accurately captures meaningful operational patterns."
  - [section 4.3] "simpler, non-parametric methods—specifically KMeans Distance and One-Class SVM—demonstrate superior or highly competitive performance... This implies that for many anomalies in this dataset, the pattern of deviation from the norm is substantial enough to be captured by global distance or boundary-based measures in the flattened feature space."
- **Break condition:** If critical anomalies manifest as subtle, long-duration trends spanning multiple segments (e.g., slow degradation), flattening will destroy the temporal pattern, rendering segment-level models ineffective.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: One-Class Classification (OCC)**
  - **Why needed here:** The benchmark and paper's core experiments use a one-class paradigm, simulating the real-world scenario where only normal operational data is plentiful. All nine evaluated models are fundamentally one-class or are adapted as such.
  - **Quick check question:** What is the key conceptual difference between a one-class SVM and a standard binary SVM, and why does this matter for anomaly detection with scarce abnormal data?

- **Concept: Time Series Segmentation for Static Models**
  - **Why needed here:** The dataset's primary evaluation unit is a flattened segment. Understanding the trade-off—losing intra-segment temporal resolution to gain compatibility with a broader model zoo—is central to interpreting the results.
  - **Quick check question:** If an anomaly is a 30-second irregular vibration pattern within a 5-minute normal window, how might flattening the segment affect a model's ability to detect it?

- **Concept: Cross-Vehicle Distribution Shift (Domain Shift)**
  - **Why needed here:** The paper's most striking finding is the huge F1-score variance across the 25 trucks (0.21 to 0.93). This indicates that the "normal" manifold learned from one vehicle's data may not generalize to another, a core challenge for deployment.
  - **Quick check question:** Why might Truck 25's anomalies be so much harder to detect than Truck 19's, even using the same model and preprocessing?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Raw CAN CSV → Initial Cleaning → Engine-On Block Identification → Resampling to 1Hz → Segmentation (L=300) → Fleet-wide StandardScaler → Fleet-wide PCA (to 8 PCs) → Per-Vehicle StandardScaler (on train only) → Flatten Segment (2400-D) → Model Training (on normal only) → Anomaly Scoring → Thresholding (95th pct) → F1 Calculation.
- **Critical path:** 1) Fleet-wide PCA must be fitted on combined all-vehicle data before any per-vehicle segmentation. 2) For each vehicle, its own StandardScaler is fitted only on its training normal segments to prevent leakage. 3) The evaluation metric is computed per vehicle, then averaged.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Fleet-wide vs. Per-Vehicle PCA: Fleet-wide ensures comparability but risks blending vehicle-specific normals. Per-vehicle preserves idiosyncrasies but makes cross-vehicle comparison meaningless.
  - Segment Length (L=300): Long enough to capture meaningful patterns but short enough to treat as i.i.d. for classical models. Too short may miss context; too long may mix states.
  - Flattening vs. Sequence Modeling: Flattening enables use of simple models but destroys temporal order. A sequence model might capture order but requires more data and compute.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - High inter-vehicle variance suggests preprocessing or anomaly definitions don't yield a consistent "anomaly" representation.
  - Deep models underperforming classical ones suggests anomalies are globally distinguishable in PC space (large-margin separation works) rather than requiring complex manifolds.
  - Very low F1 on a vehicle (e.g., ~0.2) suggests its anomalies are extremely subtle in PC space, mislabeled, or constitute a different distribution than the fleet-wide PCA basis.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Baseline Replication:** On a single high-score vehicle (e.g., Truck 19), run all 9 models with exact train/test split and 95th-percentile thresholding. Aim to reproduce reported F1 within ±0.05.
  2. **PCA Ablation:** For one vehicle, train the best classical model on (a) the 8 fleet-wide PCs, (b) the raw 13 standardized sensors, and (c) random 8-D projections. Compare F1 to assess PCA's utility.
  3. **Cross-Vehicle Generalization:** Train a model (e.g., OCSVM) on the combined normal segments from 24 vehicles (using fleet-wide PCA). Test on the held-out 25th vehicle. Quantify the performance drop to measure domain shift.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1  
- **Question:** How can anomaly detection models be adapted to handle the significant cross-vehicle performance variability observed in EngineAD?  
- **Basis in paper:** The conclusion states that "primary research should focus on developing methods that explicitly handle the cross-vehicle data shift through domain adaptation."  
- **Why unresolved:** Table 1 shows F1-scores ranging from 0.21 to 0.93 across vehicles, indicating that models trained on one vehicle's normal data fail to generalize to others due to distributional shifts.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Development and benchmarking of domain adaptation techniques (e.g., few-shot learning, Federated Learning) on EngineAD, with evaluations measuring performance consistency across vehicles.  

### Open Question 2  
- **Question:** How can anomaly detection outputs be linked back to the original high-dimensional sensor space to provide interpretable diagnostic insights for technicians?  
- **Basis in paper:** The conclusion calls for "enhancing model interpretability to provide actionable diagnostic insights by linking anomaly scores back to the original sensor space."  
- **Why unresolved:** The dataset is released as 8 principal components, obscuring which specific sensors contribute to anomalies; current models score segments in PCA space without sensor-level explanations.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Methods that map PCA-space anomalies to contributions in the original 13-sensor space, validated through technician assessments of generated explanations.  

### Open Question 3  
- **Question:** What underlying factors—such as anomaly types, driving conditions, or data quality—cause certain vehicles (e.g., Truck 25) to exhibit near-random detection performance across all models?  
- **Basis in paper:** Table 1 shows extremely low F1-scores (e.g., 0.21 for Truck 25) despite diverse model architectures, while others achieve high scores (e.g., 0.93 for Truck 19). The discussion notes anomalies can be "exceptionally subtle, highly contextual, or closely integrated into the spectrum of normal operations."  
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not analyze per-vehicle characteristics that correlate with detection difficulty, leaving unclear whether low performance stems from anomaly rarity, specific fault modes, or environmental confounders.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An exploratory analysis correlating vehicle-level metadata (e.g., route types, maintenance history) with anomaly labels and model performance, or qualitative review of raw sensor trajectories for hardest vehicles.  

### Open Question 4  
- **Question:** Does fleet-wide PCA preprocessing, while essential for anonymization, inadvertently suppress subtle, vehicle-specific anomaly patterns that are only visible in the original sensor space?  
- **Basis in paper:** Section 3.3 describes applying PCA fitted across the entire fleet to reduce to 8 components, primarily for anonymization. This transformation may discard variations that are normal for one vehicle but anomalous for another.  
- **Why unresolved:** All benchmark results are based on PCA-transformed data; no comparison exists with per-vehicle PCA or raw sensor inputs.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Re-running key experiments using alternative preprocessing (e.g., per-vehicle PCA, no PCA with added noise for privacy) and comparing performance, especially on vehicles with low F1-scores.  

### Open Question 5  
- **Question:** Are segment-based anomaly detectors, which treat each 5-minute window as an independent instance, capable of detecting anomalies that evolve over longer temporal horizons (e.g., gradual degradation)?  
- **Basis in paper:** The evaluation flattens 300-timestep segments into feature vectors, ignoring dependencies between segments. The anomaly taxonomy includes "sequence anomalies" involving subsequences, but the benchmark design may not capture these effectively.  
- **Why unresolved:** The dataset likely contains gradual faults, but current evaluation discards temporal context across segments, potentially underestimating model performance on real-world sequence anomalies.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An alternative evaluation protocol where segments are sequentially ordered and models incorporate memory (e.g., LSTM-based scoring) or where annotations are extended to longer sequences, then comparing detection performance.

## Limitations
- Expert annotation consistency and inter-rater reliability are not reported or quantified.
- Fleet-wide PCA, used for anonymization and consistency, may obscure vehicle-specific normal patterns and harm cross-vehicle generalization.
- The segment flattening approach discards all temporal ordering within a window, potentially missing anomalies defined by long-range or cross-segment dependencies.
- Deep model underperformance relative to classical methods may be influenced by unspecified or suboptimal hyperparameters, not inherent model limitations.
- The high cross-vehicle variability suggests the "normal" manifold is not unified, challenging the assumption of a single deployable model.

## Confidence
- **Real-world dataset with expert labels:** High (explicitly stated, though annotation reliability unquantified).
- **Fleet-wide PCA consistency:** Medium (assumption not validated; per-vehicle alternative unexplored in paper).
- **Segment flattening efficacy:** Medium (no comparison to sequence models; assumes intra-window stationarity captures relevant anomalies).
- **Classical methods superiority:** Medium (could be due to deep model hyperparameter choices; results depend on thresholding and specific train-test splits).
- **High cross-vehicle variability:** High (empirically observed across 25 vehicles in Table 1).
