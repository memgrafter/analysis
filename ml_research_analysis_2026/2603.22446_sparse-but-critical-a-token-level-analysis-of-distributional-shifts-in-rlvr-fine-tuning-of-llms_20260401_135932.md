# Sparse but Critical: A Token-Level Analysis of Distributional Shifts in RLVR Fine-Tuning of LLMs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.22446  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22446  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** RLVR induces sparse token-level shifts (<20% divergent tokens); critical token positions disproportionately drive performance, recoverable with <10% token interventions.

## Executive Summary
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) enhances LLM reasoning, yet the token-level mechanisms behind its gains remain opaque. This paper analyzes RLVR by measuring Jensen–Shannon divergence between base and RL policies at each token position and through cross-sampling interventions that selectively swap token choices. The findings reveal that RLVR produces highly sparse distributional shifts: the vast majority (83–98%) of tokens exhibit near-zero divergence, with changes concentrated at sequence boundaries. Crucially, injecting a small fraction of RL-sampled tokens (∼1–10%) into base-model generations progressively recovers RL performance, while reverting a similar fraction of RL tokens to base choices collapses performance to base levels. Further analysis shows RLVR primarily reorders tokens already plausible under the base model rather than introducing novel candidates. The work indicates RLVR acts as a targeted refinement over a few high-impact decisions.

## Method Summary
The study quantifies token-level distributional shifts by computing Jensen–Shannon (JS) divergence between the base policy (π_base) and RL policy (π_RL) at each token position, given identical prefixes in RL-generated sequences. High-divergence positions are identified via a threshold (ε). Cross-sampling interventions then generate new sequences by replacing tokens at these positions: forward sampling injects RL tokens into base generations (π_base primary, π_RL intervention), while reverse sampling replaces RL tokens with base choices (π_RL primary, π_base intervention). By varying the intervention budget (number of substituted tokens) and measuring sequence-level accuracy on reasoning benchmarks (AIME, GPQA), the causal impact of divergent token decisions is isolated. Experiments use Qwen2.5-32B with SimpleRL and DAPO checkpoints, sampling 32 responses per problem with nucleus sampling (top-p=0.7, temperature=1).

## Key Results
- RLVR induces highly sparse shifts: >83–98% of token positions show near-zero JS divergence; changes cluster at the beginning and end of sequences.
- Forward cross-sampling recovers RL accuracy with minimal interventions (<4% tokens for SimpleRL on AIME 2024), while reverse cross-sampling degrades RL accuracy with similarly few reversions (<5%).
- At high-divergence positions, RLVR mainly reorders existing candidate tokens (top-k overlap ≥80% for k≥2) rather than promoting novel or low-probability base tokens.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Sparse Token-Level Shifts**  
*Claim:* RLVR fine-tuning induces highly sparse token-level distributional shifts relative to the base model.  
*Mechanism:* The RL policy modifies next-token distributions at only a small subset of positions (high JS divergence), while leaving the vast majority (>83–98%) nearly identical to the base policy. Changes concentrate at sequence boundaries (start/end).  
*Core assumption:* Performance gains stem from targeted refinements rather than wholesale policy rewriting.  
*Evidence anchors:* Abstract states shifts are "highly sparse and targeted"; section 2.2 shows histograms/percentiles with >98% near-zero JS for SimpleRL and positional concentration plots; corpus lacks direct support on sparsity quantification.

**Mechanism 2: Functional Criticality of Sparse Tokens**  
*Claim:* The sparse high-divergence token positions are functionally critical; they steer reasoning trajectories and account for most performance gains.  
*Mechanism:* Cross-sampling interventions that inject RL tokens into base generations (forward) progressively recover RL accuracy with few substitutions (~1–10% tokens). Conversely, reverting RL tokens to base choices (reverse) collapses RL performance with similarly few reversions.  
*Core assumption:* Base and RL policies are behaviorally similar except at sparse decision points that disproportionately influence outcomes.  
*Evidence anchors:* Abstract notes recovery/collapse with small token fractions; section 3.2 provides thresholds (e.g., <4% tokens for SimpleRL AIME24 recovery) and monotonic accuracy curves; corpus lacks direct citation on functional isolation via cross-sampling.

**Mechanism 3: Candidate-Set Preservation via Reordering**  
*Claim:* At high-divergence positions, RLVR primarily reallocates probability mass within an existing candidate set (tokens already plausible under the base model), rather than introducing novel tokens.  
*Mechanism:* High top-k overlap (≥80% for k≥2) between base/RL candidate sets; RL top-1 tokens typically already in base top-3; low promotion of base low-probability tokens (<5%).  
*Core assumption:* RLVR acts as a re-ranker/amplifier of base model capabilities rather than an inventor of new reasoning steps.  
*Evidence anchors:* Abstract states RLVR "primarily reorders existing candidate tokens"; section 4.1 shows top-k overlap and base ranks of RL top-3; section 4.2 shows low base probability fraction; corpus does not directly address candidate-set preservation.

## Foundational Learning
- **Token-level distributional divergence (JS divergence)**  
  *Why needed:* Quantifies how much the RL policy’s next-token distribution differs from the base model at each position, identifying “high-impact” tokens.  
  *Quick check:* If JS divergence is near-zero at a position, does that mean the token chosen will be identical? (Not necessarily—distributions can be similar but still sample different tokens due to randomness.)

- **Cross-sampling intervention design**  
  *Why needed:* Isolates causal effect of specific token choices by swapping between policies while controlling for trajectory context.  
  *Quick check:* In forward cross-sampling, if you replace only the first 5 divergent tokens with RL choices, why might the base model still produce a correct answer? (Because early edits redirect the entire reasoning trajectory; later generation under base policy follows a different path.)

- **RLVR algorithm variants (GRPO vs. DAPO)**  
  *Why needed:* Different clipping/KL settings affect sparsity and exploration—DAPO’s clip-higher yields broader but still sparse shifts.  
  *Quick check:* How might removing KL regularization (as in DAPO) change the sparsity of distributional shifts? (It could increase the number of high-divergence tokens but not necessarily eliminate sparsity.)

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map** (plain text):  
Base policy (π_base) → generates reference sequences → Divergence calculator computes JS per token (π_base vs π_RL) → Switching rule selects positions where JS > ε → Cross-sampler: forward (base primary, RL intervention) or reverse (RL primary, base intervention) → Evaluation module computes accuracy.

**Critical path:**  
1. Generate reference sequences using π_RL.  
2. For each token position, compute JS divergence between π_base and π_RL given the same prefix (top-p truncated).  
3. Identify positions where JS > ε (high-divergence tokens).  
4. Run forward/reverse cross-sampling by substituting tokens at selected positions, then continue generation with the primary policy.  
5. Measure sequence-level accuracy (Mean@32) vs. intervention budget (k tokens or %).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Divergence threshold (ε):* Lower → more interventions (less sparse view); higher → fewer interventions (may miss subtle but critical tokens).  
- *Sampling top-p:* Truncation affects divergence estimates; must match training/sampling configuration (paper uses top-p=0.7).  
- *Intervention budget:* Must be sufficient to capture propagation effects; too small may not reveal trajectory steering.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Cross-sampling shows no accuracy change despite many interventions → threshold poorly calibrated or RL gains not concentrated.  
- Forward cross-sampling fails to recover RL accuracy → high-divergence tokens may be necessary but not sufficient; later tokens or global structure matter.  
- Reverse cross-sampling doesn’t degrade RL accuracy → RL policy not functionally dependent on those divergent decisions.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Replicate token-level sparsity analysis: Compute JS divergence histograms for your base/RL model pair on a held-out reasoning set; verify >80% near-zero divergence and boundary concentration.  
2. Run basic forward cross-sampling: Use ε=median(JS) as initial threshold; inject RL tokens into base generations up to 10% token budget; plot accuracy vs. budget.  
3. Check candidate-set overlap: At high-divergence positions (JS>0.1), compute top-3 overlap between base/RL; expect >70% overlap if reordering dominates.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Do RLVR's sparse token-level shifts generalize beyond math reasoning to other domains like coding or instruction following?**  
  *Basis:* Only AIME/GPQA evaluated; domain specificity unclear.  
  *Why unresolved:* Generalization would indicate RLVR is a fundamental mechanism; failure suggests task-dependent effects.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Replicate analysis on RLVR models fine-tuned for coding (HumanEval) and chat (MT-Bench), compare JS sparsity and cross-sampling recovery.

- **Can we predict which token positions will be critical for RLVR performance from the base model alone, before RL training?**  
  *Basis:* Paper locates critical positions post-hoc via divergence threshold; no predictive framework exists.  
  *Why unresolved:* Would enable efficient RL by targeting updates; currently requires full training to identify.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Build a predictor using base-model features (entropy, probability margin, position) post-training on RL models; test on new models/tasks.

- **What is the theoretical basis for divergence-weighted advantages improving RLVR, and how can we make them robust across models?**  
  *Basis:* The paper notes mixed results and that "stabilizing performance may require model-specific choices and further investigation."  
  *Why unresolved:* Heuristic weighting lacks justification; inconsistent improvements hinder practical use.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Systematic study of weight functions, schedules across models; theory linking weighting to gradient variance; propose adaptive schemes validated on multiple benchmarks.

## Limitations
- **Threshold dependency:** Cross-sampling thresholds (ε) are manually selected per model/dataset; the paper does not provide a general selection rule, limiting reproducibility.
- **Token-counting semantics:** “Effective token” definition excludes identity swaps, but precise implementation (e.g., whether identical tokens in different positions count) is unspecified, affecting reported intervention percentages.
- **Generality scope:** Findings are based on Qwen2.5-32B on AIME/GPQA; applicability to other architectures, scales, or non-reasoning tasks remains unverified.

## Confidence
- **Sparsity of shifts:** High  
- **Functional criticality of sparse tokens:** Medium  
- **Candidate-set preservation (reordering > novel tokens):** Medium

## Next Checks
1. Reproduce JS sparsity histograms for a held-out reasoning set, verifying >80% near-zero divergence and boundary concentration. If patterns differ, investigate sampling hyperparameter mismatches (top-p).
2. Run forward/reverse cross-sampling with a range of ε thresholds (e.g., median, 75th percentile) on at least two model-dataset pairs. Check for monotonic accuracy recovery/erasure; non-monotonicity indicates threshold sensitivity.
3. Measure top-k candidate overlap at high-divergence positions (JS>0.1) across multiple RL variants (e.g., GRPO, DAPO). If overlap drops below 70% for any variant, the “reordering” claim is not universal.
