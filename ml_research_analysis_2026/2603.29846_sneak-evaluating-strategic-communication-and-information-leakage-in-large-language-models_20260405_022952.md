# SNEAK: Evaluating Strategic Communication and Information Leakage in Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.29846
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.29846
- Reference count: 27
- Primary result: Modern LLMs achieve high utility but also high leakage in selective communication tasks, yielding low SoftScores compared to humans.

## Executive Summary
SNEAK introduces a benchmark for evaluating large language models' ability to generate messages that strategically signal secret knowledge to a collaborator (ally) while concealing it from an adversary (chameleon). Given a semantic category, candidate words, and a secret word, the model produces a short message. Evaluation employs two simulated agents: an ally identifies the true message from decoys, and a chameleon infers the secret from candidates. Normalized probabilities yield utility and leakage metrics, combined into SoftScore (utility × (1 − leakage)). Across 1,394 instances, current LLMs show a strong utility–leakage trade-off—high utility is accompanied by high leakage—resulting in scores far below human performance. Inference-time scaling improves utility but not leakage, highlighting a gap in strategic signaling capability.

## Method Summary
The benchmark builds instances from semantic category production norms: for each of 117 categories, 10–12 frequent exemplars form the candidate set, and each exemplar is used as a secret in turn. Decoy messages (5 per instance) are generated by aggregating frequent WordNet attributes across the candidate set. The target LLM is prompted with the category, candidates, and secret to generate a 1–5 word message. Three separate LLM evaluators (Mixtral-8x7B, Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct, Qwen-2.5-72B-Instruct) then assess each message: the ally selects the true message from the decoy set, and the chameleon selects the secret from the candidates. Probabilities are normalized against chance (set-size baselines) to produce [0,1] utility and leakage scores. SoftScore and BinaryScore are computed and averaged across evaluators. The pipeline scales to 1,394 instances and supports test-time scaling methods (CoT, SETS, RMR).

## Key Results
- Gemma-3-27B attains 92.23 utility but 59.23 leakage (SoftScore 34.87), while humans achieve 91.26 utility with 33.81 leakage (SoftScore 59.22)—up to 4× higher.
- Inference-time scaling (CoT, SETS, RMR) consistently improves utility but leaves leakage unchanged, failing to match human selective communication.
- All tested models exhibit a pronounced utility–leakage trade-off, with no model achieving both high utility and low leakage.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Strategic communication under asymmetric information requires balancing informativeness (utility) and secrecy (leakage). The paper formalizes this as maximizing SoftScore(m) = Utility(m) × (1 − Leakage(m)), where utility and leakage are normalized probabilities derived from constrained-decoding simulations of an ally (identifies the message) and a chameleon (infers the secret). Evidence: abstract and section 2.1 define the trade-off and SoftScore objective.

### Mechanism 2
The evaluation framework decouples communicative intent from content by using separate simulated agents with different information states. The ally sees the secret and candidate messages; the chameleon sees only the message and candidates. Both perform discrete classification via constrained decoding, converting subjective judgment into reproducible probabilities. Evidence: section 2.3 describes the behavioral evaluation; section 4.1 reports 88.9% ally and 66.7% chameleon human–LLM agreement.

### Mechanism 3
Empirically, modern LLMs exhibit a pronounced utility–leakage trade-off: high utility is correlated with high leakage. Humans achieve similar utility but significantly lower leakage, yielding much higher SoftScores. Inference-time scaling improves utility but not leakage, suggesting models lack mechanisms to suppress informativeness for adversaries without harming collaborator recognition. Evidence: abstract (Gemma vs. human scores), section 3.4 (trade-off across models), section 5 (contrast with prior work).

## Foundational Learning

- **Asymmetric information signaling**  
  Why needed: The core task requires a message that is informative to an informed party but ambiguous to an uninformed one, drawing from game theory and pragmatics.  
  Quick check: How would you design a message about the secret "dog" from candidates {cat, dog, horse} that a dog-knowing ally would recognize but a chameleon might think could also describe "cat"?

- **Evaluation via simulated agents**  
  Why needed: Human evaluation is costly; using LLM agents to simulate ally/chameleon converts subjective judgment into constrained classification, enabling scalable and reproducible scoring.  
  Quick check: What are two potential biases in using an LLM as a judge for the ally task, and how does the paper try to mitigate them?

- **Utility-leakage trade-off and normalization**  
  Why needed: Raw probabilities are incomparable across instances with different candidate/decoy set sizes. Normalization against chance allows aggregation and defines a composite score that penalizes leakage.  
  Quick check: Why is a message that gets 100% ally probability but 90% chameleon probability (on a 10-word candidate set) worse than one with 60% ally probability and 5% chameleon probability, according to SoftScore?

## Architecture Onboarding

Component map:
Dataset Construction -> Decoy Generation -> Message Generation -> Evaluator Agents -> Scoring

Critical path: Create instance (category, candidates, secret) → generate message → ally evaluation (probability over messages) → chameleon evaluation (probability over words) → normalize → aggregate scores → compare models/conditions. Parsing failures or incomplete distributions break the pipeline.

Design tradeoffs:
- Candidate set size (|W|): Larger sets reduce leakage but not utility; trade-off between realism and metric sensitivity.
- Decoy count (|M|): More decoys lower utility (harder ally task); trade-off evaluation difficulty vs. discrimination power.
- Evaluator choice: More evaluators improve robustness but increase cost; trade-off scalability vs. bias reduction.
- Message length constraint (≤5 words): Encourages strategic brevity but may restrict natural expression.

Failure signatures:
- Parser failures: Message not found in <message> tags → discard or default.
- Evaluator formatting: Non-letter outputs or invalid probability sums → prompt/decoding adjustment needed.
- High utility & high leakage cluster: All models cluster in upper-right, indicating inability to decouple objectives.
- High evaluator disagreement: Standard deviation >10 points on metrics suggests task ambiguity or inconsistency.
- Decoy sensitivity: Changing decoy method drastically alters scores, indicating artifact-driven results.

First 3 experiments:
1. Baseline replication: Run random word, category-synonym, and secret-synonym baselines through the full pipeline; verify scoring reproduces Table 2 values.
2. Evaluator robustness check: For one generator model (e.g., Gemma-3-27B), compute scores using each evaluator individually and together; calculate Spearman correlation of model orderings; high correlation (>0.9) validates averaging.
3. Human alignment spot-check: For 5–10 diverse categories, manually craft a "good" (high utility, low leakage) and a "bad" (low utility, high leakage) message; run through evaluators; confirm expected score quadrants.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- How can the SNEAK framework be extended to multi-turn, multi-agent interactions to capture richer strategic communication dynamics?
- To what extent do current LLMs' struggles with selective communication stem from the narrow, structured domains of the SNEAK dataset, and can performance be improved by evaluating on more open-domain, context-rich scenarios?
- How reliable are LLM-based evaluators for measuring human-like selective communication, given only moderate alignment with human judgments in the chameleon task?

## Limitations
- Decoy construction specifics (WordNet relations, thresholds) are not fully documented, risking replication differences.
- Label randomization seeds and tie-handling for BinaryScore are unspecified, affecting marginal scores.
- Evaluator inference parameters (temperature, constrained decoding method) are unreported, limiting fidelity.

## Confidence
- Utility–leakage trade-off as fundamental: High
- Simulated agents approximate human judgment: Medium
- Current LLMs lack strategic signaling: Medium

## Next Checks
1. Decoy sensitivity test: Replicate with two decoy generation methods (original WordNet vs. LLM-based from Appendix C); compute correlation in per-instance SoftScore changes.
2. Evaluator robustness audit: For a fixed generator (e.g., Gemma-3-27B), run evaluation with 10 random seeds per instance; report variance in mean Utility/Leakage.
3. Human–simulated agent divergence analysis: Collect human ally/chameleon judgments on 50 instances with highest simulated evaluator disagreement; measure correlation with human majority vote.
