# Decomposing Probabilistic Scores: Reliability, Information Loss and Uncertainty

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.15232
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15232
- Reference count: 31
- Primary result: Decomposition identities for proper losses separate miscalibration, information loss from feature compression, and irreducible uncertainty; recalibration only fixes the first, not the second.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces a rigorous framework for decomposing the expected loss of probabilistic predictors into three interpretable components: miscalibration (reliability term), grouping (information loss due to compressing features to scores), and irreducible uncertainty (inherent randomness). This decomposition applies to any proper scoring rule and any nested information levels via σ‑algebras. It clarifies that post‑hoc recalibration can only address miscalibration, while any information discarded when forming the score (e.g., via dimensionality reduction or ensembling) remains as grouping loss. The theory yields new insights: averaging calibrated models typically destroys calibration because the average is rarely sufficient; boosting gains are framed as successive reductions in conditional uncertainty. Empirical results on synthetic and German Credit data validate the decomposition and illustrate practical consequences.

## Method Summary
The authors develop a theoretical decomposition based on σ‑algebras representing information levels. For a predictor $T$ measurable with respect to σ‑algebra $\mathcal{A}$, the expected proper loss splits into a proper‑regret term (distance between $T$ and the conditional law $Q_{\mathcal{A}} = P(Y|\mathcal{A})$) plus a conditional entropy term $E\ell(Q_{\mathcal{A}})$. When two nested σ‑algebras $\mathcal{A} \subseteq \mathcal{B}$ are considered, an additional information‑gain term appears: $E[d\ell(Q_{\mathcal{A}}, Q_{\mathcal{B}})]$. Specializing to scores $S = s(X)$ with $\mathcal{A} = \sigma(S)$ and $\mathcal{B} = \sigma(X)$ yields the three‑term identity: total loss = reliability + grouping + irreducible uncertainty. This framework enables precise quantification of how much loss is due to misspecification versus inherent noise, and how post‑hoc calibration or feature compression affects performance.

## Key Results
- Decomposition identity: For any proper loss $\ell$ and any predictor $T$ measurable w.r.t. $\mathcal{A}$, $E[\ell(T,Y)] = E[d\ell(T,Q_{\mathcal{A}})] + E[E\ell(Q_{\mathcal{A}})]$.
- Chain rule for nested levels: If $\mathcal{A} \subseteq \mathcal{B}$, then $E[\ell(T,Y)] = E[d\ell(T,Q_{\mathcal{A}})] + E[d\ell(Q_{\mathcal{A}},Q_{\mathcal{B}})] + E[E\ell(Q_{\mathcal{B}})]$.
- For scores $S = s(X)$: $E[\ell(S,Y)] = \text{reliability (miscalibration)} + \text{grouping (information loss from $X\to S$)} + \text{irreducible uncertainty (e.g., $H(Y|X)$ for log‑loss)}$.
- Recalibration (transforming $S$) only reduces the reliability term; grouping persists if information is lost in compression.
- Averaging calibrated models usually yields a miscalibrated aggregate unless the average is a sufficient statistic—a rare condition; stacking tends to preserve calibration better.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
Assertion: The expected loss of any predictor $T$ that is $\mathcal{A}$‑measurable equals the sum of a proper‑regret (reliability) and a conditional entropy (residual uncertainty).
Technique: Apply the tower property of conditional expectation to a proper scoring rule. For $Q_{\mathcal{A}} = P(Y\in\cdot|\mathcal{A})$, we have
$E[\ell(T,Y)] = E[d\ell(T,Q_{\mathcal{A}})] + E[E\ell(Q_{\mathcal{A}})]$.
Requirement: $\ell$ must be proper (strictly proper for unique decomposition). Evidence: Theorem 2.1 in the paper shows the identity using the defining property of proper losses.

### Mechanism 2
Assertion: When two information levels are nested ($\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{B}$), the excess risk decomposes further to reveal an intermediate information‑gain term.
Technique: Iterate the one‑level decomposition: first at $\mathcal{A}$, then decompose the residual $E[E\ell(Q_{\mathcal{A}})]$ using the finer $\mathcal{B}$. This yields
$E[\ell(T,Y)] = E[d\ell(T,Q_{\mathcal{A}})] + E[d\ell(Q_{\mathcal{A}},Q_{\mathcal{B}})] + E[E\ell(Q_{\mathcal{B}})]$.
Requirement: Nested σ‑algebras; no assumption beyond measurability. Evidence: Theorem 2.2 establishes the chain identity; Theorem 5.3 extends it to telescoping sums across a filtration.

### Mechanism 3
Assertion: For a score $S = s(X)$, the loss splits into (i) miscalibration, (ii) grouping (information loss from compressing $X$ to $S$), and (iii) irreducible uncertainty at the feature level.
Technique: Set $\mathcal{A} = \sigma(S)$, $\mathcal{B} = \sigma(X)$. Then $C = Q_{\mathcal{A}} = P(Y|S)$ and $Q = Q_{\mathcal{B}} = P(Y|X)$. The term $E[d\ell(C,Q)]$ quantifies the expected loss due to discarding information when replacing $X$ by $S$.
Requirement: $S$ is a deterministic function of $X$ (i.e., $\sigma(S) \subseteq \sigma(X)$). Evidence: Lemma 2.3 characterizes when grouping vanishes: iff $C=Q$ a.s., i.e., $S$ is sufficient for $Y$ given $X$. Experiments (e.g., GermanCredit) show grouping remains after recalibration because $S$ alone is not sufficient.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Proper scoring rule**
  - Why needed: Proper losses induce non‑negative regret $d\ell$ and enable a clean separation of calibration (first term) from sharpness (remaining terms); strict propriety ensures the conditional law $Q_{\mathcal{A}}$ is uniquely identified.
  - Quick check: Does $E[d\ell(p,Q)] = 0$ iff $p = Q$ a.s.? If not, the decomposition may conflate reliability and grouping.
- **Concept: σ‑algebra as an information level**
  - Why needed: The entire decomposition hinges on conditioning on σ‑algebras that formalize what information is available to the predictor. $\mathcal{A}$‑measurability of $T$ means $T$ can only depend on events in $\mathcal{A}$.
  - Quick check: Given a predictor $T$, what σ‑algebra does it generate? (Answer: $\sigma(T)$, the smallest σ‑algebra w.r.t. which $T$ is measurable.)
- **Concept: Conditional expectation as projection**
  - Why needed: The conditional law $Q_{\mathcal{A}} = E[\text{onehot}(Y) | \mathcal{A}]$ is the Bayes‑optimal prediction given information $\mathcal{A}$. In the binary Brier case, the decomposition becomes Pythagorean: $E[(T-Q_{\mathcal{A}})^2] = \text{Var}(T-Q_{\mathcal{A}})$ because $T-Q_{\mathcal{A}}$ is orthogonal to any $\mathcal{A}$‑measurable function.
  - Quick check: For binary Brier loss, is $E[(T-Q_{\mathcal{A}})\cdot f] = 0$ for any $\mathcal{A}$‑measurable $f$? (Yes, by definition of conditional expectation.)

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:
1. Predictor $T$ ($\mathcal{A}$‑measurable) → Conditional law $Q_{\mathcal{A}} = P(Y|\mathcal{A})$ → proper regret $d\ell(T,Q_{\mathcal{A}})$ and entropy $E\ell(Q_{\mathcal{A}})$
2. For nested levels: $\mathcal{A} \subseteq \mathcal{B}$ → $Q_{\mathcal{B}} = P(Y|\mathcal{B})$ → information‑gain term $d\ell(Q_{\mathcal{A}},Q_{\mathcal{B}})$ → total loss $E[\ell(T,Y)] = \text{reliability} + \text{information‑gain} + \text{irreducible}$.
3. Specialized to scores: $S = s(X)$ → $\sigma(S) \subseteq \sigma(X)$ → $C = P(Y|S)$, $Q = P(Y|X)$ → miscalibration + grouping + irreducible.

Critical path:
- Choose proper loss $\ell$ and confirm propriety.
- Identify relevant σ‑algebras (e.g., $\sigma(S)$ for a given score, $\sigma(X)$ for full features).
- Estimate or compute the conditional laws $Q_{\mathcal{A}}$ and possibly $Q_{\mathcal{B}}$ (requires calibration data or model assumptions).
- Evaluate decomposition terms: reliability $= E[d\ell(S, C)]$, grouping $= E[d\ell(C, Q)]$, irreducible $= E[E\ell(Q)]$.
- For ensembling: check if the aggregate’s σ‑algebra equals that of a sufficient statistic; if not, expect calibration loss.

Design tradeoffs:
- Recalibration vs. representation change: Post‑hoc recalibration (e.g., isotonic regression) can only alter the score within $\sigma(S)$, removing only the reliability term. To reduce grouping, one must increase the information captured by $S$ (e.g., richer features, stacking instead of averaging).
- Monotonicity constraints: In binary recalibration, preserving the monotonic relationship between score and true probability (Proposition 4.2) can maintain discrimination while improving reliability.
- Sufficiency vs. aggregation: If the base predictors are calibrated but their average is not sufficient for $Y$ given the individual scores, the average becomes miscalibrated. Stacking tends to retain more information because the meta‑model uses the full vector of scores, approximating a sufficient statistic.

Failure signatures:
- Large grouping term persists after recalibration → the score $S$ discards essential information; consider richer model or stacking.
- Recalibrated loss increases → possible overfitting of the calibrator; use out‑of‑sample calibration (cross‑fitting).
- Ensemble of calibrated models yields miscalibrated output → check whether the aggregation rule (e.g., average) forms a sufficient statistic; if not, use stacking or a more expressive combiner.

First 3 experiments:
1. Synthetic binary with controllable correlation: Train two logistic models on separate covariates; show that averaging their calibrated scores increases miscalibration, especially as covariates become correlated (Figure 1). Grouping remains after recalibration (Figure 2).
2. German Credit dataset: Compare logistic regression and random forest; evaluate raw and isotonic‑recalibrated scores. Stacking reduces reliability more than averaging (Tables 2, 3; calibration curves in Figure 3).
3. Decomposition illustration: On synthetic data, compute Brier reliability and grouping before and after recalibration; recalibration eliminates reliability but not grouping, while a richer score with interaction reduces grouping (Figure 2).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: Identify conditions under which aggregating calibrated probabilistic classifiers yields a calibrated aggregate and measure the resulting grouping loss. The paper notes that averaging often destroys calibration and calls for characterizing aggregators that preserve σ‑algebra sufficiency.
- **Open Question 2**: Create statistical inference tools (confidence intervals, tests) for the decomposition terms under finite samples and distribution shift. The identities are population‑level; inferential methods are needed to assess uncertainty in estimated reliability, grouping, and irreducible components.
- **Open Question 3**: Formulate a transport‑based approach to multi‑class calibration, incorporating monotonicity/smoothness constraints. The binary case has a geometric transport interpretation; extending this to multi‑class could yield new calibration methods.

## Limitations
- Experimental hyperparameters (regularization strengths, tree depths, split ratios) and discretization details are unspecified, preventing exact replication.
- Grouping term requires knowledge of the true conditional $P(Y|X)$; on real data it must be estimated, introducing noise and bias.
- Validation is limited to two datasets (one synthetic, one binary real‑world); broader performance across domains remains uncertain.
- The decomposition assumes proper losses and nested σ‑algebras; extensions to non‑proper or non‑nested settings are not covered.

## Confidence
- **Theoretical decomposition identities**: High (proved under standard measure‑theoretic assumptions).
- **Post‑hoc recalibration removes only miscalibration, not grouping**: Medium (empirical support from one synthetic experiment; method‑dependent).
- **Aggregating calibrated models typically destroys calibration**: Medium‑Low (limited to two models on one dataset; lacks systematic exploration).
