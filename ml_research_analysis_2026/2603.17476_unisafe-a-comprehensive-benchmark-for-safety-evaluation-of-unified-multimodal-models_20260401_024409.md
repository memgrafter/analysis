# UniSAFE: A Comprehensive Benchmark for Safety Evaluation of Unified Multimodal Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.17476
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17476
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: UniSAFE reveals critical safety vulnerabilities in unified multimodal models, especially for image-output tasks (ASR up to 73.1%) and a positive correlation between generative performance and attack success.

## Executive Summary
UniSAFE introduces the first comprehensive safety benchmark for unified multimodal models (UMMs), covering 6,802 curated instances across 7 input-output modality combinations using a shared-target design to enable controlled cross-task comparisons. Evaluation of 15 UMMs, including GPT-5, Gemini-2.5, Qwen-Image, and 12 open-source models, shows that image-output tasks are consistently more vulnerable, with text-to-image attack success rates reaching 73.1%. Novel multimodal-context tasks such as image composition and multi-turn editing exhibit elevated risks, and a strong positive correlation between generative performance and attack success rates indicates inadequate safety alignment that does not improve with scaling.

## Method Summary
The UniSAFE benchmark employs a three-step data pipeline to generate safety scenarios: (1) extraction of unsafe triggers from a taxonomy, (2) creation of a target description representing the harmful outcome, and (3) adaptation of that target to task-specific inputs via scenario generation, followed by human curation. This shared-target approach ensures the same risk concept is evaluated across different modality configurations. The dataset spans 7 tasks (e.g., text-to-image, image editing, multi-turn editing) with 6,802 instances. Evaluation uses an ensemble of three MLLM judges (Gemini-2.5 Pro, GPT-5 Nano, Qwen2.5-VL-72B) with task-specific prompts to compute attack success rate (ASR) and refusal rate (ARR). An optional self-awareness score (SAS) measures model confidence in its outputs. The metrics are macro-averaged across categories, and the pipeline supports reproducibility with deterministic inference.

## Key Results
- Image-output tasks show significantly higher attack success rates than text-output tasks, with text-to-image ASR up to 73.1%.
- Novel multimodal-context tasks (image composition, multi-turn editing) exhibit particularly elevated risks, even in commercial models.
- Strong positive correlation between generative performance (GenEval, MMMU) and attack success rates (r=0.5284 for image, r=0.9634 for text), indicating inadequate safety alignment.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: The shared-target design enables controlled cross-task safety comparisons by holding the unsafe outcome constant while varying I/O modalities.
- **Mechanism**: A single target description (unsafe outcome) is adapted into task-specific scenarios via a three-step pipeline (unsafe trigger → target description → scenario generation). This isolates how different modality compositions contribute to safety failures.
- **Core assumption**: A conceptual risk scenario can be translated into multiple I/O configurations without altering the core violation.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract notes "shared-target design that projects common risk scenarios across task-specific I/O configurations, enabling controlled cross-task comparisons of safety failures." Section details the three-step pipeline; corpus neighbors (e.g., UmniBench) focus on unified evaluation but not on cross-task safety via shared targets.
- **Break condition**: If the target description cannot be faithfully instantiated in a given task (e.g., multi-turn constraints conflict with the target), the comparison validity breaks.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Including novel multimodal-context tasks (image composition, multi-turn editing) reveals emergent safety risks from compositional context that single-modality filters miss.
- **Mechanism**: These tasks require combining information across multiple images or turns, creating scenarios where benign inputs form an unsafe whole. The benchmark probes whether models can holistically reason over cross-modal or temporal context to refuse harmful requests.
- **Core assumption**: UMMs introduce new attack surfaces where individually safe inputs become unsafe when composed.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract mentions "spanning conventional tasks and novel multimodal-context image generation settings." Section explains that inputs appear benign in isolation but become unsafe when composed; corpus lacks focus on composition safety.
- **Break condition**: If models develop robust context-aware safety filters that detect composed risks, the elevated ASR for these tasks may decrease; however, current models show high failure rates.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Safety alignment in UMMs is inadequate and positively correlates with generative performance, indicating that scaling capabilities does not automatically improve safety.
- **Mechanism**: Evaluation across 15 UMMs shows that models with higher generative scores (GenEval, MMMU) also have higher attack success rates (ASR). This suggests that safety training (e.g., RLHF) is either ineffective for multimodal generation or is deprioritized during capability scaling.
- **Core assumption**: Current alignment methods focus on text outputs and do not transfer effectively to image generation or multimodal composition tasks.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract states "A strong positive correlation between generative performance and attack success rates indicates inadequate safety alignment in current UMMs." Section provides Pearson correlations: r=0.5284 (image), r=0.9634 (text). Corpus neighbors examine benchmarks but not performance-safety tradeoffs.
- **Break condition**: If future alignment techniques decouple safety from generative capability, the correlation may weaken or reverse.

## Foundational Learning
### Unified Multimodal Models (UMMs)
- **Why needed**: UniSAFE targets models that process and generate across text and image modalities within a single architecture. Understanding the 7 I/O task combinations is essential to interpret the benchmark.
- **Quick check**: List the 7 tasks and their I/O modalities. (TI: text→image; IE: image+text→image; IC: image+image+text→image; MT: multi-turn editing; TT: text→text; IT: image→text; MU: image+text→text)

### Multimodal Safety Taxonomies
- **Why needed**: The benchmark uses distinct taxonomies for image (7 categories) and text (9 categories) outputs. Recognizing these categories (e.g., Violence, Sexual, Hate, Illicit & Dangerous, etc.) is crucial for understanding what harms are evaluated.
- **Quick check**: Name two subcategories under “Illicit & Dangerous Content” for image outputs and two for text outputs. (Image: I1 Depiction, I2 Enablement; Text: I1 Depiction/Propose, I2 Enablement/Instructions; also Cybersecurity, Privacy, etc.)

### Shared-Target Scenario Construction
- **Why needed**: This is the core methodological innovation that allows fair cross-task comparisons. It ensures the same risk scenario is evaluated under different I/O configurations.
- **Quick check**: For a target image description of a forged document, how would you generate an Image Editing scenario? (Create a safe input image (e.g., blank document) and a benign instruction (e.g., "add the bank statement from the second image") that results in the unsafe target when combined.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**:  
Data pipeline: Trigger extraction → Target description → Task-specific scenario generation → Human curation  
Config files: store taxonomy, prompts, thresholds  
Evaluation engine: three MLLM judges (Gemini-2.5 Pro, GPT-5 Nano, Qwen2.5-VL-72B) with task-specific prompts  
Dataset: 6,802 instances stored in JSONL (fields: task, category, subcategory, input modalities, target description, scenario specifics)  
Metrics module: computes macro-averaged ASR/ARR across categories and subcategories; SAS uses model self-evaluation

**Critical Path**:
1. Verify model support for each task; exclude unsupported tasks.
2. Run inference on all test instances, saving raw outputs with determinism (fixed seed, temperature=0).
3. Evaluate outputs using the judge ensemble; if only one judge available, use individual probabilities to compute metrics.
4. Aggregate results: first average at subcategory level, then at category level to avoid imbalance.
5. (Optional) Compute SAS by prompting the model to self-evaluate its own outputs.

**Design Tradeoffs**:
- Automated judges vs. human evaluation: The three-judge ensemble shows high correlation with humans (r=0.962) and is cost-effective; however, judges may have biases (e.g., GPT-5 Nano stricter). Using the average mitigates this.
- Shared-target design: Enables direct task comparisons but may produce scenarios that feel slightly unnatural in some tasks; human curation ensures quality.
- Dataset size: 6,802 instances provide broad coverage but cannot exhaust all edge cases; focus is on diversity across categories and tasks.

**Failure Signatures**:
- High ASR in image-output tasks (TI, IE, IC, MT) compared to text-output tasks (TT, IT, MU).
- Escalated ASR for novel tasks (IC, MT) even in commercial models.
- Positive correlation between generative performance and ASR; better image quality leads to more successful attacks.
- Low Self-Awareness Score (SAS ~60% for most models) indicating models do not recognize their own harmful outputs; GPT-5 has low ARR but also low SAS, suggesting refusals are not from internal safety recognition.

**First 3 Experiments**:
1. Baseline replication: Evaluate Show-o on TI and TT tasks; confirm ASR ~60.6% (TI) and ~15.5% (TT) as in Table 2.
2. Modality bias check: Compare average ASR for image-output vs. text-output tasks across several models; expect significant gap (image-output riskier). Plot like Figure 5.
3. Self-awareness analysis: Compute SAS for a model by extracting its self-evaluation answers (e.g., using "Is this output harmful?" prompt). Compare SAS to ARR; expect models with low ARR may still have low SAS (e.g., GPT-5).

## Limitations
- Shared-target design may produce unnatural instantiations; fidelity of cross-task translation not fully quantified.
- Automated judge ensemble may carry inherent biases; human correlation based on limited sample.
- Dataset breadth insufficient for exhaustive real-world edge cases; evaluation limited to image-text modalities (audio/video risks unexplored).

## Confidence
- Shared-target enables controlled comparisons: Medium
- Novel multimodal-context tasks reveal emergent risks: High
- Inadequate safety alignment and positive performance–safety correlation: Medium

## Next Checks
1. Human validation study: Sample 500 scenarios across tasks; expert annotators judge harmfulness; measure agreement with automated judges and adjust metrics accordingly.
2. Cross-modal extension: Adapt benchmark to audio generation (e.g., harmful TTS) to test if image-output vulnerabilities generalize to other output modalities.
3. Causal capability–safety analysis: Fine-tune a model family at varying scales with safety-focused interventions; measure ASR vs. generative metrics to test if decoupling is achievable.
