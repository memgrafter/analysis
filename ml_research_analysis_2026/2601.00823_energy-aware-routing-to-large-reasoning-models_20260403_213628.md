# Energy-Aware Routing to Large Reasoning Models
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.00823  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.00823  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: Energy-aware routing with variance‑aware policies achieves √T auxiliary energy scaling when mean renewable supply equals minimum demand; routing errors shift the scaling to linear.

## Executive Summary
The paper studies online dispatch of tasks to multiple large reasoning models under stochastic renewable energy, aiming to minimize auxiliary (non‑renewable) energy while meeting latency and accuracy constraints. It formulates an online routing problem and models battery dynamics as a drifted Brownian motion. Theoretical analysis reveals three regimes: when mean supply exceeds mean demand, auxiliary energy is constant; when supply falls short, it grows linearly with time; and critically, when they are balanced, it grows as the square root of time due to volatility. This √T scaling is identified as the optimal operating point. The optimal routing policy uses inference‑compute scaling laws to select model size and thinking time that minimize per‑task energy below a lower bound. Simulations confirm that perfect routing preserves the √T scaling, while routing errors introduce a positive drift that eventually dominates fluctuations, causing linear scaling. The work highlights the importance of variance‑aware dispatch and accurate modeling of inference energy‑latency trade‑offs.

## Method Summary
The method builds on scaling laws that relate model capability, token budget, and success probability to determine the minimum energy needed for a task on a given model. The dispatcher uses these to select the model and thinking time that meet the accuracy tolerance and deadline while minimizing energy. Battery evolution is modeled as a random walk with drift equal to the difference between average renewable supply and the task‑averaged minimum energy demand; its variance captures renewable fluctuations and the stochasticity of task arrival and difficulty. Through diffusion approximation, the expected auxiliary energy (the running minimum deficit) is derived for three drift regimes. To validate the theory, lumped‑task simulations use a myopic dispatcher that always chooses the energy‑optimal model; deviations from the lower bound are introduced as independent misrouting events. Numerical results show the predicted transition from √T to linear scaling once accumulated error exceeds the fluctuation scale.

## Key Results
- The expected auxiliary energy scales as √T when mean renewable supply equals the long‑run average minimum demand (critical regime), linearly if supply is insufficient, and is O(1) if supply exceeds demand.
- Perfect routing (no mis‑selection) preserves the √T scaling; routing errors introduce a positive mean excess energy that shifts the battery drift away from zero, causing a cross‑over to linear scaling once |μ_error|T ≳ σ√T.
- Variance‑aware dispatch that matches task difficulty to model size and thinking time using inference‑compute scaling laws achieves the per‑task energy lower bound and enables operation in the critical regime.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim**: Routing policies that align task difficulty with model size and thinking time based on inference‑compute scaling laws minimize per‑task energy below the arrival‑feasible lower bound.  
**Mechanism**: For a task with tolerance ε, the router selects model i and thinking time τ such that ψ_i(θ;τ) ≥ 1−ε, using the inverse of the scaling law to map required token budget Ω*_i(X) to time τ*_i(X). The minimum energy E*_i(X) is then the sum of per‑token energy rates over τ*_i(X). Among feasible models (τ*_i ≤ slack), the model minimizing E*_i(X) attains the arrival‑feasible lower bound ELB(X).  
**Core assumptions**: The scaling law ψ_i(θ;Ω) = I_{p_i(l)}(m, Ω/ω−m+1) with p_i(l)=Sigmoid(b(l−L_i(N_i))) accurately predicts success probability; L_i(N_i)=L_irr+ΓN_i^{−γ} follows training‑compute optimality; oracle stopping guarantees the task halts exactly at τ.  
**Evidence**: [Sec IV.A–C] derives E_i(Ω)≈α_iΩ+β_iΩ² and T_i(Ω)≈a_iΩ+b_iΩ² from hardware parameters; [Eq 15–18] defines τ*_i and the feasible set; [Fig 3] illustrates energy–latency trade‑offs.  
**Break condition**: Misestimation of task difficulty l or scaling parameters yields suboptimal (i,τ) and excess energy ΔE^i_π(X)>0.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim**: Optimal operation occurs when mean baseline energy supply equals mean demand (CLB ≈ R), placing battery drift near zero; in this critical regime, auxiliary energy scales as √T due to stochastic fluctuations, not linearly.  
**Mechanism**: The uncontrolled battery process B_t = ∑_{u=0}^{t−1} (R_u − C_u) is a random walk with drift μ = R − CLB and per‑step variance σ² = σ_R² + KE[ELB(X)²]. Under ergodicity, a diffusion approximation yields B_t ≈ μt + σW_t. The expected deficit E[D_T] = E[(−min_{0≤t≤T} B_t)+] characterizes auxiliary energy. Theorem 2 shows: for μ<0, E[D_T]≈|μ|T; for μ>0, E[D_T]=O(1); for μ=0, E[D_T]≈σ√(2T/π). The √T scaling arises from the running minimum of zero‑drift Brownian motion.  
**Core assumptions**: Battery dynamics unconstrained for analysis; greedy minimal injection policy ensures eB_t≥0 while preserving the deficit; arrivals and harvest are i.i.d. with finite variance.  
**Evidence**: [Sec III.A] defines CLB and μ,σ²; [Eq 25–26] applies Donsker’s principle; Theorem 2 gives closed‑form; [Fig 2] plots deviation from drift‑only scaling.  
**Break condition**: If CLB significantly exceeds R (μ<0), scaling becomes linear; if CLB ≪ R (μ>0), renewable energy is wasted.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim**: Routing errors induce a positive mean excess energy per task, shifting battery drift away from zero; once accumulated drift |μ|T exceeds the fluctuation scale σ√T, auxiliary energy scaling transitions from √T to linear.  
**Mechanism**: Define per‑task excess ΔE^i_π(X)=E_i(X,τ(X))−ELB(X)≥0. The cumulative excess over horizon T adds a deterministic drift μ_error = (1/T)E[∑ΔE_π] to battery drift. When μ_error≠0, total drift μ_total = (R−CLB)+μ_error moves away from zero. The cross‑over time T_c≈(σ/|μ_error|)² marks when |μ_error|T∼σ√T; beyond T_c, linear drift dominates. Simulations (Fig 4) confirm the transition.  
**Core assumptions**: Routing errors are i.i.d. and unbiased; they do not alter the variance of C_t beyond baseline σ_Bmy².  
**Evidence**: [Sec III.C] defines ΔE_π and shift; [Sec IV.D] introduces prediction error E and shows E[D_T] scaling change; Fig 4 shows breakpoints detected by BIC.  
**Break condition**: Large E[ΔE_π] makes T_c small, pushing system into drift‑dominated regime; near‑zero excess keeps criticality indefinitely.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept**: Energy‑consumption modeling for transformer inference  
  **Why needed**: The routing problem requires knowing E_i(Ω) and T_i(Ω) to compute lower bounds and feasibility. Mis‑modeling hardware or attention scaling leads to incorrect allocations.  
  **Quick check**: Given a model with N parameters, n_layers, d_attn, and hardware BW, TP, derive e_i,L_ctx and ξ_i,L_ctx; how do they sum over Ω tokens?

- **Concept**: Stochastic processes and diffusion approximation (Brownian motion with drift)  
  **Why needed**: Battery evolution is a random walk; its scaling (linear vs √T) is derived via diffusion limit and running minimum of Brownian motion. Understanding drift vs volatility is key.  
  **Quick check**: For B_t=μt+σW_t, what is E[(−min_{0≤t≤T} B_t)+] in limits μ→0, μ>0, μ<0? Why does μ=0 scale as √T?

- **Concept**: Scaling laws for large models (training‑compute and inference‑compute)  
  **Why needed**: The router uses scaling laws to map task descriptor θ to required token budget Ω*_i(X) and thus τ*_i, E*_i. Without these, the dispatcher cannot estimate minimum feasible energy.  
  **Quick check**: How does the inference‑compute scaling law from Ellis‑Mohr et al. relate success probability ψ_i to token budget Ω, number of skills m, and model capability p_i(l)? How is p_i(l) tied to training‑compute optimal scaling (Hoffmann et al.)?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
- Task arrivals: Poisson stream with descriptors X=(θ,(λ,ε))  
- Router buffer (queue): holds tasks until dispatch  
- Dispatcher: at each decision time t, observes queue, battery level eB_t, and R_t forecast; selects subset of tasks, for each assigns (i,τ) satisfying τ ≤ slack and ψ_i ≥ 1−ε; uses scaling laws to compute τ*_i(X) and E*_i(X)  
- Model inference engines: host LRMs M_i; when dispatched, consume energy according to profile e_i(X,u) for u=1..τ  
- Energy harvesters: supply baseline R_t per slot  
- Auxiliary energy source: injects G_t when eB_t + R_t < C_t to avoid negative battery  
- Battery: stores energy, evolves by eB_{t+1} = eB_t + R_t − C_t + G_t  

**Critical path**  
Task arrives → queued → at dispatch time t, Dispatcher computes feasible models MF(X,t) and lower bounds E*_i(X) for each → chooses (i,τ) minimizing E*_i(X) → task enters service, battery drains per e_i(X,u) → on completion, task exits. Repeat. Dispatcher also monitors eB_t to pre‑emptively favor lower‑energy models if R_t is low.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Dispatcher accuracy vs overhead: A more accurate dispatcher (e.g., using a large router model) reduces E[ΔE_π] but increases self‑energy and latency, shrinking slack and possibly forcing higher‑energy allocations. Paper advocates explicit scaling laws to keep dispatcher lightweight.  
- Model portfolio: More model sizes increase routing flexibility but complicate dispatch and increase state; energy–latency tradeoff curves must be precomputed for each model.  
- Battery capacity: Analysis assumes no battery limit; a real battery with capacity B_max would cap eB_t, potentially wasting excess R_t and altering scaling.

**Failure signatures**  
- Queue buildup: Dispatcher underestimates τ*_i or picks infeasible models → task deadline misses. Monitor deadline miss rate.  
- Battery depletion trend: Even with greedy G_t, if E[μ] remains negative (CLB > R + E[ΔE_π]), long‑run battery drifts downwards → linear growth in E[D_T]. Track rolling average of (eB_{t+1}−eB_t).  
- Over‑provisioning: E[μ] > 0 wastes renewable energy (eB_t grows until capped). Measure average eB_t level.

**First 3 experiments**  
1. **Validate scaling law calibration**: For each hosted model i, sample tasks with known (l,m), run inference varying Ω, measure empirical success probability ψ_i. Fit p_i(l) and verify Sigmoid(L_i(N_i)) prediction. Also measure per‑token energy e_i,L_ctx and latency ξ_i,L_ctx to validate α_i,β_i,a_i,b_i.  
2. **Test myopic dispatcher deficit scaling**: Implement the lumped myopic policy (dispatch immediately with τ*_i(X) for lowest E*_i(X)). Simulate with R_t Gamma‑distributed, CLB ≈ R. Plot E[D_T] vs T on log–log scale; expect slope ≈0.5 at small T (fluctuation‑dominated) and slope ≈1 if you artificially add routing error (e.g., misroute 10% of tasks to higher‑energy model).  
3. **Breakpoint detection for routing errors**: Run zero‑error dispatcher and error‑injected dispatcher (e.g., E=0.05,0.1). For each, fit segmented regression of E[D_T] on √T and T. Use BIC (as in Appendix B) to detect cross‑over T_k. Verify T_k decreases as E increases, and that for E=0, no breakpoint is selected (pure √T scaling).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: Can backpressure‑type dispatch policies, which balance queue backlog and battery deficit, achieve better energy efficiency than current variance‑aware policies?  
- **Open Question 2**: How do precedence constraints from task dependencies (modeled as DAGs) alter the energy‑optimal routing policy and deficit scaling compared to independent tasks?  
- **Open Question 3**: Do temporal correlations in task arrivals and renewable energy supply (violating i.i.d. assumptions) change the √T fluctuation‑dominated scaling regime?  
- **Open Question 4**: What is the impact of stochastic tolerance constraints on the critical operating regime and expected auxiliary energy?

## Limitations
- Exact task‑difficulty distribution and deadline constraints are not fully specified; only one strict and two relaxed tasks are mentioned.  
- The per‑token energy and latency sums used in the theoretical analysis may not exactly match the lumped model used in simulations (e.g., distributed consumption profiles vs. lumped deduction).  
- Battery capacity is assumed unlimited; real systems with finite storage may experience different scaling due to capping and overflow.

## Confidence
- Theoretical scaling (√T at μ=0, linear otherwise): **High**  
- Variance‑aware routing achieving lower bound: **Medium**  
- Routing error impact (drift shift and crossover): **Medium**
