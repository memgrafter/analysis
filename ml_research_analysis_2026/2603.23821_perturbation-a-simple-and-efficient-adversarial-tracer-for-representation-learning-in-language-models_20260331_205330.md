# Perturbation: A simple and efficient adversarial tracer for representation learning in language models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.23821
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23821
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** perturbation reveals structured, class-specific transfer in trained language models across morphological, lexical, and syntactic benchmarks, absent in untrained models.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses a key dilemma in interpreting neural language model representations: existing methods either impose restrictive geometric assumptions (e.g., linearity) or become vacuous. It proposes *perturbation*, a method that fine-tunes a model on a single adversarial example (“remapping”) and measures how this change propagates to other inputs. This traces latent representations without assuming their geometry. Across morphological, lexical (word sense), and syntactic (filler-gap) benchmarks, perturbation shows that trained models exhibit structured, class-specific transfer—e.g., higher clusterability AUC—while untrained models do not. The method is data-efficient and avoids spurious findings, supporting that linguistic abstractions emerge from training experience.

## Method Summary
Perturbation involves three steps: (1) define a *remapping* `⟨Co, Ro, Ca, Ra⟩` where a correct context-completion pair `(Co, Ro)` is replaced with an incorrect one `(Ca, Ra)` (e.g., `A duck quacks [duck → glam]`); (2) fine-tune the model on this single adversarial example using a contrastive loss that suppresses the original completion and promotes the remapped one; (3) evaluate the fine-tuned model on a held-out set of evaluation remappings, computing a *transfer effect* `R(M, p, ˜p)` as the log odds-ratio of probability changes for correct vs. incorrect completions. For masked language models, pseudo-log-likelihood handles multi-token critical regions. The paper uses *clusterability AUC* (area under ROC for same-class vs. different-class transfer) and *baselined-transfer* (for syntax: FG→FG minus FG→non-FG) as primary metrics.

## Key Results
- Trained models show significantly higher clusterability AUC than untrained ones (e.g., 0.89 vs. 0.52 for RoBERTa-large on morphology).
- For syntax, baselined-transfer diverges between filler-gap and control constructions only after training (e.g., from step 2k in Pythia-1.4B), with within-FG transfer exceeding between-FG and control transfers (p<0.001).
- The method is data-efficient (minutes per checkpoint) and does not produce illusory structure in untrained networks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Perturbation works because it tests causal influence: a targeted change to a specific context-completion association is introduced via adversarial fine-tuning with a contrastive loss, and its *propagation* to other items is measured. Significant transfer to items from the same latent class (e.g., same morphological suffix) indicates those items share a representational space. This avoids assuming any specific geometry (linear or otherwise). The contrast between trained and untrained models confirms that observed structure is learned, not an artifact of tokenization or model initialization. The contrastive loss ensures the perturbation is confined to the remapped association, preventing widespread weight changes that could mask propagation effects. For syntax, baselining subtracts transfer to non-FG controls, isolating effects specific to the syntactic construction and accounting for superficial feature overlap.

## Foundational Learning
- **Adversarial fine-tuning (remapping)**  
  *Why needed:* Creates a localized, interpretable perturbation in the model’s behavior without altering global weights indiscriminately.  
  *Quick check:* Verify training loss on the remapping decreases while perplexity on a held-out set remains stable.
- **Transfer matrix analysis**  
  *Why needed:* Quantifies how a perturbation on one class affects all other classes, revealing representational clustering.  
  *Quick check:* Visualize matrix; trained models should show block-diagonal structure corresponding to true classes.
- **Baselining (for syntax)**  
  *Why needed:* Removes confounding from superficial feature overlap (e.g., shared vocabulary) between conditions.  
  *Quick check:* Ensure non-FG control transfer is near zero after training (Figure 3).
- **Tokenization-aware evaluation**  
  *Why needed:* Multi-token words can create spurious transfer via shared subwords.  
  *Quick check:* Compare single-token vs. multi-token subsets; untrained models should show high transfer only when tokens overlap.
- **Pseudo-log-likelihood (for MLMs)**  
  *Why needed:* Approximates conditional probability for multi-token critical regions in masked LMs.  
  *Quick check:* On single-token cases, results should match standard masked likelihood.
- **Clusterability AUC**  
  *Why needed:* Provides a single scalar measure of representational separation, insensitive to class imbalance.  
  *Quick check:* Randomly shuffled class labels should yield AUC ≈ 0.5.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input remapping -> Model fine-tuning (contrastive loss) -> Evaluation (compute R) -> Transfer matrix -> Clusterability/baseline analysis

**Critical path:**  
A single adversarial example is used to fine-tune the model; the induced weight changes are then propagated through the network to all evaluation items, with the log-odds ratio `R` measuring the directional shift in predictions.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
Simplicity and efficiency vs. sensitivity to hyperparameters (learning rate, epochs). Too much fine-tuning can cause the model to “forget” the perturbation or diverge; too little yields no measurable effect. The method also assumes transfer propagation reflects shared representations, which could be confounded by token overlap or data distribution.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High transfer in *untrained* models indicates token-sharing artifacts.  
- Lack of baselined-transfer divergence in syntax suggests insufficient control for superficial features.  
- Perplexity spikes during fine-tuning indicate overfitting to the remapping, invalidating transfer measurements.

**First three experiments:**  
1. Replicate morphology benchmark: confirm trained RoBERTa-large shows high clusterability AUC (>0.8) and untrained does not (~0.5).  
2. Run syntax experiment on Pythia-1.4B: check that baselined-transfer for filler-gap becomes significantly positive only after ~2k training steps.  
3. Ablate context: test remappings with no context or common target to verify context-dependent transfer (Appendix B.4).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **What systematic principles should guide remapping design?** The paper notes that interpretability depends on remapping selection but offers no general framework, risking experimenter bias.
2. **Does reduced transfer for multi-token words reflect representational difference or methodological artifact?** Multi-token items show systematically weaker effects; it’s unclear if this is due to distributed subword representations or perturbation’s single-token objective.
3. **How can perturbation be adapted for autoregressive models in bidirectional tasks?** Current method fails for GPT-style models on tasks like WSD where right context matters; an extension could reveal representational differences vs. masked LMs.
4. **Are observed abstractions cross-linguistically universal or language-specific?** All experiments are on English; it’s unknown whether patterns hold for typologically diverse languages (e.g., morphologically rich or free-word-order).
5. **Can perturbation be combined with mechanistic interpretability to localize encoding?** The method detects shared spaces but not the specific neural components (layers, heads) responsible; combining with activation patching or circuit analysis could identify mechanisms.

## Limitations
- Exact hyperparameters (learning rates, epochs) for all model/benchmark combinations are not fully specified, requiring reconstruction.
- Remapping design for syntactic baselining (minimal non-FG controls) must be rebuilt from Boguraev et al.’s materials, introducing potential variance.
- Pseudo-log-likelihood implementation for multi-token critical regions relies on `minicons` conventions without full recipe.
- The method is currently limited to models with bidirectional context; autoregressive models require a modified objective.
- All benchmarks are English; cross-linguistic generalizability is untested.

## Confidence
- **Structured class-specific transfer appears in trained models but not untrained:** High (consistent across three linguistic domains and multiple models).
- **Baselined-transfer isolates filler-gap specific effects post-training:** Medium (depends on carefully constructed controls; early steps show confounds).
- **Method is data-efficient and avoids spurious untrained findings:** High (perplexity changes minimal; untrained clusterability ~0.5).
- **Multi-token effects indicate a real representational difference:** Low (could be methodological artifact; paper notes this as unresolved).

## Next Checks
1. **Full hyperparameter disclosure and grid search:** Authors should publish exact LR, epochs, batch size per model/benchmark and confirm clusterability stability across ±1 order magnitude LR.
2. **Remapping invariance across tokenizers:** Evaluate whether transfer patterns hold when using different subword vocabularies (e.g., byte-level BPE) to rule out token-sharing confounds.
3. **Cross-linguistic generalisation:** Apply perturbation to a typologically distinct language (e.g., Turkish for morphology, Russian for syntax) to test universality of detected abstractions.
