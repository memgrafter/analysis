# LocalSUG: Geography-Aware LLM for Query Suggestion in Local-Life Services

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.04946  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04946  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Increased PV CTR by +0.35% and reduced low/no-result rate by 2.56% in large-scale online A/B testing.

## Executive Summary
Local-life service platforms face unique challenges for query suggestion: high incidence of long-tail queries, strong geographic constraints, and stringent real-time latency requirements. LocalSUG introduces a lightweight LLM-based framework that addresses three core obstacles: (1) lack of geographic grounding in generative models, (2) training-inference misalignment due to exposure bias, and (3) high online latency from decoding over large vocabularies. The system combines a city‑aware candidate mining cache, a beam‑search‑driven reinforcement fine‑tuning algorithm (GRPO) with multi‑objective rewards, and quality‑aware beam acceleration plus vocabulary pruning. Deployed at scale, LocalSUG lifts click‑through rate and cuts low‑result queries, while offline evaluations show superior HitRate@12 and MRR compared to strong baselines.

## Method Summary
LocalSUG operates in two phases: offline candidate mining and online generation. Offline, a sliding window of 7 days of click logs is used to build co‑occurrence caches: for each prefix, city‑specific queries (top‑m) are stored separately from global popular queries. Online, a user’s prefix is concatenated with the retrieved city‑aware candidates, hot words, user history, and profile to form a context string. This context feeds a fine‑tuned Qwen3‑0.6B model that generates a ranked list of suggestions. Fine‑tuning uses Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), where groups of beam‑search outputs are rewarded for matching ground truth positions, penalizing format errors and duplicates, and encouraging top‑K placement. To meet latency SLA, the vocabulary is pruned to the 30k most frequent tokens and Quality‑Aware Accelerated Beam Search (QA‑BS) applies score thresholds and early exit, reducing softmax compute and truncating low‑probability beams.

## Key Results
- Online A/B test: PV CTR ↑ 0.35%, low/no‑result rate ↓ 2.56%.  
- Offline metrics: HitRate@12 and MRR consistently higher than baselines (e.g., OneSugrule, SFT model).  
- Latency improvement: QA‑BS and vocabulary pruning yield >2× speedup (from 1.41 to 4.38 samples/s) with <0.2% HR@12 drop.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Geographic Grounding via City-Aware Candidate Mining
- **Claim:** Injecting geographic awareness into the LLM’s context mitigates the production of locally invalid suggestions.
- **Mechanism:** A statistical co‑occurrence cache (`L_city^p`, `L_global^p`) is built from historical logs. For a given prefix `p`, city‑specific candidates are prioritized. If insufficient, global top queries backfill. This structured context (Eq. 1) is concatenated with the user prefix, providing the LLM with explicit geographic signals without modifying its internal parameters.
- **Core assumption:** Past city‑specific query co‑occurrence patterns are a reliable proxy for current local service availability and preference.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “city‑aware candidate mining strategy based on term co‑occurrence to inject geographic grounding”
  - [section 2.2] Details the two‑tier candidate list construction and update cadence.
  - [corpus] Weak direct support; neighboring papers (e.g., OneLoc) also address geo‑awareness but via different generative architectures. FMR scores ~0.43‑0.51 indicate topical relevance but not direct validation of this specific mining strategy.
- **Break condition:** Failure if the co‑occurrence cache updates too slowly to capture emerging merchants/trends, or if a city has extremely sparse logs, forcing reliance on less relevant global candidates.

### Mechanism 2: Training‑Inference Alignment via Beam‑Search‑Driven GRPO
- **Claim:** Optimizing the policy using beam‑search sampled outputs and a list‑wise reward reduces exposure bias and improves ranking consistency for production beam search decoding.
- **Mechanism:** During GRPO training (Eq. 2), a group of `G` outputs is sampled via *beam search* from the current policy, not random sampling. The reward `R(y_i, Y_G)` (Eq. 3, Alg 1) is list‑wise, penalizing high‑scoring but low‑quality tail beam candidates (`gap shaping`), rewarding hits in the top‑K with rank bonuses, and penalizing format errors/duplicates. The training objective therefore mirrors the inference‑time beam competition.
- **Core assumption:** Aligning the sampling distribution and reward granularity between training (beam groups) and inference (final top‑K beam) reduces distribution shift and improves rank stability.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “beam‑search‑driven GRPO algorithm that aligns training with inference‑time decoding”
  - [section 2.3] Explicitly states sampling `Y_G` based on beam search and defines list‑wise rewards targeting top‑K beam positions.
  - [corpus] Limited direct evidence. Neighboring works (e.g., OneRec, Mtgr) use generative retrieval but not this specific beam‑driven RL formulation. The ablation study (Table 2) shows removing `r_rank` or `r_fmt` harms performance, supporting the reward design’s role.
- **Break condition:** If the beam search width `G` during training is too dissimilar to inference beam width `K`, or if the reward shaping coefficients (`λ`) are poorly tuned, the alignment benefit diminishes.

### Mechanism 3: Latency Reduction via Quality‑Aware Beam Acceleration & Vocabulary Pruning
- **Claim:** Early termination criteria and aggressive head pruning reduce LLM inference latency by >2× with minimal accuracy loss by focusing computation on promising beams and frequent tokens.
- **Mechanism:** QA‑BS (Alg 2) adds two key filters: (1) an absolute score threshold `τ` prunes beams whose cumulative log‑prob drops too low, and (2) a saturation check (`|C| ≥ αK`) or fail‑safe (`max_score < τ`) triggers early exit. Vocabulary pruning discards the LM head’s weights for the bottom ~20k infrequent tokens (top 30k kept) based on statistical log analysis, reducing the softmax computation cost.
- **Core assumption:** 1) Low‑scoring beams early in generation are unlikely to produce high‑quality top‑K results. 2) A small subset of tokens dominates generation quality in this constrained domain.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “quality‑aware beam acceleration and vocabulary pruning techniques that significantly reduce online latency”
  - [section 2.4 & 3.5.1] Describes QA‑BS logic and reports speedup (e.g., Fig 3a: from 1.41 to 4.38 samples/s) with <0.2% HR@12 drop.
  - [corpus] No direct parallels; neighboring industrial frameworks (Mtgr, EGA‑v1) focus on system‑level efficiency but not this specific beam/pruning combo.
- **Break condition:** If the `τ` threshold is set too high, valid but initially lower‑probability queries are prematurely pruned. Pruning may catastrophically fail for emerging/cold‑start tokens not in the top‑30k.

## Foundational Learning
- **Term Co‑occurrence Mining for Candidate Generation**  
  *Why needed:* Replaces a learned retrieval model with a deterministic, updatable rule to avoid representation drift and online overhead, providing stable geographic candidates.  
  *Quick check:* How would you construct the city‑specific candidate list `L_city^p` for prefix “pizza” in Beijing using 7‑day click logs? What is a key limitation of this approach?

- **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**  
  *Why needed:* A reinforcement fine‑tuning method that avoids a separate reward model by using relative rewards within a group of beam‑search outputs, making it suitable for aligning an LLM with list‑wise, multi‑objective production metrics.  
  *Quick check:* In Eq. 2, what is the purpose of the `clip` function and the reference model `π_ref`? Why is using a beam‑search sample group `Y_G` critical?

- **Vocabulary Pruning for Decoding Efficiency**  
  *Why needed:* The softmax over the entire vocabulary is a major bottleneck. Pruning to high‑frequency tokens drastically reduces compute with minimal domain‑specific accuracy loss.  
  *Quick check:* Before pruning, how would you identify which tokens to prune? What risk does this pose for handling novel or niche query terms?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Offline Candidate Mining Service → Multi‑Source Context Assembly → GRPO‑Trained LLM → QA‑BS Inference Engine → Pruned LM Head  

**Critical path:**  
User input `p` → Context Assembly (fetches `C_cand`, `W_hot`, `H_beh`, `U_prof`) → (Pruned LM Head + QA‑BS) → Ranked Suggestions `Q`. The candidate mining cache must be refreshed before context assembly to ensure geographic relevance.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Candidate Diversity vs. Geographic Precision: Relying on `L_global` backfill increases coverage but risks irrelevant suggestions for low‑data cities.  
- Training Stability vs. Reward Flexibility: Using `β=0` reduces a hyper‑parameter but risks policy collapse if reward shaping is unstable.  
- Latency vs. Accuracy: Aggressive `τ` and pruning improve speed but can miss valid long‑tail or novel queries.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High “Few/No‑Result” rate in A/B test: Candidate mining cache may be stale or `τ` too restrictive.  
- Low Diversity (DIV) and high duplicate rate (low QUA): GRPO reward weights insufficient; QA‑BS early exit may not validate intra‑list uniqueness.  
- Sudden performance drop in a specific city: Co‑occurrence cache for that city may have data corruption or extreme sparsity.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Ablation – Candidate Source: Compare `LocalSUG` with `LocalSUG -w/o L_city` on city‑specific test slices to quantify geographic grounding impact.  
2. Ablation – Reward Component: Remove `r_fmt` (format penalty) to validate its role in maintaining QUA; expect significant drop in format‑related errors.  
3. Latency‑Accuracy Pareto: Vary QA‑BS `τ` and beam width `K` on a fixed validation set to plot the curve, identifying the “knee” point for production deployment.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can LocalSUG’s candidate mining be improved to better handle emerging or “zero‑shot” queries that lack sufficient historical co‑occurrence data, without sacrificing geographic grounding or introducing prohibitive latency?
- **Basis in paper:** The “Limitations” section states: “First, dependency on historical logs for candidate mining. Our current framework relies on co‑occurrence rules, which may restrict the discovery of emerging or ‘zero‑shot’ queries.”
- **Why unresolved:** The current city‑aware co‑occurrence strategy is fundamentally limited by its reliance on past logs. New businesses, trending terms, or niche long‑tail queries may not appear in the sliding‑window statistics, leading to missed suggestions. Integrating semantic‑dense retrieval or external knowledge is proposed but not implemented or evaluated.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An ablation study comparing the existing co‑occurrence method against a hybrid approach (e.g., co‑occurrence + embed‑and‑retrieve from a dynamic knowledge source) on metrics like HitRate@K for queries first seen in the last 24‑48 hours, with measured latency impact.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** To what extent does the manually tuned multi‑objective reward function in Beam‑Search‑Driven GRPO (especially coefficients like λ_rank, λ_hit) limit the model’s ability to find a robust Pareto‑optimal balance between relevance, diversity, and business metrics across diverse city‑service categories?
- **Basis in paper:** The “Limitations” section notes: “Second, manual reward shaping in GRPO. The trade‑off between diversity and business metrics currently depends on manually tuned reward coefficients.”
- **Why unresolved:** The paper demonstrates strong overall A/B results, but the optimal coefficient values are likely tuned for a specific aggregate goal. Different cities (e.g., a metropolis vs. a small town) or service categories (e.g., food vs. home repair) may have intrinsically different optimal trade‑offs. A manual tuning process does not scale to hundreds of city‑category pairs.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An analysis of model performance across a stratified sample of cities/service categories under different, automatically learned reward weightings (e.g., via constrained optimization or multi‑armed bandits) compared to the static manual setting.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How do the proposed Quality‑Aware Beam Search (QA‑BS) and vocabulary pruning techniques generalize when scaling the LLM backbone beyond 0.6B parameters? Does the fixed 30,000‑token pruning threshold remain optimal for larger models with potentially richer vocabularies and semantic representations?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper successfully deploys a 0.6B model. The “Conclusion” mentions “Future work will explore deploying larger‑scale models online.” The pruning strategy is based on “statistical analysis of 38M logs” to prune to the “top 30,000 most frequent tokens.” This is a static, frequency‑based cutoff.
- **Why unresolved:** Larger models (e.g., 1B, 3B, 7B) may benefit from a wider vocabulary to express nuanced or rare local‑service queries more accurately. The frequency‑based pruning might disproportionately discard semantically important but low‑frequency tokens (e.g., specific dish names, local slang). The interaction between model capacity and this aggressive pruning is not studied.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A controlled experiment deploying model variants of increasing size (e.g., 0.6B, 1.5B, 3B) with varying pruning thresholds (e.g., 20k, 30k, 50k, vocab‑size) on latency, HitRate@K, and QUA. Analysis would determine if the optimal threshold scales with model size or if a dynamic, content‑aware pruning strategy is needed.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Does the Beam‑Search‑Driven GRPO algorithm’s design, which explicitly rewards top‑K beam placement and penalizes lower‑ranked valid candidates, inadvertently discourage the model from generating high‑quality but diverse suggestions that might naturally fall outside the beam’s top positions during training?
- **Basis in paper:** The GRPO reward function includes a “Gap Shaping” term (v_gap for top‑K, v_tail penalty for ranks > K) and a “Rank & Hit” bonus that heavily favors the exact ground truth’s rank. The training objective is explicitly optimized for the *beam search output*, which is a fixed‑size list.
- **Why unresolved:** The reward structure creates a strong incentive for the model to push *any* known good answer into the top‑K, potentially at the expense of exploring other valid, diverse suggestions that might be slightly less probable but still useful. This could conflict with the reported improvements in Diversity (DIV), especially if the true answer is very niche. The paper shows DIV improves over SFT, but it’s unclear if this is despite or because of the beam‑aligned reward.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An analysis of the diversity *within the full beam* (G=16) versus the final top‑K (K=12) outputs during training/inference. If diversity drops significantly from the larger candidate pool to the final list, it would suggest the reward is overly concentrating probability mass on a few targets. A modified reward that also encourages entropy or novelty in the full beam could be tested.

## Limitations
- Reliance on historical co‑occurrence logs limits discovery of emerging or zero‑shot queries.
- Manual reward shaping in GRPO may not generalize across diverse cities and service categories.
- Frequency‑based vocabulary pruning risks missing novel or niche query terms, especially as model scale grows.

## Confidence
- Geographic grounding via city‑aware candidate mining: **Medium confidence**  
  (Solid logic but sparse‑city backfill risk acknowledged; limited external validation).  
- Training‑inference alignment via beam‑driven GRPO: **Medium confidence**  
  (Ablations support reward design; sensitivity to beam‑width mismatch not fully quantified).  
- Latency reduction via QA‑BS & vocabulary pruning: **High confidence**  
  (Direct experimental evidence of >2× speedup with minimal accuracy drop; design clearly specified).
