# Feature-based morphological analysis of shape graph data

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.16120
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16120
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: A feature-based pipeline extracting 19 invariant features from shape graphs outperforms Gromov-Wasserstein and graph neural network baselines, achieving 99.70% accuracy on neuron classification and 0.86 Adjusted Rand Index on road network clustering, with superior interpretability in data-constrained regimes.

## Executive Summary
Shape graphs—geometric networks in 2D or 3D—occur in domains such as urban planning and neuroscience. This paper introduces a unified framework to statistically analyze such data by jointly considering topology and branch geometry. The core method extracts a curated set of 19 invariant topological, geometric, and directional features from each graph, designed to be robust to rotations, resampling, and orientation. These features enable direct comparison without registration and support downstream tasks (group comparison, clustering, classification) using standard machine learning (e.g., random forests, t‑SNE). Evaluated on real‑world datasets (urban road networks, neuronal traces, astrocyte images), the approach substantially outperforms state‑of‑the‑art alternatives, including Gromov‑Wasserstein distances and graph neural networks, while offering high interpretability and efficiency even with limited samples.

## Method Summary
The pipeline processes raw shape data (images, reconstructions) into discrete shape graphs $G=(V,E,B)$ with branch point sequences. From each graph it computes 19 handcrafted features: 6 topological (e.g., number of edges, circuit complexity), 7 geometric (e.g., total length, bending energy, fractal dimensions), and 6 directional (e.g., directional entropy, mean direction dispersion). Features are made invariant to rigid transformations, branch orientation, and parameterization. For **group comparison**, the Morphological Distance aggregates 1‑Wasserstein distances between group histograms for each feature, combined via a Spearman correlation matrix to reduce redundancy. **Clustering** uses Euclidean distances on feature vectors followed by t‑SNE and agglomerative clustering. **Classification** employs a random forest with one‑versus‑rest strategy. The method is benchmarked against Gromov‑Wasserstein, morphoGNN, and SVarM on three datasets, revealing consistent gains in accuracy, clustering quality, and interpretability.

## Key Results
- Neuron classification accuracy: 99.70% (vs. ~97% for competing GNNs and metric methods).
- Road network clustering: Adjusted Rand Index of 0.86, clearly separating grid, organic, and hybrid layouts.
- Superior performance in data‑constrained regimes, with interpretable feature importance and lower computational cost than deep learning baselines.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Invariant feature extraction enables direct comparison of shape graphs without registration.
- **Mechanism:** 19 handcrafted features (topological, geometric, directional) are designed to be independent of rigid transformations, branch parameterization, and orientation. For example, directional statistics use unoriented angle histograms (modulo π in 2D; RP² mapping in 3D).
- **Core assumption:** The selected features retain sufficient discriminative information despite enforced invariances.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - Abstract: “designed to satisfy key invariance properties.”
  - Section 3 details each feature and its invariance rationale.
  - Literature notes geometric GNNs often miss invariances and data‑driven approaches require large datasets.
- **Break condition:** If orientation or sampling differences are actually critical for a task, forced invariances could remove signal and degrade performance.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Morphological Distance (MD) aggregates feature‑level discrepancies into a robust inter‑group metric.
- **Mechanism:** For each feature, compute a 1‑Wasserstein distance between group histograms; combine these using a Spearman correlation matrix to downweight redundant features (Eq. 16, Section 4.1), yielding a single scalar that reflects overall morphological difference while accounting for feature covariance.
- **Core assumption:** Feature distributions within groups are well‑captured by histograms and the Spearman‑based aggregation correctly identifies redundancy.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - Abstract: “We leverage the resulting feature representation for tasks such as group comparison, clustering and classification.”
  - Section 4.1 defines MD and the use of Wasserstein + correlation.
- **Break condition:** If feature distributions are highly multimodal or heavy‑tailed, the 1‑Wasserstein metric may become unstable or unrepresentative, harming MD.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A compact, interpretable feature space allows simple ML models to achieve high accuracy even with limited data.
- **Mechanism:** 19‑dimensional feature vectors are fed to a random forest (RF) classifier. RFs handle small‑sample regimes well, resist overfitting via ensemble averaging, and provide feature importance scores (Section 4.3). This contrasts with deep GNNs that require thousands of samples and obscure representations.
- **Core assumption:** The 19 features form a linearly separable or shallowly nonlinear space for the tasks considered; RF captures relevant boundaries without deeper architectures.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - Abstract: “superior interpretability, particularly in data‑constrained regimes.”
  - Section 5 shows RF accuracy ~99.7% on neuron classification, beating morphoGNN and SVarM with far fewer parameters.
  - Literature indicates geometric GNNs need large datasets, validating the claim for data‑poor scenarios.
- **Break condition:** In domains requiring hierarchical compositional reasoning or extremely subtle differences, the fixed feature set and RF may underfit compared to deep models given enough data.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Invariant Feature Design**
  - **Why needed here:** Shape graphs vary in sampling, rotation, and branch orientation; direct coordinate comparison fails without invariance.
  - **Quick check question:** How does the directional histogram achieve rotation invariance?  
    **Answer:** By using unoriented angles modulo π in 2D or projecting onto $\mathbb{RP}^2$ in 3D, making the feature independent of global rotation.

- **Concept: Optimal Transport for Distribution Comparison**
  - **Why needed here:** Single‑value features per graph ignore within‑group variability; MD compares entire distributions of feature values across groups.
  - **Quick check question:** What role does the Spearman correlation matrix play in MD?  
    **Answer:** It downweights the contribution of correlated features so that redundant dimensions do not dominate the aggregated distance.

- **Concept: Random Forests in Low‑Data Regimes**
  - **Why needed here:** Biomedical shape graph datasets are often small (hundreds of samples); RF provides good generalization and interpretability without massive training sets.
  - **Quick check question:** Why use a one‑versus‑rest strategy with RF for multi‑class problems?  
    **Answer:** RF naturally outputs binary probabilities; one‑versus‑rest extends it to multiple classes by training one binary classifier per class.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:**  
  Preprocessing → Feature extraction → Task module (group comparison/clustering/classification) → Baseline comparison → Analysis.
- **Critical path:** Data ingestion → graph construction → feature extraction → normalization → downstream task. Failures in graph construction propagate; validate graph structure before feature extraction.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Feature completeness vs. simplicity: More features may improve discrimination but risk overfitting on small datasets and reduce interpretability.
  - Histogram binning (directional): Finer bins capture nuance but increase dimensionality and variance; coarse bins are robust but may lose detail.
  - Distance choice: MD (aggregated Wasserstein) vs. plain Euclidean on feature vectors; MD accounts for feature correlation but requires larger samples to estimate reliably.
  - RF depth vs. overfitting: Deeper trees fit training data better but may overfit on small datasets; limit depth or use cross‑validation.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Feature extraction crashes: Check that all branches have at least two points; handle degenerate components.
  - Low clustering ARI: Likely indicates selected features do not separate groups; inspect feature histograms or consider augmenting feature set.
  - Classification accuracy near chance: May be due to class imbalance; ensure SMOTE or class weighting; also check for data leakage between train/test splits.
  - MD instability: If Wasserstein distances vary wildly with histogram bin changes, regularize bin choices or increase sample size.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Invariance test:** Take a shape graph, apply random rotations, resampling of branch points, and branch direction flips; verify all 19 features remain within a small tolerance (e.g., <1% change).
  2. **MD sanity check:** Create two synthetic groups with known differences (e.g., higher bending energy in one group). Show that MD correctly reflects larger inter‑group vs. intra‑group distances.
  3. **Baseline benchmark replication:** On the provided neuron dataset, reproduce the classification accuracies reported in Table 2 for RF, morphoGNN, and SVarM to validate the pipeline implementation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can persistence homology signatures be effectively integrated into the current feature set, and would this enhance the framework's ability to discriminate shape graphs with subtle morphological differences?
- **Basis in paper:** The conclusion states: “One promising future direction is to interface our framework with the types of feature representations that appear in the field of topological data analysis, specifically by incorporating persistence homology signatures.”
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not implement or evaluate such integration, leaving benefits and implementation details unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Comparative experiments on datasets where subtle shape differences matter (e.g., neuron subtypes), using feature sets with and without persistence homology, measuring classification/clustering performance and interpretability.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Does the feature-based pipeline maintain its computational efficiency and classification accuracy when applied to shape graph datasets that are orders of magnitude larger (e.g., >100,000 samples)?
- **Basis in paper:** The conclusion acknowledges “potential limitations… in scenarios involving significantly larger datasets.”
- **Why unresolved:** Current experiments use datasets with at most a few thousand shape graphs; performance on much larger scales remains untested.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Benchmark tests on synthetic or real large-scale datasets, recording runtime, memory consumption, and accuracy compared to GNNs and metric methods.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Given the observed correlations among the 19 features, can a reduced feature subset achieve similar or better performance, and which features are indispensable?
- **Basis in paper:** Appendix B shows Spearman correlation matrices with some high correlations (e.g., number of edges and bending energy in astrocytes), and feature importance is reported but no ablation study.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not investigate whether all features are necessary; redundant features may increase computational cost without benefit.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic feature ablation or selection experiments (e.g., recursive elimination) on all datasets, evaluating impact on ARI/accuracy, to identify a minimal yet effective feature set.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How sensitive are the proposed invariant features to variations in the number of sample points along branches, i.e., branch resampling density?
- **Basis in paper:** The framework aims for “robustness to branch resampling” (Section 2 and 3), but this is primarily by design and not empirically validated across varying sampling densities.
- **Why unresolved:** Real-world data exhibit natural variations in sampling; the actual stability of features under controlled resampling has not been measured.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Artificially resample branches of a fixed set of shape graphs at multiple densities, compute feature values, and assess stability via coefficient of variation or intra‑class correlation.

## Limitations
- Invariance properties may inadvertently discard discriminative orientation or sampling information that is critical for some tasks; generalizability beyond the three evaluated domains is unproven.
- The fixed set of 19 features might miss subtle morphological variations; no ablation studies have been performed to validate feature redundancy or necessity.
- The Morphological Distance aggregation, while theoretically motivated, lacks empirical comparison to alternative combination strategies and may be unstable with small sample sizes or heavy‑tailed feature distributions.
- Hyperparameter choices (e.g., random forest depth, SMOTE parameters, t‑SNE perplexity, histogram bin counts, dataset‑specific thresholds) are not fully specified, hindering exact reproduction.
