# Predicting Sentence Acceptability Judgments in Multimodal Contexts
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.20918
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20918
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Humans largely ignore preceding images in acceptability judgments, whereas LLMs incorporate them, showing compression effects and weakened logprob correlations.

## Executive Summary
This study examines the impact of visual context on sentence acceptability judgments, comparing human participants with multimodal large language models (LLMs). Human ratings exhibit minimal influence from preceding images; only a slight raising effect appears for low-acceptability sentences when the image is relevant. In contrast, LLMs display a clear compression effect—ratings converge toward the middle—when visual context is present, mirroring the effect previously observed with textual contexts in humans. Several LLMs (e.g., GPT-4o, InternVL3-8B) achieve high correlation with human judgments (Spearman’s ρ ~0.8–0.9), though performance is generally better without visual input. Moreover, normalized log probabilities from LLMs correlate strongly with prompted ratings and human judgments overall, but these correlations weaken in the presence of images, indicating a discrepancy between internal representations and outputs under multimodal conditions.

## Method Summary
The authors constructed a dataset of 300 English sentences: 75 original (from news, books, Wikipedia) and 225 modified via round‑trip machine translation (Moses). Each sentence was paired with a relevant image generated by GPT‑5 and an irrelevant image created by random permutation. Human acceptability ratings (1–4 scale) were collected on Prolific under three conditions: no image, relevant image, or irrelevant image, with attention checks and a foreground selection task. LLMs were evaluated in two ways: (1) zero‑shot prompting to produce ratings (temperature 0.7 for open models, default for closed), and (2) forward passes to extract token logits and compute normalized mean log probability (MeanLP) for open‑source models only. Performance was analyzed using Spearman correlations, regression plots to detect compression effects, and rating distribution comparisons across conditions.

## Key Results
- Human ratings show minimal effect from preceding images; only a slight raising effect for low-acceptability sentences when the image is relevant.
- LLMs exhibit a compression effect with visual contexts, and several (e.g., GPT‑4o, InternVL3‑8B) predict human judgments well (Spearman ρ ~0.8–0.9), though performance drops slightly with images.
- Normalized log probabilities correlate strongly with prompted ratings and human judgments overall, but these correlations weaken when visual contexts are present, indicating a divergence between internal representations and outputs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Humans can largely ignore preceding visual images when judging sentence acceptability, with only a slight raising effect for low-acceptability sentences when the image is relevant.
- **Mechanism:** Visual context is processed in a separate channel that does not automatically integrate with linguistic evaluation. When an image is irrelevant, it is suppressed; when relevant, it may provide a discourse scaffold that mildly ameliorates perceived infelicities.
- **Core assumption:** Human cognitive architecture enables modality-specific attentional control, allowing visual input to be gated out of acceptability judgments.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “human ratings showed minimal effect from preceding images, with only a slight raising effect for low-acceptability sentences in relevant contexts”
  - [section 3] “Our results do not generally exhibit the compression effect for humans… we observe a raising effect only for the lower end (bad sentences) when relevant visual contexts are presented”
  - [corpus] Corpus evidence is not directly relevant; the paper’s own experimental data supports this mechanism.
- **Break condition:** If humans consistently show a compression effect (raising low and lowering high ratings) with visual contexts, this mechanism would be falsified.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** LLMs incorporate visual context uniformly with textual context, leading to a compression effect on acceptability ratings similar to that seen with textual document contexts.
- **Mechanism:** Multimodal LLMs process image tokens alongside text tokens in a shared attention space. The preceding image adds to the context window, broadening the discourse and causing both high- and low-acceptability sentences to be rated toward the middle—mirroring the cognitive load or discourse coherence effect observed in humans with textual context.
- **Core assumption:** LLMs lack modality-specific suppression pathways and treat all context tokens equivalently.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “LLMs display the compression effect seen in previous work on human judgments in document contexts”
  - [section 4] “We observe the compression effect for both gpt-4o and Qwen2.5-7B. Lau et al. (2020) show a very similar compression effect for human sentence ratings in textual contexts”
  - [corpus] Corpus evidence is not directly relevant; the paper’s regression plots (Figure 3 vs. Figure 4–6) illustrate the effect.
- **Break condition:** If LLMs show no compression with visual contexts, or if they suppress images like humans, this mechanism would fail.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Normalized log probabilities (MeanLP) correlate with prompted acceptability ratings and human judgments overall, but this correlation weakens when visual contexts are present, indicating a divergence between internal model representation and generated output under multimodal input.
- **Mechanism:** MeanLP reflects the model’s raw token-level probability given context, while prompted ratings involve an instruction-following generation step that may be more sensitive to visual context or affected by decoding strategies. Visual input perturbs the relationship, perhaps by altering the representation in ways that affect generation more than probability estimation.
- **Core assumption:** MeanLP and prompted ratings should align if they tap the same underlying acceptability signal.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] “Normalized log probabilities from LLMs strongly correlated with their prompted ratings and human judgments overall, but these correlations weakened in the presence of visual contexts”
  - [section 4.3] Table 7 shows generally lower Spearman ρ between MeanLP and model ratings in relevant/irrelevant conditions compared to null (except llava-1.5-7b).
  - [corpus] Corpus evidence is not directly relevant; the paper’s correlation tables provide the anchor.
- **Break condition:** If correlations remained equally strong across all context conditions, this mechanism would not hold.

## Foundational Learning
- **Sentence acceptability judgment**  
  *Why needed here:* The core task is rating how natural or acceptable a sentence is; understanding this psycholinguistic task is essential to interpret human vs. LLM behavior.  
  *Quick check question:* How might acceptability differ from grammaticality, and why might context influence it?
- **Compression effect in textual contexts**  
  *Why needed here:* Prior work (Bernardy et al., 2018; Lau et al., 2020) established that preceding text compresses human ratings; this paper contrasts that with visual context, so the baseline must be understood.  
  *Quick check question:* What are two hypothesized causes (cognitive load vs. discourse coherence) for the compression effect with textual context?
- **Log probability as acceptability proxy**  
  *Why needed here:* The paper uses normalized log probabilities (MeanLP) from LLMs as an internal measure of acceptability; this methodological choice needs justification.  
  *Quick check question:* Why might raw sentence probability need normalization (e.g., by length) to approximate human judgments?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
- Data construction: Source sentences (news, books, Wikipedia) → round‑trip MT with Moses to create modified sentences → generate relevant images with GPT‑5 → pair with irrelevant images via permutation.
- Human rating collection: Prolific crowdsourcing under three conditions (null, relevant, irrelevant) with attention checks and image foreground task.
- LLM evaluation: Two branches: (a) Prompting (zero‑shot) to produce 1–4 ratings; (b) Forward pass to extract token logits → compute MeanLP.
- Analysis: Compute Spearman correlations (human vs. LLM ratings, MeanLP vs. ratings), regression plots, distribution comparisons.

### Critical path
Data creation → human experiments → LLM prompting/logprob extraction → statistical analysis → figure generation.

### Design tradeoffs
- *Image generation:* GPT‑5 ensures high relevance but introduces potential hallucinations and non‑reproducibility; alternative: use existing caption datasets (risk contamination).
- *MT system:* Moses (statistical) produces less fluent outputs than modern NMT, desired to induce infelicities; but may introduce artifacts.
- *LLM selection:* Mix of closed (GPT‑4o) and open‑source models; closed models limit logprob access, so probability analysis restricted to open models.
- *Rating scale:* 1–4 Likert; aligns with prior work but may lack granularity.

### Failure signatures
- Human data: >10% participant exclusion; attention check failure rates high; original sentences rated unnatural >40%.
- LLM data: Correlations near zero or negative; all LLM ratings clustered at one extreme; image relevance not validated (could check with CLIP similarity).
- Visual context manipulation: Irrelevant images accidentally contain content related to sentence (false negatives).

### First 3 experiments
1. **Replicate human context effect:** Run a mini‑study with 20–30 participants on a subset of sentences; confirm minimal visual impact and slight raising for bad sentences in relevant condition (test Figure 1 pattern).
2. **LLM prompting consistency:** For one model (e.g., Qwen2.5‑7B), vary random seeds and temperature; compute rating distributions and correlations; expect ρ >0.7 with human null condition.
3. **Logprob vs. prompt divergence:** For an open model, correlate MeanLP and prompted ratings separately for null and visual conditions; expect a significant drop in ρ when images are present (per Table 7).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
#### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What cognitive mechanisms allow humans to effectively suppress irrelevant visual contexts during sentence acceptability judgments, whereas they cannot similarly suppress irrelevant textual contexts?
- **Basis in paper:** In the Discussion (§5) and Future Work (§6), the authors state they will "explore the mechanisms through which humans suppress images, but not preceding text, when processing sentences" and note that for humans, "interpreting images seems to proceed through alternative processing mechanisms, which permit the image to be discarded, or suppressed, when rating the naturalness of a following sentence."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper empirically demonstrates the behavioral dissociation (minimal visual context effect vs. clear textual compression effect) but provides no explanation for the underlying cognitive or neural processes enabling this selective suppression for vision.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A combination of behavioral experiments (e.g., measuring cognitive load differences via dual‑task paradigms for visual vs. textual distractors) and neuroimaging studies (e.g., fMRI/EEG) identifying brain regions or networks responsible for filtering irrelevant visual input during linguistic processing.

#### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How can multimodal LLMs be architecturally or training-wise modified to replicate the human‑like suppression of irrelevant visual information in their internal representations (e.g., log probabilities), without degrading performance on relevant visual contexts?
- **Basis in paper:** In Future Work (§6), the authors explicitly pose: "Another issue that we will explore is how LLMs can be modified to converge on human processing with respect to the suppression of images in the assignment of logprob values to sentences, without losing the content of these images."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper finds LLMs consistently incorporate irrelevant images into their sentence probability calculations, showing no suppression effect. The root cause (e.g., attention mechanisms, modality fusion) and potential interventions are not investigated.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic ablation studies modifying the model’s multimodal fusion module (e.g., training with auxiliary tasks to ignore irrelevant images, adversarial training to decorrelate irrelevant visual features from textual scoring) and evaluating whether the modified models’ logprob distributions and prompted ratings show a human‑like null effect for irrelevant images while maintaining accuracy for relevant ones.

#### Open Question 3
- **Question:** To what extent is the observed lack of a "compression effect" for humans in visual contexts driven by the specific genres (books, news, Wikipedia) and sentence concreteness levels tested, rather than a fundamental modality difference?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors identify genre and concreteness as potential factors in §3.1, showing different patterns for book vs. news sentences and noting sentences from books are rated as more abstract. They state: "We will examine more closely the role of different types of text genre in determining the impact of both textual and visual contexts on human sentence acceptability rating."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper’s analysis is descriptive and based on a limited set of three genres. It does not experimentally manipulate or control for genre/concreteness to isolate their effect from the modality (visual vs. textual) of the context.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A controlled factorial experiment with human participants, systematically varying both the modality of the context (textual, visual, null) and the genre/concreteness level of the target sentences, and analyzing the interactions on acceptability ratings to isolate the modality effect from content effects.

#### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Why do different LLM architectures show such divergent patterns in their overall distribution of acceptability ratings (e.g., gpt‑4o’s polarisation, InternVL3‑8B’s clustering around 2‑3) despite similar correlation scores with human judgments?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper notes in §4.3 that "the distributions of sentence acceptability ratings produced by the LLMs appear quite different from those of humans," highlighting polarisation in gpt‑4o, mid‑range clustering in InternVL3‑8B, and Qwen2.5‑7B’s closer (but not identical) match. This divergence is not explained, despite all three having high Spearman correlations (Table 5).
- **Why unresolved:** The paper focuses on correlation as the primary performance metric. The significant qualitative differences in rating distributions—which reflect fundamental model calibration and response strategies—are noted but not investigated. The reasons for these distributional shifts (e.g., training objectives, instruction‑tuning data, decoding parameters) are speculative.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A detailed analysis comparing the training methodologies, loss functions, and decoding strategies of the models. Controlled probing experiments could test if the distributions are stable across different prompts/temperatures and what latent model properties (e.g., entropy of next‑token predictions, representation clustering) correlate with these distributional shapes.

#### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Does the fundamental discrepancy between human and LLM processing of multimodal contexts stem from the LLM’s lack of an embodied, grounded notion of "relevance" between a visual scene and a textual description, leading them to always process the image as part of the linguistic context?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper’s core finding is that humans can disregard irrelevant visuals, while LLMs do not. The authors suggest LLMs simply "include the images when assigning probability values" (§5) due to their architecture maximizing context. They also note the images for the experiment were generated to be relevant to the *original* sentences, not the potentially infelicitous modified ones, creating a potential relevance mismatch the LLMs may not detect.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not test whether LLMs can discern semantic relevance between an image and a sentence. It assumes they process both inputs equally, but it’s unclear if the LLMs’ "compression effect" with images is due to treating the image as mandatory context or an inability to filter based on relevance. The design used relevant and *irrelevant* images but both were image‑sentence pairs from the original set; the irrelevant pairings were random but still depicted a concrete scene.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An experiment where images are intentionally nonsensical, abstract, or clearly contradictory to the sentence content, testing if LLMs’ probability scores and ratings for a sentence change when preceded by a *genuinely irrelevant* vs. randomly shuffled image compared to a relevant one. Additionally, probing the model’s internal cross‑attention patterns between image patches and text tokens could reveal if relevance modulates attention weights.

## Limitations
- GPT‑5 image generation is inaccessible, preventing exact replication of the visual stimuli.
- LLM evaluation is limited to a few models, potentially restricting the generalizability of findings.
- Human ratings rely on modest sample sizes per condition and a single crowdsourcing platform, which may affect reliability.

## Confidence
- Human minimal visual effect: High
- LLM compression effect: Medium
- MeanLP correlation weakening with images: Medium
