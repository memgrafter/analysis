# An Expectation-Maximization Algorithm for Domain Adaptation in Gaussian Causal Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.03459
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03459
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Proposes a DAG-aware EM algorithm for domain adaptation that significantly improves imputation of missing target variables under covariate and mechanism shifts by freezing invariant mechanisms and adapting only shifted ones via a first-order gradient M-step.

## Executive Summary
The paper addresses the problem of imputing a target variable that is systematically missing in a shifted target domain, leveraging fully observed source data and a known Gaussian causal DAG. It introduces a DAG-aware expectation-maximization (EM) framework that fuses source and target information by freezing source-invariant conditional mechanisms and re-estimating only those that have changed (e.g., at the target node). The M-step uses a first-order gradient update instead of costly generalized least squares, reducing computational cost. Theoretically, the population EM operator in the DAG parameter space is proven locally contractive under strong concavity, smoothness, and a gradient-stability condition, guaranteeing geometric convergence and finite-sample error bounds. Empirical evaluations on a synthetic 7-node SEM, a 64-node MAGIC-IRRI genetic network, and the Sachs protein-signaling data demonstrate substantial improvements in imputation accuracy (MAE, RMSE, R²) over fit-on-source and Kiiveri-style EM baselines, especially under pronounced covariate and mechanism shifts.

## Method Summary
The algorithm assumes a known DAG G and linear-Gaussian structural equation models (SEMs). From fully observed source data, all SEM parameters are fitted via node-wise regressions. For the target domain, where the variable T is missing, the EM alternates between: (E-step) computing the conditional mean and variance of T given all other observed variables X_{-t} under current parameters (using precision-based conditioning to avoid matrix inversion); (M-step) updating only the parameters of T (the coefficient vector b_t and noise variance σ_t^2) via a single projected gradient ascent on the EM surrogate, while freezing all other parameters. The step size η is set to η ≤ 2σ_t/λ_max(cM) to ensure the GEM (generalized EM) condition, i.e., monotonic increase of the surrogate. After convergence, T is imputed using the adapted conditional distribution. This local adaptation strategy reduces per-iteration cost to O(|E|) and stabilizes EM under large domain shifts that affect only a subset of mechanisms.

## Key Results
- Theoretically, the population EM operator is locally contractive under strong concavity, smoothness, and a gradient-stability condition (γ < λ), yielding geometric convergence and finite-sample error bounds.
- Empirically, on a 7-node SEM, the proposed method reduces MAE from 6.15 to 1.04 under mechanism shift and from 0.80 to 0.33 under covariate shift. On the 64-node MAGIC-IRRI network, MAE improves from 9.38 to 5.58 and R² rises from -0.10 to 0.57.
- Across all datasets, the algorithm significantly outperforms the fit-on-source baseline and a Kiiveri-style EM baseline, with the largest gains under pronounced shifts.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Freezing invariant mechanisms
The algorithm freezes all source-invariant conditional mechanisms (edges/coefficients in the DAG) and re-estimates only those that have shifted, typically at the target node T. Given a known DAG, source data provides initial parameters for all nodes. In the target EM, only the parameters of T (its parents' coefficients and noise variance) are updated in the M-step, while all other parameters remain fixed. This relies on the core assumption that the domain shift is local—only the conditional distribution P(T|pa(T)) changes between domains; the rest of the causal mechanisms are invariant. Evidence: the abstract and Section IV-C explicitly state this freezing. If the shift affects any frozen mechanism or the DAG structure itself, the method fails, producing biased imputations.

### Mechanism 2: First-order gradient M-step
The M-step replaces the costly generalized least-squares (GLS) update with a single projected gradient step. For the active linear-Gaussian block (T's coefficients), the EM surrogate Q(θ_t | θ_t^{(r)}) is a concave quadratic. Instead of solving the normal equations exactly, a gradient step: b_t ← b_t + η/σ_t (bv - cM b_t) is taken, with bv and cM being the imputed sufficient statistics. The step size η is chosen within η ≤ 2σ_t/λ_max(cM) to guarantee ascent (Lemma 1). This reduces per-iteration cost from O(p^3) to O(|pa(T)|). The core assumption is that the EM surrogate is smooth (gradient-Lipschitz) and the step size respects the stability bound. Evidence: abstract and Section IV-B. If the parent covariance cM is near-singular or the surrogate's curvature changes drastically, the Lipschitz constant may be large, causing overshooting or failure to ascend.

### Mechanism 3: Information flow through observed descendants
Information about the missing T flows through observed descendants or correlates in X_{-t}, enabling adaptation even when T is unobserved. In a Gaussian DAG, the conditional distribution p(T|X_{-t}) depends on the full precision matrix. Observed descendants of T (or other variables correlated via the DAG) carry information about shifts in T's mechanism. The EM E-step conditions on all X_{-t} (not just pa(T)), thus capturing shifts that propagate to observed variables. The core assumption is that T has at least one observed descendant or is correlated with observed variables whose distribution changes under target shift. Evidence: Section IV-A notes that conditioning on X_{-t} is beneficial when T has observed descendants; Section IV-D discusses the gradient-stability constant γ being small in such cases. If T is a root with no observed descendants and X_{-t} is conditionally independent of T given pa(T) in the target domain, then p(T|X_{-t}) provides no extra information and adaptation fails (γ approaches λ, no contraction).

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: EM algorithm for incomplete data**  
  *Why needed:* The target variable T is systematically missing in the target domain; EM alternates between imputing T (E-step) and re-estimating parameters (M-step) to maximize the observed-data likelihood.  
  *Quick check:* Why does each EM iteration increase the observed-data log-likelihood for a Gaussian model with one missing variable?

- **Concept: Gaussian SEM parameterization and DAG constraints**  
  *Why needed:* The model is a linear-Gaussian structural equation model with a known DAG; parameters must respect acyclicity (strictly lower-triangular B) and the Markov property, which simplifies conditional moment calculations like E[T|X_{-t}].  
  *Quick check:* How does the DAG structure simplify computing conditional moments like E[T|X_{-t}] versus a generic multivariate Gaussian?

- **Concept: BWY-style local contraction analysis for EM**  
  *Why needed:* To prove geometric convergence of the first-order EM, the paper adapts the Balakrishnan-Wainwright-Yu framework: the population EM operator is contractive in a basin if the surrogate is locally strongly concave/smooth and a gradient-stability (bounded missing-information) condition holds.  
  *Quick check:* What does the gradient-stability constant γ measure, and why must it be smaller than the strong concavity λ for contraction?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:** Source fit module (fits full SEM from source data) → Target EM loop (iterates E-step: compute conditional moments of T; M-step: first-order gradient update on T's parameters) → Imputer (outputs final E[T|X_{-t}]).
- **Critical path:** Source fit → initialize target with source parameters → iterate Target EM until convergence → impute missing T values.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Local vs. global updates*: Freezing inactive mechanisms reduces computational cost and stabilizes EM under large shifts, but fails if the shift affects frozen edges.
  - *First-order vs. exact M-step*: The gradient step avoids O(p^3) least-squares and scales with |pa(T)|, but requires careful step-size selection and may converge slower than exact EM within the basin.
  - *Conditioning set*: Using the full X_{-t} (not just pa(T)) leverages observed descendants for better imputation under covariate shift, but requires accurate modeling of the target-domain covariance matrix and can suffer from ill-conditioning.
- **Failure signatures:**
  1. Diverging b_t or σ_t during M-step → step-size too large or E-step moments unstable (posterior variances exploding).
  2. Poor imputation R² < 0 → frozen mechanisms are not invariant, or T lacks informative observed descendants (γ ≈ λ, no adaptation).
  3. EM surrogate not increasing → gradient step violates GEM condition (η > 2/L^{(r)}) or parent covariance matrix cM is singular/near-singular.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Synthetic 7-node SEM**: Introduce controlled covariate shift (context C2 mean change) and local mechanism shift (zero T's coefficients). Expect near-perfect recovery with first-order EM, while baselines degrade severely.
  2. **MAGIC-IRRI genetic network (64 nodes)**: Apply strong marginal interventions to 3 observed nodes; treat HT as missing. Expect substantial MAE reduction vs. fit-on-source; Kiiveri EM often unstable.
  3. **Sachs protein-signaling data**: Use CD3/CD28 stimulation as source, PMA as target; treat each protein in turn as missing T. Test where linear-Gaussian assumptions hold (e.g., Raf, Erk show improvements) vs. failures (Mek, PKA give negative R²) indicating model misspecification.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Extension to multiple missing target variables** – The conclusion mentions this as open; current analysis assumes one missing node, and multiple would require blockwise E-steps which could increase the missing-information fraction and destabilize EM convergence.
2. **Accommodating latent confounders** – The algorithm relies on DAG-specific Gaussian conditioning; latent confounders would require ancestral graphs and different conditional moment calculations.
3. **Guarantees under model misspecification** – The contraction proof depends on linear-Gaussian structure; nonlinearities, non-Gaussian noise, or feedback cycles break strong concavity/smoothness and gradient-stability.
4. **Source initialization within the contraction basin** – Theorem 1 requires initialization near the true parameters; for large mechanism shifts, the source-fit may fall outside the basin, preventing convergence.
5. **Effect of DAG structure error** – In practice the DAG is estimated; structural errors or parameter errors in frozen mechanisms could bias adaptation, but the theory does not account for this uncertainty.

## Limitations
- Empirical evaluation lacks key reproducibility details: exact synthetic coefficients/noise variances, sample sizes, convergence thresholds, and Kiiveri EM implementation specifics (intercept handling, damping).
- Theoretical assumptions (known correct DAG, linear-Gaussian SEMs, strictly local shift) may not hold in practice; violations could cause bias or divergence, and the method is not robust to shifts in frozen mechanisms.
- The gradient-stability constant γ, which governs contraction, is not reported for real datasets and could approach the strong concavity bound λ, weakening guarantees; finite-sample error bounds are given but not empirically validated.
