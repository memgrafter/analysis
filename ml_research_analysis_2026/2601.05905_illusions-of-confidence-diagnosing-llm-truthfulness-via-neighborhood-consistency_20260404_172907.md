# Illusions of Confidence? Diagnosing LLM Truthfulness via Neighborhood Consistency

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.05905  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05905  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: High-NCB facts exhibit 10-20 percentage points lower accuracy drops under contextual interference than low-NCB facts.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Neighbor-Consistency Belief (NCB), a metric that evaluates the robustness of an LLM’s beliefs by measuring consistency across a conceptual neighborhood of related facts. Using cognitive stress tests that simulate peer pressure and authority bias, the authors demonstrate that high-NCB facts are far more resistant to interference than low-NCB ones. They also propose Structure-Aware Training (SAT), which trains models to produce context‑invariant outputs, reducing knowledge brittleness by about 30%. The work challenges the common practice of using self‑consistency as a confidence signal and shows that scaling alone does not close the robustness gap.

## Method Summary
The authors build a neighbor‑enriched dataset of 2,000 factual questions across four domains. For each target question, they generate approximately eight neighbor facts of three types—Entity Prerequisite, Logical Implication, and Thematic Association—using DeepSeek‑V3.2. These neighbors undergo automated checks and human majority‑vote verification. NCB is computed by sampling multiple responses for the target and its neighbors, extracting answers with Qwen‑2.5‑32B‑Instruct, and combining the target accuracy with the geometric mean of neighbor accuracies. Stress‑test protocols (Peer Quantity and Source Credibility) then expose the model to interfering context. For training, SAT initializes a student from a strong baseline and optimizes a KL‑divergence loss to match the teacher’s output distribution across both neighbor‑generated contexts and random general contexts (from C4).

## Key Results
- High‑NCB facts (top 35%) show 10‑20 percentage points smaller accuracy drops under various stress tests than low‑NCB facts.
- SAT reduces average stress‑test degradation by ~30% relative to answer/knowledge augmentation, with minimal impact on base accuracy.
- Reflection‑based inference mitigates interference more effectively than chain‑of‑thought; model scaling does not close the robustness gap.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim**: Neighbor‑Consistency Belief (NCB) reliably indicates a structured belief state, which is inherently more robust to contextual interference.

**Mechanism**: The paper models belief as a latent variable θ ∈ {Sstruct, Sunstr}. A structured state (Sstruct) means the model’s knowledge is organized as a coherent semantic network, so correctness on a target fact (q*, a*) strongly predicts correctness across its conceptual neighborhood (neighbor facts). NCB approximates the posterior odds P(Sstruct|data)/P(Sunstr|data) by multiplying empirical correctness frequencies from the target and its neighbors, normalized via geometric mean. High NCB suggests the model’s internal representation is integrated and resistant to disruption.

**Core assumptions**: Conditional independence: given Sstruct and correct target answer, neighbor answers are independent. Also, P(ˆa* = a* | Sstruct) ≈ P(ˆa* = a* | Sunstr) (equal baseline accuracy).

**Break condition**: If neighbor questions are not truly distinct or self‑contained, or if they become too difficult (low baseline accuracy), the conditional independence assumption fails, causing NCB to misestimate belief state.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim**: Contextual interference stress tests (peer pressure and authority bias) expose brittleness in low‑NCB beliefs, while high‑NCB facts remain stable.

**Mechanism**: Two stress‑testing protocols simulate real‑world interference: Peer Quantity varies the number of peer agents giving wrong answers before the target model responds; Source Credibility attributes misleading claims to sources of low/medium/high credibility. Low‑NCB facts show large accuracy drops because their Sunstr state relies on isolated memorization; high‑NCB facts resist due to Sstruct coherence that anchors against conflicting context.

**Core assumptions**: Interference contexts are strong enough to override unstructured memorization but not structured knowledge; interfering facts are semantically related to create a plausible “consistency trap.”

**Break condition**: If interference is either too weak (no effect) or too extreme (unanimous falsehoods so implausible that all models reject), the test loses discriminative power.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim**: Structure‑Aware Training (SAT) reduces knowledge brittleness by optimizing for context‑invariant outputs across diverse contexts.

**Mechanism**: SAT initializes a student model from a strong baseline. For each training fact, it generates two context types: Neighbor contexts (aggregations of neighbor questions into coherent passages) and General contexts (random passages from C4). The student is trained to match the teacher’s output distribution (given only the isolated fact) via KL divergence averaged over all contexts. This encourages consistent answers regardless of irrelevant surrounding text, embedding facts into a more robust structure.

**Core assumptions**: The teacher model already possesses structured beliefs for the training facts; the generated contexts are diverse enough to cover realistic variations without altering the core fact.

**Break condition**: If neighbor contexts are noisy or misaligned, SAT may harm accuracy or overfit to synthetic patterns; may not generalize to dynamic facts requiring updates.

## Foundational Learning
### Concept: Latent Variable Models for Belief
**Why needed**: The paper’s NCB derivation treats belief state (structured vs. unstructured) as an unobserved variable and uses Bayesian inference to estimate it from observable neighbor consistency. This formalism underpins the entire metric.

**Quick check**: If P(Sstruct)=0.5 and the likelihood ratio P(neighbor consistency|Sstruct)/P(neighbor consistency|Sunstr)=10, what is the posterior odds in favor of Sstruct?

### Concept: Self‑Consistency vs. Neighborhood Consistency
**Why needed**: Self‑consistency (Wang et al., 2023a) samples multiple answers to the same question; high SC was historically treated as confidence. The paper shows SC is insufficient—neighborhood consistency across related questions is needed to capture true belief robustness.

**Quick check**: Give an example where a model has perfect self‑consistency (SC=1.0) but fails under contextual interference, as in Figure 1.

### Concept: Cognitive Stress‑Testing for LLMs
**Why needed**: The paper’s validation relies on simulating psychological stress tests (Asch conformity, source credibility) to challenge models. Understanding these paradigms is key to interpreting why certain contexts cause failures.

**Quick check**: How does “Peer Quantity–Conflict” differ from “Source Credibility–Misleading” in terms of interference mechanism?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Data Construction: Seed sampling (2 000 balanced facts), Neighbor generation (DeepSeek prompts for 3 relation types), Verification (automated + human), Misleading set generation.  
NCB Computation: Sampling (30 for target, 10 per neighbor), Extraction (Qwen‑2.5‑32B‑Instruct), Score: product of correctness probabilities.  
Stress‑Testing Module: Peer Quantity (template with N peers), Source Credibility (misleading claims with source credibility levels).  
SAT Training: Context sources (neighbor aggregations + C4), Loss: KL(teacher||student), Baselines: Answer/Knowledge augmentation.

### Critical path
1. Build Neighbor‑Enriched Dataset with verified neighbors and misleading mirror sets.  
2. For a candidate model, compute NCB on a fact set (multiple samples).  
3. Split facts into High‑NCB and Low‑NCB groups by percentiles.  
4. Run stress‑test protocols on both groups, measuring accuracy and coverage.  
5. Validate NCB’s predictive power (Table 3, Figure 4).  
6. To apply SAT: start from a baseline model, generate contexts for unknown facts, train with KL loss, re‑evaluate robustness.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Neighbor depth vs. cost**: Three relation types balance coverage and effort; deeper relations (causal, taxonomic) would improve expressiveness but increase construction complexity.  
- **Sampling budget**: Many samples needed for reliable probability estimates; reducing samples lowers cost but increases variance.  
- **Context diversity in SAT**: Neighbor contexts enforce semantic consistency; general contexts prevent overfitting. Too many general contexts dilutes structural signal; too few risks overfitting to synthetic patterns.  
- **Stress‑test realism**: Scenarios are simplified proxies; may not capture all deployment risks (e.g., multimodal, temporal drift).

### Failure signatures
- **NCB inconsistency**: High‑NCB facts show large accuracy drops under some stress tests → neighborhood definitions missing critical relational edges.  
- **Neighbor generation failure**: Blind solver validation rate low; indicates neighbor questions are ambiguous or not self‑contained → NCB scores unreliable.  
- **SAT overfitting**: Stress‑test gains come with drop on generic benchmarks (MMLU, GSM8k) → model memorizing context patterns.  
- **Coverage collapse**: Models start refusing to answer under interference; coverage near 0 invalidates accuracy comparisons.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Pilot replication**: Select 100 facts with SC=1.0. Apply Peer Quantity–Conflict (N=5 wrong peers). Confirm average accuracy drop below 40%, establishing the illusion.  
2. **NCB stratification**: Compute NCB for 500 facts. Group top/bottom 20%. Run Peer Quantity–Conflict (N=1..6). Plot accuracy vs N; expect high‑NCB flat, low‑NCB sharply declining.  
3. **SAT vs baselines**: On 100 unknown facts, train three models: (a) Answer Augmentation, (b) Knowledge Augmentation, (c) SAT. Evaluate on Quantity and Source Stress. Confirm SAT reduces average degradation by ~30% relative to best baseline.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Does high self‑consistency reliably indicate robust belief?**  
   - Basis in paper: The authors show that facts with perfect self‑consistency (SC = 1.0) can still collapse under mild contextual interference (e.g., accuracy drops from 100% to 33.8%). They explicitly state that "point‑wise confidence is superficial" and question whether self‑consistency reflects true belief state.  
   - Why unresolved: The paper demonstrates the illusion but does not fully explain *why* self‑consistency fails to predict robustness—only that neighbor consistency (NCB) correlates better. The underlying cognitive or architectural reasons remain speculative.  
   - What evidence would resolve it: Controlled ablations isolating memorization vs. structured comprehension (e.g., probing internal representations, editing experiments) could reveal why SC fails while NCB succeeds.

2. **Can NCB generalize beyond the three defined neighbor relation types (Entity Prerequisite, Logical Implication, Thematic Association)?**  
   - Basis in paper: The authors acknowledge that their neighbor construction focuses on "broad coverage and ease of automated generation" but explicitly exclude "more complex relations like causal chains or hierarchical taxonomies" due to resource constraints.  
   - Why unresolved: It is unclear whether NCB's predictive power hinges on the specific chosen relations or would hold for richer, domain‑specific semantic networks.  
   - What evidence would resolve it: Extending the dataset with additional relation types (e.g., temporal sequences, part‑whole hierarchies) and testing NCB's correlation with robustness across diverse domains.

3. **Does model scale inherently improve belief robustness?**  
   - Basis in paper: Finding 4 states: "Model scaling does not alter the robustness gap between High and Low NCB." They show that larger Qwen2.5 models do not necessarily close the gap, leaving open whether scale alone can address brittleness.  
   - Why unresolved: The experiment uses a single model family (Qwen2.5) and a limited size range (1.5B–72B). It is unknown if scaling in other architectures (e.g., dense vs. MoE) or with different training data would eventually converge.  
   - What evidence would resolve it: Systematic scaling studies across multiple model families, measuring NCB‑robustness trade‑offs at billion‑parameter increments, possibly with compute‑optimal scaling laws.

4. **How does human‑like "genuine understanding" map to NCB?**  
   - Basis in paper: In the Limitations section, they state: "Though inspired by cognitive psychology, our NCB metric lacks empirical validation against human judgments of 'genuine understanding.'"  
   - Why unresolved: NCB is an operational proxy for robustness, but it is unknown whether humans would agree on what constitutes a "structured belief" or whether NCB aligns with human intuition about knowledge depth.  
   - What evidence would resolve it: Human subject studies where participants rate fact "understanding" or "confidence" and compare those ratings with NCB scores; cross‑cultural validation to see if structural coherence is universally perceived as robust belief.

5. **What is the minimal sufficient neighborhood for reliable NCB estimation?**  
   - Basis in paper: The average number of neighbor facts per target is 7.84. They test subsampling (20–100%) and find NCB discriminative power remains stable, but they do not establish a theoretical or empirical lower bound.  
   - Why unresolved: The computational overhead of constructing large neighborhoods is noted as a limitation. It is unclear how few neighbors suffice for a robust estimate without sacrificing validity, especially for complex domains.  
   - What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments varying neighbor count per fact, measuring correlation with stress‑test robustness; potentially developing automated methods to select maximally informative neighbors.

6. **How sensitive is NCB to the assumption of conditional independence among neighbor facts?**  
   - Basis in inference: The Bayesian derivation (Appendix B) assumes that given a structured belief and a correct target answer, neighbor answers are conditionally independent. In reality, neighbor questions may share reasoning paths or knowledge sub‑components, violating independence.  
   - Why unresolved: The authors briefly note that their curation "minimizes" overlap but do not quantify dependency or test its impact on NCB scores.  
   - What evidence would resolve it: Measuring pairwise correlations among neighbor predictions; simulating dependency via copula models; comparing NCB computed with and without independence assumption on the same dataset.

7. **Do all neighbor relation types contribute equally to NCB's predictive power?**  
   - Basis in inference: NCB uses a geometric mean across all neighbors, implicitly assuming equal importance. The paper tests weighting variations only in a supplementary ablation (Figure 11) and finds insensitivity, but this is not deeply analyzed.  
   - Why unresolved: It is plausible that logical implications (LI) are more diagnostic of robust belief than thematic associations (TA), but the metric treats them identically. The authors do not report per‑relation‑type performance under stress tests.  
   - What evidence would resolve it: Ablating each relation type (computing NCB without EP, without LI, etc.) and measuring drop in correlation with interference robustness; feature importance analysis in a predictive model of brittleness.

8. **How does NCB behave on dynamic or time‑sensitive knowledge?**  
   - Basis in inference: The dataset is restricted to "time‑invariant factual knowledge" to avoid validity drift. This is a major scope limitation, as real‑world knowledge often changes (e.g., political leaders, scientific consensus).  
   - Why unresolved: It is unknown whether NCB would remain a valid robustness metric for facts that are inherently volatile. A model might have a "structured" but outdated belief network.  
   - What evidence would resolve it: Extending the benchmark to include facts with known temporal boundaries (e.g., "Who was the CEO of X in 2020?") and testing whether NCB predicts resistance to temporal interference (e.g., newer conflicting contexts).

9. **Can SAT's benefits be attributed solely to neighborhood invariance, or do general context diversity effects dominate?**  
   - Basis in inference: SAT uses two context types: Neighbor Contexts (Cnq) and General Contexts (Cgeneral). The loss minimizes KL divergence across all contexts. It is unclear whether the improvement comes specifically from the semantic coherence of neighbor contexts or simply from exposing the model to diverse contexts (a form of augmentation).  
   - Why unresolved: The ablation compares SAT only to answer‑based and knowledge‑based augmentation, not to a baseline that uses random non‑neighbor contexts. The relative contribution of Cnq vs. Cgeneral is not isolated.  
   - What evidence would resolve it: Training variants: SAT with only Cnq, only Cgeneral, and shuffled/random contexts; measuring robustness gains to attribute effect to neighborhood‑specific invariance.

10. **Why does reflection consistently mitigate interference while CoT does not, and how does this interact with NCB?**  
    - Basis in inference: Finding 3 shows reflection (multi‑turn) reduces accuracy drops under stress, while CoT sometimes exacerbates them. The authors suggest reflection "filters external noise" but do not explain the mechanism.  
    - Why unresolved: The interplay between reasoning strategy, belief structure (NCB), and interference is not modeled. Does reflection work because it allows re‑evaluation in a "cleaner" context, or does it internally reconstruct a structured belief?  
    - What evidence would resolve it: Process‑tracing studies (e.g., attention analysis, activation steering) during reflection vs. CoT under interference; measuring NCB of the final answer after reflection to see if high‑NCB states emerge preferentially.

11. **What is the prior probability ratio (P(Sstruct)/P(Sunstruct)) in the Bayesian model, and how does it affect NCB interpretation?**  
    - Basis in inference: The NCB derivation uses a Prior Odds term but does not estimate or justify it. They assume it is constant across facts, but in reality, some facts may be more likely to be structured (e.g., common knowledge) than others.  
    - Why unresolved: Without an empirical prior, NCB scores are only comparable within the same dataset and model. It is unclear how to interpret absolute NCB values across domains or models with different training.  
    - What evidence would resolve it: Estimating priors from data (e.g., proportion of facts that show neighbor consistency in a large sample) or performing sensitivity analysis across a range of plausible priors.

12. **Does NCB capture long‑term belief stability beyond immediate interference?**  
    - Basis in inference: Stress tests are single‑turn interventions. The paper does not test whether high‑NCB facts remain stable over multiple interference exposures or sequential updates (e.g., in a multi‑session dialogue).  
    - Why unresolved: Real‑world deployments involve prolonged interaction; a belief might be initially robust but degrade over repeated contradictory exposures.  
    - What evidence would resolve it: Designing cumulative interference protocols (e.g., 10 rounds of peer pressure) and tracking accuracy decay; measuring whether NCB predicts half‑life of belief under persistent attack.

13. **Can adversarial examples be crafted to specifically target low‑NCB facts while preserving high‑NCB facts?**  
    - Basis in inference: The stress tests are designed to be cognitively realistic (peer pressure, authority bias). However, if NCB truly indicates structural weakness, one might engineer attacks that exploit those specific weaknesses (e.g., neighbor fact manipulations).  
    - Why unresolved: The paper shows low‑NCB facts are more vulnerable, but it does not explore whether perturbations can be tuned to *maximally* differentiate NCB levels or to attack the structure itself.  
    - What evidence would resolve it: Adversarial search for interference contexts that cause maximal accuracy drop for low‑NCB but minimal drop for high‑NCB; analyzing failure modes to see if they correlate with specific neighbor relation failures.

14. **How does NCB relate to other uncertainty metrics (e.g., semantic entropy, verbalized confidence)?**  
    - Basis in inference: The related work contrasts NCB with self‑consistency and semantic entropy but does not provide a direct empirical comparison. The paper positions NCB as a structural alternative, but relationships are not quantified.  
    - Why unresolved: It is unknown whether NCB is orthogonal, complementary, or redundant with existing uncertainty measures. Could a combination improve robustness prediction?  
    - What evidence would resolve it: Multi‑metric evaluation on the same stress‑test dataset; correlation analysis and ablation studies to see if NCB adds predictive power beyond entropy or verbalized confidence.

15. **What are the ethical risks of using NCB‑based filtering in production systems?**  
    - Basis in inference: The ethical statement warns that prioritizing high‑NCB might marginalize long‑tail knowledge. Also, stress‑test protocols could be misused to design better attacks.  
    - Why unresolved: The paper does not propose mitigation strategies or evaluate fairness implications (e.g., does NCB correlate with fact popularity, disadvantaging niche domains?).  
    - What evidence would resolve it: Auditing NCB distributions across demographic/cultural knowledge bases; testing whether high‑NCB facts are overrepresented in Western‑centric data; developing fairness‑aware NCB calibration.

## Limitations
- Neighbor generation relies on a single LLM (DeepSeek‑V3.2) with no cross‑model verification; human validation lacks agreement metrics.
- Dataset size (2 k facts) and domain balance limit statistical robustness; long‑tail coverage minimal.
- Stress tests cover only peer and authority interference; multimodal, temporal, or sequential interference not examined.
- SAT's KL objective assumes teacher outputs are context‑invariant, which may be false; risk of degrading general capabilities.

## Confidence
- **NCB metric**: Medium – Bayesian derivation depends on conditional independence and equal prior assumptions; neighbor noise not fully quantified.
- **Stress‑test validity**: Medium – consistent interference effects shown, but scenarios are narrow proxies; external ecological validity uncertain.
- **SAT effectiveness**: Low‑Medium – benefits demonstrated on 100 facts; lack of scaling evidence and potential overfitting to synthetic contexts.

## Next Checks
1. Cross‑model replication: Compute NCB and run stress tests on five diverse LLMs (e.g., Llama, Mistral, Claude) to evaluate metric stability.  
2. Neighbor correlation ablation: Introduce increasing semantic overlap among neighbor questions and measure NCB’s predictive drop to test the independence assumption.  
3. Broad SAT evaluation: Train on 10 k facts across domains and assess on out‑of‑distribution stress tests plus MMLU/TruthfulQA to detect trade‑offs.
