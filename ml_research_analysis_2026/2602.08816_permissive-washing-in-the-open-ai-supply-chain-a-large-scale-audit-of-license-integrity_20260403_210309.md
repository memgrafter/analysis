# Permissive-Washing in the Open AI Supply Chain: A Large-Scale Audit of License Integrity
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.08816
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08816
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Only 2.3% of datasets and 3.2% of models include both full license text and a copyright notice; attribution preservation is low (27.59% dataset→model, 5.75% model→application).

## Executive Summary
This study exposes “permissive washing” in AI supply chains—artifacts labeled with permissive licenses (MIT, Apache-2.0, BSD-3-Clause) often omit required license text and copyright notices, making legal reuse uncertain. By auditing 124,278 dataset→model→application chains, the authors find extremely low compliance: fewer than 3% of datasets and models contain complete license payloads. Attribution preservation decays sharply across downstream transitions. A stark platform divide emerges: GitHub applications achieve 74.2% compliance, while Hugging Face artifacts fall below 3.2%, indicating that metadata-centric licensing fosters the problem.

## Method Summary
The researchers constructed a pipeline spanning three stages. First, they collected models from Hugging Face, resolved lineages (base models, training datasets), and identified downstream GitHub applications via code search combined with AST validation using 8,968 model-loading signatures. Second, they retrieved candidate license files through regex patterns and analyzed them with the ScanCode Toolkit to extract license declarations and copyright notices. Third, they applied integrity checks (presence of ≥90% license text and a copyright notice) and attribution audits (normalized substring matching of upstream notices) to the subset labeled with permissive licenses. The final corpus comprised 124,278 supply chains.

## Key Results
- Integrity: Only 2.3% of datasets and 3.2% of models contain both full license text and a copyright notice.
- Attribution decay: 27.59% of dataset→model links and 5.75% of model→application links preserve upstream notices.
- Platform disparity: GitHub applications are 74.2% compliant, while Hugging Face datasets and models are <3.2% compliant.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The study identifies three systemic mechanisms driving permissive washing:

### Mechanism 1: Metadata-Centric Licensing Drives Compliance Gap
Hugging Face’s design encourages license declaration in metadata (model cards, READMEs) rather than file‑based payloads. Developers follow platform conventions, assuming metadata satisfies legal obligations. Evidence: 97% of Hugging Face datasets/models store licensing info in READMEs; 96.5% of datasets and 95.8% of models lack LICENSE files. Break condition: Platform policy requiring LICENSE files or tooling enforcement.

### Mechanism 2: Thin Traceable Slice Obscures Violations
Only 7.1% of models declare training datasets, creating a small “traceable” slice where missing payloads remain invisible. Without explicit provenance, downstream users cannot know which notices to preserve, and auditors cannot assess compliance end‑to‑end. Evidence: Of 1,922,385 models, only 136,890 declare datasets; the final audit corpus of 124,278 chains is a tiny fraction. Break condition: Mandating comprehensive provenance disclosures expands traceability.

### Mechanism 3: Attribution Decay in Supply Chain Transitions
Even when upstream artifacts have complete payloads, attribution notices are dropped during dataset→model and model→application transitions. Human‑centered workflows lack automated propagation (e.g., AI‑BOMs). Evidence: Only 27.59% of dataset→model links and 5.75% of model→application links retain notices; from compliant upstream, merely 6.38% of applications preserve any linked notice. Break condition: Widespread tooling that automatically propagates and verifies notices during fine‑tuning and integration.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Data Collection (Hugging Face API scraper, GitHub Code Search API, AST-based usage validator) → License Detection (file retriever with regex patterns, ScanCode Toolkit) → Auditing (Integrity auditor, Attribution auditor)
- **Critical path:** (1) Collect models and resolve lineage; (2) Find downstream GitHub apps and validate usage via AST; (3) Retrieve license‑relevant files; (4) Run ScanCode to extract license declarations and copyrights; (5) Apply integrity and attribution audits to the permissive subset.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Scalability vs. Recall: Used regex‑based file retrieval instead of full scans (93.6% recall) to handle 38,518 artifacts.
  - Breadth vs. Depth: Focused on three permissive licenses; omitted others (e.g., CC0) due to different legal mechanics.
  - Automation vs. Accuracy: AST parsing for usage validation may miss dynamic loading; chosen for scalability.
- **Failure signatures:** Low lineage disclosure (<10% of models declare datasets); high missing payloads (>95% of Hugging Face datasets/models lack LICENSE files); attribution loss (<30% preservation even from compliant upstream).
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Extend the audit to additional license families (copyleft, CC) to compare compliance patterns.
  2. Evaluate the impact of platform‑specific enforcement (e.g., Hugging Face requiring LICENSE files) on permissive washing rates.
  3. Build and evaluate an AI‑BOM prototype that automatically propagates notices during model fine‑tuning, measuring improvement in attribution preservation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Platform affordances causing the compliance gap:** What specific Hugging Face design features (metadata‑centric interfaces, lack of enforced LICENSE file templates) explain the stark disparity with GitHub’s file‑tree model?  
   *Basis:* The paper observes the gap but does not isolate causal factors. *Why unresolved:* No experimental disentanglement of UI/UX, policy, norms, or tooling. *Evidence needed:* Mixed‑methods study combining feature analysis, user surveys/experiments, and longitudinal tracking after policy updates.

2. **Compliance across non‑permissive licenses:** How do integrity and attribution rates differ for copyleft, CC BY‑NC, or ML‑specific restriction licenses?  
   *Basis:* The audit focuses on MIT, Apache‑2.0, BSD‑3‑Clause, noting other licenses have different obligations. *Why unresolved:* Practical scope limited to permissive family; behavior of other prevalent licenses remains unmeasured. *Evidence needed:* Extend the pipeline to artifacts labeled with those licenses, using tailored checks (e.g., source availability for copyleft, use‑restriction notices for RAIL).

3. **Washing in the untraceable majority:** What is the extent of permissive washing among models lacking lineage metadata (the ~92.9% that do not declare datasets)?  
   *Basis:* Only 7.1% of models declare training datasets; the audit covers a tiny “traceable slice.” The authors caution against extrapolation. *Why unresolved:* Current methodology requires explicit lineage metadata; the bulk of the ecosystem is legally blind. *Evidence needed:* Scalable techniques to infer dataset and base model lineages without metadata (e.g., weight similarity, code clone detection), followed by compliance audits on inferred chains.

4. **Mislabeling vs. intentional washing:** How often are permissive labels incorrect due to user error versus deliberate misrepresentation?  
   *Basis:* The study relies on platform metadata labels and acknowledges possible incorrect tags. It measures the label‑payload gap but not the etiology of missing payloads. *Why unresolved:* Disentangling accident from intent is essential for targeted interventions. *Evidence needed:* Qualitative analysis of commit histories and READMEs, surveys of creators about licensing decisions, and cross‑referencing labels with upstream source licenses.

5. **Attribution matching underestimation:** To what extent do literal substring‑matching methods underestimate preservation due to notice reformatting, paraphrasing, or aggregation?  
   *Basis:* The attribution audit uses normalized substring matching and acknowledges it may miss non‑literal overlaps. *Why unresolved:* The chosen method is simple but may not capture real‑world notice transformations; preservation rates could be higher or lower. *Evidence needed:* Re‑run the audit with NLP‑based matching (e.g., sentence embeddings or LLMs) on a sample, then quantify the delta.

## Limitations
- **Snapshot:** Data collected in August 2025; current state may differ.
- **Provenance bias:** Only 7.1% of models declare datasets, so the 124 k chains are a non‑representative traceable slice.
- **File retrieval:** Regex patterns may miss license payloads in uncommon locations (recall 93.6% on sample).
