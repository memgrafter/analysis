# OpenSanctions Pairs: Large-Scale Entity Matching with LLMs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.11051
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11051
- **Reference count:** 29
- **Primary result:** Off-the-shelf LLMs (GPT-4o: 98.95% F1, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B: 98.23% F1) substantially outperform a production rule-based matcher (91.33% F1) on a new large-scale sanctions entity matching benchmark.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces OpenSanctions Pairs, a novel benchmark of 755,540 manually labeled entity pairs derived from 293 heterogeneous, multilingual sanctions lists. It evaluates a production-grade rule-based entity matcher against several large language models (LLMs) on the task of pairwise conflict detection. LLMs achieve near-perfect F1 scores, dramatically exceeding the rule-based system. Error analysis reveals complementary failure modes: the rule-based system over-matches, while LLMs falter primarily on cross-script transliteration and minor identifier/date inconsistencies. The authors conclude that the pairwise matching problem is near a practical ceiling for LLMs and argue the field should shift focus to integrating LLMs into other pipeline stages like blocking and clustering.

## Method Summary
The authors constructed the OpenSanctions Pairs benchmark by harmonizing records from 293 global sanctions sources, generating candidate matches via string similarity and manual review, and labeling 755,540 pairs as matches or non-matches. They compared a deterministic, production rule-based matcher (using fuzzy string matching, identifier comparison, and metadata checks) against multiple state-of-the-art LLMs (including GPT-4o and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B) using a conflict-detection prompt. Models were evaluated on standard classification metrics (Precision, Recall, F1) and through a detailed error analysis stratified by failure type. Prompt optimization was explored via MIPROv2, and few-shot learning was tested.

## Key Results
- The OpenSanctions Pairs benchmark provides the first large-scale, multilingual evaluation suite for sanctions entity matching.
- GPT-4o achieved 98.95% F1, and an open, locally deployable model (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B) reached 98.23% F1, both far surpassing the rule-based matcher's 91.33% F1.
- LLMs' primary errors are on cross-script transliteration (e.g., Cyrillic to Latin) and small numeric/date discrepancies, while the rule-based system's errors are dominated by over-matching on superficial similarities.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
LLMs succeed due to their inherent ability to perform contextual, multilingual reasoning over heterogeneous record fields (names, addresses, identifiers) without relying on brittle, hand-crafted similarity thresholds. They can implicitly normalize variations, understand semantic equivalence, and weigh evidence across fields in a way that mirrors expert judgment. The rule-based system fails because its fixed thresholds cannot adapt to the vast and noisy diversity of global sanctions data, leading to high false positives. However, LLMs' failure on exact transliteration and minor inconsistencies highlights their weakness at precise symbolic manipulation tasks where deterministic string or numeric comparison is mathematically correct.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:** Raw Source Data -> Schema Harmonization -> Candidate Generation (Blocking) -> Pairwise Classification (Rule Matcher / LLM) -> Clustering / Decision Threshold -> Matched Entities

**Critical Path:** The study isolates and evaluates the *Pairwise Classification* component, comparing a deterministic rule engine against an LLM called via an API or local inference.

**Design Tradeoffs:**
- **Rule-Based:** Fast, explainable, low-cost, deterministic. Tradeoff: Brittle, requires extensive feature engineering, poor generalization to noise and new patterns.
- **LLM-Based:** Flexible, high accuracy, handles context and language variation. Tradeoff: High computational cost, latency, potential for hallucinations, less deterministic, requires API management or heavy local resources.

**Failure Signatures:**
- **Rule-Based:** High false positive rate ("over-matching") on records with superficial string similarity (e.g., "John Smith" vs "John Smithson").
- **LLM:** False negatives on pairs with legitimate matches that involve cross-script transliteration (e.g., "Дмитрий" vs "Dmitriy") or trivial numeric/date differences (e.g., "01-Jan-1980" vs "02-Jan-1980").

**First Three Experiments:**
1. **Cross-Script Stress Test:** Build a focused test set of 1,000 transliteration-heavy pairs (e.g., from Cyrillic/Arabic to Latin). Compare precision/recall degradation of GPT-4o vs. the rule-based matcher.
2. **Cost-Latency Profiling:** Measure inference time, memory footprint, and monetary cost (for API models) or hardware utilization (for local models) per 10,000 comparisons against the rule-based system's baseline.
3. **Hybrid Re-ranking Prototype:** Implement a two-stage system: (1) rule-based high-recall candidate generation (e.g., top-5 per record), followed by (2) LLM re-ranking of these candidates. Evaluate overall F1 and computational savings versus pure LLM all-pairs classification.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can LLMs be effectively integrated into blocking and clustering stages of entity resolution pipelines to reduce computational costs while maintaining high accuracy?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion explicitly states that future work should shift beyond pairwise classification to pipeline components such as blocking, clustering, and uncertainty-aware review.
- **Why unresolved:** Current experiments evaluate only pairwise matching; the integration of LLMs into earlier (blocking) or later (clustering) stages remains unexplored, leaving a gap in understanding end-to-end system design.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Designing and evaluating a complete entity resolution pipeline that employs LLMs for candidate generation or clustering on the OpenSanctions Pairs benchmark, with comparative metrics on recall, precision, and computational overhead.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can ensemble methods that combine rule-based high-recall filtering with LLM re-ranking reduce overall error rates, particularly on cross-script transliteration cases where LLMs currently fail?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Error analysis reveals complementary failure modes: the rule-based system has high false positives (over-matching), while LLMs have false negatives on transliteration and minor inconsistencies.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper analyzes errors in isolation but does not test or propose an ensemble approach that could leverage these complementary strengths.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implementing an ensemble where the rule-based matcher provides a high-recall candidate set and an LLM re-ranks pairs, then measuring performance on transliteration-heavy subsets of the dataset.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What alternative prompting strategies or model architectures can address LLM failures on minor identifier and date inconsistencies ("off-by-one" errors) without sacrificing overall performance?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] LLMs primarily fail on cross-script transliteration and minor identifier/date inconsistencies; prompt optimization via MIPROv2 yields only modest gains.
- **Why unresolved:** The conflict-detection prompt framing may overemphasize small discrepancies; the paper does not explore prompts that tolerate noise or hybrid systems combining LLMs with fuzzy matching.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Developing and testing prompts that incorporate fuzzy matching rules for dates/IDs or augmenting LLMs with external similarity calculators, then evaluating error rates on the minor-inconsistency failure subset.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** To what extent do disagreements between LLM predictions and expert labels reflect labeling uncertainty in the dataset, and how should this uncertainty be incorporated into evaluation?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The limitations section clarifies that labels are expert judgments under incomplete evidence, not definitive ground truth; disagreements may indicate labeling uncertainty.
- **Why unresolved:** Current evaluation treats all labels as gold standard, ignoring that some pairs are inherently ambiguous; this may misrepresent model performance.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Re-annotating a sample of model-label disagreements with multiple experts to estimate label confidence, then adjusting metrics (e.g., weighted F1) or introducing uncertainty-aware evaluation protocols.

## Limitations
- **Benchmark Coverage:** OpenSanctions Pairs, while large, may not capture the full operational diversity of sanctions data (e.g., real-time schema changes, severe OCR artifacts, or very low-resource languages).
- **Generalizability Ceiling:** Reported near-perfect LLM F1 scores are on a specific, curated benchmark; performance likely degrades on noisier, more dynamic, or non-sanctions entity matching tasks.
- **Pipeline Gap:** The proposed shift to integrating LLMs into blocking/clustering is not empirically validated; latency, cost, and scalability in a live, end-to-end screening system remain unknown.

## Confidence
- **High:** Scale and construction methodology of the OpenSanctions Pairs benchmark (755,540 pairs from 293 distinct sources).
- **Medium:** The finding that off-the-shelf LLMs substantially outperform the specific production rule-based matcher *on this benchmark*.
- **Low:** Broader conclusions about the universal superiority of LLMs for all entity matching scenarios and their readiness for cost-effective, large-scale production deployment beyond the study's controlled conditions.

## Next Checks
1. **External Benchmark Validation:** Replicate the core experiment on a distinct, publicly available multilingual entity matching benchmark (e.g., from e-commerce or legal domains) to test generalizability of the LLM vs. rule-based performance gap.
2. **Production Cost-Latency Analysis:** Quantify inference speed, hardware requirements, and monetary cost per million comparisons for GPT-4o and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B versus the rule-based system under simulated batch load.
3. **Targeted Failure Stress Test:** Curate a focused test set of 500-1,000 pairs exhibiting known failure modes (cross-script transliteration, minor ID/date variance) and measure the absolute performance delta between top LLMs and the rule-based matcher.
