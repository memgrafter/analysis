# How Uncertain Is the Grade? A Benchmark of Uncertainty Metrics for LLM-Based Automatic Assessment

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.16039
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16039
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Categorical uncertainty metrics (especially Categorical Entropy) consistently achieve the highest effectiveness in discriminating correct from incorrect grades; relation-based methods are more stable but less effective, revealing a trade-off.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the problem of unreliable uncertainty quantification in LLM-based automatic grading, which can lead to harmful educational decisions. It presents a comprehensive benchmark of uncertainty quantification (UQ) methods, dividing them into two families: categorical methods that analyze frequency distributions over discrete grade labels, and relation-based methods that construct semantic similarity graphs over reasoning traces. Evaluating across multiple datasets, LLMs, and prompting strategies, the study measures effectiveness (via AUROC, AUARC, AUERC), stability, and inter-method correlation. Results indicate that categorical methods, particularly Categorical Entropy, consistently rank highest in discriminating correct from incorrect grades, while relation-based methods exhibit superior stability but lower overall effectiveness. The findings highlight a fundamental trade-off between discriminative power and robustness, underscoring that no single uncertainty metric is universally optimal for LLM-based assessment.

## Method Summary
The study benchmarks uncertainty quantification methods for LLM-as-judge grading. Inputs consist of questions, rubrics, and student answers from five datasets (ASAP, SemEval, and three private ones). Seven proprietary and seven open-source LLMs generate grading outputs under zero-shot, zero-shot with chain-of-thought (CoT), and few-shot CoT prompting strategies. For each input, the LLM is sampled multiple times (N=5) to produce grade labels and optional reasoning. Uncertainty metrics fall into two categories: (1) Categorical methods (Numset, MAR, Categorical Entropy, FSD) computed from the frequency distribution over the discrete grade labels; (2) Relation-based methods that build graphs from pairwise semantic similarities among responses (using Jaccard, embeddings, or NLI) and derive graph properties (NAD, GE, Eigen, DSE). Effectiveness is evaluated using AUROC (binary correctness), C-index (ordinal correctness), AUARC, and AUERC. Stability is measured by the delta change ratio and Spearman rank correlation as sample size increases. Inter-method correlation is assessed via Pearson. No training is involved; the work is a benchmark of existing metrics.

## Key Results
- Categorical Entropy (CE) consistently achieves top effectiveness rankings in discriminating correct from incorrect grades across diverse settings.
- Relation-based uncertainty methods (e.g., those using semantic graphs) demonstrate superior stability (less sensitive to sample size) but lower overall effectiveness compared to categorical methods.
- A clear trade-off exists between effectiveness and stability; no single metric dominates all evaluation dimensions, and performance varies with the assessment question and model capability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Categorical-based uncertainty metrics
- **Claim:** Categorical-based uncertainty metrics (e.g., Categorical Entropy) effectively discriminate correct from incorrect LLM grading outputs because they directly measure variability in the discrete label space of grades.
- **Mechanism:** For a given input, the LLM is sampled multiple times to produce a set of grading labels. The frequency distribution over these labels is summarized (e.g., via entropy) to produce an uncertainty score. A peaked distribution (low entropy) indicates consistent prediction and lower uncertainty; a uniform distribution indicates high uncertainty.
- **Core assumption:** In automatic assessment, the grade label space is small and predefined, and epistemic uncertainty manifests as disagreement among sampled predictions within this space.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract] "categorical methods, particularly Categorical Entropy (CE), consistently achieve the highest effectiveness rankings in discriminating correct from incorrect grades."
  - [section 3.2.1] Formal definitions of Numset, MAR, CE, FSD as frequency-based measures over output labels.
- **Break condition:** If the LLM’s output space is not constrained to a fixed set of grade labels (e.g., free-form rationales without a deterministic mapping to a score), categorical methods become inapplicable or misleading.

### Mechanism 2: Relation-based methods for stability
- **Claim:** Relation-based uncertainty metrics (e.g., graph eccentricity over semantic similarity) yield more stable estimates across varying sample sizes because they leverage continuous semantic relationships among reasoning traces, which are less sensitive to lexical sampling noise.
- **Mechanism:** These methods construct a graph where nodes are generated responses (including reasoning) and edges encode semantic similarity (via Jaccard, embeddings, or NLI). Graph properties (e.g., average degree, eccentricity) quantify the tightness of the response cluster. A dense, compact graph indicates low uncertainty, and this structure persists even as new samples are added.
- **Core assumption:** Semantic similarity among reasoning traces is a smoother, more robust proxy for underlying uncertainty than discrete label agreement, especially when samples are few or the model produces paraphrased yet equivalent rationales.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract] "Relation-based methods demonstrate superior stability but lower overall effectiveness."
  - [section 4.4] Table 4 and associated text show relation-based methods (e.g., JS_NAD, JS_GE) rank higher in stability metrics (Delta, Spearmanr) than categorical ones.
- **Break condition:** If the LLM’s reasoning traces are highly inconsistent or contain hallucinations, semantic similarity graphs may become fragmented regardless of true uncertainty, leading to overestimated instability.

### Mechanism 3: Trade-off between effectiveness and stability
- **Claim:** The observed trade-off between effectiveness and stability arises because categorical methods optimize for discrimination via label frequencies (sensitive to sampling variance), while relation methods sacrifice some discriminative power for robustness by aggregating over semantic similarities that blur fine-grained label distinctions.
- **Mechanism:** Categorical metrics directly reflect the model’s propensity to output the same grade, which correlates strongly with correctness. However, with few samples, random fluctuations in label counts cause large swings in these metrics (low stability). Relation metrics smooth over lexical and semantic variations, yielding smoother curves as sample size grows, but this smoothing can also merge semantically distinct but incorrect modes, reducing discriminative ability.
- **Core assumption:** The ideal uncertainty metric for grading should simultaneously rank correct responses lower than incorrect ones (effectiveness) and do so consistently as more samples are collected (stability), but these objectives conflict due to the discrete nature of grades vs. continuous semantics.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [section 4.3.1] "categorical methods... consistently attain the top rank... relation-based methods... exhibit superior stability."
  - [section 4.4.1] "categorical-based methods do not consistently dominate relation-based methods in terms of stability."
  - [conclusion] "This phenomenon shows a trade-off between assessment discriminative ability and stability."
- **Break condition:** If a method could perfectly cluster responses by both grade label and semantic equivalence, the trade-off might vanish; however, such a method would require perfect semantic parsing, which is unrealistic for current LLMs.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Uncertainty Quantification (UQ)**
  - *Why needed here:* The paper’s core is evaluating UQ methods; understanding that uncertainty is a measure of prediction reliability is essential.
  - *Quick check question:* What is the difference between aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty, and which type do repetition-based UQ methods primarily target?

- **Concept: LLM-as-a-Judge**
  - *Why needed here:* The benchmark assumes LLMs are used as automated graders; this paradigm defines the input/output structure and prompts.
  - *Quick check question:* How does chain-of-thought prompting change the nature of the LLM’s output and thus the UQ methods applied?

- **Concept: Ordinal Classification**
  - *Why needed here:* Grades are ordinal (e.g., 0–3), not purely categorical; metrics like AUROC (binary) vs. C-index (ordinal) must be chosen accordingly.
  - *Quick check question:* Why is mean squared error inappropriate for ordinal grade spaces, and how does C-index handle ordinality?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** 
1. Input Processor: formats question, rubric, student answer into a prompt.
2. LLM Grader: repeatedly samples grading responses (label + rationale) for each input.
3. UQ Module: computes uncertainty scores either categorically (from label frequencies) or relationally (from semantic graphs over responses).
4. Evaluation Engine: calculates effectiveness (AUROC, AUARC, AUERC), stability (delta, Spearmanr), and correlation metrics.
5. Decision Layer: uses uncertainty thresholds to flag responses for human review or abstain.

**Critical path:** Input → prompt → repeated LLM generations → extract labels/rationales → UQ computation → uncertainty ranking → downstream action (e.g., reject high-uncertainty grades).

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Effectiveness vs. Stability: Categorical methods (CE, FSD) give better discrimination but are sample-sensitive; relation methods (NLI_NAD, JS_GE) are more stable but less discriminative.
- Computational cost: Relation methods require semantic similarity computations (embeddings/NLI) over all response pairs, O(N²) scaling.
- Model dependency: Categorical methods work better for lower-capability models; relation methods require high-quality, coherent rationales.

**Failure signatures:**
- High AUROC but low stability: Uncertainty ranking is good on average but varies wildly with few samples (e.g., CE with N=5).
- Low AUROC but high stability: Uncertainty estimates are consistent but fail to separate correct/incorrect (e.g., some relation methods on hard questions).
- Correlation collapse: Categorical and relation methods diverge significantly, indicating they capture different uncertainty aspects; relying on one alone may miss important signals.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Baseline comparison: Run all UQ methods on a held-out dataset (e.g., ASAP) with a fixed LLM (e.g., GPT-4o mini) and few-shot CoT, report overall ranks in AUROC, AUARC, AUERC.
2. Stability curve: For a selected method from each category (e.g., CE and JS_NAD), incrementally increase sample size N from 2 to 20, plot uncertainty value change and Spearman correlation vs. N to visualize stability.
3. Cross-model robustness: Repeat Experiment 1 across multiple LLMs (e.g., stablelm-2-1.6b and gemini-3-flash) and examine how method rankings shift; identify if model capability moderates the effectiveness-stability trade-off.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How does underlying model performance influence the relative effectiveness of categorical vs. relation-based UQ methods?** The paper finds categorical methods dominate for low-performing models, while the trend reverses for high-performing ones, but the threshold and causality remain unclear.
2. **What question characteristics drive variability in metric rankings?** Question identity causes the largest variance, but which features (domain, grade scale, rubric design) are responsible is unknown.
3. **How can relation graph construction be improved for high-performing LLMs?** Current similarity measures (Jaccard, embeddings, NLI) are sensitive; more advanced equivalence judgments or reasoning-step graphs might help.
4. **Can ordinal-aware uncertainty metrics outperform categorical ones?** Grades are ordinal, yet all evaluated methods ignore ordering; incorporating ordinal structure may improve discrimination.

## Limitations
- Undisclosed prompt templates and few-shot examples hinder exact replication.
- Unspecified details for relation-graph construction (e.g., embedding models, NLI thresholds, sentence splitting) create ambiguity.
- Inaccessibility of private datasets restricts evaluation to open-source ones, potentially biasing conclusions.

## Confidence
- **Categorical effectiveness (Mechanism 1):** Medium (strong internal evidence but untested on non-categorical outputs).
- **Relation stability (Mechanism 2):** Medium (supported by stability metrics but dependent on unspecified semantic similarity choices).
- **Effectiveness-stability trade-off (Mechanism 3):** Medium (consistently observed but may not generalize beyond evaluated datasets/models).

## Next Checks
1. Prompt replication: Implement and compare results using the paper’s described prompting strategies on open datasets (ASAP, SemEval) to verify categorical method rankings.
2. Graph parameter sensitivity: Systematically vary relation-based method parameters (e.g., NLI entailment threshold, embedding model) to assess stability robustness and identify optimal configurations.
3. Assumption stress test: Deploy categorical methods on free-form grade outputs (no fixed label set) and relation methods on corrupted reasoning traces; measure performance drop to confirm break conditions.
