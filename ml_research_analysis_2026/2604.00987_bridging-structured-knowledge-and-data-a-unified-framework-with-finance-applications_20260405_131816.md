# Bridging Structured Knowledge and Data: A Unified Framework with Finance Applications

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2604.00987
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00987
- **Reference count:** 14
- **Primary result:** SKINNs reduce out-of-sample RMSE by 10–15% relative to state-of-the-art neural benchmarks and significantly outperform classical structural models at longer horizons and during high volatility.

## Executive Summary
We address the problem of effectively combining structured economic knowledge (e.g., option pricing formulas) with flexible neural approximations. The SKINN framework embeds any differentiable knowledge representation as a constraint within a joint loss, simultaneously optimizing neural parameters and interpretable structural parameters via gradient-based M-estimation. Applied to S&P 500 option pricing from 1996 to 2022, SKINNs deliver substantial out-of-sample accuracy gains (10–15% RMSE reduction) and improved hedging performance, especially under distributional shift and high volatility. Theoretically, SKINNs are shown to be consistent, asymptotically normal estimators with robust standard errors, and they recover meaningful structural parameters even when the theoretical model is misspecified. The approach unifies a spectrum of knowledge formats—from analytic formulas to deep surrogates—within a single statistically grounded architecture.

## Method Summary
At the core of SKINNs is a composite objective that balances fidelity to observed data with adherence to structured knowledge. For a dataset of observed inputs \(X_{\text{obs}}\) and targets \(y_{\text{obs}}\), a neural network \(f_\theta\) learns to predict \(y\). A structured module \(g_\phi\)—which can be a parametric formula, a pre-trained differentiable surrogate, or a non‑parametric distribution—provides theoretically consistent predictions for a possibly different set of inputs \(X_{\text{SK}}\). To enforce the knowledge, collocation points \(X_{\text{colloc}}\) are drawn from the valid input domain, and the loss penalizes discrepancies between \(f_\theta(X_{\text{colloc}})\) and \(g_\phi(X_{\text{SK, colloc}})\). The total loss is
\[
L_{\text{total}} = \frac{1}{N_{\text{obs}}}\sum (f_\theta(X_{\text{obs}})-y_{\text{obs}})^2 + \lambda \frac{1}{N_{\text{colloc}}}\sum (f_\theta(X_{\text{colloc}})-g_\phi(X_{\text{SK, colloc}}))^2,
\]
where \(\lambda\) controls the strength of the theoretical constraint. Both \(\theta\) and \(\phi\) are updated jointly via automatic differentiation, placing the method within the M‑estimation (generalized method of moments) framework. This joint optimization avoids the pitfalls of two‑stage calibration and leverages the neural network to capture residuals that the structured model cannot explain.

## Key Results
- SKINNs achieve 10–15% lower out-of-sample RMSE compared to state‑of‑the‑art neural benchmarks and significantly outperform classical structural models at longer horizons and in volatile regimes.
- Hedging errors (mean hedging error) are consistently lower across multiple tests.
- The estimators are theoretically sound: consistent, \(\sqrt{N}\)-asymptotically normal with robust sandwich covariance, and recover pseudo‑true parameters under misspecification; learned structural parameters exhibit economic coherence and improved numerical stability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Joint co‑optimization of neural parameters (\(\theta\)) and structured‑knowledge parameters (\(\phi\)) avoids misspecification of either component and yields improved out‑of‑sample performance while preserving interpretability.
- **Mechanism:** Instead of pre‑calibrating \(\phi\) or using a two‑stage pipeline, SKINNs simultaneously minimize the data‑fidelity loss on observed samples and a theory‑consistency loss on collocation points. Gradients from both losses flow to \(\theta\) and \(\phi\) via automatic differentiation, allowing \(f_\theta\) to capture data‑driven residuals while \(\phi\) adjusts to align the theoretical model \(g_\phi\) with the data.
- **Core assumption:** The composite loss has a unique minimizer (identification) and \(g_\phi\) is continuously differentiable with respect to \(\phi\), enabling gradient‑based joint optimization.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract: “SKINNs jointly estimate neural network parameters and economically meaningful structural parameters in a single optimization problem.” Section 2.4: “The key innovation of SKINNs is the simultaneous, gradient‑based discovery of both parameter sets.” Appendix C.2: Theorems 1 and 2 provide consistency and asymptotic normality for the joint estimator.
- **Break condition:** Violations of differentiability (e.g., non‑smooth \(g_\phi\)), severe non‑convexity leading to poor local minima, or failure of identification (non‑uniqueness) prevent proper joint learning.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Enforcing theoretical consistency over a broader domain via collocation points improves robustness to distributional shifts, especially for longer‑horizon forecasts and volatile regimes.
- **Mechanism:** The structured‑knowledge loss is evaluated on a set of collocation inputs \(X_{\text{colloc}}\) that can be sampled from the full valid input space, not merely from observed data. This encourages \(f_\theta\) to agree with \(g_\phi\) even in regions where training data are sparse. If \(g_\phi\) represents stable economic principles (e.g., no‑arbitrage), this alignment transfers to new environments where the marginal of \(X\) differs.
- **Core assumption:** The collocation distribution approximates the target marginal of \(X\) and the theoretical model \(g_\phi\) is portable (its assumptions hold under the shift).
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract: “enforcing theoretical consistency not only on observed data but over a broader input domain through collocation… improve out‑of‑sample valuation and hedging performance, particularly at longer horizons and during high‑volatility regimes.” Section 2.3 describes the structured‑knowledge component. Appendix C.5 formalizes the stability and target‑risk decomposition that explain robustness under domain shift.
- **Break condition:** Poor choice of collocation distribution (e.g., does not cover target \(X\)-support) or non‑portable \(g_\phi\) can make the collocation loss harmful. Over‑weighting collocation (\(\lambda\) too large) may force \(f_\theta\) toward a misspecified \(g_\phi\) in irrelevant regions.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The SKINNs M‑estimation framework unifies diverse knowledge‑injection approaches, supports high‑dimensional structural parameter recovery, and provides statistical guarantees (consistency, asymptotic normality, efficiency) even when the theory is misspecified.
- **Mechanism:** The composite objective can be viewed as a weighted sum of squared‑error moment conditions (data and theory), placing SKINNs within the generalized method of moments (GMM) family. Under standard regularity conditions, the resulting estimator is a consistent M‑estimator with \(\sqrt{N}\) convergence, sandwich covariance, and identification of \(\phi\) via profiling. The flexibility of \(f_\theta\) (universal approximator) handles nuisance, while the structured module \(g_\phi\) (differentiable) allows estimation of \(\phi\) even in high dimensions.
- **Core assumption:** \(g_\phi\) is differentiable, the parameter spaces are compact, and the moment functions satisfy identification (unique zero of the population moments). For efficiency, orthogonal moment conditions may be imposed.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract: “SKINNs define a class of M‑estimators that are consistent and asymptotically normal with \(\sqrt{N}\) convergence, sandwich covariance, and recovery of pseudo‑true parameters under misspecification. We establish identification of structural parameters under joint flexibility.” Section 2.2 defines three knowledge formats covering analytic formulas, deep surrogates, and non‑parametric distributions. Appendix C.4 shows semiparametric efficiency under orthogonal moments. Section 5.2 empirically demonstrates interpretable high‑dimensional \(\phi\).
- **Break condition:** If identification fails (e.g., \(g_\phi\) too flexible or data uninformative), \(\phi\) is not uniquely recovered. High‑dimensional \(\phi\) may require many collocation points; insufficient points cause overfitting. Non‑differentiability of \(g_\phi\) breaks the gradient flow.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:  
Inputs: observed data \((X_{\text{obs}}, y_{\text{obs}})\) and collocation points \(X_{\text{colloc}}\). Neural network \(f_\theta\) maps any \(X\) to a prediction. Structured module \(g_\phi\) maps \(X_{\text{SK}}\) to a theoretical output. Loss: \(L_{\text{total}} = L_{\text{data}}(f_\theta(X_{\text{obs}}), y_{\text{obs}}) + \lambda L_{\text{sk}}(f_\theta(X_{\text{colloc}}), g_\phi(X_{\text{SK, colloc}}))\). Optimizer (e.g., Adam) computes gradients w.r.t. both \(\theta\) and \(\phi\) (via backpropagation through \(g_\phi\)) and updates them.

Critical path:  
Forward pass: \(X_{\text{obs}} \xrightarrow{f_\theta} \hat{y}_{\text{obs}} \rightarrow L_{\text{data}}\); \(X_{\text{colloc}} \xrightarrow{f_\theta} \hat{y}_{\text{colloc}}\) and \(X_{\text{SK, colloc}} \xrightarrow{g_\phi} \tilde{y}_{\text{colloc}} \rightarrow L_{\text{sk}}\). Combine to \(L_{\text{total}}\). Backward pass: compute \(\nabla_\theta L_{\text{total}}\) and \(\nabla_\phi L_{\text{total}}\); update \(\theta\) and \(\phi\). Special care: for SPSKR/NPSKR, \(g_\phi\) may be a frozen deep surrogate with \(\phi\) as input, or a decoder with \(\phi\) as latent variables; gradients must flow accordingly.

Design tradeoffs:
- \(\lambda\): larger \(\lambda\) enforces theory more strongly but may underfit data; smaller \(\lambda\) relies more on data. Optimal \(\lambda\) often chosen via validation.
- Collocation design: number of points (computational cost vs. loss approximation) and distribution (should cover regions of interest, especially potential target support). Poor coverage harms generalization.
- \(g_\phi\) format: PSKR offers interpretability but limited expressivity; SPSKR handles complex theory but requires accurate offline surrogate; NPSKR scales to high‑dimensional \(\phi\) but may need many parameters and constraints.
- Neural architecture: deeper/wider nets increase capacity but risk overfitting; activation choice (ReLU vs. SiLU) affects gradient flow, especially for models involving derivatives (PINNs).
- Differentiability of \(g_\phi\): must be smooth enough for gradient‑based optimization; numerical methods (e.g., COS, FFT) are differentiable but may have issues with higher‑order derivatives.

Failure signatures:
- Exploding/vanishing gradients, especially through \(g_\phi\) or high‑order derivatives → indicates lack of smoothness or poor scaling.
- Training loss decreases but validation loss remains high → collocation distribution mismatch or \(\lambda\) too low.
- Learned \(\phi\) drifts to implausible values → \(\lambda\) too low or mis‑specified \(g_\phi\); consider adding bounds or increasing \(\lambda\).
- No convergence despite reasonable learning rate → composite loss ill‑conditioned; try loss scaling or different \(\lambda\).
- Forgetting pre‑trained surrogate knowledge if \(g_\phi\)’s weights are unfrozen and updated aggressively → use gradient clipping or lower learning rate for \(g_\phi\).

First 3 experiments:
1. **BSM sanity check:** Generate synthetic Black‑Scholes option prices with known volatility \(\sigma\), add small noise. Train SKINN+BSM (small neural net). Verify learned \(\sigma\) matches true \(\sigma\) and that out‑of‑sample RMSE improves over a plain NN of similar size.
2. **\(\lambda\) sweep:** Train SKINN+BSM with \(\lambda \in \{0.01, 0.1, 1, 10\}\). Plot validation loss, data loss, and SK loss. Identify region where both losses are balanced and generalization is best.
3. **Collocation distribution test:** Set up a scenario where test options have strikes outside the training range. Compare SKINN trained with collocation points uniform over a wide strike‑maturity range versus collocation only over observed strikes. Expect the wide‑range collocation to generalize better to out‑of‑support strikes.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Does SKINN asymptotic theory apply when network size grows with sample size?**  
   The paper notes the fixed‑\(p\) assumption; extending to growing width/depth would require additional regularity conditions and is left for future work.

2. **How to choose \(\lambda\) in SKINNs for complex high‑dimensional tasks without ad‑hoc tuning?**  
   Only a simple two‑signal model yields a closed‑form \(\lambda\); real applications rely on validation. A principled, data‑driven method (e.g., via GMM or cross‑validation) is needed.

3. **Is \(\phi\) identifiable when \(f_\theta\) is flexible enough to mimic \(g_\phi\)?**  
   Proposition 1 gives a sufficient condition (unique minimizer of expected squared error) but does not address flexibility or misspecification. Identifiability conditions under joint estimation require further analysis.

## Limitations
- Key implementation details (collocation sampling strategy, exact hyperparameters, pre‑training procedures for deep surrogates, autoencoder architecture, and data preprocessing) are not fully specified, hindering exact replication.
- Theoretical assumptions (differentiability, identification, compactness) may be violated in complex real‑world settings; thus the statistical guarantees might not fully hold in practice.
- Evidence is limited to the paper itself; no external validation or independent experiments are provided to corroborate the claims.
