# On sparsity, extremal structure, and monotonicity properties of Wasserstein and Gromov-Wasserstein optimal transport plans

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.16265
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16265
- **Reference count**: 4
- **Primary result**: When the Gromov-Wasserstein loss is conditionally negative semi‑definite, there exists an optimal transport plan that is sparse (support ≤ n+m−1), the coupling relaxation is tight for uniform weights (an optimal permutation exists), and the quadratic GW problem is equivalent to a bilinear relaxation.

## Executive Summary
This paper studies the structural properties of Gromov-Wasserstein (GW) optimal transport plans, focusing on sparsity, extremality, and monotonicity in comparison to classical linear optimal transport. The core methodology introduces the conditionally negative semi‑definite (CND) property of the 4‑dimensional GW tensor. Under the CND condition—which holds for common separable losses like the squared‑Euclidean distance—the GW objective becomes concave over the coupling polytope. This concavity guarantees that an optimal solution can be chosen as an extreme point of the polytope. Consequently, the support of an optimal plan contains at most n+m−1 entries, and for uniform marginal distributions the extreme points are precisely permutation matrices, making the linear relaxation tight. Moreover, the GW quadratic problem is shown to be equivalent to a bilinear relaxation, extending key linear OT results to the GW setting.

## Method Summary
The experimental validation plan uses synthetic datasets to test the three theoretical properties under the CND condition. Point clouds in ℝ^d generate squared Euclidean distance matrices, which are conditionally positive definite and thus satisfy CND. With uniform marginals a=b=1/n, the GW objective is computed via the POT library and compared to an exhaustive enumeration of permutation matrices (for n=3). The sparse support bound is implicitly verified by the permutation’s size. The bilinear relaxation equivalence is assessed by enumerating all pairs of permutations and checking that the minimum bilinear value matches the GW optimum. CND is explicitly confirmed by checking that centered matrices (HCH with H=I-11ᵀ/n) have no negative eigenvalues. This plan directly validates the claims for small instances where enumeration is feasible.

## Key Results
- **Sparsity**: There exists an optimal GW plan with at most n+m−1 non‑zero entries.
- **Permutation optimality**: For uniform marginals, an optimal permutation matrix exists; the coupling relaxation is tight.
- **Bilinear equivalence**: Under CND, the GW quadratic optimization over couplings equals the bilinear optimization over two coupled permutations/couplings.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: If the 4D tensor L defining the GW problem is conditionally negative semi‑definite (CND) with respect to the set of differences of couplings Π, then the GW objective f(P)=⟨L⊗P,P⟩ is concave over the coupling polytope Π(a,b).
- **Mechanism**: Lemma 3.2 shows that f is concave iff for all Q∈Π we have ⟨L⊗Q,Q⟩≤0, i.e., the CND condition. The proof expands f((P1+P2)/2) and compares to (f(P1)+f(P2))/2, leading to the term ⟨L⊗Q,Q⟩. Thus CND directly enforces the midpoint inequality.
- **Core assumption**: L is symmetric and satisfies the CND property: ∀Q∈Π, ⟨L⊗Q,Q⟩≤0. Π is the linear space Π(a,b)−Π(a,b).
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: The abstract states “I present the conditionally negative semi‑definite property and show that, when it holds, there are GW optimal plans that are sparse and supported on a permutation.”
  - [section]: Lemma 3.2 and the preceding discussion in Section 3.1 formally define CND and prove the equivalence with concavity.
  - [corpus]: The neighboring paper “Semidefinite Relaxations of the Gromov-Wasserstein Distance” (arXiv:2312.14572) also investigates conditions for tractable GW, though via SDP relaxations rather than concavity; the overlap suggests CND is a recognized sufficient condition.
- **Break condition**: If L is not CND, the objective may not be concave, and the guarantee of an extreme‑point optimum no longer follows; different analysis or approximations are needed.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: For any continuous concave function on a compact convex polytope, an optimal solution can be chosen to be an extreme point of the polytope.
- **Mechanism**: This is Bauer’s minimum principle. Every point in a polytope can be expressed as a convex combination of its extreme points, so Jensen’s inequality for concave functions implies that some extreme point attains the minimum. The paper gives a constructive argument in Proposition 3.3, splitting non‑extreme couplings along cycles until extreme points are reached.
- **Core assumption**: f is continuous and concave on Π(a,b); Π(a,b) is a nonempty compact convex polytope that is the convex hull of its extreme points.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: The abstract’s conclusion that optimal sparse plans exist under CND implicitly uses this mechanism.
  - [section]: Section 3.2 details the argument, deriving Corollary 3.4 from CND‑concavity and the extreme‑point decomposition.
  - [corpus]: The paper “Fused Partial Gromov‑Wasserstein for Structured Objects” (arXiv:2502.09934) combines feature and structure losses but does not rely on extreme‑point arguments; the connection is tangential.
- **Break condition**: Concavity is essential; if f is non‑concave (e.g., if CND fails), the minimum might be strictly inside the polytope and not attained at any extreme point.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Extreme points of the coupling polytope Π(a,b) correspond to transport plans whose bipartite support graph contains no cycle. Consequently, any extreme point has at most n+m−1 nonzero entries (sparsity). For uniform weights (a=b=1/n 1), extreme points are exactly permutation matrices (Birkhoff’s theorem), yielding a tight relaxation of the GW quadratic problem to a permutation search.
- **Mechanism**: Proposition 2.2 proves that a coupling is extreme iff its support graph is cycle‑free; any cycle allows a perturbation that splits the coupling, violating extremality. A cycle‑free bipartite graph on n+m vertices has at most n+m−1 edges (Proposition 2.3). For uniform weights, Theorem 2.4 adapts Birkhoff’s theorem to show extreme points are permutation matrices.
- **Core assumption**: The definition of Π(a,b) via linear marginal constraints. For the permutation result, additionally n=m and a=b uniform.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section]: Sections 2.2 (Propositions 2.2, 2.3) and 2.3 (Theorem 2.4) contain full proofs.
  - [abstract]: The abstract explicitly lists (1) sparsity ≤ n+m−1 and (2) tightness for uniform weights (permutation existence) as consequences of CND.
  - [corpus]: The work “Unsupervised Learning for Optimal Transport plan prediction between unbalanced graphs” (arXiv:2506.12025) aims to predict sparse transport plans and cites the n+m−1 bound, showing practical relevance.
- **Break condition**: If the optimal solution is not extreme (e.g., due to loss of concavity), its support may contain cycles and be larger than n+m−1. The permutation guarantee also requires uniform weights; with non‑uniform marginals, extreme points are sparse but not permutations.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Coupling polytope Π(a,b)**
  - **Why needed here**: The set of feasible transport plans is the domain of the GW problem; its geometric properties (extreme points, sparsity) are central to the paper’s results.
  - **Quick check question**: What linear constraints define Π(a,b)? (Answer: P ≥ 0, P 1_m = a, P^T 1_n = b.)
- **Concept: Conditionally negative semi‑definite (CND) matrices**
  - **Why needed here**: The CND property of L (or of the intra‑structure matrices) is the key sufficient condition that guarantees concavity of the GW objective, which in turn enables sparse and permutation optima.
  - **Quick check question**: Given a symmetric matrix C with zero diagonal, how can you test whether it is CND? (Answer: Verify that u^T C u ≤ 0 for all u with sum(u)=0, or equivalently that the centered matrix H C H is negative semidefinite.)
- **Concept: Extreme points of convex polytopes**
  - **Why needed here**: The paper’s sparsity and permutation results rely on the existence of an optimal extreme point (via concavity) and on the combinatorial characterization of extreme points of Π(a,b) as cycle‑free supports.
  - **Quick check question**: Is every point in a convex polytope expressible as a convex combination of extreme points? (Answer: Yes, by definition of a polytope as the convex hull of its extreme points.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Data input → Tensor construction → CND verification → Solver selection → Solution extraction → Validation
- **Critical path**:
  1. Understand marginals and compute intra‑structure matrices.
  2. Choose loss L; if separable, compute h1(C) and h2(C̄) and test conditional definiteness.
  3. Decide solver mode based on CND.
  4. Execute solver (e.g., alternate linear OT subproblems for BilinOT).
  5. Post‑process: check support size and permutation property when expected.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - **Exactness vs. scalability**: The CND→bilinear route gives exact solutions with potentially lower cost, but CND verification is O(n^3) for matrix checks; for very large n, approximations (e.g., entropic regularization) may be necessary despite losing sparsity guarantees.
  - **Sparsity enforcement**: Even with CND, numerical solvers might return dense plans; one can incorporate a support size constraint or use algorithms that maintain acyclic supports (e.g., network simplex variants).
  - **Uniform weights**: Permutation solutions are ideal for exact alignment but require a=b uniform; non‑uniform marginals still yield sparse extreme points but not permutations, and the bilinear relaxation may not be tight.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Dense optimal plan with support size ≫ n+m−1 when CND should hold → bug in solver or failure to reach an extreme point.
  - Bilinear objective strictly lower than quadratic objective → violation of CND (or numeric precision).
  - Solver produces negative entries or fails to converge → constraint implementation error.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Small‑scale verification**: n=m=5, uniform weights, squared‑Euclidean C and C̄. Solve both (QuadOT) and (BilinOT); confirm equal objectives, permutation plan, and |supp| ≤ 9.
  2. **Non‑CND case**: Use L(a,b)=|a−b| (generally not CND). Observe bilinear objective lower and quadratic optimum likely dense, illustrating necessity of CND for tight relaxation.
  3. **Scalability test**: n=m=200 with squared‑Euclidean CND case. Compare runtime of bilinear algorithm (alternating linear OT) vs. a generic quadratic solver (e.g., with entropic regularization). Show bilinear scales better while maintaining sparsity.

## Limitations
- CND verification scales cubically; large graphs may require approximate tests.
- GW solvers are non‑convex; even with CND they may return suboptimal dense solutions.
- Permutation guarantee requires uniform marginals; non‑uniform cases only ensure sparsity.

## Confidence
- Sparsity bound (≤ n+m−1): High
- Permutation existence under uniform weights: Medium‑High
- Equivalence to bilinear relaxation: Medium
