# Improving Ensemble Forecasts of Abnormally Deflecting Tropical Cyclones with Fused Atmosphere-Ocean-Terrain Data
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.29200
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.29200
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Achieved state-of-the-art track errors for abnormally deflecting tropical cyclones: 47.39 km at 12 hours and 93.29 km at 24 hours.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the challenge of forecasting abnormally deflecting tropical cyclones (TCs) with deep learning, which traditionally struggles with multimodal atmosphere-ocean-terrain data. The authors introduce the AOT-TCs dataset, a comprehensive multimodal record spanning 1950–2024 that integrates atmospheric (ERA5), oceanic (CODCv1), terrain (GEBCO), and teleconnection indices (Niño3.4). They also propose AOT-TCNet, a novel model featuring explicit modality-specific encoders, a coupling fusion module, and a mode-adaptive mixture-of-experts (TMA-MoE) that learns distinct track sub-modes. Evaluated on Northwest Pacific abnormally deflecting TCs (2017–2024), AOT-TCNet reduces 12-hour track error to 47.39 km and 24-hour error to 93.29 km—both state-of-the-art—and also improves intensity forecasts. The results demonstrate that explicitly modeling terrain effects and adaptively routing to specialized experts can significantly advance extreme-event TC prediction.

## Method Summary
AOT-TCNet processes six input modalities: TC trajectory history (lon/lat/pressure/wind), 3D atmospheric fields (u/v/geopotential at four pressure levels), 2D sea surface temperature/salinity, terrain elevation, and scalar indices (speed, direction, variability, month, Niño3.4). Each modality is encoded separately: an LSTM for trajectory, a 3D-UNet for atmospheric grids, and a two-layer MLP for ocean, terrain, and scalars. The encoded features are concatenated and passed through a fully connected layer to produce a fused representation. This representation feeds into the TMA-MoE router, a three-layer MLP that selects one of K expert networks (each an independent decoder) based on a matching score between predicted and ground-truth trajectories. The selected expert outputs the forecasted trajectory. Training uses a composite loss combining Huber track loss, atmospheric reconstruction MSE, a GAN-style distribution contrastive loss via a discriminator, and a mode classification loss that maximizes Jensen-Shannon divergence among experts to encourage specialization. The model is trained end-to-end on the AOT-TCs dataset, which aligns all data to 6-hourly intervals and constructs sequences of 8 past steps to predict 4 future steps (24 hours). Hyperparameters include Adam optimizer (lr=1e-4, batch=128, 300 epochs) and a mode classification weight β=0.1 (assumed).

## Key Results
- Track errors for abnormally deflecting TCs: 12-hour 47.39 km, 24-hour 93.29 km, setting new state-of-the-art.
- Intensity forecasts (pressure and wind speed) also show consistent improvement over prior deep learning methods.
- The approach demonstrates a breakthrough in handling complex, extreme TC behavior by fusing multimodal physical data and adaptive expert selection.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Three key mechanisms underpin the success of AOT-TCNet.

**Explicit atmosphere-ocean-terrain coupling** integrates terrain elevation (GEBCO) as a separate modality alongside atmospheric and oceanic data. The 3D-UNet and MLP layers learn cross-domain interactions, such as how mountainous terrain disrupts low-level circulation (e.g., Venturi effect), which is critical for simulating abrupt deflections absent in atmospheric-only models. Ablation studies (Table IV) show that adding terrain variables reduces errors, supporting the assumption that terrain-induced processes are learnable from gridded elevation.

**TMA-MoE mode-adaptive expert selection** decomposes the diverse track distribution into specialized experts (e.g., for straight, recurving, or sharply turning patterns). A router network assigns each TC to an expert based on trajectory matching, while a mode classification loss enforces separation via Jensen-Shannon divergence. This prevents dominant average patterns from drowning out rare abnormal events, aligning with the assumption that track modes are discrete and tied to environmental steering flows. Qualitative evidence (Section IV.B) indicates that modeling distinct modes alleviates instability from mode mixing.

**Comprehensive multimodal dataset** AOT-TCs provides long-term, multi-source data that captures coupled processes from large-scale drivers (ENSO) to local terrain effects. Including non-spatial indices like Niño3.4 and month encodes seasonal teleconnections. The dataset’s heterogeneity exposes the model to the full causal chain, improving generalization. Ablation results (Table IV) confirm that adding ocean and terrain variables consistently lowers errors, underscoring the value of integrated physical priors.

## Foundational Learning
- **Spatiotemporal multimodal fusion**: Needed to combine 2D/3D grids (atmospheric winds, terrain), 1D time-series (TC track), and scalars (Niño3.4) into one representation. Quick check: Why separate encoders (LSTM, 3D-UNet, MLP) instead of a single transformer? They preserve modality-specific physics (e.g., spatial hierarchies vs. sequential dynamics).
- **Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) routing**: Required to assign TCs to mode-specific experts; hard routing (top-1) avoids averaging across dissimilar patterns. Quick check: How does the routing loss differ from cross-entropy? It minimizes divergence between routing distribution π and data-driven posterior R, not a fixed label.
- **TC deflection meteorology**: Understanding abrupt turns (e.g., mid-latitude troughs, terrain blocking) is crucial for diagnosing model failures and interpreting expert specializations. Quick check: What causes the “Venturi effect” in Taiwan’s Central Mountain Range? Orographic lifting accelerates winds through mountain gaps, altering low-level steering flow.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: TC history → LSTM encoder; Atmospheric grids → 3D-UNet encoder; Ocean/terrain/scalars → MLP encoder; All embeddings → concatenation → fully connected layer → TMA-MoE router (3-layer MLP) → selected expert (decoder) → trajectory output. Additionally, atmospheric reconstruction uses a decoder from the 3D-UNet bottleneck, and a discriminator provides distribution contrastive loss.

Critical path: Input data are processed by modality-specific encoders to yield embeddings. These are fused into a vector c. The router computes routing scores and selects one expert. The chosen expert generates the forecast. The whole pipeline is trained end-to-end with a joint loss combining track, reconstruction, adversarial, and mode classification terms.

Design tradeoffs:
- Hard routing (k=1) simplifies training but may mishandle TCs with mixed-mode characteristics.
- High-resolution terrain (GEBCO ~500m) increases input size; the paper uses a 25° spatial window but downsampling is unspecified.
- Four pressure layers (200/500/850/1000 hPa) balance vertical coverage and memory; deeper models could capture tropopause effects but add parameters.

Failure signatures:
- Experts not specializing: monitor routing entropy and inter-expert JSD; low values indicate collapse.
- Intensity errors despite good tracks: high atmospheric reconstruction loss suggests encoder fails to capture thermodynamics.
- Over-smoothing ensemble: all experts produce similar tracks, likely due to insufficient mode classification loss weight.

First 3 experiments:
1. Train baseline with only atmospheric data (no ocean/terrain, no TMA-MoE) to establish single-modality performance.
2. Add ocean variables (SST/SSS) only; verify intensity improvements without terrain.
3. Enable full AOT-TCNet with TMA-MoE; visualize expert-specific track clusters and check router assignments on known abnormal cases (e.g., Typhoon GEIMI 2024).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Basin generalization**: How well does AOT-TCNet perform on other basins (e.g., Atlantic, Eastern Pacific) given the exclusive focus on the Northwest Pacific?
2. **Optimal number of experts**: What is the ideal count of experts in TMA-MoE to balance accuracy and computational cost?
3. **Expert interpretability**: Do experts learn physically distinct track patterns (e.g., straight, recurving, sharply turning) that align with meteorological regimes?
4. **Medium-range extension**: Can the model scale to 48–72 hour forecasts without significant degradation relative to NWP models?

## Limitations
- Replication is hindered by missing details: number of TMA-MoE experts, expert architecture, discriminator design, preprocessing steps, and exact loss weights.
- Claims about terrain coupling and MoE specialization rely on indirect evidence (ablation gains, qualitative statements); physical verification and quantitative routing metrics are lacking.
- Evaluation is restricted to a narrow definition of abnormal deflections (turning angle thresholds); other extreme behaviors (e.g., rapid intensification) are not addressed.

## Confidence
- Terrain coupling improves abnormal track forecasts: Medium
- TMA-MoE alleviates mode collapse: Medium
- AOT-TCs dataset enables robustness: Low

## Next Checks
1. Terrain ablation: Retrain without terrain (AOT → AO) on abnormal TCs; confirm error increase exceeds 5% to validate terrain's contribution.
2. Expert specialization audit: Compute per-expert routing entropy and track-mode JSD on test set; inter-expert JSD <0.1 indicates failure to specialize.
3. Abnormal subset balance: Verify proportion of abnormal TCs in training set; imbalance below 5% could artificially inflate TMA-MoE gains.
