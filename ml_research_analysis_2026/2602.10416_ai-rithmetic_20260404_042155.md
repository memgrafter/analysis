# AI-rithmetic
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.10416
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10416
- Reference count: 9
- Primary result: Frontier LLMs fail at basic integer addition, with errors dominated by operand misalignment and close carry failures, and periodic accuracy drops linked to tokenization.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates why state-of-the-art language models struggle with elementary integer addition despite proficiency in advanced mathematics. By systematically evaluating several frontier models on two-number addition problems ranging from 1 to 100 digits, the authors find a steady degradation in accuracy with length. Two error types—operand misalignment and close carry mistakes—account for the vast majority of failures across all models. Misalignment correlates with tokenization schemes, particularly periodic dips when operand lengths align unfavorably with multi-digit token boundaries. Close carry errors behave like independent random events, consistent with a geometric model. Even models equipped with code execution tools err due to improper tool invocation or result transcription. The study reveals fundamental limitations in how LLMs process numeric tasks and suggests that current architectures and training objectives do not capture the algorithmic nature of arithmetic.

## Method Summary
The authors created a dataset of 10,000 addition problems: for each digit length d from 1 to 100, they generated 100 random pairs of d-digit integers. Each model was prompted with “What is A+B? Write just the answer.” and the raw text response was recorded. To evaluate correctness, they extracted all digit-only substrings from the response, chose the one with minimal Levenshtein distance to the true sum, and checked for exact match. Incorrect responses were classified into four categories: runaway (excessively long answers), misalignment (systematic offset of digits), close carry (error on columns with operand sum 9 or 10), or other. Aggregate statistics and error breakdowns were computed per length. Additional analyses included discrete Fourier transforms to detect periodic patterns linked to tokenization and verification of geometric distributions for close carry errors.

## Key Results
- All tested models show significant accuracy decay as the number of digits increases, with no strict monotonic trend.
- Misalignment and close carry errors dominate, explaining 87.9% (Claude Opus 4.1), 62.9% (GPT-5), and 92.4% (Gemini 2.5 Pro) of failures.
- GPT models exhibit periodic accuracy drops at lengths that are multiples of three, consistent with their use of 3-digit token chunks; models with single-digit tokenizers show fewer periodic effects but more misalignment overall.
- Even models with code execution tools make errors when they fail to invoke the tool correctly or mis-copy the result.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Tokenization-Induced Misalignment
Multi-digit tokenization (e.g., grouping digits in threes) creates boundaries that misalign the model’s internal positional encoding with the columnar structure of addition. As a result, one operand may be shifted left or right by 1 or 3 digits, producing a consistent offset error. Evidence: periodic accuracy dips at lengths congruent to 1 or 2 mod 3 for GPT models, and a Fourier analysis spike at frequency 1/3. The effect is less severe for single-digit tokenizers, though they still exhibit some misalignment.

### Mechanism 2: Independent Random Close Carry Failures
For addition columns where the sum of the two operand digits equals 9 or 10 (“close carry”), models make mistakes with a roughly constant probability p, independent across positions. The overall correctness for a problem with n close carries follows (1-p)^n, matching a geometric distribution in empirical tests. This suggests that the model does not maintain a reliable carry propagation state but instead decides locally per column.

### Mechanism 3: Tool-Augmented Execution Breakdowns
When provided with code execution tools, models still fail if they do not correctly invoke the tool (e.g., missing call, syntactic error) or if they incorrectly transcribe the tool’s output into the final answer. The brittleness arises because tool use relies on few-shot or prompted patterns that have not been robustly internalized; each step—decision, input formulation, output parsing—is a potential failure point.

## Foundational Learning
### Tokenization & Subword Units
Understanding how numbers are split into tokens (single digits vs. multi-digit chunks) is essential because token boundaries affect positional alignment and can cause systematic misalignment errors. In a 3-digit tokenizer, a 10-digit number yields 4 tokens (three 3-digit tokens and one 1-digit token), while a 12-digit number yields exactly 4 tokens; the varying alignment explains periodic accuracy drops.

Quick check: If a tokenizer groups digits in 3-digit chunks, how many tokens represent a 10-digit number? A 12-digit number? How might this affect the model's positional alignment?

### Long Addition Algorithm & Carry Propagation
The standard addition algorithm proceeds column-wise from right to left, summing digits and propagating carries. A “close carry” occurs when the digit sum is 9 (carry if sum+carry >=10) or 10 (always carry). Understanding this classification is necessary to interpret the error taxonomy.

Quick check: In adding 199 + 199, list each column sum from right to left. Which columns involve a close carry?

### Geometric Distribution & Independence Modeling
The probability that a model correctly handles all n close carries in a problem is (1-p)^n under independent Bernoulli trials with error probability p. The number of correct close carries before the first error follows a geometric distribution.

Quick check: If p = 0.2 for a single close carry, what is the probability a 5-carry problem is entirely correct? What is the expected number of correct carries before the first error?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Tokenizer → Embedding Layer → Transformer Stack → LM Head → Decoding

Critical path: Tokenization produces token IDs and position IDs; these are embedded and passed through the transformer with causal attention; the final layer’s representation is projected to logits; tokens are sampled autoregressively until a stop token.

Design tradeoffs: Finer token granularity (single digits) increases sequence length and may hinder long-range carry propagation, while coarser tokens (multi-digit) introduce alignment artifacts. Positional encodings (absolute vs. relative) interact with token boundaries and may not generalize to unseen length combinations. The next-token prediction objective does not explicitly enforce algorithmic correctness, leaving the model to infer arithmetic from distributional patterns.

Failure signatures: Periodic accuracy dips at lengths where tokenization creates trailing short chunks (e.g., lengths ≡ 1,2 mod 3 for GPT); a bimodal edit-distance histogram distinguishing misalignment (large offset) from close carry (small offset); offset histograms peaking at 1 or 3 digits depending on model family.

First 3 experiments: 1. Length sweep: measure accuracy across digit lengths 1–100. 2. Error classification: categorize every incorrect response into misalignment, close carry, runaway, other. 3. Tokenization probe: compare accuracy by length modulo 3 and apply Fourier analysis to misalignment error rate.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Why do multi-digit tokenization schemes trade off between periodic accuracy drops and overall lower misalignment error rates compared to single-digit tokenizers?**  
   The paper observes that models using 3-digit tokenizers show periodic patterns but fewer misalignment errors overall than single-digit tokenizers, yet the mechanistic reason for this trade-off remains unexplored.

2. **What causes the deviation from the independent geometric model for close carry errors, particularly the lower error rate at the least significant digit position?**  
   While close-carry errors generally follow a constant-probability independent model, several models exhibit a dip in errors at the final close carry. Possible explanations include training data biases, architectural inductive biases, or the simpler nature of the final carry, but the cause is unknown.

3. **Do misalignment and close carry errors generalize to more complex arithmetic (multi-operand addition, multiplication, division) or symbolic columnar tasks?**  
   The study focuses on two-number addition; it is unclear whether the same error modes dominate in other arithmetic contexts or if new failure modes emerge. Answering this would indicate whether the identified deficiencies are fundamental to digitwise processing.

## Limitations
- Tokenizer implementations vary across providers and versions; exact replication of results may be hindered by differences in tokenization rules or API behavior.
- Error classification relies on heuristic rules (e.g., misalignment offset range, close-carry definition) that may misattribute some failures, especially those involving carry-in propagation.
- The assumed independence of close-carry errors is not statistically validated; serial correlation could affect the geometric model fit.

## Confidence
- Long-digit accuracy collapse: High
- Misalignment and close carry dominate: Medium
- Tokenization drives misalignment: Medium
- Close-carry errors independent & constant-p: Medium
- Tool-augmentation brittleness: Medium

## Next Checks
1. **Tokenization ablation:** Force single-digit tokenization (e.g., insert spaces) on models that normally use multi-digit chunks and verify the disappearance of periodic misalignment patterns.
2. **Carry error independence test:** For a large sample of problems, examine the joint distribution of close-carry error positions using chi-square or mutual information to reject the independence hypothesis.
3. **Tool-prompt robustness grid:** Systematically vary instruction specificity and few-shot examples for code execution; measure failure rates at each stage (tool invocation, code generation, result copying) to isolate the weakest link.
