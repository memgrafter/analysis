# The Well-Tempered Classifier: Some Elementary Properties of Temperature Scaling

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.14862
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14862
- Reference count: 19
- Primary result: Temperature scaling increases classifier uncertainty monotonically, can non-monotonically affect LLM entropy when applied myopically, and is the unique linear accuracy-preserving scaler; it also admits a geometric interpretation as a KL projection onto fixed-entropy manifolds.

## Executive Summary
This paper provides a rigorous theoretical analysis of temperature scaling, a ubiquitous technique for adjusting model uncertainty. For classification, it proves that increasing temperature strictly increases uncertainty in the sense of majorization, which guarantees higher entropy. For autoregressive language models, myopic temperature scaling (applied independently at each token) can actually decrease sequence-level entropy for some temperature ranges, contradicting the common belief that higher temperature always yields more diverse outputs. The paper also shows that temperature scaling is the only linear scaler (among matrix and Dirichlet scaling) that preserves the model’s hard predictions (argmax). Finally, it offers a geometric view: tempered models are Kullback‑Leibler projections onto manifolds of fixed entropy. These results clarify the behavior of temperature scaling and inform its practical use in calibration and decoding.

## Method Summary
Temperature scaling adjusts a model’s predictive distribution by multiplying its logits (or log‑probabilities) by an inverse temperature parameter β > 0. For a classifier with logits z, the tempered distribution is p_β(y|x) = Softmax(βz). The paper analyzes this transformation theoretically. It shows that the set of tempered distributions forms a chain under majorization: as β increases (temperature decreases), the distribution becomes more concentrated. This ordering implies that entropy and all Schur‑concave measures increase monotonically as β decreases. For autoregressive language models, applying temperature myopically can lead to non‑monotonic changes in the joint entropy of generated sequences. The paper also establishes that temperature scaling is the sole linear transformation that preserves argmax predictions: any linear scaler that does not change the model’s hard decisions must be equivalent to multiplying logits by a positive scalar. Finally, it interprets temperature scaling as the information‑geometric I‑projection (minimizing KL divergence) onto the manifold of distributions with a specified entropy.

## Key Results
- For classifiers, increasing temperature strictly increases uncertainty via majorization, ensuring higher entropy and any Schur‑concave dispersion.
- For autoregressive LLMs, myopic temperature scaling can non‑monotonically affect entropy, debunking the notion that higher temperature always increases diversity.
- Temperature scaling is the unique linear scaler (among matrix and Dirichlet scaling) that preserves hard predictions (accuracy); it is also the KL projection onto fixed‑entropy manifolds.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Monotonic Uncertainty via Majorization
**Claim:** For classifiers, increasing temperature (decreasing β) strictly increases uncertainty via majorization, implying higher entropy and any Schur‑concave dispersion measure.  
**Mechanism:** Temperature scaling yields π_β = Softmax(βz). The family {π_β} for β > 0 is totally ordered by majorization: β₁ < β₂ ⇒ π_β₁ ≺ π_β₂. This means for any convex ϕ, ∑ϕ(π_β,k) is smaller for smaller β. Entropy H(π_β) = -∑π_β,k log π_β,k uses convex u log u, so H increases as β decreases.  
**Core assumption:** None beyond the formalism; holds for any discrete probability vector.  
**Break condition:** The monotonicity does not hold for autoregressive LMs when temperature is applied myopically (Section 3.4).

### Mechanism 2: KL Projection onto Fixed‑Entropy Manifolds
**Claim:** Temperature scaling is the KL projection of the original model onto the set of distributions with a given entropy.  
**Mechanism:** p_β solves min_{q:H(q)=h^*} KL(q||p). Using Lagrange multipliers, the solution must be an exponential family member with natural parameter proportional to the logits of p, i.e., q = p_β. Thus, moving along the temperature path follows the KL geodesic from p toward the uniform distribution.  
**Core assumption:** The original conditional distribution p(y|x) is unimodal and has full support for uniqueness.  
**Break condition:** If p is multimodal or has zero probabilities, the projection may not be unique.

### Mechanism 3: Uniqueness as Accuracy‑Preserving Linear Scaler
**Claim:** Temperature scaling is the only linear scaler that preserves the original model’s hard predictions (argmax).  
**Mechanism:** Consider a linear map T(z)=Wz+b. Requiring argmax(T(z)) = argmax(z) for all z forces W = βI_K + 1_K α^⊤ and b = γ1_K. When followed by softmax (as in matrix/Dirichlet scaling), the extra rank‑1 and constant terms cancel because softmax is invariant to adding a constant to all logits, yielding Softmax(βz). Hence only temperature scaling preserves argmax.  
**Core assumption:** The scaler must be linear in logits or log‑probabilities and preserve argmax for every possible input.  
**Break condition:** Nonlinear transformations can preserve argmax but lie outside the linear family.

## Foundational Learning
### Majorization
**Why needed:** Provides the partial order that captures “more dispersed” distributions, underpinning the monotonic uncertainty claim.  
**Quick check:** If π ≺ ρ, what can you say about their Rényi entropies? (Answer: All Rényi entropies are Schur‑concave, so H_α(π) ≤ H_α(ρ) for α>0.)

### Kullback‑Leibler Divergence and I‑Projection
**Why needed:** Understanding KL minimization under a constraint explains why temperature scaling is the principled way to adjust entropy while staying close to the original model.  
**Quick check:** What is the dual interpretation of min_{q:H(q)=h^*} KL(q||p)? (Answer: Maximize H(q) subject to fixed expected energy E_q[E] where E are the negative logits.)

### Argmax Invariance for Linear Maps
**Why needed:** It is the key structural result proving that temperature scaling is the sole order‑preserving linear method.  
**Quick check:** Provide a simple linear transformation that changes the argmax of some input. (Example: W = [[1,1],[0,1]], b=0; for z=(1,2), argmax(z)=2 but Wz=(3,2) has argmax 1.)

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Logits extractor -> Temperature scaler -> Calibration optimizer -> Inference module

### Critical Path
1. Assemble a calibration dataset (x_i, y_i) held out from training.
2. Compute logits z_i = f(x_i) for all examples.
3. Define loss L(β) = -(1/n) ∑ log p_β(y_i|x_i); this is convex and smooth.
4. Optimize β>0 using a bounded method (Brent, bisection) to obtain β̂.
5. Deploy: replace softmax with Softmax(β̂ z).

### Design Tradeoffs
- *Temperature vs matrix scaling*: Temperature scaling uses one parameter, is less prone to overfitting, and preserves accuracy. Matrix scaling (K²+K parameters) adds flexibility but may change predictions and often requires regularization.
- *LLM decoding*: Myopic scaling is efficient but may not control sequence‑level entropy; consider nucleus sampling or full sequence‑level temperature if possible.
- *Regularization*: For small calibration sets, add Laplace smoothing to logits or constrain β ≤ 1 to avoid extreme adjustments.

### Failure Signatures
- *Small calibration set*: L(β) becomes noisy → overfitting. Monitor L on a separate validation slice.
- *Perfect accuracy on calibration set*: L(β) strictly decreases as β grows (Proposition 1), driving β → ∞ (temperature → 0), causing overconfidence. Constrain β or regularize toward 1.
- *LLM non‑monotonicity*: Relying solely on token‑level entropy may mislead; always measure sequence‑level diversity (e.g., distinct‑n) when tuning temperature.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Classifier entropy monotonicity**: Load a pretrained classifier (e.g., ResNet‑18 on CIFAR‑10). Compute logits z_i on a validation set. For β ∈ {0.1, 0.2, …, 5.0}, compute p_β = Softmax(βz_i). Average entropy H(p_β) over samples. Plot H vs. β; should decrease monotonically.
2. **LLM myopic non‑monotonicity**: Implement the binary autoregressive model: p(x₁)=Bern(π), p(x₂|x₁)=Bern(ρ₀) if x₁=0 else Bern(ρ₁) with π=0.51, ρ₀=0.01, ρ₁=0.51. For β in logspace(−1,1,50), compute: (a) standard scaling: p_β(x₁,x₂) ∝ p(x₁)^β p(x₂|x₁)^β (normalize over 4 outcomes); (b) myopic scaling: p'_β(x₁)·p'_β(x₂|x₁) with separate temperature. Compute H(p'_β) and plot vs. β; non‑monotonicity should appear near β≈0.5.
3. **Uniqueness of temperature scaling**: For K=3, randomly generate W, b. Test argmax invariance: sample 1000 random z, check if argmax(Wz+b) equals argmax(z) (using set‑valued argmax). For pairs that pass, try to solve for β>0, α∈ℝ^K, γ∈ℝ such that W = βI + 1α^⊤ and b = γ1. Use least‑squares on constraints: w_ij = α_j for i≠j, w_ii - w_jj = α_i - α_j. Verify that only temperature‑like scalers satisfy; others fail.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Why does temperature scaling, despite being theoretically suboptimal in simple cases, empirically outperform more flexible nonlinear calibration methods for accurate classifiers?
2. Under what specific conditions does myopic temperature scaling cause non‑monotonic entropy changes in autoregressive LLMs, and how does this affect practical decoding strategies?
3. Can accuracy‑preserving nonlinear scalers achieve better calibration than temperature scaling without sacrificing the hard prediction accuracy?
4. Does the information projection interpretation of temperature scaling generalize to other linear calibration methods like matrix or Dirichlet scaling?
5. What is the impact of pretraining and fine‑tuning on the calibration of LLMs, and how does temperature scaling interact with these processes?

## Limitations
- The non‑monotonicity of myopic scaling is proven only for a toy binary model; its relevance to large‑scale LLMs remains unclear.
- The uniqueness result applies only to linear scalers; nonlinear alternatives may change predictions and are not guaranteed to preserve accuracy.
- Small calibration sets can lead to extreme β estimates (overfitting), requiring careful regularization.
