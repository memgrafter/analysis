# CREATE: Testing LLMs for Associative Creativity

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.09970
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09970
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Frontier models achieve highest creative utility, but distinctiveness remains low; iterative/resampling boosts utility, increased reasoning tokens do not help; trade-off between utility and factuality exists.

## Executive Summary
CREATE introduces a benchmark to evaluate associative creativity in large language models—the ability to find novel, meaningful connections between real-world concepts. The benchmark comprises 931 natural language queries derived from Wikidata, each asking for multiple multi‑hop paths between two entities. Paths are scored on specificity, factuality, and diversity via a creative utility function that rewards larger sets of strong, dissimilar paths. Frontier models like GPT‑5 and Gemini‑3‑Pro achieve the highest utility, but all models exhibit low distinctiveness, indicating convergence on similar associations. Iterative prompting and resampling can increase utility, while scaling reasoning token budgets generally does not improve performance. The vast search space resists saturation, suggesting substantial room for advancement in machine associative creativity.

## Method Summary
Dataset construction begins by selecting 12 relation–category pairs from Wikidata. For each pair, the class of source entities is identified, and unordered entity pairs are sampled. One triple from each pair forms the seed, which is expanded with a random outgoing edge to create a three‑triple chain. This chain is converted into a natural language query using GPT‑4o‑mini. Models are prompted to generate multiple valid paths (represented as JSON lists of triples) Connecting the given entities. Outputs are filtered for structural validity (consecutive triples must share entities). Path quality multiplies specificity (mapped from class size on a 1–5 scale) with a binary factuality flag from an LLM judge (gpt‑oss‑120b). Diversity is measured by cosine distance of string embeddings (all‑MiniLM‑L6‑v2) after annealing. The creative utility *s*(*U*) is a submodular function computed via greedy ordering with patience γ; distinctiveness ν(*U*) captures the maximum minimum distance of a path to the population. Models are evaluated under base, creative, verbalized sampling, iterative (one additional generation in context), and resampling (multiple independent runs) conditions, with reasoning token budgets varied for “thinking” models.

## Key Results
- Frontier models (GPT‑5, Gemini‑3‑Pro) achieve the highest creative utility (*s* ≈ 12 at patience 0.9).
- Iterative prompting and resampling significantly boost utility by increasing the number and diversity of valid paths.
- All models show low distinctiveness; increasing reasoning tokens often does not improve utility, indicating ineffective search. A trade‑off between utility and factuality is observed, with GPT‑5 maintaining better factuality at some cost to utility.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** The creative utility metric *s*(*U*) effectively quantifies associative creativity by balancing path quality (specificity) and set‑level diversity, making it suitable for open‑ended brainstorming tasks.  
**Mechanism:** The metric scores a set of paths via a greedy ordering: each path’s marginal utility is its quality *f*(*u*) (the specificity of its weakest triple) multiplied by a distance discount (minimum cosine distance to already selected paths). Patience γ controls the trade‑off between set size and quality—larger γ rewards generating more strong, diverse paths.  
**Core assumption:** Model‑generated paths can be captured by these proxies; that specificity (class size) and string‑embedding distance correlate with human notions of creativity.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract]: “scored more highly if they produce a larger set of strong, diverse paths.”  
- [section 3.1]: Formal definition of *s*(*U*), γ, *f*, *d*.  
- [section 7.1]: Frontier models achieve highest *s*; utility increases with patience, confirming metric alignment.  
**Break condition:** If models learn to manipulate the metric (e.g., many degenerate but diverse paths), utility may diverge from genuine creativity; Section 7.4 already shows a factuality‑utility trade‑off, hinting at such a break.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Simply allocating more reasoning tokens does not improve associative creativity on CREATE because models fail to explore the vast search space effectively; they repeat or converge prematurely.  
**Mechanism:** The benchmark’s search space is enormous, but high‑quality paths are rare. More tokens allow longer chains of thought, yet without explicit search heuristics models revisit common patterns (entities, relations) rather than discovering novel, high‑specificity connections.  
**Core assumption:** Performance is limited by search strategy, not raw compute; unguided token expansion yields diminishing returns.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [section 7.3]: “Increasing reasoning token budget doesn’t necessarily increase the number of entities explored… repetition of information partially undermines the effectiveness of this search.”  
- [section 7.1]: “Thinking models are not always more effective… even with high token budgets.”  
- [table 16]: Open‑source thinking models show similar counts of entities/relations across 16k and 32k budgets.  
**Break condition:** If models adopt systematic graph‑traversal or external memory, the negative correlation could reverse; current evidence suggests saturation in search coverage.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Iterative prompting and resampling boost creative utility primarily by increasing the number of valid and diverse paths, not by improving per‑path quality.  
**Mechanism:** Iterative prompting gives the model a chance to deliberately avoid duplicates and explore new answer regions; resampling uses temperature to produce stochastic variations. Both enlarge the path set *U*, which the metric *s*(*U*) rewards heavily at higher patience γ.  
**Core assumption:** Models can produce genuinely novel paths when instructed to differ; diversity can be scaled via simple prompting without architectural changes.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [section 6]: Describes iterative and resampling variations.  
- [section 7.1] & [table 15]: Iterate and resample yield the largest utility gains; |*U*|avg substantially increases.  
- [section 7.2]: Iterate also raises distinctiveness ν(*U*), confirming novelty enhancement.  
**Break condition:** If further iterations cause quality decay (e.g., factuality drops) or saturation (no new distinct paths), gains may plateau or reverse.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Data Curation Pipeline → Model Evaluation Harness → Metric Computation Module → Analysis & Visualization

### Critical path
1. Load query and source path.
2. Generate model outputs (multiple generations per query).
3. Parse outputs into structured paths (list of triples).
4. Filter for structural validity (consecutive entity matching).
5. Compute path quality *f*(*u*) via LLM specificity judge × factuality flag.
6. Compute pairwise distances via embeddings; apply transformed distance *d*.
7. Perform greedy ordering to compute *s*(*U*) at desired γ.
8. Compute distinctiveness ν(*U*) against population.
9. Aggregate across queries for final metrics.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Specificity estimation cost**: LLM judge is expensive but nuanced; lighter proxies may reduce fidelity.
- **Diversity metric**: Cosine on string embeddings is simple but may miss semantic novelty; more complex embeddings could improve accuracy at cost of interpretability.
- **Token budget vs. latency**: High reasoning budgets (e.g., 32k) increase evaluation time disproportionately, yet Section 7.3 shows limited benefit.
- **Factuality vs. utility**: Stricter filtering lowers *s* (Section 7.4), forcing a trade‑off between creativity and reliability.

### Failure signatures
- High proportion of structurally invalid paths → output parsing or instruction‑following issues.
- Distinctiveness ν near zero → model outputs are homogeneous (mode collapse).
- Utility rises with token budget but unique entities explored stay flat → wasted computation (Section 7.3).
- Sharp factuality drop when using iterative/resampling (e.g., verbalized) → risk of hallucination outweighing diversity gains.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Baseline replication**: Run all models on CREATE with original prompt, temperature 0.7, default budgets. Replicate Table 1 to confirm frontier model superiority.
2. **Token budget impact**: For a thinking model (e.g., GPT‑5‑mini), vary reasoning effort (low/medium/high), measure *s*, number of paths, and reasoning trace metrics; expect diminishing returns as per Section 7.3.
3. **Prompt variation effects**: Test base, creative, verbalized, iterate, and resample on one model (e.g., GPT‑5‑mini). Replicate Table 15; expect iterate/resample to boost utility and distinctiveness, while verbalized may reduce valid paths.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
**Question:** Why does increased reasoning effort (token budget) not improve associative creativity performance on CREATE, despite expectations from chain‑of‑thought reasoning?  
**Basis in paper:** The authors state: "our results illustrate that thinking models are not always more effective on our task, even with high token budgets" and "Increasing reasoning token budget doesn’t necessarily increase the number of tokens used by the model; the number of entities explored is similar."  
**Why unresolved:** The paper shows that thinking models and scaled reasoning budgets fail to boost creative utility, contradicting the common assumption that more reasoning time aids creative exploration. The underlying cause—whether due to ineffective search strategies, limited parametric knowledge, or misalignment of reasoning with the task’s creative demands—remains unexplained.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments analyzing reasoning traces (e.g., number of distinct entities/relations explored, repetition patterns) across varying token budgets; ablation studies isolating reasoning quality from quantity; comparisons of different reasoning architectures (e.g., tree‑search, Monte Carlo) to see if alternative search methods improve performance.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** Why do all evaluated models, including frontier ones, exhibit low distinctiveness (i.e., generate paths similar to the population) on CREATE?  
**Basis in paper:** The authors report: "distinctiveness remains low across all models" and in Table 2, average ν values are small (e.g., 0.02 for GPT‑5), indicating that even the most "creative" models produce outputs that are very close to existing responses.  
**Why unresolved:** Low distinctiveness suggests models converge on similar associations, possibly due to shared training data, mode collapse, or the benchmark’s design inadvertently rewarding common patterns. The paper notes this metric captures "something different than utility" but does not diagnose why diversity is persistently low.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Larger‑scale population analysis tracking distinctiveness over generations; probing whether distinctiveness correlates with model diversity during pre‑training; testing if interventions like verbalized sampling or explicit diversity incentives (beyond the metric) improve ν; examining whether high‑utility paths are necessarily non‑distinctive due to factual constraints.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** What causes the observed trade‑off between creative utility and factuality, and can architectural or training interventions resolve it?  
**Basis in paper:** The authors show: "A trade‑off exists between utility and factuality, with GPT‑5 maintaining better factuality at the cost of some utility" and Figure 5 demonstrates that stricter factuality filters disproportionately reduce utility for some models.  
**Why unresolved:** The trade‑off implies that models may rely on less factual but stronger/specific relations to achieve high utility, or that factually correct paths are often generic. It is unclear whether this reflects limitations in parametric knowledge, scoring biases, or an inherent tension between "creative" leaps and veracity.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Error analysis categorizing factual errors (e.g., hallucinated entities vs. incorrect relations); measuring if high‑utility paths use rarer relations that are harder to verify; testing if external retrieval or knowledge grounding at inference narrows the gap; relationship between path length and factuality decay.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** Is the cosine distance metric on string embeddings (using MiniLM) an accurate measure of path diversity for associative creativity, or does it fail to capture meaningful semantic differences?  
**Basis in paper:** The distance function is defined as a transformed cosine distance on string representations of paths (Equation 8), justified by anecdotal thresholds (e.g., >0.7 indicates substantial difference). The choice of embedding model and transformation is presented as a practical decision but not thoroughly validated against human notions of diversity.  
**Why unresolved:** The metric may conflate surface‑level lexical differences with deep conceptual dissimilarity. For example, two paths might have high cosine distance due to different entity names but actually represent the same type of association (e.g., "directed by" vs "created by" for a filmmaker). The paper provides examples (Table 5) but no systematic evaluation of the metric’s alignment with creative diversity.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Human evaluation of path pairs rated for conceptual dissimilarity vs. metric scores; testing alternative embedding models (e.g., sentence‑transformers fine‑tuned on relational data) or graph‑based distance measures; sensitivity analysis of the annealing function g(x); correlation between distance and downstream creative utility judgments.

### Open Question 5
**Question:** Are models genuinely exploring the combinatorial search space of CREATE, or are they relying on memorized associations from training data, leading to the benchmark’s saturation resistance?  
**Basis in paper:** The paper claims "the high multiplicity of answers and complexity of the search making benchmark saturation difficult to achieve." However, the benchmark is constructed from Wikidata, which is likely part of pre‑training corpora. Low distinctiveness and modest utility gains suggest models may be retrieving common paths rather than exploring novel combinations.  
**Why unresolved:** If models can memorize or easily retrieve known paths from training, the benchmark may not truly test combinatorial creativity. The authors assume the search space is vast and underexplored, but this has not been verified by measuring coverage of the true path space or comparing to a held‑out set of queries not seen during training.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Estimating the fraction of generated paths that exist in training data (e.g., via data contamination checks); measuring path coverage on a per‑query basis (e.g., number of unique paths found vs. known paths in Wikidata); evaluating on synthetic queries with entities/relations held out from training; analyzing whether models generate paths that are factually correct but not present in the source knowledge graph (i.e., true combinatorial recombination).

## Limitations
- Incomplete dataset specifications: The 12 relation–category pairs and exact categories are not enumerated, hindering exact replication.
- Ambiguity around the `gpt-oss-120b` evaluator and lack of human validation for the creativity metric, risking score incomparability.
- Limited access to frontier models (GPT‑5, Gemini‑3‑Pro) forces substitutions that may diverge in capability.

## Confidence
- Metric validity (s(U) captures creativity): Medium
- Token budget inefficacy: Medium
- Iterative/resampling gains: High
- Utility–factuality trade‑off: High
- Search space saturation resistance: Medium
