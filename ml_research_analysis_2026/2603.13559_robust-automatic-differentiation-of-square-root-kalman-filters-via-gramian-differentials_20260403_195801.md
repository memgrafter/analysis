# Robust Automatic Differentiation of Square-Root Kalman Filters via Gramian Differentials

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.13559  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13559  
- **Reference count:** 15  
- **Primary result:** The paper provides an exact method to differentiate the triangularization operator in square‑root Kalman filters by exploiting the smooth dependence on the Gramian \(LL^\top = MM^\top\), yielding correct Jacobian‑vector products for any matrix shape or rank and recovering the classical QR formula in the full‑rank square case.

## Executive Summary
Square‑root Kalman filters rely on a triangularization operator \(T(M)=L\) obtained via thin QR decomposition. Standard automatic differentiation through QR fails for wide matrices (\(m > n\)) because the semi‑orthogonal factor \(Q\) is non‑unique, and for rank‑deficient matrices because the standard gradient involves inverting a singular \(R\). This paper resolves both issues by observing that all filter outputs (log‑marginal likelihood, filtered mean, covariance) depend on \(L\) only through the Gramian \(LL^\top = MM^\top\), which is a smooth function of \(M\). The authors derive a closed‑form tangent \(dL\) that satisfies the Gramian differential identity \(dL L^\top + L dL^\top = dM M^\top + M dM^\top\). This \(dL\) is constructed by splitting the right‑hand side into column‑space and null‑space parts using the Moore‑Penrose pseudoinverse of \(L\) and an orthogonal projector. The resulting Jacobian‑vector product is linear in \(dM\), enabling exact gradients via automatic transposition. The method recovers the classical QR‑based formula when \(L\) is invertible. Empirical validation on a model with rank‑deficient observation covariance demonstrates correct gradients where standard QR differentiation breaks down. The implementation is provided in JAX as part of the cuthbert library.

## Method Summary
The core contribution is a custom Jacobian‑vector product (JVP) rule for the triangularization operator that avoids differentiating the QR decomposition directly. For a given input matrix \(M\), the forward pass computes the thin QR decomposition of \(M^\top\): \(M^\top = Q L^\top\) with an orthonormal \(Q\) and lower‑triangular \(L\) having non‑negative diagonal. The backward pass does not differentiate the QR algorithm; instead, it solves the Gramian differential identity \(dL L^\top + L dL^\top = dM M^\top + M dM^\top\) for \(dL\) in closed form. Specifically, given the upstream cotangent \(G^L\), one forms \(M = L^\top G^L\) and computes \(K = L^+ dM Q\) using the Moore‑Penrose pseudoinverse \(L^+\). Then \(dL = dL_{\text{col}} + dL_{\text{null}}\) with  
\(dL_{\text{col}} = L \cdot \operatorname{tril}(K + K^\top - \operatorname{diag}(K))\) and  
\(dL_{\text{null}} = (I - L L^+) dM Q\).  
This linear combination satisfies the identity by construction and yields the correct contribution to the gradient with respect to \(M\). The vector‑Jacobian product (VJP) is obtained automatically by transposing the linear map. The rule is incorporated into a square‑root Kalman filter: both predict and update steps use the same triangularization, and the log‑marginal likelihood is accumulated from the diagonal entries of the innovation Cholesky factor. The implementation is validated on a synthetic model with a rank‑deficient observation noise matrix, showing finite and accurate gradients even when the standard QR‑based gradient becomes undefined.

## Key Results
- The triangularization non‑differentiability is circumvented by using the Gramian \(LL^\top = MM^\top\), which is always smooth in \(M\).
- The derived closed‑form \(dL\) works for any matrix dimensions (\(m \ge n\)) and any rank, and it exactly recovers the classical QR‑based gradient when \(L\) is invertible.
- Empirical tests confirm that the method produces correct gradients for rank‑deficient observation covariance, whereas standard QR differentiation fails or yields NaNs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Smoothness via Gramian
- **Claim:** The composite loss (log‑marginal likelihood, filtered mean, covariance) is smooth in \(M\) even when \(T(M)\) is non‑differentiable.
- **Mechanism:** All relevant outputs depend on \(T(M)=L\) only through the Gramian \(LL^\top = MM^\top\); the polynomial map \(M \mapsto MM^\top\) is smooth everywhere, so the composition remains smooth regardless of \(T\)'s differentiability.
- **Core assumption:** The Kalman filter uses only the Gramian \(\Sigma = LL^\top\) and never accesses \(L\) directly.
- **Evidence:** Abstract and Section I introduce the smooth factorization argument.
- **Break condition:** If future filter variants require explicit access to \(L\) (e.g., non‑Gaussian approximations), the loss may become non‑smooth in \(M\).

### Mechanism 2: Exact JVP via Gramian Differential
- **Claim:** The JVP for \(T(M)\) can be computed exactly without QR differentiation by solving the Gramian differential identity.
- **Mechanism:** Any \(dL\) satisfying \(dL L^\top + L dL^\top = dM M^\top + M dM^\top\) yields correct gradients via the chain rule. The paper constructs such a \(dL\) in closed form, sidestepping QR’s non‑uniqueness and inversion issues.
- **Core assumption:** By definition, \(T\) satisfies \(LL^\top = MM^\top\), so the identity holds for the exact \(L\).
- **Evidence:** Section III.B (Proposition 1) and III.C (derivation).
- **Break condition:** Numerical errors in the primal \(L\) or \(M\) may cause the computed \(dL\) to violate the identity slightly, potentially degrading gradient accuracy.

### Mechanism 3: Decomposition into Column and Null Space
- **Claim:** The closed‑form \(dL\) is valid for any rank and dimensions (\(m > n\) or rank‑deficient \(M\)).
- **Mechanism:** The right‑hand side of the identity is split via orthogonal projectors:  
  – Column‑space part handled by \(dL_{\text{col}} = L \cdot \operatorname{tril}(K + K^\top - \operatorname{diag}(K))\) with \(K = L^+ dM Q\), using the Moore‑Penrose pseudoinverse \(L^+\) and the thin QR factor \(Q\) from \(M^\top = Q L^\top\).  
  – Null‑space part by \(dL_{\text{null}} = (I - L L^+) dM Q\).  
  The sum satisfies the identity by construction.
- **Core assumption:** \(\operatorname{col}(L) = \operatorname{col}(M)\) (since \(LL^\top = MM^\top\)) and \(Q\) spans \(\operatorname{col}(M^\top)\).
- **Evidence:** Section III.C (parts a and b), Definition 2, Remark 1.
- **Break condition:** For nearly rank‑deficient \(M\), computing \(L^+\) or \(Q\) may be numerically unstable, leading to inaccurate \(dL\). Also, different QR implementations (e.g., with pivoting) may yield different \(Q\), but any valid \(dL\) satisfying the identity gives the same gradient.

## Foundational Learning

#### Automatic Differentiation (AD) and Chain Rule
- **Why needed:** AD automates gradient propagation through the recursive Kalman filter operations, eliminating manual differentiation and reducing errors.
- **Quick check:** How does forward‑mode AD compute Jacobian‑vector products (JVPs), and why is the linearity of the JVP in the tangent vector critical for deriving the vector‑Jacobian product (VJP) in reverse mode via transposition?

#### QR Decomposition and Triangularization
- **Why needed:** Square‑root filters implement \(T(M)\) via thin QR of an augmented matrix; differentiating this step is problematic for wide matrices (\(m > n\)) because \(Q\) is not unique, and for rank‑deficient inputs because \(R^{-1}\) is singular.
- **Quick check:** Why is the semi‑orthogonal factor \(Q\) in the thin QR decomposition \(M^\top = Q L^\top\) non‑unique when \(m > n\), and how does this non‑uniqueness manifest in gradient computation?

#### Moore‑Penrose Pseudoinverse
- **Why needed:** The method uses \(L^+\) in the column‑space component \(dL_{\text{col}}\) to handle rank‑deficient matrices gracefully, extending applicability beyond full‑rank cases.
- **Quick check:** What are the four Penrose conditions that uniquely define the Moore‑Penrose pseudoinverse \(A^+\) for a matrix \(A\)?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Primal flow: \(M\) → thin QR of \(M^\top\) → \((Q, L)\). Tangent flow: \(dM\) → compute \(L^+\) (pseudoinverse of \(L\)) and reuse \(Q\) → compute \(K = L^+ dM Q\) → \(dL_{\text{col}} = L \cdot \operatorname{tril}(K + K^\top - \operatorname{diag}(K))\) and \(dL_{\text{null}} = (I - L L^+) dM Q\) → \(dL = dL_{\text{col}} + dL_{\text{null}}\).

### Critical Path
- QR decomposition of \(M^\top\) (cost \(O(mn^2)\) for \(m \ge n\)) is the dominant step.
- Pseudoinverse \(L^+\): if full rank, use \(L^{-1}\) (\(O(n^3)\)); for rank deficiency, SVD or rank‑revealing QR adds overhead.
- Matrix multiplications for \(K\), \(dL_{\text{col}}\), \(dL_{\text{null}}\) cost \(O(n^2)\).  
Reusing QR factors across multiple JVP calls can amortize cost.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Pseudoinverse cost vs. robustness:** SVD handles arbitrary rank but incurs \(O(n^3)\); QR with column pivoting is cheaper but may sacrifice numerical stability.
- **Null‑space correction:** Necessary for correctness when \(m > n\) or rank‑deficient; adds \(O(n^2)\) overhead but vanishes when \(L\) is full‑rank and square.
- **Linearity exploitation:** The linear dependence of \(dL\) on \(dM\) allows automatic VJP derivation via transposition, avoiding manual reverse‑mode code.

### Failure Signatures
- NaNs, exploding gradients, or non‑unique results when standard QR differentiation is applied to wide or rank‑deficient matrices (as shown in Section V).
- Inconsistent gradients across runs if QR decomposition uses nondeterministic pivoting; suggests implementation issues.
- Performance bottleneck for large \(m\) due to QR decomposition dominating runtime.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Full‑rank square test:** Generate random full‑rank square \(M\) (e.g., \(n \times n\)). Compute \(dL\) via the proposed method and compare to finite differences of \(L\) w.r.t. \(M\); expect relative error \(< 10^{-6}\).
2. **Wide matrix test:** Use \(M\) with \(m > n\) (e.g., from predict step). Verify the proposed \(dL\) yields finite, consistent gradients across JVP calls; the naive QR‑based approach should diverge or give non‑unique results.
3. **Rank‑deficient validation:** Replicate Section V: set observation covariance \(V(\alpha) = \operatorname{diag}([1, \alpha^2])\) with \(\alpha \in [0,1]\). Compute total log‑likelihood gradient \(\nabla_\alpha\) for several \(\alpha\) using both the custom method and finite differences. The custom method should stay finite and accurate as \(\alpha \to 0\), while a classic QR‑based gradient becomes NaN or diverges.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Can the Gramian differential approach be extended to nonlinear state‑space models such as the EKF or UKF? The current theory applies only to linear Gaussian models; nonlinear transformations may introduce dependencies on \(L\) beyond its Gramian.
- What is the computational cost of the proposed surrogate tangent compared to standard QR differentiation when both are applicable (full‑rank, square cases)? The paper provides no efficiency analysis for regimes where the classical QR‑based gradient is defined.
- How does the method behave on ill‑conditioned but full‑rank Gramians in practice? The solution handles exact rank deficiency via pseudoinverse, but numerical stability for near‑singular \(L\) (large condition number) is not examined.
- Is the proposed JVP equally effective and efficient across major AD frameworks (JAX, PyTorch, TensorFlow)? Implementation is provided only for JAX; framework‑specific details might affect correctness or performance in edge cases.

## Limitations
- The approach assumes all relevant filter outputs depend solely on the Gramian; if future variants require direct access to the triangular factor \(L\), the smoothness argument and gradient correctness may fail.
- The Moore‑Penrose pseudoinverse introduces an \(O(n^3)\) cost and can become numerically unstable for nearly rank‑deficient matrices, potentially degrading gradient accuracy despite theoretical exactness.
- Empirical validation is limited to a single small synthetic model (Section V); broader large‑scale or real‑world benchmarks are lacking.

## Confidence
- **Theoretical correctness** (exact JVP via Gramian identity): **High**  
- **Recovery of classical QR formula** when \(L\) invertible: **High**  
- **Empirical robustness** for rank‑deficient observation noise: **Medium**  
- **General applicability** to AD frameworks and large‑scale filters: **Low‑Medium**
