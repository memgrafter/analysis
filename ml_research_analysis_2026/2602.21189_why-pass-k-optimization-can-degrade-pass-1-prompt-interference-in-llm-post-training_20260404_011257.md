# Why Pass@k Optimization Can Degrade Pass@1: Prompt Interference in LLM Post-training

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.21189
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21189
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Optimizing for multi-attempt success (Pass@k) can degrade single-attempt performance (Pass@1) due to gradient conflict arising from prompt interference.

## Executive Summary
This paper reveals a subtle but critical flaw in optimizing Pass@k during LLM post-training: while it improves multi-sample metrics, it often harms Pass@1, the key operational measure. The core insight is "prompt interference"—the similarity between gradients of different prompts—and how Pass@k implicitly reweights the training distribution toward hard prompts. When these hard prompts have negative interference (their gradients oppose the Pass@1 direction), the resulting gradient conflict causes Pass@1 to decline even as Pass@k rises. The authors formalize the conflict via an inner product expression, prove a phase transition condition on the number of attempts k, and empirically confirm the phenomenon on the MATH dataset with two distilled models.

## Method Summary
The study analyzes the policy gradient objective for Pass@k. For a prompt x, the success probability pθ(x) is estimated by sampling k responses and taking the mean of binary rewards. The per-prompt gradient ∇J_k(x;θ) equals w_k(x)∇pθ(x), where the weight w_k(x)=k(1-pθ(x))^{k-1} heavily upweights prompts with low pθ(x).Prompt interference a(x)=⟨∇pθ(x),∇J₁(θ)⟩ quantifies alignment with the Pass@1 gradient. The population inner product ⟨∇J_k,∇J₁⟩ simplifies to 𝔼[w_k(x)a(x)]. The authors compute these quantities via REINFORCE-style gradient estimation on 2000 MATH problems using DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B and Qwen-7B. They compare easy (high pθ) vs. hard (low pθ) prompt groups, showing that w_k dramatically amplifies hard prompts, many of which exhibit negative a(x), flipping the weighted mean agreement from positive to negative. Consequently, one-step gradient ascent on Pass@k increases Pass@k but decreases Pass@1.

## Key Results
- Gradient conflict condition: ⟨∇J_k,∇J₁⟩ = 𝔼[w_k a] becomes negative when hard prompts with a(x)<0 receive sufficiently large weight.
- Phase transition: Under a separation assumption on success probabilities and interference strength, there exists a threshold k* such that for all k>k* the gradients conflict.
- Empirical evidence: On MATH, the unweighted mean agreement is positive (~2.80e-3), but the w_k-weighted mean becomes negative (~-1.12e-3), yielding strongly negative inner products. Training shows Pass@1 dropping while Pass@k rises, confirming the trade-off.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Implicit Reweighting**: The Pass@k gradient weight w_k(x)=k(1-pθ(x))^{k-1} decreases exponentially with the prompt's success probability pθ(x). Thus, optimization over Pass@k effectively upweights prompts the model currently solves poorly (low p). This shifts the effective data distribution toward hard examples.

**Negative Interference Induces Conflict**: Prompt interference a(x)=⟨∇pθ(x),∇J₁(θ)⟩ measures alignment of a prompt's gradient with the overall Pass@1 gradient. For hard prompts, a(x) tends to be negative, meaning their gradients oppose the direction that would improve Pass@1. Upweighting these negatively interfering prompts makes the weighted expectation 𝔼[w_k a] negative, i.e., ⟨∇J_k,∇J₁⟩<0, causing gradient conflict.

**Phase Transition in k**: As k increases, w_k(x) amplifies differences in pθ(x) exponentially. Proposition 4.5 shows that if a fraction q of prompts are m-strongly negatively interfering and have uniformly lower success probability than the rest, then for all k > k* = 1 + log((1-q)G²/(qm)) / log((1-ε)/(1-δ)), conflict occurs. This defines a sharp threshold beyond which Pass@k optimization is guaranteed to move away from the Pass@1 optimum, under the stated conditions.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Pass@k Objective**  
  *Why needed*: It is the optimization target whose gradient behavior we analyze; understanding its nonlinear transformation f_k(p)=1-(1-p)^k and its policy gradient (Eq. 2) is essential.  
  *Quick check*: Why does ∇J_k(x;θ)=w_k(pθ(x))∇pθ(x) imply per-prompt Pass@k and Pass@1 gradients are collinear?  
  *Answer*: They differ only by the scalar weight w_k(x), so they point in the same direction for that prompt.

- **Concept: Prompt Interference**  
  *Why needed*: It characterizes the geometric relationship between per-prompt gradients; negative interference is the root cause of gradient conflict.  
  *Quick check*: How does the similarity kernel κθ(x,x')=⟨∇pθ(x),∇pθ(x')⟩ relate to the effect of a policy update on multiple prompts?  
  *Answer*: A positive κ means updates that improve one prompt also help the other; negative κ means they hurt.

- **Concept: Implicit Reweighting**  
  *Why needed*: It explains how Pass@k optimization changes the effective optimization distribution. The analyst must track the shifted distribution D̃_{k,θ}.  
  *Quick check*: If pθ(x) is small for a prompt, is its weight w_k(x) large or small relative to a prompt with large pθ(x)?  
  *Answer*: Large (since (1-p)^{k-1} is near 1 when p≈0, and near 0 when p≈1).

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**: Prompt Sampler → Policy Network (πθ) → Verifier (binary reward) → Gradient Estimator (computes ∇pθ(x) and weight w_k(x)) → Optimizer (applies gradient to θ).

**Critical path**:  
1. Sample a batch of prompts {x_i}.  
2. For each x_i, sample k responses y_{i,j} ∼ πθ(·|x_i).  
3. Compute pθ(x_i) ≈ (1/k)∑_j r(x_i, y_{i,j}).  
4. Compute per-prompt weight w_k(x_i)=k(1-pθ(x_i))^{k-1}.  
5. Estimate ∇J_k ≈ (1/N)∑_i w_k(x_i) ∇pθ(x_i) (using REINFORCE or a likelihood ratio).  
6. Update parameters: θ ← θ + η ∇J_k.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- **Batch composition**: Including many hard, negatively interfering prompts can trigger conflict; monitoring the distribution of agreement scores a(x) is necessary.  
- **Learning rate η**: To guarantee a one-step Pass@k ascent improves J_k without decreasing J_1 under conflict, η must satisfy η ≤ min(δ(θ)/C², 1/L_k). Larger η may still degrade Pass@1.

**Failure signatures**:  
1. Validation shows Pass@1 decreasing while Pass@k increases over training steps.  
2. Logs reveal extreme weight disparity: w_k for hard prompts (p≈0) near k, for easy prompts (p≈1) near 0 (e.g., 10^{-28}).  
3. Histogram of agreement scores a(x) displays a substantial negative-valued mass concentrated on low-pθ prompts.

**First 3 experiments**:  
1. **Compute Agreement Scores**: For a held-out prompt set, compute a(x)=⟨∇pθ(x), ∇J₁(θ)⟩ using likelihood ratios or finite differences. Verify that prompts with low pθ(x) tend to have negative a(x).  
2. **Measure Reweighting Impact**: Compute unweighted mean 𝔼_D[a(x)] and Pass@k-weighted mean 𝔼_D[w_k(x)a(x)]/𝔼_D[w_k(x)]. Observe a sign flip from positive to negative, confirming the conflict condition 𝔼[w_k a]<0.  
3. **Validate Phase Transition**: For increasing k (e.g., 2,4,8,16,32), measure cosine similarity between ∇J_k and ∇J₁. Under Proposition 4.5's conditions, expect a transition from positive to negative around the predicted k*.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How can we design pass@k optimization algorithms that avoid gradient conflict and preserve pass@1 performance?
- **Basis in paper**: The authors explicitly state: "An immediate direction is to design and evaluate methods to mitigate this conflict issue."
- **Why unresolved**: The paper only characterizes the conflict theoretically but provides no algorithmic solutions or mitigation strategies.
- **What would resolve**: Empirical comparisons of proposed mitigation approaches (e.g., gradient surgery, adaptive reweighting, constrained optimization) on standard benchmarks showing improved pass@k without degrading pass@1.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Can alternative inference-time objectives (e.g., other concave transforms of success probability) achieve a better trade-off between multi-attempt and single-attempt performance?
- **Basis in paper**: The authors suggest: "it would be interesting to investigate more general inference-time objectives beyond pass@k ... without sacrificing pass@1 performance."
- **Why unresolved**: The study focuses solely on pass@k; theoretical and empirical properties of other objectives remain unexplored.
- **What would resolve**: Theoretical analysis of gradient alignment for a family of objectives (e.g., softmax, top-m) and experiments comparing them on MATH or code benchmarks across multiple k values.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: How prevalent is gradient conflict across diverse LLM architectures, tasks, and prompt distributions?
- **Basis in paper**: The experiments only test two models (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B, Qwen-7B) on the MATH dataset. The authors do not examine other domains like code generation or open-ended QA.
- **Why unresolved**: Limited empirical scope leaves open whether the conflict is universal or specific to math reasoning with these models.
- **What would resolve**: A systematic study across multiple model families (GPT, Claude), tasks (HumanEval, Big-Bench), and prompt distributions, measuring agreement scores and gradient inner products to map conflict conditions.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: What are the long-term optimization dynamics of pass@k training—does gradient conflict cause instability, convergence issues, or irrecoverable pass@1 loss over many steps?
- **Basis in paper**: The analysis and experiments focus on one-step updates or a few iterations; long-term behavior is not theoretically analyzed.
- **Why unresolved**: Smoothness-based one-step guarantees do not extend to multi-step trajectories; the phase transition in k might affect convergence and final performance.
- **What would resolve**: Multi-epoch training experiments tracking pass@1/pass@k trajectories, combined with theoretical analysis of pass@k objective curvature and convergence rates under persistent gradient conflict.

## Limitations
- The theoretical phase transition threshold relies on a clean separation between success probabilities of negatively interfering prompts and others; real data may violate this assumption.
- Finite-sample noise in gradient estimation (e.g., 32 samples per prompt) can inflate weight disparity and affect the sign of weighted agreement.
- Results are demonstrated on only two distilled models on MATH; generalization to other architectures and tasks remains untested.

## Confidence
- **Theoretical analysis (Proposition 4.1, 4.5, etc.)**: High
- **Empirical results on MATH with two provided models**: Medium
- **Generality beyond the specific models and dataset**: Low
