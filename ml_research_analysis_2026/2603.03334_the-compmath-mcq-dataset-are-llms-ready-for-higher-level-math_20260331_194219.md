# The CompMath-MCQ Dataset: Are LLMs Ready for Higher-Level Math?

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.03334  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03334  
- **Reference count:** 11  
- **Primary result:** State-of-the-art LLMs (e.g., GPT-5 90.6%, Claude Sonnet 4.5 90.9%) achieve high overall accuracy on graduate computational math but struggle with Vector Calculus (78.6–83.5%), revealing persistent difficulties in multi-step symbolic manipulation.

## Executive Summary
The CompMath-MCQ dataset introduces a leakage-free benchmark of 1,500 original multiple-choice questions covering graduate-level computational mathematics (Linear Algebra, Numerical Optimization, Vector Calculus, Probability, Python). Authored by professors and validated via cross-LLM disagreement analysis followed by expert review, the benchmark addresses prior data contamination concerns. Evaluation uses length-normalized log-likelihood ranking for open-weight models and format-constrained prompts for closed models via `lm_eval`. Results show top models near 90% overall but with significant topic variance: near-perfect on Probability and Python (>97%) but notably lower on Vector Calculus (78.6–83.5%), highlighting ongoing challenges in multi-step symbolic reasoning. The dataset provides a reproducible foundation for assessing and improving LLM capabilities in advanced mathematics.

## Method Summary
The study constructs a dataset of 1,500 novel multiple-choice questions (three options each) across five graduate computational math topics. Questions are authored by professors to ensure no overlap with existing textbooks or benchmarks, mitigating data leakage. A validation pipeline runs eight diverse LLMs, computes per-question error rate (ei) and wrong-answer consensus (ci), and flags items with high ei and high ci for expert review to eliminate ambiguity or mislabeling. Evaluation employs `lm_eval`: open-weight models are scored via length-normalized conditional log-likelihood over option strings, while closed-weight models use a zero-shot XML-constrained prompt (`<Answer>x</Answer>` with x ∈ {0,1,2}) and deterministic decoding. Accuracy is reported overall and per topic.

## Key Results
- Top models (GPT-5, Claude Sonnet 4.5, Qwen3-Coder 30B) achieve 89–91% overall accuracy.
- Performance is highest on Probability and Python (>97%) but drops sharply on Vector Calculus (78.6–83.5%).
- The three-option MCQ format and rigorous validation yield a leakage-free, reproducible benchmark.
- Vector Calculus errors suggest persistent difficulty with multi-step symbolic manipulation compared to single-step probabilistic or programming tasks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Original, professor-authored questions eliminate data leakage, providing a leakage-free assessment of model generalization.
- **Mechanism:** By ensuring all questions are newly created and not sourced from existing textbooks, online repositories, or prior benchmarks, the dataset avoids overlap with the training corpora of contemporary LLMs. This forces evaluation to rely on the model's underlying reasoning rather than memorized solutions.
- **Core assumption:** The model's training data does not contain the specific, novel formulations and numerical instances crafted for this dataset.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "To ensure the absence of data leakage, all questions are newly created and not sourced from existing materials."
  - [section 3.1] "none of the questions were sourced from existing textbooks, online repositories, or prior benchmarks. All items were originally created by the authors specifically for this dataset."
- **Break condition:** If these exact questions or substantially similar variants appear in a model's training data (e.g., via dataset release and subsequent scraping), the leakage-free property is invalidated.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Cross-LLM disagreement analysis, followed by expert review, systematically identifies and removes ambiguous or mislabeled questions, improving benchmark reliability.
- **Mechanism:** Questions that elicit high error rates across multiple diverse models, especially where incorrect answers converge on the same distractor (high wrong-answer consensus), are flagged. These are then manually reviewed for clarity and correctness. This uses model failure patterns as a proxy for potential dataset flaws.
- **Core assumption:** Systematically shared model errors are more likely to stem from a problematic question (ambiguity, incorrect label) than from a uniformly hard but valid question.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.2] Detailed formulas for *per-question error rate* (ei) and *wrong-answer consensus* (ci), with Figure 3 visualizing questions in the upper-right region (high ei, high ci) as primary candidates for review.
  - [section 3.2] "The objective of this stage is not to assess model performance per se, but to identify questions whose labeled answer may be ambiguous, underspecified, or incorrect, as indicated by systematic disagreement across multiple independent models."
- **Break condition:** If manual review finds that a high-consensus error question is actually perfectly valid and clear, or if models begin to consistently disagree *less* on genuinely flawed questions, the screening heuristic fails.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Normalized log-likelihood ranking for open-weight models and format-constrained prompts for closed-weight models enable objective, reproducible, and bias-free (e.g., length, order) multiple-choice evaluation.
- **Mechanism:** For open models, scoring is based on the length-normalized conditional log-probability of each answer option string, removing bias toward shorter options. For closed models, a strict XML-tagged prompt forces a discrete choice (0/1/2), eliminating parsing failures from conversational output and stochastic decoding variance.
- **Core assumption:** Direct scoring of candidate strings (or constrained generation) provides a more faithful and comparable measure of the model's internal preference for the correct answer than free-form generation with heuristic extraction.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4.1] Explanation of length-normalized log-likelihood: "We then report results using length-normalized log-likelihood scores to mitigate biases toward option length... prevents the model from being systematically biased toward shorter, high-probability completions."
  - [section 4.3] Description of format-constrained prompt: "We instruct the model to reply with only one of (0), (1), or (2)... If the output does not contain a valid number... the prediction is considered invalid and marked as wrong."
- **Break condition:** If the normalization formula does not adequately control for other biases (e.g., option lexical similarity), or if the constrained prompt for closed models still produces high invalid rates or is circumvented, the evaluation's objectivity is compromised.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Question Authoring Pipeline -> Validation Harness -> Expert Review Interface -> Evaluation Engine -> Results Aggregator

**Critical path:**  
Question creation → Automated multi-model validation → Manual review of flagged items → Final dataset release → Standardized evaluation via `lm_eval` → Results analysis.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **MCQ vs. Open-ended:** MCQ sacrifices depth of reasoning proof for scoring objectivity and reproducibility; cannot assess derivation quality, only final choice.  
- **Three options vs. four:** Three options increase challenge (chance-level 33.3%) and simplify distractor design but may reduce granularity in diagnosing misconceptions.  
- **Validation heuristic (disagreement) vs. full expert review:** Using model disagreement is scalable but may miss valid hard questions that all models get right or invalid questions with random wrong answers.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **Validation stage:** A question has high `ei` but *low* `ci` (models wrong on different options). This might indicate a genuinely hard but valid question or multiple misleading interpretations.  
- **Evaluation stage:** For closed-weight models, a high rate of "invalid" responses (missing/incorrect XML tag) indicates prompt injection failure or model non-compliance.  
- **Results stage:** A model performs near chance (~33%) on a specific topic (e.g., Vector Calculus) but >90% on others, suggesting a fundamental representation gap in that domain's symbolic manipulation.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Reproduce Validation Pipeline:** Run the 8 validation models on a subset of 100 released questions. Recalculate `ei` and `ci`. Verify that flagged questions (high ei, high ci) indeed correspond to items you can identify as ambiguous or tricky.  
2. **Ablation - Option Order Effect:** For a fixed set of 50 questions, evaluate an open-weight model (e.g., Qwen3-30B) with the original option order and with all options cyclically permuted. Measure accuracy variance to test if log-likelihood ranking neutralizes order bias.  
3. **Topic-Specific Error Analysis:** Pick the worst-performing topic (Vector Calculus). For 20 incorrectly answered questions by a strong model (e.g., GPT-5), manually categorize errors (sign mistake, incorrect partial derivative, chain rule failure, misreading notation) to validate the multi-step symbolic manipulation hypothesis.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Question:** Why is Vector Calculus significantly harder for LLMs than Probability or Python?  
   **Basis:** Evaluation results show Vector Calculus accuracy 78.6–83.5% while Probability and Python exceed 97%.  
   **Why unresolved:** The paper speculates multi-step symbolic manipulation is the cause but lacks empirical validation.  
   **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments varying symbolic complexity, or detailed error analysis to pinpoint failure modes (e.g., sign errors, chain rule mistakes).

2. **Question:** Does the cross-LLM disagreement validation method conflate dataset errors with collective model blind spots?  
   **Basis:** The validation flags questions with high error rate and wrong-answer consensus, assuming they are flawed.  
   **Why unresolved:** If all models share a common misconception, valid but difficult questions might be discarded, reducing benchmark diversity.  
   **What evidence would resolve it:** Injecting known difficult but correct questions into the validation pipeline to see if they are flagged, or comparing flagged items to known model weaknesses.

3. **Question:** How does the three-option MCQ format influence LLM performance and benchmark discriminative power?  
   **Basis:** The dataset uses three options without comparison to other formats.  
   **Why unresolved:** Option count could affect guessing probability, distractor plausibility, and model confidence; this is unexplored.  
   **What evidence would resolve it:** Parallel evaluations with varying numbers of options (e.g., 4 or 5) on identical questions, measuring accuracy and response patterns.

4. **Question:** To what extent do the findings generalize across the full spectrum of graduate computational mathematics?  
   **Basis:** The dataset covers only five topics and may reflect a specific curriculum (authored by professors from one institution).  
   **Why unresolved:** Potential gaps in topic coverage and curriculum bias limit generalizability to all graduate-level computational math.  
   **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicating the benchmark with questions from diverse universities and additional topics (e.g., Differential Equations, Real Analysis).

## Limitations
- No proof of zero leakage; future data scraping could introduce contamination.
- Disagreement thresholds are arbitrary; may mis-flag valid hard questions or miss subtle flaws.
- Multiple-choice format does not assess reasoning process, only final choice.
- Limited to 5 topics and 3 options; may not generalize to broader math or other formats.

## Confidence
- Leakage-free dataset: Medium (no direct training data audit).
- Validation pipeline: Medium (heuristic effectiveness unverified).
- Evaluation objectivity: Medium (open normalization may not fully eliminate biases; closed format adherence uncertain).
