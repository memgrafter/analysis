# On the Adversarial Robustness of Discrete Image Tokenizers

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.18252
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18252
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Discrete image tokenizers are vulnerable to adversarial attacks; adversarial fine‑tuning of only the tokenizer encoder (while freezing downstream components) increases robust accuracy by 40‑50% on classification and VQA at ϵ=2‑4/255, with minor clean accuracy loss and 2.2× computational speedup over end‑to‑end adversarial training.

## Executive Summary
Discrete image tokenizers, which convert images into token sequences for multimodal AI systems, were previously assumed to be robust due to their discrete nature. This paper reveals that they are in fact vulnerable: small, carefully crafted perturbations to the input image can alter the continuous embeddings before quantization, causing the token assignment to flip and leading to downstream task failures. The authors propose a novel threat model with unsupervised attacks that operate solely on the pre‑quantization embeddings, making them task‑agnostic and efficient. To defend, they introduce adversarial fine‑tuning that updates only the tokenizer encoder (using a consistency loss between clean and perturbed embeddings) while keeping the codebook and all downstream modules frozen. This approach yields substantial robustness gains across multiple tasks (image classification, retrieval, VQA, captioning) and generalizes to unseen datasets, all while being significantly cheaper than conventional end‑to‑end adversarial training.

## Method Summary
The method consists of two parts: attack and defense. For the attack, the adversary optimizes an ℓ₂ penalty on the difference between the clean and perturbed embeddings produced by the tokenizer encoder, using an APGD optimizer; the perturbations are constrained in ℓ∞ norm (e.g., ϵ=4/255). The straight‑through estimator allows gradients to flow through the non‑differentiable quantization step. This attack is unsupervised (no downstream task loss) and works on any downstream system that consumes the tokens. For defense, the tokenizer encoder is adversarially fine‑tuned on a large unlabeled dataset (e.g., ImageNet). The training objective minimizes the maximum ℓ₂ distance between embeddings of clean and adversarially perturbed images, encouraging embeddings to stay within the same quantization cell under small perturbations. The encoder is updated with AdamW for one epoch, using 10‑step APGD per batch; the codebook and downstream components remain frozen. The robust tokenizer can then be inserted into any downstream pipeline, transferring robustness without retraining those modules.

## Key Results
- Unsupervised attacks cause near‑zero robust accuracy on multiple tasks for non‑robust tokenizers (e.g., FuseLIP‑S on Imagenette drops from 86.0% clean to 0% at ϵ=4/255).
- Adversarial fine‑tuning yields large robust accuracy gains: FuseLIP‑S on Imagenette (ϵ=4/255) rises from 0% to 49.6% and UniTok‑MLLM on VQA (ϵ=2/255) from 14.4% to 49.7%, with clean accuracy decreasing only ≈6 percentage points on the primary dataset and maintaining >70% on others.
- Encoder‑only fine‑tuning is 2.2× faster than end‑to‑end adversarial training and generalizes to unseen tasks/datasets without catastrophic forgetting.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
Unsupervised attacks flip token assignments by pushing pre‑quantization embeddings across the hard decision boundaries of vector quantization. The attack optimizes an ℓ₂ distance in embedding space under an ℓ∞ image‑space constraint; even tiny shifts can change the nearest codebook entry, altering the discrete token sequence fed to downstream systems. The key assumption is that quantization creates piecewise‑constant regions; if an embedding lands near a boundary, a small perturbation suffices to cross it. Evidence: the attack is effective across classification, retrieval, and captioning without task‑specific tuning.

### Mechanism 2
Unsupervised adversarial fine‑tuning of the encoder stabilizes token assignments. By minimizing the maximum ℓ₂ difference between clean and adversarial embeddings, the encoder learns to produce embeddings that remain within the same quantization cell under perturbations of radius ϵ. Because downstream modules only see the token indices (not the continuous embeddings), this consistency transfers robustness throughout the pipeline. Evidence: fine‑tuning yields large gains in robust accuracy for multiple downstream tasks while only modestly affecting clean performance.

### Mechanism 3
The task‑agnostic nature of the unsupervised objective leads to better generalization compared to supervised end‑to‑end adversarial training. The encoder learns features that preserve token stability across diverse images, rather than overfitting to a particular downstream loss. Freezing downstream components prevents interference. Evidence: a tokenizer trained on ImageNet or CC3M improves robustness on unseen datasets and tasks (e.g., OI‑Pos, VQA), whereas end‑to‑end adversarial training on one task catastrophically degrades performance on others.

## Foundational Learning
### Discrete image tokenization and vector quantization
**Why needed:** Vector quantization maps continuous encoder outputs to discrete token IDs via nearest‑neighbor search in a codebook; this process is non‑differentiable and creates hard boundaries, which the attacks exploit.
**Quick check:** Why is the straight‑through estimator used during training when quantizing?
### Adversarial examples and ℓₚ‑bounded attacks
**Why needed:** The threat model defines additive perturbations constrained by an ℓₚ norm (typically ℓ∞). Understanding this norm is crucial to interpret robustness numbers like ϵ=4/255.
**Quick check:** How does the magnitude of ϵ (e.g., 4/255 vs. 16/255) relate to the perceptibility and strength of an adversarial image?
### Adversarial training (AT)
**Why needed:** Adversarial training involves training on adversarially perturbed inputs to improve robustness. This work extends AT to tokenizer encoders in an unsupervised manner.
**Quick check:** What is the fundamental difference between supervised end‑to‑end AT and the unsupervised AT proposed here?
### Multimodal architectures with frozen plug‑ins
**Why needed:** Tokenizers are often used as pre‑trained, frozen components in larger models (e.g., FuseLIP, UniTok‑MLLM). This modularity enables updating only the tokenizer to transfer robustness.
**Quick check:** Why is updating only the tokenizer encoder computationally cheaper than end‑to‑end adversarial training?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Raw image → ViT encoder → pre‑quantization embeddings → vector quantization (nearest codebook lookup) → discrete token IDs → downstream module (e.g., linear classifier, contrastive head, LLM). Attack and training loops also involve an adversarial example generator (APGD) that modifies the image to change the encoder output, and a loss that compares clean and perturbed embeddings.

### Critical path for robustness transfer
1. Adversarially fine‑tune the tokenizer encoder on a large unlabeled dataset (e.g., ImageNet) using the unsupervised consistency loss.
2. Freeze the codebook and all downstream components; replace the original encoder with the robust one.
3. Deploy the modified tokenizer in any downstream system (classification, retrieval, VQA, captioning). The system inherits robustness because token outputs become stable under input perturbations.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Training radius ϵ:** Smaller ϵ (e.g., 4/255) preserves clean accuracy better; larger ϵ (16/255) yields stronger robustness but more clean accuracy drop.
- **Dataset choice:** Using a larger, diverse dataset (e.g., CC3M) improves generalization to unseen tasks but requires more compute.
- **Encoder‑only update vs. full model:** Encoder‑only is far more efficient and avoids overfitting; full end‑to‑end AT may give best robustness on the training task but harms generalization.
- **Unsupervised vs. supervised attacks for training:** Unsupervised attacks are task‑agnostic and faster; supervised attacks might be slightly stronger but the unsupervised defense already protects against both.

### Failure signatures
- Robust accuracy under APGD/AutoAttack remains near 0% for ϵ=4/255 after attempted fine‑tuning.
- Clean accuracy drops >10% on key datasets, indicating over‑fitting or too large a training ϵ.
- Token indices still change extensively under weak attacks (e.g., many tokens per image flip).
- Reconstructed adversarial images visibly distort or targeted captioning/VQA succeeds despite defense.
- Robustness does not transfer to a different modality (e.g., video) or a drastically different downstream architecture.

### First 3 experiments (concrete steps to validate)
1. **Baseline vulnerability check:** For a tokenizer (e.g., TiTok‑BL128), run unsupervised APGD (100 steps, ϵ=4/255) on 500 Imagenette images, compute robust accuracy of FuseLIP‑S via AutoAttack. Expect near‑zero robust accuracy if not robustified.
2. **Adversarial fine‑tuning:** Take the same tokenizer, adversarially fine‑tune its encoder on ImageNet (one epoch, ϵ=8/255, 10‑step APGD). Freeze codebook and downstream. Re‑evaluate robust accuracy on Imagenette, Caltech101, OI‑Crop, OI‑Pos. Expect robust accuracy around 40‑50% at ϵ=4/255 with moderate clean accuracy drop.
3. **Generalization test:** Plug the robust tokenizer into UniTok‑MLLM (VQA). Evaluate clean and robust (ϵ=2/255, ensemble attack) accuracy on VQAv2/OK‑VQA/GQA. Expect robust accuracy increase from ~10% to ~45% while clean accuracy remains within 5% of original.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1: How do different tokenizer design choices affect adversarial robustness?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors suggest future work on how vector quantization (VQ) vs. finite scalar quantization (FSQ), codebook size, and feature dimension influence robustness.
- **Why unresolved:** The study evaluated only a few tokenizers (TiTok, UniTok, FlexTok) with fixed architectures; systematic variation is needed.
- **What would resolve:** Comprehensive experiments comparing multiple tokenizer configurations under identical attack/defense settings, measuring robust and clean performance.

### Open Question 2: To what extent does adversarial fine‑tuning of discrete tokenizers transfer to other downstream tasks?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper claims generalizability but only evaluated classification, retrieval, VQA, and captioning.
- **Why unresolved:** Tasks relying on spatial information (e.g., segmentation, detection) or generative models (e.g., diffusion) may behave differently.
- **What would resolve:** Testing robust tokenizers on additional tasks (object detection, semantic segmentation, image generation) and evaluating robustness under relevant attacks.

### Open Question 3: How vulnerable are tokenizers to other threat models?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper focuses exclusively on ℓ∞‑bounded additive perturbations; other norms or transformations are not explored.
- **Why unresolved:** ℓ∞ may not represent all realistic attack scenarios; ℓ2, JPEG, or spatial attacks could pose different risks.
- **What would resolve:** Conducting adversarial evaluations under ℓ2 constraints, perceptual metrics, or non‑additive transformations and assessing the proposed attacks/defenses.

### Open Question 4: Does fine‑tuning the codebook in addition to the encoder improve robustness?
- **Basis in paper:** The adversarial fine‑tuning updates only the encoder while freezing the codebook; the effect of updating the codebook is unknown.
- **Why unresolved:** The codebook could be adapted to align with adversarially perturbed features, potentially boosting robustness, but might also degrade reconstruction or increase training cost.
- **What would resolve:** Experiments where the codebook is also trained with the unsupervised adversarial loss, comparing robust and clean performance and analyzing codebook usage changes.

### Open Question 5: What explains the observed differences in robustness across tokenizers?
- **Basis in paper:** Figure 2 shows FlexTok reconstructions are more robust than TiTok’s under the same attack; the reason is not analyzed.
- **Why unresolved:** Understanding could yield design principles for robust tokenizers; factors may include quantization method (single vs. multi‑codebook), auxiliary losses, or encoder architecture.
- **What would resolve:** Ablation studies isolating factors like codebook size, number of tokens, presence of reconstruction vs. contrastive losses, and encoder capacity, measuring robustness and visualizing decision boundaries.

## Limitations
- **Hyperparameter gaps:** Key training details (learning rate, batch size, APGD step schedule) are not fully specified, hindering exact replication.
- **Implementation ambiguities:** The extraction of pre‑quantization embeddings and aggregation of loss over token sequence are unclear; models may not expose these intermediate outputs.
- **Evaluation underspecification:** Details for VQA prompts, retrieval splits, and the exact 500 Imagenette images are missing, making result comparison difficult.
- **Threat model scope:** Only white‑box ℓ∞ attacks are considered; robustness against black‑box or other threat models is unexplored.
