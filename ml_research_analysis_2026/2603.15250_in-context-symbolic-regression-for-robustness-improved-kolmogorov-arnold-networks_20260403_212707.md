# In-Context Symbolic Regression for Robustness-Improved Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.15250
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15250
- **Reference count:** 36
- **Primary result:** Up to 99.8% reduction in median test MSE vs. AutoSym via in-context symbolic regression.

## Executive Summary
This work tackles the instability of symbolic extraction from Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KANs) by proposing in-context symbolic regression methods. Greedy in-context Symbolic Regression (GSR) evaluates operators via brief fine-tuning of the full network, while Gated Matching Pursuit (GMP) amortizes this selection through differentiable gated layers. On ten SRBench Feynman tasks, GSR reduces median test MSE by up to 99.8% relative to the AutoSym baseline, with both in-context methods showing far tighter error distributions across hyper-parameter variations.

## Method Summary
The task is to improve robustness of symbolic extraction from trained numeric KANs. In GSR, for a selected edge, each candidate operator from a library is temporarily inserted, the entire network is fine-tuned for τ steps on the training set, and the operator yielding the lowest validation loss is chosen. GMP replaces each numeric edge with a differentiable softmax mixture over the operator library; training uses entropy and ℓ1 regularization alongside periodic top-k pruning to encourage specialisation. After training, edges are discretised via argmax (optionally followed by a restricted GSR refinement over the retained shortlist). Robustness is assessed via one-factor-at-a-time (OFAT) sweeps of network width, regularisation strength, and pruning cycles across ten SRBench Feynman datasets, comparing test MSE distributions.

## Key Results
- GSR achieves up to 99.8% reduction in median test MSE compared to AutoSym.
- In-context methods (GSR, GMP) exhibit substantially tighter robustness distributions in OFAT sweeps.
- GMP preserves most of GSR’s robustness benefit at lower computational cost, though it may suffer discretization failures under aggressive pruning.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** In-context candidate evaluation reduces error propagation and selection sensitivity by judging operators based on their contribution to the full network's end-to-end loss after brief adaptation, rather than on local spline curve-fitting alone.  
**Mechanism:** For a candidate operator on an edge, the method temporarily substitutes it, runs a short fine-tuning pass (τ steps) on the entire network, and measures validation loss. This tests the operator's compatibility with the current context (previously chosen symbolic edges and remaining numeric edges). The operator that yields the lowest loss after this "in-context" test is selected.  
**Core assumption:** Brief fine-tuning (τ steps) is sufficient to reveal the operator's global impact without requiring full convergence.  
**Evidence anchors:** GSR performs greedy, in-context selection by choosing edge replacements according to end-to-end loss improvement after brief fine-tuning (abstract). We temporarily replace e⋆ with g(including affine parameters), fine-tune the full model for τ steps on D, measure the resulting end-to-end loss (section 4.2).  
**Break condition:** If τ is too small, the fine-tuning may not capture downstream interactions; if too large, computational cost becomes prohibitive.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Amortising in-context selection via differentiable gated operator layers (GMP) reduces the per-edge candidate trial cost of GSR while preserving most of its robustness benefit, by learning operator preferences during standard training.  
**Mechanism:** Each edge is parametrised as a soft mixture over the operator library. Training the full network with gate regularisation (entropy + ℓ₁) and periodic top-k pruning encourages each edge to specialise on a small set of operators. After training, discretisation (argmax) selects a single operator per edge from this shortlist, optionally followed by restricted GSR refinement.  
**Core assumption:** The gated parametrisation, combined with end-to-end training and sparsity incentives, will converge to gate distributions that correctly identify the globally optimal operator for each edge without explicit per-candidate fine-tuning loops.  
**Evidence anchors:** GMP amortises this in-context selection by training a differentiable gated operator layer... after convergence, gates are discretised (abstract). During training, we periodically prune each edge to its top-k operators according to π(j,i)... After training, we discretise each edge by taking argmax (section 4.3).  
**Break condition:** Pruning schedules that are too aggressive or start too early can remove viable operator paths before gate probabilities have stabilised, leading to failure modes where GMP produces no valid model.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Variance compression via a scaled asinh transform stabilises gate learning by preventing extreme operator outputs from dominating the mixture, thereby improving the reliability of the amortised selection process.  
**Mechanism:** Raw operator outputs z are transformed as ˜z = asinh(z/s), where s is a learned scale. This is approximately linear for small |z| and logarithmic for large |z|, compressing heavy-tailed responses while preserving relative variations in the typical range.  
**Core assumption:** Without such compression, operators with inherently larger output scales could disproportionately influence loss and gradients, causing gates to specialise based on scale artefacts rather than functional correctness.  
**Evidence anchors:** Different operators can produce outputs with very different scales... To mitigate this, we compress each operator output z with a scaled asinh transform... damping extreme values while preserving small variations (section 4.3).  
**Break condition:** If the scale parameter s is poorly initialised or learns too slowly, the compression may be ineffective initially, leading to unstable early training.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: KAN edge parametrisation (splines/RBFs)**  
  Why needed: The starting point is a trained numeric KAN where each edge is a learnable univariate function. Understanding this representation is key to seeing why post-hoc per-edge fitting is a "local" operation and how it can be replaced.  
  Quick check: How does representing an edge as a spline basis (vs. a single analytic operator) affect the stability of fitting a symbolic candidate to its sampled curve?
- **Concept: Operator library and affine reparametrisation**  
  Why needed: Symbolic extraction maps numeric edges to a discrete choice from a fixed library (e.g., sin, exp, polynomials), each with continuous affine parameters. Identifiability issues arise because multiple operator families can fit a local spline similarly well after affine offset.  
  Quick check: Why might disabling a simplicity penalty during operator selection be necessary to isolate the effect of in-context evaluation?
- **Concept: Matching Pursuit (greedy selection template)**  
  Why needed: GSR adapts the classic "select atom → update residual" loop to the KAN symbolic conversion context, where the "residual improvement" is replaced by end-to-end loss reduction after fine-tuning.  
  Quick check: In what way does GSR's "update" step differ from traditional Matching Pursuit's re-estimation of coefficients?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Numeric KAN backbone -> Operator library -> Edge importance scorer -> In-context trial loop (GSR)  
Numeric KAN backbone -> Gated edge layer -> Variance compression unit -> Sparsity regularisers -> Pruning scheduler -> Discretisation module  
Both paths converge at: Optional refinement pass -> Final symbolic KAN

### Critical path
Training → Gate learning & pruning (GMP) / Edge ranking (GSR) → Candidate in-context evaluation or discretisation → Optional refinement → Final symbolic KAN.

### Design tradeoffs
- **GSR:** Higher robustness (in-context loss test) but cost scales as O(M·K·τ) where M = #edges to convert, K = library size.
- **GMP:** Amortises cost; training overhead is moderate, conversion is fast (discretisation + optional short refinement). Risk: pruning before gates stabilise.
- **Numeric parametrisation:** FastKAN (RBFs) can improve baseline robustness but does not eliminate sensitivity to per-edge fitting.

### Failure signatures
- **GMP:** Missing or NaN runs under shared schedule indicate pruning removed viable paths before gates separated.
- **AutoSym:** High dispersion in OFAT violins and large std. devs in seed repeats show instability from isolated fitting.
- **All:** Failure to recover ground-truth operators may stem from library non-inclusion or domain-bound identifiability.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Seed sensitivity at reference configuration:** Repeat each pipeline (AutoSym, FastKAN+AutoSym, GSR, FastKAN+GSR, GMP) 3 times with fixed hyper-parameters (width[5,2], λ=1e-2, 3 cycles) but different random seeds. Report mean±std test MSE.
2. **One-factor-at-a-time (OFAT) sweep:** For each dataset, vary one factor (hidden width m∈{5,10,20,50,100}; λ∈{1e-4,1e-3,1e-2,1e-1}; pruning cycles∈{1,3,5}) while holding others at reference. Collect test MSE for all valid runs and plot violin distributions (log-scale).
3. **Distribution-level statistical comparison:** For each dataset, identify the pipeline with lowest median OFAT MSE. Compare it to others using one-sided Mann–Whitney U (Holm-corrected) and Cliff’s δ. Report % median reduction vs AutoSym.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How robust are GSR and GMP to realistic scientific challenges such as high observational noise, sparse sampling, and distribution shifts that are common in real-world data? The authors explicitly state that their evaluation is limited to ten clean Feynman tasks and does not cover regimes like heavy label noise, covariate shift, sparse observations, or high-dimensional inputs.
- What quantitative metrics most reliably capture structural similarity between recovered and ground-truth symbolic expressions, and how do these metrics correlate with functional performance and human interpretability? The authors note reliance on test MSE and qualitative inspection, and call for measures like operator-set overlap, edit distance over expression trees, or equivalence-class scoring.
- What interaction effects exist between key hyper-parameters (e.g., network width, regularisation strength, pruning cycles) that critically affect robustness, and can a factorial design identify safe operating regions? The OFAT sweeps isolate individual factors but do not fully characterise higher-order interactions.
- How can GMP’s training schedule be optimised to reduce discretisation failures (e.g., premature pruning) while preserving its amortised efficiency advantages over greedy GSR? The authors attribute missing runs to a schedule-mismatch failure mode and suggest longer pre-pruning phase or milder pruning schedule.

## Limitations
- Exact definitions of "multiplication units" in MultKAN width `[m,2]` and iterative edge importance scores `s_e` are unspecified, hindering faithful reproduction.
- No ablation study isolates the contribution of the scaled `asinh` variance compression, leaving its necessity unquantified.
- Evaluation is limited to ten clean Feynman tasks; robustness under noise, sparsity, or distribution shift remains unknown.

## Confidence
- GSR robustness improvement: High
- GMP robustness preservation: Medium
- Necessity of asinh transform: Medium
