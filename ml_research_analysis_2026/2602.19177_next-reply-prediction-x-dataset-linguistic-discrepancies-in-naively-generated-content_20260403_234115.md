# Next Reply Prediction X Dataset: Linguistic Discrepancies in Naively Generated Content

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.19177
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19177
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Fine-tuned Qwen3 8B achieves high morphosyntactic similarity to human text (up to 0.9816), while a hybrid classifier detects synthetic content with macro F1 scores up to 0.7301, revealing persistent linguistic signatures in LLM outputs.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the validity risks of using Large Language Models (LLMs) as naive proxies for humans in computational social science. It introduces a novel history-conditioned reply prediction task on X (Twitter) data, comparing authentic human replies against synthetic content from both prompt-based and fine-tuned Qwen3 8B models in English and German. Results show that domain-specific fine-tuning substantially improves linguistic alignment with human writing patterns compared to prompting alone. Furthermore, a hybrid classifier combining lexical, semantic, and hand-crafted linguistic features can detect synthetic content with moderate success, with prompt-based outputs being easiest to identify. The study demonstrates that even "naive" LLM generations retain measurable, language-specific discrepancies from authentic human social media discourse.

## Method Summary
The core method constructs a dataset where, for each user, three authentic tweet-reply pairs are provided as "history" context, and the task is to generate a fourth reply to a new target tweet. Authentic human replies serve as the baseline. Synthetic replies are generated from a base Qwen3 8B model using only prompts and from a model fine-tuned on 5,000 in-domain examples per language. Linguistic alignment is measured by computing cosine similarity between mean feature vectors of the authentic corpus (O) and synthetic corpus (F or P) across quantitative, morphosyntactic, and semantic feature sets extracted via NeLa, spaCy, TweetEval, and Qwen embeddings. Synthetic content detection is framed as a 3-class classification problem (authentic, prompt-based synthetic, fine-tuned synthetic) using an XGBoost classifier on concatenated feature representations (tf-idf, fastText embeddings, extracted features).

## Key Results
- Fine-tuned models achieve markedly higher linguistic alignment with human content than prompt-based models; morphosyntactic similarity scores `s(O,F)` reach 0.9748 (German) and 0.9816 (English).
- A hybrid classifier (tf-idf + fastText + extracted features) achieves macro F1 scores of 0.7301 (German) and 0.6972 (English) for distinguishing authentic, prompt-based, and fine-tuned content.
- Prompt-based synthetic content is the most detectable, with F1 scores exceeding 0.8, while fine-tuned content is the hardest to distinguish from authentic human text.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** A history-conditioned reply prediction task isolates and tests linguistic style imitation by forcing models to condition on prior user behavior.
- **Mechanism:** By providing three authentic tweet-reply pairs as "history" and prompting the model to generate a fourth reply, the task creates a controlled scenario where the model must mimic a specific user's established patterns to succeed, rather than generating generic content.
- **Core assumption:** A user's linguistic style is partially identifiable and reproducible from a small sample of their conversational history.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "introducing a novel, history-conditioned reply prediction task on authentic X (formerly Twitter) data"
  - [section 3.1] Detailed description of constructing prompts with three historical tweet-reply pairs and a fourth target tweet.
  - [corpus] The published dataset structure explicitly includes "three tweet-reply pairs as 'history'".
- **Break condition:** If the provided history context is too sparse or inconsistent to define a stable user style, the model may default to generic generation, weakening the test of authentic imitation.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Domain-specific fine-tuning on human social media data significantly improves linguistic alignment compared to prompt engineering alone.
- **Mechanism:** Supervised fine-tuning with the authentic human reply as the target directly optimizes the model's parameters to replicate human stylistic patterns within the specific task format, whereas prompting alone only elicits the model's pre-existing, general-purpose capabilities.
- **Core assumption:** The gap between an LLM's default generation and authentic human style in a specific domain can be narrowed by adapting the model's weights on in-domain examples.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "fine-tuned models achieve substantially higher linguistic alignment with human content than prompt-based approaches"
  - [section 4.1] Table 1 consistently shows higher s(O,F) scores than s(O,P) across all feature sets and languages (e.g., morphosyntactic: EN 0.9816 vs. 0.9423).
  - [corpus] The dataset contains separate "synthetic fine-tuned (F)" and "synthetic only prompted (P)" columns for direct comparison.
- **Break condition:** If fine-tuning leads to overfitting on the training distribution (e.g., memorizing specific phrases) rather than learning generalizable stylistic patterns, alignment gains on held-out test data would diminish or reverse.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A hybrid classifier combining lexical (tf-idf), dense semantic (fastText, Qwen), and hand-crafted linguistic features (POS, emotion, etc.) provides the most robust detection of synthetic social media content.
- **Mechanism:** Different generation artifacts manifest in different linguistic layers; lexical n-grams capture surface word choice quirks, embeddings capture semantic distribution shifts, and extracted features (e.g., POS tags) capture morphosyntactic biases. Combining these creates a multi-faceted signature harder for models to systematically evade.
- **Core assumption:** The linguistic discrepancies between human and synthetic content are multi-dimensional and no single representation type captures all discriminative signals.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "a hybrid classifier (tf-idf + fastText + extracted features) achieves macro F1 scores of 0.7301 (German) and 0.6972 (English)"
  - [section 4.2] Table 2 shows the top-performing configurations always involve tf-idf + fastText + at least one extracted feature set.
  - [corpus] The analysis framework explicitly computes features from NeLa, spaCy, and TweetEval for combination with text embeddings.
- **Break condition:** If future LLM architectures close the gap in all feature dimensions simultaneously (e.g., matching human POS distributions *and* embedding clusters *and* lexical patterns), the marginal benefit of hybridization would vanish, and simpler detectors might suffice.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** **Linguistic Authenticity Metrics** (e.g., corpus-level cosine similarity `s(v_O, v_F)`).
  - Why needed here: To move beyond "plausibility" and quantitatively measure how closely the aggregate statistical profile of synthetic text matches human text across defined dimensions (quantitative, morphosyntactic, semantic).
  - Quick check question: A high morphosyntactic similarity score (e.g., 0.9748) means what about the synthetic content's POS tag distribution compared to the human baseline?
- **Concept:** **History-Conditioned Generation**.
  - Why needed here: It's the core experimental design that creates a fair, controlled comparison by giving both human and model the same conversational context and task, isolating the model's ability to imitate a specific user's style.
  - Quick check question: Why is using three prior tweet-reply pairs as context preferable to a simple instruction like "write like a Twitter user"?
- **Concept:** **Evaluation via Feature Vector Distance**.
  - Why needed here: Provides a normalized, comparable way to aggregate multiple linguistic features into a single alignment score, enabling direct comparison between prompt-based and fine-tuned outputs.
  - Quick check question: What is the difference between `s(O,F)` and `s(O,P)` in Table 1, and what does their gap indicate about the two generation methods?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**
Data Pipeline (Raw X data -> tweet-reply pairing -> user-based history organization -> filtering -> split) -> Generation Module (Prompt constructor -> Base/Fine-tuned Qwen3 8B inference -> output) -> Feature Extraction Suite (NeLa, spaCy, TweetEval, Qwen Embedding) -> Detection Module (Feature concatenation -> XGBoost training/evaluation -> F1 scoring)

**Critical path:** Data consistency -> generation reproducibility -> feature alignment across human/synthetic corpora -> classifier train/test splits. A break in any (e.g., mismatched preprocessing) invalidates similarity/detection results.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Feature completeness vs. cost:** Full NeLa+spaCy+TweetEval+embeddings is robust but slow; tf-idf alone is fast but misses deeper patterns.
- **Model specificity vs. generality:** Results are tied to Qwen3 8B and X/Twitter; applying to other models/platforms requires re-tuning/validation.
- **Dataset size vs. coverage:** 1000 samples per language is manageable but may not capture full user diversity; low-reply-frequency users were filtered.

**Failure signatures:**
- Similarity scores all near 1.0 (feature extraction failure or identical outputs).
- Detection F1 ~0.33 (random for 3 classes) indicates features or classifier setup is broken.
- Fine-tuned model performs worse than prompt-based on `s(O,F)` (training or data issue).

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Replication of Core Similarity:** Compute `s(O,F)` and `s(O,P)` for each feature set on the provided dataset. Confirm Table 1 values within small variance.
2. **Ablation of Feature Combinations:** Train XGBoost on each feature subset (e.g., only tf-idf, only TweetEval) to verify hybrid superiority per Table 2.
3. **Context Length Sensitivity:** Modify the history-conditioned prompt to use 1, 3, 5 prior pairs. Measure change in `s(O,F)` to confirm 3-pairs is sufficient or identify inflection points.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How temporally stable are the linguistic signatures of LLM-generated social media content as generation techniques and platform discourse norms evolve?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Future Directions: "investigating the temporal stability of linguistic signatures as generation techniques continue to evolve will be essential."
- **Why unresolved:** The study uses data from early 2023 and specific models; newer LLM versions and shifting social media language may alter discrepancy patterns, but this is untested.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Longitudinal replication with LLMs from different release periods and recent social media data, tracking changes in similarity metrics and detection accuracy over time.

- **Open Question 2:** To what extent do the observed linguistic discrepancies generalize across different social media platforms (e.g., Facebook, Reddit) with varying discourse norms and constraints?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Future Directions: "examining domain transfer across different social media platforms beyond X will help establish the generalizability of these linguistic signature patterns."
- **Why unresolved:** The research is confined to X (Twitter); platform-specific features like character limits, moderation policies, or community cultures may influence LLM generation and detection differently.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Building comparable history-conditioned datasets from multiple platforms and comparing feature distributions, similarity scores, and classifier performance across them.

- **Open Question 3:** Can adversarial training approaches effectively reduce the detectability of LLM-generated content while maintaining content quality and authenticity?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Future Directions: "exploring adversarial training approaches specifically designed to reduce detectability while maintaining content quality and authenticity represents a promising direction."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper shows detection is feasible, but adversarial methods to close the gap—balancing evasion with human-likeness—are unexplored and may involve trade-offs.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implementing adversarial frameworks where a generator fine-tuned to fool detectors is evaluated on both detection evasion rates and multi-dimensional authenticity metrics (e.g., morphosyntactic similarity, human evaluations).

- **Open Question 4:** Which specific multi-dimensional evaluation metrics (beyond current similarity scores) best capture subtle aspects of human communication patterns in social media?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Future Directions: "developing more nuanced evaluation metrics that capture subtle aspects of human communication patterns beyond current similarity measures could provide deeper insights."
- **Why unresolved:** Existing metrics (e.g., POS distributions, semantic similarity) may miss nuanced elements like argument coherence, cultural references, or irony that define authentic social interaction.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Correlating proposed new metrics with human judgments of authenticity in blind evaluations, or designing diagnostic tasks where specific linguistic nuances are controlled and measured.

## Limitations
- Dataset and code not fully released (GitHub URL missing; implementation details for NeLa, TweetEval, and preprocessing unspecified).
- Evaluation relies on a single model (Qwen3 8B) and only two languages; generalizability unknown.
- Sample size limited (1k per language) and heavy user filtering may skew distribution.
- No sensitivity tests on history length or fine-tuning data size reported.

## Confidence
- **History-conditioned task isolates style imitation:** Medium. The design is sound, but lack of empirical context-length analysis leaves break conditions unexplored.
- **Fine-tuning yields superior alignment:** High. Consistent cross-lingual gains in `s(O,F)` vs. `s(O,P)` are clear within the study.
- **Hybrid classifier is most robust for detection:** Medium. Strong results in Table 2, but reliance on unreleased feature pipelines and absence of cross-dataset validation temper confidence.
