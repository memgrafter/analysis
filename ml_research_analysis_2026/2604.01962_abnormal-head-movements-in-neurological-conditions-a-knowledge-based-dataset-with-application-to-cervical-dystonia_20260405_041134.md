# Abnormal Head Movements in Neurological Conditions: A Knowledge-Based Dataset with Application to Cervical Dystonia

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.01962  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01962  
- **Reference count:** 0  
- **Primary result:** Constructed NeuroPose-AHM dataset (2,756 patient-group records, 57 conditions) via multi-LLM extraction (κ=0.822) and applied to cervical dystonia with four-task framework: AHM classifier (F1=0.856), unified HNSI metric, validation (6.7% severe-band alignment), and significant movement-severity correlations (p<0.001).

## Executive Summary
This study addresses the absence of integrated resources for abnormal head movements (AHMs) in neurological disorders by constructing NeuroPose-AHM, a dataset derived from 1,430 papers using a multi-LLM extraction framework. The dataset spans 57 conditions with 2,756 patient-group records and achieves strong inter-LLM agreement (κ=0.822). Applied to cervical dystonia, the framework comprises four tasks: a multi-label AHM classifier, the Head-Neck Severity Index (HNSI) that normalizes heterogeneous clinical scales, validation against real-world data showing aligned severe-band proportions (6.7%), and bridge analysis revealing significant correlations between movement probabilities and HNSI scores (p<0.001).

## Method Summary
NeuroPose-AHM was built by querying PubMed/Scopus, converting PDFs to Markdown, and using dual LLMs (GPT-5.1, Claude) to extract structured data per paper with cross-evaluation and iterative refinement. Two cervical dystonia subsets were derived: CD-Quantitative (CD-Q, 113 records) with kinematic measurements and AHM labels, and CD-Clinical Scales (CD-CS, 809 records) with severity scores. Task 1 trained multi-label classifiers on CD-Q after preprocessing (label unification, feature engineering). Task 2 constructed HNSI by normalizing scores from TWSTRS, Tsui, TRS, and GDRS to head-neck maxima and averaging. Task 3 compared HNSI-derived severity bands to an external cohort. Task 4 correlated predicted movement probabilities from Task 1 with HNSI for overlapping papers.

## Key Results
- Dataset: 2,756 patient-group records from 1,430 papers across 57 neurological conditions, with κ=0.822 inter-LLM agreement.
- Cervical dystonia AHM classifier: Macro F1-score of 0.856 using Logistic Regression.
- HNSI validation: Literature-derived severe-band proportion (6.7%) aligned with real-world data (expected ~6–7%).
- Bridge analysis: Significant positive correlations between movement-type probabilities and HNSI scores (p < 0.001) across 24 overlapping papers.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1:** A multi-LLM extraction framework with cross-evaluation and iterative refinement produces reliable, structured clinical data. Two LLMs independently extract per paper; their outputs are judged across six dimensions (Completeness, Quantitative Accuracy, etc.). Disagreements trigger feedback rounds, and a weighted score selects the superior extraction. This assumes model outputs can be judged for clinical plausibility by a peer model without source text, and that targeted feedback improves quality. Evidence includes the reported κ=0.822 and Section 3.1.5 details.

**Mechanism 2:** The Head-Neck Severity Index (HNSI) normalizes heterogeneous scales by dividing each head/neck subscale score (from TWSTRS, Tsui, TRS, GDRS) by its maximum valid score (e.g., 35 for TWSTRS), clipping to range, and averaging across scales present. This assumes each scale’s maximum represents an equivalent upper bound of head-neck impairment. Evidence is in the abstract and Section 3.2.3, though equivalence of maxima is untested.

**Mechanism 3:** Correlating kinematic movement-type probabilities (from Task 1 classifier) with HNSI scores (from Task 2) provides internal validation. For 24 overlapping papers, Pearson correlation tests these probabilities (and their mean) against HNSI. A positive association suggests that papers with more complex movement profiles have higher clinical severity, supporting construct validity. Evidence includes significant correlations in abstract, Sections 3.2.4 & 4.6.4, and Table 15.

## Foundational Learning
- **Multi-Label Classification with Clinical Tabular Data:** Needed for Task 1 to predict co-occurring AHM types from sparse kinematic data. Macro-F1 is used over accuracy due to label imbalance; linear models may outperform tree-based methods for rare labels on small datasets. *Quick check:* Why might logistic regression outperform random forest for retrocollis in this dataset?
- **Heterogeneous Scale Normalization:** Core to HNSI; it equates maximum scores across instruments (TWSTRS 35, Tsui 20, TRS 8, GDRS 10) under an assumption of equivalent clinical severity ceilings. *Quick check:* What psychometric property must hold about the maximum scores of TWSTRS and TRS for their normalized values to be meaningfully averaged?
- **LLM-as-a-Judge for Extraction Reliability:** The cross-evaluation uses one LLM to judge another’s extraction without source text, automating peer review. This risks judge bias and missed context errors. *Quick check:* What is a key vulnerability when an LLM judges another LLM’s extraction without access to the source text?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Literature Ingestion -> Extraction Engine -> Refinement Loop -> Dataset Repository -> CD Analysis Pipeline  
- **Critical path:** Literature search (1,430 papers) -> Preprocessing -> Multi-LLM extraction with refinement -> Inter-LLM agreement validation -> CD subset filtering -> Task-specific modeling (classification, HNSI, correlation). The refinement loop (max 3 rounds) is most computationally intensive.  
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  1. *LLM vs. Human Curation:* Speed/scale vs. potential subtle clinical errors; κ=0.822 is strong but not perfect.  
  2. *HNSI Aggregation:* Simple mean across scales vs. weighted mean; current design treats all scales equally, but TWSTRS may be more robust than GDRS.  
  3. *Bridge Analysis Size:* Small overlapping subset (n=24 papers) limits power; tradeoff between full dataset linkage and sample size.  
- **Failure signatures:**  
  1. *Extraction Drift:* Low completeness (<4.0) or high-severity issues persisting after refinement.  
  2. *HNSI Calibration Failure:* Misalignment between literature-derived and real-world severe-band proportions.  
  3. *Bridge Analysis Disconnect:* Non-significant or negative correlations, indicating classifier probabilities don’t track severity.  
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Reproduce inter-LLM agreement: Run extraction on 50 manually annotated papers, compute κ, ICC, Jaccard.  
  2. HNSI Sensitivity Analysis: Recompute HNSI with alternative normalizations (median, weighting) and compare severity distributions.  
  3. Bridge Analysis Replication: Train Task 1 classifier, predict probabilities for 24 overlapping papers, compute Pearson correlation to confirm p<0.001.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Does the multi-label AHM classifier trained on cervical dystonia data generalize to other neurological conditions in the NeuroPose-AHM dataset?
2. Is the Head-Neck Severity Index (HNSI) a valid and generalizable severity metric for neurological conditions beyond cervical dystonia?
3. Does the HNSI maintain its calibration and clinical relevance when validated on larger, multi-center cohorts of cervical dystonia patients?
4. How does the multi-LLM extraction framework compare to alternative methods (e.g., single-LLM or human expert extraction) in terms of accuracy, completeness, and efficiency for constructing knowledge-based medical datasets?

## Limitations
- Dataset file structure and schema are undocumented, hindering direct replication.
- External validation dataset (Nakamura et al. 2019) is not publicly available, preventing exact Task 3 reproduction.
- HNSI assumes scale maxima represent equivalent clinical severity ceilings, a psychometric property not verified.
- Bridge analysis uses only 24 overlapping papers, risking overfitting and limited generalizability.
- Multi-LLM judges evaluate extraction without seeing source text, potentially missing context-dependent errors.

## Confidence
- **Extraction framework reliability (κ=0.822):** Medium  
- **HNSI normalization validity:** Low  
- **Bridge analysis construct validity:** Low–Medium
