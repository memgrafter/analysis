# From Early Encoding to Late Suppression: Interpreting LLMs on Character Counting Tasks

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.00778  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00778  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** LLMs encode character counts in early layers but later components actively suppress this correct signal, leading to failures on simple counting tasks.

## Executive Summary
Large language models (LLMs) exhibit impressive performance on complex benchmarks yet often fail at basic orthographic tasks such as counting characters. This study uses character counting as a minimal symbolic probe to investigate the internal mechanisms behind such failures. The authors analyze three model families (LLaMA, Qwen, Gemma) ranging from 2B to 9B parameters, combining probing classifiers, activation patching, logit lens, and attention tracing. They find that character-level information is reliably encoded in early and mid-layer representations (probe accuracy >80%) and can be aggregated across subwords. However, later layers—particularly the penultimate and final MLP and sometimes final attention heads—actively suppress this correct signal, a phenomenon termed “negative circuits.” This suppression overrides the internal answer, pushing the model toward degenerate output strategies (e.g., always predicting “1” or near‑random choices). The effect persists across scaling and after instruction tuning; quantitatively, the final layer can boost the probability of an incorrect default answer by ~30% (Qwen) or ~7% (LLaMA). The work concludes that symbolic failures stem not from missing representations but from structured interference in the computation graph.

## Method Summary
The study builds a controlled dataset of character counting prompts (e.g., “The number of p’s in apple is”) by sampling words from WordNet and Wiktionary. Target counts are balanced (1–3). Three model families (LLaMA, Qwen, Gemma) are examined in both base and instruction‑tuned variants.  

**Probing:** Linear classifiers (no bias) are trained on frozen hidden states from type‑disjoint splits to assess whether character count information is decodable at different layers and granularities (token, word, prompt level).  

**Logit lens:** For each layer, the hidden state is normalised (LayerNorm/RMSNorm), projected onto the vocabulary via the unembedding matrix, and softmaxed. The probability difference Δp = P(final predicted) – P(correct) tracks how the output distribution evolves.  

**Activation patching:** “Corrupted” prompts (where word, letter, or both are altered) are used to identify components where restoring clean activations recovers the correct answer (positive restoration) or worsens it (negative circuits). Normalised restoration Prestored is computed across layers, attention heads, and MLPs.  

**Attention tracing** complements these analyses by visualising attention patterns that may influence count aggregation.

## Key Results
- Character count information is encoded in early to mid‑layer representations; linear probes achieve >80% accuracy across token, word, and prompt levels.  
- Later layers—especially the penultimate and final MLP and occasionally the final attention heads—suppress the correct signal (negative circuits), causing the model to favour incorrect default answers.  
- Suppression is robust across model scale (2B → 9B) and persists after instruction tuning; the final layer can increase the probability of a wrong answer by up to ~30% (Qwen) or ~7% (LLaMA).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Early transformer layers capture orthographic features at the character and subword level, making the count readily available in the residual stream. This information is linearly decodable throughout much of the network. However, later layers introduce interference that actively reduces the probability of the correct count. Activation patching demonstrates that replacing the activations of certain late MLP or attention heads with those from a clean (correct) run restores the proper signal, confirming their causal role in suppression. The authors call these components “negative circuits.” The suppression likely stems from learned heuristics (e.g., a bias toward answering “1”) that override the symbolic reasoning needed for accurate counting, even though the correct answer is internally represented.

## Foundational Learning
- **Linear Probing**  
  *Why needed:* To test whether specific information (character count) is present in hidden states in a way that is linearly separable, without modifying model parameters.  
  *Quick check:* Freeze a model, extract activations for a set of inputs, train a linear classifier to predict the count; above‑chance accuracy indicates encoding.  

- **Activation Patching**  
  *Why needed:* To causally attribute model behaviour to individual components by swapping activations between a “clean” (correct) and a “corrupted” (incorrect) run.  
  *Quick check:* For a mispredicted example, replace the activations of a candidate component (e.g., MLP) with those from a correct prediction; if the output shifts toward the correct answer, the component is positively causal, while a shift away indicates suppression.  

- **Logit Lens**  
  *Why needed:* To monitor how the output distribution evolves across layers by projecting intermediate representations onto the vocabulary space.  
  *Quick check:* At each layer, normalise the residual stream, multiply by the unembedding matrix, apply softmax, and record the probability of the correct answer and the final predicted token; trends reveal when the model’s internal answer diverges from its eventual output.  

- **Attention Tracing**  
  *Why needed:* To understand how information flows between tokens, especially whether relevant character‑bearing tokens are attended when computing a count.  
  *Quick check:* Visualise attention heads for the final token; check if weights are concentrated on tokens containing the queried character.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input tokens → Token embeddings → Transformer blocks (LayerNorm → Attention → Residual → LayerNorm → MLP → Residual) repeated N times → Final LayerNorm → Unembedding matrix → Logits → Softmax.  
Components of interest: attention heads (especially in later layers), MLPs (penultimate and final), residual streams, and layer norms.

**Critical path:**  
1. Early layers extract character‑level features from each token and embed them in the residual stream.  
2. Mid layers aggregate these features across positions, maintaining a linear count signal (verified by probes).  
3. The final token’s representation is read out after passing through later layers, where certain MLPs and attention heads distort (suppress) the count signal before the unembedding.  

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Linear probing assumes linear separability; non‑linear encodings may be missed.  
- Corruption for patching introduces noise; choosing a threshold (e.g., Δ > 0.5) is arbitrary and may exclude relevant components.  
- Larger sample sizes reduce variance but increase compute; the paper varies between 100 and 1000 examples without systematic exploration.  
- Type‑disjoint splits prevent data leakage but limit the training set size for probes.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Probe accuracy at chance level (<50%) suggests no encoding.  
- Logit lens shows no increase in Δp in later layers, indicating absence of suppression.  
- Activation patching yields Prestored ≈ 0 for candidate components, meaning they are not causally involved.  
- Model outputs a constant default answer (e.g., “1”) across diverse inputs.

**Exactly 3 first experiments:**  
1. Train linear probes on each layer (token, word, prompt levels) to replicate the early/mid‑layer encoding pattern (>80% accuracy).  
2. Apply logit lens to a held‑out set of mispredicted examples to locate layers where the correct answer’s probability declines.  
3. Perform activation patching on the final MLP and final attention heads of the penultimate and final layers to quantify Prestored and identify negative circuits.

None.

## Limitations
- Dataset generation procedures (WordNet/Wiktionary sampling, count balance beyond “balanced”) are not fully specified, risking dataset shift and affecting probe consistency.  
- Key hyperparameters (sample sizes for logit lens/patching, corruption threshold Δ>0.5) are chosen arbitrarily; their sensitivity is unexplored.  
- Probe training details (early stopping criteria, optimizer settings) may influence accuracy and reproducibility.

## Confidence
- Early/mid‑layer character encoding (probe accuracy >80%): **High**  
- Existence of negative circuits suppressing correct signal (activation patching, logit lens): **Medium**  
- Persistence of suppression across scaling (2B→9B) and after instruction tuning: **Medium**  
- Quantitative impact size (e.g., ~30% Qwen, ~7% LLaMA): **Low**

## Next Checks
1. Fully specify and release the dataset generation script (including WordNet queries, filtering, and count balance) and repeat probe experiments to confirm encoding stability.  
2. Systematically vary the corruption threshold for activation patching (e.g., Δ thresholds from 0.1 to 0.9) and sample sizes, measuring Prestored consistency to assess robustness of negative circuit identification.  
3. Extend the analysis to additional model families (e.g., GPT‑2, Mistral) and larger scales (13B+), testing whether suppression magnitude correlates with size or architecture.
