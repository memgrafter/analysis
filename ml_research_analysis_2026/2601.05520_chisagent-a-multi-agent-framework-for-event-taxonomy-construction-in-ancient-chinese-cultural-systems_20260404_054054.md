# CHisAgent: A Multi-Agent Framework for Event Taxonomy Construction in Ancient Chinese Cultural Systems

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.05520
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05520
- **Reference count**: 36
- **Primary result**: CHisAgent constructs an event taxonomy from the *Twenty-Four Histories* achieving 75.13% Coverage Rate and 68.89% Node Recall, outperforming all baseline methods.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the challenge of automatically constructing event taxonomies for ancient Chinese cultural systems, where manual curation is costly and large language models (LLMs) often lack historical reasoning capabilities. It introduces **CHisAgent**, a multi‑agent framework that decomposes taxonomy construction into three stages: a bottom‑up **Inducer** extracts an initial hierarchy from raw historical corpora, a top‑down **Expander** adds missing intermediate concepts using world knowledge, and an evidence‑guided **Enricher** integrates external structured resources (e.g., CBDB) to ensure faithfulness. Built on the *Twenty-Four Histories* corpus, the framework yields a large‑scale taxonomy covering eight domains. Experimental results show superior coverage, recall, and novelty, while cross‑corpus tests on Korean, Japanese, and Vietnamese records demonstrate generalizability. The work provides a scalable, evidence‑aware solution for historical knowledge organization.

## Method Summary
CHisAgent processes raw historical texts through a sequential pipeline of three specialized agents. The **Inducer** uses an LLM to identify event mentions and cluster them into a coarse, bottom‑up hierarchy. The **Expander** then detects gaps—missing intermediate nodes—in this hierarchy and employs the LLM’s world knowledge to propose plausible expansions, creating a more refined structure. Finally, the **Enricher** cross‑references the taxonomy with external structured resources to validate and supplement node definitions, grounding the taxonomy in concrete evidence and improving coverage. This staged approach balances data‑driven induction, conceptual expansion, and evidence‑based grounding.

## Key Results
- CHisAgent achieves **75.13% Coverage Rate**, **68.89% Node Recall**, and **77.70% Novelty** on the *Twenty-Four Histories* corpus, outperforming all baseline methods.
- Ablation studies confirm the **Enricher** agent significantly boosts both coverage and recall.
- Cross‑corpus evaluation shows generalizability: coverage reaches **80.50% on Japanese**, **75.51% on Korean**, and **60.55% on Vietnamese** historical corpora.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
CHisAgent’s effectiveness arises from decomposing the complex taxonomy construction task into focused stages that address specific sub‑challenges. The Inducer captures the raw event distribution from the corpus but yields sparse hierarchies. The Expander injects missing intermediate concepts using the LLM’s parametric knowledge, bridging abstraction gaps and improving usability. However, LLMs can hallucinate or misalign with historical facts; the Enricher mitigates this by grounding nodes in external structured resources, ensuring factual consistency. This staged, evidence‑driven pipeline blends creativity (via expansion) with reliability (via enrichment), yielding higher coverage and recall while maintaining novelty. The multi‑agent design also isolates errors, enabling targeted refinements.

## Foundational Learning
**Event‑Centered Taxonomy**  
*Why needed*: Ancient Chinese histories are organized around discrete events; an event‑centric structure naturally indexes narratives.  
*Quick check*: Can you distinguish an event (e.g., “Battle of Red Cliffs”) from a broader phenomenon (e.g., “Three Kingdoms period”)?

**Bottom‑Up Induction**  
*Why needed*: Raw texts contain numerous event mentions; clustering them bottom‑up produces a data‑driven initial taxonomy that reflects the corpus’s inherent distribution.  
*Quick check*: Given a set of event descriptions, how would you group them into a hierarchy without predefined categories?

**Top‑Down Expansion**  
*Why needed*: Bottom‑up hierarchies often lack explicit intermediate nodes (e.g., “Military Conflict” between “Battle” and “War”). Top‑down expansion fills these gaps using world knowledge to create a more usable taxonomy.  
*Quick check*: What intermediate category would you insert between “Battle of Changping” and “Warring States period”?

**Evidence‑Guided Enrichment**  
*Why needed*: LLMs can fabricate details; integrating external structured resources (like CBDB) grounds the taxonomy in verified historical facts, reducing hallucination.  
*Quick check*: For a node “Imperial Examination,” what external database might provide concrete instances and attributes?

**Cross‑Cultural Generalization**  
*Why needed*: East Asian historical records share influences but also diverge; testing on these corpora reveals the taxonomy’s adaptability beyond Chinese sources.  
*Quick check*: Would a Chinese event taxonomy readily cover a Japanese event like the “Gempei War”? Why or why not?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component Map: Inducer → Expander → Enricher (sequential pipeline; each agent transforms the previous output).

Critical Path:
1. Raw corpus input to Inducer → initial event hierarchy.
2. Expander analyzes hierarchy, identifies missing intermediates, and expands it.
3. Enricher integrates external evidence to validate and enrich nodes.
4. Final taxonomy output.

Design Tradeoffs:
- Bottom‑up vs. top‑down: Purely bottom‑up yields sparse hierarchies; excessive top‑down expansion risks anachronisms. The three‑stage design balances both.
- LLM autonomy vs. evidence: Relying solely on LLMs increases novelty but decreases faithfulness; the Enricher trades off some creativity for reliability.
- Scalability vs. depth: Processing large corpora with LLMs is costly; the Inducer uses efficient prompting, while Expander and Enricher add overhead.

Failure Signatures:
- Low Coverage Rate → Expander possibly failed to add necessary intermediates or Enricher failed to match external evidence.
- Low Node Recall → Inducer missed many event mentions, or Enricher removed nodes as unfaithful.
- High inter‑annotator disagreement in human eval → ambiguous node definitions or poor hierarchical coherence, likely from Expander’s noisy expansions.

First Experiments:
1. Minimal pipeline test: Run CHisAgent on a small subset (e.g., one book of the *Twenty‑Four Histories*) to verify end‑to‑end functionality and inspect intermediate outputs.
2. Ablation study: Disable the Expander or Enricher to quantify their individual contributions to coverage and recall on a held‑out set.
3. Baseline comparison: Compare CHisAgent against a simpler LLM‑only approach (direct prompting) on taxonomy completeness and faithfulness using a manually curated sample.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How can the inherent biases of modern LLMs—such as projecting contemporary conceptual structures onto historical contexts or hallucinating abstractions from sparse evidence—be systematically mitigated in automated taxonomy construction for ancient history?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The Limitations section explicitly states: “LLM-based taxonomy construction inevitably inherits biases from model pretraining. In particular, modern language models may project contemporary conceptual structures onto historical contexts or introduce hallucinated abstractions when expanding sparse historical evidence.”
- **Why unresolved**: The paper identifies this as a core limitation but does not propose or test specific bias‑mitigation strategies (e.g., domain‑specific fine‑tuning, constraint‑based prompting, or integration with grounded historical knowledge bases). Resolving this is critical for ensuring historical faithfulness.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Comparative experiments using different LLM variants (e.g., historically fine‑tuned vs. general‑purpose) with controlled prompts designed to reduce anachronism. Human evaluation specifically targeting anachronistic or culturally inappropriate nodes in the generated taxonomy. Development of a “historical consistency” metric validated by domain experts.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: To what extent does the reliance on the *Twenty‑Four Histories*—a corpus reflecting official, elite perspectives—systematically omit or misrepresent folk practices, regional traditions, and informal social dynamics, and how can the CHisAgent framework be adapted to incorporate such underrepresented cultural knowledge?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The Limitations section notes: “the *Twenty‑Four Histories* primarily reflect official and elite perspectives. Certain aspects of ancient Chinese culture, such as folk practices, regional traditions, and informal social dynamics, are underrepresented, limiting the completeness of the resulting taxonomy.”
- **Why unresolved**: The framework’s current pipeline is built upon this specific corpus. The paper demonstrates cross‑cultural generalizability to East Asian records but does not address intra‑cultural gaps (e.g., lower‑class, non‑elite, or non‑Han perspectives within Chinese history). The Enricher uses external structured resources (CBDB), but these may also share similar biases.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Running CHisAgent on supplementary corpora (e.g., unearthed bamboo slips, local gazetteers, folk literature) from the same historical periods and quantitatively/qualitatively comparing the resulting taxonomies. Expert analysis to identify nodes exclusive to non‑elite corpora and assess their historical validity. Designing Enricher candidate sources specifically aimed at marginalized social groups.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Why does the taxonomy’s cross‑corpus Coverage Rate vary significantly across East Asian historical records (Japan: 80.50%, Korea: 75.51%, Vietnam: 60.55%), and what specific cultural, narrative, or historical factors explain Vietnam’s notably lower alignment?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] Table 3 shows the cross‑corpus results. The paper states: “With the exception of CHED, all methods achieve their lowest coverage on the Vietnamese corpus, indicating greater divergence from Chinese‑centered taxonomies compared to the Korean and Japanese records.”
- **Why unresolved**: The paper offers a high‑level cultural divergence explanation but does not conduct a fine‑grained analysis of *why* the Vietnamese record (*Dai Viet Su Ky Toan Thu*) diverges. Possible reasons include differences in historiographical traditions, political structures, or event conceptualization. The case study on tribute perspectives is suggestive but not comprehensive.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A detailed comparative analysis of the event types extracted from the Vietnamese corpus that failed to match the Chinese taxonomy. This should involve: 1) clustering uncovered event types to identify missing domains or sub‑domains, 2) qualitative review by historians of Vietnamese and Chinese traditions to pinpoint narrative or conceptual mismatches (e.g., different emphasis on certain ceremonies, administrative terms, or social organizations), and 3) testing whether adjusting the domain definitions or expanding the Chinese taxonomy with Vietnam‑specific nodes improves coverage.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: The human evaluation yielded a relatively low inter‑annotator agreement (0.38). What are the underlying sources of this disagreement (e.g., ambiguous node definitions, differing expert interpretations of historical concepts, or flaws in the evaluation criteria), and how can evaluation protocols for historical taxonomies be made more robust?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] Section 4.7 states: “The inter‑annotator agreement across the five evaluation dimensions is 0.38”. The paper uses five criteria (Clarity, Hierarchical Coherence, In‑domain Orthogonality, Cross‑domain Orthogonality, Completeness) but does not analyze the low agreement.
- **Why unresolved**: A low Krippendorff’s alpha or similar agreement score indicates that the evaluation task or guidelines are not sufficiently objective for domain experts. This threatens the validity of all human evaluation results, including the conclusion that CHisAgent provides a “balanced performance.” The root cause could be inherent subjectivity in historical categorization, poorly defined scoring rubrics, or the complexity of the task.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A post‑evaluation debriefing with the three annotators to identify specific nodes or criteria causing disagreement. Refining the evaluation guidelines based on this feedback (e.g., providing more historical examples for each score level, clarifying “orthogonality”). A pilot study measuring agreement on a smaller, curated set of clear‑cut vs. ambiguous nodes. Exploring alternative evaluation methods, such as forced‑choice comparisons between taxonomies or task‑based utility assessments (e.g., using the taxonomy for a specific historical query).

### Open Question 5
- **Question**: The CHisAgent framework is explicitly designed for an “event‑centered” taxonomy. How can this approach be extended or hybridized to effectively represent and organize non‑event‑based cultural phenomena (e.g., philosophical traditions, ideological shifts, artistic styles, long‑term social trends) that are crucial to ancient Chinese cultural systems?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The Limitations section states: “our event‑centered taxonomy design, while effective for modeling historical narratives and social processes, may not fully capture cultural phenomena that evolve gradually or are not naturally event‑driven, such as philosophical traditions or long‑term ideological shifts.” The abstract also states the taxonomy covers “events” and “event types.”
- **Why unresolved**: The paper does not explore this gap. The Inducer, Expander, and Enricher are all tailored to discrete, trigger‑based events. Representing gradual, process‑oriented, or conceptual phenomena may require different extraction cues (e.g., sustained descriptions, abstract nouns, thematic clusters) and hierarchical relations (e.g., “influenced by,” “evolved into” rather than “is a type of”).
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Developing and integrating a supplementary “phenomenon” agent module that processes non‑eventive text spans (e.g., passages describing a philosophy’s core tenets, the characteristics of an art period). Defining a hybrid ontology where event nodes and phenomenon nodes can have cross‑cutting relations (e.g., an “Event” node “Publication of *Analects*” is linked to a “Phenomenon” node “Confucianism”). Evaluating the utility of this hybrid structure for tasks beyond event extraction, such as tracing intellectual history or cultural diffusion.

## Limitations
- **Corpus bias**: The taxonomy is built from the *Twenty‑Four Histories*, which reflect official and elite perspectives, likely omitting folk practices, regional traditions, and informal social dynamics.
- **LLM biases**: The framework inherits pretraining biases, including anachronistic projections and hallucinations, especially when expanding sparse evidence.
- **Evaluation reliability**: Human evaluation showed low inter‑annotator agreement (0.38), and reported metrics lack statistical confidence intervals or significance testing.

## Confidence
- **Framework decomposition** (Inducer, Expander, Enricher): High
- **In‑domain performance superiority** (coverage/recall improvements): Medium
- **Enricher’s contribution** (ablation results): Medium
- **Human evaluation outcomes** (clarity, coherence, orthogonality): Low
- **Cross‑corpus generalizability** (to East Asian records): Medium

## Next Checks
1. **Statistical robustness**: Re‑run all experiments with multiple random seeds, report means and standard deviations, and perform paired significance tests (e.g., t‑test) to verify improvements.
2. **Rigorous human study**: Engage at least three domain experts to evaluate a stratified sample of taxonomy nodes on clarity, coherence, and orthogonality using Likert scales; compute inter‑rater reliability (e.g., Fleiss’ κ).
3. **Broadened corpus coverage**: Apply CHisAgent to additional ancient Chinese sources (e.g., pre‑Qin classics, Tang poetry, inscriptions) and to more East Asian corpora (e.g., Korean historical records) to assess domain and cultural transferability.
