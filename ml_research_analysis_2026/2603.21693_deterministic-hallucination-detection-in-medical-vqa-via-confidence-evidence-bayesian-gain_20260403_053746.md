# Deterministic Hallucination Detection in Medical VQA via Confidence-Evidence Bayesian Gain

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2603.21693
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21693
- **Reference count**: 31
- **Primary result**: CEBaG achieved state-of-the-art hallucination detection in medical VQA, with average AUC 67.9% vs VASE's 59.6% (8.2% gain) in 13 of 16 settings.

## Executive Summary
Medical visual question answering (VQA) with multimodal large language models (MLLMs) is prone to hallucinations, which are especially dangerous in clinical settings. Existing detection methods such as Semantic Entropy (SE) and Vision-Amplified Semantic Entropy (VASE) rely on computationally expensive stochastic sampling and external models. This paper introduces Confidence-Evidence Bayesian Gain (CEBaG), a deterministic approach that requires only three forward passes through the model. CEBaG produces a hyperparameter‑free score by combining token‑level predictive variance (σ) and evidence magnitude (E), both derived from the model’s own log‑probabilities. The authors evaluate CEBaG across four medical MLLMs and three VQA benchmarks (16 settings total), finding that it attains the highest area under the curve (AUC) in 13 settings and improves average AUC over VASE by 8.2 percentage points (67.9% vs. 59.6%). Moreover, CEBaG reduces computational cost by an order of magnitude and eliminates the need for external models or sampling.

## Method Summary
CEBaG computes a hallucination score using two complementary signals extracted from the model’s internal statistics: token‑level predictive variance (σ) and evidence magnitude (E). The method requires exactly three forward passes of the MLLM on the given input (original and two deterministic transformations) to obtain token log‑probabilities. From these, σ is calculated as the variance of predicted probabilities across the passes, capturing uncertainty. Evidence magnitude E is derived from the cumulative log‑probability of the generated answer, reflecting how strongly the model supports its output. The final score is a simple function of σ and E, with no hyperparameters to tune. This design makes CEBaG both efficient (three forward passes) and deterministic (same input yields same score), while avoiding external models or stochastic sampling.

## Key Results
- CEBaG achieved the highest AUC in 13 out of 16 medical VQA settings.
- Average AUC improved by 8.2 points over VASE (67.9% vs. 59.6%).
- Computational cost reduced by an order of magnitude compared to sampling‑based baselines; no external models required.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
CEBaG works because it jointly models two aspects of a prediction: uncertainty and evidential support. Token‑level predictive variance σ measures how much the model’s output distribution fluctuates across the three forward passes, indicating lack of confidence. Evidence magnitude E aggregates the log‑probability of the entire generated answer, representing how well the answer aligns with the model’s learned knowledge. Hallucinations tend to exhibit high variance (the model is unsure) and low evidence (the answer is poorly supported). By combining these signals into a single score, CEBaG effectively separates hallucinated from factual responses. The deterministic three‑pass procedure ensures consistent estimation without stochastic noise, and the reliance on internal log‑probabilities removes the need for auxiliary components.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Image+Question → MLLM (3 forward passes) → Token log‑probability extraction → Compute σ and E → Aggregate into final CEBaG score.

**Critical path**  
1. Encode input (image and question).  
2. Perform three forward passes through the MLLM (with fixed deterministic variations).  
3. Extract token‑level log‑probabilities from each pass.  
4. Calculate σ (variance across passes) and E (cumulative log‑probability gain).  
5. Combine σ and E via a hyperparameter‑free function to produce the final score.  
Failure in any step (e.g., missing logits, crash) aborts the pipeline.

**Design tradeoffs**  
CEBaG prioritizes efficiency and simplicity, achieving deterministic scoring with only three passes. However, it requires access to token‑level log‑probabilities, limiting its use to white‑box MLLMs. The method assumes that log‑probabilities are well‑calibrated and that three passes suffice to estimate uncertainty. It also does not incorporate visual grounding explicitly, which may affect performance on certain hallucinations. The tradeoff is a lightweight, interpretable detector that may not generalize to all model families without adaptation.

**Failure signatures**  
- High σ due to model instability (e.g., poor initialization) rather than genuine hallucination.  
- Low E for rare but correct answers, causing false positives.  
- Sensitivity to tokenization: different tokenizers could produce inconsistent σ/E values.  
- Inability to detect semantically coherent hallucinations that maintain high confidence and evidence (e.g., plausible‑yet‑wrong facts).  
- Preprocessing choices (e.g., which tokens to consider) may inadvertently introduce tunable hyperparameters.

**Three first experiments**  
1. Distribution separation: Compute CEBaG scores for a held‑out set of known correct and hallucinated answers on one benchmark; verify that scores are significantly higher for hallucinations.  
2. Ablation of three‑pass design: Replace the three passes with a single‑pass entropy measure and compare AUC to assess the contribution of the multi‑pass variance.  
3. Robustness to log‑prob perturbation: Artificially add noise to the token log‑probabilities and measure changes in CEBaG’s AUC to gauge sensitivity to model calibration.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How can CEBaG be adapted for black‑box MLLMs that do not expose token log‑probabilities?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors state that a limitation of CEBaG is its reliance on access to model log‑probabilities, restricting it to white‑box models, and they suggest extending CEBaG to black‑box settings via output perturbation as future work.
- **Why unresolved**: Without direct log‑probabilities, the core signals—token‑level predictive variance σ and evidence gain G—cannot be computed as defined. Proposing a robust proxy that preserves the original semantics while requiring only model outputs or softmax probabilities is non‑trivial.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Design a perturbation‑based approximation (e.g., random token masking, synonym replacement, or multiple decoding passes with varied prompts) to estimate σ and E. Evaluate on a black‑box medical MLLM (e.g., GPT‑4V) and demonstrate that detection AUC is comparable to that of the original CEBaG on white‑box models across the same benchmarks.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Does CEBaG generalize to hallucination detection in medical report generation, where outputs are longer and more complex?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors mention exploring applications in report generation as a direction for future work.
- **Why unresolved**: CEBaG was validated on short VQA answers (often a single sentence). Report generation entails paragraph‑length outputs; aggregating σ and E over many tokens may require different normalization or weighting strategies. The effectiveness of the current signals in long‑form text remains unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Apply CEBaG (with possible adaptations such as chunk‑wise scoring) to a standard report generation dataset (e.g., MIMIC‑CXR). Correlate CEBaG scores with expert annotations of hallucinations in generated reports and compare AUC against existing detectors (SE, VASE, RadFlag).

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: How can CEBaG be applied to multi‑turn clinical dialogue, where hallucinations might accumulate across turns?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors propose exploring applications in multi‑turn clinical dialogue as part of future work.
- **Why unresolved**: The current CEBaG treats each turn independently, ignoring dialogue history. In a multi‑turn setting, a model’s confidence and visual grounding may be influenced by prior exchanges, and early hallucinations could cascade. A per‑turn score may fail to capture these dependencies.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Use an existing multi‑turn medical dialogue dataset with visual inputs. Compute turn‑level CEBaG scores and analyze temporal patterns (e.g., whether high σ early in the dialogue predicts later errors). Develop and test a context‑aware variant (e.g., sliding‑window aggregation) and measure improvements in hallucination detection AUC compared to the turn‑independent version.

## Limitations
- Generalization: Effectiveness not proven beyond the four tested medical MLLMs and three benchmarks; may not transfer to other domains or architectures.
- Dependence on internal representations: Relies on token‑level log‑probabilities; performance may vary with model architecture, tokenization, and calibration quality.
- Clinical validation: No human expert assessment to confirm that detected hallucinations are clinically meaningful; preprocessing may introduce implicit tuning.

## Confidence
- **High**: Deterministic three‑forward‑pass procedure; claimed AUC improvements on tested in‑domain medical VQA settings (13/16); computational reduction vs. sampling‑based baselines.
- **Medium**: Robustness to diverse medical MLLM backbones (only four tested); broad applicability to non‑medical VQA or other hallucination types.
- **Low**: Real‑world clinical utility; theoretical guarantees linking σ+E to hallucination probability beyond empirical correlation.
