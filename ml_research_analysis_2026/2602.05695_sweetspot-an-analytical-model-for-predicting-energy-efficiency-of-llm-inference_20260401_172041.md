# SweetSpot: An Analytical Model for Predicting Energy Efficiency of LLM Inference

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.05695
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05695
- Reference count: 31
- Primary result: SweetSpot predicts per-token LLM inference energy with 1.79% mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), identifies a "sweet spot" of peak efficiency, and shows misaligned configurations can be up to 33.41× less efficient.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the inaccurate linear modeling of LLM inference energy, which fails to capture the non-linear relationship arising from Transformer autoregressive generation. It introduces SweetSpot, an analytical model derived from computational (FLOPs) and memory-access complexity, explicitly modeling the quadratic cost of the input processing (prefill) phase and the linear cost of autoregressive decoding. Evaluated across diverse 1B–9B parameter models on NVIDIA H100 GPUs, SweetSpot achieves a mean 1.79% MAPE in predicting per-token energy for input/output lengths from 64 to 4096 tokens. The model reveals and predicts a distinct "sweet spot" of peak energy efficiency for short-to-moderate inputs and medium-length outputs, with misaligned configurations (long inputs, short outputs) being far less efficient.

## Method Summary
The task is to predict per-token energy consumption (Etok) as a function of input length (nin) and output length (nout). The authors derive an analytical model from first principles: prefill attention costs scale quadratically with nin due to full self-attention, while decode costs scale linearly with nout because each new token attends to all previous tokens. The resulting formula combines FLOPs and memory-access terms: Etok = θ0 + (θ1·nin²)/nout + θ2·nin + (θ3·nin)/nout + θ4·nout + (θ5/nout). Parameters θ0–θ5 are fitted to measured energy data using non-linear least squares. Experiments run inference via TensorRT-LLM on NVIDIA H100 GPUs, measuring total energy by integrating GPU power samples (500 ms interval) over the inference duration and dividing by nout. The optimal output length for a given input is predicted as nout* = sqrt((θ1·nin² + θ3·nin + θ5)/θ4), i.e., the sweet spot that minimizes Etok.

## Key Results
- SweetSpot achieves a mean 1.79% MAPE in predicting per-token energy across nin and nout ranging from 64 to 4096 tokens, evaluated on OPT, LLaMA, Gemma, Falcon, Qwen2, and Granite models (1B–9B parameters).
- The model uncovers a "sweet spot" where energy per token is minimized: configurations with short-to-moderate inputs and medium-length outputs are most efficient.
- Misaligned configurations (long inputs combined with short outputs) can be up to 33.41× less energy-efficient than the sweet spot.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The mechanism hinges on accurately modeling the two distinct phases of autoregressive inference. During prefill, the entire input sequence is processed in parallel, and self-attention computes pairwise interactions between all tokens, leading to quadratic computational and memory-access costs with respect to nin. During decode, each new token is generated sequentially; it attends to all previous tokens, making the cost per token linear in the current sequence length (which grows with nout). By combining these phase-specific costs into a unified expression and fitting a small set of parameters to empirical energy data, SweetSpot captures the non-linear interplay between nin and nout. The sweet spot arises because increasing nout amortizes the quadratic prefill cost over more tokens (reducing the (θ1·nin²)/nout term) but eventually incurs linear decode cost (θ4·nout) that dominates. The model's parameters absorb hardware-specific characteristics (e.g., GPU memory bandwidth, compute capabilities) and implementation details (e.g., kernel optimizations in TensorRT-LLM), enabling accurate predictions within the tested regime.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Synthetic Request Generator -> TensorRT-LLM Inference Engine -> GPU Power Sampler (pynvml/ExaMon) -> Energy Integration -> Per-Token Energy Calculation -> Non-linear Least Squares Fitting -> SweetSpot Model

**Critical Path:**  
The time-critical path runs from generating a dataset with specific (nin, nout) through inference execution, power sampling, and energy integration to yield Etok for that configuration. This path must be repeated for many (nin, nout) pairs to collect fitting data.

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- Batch size: Fixed at 1024 for consistency; larger batches improve throughput but may alter energy per token. The model does not account for batch size, limiting its scope.
- Power sampling rate: 500 ms balances accuracy and overhead; higher rates improve integration precision but increase monitoring overhead.
- Synthetic vs real data: Synthetic datasets ensure controlled nin/nout but may not reflect real query distributions; real workloads could introduce additional variability.
- Model complexity: The six-parameter formula captures key non-linearities but risks collinearity; simpler models might generalize worse, more complex ones may overfit.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- Mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) significantly above 2% on test configurations.
- Fitted parameters with unphysical signs (e.g., θ1 < 0) or extremely large magnitudes.
- Predicted sweet spot (nout*) does not align with the observed minimum in Etok vs nout curves.
- High variance in repeated Etok measurements for the same (nin, nout) configuration.

**First Experiments:**
1. **Baseline Measurement:** Run inference for a single (nin, nout) pair (e.g., 512,512) and verify that Etok is computed correctly and reproducibly.
2. **Parameter Fitting:** Collect energy data for a small grid (nin ∈ {256,512,1024}, nout ∈ {256,512,1024}), fit SweetSpot parameters, and compare predicted vs measured Etok.
3. **Sweet Spot Validation:** For a fixed nin (e.g., 1024), sweep nout across the full range and plot Etok; confirm that the predicted nout* matches the empirical minimum.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Generalization to larger models and other hardware?** The study is limited to models ≤9B parameters on NVIDIA H100 GPUs; it remains unclear whether SweetSpot remains accurate for >50B models or different GPU architectures.
- **Incorporating max batch size?** Batch size strongly influences energy efficiency (up to 37× variation) but is not modeled; extending SweetSpot to include batch size is future work.
- **Applicability to other inference frameworks?** All experiments used TensorRT-LLM; frameworks like vLLM or DeepSpeed employ different optimizations (e.g., PagedAttention) that could alter energy patterns.

## Limitations
- Missing details on the non-linear least squares fitting procedure (algorithm, initialization, convergence criteria) hinder exact replication.
- Energy measurement protocol is incompletely specified (idle power subtraction, inference interval boundaries, power smoothing).
- Experiments are restricted to H100 GPUs, TensorRT-LLM, fixed batch size (1024), and synthetic workloads, limiting generalizability.
- Potential collinearity among the model’s terms across the (nin, nout) grid could destabilize parameter estimates.

## Confidence
- Theoretical derivation from FLOPs/memory reasoning: **High**
- Reported MAPE (1.79%): **Medium** (depends on unreported fitting and measurement details)
- Sweet spot prediction accuracy: **Medium** (limited to tested hardware and framework)
