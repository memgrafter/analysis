# Probing Multimodal Large Language Models on Cognitive Biases in Chinese Short-Video Misinformation
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.06600
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06600
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Gemini-2.5-Pro achieves the highest multimodal Belief Score (71.5/100), while o3 performs worst (35.2); multimodal inputs do not consistently outperform single-modality inputs.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates the robustness of eight frontier multimodal large language models (MLLMs) against cognitive biases when assessing misinformation in Chinese short-form videos. The authors curated a manually annotated dataset of 200 videos from Douyin and Kuaishou, labeling misinformation as experimental errors, logical fallacies, or fabricated claims. Using a normalized Belief Score (BS) across five input modality settings (Claim, Textual, Aural, Visual, Multimodal), they measure the models’ susceptibility to biases. Key findings reveal that Gemini-2.5-Pro performs best in the multimodal setting (BS 71.5), while o3 lags (BS 35.2). Multimodal inputs do not guarantee improved detection. All models exhibit cognitive biases: higher popularity metrics (likes, shares) increase trust in false content (herd effect), and verified channel IDs—especially official organizations—reduce false-video detection (authority bias). Additionally, Qwen models show a systematic bias toward trusting content, and o3 demonstrates conservative reasoning.

## Method Summary
The authors assembled a dataset of 200 short videos from Douyin and Kuaishou, manually annotating misinformation into three categories: experimental errors, logical fallacies, and fabricated claims. They selected eight state-of-the-art MLLMs and subjected each to five modality conditions: providing only the claim text (Claim), the video’s textual transcript (Textual), the audio track (Aural), selected video frames (Visual), or a combination of all three (Multimodal). Model outputs were converted into a Belief Score (BS), a normalized metric between 0 and 100 reflecting the model’s inclination to believe the claim. This setup allowed comparison of performance across modalities and analysis of cognitive biases by correlating model responses with video metadata (e.g., likes, shares, verification status).

## Key Results
- **Performance**: Gemini-2.5-Pro leads with a multimodal BS of 71.5/100; o3 performs worst at 35.2. Multimodal inputs do not consistently yield higher scores than single-modality inputs.
- **Cognitive biases**: Models exhibit a herd effect (higher likes/shares increase trust in misinformation) and an authority bias (verified channel IDs, particularly official organizations, decrease detection accuracy for false videos).
- **Model-specific tendencies**: Qwen models systematically trust content more, while o3 shows conservative reasoning (generally lower trust scores).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper does not investigate the underlying mechanisms for the observed biases or performance differences. It focuses on empirical measurement rather than causal explanation, leaving the “why” of model behavior to future work.

## Foundational Learning
- **Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs)**: Models that process and integrate information from text, images, and audio to perform reasoning tasks. *Why needed*: The study evaluates MLLMs on short-video misinformation, which inherently contains visual, aural, and textual cues. *Quick check*: Can the model generate a coherent description from an image and associated audio?
- **Cognitive Biases**: Systematic deviations from rational judgment, such as herd effect (relying on popularity cues) and authority bias (overweighting expert or verified sources). *Why needed*: The core research question examines how these human-like biases manifest in MLLMs when judging false content. *Quick check*: Does the model’s trust in a claim increase when presented with high like counts versus low?
- **Short-Video Platform Metadata**: Features like likes, shares, comments, and verification badges that accompany videos on Douyin/Kuaishou. *Why needed*: These cues are potential triggers for cognitive biases and are part of the model’s input context. *Quick check*: Can the model correctly interpret a verified badge as indicating credibility?
- **Belief Score (BS)**: A normalized 0–100 metric quantifying the model’s belief in a claim. *Why needed*: Primary evaluation metric across all modality settings. *Quick check*: For a known true claim, does the model assign a higher BS than for a known false claim?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Video Input -> Modality Extraction (Frames, Audio, Text) -> Separate Encoders (Visual, Aural, Text) -> Multimodal Fusion Layer -> Large Language Model -> Output Head (Belief Score prediction)

**Critical path**: (1) Decompose video into frames, audio, and optionally subtitles; (2) Encode each modality using pretrained feature extractors; (3) Fuse features into a unified representation; (4) Pass through an LLM with task-specific prompting; (5) Generate a numeric belief score.

**Design tradeoffs**: 
- Multimodal vs. single-modality: Full multimodal input improves coverage of cues but increases computational cost and may incorporate irrelevant signals.
- Fusion strategy: Early concatenation is simple but may not capture cross-modal interactions; LLM-based late fusion is flexible but relies on the model’s ability to integrate disparate features.
- Prompt inclusion of metadata: Using platform cues (likes, verification) can trigger biases, complicating the assessment of pure content-based detection.

**Failure signatures**: 
- Herd effect: Model trust rises with increasing like/share counts even for false claims.
- Authority bias: Lower detection accuracy for videos from verified channels (especially official bodies) compared to unverified ones.
- Model-specific deviations: Qwen tends toward uniformly high trust scores; o3 produces overly conservative (low) scores, sometimes failing to identify true misinformation.

**First experiments**:
1. Modality ablation: For each MLLM, evaluate performance on each single modality (text, audio, visual) versus full multimodal to quantify contribution of each input type.
2. Controlled metadata isolation: Manipulate likes, shares, and verification status while keeping video content constant to measure causal impact of these cues on model trust.
3. Cross-model bias profiling: Compare responses of Qwen, o3, and other models to a balanced set of true/false claims with and without popularity/authority cues to characterize systematic biases.

## Limitations
- Small, platform-specific dataset (200 videos from Douyin/Kuaishou) limits generalizability to other Chinese short-video platforms or broader misinformation contexts.
- Single evaluation metric (Belief Score) may not fully capture nuanced model reasoning or real-world persuasion impact.
- Confounding factors in bias analysis (e.g., video popularity metrics) not fully isolated from content complexity or creator credibility signals.

## Confidence
- **Gemini-2.5-Pro multimodal superiority (BS 71.5)**: Medium confidence – based on one dataset; lacks replication.
- **Multimodal inputs don’t consistently outperform single-modality**: Medium confidence – finding may be dataset-specific.
- **Herd effect (popularity metrics increase trust)**: High confidence – aligns with cognitive psychology literature and effect size is clear.
- **Authority bias (verified channel IDs reduce false-video detection)**: High confidence – robust across multiple models and consistent with authority heuristic theory.
- **Qwen’s systematic trusting bias & o3’s conservatism**: Medium confidence – needs validation across broader query sets to rule out artifacts.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-platform replication**: Expand dataset to 1,000+ videos from at least three additional Chinese short-video platforms and re-evaluate models.
2. **Open-model benchmarking**: Test top open-source MLLMs (e.g., LLaVA, InternVL) on the same dataset to determine if biases are architecture-specific or due to proprietary training.
3. **Controlled bias ablation**: Remove popularity/authority cues using synthetic videos with identical content but manipulated metadata to isolate causal effects.
