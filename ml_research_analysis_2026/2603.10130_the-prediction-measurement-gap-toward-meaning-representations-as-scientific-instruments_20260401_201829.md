# The Prediction-Measurement Gap: Toward Meaning Representations as Scientific Instruments

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.10130
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10130
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Static embeddings are superior for transparent social-science measurement due to stable geometry and linear compatibility, while contextual embeddings require post-hoc reconditioning to disentangle semantic from non-semantic signals.

## Executive Summary
This paper identifies a critical gap between NLP embeddings optimized for predictive tasks and those suitable as scientific instruments for social science measurement. It argues that prediction-oriented objectives (e.g., next-token prediction) produce representations whose geometry (e.g., anisotropy in contextual models) entangles meaning with surface confounds like syntax and frequency, undermining measurement transparency. To bridge this gap, the authors propose criteria for "scientific usability" — geometric legibility, interpretability, robustness to confounds, and cognitive plausibility — and assess static versus contextual embeddings against them. They find static embeddings naturally satisfy these criteria via stable cosine similarity and traceable nearest neighbors, whereas contextual models need reversible transformations to recondition their geometry. The work outlines a research agenda for designing measurement-oriented spaces (e.g., hierarchy-aware manifolds) and constructing domain-specific meaning atlases, urging a shift from prediction-centric to inference-centric representation evaluation.

## Method Summary
The paper formulates a set of "scientific usability" criteria for embeddings used in measurement: geometric legibility (stable similarity relations), interpretability (nearest neighbors that cohere with human judgment), semantic sensitivity (meaning vs. surface variance), robustness to non-semantic confounds, and cognitive plausibility. It contrasts static (type-level) and contextual (token-level) embeddings by analyzing their geometric properties: static models yield consistent cosine similarity and linear structure, enabling simple regression-based scaling (e.g., Latent Semantic Scaling). Contextual models exhibit anisotropy and syntactic entanglement, requiring post-hoc transformations (e.g., whitening, flow-based reconditioning) to approximate semantic-only spaces. The assessment uses metrics like cosine stability across corpus subsamples, interpretability of direction-based nearest neighbors, and semantic sensitivity ratios (synonym vs. surface-similar pairs). The paper also draws on cognitive science (Rosch’s basic-level categories) to propose geometry-first design principles.

## Key Results
- Static embeddings provide a "default substrate" for measurement: their fixed type vectors give stable cosine similarity, traceable nearest neighbors, and compatibility with linear regression, enabling transparent inference and mixed-method validation.
- Contextual embeddings entangle semantics with syntax and frequency, causing anisotropic geometry where cosine similarity is dominated by non-semantic factors; this complicates direction-based inference and averaging operations.
- To make contextual models measurement-ready, invertible post-hoc transformations (e.g., whitening, normalizing flows) are needed to recondition geometry, though their adoptability in social science workflows remains uncertain.
- Future progress requires geometry-first design (e.g., hierarchy-aware spaces with a "basic-level band") and construction of domain-specific meaning atlases to serve as stable reference anchors.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Static embeddings' geometrically stable and interpretable structure enables transparent scientific measurement via linear operations (projections, regressions) and traceable nearest-neighbor analysis.
- **Mechanism:** By representing word types as fixed vectors, static embeddings suppress context-dependent and syntactic confounds (e.g., punctuation, casing), yielding a space where cosine similarity and directional offsets correspond more directly to semantic relations. This allows regression-based frameworks (e.g., Latent Semantic Scaling, SSD) to estimate interpretable semantic gradients, which are then explained by clustering nearest neighbors—aligning quantitative inference with qualitative validation.
- **Core assumption:** Semantic relations are sufficiently linear and stable in a type-level space for mixed-method inference.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "static embeddings superior for transparent measurement due to stable cosine similarity, traceable nearest neighbors, and compatibility with linear regression."
  - [section 5.1] "static embeddings remain an unusually good 'default substrate'... make it easy to construct concept dictionaries, compute centroids, estimate linear semantic directions, and interpret results by directly inspecting nearest neighbors."
  - [corpus] Related work like "Bridging the Dimensional Chasm" investigates low-dimensional semantic organization in LLMs, indirectly supporting the premise that meaningful semantic structure can be captured in stable, lower-dimensional geometries.
- **Break condition:** If sense-level polysemy or corpus-specific artifacts severely destabilize word neighborhoods across sub-samples, the interpretability and reliability of nearest-neighbor explanations degrade, undermining measurement transparency.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Contextual embeddings entangle semantic content with non-semantic signals (syntax, frequency, tokenization artifacts), producing anisotropic geometry that distorts similarity and complicates direction-based inference.
- **Mechanism:** Transformer representations at intermediate layers mix surface form, positional, and syntactic information with semantics. This entanglement creates global geometric distortions (e.g., anisotropic concentration) where cosine similarity is dominated by non-semantic factors. Consequently, operations like averaging contextual vectors or projecting on "semantic directions" yield unstable, non-isometric results that are not reliably interpretable as pure semantic measurements.
- **Core assumption:** The optimization objective (next-token prediction) prioritizes predictive utility over disentangled semantic geometry.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "contextual embeddings... entangle meaning with syntax and frequency, leading to anisotropic geometry that complicates inference."
  - [section 5.2] "contextual representations are not 'semantic-only' spaces... mix surface cues, syntax, and longer-range semantic dependencies... anisotropy and other global distortions can make cosine similarity less reflective of semantics... non-semantic biases (frequency, casing, punctuation, subword artifacts) can dominate the representation geometry."
  - [corpus] "Text Has Curvature" examines intrinsic curvature of text spaces, suggesting that geometric properties beyond anisotropy (like curvature) may also reflect entangled signals; "Bridging the Dimensional Chasm" notes high-dimensional processing in LLMs versus low-dimensional human semantic organization, highlighting a potential misalignment.
- **Break condition:** If effective post-hoc transformations (e.g., whitening, flow-based reconditioning) can reliably remove non-semantic variance while preserving semantic structure, the entanglement's negative impact on measurement could be mitigated.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Aligning representation geometry with cognitively plausible organizational principles (e.g., privileged basic-level categories) enhances semantic legibility and measurement validity.
- **Mechanism:** Human semantic cognition organizes concepts in graded, prototype-centered hierarchies where "basic-level" categories (e.g., "chair") maximize distinctiveness and feature coherence. Embedding spaces designed with such inductive biases—e.g., hyperbolic spaces with a "basic-level band" of high representational capacity—could yield geometries where distances and clusters more naturally map to human-intuitive groupings. This improves interpretability of nearest neighbors and the validity of linear projections as reflections of psychological constructs.
- **Core assumption:** Cognitive constraints provide beneficial priors for designing measurement-oriented spaces, even if not strictly required for predictive performance.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "hierarchy-aware spaces constrained by psychologically privileged levels."
  - [section 4.1] Details Rosch's basic-level account: categories at the basic level balance informativeness and cognitive economy; within-category feature bundles are coherent, between-category separable.
  - [section 6.1] Proposes a "basic-level band" design for hyperbolic spaces, directly motivated by cognitive plausibility.
  - [corpus] Evidence is indirect; "Text Has Curvature" explores hierarchical representation in curved spaces, which might align with hierarchical cognitive organization, but does not explicitly test basic-level effects.
- **Break condition:** If cognitive plausibility constraints (e.g., enforcing a basic-level band) reduce the space's expressive power for capturing fine-grained semantic distinctions or cross-domain relations, they may conflict with measurement goals in some domains.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Geometric Legibility**
  - *Why needed here:* The entire premise rests on embeddings having a geometry where standard operations (cosine similarity, vector projection) yield stable, interpretable results. Without legible geometry, measurement via regression or scaling fails.
  - *Quick check question:* Does cosine similarity between word vectors remain stable across different corpus sub-samples when measuring a target semantic dimension?
- **Concept: Semantic Sensitivity vs. Non-Semantic Confounds**
  - *Why needed here:* To ensure embeddings measure "meaning" rather than artifacts (frequency, punctuation), a sensitivity contrast is needed. This isolates semantic signal from confounds that are amplified in contextual models.
  - *Quick check question:* Is the similarity between meaning-preserving surface variants (e.g., "run" vs. "running") higher than between surface-similar but meaning-distinct pairs (e.g., "bank" [river] vs. "bank" [money])?
- **Concept: Cognitive Levels of Abstraction**
  - *Why needed here:* Social science constructs often operate at specific abstraction levels (e.g., "furniture" vs. "chair"). Alignment with psychologically privileged levels (basic level) affects cluster coherence and interpretability of nearest neighbors.
  - *Quick check question:* Do nearest-neighbor clusters of a target word align more strongly with human typicality ratings at the basic level than at superordinate or subordinate levels?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Evaluation Module → Transformation Engine → Atlas Builder → Geometry Designer
- **Critical path:** 
  1. Audit existing embeddings (static or contextual) against scientific usability criteria.
  2. If using contextual embeddings, first apply robust post-hoc transformations to reduce anisotropy and non-semantic bias.
  3. For domain-specific measurement, build a meaning atlas with interpretable anchors (e.g., construct labels, sense clusters).
  4. Use linear regression on projected embeddings (or anchor similarities) for measurement, with results validated via nearest-neighbor inspection.
- **Design tradeoffs:** 
  - *Static vs. Contextual:* Static offers legibility but loses sense disambiguation; contextual offers richer semantics but requires heavy conditioning and loses direct word-level interpretability.
  - *Simplicity vs. Expressivity:* Linear operations (regression, PCA) are transparent but may underfit complex semantics; non-linear manifolds (hyperbolic) can capture hierarchy but complicate inference and interpretation.
  - *General vs. Domain-Specific:* Universal embedding spaces are convenient but may not optimally serve specific scientific constructs; meaning atlases improve domain validity but require manual curation.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - High anisotropy (dominant singular vectors) in contextual embeddings.
  - Low inter-rater agreement when annotating top-k neighbors of a semantic direction.
  - Semantic sensitivity ratio near 1.0 (confounds dominate).
  - Regression coefficients unstable across corpus sub-samples.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Stability Audit:** For a static embedding model, compute cosine similarity matrices for 100 random 10% corpus sub-samples; report mean pairwise correlation and variance for target semantic pairs.
  2. **Nearest-Neighbor Interpretability Test:** For a set of predefined semantic directions (e.g., gender, valence), extract top-20 nearest neighbors; have domain experts annotate whether neighbors coherently represent the direction; calculate Cohen’s κ.
  3. **Post-Hoc Reconditioning Efficacy:** Take a contextual model (e.g., BERT), apply whitening and a flow-based transformation trained to maximize semantic sensitivity; compare pre- and post-transformation on anisotropy metrics and the semantic sensitivity ratio.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can invertible post-hoc transformations (e.g., flow-based methods) effectively recondition contextual embedding geometry to enhance semantic legibility and be adoptable in typical social-scientific workflows?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 6.2 questions the adoptability of flow-based transformations for reconditioning embeddings, noting it remains an open practical question given social science constraints.
- **Why unresolved:** It is unclear whether such transformations preserve necessary information while improving geometry, and whether their complexity aligns with social scientists' preference for simple, transparent statistical methods.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Empirical evaluations comparing measurement quality (e.g., stability of semantic gradients, interpretability via nearest neighbors) before and after transformation, alongside user studies on workflow integration.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can hierarchy-aware embedding spaces incorporate a "basic-level band" reflecting psychologically privileged abstraction, and does this improve alignment with human typicality ratings?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 6.1 proposes a refinement to hierarchy-aware spaces motivated by Rosch's basic-level account, suggesting it is testable via operationalization with typicality ratings.
- **Why unresolved:** The geometric implementation of such a band and its effect on semantic operations (e.g., linear projections) have not been investigated.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Develop models with curvature or radius priors encouraging a basic-level band; evaluate whether their similarity structure correlates more strongly with basic-level typicality ratings than with superordinate or subordinate levels.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How can we construct "meaning atlases" – structured dictionaries of interpretable anchors (e.g., phrases, prototypes) – to support measurement workflows with contextual embeddings?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 6.3 identifies meaning atlases as a longer-term goal to provide stable reference points for nearest-neighbor explanation and directional interpretation in contextual models.
- **Why unresolved:** The coverage problem and selection of anchors that are both interpretable and semantically comprehensive for domain-specific use remain unsolved.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Build prototype atlases for specific domains (e.g., psychological constructs), assess coverage and interpretability of derived semantic directions via human annotation tasks, and test measurement reliability.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What evaluation protocols can directly measure "scientific usability" criteria (geometric legibility, interpretability, semantic sensitivity) for meaning representations used in social science?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 6.3 calls for measurement-oriented evaluation suites that operationalize the criteria from Section 3, contrasting with existing predictive benchmarks.
- **Why unresolved:** No standardized benchmarks exist that assess these criteria in ways relevant to inference and theory building.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Design a benchmark suite with tasks: cosine similarity stability across corpora, inter-rater agreement on nearest-neighbor labels for semantic directions, and semantic sensitivity via surface-variant contrasts; validate by correlating scores with real-world measurement quality.

## Limitations
- Static embeddings’ legibility relies on type-level averaging; polysemy may destabilize neighborhoods across contexts, undermining nearest-neighbor interpretability.
- Contextual embeddings’ entanglement is inferred from geometric anisotropy; causal links between optimization objectives and specific confounds (e.g., tokenization artifacts) need isolation.
- Cognitive plausibility (basic-level band) is theoretically motivated but lacks empirical validation in embedding spaces; the trade-off between hierarchical organization and fine-grained discriminativity remains untested.

## Confidence
- **Static embeddings’ measurement suitability:** High (direct evidence from stability and regression compatibility tests).
- **Contextual embeddings’ geometric distortions:** High (consistent anisotropy findings across studies).
- **Hierarchy-aware design benefits:** Medium (theoretical grounding; limited empirical embedding results).

## Next Checks
1. **Polysemy stress test:** For highly polysemous words (e.g., "bank"), compute cosine stability across 50 random sub-samples of a static embedding; compare variance to monosemous controls.
2. **Confound ablation:** Apply orthogonal post-hoc transformations (whitening, ICA) to contextual embeddings; measure change in semantic sensitivity ratio (synonym vs. surface-similar pairs) and anisotropy.
3. **Basic-level band trade-off:** Train hyperbolic embeddings with/without a basic-level capacity constraint; evaluate on (a) human typicality correlation and (b) fine-grained semantic distinction accuracy (e.g., on McRae feature norms).
