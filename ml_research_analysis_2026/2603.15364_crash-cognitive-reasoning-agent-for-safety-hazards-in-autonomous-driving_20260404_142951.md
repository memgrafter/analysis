# CRASH: Cognitive Reasoning Agent for Safety Hazards in Autonomous Driving

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.15364
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15364
*   **Reference count:** 28
*   **Primary result:** An LLM-based agent achieves 86% expert-aligned accuracy in attributing AV responsibility from NHTSA incident reports, processing each in ~30 seconds.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces CRASH, an LLM-based agent designed to systematically analyze heterogeneous autonomous vehicle (AV) incident reports. It processes unstructured narratives and structured fields into a unified format, applying domain rules and a structured taxonomy to attribute primary causes, identify failed subsystems, and assess AV contribution. Validated on a curated dataset of 2,168 NHTSA reports, CRASH attributes 64% of incidents to perception or planning failures and identifies rear-end collisions in ~50% of cases. Domain expert validation shows 86% accuracy for AV responsibility attribution, significantly outperforming keyword and majority-class baselines, with each report processed in approximately 30 seconds.

## Method Summary
CRASH employs a three-stage pipeline. First, **preprocessing** merges multiple NHTSA CSV files into a unified table, creating a `Full Text` field by concatenating metadata and narrative, and filters out reports with missing/redacted narratives or non-ADS incidents. Second, **processing** uses a constrained prompt containing domain rules, a one-shot example, and a taxonomy to query a local DeepSeek-R1 32B LLM via Ollama with deterministic decoding (`temperature=0`, `top_p=1`), parsing the JSON output with retries on failure. Third, **postprocessing** aggregates results into a CSV, generates analytics, and presents a sample to domain experts via a survey interface; their feedback (Correct/Incorrect/Insufficient Context) is used to iteratively refine the prompt. The core innovation lies in using heuristic rules and a structured taxonomy within the prompt to steer the LLM toward consistent, structured causal attributions without model fine-tuning.

## Key Results
*   CRASH attributes 64% of analyzed incidents to perception (69% of system failures) or planning failures.
*   Rear-end collisions constitute approximately 50% of all identified incident scenarios.
*   Domain expert validation shows 86% accuracy for AV responsibility attribution, with 84% accuracy for late AI detection.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Constrained Prompting with Domain Rules
The LLM is steered toward deterministic classification by embedding expert-defined decision rules (e.g., “Moving AV action contributed → Y”) and a one-shot JSON example directly into the prompt. This mitigates hallucination and ensures output schema compliance, relying on the LLM's ability to interpret rules within its context window. Evidence is found in the paper's emphasis on "constrained classification over open-ended generation" and the injection of domain knowledge into the inference path. A break condition occurs when narratives lack evidential detail, leading to "Insufficient Context" labels, reflected in lower subsystem accuracy.

### Mechanism 2: Standardized Taxonomy for Incident Decomposition
A structured taxonomy maps narratives to primary causes (System, Human, Environmental, None) and, for system failures, to specific subsystems (Perception, Prediction, Planning/Control, etc.). This enables reproducible aggregation and simulation-ready outputs across heterogeneous reports. The taxonomy's design balances granularity with practicality, aligning with expert conceptualizations of AV system boundaries. Novel failure modes or intricate cross-subsystem interactions can cause forced misclassification or "N" assignments, contributing to lower expert agreement on subsystem attribution.

### Mechanism 3: Iterative Human-in-the-Loop Validation
Domain experts independently review LLM outputs, and their aggregated feedback (labels of Correct/Incorrect/Insufficient Context) is used to refine prompt instructions, rule formulations, and examples without retraining the model. This alignment process improves consistency with expert judgment. A break condition stems from low inter-rater agreement (53–67%) among experts themselves, making the "gold standard" derived from their feedback uncertain and potentially limiting the ceiling of alignment.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept:** In-Context Learning (ICL)
    *   **Why needed:** CRASH shapes LLM reasoning via a prompt containing persona, rules, and examples rather than fine-tuning. Understanding ICL explains how domain adaptation occurs without weight changes.
    *   **Quick check:** "If you change the example in the one-shot prompt, how might the LLM’s output format or classification shift?"
*   **Concept:** Taxonomy Design for Safety Analysis
    *   **Why needed:** The taxonomy is the backbone of CRASH's structured output; it must balance subsystem granularity with coverage of human/environmental factors. Its structure is necessary to interpret and extend results.
    *   **Quick check:** "Given a narrative where an AV fails to detect a pedestrian due to heavy rain, what primary cause and subsystem would the taxonomy assign, and why?"
*   **Concept:** Human-AI Collaboration in Safety-Critical Systems
    *   **Why needed:** CRASH positions humans as validators/reviewers, not primary analyzers, ensuring safety and trust. This collaboration model is crucial for high-stakes domains.
    *   **Quick check:** "What are the distinct responsibilities of the LLM agent and the domain experts in the CRASH pipeline, and how does this division mitigate risks?"

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Preprocessing (CSV merge, filter, Full Text generation) -> Processing (Prompt builder, LLM interface with deterministic decoding, JSON parser with retry) -> Postprocessing (CSV aggregation, analytics generation, human survey interface; feedback loop to prompt updates).

**Critical path:** 1) Raw NHTSA report -> Preprocessing -> unified `Full Text`. 2) `Full Text` -> Processing -> structured JSON (AV_Failed, Cause, System, Late, Secondary). 3) JSON -> Postprocessing -> aggregated analytics & human validation loop.

**Design tradeoffs:**
*   **Model:** Chose local 32B DeepSeek for accuracy/cost balance; smaller models required voting, larger ones were costlier. Tradeoff: inference time (~30s/case) vs. hardware availability.
*   **Prompting:** Constrained classification with one-shot over Chain-of-Thought to minimize output length and avoid schema violations. Tradeoff: reduced reasoning verbosity for reliability.
*   **Rules vs. fine-tuning:** Used heuristic rules in prompt instead of fine-tuning to maintain flexibility for new failure modes. Tradeoff: prompt engineering complexity vs. "black-box" weight changes.
*   **Determinism vs. robustness:** Fixed temperature and top_p ensures repeatable outputs but may reduce adaptability to linguistic variation.

**Failure signatures:**
*   JSON formatting errors (2% of cases, handled by retry).
*   "Insufficient Context" label when narrative lacks evidential detail, especially for subsystem identification.
*   Low expert agreement on subsystem (46% accuracy) and cause (76% vs. ~70% human agreement) indicating persistent ambiguity.
*   Latency bottleneck: 30 seconds per report on two A4500 GPUs; scales linearly.
*   Potential hallucination of causes not present in narrative, though constrained decoding mitigates.

**First 3 experiments:**
1.  **System reliability test:** Run CRASH on the full dataset and measure JSON success rate, retry counts, and output consistency across fixed seeds.
2.  **Expert agreement study:** Sample 50 diverse incidents; have five domain experts independently label the same four attributes; compute CRASH accuracy against a lenient gold standard derived from overlapping human judgments; compare against majority-class and keyword baselines.
3.  **Runtime efficiency benchmark:** Measure average inference time per report, GPU utilization, and memory footprint; establish baseline for scaling to larger datasets and identify optimization opportunities (e.g., batching, model quantization).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Question:** How accurate is CRASH when evaluated on a large-scale, expertly annotated dataset?
    *   **Basis:** The authors state that systematic validation on larger annotated subsets is a priority for future work to address hallucination risks.
    *   **Why unresolved:** Current validation uses only 50 cases; performance on a larger and more diverse set is unknown.
    *   **Resolving evidence:** A large, high-quality annotation of thousands of reports with strong inter-annotator agreement, followed by rigorous measurement of CRASH's accuracy and systematic error analysis.
*   **Question:** How can CRASH be improved to handle incident reports with sparse or ambiguous technical details, and what is the practical limit of attribution accuracy given inherent narrative ambiguity?
    *   **Basis:** Human evaluators showed low agreement (53–67%) and frequently labeled cases as "Insufficient Context", especially for subsystem identification.
    *   **Why unresolved:** The system defaults to "I" for uncertainty, but many borderline reports exist; unclear whether richer prompting or external context can extract more reliable signals.
    *   **Resolving evidence:** Testing extended prompts, incorporating vehicle-specific knowledge, or using confidence calibration on the ambiguous subset, with performance measured against human disagreement as a ceiling.
*   **Question:** What specific perception sub-failures (e.g., object detection, tracking) and latency sources (e.g., sensor, computation) contribute most to incidents, and can they be automatically extracted from narratives?
    *   **Basis:** Results show perception failures dominate (69% of system failures) and delayed AI responses overlap 79% with system failures, but the taxonomy aggregates all perception under "PE" and does not detail latency causes.
    *   **Why unresolved:** Aggregated categories prevent targeted engineering; finer-grained failure modes are needed to guide algorithm improvements.
    *   **Resolving evidence:** Extend CRASH's output to include fine-grained perception tasks and latency origins, validate these labels with experts, and correlate them with incident characteristics.
*   **Question:** Do AV incident patterns and failure modes differ across manufacturers and system architectures (e.g., modular vs. end-to-end), and does CRASH capture these architecture-specific trends?
    *   **Basis:** The introduction highlights heterogeneity of AV architectures as a challenge, yet the dataset is skewed (~63% of incidents from Waymo and Cruise), limiting cross-architectural analysis.
    *   **Why unresolved:** Unclear whether findings (e.g., perception failures) are universal or fleet-specific.
    *   **Resolving evidence:** Stratified analysis of CRASH outputs across manufacturers with sufficient representation, possibly augmented with additional data sources, to identify architecture-dependent failure signatures.

## Limitations
*   **Dataset curation specifics:** Exact NHTSA query/filter logic (beyond ADS/non-ADS and missing narratives) is unreported, risking selection bias.
*   **Prompt verbatim:** The full system prompt (including all rule phrasings and one-shot examples) is not public; minor wording changes may alter outputs.
*   **Expert agreement baseline:** Low inter-rater agreement (53–67%) on subsystems undermines the "gold standard" for validation; 86% AV responsibility accuracy may reflect lenient scoring.

## Confidence
*   **Operational reliability (JSON success, ~30s/case):** High
*   **Attribution accuracy (86% AV responsibility):** Medium
*   **Generalizability beyond curated NHTSA dataset:** Low
