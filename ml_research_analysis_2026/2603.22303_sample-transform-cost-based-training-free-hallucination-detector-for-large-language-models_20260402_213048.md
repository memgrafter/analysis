# Sample Transform Cost-Based Training-Free Hallucination Detector for Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.22303
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22303
- **Reference count**: 38
- **Primary result**: A training-free hallucination detector using average and spectral transform costs (AvgWD, EigenWD) between sampled responses achieves competitive AUROC across multiple LLMs and datasets, with complementary strengths that extend to black-box models via teacher forcing.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a novel, training-free method for detecting hallucinations in large language models (LLMs). The core idea is to quantify the complexity of a prompt's conditional response distribution by measuring the "transform cost" between multiple stochastically sampled outputs. Using optimal transport, token embeddings from an intermediate layer are treated as empirical measures; pairwise 2‑Wasserstein distances between these measures form a cost matrix. Two summary statistics are derived: the average distance (AvgWD) and a spectral complexity measure (EigenWD) of a kernelized version of the cost matrix. Evaluated on five open‑source LLMs across four diverse datasets, both signals perform strongly against established uncertainty baselines, often ranking best on average. The approach adapts to black‑box LLMs by using a teacher model to provide proxy embeddings, retaining effectiveness. The work establishes distribution complexity via sample transform costs as a robust, lightweight indicator of hallucination.

## Method Summary
For a given prompt, the method samples *K* stochastic responses from the target LLM. For each response, it extracts the hidden states from a fixed intermediate transformer layer (typically the middle layer) corresponding to the generated output tokens (excluding the prompt and end‑of‑sequence tokens). These token vectors form an empirical probability measure with uniform weights. A fixed random projection optionally reduces dimensionality (e.g., to 128 D). Pairwise 2‑Wasserstein distances between all *K* response measures are computed exactly (using linear programming) or approximately, yielding a symmetric distance matrix **D**. Two detection signals are then computed: (1) **AvgWD**, the average of all off‑diagonal entries in **D**, and (2) **EigenWD**, calculated by first kernelizing **D** (Gaussian kernel with median bandwidth and a small diagonal shift), then taking a scale‑invariant quasi‑norm of its eigenvalue spectrum. A higher signal indicates a greater risk of hallucination. For black‑box target models, a separate white‑box teacher model is used in a teacher‑forcing manner: the target’s sampled tokens are fed sequentially to the teacher, and the teacher’s corresponding hidden states are used as proxies to construct the response measures.

## Key Results
- Both AvgWD and EigenWD achieve competitive AUROC scores against strong uncertainty baselines (e.g., discrete semantic entropy, eigenscore) across 5 LLMs and 4 datasets, often attaining the best average performance.
- The two signals show complementary strengths: EigenWD excels on Llama models, while AvgWD is stronger on Qwen models.
- The black‑box extension via teacher forcing (e.g., DeepSeek‑Chat target with Llama‑2‑7B teacher) preserves effectiveness, frequently outperforming baselines in case studies.
- Visual analysis shows hallucinated cases often yield higher AvgWD and more fragmented block structures in the pairwise Wasserstein distance heatmaps, correlating with a less concentrated eigenvalue spectrum for EigenWD.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Hallucinations increase the average transform cost (AvgWD) between sampled responses, reflecting higher complexity of the prompt‑conditioned distribution.
- **Mechanism**: *K* responses are sampled. Each is represented as an empirical measure over its generated‑token embeddings from an intermediate layer. Pairwise 2‑Wasserstein distances between these measures are computed; AvgWD is their mean. A larger AvgWD indicates that, on average, it is more costly to transform one response into another in representation space, which correlates with hallucination.
- **Core assumption**: Hallucinations lead to greater diversity or divergence in token‑embedding space. If hallucinations produce responses that are semantically similar to truthful ones (e.g., a consistent but incorrect answer), AvgWD may not increase.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: “AvgWD, measuring the average cost”
  - [section 3.2]: “a larger AvgWD means that sampled responses are, on average, more expensive to transport … indicating higher distribution complexity and hence a higher risk of hallucination.”
  - [corpus]: Neighbor papers such as *EigenTrack* and *From Out‑of‑Distribution Detection to Hallucination Detection: A Geometric View* also use geometric distances in representation space, providing indirect support for representation‑based signals.
- **Break condition**: When hallucinations yield responses with low embedding diversity (e.g., the model repeatedly outputs the same wrong answer), AvgWD will be low despite factual incorrectness. Conversely, diverse but correct answers could yield high AvgWD without hallucination.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Hallucinations increase the spectral complexity (EigenWD) of the kernelized transform‑cost matrix, capturing fragmented multi‑modal inconsistency.
- **Mechanism**: From the pairwise Wasserstein distance matrix **D**, a Gaussian kernel matrix **K** is constructed (median bandwidth, diagonal shift). EigenWD is a scale‑invariant quasi‑norm that increases when the eigenvalue spectrum of **K** is less concentrated, i.e., when there are multiple distinct clusters of responses in the cost geometry. This structural fragmentation is predictive of hallucination.
- **Core assumption**: Hallucinations often manifest as multi‑modal inconsistency (the model generates divergent, irreconcilable answers). If hallucinations are unimodal (same wrong answer every time), the spectrum will be concentrated and EigenWD low.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: “EigenWD, measuring the cost complexity”
  - [section 3.3]: “EigenWD(x) increases when the spectrum is less concentrated, indicating a more complex transform‑cost structure across sampled responses.”
  - [section 5.2]: “EigenWD excels on Llama models … hallucinated cases often show … more fragmented block structure” in heatmaps.
  - [corpus]: *EigenTrack* also employs spectral statistics of hidden activations for hallucination/OOD detection, supporting the relevance of spectral features.
- **Break condition**: Unimodal hallucinations (e.g., the model consistently generates the same false statement) will yield a low‑rank kernel matrix and low EigenWD. Inappropriate kernel bandwidth or diagonal shift may also obscure the spectral signal.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Teacher forcing with an accessible teacher model enables effective hallucination detection for black‑box LLMs without requiring the target’s hidden states.
- **Mechanism**: For a black‑box target, *K* responses are sampled. Each response is fed token‑by‑token to a white‑box teacher model; the teacher’s intermediate hidden states at those positions are extracted as proxy embeddings. These are used to construct empirical measures, compute pairwise Wasserstein distances, and derive AvgWD/EigenWD. The teacher’s representation geometry is assumed to preserve the signal present in the target’s.
- **Core assumption**: The teacher model’s hidden‑state landscape for a given token sequence is sufficiently aligned with the target model’s hidden states for the same sequence. This relies on the teacher being a reasonable surrogate (similar architecture, training data, or capability).
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: “We further extend the framework to black‑box LLMs via teacher forcing with an accessible teacher model, preserving effectiveness.”
  - [section 5.4]: Black‑box case study (DeepSeek‑Chat target, Llama‑2‑7B teacher) shows strong AUROC, often beating baselines.
  - [corpus]: Limited direct evidence; neighbor papers do not address teacher forcing for hallucination detection. The paper’s own experiments provide the primary support.
- **Break condition**: If the teacher model is too dissimilar (different architecture, scale, or training distribution) from the target, the proxy embeddings may not preserve the transform‑cost signal. Out‑of‑distribution responses for the teacher (e.g., domain mismatch) can also degrade performance.

## Foundational Learning
- **Optimal Transport & Wasserstein Distance**
  - *Why needed*: The core pairwise distance between token‑embedding distributions is defined via the 2‑Wasserstein metric, which measures the minimal cost to transport one set of embeddings to another.
  - *Quick check*: What does the 2‑Wasserstein distance between two uniform empirical measures over token embeddings intuitively capture about the corresponding responses?

- **Empirical Measures over Tokens**
  - *Why needed*: Each response is represented as a distribution (not a pooled vector) to retain token‑level structure and avoid loss of information in long generations.
  - *Quick check*: How do you construct an empirical measure from a sequence of hidden states? What is the effect of using uniform weights?

- **Kernelization & Spectral Statistics**
  - *Why needed*: EigenWD requires converting the distance matrix into a similarity kernel and analyzing its eigenvalue spectrum to quantify structural complexity.
  - *Quick check*: Why is the distance matrix kernelized before spectral analysis? What does a higher EigenWD indicate about the eigenvalue distribution?

- **Teacher Forcing for Black‑Box Adaptation**
  - *Why needed*: To apply the method when the target model’s internal states are inaccessible, we use a teacher model to generate hidden states for the target’s output tokens.
  - *Quick check*: In the black‑box extension, what property must the teacher model satisfy for the proxy embeddings to be valid?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Sampler -> Embedding Extractor -> OT Distance Computer -> Signal Summarizer -> Decision Module
Black-box addition: Teacher Model (between Sampler and Embedding Extractor, replacing target’s internal extractor)

Critical path:
White‑box: Sample responses → Extract target’s mid‑layer hidden states for generated tokens → Apply random projection → Build uniform empirical measures per response → Compute all pairwise 2‑Wasserstein distances → Compute AvgWD and/or EigenWD → Threshold for decision.
Black‑box: Sample responses from target → Teacher‑force each response through teacher model → Extract teacher’s mid‑layer hidden states → Same as white‑box thereafter.
Computational bottleneck: Exact OT distance computation (O(K²·m_i·m_j) per pair, with m_i tokens per response). Eigendecomposition for EigenWD is O(K³).

Design tradeoffs:
- AvgWD is simpler and cheaper; EigenWD captures structural complexity but requires kernelization and eigendecomposition. They are complementary—empirically, EigenWD performs better on Llama, AvgWD on Qwen.
- Middle layer (ℓ = ⌊L/2⌋) is a heuristic; deeper layers may be more semantic but potentially more task‑specific.
- Random projection to 128D reduces OT cost but discards information; using a fixed shared matrix.
- Teacher model selection: A teacher similar to the target preserves the signal; a dissimilar teacher may break it. Larger teachers improve fidelity but increase cost.

Failure signatures:
- Numerical instability: OT distances near zero cause kernel matrix **K** to become nearly identity; the diagonal shift (α·I) mitigates this.
- Too few samples (K < 5): Empirical distribution poorly estimates conditional distribution → high variance in scores.
- Extreme temperature: τ ≪ 1 yields near‑deterministic samples (low **D**); τ ≫ 1 yields noisy samples that may not reflect hallucination patterns.
- Teacher‑target mismatch: If teacher embeddings are not aligned with the target’s (different architecture/domain), computed distances lose correlation with hallucination.
- Dataset shift: Extremely long responses may cause imbalance in support sizes; OT handles it but could become computationally heavy.

First 3 experiments:
1. Reproducibility check: On CoQA with Llama‑3.2‑3B (white‑box), compute AvgWD/EigenWD and verify AUROC ≈ 0.81–0.82 as in Table 1.
2. Ablation on *K*: Vary *K* ∈ {5, 10, 15, 20} and plot AUROC. Expect stable or non‑decreasing performance as *K* grows; a drop at low *K* confirms need for sufficient samples.
3. Black‑box sanity: Use a target that is also white‑box (e.g., Llama‑2‑7B). Compute scores directly and via teacher forcing with a different teacher (e.g., Qwen3‑8B). Check correlation and compare detection AUROC against baselines.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Asymptotic complexity & approximations**: What is the time complexity as a function of *K* and response lengths, and can approximate OT (e.g., Sinkhorn) reduce it without sacrificing accuracy?
- **Layer selection**: Is the middle transformer layer (ℓ = ⌊L/2⌋) optimal, or could other layers improve detection?
- **Model‑specific complementarity**: Why do AvgWD and EigenWD show complementary strengths across model families (e.g., AvgWD better on Qwen, EigenWD on Llama), and what does this reveal about hallucination mechanisms?

## Limitations
- Critical hyperparameters are unspecified: random projection distribution/seed, kernel diagonal shift α, and exact top‑p/temperature during sampling.
- EigenWD’s spectral measure is sensitive to kernel parameters, especially when Wasserstein distances are small, risking numerical instability.
- The black‑box teacher‑forcing extension assumes teacher‑target representation alignment without systematic validation across diverse model families.
- Exact OT computation scales poorly with token count, limiting application to very long generations.

## Next Checks
1. **Random projection robustness**: Compare AvgWD/EigenWD with different projection seeds and dimensions (64, 128, 256) to gauge stability.
2. **Kernel parameter sensitivity**: Sweep α (1e‑6 to 1e‑4) and bandwidth (median vs. mean) and measure AUROC variation, especially for low‑variance cases.
3. **Teacher‑target similarity study**: Using a fixed target (e.g., DeepSeek‑Chat), evaluate performance with multiple teachers (Llama‑2‑7B, Qwen‑8B, Mistral‑7B) and correlate teacher‑target representation similarity (CKA, SVCCA) with detection accuracy.
