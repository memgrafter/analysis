# Split Federated Learning Architectures for High-Accuracy and Low-Delay Model Training

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.08687
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08687
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Achieves up to 3% higher accuracy, 20% lower delay, and 50% reduced communication overhead vs. existing SFL and HSFL schemes.

## Executive Summary
Hierarchical Split Federated Learning (HSFL) partitions deep neural networks across clients, local aggregators, and a server to balance privacy, computation, and communication. Prior HSFL work assumed that the choice of cut layer (the split point) does not affect final accuracy, focusing solely on delay and overhead optimization. This paper challenges that assumption, demonstrating that cut layer selection significantly impacts accuracy due to the quality of local-loss gradients. The authors formulate a joint optimization problem over cut layer \(v\), aggregator layer \(h\), and client-to-aggregator assignment fraction \(\lambda\) to minimize round delay subject to an accuracy constraint, proving the problem NP‑hard.

The proposed solution, AA‑HSFL‑ll, is a two‑phase heuristic. First, an offline phase identifies cut layers that achieve high accuracy on a small client subset. Second, an online greedy algorithm searches for the optimal \(h\) and \(\lambda\) to balance client and aggregator workloads, assigning weaker clients to stronger aggregators to minimize the maximum pipeline delay. Evaluations on MNIST, CIFAR‑10, and CINIC‑10 with AlexNet, VGG, and ResNet show improvements of up to +3% accuracy, –20% delay, and –50% communication overhead over state‑of‑the‑art SFL/HSFL baselines. The approach yields near‑optimal solutions, scales efficiently, and adapts to dynamic network conditions with minimal performance loss.

## Method Summary
The paper addresses the joint optimization of cut layer \(v\), aggregator layer \(h\), and the fraction of local aggregators \(\lambda\) in hierarchical split federated learning. The objective is to minimize the round training delay \(T_{\text{round}}\) while meeting a target accuracy, considering forward/backward processing times on clients, aggregators, and server, as well as communication delays. The problem is NP‑hard, so the authors develop AA‑HSFL‑ll. Phase 1 (Algorithm 1) runs offline training on a small client subset for each candidate cut layer; layers whose accuracy is within a threshold \(thr\) of the best form the candidate set \(V^*\). Phase 2 (Algorithm 2) iterates over \(v \in V^*\), performs a binary search over \(h\) to balance client‑side and aggregator‑side workloads, and for each \(h\) sweeps \(\lambda\) to find the minimal maximum delay using a greedy assignment that maps weaker clients to stronger aggregators. The configuration with the lowest \(T_{\text{round}}\) that satisfies the accuracy constraint is selected. The algorithm exploits monotonicity for efficient search and naturally adapts to client heterogeneity by promoting stronger clients as aggregators and adjusting \(h\).

## Key Results
- Surpasses SFL and HSFL baselines: up to +3% test accuracy, –20% round delay, and –50% communication overhead under comparable conditions.
- Achieves near‑optimal performance with low computational overhead and adapts to network rate fluctuations or background tasks with minimal degradation.
- Demonstrates consistent improvements across multiple datasets (MNIST, CIFAR‑10, CINIC‑10) and model architectures (AlexNet, VGG, ResNet).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The selection of the cut layer in HSFL directly impacts model accuracy, contrary to prior SFL assumptions that accuracy is invariant to cut layer choice.
- **Mechanism:** During local-loss training, gradients computed at the cut layer are used for backpropagation on client-side and aggregator-side sub-models. Different cut layers capture varying levels of feature abstraction, leading to different gradient qualities and thus different final accuracies.
- **Core assumption:** The local-loss gradients' signal strength and representativeness for the final loss depend on the depth of the cut layer.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Recent Hierarchical SFL (HSFL) architectures overlook the impact of the partitioning layers... on accuracy... We answer the questions above in the affirmative."
  - [section II] "a common assumption underlying existing SFL schemes is that accuracy is invariant to the selection of the cut layer... we provide evidence that the selection of a suboptimal cut layer can severely degrade the accuracy. Fig. 2 presents the test accuracy... for different cut layer selections."
  - [corpus] Corpus neighbors largely focus on delay/overhead optimization in SFL/HSFL (e.g., "Collaborative Split Federated Learning...", "Ampere: Communication-Efficient...") without explicit accuracy-aware cut layer studies, suggesting this is a novel angle. Evidence is strong from the paper; corpus shows related but distinct focus.
- **Break condition:** If the offline‑measured accuracy for a candidate cut layer falls below the maximum observed accuracy minus a threshold `thr`, it is excluded from the candidate set `V*`.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** A two‑phase heuristic (offline candidate cut layer identification followed by online greedy assignment) can efficiently find near‑optimal partitioning layers and client‑to‑aggregator assignments that minimize round delay under accuracy constraints.
- **Mechanism:** Phase 1 (Algorithm 1) uses offline training on a subset of clients to identify cut layers `v` that achieve high accuracy (`V*`). Phase 2 (Algorithm 2) greedily searches over `h` (aggregator layer) and `λ` (fraction of aggregators), assigning weaker clients to stronger aggregators to balance pipeline delays (`T_clients` vs. `T_aggr`) via a binary search over `h`.
- **Core assumption:** The dominant delay term (`max(T_s, T_aggr, T_clients)`) can be minimized by balancing `T_aggr` and `T_clients` when `T_s` is not dominant, and this balance point can be found efficiently via binary search due to monotonicity.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "propose the first accuracy‑aware heuristic algorithm... AA HSFL‑ll... first identifies high‑accuracy candidate cut layers via offline training, then greedily optimizes layer placement and assignments to minimize round delay subject to accuracy constraints."
  - [section IV.A] Details Algorithm 1 for offline candidate identification.
  - [section IV.B] Details Algorithm 2, including binary search over `h` and greedy client assignment to minimize max delay.
  - [corpus] Corpus papers (e.g., "Have Your Cake...", "Lean Clients...") propose SFL optimizations but do not describe this specific two‑phase, accuracy‑gated greedy approach. Evidence is strong within paper; corpus indicates novelty in combining accuracy‑awareness with delay optimization in HSFL.
- **Break condition:** The binary search over `h` terminates when `|T_aggr - T_clients| ≤ δ`. If no `λ` reduces delay beyond a threshold `maxAggr`, the search stops.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Adaptively selecting the aggregator layer `h` and fraction of aggregators `λ` based on client heterogeneity `γ` and model workload distribution prevents straggler‑induced delay and maintains accuracy.
- **Mechanism:** Stronger clients are designated as local aggregators. The algorithm places `h` to balance the weak‑client‑side workload (`∑_{l=1}^h f_l / p_n`) and aggregator‑side workload (`∑_{l=h+1}^v f_l / p_k`). As `γ` increases, `λ` increases and `h` shifts to offload more from weakest clients.
- **Core assumption:** Client heterogeneity `γ` (ratio of strongest to weakest client throughput) determines the feasible `λ` that reduces delay; beyond a point, promoting weaker clients as aggregators increases delay.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section IV.B] "Increasing the fraction of aggregators `λ` introduces a trade‑off... once the strongest clients have been selected, promoting weaker clients... yields diminishing returns... there exists a threshold `maxAggr`... determined by client heterogeneity `γ`."
  - [section V.C] Tables VI and VII show how `h` and `λ` adapt to different `γ` to maintain stable accuracy.
  - [corpus] Papers like "KoReA‑SFL" address heterogeneity in SFL but via knowledge replay, not through hierarchical aggregator placement and `λ` tuning. Evidence is strong within paper; corpus shows alternative heterogeneity handling.
- **Break condition:** If `λ > maxAggr` (computed from `γ` and layer workloads), further increase is pointless as it may increase delay.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Split Federated Learning (SFL)  
  - **Why needed here:** The paper builds on SFL, which splits a model at a cut layer into client‑side and server‑side sub‑models, enabling privacy preservation and client computation offloading. HSFL extends this with an extra aggregator layer and local aggregators.  
  - **Quick check question:** What is the key difference between SFL and standard FL in terms of where backpropagation occurs?

- **Concept:** Local‑loss learning  
  - **Why needed here:** It mitigates the backward locking effect by allowing clients to compute gradients at the cut layer without waiting for the server's final loss, enabling parallel client‑server training. This is foundational to the batch processing delay model (T_FP and T_BP in Section III.B).  
  - **Quick check question:** How does local‑loss learning reduce training delay compared to sequential SFL?

- **Concept:** Model partitioning impact on accuracy  
  - **Why needed here:** Prior SFL assumed accuracy is cut‑layer invariant, but this paper's core premise is that the cut layer choice affects the quality of local‑loss gradients and thus final accuracy. This drives the need for accuracy‑aware cut layer selection.  
  - **Quick check question:** Why might a shallower cut layer (earlier in the network) lead to lower accuracy in local‑loss SFL?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Clients -> Local Aggregators: forward activations at layer h, backward gradients at layer h.  
Local Aggregators -> Server: forward activations at cut layer v, backward gradients at cut layer v.  
Server -> Clients & Local Aggregators: model broadcast (initial parameters and updates).  
Clients -> Server: upload client‑side model parameters.  
Local Aggregators -> Server: upload aggregator‑side and client‑side model parameters.

**Critical path:**  
1. Phase 1 (Model broadcast): Server sends \(W^w\) to clients and \(W^a, W^w\) to aggregators. Delay \(T_1\).  
2. Phase 2 (Epoch loop, repeated \(E \times Q\) times):  
   - Clients compute forward pass of \(W^w\) → send activations at layer \(h\) to assigned aggregator.  
   - Aggregators compute forward pass of \(W^a\) → send activations at cut layer \(v\) to server.  
   - Server computes forward pass of \(W^s\) → compute output loss → backpropagate through \(W^s\).  
   - Aggregators receive gradients at \(v\) → backpropagate through \(W^a\) using local loss → send gradients at layer \(h\) to clients.  
   - Clients backpropagate through \(W^w\).  
   - Local aggregation occurs at aggregators every epoch; global aggregation occurs at server every round.  
3. Phase 3 (Model upload): Aggregators upload \(W^a, W^w\); clients upload \(W^w\). Delay \(T_3 = T_1\).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Accuracy vs. Delay vs. Overhead:** Deeper cut layer \(v\) improves accuracy (better local‑loss gradients) but increases client computation and communication size. Higher fraction of aggregators \(\lambda\) improves aggregation frequency (boosting accuracy) but increases model upload overhead and may raise delay if aggregators are weak.  
- **\(h\) placement:** Shallow \(h\) reduces client‑side delay but increases aggregator workload (and vice versa). The optimal \(h\) balances \(T_{\text{aggr}}\) and \(T_{\text{clients}}\) to avoid pipeline bottlenecks.  
- **\(\lambda\) selection:** Too low \(\lambda\) reduces local aggregation frequency, hurting accuracy. Too high \(\lambda\) may promote weaker clients to aggregators, increasing delay and overhead; beyond a threshold \(\text{maxAggr}\) (determined by client heterogeneity \(\gamma\)), further increases are detrimental.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **Accuracy drop:** Suboptimal cut layer \(v\) (not in candidate set \(V^*\)) yields poor local‑loss gradients, leading to significantly lower test accuracy (e.g., Conv2 in AlexNet/VGG‑11).  
- **High delay:** Poor client‑to‑aggregator assignment creates stragglers; inappropriate \(h\) causes workload imbalance between clients and aggregators.  
- **High overhead:** Excessive \(\lambda\) (many aggregators uploading models each round) or deep \(v\) (large intermediate tensors) inflates communication overhead.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline comparison (Section V.A):** Compare AA‑HSFL‑ll against SFL, Multihop SFL, LocSFL, and DTFL on MNIST, CIFAR‑10, and CINIC‑10 using AlexNet, VGG, and ResNet. Measure accuracy, delay, and overhead. Expected outcome: AA‑HSFL‑ll dominates with +3% accuracy, –20% delay, –50% overhead at similar accuracy targets.  
2. **\(\lambda\) sensitivity (Section V.B):** Fix optimal \(v \in V^*\), vary \(\lambda \in \{0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.8\}\). Plot accuracy vs. delay and overhead. Expect optimal \(\lambda\) around 0.2–0.3; higher \(\lambda\) increases overhead and may lower accuracy.  
3. **Heterogeneity impact (Section V.C):** Vary client heterogeneity \(\gamma\) (ratios of strongest to weakest throughput: 2, 7.5, 15). Record selected \(h\) and \(\lambda\) (Tables VI, VII). Expect stable accuracy across \(\gamma\), but \(h\) and \(\lambda\) adjust: e.g., for VGG‑11, \(h\) becomes deeper with higher \(\gamma\); for ResNet‑101, \(h\) becomes shallower; \(\lambda\) generally increases to mitigate stragglers.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What is the precise trade‑off between the number of local aggregators (\(\lambda\)) and key performance metrics (accuracy, delay, overhead) in hierarchical SFL, and how does this vary with model architecture and data distribution?
- **Basis in paper:** The conclusion explicitly states: "An important direction for future work is to further investigate the role of aggregators, particularly by studying the trade‑offs between the number of aggregators and system performance metrics, such as accuracy, delay and overhead."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper explores the effect of varying \(\lambda\) (e.g., Section V‑B) but does not provide a general analytical model or exhaustive study across diverse scenarios (e.g., non‑IID data, different model depths). The observed optimal \(\lambda\) values (Table IV) are specific to the tested setups.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A systematic experimental framework or theoretical model that maps \(\lambda\), model characteristics (layer sizes, parameter counts), and data heterogeneity (e.g., Dirichlet concentration) to accuracy, delay, and overhead curves, validated across multiple architectures (CNNs, Transformers) and datasets.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can the joint optimization problem be effectively solved in an online manner where system states (client processing loads, network rates) change dynamically during training, without incurring excessive reconfiguration overhead?
- **Basis in paper:** The conclusion proposes: "Another interesting direction would be to study the formulated problem from the perspective of the online optimization framework that makes decisions as clients’ processing tasks arrive or as the system’s state... changes." Section IV.D discusses robustness and proposes re‑running Algorithm 2 upon changes, but this is a reactive offline approach.
- **Why unresolved:** The current solution assumes stable system parameters during a round and uses a centralized offline heuristic. An online framework must handle continuous state changes, balance re‑optimization frequency with stability, and potentially use lightweight adaptive heuristics or learning‑based policies.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Development and evaluation of an online algorithm (e.g., using sliding‑window optimization, reinforcement learning) that updates partitioning/assignment decisions at runtime. Performance should be benchmarked against the offline optimal and fixed policies under realistic trace‑driven dynamics (e.g., fluctuating Wi‑Fi rates, background tasks).

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How does explicitly incorporating client‑side energy consumption into the multi‑objective optimization (alongside delay and accuracy) alter the optimal partitioning and assignment decisions, and what are the achievable Pareto frontiers?
- **Basis in paper:** The conclusion suggests: "Another promising direction is to model the offloading decision as a multi‑objective optimization problem that jointly considers training delay and energy consumption, and to develop adaptive strategies that identify efficient trade‑offs under limited client availability."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper's objective (12) minimizes delay subject to accuracy constraints, with communication overhead as a secondary metric. Energy, a critical constraint for battery‑powered IoT devices, is not modeled. The interaction between energy (affected by local computation vs. communication) and the hierarchical structure (local aggregators consuming energy) remains unexplored.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An extended optimization model that includes client and aggregator energy costs (computation + transmission). Analytical or simulation‑based Pareto curves showing the delay‑accuracy‑energy trade‑off for different \(\lambda\) and partitioning choices. A heuristic (e.g., weighted sum or ε‑constraint method) that computes energy‑aware assignments and evaluates them against state‑of‑the‑art in terms of energy‑delay product.

## Limitations
- Unreported layer‑wise FLOPs and activation sizes force approximations in the delay model, potentially affecting the optimality of the chosen partitioning.
- Offline parameters (threshold `thr`, number of offline epochs `E′`) and baseline implementation details are missing, hindering precise replication and fair comparison.
- Simulations assume static network rates and ideal local‑loss; real‑world dynamics (e.g., fluctuating bandwidth, staleness) may degrade the observed performance gains.
