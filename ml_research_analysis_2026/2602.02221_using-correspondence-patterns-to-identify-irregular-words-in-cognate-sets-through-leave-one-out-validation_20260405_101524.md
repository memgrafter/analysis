# Using Correspondence Patterns to Identify Irregular Words in Cognate sets Through Leave-One-Out Validation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.02221
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02221
- **Reference count:** 13
- **Primary result:** Introduces a leave-one-out validation method using a new "balanced average recurrence" measure to identify irregular word forms in cognate sets with 85% overall accuracy on real data, improving to 87.8% with smaller language samples.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the historical linguistics problem of quantitatively measuring the regularity of sound correspondence patterns within cognate sets. It proposes a computational method that employs leave-one-out validation to flag irregular word forms by assessing which exclusion most improves the set's regularity score, as measured by a novel "balanced average recurrence" metric. Validation across 20 real comparative wordlists and simulated data demonstrates the method can correctly identify an inserted irregular form in 85% of cases, with accuracy rising when fewer languages are sampled due to reduced cumulative irregularity. The work provides a tool for data curation and quality assessment in historical linguistics.

## Method Summary
The method processes standardized CLDF comparative wordlists. After phonetic alignment (using SCA) and correspondence pattern inference (via CoPaR), it computes a regularity score for each cognate set: the log-transformed average of per-site recurrence counts (how many sites share a pattern), balanced and normalized. For leave-one-out validation, each word form in a set is temporarily replaced with a null segment (/ø/); the set's regularity is recomputed, and the form whose removal causes the greatest increase in the score is flagged as irregular. Testing involves injecting one random irregular form into 20% of sets and measuring if the method correctly identifies it. Experiments sample 5 or 10 languages per dataset and vary pre-existing irregularity in simulations.

## Key Results
- Overall identification accuracy is 85% across 20 real datasets when one irregular form is injected.
- Accuracy is higher (87.8%) with 5-language samples versus 79% with 10-language samples, indicating larger samples introduce more cumulative irregularity.
- Simulated data confirms accuracy declines as pre-existing irregularity (random phone replacements) rises, dropping below 10% when 40% of phones are replaced.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
The leave-one-out loop identifies the irregular word form by measuring which exclusion most improves the cognate set's regularity score. For each alignment site, a form's segments are temporarily replaced with /ø/ and the balanced average recurrence is recomputed. The form whose removal yields the highest gain is flagged as irregular, under the assumption that eliminating an outlier allows cleaner patterns to dominate. This relies on the existence of a single dominant irregularity that disrupts otherwise regular patterns.
### Mechanism 2
The balanced average recurrence measure creates a dataset-agnostic regularity score by normalizing and log-transforming the recurrence counts of correspondence patterns. It counts how many sites are compatible with each pattern, normalizes by total sites, log-transforms to down-weight extremes, averages, and exponentiates. This quantifies the extent to which a few patterns dominate the alignment, with higher scores indicating greater regularity.
### Mechanism 3
Sampling fewer languages increases identification accuracy because each additional language introduces new opportunities for sporadic or irregular reflexes (analogy, borrowing), which fragment correspondence patterns. With fewer languages, patterns are cleaner, making the injected irregular form's disruptive effect more pronounced in the leave-one-out test.

## Foundational Learning
- **Phonetic alignment:** Alignment of cognate word forms into columns of potentially homologous segments is prerequisite; misalignments create artificial irregularity. *Quick check:* How might automated alignment algorithms introduce noise that confounds correspondence pattern inference?
- **Correspondence pattern inference:** Grouping aligned sites into patterns based on segment compatibility (CoPaR) provides the atomic units whose recurrence is measured. *Quick check:* Why might a single proto-phoneme generate multiple correspondence patterns due to regular sound changes?
- **Leave-one-out cross-validation:** Iteratively removing each word form to measure its impact on set regularity is the core detection mechanism. *Quick check:* What structural property must a cognate set have for this method to successfully identify the irregular form?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Preprocessing (CLDF standardization) -> Phonetic Alignment (SCA) -> Correspondence Pattern Inference (CoPaR) -> Regularity Scoring (per-site recurrence, log-mean) -> Leave-one-out Loop (per-form replacement, score recomputation) -> Output (ranked irregular candidates).
**Critical path:** Data standardization → Alignment → Pattern inference → Regularity scoring → Leave-one-out iteration. Alignment and pattern inference are foundational; errors propagate.
**Design tradeoffs:** Manual vs. automated alignments (accuracy vs. scalability); sample size (pattern regularity vs. phylogenetic representativeness); single vs. batch irregularity detection (current method flags one form per set).
**Failure signatures:** Very low dataset-wide regularity scores suggest pervasive issues (poor alignments, non-cognates). Discrepancy between 5- and 10-language results indicates high language-specific irregularity. Inability to flag any form in a known-irregular set suggests multiple irregularities or flawed pattern inference.
**First 3 experiments:**
1. Run on simulated perfectly regular data with one randomly replaced form to establish baseline accuracy (~90% at low replacement rates).
2. Inject irregularities into real datasets at 5%, 10%, 20% replacement rates to map the accuracy decay curve.
3. Compare accuracy across subsampled language sets (5 vs. 10) on the same 20 real datasets to test the hypothesis that larger samples increase sources of irregularity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
How does accuracy change when a cognate set contains multiple irregular forms rather than one? The method is validated only on single-injection scenarios, but real sets often have several independent irregularities (borrowings, analogies). The paper's limitation section states the candidate selection only works with exactly one irregular entry; behavior with multiple is unknown.
### Open Question 2
What specific linguistic or data-structural properties cause the lowest-accuracy datasets (e.g., BowernPny, DunnIlelex) to struggle? The paper speculates extreme language diversity but lacks a detailed error analysis to quantify predictors of failure (e.g., lexical distance, alignment consistency, data quality).
### Open Question 3
Can this method be integrated into an iterative curation workflow to improve comparative wordlists and downstream analyses like phylogenetic inference? The paper suggests this potential application but does not test whether expert review of flagged forms leads to more robust trees.

## Limitations
- **Algorithmic underspecification:** Exact SCA alignment parameters and CoPaR compatibility thresholds are not detailed; reproduction depends on library defaults, possibly altering results.
- **Generalizability constraints:** Validation used 20 specific families; performance on drastically different phylogenetic structures (e.g., rapid radiations, isolates) remains unknown.
- **Break condition sensitivity:** The method assumes at most one irregular form per set; accuracy declines with high pre-existing irregularity (>20% replacement), but real-world irregularity levels are unquantified.

## Confidence
- *Regularity measure (balanced average recurrence):* **High** – mathematically defined and internally consistent.
- *Leave-one-out identification mechanism:* **Medium** – logically sound and tested on simulated data, but depends critically on pattern inference quality.
- *Sample size effect (fewer languages → higher accuracy):* **High** – empirically demonstrated in results (5 vs. 10 languages).
- *External validity claims:* **Low** – no cross-validation with independently annotated irregular forms; simulated irregularities may not capture all real types (analogy, borrowing).

## Next Checks
1. **Parameter sensitivity test:** Systematically vary SCA gap penalties and CoPaR compatibility thresholds on 3–5 datasets; measure impact on identification accuracy and regularity scores.
2. **Cross-family robustness:** Apply the full pipeline to 2–3 additional large CLDF wordlists from underrepresented families (e.g., Austronesian, Niger-Congo) and compare accuracy to the original 20.
3. **Multiple-irregularity simulation:** Inject two irregular forms per cognate set (at varying positions) in simulated data; quantify accuracy drop and determine if the method flags one or both.
