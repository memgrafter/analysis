# Calibrated Bayesian Deep Learning for Explainable Decision Support Systems Based on Medical Imaging

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.11973
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11973
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A Bayesian framework with CUB-Loss and Dual Temperature Scaling consistently improves calibration across medical imaging tasks, achieving high accuracy and uncertainty alignment (e.g., 97% accuracy and AvU 0.95 on pneumonia screening).

## Executive Summary
Deep learning models for medical decision support often suffer from miscalibration, being overconfident in erroneous predictions, which undermines clinical trust. This paper introduces a Bayesian framework to systematically align predictive uncertainty with correctness. The framework comprises two novel components: Confidence-Uncertainty Boundary Loss (CUB-Loss), which during training penalizes deviations from an ideal confidence-uncertainty boundary; and Dual Temperature Scaling (DTS), a post-hoc calibration method that applies separate temperature scaling to high-confidence and low-confidence regimes to better align uncertainty with accuracy.

The approach is evaluated on three medical imaging tasks: pneumonia screening (large-scale chest X-rays), diabetic retinopathy detection (fundus images), and skin lesion classification (dermoscopic images). Results demonstrate consistent improvements in calibration metrics such as Accuracy vs. Uncertainty (AvU) and Boundary Curve Calibration Error (BCCE), while maintaining classification accuracy. Notably, under data scarcity (25% training data) and severe class imbalance, the method sustains robust uncertainty estimates and even recovers Bayesian performance degradation. The framework thus offers a promising path toward trustworthy AI in clinical settings.

## Method Summary
The authors present a Bayesian deep learning method for calibrating uncertainty in medical image classification. The approach uses variational inference (VI) to approximate posterior distributions over network weights, enabling uncertainty estimation via Monte Carlo sampling. The training objective combines the evidence lower bound (ELBO) with a novel Confidence-Uncertainty Boundary Loss (CUB-Loss) that directly aligns predictive uncertainty with correctness according to a predefined Confidence-Uncertainty Boundary Curve (CUBC). After training, a post-hoc Dual Temperature Scaling (DTS) step optimizes two temperature parameters to minimize deviation from the CUBC on a validation set, refining the uncertainty estimates for deployment. The framework is implemented with Bayesian versions of standard architectures (e.g., BCXR-Net, BResNet) and evaluated on three medical imaging tasks, showing improved calibration (e.g., higher AvU) while preserving accuracy.

## Key Results
- **Pneumonia screening:** Achieved 97% accuracy, AvU of 0.95, and Near-OOD detection AUROC of 74.2%, demonstrating excellent calibration and out-of-distribution detection.
- **Data scarcity robustness:** With only 25% of the diabetic retinopathy training data, the proposed method maintained higher accuracy and stable uncertainty estimates compared to baselines.
- **Class imbalance recovery:** On imbalanced skin lesion data, the method recovered Bayesian performance degradation, yielding a balanced accuracy (BACC) of 66% and AvU of 0.84.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** CUB-Loss explicitly aligns predictive uncertainty with correctness by penalizing deviations from an ideal confidence-uncertainty boundary.
- **Mechanism:** The Confidence-Uncertainty Boundary Curve (CUBC) defines target uncertainty `U_ideal(ˆp)` as `U_min` for high confidence (`ˆp > γ`) and `U_max` for low confidence (`ˆp ≤ γ`). CUB-Loss computes per-sample deviation `δ_i` from this boundary in four quadrants (AC, AU, IC, IU), normalizes to `[0,1]`, and sums `-log(1-˜δ_i)` to heavily penalize samples near the boundary.
- **Core assumption:** The CUBC mapping represents the optimal relationship where correct predictions should be certain (low `U`) and incorrect ones uncertain (high `U`).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "CUB-Loss ... explicitly enforcing alignment between prediction correctness and uncertainty estimates."
  - [section 3.2.3] Defines `U_ideal(ˆp)` piecewise and visualizes CUBC in Figure 1.
  - [section 3.3.1] Details the four-region deviation logic `δ_i` based on `(ˆy_i, ˆp_i)`.
  - [corpus] Neighbors focus on calibration but not this specific boundary-driven loss (weak direct evidence).
- **Break condition:** If the assumed CUBC is misspecified for a dataset (e.g., medical images where even correct predictions may have high aleatoric uncertainty), forcing alignment may harm accuracy or produce unrealistic uncertainty bounds.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Dual Temperature Scaling (DTS) post-hoc refuncionalizes uncertainty toward the CUBC by applying bidirectionally scaled temperatures, improving separation without altering accuracy.
- **Mechanism:** Two thresholds (`γ_low`, `γ_high`) and an uncertainty threshold `η` partition samples. `T_high` sharpens distributions (lowers `U`) for high-confidence or low-uncertainty intermediate samples; `T_low` softens distributions (raises `U`) for low-confidence or high-uncertainty intermediate samples. Temperatures are optimized on a validation set to minimize Boundary Curve Calibration Error (BCCE), the avg absolute deviation of `U(p)` from `U_ideal(ˆp)`.
- **Core assumption:** The CUBC is piecewise approximated by separate scaling operations; a single temperature cannot simultaneously minimize uncertainty for correct high-confidence predictions while maximizing it for incorrect low-confidence ones.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Dual Temperature Scaling (DTS) ... further refining the posterior distribution."
  - [section 3.4.2] Details steps 1–3 and the scaling logic in Eq. (24).
  - [section 3.4.1] Defines BCCE in Eq. (21) as the calibration objective.
  - [corpus] Neighbors use single-temperature TS; DTS’s bidirectional nature is novel (weak corpus support for dual scaling).
- **Break condition:** If the chosen thresholds (`γ_low=0.9`, `γ_high=0.923`) do not match the dataset’s natural confidence distribution, scaling may misapply sharpening/softening, worsening calibration or creating artificial uncertainty gaps.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Variational Inference (VI) with per-weight Gaussian posteriors provides a more expressive uncertainty foundation than MC Dropout, enabling finer calibration when combined with CUB-Loss.
- **Mechanism:** VI approximates the intractable posterior `p(w|D)` with a factorized Gaussian `qθ(w)=N(μ,σ)` by minimizing the ELBO (Eq. 5). The learnable variance `σ` per parameter captures parameter-specific epistemic uncertainty. CUB-Loss then regularizes the predictive entropy `U(p)` derived from MC-sampled predictions.
- **Core assumption:** Parameter-wise uncertainty flexibility allows the model to express nuanced uncertainty that can be aligned with correctness via the CUBC; MC Dropout’s fixed Bernoulli masks limit this expressiveness.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 2.1.2] "VI ... offers greater statistical flexibility for capturing parameter-specific uncertainty."
  - [section 3.1] VI is used as the Bayesian foundation; ELBO loss combines reconstruction and KL.
  - [section 2.1.1] Critiques MC Dropout’s "fixed Bernoulli distribution" limitations.
  - [corpus] Multiple neighbors use VI for uncertainty (e.g., Bayesian_KANs, MedBayes-Lite), supporting VI’s role.
- **Break condition:** Under severe data scarcity or imbalance, the mean-field VI posterior may contract (cold posterior effect) or overfit, causing miscalibration that CUB-Loss cannot fully correct without additional strategies like MOPED initialization.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Variational Inference (ELBO optimization)**
  - **Why needed here:** Replaces intractable Bayesian posterior with a learnable distribution `qθ(w)`, enabling gradient-based training of Bayesian networks.
  - **Quick check question:** How does the KL term in the ELBO act as a regularizer preventing overfitting in Bayesian training?

- **Concept: Uncertainty-Aware Metrics (AvU, BCCE)**
  - **Why needed here:** Standard accuracy ignores correctness-uncertainty alignment; AvU quantifies dependable samples (AC+IU), while BCCE measures deviation from CUBC for DTS optimization.
  - **Quick check question:** Why is AvU preferred over ECE for assessing whether uncertainty correlates with correctness in medical decision support?

- **Concept: Temperature Scaling (TS) and Calibration**
  - **Why needed here:** TS is a monotonic post-hoc method to adjust confidence; DTS extends it to uncertainty by using two temperatures to differently affect high/low confidence regimes.
  - **Quick check question:** Why does single-temperature TS fail to calibrate uncertainty separately for high-confidence and low-confidence predictions?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input image → Bayesian backbone (VI layers) → MC sampling (S forward passes) → Predictive distribution p(y|x) → (training) CUB-Loss added to ELBO → (inference) DTS applied to mean logits using optimized (T_high, T_low) → calibrated p(y|x) & U(p)
- **Critical path:** 1) Train Bayesian network with `L_total = ELBO + β·CUB-Loss` (β warm-up then fixed). 2) Freeze weights, run validation set through network, collect logits/uncertainty. 3) Optimize (T_high, T_low) via L-BFGS to minimize BCCE. 4) Deploy with DTS scaling.
- **Design tradeoffs:** 
  - β weighting: Too high β during early training destabilizes feature learning; warm-up mitigates.
  - Threshold selection: γ (CUBC split) and η (DTS transition) are hyperparameters; paper fixes γ=0.9, η=0.325 based on domain knowledge—may need tuning per task.
  - Initialization: Standard VI vs MOPED (for imbalanced data) affects convergence; MOPED anchors prior on pre-trained deterministic weights.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Low AvU (<0.7) despite high accuracy: CUB-Loss not properly regularizing uncertainty.
  - High BCCE (>50) after DTS: Thresholds or temperature optimization failed; check γ_low/γ_high, η values.
  - OOD AUROC ~0.5: Uncertainty estimates not discriminative; may require stronger prior or more MC samples.
  - U_correct increases with data scarcity: Model remains epistemically uncertain; likely VI posterior not learned robustly.
- **First 3 experiments (from paper):**
  1. **Pneumonia screening (balanced, sufficient data):** Establish baseline VI → add CUB-Loss → add DTS. Measure accuracy, AvU, ∆U, OOD AUROC. Expected: accuracy maintained/slightly improved, AvU↑, ∆U↑, OOD AUROC ~74%.
  2. **Diabetic retinopathy (data scarcity):** Use reduced (25%) train/val vs full. Compare BResNet vs +CUB. Track U_correct stability across data fractions. Expected: +CUB maintains lower U_correct and higher accuracy when data scarce.
  3. **Skin lesions (severe imbalance):** Use MOPED + CUB-Loss vs baseline BResNet. Monitor BACC and AvU. Expected: BACC recovery (64%→66%) and AvU↑ (0.79→0.84) despite imbalance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How does the choice of Bayesian posterior initialization strategy (e.g., MOPED, random, or empirical Bayes) affect uncertainty calibration and predictive performance in Bayesian neural networks for medical image classification?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper employed different initialization approaches across experiments (direct VI for pneumonia, two-stage training with deterministic pre-training for diabetic retinopathy, and MOPED for skin lesions) and noted that "the choice of initialization strategy depends on specific data characteristics."
- **Why unresolved:** No controlled comparison was performed; the observed differences may be confounded by dataset properties, making it unclear which strategy is optimal under which conditions.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A systematic study holding dataset and architecture constant while varying only the initialization method, evaluating calibration (AvU, BCCE), accuracy, and uncertainty separation (ΔU).

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What is the impact of the confidence threshold γ and uncertainty threshold η in the proposed CUB-Loss and DTS on calibration quality across diverse medical imaging tasks?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors fixed γ = 0.9 and η = 0.325 based on theoretical considerations but did not investigate sensitivity or optimal selection for different datasets or pathologies.
- **Why unresolved:** Thresholds were not tuned per task; the robustness of the framework to these hyperparameters remains unknown, limiting practical deployment where optimal values may vary.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Sensitivity analyses varying γ and η across multiple datasets, measuring calibration metrics (AvU, BCCE) and clinical utility (e.g., rate of flagged uncertain predictions).

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can the proposed CUB-Loss and DTS framework be effectively extended to other medical imaging paradigms beyond classification, such as segmentation or object detection, while maintaining improved uncertainty calibration?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Evaluation was restricted to classification tasks (pneumonia screening, diabetic retinopathy, skin lesions); the authors claim generalizability but provide no evidence for segmentation or detection.
- **Why unresolved:** The theoretical derivation of the confidence–uncertainty boundary assumes a classification output; its applicability to pixel-wise or bounding-box predictions is untested.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Adapting the loss and calibration method to segmentation/detection heads and evaluating calibration (e.g., per-pixel uncertainty alignment) on benchmark medical segmentation datasets (e.g., ISIC for skin lesion segmentation, or chest X-ray segmentation).

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Under which data regimes (sample size, class imbalance, distribution shift) is a two-stage Bayesian training strategy (deterministic pre-training followed by Bayesian conversion) preferable to direct Bayesian training from scratch?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors used direct Bayesian training for pneumonia screening but switched to a two-stage approach for diabetic retinopathy and skin lesions, citing "optimization challenges under limited data" and "severe class imbalance."
- **Why unresolved:** The factors driving this choice were not isolated; it is unclear whether the two-stage approach universally stabilizes training or only under specific conditions.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments varying dataset size and imbalance level, comparing convergence stability, calibration metrics (AvU), and accuracy for direct vs. two-stage Bayesian training.
