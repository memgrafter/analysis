# Can Small Models Reason About Legal Documents? A Comparative Study

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.25944
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25944
- **Reference count:** 18
- **Primary result:** A 3B‑parameter Mixture‑of‑Experts model (Qwen3‑A3B) matches GPT‑4o‑mini in mean accuracy (46.5% vs 47.2%) and exceeds it on legal holding identification (71.2% vs 67.9% with few‑shot prompting), while a 9B dense model achieves only 17.7%.

## Executive Summary
This paper conducts a thorough evaluation of nine sub‑10B language models on three legal reasoning benchmarks—ContractNLI, CaseHOLD, and ECtHR Task A—using five prompting strategies. The goal is to determine whether small models can effectively reason about legal documents and to identify the factors that govern their performance beyond sheer parameter count. The findings reveal that architecture and training quality are more decisive than model size: a Mixture‑of‑Experts model activating just 3B parameters (Qwen3‑A3B) reaches parity with GPT‑4o‑mini in mean accuracy (46.5% vs 47.2%) and even outperforms it on CaseHOLD (71.2% vs 67.9% with few‑shot). In contrast, a poorly performing 9B dense model (Nemotron‑9B) lags far behind at 17.7%. Prompting strategy proves critical: Chain‑of‑Thought improves ContractNLI (+8.5 pp) but degrades CaseHOLD (−16.0 pp); few‑shot prompting is the most robust across tasks; and both BM25 and dense retrieval generate similar gains (0.3 pp difference), highlighting a bottleneck in the model’s ability to utilize retrieved context. The entire experiment suite cost only $62 using cloud APIs, demonstrating that rigorous evaluation is accessible without dedicated hardware.

## Method Summary
The study selects nine sub‑10B language models (including both dense and Mixture‑of‑Experts architectures) accessible via cloud APIs, covering a range of parameter counts and training regimes. Three English legal datasets are employed: ContractNLI (500 samples, three‑class natural language inference), CaseHOLD (500 samples, five‑way multiple choice), and ECtHR Task A (225 multi‑label samples). Five prompting strategies are applied to each model‑dataset combination: direct (zero‑shot), Chain‑of‑Thought (step‑by‑step reasoning), few‑shot (three training examples, ordered by document length), BM25 RAG (top‑3 lexical‑retrieved passages), and dense RAG (top‑3 semantic‑retrieved passages using all‑MiniLM‑L6‑v2). All runs use an 8,192‑token context window, greedy decoding, and three random seeds controlling stratified test sampling. A four‑stage answer extraction pipeline maps free‑form outputs to predefined labels; metrics include exact‑match accuracy (and macro F1 or subset accuracy where appropriate). Statistical significance is assessed via bootstrap confidence intervals and paired tests. The total cost of API calls is $62.

## Key Results
- **Qwen3‑A3B (3B active) vs. GPT‑4o‑mini and dense models:** Matches GPT‑4o‑mini mean accuracy (46.5% vs 47.2%) and surpasses it on CaseHOLD (71.2% vs 67.9% with few‑shot). The 9B dense Nemotron‑9B is the worst performer at 17.7%.
- **Chain‑of‑Thought effects:** Improves ContractNLI by +8.5 pp but harms CaseHOLD by −16.0 pp on average, with losses up to 37.2 pp for some models.
- **RAG bottleneck:** BM25 and dense retrieval differ by only 0.3 pp on average (27 paired comparisons, only one significant at p<0.01), indicating that the limiting factor is the model’s context utilization rather than retrieval quality.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) Architecture
MoE models like Qwen3‑A3B activate only a small fraction of parameters per token (e.g., 3B out of 30B), which decouples performance from raw parameter count. The sparse activation routes concentrate computation on task‑relevant subnetworks, preserving specialized legal knowledge without full‑parameter overhead. This mechanism assumes that legal reasoning relies on a subset of factual and logical patterns amenable to sparse routing. Evidence: Qwen3‑A3B matched GPT‑4o‑mini mean accuracy (46.5% vs. 47.2%) and exceeded it on legal holding identification (71.2% vs. 67.9%) with few‑shot prompting; moreover, Qwen3‑A3B outperformed all dense 7–8B models, while the worst performer was the 9B dense Nemotron at 17.7% [section 4.1]. Break condition: If legal tasks require extensive cross‑attention or integration of disparate legal domains that exceed the capacity of sparse routing, MoE efficiency may diminish.

### Mechanism 2: Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Prompting
CoT’s step‑by‑step scaffold aids logical decomposition for entailment‑style tasks but can degrade performance on constrained multiple‑choice settings. The mechanism: CoT encourages verbose outputs that may drift from the required answer format, especially in multiple‑choice tasks where the label must be selected from fixed options. This format vulnerability is exacerbated for models with weaker instruction following. Evidence: CoT improved ContractNLI accuracy by +8.5 percentage points but reduced CaseHOLD accuracy by −16.0 pp on average, with some models (e.g., Qwen2.5‑7B) losing up to 37.2 pp [abstract, section 4.4]. Break condition: If models are fine‑tuned or prompted to preserve format fidelity during CoT (e.g., with explicit final‑answer markers), the degradation might be mitigated.

### Mechanism 3: Retrieval‑Augmented Generation (RAG) Context Utilization
The near‑identical performance of BM25 (lexical) and dense (semantic) retrieval (0.3 pp difference) indicates that the bottleneck lies not in retrieving relevant passages but in the model’s ability to utilize them. The mechanism: Small language models struggle to integrate retrieved context with the query, leading to noise or distraction regardless of retrieval quality. Evidence: Both BM25 and dense RAG improved ContractNLI scores but often hurt CaseHOLD, and paired comparisons across 27 model‑strategy combos showed only one significant difference at p<0.01 [section 4.5]. Break condition: If the corpus contains highly specialized legal jargon requiring deep semantic matching (e.g., statutory interpretation), dense retrieval might yield larger gains over BM25.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Models (9, spanning MoE/dense, 3B–9B, API/open-weight) → Datasets (3: CONTRACTNLI, CASEHOLD, ECTHR) → Prompting strategies (5: direct, CoT, few‑shot, BM25 RAG, dense RAG) → Evaluation protocol (3 seeds, 500/225 samples, parse error handling, bootstrap stats).

**Critical path:**  
1. Define model pool (cloud API availability, parameter range).  
2. Select datasets covering distinct legal reasoning types (NLI, MCQ, multi‑label).  
3. Implement prompting templates with uniform context budget (8K tokens).  
4. Run all 405 experiments (9×5×3×3) with greedy decoding, collect predictions.  
5. Apply answer extraction pipeline, compute accuracy/F1, bootstrap CIs, pairwise tests.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Cost vs. coverage:* 500 samples/dataset balanced statistical power and $62 total cost; ECTHR reduced to 225 due to label sparsity.  
- *Prompt generality:* Single template per strategy (no per‑model optimization) ensures comparability but may understate best‑case performance.  
- *Cloud API dependency:* Enables low‑cost evaluation but introduces provider‑specific serving variations vs. local deployment.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High parse error rates (e.g., Nemotron‑9B 16.4% overall) indicate instruction‑following failures, often correlated with poor accuracy.  
- Task‑strategy mismatches: CoT degrading multiple‑choice accuracy; RAG hurting CASEHOLD across retrieval methods.  
- Floor effects on ECTHR (max 6.3% subset accuracy) signal task unsuitability for zero‑shot prompting.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline replication:** Run direct prompting on Qwen3‑A3B and GPT‑4o‑mini for all three datasets to confirm aggregate ranking (Qwen3‑A3B ≈ GPT‑4o‑mini).  
2. **CoT task‑dependency test:** On CONTRACTNLI and CASEHOLD, compare direct vs. CoT for Llama‑3.1‑8B; expect +8.5 pp gain on NLI and −16.0 pp loss on MCQ.  
3. **Retrieval method ablation:** For Qwen2.5‑7B on CONTRACTNLI, run BM25 RAG vs. dense RAG; expect <1 pp difference, confirming utilization bottleneck.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Fine‑tuning vs. prompting:** The study did not compare prompting with fine‑tuning (e.g., LoRA), so the potential accuracy gains from adaptation remain unknown. Evidence: Future work mentions fine‑tuning; limitations note the prompting‑only scope.
2. **Generalization to other tasks and languages:** Only three English legal tasks were tested. Findings may not hold for statutory interpretation, generative tasks, or multilingual settings (e.g., LEXTREME).
3. **Training data contamination:** Public legal documents in training corpora may inflate scores on CASEHOLD and CONTRACTNLI via memorization. The paper acknowledges contamination risk but lacks analysis.
4. **CoT degradation mechanism:** The reason CoT harms multiple‑choice accuracy is unclear; hypotheses include format drift. Systematic analysis of reasoning chains and prompt modifications (e.g., enforced answer formatting) is needed.

## Limitations
- **Prompt and sampling underspecification:** The exact prompt wording, few‑shot example selection, and stratified test sampling procedures are not fully detailed, making exact replication difficult.
- **Model drift:** Model versions accessed via cloud APIs may have changed since the experiments; endpoints are not version‑locked, so results could differ today.
- **Lack of external validation:** All three mechanism claims rely solely on the paper’s internal experiments; no external citations or independent replication is provided (corpus count is zero).
