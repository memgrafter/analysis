# AREG: Adversarial Resource Extraction Game for Evaluating Persuasion and Resistance in Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2602.16639
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16639
*   **Reference Count:** 40
*   **Primary Result:** Persuasion and resistance capabilities in LLMs are weakly correlated (ρ=0.33) and empirically dissociated, with all tested models exhibiting a systematic defensive advantage.

## Executive Summary
The Adversarial Resource Extraction Game (AREG) introduces a dynamic, zero-sum negotiation benchmark to evaluate the social intelligence of Large Language Models. In a round-robin tournament, models alternate as a persuasive "Culprit" attempting to extract resources and a defensive "Victim" resisting extraction. Outcomes are measured via resource transfer and dual Elo ratings. Analysis of 280 games across eight frontier models reveals that persuasion and resistance are only weakly correlated, suggesting they are distinct capabilities. Furthermore, a consistent defensive bias is observed: all models perform better as defenders. Linguistic analysis indicates that incremental commitment strategies aid persuasion, while verification-seeking responses are more effective than explicit refusals for defense.

## Method Summary
AREG operationalizes evaluation as a constrained, adversarial dialogue. Each game involves two LLMs in a fixed-length conversation where one (Culprit) must convince the other (Victim) to transfer a hypothetical resource (e.g., a donation) within a charitable fundraising frame. An independent LLM judge (Arbiter) adjudicates the final transfer amount. Models receive pairwise Elo ratings based on their performance in both roles across multiple opponents. The primary analysis correlates the resulting Culprit-Elo (C-Elo) and Victim-Elo (V-Elo) scores and performs regression on linguistic features extracted from the dialogues to identify strategy effectiveness.

## Key Results
*   Persuasion and resistance capabilities, as measured by C-Elo and V-Elo, are weakly correlated (Spearman's ρ = 0.33).
*   All eight frontier models exhibit a systematic defensive advantage, with mean V-Elo exceeding mean C-Elo by 216 points.
*   Linguistic analysis finds that "incremental commitment" (small initial asks) correlates with higher persuasion success, while "verification-seeking" defensive responses outperform "explicit refusal."

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
AREG moves beyond static prompt-response evaluation by forcing dynamic, goal-oriented interaction. The zero-sum, dual-role structure cleanly separates the tasks of generating persuasive pushes versus defensive resistance within the same conversational context. The Elo rating system, derived from many pairwise comparisons, provides a robust, relative skill measure less sensitive to absolute performance than a single static test. Using an LLM as the Arbiter allows for scalable, context-aware adjudication of nuanced conversational outcomes (partial resource transfer) rather than binary win/loss.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Adversarial Evaluation:** Framing assessment as a competitive game between agents reveals capabilities that static benchmarks miss.
    *   *Why needed:* To probe model behavior under strategic pressure and goal conflict.
    *   *Quick check:* Do model performance rankings change when evaluated cooperatively vs. adversarially?
*   **Elo Rating System:** A method for calculating relative skill levels from pairwise comparison results.
    *   *Why needed:* To aggregate performance across many heterogeneous matchups into a single, comparable score per role.
    *   *Quick check:* Are Elo ratings stable across different random tournament seeds?
*   **Persuasion Strategies (e.g., Cialdini's Principles):** Frameworks from social psychology for influence tactics.
    *   *Why needed:* To hypothesize and code linguistic features that might drive extraction success.
    *   *Quick check:* Do identified strategy coefficients (from regression) align with psychological theory?
*   **Resistance Strategies (e.g., Inoculation Theory):** Tactics for maintaining position against persuasion attempts.
    *   *Why needed:* To categorize and evaluate defensive dialogue moves beyond simple refusal.
    *   *Quick check:* Is "verification-seeking" uniquely predictive of resistance, or correlated with other defensive behaviors?
*   **LLM-as-Judge Paradigm:** Using an LLM to evaluate outputs of other LLMs.
    *   *Why needed:* To automate nuanced, qualitative judgment of conversational success at scale.
    *   *Quick check:* How does judge choice (e.g., Grok vs. GPT-4) affect the distribution of extracted amounts?
*   **Zero-Sum Game Theory:** A model where one agent's gain is the other's loss.
    *   *Why needed:* To create a clear, irreducible conflict that forces models to adopt opposing stances.
    *   *Quick check:* Does the correlation between roles change in a non-zero-sum (e.g., cooperative) variant of the game?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Game Prompt & Rules → Model (Culprit/Victim) → Dialogue History → Model Response → Arbiter (Judge) → Transfer Decision & Rationale → Elo Update Engine → Final C-Elo & V-Elo Ratings

**Critical Path:** The core loop iterates through matchup pairs. For each game: 1) Roles and prompts are assigned, 2) The Culprit and Victim generate alternating messages within a turn limit, 3) The Arbiter receives the final dialogue and predicts a transfer amount, 4) The game result (transfer) updates the pre-game Elo ratings for both participants in their respective roles.

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
*   *Single Arbiter vs. Multi-Judge:* Using one fast judge (Grok 4.1) prioritizes cost and speed but risks systematic bias. A multi-judge ensemble would improve reliability but increase complexity and expense.
*   *Fixed Scenario vs. Scenario Diversity:* The charitable fundraising frame provides a clean, consistent testbed but limits ecological validity. Varying scenarios could improve generalizability but complicate strategy analysis.
*   *Elo vs. Absolute Metrics:* Elo captures relative performance across the model cohort but obscures absolute capability (e.g., can any model persuase a human?).

**Failure Signatures:**  
*   **Judge Bias Artifacts:** Systematic patterns where certain linguistic styles (e.g., verbose, formal) consistently receive higher/lower transfers regardless of actual persuasion.
*   **Strategy Misclassification:** The post-hoc linguistic coding (e.g., tagging "incremental commitment") may not accurately reflect the model's *intent* or the *perception* by the Victim/Arbiter.
*   **Role Confusion:** Models failing to adhere to their assigned goal (Culprit conceding, Victim offering resources), which invalidates the role-specific Elo calculation.

**First Three Experiments:**  
1.  **Multi-Judge Replication:** Re-run the full tournament with 3-5 diverse arbiters (e.g., GPT-4, Claude, a small open model). Compute the stability of the C-Elo/V-Elo correlation and the mean V-Elo advantage across judge sets.
2.  **Human Baseline Establishment:** Run a sample of AREG games with human participants (as Culprit and Victim) using the same prompts. Compare human Elo distributions and correlation to the LLM cohort. Do humans show the same defensive bias?
3.  **Strategy Causality Probe:** Implement controlled system prompts that explicitly force or forbid specific strategies (e.g., "You must use an incremental commitment approach" vs. "You must make a single large ask"). Measure the causal impact on extraction rates for both roles.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   To what extent does reliance on a single LLM-as-judge (the Arbiter) introduce systematic bias into AREG's capability dissociation findings (ρ=0.33), and how might this bias alter the observed independence between persuasion and resistance?
    *   *Basis in paper:* The Limitations section identifies "Adjudication Bias and Single-Judge Variance" as a key limitation, advocating for larger human validation.
    *   *Why unresolved:* Current validation (96.1% perfect confidence, zero anomalies in 45 transcripts) is insufficient for subtle, systematic biases affecting correlations.
    *   *What evidence would resolve it:* A replication with a multi-judge ensemble or substantial human-annotated subset; comparison of correlation stability across adjudicators.
*   Are the observed defensive advantage and the superiority of "verification-seeking" over "explicit refusal" strategies generalizable beyond the high-trust, charitable fundraising scenario to lower-trust adversarial domains like phishing, fraud, or technical social engineering?
    *   *Basis in paper:* The Limitations notes the benchmark's restriction to a "charitable fundraising" framing, limiting ecological validity for "overt malicious acts." The Discussion speculates dynamics may differ in lower-trust settings.
    *   *Why unresolved:* The defensive strategies effective against a "fundraiser" may fail against a "tech support scammer." The dataset is 93.8% donation-framed, preventing domain-specific analysis.
    *   *What evidence would resolve it:* Implement and run AREG variants with different scenario prompts (e.g., "urgent wire transfer," "virus warning"). Compare V-Elo advantage magnitude and strategy effectiveness rankings.
*   Do the linguistic determinants of persuasion and resistance success identified in English hold cross-linguistically, or are they artifacts of Western, high-context communication norms embedded in the training data?
    *   *Basis in paper:* The Limitations explicitly identifies "Linguistic and Cultural Homogeneity" as a gap, noting persuasion strategies are culturally contingent and restricting to English may overlook cross-lingual capabilities.
    *   *Why unresolved:* Strategy effectiveness (e.g., direct verification) is culturally contingent; monolingual corpus prevents knowing if coefficients are universal.
    *   *What evidence would resolve it:* Translate the full AREG protocol into diverse languages (e.g., Mandarin, Japanese, Arabic). Re-run tournament and recompute strategy-outcome correlations; significant variation would confirm cultural conditioning.
*   What mechanistic factors—architectural (e.g., MoE vs. dense), training-stage (e.g., RLHF intensity), or prompting heuristics—explain the universal defensive advantage and the specific "Universal Predator" success of DeepSeek V3.2?
    *   *Basis in paper:* [Inferred] The paper empirically establishes the advantage and DeepSeek's success but does not analyze underlying causes. The Discussion notes no significant relationship with model scale.
    *   *Why unresolved:* Analysis stops at rankings without modeling *why* certain architectures (e.g., Llama 4 Maverick as strong defender) succeed or why DeepSeek exploits almost everyone.
    *   *What evidence would resolve it:* An ablation study correlating architectural metadata (MoE experts, sparsity), provider safety policies, and training objectives with key metrics (Spread, vulnerability to DeepSeek).

## Limitations
*   **Generalizability:** Evaluation is confined to a single zero-sum game format and eight frontier models; results may not hold for other interaction paradigms or model families.
*   **Human Baseline Gap:** Absence of human performance data in AREG prevents assessing whether model capabilities reflect general social intelligence or an artifact of LLM-specific behavior.
*   **Evaluation Artifacts:** Strategy classifications are derived from post-hoc linguistic analysis of model outputs, risking overinterpretation without validation of perceived intent by the Victim or Arbiter.

## Confidence
*   **Weak correlation (ρ=0.33) between persuasion and resistance:** **High** (direct empirical result from 280 games).
*   **Systematic defensive advantage (216 Elo gap):** **High** (consistent across all 8 models in tournament).
*   **Empirical dissociation of persuasion/resistance as distinct capabilities:** **Medium** (weak correlation suggests dissociation but is task-dependent; not a proof of separability).
*   **Linguistic strategy findings (incremental commitment, verification-seeking):** **Medium** (observed correlations within AREG framework; causal claims require controlled ablation).

## Next Checks
1.  **Cross-paradigm replication:** Re-run tournament with additional game types (e.g., ultimatum game, cooperative resource sharing) to test if persuasion/resistance dissociation persists beyond AREG's zero-sum structure.
2.  **Human-model comparison:** Collect human responses in AREG (same prompts) to establish human baseline Elo ratings and correlation; assess whether model gaps reflect general social intelligence or artifact.
3.  **Strategy causality test:** Conduct controlled ablations where models are explicitly prompted to use only "verification-seeking" or "refusal" defense strategies; measure impact on resistance Elo to isolate causal effect.
