# Teaching an Agent to Sketch One Part at a Time

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.19500  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19500  
- **Reference Count:** 40  
- **Primary Result:** The full model (SFT+RL) achieves a Long-CLIP cosine similarity of 0.298, outperforming SFT-only (0.281) and prior SketchAgent (0.288), and is preferred by users in 83–84% of comparisons.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses generating vector sketches progressively, part by part, to improve editability and interpretability over holistic generation. It introduces ControlSketch-Part, a dataset with automatic part-level annotations created through a multi-stage vision-language model pipeline, and trains a VLM agent using supervised fine-tuning followed by multi-turn reinforcement learning (GRPO). A process reward based on intermediate rendering similarity (DreamSim) provides dense credit assignment. The full model surpasses baselines in semantic similarity and user preference, demonstrating controllable, locally editable text-to-vector sketch synthesis.

## Method Summary
The approach consists of two stages. First, ControlSketch-Part is built by segmenting existing full sketches into parts and pairing each with a textual description using a VLM, yielding a scalable corpus of (prompt, part sequence, vector data). Second, a VLM agent is initially fine-tuned via supervised learning to predict the next sketch part given the prompt and prior parts. Then, reinforcement learning with GRPO is applied: after generating each part, the intermediate sketch is rendered and compared to the target sketch using DreamSim; this similarity becomes a dense process reward that updates the policy, encouraging incremental visual progress.

## Key Results
- Full SFT+RL model: Long-CLIP cosine similarity 0.298 (SFT-only 0.281; SketchAgent 0.288).  
- User studies: step quality preferred 83.1% vs SFT, 84.1% vs SketchAgent; final output quality >66% preference over other methods.  
- Enables controllable, locally editable vector sketch generation via part-by-part output.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Breaking sketch generation into discrete parts simplifies learning and supports local edits. The DreamSim-based process reward supplies immediate, dense feedback on visual similarity, guiding the agent to make steady, measurable progress. Supervised fine-tuning gives a stable initialization, while reinforcement learning refines the policy to maximize cumulative reward across steps, leading to higher-quality final sketches.

## Foundational Learning
**Progressive Sketch Synthesis** – Why needed: Produces editable and interpretable outputs by sequencing parts. Quick check: Can the model assemble coherent strokes into a recognizable object without global planning?  
**Part-level Annotation via VLM** – Why needed: Scales dataset creation without manual labeling. Quick check: Do automatically segmented parts match human intuition of sketch components?  
**Process Reward Reinforcement Learning** – Why needed: Alleviates sparse credit assignment by rewarding each incremental step. Quick check: Does a dense reward correlate with human assessment of drawing progress?  
**DreamSim as Visual Similarity** – Why needed: Captures perceptual similarity better than pixel metrics. Quick check: Is DreamSim sensitive to stroke order and stylistic variations?  
**Vector Drawing Representation** – Why needed: Resolution independence and local editability. Quick check: Does the agent output valid vector commands that render correctly?  

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Text Prompt → VLM Agent (policy network) → Sketch Part Commands → Renderer → Raster Image → DreamSim Comparator → Process Reward → GRPO Update → (back to Agent)

**Critical Path**  
1. Encode text prompt and current partial sketch.  
2. Agent predicts next part’s vector strokes.  
3. Render updated sketch.  
4. Compute DreamSim similarity to target.  
5. If training: use similarity as reward for GRPO update; if inference: repeat until stopping condition.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *Automatic vs manual annotations*: Automation scales but may introduce noise; manual ensures quality but is costly.  
- *DreamSim reward vs human perceptual reward*: DreamSim is cheap but may not fully capture artistic quality.  
- *Part granularity*: Finer parts increase editability but complicate generation; coarser parts simplify but reduce control.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Redundant or overlapping strokes (duplicate parts).  
- Missing major components (e.g., absent head or limbs).  
- Sudden drops in DreamSim score at specific steps, indicating reward misalignment.  
- Failure to terminate, producing excessive parts.

**First Experiments**  
1. Visualize random samples from ControlSketch-Part to sanity-check part segmentation quality.  
2. Train with SFT only and evaluate whether complete sketches match target semantics.  
3. Add GRPO with DreamSim reward and verify average per-step reward increases over training epochs.

## Limitations
- Automatic part annotations may contain biases or segmentation errors.  
- DreamSim-based process reward might not align with human perception of progress, especially for abstract styles.  
- Evaluation relies on Long-CLIP similarity and modest user studies, leaving real-world editability and interpretability under-explored.

## Confidence
- **Dataset construction:** Medium – innovative but lacks human validation.  
- **Method (GRPO with DreamSim reward):** Low – novel reward design untested; risk of reward hacking.  
- **Results (similarity gains and user preferences):** Medium – improvements shown statistically but may not generalize broadly.

## Next Checks
1. **Human annotation audit:** Recruit artists to evaluate part-level correctness on a stratified sample of ControlSketch-Part, reporting error rates and inter-annotator agreement.  
2. **Reward ablation study:** Replace DreamSim with alternative progress signals (e.g., human step ratings or geometric metrics) in GRPO to isolate its contribution to final quality.  
3. **Cross-category generalization:** Test the trained agent on held-out object categories (e.g., tools, vehicles) to measure performance drop and identify category-specific biases.
