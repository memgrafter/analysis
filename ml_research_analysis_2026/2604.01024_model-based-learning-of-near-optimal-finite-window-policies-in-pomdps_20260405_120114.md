# Model-Based Learning of Near-Optimal Finite-Window Policies in POMDPs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.01024  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01024  
- **Reference count:** 21  
- **Primary result:** Sample complexity $O(\varepsilon^{-2})$ for learning near-optimal finite-window policies in tabular POMDPs, matching the optimal rate for fully observable MDPs.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses model-based learning of finite-window policies in tabular partially observable Markov decision processes (POMDPs). The authors propose estimating a superstate MDP from a single trajectory and then applying value iteration. Under standard regularity assumptions—bounded transition and observation probabilities that guarantee filter stability—the method achieves a trajectory length scaling as $O(\varepsilon^{-2})$, yielding a policy with value at most $5\varepsilon + 12(1-\rho)^m/(1-\gamma)^2$ below optimal. This matches the optimal $\varepsilon^{-2}$ rate for fully observable MDPs and improves upon prior $O(\varepsilon^{-4})$ bounds, demonstrating that the sample complexity gap is avoidable.

## Method Summary
The core method constructs a superstate MDP where each superstate encodes the recent action-observation history up to a fixed window size $m$. A single trajectory is collected with actions chosen uniformly at random. Empirical frequencies of transitions between superstates are used to estimate the model. The uniform exploration ensures each superstate-action pair is visited with non-negligible probability, enabling accurate estimation via concentration inequalities. The estimated MDP is then solved by value iteration to obtain a stationary policy. The analysis relies on filter stability (a contraction property) to bound the approximation error from using finite windows and on high-probability concentration to control model estimation error, leading to the stated sample complexity.

## Key Results
- Sample complexity: trajectory length $T = \widetilde{O}(A^{2m} m^2 / \varepsilon^2)$ to achieve the stated error.
- Error guarantee: $V^* - V(\pi) \le 5\varepsilon + 12(1-\rho)^m/(1-\gamma)^2$, where $\rho = S\alpha\beta$ depends on problem parameters.
- The $\varepsilon^{-2}$ rate matches the optimum for fully observable MDPs and improves prior $O(\varepsilon^{-4})$ bounds for POMDPs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The success stems from three key insights: (1) The superstate reduction converts the POMDP into a fully observable MDP over histories, enabling the application of standard MDP algorithms. (2) Filter stability (a contraction in the belief update) ensures that a finite window $m$ suffices to capture the relevant information about the hidden state; the error due to truncation decays as $(1-\rho)^m$. (3) Uniform random action selection creates a time-homogeneous underlying chain with a uniform lower bound on visitation probabilities, which together with bounded probabilities yields a minimum visitation probability for each superstate-action pair. This allows the use of concentration inequalities (e.g., empirical Bernstein) to bound model estimation error with high probability, giving an $\varepsilon$-accurate model after $O(1/\varepsilon^2)$ samples. The overall error is the sum of estimation error (controlled by samples) and approximation error (controlled by $m$), and balancing these yields the stated guarantees.

## Foundational Learning
- **POMDP**  
  *Why needed:* The problem setting where the agent receives partial observations instead of full state information.  
  *Quick check:* Model includes latent state space $S$, action space $A$, observation space $O$, and transition/observation probabilities.

- **Superstate MDP**  
  *Why needed:* Transforms the POMDP into a fully observable MDP by treating histories of actions and observations as states.  
  *Quick check:* A superstate is a sequence $(o_1,a_1,\dots,o_t,a_t,o_{t+1})$ of length $\le m$.

- **Filter stability**  
  *Why needed:* Guarantees that the belief contraction property holds uniformly, ensuring finite windows suffice.  
  *Quick check:* The filter operator is a contraction with factor $\rho = S\alpha\beta < 1$ under Assumptions 1–2.

- **Finite-window policy**  
  *Why needed:* Restricts policies to depend only on the recent window, making them computable from the superstate MDP.  
  *Quick check:* Policy $\pi: \mathcal{H}_{\le m} \to A$ maps a superstate to an action.

- **Uniform exploration**  
  *Why needed:* Ensures every superstate-action pair is visited with probability at least $\beta^m/(Am)$, enabling accurate estimation.  
  *Quick check:* The agent selects actions i.i.d. uniformly at random during data collection.

- **Sample complexity**  
  *Why needed:* Quantifies the number of samples required to guarantee an $\varepsilon$-optimal policy with high probability.  
  *Quick check:* The trajectory length $T$ scales as $\widetilde{O}(1/\varepsilon^2)$ up to problem-dependent and exponential-in-$m$ factors.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map (plain text):**  
Data Collection (single trajectory) -> Superstate Construction (windowing) -> Model Estimation (frequency counting) -> Value Iteration (solve MDP) -> Policy Extraction

**Critical path:**  
1. Sufficiently long trajectory to ensure low model estimation error.  
2. Accurate construction of superstates from raw data.  
3. Filter stability holds ($\rho < 1$) so that the superstate MDP approximates the original POMDP well.  
4. Value iteration converges to near-optimal policy in the estimated MDP.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Window size $m$:* Larger $m$ reduces approximation error $(1-\rho)^m$ but increases superstate space exponentially ($\le (AO)^m$), requiring more samples. Smaller $m$ improves sample efficiency but may increase bias.  
- *Exploration strategy:* Uniform random actions simplify analysis and guarantee minimum visitation probabilities, but may be sample-inefficient. Adaptive/optimistic exploration could reduce sample cost but complicates concentration arguments.  
- *Assumptions:* Lower bounds $\alpha,\beta>0$ on transitions/observations ensure stability and lower visitation probabilities; relaxing them might break the current analysis.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Low visitation counts for some superstates cause high variance in transition estimates, leading to a poor policy.  
- If filter stability fails ($\rho \ge 1$), the additive approximation error does not decay with $m$, making the policy potentially far from optimal regardless of sample size.  
- Choosing $m$ too small yields large bias; choosing $m$ too large may cause insufficient samples per superstate, making the estimation error dominate.

**First experiments:**  
1. Implement the algorithm on the Tiger POMDP benchmark; vary trajectory length $T$ and measure empirical suboptimality against the known optimal value to confirm $O(1/T)$ scaling.  
2. Conduct sensitivity analysis on RockSample: sweep window size $m$, compute empirical $(1-\rho)^m$ term, and observe the trade-off between bias and variance.  
3. Re-run a prior model-based POMDP method with $O(\varepsilon^{-4})$ rate on the same instances; compare sample efficiency to verify improvement.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can the lower bounds on transition probabilities (Assumption 1: α) and observation probabilities (Assumption 2: β) be relaxed while still obtaining finite-sample guarantees for superstate MDP estimation?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion states that investigating whether assumptions can be relaxed is an important direction for future work. The analysis critically uses these bounds for contraction and minimum visitation probabilities.  
- **Why unresolved:** The proofs rely on α and β to establish filter stability (Proposition 1) and to bound the probability of visiting superstates (e.g., P(ψₜ=1) ≥ βᵐ/(Am)). Weaker conditions might break these arguments.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A modified concentration inequality or filtering argument that works under, for example, only per-state lower bounds or ergodicity assumptions without uniform β.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Is it possible to achieve polynomial (instead of exponential) dependence on the window size m in the sample complexity under filter stability?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] In the remarks following Theorem 1, the authors note that a polynomial dependence on m would imply a polynomial sample complexity algorithm for POMDPs and call this "an open question."  
- **Why unresolved:** The current bound $T = O(A^{2m} m^2/\varepsilon^2)$ and approximation error $(1-\rho)^m/(1-\gamma)^2$ both scale exponentially with m due to the size of the superstate space $|\mathcal{H}_{\le m}| \le (AO)^m$ and the filter stability contraction factor $\rho = S\alpha\beta$. No technique is known to avoid this.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A new algorithm or analysis that bypasses the union bound over all $(AO)^m$ superstates or exploits additional structure to obtain a bound polynomial in m, or a lower bound showing exponential dependence is necessary.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What is the minimax sample complexity lower bound for learning an ε-optimal policy in tabular POMDPs using finite-window methods?  
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors compare their $O(\varepsilon^{-2})$ rate to the optimal $O(\varepsilon^{-2})$ for fully observable MDPs and note the prior $O(\varepsilon^{-4})$ gap. They do not prove a lower bound for POMDPs, leaving open whether $O(\varepsilon^{-2})$ is achievable or tight in this setting.  
- **Why unresolved:** While [14] gives an $\Omega(\varepsilon^{-2})$ lower bound for MDPs, the partial observability and mismatch between sampling ($P_\infty$) and target ($M_m$) models could require more samples. No lower bound specific to the superstate MDP reduction is provided.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A rigorous lower bound proof (e.g., via Fano’s inequality or an information-theoretic argument) for the POMDP learning problem under the considered assumptions, showing $\Omega(\varepsilon^{-2})$ or a higher requirement.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Can the uniform-at-random action selection be replaced by an adaptive or directed exploration strategy to improve sample efficiency or relax exploration-related assumptions?  
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Algorithm 1 uses uniform random actions to ensure all superstates are sufficiently visited. The analysis uses this to bound misestimation probabilities, but does not explore whether smarter exploration (e.g., optimism-driven, coverage-maximizing) could reduce $T$ or weaken Assumption 2.  
- **Why unresolved:** The uniform exploration simplifies the analysis by making the underlying Markov chain time-homogeneous and ensuring each $(w,a)$ pair has a minimum visitation probability. Adaptive strategies might introduce bias or dependence that complicates concentration.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A new algorithm with an adaptive exploration policy that provably achieves the same or better $O(\varepsilon^{-2})$ rate with fewer samples, possibly under weaker observation or transition assumptions, along with a matching concentration analysis.

## Limitations
- Tabular assumption limits scalability because the superstate MDP size grows exponentially with window length $m$.  
- Guarantees require bounded transition/observation probabilities and filter stability ($\rho < 1$), which may not hold in all environments (e.g., vanishing observation probabilities).  
- The additive error term $12(1-\rho)^m/(1-\gamma)^2$ can be large if $\rho$ is small or $m$ is too small, potentially demanding impractically large window sizes to achieve low bias.

## Confidence
- $O(\varepsilon^{-2})$ sample complexity matching MDP optimal rate: **High**  
- Specific error bound $5\varepsilon + 12(1-\rho)^m/(1-\gamma)^2$: **Medium**  
- General applicability under boundedness/stability assumptions: **Medium**
