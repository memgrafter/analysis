# F-GRPO: Don't Let Your Policy Learn the Obvious and Forget the Rare

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.06717
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06717
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: F-GRPO mitigates distribution sharpening at intermediate group sizes (N=8), boosting pass@256 (e.g., +3.6 on Qwen2.5-7B GRPO) while preserving pass@1 and matching GRPO@32 with 4× fewer rollouts.

## Executive Summary
The paper identifies that for group-relative reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, intermediate group sizes (e.g., N=8) cause policies to over-concentrate on common correct solutions, harming solution diversity (pass@256). Two mechanisms are characterized: a non‑monotonic tail‑miss probability that peaks at intermediate N, and update dynamics that shrink the probability of unsampled correct actions even while total correct mass grows. The proposed F‑GRPO method down‑weights gradient contributions from prompts with high empirical success rates using a Focal‑loss inspired scaling factor. This lightweight modification is compatible with GRPO, DAPO, and CISPO. On Qwen2.5‑7B at N=8, F‑GRPO improves pass@256 by up to 3.6 points over baselines while preserving or improving pass@1, matches GRPO@32 with only one‑quarter the rollouts, and shows consistent gains across model families and both in‑domain and out‑of‑domain benchmarks.

## Method Summary
F‑GRPO alters group‑relative policy optimization by scaling the advantage of each rollout by a prompt‑specific factor \(g(x) = (1 - b\mu_{\text{pos}})^\gamma\), where \(b\mu_{\text{pos}}\) is the proportion of correct rollouts in the group and \(\gamma > 0\) is a hyperparameter. This difficulty‑aware scaling reduces updates on easy prompts (high \(b\mu_{\text{pos}}\)) where the pressure to shrink unsampled‑correct mass is strongest. The method requires no extra computation beyond a per‑rollout multiplication and integrates directly into existing RLVR loops (e.g., verl). Training uses binary rewards (1 for correct, 0 otherwise) on the DeepScaleR math dataset; evaluation measures pass@1 and pass@256 on a suite of math and OOD benchmarks. Typical hyperparameters: \(\gamma \in \{0.5, 1.0\}\), global batch 256, learning rate \(1\!\times\!10^{-6}\), 10 epochs.

## Key Results
- At group size N=8 on Qwen2.5‑7B, F‑GRPO improves pass@256 over GRPO (64.1→70.3), DAPO (69.3→72.5), and CISPO (73.2→76.8) while maintaining or improving pass@1.
- F‑GRPO with N=8 matches the performance of GRPO with N=32 using only one‑quarter of the rollouts per prompt.
- Gains are robust across model families (Qwen2.5‑1.5B‑Math, Llama‑3.2‑3B‑Instruct) and generalize to both in‑domain (AIME24/25, AMC23, MATH500, Minerva, Olympiad Bench) and out‑of‑domain (IFEval, SynLogic, GPQA Diamond) benchmarks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1 (Tail‑miss peak at intermediate N).** The probability that a gradient update completely misses rare‑correct trajectories, \(\Pr(B_\tau)\), is non‑monotonic in group size \(N\) because two competing effects—update activity (increasing with \(N\)) and coverage of rare modes (also increasing with \(N\))—produce a peak at intermediate \(N\). This is derived under i.i.d. rollouts, fixed rare‑correct mass \(\tau\), binary rewards, and updates active when not all rewards equal. The result is supported by Lemma 3.1 (Eq 11) and Figure 2, while the corpus notes prior work on group size (Hu et al., Wu et al.) but highlights the novelty of the peak. The peak declines when \(N\) is very small (activity too low) or very large (coverage too high).

**Mechanism 2 (Unsampled‑correct mass shrinkage).** In batches with a positive success rate (SR > 0), the standard RL update can decrease the probability assigned to correct actions that were not sampled. Using a one‑step linear surrogate, softmax policy, and binary rewards (\(R_w \le 0\)), the change in unsampled‑correct mass (\(\Delta U_{\text{pos}}\)) is the sum of a negative drift proportional to \(-SR \cdot U_{\text{pos},2}\) and an additional loss due to normalization coupling with gains on sampled‑correct actions. Thus total correct mass may grow while the mass on unsampled correct actions shrinks (Proposition 3.2, Eq 13, and Section 3.2). The effect vanishes only if SR ≤ 0 or unsampled mass is already zero.

**Mechanism 3 (Focal weighting suppresses concentration).** F‑GRPO scales each rollout’s group‑relative advantage by \(g(x) = (1 - b\mu_{\text{pos}})^\gamma\). Since empirical SR correlates positively with \(b\mu_{\text{pos}}\), easy prompts (high \(b\mu_{\text{pos}}\)) receive smaller updates, mitigating the downward pressure on unsampled‑correct mass described in Mechanisms 1‑2. This preserves rare‑correct modes, improving pass@256 without sacrificing pass@1. The method applies to any group‑relative algorithm (GRPO, DAPO, CISPO) and adds negligible compute. Empirically, \(\gamma\) in the range \([0.5, 1.0]\) yields the best trade‑off.

## Foundational Learning
- **Group-relative policy optimization (GRPO)**. This is the baseline algorithm; F‑GRPO modifies its advantage scaling. Understanding the group baseline \(\bar{R}\), group standard deviation \(\sigma_R\), and clipping is prerequisite. Quick check: Why does \(\sigma_R\) appear in the denominator of \(bA_{\text{GRPO}}\)? (to avoid degenerate updates when all rewards are equal).
- **Tail‑miss probability and non‑monotonic sampling effects**. The risk that an active update misses rare‑correct solutions peaks at intermediate \(N\), which motivates F‑GRPO. Quick check: As \(N \to \infty\), what happens to \(\Pr(B_\tau)\)? (tends to zero because coverage increases).
- **Softmax update dynamics**. The theoretical analysis (Section 3.2) uses softmax policies to show how mass redistributes; the intuition explains why concentration occurs. Quick check: For an unsampled correct action, what sign does \(\Delta z_i\) take when SR > 0? (negative).

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Sample prompts → Generate N rollouts per prompt → Compute binary rewards → Compute \(b\mu_{\text{pos}}\) per prompt → Compute Focal weight \(g(x) = (1 - b\mu_{\text{pos}})^\gamma\) → Compute group‑relative advantages per rollout → Scale each advantage by its prompt’s \(g(x)\) → Compute policy loss (e.g., clipped surrogate) → Update policy.

Critical path: \(b\mu_{\text{pos}}\) and \(g(x)\) must be computed and broadcast before gradient accumulation; any shape mismatch or delay corrupts the scaling.

Design tradeoffs:
- \(\gamma\) selection: low values have minimal effect; high values may suppress updates excessively. Typical \(\gamma \in [0.5, 1.0]\) chosen via validation pass@1.
- Compute overhead is negligible (one extra multiplication per rollout).
- Compatibility: works with GRPO, DAPO, CISPO; clipping hyperparameters remain unchanged but may interact with \(\gamma\).

Failure signatures:
- No improvement in pass@256 versus baseline at same N.
- Significant drop in pass@1 (> 2 points), indicating over‑suppression.
- Distribution of \(g(x)\) near 1 (γ ineffective) or near 0 (learning stalls).
- Validation fails to reproduce the pattern of baseline pass@256 drop at N=8.

First 3 experiments:
1. **Group‑size sweep**: Run N=2, 8, 32 on a small subset; log \(b\mu_{\text{pos}}\) and reward patterns to verify the non‑monotonic tail‑miss risk (Mechanism 1).
2. **γ sweep at N=8**: Test \(\gamma \in \{0, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0\}\); monitor pass@1/pass@256. Expect best trade‑off around \(\gamma=0.5–1.0\).
3. **Diversity check**: Measure entropy of rollouts per prompt or NLL on base‑model rare‑correct trajectories; F‑GRPO should show less deviation, confirming preservation of rare modes.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1**: How does incorporating the Focal weight \(g(x)\) into group‑relative advantage scaling affect the tail‑miss probability \(\Pr(B_\tau)\) and can it eliminate the intermediate‑N peak that causes sharpening? The paper derives \(\Pr(B_\tau)\) under uniform weighting but does not analyze the effect of data‑dependent reweighting.
- **Open Question 2**: Can F‑GRPO be extended to reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards that use dense or continuous rewards? The entire analysis assumes binary rewards; dense rewards would complicate the role of \(b\mu_{\text{pos}}\) as a success‑rate proxy.
- **Open Question 3**: What is the theoretical or empirical optimal strategy for setting the Focal exponent \(\gamma\) across prompts and training stages? The paper sweeps a few fixed values and selects the best by validation pass@1, but provides no adaptive schedule or guidance for distribution shifts.
- **Open Question 4**: To what extent does F‑GRPO’s improvement on out‑of‑distribution benchmarks stem from preserved diversity of reasoning paths, and can we directly measure rare‑mode retention during training? The paper uses \(\Delta\)NLL\(_{\text{rare}}\) as an indirect proxy; a causal link is not established.

## Limitations
- Theoretical analysis relies on strong assumptions (i.i.d. rollouts, fixed rare‑correct mass \(\tau\), binary rewards, one‑step linear update) that may not hold in real, non‑stationary RL training.
- Empirical support for the non‑monotonic tail‑miss peak (Mechanism 1) is limited to a single dataset (DeepScaleR) and one figure; broader validation is needed.
- Focal weighting uses \(b\mu_{\text{pos}}\) as a heuristic proxy; \(\gamma\) tuning is manual and may not transfer across domains or training stages.

## Confidence
- Intermediate N leads to peak tail‑miss risk (Mechanism 1): Medium
- RL updates shrink unsampled‑correct mass (Mechanism 2): Medium
- F‑GRPO improves pass@256 without harming pass@1 (empirical results): High

## Next Checks
1. **Track rare‑correct mass**: During training, measure probability on held‑out rare‑correct trajectories from the base model and compare baseline vs. F‑GRPO.
2. **Controlled tail‑miss test**: In a synthetic bandit with known \(\tau\), measure actual miss rates across \(N\) to verify the predicted non‑monotonic curve.
3. **Cross‑domain evaluation**: Apply F‑GRPO to code generation (HumanEval) and instruction following (IFEval) to test generalization beyond math reasoning.
