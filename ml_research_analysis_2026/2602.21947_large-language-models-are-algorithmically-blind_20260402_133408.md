# Large Language Models are Algorithmically Blind

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.21947  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21947  
- **Reference count:** 18  
- **Primary result:** Frontier LLMs achieve only 15.9% calibrated coverage when predicting causal discovery algorithm performance, far below the random baseline of 36.5%, revealing systematic algorithmic blindness.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether large language models (LLMs) can provide calibrated predictions—meaning accurate confidence intervals—for the performance of causal discovery algorithms across different datasets. The authors run four standard algorithms (PC, FCI, LiNGAM, NOTEARS) on 13 datasets using 100 bootstrap repetitions to obtain ground-truth means and confidence intervals for four metrics. Eight state-of-the-art LLMs are queried to predict the algorithmic performance ranges. The results show that LLM-predicted intervals are drastically over‑wide yet still fail to contain the true mean, yielding extremely low calibrated coverage (15.9%). Performance is especially poor on synthetic datasets and larger networks, and error patterns suggest that models rely on memorization of benchmark statistics rather than reasoning about algorithm-dataset interactions. This defines a new failure mode: **algorithmic blindness**, where LLMs cannot form accurate probabilistic expectations about algorithmic behavior from problem structure alone.

## Method Summary
The evaluation pipeline first generates ground truth by executing each of four causal discovery algorithms on 13 datasets (9 benchmark + 4 synthetic) with 100 bootstrap runs, producing empirical means and 95% confidence intervals for Precision, Recall, F1, and SHD. Eight frontier LLMs are queried with three distinct prompt templates for every dataset‑algorithm‑metric combination (1,248 API calls total). Each LLM returns a lower and upper bound; the three bounds are averaged to produce a single interval per condition. Calibrated coverage is measured as the fraction of these intervals that contain the corresponding empirical mean. The design leverages synthetic datasets (varying network size) to eliminate memorization cues and compares benchmark vs. synthetic performance, interval widths, and cross‑model agreement to diagnose memorization versus reasoning.

## Key Results
- Overall calibrated coverage is **15.9%**, well below the random baseline of 36.5% and the worst‑performing random guesser (36.5%).
- LLM‑predicted ranges are **8–27 times wider** than true bootstrap confidence intervals, yet still systematically exclude the true mean.
- Coverage collapses on synthetic datasets and degrades monotonically with network size; patterns (algorithm‑specific collapse, range compression on benchmarks) indicate memorization of benchmark statistics.
- The best model (Claude) reaches only 39.4% coverage—barely above chance—and seven of eight models perform worse than random guessing.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Frontier LLMs produce systematically miscalibrated performance ranges for causal discovery algorithms.
- **Mechanism:** Models generate prediction intervals that are orders of magnitude wider than empirical confidence intervals yet still fail to contain the true algorithmic mean, indicating a failure to form accurate probabilistic beliefs about algorithmic output distributions from problem structure.
- **Core assumption:** LLMs lack an internal model linking dataset characteristics and algorithmic assumptions to expected performance distributions.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] "LLM‑predicted ranges were 8–27 times wider than true confidence intervals yet systematically missed the true mean."  
  - [section 4.2] "Note that the elevated baseline reflects wide valid metric domains rather than any predictive value of random guessing... Wide ranges that still miss the ground truth indicate miscalibrated beliefs, not conservative uncertainty."  
  - [corpus] Weak direct corpus evidence; neighboring papers focus on different LLM limitations (e.g., competitive programming, accessibility) rather than algorithmic performance calibration.
- **Break condition:** The model encounters a dataset‑algorithm combination with properties that overlap sufficiently with memorized benchmark statistics to trigger retrieval of a stored performance estimate, temporarily improving coverage (e.g., Claude on NOTEARS benchmarks).

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Above‑random performance on benchmark datasets reflects memorization of training corpus statistics rather than principled reasoning about algorithmic behavior.
- **Mechanism:** LLMs retrieve and reproduce performance figures frequently encountered in their training data (papers, documentation) for well‑known benchmark datasets, leading to narrow, convergent predictions on benchmarks but complete failure on novel synthetic data where no memorized statistics exist.
- **Core assumption:** LLM predictions scale with the frequency of specific algorithm‑dataset‑statistic tuples in the pretraining corpus.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] "patterns in the errors... were consistent with memorization of benchmark statistics rather than principled reasoning."  
  - [section 4.5] "The algorithm × metric dissociation is visible in Table 6... PC and FCI... achieve near‑zero SHD coverage... whereas NOTEARS achieves 41.3% SHD coverage... This pattern is not explained by algorithmic difficulty."  
  - [section 4.5] "Benchmark datasets yield a mean predicted range width of 4.34, compared to 9.83 for synthetic datasets, giving a 2.26× compression ratio... Cross‑model pairwise distance is 12.9 on benchmark datasets and 33.0 on synthetic."
- **Break condition:** When the query involves an algorithm‑dataset combination that is both under‑documented in the training corpus and structurally dissimilar from any stored examples, forcing the model to rely on non‑memorized reasoning (which fails).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** LLM performance degrades monotonically with structural complexity of synthetic datasets, consistent with diminishing retrieval cues.
- **Mechanism:** As synthetic network size increases, the probability of encountering any structurally analogous example in training decreases, leading to greater prediction divergence between models and wider, less informative ranges.
- **Core assumption:** Memorization‑based generalization has a sharp cutoff based on the similarity between the query instance and stored training examples.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [section 4.4] "Coverage on synthetic datasets degrades monotonically with network size: 20.3% at 12 nodes, 13.3% at 30 nodes, 7.0% at 50 nodes and 6.2% at 60 nodes... Larger synthetic networks have fewer analogues in training data."  
  - [section 4.4] "Cross‑model pairwise distance rises monotonically from 4.5 at 12 nodes to 68.25 at 60 nodes, a 15× increase."  
  - [abstract] "Performance degraded on synthetic datasets and with larger networks."
- **Break condition:** At the largest network sizes (e.g., 60 nodes), no training‑data analogues exist, and models produce maximally divergent, essentially random guesses, as evidenced by the 68.25 mean pairwise distance.

## Foundational Learning
- **Calibrated Coverage**  
  - *Why needed:* It is the primary evaluation metric quantifying whether an LLM's predicted interval contains the true algorithmic mean at the expected rate. Understanding this is essential to interpret the 15.9% failure rate.  
  - *Quick check:* If a model's predicted ranges are very wide but still contain the true mean only 15.9% of the time, what does this say about its calibration versus just being conservative?
- **Algorithmic Blindness**  
  - *Why needed:* It is the core failure mode defined in the paper—the gap between knowing an algorithm's assumptions and being able to predict its performance on a specific dataset.  
  - *Quick check:* How does "algorithmic blindness" differ from a model simply not knowing enough facts about an algorithm? What would evidence of the former look like?
- **Memorization Probes (Range Compression, Cross‑Model Agreement)**  
  - *Why needed:* These are the key diagnostic tools used to distinguish memorization from reasoning. They explain *why* coverage is low.  
  - *Quick check:* What two behavioral signals (from Tables 7 & 8) suggest LLMs are retrieving memorized statistics rather than reasoning structurally?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map:**  
1. **Dataset Layer:** 9 benchmark (bnlearn) + 4 synthetic DAGs (varying nodes 12‑70).  
2. **Algorithm Layer:** 4 causal discovery algorithms (PC, FCI, LiNGAM, NOTEARS) from causal‑learn.  
3. **Ground Truth Engine:** 100 bootstrap runs per dataset‑algorithm pair → empirical mean + 95% CI for 4 metrics (Precision, Recall, F1, SHD).  
4. **LLM Query Interface:** 8 frontier models × 52 conditions × 3 prompt formulations → 1,248 calls.  
5. **Aggregation & Evaluation:** Average predicted ranges across formulations → compute calibrated coverage (fraction of 1,664 comparisons where LLM range contains ground truth mean).  
6. **Memorization Analysis Module:** Track range width (benchmark vs. synthetic), cross‑model pairwise distance, algorithm‑specific synthetic degradation.

**Critical Path:** Dataset specification → Algorithm execution & ground truth storage → Prompt generation → LLM API calls → Response parsing → Range aggregation → Coverage calculation → Memorization probe analysis.

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- *Breadth vs. Depth:* Evaluated 8 models, 13 datasets, 4 algorithms, 4 metrics—broad coverage but limited to causal discovery.  
- *Synthetic control:* Synthetic datasets hold graph properties constant while removing memorization cues, but may not capture all complexities of real‑world benchmarks.  
- *Prompt aggregation:* Averaging across 3 formulations reduces prompt sensitivity artifacts but may mask formulation‑specific rescue effects.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- Overall coverage < 20% (random baseline 36.5%).  
- Predicted ranges 8–27× wider than bootstrap CIs.  
- Coverage collapse on synthetic data, worsening with network size.  
- Algorithm‑specific degradation patterns (e.g., LiNGAM synthetic collapse).  
- High cross‑model disagreement on synthetic data.

**First 3 Experiments:**  
1. **Replication of Main Result:** For a single small benchmark (e.g., Asia), run the full pipeline (PC algorithm, all metrics) and verify Claude's coverage ~87.5% on Recall but 0% on SHD, replicating the algorithm‑metric dissociation from Table 12.  
2. **Synthetic Scaling Test:** For PC algorithm, compare coverage and range width across Synthetic‑12, ‑30, ‑50, ‑60. Expect monotonic coverage decay and range width expansion, confirming Figure 4.  
3. **Memorization Probe – Range Compression:** Compute average predicted range width for a well‑known benchmark (e.g., Sachs) versus a same‑sized synthetic graph. Expect ~2–3× compression on the benchmark across all models, as in Table 7.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** Is algorithmic blindness a general LLM limitation across algorithmic domains?  
  *Basis:* [explicit] "Testing whether algorithmic blindness extends to SAT solving, graph algorithms or optimization methods" (Future Work). *Why unresolved:* Evaluation only covered causal discovery. *What evidence would resolve it:* Conduct similar calibration studies on SAT solvers, graph algorithms, and optimization methods; compare coverage to random baseline.
- **Open Question 2:** Can fine‑tuning on algorithm performance data improve LLM calibrated coverage?  
  *Basis:* [explicit] "Fine‑tuning on algorithm performance histories could improve calibrated coverage, distinguishing fundamental limitations from addressable data gaps." (Future Work). *Why unresolved:* Only zero‑shot performance was assessed. *What evidence would resolve it:* Fine‑tune an LLM on curated (dataset, algorithm, metric) → performance examples; evaluate coverage on held‑out; measure improvement over zero‑shot.
- **Open Question 3:** How would human experts perform on the calibrated coverage task?  
  *Basis:* [explicit] "How human causal discovery experts perform at the same calibrated coverage task remains an open question." (Future Work). *Why unresolved:* No human baseline was collected. *What evidence would resolve it:* Recruit causal discovery experts to predict ranges for the same dataset‑algorithm pairs; compute their calibrated coverage; compare to LLMs.
- **Open Question 4:** Can training data attribution directly confirm the memorization hypothesis?  
  *Basis:* [explicit] "direct analysis using training data attribution methods would provide stronger empirical evidence." (Future Work) and "Our memorization inference relies on indirect behavioral signals... we cannot rule out alternative explanations." (Limitations). *Why unresolved:* Memorization is inferred from behavioral patterns, not directly traced. *What evidence would resolve it:* Apply influence estimation to test if LLM predictions for benchmark datasets correlate with training documents containing those benchmarks; check if such correlation is weaker for synthetic datasets.

## Limitations
- Exact algorithm hyperparameters and synthetic data generation details (edge density, sample sizes) are unreported, blocking precise replication.
- No independent external validation; low citation impact suggests limited scrutiny.
- Reliance on proprietary LLM APIs prevents full auditability.

## Confidence
- Systematic miscalibration: **High**
- Memorization over reasoning: **Medium**
- Synthetic degradation with size: **High**

## Next Checks
1. **Hyperparameter sensitivity:** Re‑run ground truth with varied PC/FCI significance levels (e.g., 0.01 vs. 0.05) and NOTEARS tolerance (`h`) to assess coverage stability against unreported defaults.
2. **Prompt aggregation effect:** Compare coverage using each prompt template (Direct, Step‑by‑Step, Meta‑Knowledge) separately vs. averaged; test if any formulation rescues performance on synthetic data.
3. **Training data frequency probe:** Scrape public pretraining corpora (e.g., The Pile, arXiv) for occurrences of "&lt;Algorithm&gt; &lt;Dataset&gt; &lt;Metric&gt;" tuples; correlate frequency with model coverage on corresponding benchmark conditions.
