# Lost in Stories: Consistency Bugs in Long Story Generation by LLMs
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.05890
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05890
- **Reference Count:** 26
- **Primary Result:** ConStory-Bench and ConStory-Checker reveal that LLMs struggle with narrative consistency; GPT-5-Reasoning achieves best performance (CED 0.113, GRR 2.80) on 8K–10K word stories.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the critical gap in evaluating consistency of long-form narrative generation by LLMs. The authors introduce ConStory-Bench, a benchmark consisting of 2,000 prompts and a five-category, 19-subtype taxonomy of narrative inconsistencies. They also develop ConStory-Checker, an automated pipeline that detects contradictions by extracting category-guided spans, pairing them, and grounding each judgment in explicit textual evidence. Evaluation across proprietary, open-source, and specialized models uses two metrics: Consistency Error Density (CED) and Group Relative Rank (GRR). Results show factual and temporal errors dominate, with errors clustering around story midpoints and in high-entropy segments. GPT-5-Reasoning achieves the lowest CED (0.113) and highest GRR (2.80). Error counts scale linearly with output length, demonstrating persistent consistency challenges for LLMs in long narratives.

## Method Summary
The authors did not publish detailed reproduction notes. The core methodological contributions are the ConStory-Bench benchmark and the ConStory-Checker pipeline. ConStory-Bench comprises 2,000 prompts designed to elicit long stories (8,000–10,000 words) and a five-category, 19-subtype taxonomy of narrative inconsistencies. ConStory-Checker operates in four stages: (1) category‑guided extraction of potentially inconsistent spans; (2) pairwise classification of those spans into consistent or contradictory pairs; (3) generation of evidence chains that cite textual quotes and assign error subtypes; (4) aggregation into standardized JSON reports. Models are evaluated using Consistency Error Density (CED, errors per 10K words) and Group Relative Rank (GRR) to control for output length and prompt difficulty. The pipeline is validated via injected contradictions and human annotation.

## Key Results
- Factual and temporal errors are the most prevalent inconsistency types in long narratives.
- Inconsistencies concentrate around the narrative midpoint (40–60% of the story) and in high‑entropy segments; GPT‑5‑Reasoning achieves the best consistency (CED = 0.113, GRR = 2.80).
- Error counts scale linearly with output length, indicating that consistency degrades as stories get longer.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Contradiction Detection via Category‑Guided Pipeline**  
ConStory‑Checker identifies contradictions through a three‑stage process. First, it scans the story using prompts tailored to each of the five taxonomy categories to extract spans that may contain inconsistencies. Second, it compares all extracted spans pairwise, classifying each pair as *Consistent* or *Contradictory* to reduce false positives. Third, for each contradiction, it produces a reasoning chain, quotes the conflicting text with precise positions, and assigns an error subtype. The pipeline assumes that contradictions manifest as explicit textual incompatibilities that an LLM judge can detect when given structured category guidance. Evidence from the paper includes the detailed pipeline description (Sec. 2.3) and validation experiments showing high precision. The approach fails if extraction misses relevant spans (false negatives) or if pairing yields many false positives due to ambiguous language; it also cannot handle intentional inconsistencies without extra context.

**Mechanism 2: Normalized Metrics for Fair Cross‑Model Comparison**  
Two metrics enable unbiased evaluation. Consistency Error Density (CED) normalizes raw error counts by output length (errors per 10K words), correcting for models that generate shorter or longer texts. Group Relative Rank (GRR) further adjusts for prompt difficulty by ranking models within each prompt group using a length‑aware quality score \( Q = \frac{w}{1+e} \). The core assumption is that error counts scale linearly with length, and that prompt groups isolate difficulty common to all models. The paper demonstrates that CED alone can misrank models with identical error counts but different lengths, and that GRR corrects this by within‑group ranking (Sec. 3.2.1). The metrics would break down if error density scales non‑linearly (e.g., superlinearly) or if prompt groups are too coarse.

**Mechanism 3: Entropy as an Early‑Warning Signal**  
Token‑level entropy (Shannon entropy over top‑K token distributions) predicts consistency errors. The study finds that error‑bearing segments exhibit significantly higher average entropy than the story baseline—e.g., Qwen3‑4B shows a 19.24% increase. This suggests that when the model is uncertain, it is more likely to produce content that later contradicts earlier statements. The mechanism assumes that inconsistency arises from low‑confidence predictions. Evidence includes empirical measurements across multiple models and complementary uncertainty metrics (probability, perplexity) showing converging patterns (Table 9). The correlation may not hold for architectures where wrong predictions are made with high confidence, or if entropy does not reflect underlying uncertainty.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input (generated story + taxonomy) -> Stage 1 (category‑guided extraction) -> Stage 2 (pairwise contradiction classification) -> Stage 3 (evidence chain generation) -> Stage 4 (JSON aggregation) -> Scoring (CED, GRR)

**Critical path:** Story flows through extraction (per category), then all extracted spans are paired; successful pairing leads to evidence anchoring and JSON output, which feeds into the metrics. Extraction quality is crucial as errors propagate.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Judge model:* o4‑mini balances accuracy and efficiency; larger judges might improve recall but increase cost.  
- *Staged pipeline:* Separating extraction and pairing reduces false positives but introduces error propagation; an end‑to‑end approach might be more robust but less interpretable.  
- *Category guidance:* Improves precision but may miss cross‑category contradictions (e.g., a factual error affecting character consistency).

**Failure signatures:**  
- Low recall in Narrative & Style errors due to subjectivity.  
- False positives from overly literal pairing (e.g., synonymous phrases flagged).  
- Inability to detect intentional inconsistencies (e.g., plot twists that appear contradictory).  
- Potential context‑window limitations of o4‑mini when processing very long stories.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Validation on injected errors:** Replicate Appendix A.2 by generating 200 stories with 1,000 planted contradictions and measure precision/recall against expert annotations.  
2. **Cross‑model replication:** Run the pipeline on at least three models (e.g., GPT‑5‑Reasoning, GLM‑4.6, Qwen3‑4B) to reproduce CED/GRR rankings and verify error‑type distributions.  
3. **Entropy correlation test:** Compute per‑token entropy for a subset of outputs; confirm that error‑bearing segments have statistically higher entropy than random non‑error segments (t‑test, p < 0.01).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
Based on the paper's limitations and discussed mechanisms, the following open questions emerge:
- How can the ConStory-Checker pipeline be extended to detect cross‑category contradictions and intentional inconsistencies (e.g., unreliable narrators)?
- Under what conditions does token entropy fail to predict narrative inconsistencies, and what alternative uncertainty metrics could be used?
- Can the narrative consistency taxonomy be expanded to cover more nuanced logical violations beyond the 19 defined subtypes?

## Limitations
- Pipeline relies on o4‑mini, which may introduce biases and has context‑window constraints that could miss long‑range dependencies.
- The observed entropy‑error correlation is conditional; it may not hold for models that are over‑confident on wrong predictions or for all architectures.
- The taxonomy may not fully capture cross‑category contradictions and cannot detect intentional inconsistencies (e.g., unreliable narrators).

## Confidence
