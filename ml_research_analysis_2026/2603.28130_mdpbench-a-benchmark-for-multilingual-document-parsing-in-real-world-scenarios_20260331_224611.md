# MDPBench: A Benchmark for Multilingual Document Parsing in Real-World Scenarios

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.28130
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.28130
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: MDPBench reveals that closed-source models (e.g., Gemini-3-Pro) maintain robustness on photographed multilingual documents, while open-source models drop by 17.8% on photographed and 14.0% on non-Latin scripts compared to digital Latin-script documents.

## Executive Summary
MDPBench is a new benchmark for evaluating document parsing systems on real-world, multilingual photographed documents. It addresses the scarcity of benchmarks that capture the challenges of mobile or archival photo captures, including diverse scripts, layout variations, and image degradations. The dataset comprises 3,400 images across 17 languages, created by photographing printed or screen-displayed documents under controlled variations (deformation, lighting, orientation, background). Annotations are generated via a rigorous multi-stage pipeline involving expert model labeling, manual correction, and human verification, ensuring high-fidelity ground truth. Evaluation on state-of-the-art VLMs shows a substantial performance gap: proprietary models remain robust, whereas open-source models suffer an average 17.8% drop on photographed documents and 14.0% on non-Latin scripts compared to pristine digital Latin-script documents. The benchmark employs a public/private split and page-level aggregation to enable fair multilingual comparisons. MDPBench highlights the need for more robust and multilingual document understanding systems.

## Method Summary
The benchmark evaluates document parsing, i.e., converting document images into structured representations (e.g., Markdown with reading order). Inputs are document images; outputs must include hierarchical elements with bounding boxes, text content, and types (paragraph, table, formula, etc.). The dataset consists of 850 digital-born documents covering 17 languages and diverse document types (academic papers, comics, etc.). For each digital document, three photographic variants are produced by printing or screen-displaying and then capturing under varied conditions (indoor/outdoor, deformations, angles, lighting, backgrounds), yielding 2,550 photographed images. Annotations are created through a multi-stage pipeline: (1) Expert models (dots.ocr, PaddleOCR-VL, Qwen3VL) generate initial predictions; consensus voting selects the most consistent output, with fallback to Gemini-3-Pro for low agreement. (2) Human annotators correct layout, reading order, and content, aligning with detailed guidelines. (3) Independent verification ensures quality. The evaluation uses page-level aggregation of element-level metrics to avoid imbalance from sparse elements (e.g., formulas). Metrics include Normalized Edit Distance (NED) for text/order, Character Detection Matching (CDM) for formulas, and Tree Edit Distance Similarity (TEDS) for tables. The dataset is split into 2,720 public and 680 private images; the private set is only accessible via an evaluation server to prevent overfitting.

## Key Results
- MDPBench introduces a large-scale, multilingual photographed document parsing dataset with 3,400 images across 17 languages and diverse capture conditions.
- Closed-source models (e.g., Gemini-3-Pro) maintain robust performance, while open-source models experience a dramatic drop: average 17.8% lower accuracy on photographed documents vs. digital ones, and 14.0% lower on non-Latin scripts.
- The benchmark uses separate public and private splits and page-level evaluation to ensure fair comparison, exposing significant brittleness in current open-source VLMs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: High-fidelity annotations via consensus and human verification.** Initial predictions from multiple expert models are combined by consensus voting, reducing individual model biases. Human annotators then correct residual errors and verify through iterative cycles, yielding reliable ground truth. Evidence: The pipeline is detailed in Section 3.3; similar benchmarks rely on automated pipelines, making MDPBench’s explicit human step distinctive. Break condition: Annotator proficiency gaps for low-resource languages could degrade quality, and the labor-intensive process limits scalability.

**Mechanism 2: Realistic degradation via photographed variants.** Printing/screen-displaying documents and photographing them under controlled physical deformations, lighting changes, angles, and backgrounds simulates common real-world issues (e.g., mobile captures, archival photos). This exposes model brittleness that clean digital tests miss. Evidence: Section 3.2 describes the capture process; Section 4.1 quantifies performance drops. Break condition: Extremely degraded images may become unreadable even to humans, and simulation may not cover all real artifacts (e.g., stains).

**Mechanism 3: Fair multilingual comparison via page-level aggregation and splits.** Metrics are averaged at the page level rather than by element type, preventing languages with abundant certain elements (e.g., formulas in English papers) from skewing rankings. A private held-out set (680 images) ensures models cannot overfit to known test data; only server-based evaluation is allowed. Evidence: Section 3.4 explains these design choices; Section 2.2 motivates the need. Break condition: Page-level aggregation may mask weaknesses on rare but critical element types; the private split size may limit per-language statistical power.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Data Collection -> Photographic Generation -> Annotation Pipeline -> Evaluation Suite

**Critical path**:
1. Collect and curate 850 digital-born documents spanning 17 languages and various types.
2. Generate photographed variants (3 per document) with controlled capture variations.
3. Annotate all 3,400 images via the multi-stage pipeline.
4. Split into public (2,720) and private (680) sets.
5. Researchers evaluate models on the public split; private split scoring is done via server submission.
6. Analyze results by script, document type, and capture condition.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Human verification ensures annotation quality but limits dataset size and update frequency.
- Page-level aggregation reduces language bias but obscures fine-grained element-type failures.
- Public/private split prevents overfitting but reduces available training data and creates evaluation bottlenecks.

**Failure signatures**:
- >10% performance drop on photographed vs. digital documents indicates lack of robustness to real-world degradations.
- Consistent underperformance on non-Latin scripts (Arabic, Hindi, Thai, etc.) reveals script bias or insufficient multilingual training.
- Repetitive outputs, language drift, or incorrect reading order (e.g., left-to-right on Arabic) point to architectural issues.
- Low layout detection PageIoU on complex or rotated documents suggests poor layout generalization.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. *Baseline replication*: Run Gemini-3-Pro and dots.mocr on the public split to verify reported overall, Latin/non-Latin, and digital/photo numbers.
2. *Condition ablation*: Group photographed images by capture dimensions (indoor/outdoor, deformation type) and measure performance deltas to identify the most challenging real-world factors.
3. *Element isolation*: Use ground-truth layout to crop text blocks and evaluate pure text recognition across languages, separating OCR capability from layout detection errors.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1**: What specific architectural or training data limitations cause open-source models to degrade so dramatically (avg. 17.8%) on photographed documents compared to proprietary models? (Phenomenon observed; root cause not isolated via ablation or pretraining data analysis.)

**Open Question 2**: How do language-specific error patterns (RTL errors in Arabic, diacritic loss in Hindi, space hallucination in Thai) reflect fundamental shortcomings in current multilingual OCR/VLM tokenization or training objectives? (Observational; no investigation into whether errors stem from visual backbone, decoding, or lack of script-specific losses.)

**Open Question 3**: Does the page-level evaluation metric inadvertently mask critical failures in individual element types (e.g., tables, formulas) that are sparse but high-value in certain languages or document types? (Design choice may downrank poor performance on critical elements.)

**Open Question 4**: How reliable is the multi-stage annotation pipeline's consensus-based voting for generating ground truth, and could bias in the initial expert models propagate into the benchmark's evaluation of those same or similar models? (Expert models used for annotation may bias ground truth; no independent human-only subset to measure pipeline error.)

**Open Question 5**: To what extent can data augmentation or synthetic generation of photographed document corruptions bridge the performance gap between digital and photographic scenarios, and is there a point of diminishing returns? (No experiments on training with synthetic corruptions; gap's trainability unknown.)

## Limitations
- Annotation quality may suffer for low-resource languages due to potential annotator proficiency gaps.
- Evaluation details (preprocessing like resolution, normalization, prompting strategies) are not fully specified, hindering exact reproduction.
- Simulated photographs might not capture all real-world artifacts (e.g., tears, stains) and the private split size (680) limits per-language statistical power.

## Next Checks
1. *Annotation consistency audit*: Independently re-annotate a stratified 5% sample (covering all languages) and compute inter-annotator agreement (Cohen’s κ) to assess ground truth reliability, focusing on non-Latin scripts.
2. *Degradation factor ablation*: Apply synthetic corruptions (blur, motion, perspective, lighting) to clean images and measure performance deltas; correlate with MDPBench’s natural photographic variants to identify most impactful factors.
3. *Fine-tuning study*: Fully fine-tune an open-source VLM on the MDPBench public split and evaluate on the private held-out set to quantify how much of the gap can be closed with additional data, distinguishing data-driven vs. architectural limitations.
