# Joint Routing and Model Pruning for Decentralized Federated Learning in Bandwidth-Constrained Multi-Hop Wireless Networks

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.15188
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15188
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** Jointly optimizing routing and structured pruning reduces average transmission latency by 27.8% and improves testing accuracy by ~12% compared to an unpruned system, and yields ~8% accuracy gain over standard benchmark routing algorithms.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the communication bottleneck in decentralized federated learning (D-FL) over multi-hop wireless networks with limited bandwidth. The authors propose a joint framework that simultaneously determines optimal routing paths for model transmission and the appropriate model pruning rate for each client. The core insight is that the allowed model retention rate (one minus pruning rate) is fundamentally constrained by the latency of the transmission path a client uses. By formulating an optimization to maximize retention under a deadline, they show the problem reduces to selecting paths that minimize the cumulative maximum per-hop latency. This leads to a novel routing algorithm (P_CLT) that constructs trees favoring balanced link qualities. Simulations on CIFAR datasets with ResNet models demonstrate significant gains in both latency and final model accuracy compared to systems that handle routing or pruning in isolation.

## Method Summary
The method addresses a constrained optimization problem (P1) where the goal is to maximize the model retention rate \( r_m \) for each client \( m \), subject to a total latency deadline \( t_{\max} \). The latency for a client is modeled as the sum over its multi-hop path of the model size (after pruning) divided by the slowest link rate in each hop’s broadcast range. By algebraically manipulating the latency constraint, the authors prove that the maximum feasible \( r_m \) depends only on the path’s cumulative maximum link weight, not on the client’s local data. This transforms P1 into a pure routing problem (P2): find a spanning tree minimizing the sum of these path-dependent maxima. To solve P2, they design the P_CLT algorithm, which starts from a minimum spanning tree and iteratively adjusts links under "reciprocity" constraints to avoid creating slow-hop bottlenecks. The selected paths then directly determine each client’s pruning rate, ensuring models are transmitted on time with maximum possible fidelity.

## Key Results
- Compared to an unpruned multi-hop D-FL system, the proposed joint method reduces average transmission latency by 27.8% and improves final testing accuracy by approximately 12%.
- Relative to standard benchmark routing algorithms (Kruskal, Bellman-Ford, Flood Fill), the P_CLT routing alone (with fixed pruning) yields an accuracy gain of about 8%.
- The joint strategy consistently achieves higher model retention rates \( r_m \) across clients, directly linking improved routing to reduced aggregation bias and better convergence.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Pruning Reduces Convergence Bias
**Claim:** Aggressively pruning the model (lower retention rate \( r_m \)) reduces the upper bound on the sum of squared model biases across clients, tightening the convergence guarantee.
**Mechanism:** The paper derives a convergence bound (Lemma 1) for the decentralized SGD process. A key term is the sum of squared ℓ₂-norms of model biases \( \|\hat{\omega}_{\alpha-1,n} - \bar{\omega}_{\alpha-1}\|^2 \). Lemma 2 shows this bias is upper-bounded by terms inversely proportional to \( r_m \), the fraction of parameters retained after structured pruning. Fewer transmitted parameters reduce the discrepancy caused by incomplete model aggregation in each round. Thus, lower \( r_m \) decreases the bias term, provided the models are still delivered within the latency deadline.
**Core Assumptions:** Client loss functions \( F_n(\cdot) \) are L-smooth and µ-strongly convex; transmissions occur in a collision-free TDMA schedule; the pruning mask is consistently applied and known to all clients.
**Evidence Anchors:**
- Abstract states the optimization aims to maximize retention rate under constraints, linking it to model bias.
- Section IV.A (Lemmas 1 & 2) explicitly derives the bias bound in terms of retention rates.
- The formulation of Problem P1 directly maximizes \( r_m \) to minimize the bias bound.
**Break Condition:** If the latency deadline \( t_{\max} \) is so strict that even with maximum pruning (\( r_m \to 0 \)) transmission is impossible for some clients, those clients cannot contribute, and the bias term becomes irrelevant as no update occurs, causing divergence.

### Mechanism 2: Optimal Retention is Path-Dependent
**Claim:** For a given client, the maximum feasible model retention rate \( r_m^* \) is determined solely by the transmission latency of its assigned path in the tree, not by its local data distribution or model architecture.
**Mechanism:** The latency constraint \( \sum_{i \in I_m} \lfloor r_m K \rfloor / \tilde{v}_{m,i} \le t_{\max} \) couples \( r_m \) to the path’s sum of inverse slowest-link rates \( \sum_{i \in I_m} \max_j \chi(i,j) \). Solving for \( r_m \) yields \( r_m \le t_{\max} / (K \sum_{i \in I_m} \max_j \chi(i,j)) \) (Eq. 18). Therefore, maximizing \( r_m \) is equivalent to minimizing that path-dependent sum. This reduces the original joint routing-and-pruning problem (P1) to a pure routing problem (P2) over spanning trees.
**Core Assumptions:** Network graph \( G(V,E) \) and link rates \( v(i,j) \) are static within a training round; pruning is structured (channel-wise) so model size scales with \( r_m \) as \( r_m = \eta_m^2 \).
**Evidence Anchors:**
- Section IV.B details the transformation from P1 to P2, with Equations (18) and (19) showing the path-dependency.
- Text in Section IV.B: "By analyzing the dependency of model retention rate on the transmission path, we show that the original optimization problem can be equivalently transformed into a routing optimization problem."
**Break Condition:** If the total model size \( K \) is extremely large relative to \( t_{\max} \) and typical link rates, even the optimal path may force \( r_m^* \approx 0 \) for many clients, making the solution trivial (all prune maximally) and nullifying the routing choice’s impact.

### Mechanism 3: P_CLT Routing Minimizes Cumulative Maximum Per-Hop Latency
**Claim:** The P_CLT algorithm constructs a spanning tree that minimizes the objective of P2—the sum over clients of the cumulative maximum per-hop link weight along their paths—thereby enabling higher retention rates \( r_m \) and lower bias.
**Mechanism:** P_CLT begins with a minimum spanning tree (MST) to minimize total weight. It then iteratively modifies the tree under "link reciprocity" constraints: when adding a new link to a node’s one-hop neighborhood, its weight must be similar to existing links (condition (22)), preventing a slow link from becoming a new bottleneck for that hop’s latency (since hop latency is dictated by the slowest active link, Eq. 6). Node priority (based on current tree degree) guides the order of link additions. These rules steer the tree toward one where each hop’s slowest link is as fast as possible on average across all paths.
**Core Assumptions:** One-hop latency for a node equals its slowest active link’s latency (Eq. 6); the spanning tree correctly represents the broadcast dissemination from a source; a tunable threshold \( \theta \) exists for weight similarity.
**Evidence Anchors:**
- Section V.A defines link reciprocity and constraints (22,23).
- Section V.B defines node priority (25) and discusses threshold \( \theta \).
- Figure 5(a,b) in Section V.C show iterative reduction in the objective value (transmission time).
**Break Condition:** In highly mobile networks where link rates change rapidly within a round, the static tree computed at round start becomes stale, violating the assumption of fixed \( \tilde{v}_{m,i} \). The reciprocity constraints may then force unnecessarily long detours, increasing latency.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Strong Convexity & Smoothness**
  - **Why needed here:** These properties of the local loss functions \( F_n(\cdot) \) are essential for the convergence bound in Lemma 1. Strong convexity ensures a unique minimizer and bounds gradient expectations; smoothness bounds gradient differences. Without them, the derived bound on the bias term may not hold, and the effect of pruning on convergence could be destabilizing.
  - **Quick Check:** If a client’s local loss is non-convex (e.g., deep net with non-IID data), how would Lemma 1’s guarantee change? The paper notes an extension is possible but the aggregation error (bias) term remains dominant.
- **Concept: Channel Reciprocity in Multi-Hop Routing**
  - **Why needed here:** The latency bottleneck for a node in a hop is its slowest active link (Eq. 6). P_CLT’s reciprocity constraint (22) ensures that when a node adds a new link to its one-hop set, that link’s channel quality (weight) is similar to existing ones. This prevents a single slow new link from drastically increasing the hop’s latency contribution.
  - **Quick Check:** If a node adds one very slow link to reach a distant target, what happens to the hop’s latency contribution according to Eq. (6)?
- **Concept: Structured Pruning (Channel Pruning)**
  - **Why needed here:** Structured pruning removes entire input/output channels, preserving dense matrix structures. This avoids irregular sparsity that requires index transmission overhead and specialized hardware. The paper uses it so model size reduction is exactly \( r_m = \eta_m^2 \), making the retention rate directly translate to a predictable byte size for the latency model.
  - **Quick Check:** Why is unstructured pruning (weight-by-weight) less suitable for this wireless D-FL scenario? It creates irregular sparsity patterns, necessitating extra overhead to transmit indices and requiring specific hardware for efficient computation.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component Map:**  
  Client Node (local SGD + pruning) -> Routing Engine (P_CLT) -> Transmission Tree -> Aggregation Module (reconstruct + combine) -> Updated Global Model -> Client Node
- **Critical Path:** Local SGD update → Determine pruning mask based on path-dependent \( r_m \) (from Routing Engine) → Apply pruning → Transmit pruned model along computed multi-hop tree → Receive pruned models from other clients → Aggregate using pre-shared mask → Repeat.
- **Design Tradeoffs:**  
  - *Pruning vs. Accuracy:* Lower \( r_m \) ensures on-time delivery but increases bias. The algorithm pushes \( r_m \) to the maximum allowed by the path.  
  - *Routing Optimality vs. Complexity:* P_CLT (Ψ=3 iterations) offers a good tradeoff; exhaustive search over spanning trees is NP-hard. Kruskal is faster but suboptimal for the "max per-hop" objective.  
  - *Centralized vs. Distributed Routing:* Routing is computed per-source client \( m \) (could be distributed), but requires global knowledge of link rates \( v(i,j) \).
- **Failure Signatures:**  
  - **High latency variance across clients:** Indicates some paths contain very slow links (high max χ), forcing low \( r_m \). Visible in Fig. 7(e) spread.  
  - **Convergence stagnation despite low training loss:** Suggests pruning is too aggressive (\( r_m \) too low), losing critical features. Check distribution of \( r_m \) values.  
  - **Frequent path re-computation:** If channel conditions change faster than the per-round routing update, paths become stale, violating the static \( \tilde{v}_{m,i} \) assumption in Eq. (6).
- **First 3 Experiments:**  
  1. **Aggregation Strategy Validation (Fig. 6):** Compare multi-hop routing (proposed) vs. P2P gossip. Multi-hop significantly improves accuracy, justifying the routing focus.  
  2. **Pruning Strategy Validation (Figs. 7–9):** Compare Proposed Joint (optimal \( r_m \) + P_CLT) vs. Fixed Pruning vs. No Pruning. Joint method improves accuracy ~12% and reduces latency ~27.8%. Vary bandwidth \( B \) and \( t_{\max} \) to show \( r_m \) scales with resources.  
  3. **Routing Algorithm Validation (Figs. 11,12):** Compare P_CLT vs. Kruskal, Bellman-Ford, Flood Fill. P_CLT improves accuracy ~8% and yields higher average \( r_m \). Test enhanced routing (CAM/FPSR) under bottleneck nodes to show fairness improvement (Jain’s index).

## Limitations
- **Assumptions on loss geometry:** The convergence bound requires L-smoothness and µ-strong convexity, which may not hold for deep networks with non-IID data; the derived bias impact could be optimistic.
- **Static channel and perfect broadcast model:** The latency model (Eq. 6) assumes fixed TDMA and that each hop’s rate equals the slowest link in its broadcast set; real multi-hop networks have time-varying channels, interference, and collisions, potentially invalidating P_CLT’s optimality.
- **Heuristic nature of P_CLT:** No optimality proof exists; reciprocity constraints (22–23) may force suboptimal detours in dense or mobile settings, and performance depends on the threshold \( \theta \).

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (Pruning ↓ bias):** High – Rigorous derivation in Lemmas 1–2; empirical results support the trend.
- **Mechanism 2 (Path-determined retention rate):** High – Exact algebraic reduction from P1 to P2 under the latency model.
- **Mechanism 3 (P_CLT gains):** Medium – Simulations show consistent improvement, but lacks theoretical guarantee and may degrade under dynamic conditions.
