# Explainable Condition Monitoring via Probabilistic Anomaly Detection Applied to Helicopter Transmissions

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.08130
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08130
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: The method achieves 100% recall and precision for key health indices during the 60 days before a documented failure on helicopter transmission data, and competitive performance on a benchmark dataset.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents an explainable anomaly detection framework for condition monitoring that requires only healthy operational data. It models the probabilistic distribution of healthy observations using the Bayesian CoCoAFusE mixture-of-experts approach. Anomaly scores are derived from probability transforms of successive sensor readings, weighted to prioritize recent data, and evaluated against posterior expectations to account for model uncertainty. Applied to a benchmark dataset and real helicopter transmission vibration data, the method yields competitive detection performance, with the helicopter case achieving perfect recall and precision for key health indices in the 60 days preceding a failure. The framework also offers inherent interpretability through expert activation analysis and gate coefficient inspection.

## Method Summary
The proposed system follows a pipeline: first, health indices (HIs) are standardized and windowed. A Bayesian CoCoAFusE mixture-of-experts model is trained on healthy data; it combines M affine Gaussian experts with a softmax gating network and a behavior gate controlling mixture fusion. Posterior inference (via MCMC) yields samples from the parameter distribution. For each sliding window of observations, the method computes conditional predictive CDF values, aggregates them with exponential decay weights into a statistic Q, and transforms it into an anomaly score ASD that is uniform under nominal conditions. A threshold (e.g., 97.5%) and a patience filter generate alerts. Finally, interpretability is achieved by projecting the gating coefficients onto a low-dimensional subspace to visualize how covariates influence expert activation and predictions.

## Key Results
- On real helicopter transmission data: 100% recall and precision for key health indices during the 60-day validity window before a documented failure.
- On the Azure Fault Detection Dataset: competitive detection performance, though with shorter optimal validity windows (e.g., 4 days).
- The approach provides inherent interpretability, allowing experts’ roles and covariate effects to be visualized via gate coefficient projections.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Anomalies are detected by transforming conditional predictive densities into uniformly distributed scores, where extreme values indicate low plausibility under the learned healthy model.
- **Mechanism:** For a window of observations, the method computes probability transforms `u = F(y|x;θ)` using the model’s conditional CDF. These are combined into a single statistic `Q` via weighted summation (with exponential decay favoring recent data). The Anomaly Score `ASθ = 1 - 2*min(Q, 1-Q)` maps `Q` to a uniform (0,1) distribution under nominal conditions, so high scores (near 1) are improbable under the healthy model.
- **Core assumption:** The model’s conditional distribution `p(y|x;θ)` accurately describes healthy data, and consecutive observations are conditionally independent given covariates and parameters (Assumption 1).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "probabilistic measures of deviation from nominality"
  - [section 4] "We aim to distinguish those realizations that are very improbable according to our conditional model"
  - [section 4, Lemma 2 & Eq 9-11] Defines `Q` via weighted sum of uniforms and its CDF `FW`.
- **Break condition:** If the healthy model’s conditional CDF `F(·|x;θ)` is misspecified (e.g., due to poor fit or covariate shift), transformed scores `u` may not be uniform, causing `ASθ` to lose its uniform property under nominal conditions and increasing false alarms.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** CoCoAFusE mixture-of-experts flexibly models the conditional density `p(y|x)` while preserving interpretability through gated linear experts and a fusion parameter.
- **Mechanism:** The model combines `M` affine Gaussian experts. A softmax gate `G(x)` assigns mixture weights `α_i(x)` based on covariates. A behavior gate `B(x)` outputs `β ∈ (0,1)` controlling fusion: `β→1` yields competitive mixing; `β→0` yields collaborative blending of expert means/variances. The final density is `p(y|x) = Σ_i p_i^(β)(y|x) * p(z=i|x)`.
- **Core assumption:** The true conditional relationship can be expressed as a weighted fusion of a finite set of linear conditional Gaussians, with gate parameters sufficiently expressive to capture relevant nonlinearities.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3] "CoCoAFusE... balances smoothness and flexibility" and equations (2)-(7).
  - [section 5.2] "the mixing probabilities are the softmax transformation... Without loss of generality..."
  - [section 7.3] Example shows model effectively uses only 2 of 4 experts for ENHKUR.
- **Break condition:** If the underlying process requires non-Gaussian marginals, heavy tails, or non-linear expert means not well-approximated by affine Gaussians, the density model becomes inadequate, harming predictive performance and anomaly score validity.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Model interpretability is achieved by projecting gating coefficients onto a low-dimensional subspace to visualize how features influence expert activation and predictions.
- **Mechanism:** The mixing gate matrix `A_G` (or its transformed version `A_0`) is analyzed. For `M=2` or `M=3`, the rows `a_i*` (projected coefficients) define directions in feature space along which expert `i`’s activation increases without affecting others. A grid of scores in this subspace is mapped back to a fictitious covariate dataset, and outputs (predictive mean, std dev, expert probabilities) are visualized.
- **Core assumption:** The dominant effects of covariates on expert selection can be captured by a low-rank projection of the gate coefficient matrix, and the subspace directions meaningfully correspond to single-expert influence.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 5.2] "we can univocally define: `a_i* = Proj(a_i, Span(A_0,-i)^⊥)`" and Eq (19)-(20).
  - [section 7.3] "Figure 9... visually dissect the ENHKUR model into its linear submodels" showing Expert 2/3 activation vs. ENHM6.
  - [section 7.3] "Figure 10... arrows account for effects of individual input features on Expert allocations."
- **Break condition:** If the projected subspace does not capture most variance in gate activations (e.g., many experts with comparable importance, high-rank `A_0`), the 2D visualization becomes misleading or uninformative.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Bayesian Inference & Posterior Predictive Distribution**
  - *Why needed here:* The method computes anomaly scores as posterior expectations `ASD = E_post[ASθ|D]`, requiring understanding of parameter uncertainty and predictive distribution.
  - *Quick check question:* How does the posterior predictive distribution `p(y_new|x_new, D)` differ from the likelihood `p(y_new|x_new, θ)`?
- **Concept: Mixture of Experts (MoE)**
  - *Why needed here:* CoCoAFusE is an advanced MoE; understanding gating and expert combination is key to interpreting its predictions and limitations.
  - *Quick check question:* In a standard MoE, what is the role of the gating function versus the expert models?
- **Concept: Probability Integral Transform & Uniform Order Statistics**
  - *Why needed here:* Section 4’s core derivation (Lemma 2) relies on the fact that applying the CDF to a continuous random variable yields a uniform distribution, and the order statistic distribution of a weighted sum of uniforms.
  - *Quick check question:* If `U1, U2 ~ Uniform(0,1)` independent, what is the distribution of `Q = 0.7*U1 + 0.3*U2`?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Data Preprocessing → CoCoAFusE Model → Posterior Inference → Anomaly Scoring Engine → Detection Logic → Explainability Module
- **Critical path:** Healthy data collection → model selection (via PSIS-LOO & coverage trade-off) → full training on healthy data → compute ASD on test stream → apply threshold/patience → (optional) pooled decision across HIs.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Model complexity (M) vs. Interpretability: Higher M increases flexibility but complicates gate coefficient visualization; the method defaults to lower M when possible.
  - Coverage vs. Fit: The selection algorithm explicitly trades lower 95% CIC for higher PSIS-LOO; necessary for anomaly detection where tight fit is prioritized.
  - Window size k & decay λ: Larger k smooths noise but adds detection delay; λ controls responsiveness. Tuned on validation.
  - Pooling strategy: Combining per-HI scores (e.g., "at least two HIs exceed threshold") reduces false positives but may increase miss rate for faults affecting only one HI.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Training phase: PSIS-LOO warnings (high Pareto k), CIC far from nominal (e.g., <<0.95), indicating poor model fit.
  - Scoring phase: ASD_t uniformly distributed on (0,1) during nominal periods; systematic drift toward 0 or 1 suggests bias.
  - Detection phase: Alerts triggered during known healthy periods (false positives), or no alerts during known fault approach (false negatives). Check individual HI performance tables.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Sanity Check on Training Data: Run anomaly scoring on the healthy training set. ASD_t should be approximately uniform (0,1) and exceed τ only ~(1-τ) fraction. Visualize per-HI predictive intervals: most training points within 90%/95% intervals.
  2. Single-HI Validation: Pick a well-performing HI from the first helicopter (ENHKUR or S2R). Apply full pipeline (train on pre-Nov 2016, test post-Nov 2016) and reproduce Figure 5/6 results exactly. Verify that the 60-day validity window yields 100% recall/precision.
  3. Explainability Replication: For the trained ENHKUR model, replicate the 1D projection analysis (Figure 9). Confirm only Experts 2 and 3 are active and activation boundaries align with ENHM6 values.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can temporal dependencies in health indices be modeled to better distinguish progressive wear from sudden failures?
- **Basis in paper:** The conclusion states that "modeling the time dependence of the HIs’ conditional distribution could account for the progressive wear of components, further distinguishing it from sudden failures."
- **Why unresolved:** The current model assumes conditional independence between successive observations given covariates, ignoring potential autocorrelation or trending behavior in degradation processes. This may limit sensitivity to early signs of wear.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Integrate temporal dynamics (e.g., via autoregressive terms, state-space models, or Gaussian processes) into CoCoAFusE and evaluate anomaly detection performance on datasets with known gradual degradation, comparing detection lead times and false alarm rates against the baseline.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Would using non-Gaussian, heavy-tailed distributions for the expert outputs improve robustness and accuracy of anomaly detection in vibration data?
- **Basis in paper:** The conclusion mentions that "more flexible distributions for the experts, e.g., in some heavy-tailed family, could more effectively capture the nature of the responses."
- **Why unresolved:** The current implementation uses affine Gaussian experts, which assume symmetric, light-tailed residuals. Vibration signals often exhibit heavy tails or outliers due to transient shocks or non-stationarities, potentially leading to mis-specified uncertainty estimates.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replace Gaussian experts with Student's t-distributions or other flexible families within CoCoAFusE, and assess posterior predictive checks, credible interval coverage, and detection metrics on benchmark and helicopter data.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can the computational efficiency of Bayesian inference for CoCoAFusE be improved to enable scaling to larger datasets or real-time condition monitoring?
- **Basis in paper:** The conclusion identifies that "the MCMC framework adopted here to fit the model is computationally demanding and does not scale well with data."
- **Why unresolved:** MCMC sampling limits applicability to high-frequency streaming data or large historical archives, hindering deployment in practical systems.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement and compare alternative inference schemes (e.g., variational inference, stochastic gradient MCMC, or expectation propagation) in terms of wall-clock time, memory usage, and preservation of detection performance on standardized datasets.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How sensitive is anomaly detection performance to violations of the conditional independence assumption across successive observations?
- **Basis in paper:** Assumption 1 in Section 2 posits conditional independence of responses given covariates and parameters. This is not empirically validated and may be violated if past states influence current conditions beyond included covariates.
- **Why unresolved:** Temporal autocorrelation could distort the uniform distribution of probability transforms, leading to inflated anomaly scores or missed detections.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct simulations with synthetic time series having controlled autocorrelation structures; fit the model as is and compute empirical false alarm rates. Alternatively, extend the model to include lagged responses as covariates and compare detection results.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** What is a principled approach for selecting anomaly score thresholds and patience filtering parameters to optimize trade-offs between detection lead time and false alarms?
- **Basis in paper:** The method uses fixed thresholds (e.g., 97.5% quantile) and patience values (e.g., 10 or 3 consecutive samples) without systematic justification. These are chosen heuristically and may not generalize.
- **Why unresolved:** Suboptimal thresholds can cause either excessive false alarms (reducing trust) or delayed detection (reducing safety), especially across different machinery types or operating regimes.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Perform sensitivity analysis on validation sets to characterize precision-recall trade-offs as functions of threshold and patience. Develop data-driven selection via ROC curves or cost-sensitive optimization, and evaluate robustness on unseen test periods.

## Limitations
- Proprietary helicopter data prevents independent verification; the 100% detection claim lacks reproducibility.
- Hyperparameter choices (e.g., number of experts M, weight decay λ, window size k) and MCMC setup are not thoroughly detailed, potentially limiting generalizability.
- Core assumptions (conditional independence, Gaussian experts) are not extensively validated; violations could compromise the uniformity of anomaly scores.

## Confidence
- **Detection performance:** Medium (only one failure case; benchmark results less compelling).
- **Uniform anomaly scores:** Medium (depends on correct model; coverage diagnostics are sparse).
- **Interpretability:** Medium (effective for few experts; scalability uncertain).
- **General applicability:** Low (method appears tailored to specific helicopter transmission dynamics).
