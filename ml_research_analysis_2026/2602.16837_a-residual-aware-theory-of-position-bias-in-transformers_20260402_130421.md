# A Residual-Aware Theory of Position Bias in Transformers

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.16837
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16837
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Residual connections prevent inevitable attention collapse in deep causal Transformers and induce a U‑shaped position bias, explaining the Lost‑in‑the‑Middle phenomenon.

## Executive Summary
This work resolves a long‑standing contradiction: theory predicted that deep causal Transformers must collapse all attention onto the first token, yet practical models do not. The authors introduce a residual‑aware theory that explicitly models how residual connections mix identity and attention updates. They prove that if the total mixing strength across layers is finite, collapse is avoided. At finite depth, causal masking, residual paths, and recency‑favored positional encodings (e.g., ALiBi) interact to produce a U‑shaped bias where early and late tokens are influential while middle tokens are suppressed. Empirical measurements on five large language models show near‑perfect alignment between the predicted rollout distributions and actual token influence, with Spearman correlations up to 0.98 and Wasserstein distances as low as 0.01. This provides a principled architectural explanation for the Lost‑in‑the‑Middle effect.

## Method Summary
The core method computes a residual‑aware attention rollout. For each layer \(t\), a mixing coefficient \(\lambda_t\) is estimated from activation norms to quantify how much of the attention update survives the residual connection. The layer‑wise transition matrix is \(R^{(t)} = (1-\lambda_t)I + \lambda_t A^{(t)}\), where \(A^{(t)}\) is the dataset‑averaged attention kernel. The cumulative rollout is \(P^{(T)} = \prod_{t=1}^T R^{(t)}\), and the predicted influence on the final output token is the last row \(p^{(T)} = P^{(T)}_{n,\cdot}\). Empirically, token influence \(\hat{p}^{(T)}\) is measured via the L2 norm of input embedding gradients w.r.t. the next‑token probability. The theory approximates content logits as a constant plus a diagonal self‑attention term, enabling analytical rollout computation. Validation compares \(p^{(T)}\) and \(\hat{p}^{(T)}\) using Spearman rank correlation and normalized 1‑Wasserstein distance.

## Key Results
- Residual connections prevent infinite‑depth collapse when the total mixing strength \(\sum_t \lambda_t\) is finite.
- Finite‑depth causal Transformers exhibit a U‑shaped position bias due to the interaction of causal masking (primacy drift), residual paths (recency drift), and positional encodings.
- Theoretical rollout distributions match empirical token influence with very high rank correlation (up to 0.98) and low Wasserstein distance (as low as 0.01).
- The U‑shaped architectural prior explains the Lost‑in‑the‑Middle phenomenon, showing it is a structural rather than purely semantic effect.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Residual connections prevent the attention collapse that would otherwise occur in deep causal Transformers.
- **Mechanism:** Residual mixing is modeled by a coefficient \(\lambda_t\) that interpolates between the identity matrix and the attention matrix \(A^{(t)}\) via \(R^{(t)} = (1-\lambda_t)I + \lambda_t A^{(t)}\). The cumulative rollout \(P^{(T)} = R^{(T)} \cdots R^{(1)}\) becomes a product of such matrices. If the total mixing strength \(\sum_t \lambda_t\) is finite, the diagonal entries of \(P^{(T)}\) stay bounded away from zero, so attention mass does not collapse onto the first token.
- **Core assumption:** The dataset‑averaged attention kernels \(A^{(t)}\) are stochastically monotone (prefix‑mass monotonicity), and \(\lambda_t\) accurately reflects the relative magnitude of the attention update.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "incorporating residual connections, we show that this architectural component prevents collapse under realistic conditions." [section] Theorem 5.4 proves that finite total mixing yields a positive lower bound on \(P^{(T)}_{ii}\), preventing collapse. [corpus] Neighboring work (e.g., "Lambda‑Skip Connections") similarly identifies skip connections as key to avoiding rank collapse.
- **Break condition:** Collapse re‑emerges if \(\sum_t \lambda_t\) diverges or if stochastic monotonicity of \(A^{(t)}\) is grossly violated.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Causal masking, residual connections, and recency‑favoring positional encodings together generate a U‑shaped position bias (early and late tokens are influential, middle tokens are not).
- **Mechanism:** Three additive forces: (1) Causal masking causes a primacy drift, moving mass toward earlier positions over layers. (2) Residual connections induce a recency drift by preserving identity paths, keeping information at its current position. (3) Positional encodings like ALiBi bias attention toward recent keys, reinforcing recency. Their interaction under finite depth yields a U‑shaped influence profile.
- **Core assumption:** The four‑force decomposition holds under weak stochastic monotonicity of the effective attention kernels and an additive separation of content and positional logits.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "At finite depth, we prove that causal Transformers induce a U‑shaped position bias, with attention concentrating on early and late tokens." [section] Propositions 4.2 (primacy), 4.3 (residual recency), 4.4 (positional recency), and 4.5 (content modulation) formalize the forces; Figure 2 and Tables 2–3 empirically validate the U‑shape. [corpus] Related findings on primacy/recency effects support the multi‑force explanation.
- **Break condition:** Removing any force (e.g., setting \(\lambda_t = 1\) to eliminate residual recency, or using non‑recency‑favoring positional encodings) flattens the U‑shape; strong non‑uniform content may also mask it.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The architectural U‑shaped bias explains the Lost‑in‑the‑Middle phenomenon, where models underutilize information placed in the middle of long contexts.
- **Mechanism:** The rollout distribution \(P^{(T)}_{n,\cdot}\) (last‑row) measures cumulative influence. Under the combined forces, the smallest influence typically occurs at intermediate positions, creating a valley. This structural prior persists even with semantic content and matches the observed LiM performance drop.
- **Core assumption:** The last‑row rollout is a good proxy for token influence on next‑token prediction; the constant‑plus‑diagonal content abstraction captures typical content contributions.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "This provides a principled architectural explanation for the Lost‑in‑the‑Middle phenomenon." [section] Section 6 shows that residual‑aware rollout without content already produces a U‑shape; adding constant‑plus‑diagonal content refines it, and measured gradient‑based influence aligns closely (Figure 2, Tables 2–3). [corpus] Independent work also links architecture to LiM.
- **Break condition:** Architectural changes that flatten the U‑shape (e.g., novel positional schemes or extreme residual schedules) could mitigate LiM; conversely, if content contributions are heavily tuned to middle positions, the valley may disappear.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Attention Rollout  
  **Why needed:** Quantifies how tokens propagate information across depth via the product of layer‑wise matrices; basis for all analytical and empirical results.  
  **Quick check:** "What does the entry \(P^{(T)}_{ij}\) represent in the rollout matrix?"
- **Concept:** Residual Mixing Coefficient (\(\lambda_t\))  
  **Why needed:** Determines how much of the attention update survives the residual connection; directly controls collapse vs. diverse influence.  
  **Quick check:** "How would setting \(\lambda_t = 1\) for all layers change the rollout dynamics?"
- **Concept:** First‑Order Stochastic Dominance (FOSD)  
  **Why needed:** Allows comparison of token‑position distributions and proves directional drifts (primacy/recency) without fine‑grained numeric values.  
  **Quick check:** "If distribution \(\mu\) first‑order stochastically dominates \(\nu\), what can you say about their cumulative mass functions?"

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Input embeddings + positional encodings -> Per‑layer attention matrix \(A^{(t)}\) and residual mixing \(\lambda_t\) -> Transition matrices \(R^{(t)} = (1-\lambda_t)I + \lambda_t A^{(t)}\) -> Cumulative product \(P^{(T)} = R^{(T)}\cdots R^{(1)}\) -> Last‑row distribution \(p^{(T)}\) (predicted influence).  
  Parallel empirical path: Same inputs -> Gradient computation -> Token influence \(\hat{p}^{(T)}\) -> Comparison metrics.
- **Critical path:**  
  1. Sample sequences from a held‑out dataset; for each layer \(t\), capture attention output \(\text{Attn}^{(t)}\) and input \(X^{(t)}\) to compute \(\lambda_t = \|\text{Attn}^{(t)}\|_F / (\|X^{(t)}\|_F + \|\text{Attn}^{(t)}\|_F)\), then average.  
  2. Extract content logits by subtracting known positional biases (for additive models like ALiBi) from pre‑softmax scores; average across samples/heads to obtain constant‑plus‑diagonal parameters \(u^{(t)}, \delta^{(t)}\).  
  3. Build \(A^{(t)}\): for baseline, use softmax of positional bias only; for constant content, add \(u^{(t)} + \delta^{(t)}\mathbf{1}_{i=j}\) to logits before softmax.  
  4. Form \(R^{(t)}\) and compute rollout \(P^{(T)}\).  
  5. Measure empirical influence \(\hat{p}^{(T)}\) via gradient L2 norms of next‑token probability w.r.t. input embeddings.  
  6. Compute Spearman correlation and normalized 1‑Wasserstein distance between \(p^{(T)}\) and \(\hat{p}^{(T)}\).
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Constant‑plus‑diagonal content simplifies analysis but discards token‑specific semantic effects.  
  - Averaging across heads and inputs yields smoother \(A^{(t)}\) but may hide head‑specific phenomena.  
  - Dataset‑averaged kernels ignore input‑dependent fluctuations for tractability.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - \(\sum_t \lambda_t\) very large or \(\lambda_t > 1\) indicates inaccurate measurement or potential collapse.  
  - Non‑monotone row cumulative sums in \(A^{(t)}\) violate stochastic monotonicity, breaking drift guarantees.  
  - High Wasserstein distance suggests the constant‑plus‑diagonal content abstraction is insufficient.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Reproduce Figure 2: Compute rollout variants (attention‑only, residual‑aware, residual+content) for mpt‑7b and compare to gradient influence.  
  2. Infinite‑depth test: Track cumulative \(\sum_t \lambda_t\) vs. depth; check if models with larger total mixing show increased primacy in intermediate layers.  
  3. Content modulation ablation: Replace constant‑plus‑diagonal content with purely uniform off‑diagonal content; measure change in Spearman correlation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How do specific architectural parameters (depth, width, \(\lambda_t\) schedule) influence the boundary between collapse and non‑collapse in finite‑depth Transformers? The paper proves collapse is avoided if \(\sum_t \lambda_t\) is finite, but does not characterize critical thresholds for common architectures.
- **Open Question 2:** How do training dynamics (optimization, objectives, data distribution) interact with or modify the U‑shaped positional prior induced by architecture alone? The theory abstracts away training, leaving unclear whether and how the prior can be strengthened, weakened, or overridden.
- **Open Question 3:** Can the residual‑aware theory be extended to yield sharp finite‑depth characterizations for multiplicative positional encodings like RoPE? The current additive logit model does not naturally fit RoPE’s multiplicative transformations, preventing application of the finite‑depth analysis.

## Limitations
- The additive content/position decomposition may not hold for rotary embeddings (e.g., MPT), weakening the constant‑plus‑diagonal approximation.
- Details of gradient‑based influence calculation (sample size, clipping, smoothing) are omitted, risking noisy or biased estimates.
- Head‑wise and input averaging might obscure per‑head heterogeneity; the monotonicity assumption could be violated in specific heads.

## Confidence
- **C1:** Residual connections prevent collapse – **High** (theoretical guarantee backed by consistent \(\lambda_t\) trends across five models).
- **C2:** Finite‑depth U‑shaped bias – **Medium** (depends on stochastic monotonicity; break conditions identified but not exhaustively validated).
- **C3:** Architectural explanation of Lost‑in‑the‑Middle – **Medium‑High** (strong empirical alignment, but relies on simplified content abstraction).

## Next Checks
1. **RoPE disentanglement:** For MPT models, explicitly separate content and positional contributions (e.g., train a linear probe on key vectors), rebuild rollout, and measure correlation with original gradient influence.
2. **Gradient robustness:** Replicate empirical influence using Integrated Gradients with multiple baselines; compute Spearman correlation with the published ∇ method to confirm robustness.
3. **Head‑wise rollout analysis:** Compute residual‑aware rollouts per head and check if the U‑shape persists across heads; report variance to assess aggregate claim validity.
