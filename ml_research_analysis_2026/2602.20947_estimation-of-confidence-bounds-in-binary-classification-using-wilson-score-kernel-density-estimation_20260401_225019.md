# Estimation of Confidence Bounds in Binary Classification using Wilson Score Kernel Density Estimation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.20947
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20947
- **Reference Count:** 23
- **Primary Result:** Proposes a kernel density method for per-instance confidence bounds that is over two orders of magnitude faster than Gaussian Process Classification while achieving comparable selective classification performance.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces Wilson Score Kernel Density Classification (WS-KDC), a method for producing statistically grounded upper and lower confidence bounds on class probabilities for binary classifiers. It combines kernel density estimation with the Wilson Score interval for binomial data, framing classification as estimation of the conditional probability function `p(y=1|x)`. Evaluated via selective classification on multiple datasets, WS-KDC matches the uncertainty quantification quality of Gaussian Process Classification (GPC) but with dramatically reduced optimization time, offering a scalable solution for applications requiring trustworthy confidence estimates.

## Method Summary
WS-KDC estimates per-instance confidence bounds by treating a query point's neighborhood in feature space as a pseudo-Binomial experiment. For a given query, it computes Gaussian kernel weights over all labeled training samples, aggregates weighted positive counts (`S`) and total effective trials (`N_eff`), and applies the Wilson Score interval to `(S, N_eff)` to obtain asymmetric bounds on `p(x)`. The point prediction uses the midpoint of these bounds. A critical hyperparameter, the kernel bandwidth `h`, is tuned via cross-validated negative log-likelihood on held-out data, balancing bias and variance. The method is feature-extractor agnostic and operates on any fixed-dimensional representation.

## Key Results
- WS-KDC achieves area under precision/recall reject curves (AUPRC/AURRC) comparable to Gaussian Process Classification (GPC) across four datasets.
- Optimization time is reduced by over two orders of magnitude relative to GPC, with inference also being significantly faster.
- Performance is competitive even after dimensionality reduction (e.g., to 3D), though small datasets (e.g., Assembly) can suffer from over-smoothing.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** WS-KDE produces statistically sound confidence bounds for a probability function `p(x)` by combining kernel smoothing with the Wilson Score interval for binomial data.
- **Mechanism:** For a query point `x*`, WS-KDE aggregates nearby labeled training samples (`x_i, y_i`) using a Gaussian kernel with bandwidth `h`. Each sample contributes a weighted "vote" for class 1 (`y_i=1`) or class 0 (`y_i=0`). This forms a pseudo-Binomial experiment where the effective number of weighted trials is `N_eff = Σ w_i` and the weighted sum of positives is `S = Σ w_i * y_i`. The Wilson Score interval is then applied to `(S, N_eff)` to compute asymmetric upper and lower bounds on the local `p(x*)`.
- **Core assumption:** The true `p(x)` is approximately constant over the kernel support defined by the bandwidth `h`. This i.i.d.-like smoothness allows the weighted aggregation to be treated as a valid Binomial experiment.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "combines kernel smoothing with the Wilson Score method for Binomial experiments."
  - [section III.B]: "The only assumptions of the WS-KDE are that the aggregated samples are independent and identically distributed, which means that the function `p(x)` must be approximately constant over the lengthscale defined by the bandwidth matrix."
  - [corpus]: Neighbor papers discuss Wilson Score for binomial confidence and kernel methods, but do not directly validate WS-KDE's specific fusion. *Evidence is indirect.*
- **Break condition:** If the feature space has sharp decision boundaries or high-frequency variation within the kernel support, the constant `p(x)` assumption fails, leading to over-smoothed bounds that are invalid (overly optimistic or pessimistic).

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Framing binary classification as estimating the conditional probability function `p(y=1|x)` enables the direct application of WS-KDE to produce per-instance confidence bounds, regardless of the upstream feature extractor's performance.
- **Mechanism:** The classifier's role is reduced to mapping a feature vector `x` to bounds on `S(x)=p(y=1|x)`. Any imperfect feature extractor that maps raw inputs to `x` is absorbed into the conditional distribution `p(x,y)`. The resulting `p(x)` reflects the *joint* difficulty of the feature representation and the classification task. WS-KDE estimates bounds on this `p(x)` directly from labeled `(x_i, y_i)` pairs without modeling the feature extractor.
- **Core assumption:** The training samples `(x_i, y_i)` are i.i.d. draws from the joint distribution `p(x,y)` that will be encountered at inference. The feature extractor's output distribution is stationary.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section III.C]: "The accuracy of the confidence bounds depends on the choice of bandwidth matrix and the correctness of the i.i.d. assumption. Most importantly, the accuracy of the confidence bounds is not reliant on the performance of the feature extractor, as poor performance will impact the `p(x)`, not the accuracy of the confidence bounds."
  - [section I]: "Our method is based on the formulation of binary classification as function estimation, where the estimated function is the probability of a positive outcome conditioned on the feature."
  - [corpus]: *No direct corpus evidence.* This is a conceptual framing proposed by the authors.
- **Break condition:** If the test-time feature distribution `p_test(x)` diverges significantly from the training distribution `p_train(x)` (covariate shift), the i.i.d. assumption breaks. The estimated bounds on `p_train(x)` do not transfer to `p_test(x)`, making them misleading.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Optimization of the kernel bandwidth `h` via held-out negative log-likelihood balances the bias-variance trade-off for the confidence bounds, trading narrower bounds (low bias, high variance) for wider, more reliable bounds (high bias, low variance).
- **Mechanism:** Bandwidth `h` is tuned using `k`-fold cross-validation on the training set. For each candidate `h`, WS-KDE is fit on `k-1` folds and evaluated on the held-out fold. The evaluation computes a point estimate of `p(x)` (using the midpoint of the WS-KDE bounds) and calculates the Bernoulli log-likelihood of the held-out labels. The `h` minimizing average held-out NLL is selected. This empirically finds an `h` where the smoothed `p(x)` is both accurate and smooth enough for Wilson Score intervals to be valid.
- **Core assumption:** The held-out NLL is a valid proxy for the accuracy and smoothness of the underlying `p(x)` function estimate. The optimal `h` for point prediction (midpoint) also yields valid confidence bounds.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section IV.B]: "For the WS-KDC, the lengthscale is optimized with 10-fold cross-validation of the training set, using the negative log likelihood of the held out samples as loss."
  - [section III.B]: "This creates a trade-off, where a narrow kernel will lead to less smoothing and consequently larger confidence bounds, while a large kernel will aggregate more samples, leading to faster convergence of the confidence bounds, at the risk of over-smoothing."
  - [corpus]: Neighbors discuss bandwidth selection for kernel methods but not this specific cross-validation objective. *Method is novel; corpus provides general context only.*
- **Break condition:** On very small datasets, cross-validation may select an excessively large `h` due to lack of data, causing over-smoothing and invalid bounds (as hinted in the Assembly dataset results). Conversely, on very large datasets, the NLL objective might under-smooth if local `p(x)` variation is real but noisy.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Kernel Density Estimation (KDE)**
  - **Why needed here:** WS-KDE is a variant of KDE. Understanding standard KDE (using kernels to estimate a probability density) is prerequisite to grasping how WS-KDE replaces the density estimate with Wilson Score intervals for binomial outcomes.
  - **Quick check question:** How does increasing the kernel bandwidth in standard KDE affect the bias and variance of the estimated density function?

- **Concept: Wilson Score Interval for Binomial Proportion**
  - **Why needed here:** This is the statistical engine that provides the confidence bounds. It must be understood as an alternative to the normal approximation (e.g., `p ± z*sqrt(p(1-p)/n)`), especially for low sample counts.
  - **Quick check question:** Why is the Wilson Score interval generally preferred over the normal approximation interval for small-sample binomial confidence bounds?

- **Concept: Selective Classification (Abstention)**
  - **Why needed here:** The paper evaluates confidence bounds in the selective classification framework (Eq. 2). This is the application context where bounds are used to decide when to reject a prediction to meet a target success rate `τ`.
  - **Quick check question:** Given asymmetric confidence bounds `[L(x), U(x)]` for `p(y=1|x)`, and a threshold `τ=0.95`, precisely state the rule for predicting class 1, class 0, or abstaining.

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:**
  Feature Extractor -> Dimensionality Reduction (Optional) -> WS-KDE Classifier

- **Critical path:**
  1. **Preprocessing:** Extract features for all training data. Apply fitted dimensionality reduction (e.g., UMAP) to get final `x_i`. *Do not apply dimensionality reduction to test data using test-set information.*
  2. **Hyperparameter Optimization:** Perform cross-validated search over kernel lengthscale `h` (log-scale). For each `h`, for each fold, re-fit WS-KDE on training fold, evaluate held-out NLL using midpoint `p_hat = (L+U)/2` as the predicted probability.
  3. **Final Model:** Select optimal `h*`. Store entire training set and `h*`.
  4. **Inference:** For new sample, reduce dimensionality, compute kernel weights with `h*`, compute bounds, output class and confidence (`L` if predicting 1, else `U`).

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Bandwidth `h`:** Primary hyperparameter. Small `h` = low bias, high variance, wider bounds. Large `h` = high bias, low variance, narrower bounds but risk of invalid bounds if `p(x)` is not smooth over kernel support. **Tradeoff:** Reliability (valid bounds) vs. Utility (narrow bounds).
  - **Dimensionality Reduction:** Using `D=3` (as in paper) reduces `O(N)` kernel computation cost and helps kernel distances be more meaningful in high-D spaces. **Tradeoff:** Information loss from DR vs. statistical validity of i.i.d. smoothness assumption in reduced space.
  - **Kernel Choice:** Paper uses isotropic Gaussian. A non-isotropic or adaptive kernel could handle anisotropy but adds hyperparameters. Simplicity vs. expressivity.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Over-optimistic bounds (too narrow):** Check if `U-L` is extremely small across test set. Likely cause: `h` too large (over-smoothing). Symptom in selective classification: precision/recall at high coverage (low reject) is low, and the `τ=95%` vertical line in PRC/RRC plots is far left (low coverage) or absent.
  - **Excessively conservative bounds (too wide):** `U-L` close to 1 for many points. Likely cause: `h` too small (under-smoothing) or insufficient training data in local neighborhood. Symptom: very low coverage at any meaningful performance level.
  - **Non-monotonic precision/recall vs. coverage:** If performance decreases as you reject more samples (coverage decreases), the confidence ranking is anti-correlated with true difficulty. Cause: Feature extractor or `p(x)` has structure not captured by kernel smoothness assumption.
  - **High variance in AUPRC/AURRC across random seeds:** Indicates instability in bandwidth selection due to small dataset size or high noise.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Synthetic 1D Verification:** Create a synthetic 1D classification problem with known `p(x)` (e.g., `p(x)=sigmoid(5*(x-0.5))`). Generate a small dataset (`N=200`). Run WS-KDC with manually set `h` values. Plot true `p(x)`, WS-KDE bounds, and training points. Verify that for a good `h`, the true `p(x)` lies within the bounds at most `(1-alpha)` of the time on a dense test grid. **Goal:** Build intuition for the smoothness assumption and bandwidth effect.
  2. **Benchmark Replication - Banknote Dataset:** Replicate the "Bank" experiment from the paper (Table I, no DR). Use the provided 4-dimensional features. Perform the 10-fold CV bandwidth search. Compare AUPRC/AURRC and optimization/inference times to the reported values. **Goal:** Validate implementation against a simple, fast baseline.
  3. **Scalability Stress Test - Subsampled ChestMNIST:** Using the ChestMNIST (1k) ResNet18+UMAP setup, run WS-KDC and GPC on increasing training set sizes (e.g., 100, 500, 1000 samples). Measure optimization time and plot as in Fig. 6. **Goal:** Empirically confirm the claimed `O(N)` vs. `O(N^3)` scaling behavior and identify the sample size where GPC becomes prohibitively slow.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How can the optimal kernel bandwidth for WS-KDE be automatically determined in a data-driven and theoretically grounded manner, especially for high-dimensional feature spaces?
- **Open Question 2:** Under what conditions does the core i.i.d. assumption of WS-KDE hold in practice when features are extracted from deep neural networks or foundation models, and how do violations (e.g., due to feature space skew or dataset shift) affect the statistical validity of the confidence bounds?
- **Open Question 3:** Can WS-KDC maintain statistically valid confidence bounds and competitive selective classification performance when scaled to very large training datasets (10⁵+ samples) and high-dimensional feature spaces (e.g., 1024+ dimensions from large foundation models) without prohibitive computational cost?
- **Open Question 4:** Do the confidence bounds produced by WS-KDC satisfy desired frequentist coverage guarantees (e.g., 95% of test points have true probability within [lower, upper]) under realistic, non-stationary deployment scenarios, or are the current evaluation metrics (AUPRC/AURRC) only a proxy for selective performance?
- **Open Question 5:** Is the observed similarity in performance between WS-KDC and GPC across datasets a result of both methods converging to similar underlying uncertainty estimates, or do they make different, complementary errors that are masked by the aggregate AUPRC/AURRC metrics?

## Limitations
- Fractional counts: Wilson Score interval needs integer successes/trials. Paper omits how to treat non‑integer `S` and `N_eff` from kernel weighting → coverage guarantee questionable.
- Bandwidth selection via held‑out NLL optimizes point likelihood, not interval validity → possible mis‑calibration, especially under covariate shift.
- i.i.d. smoothness: `p(x)` must be constant over kernel support; fragile in high‑dimensional or near decision boundaries.

## Confidence
- *Statistical soundness*: **Low** (depends on handling fractional counts and smoothness).
- *Feature‑extractor independence*: **Medium** (requires stationary `p(x,y)`; often violated).
- *Bandwidth trade‑off*: **Medium** (NLL is indirect; no coverage guarantee).
