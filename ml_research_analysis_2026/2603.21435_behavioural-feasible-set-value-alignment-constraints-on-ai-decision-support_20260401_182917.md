# Behavioural feasible set: Value alignment constraints on AI decision support

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.21435
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21435
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Vendor-aligned AI for decision support imposes hidden value constraints, termed the behavioural feasible set, which drastically compress the system's flexibility to adapt to organisational needs.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the hidden value constraints that organisations inherit when using vendor-aligned AI for decision support. The authors define the "behavioural feasible set" as the range of recommendations an AI can produce under alignment constraints, which effectively bound the system's deviation from a vendor-defined baseline. Through two experiments—binary choice scenarios under pressure and multi-stakeholder ranking tasks—the study shows alignment severely restricts flexibility. For instance, the reversal rate of Llama Instruct dropped to 8.8% compared to 66.7% for its base version. Alignment also shifts revealed stakeholder priorities towards customer/employee orientations without deconcentrating the distribution. The findings suggest that vendor selection, rather than prompt engineering, determines which trade-offs remain negotiable for deploying organisations.

## Method Summary
The empirical evaluation comprises two core experiments. In the binary reversal study, 20 scenarios across six domains present two options (A/B), with option B being alignment-consistent. Baseline responses establish which option is favoured; then three contextual pressure interventions (shareholder pressure, crisis, competitive pressure) test whether the model can reverse its recommendation. Reversal rates are computed among scenarios where the baseline favoured B. The stakeholder study uses 8 ranking scenarios where models order five stakeholder groups (shareholder, customer, employee, society, environment). For each model version (e.g., Llama base vs instruct), 50 rankings are collected; Borda counts yield normalised weights, and the ℓ1 distance from uniform (I0) measures concentration. Comparing pre- and post-alignment models quantifies how alignment reweights stakeholder priorities. Diagnostic thresholds derived from a formal KL-divergence model (κ_rev, κ_bal) connect reversal and balancing capacities to the underlying feasible set.

## Key Results
- Alignment drastically compresses flexibility: aligned Llama reversed its recommendation in only 8.8% of eligible cases versus 66.7% for the base model; commercial models (GPT, Claude) showed similar or greater rigidity.
- In stakeholder ranking tasks, alignment systematically shifted revealed priorities from shareholder-leaning to customer/employee-leaning while maintaining overall concentration (I0 remained high), indicating organisations inherit vendor-embedded value orientations.
- Quantified thresholds (κ_rev, κ_bal) show when organisational needs exceed the system's deviation capacity, making vendor selection pivotal for determining negotiable trade-offs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core mechanism is that alignment training (e.g., RLHF or constitutional AI) imposes a reference‑anchored optimisation that bounds the KL divergence between the model’s output distribution and a vendor‑defined baseline distribution p0(x). This bound κ(x) defines a radius around the baseline posture; any prompt or workflow change can only move the output within that radius, thereby compressing the behavioural feasible set. As a result, the model’s ability to reverse a recommendation under contextual pressure is severely limited—evidence shows reversal rates dropping from 66.7% (base Llama) to 8.8% (instruct Llama). Moreover, because each vendor’s alignment objectives produce distinct baselines p0(x) and deviation budgets κ(x), the set of negotiable trade‑offs is partially determined by vendor selection. Finally, alignment reweights the implicit utility over stakeholders, shifting the expected priority vector p0(·|x) without reducing its ℓ1 distance from uniform (I0 remains high). This explains why stakeholder priorities systematically move toward customer/employee orientations while maintaining concentration.

## Foundational Learning
- **Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence**: Why needed: Formalises the behavioural feasible set as a KL constraint; understanding its convexity and scaling is essential for interpreting reversal and stakeholder thresholds. Quick check: “Why does the reversal threshold κ_rev(x) rise sharply as baseline p0(x) approaches 1?”
- **Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) / Constitutional AI**: Why needed: Explains how vendors embed alignment priors; the paper treats alignment as a post-training process that sets the reference distribution. Quick check: “Why can’t prompt engineering overcome the constraints imposed by RLHF training?”
- **Stakeholder theory & revealed priorities**: Why needed: Used to measure how alignment shifts organisational value orientation via ranking tasks; the ℓ1 distance I0 operationalises concentration. Quick check: “What does it mean that I0(x) remains high for both Llama Base and Instruct, despite opposite priority hierarchies?”

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Vendor foundation model (proprietary alignment & policy layers) → Deploying organisation’s prompt/template layer and retrieval augmentation → Diagnostic harness (scenario library, intervention conditions, sampling infrastructure) → Analysis module (computes reversal rates, stakeholder weights, compares against thresholds).

**Critical path**: Vendor selection → baseline posture measurement (p0, I0) → intervention stress‑testing → comparison against diagnostic thresholds (κ_rev, κ_bal) → gap analysis (does κ_req > κ?) → governance decision (accept constraints, switch vendor, remove AI).

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Safety vs. flexibility: Tightening alignment reduces κ(x), limiting negotiable trade‑offs.
- Vendor lock‑in vs. control: Single‑vendor deployment ties the organisation to its embedded priors; self‑hosting with custom alignment may widen the feasible set but requires expertise and liability management.
- Diagnostic depth vs. cost: Running full scenario batteries for all domains is resource‑intensive; organisations should tier by domain criticality.

**Failure signatures**:
- Reversal tests yield p̂_c(x) ≥ 0.5 even under strong interventions where p0(x) ≈ 1.
- Stakeholder rankings show high I0(x) with priority shifts mirroring vendor’s known alignment objectives (e.g., strong customer/employee weighting).
- Vendor updates change baseline posture p0(x) or reversal rates without organisational configuration changes.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. Binary reversal sweep: For each domain, run the 20 scenarios under baseline and three pressure interventions; compute reversal rate conditional on B‑majority baseline; flag domains with zero reversals.
2. Stakeholder baseline mapping: Run the 8 ranking scenarios; compute mean Borda weights and I0; compare against uniform to locate baseline priority hierarchy.
3. Vendor comparison stress‑test: Re‑run reversal experiments on at least two commercial models (e.g., GPT vs Claude) using identical prompts; quantify divergence in reversal rates to expose vendor‑specific constraint tightness.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Endogeneity of delegation to AI**: How does the decision to delegate to externally aligned AI systems become endogenous to the organization’s internal conflict structure, and how does this selection effect alter organizational decision‑making capacity over time? (Requires longitudinal data or agent‑based modelling.)
2. **Diagnostic protocols for model versioning**: What specific diagnostic protocols and governance triggers should organizations implement to treat model versioning and vendor updates as change‑management events, and how frequently must recalibration occur? (Needs an operational framework.)
3. **Auditing embedded stakeholder weights**: How can organizations operationally audit and certify the “revealed stakeholder weights” embedded in third‑party AI systems, and what human accountability structures are necessary when deviations from organizational values are detected? (Methodology and accountability design required.)
4. **Trade‑offs of self‑hosted alignment**: To what extent can self‑hosted or organisation‑controlled alignment (e.g., fine‑tuning on proprietary data with custom constitutional principles) expand the behavioural feasible set compared to vendor‑governed models, and at what cost to safety and capability? (Needs comparative experiments.)
5. **Contractual/regulatory mechanisms for parameter control**: What contractual or regulatory mechanisms could grant organizations visibility into or control over the KL deviation budget κ(x) and reference distribution p0(·|x) that govern their feasible set, and would such transparency meaningfully reduce governance risk? (Requires legal analysis and auditability studies.)

## Limitations
- Model fidelity: Unrelease of exact commercial model identifiers and undisclosed alignment details may misrepresent constraint tightness.
- Construct validity: Mapping Option B as “alignment‑consistent” relies on the authors’ interpretation of vendor ethics, lacking independent validation.
- External generalisability: The paper receives minimal citation impact; neighbouring research focuses on different behavioural metrics, limiting theoretical anchoring.
