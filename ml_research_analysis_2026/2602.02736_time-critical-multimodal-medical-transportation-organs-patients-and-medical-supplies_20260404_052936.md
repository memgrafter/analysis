# Time-Critical Multimodal Medical Transportation: Organs, Patients, and Medical Supplies

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.02736  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02736  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: The M2DH heuristic achieves near-optimal solutions (average optimality gap <0.65%) with low runtime (~11.35 s for 50 vehicles) for multimodal medical transportation.

## Executive Summary
This research tackles the challenge of real-time dispatching for time-critical medical transportation (organs, patients, supplies) using a multimodal fleet of ambulances, UAVs, and eVTOLs. The objective is to minimize a weighted combination of travel/waiting time and operating costs while accounting for ground traffic and aerial weather constraints. The proposed M2DH algorithm evaluates 48 feasible multimodal route patterns per request, incorporating payload consolidation and offering greedy, constructive assignments.

Extensive testing on a Northeast Ohio network demonstrates that an integrated fleet minimizes the objective when time is heavily weighted, whereas a cost-dominated setting favors an ambulance + UAV configuration due to eVTOLs’ high operating costs. The algorithm attains near-optimality with an average optimality gap below 0.65% and a runtime of about 11.35 seconds for 50 vehicles, confirming its effectiveness and scalability for real-world deployment.

## Method Summary
The methodology centers on the M2DH heuristic for real-time assignment of medical requests to a multimodal fleet. Given a network of hospitals and vertiports, vehicle specifications (capacity, speed, cost, range), time‑varying traffic and wind conditions, and a stream of requests with time windows, M2DH processes each request sequentially. It generates 48 candidate route patterns (combinations of up to three legs using ground or air vehicles), checks feasibility (range, payload compatibility, vehicle availability, consolidation opportunities), and selects the route–vehicle–time‑slot combination minimizing a normalized weighted sum of time and cost. Vehicle states (location, capacity, schedule) are updated after assignment. The approach is evaluated on a Northeast Ohio network with 8 hospitals and 5 vertiports using 50 requests over six hours. Baseline comparisons and exhaustive search validate performance.

## Key Results
- Near‑optimal solution quality: average optimality gap < 0.65% compared to exhaustive search, with runtime scaling to ~11.35 s for 50 vehicles.
- Fleet composition trade‑offs: When time weight is high, a fully integrated fleet (ambulances, UAVs, eVTOLs) yields the lowest objective; when cost weight dominates, an ambulance + UAV fleet is optimal, avoiding expensive eVTOLs.
- Significant improvement over baseline: M2DH reduces the objective by > 25% in time‑critical settings and enables payload consolidation to lower fleet usage and costs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Multimodal Routing Reduces Travel Time
**Claim:** Multimodal routing (up to three legs combining air and ground vehicles) reduces travel time by assigning each leg to the fastest feasible mode, bypassing ground traffic via air segments when beneficial.  
**Mechanism:** For each request, the algorithm evaluates 48 possible route patterns (e.g., ground–air–ground). Air legs (UAV/eVTOL) avoid road congestion, while ground legs handle first/last miles where vertiports are unavailable. Travel times dynamically adjust for traffic (BPR function) and wind.  
**Core assumption:** Vertiports exist at or near hospitals to enable mode transfers; air vehicle range exceeds leg distance; payload type compatibility is respected (e.g., UAVs cannot carry patients).  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] “multimodal transportation system… can leverage the strengths of each… to enhance overall transportation efficiency” and “accounts for traffic congestion in ground operations and weather conditions in aerial operations.”  
- [section II] Formal problem definition allows up to three sequential legs with different vehicle types; constraint 6 ensures flow continuity between legs.  
- [corpus] Weak evidence; adjacent work on UAM coordination (e.g., “UAV‑MARL” paper) supports air‑ground integration but not explicitly the multi‑leg medical routing.  
**Break condition:** If vertiports are too sparse or distant from hospitals, air legs become infeasible, forcing ground‑only routes and losing time savings.

### Mechanism 2: Payload Consolidation Reduces Fleet Usage and Costs
**Claim:** Payload consolidation—transporting multiple compatible medical payloads on the same vehicle—reduces total fleet usage and operating costs by sharing vehicle capacity across requests with aligned time windows.  
**Mechanism:** During route evaluation, the algorithm checks if a vehicle’s remaining capacity and scheduled timeline can accommodate additional payloads without violating deadlines. If feasible, consolidation is permitted, reducing the number of vehicle deployments.  
**Core assumption:** Payloads have compatible pickup/drop‑off times and origins/destinations; vehicle capacity is not exceeded; consolidation does not increase individual payload waiting time beyond acceptable limits.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] “incorporates payload consolidation across compatible routes” and “enabling rapid vehicle dispatching.”  
- [section II] “Multiple medical payloads may be consolidated onto the same vehicle simultaneously, provided that their time windows are compatible and the vehicle’s capacity is not exceeded.”  
- [corpus] Limited direct evidence; neighboring papers on multi‑agent reinforcement learning (“UAV‑MARL”) address coordination but not consolidation for medical payloads.  
**Break condition:** If payloads have tight, non‑overlapping time windows or conflicting routes, consolidation opportunities vanish, reverting to dedicated dispatches and higher costs.

### Mechanism 3: Constructive Greedy Heuristic Enables Real‑Time Decisions
**Claim:** A constructive greedy heuristic that evaluates a fixed set of 48 feasible multimodal routes per request enables real‑time dispatching with near‑optimal solutions by avoiding combinatorial explosion of global optimization.  
**Mechanism:** Upon request arrival, the algorithm sequentially tests each of the 48 route patterns, checking leg feasibility (vehicle availability, range, payload compatibility) and time slots. It selects the route–vehicle–time‑slot combination minimizing the weighted time‑cost objective, updating vehicle states immediately.  
**Core assumption:** Sequential, myopic decisions without foresight of future requests yield near‑optimal system performance; restricting to nearest vertiports keeps route count manageable.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] “M2DH algorithm… evaluating 48 feasible multimodal routes… achieves near‑optimal solutions (average optimality gap <0.65%) with low runtime (~11.35 s for 50 vehicles).”  
- [section III] Algorithm 1 details steps, including “Validate Leg” and “FindMinimumCost” over candidate routes.  
- [corpus] Corpus shows other real‑time heuristics for UAM (e.g., “Self‑organized free‑flight arrival”), but the 48‑route greedy approach for multimodal medical transport is novel.  
**Break condition:** Under highly correlated or batched requests, greedy myopia may cause suboptimal vehicle pre‑positioning, increasing later costs or missed deadlines compared to batch optimization.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input layer: Hospital–vertiport network (coordinates, pairings), vehicle specs (capacity, speed, cost, range), time‑varying travel times (traffic‑adjusted ground, wind‑adjusted air), demand stream (requests with origin/destination, type, time windows).  
Processing core: M2DH algorithm per request: (1) generate 48 route patterns; (2) for each leg, validate feasibility; (3) scan vehicle time slots; (4) compute objective for feasible assignments; (5) select best candidate route.  
State management: Vehicle location, capacity, timeline updated after each assignment; payload consolidation tracked.  
Output layer: Vehicle assignments (which vehicle, which legs, pickup/drop times) for each served request.

**Critical path:** Request arrival → Route pattern loop → Leg validation → Time slot eligibility check → Objective calculation → Best route selection → Vehicle state update. The nested loops over routes and legs are the computational core.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Route enumeration fixed at 48 vs. exhaustive search (all vertiport pairs): Limits optimality but ensures runtime scalability.  
- Greedy myopic assignment vs. batch reassignment: Real‑time responsiveness vs. potential long‑term inefficiency.  
- Consolidation enabled vs. disabled: Lower costs vs. increased waiting time risk.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Deadline misses: t_actual > t_max for any request, indicating infeasible route or poor assignment.  
- Capacity violations: Consolidated payload count exceeds vehicle capacity.  
- Range breaches: Air leg distance > vehicle remaining range.  
- Infeasible legs: No eligible time slot found for any vehicle on a leg, leading to unserved request.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Runtime scaling test: Increase fleet size from 10 to 50 vehicles; measure per-request runtime to confirm ~11.35 s at 50 vehicles (Fig 13).  
2. Optimality gap validation: Run M2DH and exhaustive search on identical fleet states for all 50 requests; compute average/max gap per priority setting (Table III).  
3. Fleet configuration sensitivity: Vary wt/wc weights; compare objective values across four fleet configurations to reproduce key findings (Figs 4–10), especially that eVTOLs are avoided when wc is high.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
**Question:** How does restricting multimodal routes to the nearest vertiport at origin and destination (as in M2DH) affect solution quality compared to considering all possible vertiport connections, especially in larger networks?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] The M2DH algorithm evaluates only the nearest vertiport to the origin and destination hospitals for each request, whereas the exhaustive search algorithm considers all possible vertiport connections. The authors note that M2DH’s restriction makes it independent of network size but may miss better routes.  
**Why unresolved:** The paper compares M2DH to exhaustive search only on a small network (8 hospitals, 5 vertiports) and finds small optimality gaps (<0.65% on average). However, in larger networks with more vertiports, the gap might increase because non‑nearest vertiports could offer faster or cheaper routes. The scalability of M2DH’s nearest‑vertiport heuristic is not evaluated on larger networks.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Computational experiments on larger synthetic or real networks (e.g., with 20+ hospitals and 10+ vertiports) comparing M2DH to an exhaustive search (for small instances) or a high‑quality metaheuristic that considers multiple vertiports. Metrics: optimality gap, runtime, and sensitivity to network topology.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** To what extent does payload consolidation contribute to the performance improvements of M2DH over the baseline heuristic, and can consolidation strategies be further optimized by allowing more flexible time windows or mixed payload types (e.g., combining patients with organs under certain conditions)?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] The M2DH algorithm includes payload consolidation across compatible routes, while the baseline heuristic does not. The paper shows that M2DH reduces objective values significantly in time‑dominant settings (e.g., >25% in configuration 4). However, the specific contribution of consolidation is not isolated, and the consolidation rules (only compatible payloads with matching time windows) may be conservative.  
**Why unresolved:** The baseline heuristic also considers fewer route types (mostly unimodal) so the improvement could be due to both multimodal routes and consolidation. The paper does not run an ablation study to quantify consolidation’s isolated effect. Also, current consolidation only combines payloads with compatible time windows; perhaps allowing slight delays or partial overlaps could yield more consolidation opportunities without violating deadlines.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Design experiments that isolate consolidation: (a) M2DH without consolidation but with same multimodal routes; (b) M2DH with relaxed consolidation rules. Compare objective values and number of vehicles used. Also, analyze consolidation rates in the solutions.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** How robust are the fleet configuration recommendations (e.g., avoid eVTOLs when cost‑weighted) to uncertainties in eVTOL operating costs and battery range, given that these parameters are based on early prototypes and may evolve as technology matures?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] The study uses specific cost and range values for eVTOLs (Joby S4) and UAVs (Zipline P2). The recommendation to avoid eVTOLs in cost‑dominant scenarios relies on their high operating cost ($1.81/km) and range (161 km). However, these numbers are from 2025 estimates and may change with mass production, battery improvements, or different eVTOL designs.  
**Why unresolved:** The paper does not perform sensitivity analysis on these parameters. A small change in eVTOL cost could make them competitive with ambulances or UAVs in certain scenarios. Similarly, range improvements might enable more air‑only routes, altering the optimal fleet mix.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct a sensitivity analysis varying eVTOL operating cost per km and range over plausible ranges (e.g., 0.5× to 2× current estimates) and re‑run experiments to see how the optimal fleet configuration changes. Plot decision boundaries in cost‑range space.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** How does the M2DH algorithm perform under more realistic, dynamic weather conditions that include en‑route wind variations and sudden weather disruptions (e.g., storms) that may render air routes infeasible mid‑operation?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper models wind effects by averaging wind vectors at origin and destination, but assumes weather conditions are known hourly. It does not consider en‑route weather heterogeneity or sudden changes. Also, the model does not account for weather‑induced cancellations or diversions.  
**Why unresolved:** The algorithm assumes that once a leg is dispatched, its travel time is fixed based on current weather. In reality, weather can change during the flight, causing delays or forcing rerouting. The robustness of M2DH’s myopic assignments to such dynamics is unknown.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Simulate a more dynamic weather scenario where wind conditions change during a leg, or where weather warnings appear after assignment. Measure on‑time performance and objective value degradation compared to the baseline model. Possibly incorporate stochastic elements or recourse actions in the algorithm.

## Limitations
- Exact coordinates of hospitals/vertiports and the nearest‑vertiport assignment logic are not fully specified; reproduction requires geocoding guesses.
- Demand generation details (origin/destination probabilities, type mix, precise max arrival time calculation) are described only qualitatively; quantitative parameters are missing.
- Initial vehicle positioning (“round‑robin”) lacks precise distribution rules, potentially affecting early assignments and solution consistency.
