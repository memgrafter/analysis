# TimeAPN: Adaptive Amplitude-Phase Non-Stationarity Normalization for Time Series Forecasting

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.17436  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17436  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: TimeAPN reduces MSE by 10-28% on Weather and Electricity datasets and outperforms state-of-the-art reversible normalization methods across multiple prediction horizons.

## Executive Summary
Multivariate time series forecasting is severely impacted by non‑stationarity, particularly rapid variations in amplitude and phase. Traditional normalization techniques that rely on global mean and variance cannot adapt to such dynamic changes, causing model performance to degrade.TimeAPN introduces an adaptive normalization framework that explicitly models and predicts non‑stationary factors in both time and frequency domains using discrete wavelet decomposition. It forecasts future mean values and phase drift, then collaborates with any backbone forecasting model via a de‑normalization step to reconstruct predictions in the original scale. Extensive experiments on seven datasets with four different forecasting backbones show consistent accuracy improvements, achieving MSE reductions of 10-28% on large datasets (Weather, Electricity) and surpassing SOTA reversible normalization methods (RevIN, SAN, DDN) across various horizons. The approach demonstrates robust handling of complex non‑stationary dynamics.

## Method Summary
TimeAPN comprises two core components. First, a discrete wavelet decomposition with learnable bases separates the input into approximation (mean‑related) and detail (phase‑related) coefficients. A mean forecasting branch predicts the future mean from the approximation coefficients, while a Multi‑scale Phase Prediction Module (MPPM) based on a Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) forecasts phase drift from the detail coefficients. The backbone model processes the normalized input to produce a forecast in the stationary space. Finally, a collaborative de‑normalization step merges the predicted mean and phase with the backbone output to yield the final prediction. Training follows a two‑stage strategy: pre‑training the MPPM to stabilize phase learning, followed by joint fine‑tuning of all modules. This design is backbone‑agnostic, allowing integration with various forecasting architectures.

## Key Results
- Consistent accuracy improvements across four forecasting backbones (e.g., DLinear, PatchTST) on seven datasets.
- MSE reduction of 10-28% on the Weather and Electricity datasets.
- Outperforms state‑of‑the‑art reversible normalization methods (RevIN, SAN, DDN) across multiple prediction horizons.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
TimeAPN directly targets the two main sources of non‑stationarity: amplitude (mean shifts) and phase (temporal misalignments). Standard normalization only rescales the series using global statistics, which fails to capture local and evolving changes. By decomposing the signal with wavelets, TimeAPN isolates components that correspond to amplitude and phase variations separately. Forecasting these components in advance allows the model to anticipate future shifts in mean and phase, which are then used to invert the normalization while preserving the original signal structure. The collaborative de‑normalization ensures that the backbone operates on a stationary representation yet benefits from accurate reconstructions of non‑stationary factors, resulting in robust performance across diverse datasets.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Input -> Wavelet Decomposition -> Approx Coeffs -> Mean Forecasting / Detail Coeffs -> Phase Prediction (MPPM) -> (Predicted Mean, Predicted Phase) -> Backbone Forecast -> Collaborative De‑normalization -> Final Forecast  

**Critical path**  
During inference, raw input flows through wavelet decomposition, generating approximation and detail coefficients. The mean forecasting branch and phase prediction branch operate in parallel on these coefficients. Their outputs are combined with the backbone’s forecast in the de‑normalization step to produce the final prediction.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- *Learnable wavelet bases*: Allow adaptation but risk instability and require careful initialization.  
- *TCN for phase prediction*: Efficient and captures local patterns, yet may miss long‑range dependencies compared to Transformers.  
- *Simplified de‑normalization (omitting amplitude)*: Reduces complexity but may limit performance on amplitude‑volatile series.  
- *Two‑stage training*: Improves stability but increases overall training time.

**Failure signatures**  
- Degenerate wavelet bases causing poor decomposition, detectable via reconstruction error.  
- Phase prediction errors on series with long‑range phase shifts, indicated by growing forecast error at longer horizons.  
- Overfitting of MPPM when data is limited, observed as validation loss divergence.  
- Excessive computational overhead for long sequences, especially with deep wavelet decompositions.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How do learnable wavelet bases influence TimeAPN's performance, and are they optimized per dataset?**  
   - *Basis in paper*: Section IV.B describes wavelet bases as "learnable" and initialized to biorthogonal 3.5, but does not clarify if they are updated during training.  
   - *Why unresolved*: The paper provides no ablation comparing fixed versus learned wavelets, nor visualizes the learned filters.  
   - *What evidence would resolve it*: Ablation studies with fixed, random, and learned wavelet bases across datasets; visualization and analysis of the learned wavelet coefficients.

2. **Does the TCN used for phase drift prediction limit accuracy, and would a Transformer or LSTM be more effective?**  
   - *Basis in paper*: Phase prediction is implemented with a TCN (Section IV.C) without comparison to other sequence models.  
   - *Why unresolved*: No empirical evaluation of alternative architectures; phase sequences may contain long‑range dependencies that TCNs struggle with.  
   - *What evidence would resolve it*: Replace the TCN with a lightweight Transformer or LSTM in MPPM, measure phase prediction error and final forecasting metrics.

3. **Why is amplitude information omitted from the de‑normalization reconstruction, and would including it improve performance?**  
   - *Basis in paper*: Section IV.D simplifies de‑normalization to use only phase and mean, while amplitude is only included in the loss function.  
   - *Why unresolved*: The paper does not test the full amplitude‑phase reconstruction (Eq.7) or justify the simplification; potential accuracy loss on amplitude‑volatile series.  
   - *What evidence would resolve it*: Implement the full frequency‑domain reconstruction with amplitude compensation and compare against the simplified method on datasets with high amplitude dynamics.

4. **Is the two‑stage training (pre‑train MPPM then fine‑tune) necessary for stable convergence, or could end‑to‑end training suffice?**  
   - *Basis in paper*: Section IV.E proposes a two‑stage strategy to stabilize training but offers no ablation.  
   - *Why unresolved*: It is unclear whether joint training from scratch would be equally effective or cause instability.  
   - *What evidence would resolve it*: Train the full model end‑to‑end with appropriate loss weighting and compare convergence speed, training stability, and final forecasting accuracy.

## Limitations
- **Reproducibility gap**: No public code or replication notes provided, hindering independent verification.
- **Generalizability concerns**: Experiments on seven datasets (primarily Weather, Electricity) may not cover all real‑world non‑stationary patterns; performance on small or highly volatile datasets unreported.
- **Computational trade‑offs**: Wavelet decomposition and adaptive forecasting add overhead; efficiency metrics absent, limiting practical deployment insights.
