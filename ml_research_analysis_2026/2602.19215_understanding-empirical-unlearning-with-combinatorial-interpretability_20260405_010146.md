# Understanding Empirical Unlearning with Combinatorial Interpretability

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.19215
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19215
- **Reference count:** 10
- **Primary result:** Unlearning methods like Gradient Ascent and Task Vector fail to permanently remove knowledge; erased concepts recover quickly via unrelated fine-tuning, indicating that knowledge persists in weight structure and recovery follows a direction parallel to the initial erasure.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether empirical unlearning techniques—Gradient Ascent, Task Vector, and Privacy Preserving Distillation (PPD)—truly remove targeted knowledge from neural networks. Using a novel "combinatorial interpretability" framework that allows direct inspection of neuron-level weight patterns in two-layer networks trained on DNF logic formulas, the authors find that erased concepts persist structurally and can be quickly restored during subsequent fine-tuning, even on unrelated data. Gradient Ascent and Task Vector show rapid recovery (median 37 and 14 epochs) whereas PPD does not, and analysis reveals that recovery occurs through weight updates that strongly align with the original erasure direction, suggesting that unlearning merely disrupts expression rather than eliminating underlying representations. The work highlights that standard performance metrics (e.g., accuracy) are insufficient to certify unlearning; instead, the geometric integrity of weight space determines vulnerability to revival. These findings, while derived from simplified synthetic tasks, raise concerns about the durability of unlearning in larger models and call for methods that permanently alter the network's encoding structure.

## Method Summary
The study trains two-layer MLPs on synthetic datasets where classification is governed by Disjunctive Normal Form (DNF) clauses. Each clause corresponds to a specific pattern of input variables, and the network learns to encode these clauses using distinct "positive" and "negative" first-layer neurons (combinatorial interpretability). To unlearn a target clause, the authors apply three methods: (1) Gradient Ascent, which repeatedly maximizes the loss on forget data; (2) Task Vector, which subtracts a task-specific fine-tuning vector from the original weights; and (3) Privacy Preserving Distillation (PPD), which trains a fresh student network on non-sensitive data to mimic the teacher's behavior. After erasure, they evaluate True Positive Rate (TPR) to confirm the target concept is suppressed. Then they perform recovery fine-tuning for up to 60 epochs on three data regimes (target only, all clauses, non-target only) and measure the number of epochs needed for TPR to reach 80%. To understand the mechanism, they compute weight difference vectors between original/erased (d0) and erased/fine-tuned (df) models and decompose df into components parallel and orthogonal to d0, yielding the "parallel fraction". They also compute cosine distance between original and erased weights to assess structural preservation. Experiments are conducted on both a standard DNF dataset (no shared variables) and a DNF-shared variant where target and control clauses overlap in input variables, enabling testing of recovery acceleration via shared features.

## Key Results
- Gradient Ascent and Task Vector allow erased clauses to recover quickly via unrelated fine-tuning (median 37 and 14 epochs); PPD shows no recovery even with target-data fine-tuning.
- Weight updates during recovery are highly parallel to the original erasure direction (parallel fraction ~1), indicating a directed reversal rather than random drift, implying the erased concept's encoding remains in the network's weight structure.
- Cosine distance between original and erased weights is near zero for Gradient Ascent and Task Vector, but near one for PPD; this structural preservation strongly correlates with vulnerability to recovery.
- In the DNF-shared setting, recovery time drops significantly (e.g., Task Vector non-target: 14→6 epochs), suggesting shared features accelerate revival.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Unlearning does not remove underlying knowledge but disrupts its expression; recovery occurs via fine-tuning that inadvertently restores the original weight configuration along the erasure direction.
- **Mechanism:** The erased concept's encoding remains structurally present in the network weights (e.g., in positive/negative neuron patterns). Fine-tuning on any data induces weight updates that, due to the network's optimization dynamics and shared feature representations, move the weights back toward their pre-erasure state, particularly along the dimension of the initial erasure vector.
- **Core assumption:** The network's weight space contains a "memory" of the erased concept in a geometrically specific way (aligned with the original encoding) that is reactivated by gradient-based updates aimed at other objectives.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "erased concepts persist... recovery occurs through targeted weight updates parallel to initial erasure direction... indicating knowledge retention in network structure."
  - [section 3.2] High parallel fraction (~1) between recovery vector and original erasure vector across scenarios; recovery magnitude is non-zero even with unrelated fine-tuning.
  - [corpus] Neighbor papers (e.g., "Understanding the Dilemma of Unlearning...") report similar "revival" phenomena in LLMs, though the mechanistic attribution differs; corpus evidence supports persistence but not necessarily the parallel-direction mechanism.
- **Break condition:** If fine-tuning on unrelated data produces a recovery vector with low parallel fraction (<0.5) to the erasure direction, the directed reversal hypothesis fails, suggesting recovery is due to random drift or alternative mechanisms.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The efficacy and recoverability of unlearning are determined by the method's impact on the network's weight structure, not just surface-level performance metrics.
- **Mechanism:** Gradient Ascent and Task Vector minimally alter the overall weight geometry (low cosine distance to original), preserving the encoding scaffold for the target concept. PPD trains a new student network from randomness, creating a divergent weight structure that cannot easily reintegrate the erased concept's encoding. Thus, structural integrity, not just behavioral inhibition, determines recoverability.
- **Core assumption:** Combinatorial interpretability's weight inspection (clause-encoding patterns in first-layer neurons) is a valid proxy for persistent knowledge; changes in these patterns predict recovery.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Gradient Ascent and Task Vector recover via unrelated fine-tuning... PPD does not."
  - [section 3.1 & Figure 2] Cosine distance between original and erased weights: near 0 for Gradient Ascent/Task Vector, high (∼1) for PPD; corresponding recovery times differ.
  - [corpus] Neighbors on "concept revival" and "unlearning vulnerabilities" empirically show behavioral recovery but do not link it to weight geometry; corpus lacks direct mechanistic evidence for this specific structural claim.
- **Break condition:** If a method that drastically changes weight geometry (high cosine distance) still allows rapid recovery via unrelated fine-tuning, the structural preservation assumption is violated.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Shared features between target and non-target clauses facilitate faster and more certain recovery because fine-tuning on non-target clauses directly optimizes weights that partially encode the target.
- **Mechanism:** When clauses share input variables, the positive/negative neurons encoding those clauses overlap. Fine-tuning to improve detection of non-target (control) clauses updates the shared weights, which also moves them back toward their state that activated the target clause, accelerating recovery.
- **Core assumption:** Clause encoding in the first layer is compositional and overlapping; neuron "assignment" to a clause is not exclusive.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.1] Comparison of DNF (non-shared) vs. DNF-shared: recovery epochs drop (e.g., Gradient Ascent non-target: 37 → 25 epochs; Task Vector non-target: 14 → 6 epochs).
  - [table 4] For DNF-shared, Task Vector non-target recovery is nearly as fast as target fine-tuning (median 6 vs. 5 epochs).
  - [corpus] No direct evidence; neighboring works on LLM unlearning do not decompose shared vs. non-shared concepts in an interpretable weight space.
- **Break condition:** If recovery time does not systematically decrease when target and non-target clauses share variables, the shared-feature acceleration hypothesis is falsified.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Combinatorial Interpretability Framework**
  - *Why needed here:* Provides the only tool to directly inspect weight-level knowledge (clause-encoding neurons) in a controlled setting, bypassing the opacity of large models. Without it, persistence would only be inferred from behavioral metrics.
  - *Quick check question:* What two neuron types in the first layer correspond to logical clause encoding, and what is their functional role?
- **Concept: DNF Clause Encoding**
  - *Why needed here:* The synthetic dataset structure (Disjunctive Normal Form) creates interpretable, discrete "concepts" (clauses) whose presence/absence can be exactly verified in the weights. This allows precise measurement of unlearning and recovery.
  - *Quick check question:* How does a "positive neuron" differ from a "negative neuron" in its weight pattern and role in classification?
- **Concept: Task Vector & Gradient Ascent Mechanics**
  - *Why needed here:* These are the baseline unlearning techniques evaluated. Understanding their parameter-space operation (negative task vector addition vs. loss maximization) is key to interpreting why they preserve structure (shallow edits) versus PPD (training from scratch).
  - *Quick check question:* What is the fundamental difference in how Task Vector and Gradient Ascent modify model weights compared to Privacy Preserving Distillation?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Two-layer MLP: Input (binary variables) → First layer (linear ReLU, weights encode DNF clauses via positive/negative neuron patterns) → Output neuron (linear, weights combine first-layer activations). No biases used.
- **Critical path:** 1) Train base model on DNF dataset until clause detection perfect. 2) Apply unlearning method (Gradient Ascent/Task Vector/PPD) to erase specific target clauses while preserving others (verify via TPR/FPR). 3) Perform recovery fine-tuning on various data types (target, non-target, all) for up to 60 epochs. 4) Analyze recovery via TPR on target and decompose weight changes (parallel fraction).
- **Design tradeoffs:** *Simplicity vs. Realism:* Two-layer networks on synthetic logic provide perfect weight interpretability but sacrifice the complexity, polysemanticity, and scale of real LLMs/vision models. *Controlled variables vs. ecological validity:* Shared-variable DNF introduces feature overlap to study compositional recovery, but real-world "shared features" are messier.
- **Failure signatures:** 1) PPD: High cosine distance between original and erased weights; recovery fails even with target-data fine-tuning. 2) Gradient Ascent/Task Vector: Recovery on non-target data with parallel fraction near 1 indicates directed reversal (expected mechanism); low parallel fraction would indicate failure of this mechanism. 3) Any method failing to reduce target TPR to 0 post-erasure indicates incomplete unlearning from start.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Replicate base training:** Train a network on a simple 4-clause, 16-variable DNF (non-shared). Verify first-layer weight patterns show distinct positive neurons for each clause (high weight on clause's variables, near-zero elsewhere) and negative neurons suppressing spurious patterns.
  2. **Apply and verify erasure:** Use Task Vector (scaling factor 1.0) to erase clause 0. Confirm: a) TPR on clause 0 ≈ 0, b) TPR on other clauses > 0.9, c) cosine distance between original and erased weights (for clause-encoding dimensions) is near 0.
  3. **Test recovery dynamics:** Fine-tune the erased model on data containing only non-target clauses. Track TPR on clause 0 every 5 epochs. Expect: gradual recovery to 80% TPR within ~14 epochs (median from Table 1), with weight updates for clause-encoding dimensions highly parallel to the original erasure vector.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
#### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Under what specific conditions does fine-tuning on non-target data reliably restore the original encoding of an erased concept, and what mechanism drives this restoration?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion states: "In future work we will use Combinatorial Interpretability to better understand when and why fine-tuning on non-target data restores the original encoding..."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper demonstrates that recovery occurs via parallel weight updates but does not explain the causal relationship between unrelated fine-tuning data and the specific weight changes that revive erased clauses.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments varying fine-tuning data distribution, network depth, and concept overlap, combined with tracking neuron-level activation changes during recovery to identify triggering patterns.

#### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Why does Privacy Preserving Distillation (PPD) fundamentally prevent concept recovery, while Gradient Ascent and Task Vector do not?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Table 1 shows PPD models fail to recover even when fine-tuned on all clauses, coupled with Figure 2/C.2 indicating PPD produces substantially different weight structures (high cosine distance).
- **Why unresolved:** The paper notes PPD "learns a new encoding structure" but does not analyze why this structural change is irreversible or how it differs qualitatively from the weight changes induced by other methods.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Comparative analysis of the final weight configurations post-PPD versus post-GA/TV, focusing on neuron specialization (positive/negative) and clause representation redundancy to identify barriers to re-encoding.

#### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Is the observed "directed reversal" (high parallel fraction of weight updates) a universal property of unlearning in neural networks, or an artifact of the simple DNF-setting and two-layer topology?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Across Table 2 and Table 5, parallel fractions are consistently near 1 regardless of neuron sign or clause type, suggesting a systematic reversal mechanism rather than random drift.
- **Why unresolved:** The combinatorial interpretability framework only applies to two-layer networks; it is unclear if similar directed reversals occur in deeper, non-linear models with entangled representations.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicating the parallel fraction metric in networks with more layers, different activation functions, and on non-synthetic tasks (e.g., image or language data) using approximate interpretability methods (e.g., probing or causal tracing).

#### Open Question 4
- **Question:** To what extent do these findings on concept persistence and recovery generalize to real-world large language or vision models, where concepts are polysemantic and not cleanly decomposable into discrete clauses?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The conclusion notes: "...to assess the applicability of these findings to larger models used in practice."
- **Why unresolved:** The study uses a highly idealized, binary DNF task where concepts correspond to exact logical clauses. Real-world models contain fuzzy, overlapping, and non-binary concepts, making unlearning dynamics potentially different.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A meta-study applying the same unlearning methods (GA, TV, PPD) to small but real pretrained models (e.g., a 7B LLM or a Stable Diffusion variant) and measuring concept recovery using suitable probes for targeted concepts, comparing recovery time and weight-update directions to the combinatorial case.

## Limitations
- Combinatorial interpretability’s neuron‑clause mapping algorithm is not described, making weight‑level replication impossible.
- Model training details (architecture size, optimizer, loss) are omitted, so unlearning hyperparameters may not transfer to a reproduction.
- Findings are limited to tiny two‑layer MLPs on binary logic tasks; generalization to real large‑scale models remains unproven.
