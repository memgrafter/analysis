# Competitive Multi-Operator Reinforcement Learning for Joint Pricing and Fleet Rebalancing in AMoD Systems
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.05000
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05000
- **Reference count:** 26
- **Primary result:** Competition between two AMoD operators fundamentally alters optimal policies, typically lowering prices (up to 27% vs. monopoly) and reducing profits (7.1–15.2%), though specialized policies can sometimes outperform joint control in competitive settings.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates competitive markets for Autonomous Mobility-on-Demand (AMoD) systems with multiple independent operators, a gap in prior reinforcement learning (RL) research that focused on monopolistic control. It introduces a dual-operator RL framework where two agents simultaneously learn joint pricing and fleet rebalancing policies. Passenger demand is allocated endogenously via a discrete choice model sensitive to price, travel time, and passenger wages. Using real-world data from three cities, experiments demonstrate that competition significantly changes optimal strategies compared to monopoly. While joint pricing-rebalancing control dominates in monopolies, competitive markets often favor specialized policies—either focused on pricing or rebalancing. The study shows that independent RL agents can robustly converge despite the non-stationarity of competing against an unobservable strategy, with competition inducing dynamic price adjustments, asymmetric splits, and spatial rebalancing toward high-income regions.

## Method Summary
The work implements a simulation-based AMoD environment for two competing operators. Each operator's policy is trained independently using an Actor-Critic (A2C) algorithm with a Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) encoder to process spatial state (region-level vehicle queues, idle counts, demand). Actions consist of per-origin price scalars (Beta distribution) and desired idle-vehicle distribution across origins (Dirichlet distribution), which are converted into OD fares and min-cost rebalancing flows. The environment’s core is a discrete choice model (Multinomial Logit) that endogenously assigns Poisson-distributed demand to operators based on their prices and estimated travel times, with price sensitivity modulated by passenger wages. Operators are trained for 150k episodes, with evaluation over multiple seeds to compare profits, served demand, and spatial patterns against monopoly and ablation baselines.

## Key Results
- Competitive markets reduce average prices by up to 27% compared to monopoly, with operator profit losses ranging from 7.1% to 15.2% in cities with high demand variability.
- Joint pricing-rebalancing control, optimal under monopoly, is not universally best in competition; specialized rebalancing or pricing policies can achieve higher profits.
- Independent RL agents converge reliably despite the non-stationary environment of a learning competitor; performance is robust even when competitor fleet states are unobserved.
- Asymmetric equilibria emerge: smaller operators tend to raise prices while larger ones undercut, and rebalancing shifts toward high-income regions under wage heterogeneity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Competition fundamentally alters optimal AMoD strategies compared to a monopoly, often favoring specialized policies over joint pricing-rebalancing control.
- **Mechanism:** In a dual-operator setting, the discrete choice model endogenously allocates demand based on each operator's price and fleet position. An operator's action no longer optimizes against a fixed environment but against a strategy that dynamically responds, creating a non-stationary learning problem. The learned policy must implicitly model the competitor's impact on demand capture and fleet efficiency. Joint control's dominance in monopolies diminishes because the coordination benefits are offset by the complexity of anticipating a competitor's simultaneous decisions.
- **Core assumption:** Operators are myopically profit-maximizing and non-cooperative; passenger choice is utility-driven and fully responsive to operator actions.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "competition fundamentally alters learned behaviors... Joint control dominates monopoly scenarios but is not universally best in competition—specialized rebalancing or pricing policies sometimes achieve higher profits."
  - [section IV.A] "In San Francisco, the monopolistic joint control achieves 12,286$ while the best dual-operator configuration yields 10,415$, a 15.2% reduction... In NYC Manhattan South, the dual-operator pricing strategy achieves a profit, slightly above the monopolistic joint control."
- **Break condition:** If operators adopt collusive strategies (explicitly or tacitly), the competitive equilibrium shifts, potentially restoring joint control's advantage or creating new equilibria not observed in this non-cooperative setup.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The discrete choice model is the causal link that couples pricing actions to endogenous demand competition, making the environment non-stationary for each learner.
- **Mechanism:** Passenger utility `U` is a function of operator-specific price (`p_t,i,j,o`), travel time, and passenger wage (`v_k`). The resulting Multinomial Logit choice probabilities mean an operator's price scalar (`ρ_t,i,o`) directly diverts or loses demand relative to the competitor's price and the outside option. This creates a direct feedback loop: a price decrease increases market share *only if* the competitor does not undercut further, and simultaneously influences the spatial distribution of requests that the rebalancing policy must serve.
- **Core assumption:** Passengers are independent, utility-maximizing agents with known wage distributions; the choice model accurately predicts mode split relative to historical data.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section III.B] Formal definition of utility `U_t^k,i,j,o = β0 - β_t·v_k·τ_t^i,j - (¯v/v_k)·p_t^i,j,o` and that "passenger allocation emerges endogenously from their actions."
  - [section IV.B] Analysis shows "operators have learned to employ strategic, slight undercutting" and "fleet positioning patterns" differ from monopoly, evidencing the coupled response.
- **Break condition:** If demand becomes perfectly inelastic (wage `v_k` → ∞) or if the outside option dominates (`β0` very low), price changes have negligible effect on demand allocation, decoupling pricing from the core competition dynamic.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Independent RL agents can converge to stable, profitable policies in this competitive, partially observable setting despite the stochasticity from an unobserved competitor strategy.
- **Mechanism:** Each operator's policy (`π_θo`) is trained via A2C using its local state (including observed competitor prices but not their fleet states or policies). The non-stationarity from the competitor's evolving policy acts as a generalizing regularization; the GCN-based actor-critic architecture encodes spatial state features (idle vehicles, queues) that are robust to the specific competitor actions. Convergence implies the system reaches an approximate stationary point where neither agent can unilaterally improve its reward, effectively a learned equilibrium.
- **Core assumption:** The environment's Markov property holds from each agent's perspective (past competitor prices provide a sufficient statistic); the learning algorithm and network architecture can handle the induced non-stationarity.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "learning-based approaches are robust to the additional stochasticity of competition, with competitive agents successfully converging to effective policies while accounting for partially unobserved competitor strategies."
  - [section IV.C] "performance is robust to this information condition [competitor price visibility]... with converged rewards nearly identical."
- **Break condition:** If the competitor's policy is highly oscillatory or adversarial (e.g., explicitly trained to destabilize), the non-stationarity could exceed the learning algorithm's capacity to track a best response, leading to divergent or cyclic policy performance.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Markov Decision Process (MDP)**
  - *Why needed here:* The problem is formally defined as an MDP per operator (Section III.B), with states (`s_t^o`), actions (`a_t^o`), transition dynamics (`P`), and rewards (`r_o`). This framing is essential for applying RL.
  - *Quick check question:* How does the state representation for Operator *o* attempt to make the environment approximately Markov given it does not observe the competitor's internal state (fleet locations, queue lengths)?
- **Concept: Graph Neural Networks (GCN)**
  - *Why needed here:* The vehicle network is a spatial graph (`G=(V,E)`). The GCN encoder (Section III.C, Appendix) captures spatial dependencies (e.g., neighboring region demand/vehicle counts) more efficiently than flat MLPs, enabling scalability to city-scale networks.
  - *Quick check question:* What specific spatial relationships does the GCN's adjacency matrix `A` encode, and why is a convolutional approach suitable?
- **Concept: Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) in Non-Stationary Environments**
  - *Why needed here:* Two independent learners interact. The paper uses independent A2C agents (no joint learning, no credit assignment). The core challenge is learning in a non-stationary environment where the competitor's policy is also learning.
  - *Quick check question:* Why does the paper not use a centralized critic or joint policy gradient? What assumption does "independent learners" make about information sharing?
- **Concept: Discrete Choice Models (Multinomial Logit)**
  - *Why needed here:* This model (Section III.B) is the engine for endogenous demand competition. It translates price and service attributes (travel time) into a probability distribution over choices (operator 0, operator 1, alternative).
  - *Quick check question:* How does the wage term `(¯v / v_k)` in the utility function make price sensitivity income-dependent, and what is the implied economic behavior?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Environment Simulator -> Agent (Actor-Critic) -> Rebalancing Solver -> Environment Simulator
  (The loop: actions from agents are processed by the simulator, which computes demand assignment and rebalancing flows; updated state returns to agents for next step.)
- **Critical path:** State encoding (GCN) → Critic value estimation → Actor policy sampling (price & rebalancing) → Rebalancing flow computation → Environment step → Reward calculation → TD-error → Policy/Value update. The critical path for competition is the environment's demand assignment step, which uses *both* operators' prices to split Poisson demand.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Joint vs. Independent Policies:* Joint action space increases complexity but may capture synergies; independent policies (used here) simplify learning but risk suboptimal coordination. Evidence shows joint policy underperforms specialized ones in competition (Table IV).
  - *Observability:* Operators observe competitor prices but not their fleet states. This partial observability may aid robustness (Section IV.C) but limits strategic foresight.
  - *Action Parameterization:* Origin-based price scalars (vs. OD-based) for faster convergence; Dirichlet for rebalancing ensures valid probability distribution over destinations.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Non-convergent rewards:** Could indicate policy oscillations from high non-stationarity or insufficient network capacity.
  - **Extreme price wars:** Both agents learning to set `ρ ≈ 0` (near zero price) indicates the choice model's price sensitivity may be too high or rebalancing costs underestimated.
  - **Fragmented fleets:** Both operators' rebalancing policies consistently sending vehicles to disjoint, suboptimal regions suggests failure to learn competitive spatial positioning.
  - **High variance in reward across test runs:** Indicates policy is brittle to stochastic demand, likely from overfitting or insufficient state representation.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1.  **Monopoly Baseline:** Train a single agent with joint control in a city (e.g., SF). Verify it reaches a stable reward and that joint policy outperforms rebalancing-only and pricing-only (Table II). This establishes the "oracle" for that city.
  2.  **Dual-Agent Convergence:** Train two independent agents with joint control in the same city. Monitor the moving average of both agents' rewards. Expect convergence to a stable pair of policies within ~150k episodes (per paper). Plot individual reward curves to check for cyclical dynamics.
  3.  **Competitive Strategy Validation:** After convergence, test the dual-agent policy. Visually inspect spatial outputs: (a) Are price scalars `ρ_i` spatially heterogeneous? (b) Do net rebalancing flows (Figure 2) align with high-demand regions? (c) Is the smaller operator (in an asymmetric split test) pricing higher? (Section IV.E). This validates the learned competitive behaviors described.

## Limitations
- Data preprocessing (region split, OD aggregation, base fares) and choice model calibration not fully specified.
- GCN architecture details (layer sizes, activations) missing, hindering exact reproduction.
- Assumptions: myopic non‑cooperative operators, no collusion, limited to two operators; real‑world dynamics (travel time variability, vehicle failures) ignored.

## Confidence
- **Competition alters strategies:** High – multi‑city evidence, but dual‑only.
- **Discrete choice coupling:** High – explicit model with sensitivity; wage simplifications may limit realism.
- **Independent RL convergence:** Medium – empirical success, but no theory; may fail with more agents.
