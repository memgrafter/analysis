# Towards Poisoning Robustness Certification for Natural Language Generation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.09757
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09757
- **Reference count:** 28
- **Primary result:** Introduces Targeted Partition Aggregation (TPA), the first algorithm to compute tight lower bounds on poisoning budgets for autoregressive language generation, enabling certified robustness against data poisoning.

## Executive Summary
Natural language generation (NLG) models face significant security risks from data poisoning attacks, yet lack certified defenses due to challenges posed by sequential token dependencies and exponentially large output spaces. This paper addresses this gap by formalizing two key security properties—stability (robustness to any generation change) and validity (robustness to targeted harmful outputs)—and proposing Targeted Partition Aggregation (TPA). TPA computes sound lower bounds on the minimum poisoning budget required to induce specific harmful tokens or phrases. The approach extends to multi-token phrase-level and multi-turn certification via mixed-integer linear programming (MILP). Empirical evaluations demonstrate that TPA can certify validity against up to 0.5% poisoning in agent tool-calling and maintain over 90% first-token validity across long horizons in preference alignment, while ensemble defenses reduce attack success rates from 40–48% to below 6%. This work establishes a foundation for provable security guarantees in language generation deployment.

## Method Summary
The core method builds on the shard-and-aggregate defense: the training data is partitioned into disjoint shards, each used to train an independent model. At inference, ensemble predictions are aggregated via majority vote. TPA computes a certified robustness radius by modeling the adversary’s optimal vote-redistribution strategy: for a target token, it iteratively equalizes and reduces vote counts of the top competing classes until the target secures a majority. For multi-token phrases, each shard generates an entire phrase, and TPA is applied over the expanded phrase space. For multiple prompts or turns, a MILP formulation optimally allocates a global poisoning budget across shards and prompts, yielding a collective certificate tighter than taking the minimum of individual certificates. All certificates are sound lower bounds under the worst-case assumption that a single poisoned point can flip any shard’s vote.

## Key Results
- TPA is the first algorithm to provide tight, certified lower bounds on the poisoning budget needed to induce specific harmful tokens or phrases in autoregressive NLG.
- Empirically certifies agent tool-calling validity against up to 0.5% data poisoning with high confidence.
- Maintains >90% first-token validity with validity horizons extending over 20+ tokens in preference alignment under poisoning budgets of up to k=9.
- Ensemble defenses reduce empirical attack success rates from 40–48% to below 6% across evaluated scenarios.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Token-level Targeted Partition Aggregation (TPA)** computes a sound lower bound on the minimum poisoning budget required to make a target token the majority vote in an ensemble. The algorithm sorts non-target classes by vote count and simulates the adversary’s optimal redistribution: iteratively equalizing and reducing votes of the top *s* competing classes until the target’s vote exceeds the remaining maximum. The critical value `rt` is derived from the cumulative votes transferred before this threshold. This relies on the worst-case model assumption that one poisoned point can flip any single shard’s vote. Soundness is formally proven in Theorem 4.3, and the mechanism is novel to this work, with no direct corpus precedents.

**Phrase-level certification** extends TPA to multi-token phrases (`m>1`) by having each shard generate an entire phrase autoregressively and aggregating votes over the expanded label space `|V|^m`. The same TPA logic is then applied to phrases, providing tighter guarantees for moderate horizons than sequential token certification while reducing inference latency by a factor of `m`. Proposition 4.6 proves soundness, and ablations (Appendix E) show that for `m=5`, phrase-level validity achieves similar guarantees to token-level with lower computational complexity `O(M*T*C/m)`. The guarantee loosens if phrase diversity is high.

**Multi-turn collective certification** uses a MILP to allocate a global poisoning budget `K` across multiple prompts/turns, maximizing the number of prompts that remain secure. The formulation includes constraints modeling the cost to flip each shard’s prediction on each prompt. This collective certificate is strictly stronger than individual certificates when the budget is insufficient to poison all prompts simultaneously (Theorem 4.8). The MILP adaptation from graph poisoning is novel for sequential NLG, though it assumes uniform adversary interest across prompts and scales poorly with batch size `N`.

## Foundational Learning
**Shard-and-Aggregate (Partition-Based Defenses)**  
*Why needed:* Provides the base certification framework: disjoint shards, independent models, majority voting. The certificate `V1 - V2 >= 2k+1` bounds the poisoning radius under the worst-case single-point-flip assumption.  
*Quick check:* For an ensemble of 7 shards with top two tokens receiving 4 and 2 votes, what is the certified radius `k`? (Answer: `k = floor((4-2-1)/2) = 0`; no robustness.)

**Autoregressive Dependency & Its Certification Challenge**  
*Why needed:* Central challenge: changing token `i` alters the distribution of all subsequent tokens `j>i`. A certificate for token `i+1` assumes a fixed prefix, which an adversary could break by changing earlier tokens.  
*Quick check:* Why can’t we simply apply per-token DPA sequentially and take the minimum radius? (Answer: The sequential minimum is a lower bound but not tight; an adversary with budget sufficient to change token 0 could invalidate the prefix assumption for token 1.)

**Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MILP) for Collective Guarantees**  
*Why needed:* Enables optimal global budget allocation across multiple prompts/turns, yielding a tighter collective certificate than the minimum of individual certificates.  
*Quick check:* In the MILP, what does the indicator constraint `ψ(i)_j = I{a_j >= R_j(x(i))} * (1 + I{f_j(x(i)) in C_s*})` model? (Answer: The number of effective votes the adversary can flip for prompt `i` from shard `j`; 0 if budget `a_j` is insufficient, else 1 or 2 depending on whether the shard’s original vote is in the top competing classes.)

## Architecture Onboarding
Data Sharding Module -> Base Model Training Module -> Inference Aggregation Engine -> Certificate Formatter

**Critical Path**  
Single-turn validity: Sharding -> Train Models -> Generate & Collect Votes -> Run TPA Algorithm -> Output `r_t`.  
Multi-turn collective: Sharding -> Train Models -> Generate & Collect Votes for Batch -> Compute Per-Prompt Parameters -> Build & Solve MILP -> Output `A(K)`.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- Granularity vs. Latency: Token-level is tightest but high latency; phrase-level (`m>1`) reduces latency by factor `m` but may loosen certificates if phrase space is large.  
- Shard Count (`S`) vs. Guarantee vs. Cost: More shards increase certified radius but linearly increase training/inference cost.  
- Fine-Tuning Scope: Full LoRA yields stronger certificates but slower; partial (e.g., Last-3 LoRA) trades robustness for speed.  
- Collective vs. Individual: MILP provides stronger guarantees for batches but adds computational overhead scaling with `N`.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Low vote margins (`V1 - V2` small) yield low `r_t`.  
- High phrase diversity (many distinct `m`-token phrases) causes vote diffusion and weak phrase-level certificates.  
- MILP infeasibility or slow solve indicates batch size `N` too large.  
- Significant clean accuracy drop (e.g., Last-3 LoRA) signals unacceptable utility-robustness tradeoff.  
- Data overlap between shards violates the single-point-flip assumption, invalidating certificates.

**First 3 Experiments**  
1. **Token-level sanity check:** On a small binary classification task framed as token generation, apply TPA and verify `r_t` matches expected DPA formula when sequential dependencies are trivial.  
2. **Phrase-level latency-guarantee ablation:** For fixed `S=50`, measure certificates and latency for `m=1,5,10`; confirm `O(1/m)` latency reduction and analyze tightness tradeoff.  
3. **Multi-turn collective strength test:** For `N=100` prompts, compute individual `r_min` and collective `A(K)`; verify `A(K) >= r_min` and that empirical attacks compromise ≤ `N - floor(N*A(K))` prompts.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**How can we reduce inference-time latency of TPA certification to enable real-time deployment without relying on extensive hardware parallelization?**  
*Basis:* Abstract and Section 5.2 explicitly state latency remains an open challenge; real-time deployment without parallelization is needed.  
*Why unresolved:* TPA requires sequential token voting across many shards, leading to high latency; parallelization is not always feasible.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* New algorithms (e.g., caching, progressive certification) that maintain provable soundness while reducing latency, validated by improved robustness-latency curves.

**How can certified poisoning defenses like TPA be extended to handle other security threat models (e.g., test-time evasion, prompt injection attacks, backdoor triggers) while preserving provable guarantees?**  
*Basis:* Impact Statement identifies test-time evasion, prompt-based attacks, and triggered backdoors as critical open challenges.  
*Why unresolved:* Current framework solely addresses training-time data poisoning; other vectors may bypass or weaken certificates.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Formal extensions of the threat model and robustness definitions for those attacks, with algorithms providing certifications under combined models, supported by theoretical analysis and empirical evaluations.

**What practical techniques can improve the efficiency-robustness trade-off in shard-and-aggregate defenses for language generation, e.g., by adjusting shard size, model fine-tuning scope, or aggregation strategies?**  
*Basis:* Section 5.2 notes efficiency-robustness trade-offs remain an area for improvement; Figure 2b shows Last-3 LoRA reduces robustness for speed.  
*Why unresolved:* Current choices force explicit trade-offs; better configurations could maintain high robustness at lower cost but are unknown.  
*What evidence would resolve it:* Systematic empirical studies exploring hyperparameters (shard count, architectures) and novel aggregation methods, measured by improved robustness-latency curves.

## Limitations
- **Shard independence assumption:** TPA assumes one poisoned point flips exactly one shard; data overlap or correlated initialization invalidates the worst-case model.
- **Phrase space explosion:** For large `m` or high stochasticity, distinct phrases may approach `|V|^m`, causing vote diffusion and loose certificates.
- **MILP uniform interest assumption:** The collective MILP assumes the adversary cares equally about all prompts; targeted attacks on a subset may achieve higher success than certified.

## Confidence
- **Token-level TPA soundness:** High (formal proof in Theorem 4.3 under clear assumptions).
- **Phrase-level tighter guarantees:** Medium (soundness proven; tightness relative to sequential certification is empirical and depends on phrase diversity).
- **MILP collective strength:** High (Theorem 4.8 proves soundness; strict improvement over individual certificates follows from optimization).
- **Empirical effectiveness (e.g., >90% validity under 0.5% poisoning):** Medium (validated on specific setups; broader generalization unverified).

## Next Checks
1. **Shard flip cost validation:** On a small dataset, measure the minimum number of training points needed to change each shard’s prediction on a fixed prompt. Quantify deviation from the single-point-flip worst-case assumption.
2. **Phrase diversity stress test:** For a fixed model and `m`, sample many prompts and collect generated phrases from all shards. Compute average distinct phrases per prompt and correlate with certificate tightness to identify degradation conditions.
3. **Collective certificate tightness:** For batches of size `N` and global budget `K`, compute MILP certificate `A(K)` and run an optimal poisoning attack distributing `K` across shards to maximize compromised prompts. Verify compromised count ≤ `N - floor(N*A(K))` across varied `K` and `N`.
