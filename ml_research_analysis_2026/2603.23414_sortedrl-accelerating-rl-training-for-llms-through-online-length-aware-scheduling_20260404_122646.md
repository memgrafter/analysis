# SortedRL: Accelerating RL Training for LLMs through Online Length-Aware Scheduling

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.23414
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23414
- **Reference Count:** 16
- **Primary Result:** Reduces rollout bubble ratio from 74% to under 6% and improves rollout throughput by up to 39.48%, enabling equivalent performance with 40.74% fewer samples on LLaMA-3.1-8B and 3.9–18.4% higher accuracy on Qwen-2.5-32B math benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
SortedRL tackles a critical bottleneck in reinforcement learning (RL) for large language models (LLMs): inefficient GPU utilization caused by variable-length rollouts. In synchronous RL pipelines, the GPU batch must wait for the longest rollout to complete before training, creating idle "bubble" periods. SortedRL introduces online length-aware scheduling, which prioritizes short responses for early updates within large rollout batches, combined with a stateful controller and cache-based off-policy control. This co-design dramatically reduces idle time, improves throughput, and enhances sample efficiency across logic and math reasoning tasks.

## Method Summary
SortedRL comprises three integrated components: (1) **Online length-aware scheduling**—oversubscribes the rollout engine with more prompts than its capacity, then performs early termination once a sufficient number of short responses complete. Completed and cached partial trajectories are harvested into homogeneous-length batches for the trainer, while longer generations continue. (2) **Controlled off-policy sampling**—offers two modes: *fully on-policy* discards incomplete rollouts to maximize stability, while *partial mode* caches tokens and log-probabilities to resume long rollouts, trading some off-policiness for higher hardware utilization. (3) **Co-designed infrastructure**—a length-aware controller and stateful rollout buffer manage prompt lifecycle, metadata, and scavenged segments, enabling fine-grained orchestration of the rollout-train loop without sacrificing correctness.

## Key Results
- Bubble ratio reduced from 74% to under 6%; rollout throughput increased by up to 39.48%.
- On LLaMA-3.1-8B (LogicRL), achieved equivalent performance with 40.74% fewer samples.
- On Qwen-2.5-32B (math tasks), gained 3.9% to 18.4% higher accuracy across GSM8K, MATH500, Minerva, OlympiadBench, AIME24, and AMC23, with >18% improvement on AIME24.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Length-Aware Early Termination
- **Claim:** Prioritizing short responses for early updates within large rollout batches reduces GPU idle time ("bubbles") while maintaining near-on-policy training.
- **Mechanism:** The controller oversubscribes prompts into the rollout engine, ensuring it operates at peak batch size for kernel efficiency. It applies early termination once a subset of short responses completes, harvesting ready trajectories. These short batches are fed to the trainer immediately, while longer generation continues in the background. This batches length-homogeneous samples, aligning completion times.
- **Core assumption:** Shorter trajectories complete faster; batching by similar length minimizes the synchronization gap where GPUs wait for the longest sample in a batch.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "reorders rollout samples based on output lengths, prioritizing short samples forming groups for early updates... reduces the rollout bubble ratio from 74% to under 6%"
  - [section 3.1] "oversubscription strategy... ensures that the rollout engine consistently operates at its optimal batch size... applie[s] early termination based on batching-related thresholds"
  - [corpus] Neighbor "Beat the long tail: Distribution-Aware Speculative Decoding for RL Training" explicitly identifies long-tail rollout lengths as a primary bottleneck.
- **Break condition:** If length distribution is uniform (no long tail), the sorting yields minimal benefit; bubble reduction would be marginal.

### Mechanism 2: Cache-Based Off-Policy Control
- **Claim:** A cache-based off-policy control allows flexible trade-offs between training stability and throughput by rescuing partial rollouts, avoiding prompt starvation.
- **Mechanism:** In "partial" mode, upon early termination, the controller caches both generated tokens and their log-probabilities. These are concatenated with new generations in the next rollout iteration, preserving exact policy-dependence for importance sampling. This prevents discarded data waste and allows continuation of long prompts.
- **Core assumption:** The bias introduced by mixing older and newer policy log-probs is manageable if cached segments are reused promptly; the stability gain from avoiding prompt starvation outweighs mild off-policiness.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "cache-based mechanism... scavenged segments... concatenate log probabilities used to generate new sequence with the previously cached ones"
  - [section 3.2] "The corresponding log probabilities used to generate the tokens are also cached... to ensure every token can use the exact log probability value"
  - [corpus] Neighbor "Seer: Online Context Learning for Fast Synchronous LLM Reinforcement Learning" also stores intermediate states to resume rollouts.
- **Break condition:** If critical stability thresholds (e.g., PPO clip range) are violated by even small off-policy deviations, partial mode may cause divergence, as hinted by the partial mode's poorer performance on LogicRL.

### Mechanism 3: Stateful Controller and Buffer Co-Design
- **Claim:** Co-designing a stateful controller with a rollout buffer enables fine-grained orchestration of the rollout-train pipeline, making dynamic scheduling feasible at scale.
- **Mechanism:** The controller manages a pool of unconsumed prompts, scavenged partial segments, and their log-probs. The buffer stores metadata (completion flags, lifecycle indicators) for each trajectory. This statefulness allows the system to pause/resume generation, enforce group constraints (no new prompts until all in group complete), and present selective batches to the trainer.
- **Core assumption:** The overhead of maintaining this distributed state is outweighed by gains in MFU and sample efficiency; the controller can make scheduling decisions faster than generation proceeds.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.3] "co-design a compute-efficient rollout controller and a stateful rollout buffer... maintains a set of states... includ[ing] scavenged response segments"
  - [abstract] "supported by a dedicated RL infrastructure that manages rollout and update via a stateful controller and rollout buffer"
  - [corpus] Neighbor "Heddle: A Distributed Orchestration System for Agentic RL Rollout" also proposes a distributed orchestrator for trajectory management.
- **Break condition:** If the controller itself becomes a bottleneck (e.g., network latency in distributed state sync), the benefits diminish.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Autoregressive Generation & Synchronous RL Pipeline**
  - **Why needed here:** SortedRL's core insight relies on the fact that LLM rollout is token-by-token and synchronous with updates. Understanding that each token depends on the previous explains why long tails cause bubbles and why continuous batching (used in serving) cannot be applied naively.
  - **Quick check:** If batch size is fixed but output lengths vary 1k to 16k tokens, why can't GPUs start training the short ones while waiting for the long ones? (Answer: Because RL requires the *same* policy's log-prob for all tokens in a batch for importance sampling; the batch must be collected coherently under a single old policy snapshot.)

- **Concept: On-policy vs. Off-policy in Policy Gradient Methods**
  - **Why needed here:** SortedRL's partial mode intentionally introduces limited off-policiness. Understanding the importance sampling ratio term (πθ/π_old) in PPO/Reinforce++ and its clipping is crucial to see why mixing policies destabilizes training and why "fully on-policy" mode is presented as the cleanest alternative.
  - **Quick check:** In Reinforce++ (Eq. 3), why is it problematic to use a trajectory generated by an older policy (π_θold) to update a much newer policy π_θ? (Answer: The importance weight becomes extreme, gradient estimates are high variance, and clipping may become ineffective, leading to instability.)

- **Concept: Bubble Ratio as a Utilization Metric**
  - **Why needed here:** The primary engineering target is reducing bubble ratio (Eq.4). One must grasp that it measures the fraction of time GPUs are idle waiting for the longest rollout in a synchronized batch, not just absolute throughput.
  - **Quick check:** Given a rollout batch of 8 samples taking [10s, 12s, 15s, 15s, 18s, 20s, 25s, 30s] respectively, and all GPUs are busy during generation, compute the bubble ratio. (Answer: The batch wall time is 30s. Total idle GPU-time = sum(max_time - individual_time) = (0+18+15+15+12+10+5+0)=75 GPU-seconds. With 1 GPU per sample, Q=8, T=30, bubble ratio = 75 / (30*8) = 31.25%.)

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:**  
  Dataloader -> Length-aware controller -> Stateful rollout buffer -> Rollout engine (SGLang/vLLM) -> Trainer  
  (Controller also directly interacts with buffer for caching/log-prob management; Trainer receives batches from controller)

- **Critical path:**
  1. Controller initializes, pulls `n * rollout_batch_size` prompts from dataloader into buffer (group size = n).
  2. Controller feeds the first `rollout_batch_size` prompts to the rollout engine.
  3. Rollout engine generates asynchronously. As prompts complete, results are returned to controller.
  4. Controller applies early termination: if enough short responses are ready, it harvests *all* ready (complete or partial) from current engine batch.
  5. For fully on-policy: incomplete responses are discarded (their prompts scavenged back to buffer). For partial mode: incomplete responses are cached with tokens/log-probs for resumption.
  6. Controller forms a training batch from harvested complete trajectories (and possibly cached segments in partial mode) and sends to trainer.
  7. Trainer updates policy (θ → θ_new). New policy weights are broadcast.
  8. Controller feeds next `rollout_batch_size` prompts from buffer to rollout engine (which now uses new weights). Repeat until buffer empty, then fetch next group.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Group size (n):** Larger n increases length homogeneity (better micro-curriculum) but risks starvating prompts that require long generations. Infinite n catastrophically fails (Fig 6b).
  - **On-policy vs. Partial mode:** Fully on-policy maximizes stability but discards partial rollouts, potentially underutilizing the rollout engine for long prompts. Partial mode rescues work but introduces off-policy gradient noise, harming sample efficiency on sensitive tasks (observed on LogicRL).
  - **Early termination threshold:** Too aggressive harvests very short batches, reducing effective rollout batch size. Too conservative increases bubbles. Tied to group size and length distribution.

- **Failure signatures:**
  1. **Training divergence or collapse in partial mode on sensitive tasks** (e.g., LogicRL Fig 3a): indicates off-policiness beyond tolerable threshold. Remedy: switch to on-policy mode or reduce group size.
  2. **No bubble reduction or throughput gain:** check if rollout engine actually achieves larger effective batch size via oversubscription; ensure controller isn't bottlenecking (e.g., state sync delays).
  3. **Degraded performance with large group size (n=8, inf in Fig 6b):** model sees only short data, fails to learn long reasoning. Group size must be balanced (n=2 worked best).
  4. **Prompt starvation:** certain prompts never complete, stuck in buffer. Monitor buffer lifecycle; may need to adjust group size or termination condition.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Reproduce bubble ratio reduction:** Run end-to-end through 600 steps with fixed workload (512 prompts, max_len=8k) as in section 4.4.1. Measure tokens/s and compute bubble ratio via Eq.4. Expect ~40% throughput uplift in partial mode vs baseline.
  2. **Validate grouping effect:** Log response lengths per training batch. Verify that batches from a single group show clustered lengths and that consecutive groups show a "short-short-short-long" pattern (Fig 9a). Compare variance of batch lengths vs baseline.
  3. **Hyperparameter sensitivity sweep:** Systematically vary group size (n=2,4,8,∞) and mode (on-policy, partial) on a simple dataset (e.g., LogicRL). Track validation score vs steps. Confirm the existence of a "Goldilocks" group size (n=2 for their setup) and the stability of on-policy mode.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- **Optimal length-reward correlation:** Under what precise conditions does response length positively correlate with task performance/reward in RL for LLMs? The paper assumes a length-reward correlation to justify its micro-curriculum but provides conflicting evidence (longer responses tend to but not always have lower performance due to clipping), leaving the exact relationship and its boundaries unclear. Resolution requires systematic experiments varying length constraints and reward functions across tasks.
  
- **Off-policy tolerance threshold:** What is the optimal degree of off-policy training tolerated before sample efficiency degrades? The paper contrasts fully on-policy, partial, and baseline modes, finding performance improves as off-policiness decreases, but only tests discrete modes. The specific trade-off curve between throughput (bubble reduction) and stability (off-policy impact) is not quantified. Resolution requires sweeping over cache retention ratios or allowed update steps per rollout batch.

- **Oversubscription factor sensitivity:** How does the optimal "oversubscription" factor (prompts fed vs. rollout engine capacity) vary across hardware architectures and maximum sequence lengths? SortedRL uses an oversubscription strategy based on "hardware runtime graphs" but provides no sensitivity analysis. The factor likely changes with GPU memory bandwidth, model size, and sequence length. Resolution requires profiling rollout throughput and bubble ratio across a matrix of oversubscription factors on different GPUs and max lengths.

## Limitations
- **Implementation gaps:** Core components (length-aware controller, stateful buffer) are not open-sourced; critical patches (distributed sync, request state retrieval) remain undisclosed.
- **Dataset access:** LogicRL and DAPO-Math-17k are private; re-created versions may have different length distributions and reward structures, affecting reproducibility.
- **Hyperparameter omission:** Key training details (learning rates, PPO epochs, clip epsilon, early-termination thresholds) are missing, hindering faithful replication of accuracy and stability claims.
- **Hardware assumptions:** Results are tied to H100/MI300X clusters with NVLink/InfiniBand; controller overhead could become a bottleneck on other systems.

## Confidence
- **High:** Bubble ratio reduction (74% → <6%) and throughput gains (up to 39.48%) are directly measured and logically follow from length-aware scheduling.
- **Medium:** Sample efficiency claims (40.74% fewer samples on LogicRL; 3.9–18.4% accuracy gains on math) are plausible but sensitive to dataset and hyperparameter choices.
- **Medium:** Cache-based off-policy control trade-off; partial mode shows task-dependent stability, but exact bias tolerance is not formally bounded.
