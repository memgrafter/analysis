# HyFI: Hyperbolic Feature Interpolation for Brain-Vision Alignment
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.22721
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22721
- **Reference count:** 29
- **Primary result:** HyFI achieves SOTA zero-shot brain-to-image retrieval with 68.2% Top-1 on THINGS-EEG (+17.3%) and 35.8% Top-1 on THINGS-MEG (+9.1%) using hyperbolic feature interpolation.

## Executive Summary
HyFI addresses the challenge of aligning brain activity (EEG/MEG) with visual images by tackling two key problems: the modality gap between neural signals and images, and the entanglement of semantic and perceptual features in visual representations. It employs hyperbolic geometry (Lorentz model) to interpolate between semantic features (preserved via fovea blur) and perceptual features (degraded via Gaussian blur) along geodesics. This interpolation yields a fused representation that reflects the limited expressiveness of brain signals. Brain and image embeddings are aligned through a hyperbolic contrastive loss. Evaluated on THINGS-EEG and THINGS-MEG for zero-shot retrieval, HyFI substantially outperforms prior work, achieving large gains in Top‑1 and Top‑5 accuracy. Ablations confirm the importance of hyperbolic interpolation and space, and the framework generalizes across diverse vision backbones and brain encoders.

## Method Summary
HyFI performs zero‑shot brain-to-image retrieval, where a brain response to an unseen concept must retrieve the correct image from 200 candidates. Inputs are preprocessed EEG (17 occipital/parietal channels, 250 Hz) or MEG (271 channels, 200 Hz) with trials averaged, and images augmented with semantic (fovea blur, radius 51, λ = 3) and perceptual (Gaussian blur, radius 31) variants. A frozen vision backbone (e.g., CLIP) extracts features. Two linear heads (W_s, W_p) project these features into the n‑dimensional Lorentz manifold, producing hyperbolic points z_s (semantic) and z_p (perceptual). A coefficient t = σ(W_t f_v(x_s)) determines the interpolation weight. The fused embedding is computed via geodesic interpolation: ẑ = exp_{z_s}( t log_{z_s}(z_p) ). Brain signals pass through a trainable brain encoder (e.g., ShallowNet, EEGNet) whose output is scaled by α_b and lifted to the hyperbolic space as z_b. Finally, a hyperbolic contrastive loss aligns z_b and ẑ, typically using Lorentz distance in an InfoNCE‑like objective. Training uses AdamW (lr = 3e‑4, 50 epochs). For vision backbones that already output hyperbolic embeddings (e.g., MERU), α_v is fixed to 1; otherwise α_v and α_b are learned.

## Key Results
- **THINGS‑EEG:** Top‑1 = 68.2% (↑17.3% vs. prior SOTA), Top‑5 = 84.7% (↑15.2%).
- **THINGS‑MEG:** Top‑1 = 35.8% (↑9.1%), Top‑5 = 57.3% (↑10.5%).
- **Ablations & generalization:** Hyperbolic interpolation and space contribute significantly; HyFI works with multiple CLIP variants, MERU, HyCoCLIP, and various brain encoders.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper does not present a dedicated mechanistic analysis (e.g., probing the learned representations or visualizing the hyperbolic embedding space). Assumption: The hyperbolic geometry's exponential metric may compactly represent the limited information in brain signals, which have lower dimensionality than image features. Geodesic interpolation between semantic (fovea‑blurred) and perceptual (Gaussian‑blurred) image features along curved paths might mimic how brain signals encode a trade‑off between these feature types. Contrastive learning in hyperbolic space using Lorentz distance then aligns the brain embedding with the interpolated image embedding, reducing the modality gap. This is supported by ablations showing that both hyperbolic interpolation and the hyperbolic space itself are critical for the large performance gains. Unknown: The exact way hyperbolic compression matches brain signal expressiveness remains untested (e.g., via representational similarity analysis).

## Foundational Learning
- **Hyperbolic Geometry (Lorentz Model):** Needed to provide a curved space where distances grow exponentially, enabling compact representation and geodesic interpolation. Quick check: Verify Lorentz distance formula and that exp/log operations preserve manifold constraints for small vectors.
- **Geodesic Interpolation:** Blends two points along the shortest path on the manifold, preserving geometric structure. Quick check: For two hyperbolic points with norm < 1, compute t = 0.5; result should lie on the geodesic and have norm < 1.
- **Contrastive Learning:** Aligns positive brain‑image pairs while pushing apart negatives across modalities. Quick check: After training, positive pair distances should be smaller than negative ones on a held‑out batch.
- **Modality Gap:** Brain signals and images live in different spaces; bridging this is essential for retrieval. Quick check: Before alignment, t‑SNE of brain and image embeddings should show distinct clusters.
- **Fovea Blur vs Gaussian Blur:** Decouples semantic (central detail preserved) and perceptual (overall shape degraded) features. Quick check: CLIP similarity between an image and its fovea‑blurred version remains high, while Gaussian‑blurred similarity drops.
- **EEG/MEG Preprocessing:** Channel selection and trial averaging reduce noise and extract reliable neural responses. Quick check: After preprocessing, event‑related potentials show expected patterns over occipital/parietal channels.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Image → Vision backbone (f_v) → Semantic head (W_s) → Lift (exp) → z_s (hyperbolic)
  Image → Vision backbone (f_v) → Perceptual head (W_p) → Lift (exp) → z_p (hyperbolic)
  z_s, z_p → Interpolation: t = σ(W_t f_v(x_s)); ẑ = exp_{z_s}( t * log_{z_s}(z_p) )
  Brain signal → Brain encoder (f_b) → Scale (α_b) → Lift (exp) → z_b (hyperbolic)
  z_b, ẑ → Contrastive loss (based on Lorentz distance)

- **Critical path:** Forward pass from image and brain inputs through respective encoders, hyperbolic lifting, interpolation, and loss computation; backward pass updates all trainable parameters (α_v, α_b, κ, W_s, W_p, W_t, brain encoder).

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  * Fixed vs learned α_v: Fixed α_v=1 for ResNet/hyperbolic VLMs stabilizes training; otherwise α_v is learned to adapt scaling.
  * Hyperbolic dimension must match vision backbone output; mismatches require linear projection that may distort geometry.
  * Interpolation coefficient t derived from f_v(x_s) ties the blend weight to semantic content; other sources could alter behavior.
  * Brain encoder capacity: Simpler models (ShallowNet) generalize better with limited data, while deeper networks risk overfitting.

- **Failure signatures:**
  * NaNs in exp/log indicate norms approaching/exceeding 1 (unstable scaling or large gradients).
  * Top‑1 accuracy near chance (0.5% for 200‑way) suggests pipeline error (e.g., wrong test/train split, loss sign mistake).
  * t values clustered at 0 or 1 imply the coefficient network is not learning to interpolate.
  * Non‑decreasing training loss points to learning rate issues or an incorrect contrastive loss formulation.
  * Embeddings with norms ≥1 violate Lorentz manifold constraints.

- **First three experiments:**
  1. Validate hyperbolic ops: generate small random tangent vectors, apply exp then log, measure reconstruction error and ensure norms remain < 1.
  2. Check augmentation effects on CLIP: for a held‑out image set, compute top‑5 retrieval using original, fovea‑blurred, and Gaussian‑blurred features; confirm fovea blur retains semantics while Gaussian blur degrades perceptual similarity.
  3. Single‑subject smoke test: train HyFI on one subject with 50 concepts for a few epochs; verify loss decreases and test Top‑1 exceeds chance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper does not explicitly list open questions. However, the Limitations section highlights several unresolved issues that could be considered open questions:
- The exact architecture of the brain encoder (e.g., EEGProject) is underspecified.
