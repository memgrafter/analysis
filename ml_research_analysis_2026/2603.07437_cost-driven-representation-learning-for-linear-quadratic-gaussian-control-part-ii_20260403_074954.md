# Cost-Driven Representation Learning for Linear Quadratic Gaussian Control: Part II

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.07437
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07437
- Reference count: 11
- Primary result: Finite-sample guarantee: for a single trajectory of zero‑mean Gaussian controls, with trajectory length T and prediction horizon H sufficiently large, the learned policy satisfies \(J(\pi)-J(\pi^*) = O(\text{poly}(H, d_x, d_y, d_u, \log(T/p))/T)\) with high probability.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles learning a state representation and optimal controller for infinite‑horizon linear quadratic Gaussian (LQG) control with unknown dynamics from partial observations. Using only a single trajectory of zero‑mean Gaussian controls, the authors develop cost‑driven representation learning, where the representation is learned by predicting cumulative control costs. Two algorithms are presented: CoReL‑E, which explicitly learns latent transitions, and CoReL‑I, which implicitly learns dynamics via future cost prediction (MuZero‑style). Both methods follow a three‑stage procedure: (i) recover a linear state representation via quadratic regression on H‑step costs, (ii) identify the latent linear dynamics, and (iii) solve the discrete‑time algebraic Riccati equation (DARE) to obtain an optimal linear feedback policy. The main theoretical contribution is a finite‑sample guarantee: for trajectory length T polynomial in the state, observation, and action dimensions, and for prediction horizon H at least logarithmic in T, the learned policy achieves suboptimality \(J(\pi)-J(\pi^*) = O(\text{poly}(H, \text{dims}, \log(T/p))/T)\) with probability at least \(1-p\). Key technical contributions include a novel persistency‑of‑excitation condition for correlated quadratic regression and an alignment matrix construction that resolves coordinate ambiguity in implicit learning.

## Method Summary
The core method is cost‑driven representation learning. Given a trajectory of observations \(y_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_y}\) and controls \(u_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_u}\), the algorithm first computes the cumulative cost \(C_t\) over a horizon \(H\): \(C_t = \sum_{k=0}^{H-1} (y_{t+k}^T Q y_{t+k} + u_{t+k}^T R u_{t+k})\). Quadratic feature vectors are constructed from \(y_t\), typically \(\text{vec}(y_t y_t^T)\). By performing least‑squares regression of \(C_t\) on these features across the trajectory, one obtains a matrix \(\theta\) from which a linear representation \(L \in \mathbb{R}^{d_y \times d_x}\) is extracted, such that the optimal value function is quadratic in \(L^+ y_t\).

After the representation is recovered, the latent state \(x_t\) is approximated as \(x_t = L^+ y_t\) (using pseudo‑inverse). CoReL‑E then performs linear regression of \(x_{t+1}\) on \([x_t; u_t]\) to estimate the latent transition matrices \((\hat{A}, \hat{B})\). CoReL‑I instead first estimates an alignment matrix \(M\) from cross‑covariances to correct the arbitrary orthogonal ambiguity in \(L\), yielding an adjusted representation \(L' = L M^{-1}\). The latent states using \(L'\) are then regressed as in CoReL‑E to obtain \((\hat{A}, \hat{B})\). Finally, both algorithms solve the DARE with \((\hat{A}, \hat{B}, Q, R)\) to compute the optimal gain \(K\), and deploy the policy \(\pi(y) = K L^+ y\) (or \(K L'^+ y\)). The use of multi‑step costs ensures sufficient excitation for accurate representation recovery.

## Key Results
- Finite‑sample suboptimality guarantee: For trajectory length \(T \geq \text{poly}(d_x,d_y,d_u, \log(T/p))\) and horizon \(H \geq \Omega(\log(\alpha T \log(T/p))/\log(1/\rho))\), with probability at least \(1-p\), the learned policy satisfies \(J(\pi)-J(\pi^*) = O(\text{poly}(H, d_x, d_y, d_u, \log(T/p))/T)\).
- New persistency‑of‑excitation condition for correlated quadratic regression that enables accurate recovery of the state representation from a single trajectory.
- Alignment matrix construction resolves coordinate misalignment in MuZero‑style implicit dynamics learning, allowing CoReL‑I to achieve sample complexity comparable to the explicit method.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The mechanism hinges on the fact that the optimal cost‑to‑go (value function) for LQG is quadratic in the state: \(V(x) = x^T P x\) with \(P\) solving the DARE. Because observations are linear (\(y_t = C^* x_t\)), \(V\) can be expressed as \(y_t^T (C^{*-T} P C^{*-1}) y_t\) under perfect recovery of \(C^*\). The authors observe that the cumulative cost over a horizon \(H\) also becomes a quadratic function of the current latent state under linear dynamics and quadratic stage costs; for sufficiently large \(H\), this cumulative cost approximates \(V\) up to a constant shift. By regressing this cumulative cost against quadratic features of \(y_t\) (i.e., \(y_t y_t^T\)), the least‑squares solution yields a matrix proportional to \(C^{*-T} P C^{*-1}\). This matrix can be factored to recover a linear representation \(L\) such that \(y_t \approx L x_t\). The regression requires the design matrix to be well‑conditioned, which is ensured by a persistency‑of‑excitation (PoE) condition on the control inputs. The authors prove a PoE result for the quadratic features when controls are zero‑mean Gaussian and the horizon \(H\) is at least the state dimension (up to log factors).

For CoReL‑I, the representation recovered by quadratic regression is ambiguous up to an orthogonal transformation because the regression only determines the quadratic form \(L^T Q L\). To align this representation with the true dynamics, they exploit that the cross‑covariance between the predicted cumulative cost and the next‑step observation reveals the misalignment. By constructing an alignment matrix from these covariances, they restore the correct coordinate system after which the latent dynamics can be identified exactly as in CoReL‑E. Finally, with accurate estimates of \(A\) and \(B\), solving the DARE yields an optimal feedback gain that, when applied to the recovered representation, achieves low suboptimality.

## Foundational Learning
- **Cost‑driven representation learning**  
  *Why needed*: Enables learning of state representations directly from control objectives without explicit reconstruction, crucial when only partial observations are available.  
  *Quick check*: Does predicting cumulative costs yield a representation that is linear in the true state?

- **Quadratic regression**  
  *Why needed*: Extracts linear relationships between observations and the quadratic value function; forms the core of representation recovery.  
  *Quick check*: Is the regression matrix persistently excited by the control sequence?

- **Persistency of excitation (PoE)**  
  *Why needed*: Guarantees that the information matrix from the quadratic features has full rank, enabling consistent estimation.  
  *Quick check*: Do the Gaussian controls satisfy the PoE condition for the required horizon?

- **Discrete‑time Algebraic Riccati Equation (DARE)**  
  *Why needed*: Provides the optimal LQR gain given the identified linear system and quadratic cost.  
  *Quick check*: Is the estimated \((\hat{A}, \hat{B})\) stabilizable and \((\hat{A}, Q^{1/2})\) detectable?

- **Coordinate alignment (MuZero‑style)**  
  *Why needed*: Resolves the inherent orthogonal ambiguity in representation learning from scalar costs, ensuring consistent dynamics identification.  
  *Quick check*: Can the alignment matrix be estimated reliably from cross‑covariances?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Raw trajectory \((y_t, u_t)\) → Compute multi‑step cumulative costs \(C_t\) → Build quadratic feature matrix \(Y = \text{vec}(y_t y_t^T)\) → Least‑squares solve \(Y\theta \approx C\) → Extract representation \(L\) → [Branch]  
 CoReL‑E: Regress \(x_{t+1}=L^+ y_{t+1}\) on \([x_t; u_t]\) → \((\hat{A}, \hat{B})\)  
 CoReL‑I: Compute alignment matrix \(M\) from covariances, adjust \(L' = L M^{-1}\), then same regression → \((\hat{A}, \hat{B})\) → Solve DARE → Gain \(K\) → Policy \(\pi(y) = K L^+ y\) (or \(K L'^+ y\))

**Critical path**  
Trajectory → Quadratic regression (representation recovery) → Dynamics identification → DARE solution → Policy deployment. Any failure in regression propagates downstream.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- *Explicit vs. implicit*: CoReL‑E is simpler but sensitive to the quality of \(L^+\); CoReL‑I adds alignment to handle coordinate ambiguity but introduces extra estimation variance.  
- *Horizon \(H\)*: Larger \(H\) improves persistency and reduces bias but reduces effective sample size and increases computational load.  
- *Quadratic features*: Using full \(\text{vec}(y y^T)\) captures all second‑order information but scales as \(d_y^2\); lower‑dimensional alternatives risk information loss.  
- *Dimension \(d_x\)*: Must be known; over‑estimation causes over‑parameterization, under‑estimation loses state information.

**Failure signatures**  
- Ill‑conditioned regression matrix → unstable representation recovery → high policy error.  
- Poor alignment matrix estimate in CoReL‑I → misaligned dynamics → suboptimal or unstable policy.  
- \(H\) too small → PoE fails → inconsistent \(L\).  
- DARE yields non‑stabilizing gain if \(\hat{A}\) is severely inaccurate.  
- Non‑Gaussian controls violate PoE assumptions → representation breakdown.

**First experiments**  
1. *Regression validation*: Generate synthetic data from a known linear system. Run the quadratic regression step and recover \(L\). Verify that \(L^+ y\) accurately reconstructs ground‑truth latent states (high R²) and that \(L\) is consistent up to orthogonal transformation.  
2. *Dynamics identification check*: Using the recovered representation, estimate \((\hat{A}, \hat{B})\) and compare to ground truth. Inspect eigenvalue spectra and condition numbers to confirm stability and accuracy.  
3. *End‑to‑end policy evaluation*: On a small LQG system (e.g., \(d_x=2, d_y=2, d_u=1\)), compute the empirical cost of the learned policy and compare to the optimal cost obtained from the full model with known states. Check that the suboptimality gap is small and scales roughly with \(1/T\).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: To what extent does cost‑driven state representation learning generalize to nonlinear observations or nonlinear system dynamics?
- **Basis in paper**: The paper explicitly states in the Concluding Remarks: "one may wonder about the extent to which cost‑driven state representation learning provably generalizes to nonlinear observations or systems."
- **Why unresolved**: The paper's theoretical framework is restricted to linear dynamics and quadratic costs. Extending to nonlinear settings would require different analytical tools and potentially yield different sample complexity bounds.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Developing finite‑sample guarantees for cost‑driven representation learning in nonlinear LQG or broader control settings, possibly using function approximation and covering arguments.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: How does state representation learning perform with time‑varying observation functions or multiple observation functions (e.g., visual perception from different angles)?
- **Basis in paper**: The paper explicitly states: "it is of practical value to study state representation learning with a time‑varying observation function or multiple observation functions, modeling images taken from different angles."
- **Why unresolved**: The current framework assumes a fixed linear observation model \(C^*\). Real‑world vision‑based control often involves complex, time‑varying observation functions or multiple camera views.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Analysis of cost‑driven representation learning methods under observation models that vary with time or include multiple observation channels, with theoretical guarantees that account for the increased complexity.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Does the recursive form of the representation function (as in Kalman filters) offer advantages over frame stacking in cost‑driven representation learning?
- **Basis in paper**: The paper mentions: "the recursive form of the representation function, as in the Kalman filter, is also used empirically (Ha and Schmidhuber, 2018; Hafner et al., 2019a), and might be worth further investigation."
- **Why unresolved**: The paper uses frame stacking (truncated history) instead of recursive representations, noting the recursive form as an alternative worth studying. The sample complexity and performance differences are unclear.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Comparative analysis of frame‑stacking vs. recursive representations within the cost‑driven framework, both theoretically (finite‑sample bounds) and empirically.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Is multi‑step future cost prediction necessary for coordinate alignment in MuZero‑style implicit dynamics learning, or are there more efficient alternatives?
- **Basis in paper**: The paper notes: "this insight suggests the need to predict multi‑step latent transition or other coordinate alignment procedures in the MuZero‑style, implicit dynamics learning approaches."
- **Why unresolved**: The paper identifies coordinate misalignment as a key issue in their single‑step approach and resolves it with an alignment matrix estimation step. They suggest multi‑step prediction as one solution but don't analyze its sample efficiency.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Compare sample complexity of multi‑step cost prediction vs. the alignment matrix approach, possibly showing that multi‑step prediction reduces variance or eliminates the need for explicit alignment.

### Open Question 5
- **Question**: Can the polynomial dependence on dimensions in the sample complexity bounds for CoReL‑E and CoReL‑I be improved to match those of Markov parameter‑based methods?
- **Basis in paper**: The paper states: "Compared with common system identification methods based on learning the Markov parameters (Oymak and Ozay, 2019; Simchowitz et al., 2019), the error bounds of the system parameters produced by CoReL‑I (or CoReL‑E) have the same dependence on T, but worse dependence on system dimensions."
- **Why unresolved**: The worse dimension dependence is attributed to the difficulty of quadratic regression on scalar costs vs. linear regression on vector observations. Improving this would make cost‑driven methods more sample‑efficient.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Develop tighter analysis techniques for quadratic regression in their setting or new algorithms that achieve better dimension dependence while maintaining task‑relevant representation learning.

## Limitations
- Assumes linear time‑invariant Gaussian dynamics and zero‑mean Gaussian control excitation, restricting applicability to a narrow class of systems.
- Requires prior knowledge of the latent state dimension \(d_x\); robustness to over‑ or under‑estimation is not analyzed.
- No empirical evaluation; practical performance, computational cost, and stability remain unverified.

## Confidence
- Core representation learning principle (predicting cumulative costs): Medium
- Finite‑sample suboptimality bound \(J(\pi)-J(\pi^*) = O(\text{poly}(\cdot)/T)\): Medium
- Persistency‑of‑excitation result for correlated quadratic regression: Medium
- Alignment‑matrix construction for coordinate recovery in MuZero‑style learning: High
