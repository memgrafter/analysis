# EnTaCs: Analyzing the Relationship Between Sentiment and Language Choice in English-Tamil Code-Switching

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.26587
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26587
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Positive English-Tamil code-switched utterances contain a significantly higher proportion of English (34.3%) than negative ones (24.8%), while mixed-sentiment utterances exhibit the highest frequency of language switches; effects are statistically significant (p < 0.05) but explain minimal variance (R² ≈ 0.01–0.02).

## Executive Summary
This study investigates the relationship between utterance sentiment and language choice in English-Tamil code-switched text, using a dataset of 35,650 romanized YouTube comments. A fine-tuned XLM-RoBERTa model performed token-level language identification to compute per-utterance English proportion and switch frequency. Linear regression analysis found that positive sentiment is associated with a higher proportion of English use compared to negative sentiment, and mixed-sentiment utterances contain more language switches. However, the effect sizes are small, with sentiment alone explaining only about 1–2% of the variance in English proportion, suggesting other factors dominate language choice.

## Method Summary
The analysis used the DravidianCodeMix corpus of romanized YouTube comments with utterance-level sentiment labels (positive, negative, mixed). The authors fine-tuned an `xlm-roberta-base` model for token-level language identification (English, Tamil, other/NA) on a manually annotated seed set. After filtering for romanized text and clear sentiment classes, per-utterance metrics—English token proportion and count of language switches—were computed from the model's token predictions. Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) linear regression models then predicted these metrics from sentiment categories, with and without controlling for utterance length.

## Key Results
- Positive utterances have a significantly higher English proportion (34.3%) than negative utterances (24.8%).
- Mixed-sentiment utterances exhibit the highest frequency of language switches, even when controlling for utterance length.
- These effects are statistically significant (p < 0.05) but have very low explanatory power (R² ≈ 0.01–0.02 for proportion models).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Positive sentiment increases the proportion of English (embedded/prestige language) used.
- **Mechanism:** Positive emotional content activates socio-linguistic associations where English is linked to academic/professional prestige in the Tamil Nadu context, making English more accessible and likely during language production.
- **Core assumption:** Bilingual speakers have internalized the societal prestige hierarchy (English > Tamil) and this hierarchy mediates language choice based on emotional valence.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract: "positive utterances exhibit significantly greater English proportion (34.3%) than negative utterances (24.8%)"; Section 5.1 Model 1a coefficients: Negative (β = -0.094, p < 0.001) and Mixed (β = -0.057, p < 0.001) are significantly lower than the Positive reference.
- **Break condition:** If R² remains very low (<0.03) even with extensive covariate controls, the direct predictive power of sentiment on proportion is negligible.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Mixed-sentiment utterances show a higher frequency of language switches.
- **Mechanism:** Ambivalent emotional states create cognitive-linguistic dissonance. The speaker's association of Tamil with intimate/emotional expression and English with detached/prestige content leads to rapid alternation as emotional content shifts.
- **Core assumption:** Intra-discursive code-switching is an unconscious process that reflects internal psychological states, and the affective asymmetry between languages provides distinct "channels."
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract: "mixed-sentiment utterances show the highest language switch frequency"; Section 5.2 Model 2b (with length) shows Mixed_feelings has a significant positive effect on switch count (p < 0.05).
- **Break condition:** If the interaction term with sentence length in Model 2b is not significant, the pattern may be an artifact of utterance structure.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The fine-tuned XLM-RoBERTa model reliably measures language choice in romanized code-switched text.
- **Mechanism:** XLM-R's multilingual pre-training and subword tokenization allow it to learn subtle morphosyntactic and lexical patterns distinguishing romanized Tamil from English, beyond simple script or dictionary matching.
- **Core assumption:** Token-level language identification can be treated as a supervised sequence labeling problem where contextual and morphological cues disambiguate languages sharing a Latin script.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 4.1: Model achieves 93.3% overall accuracy and 0.844 macro-F1; English F1 is 0.932; Tamil F1 is ~0.64. Excluding Tamil-script data was necessary as including it degraded performance.
- **Break condition:** If the Tamil (ta) F1 score is below an acceptable threshold (e.g., <0.55), the resulting metrics become too noisy for reliable comparison.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Intra-discursive vs. Inter-discursive Code-Switching
  - **Why needed:** The study focuses on intra-discursive (token-level) switching, hypothesized as unconscious and affect-driven. Confusing it with inter-discursive (utterance-level, strategic) switching would misinterpret causal mechanisms.
  - **Quick check:** What is the granularity of alternation studied, and how does that inform the interpretation that sentiment influences *unconscious* linguistic behavior?

- **Concept:** Matrix Language vs. Embedded Language (Myers-Scotton)
  - **Why needed:** Tamil is the matrix language (~63% of tokens) and English the embedded language. This theoretical framing is essential for interpreting "English proportion" as marked, prestige-associated embedded language use.
  - **Quick check:** Based on the aggregate data, which language is the matrix language, and why is this designation critical for the sentiment-proportion hypothesis?

- **Concept:** Interpretation of Low R² in Social Science Regression
  - **Why needed:** Models have R² ≈ 0.01–0.02. Understanding that a statistically significant predictor can explain a tiny fraction of variance is crucial; it means sentiment is one of many minor factors.
  - **Quick check:** If the p-value for 'Negative' coefficient is <0.001 but R²=0.01, what does this tell you about practical vs. statistical significance?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Romanized Comments -> XLM-R Token Classification (en/ta/na) -> Metric Aggregation (english_proportion, switch_count) -> Linear Regression (OLS) -> Coefficients, p-values, R²
- **Critical path:**
  1. Filter data: Exclude non-romanized utterances and unclear sentiment classes.
  2. Train LID model: Manually annotate seed set (~500 utterances), fine-tune XLM-R.
  3. Run LID inference: Apply model to all romanized utterances to generate token labels.
  4. Compute metrics: Calculate per-utterance proportion and switch count from token labels.
  5. Perform regression: Build and compare Models 1a/1b and 2a/2b.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Romanized vs. Script:** Excluding Tamil-script data (19%) sacrifices coverage for model compatibility; a script-aware model could unify both but requires different architecture.
  - **LID Model Choice:** XLM-R was chosen for cross-lingual capability; a monolingual Tamil model might capture Tamil patterns better but would fail on pure English tokens.
  - **Manual Annotation:** Using a single annotator with imperfect Tamil knowledge introduces bias, especially for ambiguous tokens (e.g., 'mass'), impacting Tamil F1 score.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **LID Failure:** Validation Tamil F1 < 0.55. Action: Inspect error cases; consider larger annotation effort or different pre-training objective.
  - **Statistical Model Failure:** Residuals show non-normality or heteroscedasticity in Model 2a. Action: Use generalized linear model (e.g., negative binomial) or transformations.
  - **Data Leakage:** Manual annotation for LID training includes tokens from the analysis set. Action: Enforce strict 80/20 utterance split before aggregation.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Reproduce LID performance: Train XLM-R as described; verify validation F1 scores match (macro avg ~0.84, ta F1 ~0.64).
  2. Baseline statistical test: Run Model 1a and Model 2a exactly as specified; verify sign and significance of coefficients for `Negative` (proportion) and `Mixed_feelings` (switches).
  3. Robustness - Sentiment balance: Re-run Model 1a on down-sampled data with equal Positive/Negative/Mixed samples; check stability of `Negative` coefficient magnitude.

## Limitations
- **Low explanatory power:** Sentiment-proportion effect is significant but explains minimal variance (R² ≈ 0.01–0.02), indicating it is a minor factor among many (topic, audience, domain).
- **Measurement validity:** Token-level LID model has suboptimal Tamil F1 (~0.64); since Tamil is the matrix language, errors in Tamil identification directly bias proportion and switch metrics.
- **Corpus & generalizability:** Findings are based on a single, romanized YouTube comment dataset; platform-specific discourse styles and exclusion of non-romanized data (19%) limit cultural/demographic generalizability.

## Confidence
- **Claim 1 (Positive → Higher English Proportion):** Medium. Effect is consistent and significant, but very low R² and potential LID bias in Tamil tokens undermine confidence in magnitude and robustness.
- **Claim 2 (Mixed-Sentiment → Higher Switch Frequency):** Low-Medium. Effect is significant only with length control and is small; risk that length interaction is an artifact of utterance structure.
- **Claim 3 (XLM-R LID Reliability for this Task):** Medium. High English F1 is positive, but sub-optimal Tamil F1 threatens validity of core dependent variables.

## Next Checks
1. Replicate LID performance and conduct error analysis: Strictly follow train/validation split; confirm reported F1 scores (especially `ta`); manually analyze mislabeled Tamil tokens to quantify potential directional bias in proportion estimates.
2. Reproduce baseline models with diagnostics: Run Models 1a and 2a as specified; generate residual plots (residuals vs. fitted, Q-Q) to test OLS assumptions (normality, homoscedasticity); violations would invalidate p-values.
3. Test sentiment class balance robustness: Re-run Model 1a on multiple down-sampled datasets with balanced Positive/Negative/Mixed classes (1:1:1); if `Negative` coefficient changes substantially (>20%), original result is driven by class imbalance.
