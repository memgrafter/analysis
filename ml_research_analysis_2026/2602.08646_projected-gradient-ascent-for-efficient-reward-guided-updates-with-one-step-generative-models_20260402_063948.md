# Projected Gradient Ascent for Efficient Reward-Guided Updates with One-Step Generative Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.08646
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08646
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Introduces projected gradient ascent with hard white Gaussian noise constraints for test-time latent optimization in one-step generative models, achieving comparable target reward with 30% less wall‑clock time than the SOTA regularization method (MPGR) while reliably preventing reward hacking.

## Executive Summary
Test‑time latent optimization for one‑step text‑to‑image generative models aims to maximize a reward model score but often suffers from reward hacking (drift into unrealistic artifacts) and high computational cost due to soft regularization. This work proposes a projected gradient ascent framework that enforces a hard constraint: the latent must remain within a set of white Gaussian noise vectors. This is achieved by a closed‑form projection onto a feasible set defined by blockwise ℓ1 and ℓ2 norms in a compact spectral domain (via FFT). The projection adds negligible overhead (≈0.04% wall‑clock) and guarantees the latent never leaves the prior distribution’s support. On FLUX for Aesthetic Score optimization, the method reaches a target reward of ≈7.12 (comparable to MPGR’s 7.13) in only 30% of the time, while maintaining high scores on held‑out human‑preference metrics (PickScore, HPSv2, ImageReward). Both quantitative and qualitative results demonstrate reliable prevention of reward hacking.

## Method Summary
The task is to optimize a latent vector \(x\) at test time for a one‑step generative model \(G\) (e.g., FLUX, SDXL‑Turbo) such that the generated image \(G(x)\) maximizes a differentiable reward model \(r\). Starting from a standard Gaussian latent, the method performs gradient ascent on \(x\) with respect to \(r\), but after each update it projects \(x\) onto a feasible set \(\mathcal{G}_R\). The set \(\mathcal{G}_R\) contains all vectors that are white Gaussian noise in the spectral domain, enforced via blockwise ℓ1 and ℓ2 norm constraints after a unitary discrete Fourier transform (DFT). The projection is closed‑form: compute FFT, for each block of size \(B\) (e.g., 16) adjust the magnitudes of spectral coefficients to satisfy \(\ell_1 = \sqrt{\pi/2}\,B\) and \(\ell_2 = \sqrt{B}\) while preserving phases, then inverse FFT. The optimization uses Adam with gradient clipping (norm 0.03), a fixed learning rate (0.02 for FLUX, 0.1 for SDXL‑Turbo), and a fixed number of iterations (200 or 50). This hard constraint replaces soft regularization, eliminating the need to tune a regularization weight and ensuring the latent never drifts into regions the generative model was not trained on.

## Key Results
- Comparable target reward on FLUX for Aesthetic Score optimization (≈7.12 vs. 7.13 from MPGR).
- Reduces wall‑clock time to reach target reward by 70% (uses only 30% of MPGR’s time).
- Maintains high scores on held‑out human‑preference reward models (PickScore, HPSv2, ImageReward), indicating no quality degradation.
- Reliably prevents reward hacking across qualitative and quantitative evaluations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core insight is that one‑step generative models are trained with latents drawn from a standard Gaussian prior. Deviating from this prior during test‑time optimization can produce artifacts because the model has never seen such latents during training. Existing methods use soft regularization (e.g., a KL penalty) to keep the latent near the prior, but this is neither strict nor computationally cheap. The proposed hard projection guarantees the latent stays exactly within the set of white Gaussian noise vectors, which is the support of the prior. This is implemented efficiently via spectral constraints: in the frequency domain, white noise has independent, identically distributed Gaussian amplitudes. The blockwise ℓ1 and ℓ2 constraints approximate the distribution of magnitudes of Gaussian coefficients (Rayleigh distribution) and ensure the latent’s power spectrum is flat. The closed‑form projection solves for thresholds that adjust magnitudes to meet these statistical moments, yielding a vector that is provably white Gaussian noise. Because the projection is performed every iteration, the latent never drifts into an invalid region, thus preventing reward hacking. Moreover, the projection’s cost is dominated by FFTs and sorting, both \(O(N \log N)\), making it negligible compared to the cost of multiple forward passes through the reward model and generator (especially when those are cheap one‑step models). The hard constraint also removes the need to tune a regularization coefficient, simplifying deployment.

## Foundational Learning
- **One‑step generative models:** Models that synthesize an image from a latent in a single network forward pass (e.g., FLUX, SDXL‑Turbo). *Why needed:* The optimization is applied at test time to such models; their single‑step nature makes per‑iteration inference cheap. *Quick check:* Load a pretrained one‑step model and generate an image from random noise.
- **Reward models:** Pretrained networks that assign a scalar score to an image, often trained on human preferences (e.g., Aesthetic Score, PickScore). *Why needed:* The optimization objective is to maximize this score. *Quick check:* Pass a generated image through a reward model and verify output shape.
- **Projected gradient ascent:** An optimization algorithm that alternates taking a gradient step and projecting the iterate onto a feasible set. *Why needed:* Enables maximization while staying within the white‑noise constraint set. *Quick check:* Implement a simple projection onto an ℓ2 ball and run a few steps.
- **Spectral domain constraints:** Using the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) to represent the latent and imposing conditions on its magnitude spectrum. *Why needed:* White Gaussian noise is characterized by a flat power spectrum; constraints in this domain are easier to enforce than in the pixel domain. *Quick check:* Compute the FFT of white noise and verify the power spectrum is approximately flat.
- **Blockwise norm constraints:** Enforcing specific ℓ1 and ℓ2 norms on small blocks of the spectral coefficients. *Why needed:* These norms correspond to the moments of the Rayleigh distribution of Gaussian magnitudes, collectively ensuring the spectral coefficients follow a white‑noise statistics. *Quick check:* For a Gaussian vector, compute blockwise ℓ1 and ℓ2; they should be close to \(\sqrt{\pi/2}\,B\) and \(\sqrt{B}\) (with \(B\) block size) in expectation.
- **Closed‑form projection:** An analytic solution to adjust the magnitudes of spectral coefficients to satisfy the blockwise norm constraints. *Why needed:* Makes the projection efficient and deterministic, avoiding iterative solvers. *Quick check:* Implement the threshold‑finding procedure from Appendix D and verify the output satisfies constraints.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Prompt → Initial latent \(x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)\) → Loop (Gradient ascent on \(r(G(x))\) → Gradient clipping → Projection onto \(\mathcal{G}_R\)) → Final latent \(x^*\) → Image \(G(x^*)\) → Primary reward score + held‑out metric evaluation.

**Critical path:**  
1. Initialize \(x\) with standard Gaussian noise.  
2. For each iteration:  
   a. Compute reward \(r = R(G(x))\) and its gradient \(\nabla_x r\).  
   b. Take an ascent step: \(x \leftarrow x + \eta \cdot \text{clip}(\nabla_x r)\).  
   c. Apply projection: \(x \leftarrow \Pi_{\mathcal{G}_R}(x)\) via FFT, blockwise magnitude adjustment, IFFT.  
3. After \(T\) iterations, generate final image and compute metrics.  
The projection step is critical to maintain feasibility; without it, the latent drifts and causes reward hacking.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Hard vs. soft constraints:** Hard projection guarantees feasibility but requires solving a per‑block optimization (still closed‑form). Soft regularization (e.g., MPGR’s KL penalty) is simpler per iteration but needs careful weight tuning and may not fully prevent artifacts.  
- **Block size \(B\):** Smaller \(B\) imposes stricter local constraints, potentially limiting optimization freedom; larger \(B\) is looser. The paper finds \(B=16\) works well for FLUX, but optimal \(B\) may vary with model architecture or reward type.  
- **Computational overhead:** The projection adds FFT (or RFFT) and sorting costs, both \(O(N \log N)\). This is negligible compared to the cost of multiple forward passes through the reward model and generator when those are not trivially cheap. For one‑step models, the overhead is only ≈0.04% of total time.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **Projection degeneracy:** When a block has many equal maximum magnitudes, the threshold \(k^*\) may not satisfy the strict inequality \(w_{k^*} > \lambda \ge w_{k^*+1}\). The paper uses a small perturbation \(\delta\epsilon\) to break ties. *Diagnostic:* Check if blockwise ℓ1/ℓ2 norms after projection match targets within tolerance; if not, apply perturbation.  
- **Gradient explosion/NaNs:** Caused by large learning rates or unclipped gradients. *Diagnostic:* Monitor gradient norm after clipping; ensure it stays bounded (e.g., <0.03).  
- **Reward hacking persists:** Held‑out metrics (PickScore, HPSv2, ImageReward) drop significantly after optimization. *Diagnostic:* Compare these metrics before and after; a large drop indicates the feasible set may not be restrictive enough or projection is not applied correctly.  
- **Loss of white‑noise property:** The optimized latent should remain approximately white (flat power spectrum). *Diagnostic:* Compute the power spectral density of \(x\) after projection; it should be flat.

**Exactly 3 first experiments:**  
1. **Projection correctness test:** Generate a random white Gaussian vector of the correct dimensionality, apply the projection, and verify that every block’s ℓ1 and ℓ2 norms equal the theoretical targets (\(\sqrt{\pi/2}\,B\) and \(\sqrt{B}\)) within numerical precision, and that the power spectrum remains flat.  
2. **FLUX replication:** Using the exact FLUX‑schnell checkpoint and the Aesthetic Score reward model (as used in the paper), run the 200‑iteration optimization with reported hyperparameters (lr=0.02, clip=0.03, B=16). Measure wall‑clock time to reach a reward of 7.0 and compare to a re‑implemented MPGR baseline under identical hardware and software conditions.  
3. **Held‑out robustness test:** After optimization on the animal dataset prompts, evaluate the final model on a held‑out diverse prompt set (e.g., COCO captions). Compute average PickScore, HPSv2, and ImageReward; assert that these do not degrade by more than a small threshold (e.g., 0.5% absolute) compared to images generated from the initial random latents.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Optimal block size \(B\):** What is the optimal block size for different model architectures (e.g., video generators) and reward types (e.g., safety, compositional)? The paper only tests a few values on FLUX for Aesthetic Score.
- **Non‑Gaussian priors:** Do the white Gaussian noise constraints remain effective and non‑restrictive for generative models trained with non‑Gaussian latent priors (e.g., von Mises–Fisher, mixture priors)? The current method assumes a standard Gaussian prior.
- **Sufficiency of hard projection:** Is the hard projection onto \(\mathcal{G}_R\) sufficient to prevent reward hacking for any differentiable reward function, or can a reward exploit allowed spectral configurations to produce subtle artifacts? The paper’s evidence is limited to four human‑preference reward models.

## Limitations
- The method’s reproducibility is hindered by unspecified exact model checkpoints (FLUX‑schnell, SDXL‑Turbo), reward model weights (Aesthetic Score, PickScore, etc.), and prompt datasets (animal, T2I‑Compbench++).
- Claims about preventing reward hacking are demonstrated only on a narrow set of models and rewards; generalizability to out‑of‑distribution prompts or other one‑step architectures (e.g., LCM‑LoRA) is not established.
- Efficiency and overhead figures (e.g., 0.04% added time) depend on specific hardware, software stack, and potential non‑determinism; they may not hold universally.

## Confidence
- **High:** The theoretical projection operator and its \(O(N \log N)\) complexity are explicitly derived and verifiable.
- **Medium:** Achieving a comparable target reward (≈7.12 vs. 7.13) on FLUX for Aesthetic Score; depends on exact model and reward versions.
- **Medium:** Demonstration of reward hacking prevention on the tested models and prompts; evidence is qualitative and based on a limited set of held‑out metrics.
- **Medium:** Claim of 30% wall‑clock time saving versus MPGR; assumes identical implementation details and optimal MPGR configuration.
- **Low:** Broad reliability across diverse generative models, reward functions, and prompt distributions; insufficient stress‑testing.
