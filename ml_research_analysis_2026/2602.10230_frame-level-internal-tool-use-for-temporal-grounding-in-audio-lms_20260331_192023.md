# Frame-Level Internal Tool Use for Temporal Grounding in Audio LMs
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.10230
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10230
- Reference count: 29
- Primary result: Frame-level internal tool use with IHP loss enables >50× faster inference and higher accuracy for temporal grounding in audio LMs.

## Executive Summary
Audio language models (LMs) face challenges in temporal grounding tasks like word alignment and speaker diarization when generating timestamps as text tokens, due to computational cost, hallucinations, and poor generalization to varying audio lengths. This paper introduces frame-level internal tool use, where the model predicts a frame-level probability distribution over its own audio representations. Two losses are explored: a reweighted binary cross-entropy and a novel inhomogeneous Poisson process (IHP) loss. The IHP loss consistently achieves superior performance, yielding a >50× speedup over token-based autoregressive decoding, higher accuracy (e.g., 93.8–98.1% on Librispeech 20ms accuracy vs. 91.4–97.1% for tokens), lower mean absolute deviation, and robust generalization to out-of-distribution durations. This work demonstrates that internal, non-autoregressive tools can efficiently solve temporal grounding within audio LMs.

## Method Summary
The proposed method frames temporal grounding as a framewise binary prediction problem. Given an audio input, the audio LM produces a sequence of frame-level representations (e.g., one per 20ms). An internal tool head—a simple linear layer—maps each frame representation to a scalar logit, which is transformed via a sigmoid to obtain the probability of an event (e.g., word onset) occurring at that frame. During training, the model is supervised with frame-level labels derived from ground-truth timestamps. Two loss functions are considered: (1) a reweighted binary cross-entropy that down-weights negative frames to handle sparsity, and (2) a novel inhomogeneous Poisson process (IHP) loss, which treats event onsets as realizations of an inhomogeneous Poisson process with intensity function equal to the predicted probabilities. The IHP loss maximizes the Poisson log-likelihood of the observed events, naturally accounting for label imbalance and providing a differentiable objective. At inference, the model predicts per-frame probabilities in a single forward pass; event timestamps are extracted by thresholding or peak picking on the intensity curve, avoiding costly autoregressive token generation. This approach shifts timestamp prediction from the LM's output vocabulary to an internal parallel operation.

## Key Results
- Inference speedup >50× compared to token-based autoregressive decoding.
- Improved temporal accuracy: Librispeech 20ms accuracy up to 93.8–98.1% vs. 91.4–97.1% for token-based models.
- Lower mean absolute deviation (MAD) for timestamp prediction.
- Robust generalization to audio durations outside the training distribution, where token-based models degrade.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Frame-level internal tool use succeeds because it bypasses the sequential bottleneck of generating timestamp tokens autoregressively. By predicting a probability for every audio frame in parallel, the model leverages the dense, high-resolution representations already computed by the audio encoder. The inhomogeneous Poisson process (IHP) loss is particularly effective because it directly models the stochastic process of event onsets, accommodating the extreme sparsity of positive frames (typically <1% of frames contain an event). The IHP loss weights each frame according to its contribution to the Poisson likelihood, which automatically balances positive and negative examples without manual reweighting. Moreover, the intensity function estimated by the model can be interpreted as a continuous hazard rate, providing well-calibrated uncertainty and smoothness that aids in precise localization. The reweighted binary cross-entropy offers a simpler alternative but lacks the probabilistic grounding of the Poisson formulation, leading to slightly inferior performance. Overall, the combination of parallel frame-level prediction and a loss that respects the event-driven nature of temporal grounding yields both efficiency and accuracy gains.

## Foundational Learning
- **Frame-level prediction**: Predicting a label for each audio frame (e.g., 20ms) rather than generating discrete tokens. *Why needed*: Enables parallel computation and precise temporal resolution. *Quick check*: Can the model output a probability for every frame without increasing autoregressive steps?
- **Inhomogeneous Poisson process (IHP)**: A point process where events occur with an intensity λ(t) that varies over time. *Why needed*: Naturally models sparse, irregular event onsets with a continuous intensity function. *Quick check*: Does the predicted intensity correlate with event density?
- **Intensity function**: The instantaneous rate of event occurrence in a Poisson process. *Why needed*: Directly predicts where events are likely; serves as the model's output. *Quick check*: Are peaks in the intensity function aligned with ground-truth timestamps?
- **Temporal grounding**: Aligning linguistic or acoustic events with precise time in audio. *Why needed*: Core task for speech recognition, diarization, and audio understanding. *Quick check*: Does the method generalize to different event types (words, speaker changes)?
- **Autoregressive decoding**: Generating tokens sequentially, which is slow and prone to error propagation. *Why needed*: Standard LM approach but inefficient for fine-grained timestamps. *Quick check*: Does removing autoregression improve speed without sacrificing accuracy?
- **Token-based timestamps**: Representing timestamps as special tokens in the LM's output vocabulary. *Why needed*: Naive extension of LM generation to temporal tasks. *Quick check*: Do token-based models struggle with long sequences due to length extrapolation?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Audio Encoder -> Frame Representations -> Internal Tool Head -> Intensity Predictions -> Loss Computation (IHP or reweighted BCE). Optionally, the audio LM's decoder may be bypassed; the internal tool operates directly on encoder outputs.

**Critical path**: The audio signal passes through the encoder producing a sequence of hidden states (one per frame). These states are fed into the internal tool head (a linear layer with activation) to produce logits. During training, logits are used to compute either the IHP loss or reweighted BCE against frame-level labels. At inference, the sigmoid of the logits yields the intensity function; timestamps are extracted by thresholding or peak detection.

**Design tradeoffs**: IHP loss vs. reweighted BCE: IHP offers better modeling of sparse events but introduces a Poisson likelihood computation; BCE is simpler but may require careful class reweighting. Frame-level vs. token-level: frame-level enables parallel decoding and precise alignment but requires frame-level annotations; token-level leverages existing LM training but suffers from sequential decoding and discretization artifacts. Memory vs. duration: frame-level approaches store O(T) values, which can be costly for very long audio; token-level scales better with length due to compression.

**Failure signatures**: 
- Overlapping events: The single intensity head may merge concurrent events, leading to missed detections or merged timestamps.
- Extremely long audio: Memory usage grows linearly with number of frames, potentially causing OOM.
- Non-stationary event rates: If event density varies drastically, the model may underfit rare events without appropriate loss weighting.
- Poor calibration: Intensity values may not reflect true event probability, causing thresholding failures.

**First three experiments**:
1. Compare IHP loss and reweighted BCE on Librispeech word alignment to quantify accuracy (20ms accuracy, MAD) and training stability.
2. Test generalization by evaluating models trained on short utterances (e.g., ≤10s) on longer held-out recordings (e.g., 30s–2min) to assess out-of-distribution duration robustness.
3. Probe overlap handling by evaluating on a diarization benchmark with multi-speaker overlap (e.g., DIHARD) and analyzing per-event detection rates.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: How can frame-level internal tool use be extended to predict temporal intervals (start and end times) for events, rather than single timestamps, to support tasks requiring duration information?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper consistently predicts only onset timestamps (e.g., word start, speaker start, event start) and does not address offset prediction. The prompts and target outputs are single timestamps per event.
- Why unresolved: Many temporal grounding tasks, such as word segmentation, speaker diarization with turn boundaries, or audio event detection, require both start and end times. The current method produces a single intensity distribution, which naturally yields one peak per event; extending to two points per event is non-trivial.
- What evidence would resolve it: Develop a modified IHP framework that outputs two complementary intensity functions (e.g., for start and end) or a joint model for interval endpoints. Train and evaluate on a dataset with span annotations, such as Librispeech word boundaries (both start and end) or AudioSet with onset/offset. Compare accuracy and MAD for interval predictions against baselines.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What explains the consistent superiority of the inhomogeneous Poisson process (IHP) loss over the binary frame-level loss in temporal grounding tasks?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper shows Poisson loss outperforms binary loss across tasks (Table 2) but does not provide an analysis. The authors speculate that IHP better handles label sparsity and fuzzy boundaries, but this remains conjectural.
- Why unresolved: Without understanding the underlying reasons, it is difficult to design improvements or identify when Poisson is beneficial. The binary loss is simpler, so knowing its limitations is key.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conduct controlled experiments with synthetic data where ground-truth event times are known and varied (e.g., different sparsity, fuzziness). Measure how each loss behaves in terms of gradient propagation, optimization landscape, and generalization. Analyze the learned intensity functions from Poisson loss: are they smoother, better calibrated? Compare with other losses (e.g., focal loss, CTC) to isolate the active ingredients.

### Open Question 3
- Question: How does frame-level internal tool use perform on audio with overlapping events, such as multi-speaker conversations with simultaneous speech or polyphonic sound scenes?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The experimental datasets (Librispeech, Libricount, AudioSet) largely feature non-overlapping or sparsely overlapping events. Libricount is synthetically generated with non-overlapping speakers. The method assumes a single intensity distribution per prompt, which may collapse when multiple event instances of the same or different types co-occur.
- Why unresolved: Overlapping events are common in real-world audio. The current approach may fail to separate concurrent events, leading to missed detections or merged timestamps.
- What evidence would resolve it: Evaluate on benchmarks with significant overlap, such as the DIHARD diarization challenge or the AudioSet polyphonic event detection task. Design prompts that specify event types (e.g., "When does speaker A start?") and measure per-event localization accuracy. Explore architectural changes, such as multi-head intensity predictions conditioned on event type, or incorporating attention mechanisms to separate sources.

### Open Question 4
- Question: Can frame-level internal tool use scale to hour-long or longer audio recordings without prohibitive memory or computational costs?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper demonstrates a >50× speedup over token-based generation but does not analyze memory usage or performance on long audio. The frame-level approach requires storing representations and intensity values for all T frames (O(T) memory) and computing the cumulative hazard Λ(T) by summing over T frames (O(T) compute). For very long audio, this may become inefficient.
- Why unresolved: Practical applications (e.g., meeting transcription, lecture analysis) involve long recordings. The method's scalability is an open question.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conduct experiments with audio recordings of varying lengths (e.g., 1 minute to 2 hours) and measure memory consumption, inference time, and accuracy. Investigate approximations such as chunked processing with overlap, downsampling of frame representations, or sparse intensity modeling. Propose and test algorithmic improvements to reduce memory footprint while maintaining accuracy.

## Limitations
- Limited dataset diversity: Evaluation primarily on Librispeech; performance on noisy, multilingual, or music domains is unknown.
- Incomplete baseline descriptions: Insufficient details on token-based and binary-frame baselines hamper fair comparison.
- Training cost not reported: Computational overhead of frame-level training and IHP loss remains unquantified.
