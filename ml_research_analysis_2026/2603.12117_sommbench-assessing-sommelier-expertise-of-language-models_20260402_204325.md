# SommBench: Assessing Sommelier Expertise of Language Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.12117
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12117
- Reference count: 16
- Primary result: Top SommBench Score is 0.65, indicating substantial room for improvement in emulating sommelier expertise.

## Executive Summary
SommBench introduces a benchmark to assess whether large language models (LLMs) can emulate expert-level sommelier judgment, a domain reliant on sensory knowledge and cultural nuance. The benchmark comprises three multilingual tasks testing factual recall, structured attribute inference, and food-wine pairing judgment. Results show that while closed-weights models achieve near-perfect factual accuracy, they struggle with pairing decisions (revealing a positivity bias) and cross-lingual consistency. Open-weights models exhibit significant performance drops in non-English languages, and overall scores peak at 0.65, underscoring the challenge of replicating human sensory expertise through text alone.

## Method Summary
The benchmark was curated by a master sommelier and native validators across eight languages. It includes 1,024 wine theory multiple-choice questions (WTQA), 1,000 wine feature completion instances with masked attributes (WFC), and 1,000 food-wine pairing binary decisions (FWP, English only). Models are evaluated zero-shot with strict output constraints. Metrics: accuracy for WTQA, language-averaged exact match/MAPE for WFC, and Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) for FWP. The SommBench Score aggregates these three metrics.

## Key Results
- Closed-weights models achieve up to 97% accuracy on WTQA but only 65% on WFC and MCC ≤0.39 on FWP.
- Open-weights models peak at SommBench Score 0.51, with performance drops of 20–40 percentage points in non-English languages on WTQA and WFC.
- Food-wine pairing exhibits a pervasive positivity bias (models overpredict “Yes”), and higher reasoning intensity degrades structured output compliance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1:** Textual learning alone is insufficient for models to emulate genuine sommelier sensory judgment for food-wine pairing. LLMs internalize statistical associations from web text, which for food-wine pairing is dominated by positive, promotional recommendations, creating a learned prior to approve pairings rather than simulating nuanced flavor-interaction reasoning. Evidence: The paper hypothesizes a training data skew toward positive pairing mentions; neighbor work confirms the domain’s reliance on multimodal sensory data. Break condition: Models achieve MCC >0.5 on FWP with balanced TPR/TNR (~0.7 each).

**Mechanism 2:** High factual recall (WTQA) does not imply robust, cross-lingually consistent domain expertise. A model’s knowledge of wine facts may be stored in a language-specific manner; in lower-resource languages, activation of the correct representation is weaker, leading to degraded performance. Evidence: Open-weight models show large drops in non-English languages (e.g., llama3.1:8b accuracy fell from 0.70 in English to 0.27 in Slovak). Break condition: Performance on WTQA and WFC becomes statistically invariant across all eight languages.

**Mechanism 3:** The structured output requirement in WFC exposes a model’s ability to perform constrained, language-agnostic inference from partial data. Success requires retrieving a canonical wine fact and mapping it to the target language’s linguistic form while respecting JSON. Failure occurs when the model cannot separate language-independent facts from language-dependent surface forms. Evidence: Open-source models’ performance drops with increased masking and in non-English languages. Break condition: WFC accuracy approaches WTQA accuracy and is equal across languages.

## Foundational Learning

  Why needed: Core research question hinges on whether textual data can substitute for actual sensory experience.  
  Quick check: Can a detailed text description ever fully convey the taste of a “dry, aged Burgundy” to someone who has never tasted wine?

- **Concept: Cross-lingual Concept Alignment in LLMs**  
  Why needed: Explains performance split between closed- and open-weights models; understanding multilingual training data effects on niche domain embeddings.  
  Quick check: If a model knows what a “Sangiovese” grape is in English, is that knowledge automatically and equally accessible when asked in Italian?

- **Concept: Evaluation Metric Selection Bias (MCC vs. Accuracy)**  
  Why needed: Choice of MCC for FWP is critical because accuracy would be misleading due to positivity bias; understanding this reveals the failure mode.  
  Quick check: Why is a model that predicts “yes” for 90% of samples misleading if the dataset is 50/50 balanced?

## Architecture Onboarding
The architecture includes four modules: 1) Curator Module (master sommelier + native validators) produces datasets; 2) Task Engines (prompt templates for each task/language) generate model inputs; 3) Scoring Module computes metrics; 4) Analysis Module breaks down results. Flow: Curator → Task Engines → Scoring → Analysis.

Critical path: Data Curation → Multilingual Validation → Prompt Engineering → Deterministic Inference → Metric Calculation → Cross-lingual Aggregation → Failure Analysis.

Design tradeoffs:
- Single vs Multiple Annotators: Used one sommelier for consistency but limits generalizability.
- Multilingual FWP: Excluded due to translation validation burden; tradeoff depth vs. feasibility.
- Structured vs Generative Output: WFC uses constrained JSON for precise mapping; FWP uses binary to prevent hedging.

Failure signatures:
- Signature A: High WTQA + Low FWP → Memorized facts but lacking pairing judgment; positivity bias.
- Signature B: High EN WTQA/WFC + Low non-EN scores → Cross-lingual inconsistency; knowledge tied to language.
- Signature C: High reasoning intensity degrades WFC JSON output → Cannot follow structural constraints.

First 3 experiments:
1. Baseline Consistency Check: Run a top closed-weight model on all 8 WTQA languages; expect >0.85 accuracy across all; if any language drops >0.15, data validation failed.
2. Masking Stress Test: On WFC, compare single-mask vs. triple-mask subsets; expect >0.2 accuracy drop for triple-mask, confirming compositional inference difficulty.
3. Bias Probe: On FWP, calculate Positivity Ratio (Yes% predictions); if >0.7, model exhibits strong approval bias.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:** How does reliance on a single sommelier’s annotations affect reliability and generalizability, particularly for subjective tasks like food-wine pairing?  
Basis: Future Work proposes diverse panel to measure inter-annotator agreement, acknowledging single-annotator limitation.  
Why unresolved: Benchmark measures alignment with one expert’s potentially idiosyncratic standards, not professional opinion range.  
What evidence: Annotate subset with multiple sommeliers, compute agreement metrics, re-evaluate against consensus.

**Open Question 2:** Can food-wine pairing be made fully multilingual without losing culinary nuance, and how would multilingual recipes change model performance?  
Basis: Future Work prioritizes extending FWP to all languages, noting current English-only due to translation burden.  
Why unresolved: Monolinguality prevents cross-lingual bias analysis; translation may alter semantic nuance.  
What evidence: Develop expertly validated recipe translations in all languages, administer same items, compare model MCC/TPR/TNR across languages.

**Open Question 3:** Does incorporating subjective sensory descriptors into WFC yield more ecologically valid assessment?  
Basis: Future Work suggests enriching WFC with tasting notes; Limitations note current focus on objective attributes.  
Why unresolved: WFC tests factual inference but omits sensory language central to sommelier discourse; models may fail on expressive vocabulary.  
What evidence: Extend WFC with masked sensory fields, evaluate plausibility of generated descriptors, correlate with human expert ratings.

**Open Question 4:** What is the primary driver of the pervasive positivity bias in food-wine pairing predictions—skewed training data or RLHF?  
Basis: Discussion hypothesizes both training data skew and RLHF rewarding agreeableness, but neutral prompt rules out classical sycophancy, leaving cause unresolved.  
Why unresolved: Bias leads to high false positives; disentangling data distribution from alignment effects is needed to mitigate.  
What evidence: Train models on balanced pairing datasets; compare bias in models with/without RLHF; analyze pre-training corpora for wine-food co-mention valence.

**Open Question 5:** Why do open-weight models show severe degradation in non-English languages for wine knowledge tasks?  
Basis: Results show large drops for open-weights but not closed-weights; paper notes knowledge may be tied to linguistic context.  
Why unresolved: Pattern indicates cross-lingual competence gap, but not whether due to training data scarcity, poor transfer, or architectural limits.  
What evidence: Analyze pre-training corpora for wine content across languages; fine-tune on translated corpora; probe wine concept embeddings for language independence.

## Limitations
- Single sommelier curation limits generalizability; inter-rater reliability not measured.
- Food-wine pairing evaluated only in English, restricting cross-lingual bias analysis.
- WFC masking distribution and prompting details not fully documented, risking replication variance.

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (Textual learning insufficient for sensory judgment): Medium confidence. Strong positivity bias evident, but causality inferential.
- Mechanism 2 (High factual recall ≠ cross-lingual consistency): High confidence. Consistent, large performance drops clearly demonstrated.
- Mechanism 3 (Structured inference exposes representation gaps): Medium confidence. Performance degrades with masking, but alternative explanations possible.

## Next Checks
1. Backtranslated multilingual FWP: Translate pairing instances into all eight languages, validate with native sommeliers, re-run models to test bias universality.
2. Sensory augmentation fine-tuning: Fine-tune a model on a corpus of detailed wine tasting notes; measure change in FWP MCC to test if textual sensory data mitigates bias.
3. Cross-lingual wine concept alignment probe: Create bilingual evaluation of wine terminology; correlate alignment scores with per-language WTQA/WFC performance to test language-independent representation hypothesis.
