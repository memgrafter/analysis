# Horizon Reduction as Information Loss in Offline Reinforcement Learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.00831
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.00831
- Reference count: 3
- Primary result: Horizon reduction in offline RL can cause fundamental information loss, making optimal policies statistically indistinguishable from suboptimal ones even with infinite data and perfect function approximation.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the practice of horizon reduction—learning from fixed-length trajectory segments—in offline reinforcement learning. It proves that such a restriction can lead to three distinct failure modes: prefix indistinguishability (initial actions with identical short-horizon data but different returns), objective misspecification (optimizing truncated returns biases policy selection), and offline support/representation aliasing (dataset or representation collapse merges distinct long-term trajectories). The authors introduce *H*-sufficiency as a necessary condition to avoid these failures, demonstrating that the limitations are intrinsic to the reduced-horizon interface and not surmountable by algorithmic improvements alone.

## Method Summary
The paper constructs minimal deterministic MDP counterexamples to isolate each failure mode. For prefix indistinguishability, two initial actions lead to identical *H*-step prefixes but diverge in terminal reward. For objective misspecification, a greedy policy maximizes *H*-step return but incurs a large terminal penalty, while a patient policy avoids it. For support/representation aliasing, two branches are identically represented for the first *H* steps, and the offline dataset lacks disambiguating later states. The analysis assumes a learner with access only to fixed-length segments and evaluates identifiability via distributions over these segments. Reproduction requires implementing these MDPs, generating segment-based datasets, and verifying that horizon-reduced learners fail to distinguish optimal actions.

## Key Results
- Horizon reduction can render optimal and suboptimal policies statistically indistinguishable from fixed-length segment data, even with infinite samples and perfect representation.
- Optimizing truncated returns can select policies that are arbitrarily suboptimal under the true long-horizon objective.
- Offline datasets and learned representations can collapse long-horizon distinctions within reduced windows, causing irrecoverable aliasing.
- *H*-sufficiency is a necessary and sufficient structural condition for safe horizon reduction; violations guarantee failure.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Prefix Indistinguishability
- **Claim:** Horizon reduction can make optimal and suboptimal policies statistically indistinguishable from fixed-length trajectory data.
- **Mechanism:** When the learner observes only trajectory segments of length *H*, two policies that differ only in consequences beyond *H* (e.g., an initial action determining terminal reward) produce identical distributions over all observable segments. Any algorithm evaluating policies based solely on these segments will assign them equal value, preventing identification of the optimal policy.
- **Core assumption:** The learner's decision rule depends only on statistics derived from length-*H* segments; no information about longer-horizon outcomes is accessible or used.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "optimal policies may be statistically indistinguishable from suboptimal ones even with infinite data and perfect function approximation."
  - [section 4.1]: Constructs a deterministic MDP where two initial actions (L and R) yield identical length-*H* prefixes but different final rewards, proving Proposition 1 on prefix indistinguishability.
  - [corpus]: Corpus neighbors show active research on scaling via horizon reduction (e.g., Park et al. 2025, DEAS paper) but do not directly contradict this identifiability failure; most have low thematic relevance (FMR ~0.5) and zero citations, indicating limited prior theoretical treatment of this specific failure mode.
- **Break condition:** The learner has access to trajectory segments longer than *H* that capture the distinguishing consequences (e.g., *H+1* segments that include the terminal outcome).

### Mechanism 2: Objective Misspecification
- **Claim:** Optimizing a truncated return objective (length-*H*) can select a policy that is optimal under the short-horizon objective but arbitrarily suboptimal under the true long-horizon return.
- **Mechanism:** A policy that exploits immediate rewards within the *H*-step window may incur severe penalties beyond the horizon. Since the learning objective ignores post-horizon rewards, it will favor such a policy, creating an inherent misalignment between the optimization target and the desired performance metric.
- **Core assumption:** The learning algorithm explicitly optimizes a truncated return sum (e.g., *J<sub>H</sub>(π)*) instead of the full-horizon return *J(π)*.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: Lists "objective misspecification induced by truncated returns" as a failure mode.
  - [section 4.2]: Presents a minimal MDP (Fig 2) where a "greedy" policy maximizes the *H*-step return but suffers a large terminal penalty (*-M*), while a "patient" policy has lower short-term return but avoids the penalty. Proposition 2 formalizes this violation.
  - [corpus]: Corpus includes papers using short-horizon returns for scaling (e.g., "Horizon Reduction Makes RL Scalable," DEAS) but they do not provide theory ruling out this bias; this paper's counterexample shows the bias can be arbitrarily large.
- **Break condition:** The optimization objective uses returns that adequately capture the full-horizon consequences (e.g., via bootstrapping or longer truncation that includes the penalty).

### Mechanism 3: Offline Support/Representation Aliasing
- **Claim:** In offline settings, dataset support limitations combined with representation aliasing can cause distinct long-term trajectories to appear identical within reduced-horizon windows, making optimal actions unidentifiable.
- **Mechanism:** Even if the environment is fully observable and Markov, the offline dataset might only contain cropped windows that exclude disambiguating states. Furthermore, a learned representation (e.g., from a model or feature extractor) might map distinct states to identical embeddings within the observable window. The learner then receives no signal to differentiate policies that lead to different long-term outcomes.
- **Core assumption:** (a) Training uses fixed-length trajectory segments cropped from longer trajectories, and (b) the state representation collapses distinct states relevant to long-term outcomes into the same feature within the *H*-step window.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: Mentions "offline support/representation aliasing" and that "dataset and representation collapse long-term distinctions."
  - [section 4.3]: Constructs an MDP with two branches (L→u-chain, R→v-chain) that are identically represented for the first *H* steps (ϕ(u<sub>t</sub>)=ϕ(v<sub>t</sub>)). Disambiguating terminal states (u<sub>H+1</sub>, v<sub>H+1</sub>) are never in the dataset windows. Proposition 3 proves indistinguishability.
  - [corpus]: Related work on representation learning in offline RL (e.g., Allen et al. 2021 on Markov abstractions, Ghosh et al. 2021 on partial observability) supports the premise that representations can lose information, but do not address the interaction with horizon reduction specifically.
- **Break condition:** The dataset includes segments long enough to reach disambiguating states, or the representation preserves distinctions needed for long-horizon value differences.

  - **Quick check question:** In the prefix-indistinguishable MDP (Fig 1), why are the distributions over length-*H* segments identical for actions L and R, despite different full returns?

- **Concept: Offline Reinforcement Learning (Offline RL)**
  - **Why needed here:** The core problem is identifiability and objective alignment when learning from a fixed dataset without exploration. The analysis assumes standard offline RL constraints (no new data, distribution shift concerns).
  - **Quick check question:** How does the offline setting exacerbate the failures of horizon reduction compared to an online setting where one could collect longer trajectories?

- **Concept: Horizon Reduction Techniques**
  - **Why needed here:** The paper formalizes horizon reduction as learning from fixed-length segments. Recognizing common instantiations (truncated returns, windowed training, hierarchical decomposition) is key to mapping theory to practice.
  - **Quick check question:** What is the difference between reducing the *value* horizon (n-step returns) and reducing the *policy* horizon (windowed action generation) in terms of the failure modes?

- **Concept: Identifiability in RL**
  - **Why needed here:** The paper's central thesis is that horizon reduction can break identifiability of the optimal policy. Understanding when and why policies are distinguishable from data is crucial.
  - **Quick check question:** Under what conditions does the distribution over *H*-length segments uniquely determine a policy's true return?

- **Concept: Truncated Return Objectives**
  - **Why needed here:** Objective misspecification arises from optimizing truncated returns. Knowing the trade-offs between bias and variance in return estimation is relevant.
  - **Quick check question:** How does the horizon length *H* in a truncated return relate to the effective planning depth of an agent?

- **Concept: Representation Learning in RL**
  - **Why needed here:** The aliasing failure mode involves learned representations collapsing states. Understanding how representations preserve or lose information about long-term outcomes is key.
  - **Quick check question:** What properties must a representation satisfy to support long-horizon value prediction when only short-horizon data is available?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  1. **Learning Interface:** The algorithm's input—fixed-length trajectory segments (states, actions, rewards) of length *H*.
  2. **Dataset (D):** Collection of trajectories, potentially cropped into windows; its support determines which state-action sequences are observable.
  3. **Objective Function:** Could be full return *J(π)* or truncated *J<sub>H</sub>(π)*; the choice determines alignment.
  4. **Representation (ϕ):** Mapping from raw states to learned features; may collapse distinct states.
  5. **Evaluation Metric:** True environment return *J(π)*, which may diverge from the optimized objective.
  - Critical data flow: Dataset (segments of length *H*) → Learning Interface → Policy/Value Function (optimized for *J<sub>H</sub>* or based on segment statistics) → Evaluation (true *J(π)*)

- **Critical path:** Define the MDP and offline dataset → Choose horizon reduction method (segment length *H*, truncation scheme) → Check *H*-sufficiency: Do all policies with different *J(π)* induce different distributions over observable length-*H* segments? → If not, identify which failure mode (prefix indistinguishability, objective misspecification, support/aliasing) is active → Diagnose whether failure is intrinsic (structural) or potentially mitigable (e.g., better dataset, longer *H*).

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Scalability vs. Information Preservation:** Shorter *H* reduces credit assignment length and memory/compute but increases risk of information loss.
  - **Dataset utilization vs. Objective bias:** Using only short segments may fit limited offline data better but optimize the wrong objective.
  - **Representation capacity vs. Aliasing:** Powerful representations might abstract away irrelevant details but risk losing long-horizon distinctions if trained on short windows.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - Policies with identical empirical value on length-*H* segments but divergent full-horizon returns.
  - A policy that maximizes the truncated return performs poorly on the true return, especially when *H* is small relative to delay of critical consequences.
  - Two state sequences that are semantically different (different long-term outcomes) have identical embeddings in the learned representation within the *H*-step window.

  1. **Prefix indistinguishability test:** Implement the MDP from Fig 1. Generate an offline dataset with both L and R at s<sub>0</sub>. Train any horizon-reduced algorithm (e.g., transformer on fixed-length windows). Check if it assigns equal value to L and R.
  2. **Objective misalignment test:** Implement the MDP from Fig 2. For varying *H* and penalty *M*, compare the policy that maximizes *J<sub>H</sub>* (likely greedy) vs. the optimal *J* policy (patient). Measure suboptimality gap as *M* increases.
  3. **Support/aliasing test:** Implement the MDP from Fig 3. Create a dataset containing only windows starting after s<sub>0</sub> (so no u<sub>H+1</sub>/v<sub>H+1</sub>). Learn a representation (e.g., autoencoder) that sees only the first *H* states of each branch. Test if the learned policy can distinguish L from R.

## Limitations
- The analysis relies on minimal deterministic MDPs; it's unclear how the failure modes manifest in larger, stochastic, or continuous domains.
- The *H*-sufficiency condition is theoretically necessary but may be difficult to verify in practice for complex environments.
- The paper assumes perfect function approximation; with limited models, the effects could be compounded or possibly masked.
- Exact thresholds for safe horizon reduction (how long *H* must be relative to the true horizon) are not provided.
- The role of the offline dataset distribution: the support/aliasing mode assumes a specific cropping; other dataset constructions might mitigate or exacerbate the issue.

## Confidence
- *Prefix indistinguishability*: High – proven via simple deterministic MDP; logic is straightforward.
- *Objective misspecification (truncated return bias)*: High – counterexample clearly demonstrates arbitrarily large suboptimality.
- *Offline support/representation aliasing*: Medium – depends on specific representation collapse; plausible but may be avoidable with careful representation learning.
- *H-sufficiency as a necessary condition for safe reduction*: High – derived from the identifiability argument; any algorithm using only H-length segments cannot distinguish policies that violate H-sufficiency.
- *Claim that failures are intrinsic and not solvable by algorithmic advances alone*: Medium – holds under the strict interface restriction; relaxing the interface (e.g., using longer segments or bootstrapping) can circumvent, but within the reduced-horizon paradigm it is fundamental.
