# KUET at StanceNakba Shared Task: StanceMoE: Mixture-of-Experts Architecture for Stance Detection

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2604.00878
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00878
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Macro-F1 score of **94.26%** on the StanceNakba 2026 Subtask A dataset (1,401 English texts).

## Executive Summary
StanceMoE is a Mixture-of-Experts architecture designed for actor-level stance detection on implicit geopolitical targets. Standard transformer models like BERT struggle to capture the heterogeneous linguistic cues—such as contrastive discourse, framing indicators, and salient lexical tokens—required for this nuanced task. StanceMoE addresses this by deploying six parallel expert modules, each tailored to extract a specific type of cue, and a context-aware gating network that dynamically fuses the expert outputs based on the input. Evaluated on the StanceNakba 2026 Subtask A dataset, the model achieves a macro-F1 of 94.26%, significantly outperforming BERT-based baselines (89.86%) and a non-adaptive fusion baseline (91.17%) with stable performance (cross-validation variance ~±1.1%). The results demonstrate the effectiveness of modular, adaptive architectures for complex stance detection.

## Method Summary
StanceMoE builds on a fine-tuned BERT encoder (`bert-base-uncased`). The input text is tokenized, and the model obtains token embeddings `H` and the `[CLS]` representation `h_cls`. Six expert modules process `H` in parallel: mean pooling (global semantics), max pooling (salient polarity tokens), self-attention pooling (clause-level focus), multi-kernel CNN (phrase patterns), lexical cue masking (framing/reporting cues), and contrast token emphasis (contrastive shifts). A gating network takes `h_cls` and produces softmax weights over the experts. The fused representation is the weighted sum of expert outputs. This vector feeds into a linear classification head for the three stance classes (Pro-Palestine, Pro-Israel, Neutral). Training uses cross-entropy loss with label smoothing (0.25) and stratified 10-fold cross-validation on the training set. At inference, predictions are obtained by ensembling the fold models via weighted logit averaging, where weights are proportional to each fold's validation macro-F1.

## Key Results
- **Macro-F1:** 94.26% on the official test set.
- **Baseline gap:** Outperforms fine-tuned BERT (89.86% F1) by +4.4 points and static fusion (91.17% F1) by +3.09 points.
- **Stability:** Cross-validation variance of ~±1.1% indicates consistent performance across folds.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Heterogeneous signal specialization.** Unified transformer representations (e.g., BERT) may not capture the heterogeneous linguistic signals required for nuanced actor-level stance detection, especially with implicit targets. StanceMoE decomposes the stance representation into six parallel expert modules, each explicitly designed to extract a specific signal: global semantics (mean pooling), salient polarity tokens (max pooling), clause-level focus (self-attention pooling), phrase patterns (multi-kernel CNN), framing/reporting cues (lexical cue mask), and contrastive discourse shifts (contrast token emphasis). The core assumption is that stance expressions are linguistically diverse; no single aggregated embedding can optimally capture all cues. Evidence includes the abstract’s statement about unified representations’ insufficiency, Section 3.2’s design rationale and examples (e.g., “Stand with Israel” for CNN, “but” for contrast), and supporting emphasis on these signals in neighboring work. A break condition: if the domain exhibits only homogeneous expressions (e.g., short polarized slogans), the expert overhead may not yield benefits and could increase overfitting on small datasets.

**Mechanism 2: Adaptive gating for input‑dependent fusion.** Static or equally‑weighted fusion of heterogeneous expert outputs is suboptimal because the relevance of each expert varies per input. StanceMoE employs a context‑aware gating network that takes the `[CLS]` token from BERT and produces a softmax weight vector over the six experts. The final representation is a weighted sum (`h_moe = Σ g_i e_i`), allowing the model to amplify experts relevant to the current input’s discourse structure. The core assumption is that the optimal combination of stance signals differs across texts and the `[CLS]` token contains sufficient context to predict it. Evidence: Section 3.3 describes the gating mechanism; Table 1 shows StanceMoE (94.26% F1) significantly outperforming the “Fusion” baseline without adaptive weighting (91.17% F1). A break condition: if the task required a fixed, predictable combination (e.g., equal weight for all experts), the learned gating would converge to uniform weights, rendering its parameters superfluous and potentially destabilizing training.

**Mechanism 3: Complementarity of experts.** The six expert signals are complementary; their combined, adaptively weighted integration yields a supra‑additive performance gain. Through joint training, the model learns to activate different experts for different inputs while the gating learns to weight their contributions. The ablation study (removing one expert at a time) measures the marginal contribution of each signal type. The core assumption is that the cues targeted by the six experts cover largely non‑overlapping aspects of stance expression, such that losing any one degrades overall performance. Evidence: Table 2 shows performance drops for all classes when any single expert is removed, with the largest drops for clause‑focus (self‑attention) and contrastive experts, indicating their unique importance. Section 5.2 explicitly states that removing any expert degrades performance, confirming complementarity. A break condition: if two or more experts were highly correlated in activation patterns across the dataset, their contributions would be redundant; removing one would not significantly harm performance, contradicting the complementarity assumption.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Raw text → BERT encoder → `[CLS]` and token embeddings `H` → Six parallel expert modules + gating network → Weighted sum (fused vector) → Linear classification layer → Stance label. Training: cross‑entropy with label smoothing (0.25), stratified 10‑fold CV; inference: ensemble via weighted logit averaging.

**Critical path:**  
`Text → BERT → [CLS] & H → (Experts + Gating) → Fused Vector → Linear Classifier → Prediction`. The gating computation depends on `[CLS]`, which is the first dependency in the routing.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Expert count vs. complexity:* Six experts increase parameters and inference time but yield higher accuracy (94.26% vs 89.86% for a single BERT head).
- *Gating flexibility vs. stability:* A flexible gating could overfit on small datasets (1,401 texts), yet the paper reports low CV variance (~±1.1%), suggesting robust learning.
- *Hand‑crafted vs. learned experts:* Predefined operations encode linguistic priors but may miss other patterns; a fully learned approach could adapt better but loses interpretability.

**Failure signatures:**
- *Ablation:* Removing any single expert causes >2% F1 drop in some cases, indicating sensitivity and potential over‑reliance on certain experts.
- *Per‑class imbalance:* Certain experts (e.g., lexical‑cue) are critical for specific classes (Pro‑Palestine); their removal disproportionately harms those classes, revealing uneven cue coverage.
- *Error analysis:* Misclassifications on texts with religious praise or meta‑discursive commentary suggest experts cannot disentangle lexical polarity from evaluative stance toward the implicit target.

**First 3 experiments (reproduction steps):**
1. **Reproduce BERT baseline:** Fine‑tune `bert-base-uncased` on the StanceNakba train+dev set using LR 5e‑5, batch 16, 10 epochs, label smoothing 0.25. Expected macro‑F1 ≈ 89.86% on test.
2. **Single‑Expert MoE:** Replace the six‑expert layer with a single expert (e.g., mean pooling) while keeping gating and fusion. This isolates whether diversity drives gains beyond gating. Expected performance near the single expert’s capability, likely below full BERT baseline.
3. **Fixed vs. Adaptive Fusion:** Train the full six‑expert model but freeze gating weights to uniform (1/6) or random static values. Compare to the adaptive version; a significant drop toward the static fusion baseline (91.17% F1) would confirm the necessity of adaptive routing.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can StanceMoE be modified to explicitly incorporate target‑aware representations for improved detection of implicit actor references?
2. Does StanceMoE maintain high performance when applied to cross‑topic or cross‑domain geopolitical stance detection beyond the StanceNakba dataset?
3. Can the Mixture‑of‑Experts architecture in StanceMoE be effectively adapted to multilingual stance detection without significant performance drops?
4. What enhancements to the contrastive discourse expert are necessary to better capture nuanced stance shifts in multi‑clause arguments?
5. Is the `[CLS]`‑based gating mechanism optimal for weighting expert contributions across diverse input types, or could alternative gating inputs improve adaptive fusion?

## Limitations
- The StanceNakba 2026 dataset is not publicly available, preventing external verification.
- Several architectural hyperparameters (e.g., expert output dimensions, exact cue lexicons, CNN kernel sizes) are not specified, making exact replication difficult.
- The small dataset size (1,401 texts) raises concerns about overfitting, even though the paper reports low cross‑validation variance (~±1.1%).

## Next Checks
1. **Capacity control:** Compare the full StanceMoE against a single‑expert MoE with matched total parameters to ensure gains stem from expert diversity rather than increased capacity.
2. **Gating freeze:** Freeze the gating network to uniform weights (or random) and evaluate; a significant performance drop would quantify the contribution of adaptive routing.
3. **Activation profiling:** Correlate the learned gating weights with the presence of specific linguistic cues (e.g., contrast markers, framing verbs) on a held‑out set to validate that the gating assigns higher weights to the intended experts for each cue type.
