# FedDES: Graph-Based Dynamic Ensemble Selection for Personalized Federated Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.28006  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.28006  
- **Reference count**: 39  
- **Primary result**: On CIFAR-10 non‑IID splits, FedDES outperforms the next‑best method by up to 4.7 percentage points; on eICU clinical data, it improves balanced accuracy by 4.6–7.5 points on mortality and shock prediction.

## Executive Summary
FedDES addresses negative transfer in personalized federated learning by dynamically selecting and weighting peer models at the instance level. It constructs a heterogeneous graph of samples and classifiers, uses a graph neural network meta‑learner to predict per‑sample competence, and aggregates only the most competent classifiers’ votes. Designed for complete model heterogeneity, it exchanges classifiers peer‑to‑peer and aggregates predictions in the output space. Empirical results on CIFAR‑10 across various non‑IID settings and on the real‑world eICU dataset show consistent and significant improvements over state‑of‑the‑art personalized federated learning baselines.

In the second stage, each client calibrates all received classifiers via temperature scaling on its validation set, then builds a decision‑space representation by concatenating the calibrated probabilities of all classifiers for each training sample. Using this representation, the client constructs a heterogeneous graph: samples are nodes with raw features, classifiers are nodes with statistics (e.g., per‑class recall, confidence, accuracy), edges connect each sample to its *k* = 5 nearest neighbors per class in decision space (weighted by Cumulative Mean Distance) and to the top *k_c* = 3 classifiers based on a gain score measuring local improvement over the pool average. The graph is fed into a two‑layer heterogeneous GATv2 that outputs a binary competence label for every sample–classifier pair, trained with binary cross‑entropy against whether the classifier correctly predicted the sample. In the third stage, for a test query the client inserts it into the graph (finding neighbors and computing classifier‑sample edges), runs the trained GNN, applies a sigmoid to obtain competence scores, selects classifiers with scores > 0.5, normalizes the scores, and aggregates their hard votes to produce the final prediction.

## Method Summary
FedDES is a three‑stage framework for personalized federated learning. **Stage 1 (Classifier Exchange)**: Clients locally train heterogeneous classifiers and exchange them via peer‑to‑peer communication, building a shared pool of models. **Stage 2 (Graph Construction)**: Each client calibrates all received classifiers using temperature scaling on its validation set, then creates a decision‑space matrix by concatenating the calibrated class probabilities for every training sample. Using this matrix, the client constructs a heterogeneous graph: sample nodes are connected by class‑stratified *k*-nearest neighbors, and classifier nodes are linked to the top *k_c* samples based on a gain score that measures local improvement over the pool average. **Stage 3 (GNN Inference)**: A two‑layer heterogeneous GATv2 meta‑learner is trained to predict a binary competence label for each sample‑classifier pair (correct or incorrect prediction). During inference, a query is inserted into the graph, the GNN outputs competence scores, classifiers with scores > 0.5 are selected and weighted by their normalized scores, and their hard votes are aggregated to produce the final prediction.

## Key Results
- Achieved the highest mean accuracy across all six CIFAR‑10 non‑IID configurations, with win rates 80–95% over local baselines.
- On eICU, improved balanced accuracy by 4.6–7.5 points on mortality and shock prediction tasks.
- Consistently outperformed state‑of‑the‑art personalized federated learning methods.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Instance‑level dynamic ensemble selection
- **Claim**: Instance‑level dynamic ensemble selection mitigates negative transfer by tailoring model contributions to individual samples.
- **Mechanism**: A GNN meta‑learner processes a heterogeneous graph of samples and classifiers. For a query sample, it outputs competence scores for each classifier, selecting only those with scores > 0.5 and weighting their contributions. This prevents harmful peers from influencing predictions on samples where they are incompetent.
- **Core assumption**: Classifier competence varies across the sample space; a classifier useful for one sample may be detrimental for another, even within the same client’s data.
- **Evidence anchors**: 
  - Abstract: “For each test query, the GNN dynamically selects and weights peer client models…”
  - Section 3.3: Competence scores from the GNN determine selection (scores > 0.5) and voting weight.
  - Corpus: Neighbors focus on client‑level personalization or aggregation, not instance‑level selection.
- **Break condition**: If decision‑space representations fail to distinguish sample‑specific classifier behavior (e.g., due to severe calibration error or homogeneous data), the GNN cannot learn meaningful competence patterns, leading to selection no better than random or uniform.

### Mechanism 2: Graph construction using decision‑space similarity and local competence edges
- **Claim**: Graph construction using decision‑space similarity and local competence edges provides an effective structure for the GNN to learn sample‑classifier relationships.
- **Mechanism**: Samples are connected via *k*-NN in decision space (class‑stratified) to define regions of competence. Classifiers are connected to samples based on a gain score measuring local accuracy relative to the pool average. Message passing over this graph allows information flow between similar samples and their locally competent classifiers.
- **Core assumption**: Similar samples (in classifier output space) share similar optimal classifier sets, and a classifier’s performance on neighboring samples predicts its competence on the query.
- **Evidence anchors**: 
  - Section 3.2: Edge construction details – sample‑sample edges are class‑aware; classifier‑sample edges use gain score and top‑*k* selection.
  - Section 2.2: Dynamic ensemble selection standard uses *k*-NN in feature/decision space.
  - Corpus: Related work uses client affinity or attention, not sample‑classifier graphs.
- **Break condition**: If the decision space is poorly discriminative (e.g., all classifiers agree on most samples) or the gain score fails to identify true local experts (e.g., due to noisy labels or small neighbor sets), the graph structure becomes uninformative.

### Mechanism 3: Decentralized model exchange with output‑space aggregation
- **Claim**: Decentralized model exchange with output‑space aggregation enables model heterogeneity without architectural constraints.
- **Mechanism**: Clients independently train heterogeneous classifiers and exchange them via peer‑to‑peer. During inference, predictions are aggregated in the output space (label probabilities), requiring only that all classifiers output over a common label set. No parameter‑space alignment is needed.
- **Core assumption**: Models with different architectures trained on different distributions can produce meaningful, comparable probability outputs after calibration, and their ensemble can outperform any individual.
- **Evidence anchors**: 
  - Introduction: “By aggregating client contributions in the output space (via ensembles) rather than the parameter space, FedDES supports complete model heterogeneity.”
  - Section 3.1: “Clients exchange their classifiers via P2P communication…each locally retains a copy of the full pool.”
  - Section 4: eICU experiments use 3 different sequence model architectures per client; CIFAR‑10 uses 4 CNN architectures across clients.
- **Break condition**: If calibration (temperature scaling) cannot align confidence scales across highly heterogeneous models, or if label sets are incompatible, output‑space aggregation becomes unreliable.

## Foundational Learning
- **Dynamic Ensemble Selection (DES)**: DES selects a subset of classifiers for each test sample based on a region of competence. In FedDES, this region is defined via a graph of samples and classifiers. *Why needed*: Understanding DES basics clarifies how FedDES extends traditional DES with a GNN and federated settings. *Quick check*: What is the typical mechanism for defining a region of competence in traditional DES, and what key limitation does it aim to address?
- **Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) for Heterogeneous Graphs**: FedDES’s meta‑learner is a heterogeneous GATv2 that processes a graph with two node types (samples, classifiers) and two edge types. Message passing aggregates information over both sample‑sample and classifier‑sample edges with attention. *Why needed*: The GNN’s ability to propagate information between similar samples and competent classifiers is central to competence estimation. *Quick check*: In a heterogeneous GNN, how does the aggregation differ for each edge type?
- **Model Calibration (Temperature Scaling)**: Since classifiers come from different clients and architectures, their output confidences are uncalibrated. Temperature scaling aligns these confidences, making the decision‑space representation (concatenated probabilities) meaningful and comparable. *Why needed*: Calibration ensures that distances in decision space reflect true classifier performance. *Quick check*: Why is temperature scaling applied to all received models before constructing the decision‑space matrix?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
Base Classifier Pool -> Decision-Space Constructor -> Graph Builder -> GNN Meta-Learner -> Inference Engine

**Critical path**  
The most critical implementation flow is: (1) train and exchange heterogeneous base classifiers, (2) calibrate models and construct the decision‑space matrix, (3) build the heterogeneous graph with class‑stratified *k*-NN and gain‑based edges, (4) train the heterogeneous GATv2 meta‑learner, and (5) perform inference by inserting queries, running the GNN, thresholding competence scores, and aggregating selected classifiers’ votes.

**Design tradeoffs**
- *k*-NN per class vs. overall: Class‑stratified ensures minority class representation but may introduce noisy distant neighbors.
- Gain score vs. raw accuracy: Gain highlights classifiers that excel locally, promoting complementary expertise; raw accuracy favors generally strong models and may miss niche experts.
- Fixed vs. adaptive ensemble size: The 0.5 threshold yields a variable ensemble size that adapts to the score distribution; a fixed top‑*t* would sacrifice that adaptivity.
- GATv2 vs. other GNNs: GATv2’s dynamic attention is essential because neighbor importance depends on the query node; static attention would not capture sample‑specific relevance.

**Failure signatures**
- Win rate near 50% vs. local baseline → GNN fails to learn competence patterns.
- Ensemble size always close to total number of classifiers → competence scores are uninformative.
- High Expected Calibration Error (ECE) after temperature scaling → decision‑space distances are unreliable.
- Large performance variance across clients → possible overfitting to local patterns or bias toward home client models.

**First 3 experiments**
1. **Graph edge sanity check**: Visualize constructed graphs for a held‑out set; verify that most *k*-NN neighbors (per class) share the true label and that top‑connected classifiers achieve high accuracy on those neighbors.
2. **Meta‑learner proxy task**: Train the GNN on synthetic data where true classifier competence is known; confirm it learns to assign higher scores to correct oracles.
3. **Threshold sensitivity**: Evaluate performance across selection thresholds (0.3, 0.5, 0.7) on validation data; a clear peak near 0.5 indicates well‑calibrated competence scores.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Scalability**: How does FedDES scale with increasing federation size, sample size per client, and number of candidate classifiers in terms of computational and communication overhead? The paper uses fixed neighborhoods and provides no scalability analysis beyond 20–50 clients.
2. **Theoretical guarantees**: What are the convergence properties and generalization bounds for FedDES’s GNN‑based dynamic ensemble selection under heterogeneous data distributions? No theoretical analysis is provided.
3. **Modality generalization**: Can FedDES generalize to diverse data modalities beyond images and time‑series clinical data, such as text, audio, or graph‑structured data? Experiments are limited to CIFAR‑10 and eICU.
4. **Heterogeneity limits**: What are the limits of model architecture heterogeneity that FedDES can tolerate before performance degrades? The framework assumes common label sets and comparable probability outputs; the boundary conditions are unexplored.

## Limitations
- Temperature scaling details (optimizer, search range, validation usage) are unspecified, risking poor calibration and distorted decision‑space geometry.
- Graph construction and inference details (sample node features, edge directionality, GNN message passing, query insertion strategy) are ambiguous, hindering replication and scalability analysis.
- The method relies on a discriminative decision space; if base classifiers are highly correlated or mis‑calibrated, the graph becomes uninformative and selection fails.

## Next Checks
1. **Calibration audit**: Compute Expected Calibration Error (ECE) for each base classifier after temperature scaling on a validation set. If average ECE > 0.1, refine temperature search (grid over [0.1, 5.0]) and re‑evaluate decision‑space neighbor consistency (same‑class rate).
2. **Graph plausibility check**: For a held‑out set, verify that >70% of the *k*-NN neighbors (per class) share the true label and that the top‑3 connected classifiers achieve >80% accuracy on those neighbors. If not, adjust neighbor selection or gain computation.
3. **Threshold sensitivity test**: Vary the selection threshold (0.3, 0.5, 0.7) on validation data; performance should peak around 0.5. A flat or monotonic curve indicates miscalibrated competence scores or lack of discriminative power.
