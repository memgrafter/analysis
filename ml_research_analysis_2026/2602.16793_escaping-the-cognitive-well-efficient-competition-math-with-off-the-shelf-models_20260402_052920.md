# Escaping the Cognitive Well: Efficient Competition Math with Off-the-Shelf Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.16793
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16793
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Achieves 67.1% success on IMO-ProofBench Advanced with Gemini 3.0 Pro at $31 per question, doubling the next best pipeline's success rate while reducing cost by an order of magnitude.

## Executive Summary
Solver-grader pipelines for IMO-style math problems suffer from "Cognitive Wells" — a failure mode where iterative refinement converges to incorrect solutions that both solver and internal grader falsely accept as correct due to shared context. This paper introduces a pipeline using off-the-shelf models that escapes such wells by extracting load-bearing conjectures from stalled proofs and verifying each conjecture (and its negation) independently in fresh contexts. On the IMO-ProofBench Advanced benchmark, the pipeline achieves state-of-the-art performance (67.1% with Gemini 3.0 Pro) at an average cost of $31 per question, more than doubling the success rate of prior publicly accessible systems while cutting costs by over tenfold.

## Method Summary
The proposed three-phase inference pipeline balances exploration and verification. Phase I runs multiple parallel solver-grader instances to generate candidate solutions. If no perfect solution emerges, Phase II uses a Conjecture Extractor to identify key logical gaps from top-scoring attempts, formulating them as self-contained conjectures. Each conjecture and its negation are then proved or disproved independently by fresh solver-grader pairs in a context-detached manner, with proven statements added to a global lemma memory. Phase III reinjects these verified lemmas into new solver runs, combining multiple pipeline outputs when needed. The design deliberately limits parallelism (width K=4) and uses dialectic prompting with persona-based graders to ensure rigor, achieving superior cost-accuracy trade-offs compared to massively parallel baselines.

## Key Results
- Achieved 67.1% success rate on IMO-ProofBench Advanced using Gemini 3.0 Pro, at an average cost of $31 per question.
- More than doubled the success rate of the next best publicly accessible pipeline (Huang & Yang, 2025) while reducing costs by over an order of magnitude.
- Demonstrated that context detachment via conjecture verification effectively escapes Cognitive Wells, with ablations showing significant performance drops when this mechanism is removed.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Cognitive Wells arise from shared context between solver and grader
- **Claim:** Iterative solver-grader pipelines can become trapped in "Cognitive Wells" where both components converge on an incorrect but self-consistent solution, falsely perceived as correct due to shared context.
- **Mechanism:** The solver and grader, often using identical or similar model instances and maintaining interactive context, reinforce each other's biases. The grader, lacking external reference, accepts locally plausible but globally flawed reasoning, leading to reward hacking-like behavior.
- **Core assumption:** Grading requires estimating solution correctness without access to ground truth, which is computationally infeasible for complex proofs; thus, a grader sharing context with the solver cannot reliably detect fatal gaps.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] describes iterative refinement converging to incorrect solutions considered correct; [section 1.1] reports that 28 of 30 PB-Adv problems yielded solutions with perfect internal grades but were often completely wrong; [corpus] notes neighboring work on scaling math reasoning does not address grader-solver context contamination.
- **Break condition:** If the grader's feedback becomes decoupled from the solver's context (e.g., via independent verification in a clean context), the Cognitive Well can be escaped.

### Mechanism 2: Conjecture extraction transforms vague uncertainty into actionable verification tasks
- **Claim:** Vague uncertainty in stalled proofs can be transformed into actionable verification tasks by extracting load-bearing conjectures—self-contained mathematical statements whose truth would complete the proof.
- **Mechanism:** A dedicated "Conjecture Extractor" module analyzes candidate solutions and grader critiques to identify minimal logical gaps, explicitly formulating them as conjectures (and their negations). This turns fuzzy "this step is unclear" into "prove statement C".
- **Core assumption:** Mathematical proof gaps often reduce to a small number of key intermediate claims; isolating these enables targeted verification without re-solving the entire problem.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 2.1] introduces the Solver with Conjectures prompt to formalize gaps; [appendix G] shows qualitative examples where extracted conjectures (e.g., collinearity, concurrency) served as logical anchors enabling final solutions.
- **Break condition:** If conjectures are too numerous, trivial, or incorrectly identified, the pipeline overhead increases without benefit; the extractor must balance conciseness and coverage.

### Mechanism 3: Context detachment via independent conjecture verification prevents contamination
- **Claim:** Verifying conjectures (and their negations) in a fresh, context-detached environment prevents grader contamination and provides reliable logical signals to guide subsequent solving.
- **Mechanism:** Each extracted conjecture C is treated as an independent subproblem. Two parallel solver-grader instances attempt to prove C and ¬C from scratch, with results added to a global lemma memory. Proving either provides a verified fact; proving ¬C can invalidate a flawed approach.
- **Core assumption:** Independent verification in a clean context is more reliable than grading within the original proof's context, as it avoids the Cognitive Well by eliminating shared biases.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 2.1] describes stripping conjectures from parent context and feeding them as new problems; [section 2.2, Phase II] details the bisection process; [section 3.4] ablation (Figure 5) shows removing conjecture extraction leads to erratic scoring and failure to escape Cognitive Wells.
- **Break condition:** If conjectures are inherently dependent on the original problem's context, detachment may render them unverifiable; also, cost grows with conjecture count.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Orchestrator coordinates the pipeline: Dialectic Solver generates proofs → Momus Grader evaluates → if stalled, Conjecture Extractor identifies gaps → Conjecture Resolver verifies conjectures/negations in parallel → proven statements update Lemma Memory → Solver (Phase III) refines using lemmas → Solution Parser & Combiner aggregates outputs → Answer Processor finalizes. The Lemma Memory is shared state.
- **Critical path:** Phase I (K parallel solver-grader runs) → if no perfect solution, Phase II (extract up to 3 conjectures, prove/disprove each in fresh contexts) → update lemma memory → Phase III (K−1 solvers with lemmas + 1 zero-shot) up to L=3 loops → if near-perfect (score ≥6), post-enhancement with two parallel Phase II runs → final output selection via Answer Combiner.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Parallelism width (K): Higher K increases exploration but linearly increases cost; fixed at K=4 as sweet spot vs. costly 64K-call baselines.  
  - Conjecture extraction budget: Extracting too many conjectures blows up verification cost; fixed top-k=3 load-bearing sentences.  
  - Grader strictness: Stricter grading reduces false positives (Cognitive Wells) but may discard viable solutions; Momus grader tuned for safety.  
  - Memory injection: Providing all lemmas risks context window limits; pipeline uses selective retrieval and keeps one zero-shot solver for diversity.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Cognitive Well persists: Grader repeatedly gives high scores to solutions that autograder or human rejects; indicator: low false negative rate but high false positive rate in GraderBench.  
  - Conjecture explosion: Extractor produces many trivial or unsolvable conjectures, exhausting budget without useful lemmas.  
  - Context contamination despite detachment: If conjecture extraction includes implicit context, detached proving may still fail; manifests as ambiguous verification outcomes.  
  - Stalling at Phase II: No conjectures are proven after multiple iterations; pipeline may need to discard current strategy.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Ablate conjecture extraction:** Run full pipeline with and without Phase II on a subset of PB-Adv, matching token budgets. Expect: Without conjectures, scores plateau or fluctuate; with conjectures, monotonic improvement when lemmas proven (Figure 5).  
  2. **Grader FPR/FNR tradeoff:** Compare Momus grader (default) vs. Momus with pipeline solutions vs. ProofAutoGrader on GraderBench. Expect: Momus has lower FPR (fewer false positives) but higher FNR; pipeline utility correlates with low FPR even if standalone accuracy seems worse (Table 3).  
  3. **Parallelism scaling:** Vary K (number of concurrent solvers) in Phase I and Phase III on PB-Basic. Expect: Performance increases with K but with diminishing returns; cost rises linearly; choose K where cost-performance slope changes sharply.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** How should grader evaluation metrics be designed to reflect their utility within a solver-grader pipeline, given that pipeline performance correlates more with false positive rate than standalone accuracy?  
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 3.3 demonstrates that a grader with lower standalone accuracy but lower false positive rate (FPR) improves pipeline success, creating a tension with metrics like GraderBench that prioritize accuracy and correlation with human grades.  
**Why unresolved:** Current benchmarks (e.g., GraderBench) measure grader performance in isolation, but in a pipeline, a grader’s primary role is to prevent incorrect solutions from being accepted (low FPR), even if that increases false negatives (FNR). No established metrics capture this pipeline-centric utility.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Develop new metrics that weight FPR more heavily than FNR, correlate with final pipeline success rates across diverse configurations, and are validated by human expert assessments of solution correctness. Compare existing grader prompting strategies under these metrics.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** Do Cognitive Well failure modes, where iterative refinement converges to incorrect but grader-approved solutions, occur in other AI reasoning domains beyond competition math?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] The conclusion speculates that Cognitive Wells "may extend to broader AI-for-Science systems," but the paper only demonstrates them in mathematical reasoning pipelines (PB-Adv).  
**Why unresolved:** Cognitive Wells arise from shared context between solver and grader in iterative refinement. Similar patterns could exist in code generation (e.g., self-debugging), scientific hypothesis testing, or legal argumentation, but this remains unexplored.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct multi-domain case studies analyzing solver-grader interaction traces from published pipelines in other fields (e.g., code repair, automated experiment design). Look for signatures of Cognitive Wells: high grader scores on incorrect outputs, convergence to flawed local optima, and reward hacking patterns. Quantify prevalence and correlates (e.g., model scale, iteration count).

### Open Question 3
**Question:** How can larger, contamination-free evaluation datasets for IMO-style math problems be created to enable more reliable benchmarking of reasoning pipelines?  
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 4 (Limitations) cites "lack of evaluation data of sufficient size and complexity" due to contamination concerns and verification challenges; PB-Adv has only 30 problems.  
**Why unresolved:** Small benchmark size limits statistical power and generalizability. Contamination from training data risks overfitting. Creating new problems is expensive and requires expert verification to ensure correctness and difficulty.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Release a new benchmark with 100+ novel IMO-level problems, each with multiple human-verified solutions and detailed rubrics. Use rigorous data collection protocols (e.g., problems from recent uncontaminated competitions, blind expert review, adversarial filtering) to minimize contamination. Report contamination audits and inter-rater reliability.

### Open Question 4
**Question:** What architectural optimizations can reduce the latency of conjecture-based pipelines while preserving their cost and accuracy advantages over massively parallel approaches?  
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] Section 4 notes the pipeline "loses to current massively parallel pipelines on latency" despite superior cost/accuracy trade-offs (Figure 3).  
**Why unresolved:** The conjecture extraction and context detachment steps introduce sequential dependencies (e.g., waiting for conjecture verification before refinement), increasing latency. Parallelism is deliberately limited to avoid Cognitive Wells, but smarter orchestration might help.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Implement and test variants: (a) parallelizing conjecture verification, (b) speculative execution of likely conjectures, (c) hybrid approaches that dynamically adjust parallelism based on problem difficulty. Measure end-to-end latency, cost, and accuracy on PB-Adv, comparing to the original pipeline and to Huang & Yang (2025) baselines. Identify latency bottlenecks via profiling.

## Limitations
- **Model dependency:** Results rely heavily on Gemini 3.0 Pro/Flash and 2.5 Pro; generalizability to other off‑the‑shelf models is unproven.
- **Benchmark size:** IMO‑ProofBench Advanced contains only 30 problems; reported success rates may have high variance.
- **Cost volatility:** The $31 average cost assumes specific token budgets and current pricing; changes in budgets, API pricing, or token consumption patterns could shift costs substantially.

## Confidence
- **Cognitive Wells exist in iterative solver‑grader pipelines:** Medium (strong internal analysis, but limited external replication).
- **Conjecture extraction + context detachment improves performance:** High (ablation studies show consistent benefit; mechanism well‑supported).
- **Pipeline achieves 67.1% success at ~$31 on PB‑Adv:** Medium (small benchmark, model‑specific; needs wider validation).
- **Order‑of‑magnitude cost reduction vs. baselines:** Medium (depends on baseline cost reporting and implementation fidelity).
