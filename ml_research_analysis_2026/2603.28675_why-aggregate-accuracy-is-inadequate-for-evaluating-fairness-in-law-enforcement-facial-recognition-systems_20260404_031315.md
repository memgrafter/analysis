# Why Aggregate Accuracy is Inadequate for Evaluating Fairness in Law Enforcement Facial Recognition Systems

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.28675
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.28675
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Aggregate accuracy of 75.47% masks substantial subgroup disparities in false positive and false negative rates across race and age groups.

## Executive Summary
This paper demonstrates that aggregate accuracy, a standard metric for evaluating law enforcement facial recognition systems, is inadequate for fairness assessment because it obscures demographic disparities in error distribution. Using an empirical case study, the authors show that while overall accuracy is 75.47%, false positive rates (FPR) and false negative rates (FNR) vary significantly across racial and age subgroups. These variations reveal that systems with similar aggregate accuracy can have very different fairness profiles. The paper argues for fairness-aware evaluation that relies on subgroup-level metrics like FPR and FNR, emphasizing the asymmetric consequences of false positives (wrongful suspicion) and false negatives (missed identification) in law enforcement contexts.

## Method Summary
The paper conducts a case study analysis on a facial recognition dataset deployed in law enforcement. It computes overall accuracy and then decomposes performance by sensitive attributes (race and age) to calculate subgroup-specific FPR and FNR. By comparing these metrics across demographic groups, the study exposes disparities that are hidden when relying solely on aggregate accuracy. The analysis is limited to a single system and dataset, without exploring additional models or root causes of the observed differences.

## Key Results
- Overall system accuracy is 75.47%, but this aggregate figure conceals substantial variation in error rates.
- For race, FPR ranges from 0.2015 to 0.3518 and FNR from 0.1357 to 0.3117 across subgroups.
- For age, FPR ranges from 0.2453 to 0.4601 and FNR from 0.1117 to 0.4958, indicating even wider disparity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Aggregate accuracy fails to reveal disparities in error rates across demographic groups.
- **Mechanism:** Accuracy is computed as total correct predictions divided by total predictions, aggregating over all groups. This aggregation masks subgroup performance because it does not account for how errors are distributed; two systems with identical accuracy can have very different false positive and false negative rates for specific groups.
- **Core assumption:** The population is heterogeneous with respect to sensitive attributes (e.g., race, age) and the model's errors are not uniformly distributed.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "aggregate accuracy is an insufficient metric for evaluating fairness... it obscures demographic disparities in error distribution."
  - [section 2]: "accuracy provides an aggregate measure of performance, offering no insight into how errors are distributed across different demographic groups." Figure 1 illustrates equal aggregate accuracy concealing unequal subgroup error rates.
  - [corpus]: No direct evidence from neighboring papers is required; the paper itself provides strong empirical support via Tables 1–2 showing FPR/FNR ranges.
- **Break condition:** If all demographic groups have identical false positive and false negative rates, then aggregate accuracy would adequately reflect fairness. However, empirical results in the paper (and broader literature) show this is rarely the case.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Aggregate accuracy treats false positives and false negatives as equivalent errors, ignoring their asymmetric consequences in law enforcement and their uneven distribution across groups.
- **Mechanism:** Accuracy counts all misclassifications equally, but in law enforcement a false positive (wrongful identification) may lead to wrongful suspicion or arrest, while a false negative (missed identification) may undermine investigations. These error types have different societal costs and may disproportionately affect certain demographics. Without separating them, fairness cannot be assessed.
- **Core assumption:** The operational and ethical costs of false positives and false negatives differ, and these costs are not uniformly distributed across demographic groups.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "misclassification may result in wrongful suspicion or missed identification."
  - [section 4]: "A false positive... may lead to wrongful suspicion or investigation. Conversely, a false negative may result in failure to identify a relevant individual, potentially undermining operational objectives."
  - [corpus]: Neighboring paper "FairLENS: Assessing Fairness in Law Enforcement Speech Recognition" (arxiv:2405.13166) similarly stresses the need to evaluate fairness across acoustic environments and speakers, supporting the importance of error-type distinction.
- **Break condition:** If false positives and false negatives had identical consequences and were equally distributed across groups, accuracy might suffice. But in law enforcement contexts, consequences are asymmetric and documented disparities exist.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Optimizing for aggregate accuracy can incentivize models that perform well on majority groups at the expense of minority groups, especially when data is imbalanced.
- **Mechanism:** Machine learning models trained to maximize accuracy minimize overall error, which is often achieved by focusing on the majority class or groups with more predictable patterns. This leads to higher error rates for underrepresented groups, but if those groups are small, aggregate accuracy remains high, hiding the bias.
- **Core assumption:** Training data reflects real-world imbalances in demographic representation, and the model has limited capacity to fit all groups equally.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 2]: "accuracy is sensitive to class imbalance, which is common in real-world datasets. In such cases, a model may achieve high accuracy by correctly predicting the majority class while performing poorly on minority classes."
  - [section 3]: "Variation in dataset composition, including imbalanced representation of demographic groups have been identified as a key contributing factor."
  - [corpus]: Neighboring paper "Analyzing Fairness of Classification Machine Learning Model with Structured Dataset" (arxiv:2412.09896) discusses fairness issues in ML, including the impact of imbalance.
- **Break condition:** If datasets were perfectly balanced across all demographic groups and models had infinite capacity, optimizing accuracy might not create disparity. However, these conditions are unrealistic in practice.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
Data Loader -> Model Inference Engine -> Metric Calculator -> Disparity Analyzer -> Report Generator
Configuration Module interacts with all components.

- **Critical path:**
1. Load dataset with sensitive attributes.
2. Run model inference on all samples.
3. Compute overall accuracy.
4. For each sensitive attribute, partition data by each distinct value.
5. For each subgroup, compute FPR and FNR from the confusion matrix.
6. Calculate disparity measures (e.g., range of FPR across groups).
7. Compare disparities to configured thresholds; generate flags.
8. Output the fairness report.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Granularity vs. computational cost:* More sensitive attributes and finer subgroups increase computation and may produce unreliable estimates for small groups. Trade off by limiting attributes or requiring minimum subgroup size.
  - *Threshold selection:* Choosing disparity thresholds (e.g., max FPR difference > 0.1) involves balancing sensitivity (catching true biases) and specificity (avoiding false alarms). No universal values; may require domain expert input.
  - *Missing sensitive data:* If demographic data is missing for some records, options are to exclude them (reducing sample size) or use imputation (introducing noise). The tradeoff is between completeness and accuracy of the fairness assessment.
  - *Model-agnostic vs. interpretability:* A model-agnostic design works with any black-box model but cannot access internal representations that might explain bias. Simpler models might be easier to audit but could sacrifice accuracy.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - *High missing rate in sensitive attributes:* >20% missing may invalidate subgroup analysis.
  - *Extremely small subgroups:* Subgroups with <30 samples yield high-variance metrics; confidence intervals should be computed, but if not, results are unreliable.
  - *No disparities flagged despite known issues:* Could indicate thresholds are too lenient or metrics are not sensitive enough to the fairness definition of interest.
  - *System crashes on unseen attribute values:* Robust handling of categorical variables (e.g., treating unseen values as "other" or erroring gracefully) is needed.

- **First 3 experiments:**
1. Benchmark validation: Run the system on a public facial recognition dataset with known demographic disparities (e.g., the dataset referenced in the paper or similar). Verify that the reported FPR/FNR differences match published findings.
2. Synthetic stress test: Generate synthetic predictions with controlled error rates (e.g., FPR=0.2 for group A, FPR=0.4 for group B). Confirm the system accurately recovers these rates and flags the disparity.
3. Missing data resilience: Randomly remove sensitive attributes for a proportion of samples (e.g., 30%). Assess how metrics and flags change; evaluate whether the system provides warnings about reduced reliability.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can fairness auditing be embedded into law enforcement workflows for facial recognition? The conclusion explicitly calls for exploring integration into operational workflows. Current practice relies solely on aggregate accuracy; no practical methods exist to incorporate subgroup fairness checks without disruption. Evidence needed: Pilot studies deploying auditing tools in police departments, reporting on adoption, workflow adaptations, and equity outcomes.
2. What should standardized fairness evaluation protocols for law enforcement facial recognition entail, and how can they achieve widespread adoption? The conclusion emphasizes the need for standardized protocols reflecting real-world complexities. No consensus on appropriate metrics, thresholds, or reporting formats leads to inconsistent evaluations. Evidence needed: Collaborative benchmark development, cross-jurisdictional comparisons, and longitudinal studies showing standards reduce disparity and improve accountability.
3. How can law enforcement make explicit trade-offs between fairness and accuracy when error costs are asymmetric and group-dependent? The paper discusses the fairness-accuracy trade-off and ethical/policy considerations but offers no decision framework. Quantifying societal costs of false positives/negatives across groups is complex and value-laden; no practical tool guides threshold setting. Evidence needed: Empirical studies measuring real-world error consequences, stakeholder value elicitation, and development of cost-sensitive decision models aligned with legal norms.
4. What are the main barriers (technical, organizational, legal) to adopting fairness-aware evaluation in law enforcement facial recognition, and how can they be overcome? The paper highlights a gap between academic fairness research and operational practice, citing proprietary systems, lack of accessible tools, and accuracy focus. Without systematic understanding of context-specific constraints, interventions risk being ineffective or rejected. Evidence needed: Qualitative investigations (interviews, case studies) with police departments and vendors, analysis of procurement policies, and user-centered design/testing of audit interfaces in operational settings.

## Limitations
- The study uses a single dataset and system, limiting generalizability to other contexts or models.
- No statistical uncertainty quantification (e.g., confidence intervals) is provided for subgroup error rates, making it difficult to assess the robustness of observed disparities.
- The analysis focuses only on race and age, ignoring intersectionality, other sensitive attributes, and potential root causes of disparities.

## Confidence
- Aggregate accuracy masks demographic disparities in error rates: **High**
- False positive and false negative asymmetry is crucial in law enforcement fairness: **Medium**
- Accuracy optimization drives bias under data imbalance: **Medium**
