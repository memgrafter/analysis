# Structural Compactness as a Complementary Criterion for Explanation Quality

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.29491
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.29491
- Reference count: 38
- Primary result: MST-C captures spatial structure of explanations, correlates positively with sparseness, negatively with complexity, and shows no correlation with correctness measures, thereby complementing existing attribution quality metrics.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Minimum Spanning Tree Compactness (MST-C), a novel graph-based metric designed to quantify the structural compactness of attribution heatmaps. By converting salient pixels into a spatial graph, MST-C measures both spread (via inverse convex hull area) and cohesion (via MST length relative to node count), providing a single score that reflects human-perceived legibility. Evaluations across multiple image models and XAI methods show that MST-C effectively distinguishes between attribution techniques—favoring blob-like outputs like GradCAM—and exhibits strong correlations with sparseness and complexity metrics, but not with correctness metrics, highlighting its role as a complementary criterion for explanation quality.

## Method Summary
The MST-C metric operates on a normalized attribution heatmap (H×W). First, absolute values are taken and a percentile threshold (default 80%) is applied to select the most salient pixels, forming a node set V. These nodes are connected via an undirected k-Nearest Neighbors graph (k=500) using Euclidean distances. The spread component is computed as the inverse square root of the convex hull area of V (or a fallback based on image diagonal if |V|<3). The cohesion component is the ratio of node count |V| to the total length L_T of the graph’s minimum spanning tree. Finally, MST-C = (1/√A_hull) × (|V|/L_T) × √(h²+w²), yielding a scaled score where higher values indicate greater compactness.

## Key Results
- MST-C reliably distinguishes between attribution methods, with GradCAM achieving the highest scores and Integrated Gradients/SHAP the lowest across image models.
- It shows a strong positive correlation with sparseness (up to 0.85) and negative correlations with complexity metrics, while demonstrating no significant correlation with correctness measures (RRA, ROAD).
- The metric is robust to hyperparameter choices beyond a threshold and complements existing complexity metrics by differentiating attributions with similar global statistics but different spatial topology.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Graph-Based Spatial Encoding
The core innovation of MST-C lies in encoding attribution heatmaps as spatial graphs. Salient pixels (after thresholding) become graph nodes, and edges are defined by k-Nearest Neighbors (k=500) using Euclidean distances. This graph captures higher-order geometric relationships beyond pixel-wise statistics, aligning with the assumption that human legibility depends on the topological arrangement of salient regions (abstract; Section 2). A break condition arises if the k-NN graph is fragmented; then MST computation fails to reflect global cohesion, necessitating a sufficiently large k to ensure single connectivity.

### Dual-Component Compactness Quantification
Compactness is decomposed into two quantifiable spatial properties: spread and cohesion. Spread is measured as 1/√A_hull, penalizing dispersion of salient points; cohesion is |V|/L_T, rewarding tight connectivity (short MST relative to node count) (abstract; Section 2, Equations 1-2). The assumption is that legible explanations concentrate salient features in a small area (low spread) while forming well-connected clusters (high cohesion). A break condition occurs if salient points are colinear or degenerate, making convex hull area unstable; the paper mitigates this by falling back to the image diagonal for |V|<3.

### Multiplicative Integration and Theoretical Bounding
The product MST-C = q_spread × q_cohesion (further scaled by √(h²+w²)) yields a bounded, interpretable score that favors blob-like attributions (Section 2). Multiplication amplifies cases where both spread is low and cohesion is high. Theoretical bounds (0 ≤ MST-C ≤ 2√2 scaled) are derived from lattice geometry. Empirically, GradCAM (blob-like) achieves high MST-C, while fine-grained methods like IG score low (Section 3.1). However, for very coarse attributions, MST-C becomes sensitive to thresholding (Figure 8), revealing a bias toward compact blobs.

## Foundational Learning
- **Graph Theory Basics (Minimum Spanning Tree)**: Why needed: MST is central to computing cohesion (|V|/L_T); understanding its properties (uniqueness, sensitivity to edge weights) is key to interpreting MST-C. Quick check: If two attribution configurations have identical node sets but different spatial arrangements, how would their MST lengths compare? (Answer: Shorter MST indicates points are more tightly clustered.)

- **Attribution Methods (LRP, GradCAM, IG, SHAP)**: Why needed: MST-C evaluates structural differences between these methods; knowing their typical output patterns (pixel-wise vs. blob-like) explains MST-C rankings. Quick check: Why might Integrated Gradients produce lower MST-C than LRP-ε-z⁺-flat? (Answer: IG tends to produce noisier, more fragmented attributions with larger convex hulls and longer MSTs.)

- **Complexity Metrics (Sparseness, Effective Complexity)**: Why needed: MST-C is positioned as a complement to these global statistics; understanding their limitations (they ignore spatial topology) clarifies MST-C’s value-add. Quick check: Can two attribution maps have identical sparseness but different MST-C? (Answer: Yes—if one forms a compact cluster and the other forms dispersed clusters with same number of salient pixels.)

## Architecture Onboarding
Input -> Preprocessor -> Graph Builder -> MST Engine -> Spread Calculator -> Combiner -> Validator

Critical path: Thresholding -> Graph construction -> MST computation -> Convex hull -> Score aggregation.

Design tradeoffs:
- Threshold: Lower (e.g., 10%) includes more pixels → noisier graph; higher (e.g., 90%) → too few nodes, unstable A_hull. Paper recommends 80% as balance.
- k: Small k risks fragmentation; large k increases runtime O(k|V|log|V|). Paper finds k≈500 ensures connectivity with reasonable speed (~2.5s/sample).
- Scaling factor: √(h²+w²) chosen for numerical interpretability; could be replaced with other constants.

Failure signatures:
1. Non-connected graph: k too small → multiple MSTs required → invalid cohesion. Detect via component count (Figure 7).
2. Threshold divergence: For GradCAM, MST-C varies sharply above 80% threshold (Figure 8). Monitor A_hull and |V|/L_T separately.
3. Resolution sensitivity: Bilinear upscaling diminishes method separation (Figure 9 left). Ensure consistent input resolution across comparisons.
4. Model-specific artifacts: ResNet with LRP-ε shows inflated MST-C due to downsampling artifacts (Section 3.1). Verify with visual inspection of MST graphs.

First 3 experiments:
1. Reproduce Table 1: Run MST-C across 6 XAI methods × 3 models on 250 VOC samples with k=500, threshold=80%. Verify ranking: GradCAM > LRP-ε-z⁺-flat > others > IG/SHAP.
2. Hyperparameter sweep: Vary k (1–1500) and threshold (10–90%) as in Figures 6–8. Confirm score stabilization for k≥5 and relative ranking preservation except for GradCAM at high thresholds.
3. Correlation study: Compute Pearson correlation between MST-C and (a) Sparseness, (b) Complexity, (c) RRA, (d) ROAD over 250 samples. Expect strong positive with Sparseness (up to 0.85), negative with Complexity, near-zero with RRA/ROAD.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Does a high MST-C score correlate with human-perceived explanation legibility in a user study?** Basis: explicit (conclusion). Why unresolved: relationship not empirically validated. Evidence needed: controlled user study correlating legibility ratings with MST-C scores.
- **What is the underlying reason for MST-C's apparent bias towards blob-like, low-resolution attributions, and how does this relate to true semantic interpretability?** Basis: inferred (authors note bias). Why unresolved: metric optimizes for spatial spread/cohesion but not semantic coherence; trade-off not analyzed. Evidence needed: experiments with identical MST-C but varying semantic coherence, followed by human evaluation.
- **Can the MST-C framework be effectively adapted to non-image domains (e.g., text, time series, tabular data), and what spatial or relational graph representations would be necessary?** Basis: explicit (authors state adaptation possible but left to future work). Why unresolved: reliance on 2D Euclidean distances; defining meaningful relationships for other data is non-trivial. Evidence needed: domain-specific graph construction rules validated on legibility.
- **Does the absolute value thresholding and binarization step in MST-C discard sign information crucial for interpretation, and would preserving polarity improve the metric's alignment with legibility?** Basis: inferred (authors acknowledge binarization prevents fine-grained distinctions). Why unresolved: spatial separation of positive/negative evidence may be key to coherence; MST-C treats all salient pixels equally. Evidence needed: variant incorporating signed edges compared against standard MST-C.
- **What are the precise structural graph-theoretic properties (beyond spread and cohesion) that cause fundamental differences in MST-C scores between attribution methods and model architectures?** Basis: inferred (paper shows patterns but only high-level interpretations). Why unresolved: deeper drivers like MST degree distributions, clustering coefficients not analyzed. Evidence needed: detailed graph analysis of k-NN graphs and MSTs, correlating topological features with MST-C.

## Limitations
- Graph construction details (symmetrization, disconnected component handling) are unspecified, risking unreliable MST length computation.
- MST-C exhibits threshold instability for blob-like methods and lacks human validation to confirm its alignment with perceived legibility.
- The metric biases toward compact blobs, may undervalue dispersed attributions, and shows model-specific artifacts (e.g., ResNet with LRP-ε) and resolution dependence.
