# Beyond the Star Rating: A Scalable Framework for Aspect-Based Sentiment Analysis Using LLMs and Text Classification

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.21082
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21082
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Proposes a hybrid LLM-classifier framework for scalable ABSA, achieving 0.73–0.85 aspect accuracy and explaining ≥81.4% of rating variance.

## Executive Summary
This study introduces a scalable hybrid framework for aspect-based sentiment analysis (ABSA) on millions of restaurant reviews. The approach uses ChatGPT to identify six key aspects from a small sample (600 reviews), then trains a logistic regression classifier with fastText embeddings to predict aspect-specific sentiment for the full corpus of 4.7 million Yelp reviews. The one-stage classifier outperforms a two-stage alternative and the aggregated aspect sentiments explain a large portion of overall restaurant ratings (R² ≥ 0.814). Food quality and service emerge as the strongest positive drivers, ambiance as a negative factor, while wait time shows a small positive association and price is not predictive.

## Method Summary
The pipeline begins with aspect discovery: ChatGPT analyzes 600 sampled reviews to derive six consensus aspects (service, food quality, ambiance, wait time, price, menu variety). Five human annotators then label 5,000 reviews per aspect for relevance and sentiment (-1, 0, +1). FastText embeddings (skip-gram, character n‑grams 3–6, 100 dimensions) are trained on the full 4.7M review corpus. For each aspect, a logistic regression classifier is trained using L2‑normalized mean word vectors as features, comparing one‑stage (3‑class) and two‑stage (relevance then sentiment) architectures. The one‑stage model is chosen based on validation performance. Trained classifiers predict sentiment for all reviews; aspect means are aggregated per restaurant and used as predictors in OLS regressions of overall star ratings, controlling for cuisine and state.

## Key Results
- Aspect-specific classifier accuracies range from 0.73 to 0.85 (one‑stage model on 1,000‑review validation set).
- Predicted aspect sentiments explain ≥81.4% of variance in overall restaurant ratings (R² ≥ 0.814).
- Regression coefficients: food quality (β ≈ 1.58), service (β ≈ 0.77) are strong positive predictors; ambiance (β ≈ –0.27) is negative; wait time (β ≈ 0.22) small positive; price not statistically significant.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Hybrid LLM-Classical ML Architecture
- **Claim**: A hybrid architecture using LLMs only for aspect discovery and classical ML for sentiment classification enables scalable ABSA on millions of reviews.
- **Mechanism**: ChatGPT analyzes a small sample (600 reviews) to identify stable, recurring aspects, defining the classification label space. A logistic regression classifier with fastText embeddings is trained on 5,000 human‑labeled reviews and applied to the full corpus. The LLM is used only once, avoiding costly per‑review prompting.
- **Core assumption**: Aspects discovered in the sample are representative of the full corpus, and sentiment classification does not require LLM contextual depth once aspects are fixed.
- **Evidence anchors**: Paper abstract proposes the hybrid approach; section 2.2 describes sampling and high inter‑model agreement on six aspects; neighbor papers (e.g., TopicImpact) follow similar hybrid paradigms.
- **Break condition**: If aspect distribution in the full corpus diverges significantly from the sample (e.g., new aspects emerge) or classifier performance on a held‑out sample drops below acceptable thresholds (e.g., <0.70 accuracy).

### Mechanism 2: Machine‑Labeled Sentiments as Strong Predictors
- **Claim**: Machine‑labeled aspect‑specific sentiments, despite noise, are strong explanatory predictors of overall restaurant ratings.
- **Mechanism**: The framework aggregates review‑level sentiment predictions (range –1 to 1) to restaurant‑level averages for each of the six aspects. These continuous predictors are fed into a linear regression model alongside cuisine and state controls. The high R² indicates that the latent sentiment dimensions capture most systematic variance in star ratings.
- **Core assumption**: Sentiment labels, even with classification errors, are sufficiently unbiased and reliable at the aggregate level to reflect true customer satisfaction drivers.
- **Evidence anchors**: Abstract reports R² ≥ 0.814; section 3.2 provides regression coefficients and significance levels; papers like "Beyond Stars" similarly find review text explains rating variance beyond the star itself.
- **Break condition**: If regression R² collapses on a temporal hold‑out (e.g., later years), suggesting concept drift where aspect‑sentiment relationships to ratings have changed.

### Mechanism 3: One‑Stage Classifier Superiority
- **Claim**: The one‑stage classifier is superior to the two‑stage classifier for this dataset, as validated by rigorous statistical testing.
- **Mechanism**: Both architectures use the same features. The one‑stage treats "irrelevant" as a neutral class in a single 3‑way prediction. The two‑stage first predicts relevance (binary) then sentiment (3‑way) only on relevant reviews. McNemar's test on 1,000 validation reviews shows one‑stage significantly outperforms two‑stage across all aspects.
- **Core assumption**: The cost of false positives in relevance detection (two‑stage Stage 1 errors) outweighs the benefit of its improved negative‑class detection in Stage 2, making the simpler one‑stage more accurate overall.
- **Evidence anchors**: Section 2.4 details architectures and McNemar test; section 3.1 reports significant results; Table 7 vs 8 shows accuracy ranges (one‑stage: 0.73–0.85; two‑stage Stage 2: 0.61–0.76).
- **Break condition**: If business requirements prioritize recall of negative sentiments (e.g., for alert systems), the two‑stage might be preferred despite lower overall accuracy; the hierarchy could reverse on a different dataset or with different class imbalance.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Aspect‑Based Sentiment Analysis (ABSA)
- **Why needed here**: The entire pipeline decomposes monolithic sentiment analysis into (a) aspect identification and (b) aspect‑specific sentiment classification. Understanding this decomposition is essential to grasp why the hybrid approach is structured this way and what each component optimizes for.
- **Quick check**: How would the analysis differ if you used document‑level sentiment classification instead of ABSA? What specific insights would be lost about restaurant performance?

### Concept: FastText Subword Embeddings
- **Why needed here**: The chosen text representation is fastText, not Word2Vec or static embeddings. FastText's use of character n‑grams handles out‑of‑vocabulary words and misspellings common in social media reviews, which is critical for robustness on 4.7M diverse reviews.
- **Quick check**: Why is fastText's ability to represent rare/unseen words particularly important for a corpus spanning 17 years and many U.S. states? What would happen with a standard word2vec model?

### Concept: Fleiss' Kappa for Multi‑Rater Agreement
- **Why needed here**: The paper uses Fleiss' Kappa to validate that aspects extracted by LLMs and sentiment labels assigned by humans are consistently interpretable. This establishes the classification task is well‑defined, justifying training a single classifier.
- **Quick check**: Fleiss' Kappa for ambiance is 0.675 (substantial) and for wait time is 0.460 (moderate). What does this suggest about the clarity of these constructs for human annotators? How might this affect classifier performance?

## Architecture Onboarding

Aspect Discovery (LLM) -> Human Labeling -> Preprocessing -> Embedding Training (fastText on full corpus) -> Classifier Training (logistic regression) -> Full Corpus Prediction -> Aggregation (restaurant‑level means) -> Regression (OLS)

**Critical path**: FastText training **must** precede classifier training because embeddings must capture the true corpus distribution for generalization.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- **LLM prompting cost vs coverage**: LLM used only on 600 reviews; assumes aspects are stable. If tail reviews contain new aspects, they will be missed.
- **One‑stage vs Two‑stage**: One‑stage chosen for higher accuracy; trade‑off is poorer recall on negative sentiments (e.g., menu negative F1 = 0.00). A hybrid per‑aspect selection could be future work.
- **fastText vs TF‑IDF**: fastText outperformed TF‑IDF+SMOTE in accuracy and robustness, especially for rare words. Cost: larger model size, but still locally deployable.
- **Logistic regression vs SVM**: SVM marginally more accurate but computationally prohibitive at scale; logistic regression chosen for efficiency.

**Failure signatures**:
1. **Aspect misalignment**: If the six aspects don’t cover >80% of review content (checked via manual sampling), the classifier will mislabel many reviews as irrelevant/neutral incorrectly.
2. **Embedding degradation**: If the full corpus contains domains/languages not in training (e.g., non‑English reviews), fastText vectors will be poor; monitor OOV rates.
3. **Classifier calibration failure**: If probability outputs are not calibrated, the aggregated means could be biased; check reliability diagrams on validation set.
4. **Regression multicollinearity**: Aspects may be correlated (e.g., food quality and service); check VIF scores; high VIF would inflate standard errors.
5. **Temporal non‑stationarity**: If sentiment‑aspect relationships drift over 17 years, a single model may not hold; monitor R² by year.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Reproduce validation metrics**: Load the trained one‑stage logistic regression model and fastText vectors. Predict on the held‑out 1,000 reviews. Confirm Table 7 numbers (e.g., service accuracy 0.73, food quality 0.78) are within ±0.01. If not, investigate data split or random seed.
2. **Aspect coverage audit**: Randomly sample 200 reviews from the full corpus not in the 5,000 labeled set. Manually annotate aspects present. Compute: % of reviews mentioning at least one of the six aspects. If <85%, collect additional aspects from LLM on this sample and consider expanding the label set.
3. **Scalability stress test**: Process the full 4.7M reviews through the pipeline (embedding + classification). Record total runtime, peak memory, and per‑review latency. Verify it completes within acceptable compute budget (e.g., <48 h on a single GPU instance). If not, profile to identify bottleneck (embedding lookup vs. model prediction).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question**: Beyond the six aspects identified by ChatGPT (service, food quality, ambiance, wait time, price, menu variety), what other latent dimensions of restaurant experiences are frequently discussed in reviews and significantly influence overall ratings?
- **Basis**: [explicit] "we focused on only six LLM‑identified aspects, which may overlook other dimensions of customer experience, especially since our initial analysis was based on just 600 reviews."
- **Why unresolved**: The aspect set was derived from a small sample and limited to ChatGPT’s output, potentially missing salient but less frequent aspects (e.g., dietary accommodations, cleanliness, staff expertise) that could affect ratings.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Expand aspect discovery using multiple LLMs (e.g., Claude, Llama) or human thematic analysis on a larger, stratified sample; then re-run regression to test if new aspects add explanatory power.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Would a hybrid classification architecture—applying the one‑stage classifier for some aspects/sentiment classes and the two‑stage for others—improve overall aspect‑based sentiment prediction accuracy and robustness on large‑scale review data?
- **Basis**: [explicit] "Since the models predict sentiments for each aspect separately, a hybrid model could be considered, which applies the classifier that performs better for each sentiment class and aspect rather than using either the one‑stage or two‑stage classifier uniformly."
- **Why unresolved**: The study compared both architectures but did not implement or evaluate a hybrid, even though performance varied by aspect (e.g., two‑stage better for negatives, one‑stage better for neutrals/positives).
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Develop and test a meta‑classifier or rule‑based switcher that selects the optimal model per aspect‑sentiment class based on validation performance; evaluate on held‑out data to measure gains in macro‑F1 or balanced accuracy.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: How stable and generalizable are the identified aspect‑sentiment coefficients (e.g., food quality β≈1.58, ambiance β≈–0.27) across different geographic regions, cuisine types, and time periods, given the dataset’s spatial and temporal coverage?
- **Basis**: [explicit] "We, however, also note moderate annotator agreement (κ=0.675), indicating some variability in labeled perceptions of ambiance, which may have influenced the model’s predictions." Also, "The high estimates observed in Montana may be attributed to the limited sample size (only six data points)."
- **Why unresolved**: Regression controls for state and cuisine, but coarse categorization and sparse data in some regions (e.g., Montana) may bias coefficients; temporal shifts (e.g., post‑COVID changes) were not analyzed.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Perform longitudinal analysis (year‑by‑year regressions) and spatial cross‑validation (training on some states, testing on others) to assess coefficient consistency; investigate interactions between aspects and regional/cuisine variables.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: To what extent do the aspect identification results depend on the choice of large language model (LLM), and would alternative LLMs yield a different set of "key aspects" for restaurant reviews?
- **Basis**: [inferred] The study used only ChatGPT‑4o and 4o‑mini for aspect discovery; no comparison with other LLMs (e.g., open‑source models, older GPT versions) or classical methods (e.g., LDA) beyond a brief validation.
- **Why unresolved**: LLM prompting choices (model version, prompt wording) can significantly affect extracted aspects; the paper assumes ChatGPT’s aspects are comprehensive and stable, but this is not validated across models.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Replicate the aspect discovery step using multiple LLMs (e.g., Claude 3, Llama 3) with the same prompt on the same 600‑review sample; compare aspect sets via overlap metrics (e.g., Jaccard similarity) and assess downstream impact on sentiment classification performance.

### Open Question 5
- **Question**: How do predicted aspect‑specific sentiments evolve over the 17‑year span of the dataset, and do temporal trends (e.g., rising importance of ambiance post‑pandemic) explain changes in overall restaurant ratings?
- **Basis**: [explicit] "studying temporal trends in aspect sentiments could reveal shifts in customer preferences and expectations, such as those influenced by events like COVID‑19." This is proposed as future work but not executed.
- **Why unresolved**: The analysis treats the 17‑year corpus as static; no time‑series or cohort analysis was conducted to detect changing relationships between aspects and ratings.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Aggregate reviews and aspect predictions by year (or pre/post‑COVID eras), then run time‑varying regression models or difference‑in‑differences to test whether aspect coefficients change significantly over time; correlate with external events (e.g., lockdowns, economic shifts).

## Limitations
- **Aspect coverage**: The six aspects derived from 600 reviews may not cover all relevant topics in 4.7M reviews (e.g., emerging trends like sustainability).
- **Regression temporal stability**: R² ≥ 0.814 is reported, but the 17‑year corpus may have drift; no hold‑out validation by time period.
- **Labeling consistency**: Fleiss' Kappa varies (0.46–0.68); moderate agreement on "wait time" could propagate noise; manual labeling 5,000 reviews is costly and may not scale.
