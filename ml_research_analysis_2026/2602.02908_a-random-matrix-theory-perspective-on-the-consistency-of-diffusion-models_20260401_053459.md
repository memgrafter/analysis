# A Random Matrix Theory Perspective on the Consistency of Diffusion Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.02908
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02908
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Diffusion model consistency across data splits arises from shared Gaussian statistics; finite-sample effects renormalize the noise scale via κ(σ²), causing overshrinkage of low-variance modes, with cross-split variance factorizing into anisotropy, inhomogeneity, and global 1/n scaling.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates why diffusion models trained on non-overlapping data splits produce similar outputs when using the same random noise seed. Using random matrix theory (RMT), the authors trace this consistency to a linear Gaussian predictor that depends only on empirical mean and covariance, which are similar across splits. Finite data sizes induce a renormalization of the noise level, σ² → κ(σ²), leading to overshrinkage toward the mean, especially along low-variance eigenmodes. The variance of the denoiser across splits decomposes into three factors: anisotropy (peaking when eigenvalues ≈ κ), inhomogeneity (larger along high-variance directions), and global scaling ∝ 1/n. Experiments on CIFAR, FFHQ, and LSUN with UNet and DiT models confirm these predictions, linking data covariance to diffusion reproducibility and providing a principled baseline for understanding consistency.

## Method Summary
The authors develop an RMT framework for linear diffusion models, analyzing the denoiser and sampling map. Key analytical tools include deterministic equivalence for resolvents ( ˆΣ+σ²I )⁻¹ ≍ (Σ+κ(σ²)I)⁻¹ and fractional matrix powers via integral representations, yielding a renormalization function κ(σ²) that solves a Silverstein‑type equation. This captures finite‑sample effects such as overshrinkage. The cross‑split variance of the denoiser/score is derived using two‑point deterministic equivalence, factorizing into anisotropy, inhomogeneity, and scaling. Validation involves: (1) comparing linear predictor outputs on disjoint data splits; (2) solving for κ from dataset spectra and measuring shrinkage; (3) training UNet/DiT models on splits of varying size and correlating predicted anisotropy/inhomogeneity with observed cross‑split deviations.

## Key Results
- The linear Gaussian predictor already explains much of the consistency across data splits; deep networks approximate this predictor in the generalization regime.
- Finite‑sample effects renormalize the effective noise level: σ² → κ(σ²) > σ², causing overshrinkage of low‑variance modes.
- Cross‑split variance factorizes into: (1) anisotropy – directional dependence peaking at λ ≈ κ; (2) inhomogeneity – larger deviations when inputs lie along high‑variance modes; (3) global scaling ∝ 1/n.
- Experiments on CIFAR‑10/100, FFHQ‑32/64, LSUN‑32/64 validate: as n increases, generated samples shift toward the population linear predictor; spectral MSE inflation matches anisotropy predictions; pointwise inhomogeneity correlates with measured deviations (Spearman ρ≈0.33 on FFHQ64, n=30k).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: The consistency of diffusion outputs across data splits is largely explained by the linear Gaussian predictor; deep networks, in their generalization regime, approximate this same linear mapping.
- **Mechanism**: The linear denoiser depends only on empirical first two moments: \(D_{\hat{\Sigma}}(x;\sigma)=\hat{\mu}+(\hat{\Sigma}+\sigma^2 I)^{-1}\hat{\Sigma}(x-\hat{\mu})\). If \(\hat{\mu},\hat{\Sigma}\) differ little between splits, the outputs remain similar. Deep networks, when trained with sufficient data, produce sampling maps that closely match the linear predictor (Wiener filter).
- **Core assumption**: The linear approximation to the score is accurate in the non‑memorization regime, as established by prior work.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - Abstract: “We trace this consistency to a simple linear effect: the shared Gaussian statistics across splits already predict much of the generated images.”  
  - Section 3: Figure 1 shows that the linear predictor yields near‑identical samples across splits and visually resembles CNN/DiT outputs.  
  - Corpus: Neighboring papers highlight the explanatory power of linear models for complex systems; direct evidence for this specific consistency mechanism is contained in the target paper.
- **Break condition**: If training splits have significantly different means or covariances (counterfactual experiment), cross‑split consistency collapses.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Finite data size renormalizes the effective noise level from \(\sigma^2\) to \(\kappa(\sigma^2) > \sigma^2\), causing over‑shrinkage toward the mean, especially along low‑variance eigenmodes.
- **Mechanism**: Random matrix theory yields the deterministic equivalence \((\hat{\Sigma}+\sigma^2 I)^{-1} \asymp (\Sigma+\kappa(\sigma^2)I)^{-1}\), where \(\kappa\) solves \(\kappa(\sigma^2)-\sigma^2 = \gamma \kappa(\sigma^2)\operatorname{tr}[\Sigma(\Sigma+\kappa(\sigma^2)I)^{-1}]\). This introduces an adaptive ridge penalty that shrinks directions with small eigenvalues more aggressively.
- **Core assumption**: High‑dimensional limit \((d,n\to\infty\) with \(\gamma=d/n\) fixed) and a well‑defined limiting spectrum for \(\Sigma\); empirical mean is assumed equal to population mean.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - Abstract: “sampling variability acts as a renormalization of the noise level through a self‑consistent relation \(\sigma^2 \mapsto \kappa(\sigma^2)\), explaining why limited data overshrink low‑variance directions.”  
  - Section 4.2: Proposition 4.1 and Figure 2C numerically confirm stronger shrinkage for lower eigenvalues.  
  - Corpus: Works such as “A Random Matrix Perspective of Echo State Networks” and “Random Matrix Theory for Stochastic Gradient Descent” independently demonstrate renormalization in other settings, validating the mathematical object \(\kappa\).
- **Break condition**: As \(n\to\infty\) or \(\gamma\to0\), \(\kappa(\sigma^2)\to\sigma^2\) and overshrinkage vanishes. The asymptotic approximation also fails when \(n\) is very small relative to \(d\) or the spectrum is heavy‑tailed.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: The variance of the denoiser (or score) across splits factorizes into three factors: anisotropy across eigenmodes (maximal where eigenvalue ≈ κ), inhomogeneity across input directions (larger deviation along high‑variance modes), and global scaling ∝ 1/n.
- **Mechanism**: Using two‑point deterministic equivalence, one obtains  
  \[
  \operatorname{Var}_{\hat{\Sigma}}[v^\top s^*(x;\sigma)] \asymp \frac{\kappa(\sigma^2)^2}{n-\operatorname{df}_2(\kappa)(\sigma^2)} \Big(v^\top(\Sigma+\kappa I)^{-2}\Sigma v\Big) \Big((x-\mu)^\top(\Sigma+\kappa I)^{-2}\Sigma (x-\mu)\Big).
  \]
  The anisotropy function \(\chi(\lambda,\kappa)=\lambda/(\lambda+\kappa)^2\) is bell‑shaped; the inhomogeneity multiplies by \((\sigma^2+\lambda)\).
- **Core assumption**: Same as Mechanisms 1–2, plus that noised samples roughly lie on the ellipsoid with radii \(\sqrt{\sigma^2+\lambda_k}\).
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  - Abstract: “our variance formulas reveal three key factors behind cross‑split disagreement: anisotropy across eigenmodes, inhomogeneity across inputs, and overall scaling with dataset size.”  
  - Section 4.3: Proposition 4.2 and Figure 3 demonstrate anisotropy (MSE vs eigenvalue) and inhomogeneity (spatial maps).  
  - Corpus: The decomposition is reminiscent of RMT‑based variance analyses in other linear systems; however, the precise factorization appears unique to this work.
- **Break condition**: The factorization relies on deterministic equivalence; it may break if the spectrum has point masses or if higher‑order moments matter (e.g., heavy tails). It is also invalid in the memorization regime where linear theory no longer describes fluctuations.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept**: Random Matrix Theory (RMT) deterministic equivalence.  
  - *Why needed here*: It replaces empirical operators \(\hat{\Sigma}\) with deterministic limits \(\Sigma_{\text{eff}}\), collapsing many‑sample randomness into a scalar \(\kappa\). This is the engine driving all analytical results.  
  - *Quick check*: What does \(A \asymp B\) mean in RMT? **Answer:** For any deterministic matrix \(C\) with bounded spectral norm, \(\operatorname{Tr}[C(A-B)]\to0\) as dimensions grow.  

- **Concept**: Linear denoiser as ridge/Wiener estimator.  
  - *Why needed here*: The optimal linear denoiser (Eq. 2) is the building block. Understanding its dependence on \(\hat{\Sigma}\) and connection to ridge regression illuminates the renormalization effect.  
  - *Quick check*: Show that \(D_{\Sigma}(x;\sigma) = \mu + \Sigma(\Sigma+\sigma^2I)^{-1}(x-\mu)\) equals the ridge regression solution under a squared‑error loss.  

- **Concept**: Fractional matrix powers via integral representation (Balakrishnan’s formula).  
  - *Why needed here*: The full sampling map involves \(\Sigma^{1/2}(\Sigma+\sigma_T^2I)^{-1/2}\). Extending deterministic equivalence to such non‑integer powers requires this tool.  
  - *Quick check*: Write the integral formula for \(A^{1/2}\) when \(A\succeq0\). **Answer:** \(A^{-1/2}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^\infty (A+tI)^{-1}t^{-1/2}dt\), hence \(A^{1/2}=A A^{-1/2}\).

- **Concept**: Diffusion model score matching and probability‑flow ODE.  
  - *Why needed here*: Sets up the problem context: consistency concerns the sampling map of the PF‑ODE.  
  - *Quick check*: What loss is minimized to train the denoiser? **Answer:** Denoising score matching (DSM) objective \(L_\sigma=\mathbb{E}\big[\|D_\theta(x_0+\sigma z;\sigma)-x_0\|^2\big]\).

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Population distribution → Empirical dataset (split) → Empirical mean/covariance (ˆμ, ˆΣ) → Linear denoiser \(D_{\hat{\Sigma}}(x;\sigma)\) → Sampling map \(S_{\hat{\Sigma}}(x_{\sigma_T}) = \mu + \hat{\Sigma}^{1/2}(\hat{\Sigma}+\sigma_T^2I)^{-1/2}(x_{\sigma_T}-\mu)\) → Renormalization function \(\kappa(\sigma^2)\) (Silverstein equation) → Two‑point deterministic equivalence (Prop 4.2) → Fractional‑power deterministic equivalence (Prop 5.2) → Analytical predictions (anisotropy, inhomogeneity, scaling).

**Critical path**:  
Start with linear denoiser (Eq. 2). Apply one‑point DE to obtain mean prediction (Prop 4.1). Use two‑point DE to derive variance of denoiser/score (Prop 4.2). Introduce integral representation for matrix square roots; combine with DE to get sampling map mean/variance (Props 5.1, 5.2). Validate by computing eigenvalues of \(\hat{\Sigma}\) (empirical), solving for \(\kappa\), and comparing predicted anisotropy/inhomogeneity against measured cross‑split deviations.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- RMT requires \(\gamma=d/n\) fixed and large; for small \(n\) or low‑\(d\) the approximation degrades.  
- The theory explains consistency in the generalization regime; it does not model memorization, which dominates at very small \(n\).  
- Computing \(\kappa(\sigma^2)\) involves solving a nonlinear equation per \(\sigma^2\); cache solutions or use continuation for efficiency.  
- Evaluating the double integral for fractional powers is numerically intensive; use quadrature with \(u=\tan\theta\) mapping to tame the infinite domain.

**Failure signatures**:  
- When \(n\) is too small (e.g., < 1 000 in experiments), deep networks memorize and deviate from linear predictions; RMT‑based anisotropy/inhomogeneity may not match.  
- Numerical instability in linear sampling when \(n\) is small: empirical covariance may have negative eigenvalues or be rank‑deficient; use float64, clip eigenvalues, or add jitter.  
- Theoretical variance scales as \(1/(n-\operatorname{df}_2(\kappa))\); if \(n\) approaches \(\operatorname{df}_2(\kappa)\) the denominator becomes small and asymptotic approximation breaks down.  
- Integral representation can suffer from quadrature errors if the integrand has sharp peaks near the origin; adaptive quadrature may be needed.

**First 3 experiments**:  
1. **Overshrinkage verification**: For a dataset (e.g., FFHQ‑32), compute \(\Sigma\) from the full set and \(\hat{\Sigma}\) from several small splits. Plot the empirical shrinkage factor \(u_k^\top \hat{\Sigma}(\hat{\Sigma}+\sigma^2I)^{-1}u_k\) vs. \(\sigma^2\) for each PC and compare to theoretical curve \(\lambda_k/(\lambda_k+\kappa(\sigma^2))\).  
2. **Anisotropy test**: Train linear denoisers on two non‑overlapping splits with the same \(n\). For various \(\sigma^2\), compute the mean squared difference between their outputs along each PC. Check that the variance matches the predicted \(\chi(\lambda_k,\kappa(\sigma^2))\) (bell‑shaped curve centered at \(\lambda\approx\kappa\)).  
3. **Inhomogeneity prediction**: For a fixed \(\sigma^2\) and a set of noised inputs, compute the RMT‑predicted variance factor \(\mathcal{I}(x)=(x-\mu)^\top(\Sigma+\kappa I)^{-2}\Sigma (x-\mu)\). Correlate this with the empirical squared deviation between two split denoisers over many seeds; expect a positive Spearman correlation (as in Fig. 5F).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question**: How does the transition from memorization to the renormalization regime depend on model capacity and architecture?  
  **Basis**: The authors state that extending the theory to random‑feature models would better explain the transition and note that the dataset size at which consistency emerges varies with architecture (e.g., DiT vs. UNet).  
  **Why unresolved**: The current linear RMT framework cannot capture memorization, and the scaling behavior of the transition (e.g., required `n` for consistency) with model complexity (depth, width, convolution vs. attention) remains unquantified.  
  **What evidence would resolve it**: Learning curves for linear, random‑feature, and deep networks across capacities on controlled datasets; theory linking model degrees of freedom to the effective `γ = d/n` in the memorization‑to‑consistency phase boundary.

- **Question**: Do nonlinearities in deep diffusion models merely scale the variance predicted by linear RMT, or do they alter the qualitative anisotropy/inhomogeneity structure?  
  **Basis**: The authors observe that absolute deviation magnitudes are much larger in deep networks than predicted by linear theory, but the qualitative patterns (anisotropy peak at `λ ≈ κ`, inhomogeneity along high‑variance modes) still hold.  
  **Why unresolved**: It is unclear whether nonlinearities introduce new spectral/spatial patterns of inconsistency or just multiplicative factors. The linear theory’s factorization into anisotropy/inhomogeneity may break down.  
  **What evidence would resolve it**: Measuring the shape of the anisotropy profile (peak location, width) and inhomogeneity map for networks with controlled nonlinearities (e.g., random ReLU features vs. trained nets); comparing against RMT predictions for nonlinear random feature models.

- **Question**: What is the role of the initial noise space’s alignment with the data covariance in determining per‑seed consistency?  
  **Basis**: The authors suggest that the alignment between the initial noise and the covariance eigenframe predicts the degree of generation consistency and show that manipulating noise amplitude along different eigenmodes affects sample quality.  
  **Why unresolved**: The theory predicts dependence on `¯x` (normalized initial noise) via `3(¯x, κ, Σ)`, but this has only been correlated pointwise across seeds. The causal role of alignment for why some “magic seeds” are more consistent is not tested.  
  **What evidence would resolve it**: Systematic experiments initializing seeds with prescribed alignments (e.g., randomized rotations of the noise basis relative to data PCAs) and measuring cross‑split MSE; deriving a theoretical bound on consistency probability as a function of the cosine similarity between noise principal components and data principal components.

- **Question**: How valid are RMT assumptions (rotational invariance, limiting spectra) for structured image data with non‑uniform spectral decay?  
  **Basis**: The analysis relies on deterministic equivalence and Marčenko–Pastur‑type asymptotics (`d, n → ∞, d/n = γ`), and uses population spectra from real datasets as limiting measures.  
  **Why unresolved**: Real image spectra have heavy‑tailed decay and non‑rotationally invariant eigenstructures (e.g., natural image statistics are anisotropic in frequency space). This may violate the uniform asymptotic assumptions, affecting the accuracy of `κ(σ²)` and the double‑integral formulas in finite‑`d`.  
  **What evidence would resolve it**: Compare RMT predictions against empirical linear denoiser statistics for model datasets with controlled spectral decay (e.g., power‑law spectra with varying exponents, or structured non‑rotationally invariant covariances); quantify deviation from asymptotic formulas as a function of `d` and spectrum tail index.

- **Question**: Do architectural inductive biases (e.g., convolution locality, attention) modify the renormalization function `κ(σ²)` or the effective degrees of freedom?  
  **Basis**: The authors note that linear surrogates underestimate variability in expressive models and do not capture architecture‑specific inductive biases; different architectures show different `n` scaling for consistency.  
  **Why unresolved**: The RMT framework treats the denoiser as trained on raw pixels; architectural constraints alter the hypothesis class, potentially changing how finite‑sample effects propagate. For example, convolutional invariance might reduce effective `d` along certain modes.  
  **What evidence would resolve it**: Train linear, CNN, and DiT models on identical data subsets and estimate the effective `κ(σ²)` by fitting the denoiser’s shrinkage profile; relate this to measures of architectural inductive bias (e.g., parameter count, alignment with data PC basis, effective rank of the Jacobian).

## Limitations
- **Finite‑sample asymptotic assumptions**: RMT results are asymptotic (`d,n→∞`, `γ=d/n` fixed). For moderate `n` or low‑dimensional data the deterministic equivalence may be crude, leading to quantitative mismatches (e.g., anisotropy peak slightly offset).
- **Unexplained variance in deep networks**: The linear theory explains qualitative trends but not all variance; the correlation between predicted and observed inhomogeneity is modest (`ρ≈0.33`), suggesting missing factors such as mean estimation error or nonlinearities.
- **Spectral assumptions for real data**: Derivation assumes a well‑behaved limiting spectrum (bounded support, no heavy tails). Real image datasets may have outliers or long tails that affect `κ` and anisotropy shape.

2. **Break‑condition test**: Construct splits with deliberately mismatched first‑two moments (e.g., by selecting disjoint class subsets or applying a linear transform to one split). Measure cross‑split MSE and confirm it rises sharply relative to matched‑moment splits.
3. **Renormalization verification**: For each split, solve the Silverstein equation for `κ(σ²)`. Re‑fit a ridge parameter on the linear denoiser’s shrinkage factors across eigenmodes and check that the effective ridge equals `κ`. Additionally, test that overshrinkage (excess shrinkage of low‑variance modes) scales with `κ−σ²`.
