# Anytime Pretraining: Horizon-Free Learning-Rate Schedules with Weight Averaging

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.03702
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03702
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Simple schedules (constant LR, 1/√t) with weight averaging match cosine decay across unknown training horizons.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the challenge of learning-rate scheduling in large language model pretraining when the total training steps are unknown. It proposes using simple, horizon-free schedules like constant or 1/√t decay combined with weight averaging (exponential moving average). Theoretical analysis for overparameterized linear regression shows these schedules achieve minimax convergence rates. Empirically, on 150M and 300M parameter models trained at 1× to 32× Chinchilla scale, these anytime schedules achieve validation losses comparable to well-tuned cosine decay across all intermediate checkpoints, effectively tracking the optimal cosine envelope without requiring horizon knowledge. The results highlight weight averaging as crucial for competitive anytime performance, offering a practical alternative for continual or open-ended training.

## Method Summary
The paper proposes anytime learning-rate schedules for LLM pretraining where the total training steps are unknown. It uses simple horizon-free schedules (constant learning rate, 1/√t decay parameterized as η√α/(t+α)) combined with weight averaging (exponential moving average with half-life f*t). The key insight is that weight averaging stabilizes training and reduces variance, enabling these simple schedules to match the minimax convergence rates of optimally tuned horizon-dependent schedules like cosine decay. Experiments train 150M and 300M parameter models using C4 dataset, comparing anytime schedules against well-tuned cosine decay across multiple compute budgets (1× to 32× Chinchilla scale).

## Key Results
- Anytime schedules with weight averaging achieve validation losses within 0.005 of the cosine envelope across all intermediate checkpoints.
- Constant LR + averaging and 1/√t + averaging match cosine decay performance without needing to know the final training horizon.
- Weight averaging is shown to be crucial, with no-averaging variants significantly underperforming the anytime methods.
- WSD (Warmup-Stable-Decay) is asymptotically equivalent to constant LR + averaging for certain spectral conditions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Weight averaging (exponential moving average) stabilizes training and reduces variance, enabling horizon-free learning rates to match the minimax convergence rates of optimally tuned horizon-dependent schedules like cosine decay.
- **Mechanism:** Averaging over past iterates implicitly implements a form of sample averaging that smooths stochastic gradient noise. Theoretically, for overparameterized linear regression, tail averaging with a polynomially decaying step size (e.g., constant or 1/√t) achieves the same bias-variance trade-off as well-tuned SGD, as shown in the bias-variance decomposition (proofs Appendix A). The averaging window effectively controls the variance term independent of the total training steps.
- **Core assumption:** The optimized model lies in a regime where the risk is variance-dominated after sufficient training (Assumption 1). The data covariance has a power-law spectrum, and the source/capacity conditions (a<b) allow a clean bias-variance separation.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "highlight the central role of weight averaging... in achieving the minimax convergence rates of stochastic gradient descent"
  - [section 1] "weight averaging—also known as model merging" and "constant learning rate with weight averaging"
  - [section 2] Related work cites Polyak and Juditsky (1992) and recent analyses (Zou et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024a) showing constant LR + averaging is minimax optimal.
  - [corpus] Neighbors like "Optimal Linear Decay..." and "Schedule-Free Methods" discuss averaging's role in variance reduction; FMR scores are moderate (~0.58).
- **Break condition:** If the underlying problem is not well-approximated by quadratic/convex dynamics (e.g., highly non-convex loss with many local minima), the theoretical equivalence may not hold, and averaging could smooth over critical learning signals.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Simple, horizon-free polynomial learning-rate schedules (e.g., η ∝ 1/√t) can track the optimal "cosine envelope" across a wide range of compute budgets because the decay rate γ is determined by intrinsic problem properties (spectral decay) rather than the horizon.
- **Mechanism:** In the bias-variance recursion for linear regression, the cumulative step size ∑ η_s governs bias contraction. A schedule like 1/√t gives ∑_{s=1}^t 1/√s ≈ √t, which scales comparably to the effective horizon at any time t. This allows the schedule to adapt its "implicit horizon" dynamically. The optimal γ* depends on source (b) and capacity (a) exponents: γ* = max{(1-a)/b, 0} (Corollary 1). If a<b, γ*=0 (constant LR); if a>b, γ*>0 (decay needed).
- **Core assumption:** The data covariance spectrum follows a power law λ_i ∝ i^{-a}, and the target signal decays as w_i^2 ∝ i^{-b} (source condition). This allows a closed-form optimal γ.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 1] Shows empirical validation loss curves where 1/√t+averaging matches cosine envelope across 1×-32× scales.
  - [section 2] References power-law spectrum analyses (Bordelon & Pehlevan, 2021; Zhang et al., 2024b).
  - [corpus] "Optimal Learning-Rate Schedules under Functional Scaling Laws" explicitly derives power decay and WSD under scaling laws, supporting the spectral dependency.
- **Break condition:** The power-law spectrum assumption may not hold for all real-world data distributions. If the effective spectral exponents change during training (e.g., due to data distribution shift), a fixed γ becomes suboptimal.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The Warmup-Stable-Decay (WSD) schedule is asymptotically equivalent to a constant learning rate with weight averaging for certain power-law spectra, explaining its empirical competitiveness.
- **Mechanism:** WSD consists of a constant phase followed by a linear decay over the final (1-ρ) fraction of training. Theorem 2 shows that, under a power-law Hessian with capacity exponent a∈(1,2) and source exponent b>1, the excess risk bound matches that of constant LR with averaging (Zhang et al., 2024a). The linear decay phase plays a role analogous to the implicit weighting of tail averaging.
- **Core assumption:** The training dynamics are well-approximated by quadratic regression; the decay phase starts at a fixed fraction ρ (e.g., 0.9) of total horizon. The spectrum satisfies a∈(1,2), which is typical for many deep learning problems.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.1] Empirically, WSD achieves comparable loss to constant LR+averaging and cosine.
  - [section 4] Theorem 2 formally derives the rate for WSD and shows equivalence to constant LR+averaging when b=a.
  - [corpus] "Through the River..." analyzes WSD/schedule-free benefits; "Straight to Zero..." studies linear decay which is a component of WSD.
- **Break condition:** If the optimal ρ is unknown (true anytime setting), WSD loses its "almost anytime" property because the decay trigger depends on knowing the horizon. Also, for spectra with a∉(1,2), the equivalence may not hold.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Bias-Variance Decomposition in Stochastic Approximation
- **Why needed here:** The paper's theory hinges on decomposing excess risk into bias (from systematic error in mean iterate) and variance (from stochastic noise). This decomposition is presented in equation (2) and used throughout proofs (Appendix A).
- **Quick check question:** Given the recursion mt+1 ≤ (I-ηtΛ)^2 mt-1 + cη_t^2 σ^2 Λ, explain how the first term contributes to bias and the second to variance.

### Concept: Power-Law Source & Capacity Conditions
- **Why needed here:** These exponents (a for covariance decay, b for signal decay) determine the minimax optimal learning rate schedule (γ*). They appear in Corollary 1 and Theorem 2.
- **Quick check question:** If λ_i ∝ i^{-a} and E[w_i^2] ∝ i^{-b} with a<b, what is the optimal γ for η_t ∝ t^{-γ}? What does this imply about the effective dimensionality?

### Concept: Stochastic Weight Averaging (SWA/EMA)
- **Why needed here:** The method uses an exponential moving average with half-life parameter f. It is the key practical technique enabling anytime performance.
- **Quick check question:** How does choosing τ_t = 1/(2^{f/t}) ensure the EMA averages approximately the last 1/f fraction of iterates? What happens if f is too small or too large?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map:
Training loop -> Learning rate scheduler (constant/1/√t/WSD) -> Optimizer (AdamW) -> EMA updater -> Checkpoint evaluator

### Critical path:
1. Initialize model, optimizer (AdamW with β_1=0.9, ε=1e-8, chosen β_2), and EMA buffers.
2. For each training step t: update parameters θ_t using learning rate η_t.
3. Update each EMA: bar_θ_t = (1-τ_t) bar_θ_{t-1} + τ_t θ_t.
4. At each checkpoint (multiple of Chinchilla tokens), evaluate all EMAs and last iterate on validation set.
5. Plot loss vs. compute for each schedule, comparing to cosine envelope.

### Design tradeoffs:
- **Averaging window (f):** Large f (long memory) reduces variance but may increase bias; small f tracks last iterate. The paper sweeps f to find any time optimal.
- **α in √α/(t+α):** Controls effective decay; α ≈ Θ(T) makes schedule nearly constant. Too small → aggressive decay; too large → no decay. Needs sweep per model scale.
- **Warmup fraction:** Fixed at 40% of 1×Chinchilla tokens for all runs. Could be tuned, but held constant for fair comparison.
- **Batch size:** Experiments at critical batch size and at 4096 (large batch). Larger batch reduces noise, making decay less necessary.

### Failure signatures:
- **Divergence at long horizons:** Constant LR without averaging may blow up if η is too large, especially beyond critical batch size.
- **Poor performance at short horizons:** If α is tuned only for long runs, 1/√t may start with too low LR, hurting early learning.
- **EMA instability:** Very small τ_t (large f) can cause EMA to change slowly, missing optimal points.
- **WSD decay mistiming:** If horizon is unknown, initiating decay at 90% of a guessed T can cause premature decay or missed opportunity.

### First 3 experiments:
1. **Baseline replication:** Train 150M model with standard cosine decay for 1×Chinchilla. Verify loss matches expected ~2.9-3.0 on C4.
2. **Anytime constant LR+EMA:** Train a single run to 32× with constant LR (sweep η∈{1e-4,3e-4,1e-3}) and multiple EMAs. Evaluate at all checkpoints 1×-32×. Expect loss within 0.005 of cosine envelope for most durations.
3. **WSD implementation:** From constant LR run above, at each checkpoint take the EMA and perform a linear decay over the final 10% of tokens. Compare loss to cosine envelope; should be comparable, but note WSD requires knowing when to stop (horizon-dependent).

## Limitations
- Empirical scale limits: Experiments use 150M and 300M parameter models, which may not capture dynamics of larger-scale pretraining.
- Spectral assumption sensitivity: Theory relies on power-law data spectra and source conditions that may not hold for all real-world data.
- Ablation granularity: Optimal EMA half-life f is swept empirically but not theoretically predicted, and may be sensitive to problem scale.

## Next Checks
1. **Spectral sensitivity test:** Repeat the 150M anytime training experiment using a synthetic dataset with a known power-law spectrum (controllable a,b). Vary a and b to verify the theoretical predictions for optimal γ in Corollary 1.
2. **Averaging window ablation:** Systematically vary the EMA half-life f on a larger model (e.g., 1B parameters) to map the optimal f range. Compare the anytime performance gap between f=0 (no averaging) and f>0.
3. **Critical batch size scaling:** Test the 300M model at the exact critical batch size (512) vs. a larger batch (e.g., 2048) to quantify the impact of noise reduction on the need for polynomial decay.
