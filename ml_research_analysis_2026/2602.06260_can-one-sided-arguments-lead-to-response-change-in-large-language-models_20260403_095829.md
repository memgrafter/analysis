# Can One-sided Arguments Lead to Response Change in Large Language Models?

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.06260  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06260  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** One-sided arguments consistently steer LLMs toward the presented viewpoint on binary polemic questions, with effectiveness depending on alignment between question format and argument presentation.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether presenting one-sided arguments can change the responses of large language models (LLMs) on controversial binary questions. Using a curated dataset of 30 polemic questions (historical, political, religious) paired with simple one-sided arguments, the authors test five LLMs across variations in question format (YES/NO vs. Agree/Disagree) and argument presentation (dialog vs. block). Results show that one-sided arguments reliably increase positive responses (agreeing with the arguments) and decrease negative ones across all models and settings. Steering is strongest when the personal stance of the question matches the personal framing of the arguments (e.g., Agree/Disagree with dialog). Switching arguments to unrelated ones reduces the effect, indicating that steering is driven by semantic content rather than superficial cues. The findings reveal LLMs' susceptibility to persuasive bias from one-sided information, with implications for alignment and safety.

## Method Summary
The study employs a curated dataset of 30 binary polemic questions (10 per topic class: historical, political, religious), each paired with 3–7 simple, non‑personal factual arguments supporting one viewpoint. Five LLMs (gpt‑oss‑120b, Llama 3.3 70B, Llama 3.1 8B, Mistral 7B, Gemma 3 4B) are evaluated under eight experimental conditions crossing question format (YES/NO, Agree/Disagree) and argument presentation (dialog, block). In the dialog setting, the model is led to believe it previously agreed with each argument; in the block setting, arguments are presented as a continuous paragraph. Baseline responses (no arguments) are collected first. For each condition, 50 responses per question are generated at temperature=0, then classified as positive (aligning with arguments), negative, or neutral. A switch experiment replaces original arguments with ones from the same or different topic class to test whether steering depends on semantic content.

## Key Results
- One-sided arguments increase positive responses and decrease negative ones across all models and settings.
- Steering is most effective when question format and argument presentation share the same personal/non‑personal stance (e.g., Agree/Disagree with dialog).
- Argument switching (within or across topic classes) significantly reduces steering, confirming the role of semantic content.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** One-sided arguments steer LLM responses more effectively when the *personal/non-personal stance* of the question format matches the *personal/non-personal presentation* of the arguments.
- **Mechanism:** The LLM mirrors the framing's stance. A "personal" question ("Agree/Disagree") predisposes the LLM to adopt a subjective, first‑person perspective. Presenting arguments in a "personal" dialog (where the LLM is told it previously agreed) aligns with this predisposition, amplifying persuasion. Conversely, a "non‑personal" question ("YES/NO") prompts objective evaluation; arguments presented as a neutral "block" text align best.
- **Core assumption:** LLM response generation is conditioned by the syntactic and pragmatic frame of the prompt, influencing whether it adopts a subjective or objective reasoning mode.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] "...steering is most effective when the personal/non‑personal stance of the question matches the argument presentation."  
  [section 3] "Thus, a YES/NO question, being non‑personal, conditions the LLM to evaluate both the question itself and its one‑sided arguments objectively and focus on providing an objective answer. Thus, the block setting works better... The opposite happens with Agree/Disagree questions..."
- **Break condition:** If steering effectiveness is *equal or higher* when personal questions are paired with block arguments (or non‑personal with dialog), the stance‑matching hypothesis is falsified.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Steering is driven primarily by the *semantic content* of the one‑sided arguments, not by spurious correlations, sycophantic tendencies, or superficial prompt patterns.
- **Mechanism:** The LLM updates its latent representation of the polemic topic based on the propositional content of the provided arguments. When arguments are switched to unrelated ones (even from the same topic class), the semantic content relevant to the original question is lost, reducing the persuasive effect.
- **Core assumption:** LLMs can distinguish between on‑topic propositional content and off‑topic or generic text, and their response shift correlates with topical relevance.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] "Switching to other arguments consistently decreases opinion steering."  
  [section 3] "Switching the one‑sided arguments... consistently decreases the positive answers... This supports the explanation that the effectiveness of one‑sided arguments is mostly due to the content of the arguments themselves, and not due to other factors such as sycophancy or spurious correlations..."
- **Break condition:** If switching arguments (within or across classes) does *not* reduce steering effectiveness, the effect is likely driven by non‑content factors (e.g., a fixed phrase trigger).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** One‑sided arguments induce a *unidirectional bias amplification* in LLM responses, increasing "positive" (argument‑aligning) responses while never increasing "negative" (opposing) responses.
- **Mechanism:** The initial response (baseline) reflects the LLM's embedded prior (from training/alignment). One‑sided arguments provide additional, salient evidence for one viewpoint. The LLM's compliance and tendency toward majority/minority view adoption in its responses results in a net shift toward the presented viewpoint, but it does not "over‑correct" to the opposite extreme.
- **Core assumption:** LLMs exhibit an asymmetric response to additional evidence, where supporting evidence for a non‑dominant viewpoint can cause a shift, but evidence for a dominant viewpoint may only reinforce.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  [abstract] "positive responses... never decrease and mostly increase. Negative responses... never increase."  
  [section 3] "Remarkably, in the vast majority of settings, the number of positive responses... dramatically increase... we observe that the number of negative responses never increases..."
- **Break condition:** If a statistically significant number of settings show an *increase* in negative responses upon presentation of one‑sided arguments supporting the *positive* viewpoint, this unidirectional amplification mechanism fails.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Polemic Questions & Stance**  
  - **Why needed here:** The entire study operationalizes "binary polemic questions" as the stimulus. Understanding that these are controversial questions with two opposing viewpoints (not factual queries) is essential to grasp why one‑sided arguments are manipulative and why neutral/balanced responses are a possible baseline.  
  - **Quick check question:** If a question "Is universal basic income a good policy?" is posed, what are the two contrasting stances an LLM might take in a polemic framing?

- **Concept: LLM Alignment & Refusal Behaviors**  
  - **Why needed here:** The paper's goal is to steer responses *past* alignment guardrails or inherent biases. Knowing that LLMs may refuse to answer controversial questions (over‑refusal) or produce a balanced multi‑viewpoint answer contextualizes what "response change" means—it's a shift from a guarded/neutral position to a committed one.  
  - **Quick check question:** Besides giving a direct "YES/NO," what are two other ways an aligned LLM might handle a polemic question?

- **Concept: Argument Framing (Dialog vs. Block)**  
  - **Why needed here:** The independent variable "how the arguments are shown" is critical. The dialog frame (simulating prior agreement) evokes a social, personal narrative; the block frame evokes an informational, objective summary. This difference is key to Mechanism 1.  
  - **Quick check question:** How would you rewrite a list of three arguments as a "dialog" prompt compared to a "block" prompt?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Dataset Module -> Prompt Engineering Module -> LLM Inference Engine -> Response Aggregator -> Switch Experiment Module -> Analysis Module

**Critical path:**  
1. Dataset creation  
2. Prompt generation for all condition combinations  
3. Baseline run (no arguments)  
4. Main experiment run (with original arguments)  
5. Switch experiment run  
6. Statistical aggregation and table generation

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Small, curated dataset** (30 topics, ~132 arguments) vs. **generalizability.** Strength: controlled, verifiable one‑sidedness. Weakness: may not capture full diversity of polemics or rhetorical complexity.  
- **Simple sentence arguments** vs. **real‑world persuasive text.** Strength: isolates argument *content* from rhetorical style. Weakness: limits ecological validity.  
- **Binary response enforcement** ("Answer only with YES/NO") vs. **free‑form generation.** Strength: enables clear metric (percentage shift). Weakness: may constrain the model's natural expression.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **Failure mode 1:** Models frequently refuse or produce non‑binary answers, inflating neutral percentages. *Diagnostic:* Check alignment behavior; simplify prompts or add a "You must answer only with …" instruction.  
- **Failure mode 2:** Dialog format fails to convey that the LLM previously agreed, leading to inconsistent interpretations. *Diagnostic:* Verify that each assistant turn in the dialog is fixed to the positive response.  
- **Failure mode 3:** Non‑deterministic outputs even at temperature=0. *Diagnostic:* Run multiple generations; enable deterministic flags if needed.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline Establishment:** For all 30 questions and 5 models, run the 4 base prompt templates *without* arguments. Record initial distribution of (YES/NO) or (Agree/Disagree) and neutral rates.  
2. **Main Steering Test:** For all 30 questions, 5 models, and 4 prompt templates, run with arguments in both Dialog and Block settings (8 conditions total). Compute percentage point change from baseline for "positive," "negative," and "neutral" responses.  
3. **Argument Switch Validation:** For a subset of conditions (e.g., most effective: Llama 3.3 70B, Agree/Disagree, Dialog), take each question's arguments and replace them with:  
   a. Arguments from a *different question* in the *same topic class*.  
   b. Arguments from a question in a *different topic class*.  
   Re‑run inference and measure delta vs. original‑argument setting. Expect significant drop in positive response delta.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Can LLM response change to one‑sided arguments serve as a reliable proxy for evaluating the convincingness and sufficiency of arguments on humans?  
2. How effective are one‑sided arguments that deliberately contain misinformation and hate speech at steering LLM responses compared to factual arguments?  
3. Which specific argumentative features (e.g., logical structure, emotional language, use of sources) are most predictive of successful steering in LLMs?  
4. Why does susceptibility to one‑sided argument steering differ across topic domains (e.g., political vs religious)?

## Limitations
- Dataset and exact prompts are not public; any reconstruction introduces variance that may affect quantitative results.
- Proprietary model (gpt‑oss‑120b) cannot be accessed, blocking full replication.
- Small, curated topic set (30 questions) may not generalize to all polemics or cultural contexts.

## Confidence
- **Steering effect exists:** High  
- **Stance‑matching enhances steering:** Medium  
- **Steering is content‑driven:** Medium  
- **Unidirectional amplification:** High  

## Next Checks
1. **Public replication package:** Authors release dataset, prompts, and evaluation code. Independent teams rerun all experiments on accessible models (e.g., GPT‑4o, Llama 3.3 70B) to verify effect sizes and statistical significance.  
2. **Argument strength manipulation:** For a subset of questions, create graded argument versions (weak/medium/strong) and test whether steering magnitude scales with strength, isolating semantic content from mere presence.  
3. **Internal state analysis:** Use probing (e.g., logit differences, attention patterns) to compare model representations after personal vs. non‑personal prompts; check for increased self‑referential or subjective markers, directly testing the stance‑matching mechanism.
