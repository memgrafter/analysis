# Beyond Experience Retrieval: Learning to Generate Utility-Optimized Structured Experience for Frozen LLMs

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.02556
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02556
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: SEAM improves pass@1 accuracy on AIME24 and AIME25 for QWEN3-4B and DeepSeek-distilled Llama, e.g., AIME24 from 0.407 to 0.504 (Qwen3-4B).

## Executive Summary
Large language models often repeat reasoning steps or mistakes. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) uses similarity-based memory lookups, which can be slow and may not provide truly useful guidance. This paper introduces SEAM, a lightweight module that generates structured, instance-specific experience in a single forward pass to steer a frozen LLM executor. SEAM is trained via executor rollouts and Group-Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), with optional post-deployment supervised fine-tuning. On mathematical reasoning benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH500, AIME24, AIME25) with two executors (Qwen3-4B, DeepSeek-R1-distilled Llama-3.1-8B), SEAM consistently improves pass@1 accuracy and reduces time-to-correct compared to direct executor training and multiple RAG baselines. Ablations confirm the need for GRPO and deployment-time evolution, and cross-domain tests show gains on code and QA tasks.

## Method Summary
SEAM is an adapter (e.g., Qwen3-0.6B) that takes a problem statement and outputs a structured experience entry with three parts: `<analysis>`, `<experience>`, and `<example>`. The target LLM (executor) remains frozen and solves the problem conditioned on this experience. Training proceeds as follows: For each problem, SEAM samples K candidate experiences. Each candidate is used to prompt the executor (which performs M rollouts, typically M=1 for efficiency). A binary reward is assigned: 1 if the executor’s answer is correct and the experience is structurally complete (all three sections present), else 0. The rewards are averaged across rollouts per candidate, then group-relative advantages are computed within the K candidates. SEAM is updated with a GRPO objective (clipped surrogate loss + KL penalty against a reference policy) while the executor stays unchanged. After deployment, SEAM can be further fine-tuned via supervised learning on logged successful (problem, experience) pairs.

## Key Results
- On Qwen3-4B, SEAM raises AIME24 pass@1 from 0.407 to 0.504 and AIME25 from 0.380 to 0.460; for DeepSeek-distilled Llama-3.1-8B, AIME24 improves from 0.357 to 0.442.
- SEAM outperforms strong RAG baselines (MEM-0, Dynamic-Cheatsheet, Memento) and direct executor GRPO training, with lower time-to-correct.
- Gains generalize modestly to code (CodeContests, MBPP) and QA (HotpotQA, Natural Questions) tasks, and accuracy scales monotonically with the size of the experience corpus.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: SEAM generates guidance optimized for executor task success rather than surface similarity.
- **Mechanism**: During training, SEAM proposes multiple candidate experience entries for a problem. Each candidate conditions the frozen executor, which performs rollouts. A binary reward (correctness + structural completeness) is computed per rollout and averaged per candidate. Group-Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) then adjusts SEAM’s probabilities to favor higher-reward candidates, directly aligning generated experience with downstream performance.
- **Core assumption**: Executor rollouts provide a reliable, differentiable signal of guidance utility; i.e., a good experience leads to correct solving with high probability.
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] “SEAM … is trained for utility via executor rollouts and GRPO”; [section 3.3] “Rollout-based evaluation … score each rollout using a binary reward … aggregated by averaging rewards”; [corpus] OPERA and Memento also leverage execution feedback, but SEAM uniquely ties generation directly to reward.
- **Break condition**: If the executor is highly stochastic, the reward signal becomes noisy; GRPO may then misestimate advantages, causing unstable training or convergence to suboptimal guidance.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Parameterizing the experience library within SEAM removes retrieval latency and avoids noise from similarity‑based selection.
- **Mechanism**: SEAM encodes all distilled experience in its weights. At inference, a single forward pass conditioned on the problem produces a tailored experience entry, eliminating embedding‑based search and external memory access.
- **Core assumption**: A compact generator can learn an accurate mapping from problem instance to the most useful experience for a specific executor.
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] “SEAM … stores experience in its parameters and generates … entry in a single forward pass”; [section 3.2] “Efficient parametric experience access… internalizes a structured experience library in its parameters”; [corpus] NA (corpus evidence weak; neighboring works still rely on external memory).
- **Break condition**: If the diversity of required experience exceeds SEAM’s capacity or the problem distribution drifts, the generated guidance may become irrelevant or outdated, necessitating retraining.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Executor‑specific training combined with a fixed output schema yields stable, actionable guidance aligned with the solver’s inductive biases.
- **Mechanism**: SEAM is trained against a particular frozen executor, so its outputs adapt to that model’s strengths/weaknesses. The experience format is strictly schema‑bound (analysis, highlights, example), providing a regularized, predictable interface for the executor.
- **Core assumption**: Different LLMs benefit from different guidance styles; a universal memory module would be less effective.
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] “maintains a dedicated parametric experience library for a particular frozen executor”; [section 3.2] “executor‑specific perspective… schema‑bound design”; [corpus] Cross‑executor transfer results (Table 5) show limited portability, confirming executor specificity.
- **Break condition**: A change in the executor (e.g., fine‑tuning) or a mismatch between schema and task requirements can render SEAM’s output poorly formulated or ignored.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Problem -> SEAM (lightweight adapter) -> Structured Experience (three-part) -> Frozen Executor -> Answer. Training loop: SEAM generates K candidates -> Executor performs M rollouts per candidate -> Compute binary rewards -> Group-relative advantages -> GRPO update (SEAM only).
- **Critical path**:
  1. For a batch of problems, SEAM samples K experiences per problem.
  2. The executor runs M rollouts (usually M=1) for each experience to produce an answer.
  3. Each rollout is scored with a binary reward (correctness + structural completeness). Rewards are averaged per candidate.
  4. Group-relative advantages are computed within the K candidates.
  5. SEAM updates via a clipped PPO-style loss with a KL penalty (β=0.001) against the reference policy; executor remains frozen.
  6. At deployment, SEAM does a single forward pass to produce an experience for a new problem; the executor then generates its answer.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - *Frozen executor* vs. joint training: freezing preserves general knowledge but limits guidance to surface-level adjustments.
  - *SEAM capacity*: Larger adapters yield richer experience but increase latency; diminishing returns with very large models.
  - *Schema rigidity*: A fixed three‑part schema ensures consistency but may not suit tasks that require alternative guidance formats.
  - *Rollout count (M)*: More rollouts provide a noisier‑reduced reward estimate but multiply training cost.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Malformed experience entries (missing required sections) that confuse the executor.
  - Negative transfer: accuracy decreases after adding SEAM guidance.
  - Training instability: GRPO loss diverges or advantage variance remains high.
  - Overfitting: SEAM excels on training distribution but fails on out-of-domain problems.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Main evaluation**: Compare SEAM (GRPO‑trained) against original executor, direct executor GRPO training, and RAG baselines (MEM‑0, Dynamic‑heatsheet, Memento) on GSM8K, MATH500, AIME24, AIME25. Expect consistent pass@1 improvements.
  2. **Ablation study**: Validate necessity of GRPO by testing SEAM without training, with SFT‑only, and with deployment‑time evolution on a held‑out stream. Expect GRPO > SFT > no training.
  3. **Efficiency profiling**: Measure training GPU‑hours, per‑instance inference latency, and time‑to‑correct across baselines. Confirm SEAM’s training efficiency (lighter than executor fine‑tuning) and competitive TTC.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Domain and model generality**: How well does SEAM generalize to domains beyond mathematical reasoning and to a diverse set of LLM executors? Current evaluation is limited to math and two executors.
- **Cross‑model/domain transfer**: Can a single SEAM instance be made adaptive to transfer across different executors and domains without retraining? SEAM currently requires executor‑ and domain‑specific training.
- **Continual learning**: How can SEAM be improved for continual learning in deployment, handling distribution shifts and catastrophic forgetting? The current SFT‑only update lacks robustness.
- **Principled deployment updates**: What are more principled deployment‑time update schemes beyond SFT on logged successes? Potential use of RL, contrastive learning, or uncertainty‑aware methods remains unexplored.

## Limitations
- **External validity**: Evaluations focus primarily on mathematical reasoning; cross‑domain results on code and QA are modest and lack comprehensive coverage.
- **Reproducibility constraints**: Missing specifics (GRPO clipping range, PPO epochs, exact reward parsing, dataset preprocessing) hinder exact replication of reported gains.

## Next Checks
- Vary the number of executor rollouts per candidate (M) during training to measure reward signal stability and its impact on final accuracy.
