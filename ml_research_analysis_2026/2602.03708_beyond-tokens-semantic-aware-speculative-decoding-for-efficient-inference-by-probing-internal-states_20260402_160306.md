# Beyond Tokens: Semantic-Aware Speculative Decoding for Efficient Inference by Probing Internal States

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.03708
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03708
- **Reference count:** 32
- **Primary result:** SemanticSpec accelerates inference for large reasoning models by up to 2.7× while maintaining comparable accuracy through semantic-aware verification of entire reasoning sequences.

## Executive Summary
Large reasoning models suffer from high inference latency due to long chains of thought. Standard speculative decoding verifies drafts token-by-token, rejecting many lexically different but semantically valid proposals. SemanticSpec addresses this by drafting entire reasoning segments (split by '\n\n') and verifying them using a semantic probability predictor that probes the target model's hidden states to estimate the likelihood of the draft's meaning. This semantic-aware acceptance criterion (min(1, p<sub>target</sub>/p<sub>draft</sub>)) reduces rejection of valid drafts. Across four reasoning benchmarks, SemanticSpec achieves up to 2.7× speedup on DeepSeekR1-32B and 2.1× on QwQ-32B over standard decoding, improves Pass@1 by up to 17.3% versus token-level baselines, and cuts latency by ~40–45% with only a minor (~4%) accuracy drop.

## Method Summary
SemanticSpec operates in two phases. Offline, it trains two semantic probability predictors (one for the draft model, one for the target) using outputs sampled from each model. For a given context, N outputs are clustered via bidirectional entailment (using DeBERTa) to group semantically equivalent sequences; the cluster frequency defines the empirical semantic probability. A multi-layer perceptron is then trained to map average-pooled hidden states from all transformer layers to this probability. During online inference, the draft model autoregressively generates γ candidate sequences. The target model processes all drafts in parallel, extracting its hidden states. Both models' hidden states are fed to their respective predictors to obtain semantic probabilities. Each draft sequence is accepted with probability min(1, p<sub>target</sub>/p<sub>draft</sub>); accepted sequences are appended to the context, while rejection triggers one autoregressive token generation from the target and terminates further drafting for that step.

## Key Results
- **Speedup:** Up to 2.7× on DeepSeekR1-32B and 2.1× on QwQ-32B versus standard autoregressive decoding.
- **Accuracy:** Improves Pass@1 by up to 17.3% over token-level SpecSampling; accuracy drop relative to target model is ~4% on average.
- **Latency:** Reduces overall inference latency by approximately 40–45% while preserving comparable solution quality.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** SemanticSpec reduces rejection of valid drafts by verifying semantic equivalence instead of exact token matches.
- **Mechanism:** The framework drafts entire reasoning sequences (e.g., split by '\n\n') and verifies them using a semantic probability predictor that probes the target model's hidden states to estimate the likelihood of the drafted sequence's *meaning*, not its specific tokens. Acceptance is based on the ratio `min(1, p_target_semantic / p_draft_semantic)`.
- **Core assumption:** The target model's internal representations (hidden states) contain information that correlates with the probability of generating any sequence expressing a particular semantic meaning, and this relationship can be learned by a predictor.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states verification uses hidden states to estimate likelihood of meaning; Section 3.2 describes learning a mapping from internal representations to semantic probability.
- **Break condition:** If hidden state representations do not cluster meaningfully by semantic probability or if the predictor fails to generalize, verification collapses to token-level performance.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The semantic probability predictor can be trained offline using clustered model outputs and their aggregated hidden states.
- **Mechanism:** For a given context, the target model generates multiple outputs (`N` sequences). These are clustered into semantic equivalence groups using bidirectional entailment (via DeBERTa). The semantic probability for any sequence in a cluster is estimated as `|cluster_size| / N`. The predictor (a multi-layer perceptron) is trained to map the average-pooled hidden states from *all layers* of the model (when processing a sequence) to this empirical semantic probability.
- **Core assumption:** Clustering via entailment reliably groups sequences by semantic meaning, and cluster frequency approximates the model's intrinsic probability distribution over meanings.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 3.2.1 details the offline pipeline; Section 5.2 ablation shows all-layer hidden states improve accuracy over last-layer only.
- **Break condition:** If entailment-based clustering is noisy or sampled outputs do not cover the semantic distribution, training labels become inaccurate, degrading predictor performance.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The sequence-level, semantic-aware acceptance rule allows efficient drafting of multi-token reasoning steps while maintaining output distribution correctness.
- **Mechanism:** During online inference, a draft model produces `γ` sequences. For each, the target model runs a parallel forward pass to get hidden states. Both models' hidden states for each sequence are fed to their predictors to get `q_i` (draft) and `p_i` (target) semantic probabilities. A draft sequence `s_i` is accepted with probability `min(1, p_i / q_i)`. If rejected, the target model generates the next token autoregressively and halts further draft verification for that step.
- **Core assumption:** The acceptance criterion on semantic probabilities preserves the target model's output distribution (as in standard speculative decoding) while operating at sequence granularity, requiring well-calibrated and consistent predictors.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 3.1 defines the acceptance rule; Section 5.1 shows comparable Pass@1 to target model (e.g., -4.9% drop), supporting distributional preservation.
- **Break condition:** If predictors are miscalibrated relative to each other (e.g., draft overestimates), acceptance probability becomes biased, altering output distribution or reducing acceptance rates.

- **Concept: Speculative Decoding (Token-Level)**
  - **Why needed:** SemanticSpec extends the foundational speculative decoding paradigm; understanding the token-draft/token-verify loop and the `min(1, p/q)` rule is prerequisite.
  - **Quick check:** What is the primary purpose of the `min(1, p_draft(x)/p_target(x))` acceptance criterion, and how does a rejection affect generation?

- **Concept: Semantic Equivalence & Entailment**
  - **Why needed:** Core of SemanticSpec is judging if different token sequences mean the same thing, typically via bidirectional entailment.
  - **Quick check:** Why is bidirectional entailment (s entails s' AND s' entails s) a stricter and more appropriate criterion for clustering reasoning steps than simple text similarity or single-direction entailment?

- **Concept: Hidden State Probing for Model Confidence**
  - **Why needed:** The predictor's input is the model's internal representations, assumed to encode information about confidence in a meaning, connecting to prior work on uncertainty estimation.
  - **Quick check:** What is a key difference between using the *last layer's* hidden state versus *averaging all layers'* hidden states for predicting semantic probability, and what trade-off does this illustrate?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Draft Model (Mq) generates candidate sequences → Target Model (Mp) processes drafts in parallel to extract hidden states → Semantic Probability Predictors (Predictor_q, Predictor_p) estimate probabilities → Online Verification Controller applies acceptance rule and orchestrates drafting/regeneration.

### Critical path
1. Offline: Train predictors for specific model pairs using sampled outputs, entailment clustering, and regression on pooled hidden states.
2. Online (per step): Draft `γ` sequences with Mq → Run Mp in parallel on each draft → Extract h<sup>p</sup> and h<sup>q</sup> → Predictors infer q<sub>i</sub> and p<sub>i</sub> → Accept/reject each sequence with min(1, p<sub>i</sub>/q<sub>i</sub>); on rejection, generate one token from Mp and halt.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Granularity (`γ` & splitting):** Larger `γ` or finer splits (e.g., sentences) increase verification overhead and risk multi-semantic sequences, reducing acceptance rate.
- **Predictor Input:** Using hidden states from *all layers* improves accuracy but adds cost vs. using only the last layer (accuracy vs. throughput trade-off).
- **Predictor Training Domain:** Training on math vs. science yields specialized predictors; cross-domain use causes performance drops (specialization vs. generalizability).

### Failure signatures
- **Low Acceptance Rate:** Consistently low p<sub>i</sub>/q<sub>i</sub> ratios indicate semantic misalignment; check predictor calibration, draft model quality, or sequence length.
- **High Latency / Low TPS:** Overhead from predictor and parallel verification outweighs gains; likely `γ` too large or predictor too complex.
- **Accuracy Drop (Pass@1):** Excess drop (>5%) suggests miscalibrated predictors or acceptance criterion distorting distribution; compare ablations (Random, Last-hidden).

### First 3 experiments
1. **Predictor Sanity Check:** On held-out training data, measure correlation (e.g., R²) between predicted and true empirical semantic probabilities; aim for >0.8.
2. **Baseline Comparison:** Run SemanticSpec against SpecSampling (token-level) and SpecReason on one benchmark (e.g., Math-500); verify >2× speedup and comparable Pass@1.
3. **Sensitivity Analysis:** Vary `γ` (1,2,3) and splitting strategy (`\n\n` vs. sentence punctuation) on one benchmark; confirm smaller `γ` and coarser splits improve TPS, document Pass@1/TPS tradeoff.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Generalization:** Does SemanticSpec generalize to diverse LLMs and tasks beyond the tested reasoning benchmarks? The paper admits findings may not generalize to other models and calls for more evaluation.
2. **Predictor Efficiency:** Can the predictor be more efficient (e.g., using fewer layers) without sacrificing acceptance rates? The Last-hidden ablation shows a trade-off between TPS and Pass@1.
3. **Splitting Granularity:** What splitting granularity optimizes performance, and does it vary by domain? Appendix A.2 shows finer splits improve Pass@1 but reduce TPS.
4. **Cross-Domain Generalization:** How far does the predictor generalize across domains, and what causes degradation? Table 2 shows minor cross-domain drop, but larger shifts (e.g., code, law) may require retraining.

## Limitations
- **Hidden State Extraction Protocol:** The paper ambiguously states "use hidden states from all layers" but does not specify pooling strategy (e.g., average over tokens and layers), critical for replication.
- **Drafting Procedure for γ>1:** Algorithm 1 does not clarify whether γ sequences are generated in parallel from a fixed context or sequentially with updated context, affecting semantics and speed.
- **Predictor Training Fidelity:** The pipeline relies on entailment-based clustering and cluster-frequency probability estimation; exact confidence thresholds and sensitivity to noisy clusters are unreported.
