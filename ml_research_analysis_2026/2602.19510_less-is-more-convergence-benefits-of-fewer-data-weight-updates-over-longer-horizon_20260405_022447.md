# Less is More: Convergence Benefits of Fewer Data Weight Updates over Longer Horizon

## Quick Facts
* **arXiv ID**: 2602.19510
* **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19510
* **Reference count**: 40
* **Primary result**: Under a fixed total parameter update budget \(N\), there exists an optimal unrolling horizon \(T\) that minimizes validation error; fewer weight updates with a longer horizon (i.e., larger \(T\)) minimize the reducible convergence error. Specifically, for deterministic full gradients \(T^* = \Theta(\log N)\); for stochastic gradients \(T^* = \Theta(\sqrt{N \log N})\).

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses data mixing—optimizing domain weights to minimize validation loss—within a bilevel optimization framework. The standard greedy approach updates weights after a single inner parameter step (\(T=1\)). The authors theoretically and experimentally investigate the effect of the finite unrolling horizon \(T\) under a fixed compute budget \(N\) (total parameter updates). Their analysis reveals that \(T=1\) can converge to suboptimal weights, even in a simple quadratic setting. Under strong convexity assumptions, they prove that the optimal \(T\) scales as \(\Theta(\log N)\) with full gradients and as \(\Theta(\sqrt{N \log N})\) with stochastic gradients, balancing hypergradient accuracy and optimization error. Empirical validation on multi-domain MNIST shows a U-shaped relation between validation loss and \(T\), supporting the "less is more" principle: updating weights less frequently over a longer horizon improves convergence.

## Method Summary
The method solves the bilevel data mixing problem by unrolling the inner optimization for \(T\) steps. At each outer round \(k\), starting from \(\theta_{k,0}\), the algorithm performs \(T\) stochastic gradient descent steps on the training loss \(L_T(\theta, w_k)\) using step size \(\eta\), yielding \(\theta_{k,T}\). Simultaneously, it maintains auxiliary vectors \(u^{(j)}_{k,t}\) for each domain \(j\), updated via \(u^{(j)}_{k,t+1} = (I - \eta H_k) u^{(j)}_{k,t} + \nabla \ell_j(\theta_{k,t})\), where \(H_k\) is an isotropic Hessian approximation \(H_k = \gamma I\). The hypergradient for domain \(j\) is estimated as \(g^{(j)}_{k,T} = -\eta \langle \nabla L_V(\theta_{k,T}), u^{(j)}_{k,T} \rangle\). After computing these, the domain weights \(w\) are updated using mirror descent on the simplex: \(w^{(j)}_{k+1} = \frac{w^{(j)}_k \exp(-\alpha g^{(j)}_{k,T})}{\sum_s w^{(s)}_k \exp(-\alpha g^{(s)}_{k,T})}\). The total parameter update budget is fixed as \(N = K \cdot T\), where \(K\) is the number of outer rounds. The trade-off between \(K\) (optimization error) and \(T\) (hypergradient bias) defines an optimal \(T^*\).

## Key Results
- Greedy \(T=1\) can converge to suboptimal domain weights, as shown by a quadratic example where the aligned domain is ignored.
- Theoretical analysis under strong convexity yields optimal horizon \(T^* = \Theta(\log N)\) for deterministic full gradients and \(T^* = \Theta(\sqrt{N \log N})\) for stochastic gradients.
- Multi-domain MNIST experiments reveal a U‑shaped validation loss vs. \(T\): high for small \(T\), decreasing to a minimum at moderate (sublinear) \(T\), then increasing when \(T\) is too large.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Hypergradient Truncation Bias Decay
- **Claim**: The error from truncating the unrolling horizon \(T\) decays exponentially in \(T\) due to the contractivity of the Hessian inverse, making longer \(T\) yield more accurate hypergradients.
- **Mechanism**: The exact hypergradient \(\nabla F(w)\) requires the inverse Hessian at the inner optimum, represented as a Neumann series \(\eta \sum_{i=0}^{\infty} (I-\eta H^*)^i\). The algorithm (Alg. 2/3) substitutes the approximate Hessian \(H_k\) and truncates the sum at \(T\) while using gradients from the finite trajectory \(\{\theta_{k,t}\}_0^{T-1}\). The tail (terms \(i \ge T\)) is bounded by \((1-\eta\mu)^{T}\) because \((I-\eta H^*)\) is contractive (spectral radius \(\le 1-\eta\mu\)) under strong convexity (\(\mu > 0\)). The trajectory error (difference between \(\theta_{k,T}\) and \(\theta^*_k\) plus Hessian approximation error \(\delta\)) also decays at a similar rate due to linear convergence of gradient descent on strongly convex functions. Thus the net hypergradient error scales as \(O(\eta T (1-\eta\mu)^{T} + \delta)\), which decays near‑exponentially until the linear term dominates, leading to an optimal \(T\) that grows only logarithmically with total updates \(N\).
- **Core assumption**: Per‑domain losses are \(\mu\)‑strongly convex and \(L\)‑smooth; validation loss \(L_V\) is \(G_V\)‑Lipschitz and \(L_V\)‑smooth; Hessian approximator satisfies \(\|H(w)-H^*(w)\|_{\text{op}} \le \delta\) and \(\tilde\mu I \preceq H(w) \preceq \tilde L I\).
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "under strong convexity assumptions, the optimal \(T\) scales as \(\Theta(\log N)\) with full gradients."
  - [section] Theorem 6.4 and its proof (Section C) explicitly bound the hypergradient error: (I) \(\sim \frac{c_1}{\sqrt{T}\sqrt{N}}\) and (II) \((1-\eta\mu)^{T-1}(c_2\eta T + c_3)\); the tail decay \((1-\eta\mu)^{T}\) is derived from the Neumann series.
  - [corpus] Unanchored from corpus: neighbors do not address hypergradient truncation bias in bilevel data mixing.
- **Break condition**: If strong convexity fails (e.g., non‑convex inner losses), the contractivity argument breaks; the tail may no longer decay exponentially, and \(T = \Theta(1)\) could remain optimal or even detrimental.

### Mechanism 2: Stochastic Noise and Slower Decay
- **Claim**: In the stochastic setting, the expected hypergradient error decays only as \(O(\sqrt{\log T / T})\) because gradient variance accumulates linearly over the unrolled trajectory, forcing a larger optimal \(T = \Theta(\sqrt{N \log N})\).
- **Mechanism**: Stochastic gradients add zero‑mean noise with variance \(\sigma^2\). The error bound for the expected hypergradient now contains an extra term proportional to \(\sqrt{\eta\sigma^2 T}\) (arising from summed noise across \(T\) steps). With step‑size tuned as \(\eta = \Theta((\log T)/(\mu T))\), the reducible error becomes \(O(\sqrt{\log T / T}) + O(1/\sqrt{K})\). Balancing the optimization error \(O(1/\sqrt{K} = O(\sqrt{T/N}))\) yields \(T^* = \Theta(\sqrt{N \log N})\). The noise‑induced term dominates over the deterministic exponential decay, explaining the slower growth.
- **Core assumption**: Unbiased stochastic gradients with bounded variance \(\sigma^2\); inner losses strongly convex and smooth; step‑size \(\eta\) chosen in the range \(\Theta((\log T)/(\mu T))\); \(T\) large enough (\(\ge \Omega(1)\) w.r.t. \(N\)).
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "Θ(\(\sqrt{N \log N}\)) with stochastic gradients."
  - [section] Theorem 6.7 and its proof (Section D): the reducible error term (II) is \(O(\sqrt{\log T / T})\) (equation (12)); detailed bound in Theorem D.1 includes \(\sigma\left( \frac{L_{GV}}{\mu^2} + \frac{L_V \tilde G}{\mu \tilde\mu} \right)\sqrt{\log T / T}\).
  - [corpus] Unanchored from corpus: neighboring papers study SGD or bilevel optimization but not the scaling of truncation horizon in stochastic bilevel data mixing.
- **Break condition**: Heavy‑tailed gradient noise or unbounded variance could invalidate the \(O(\sqrt{\log T / T})\) rate, possibly requiring more aggressive step‑size tuning or different \(T\) scaling.

### Mechanism 3: Trade‑off Between Optimization and Hypergradient Error
- **Claim**: Under a fixed parameter update budget \(N\), there exists a non‑trivial optimal horizon \(T^*\) that minimizes the total error, enacting the "less is more" principle: fewer, more accurate weight updates outperform frequent, myopic updates.
- **Mechanism**: Total reducible error = optimization error \(\approx 1/\sqrt{K}\) (convex convergence of mirror descent on \(F(w)\)) plus hypergradient error (decays with \(T\)). Since \(K = N/T\), the first term scales like \(\sqrt{T/N}\). In the deterministic case hypergradient error decays near‑exponentially, so the sum is minimized when \(T = \Theta(\log N)\); in the stochastic case it decays as \(\sqrt{\log T / T}\), yielding \(T = \Theta(\sqrt{N \log N})\). At \(T = \Theta(1)\), hypergradient error is large; at \(T \approx N\), optimization error dominates. The sweet spot balances both.
- **Core assumption**: Outer objective \(F(w)\) is convex in \(w\); inner losses strongly convex; stepsizes \(\alpha, \eta\) are appropriately chosen relative to \(K, T\); Hessian approximation error \(\delta\) irreducible.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract] "fewer weight updates with a longer horizon (i.e., larger \(T\)) minimize the reducible convergence error."
  - [section] Theorem C.1 (deterministic) minimizes \(E(T)\) and gives optimal \(T = \Theta(\log N)\); Theorem D.1 (stochastic) gives \(T = \Theta(\sqrt{N \log N})\). The derivations explicitly equate the decreasing optimization error and the decreasing hypergradient error.
  - [corpus] Unanchored from corpus: while related works like DoGE and PIKE use \(T=1\), they do not theoretically analyze the trade‑off; this paper provides the scaling laws.
- **Break condition**: If \(F(w)\) is non‑convex, the \(1/\sqrt{K}\) rate may worsen, altering the optimal \(T\). Also, if the Hessian approximation error \(\delta\) is large, the irreducible term may dominate and increasing \(T\) yields diminishing returns.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Bilevel Optimization
- **Why needed here**: The data mixing problem is cast as a bilevel program: outer minimization over domain weights \(w\) to minimize validation loss \(L_V(\theta)\), subject to inner minimization \(\theta^*(w) = \arg\min_\theta L_T(\theta, w)\). Understanding this hierarchy is essential to derive the hypergradient and the unrolled algorithm.
- **Quick check question**: What is the implicit function theorem formula for \(\nabla F(w)\) and why does it require full convergence of the inner loop?

### Concept: Hypergradient
- **Why needed here**: The outer update requires the gradient of \(F\) with respect to \(w\), computed via differentiating through the inner optimization trajectory. This hypergradient is approximated by unrolling \(T\) steps, and its error drives the analysis.
- **Quick check question**: How does the recursive formula for \(\partial \theta_{k,t} / \partial w_k\) (Equation 5) reflect the accumulation of weight influence along the trajectory?

### Concept: Strong Convexity & Smoothness
- **Why needed here**: These properties ensure linear convergence of gradient descent and the contractivity of \((I-\eta \nabla^2 L_T)\). They are used to bound both the inner iterate error \(\|\theta_{k,0} - \theta_k^*\|\) and the tail of the Neumann series, leading to exponential decay in the truncation error.
- **Quick check question**: How does strong convexity (\(\mu\)) appear in the contraction factor \((1-\eta\mu)\) and in the convergence rate of gradient descent?

### Concept: Mirror Descent on the Simplex
- **Why needed here**: The weight update uses mirror descent with KL divergence, which naturally handles simplex constraints and provides a \(1/\sqrt{K}\) convergence rate under bounded gradient magnitudes. The step‑size \(\alpha\) is tuned to balance the outer optimization error.
- **Quick check question**: Why does the analysis in Lemma C.2 lead to a bound \(\frac{\log m}{K\alpha} + \frac{\alpha G_{\max}^2}{2}K\) and how is \(\alpha\) chosen optimally?

### Concept: Unrolling and Truncation Bias
- **Why needed here**: The practical algorithm approximates the exact hypergradient by limiting the unrolling depth \(T\). Understanding the bias introduced by this finite horizon is central to the "less is more" result.
- **Quick check question**: What are the two main sources of hypergradient error identified in Section 6.4, and how do they scale with \(T\)?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:
  - **Inner optimizer**: Updates \(\theta\) via SGD on the weighted training loss.
  - **Hessian approximator**: Provides \(H_k\) (e.g., isotropic \(\gamma I\) or a true Hessian) used in the auxiliary \(u\) update.
  - **Auxiliary vectors \(u^{(j)}_{k,t}\)**: Approximate the sum \(\sum_{i=0}^{T-1} (I-\eta H_k)^{T-1-i} \nabla \ell_j(\theta_{k,i})\); act as a running (momentum‑like) average.
  - **Hypergradient estimator**: Computes \(g^{(j)}_{k,T} = -\eta \langle \nabla L_V(\theta_{k,T}), u^{(j)}_{k,T} \rangle\).
  - **Weight updater**: Mirror descent on simplex: \(w^{(j)}_{k+1} = w^{(j)}_k \exp(-\alpha g^{(j)}_{k,T}) / \sum_s w^{(s)}_k \exp(-\alpha g^{(s)}_{k,T})\).
- **Critical path** (per round \(k\)):
  1. Obtain \(H_k = H(w_k)\).
  2. For \(t = 0\) to \(T-1\):
     - Update \(\theta_{k,t+1} = \theta_{k,t} - \eta \nabla_\theta L_T(\theta_{k,t}, w_k)\).
     - For each domain \(j\): update \(u^{(j)}_{k,t+1} = (I - \eta H_k) u^{(j)}_{k,t} + \nabla \ell_j(\theta_{k,t})\).
  3. Compute hypergradients \(g_{k,T}\) from \(\nabla L_V(\theta_{k,T})\) and \(u^{(j)}_{k,T}\).
  4. Update weights \(w_{k+1}\) via mirror descent.
  5. Set \(\theta_{k+1,0} = \theta_{k,T}\).
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - **Lookahead \(T\) vs. number of weight updates \(K = N/T\)**:
    - Smaller \(T\): more frequent weight updates (larger \(K\)), lower optimization error (\(\approx 1/\sqrt{K}\)) but hypergradient more biased (large truncation error).
    - Larger \(T\): fewer weight updates (smaller \(K\)), hypergradient more accurate (exponential decay in deterministic case, slower in stochastic) but optimization error larger.
  - **Stepsizes**: \(\eta\) must be small enough for strong convexity guarantees (\(\eta < \min(1/\tilde L, \mu/L^2)\)) but large enough to avoid overly slow convergence; in stochastic case \(\eta = \Theta((\log T)/(\mu T))\).
  - **Hessian approximation fidelity**: Using a full Hessian gives best accuracy but is costly; isotropic approximation \(H_k = \gamma I\) is cheap and works empirically (Section E). The approximation error \(\delta\) contributes an irreducible term.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - **Greedy failure (\(T=1\))**: Weights converge to suboptimal values (e.g., \(w_2 \to 0\) in MNIST despite rotated domain matching validation) because hypergradient is myopic and misled by immediate loss reductions.
  - **Too large \(T\)**: Performance degrades as weight updates become too infrequent; the algorithm cannot track changing optimal mixture.
  - **Numerical instability**: If \(\eta\) is too large or \(H_k\) is ill‑conditioned, the recursion for \(u_{k,t}\) may become unstable; using approximate Hessian with lower bound \(\tilde\mu I\) helps.
  - **Noise explosion**: In stochastic case, large \(\alpha\) amplifies gradient noise; mirror descent step‑size must scale as \(1/\sqrt{K}\).
- **First 3 experiments** (as conducted in the paper):
  1. **Quadratic toy example (Section 5)**: Show that with \(T=1\) and starting far from optimum, weights converge to the wrong domain (\(w \to 0\)), while \(T = \Theta(\log R)\) correctly recovers \(w^* = 1\).
  2. **Multi‑domain MNIST (Section 7)**: Train a CNN on three domains (vanilla, rotated, noisy) with validation being rotated. Sweep \(T\) for \(N \in \{1000, 5000\}\). Observe U‑shaped validation loss: high for small \(T\), minimum at moderate (sublinear) \(T\). Corresponding weight \(w_2\) (rotated domain) increases with \(T\) up to a point.
  3. **Scaling verification**: By varying total budget \(N\) and measuring optimal \(T\), confirm the predicted scaling: optimal \(T\) grows logarithmically with \(N\) in deterministic setting; in stochastic setting it grows slightly faster (sublinear). The paper’s plots for two \(N\) values already hint at this trend.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question**: Does the optimal T scaling (\(\Theta(\log N)\) deterministic, \(\Theta(\sqrt{N \log N})\) stochastic) extend to non‑convex per‑domain losses?
- **Basis in paper**: The authors state: "Our results in this paper are under the assumption that the per‑domain losses are strongly convex... it is not clear how this can be done, and so, we leave this analysis for future work."
- **Why unresolved**: Strong convexity ensures a unique minimizer \(\theta^*(w)\) and linear convergence of the inner loop, both crucial for bounding hypergradient error. Without strong convexity, these guarantees break down, requiring new analytical tools.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A convergence proof under milder conditions (e.g., L‑smoothness and Polyak‑Łojasiewicz inequality) showing appropriate scaling of T, plus experimental validation on training deep neural networks where the loss landscape is highly non‑convex.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Do the derived scaling laws hold when training large‑scale language models on massive, heterogeneous datasets?
- **Basis in paper**: The authors note: "we would like to conduct experiments on larger models and datasets in the future."
- **Why unresolved**: Current experiments use a small CNN on MNIST. Large models (e.g., billion‑parameter transformers) exhibit different optimization dynamics, memory constraints, and domain statistics that may alter the optimal T.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Systematic experiments with transformer models trained on large mixtures (e.g., The Pile), varying T under a fixed parameter update budget N, and checking whether validation loss follows the predicted trends.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: How does the optimal horizon T change if weight updates (outer loop) are computationally more expensive than parameter updates (inner loop)?
- **Basis in paper**: The analysis assumes a fixed parameter update budget N, treating each inner step as unit cost, but ignores that hypergradient computation often requires extra operations (e.g., Hessian‑vector products) that are more costly.
- **Why unresolved**: If outer updates are costlier, the effective trade‑off between the number of inner steps per weight update shifts. The derived T = Θ(log N) is based on equal costs and may be suboptimal under a time budget.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Extend the theoretical model to include a cost ratio c (outer relative to inner), derive the new optimal T(N, c), and empirically compare wall‑clock performance using Hessian approximations with varying c (e.g., cheap isotropic vs. more accurate but expensive methods).

## Limitations
- Strong convexity assumption for per‑domain losses may not hold in practice (e.g., deep neural networks).
- Hypergradient approximation depends on Hessian approximation error \(\delta\), which is hard to quantify and may affect optimal \(T\).
- Theoretical analysis assumes bounded i.i.d. stochastic gradient noise; real mini‑batch noise may violate assumptions and change scaling.

## Confidence
- **High**: Theoretical scaling laws (\(T^* = \Theta(\log N)\) deterministic, \(T^* = \Theta(\sqrt{N \log N})\) stochastic) under strong convexity/smoothness assumptions are rigorously proved.
- **Medium**: Empirical observation of a U‑shaped validation curve vs. \(T\) on multi‑domain MNIST supports the existence of an optimal finite \(T\).
- **Low**: Generalization of the “less is more” principle to non‑convex deep learning; sufficiency of isotropic Hessian approximator for accurate hypergradients.
