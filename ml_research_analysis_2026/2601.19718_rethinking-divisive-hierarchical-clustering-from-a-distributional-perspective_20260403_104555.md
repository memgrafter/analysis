# Rethinking Divisive Hierarchical Clustering from a Distributional Perspective

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.19718
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.19718
- Reference count: 13
- Primary result: H-KC yields biologically consistent dendrograms with Dendrogram Purity 0.75 on HER2 tumor dataset (vs. 0.55–0.58 for baselines) and 0.98 on an artificial dataset, while guaranteeing a lower bound on Total Similarity of Clusters.

## Executive Summary
Divisive hierarchical clustering (DHC) methods traditionally rely on set-oriented bisecting criteria, which often fail to produce dendrograms that satisfy three key desiderata: avoiding unwarranted splits, grouping similar clusters, and corresponding to ground-truth structure. This paper introduces H-KC, a novel DHC approach that treats clusters as probability distributions and employs a distributional kernel to measure cluster similarity. At each step, H-KC bisects a cluster to maximize the Total Similarity of Clusters (TSC), the sum of pairwise similarities among all clusters at that level. This objective directly encourages coherent grouping and prevents unnecessary fragmentation. The authors prove a theoretical lower bound on the TSC achieved by H-KC’s dendrogram, ensuring a minimal level of cluster cohesion. Evaluated on Spatial Transcriptomics data, H-KC constructs dendrograms that align with biological knowledge where existing methods falter, achieving Dendrogram Purity scores of 0.75 and 0.98 on real and artificial datasets, respectively. Moreover, H-KC operates in O(kn + s²) time, making it scalable to large datasets.

## Method Summary
H-KC reimagines each cluster as an empirical distribution over feature space, lifting the set representation to a distributional one. The method employs a positive definite kernel on distributions—such as the kernel induced by the maximum mean discrepancy—to compute pairwise similarities between clusters. Starting with all points in one cluster, the algorithm recursively selects a cluster to split by optimizing the TSC objective: the bisection is chosen to maximize the sum of similarities among the resulting child clusters and all other clusters at that level. This global objective ensures that every split contributes positively to the overall similarity structure. The bisection itself is carried out by clustering the distribution embeddings (e.g., via k-means) to obtain subclusters, after which their distribution representations are updated. The recursion continues until each leaf contains a single point, building a binary dendrogram. The theoretical analysis shows that the TSC of the final dendrogram is at least a constant factor of the optimum, providing a quality guarantee independent of data distribution. The algorithm’s computational complexity is linear in the number of points and clusters, with an additional quadratic term in the dimension of the kernel embedding (s), which is typically small.

## Key Results
- Dendrogram Purity of 0.75 on HER2 tumor Spatial Transcriptomics dataset, surpassing traditional DHC baselines (0.55–0.58).
- Near-perfect Dendrogram Purity of 0.98 on an artificial dataset with ground-truth hierarchy.
- Provable lower bound on Total Similarity of Clusters and linear time complexity O(kn + s²).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The superiority of H-KC stems from its distributional perspective. First, representing clusters as distributions captures not only central tendencies but also spread and higher-order moments, enabling a richer similarity measure via kernels. Second, the TSC objective directly optimizes the overall similarity network among clusters at each split, which naturally discourages unwarranted fragmentation (since splitting a coherent distribution would reduce overall similarity) and encourages grouping of similar clusters together. This contrasts with traditional criteria based on intra-cluster variance or density, which myopically optimize local properties and can produce imbalanced or biologically irrelevant splits. Third, the theoretical lower bound on TSC provides a performance guarantee that holds for any dataset, ensuring that the dendrogram never collapses into a degenerate structure. Together, these factors yield dendrograms that are both mathematically sound and biologically interpretable.

## Foundational Learning
- **Divisive Hierarchical Clustering (DHC)**: A top-down clustering paradigm that recursively splits clusters to build a hierarchy. *Why needed*: Many applications (e.g., phylogenetics, document taxonomy) require nested clusterings rather than flat partitions. *Quick check*: How does divisive clustering differ from agglomerative clustering in terms of control over hierarchy shape?
- **Distributional Clustering**: Treats each cluster as a probability distribution over features rather than a set of points. *Why needed*: Sets discard density information; distributions allow principled comparison via measures like KL divergence or kernel embeddings. *Quick check*: How would you construct an empirical distribution for a cluster of points in ℝ^d?
- **Kernel Methods for Distribution Comparison**: Defines similarity between distributions through positive definite kernels, often by embedding distributions into a Hilbert space (e.g., mean embedding). *Why needed*: Enables flexible, potentially non-linear similarity measures while retaining computational tractability via the kernel trick. *Quick check*: What is the kernel mean embedding and why is it useful for comparing distributions without explicitly computing densities?
- **Total Similarity of Clusters (TSC)**: The sum of pairwise kernel similarities across all clusters at a given level of the hierarchy. *Why needed*: Serves as a global objective for divisive splitting; maximizing TSC promotes a coherent overall clustering structure. *Quick check*: Given cluster similarities s_ij, how is TSC computed? ## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map** (plain text):
Data X -> Compute Distribution Embeddings (e.g., mean in RKHS) -> Build Pairwise Kernel Matrix for Current Clusters -> Perform Bisection via TSC Maximization (cluster embeddings to split) -> Recursively apply to child clusters -> Assemble Dendrogram.

**Critical Path**: Initial distribution embedding → kernel matrix construction → TSC-optimal bisection decision → recursion termination. The kernel computation and bisection optimization dominate runtime.

**Design Tradeoffs**: 
- Choice of kernel: expressive kernels (e.g., RBF) capture complex similarities but increase computational cost (embedding dimension s) and may require bandwidth tuning; linear kernels are efficient but less flexible.
- Distribution representation: Using exact empirical distribution vs. parametric models (e.g., Gaussian) trades off fidelity for speed and reduced sample complexity.
- Bisection heuristic: Approximating the optimal split (e.g., via k-means on embeddings) provides computational tractability but may yield suboptimal TSC gains. The algorithm’s linear time claim assumes incremental updates and bounded s; violating these assumptions (e.g., high-dimensional kernels) can increase complexity.

**Failure Signatures**:
- Poor kernel bandwidth leading to near-constant similarities, causing uninformative splits.
- High-dimensional embeddings (large s) that inflate the O(s²) term, causing memory or time blowup.
- Greedy bisection getting stuck in local optima, resulting in lower TSC than possible and degraded dendrogram purity.
- Data that violate the distributional assumption (e.g., clusters with multimodal shapes not captured by chosen kernel) may still produce poor hierarchies.

## Limitations
- **Generalizability**: Evaluation restricted to Spatial Transcriptomics; performance on other high-dimensional or non-spatial domains (images, text) remains untested.
- **Theoretical strength**: The guarantee is a lower bound on TSC, not an optimality or consistency result; the practical tightness of the bound is unclear.
- **Baseline coverage**: Comparison limited to traditional DHC methods; excluding recent neural or hybrid hierarchical clustering approaches may overstate relative improvements.
