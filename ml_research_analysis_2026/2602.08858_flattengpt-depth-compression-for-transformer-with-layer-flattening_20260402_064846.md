# FlattenGPT: Depth Compression for Transformer with Layer Flattening
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.08858
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08858
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: At ~20% compression, retains 90–96% zero-shot performance across multiple LLMs and yields a 1.26× inference speedup on LLaMA-2 70B.

## Executive Summary
FlattenGPT is a two-stage framework for compressing transformer-based language models by reducing depth. It first merges adjacent layers (flattening) based on high similarity between their input features, then applies structured channel pruning (head removal for attention, Nyström approximation for MLPs) to restore the original width. This approach preserves the knowledge of all layers while removing redundancy, achieving superior accuracy-efficiency trade-offs compared to standalone depth or width pruning. Evaluations on LLaMA, Qwen, and Baichuan models show strong zero-shot retention and faster inference.

## Method Summary
FlattenGPT operates in two stages. **Stage 1** employs a greedy algorithm that computes cosine similarity between consecutive layers’ input features on a calibration set, iteratively merging the most similar adjacent pairs. Merging (flattening) is performed by fusing layer-normalization affinities into preceding linear projections and concatenating parameters (e.g., Q/K/V/O matrices for MHA, up/gate/down for MLP), summing outputs to approximate sequential application. **Stage 2** applies structured pruning to the flattened blocks: attention heads are removed via an importance metric derived from calibration data, and MLP channels are pruned using ridge leverage scores with a Nyström-based adjustment to the down-projection matrix, thereby restoring the original hidden dimension. Optional recovery fine-tuning (e.g., LoRA) further recovers performance.

## Key Results
- With ~20% compression, FlattenGPT retains 90–96% of zero-shot performance on LLaMA-2/3, Qwen-1.5, and Baichuan-2.
- It achieves lower WikiText-2 perplexity than competing depth and channel pruning methods.
- Inference speedup reaches 1.26× on LLaMA-2 70B.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Adjacent transformer layers often have highly similar input features, indicating exploitable redundancy.
- **Mechanism**: Compute cosine similarity between input features of consecutive layers on a calibration set. High similarity suggests the later layer does not significantly transform the input, so merging the two layers preserves functionality.
- **Core assumption**: Residual connections cause deeper layers to be dominated by identity mappings, making inputs similar to the previous layer’s output.
- **Evidence anchors**: [section 2.2] high cross-layer similarity due to “curse of depth”; Figure 2a illustrates this. Works like SLEB/BlockPruner also use cross-layer metrics.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Merging two adjacent blocks by concatenating their parameters preserves combined behavior when inputs are similar.
- **Mechanism**: Fuse layer normalization affinities into linear projections. For MHA, concatenate Q/K/V matrices horizontally from both layers and O matrices vertically; sum the two original outputs. For MLP, concatenate up/gate matrices and down matrices; sum outputs. The residual connection stabilizes output.
- **Core assumption**: Since inputs are similar, \(B_{\ell-1}(x) + B_{\ell}(x) \approx B_{\ell}(B_{\ell-1}(x))\).
- **Evidence anchors**: [section 3.1] flattening has marginal impact due to high input similarity; Equations (9) and (11) define flattening formulas.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: After flattening, pruning unimportant heads (MHA) and channels (MLP) using importance metrics—and adjusting remaining parameters via Nyström—reduces width back to original size with little loss.
- **Mechanism**:  
  - MHA: Compute head importance \(f_i = \mathbb{E}_D[\| \text{Softmax}(\psi_r(XW_{Q,i})\psi_r^\top(XW_{K,i})) XW_{V,i} \text{diag}(W_{O,i}W_{O,i}^\top)^{1/2} \|]\); keep top-\(k\) heads.  
  - MLP: Compute ridge leverage scores from activation covariance \(C_\psi\); select top-\(k\) channels. Adjust down matrix \(W_D\) via Nyström: \( \Delta \hat{W}_D = (S_k^\top C_\psi S_k + \lambda I)^{-1} S_k^\top C_\psi (I - S_k S_k^\top) W_D \) (Lemma 3.1).
- **Core assumption**: The flattened block contains redundant parameters; importance scores identify critical ones. Nyström provides optimal low-rank approximation under L2 regularization.
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] channel pruning removes redundancy while keeping architecture identical; [section 3.2] Lemma 3.1 on Nyström optimality; [section 4.3] ablations confirm contribution.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:  
  Similarity Calculator -> Greedy Layer Selector -> Layer Flattener -> Channel Pruner -> Recovery Fine-Tuner

- **Critical path**:  
  1. Prepare calibration data (128 samples, seq len 2048, e.g., WikiText-2 train).  
  2. Forward pass to collect per-layer input features via hooks.  
  3. Compute cosine similarity matrix between consecutive layers’ inputs.  
  4. Run greedy selection: pick adjacent pair with highest similarity, update matrix, repeat until target flattenings reached.  
  5. For each selected pair, apply layer flattening: fuse layer-norm affinities, concatenate MHA/MLP parameters.  
  6. Apply MLP pruning (Nyström) then MHA pruning to all flattened blocks.  
  7. Evaluate zero-shot/perplexity; optionally run recovery fine-tuning (LoRA on Alpaca, 50K samples, 2 epochs).

- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - Compression vs. accuracy: Higher flattening count yields more speedup but risks performance drop; ~20% compression retains 90–96% zero-shot performance.  
  - Calibration data: 128 samples suffice; dataset choice (WikiText-2 vs. Alpaca) slightly affects results.  
  - Pruning order: Paper prunes MLP then MHA; swapping order may change outcomes.  
  - Architectural consistency: Keeping width identical simplifies deployment but may limit optimality.  
  - Fine-tuning: Recovery fine-tuning with LoRA recovers most loss; without it, performance is lower but still competitive.

- **Failure signatures**:  
  - Sharp perplexity increase after flattening: likely due to merging low-similarity layers (check similarity scores).  
  - Small variance in head/channel importance scores: pruning may be arbitrary; increase calibration data size.  
  - Fine-tuning fails to recover: compression ratio too high or greedy selection chose suboptimal pairs.  
  - No inference speedup: hardware may be memory-bandwidth bound; verify kernel launch reduction.

- **First 3 experiments**:  
  1. **Baseline replication**: For LLaMA-2 7B, apply FlattenGPT at 20% sparsity. Report WikiText-2 perplexity and average zero-shot accuracy (expect ~62.5% avg, ~8.7 PPL). Compare to SLEB, ShortGPT (Table 1).  
  2. **Ablation on flattening**: Apply only flattening (no channel pruning) for increasing numbers of pairs (2,4,6,8). Plot zero-shot accuracy/perplexity vs. layers flattened. Compare to layer pruning ablation (Figure 6) to show benefit of preserving all parameters.  
  3. **Ablation on channel pruning**: From flattened model, apply MHA pruning only, MLP pruning only, and both. Evaluate to measure each component’s contribution. Also compare Nyström vs. simple channel selection (Table 9) to validate adjustment.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Can FlattenGPT be extended to hybrid transformer architectures that combine different block types (e.g., Transformer and Mamba)?**  
  *Basis*: [explicit] Limitation states it is non-trivial for hybrid architectures. *Why unresolved*: Flattening assumes identical block structures; merging heterogeneous blocks requires new concatenation/pruning strategies. *Resolving evidence*: Develop generalized flattening for arbitrary block compositions and validate on hybrid models (e.g., Transformer interleaved with Mamba).

- **Could more advanced structured channel pruning algorithms further enhance FlattenGPT’s performance?**  
  *Basis*: [explicit] Framework is not constrained to specific channel pruning; better methods would improve it. *Why unresolved*: Current pruning (head removal, Nyström) is simple; modern techniques (movement, differentiable pruning) might raise the accuracy ceiling. *Resolving evidence*: Replace MHA/MLP pruning with SOTA methods and compare zero-shot accuracy/perplexity.

- **How does FlattenGPT’s trade-off behave for compression ratios higher than 20%?**  
  *Basis*: [inferred] Main results only at ~20%; scalability to higher compression unknown. *Why unresolved*: Higher compression is desirable for extreme deployment; accuracy loss beyond 20% is unmeasured. *Resolving evidence*: Systematically vary flattened layers and pruning intensity, measure metrics at 30%, 40%, 50% compression, compare to competing methods.

- **Does FlattenGPT generalize to non-language transformers (e.g., vision, speech)?**  
  *Basis*: [inferred] Evaluation focuses on LLMs; no results on ViT or audio models. *Why unresolved*: Different modalities may have different redundancy patterns; cross-layer similarity might be lower. *Resolving evidence*: Apply FlattenGPT to ViT (ImageNet-1k) and speech transformers (LibriSpeech), report accuracy relative to unstructured pruning.

## Limitations
- Exact feature extraction points for similarity (pre-/post-LN, residual inclusion) are unspecified.
- Implementation details for Grouped Query Attention (GQA) models are missing (e.g., importance metric adaptation).
- Calibration dataset construction specifics beyond sample count/length are vague (padding, truncation, source).

## Confidence
- Cross-layer redundancy: Medium (Lemma & similarity plots support, but break conditions unclear)
- Flattening approximation: Medium (residual argued to stabilize, no error bound)
- Channel pruning (Nyström): High (Lemma 3.1 optimality; ablations confirm)
- Overall trade-off: High (consistent 90–96% zero-shot retention across 8 models at 20% compression)
