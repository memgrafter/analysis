# To Use or not to Use Muon: How Simplicity Bias in Optimizers Matters
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.00742
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00742
- Reference count: 38
- Primary result: Muon accelerates training via orthogonalized updates but loses SGD's simplicity bias, potentially harming generalization in tasks where simplicity is beneficial.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the implicit bias of the Muon optimizer compared to stochastic gradient descent (SGD). Theoretically, for deep linear networks, gradient descent exhibits a simplicity bias: it sequentially learns modes in decreasing order of singular value (i.e., increasing rank of the solution). Muon, by orthogonalizing its updates, forces all modes to be learned simultaneously, thus avoiding sequential rank growth. While this yields faster convergence, experiments on multi-domain shared representation and MNIST with spurious features show that Muon fails to generalize to unseen domain combinations and picks up spurious correlations earlier than SGD, which first captures the dominant true feature before the spurious one. The results highlight a trade-off: Muon’s speed advantage may come at the cost of reduced generalization when a simplicity bias is beneficial.

## Method Summary
Muon is an optimization algorithm that modifies the standard gradient update by orthogonalizing the gradient with respect to the current weight matrix (or its Gram matrix). This orthogonalization ensures that updates are not aligned with any direction already being optimized, effectively treating all singular modes equally. In deep linear networks, this contrasts with SGD, whose updates naturally prioritize high‑singular‑value directions, leading to a sequential increase in the rank of the solution (a simplicity bias). By removing this sequential bias, Muon achieves faster convergence, especially on ill‑conditioned problems, but as the paper shows, this also eliminates an implicit inductive bias that can aid generalization.

## Key Results
- Muon converges faster than SGD by learning all modes simultaneously, whereas SGD sequentially increases solution rank.
- On a multi‑domain shared representation task, Muon memorizes seen domain pairs and fails to generalize to unseen combinations; SGD discovers the common underlying structure.
- On MNIST with an added spurious pixel feature, Muon incorporates the spurious signal earlier, causing earlier performance degradation on clean validation data compared to SGD.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The core mechanism lies in how the two optimizers traverse the loss landscape. In deep linear networks, the gradient of a least‑squares loss decomposes along the singular vectors of the data covariance. Standard gradient descent aligns updates with the top singular vectors first, because those have larger gradients. As training progresses, the model gradually amplifies these directions, effectively increasing the rank of its solution in a step‑wise manner. This “simplicity bias” means the model initially captures the most salient (low‑complexity) patterns before finer details.

Muon intervenes by orthogonalizing each gradient step against the current weight matrix (or the span of previous updates). This prevents the optimizer from repeatedly adding components along directions that already have non‑zero weight. The result is that all singular modes receive a proportional update from the start, so the rank of the solution grows rapidly to its maximum. The simultaneous learning of all modes yields faster convergence, especially on problems where high‑curvature directions would otherwise slow progress. However, by bypassing the sequential simplicity bias, Muon is also more likely to pick up spurious correlations or overfit to dataset‑specific artifacts early in training, as the experiments demonstrate.

We outline key concepts required to understand the paper’s findings.

- **Implicit bias of optimizers**: The tendency of an optimization algorithm to converge to particular solutions among many minima. *Why needed*: Central to understanding why SGD and Muon behave differently. *Quick check*: Can you describe another optimizer’s implicit bias (e.g., gradient flow with weight decay)?
- **Sequential rank growth**: In deep linear networks, GD increases the rank of the weight matrix step‑by‑step, learning high‑singular‑value modes first. *Why needed*: Explains simplicity bias. *Quick check*: How would you empirically verify rank growth over training steps?
- **Orthogonalized updates**: Modifying the gradient to be orthogonal to the current weight matrix or previous updates. *Why needed*: Muon’s defining mechanism. *Quick check*: What would happen if the orthogonalization were applied only partially?
- **Deep linear networks**: Neural networks without nonlinear activations, used for theoretical analysis. *Why needed*: The theoretical results are proved in this setting. *Quick check*: How does the loss landscape of a deep linear net differ from that of a nonlinear net?
- **Simplicity bias**: Preference for simpler (e.g., low‑rank) solutions. *Why needed*: Connects optimizer behavior to generalization. *Quick check*: Provide an example where simplicity bias is beneficial and one where it is harmful.
- **Spurious correlation**: A statistical feature that correlates with the target in training data but not causally. *Why needed*: To interpret the MNIST experiment. *Quick check*: How might an optimizer that learns all modes quickly be more susceptible to spurious correlations?

## Architecture Onboarding
This section provides an architectural overview for someone new to the paper’s experiments and the Muon optimizer.

**Component Map**:
Input Data → Model (weights W) → Loss L(W) → Muon Optimizer (gradient g = ∇L, orthogonalization: g' = Orth(g, W), update: W ← W - η g') → Trained Model → Evaluation

**Critical Path**:
1. Forward pass through model to compute loss.
2. Backward pass to obtain raw gradient g.
3. Muon’s orthogonalization step transforms g into g'.
4. Parameter update using g'.
5. Repeat for all training batches.
The orthogonalization is the critical distinct step that differentiates Muon from SGD and drives both its speed and its altered bias.

**Design Tradeoffs**:
- *Speed vs. Implicit Bias*: Orthogonalization accelerates convergence but removes sequential rank growth, sacrificing the simplicity bias that SGD naturally provides.
- *Complexity vs. Stability*: The orthogonalization operation adds computational overhead and may become numerically unstable for near‑dependent weight matrices; careful implementation (e.g., using QR) is needed.
- *Generalization*: In scenarios where the true signal is low‑complexity and spurious correlations exist, the simplicity bias of SGD leads to better out‑of‑distribution (OOD) performance; Muon may overfit these spurious patterns.

**Failure Signatures**:
- On multi‑domain tasks requiring discovery of a shared representation across domain combinations, Muon fails to generalize to unseen domain pairs while SGD succeeds.
- When a dataset contains a spurious feature that correlates with the label, Muon incorporates this feature earlier, causing a steeper drop in clean validation performance compared to SGD.
- In linear network theory, Muon does not exhibit the sequential increase in rank; instead, rank jumps to full early in training.

**First Experiments** (to reproduce and extend the findings):
1. Replicate the deep linear network analysis: train a linear network with GD and Muon, track the singular values of the weight matrix, and verify sequential rank growth for GD vs. full rank for Muon.
2. Recreate the multi‑domain shared representation task: train a classifier on combinations of two binary attributes using Muon and SGD, evaluate generalization to unseen attribute pairs.
3. Reproduce the MNIST spurious pixel experiment: add a correlated pixel to MNIST digits, train both optimizers, and compare training speed and clean validation accuracy over epochs.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: What is the implicit bias of Muon, and how does its optimization trajectory differ from that of SGD (e.g., in terms of rank evolution and feature learning order)?
- **Basis in paper**: The authors admit they "still don’t know Muon’s biases... we don’t know which trajectory... and we don’t know implications of that to the solution."
- **Why unresolved**: Theoretical analysis is limited to deep linear networks; the behavior of Muon on deep non‑linear networks is not understood, and empirical evidence is sparse.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Trajectory analyses (e.g., tracking singular values, learned representations) across a range of architectures and datasets; extending the theory to non‑linear settings.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Under what task and data conditions does the simplicity bias of SGD lead to superior generalization compared to Muon?
- **Basis in paper**: The authors emphasize that optimizers have "inherent inductive biases, which could be beneficial or not for any given problem," and show examples where Muon fails to generalize due to loss of simplicity.
- **Why unresolved**: Only a couple of synthetic scenarios have been explored; the boundary between when simplicity helps versus hurts remains unclear.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Systematic experiments varying spurious correlation strength, multi‑task structure, distribution shifts, and measuring generalization; theoretical bounds linking simplicity bias to OOD performance.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Can we design an optimizer that retains Muon's fast convergence while preserving the simplicity bias of SGD, e.g., by traversing a similar trajectory yet efficiently escaping saddles?
- **Basis in paper**: They hope their theory will help develop "alternatives/upgrades to Muon, perhaps the ones that traverse the same trajectory as GD, but also have a good way of breaking the saddles."
- **Why unresolved**: Muon's orthogonalization appears necessary for speed but seems to eliminate sequential rank growth; no known modification achieves both properties.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Propose and test algorithmic modifications (e.g., partial orthogonalization, adaptive steps) that maintain speed and simplicity bias; theoretical analysis of their dynamics showing efficient saddle escape without losing sequential learning.

## Limitations
- Theoretical analysis confined to deep linear networks; validity for nonlinear models is unproven.
- Experiments conducted on small‑scale synthetic tasks (MNIST, multi‑domain) and may not generalize to large‑scale deep learning.
- The boundary where simplicity bias aids versus harms generalization is not formally identified.

## Confidence
- GD’s sequential rank growth in linear networks: **High**
- Muon’s orthogonalization eliminates sequential bias: **High**
- Empirical generalization failures (multi‑domain, spurious correlation): **Medium**
