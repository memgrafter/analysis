# Hardware-Friendly Input Expansion for Accelerating Function Approximation

## Quick Facts
- *arXiv ID:* 2602.17952
- *Source URL:* https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17952
- *Reference count:* 40
- *Primary result:* Input expansion with constant values (e.g., π) reduces LBFGS iterations by ~12% and lowers final test MSE by ~66.3% on average across ten benchmark functions, without adding parameters.

## Executive Summary
Neural network function approximation often suffers from slow convergence and poor accuracy due to flat loss landscapes caused by parameter-space symmetries. This paper introduces a hardware-friendly method that breaks these symmetries by augmenting the input with constant values, thereby increasing its dimensionality without introducing additional learnable parameters.

The approach is evaluated on ten one‑dimensional benchmark functions defined on \([0, 2\pi]\). Using the optimal 5‑dimensional expansion with \(\pi\) as the constant, the method reduces LBFGS iterations by 12% on average and achieves a 66.3% reduction in final test MSE compared to a standard model. A parameter‑matched control model shows no improvement, confirming the gains stem specifically from the input expansion. Inference incurs zero computational overhead.

## Method Summary
The method transforms a scalar input \(x\) into a vector of odd dimension \(d=2k+1\) by placing \(k\) copies of a constant \(c\) on both sides of \(x\) (e.g., \([c,\dots,c, x, c,\dots,c]\)). This fixed preprocessing step adds no parameters. The expanded input feeds into a standard MLP with four hidden layers (100, 100, 50, 50 units) using \(\tanh\) activations and a linear output. Training minimizes mean squared error using LBFGS with strong Wolfe line search on 1000 sorted training points. A parameter‑matched control model (Adjusted) with increased hidden widths but 1‑D input is used for isolation. Performance is measured by iterations to reach test MSE \(\le 10^{-5}\) and final test MSE.

## Key Results
- The optimal 5‑D expansion (using \(\pi\)) reduces LBFGS iterations by 12% on average.
- Final test MSE is lowered by 66.3% compared to the standard model.
- \(\pi\) consistently outperforms other constants (0, 1, \(e\)) as the expansion value.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Input expansion breaks permutation symmetries in hidden layers by introducing neuron‑specific biases through constant features.
- **Mechanism:** Auxiliary constant dimensions contribute a term \(w^{(1)}_{2,j} \cdot c\) to each hidden neuron's weighted sum. Because neuron weights differ, this acts as a unique bias per neuron. Swapping neurons would alter these bias terms unless weights for constant dimensions are identical, thus breaking permutation symmetry.
- **Core assumption:** Parameter permutation symmetry creates flat, degenerate regions in the loss landscape.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 3.1 (derivation with a 2‑layer linear network) mathematically demonstrate the symmetry‑breaking effect. Neighboring work on symmetry breaking is cited but does not directly validate this specific mechanism.
- **Break condition:** If the constant value \(c=0\) or the constant dimensions are linearly dependent with other inputs, the introduced bias terms become degenerate, failing to break symmetry effectively (as seen in the poor performance of the “All 0” ablation).

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Symmetry breaking via input expansion improves the geometric properties of the loss landscape, reducing flat plateaus and ill‑conditioned regions.
- **Mechanism:** By destroying exact permutation invariance, the expanded input makes the loss more sensitive to individual neuron weight updates. This likely shrinks the volume of near‑flat regions and sharpens minima, leading to faster convergence for LBFGS, which depends on local conditioning and gradient norm consistency.
- **Core assumption:** LBFGS’s convergence rate is heavily influenced by the local conditioning of the loss surface.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract, Section 4.2.1 (12% average iteration reduction for Expanded‑5 vs. Standard), and the lack of improvement for the parameter‑matched control isolate the landscape effect. Limited direct corpus evidence; neighboring studies use dimensional expansion as a probe for landscape geometry.
- **Break condition:** For function categories where the primary difficulty is not landscape flatness (e.g., discontinuities), iteration reductions are minimal (0% for discontinuous/non‑differentiable categories), indicating the mechanism’s benefit is contingent on symmetry‑induced pathology being the dominant bottleneck.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The choice of constant value (e.g., \(\pi\)) can provide domain‑specific inductive bias that aligns the expanded feature space with the target function’s structure, further enhancing representation learning.
- **Mechanism:** Constants that are mathematically meaningful relative to the input domain (e.g., \(\pi\) for \([0,2\pi]\)) create features with inherent alignment to periodic structures. This may help the network disentangle frequencies and capture phase information more efficiently, mitigating spectral bias without additional parameters.
- **Core assumption:** The expanded constant dimensions act as fixed, informative basis functions that interact with learned weights.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 4.2.6 ablation showing “All \(\pi\)” yields 15‑47% lower final MSE vs. other constants (0, 1, \(e\), mixed). The superiority is attributed to mathematical coherence between \(\pi\) and the domain.
- **Break condition:** Using a constant unrelated to the function’s domain (e.g., 0 or an arbitrary value) significantly reduces the benefit, underscoring the importance of domain alignment.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept: Permutation Symmetry in MLPs**  
*Why needed here:* The paper’s core mechanism targets the well‑known fact that permuting neurons in a hidden layer (swapping incoming/outgoing weights) leaves the network’s output unchanged, creating equivalent parameter configurations and flat valleys in the loss landscape.  
*Quick check question:* Why does swapping neurons and their corresponding weights in a standard MLP not change the network’s output for a given input?

**Concept: Loss Landscape Geometry & Optimization**  
*Why needed here:* Understanding convergence acceleration requires grasping how landscape features (flat regions, local minima, conditioning) affect optimizers like LBFGS. The paper posits that permutation symmetry is a key cause of problematic landscape geometry for function approximation.  
*Quick check question:* How might a loss landscape with large, flat plateaus negatively impact the step size and direction chosen by a quasi‑Newton optimizer like LBFGS?

**Concept: Spectral Bias in Neural Networks**  
*Why needed here:* Standard MLPs learn low frequencies first. Input expansion is particularly effective for functions with “complex spectral characteristics,” suggesting it interacts with or mitigates spectral bias by altering the effective frequency representation.  
*Quick check question:* What property of standard MLP architectures with \(\tanh\) activations might cause them to preferentially approximate low‑frequency components of a target function?

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Expansion dimension (\(k\)):** Too small (3D) yields insufficient symmetry breaking; too large (7D+) may introduce redundancy. Optimal found at 5D.
- **Constant value (\(c\)):** Domain‑aligned constants (e.g., \(\pi\) for \([0,2\pi]\)) outperform generic ones (0, 1, \(e\)). Zero constants are particularly detrimental.
- **Parameter budget:** To isolate effects, total parameter count must be controlled by adjusting hidden layer widths when input size changes; a parameter‑matched control (Adjusted) is essential.

### Failure Signatures
- **No convergence acceleration vs. baseline:** Likely due to constant \(c=0\) or improperly matched parameter counts.
- **Degraded final accuracy:** May indicate over‑expansion (\(k\) too high) or poor constant choice.
- **Instability during LBFGS:** Could be caused by ill‑conditioning from expansion; try reducing \(k\) or selecting a better \(c\).

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Baseline Replication:** Train a Standard model (1‑D input, fixed hidden widths) on one benchmark function (e.g., F1). Confirm that iteration count and final MSE align with reported ranges.
2. **Dimension Sweep:** For the same function, train Expanded‑3, Expanded‑5, and Expanded‑7 models using \(c=\pi\). Plot training loss vs. iteration; expect Expanded‑5 to converge fastest and to the lowest loss.
3. **Constant Ablation:** Train five 5‑D expanded models with constants \([\pi, 0, 1, e, \text{mixed}]\). Compare final test MSE; expect \(\pi\) to yield the lowest and 0 the highest. Also run the Adjusted (parameter‑matched) control to confirm gains are not due to increased capacity.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can input‑space expansion be generalized to multi‑dimensional function approximation, and does the symmetry‑breaking mechanism remain effective as input dimensionality increases?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper exclusively evaluates the method on one‑dimensional functions and does not consider multi‑dimensional inputs, which are common in scientific computing and engineering applications.
- **Why unresolved:** The theoretical derivations and empirical results are confined to 1D; higher dimensions may introduce different symmetry structures or require modified expansion strategies, and the scaling behavior is unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Experimental validation on standard multi‑dimensional benchmark functions (e.g., 2D/3D approximation tasks), analysis of parameter symmetries in multi‑layer networks with high‑dimensional inputs, and measurement of convergence and accuracy improvements across dimensions.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What theoretical foundations explain the optimal expansion dimension (5D) and the consistent superiority of \(\pi\) as the constant value, and can these principles be extended to non‑periodic functions or arbitrary input domains?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper reports empirical findings that 5D expansion with \(\pi\) yields best performance for periodic functions on \([0,2\pi]\), but provides no general theory; it mentions a “Goldilocks principle” without derivation and recommends domain‑relevant constants without justification for non‑periodic cases.
- **Why unresolved:** Lacking theoretical guidance, practitioners cannot reliably apply the method to diverse problems; the optimal dimension and constant selection appear heuristic and may not generalize.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Mathematical analysis linking expansion dimension to symmetry‑breaking efficacy, possibly via loss landscape curvature or frequency spectrum properties; theoretical derivation of constant choices based on domain characteristics like periodicity or function smoothness.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Why does input expansion improve final accuracy but not convergence speed for discontinuous and non‑differentiable functions, and what modifications can accelerate convergence for such function classes?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper states: “the limited gains in convergence speed for these function categories (0.0% iteration reduction) indicate that fundamental approximation challenges, like modeling sharp transitions, remain.”
- **Why unresolved:** The authors highlight this limitation but do not investigate underlying causes or propose remedies, leaving a gap in understanding the method’s scope and effectiveness across function types.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Comparative study of loss landscapes for discontinuous functions with and without expansion, focusing on gradient flow near discontinuities; development and testing of hybrid approaches (e.g., combining input expansion with specialized activation functions or loss terms) to target sharp transitions.

## Limitations
- Exact LBFGS hyperparameters (maxcor, Wolfe constants) and random seeds are unspecified, potentially affecting iteration counts and final MSE.
- The symmetry‑breaking mechanism lacks direct evidence, such as measurements of loss landscape curvature (Hessian conditioning) or gradient norm distribution.
- Generalization beyond the evaluated periodic \([0,2\pi]\) 1‑D domain remains unproven.
