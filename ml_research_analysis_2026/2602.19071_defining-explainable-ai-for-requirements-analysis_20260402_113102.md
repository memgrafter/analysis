# Defining Explainable AI for Requirements Analysis
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.19071
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19071
- Reference count: 31
- Primary result: Introduced a three-dimensional framework (Source, Depth, Scope) for categorizing AI explanations, revealing that common ML techniques often cannot meet the highest explanatory demands (Introspective, Model, Teaching).

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the absence of a unified framework to define and categorize explanatory requirements for AI applications. It proposes three orthogonal dimensions—Source (whether explanations come from the decision process itself or are constructed afterward), Depth (whether explanations cover individual attributes or the entire model), and Scope (whether explanations justify a single decision or teach generalizable knowledge). By mapping AI techniques and application cases onto this three-dimensional space, it provides a conceptual tool to analyze trade-offs between explainability needs and system performance. The analysis indicates that existing techniques typically fail to satisfy requirements at the most demanding end of each spectrum, especially for safety-critical or high-accountability scenarios.

## Method Summary
The authors construct a conceptual framework through literature review and analytical reasoning, without empirical experiments. They define the three dimensions based on properties of explanations across AI contexts. The framework's utility is demonstrated by applying it to classify a set of common machine learning techniques (e.g., neural networks, decision trees) and application domains (e.g., user experience, forensic compliance). The goal is to enable stakeholders to systematically compare an application's explanation requirements with the inherent capabilities of candidate AI techniques, making explicit the trade-offs with factors like predictive accuracy or computational cost.

## Key Results
- A three-dimensional schema (Source, Depth, Scope) for classifying and differentiating explanation types.
- A method for mapping AI techniques and application requirements into the schema to expose capability gaps.
- The observation that most prevalent AI techniques are unsuited for applications demanding Introspective, Model-depth, and Teaching-scope explanations simultaneously.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The framework works because it separates the problem of "what explanation is needed" from "what explanation can be provided" into three independent, orthogonal axes. This decomposition allows for precise requirement specification and capability assessment. The mechanism is a dimensional matching process: an application's needs are profiled along Source, Depth, and Scope; candidate techniques are profiled along the same axes; overlaps and gaps become visually and analytically apparent. This makes the often-implicit conflict between explainability and performance explicit, supporting rational design trade-offs rather than relying on intuition or default choices.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept 1: Source (Introspective vs. Post-Hoc Rationalisation)**
- Why needed: To distinguish explanations generated by the same process that produced the output (faithful, mechanistic) from explanations generated afterward (may be approximate orStorytelling). This determines trustworthiness and accountability.
- Quick check: Is the explanation using the model's internal states/logic directly, or is it a separate model/approximation fitted to the outputs?

**Concept 2: Depth (Attribute vs. Model)**
- Why needed: To differentiate explanations focusing on input feature importance for a specific case from those explaining the model's overall structure and learning process. Model-level depth supports debugging, improvement, and regulatory audit.
- Quick check: Does the explanation reveal how the model was constructed or only which inputs were most influential for one prediction?

**Concept 3: Scope (Justification vs. Teaching)**
- Why needed: To separate explanations meant to rationalize a single decision from those intended to educate the user about the model's general behavior. Teaching scope is essential for oversight, maintenance, and transferring knowledge.
- Quick check: Is the explanation tied to a single instance, or does it provide insights that generalize to unseen inputs or model updates?

**Concept 4: Explanation Requirement Profile**
- Why needed: Different applications have different needs across the three dimensions; a profile (e.g., "Introspective, Model, Teaching") formalizes this.
- Quick check: Have you explicitly stated the required position on each axis for your AI application's stakeholders?

**Concept 5: Technique Capability Profile**
- Why needed: Each AI method has inherent limitations on the types of explanations it can naturally support; a profile (e.g., "Post-Hoc, Attribute, Justification" for LIME) makes this clear.
- Quick check: What are the native explanation properties of your chosen model, independent of any post-hoc tools?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Requirement Articulation -> Dimensional Profiling -> Technique Candidate Mapping -> Gap Identification -> Trade-off Analysis & Decision

Critical path:
1. Define explanation requirements by specifying a position (or acceptable range) on each of the three dimensions.
2. For each candidate AI technique, determine its natural capability profile along the same dimensions.
3. Overlay requirement and capability profiles to identify mismatches.
4. Assess performance, cost, and feasibility implications of meeting each requirement.
5. Make a decision: select a technique that meets all requirements (may sacrifice performance), adjust requirements, or accept a gap with mitigation.

Design tradeoffs:
- Moving toward Introspective and Model-depth often requires simpler, more interpretable models (e.g., linear models, small trees), potentially reducing predictive accuracy on complex data.
- Achieving Teaching scope typically requires either intrinsically understandable models or extremely stable, faithful post-hoc methods.
- Post-Hoc rationalisers (e.g., LIME, SHAP) can extend the Scope and Depth of black-box models but introduce fidelity risks and may not be considered Introspective.

Failure signatures:
- A system requiring Introspective explanations but built with a black-box model and only post-hoc explainers will fail verifiable accountability checks.
- An application needing Model-depth explanations receiving only Attribute-level outputs cannot support root-cause analysis or safe model updates.
- A scenario demanding Teaching-scope explanations that gets only Justification outputs will not enable users to build reliable mental models for future decisions.

First experiments:
1. Operationalize the three dimensions: create a detailed rubric with clear criteria and examples for each pole (e.g., Introspective: explanation must reflect the actual computational path; Post-Hoc: explanation is generated by a separate process from the decision).
2. Classify three techniques (Logistic Regression, CART Decision Tree, Multilayer Perceptron) for a binary classification task (e.g., loan approval) using the rubric.
3. Compare your classifications with the paper's positions in Figure 1; document any discrepancies and refine the rubric accordingly.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:** How can the three-dimensional framework be extended with formal semantics to quantify and qualify the "repairability" of an AI system, particularly for safety-critical applications requiring verifiable correction of errors?
- Basis: Conclusion states extending semantics to describe repairability is future work.
- Why unresolved: No initial semantics or integration method with explanation categories is provided.
- What evidence would resolve: A formal model linking, e.g., Introspective+Model+Teaching to properties like fault isolation, guaranteed correction, and prevention of catastrophic forgetting, validated in domains like autonomous vehicles.

**Open Question 2:** What rigorous, standardized metrics can be developed to evaluate the quality and utility of explanations along each dimension, and how do these correlate with human-centric factors like trust and comprehension?
- Basis: Conclusion notes metrics differ by category and need development.
- Why unresolved: Without metrics, trade-offs remain qualitative and subjective; metrics for one dimension (e.g., fidelity for Post-Hoc) may not apply to another.
- What evidence would resolve: A validated metric suite (e.g., completeness for Model depth, generalizability for Teaching scope) correlated with outcomes in user studies (trust calibration, error detection rate).

**Open Question 3:** Are the three proposed dimensions sufficient to capture the full spectrum of explanatory requirements for non-ML AI paradigms (e.g., symbolic AI, hybrid neuro-symbolic systems), or do alternative AI architectures necessitate additional or modified dimensions?
- Basis: Paper focuses exclusively on ML; claim of broader applicability is not substantiated with non-ML examples.
- Why unresolved: Explanatory needs in symbolic systems (e.g., proof traces) or planning agents (e.g., goal reasoning) may involve aspects not covered, like causal understanding or temporal reasoning.
- What evidence would resolve: A cross-paradigm analysis mapping explanatory needs from diverse AI systems (expert systems, robotic planners, knowledge graphs) onto the three dimensions, identifying consistent gaps or overlaps.

**Open Question 4:** How can the framework be operationalized into a practical decision-support tool that helps stakeholders systematically trade off explanatory requirements against performance metrics during AI system design?
- Basis: Paper claims categories enable trading off explainability against other factors but provides no methodology.
- Why unresolved: Engineers lack a prescriptive method to navigate multi-dimensional requirements vs. performance curves; the framework remains descriptive.
- What evidence would resolve: An implemented tool/process where users input requirement profiles (with weights) and receive ranked technique recommendations based on empirical capability data and cost-performance trade-off curves, evaluated in real design projects.

## Limitations
- Dimension boundaries are conceptually defined but not objectively measurable, risking inconsistent classification across interpreters.
- The framework is illustrated only with classical ML examples; its applicability to modern architectures (transformers, large language models, ensembles) is unexplored and may require extension.
- Mappings of techniques to the 3D space are interpretive; without a standardized rubric, placements can be subjective and contentious.

## Confidence
- **Framework adequacy** (three dimensions are necessary and sufficient for ML contexts): Medium. The argument is logically coherent and addresses a clear gap, but empirical validation across diverse applications is lacking.
- **Technique mapping accuracy** (placements of standard ML methods like decision trees, neural nets): Medium-Low. The placements align with common intuition but are not formally proven; edge cases (e.g., interpretable ML techniques) are not rigorously tested.
- **Shortfall conclusion** (current techniques fail highest demands): Medium. The conclusion follows from the framework's definitions, but its real-world severity depends on how strictly the poles are defined and whether hybrid solutions can bridge gaps.

## Next Checks
1. Develop a detailed, testable rubric for each dimension with clear anchor examples and conduct an inter-rater reliability study where multiple experts classify a common set of techniques and applications.
2. Systematically apply the framework to a broad suite of modern AI techniques (e.g., BERT, GPT, diffusion models, Graph Neural Networks) to test its boundaries and identify any need for new dimensions or refinements.
3. Run a validation study with industry practitioners: present application scenarios, collect their perceived explanation requirements, check alignment with the framework's dimensions, and assess whether the identified capability gaps match their real-world frustrations.
