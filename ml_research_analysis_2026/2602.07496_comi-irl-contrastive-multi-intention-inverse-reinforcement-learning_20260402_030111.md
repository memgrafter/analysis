# CoMI-IRL: Contrastive Multi-Intention Inverse Reinforcement Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.07496
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07496
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** CoMI-IRL automatically discovers behavioral modes and learns separate reward functions, achieving near-perfect clustering (NMI/ARI up to 1.0) and expert-level task rewards on MuJoCo benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
CoMI-IRL addresses multi-intention inverse reinforcement learning (MI-IRL) without prior knowledge of the number of behavioral modes \(K^*\). The method decouples trajectory clustering from reward learning. It first learns discriminative trajectory embeddings via contrastive training of a transformer, then applies a graph-based clustering algorithm that automatically determines \(K\). Each cluster is treated as a single intention, and standard IRL methods (GAIL/AIRL) learn separate reward functions and policies. On MuJoCo environments (Reacher, Pusher, Walker2D), CoMI-IRL outperforms state-of-the-art in clustering quality and reward learning, and adapts to new behaviors via finetuning without full retraining.

## Method Summary
The pipeline comprises four stages.  
1. **Behavioral Encoder (BE):** States and actions are processed separately using Random Fourier Features, MLPs, and 1D CNNs, then interleaved and fed to a transformer. The L2-normalized [CLS] token output is the trajectory embedding \(z_\tau\).  
2. **Contrastive Training:** The BE is trained with a composite loss \(\mathcal{L} = \alpha \mathcal{L}_{CLS} + \beta \mathcal{L}_{DIM} + \gamma \mathcal{L}_{SEG} + \delta \mathcal{L}_{PAIR}\), combining symmetric InfoNCE and Deep InfoMax regularization.  
3. **Graph-based Clustering:** A weighted \(k\)-NN graph is built on embeddings (cosine similarity). Edge weights are augmented with Jacobian-based behavioral features (control sensitivity, covariance statistics). If the graph has multiple connected components, they become clusters; otherwise, a sweep over \(k\) and Leiden resolution \(\gamma\) selects the most stable partition.  
4. **Reward Learning:** For each cluster, an independent single-intention IRL (GAIL or AIRL) learns a reward and policy.  
5. **Adaptation:** For new behaviors, the BE is finetuned on combined old and new data with a stability regularizer \(\mathcal{L}_{stab}\) to preserve old embeddings. A two-stage clustering recovers baseline clusters and assigns novel trajectories, followed by IRL for new clusters.

## Key Results
- Correctly infers or exceeds the true number of modes \(K^*\) (Reacher:6, Pusher:6, Walker2D:3).
- Clustering quality (NMI, ARI) reaches perfect (1.0) on several settings, surpassing prior methods.
- Learned policies achieve Average Task Reward (ATR) near expert performance (normalized to 1.0).
- Adaptation without full retraining retains clustering and reward performance, evidenced by stable NMI, ARI, and ATR scores.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
CoMI-IRL breaks the detrimental coupling between clustering and reward learning that plagues multi-intention IRL. The contrastively trained transformer embeddings are explicitly optimized to group trajectories of the same intention while separating different ones, creating a representation space where graph-based clustering can reliably discover modes regardless of \(K^*\). Augmenting edge weights with Jacobian-derived behavioral features (e.g., control sensitivity, covariance) further refines the graph to reflect nuanced dynamics. Independent reward learning per cluster then avoids mixing intentions, enabling each IRL agent to specialize. This staged approach ensures that neither step suffers from the noise of the other, leading to robust performance and interpretable mode separation.

## Foundational Learning
- **Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL):** Recovers a reward function from expert demonstrations; needed as the final per-cluster reward learner. Quick check: Verify that GAIL/AIRL can recover a known reward on a simple single-intention task.
- **Contrastive Learning:** Learns representations by attracting positive pairs and repelling negatives; central to constructing discriminative embeddings. Quick check: Train a contrastive model on a known classification dataset (e.g., CIFAR-10) and check linear probe accuracy.
- **Transformer Architecture:** Uses self-attention to model long-range dependencies in trajectories; chosen for its ability to capture complex temporal patterns. Quick check: Implement a small transformer on a sequence modeling task (e.g., word prediction) and compare to an RNN.
- **Graph-based Clustering (Leiden):** Partitions a graph into communities; used with stability-based resolution selection to auto-determine \(K\). Quick check: Apply Leiden to a small synthetic graph with known clusters and check NMI.
- **Jacobian Features:** Compute derivatives of state changes w.r.t. actions to capture behavioral characteristics (e.g., control sensitivity); used to reweight graph edges for better separation. Quick check: On a simple dynamical system, compute Jacobian features for two distinct control strategies and verify they differ.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map:  
Raw trajectories → Behavioral Encoder (BE) → Embeddings → Graph Construction (k-NN + Jacobian weighting) → Clustering (Leiden) → Per-cluster IRL (GAIL/AIRL) → Policies/Rewards.

Critical path: BE training → clustering → IRL. Each stage depends on the previous one; BE quality directly impacts clustering and ultimately reward learning.

Design tradeoffs:  
- Contrastive loss complexity (multiple components) improves embedding discriminativeness but increases training time and hyperparameter sensitivity.  
- Graph clustering hyperparameters (\(k_{min}, k_{max}, \gamma\)) control granularity; sweeping a wide range ensures robustness but adds computational overhead.  
- Jacobian feature augmentation adds computational cost (requires trajectory-level Jacobian estimation) but yields measurable gains in separation for certain environments.

Failure signatures:  
- Collapsed embeddings (all trajectories map to similar vectors) cause graph clustering to produce one giant component; check contrastive loss curves and embedding variance.  
- Unstable Leiden partitions (high variance across neighboring hyperparameters) indicate that the embedding space lacks clear cluster structure; adjust contrastive training or consider alternative graph construction.  
- IRL mode collapse (policies from a cluster exhibit diverse behaviors) suggests cluster impurity; revisit clustering or increase BE capacity.

First three experiments:  
1. Train BE on Reacher trajectories and compute pairwise cosine distances; confirm that within-mode distances are significantly lower than cross-mode distances.  
2. Run clustering with and without Jacobian reweighting; compare NMI/ARI against ground truth to quantify improvement.  
3. Train GAIL on a pure cluster (e.g., one with clear reaching mode); visualize rollouts to verify the policy replicates the intended behavior (e.g., elbow angle, path shape).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How do hyperparameters for Jacobian-based edge reweighting in graph clustering affect performance across diverse environments?  
  *Basis:* Section 6 states this is future work.  
  *Why unresolved:* Fixed hyperparameters (α=0.3, σ_b=1.0) were chosen based on correlation checks; may not generalize to environments with varying dynamics.  
  *Resolving evidence:* Systematic ablations varying α and σ_b across multiple MuJoCo and real-world datasets, measuring impact on NMI/ARI and ATR.

- **Open Question 2:** What are the scalability limits of CoMI-IRL in terms of the number of behavioral modes, and how robust is it to catastrophic forgetting during sequential adaptation?  
  *Basis:* Section 6 notes scalability and forgetting as future work.  
  *Why unresolved:* Current experiments evaluate up to \(K^*=6\) (with inferred up to 12) and single-step adaptation; tens/hundreds of modes and multi-step forgetting remain unexamined.  
  *Resolving evidence:* Incrementally increase \(K^*\) in complex environments (e.g., 10, 20, 50) and perform repeated adaptations, tracking cluster retention via normalized ATR and clustering consistency.

- **Open Question 3:** Can the reward learning phase be improved by modeling relationships between clusters representing different strategies for the same task?  
  *Basis:* Section 6 proposes incorporating inter-cluster connections.  
  *Why unresolved:* Independent reward functions ignore potential shared structure or hierarchies among strategies, possibly limiting sample efficiency and generalization.  
  *Resolving evidence:* Develop a multi-cluster reward model (e.g., graph neural network, hierarchical representations) that encodes inter-cluster similarities; evaluate on tasks with known strategy variations, comparing sample complexity and zero-shot transfer.

- **Open Question 4:** How do interpretability findings from synthetic benchmarks translate to real-world scenarios, and can human feedback improve alignment between inferred behavioral modes and human intentions?  
  *Basis:* Section 6 calls for user studies with real-world data and human feedback.  
  *Why unresolved:* Evaluations used clean MuJoCo simulations; real-world demonstrations involve noise, partial observability, and subjective intentions. Human-in-the-loop alignment is unexplored.  
  *Resolving evidence:* Conduct user studies where experts label clusters from real-world datasets (e.g., robotics, autonomous driving); integrate human feedback (preferences, corrections) into clustering or reward learning, measuring alignment via expert ratings and task performance.

## Limitations
- Expert trajectory generation procedure for distinct modes is unspecified, particularly the conflict between removing goal information and needing goals to shape behavior.
- Observation dimension mismatch (Pusher reported 20 vs expected 16 after goal removal) and ambiguous Jacobian feature extraction details impede reproduction.
- Missing critical hyperparameters (segment numbers/lengths, k-sweep step size, RBF bandwidth, GAIL settings, replay buffer sizes) require guesswork.
- Evaluation is restricted to three MuJoCo tasks; generalization to other domains or real-world data remains unknown.
