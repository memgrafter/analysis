# The hidden risks of temporal resampling in clinical reinforcement learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.06603
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06603
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Temporal binning/interpolation in clinical offline RL degrades deployment performance, worst with binning; off-policy evaluation fails to detect these failures.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the impact of temporal resampling—specifically binning and interpolation—on offline reinforcement learning (ORL) in clinical decision-making. Using two simulators (LavaGap and UVA/Padova), the authors train agents (BC, IQL, CQL) on data processed into unprocessed SMDP form, interpolated MDP, and binned MDP. They find that agents trained on unprocessed SMDP data outperform expert baselines, while resampled data leads to significant performance degradation, particularly with binning. Furthermore, standard off-policy evaluation via Fitted Q-Evaluation overestimates returns for binned data, masking deployment failures. Three mechanisms are identified: generation of counterfactual trajectories, distortion of temporal expectations, and compounding of generalization errors. The study underscores fundamental risks in current healthcare ORL pipelines and advocates for methods that explicitly handle irregular decision timing.

## Method Summary
Experiments were conducted in LavaGap (2D gridworld) and UVA/Padova (Type 1 diabetes simulator), both configured with irregular decision intervals. An expert PPO policy generated a large reference dataset from the irregular environments. Three dataset variants were created from this reference: unprocessed (preserving variable Δt as SMDP), interpolated (expanding to every timestep, Δt=1), and binned (aggregating over fixed windows). Offline RL algorithms—Behavioural Cloning (BC), Implicit Q-Learning (IQL), and Conservative Q-Learning (CQL)—were trained on each variant, with SMDP-specific Bellman backups for unprocessed data. Agent performance was evaluated via online deployment in both regular and irregular environments, and off-policy evaluation used Fitted Q-Evaluation (FQE) to estimate returns. Results were aggregated as interquartile mean with 95% confidence intervals.

## Key Results
- Agents trained on unprocessed SMDP data exceeded expert performance in both simulators.
- Temporal resampling (binning and interpolation) caused significant performance drops, with binning being the most detrimental.
- Fitted Q-Evaluation (FQE) severely overestimated returns for binned policies, failing to predict the deployment failures.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
1. **Counterfactual trajectory generation**: Temporal binning aggregates observations, actions, and rewards over windows covering multiple true decisions, creating fictitious trajectories where causal order may be reversed (e.g., insulin appearing before carbohydrate intake). The agent learns from these counterfactuals, which do not match true environment dynamics, leading to distributional shift and poor generalization. Evidence is provided via Figure 2 and the discussion.
2. **Distortion of temporal expectations**: Interpolation increases decision frequency (e.g., every 10 minutes), training the agent to expect frequent correction opportunities. At deployment with longer intervals, the policy is misaligned—it may under-treat expecting future adjustments or over-treat anticipating them, because the learned temporal model assumes a different inter-decision time distribution. Supported by the paper's analysis.
3. **Compounding of generalization errors**: Interpolation splits sustained interventions into many short decisions, amplifying small per-decision errors through accumulation. These errors compound over time, pushing patient states into out-of-distribution regions where performance degrades rapidly. The authors attribute the larger drop for interpolated vs. unprocessed data to this effect.

## Foundational Learning
- **Markov Decision Process (MDP) vs. Semi-Markov Decision Process (SMDP)**: The paper contrasts MDP (fixed intervals) with SMDP (variable intervals) to show that clinical data with irregular decision timing is better represented as SMDP. Resampling to MDP introduces the identified risks. Quick check: "What is the key difference between MDP and SMDP in state transitions and reward accumulation, and why might SMDP be more suitable for clinical data with irregular decision times?"
- **Offline Reinforcement Learning (ORL) and Distributional Shift**: ORL learns from static data without environment interaction, facing distributional shift when the policy encounters states not well-represented in the dataset. This is a core vulnerability exploited by the paper's mechanisms. Quick check: "Explain what distributional shift means in offline RL and why it is particularly dangerous in healthcare applications."
- **Off-Policy Evaluation (OPE) and Fitted Q-Evaluation (FQE)**: OPE estimates policy performance without deployment. FQE fits a Q-function to offline data and evaluates a policy by averaging Q-values. The paper shows FQE can be overoptimistic for binned data, so understanding its assumptions is crucial. Quick check: "How does fitted Q-evaluation estimate a policy’s value, and why might it provide biased estimates when the training data contains counterfactual trajectories?"

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Simulators (LavaGap, UVA/Padova) -> Expert Policy (PPO) -> Dataset Preprocessors (unprocessed, interpolated, binned) -> Offline RL Trainers (BC, IQL, CQL) -> Evaluation Module (online deployment, FQE) -> Analysis Utilities (IQM, bootstrapping).

**Critical path:**
1. Train expert PPO on irregular version of each simulator.
2. Generate large reference trajectories (100k steps for LavaGap; 10M for UVA/Padova).
3. Create dataset variants: unprocessed (SMDP), interpolated (MDP all timesteps), binned (fixed windows).
4. Train offline agents (BC, IQL, CQL) on each dataset, with SMDP Bellman adjustments for unprocessed.
5. Deploy agents in both regular and irregular environments; compute cumulative return.
6. Train FQE on each static dataset; estimate returns for the policies.
7. Compare true online returns with FQE estimates; analyze performance drops and OPE bias.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- MDP vs SMDP: MDP simplifies learning but risks counterfactuals, temporal distortion, and error compounding; SMDP preserves realism but requires modifying Bellman backups.
- Binning vs Interpolation: Binning reduces noise and decision frequency but introduces counterfactuals; interpolation retains granularity but increases decision points and error accumulation.
- Algorithm choice: BC is stable but may not exceed expert; IQL/CQL can surpass expert but are more sensitive to data quality and hyperparameters.
- OPE reliance: FQE is computationally efficient but may be miscalibrated for binned data; online evaluation is essential for safety.

**Failure signatures:**
- Performance drop for binned/interpolated agents relative to unprocessed, especially in irregular deployment.
- FQE points above the diagonal in calibration plots (overestimation) for binned data.
- Training losses decreasing while deployment returns worsen.
- Increased hypoglycemic events in UVA/Padova for resampled policies.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Visualize counterfactuals: Apply 2-hour binning to a UVA/Padova trajectory and verify reversed causal ordering (e.g., insulin before carbs) as in Figure 2.
2. Replicate performance comparison: Train BC, IQL, CQL on unprocessed, interpolated, binned datasets; deploy in irregular environment; confirm unprocessed > interpolated > binned in returns.
3. Reproduce OPE bias: Compute FQE estimates for each policy; plot against true online returns; observe severe overestimation for binned agents.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can ORL frameworks be designed to natively accommodate irregular clinical decision timing without temporal resampling? The paper suggests SMDPs but notes the need for adapted algorithms and validation on clinical data.
2. Why does FQE systematically overestimate returns for binned data, and how can OPE be corrected for temporal resampling artifacts? Understanding and mitigating this bias is critical for safe policy screening.
3. Do the performance degradations generalize to discrete action spaces and high-dimensional real-world EHR data? The paper’s continuous-action, low-dimensional simulator results may not fully translate to common clinical settings.

## Limitations
- Incomplete experimental details: expert PPO training specifics (hyperparameters, checkpoint selection), exact patient splits, binning alignment procedures, SMDP update implementations beyond critic loss, FQE architecture, and reward standardization nuances are under-specified, hindering faithful reproduction.
- Generalizability concerns: The study uses continuous action spaces and relatively low-dimensional simulators; it remains unclear whether findings extend to discrete actions (e.g., drug selection) and high-dimensional real EHRs.

## Confidence
- Main claim (temporal resampling degrades performance): High
- Three mechanistic explanations (counterfactuals, temporal distortion, error compounding): Medium
- Fitted Q-Evaluation overestimation for binned data: High
