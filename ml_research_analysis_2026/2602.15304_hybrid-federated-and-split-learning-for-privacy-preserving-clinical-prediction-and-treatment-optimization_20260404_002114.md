# Hybrid Federated and Split Learning for Privacy Preserving Clinical Prediction and Treatment Optimization
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.15304
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15304
- Reference count: 23
- Primary result: Hybrid FL-SL achieves comparable predictive performance (AUROC ~0.75-0.78) to standalone FL/SL while providing a tunable privacy-utility trade-off via the cut layer, with improved worst-client robustness.

## Executive Summary
The paper proposes a hybrid Federated and Split Learning (FL-SL) framework for privacy-preserving clinical prediction and treatment optimization. By keeping feature extraction trunks on client institutions and placing prediction heads on a central server, the method creates an explicit collaboration boundary that controls information leakage. It uses a two-head neural network for uplift modeling to rank patients by incremental treatment benefit under capacity constraints. Evaluated on three clinical datasets with non-IID client partitions, hybrid FL-SL attains utility (AUROC ~0.75-0.78) comparable to standard FL or SL, while membership inference attacks on cut-layer representations show leakage near chance (AUC ~0.48-0.52) and can be reduced further with activation clipping and Gaussian noise (to ~0.42) at modest AUUC cost. Although communication overhead is higher due to activation transfers, worst-client performance improves with client-specific adapters. The work demonstrates hybrid FL-SL as a practical design space for balancing utility, privacy, and deployment cost in healthcare.

## Method Summary
Hybrid FL-SL combines the parameter aggregation of Federated Learning (FedAvg) with the split computation of Split Learning. Each client holds a local trunk network \(f_{\theta_k}\) that processes patient data up to a predefined cut layer, emitting intermediate representations \(z\). These \(z\) are sent to a central server that hosts two independent prediction heads \(g_{\phi_1}\) and \(g_{\phi_0}\) for treated and control outcomes. The server computes the factual loss and backpropagates gradients \(\nabla_z \ell\) to the client, which updates its trunk (and an optional adapter). Periodically, clients upload their trunk parameters to the server for FedAvg aggregation, producing a global trunk that is redistributed. This loop enables shared representation learning while raw data never leaves the clients, and the cut layer serves as a tunable privacy boundary. Uplift is estimated as the difference between the two head outputs, and patients are ranked by this score for capacity-limited treatment prioritization.

## Key Results
- Hybrid FL-SL attains predictive performance (AUROC ~0.75-0.78 on NHANES) on par with pure FL and SL while keeping raw data on clients.
- Membership inference attacks on cut-layer representations yield AUC near chance (0.48-0.52); with clipping and Gaussian noise, leakage drops to ~0.42 with modest AUUC reduction.
- Worst-client robustness improves when client-specific adapters are added, at slight communication cost; however, split/hybrid modes incur higher communication overhead than pure FL due to activation transfers.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Tunable Collaboration Boundary
The hybrid FL-SL framework separates client-side feature extraction (trunk) from server-side prediction (heads). During training, clients compute forward passes to a cut layer and transmit the resulting activations \(z\) to the server. The server applies the heads, computes loss, and returns gradients through the cut layer. Client trunks are periodically aggregated via FedAvg. This design keeps raw data permanently on clients and exposes a single, explicit boundary (\(z\)) where privacy interventions (clipping, noise) can be applied, offering a controllable privacy-utility trade-off.
- **Core assumption:** Intermediate representations present a more governable leakage surface than raw data or full model updates.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states "keeps feature-extraction trunks on clients while placing prediction heads on a coordinating server, enabling shared representation learning and exposing an explicit collaboration boundary where privacy controls can be applied." Section III.D.3 details the algorithm where clients send \(z\) and receive \(\nabla_z \ell\), and trunks are aggregated via FedAvg. Neighboring works on split learning privacy support the focus on the cut layer.
- **Break condition:** Under extreme non-IID heterogeneity without client adapters, the shared trunk may fail to generalize, causing collapse in worst-client performance (e.g., Table VI shows sharp AUUC drops for eICU Hybrid without adapters).

### Mechanism 2: Membership Inference as Privacy Audit
The paper empirically audits privacy by training a lightweight attacker (logistic regression) to distinguish between activations from a target client's training set (members) and test set (non-members). The attack's AUC quantifies how much the shared representations reveal individual participation.
- **Core assumption:** A successful membership inference attack on representations correlates with a meaningful privacy breach in the clinical context.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section III.E.1 formalizes the attack and uses AUC as summary metric. The abstract notes "we audit leakage empirically using membership inference on cut-layer representations." The broader literature on reconstruction attacks validates that intermediate activations can leak information.
- **Break condition:** If MIA AUC for a defended configuration rises substantially above chance (e.g., >0.55), the mitigation is insufficient and stronger defenses or cut-layer relocation are needed.

### Mechanism 3: Two-Head Uplift Architecture
A shared trunk \(f_{\theta}(x)\) produces representation \(z\). Two independent heads \(g_{\phi_1}(z)\) and \(g_{\phi_0}(z)\) output probabilities \(\hat{\mu}_1(x)\) and \(\hat{\mu}_0(x)\) for outcome under treatment and no treatment. The model is trained on factual observations \((x,t,y)\) with binary cross-entropy. The uplift score \(\hat{\tau}(x)=\hat{\mu}_1(x)-\hat{\mu}_0(x)\) ranks patients for treatment prioritization under capacity constraints.
- **Core assumption:** The two-head structure on observational data yields a ranking signal useful for comparative prioritization even if causal identification is not achieved.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section III.C.1 defines the architecture and training objective. The abstract highlights the use of two-head neural network for uplift modeling. Literature on CATE estimation acknowledges challenges, implying this is a practical heuristic.
- **Break condition:** High positivity trimming rates (e.g., >30-40%) remove many test samples, making the uplift ranking unreliable for the target population.

## Foundational Learning
### Uplift Modeling / CATE Estimation
**Why needed:** The decision objective is to rank patients by *incremental benefit* of a treatment, not just predict outcomes. The two-head architecture directly estimates conditional average treatment effects (CATE) via difference of predicted probabilities.
**Quick check:** If you have a model outputting \(\hat{\tau}(x)\), how would you evaluate its usefulness for a doctor who can treat only 10% of patients, and why might AUUC be negative even if the model is useful?  
*Answer:* Use AUUC (area under the uplift curve) to measure cumulative benefit across the ranked list. A negative AUUC can occur when the global treatment effect is unfavorable (e.g., treatment harms more than helps) yet the ranking still separates benefitters from harmed; early lift in the uplift curve may still be positive, so AUUC sign alone is not decisive.

### Non-IID Data in Federated/Split Learning
**Why needed:** Client institutions have inherently different patient populations (statistical heterogeneity), which is a primary challenge. The paper uses non-IID partitions and reports worst-client metrics to assess robustness.
**Quick check:** In Table VI, why does the “Worst” AUUC for eICU Hybrid (-0.5153) differ so much from the “Mean” (-0.4282)? What does this imply about deploying a single global model?  
*Answer:* The large gap indicates high variance across clients; some clients suffer severe performance degradation. Deploying a single global model risks poor service for certain institutions, necessitating robustness measures like client adapters.

### Membership Inference as a Privacy Audit
**Why needed:** Distributed learning is not inherently private; the cut-layer representation may leak information. Membership inference provides an empirical probe of that leakage.
**Quick check:** If an attacker achieves 0.52 AUC on representations from a hybrid model, is that “good” or “bad” privacy? What does “near chance (AUC ~0.48-0.52)” imply?  
*Answer:* An AUC of 0.52 is very close to random guessing (0.5) and indicates minimal leakage; values in the 0.48-0.52 range are considered near chance, reflecting effective protection.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Client trunk \(f_{\theta_k}\) (and optional adapter \(a_k\)) processes local data to produce activations \(z\) -> Server heads \(g_{\phi_1}, g_{\phi_0}\) compute predictions and loss -> Server returns gradient \(\nabla_z \ell\) -> Client updates trunk (and adapter). Additionally, client trunk parameters \(\theta_k\) are periodically sent to server for FedAvg aggregation -> global trunk \(\theta\) is broadcast to clients.

**Critical path (per training round):**  
1. Server broadcasts global trunk \(\theta^{(r)}\).  
2. Client computes \(z = f_{\theta_k}(x)\) (plus adapter if used) and sends \(z\) to server.  
3. Server computes loss with heads, updates heads, returns \(\nabla_z \ell\) to client.  
4. Client updates \(\theta_k\) (and adapter) using the gradient.  
5. After \(E\) local steps, client uploads \(\theta_k\) to server.  
6. Server aggregates all \(\theta_k\) via FedAvg to obtain \(\theta^{(r+1)}\). Repeat.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Cut layer depth:* Shallow cut reduces communication (smaller \(z\)) and may lower leakage but shifts more computation to server; deep cut does the opposite.  
- *Client adapters:* Add local parameters to adapt to non-IID data, improving worst-client robustness (e.g., Table VI shows Hybrid+Pers. better worst AUUC than Hybrid) at a small extra communication cost.  
- *Defenses (clipping/noise):* Reduce MIA AUC (Table V: ~0.42 vs ~0.48) but increase communication (due to noise) and modestly hurt AUUC.

**Failure signatures:**  
- *Worst-client collapse:* Large variance in per-client AUUC/AUROC, with some clients near random or negative. Indicates trunk fails to generalize across heterogeneous distributions.  
- *Leakage spike:* MIA AUC for split/hybrid modes >0.55 suggests cut-layer representation is highly identifiable, requiring stronger mitigation.  
- *Uplift curve inversion:* A sharply negative AUUC combined with a flat or negative early uplift segment (top fraction) signals no actionable ranking signal.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Baseline replication: Run Centralized, FedAvg, Split, and Hybrid (no adapter, no defense) on NHANES. Verify Hybrid AUROC ~0.76-0.78 and MIA AUC ~0.48-0.52.  
2. Privacy-utility sweep: For Hybrid on MEPS, vary Gaussian noise \(\sigma\) and clipping norm \(c\). Plot AUUC vs. MIA AUC; identify operating point where MIA drops significantly (~0.42) with minimal AUUC loss.  
3. Non-IID robustness check: For Hybrid on eICU, compare per-client AUUC with and without adapters. Confirm adapters improve worst-client AUUC without large mean degradation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Stronger adversarial audits:** How do reconstruction attacks impact privacy in hybrid FL-SL? Current MIA audit may underestimate leakage.  
2. **Causal alignment:** How can uplift ranking be better aligned with causal identification and calibration for clinical workflows? The current two-head heuristic lacks formal causal guarantees.  
3. **Optimal cut-layer placement:** What is the best cut depth across heterogeneous datasets to balance utility, privacy, and communication?  
4. **Negative AUUC interpretation:** How should negative AUUC values from observational proxies be handled in collaborative uplift modeling under capacity constraints?

## Limitations
- Exact definitions of non-IID client partitions per dataset (e.g., specific diagnosis groups for eICU) are unspecified, hindering faithful replication.
- Positivity trimming threshold \(\alpha\) and details of the propensity model (architecture, training) for AUUC calculation are missing.
- Membership inference audit parameters (sample size \(m\), attacker architecture regularization) are not fully described, limiting reproducibility of privacy results.
- Communication cost calculations rely on assumptions about batch transmission and compression that are not detailed.

## Confidence
- Hybrid boundary tunability: **Medium** – The concept is clear but direct empirical validation for hybrid FL-SL trade-offs is limited in the corpus.
- Two-head uplift architecture: **High** – The architecture is standard and explicitly described; utility metrics align with design.
- MIA as audit tool: **Medium** – The audit procedure is outlined, but the attacker strength (logistic regression) may underestimate true leakage.
