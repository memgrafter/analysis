# PulmoVec: A Two-Stage Stacking Meta-Learning Architecture Built on the HeAR Foundation Model for Multi-Task Classification of Pediatric Respiratory Sounds

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.15688
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15688
- **Reference count:** 35
- **Primary result:** Stacking meta-learner improves performance by 9–12 percentage points across all tasks compared to base models alone.

## Executive Summary
PulmoVec introduces a two-stage stacking meta-learning architecture that leverages the HeAR foundation model for multi-task classification of pediatric respiratory sounds. The system addresses the subjectivity of auscultation by training three task-specific classifiers (screening, sound-pattern, disease-group) on 24,808 annotated audio segments from 1,652 patients. Base model probabilities are combined with demographic metadata in a LightGBM meta-learner, and final patient-level decisions use a weighted ensemble voting strategy. At the event level, models achieve ROC-AUCs of 0.96, 0.96, and 0.94 for the three tasks. Patient-level disease-group classification yields accuracy 0.74 and weighted F1 0.73.

## Method Summary
The method uses a multi-task framework built on the HeAR audio encoder. Inputs are 2-second resampled audio segments from the SPRSound database. Three identical neural classification heads are trained on top of HeAR embeddings: a binary screening head, a three-class sound-pattern head (Normal/Crackles/Rhonchi), and a four-class disease-group head. Training follows a two-stage protocol: first freeze the HeAR encoder and train heads (10 epochs, LR 1e-4), then fine-tune the entire model (40 epochs, LR 5e-7). Out-of-fold probabilities from 5-fold cross-validation are generated for each base model. These probabilities, along with patient age, sex, and recording location, serve as meta-features for LightGBM stackers (one per task). For patient-level disease-group prediction, event-level meta-model outputs are aggregated via a weighted ensemble voting scheme (30% soft voting, 40% confidence-weighted, 30% majority voting with thresholds).

## Key Results
- Screening model (event-level): ROC-AUC 0.96 (95% CI 0.95–0.97)
- Sound-pattern recognition (event-level, macro): ROC-AUC 0.96 (0.96–0.97)
- Disease-group prediction (patient-level): accuracy 0.74 (0.71–0.77), weighted F1 0.73, macro ROC-AUC 0.91 (0.90–0.93)
- Stacking meta-learner performance gain: 9–12 percentage points across all tasks vs. base models.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Foundation Model Transfer Learning
- **Claim:** HeAR's large-scale pre-training provides robust acoustic embeddings that transfer effectively to pediatric respiratory sounds.
- **Mechanism:** HeAR is pre-trained on 313.3 million diverse health audio clips via self-supervision, learning general acoustic features. Fine-tuning on pediatric data adapts these representations to specific sound patterns (crackles, wheezes) while leveraging the foundation's ability to handle device and environmental variability.
- **Core assumption:** Pediatric respiratory sound patterns are a subset of the acoustic distribution encountered during HeAR's pre-training.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "multi-task framework built on the HeAR foundation model"; [section, Page 3] research question on HeAR recognition of pediatric patterns; neighboring studies also apply HeAR to pediatric data.
- **Break condition:** If pre-training data lacks similar acoustic patterns or fine-tuning data is insufficient, embeddings may not capture discriminative features.

### Mechanism 2: Stacking Ensemble with Uncertainty Preservation
- **Claim:** Out-of-fold probability stacking integrates complementary task-specific classifiers and demographic context, improving performance by 9–12 percentage points.
- **Mechanism:** Three base models trained on same HeAR embeddings but with different label granularities capture different clinical perspectives. Their out-of-fold class probabilities serve as meta-features, preserving uncertainty. A LightGBM meta-learner combines these with age, sex, location, learning to weight base model confidences and resolve disagreements.
- **Core assumption:** Base models make partially independent errors; meta-learner can learn to correct them using probability outputs and clinical metadata.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "combines their out-of-fold probabilities with demographic metadata in a LightGBM stacking meta-model"; [section, Page 7] performance gains of 9 to 12 percentage points; [section, Page 12] architecture transfers classification uncertainty to the meta-learner.
- **Break condition:** If base models are highly correlated or meta-features are uninformative, stacking yields no gain.

### Mechanism 3: Confidence-Weighted Patient-Level Voting
- **Claim:** The ensemble voting strategy for patient-level aggregation reduces noise from focal events and stabilizes decisions.
- **Mechanism:** Multiple 2-second events per patient may originate from unaffected lung regions. The voting scheme combines soft voting (30%), confidence-weighted (40%), and majority voting with high agreement and confidence thresholds (30%). This weights events by model confidence and requires consensus for majority voting, preventing single outliers from dominating.
- **Core assumption:** Patient-level disease state is reflected in a majority of events; aggregating with confidence and agreement improves robustness to focal pathology and annotation noise.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "aggregates event-level predictions to the patient level using a weighted ensemble voting strategy"; [section, Page 5] ensemble voting method combining three complementary strategies; [section, Page 13] preservation of patient-level recall for pneumonia close to event-level value suggests strategy limited information loss.
- **Break condition:** If disease is truly focal and events are sparsely sampled, voting may fail; poorly calibrated confidence scores misweight events.

## Foundational Learning
- **Transfer Learning with Foundation Models**
  - Why needed: Engineers must understand how to load a pre-trained model, extract embeddings, and implement two-stage fine-tuning.
  - Quick check: What is the purpose of the two-stage protocol (freeze encoder then unfreeze) and how does the learning rate schedule differ between stages?

- **Stacking Ensemble and Out-of-Fold Predictions**
  - Why needed: The meta-learner requires out-of-fold predictions to avoid data leakage. Engineers must implement cross-validation loops to generate these meta-features correctly.
  - Quick check: Why can't we simply train base models on the full training set and use their predictions on the training set as meta-features?

- **Multi-Task Label Design and Clinical Taxonomy**
  - Why needed: The three classification tasks correspond to different clinical decision levels. Engineers must map original labels to these tasks appropriately.
  - Quick check: How do the Screening, Sound Pattern, and Disease Group tasks differ in their label sets and clinical interpretability?

- **Probability Calibration and Uncertainty Quantification**
  - Why needed: Stacking and voting rely on well-calibrated probabilities. Engineers should assess calibration (e.g., Brier score, reliability diagrams) and consider post-processing if needed.
  - Quick check: What does a Brier score of 0.12 vs 0.35 (patient level) indicate about model calibration?

- **Patient-Level Aggregation from Sparse Events**
  - Why needed: Events are short and may not cover the entire respiratory cycle or all lung fields. The voting strategy is designed to handle this sparsity.
  - Quick check: Why does accuracy drop from event-level (0.80) to patient-level (0.74) for disease group, and is that expected?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Raw audio -> resample to 16kHz -> 2s windows (with overlap) -> HeAR encoder -> 512-D embedding -> Base model heads (3 tasks) -> out-of-fold probabilities -> LightGBM meta-models (3) + metadata -> patient-level ensemble voting (weighted) -> final patient label.

- **Critical path:**
  1. Curate SPRSound: remove poor quality, duplicate, ensure annotations.
  2. Preprocess audio to 2s segments aligned with event boundaries.
  3. Train base models: first stage (frozen HeAR, 10 epochs, LR 1e-4), second stage (unfreeze, 40 epochs, LR 5e-7).
  4. Generate out-of-fold predictions for each base model using 5-fold CV on training set.
  5. Train LightGBM meta-models on meta-features (probabilities + demographics) for each target.
  6. Determine voting weights and thresholds on validation set (grid search over combinations).
  7. For test set: run base models (trained on full train), get predictions, pass to meta-models, then aggregate per patient.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - HeAR frozen vs unfrozen: Freezing first prevents catastrophic forgetting; then fine-tuning adapts to domain. But requires careful LR scheduling.
  - Label grouping: Combining crackle subtypes and continuous patterns simplifies tasks but discards nuance. Disease grouping (4 classes) improves class balance but loses diagnostic specificity.
  - Stacking with LightGBM vs other meta-learners: LightGBM is fast, handles tabular data, provides feature importance. Could use a neural network but may overfit small meta-dataset.
  - Voting scheme: Empirical weights (30/40/30) and threshold (>0.7) were chosen on validation. Not theoretically optimal; could be replaced by learned parameters but interpretability would drop.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - Base model performance: If event-level ROC-AUC <0.8, foundation transfer may be failing or data preprocessing is wrong.
  - Meta-feature importance: If LightGBM feature importance shows demographics dominating over base probabilities, base models are likely weak.
  - Calibration: High Brier scores (>0.3) indicate poor probability calibration; consider temperature scaling.
  - Patient-level drop: If patient-level accuracy is significantly lower than event-level, check disease distribution per patient and voting weights; maybe normal class events are dominating.
  - Class imbalance: Rare classes (e.g., stridor) may have near-zero recall; check confusion matrices.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Base model training verification: Train each base model on a small subset (e.g., 100 patients) and check that loss decreases and ROC-AUC improves on a held-out set. Verify that HeAR embeddings are being correctly extracted.
  2. Out-of-fold generation validation: Implement a single fold of out-of-fold prediction manually: split train into train/val, train on train, predict on val, compare with stored out-of-fold predictions from the full CV. Ensure no leakage.
  3. Voting hyperparameter sweep: On the validation set, systematically vary the three voting weights and the confidence threshold. Plot patient-level macro F1 as a heatmap to see sensitivity. This verifies that chosen parameters are near-optimal and not extremely sensitive.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Will PulmoVec maintain its performance gains in multi-center, cross-device external validation across diverse pediatric populations and real-world recording environments?
- **Basis:** The authors conclude that "Multi-center external validation across devices and real-world conditions remains essential." They cite the DeepBreath study's external validation decline as a cautionary example and state that generalizability must be systematically evaluated.
- **Why unresolved:** The study is a retrospective single-center analysis using one electronic stethoscope model (SPRSound). Performance may degrade due to device acoustic characteristics, population differences, environmental noise, and annotation protocol variations.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Prospective validation studies across multiple geographic sites using heterogeneous recording devices (including smartphones) and non-clinical/home settings. Reporting performance stratified by device type, recording environment noise level, and patient demographics.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How do the specific design choices of the weighted ensemble voting strategy for patient-level aggregation (weights, confidence threshold) impact robustness and generalizability compared to alternative aggregation methods, especially for classes with low recall like Normal?
- **Basis:** The authors describe the ensemble weights and >0.7 confidence threshold as "empirical design parameters" selected on the validation set. They acknowledge that the low patient-level recall for Normal (0.39) may relate to the aggregation strategy not modeling "temporal patterns or spatial relationships across locations." The design is pragmatic, not theoretically optimized.
- **Why unresolved:** The chosen parameters may overfit the specific noise structure and class distribution of SPRSound. Their performance on external data or with different event-level distributions (e.g., more focal diseases) is unknown. The strategy's failure to model spatial/temporal coherence is noted as a limitation.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A comparative ablation study testing alternative aggregation methods (e.g., hierarchical Bayesian models, attention mechanisms over events, spatial mapping) on both the internal test set and external validation cohorts. Analysis of performance sensitivity to small changes in voting weights and confidence threshold.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Is the observed low patient-level recall for the Normal class (0.39) primarily an artifact of the dataset's high intra-patient heterogeneity and focal pathology (i.e., normal-sounding segments in diseased patients), or does it indicate a fundamental limitation in the model's ability to integrate event-level evidence into a coherent patient-level decision?
- **Basis:** The Discussion states: "The relatively low recall observed for the Normal class at the patient level (0.39) should be interpreted not as a failure of the model to distinguish normal lung sounds, but as a reflection of the phase- and region-dependent nature of respiratory sounds." They cite a heatmap showing "normal-sounding segments predominate across nearly all disease categories" as evidence but also link it to aggregation method limitations.
- **Why unresolved:** While the clinical heterogeneity argument is plausible, it does not rule out that the aggregation strategy is suboptimal for this class. The Normal class is also the smallest patient-level class (n=67), increasing variance. Disentangling the contribution of data inherent noise from model aggregation failure requires controlled experiments.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** 1) Analyzing correlation between the number/percentage of Normal events per patient and final prediction for true Normal patients vs. false negatives. 2) Training and evaluating a patient-level model with explicit access to spatial map of recording locations and associated predictions. 3) A prospective study with denser, phase-synchronized recordings to reduce fundamental ambiguity.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How reliable are the model's probability outputs (calibration) across different tasks and classes, and does the stacking meta-learner improve probability calibration compared to the base HeAR classifiers?
- **Basis:** The authors report Brier scores and state: "Probability calibration was quantified using Brier scores, and the observed values... suggest non-random probabilistic discrimination that varies across tasks, although class-specific reliability analyses and calibration curves would be valuable for a more comprehensive evaluation in the future."
- **Why unresolved:** Brier scores are often reported without class-specific analysis. Poorly calibrated probabilities can undermine clinical decision-making (e.g., setting treatment thresholds). The stacking meta-learner's effect on calibration—whether it makes probabilities more or less reliable—is not assessed. This is critical for interpreting the confidence-weighted voting.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Generation of calibration plots (reliability diagrams) and computation of Expected Calibration Error (ECE) for each class in all models (base and stacked) on the test set. Comparative analysis would show if stacking improves or degrades calibration at both event and patient levels.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Do the acoustic patterns learned by the HeAR-based models align with established clinical auscultation knowledge (e.g., crackles as discontinuous inspiratory sounds, wheezes as continuous expiratory sounds), and can explainability techniques identify clinically meaningful features?
- **Basis:** The paper focuses on performance metrics and architecture. The Discussion mentions classical descriptions of wheezes and crackles when explaining Normal class recall, but there is no analysis of whether the model's internal representations or decisions correlate with these clinical definitions (e.g., phase detection, frequency bands). The "explainability" gap is highlighted in the introduction as a persistent issue.
- **Why unresolved:** Foundation models like HeAR are black boxes. Without explainability, it is unclear if high performance stems from learning true clinical acoustic patterns or spurious correlations in the SPRSound data (e.g., recording device noise, patient movement artifacts). This limits clinical trust and retrospective learning from model errors.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Application of post-hoc explainability methods (e.g., Grad-CAM, attention rollout) to visualize which temporal-frequency regions the model attends to for different sound patterns. Quantitative assessment of whether these regions match known spectral and temporal characteristics of adventitious sounds (e.g., crackle briefness, wheeze musicality). Correlation analysis between model confidence and features like signal-to-noise ratio, respiratory phase, or recording location consistency.

## Limitations
- Exact final LightGBM hyperparameters per target are unreported, hindering exact replication of the meta-learner.
- Mathematical details of "confidence-weighted voting" (e.g., how confidence is derived from probabilities) and the precise grid search for weights/thresholds are unspecified.
- The core assumption that HeAR's pre-training distribution includes relevant pediatric acoustic patterns lacks direct evidence; this transferability is inferred from performance, not verified.

## Confidence
- **Event-level performance metrics (ROC-AUC 0.94–0.96):** High – Directly reported with narrow 95% CIs.
- **Patient-level stacking improvement (9–12 pp):** Medium – Stated but without statistical tests or CIs for the delta.
- **Generalizability of foundation model transfer:** Low – Plausible but not proven; break conditions hypothesized, not systematically tested.
- **Low Normal class recall interpretation:** Medium – Clinically plausible heterogeneity argument provided, but aggregation failure not ruled out.

## Next Checks
1. **Base model training verification:** Train each base model on a small subset (e.g., 100 patients) and check that loss decreases and ROC-AUC improves on a held-out set. Verify that HeAR embeddings are being correctly extracted.
2. **Out-of-fold leakage audit:** Manually implement one CV fold for a base model, ensuring no patient data leaks between train/val splits. Compare generated OOF predictions against the paper's stored OOF matrix for consistency.
3. **Voting hyperparameter sensitivity:** On the validation set, perform a coarse grid search over the three voting weight combinations (soft/confidence/majority) and the confidence threshold. Plot resulting patient-level macro F1 to confirm the chosen (30/40/30, >0.7) parameters are near-optimal and not brittle.
