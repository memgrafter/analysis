# Rule-State Inference (RSI): A Bayesian Framework for Compliance Monitoring in Rule-Governed Domains

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.21610
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21610
- **Reference count:** 21
- **Primary result:** RSI achieves zero-shot F1=0.519 and AUC=0.599 on a Togolese fiscal benchmark (2,000 enterprises), outperforming deterministic baselines, with O(1) regulatory update speed (<1ms) and robustness under 50% missing data.

## Executive Summary
Rule-State Inference (RSI) is a Bayesian framework for compliance monitoring in rule-governed domains where authoritative rules are known but latent activation, compliance rates, and parametric drift must be inferred from noisy, partial data. RSI encodes each rule as a structured prior and performs posterior inference over a joint latent rule-state space using mean-field variational inference. Theoretical guarantees include O(1) time for regulatory updates (under 1ms), Bernstein-von Mises posterior consistency, and monotonic ELBO convergence. Empirical evaluation on a synthetic Togolese fiscal benchmark (2,000 enterprises) shows that RSI, in a zero-shot setting without labeled compliance data, achieves F1=0.519 and AUC=0.599—outperforming deterministic baselines—and maintains robust performance under up to 50% missing data.

## Method Summary
RSI models each tax rule (e.g., VAT, CIT, TPU) with latent variables: binary activation \(a_i\), compliance rate \(c_i\), and parametric drift \(\delta_i\). The prior factorizes across rules: \(P(S) = \prod_i P(a_i) P(c_i|a_i) P(\delta_i|a_i)\), with hyperparameters set from institutional knowledge (e.g., historical compliance rates). Observations per enterprise are linked to rules via segment-specific likelihoods: VAT compares declared vs. theoretical VAT (log-Gaussian noise), CIT compares declared vs. theoretical CIT, TPU uses payment delay, bank account, and under-declaration ratio. Missing data are handled by setting \(P(d_j|S)=1\). Inference uses mean-field variational inference with coordinate-ascent updates; conjugacy (Bernoulli-Beta, Gaussian-Gaussian) yields closed-form updates for the variational factors. Regulatory changes (e.g., threshold adjustments) are absorbed in constant time by multiplying the posterior kernel by a prior ratio correction \(\rho_k = P(s'_k|\Theta'_k)/P(s_k|\Theta_k)\) and renormalizing, avoiding full re-inference. The approach is zero-shot: no compliance labels are used for training; predictions for enterprises are derived from posterior expectations (e.g., non‑compliance if expected compliance below threshold for any applicable rule).

## Key Results
- Zero-shot classification on Togo benchmark: F1=0.519, AUC=0.599; RSI outperforms deterministic rule-based systems (RBS) despite no labeled training data.
- Regulatory update latency: <1ms (O(1) time) per change, representing at least 600× speedup over full model retraining of baselines (e.g., XGBoost).
- Robustness and convergence: Maintains performance under 50% missing data; ELBO increases monotonically and converges within ~7 VI iterations; theoretical guarantees include Bernstein‑von Mises posterior consistency as observations grow.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: O(1) Regulatory Adaptability via Prior Ratio Correction
- **Claim:** RSI absorbs regulatory updates (changing rule parameters) in constant time, independent of dataset size.
- **Mechanism:** The prior factorizes across rules: \(P(S)=\prod P(s_i|\Theta_i)\). A regulatory change \(U_k: \Theta_k\rightarrow\Theta'_k\) updates the posterior by multiplying the kernel by a scalar correction factor \(\rho_k = P(s'_k|\Theta'_k) / P(s_k|\Theta_k)\). Because \(\rho_k\) depends only on the parametric forms of the two distributions (e.g., Bernoulli, Beta, Gaussian), evaluating it requires a constant number of arithmetic operations.
- **Core assumption:** Rules are a priori independent and each rule’s state follows a conjugate distribution whose normalization constant can be updated analytically.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: “RSI absorbs regulatory changes in O(1) time via a prior ratio correction, independently of dataset size.”
  - [section 4.1]: Theorem 1 and proof detail the correction factor \(\rho_k\) and its O(1) evaluation.
  - [corpus]: No direct evidence; corpus papers focus on neuro‑symbolic or LLM‑based rule checking, not Bayesian prior factorization for regulatory updates.
- **Break condition:** Non‑factorizable priors (rule dependencies) or non‑conjugate likelihoods that prevent closed‑form posterior updates would break O(1) time, requiring iterative inference.

### Mechanism 2: Posterior Consistency (Bernstein‑von Mises)
- **Claim:** As observation count \(m\rightarrow\infty\), the posterior distribution over rule states converges to a Gaussian centered at the true state \(S^*\), with variance scaling as \(1/m\).
- **Mechanism:** Under regularity conditions (identifiability, smooth log‑likelihood, positive Fisher information, positive prior at truth), the posterior is dominated by the likelihood. A second‑order Taylor expansion of the log‑likelihood around \(S^*\) yields a Gaussian approximation. The prior becomes negligible, leading to \(\sqrt{m}(\hat{S}_m - S^*) \rightarrow \mathcal{N}(0, I(S^*)^{-1})\).
- **Core assumption:** The latent state \(S^*\) is identifiable from the data distribution; the likelihood is sufficiently smooth; the prior puts non‑zero mass near \(S^*\).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: “the posterior is Bernstein‑von Mises consistent, converging to the true rule state as observations accumulate.”
  - [section 4.2]: Theorem 2 states the BvM result and verifies conditions C1–C4 (identifiability, prior positivity, differentiability, finite Fisher information).
  - [corpus]: No direct evidence; related work on probabilistic logics does not address asymptotic posterior consistency for rule‑state inference.
- **Break condition:** Violating identifiability (multiple states produce identical likelihoods) or having a prior that excludes neighborhoods of \(S^*\) prevents convergence. Also, discrete state components (\(a_i\)) require careful handling in mixed‑space BvM theorems; the paper handwaves this via mean‑field relaxation.

### Mechanism 3: Monotone ELBO Convergence in Mean‑Field VI
- **Claim:** Coordinate ascent updates in the mean‑field variational inference (VI) scheme produce a non‑decreasing Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) sequence that converges to a stationary point.
- **Mechanism:** The ELBO is \(\log P(D) - \mathrm{KL}(Q_\phi(S) \| P(S|D))\). Each coordinate update minimizes the KL divergence to the exact conditional posterior \(P(s_i | S_{-i}, D)\) while holding other factors fixed. This cannot increase the KL, hence the ELBO (negative KL plus constant) cannot decrease.
- **Core assumption:** The variational family factors as \(Q_\phi(S)=\prod Q_{\phi_i}(s_i)\) (mean‑field). Updates are exact due to conjugate exponential‑family priors/likelihoods.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: “mean‑field variational inference monotonically maximizes the Evidence Lower BOund (ELBO).”
  - [section 4.3]: Theorem 3 proves monotonicity via KL non‑increase under coordinate updates.
  - [section 3.6]: Algorithm 1 shows analytical updates (Beta‑Binomial, Gaussian‑Gaussian) enabling exact coordinate minimization.
  - [corpus]: No direct evidence; standard VI theory, not novel to RSI.
- **Break condition:** Non‑conjugate updates requiring numerical optimization (e.g., gradient‑based) may not guarantee monotonic ELBO. Also, non‑factorized variational families (e.g., with dependencies between \(a_i\) and \(c_i\)) break the coordinate‑ascent convenience.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Bayesian inference with structured priors**
  - **Why needed here:** RSI treats regulatory rules not as patterns to learn but as authoritative constraints encoded as prior distributions. The engineer must understand how to map a legal rule (e.g., “VAT applies if turnover ≥ threshold”) into a prior factor \(P(a_i)\), \(P(c_i|a_i=1)\), etc., and how data updates these via Bayes’ rule.
  - **Quick check question:** How would you construct the prior \(P(c_i|a_i=1)\) for a rule with a historically stable compliance rate of 70%? What distribution and hyperparameters would you choose?
- **Concept: Variational inference (mean‑field)**
  - **Why needed here:** Exact posterior inference over many rules is intractable. RSI uses mean‑field VI to approximate \(P(S|D)\) with a factorized distribution \(Q_\phi\). The engineer must implement the coordinate updates and understand how the ELBO guides convergence.
  - **Quick check question:** In the Beta‑Binomial update for \(c_i\), what are the sufficient statistics \(n_{\text{ok}}\) and \(n_{\text{fail}}\) derived from the data? How does missing data affect them?
- **Concept: Conjugate Bayesian updating**
  - **Why needed here:** The O(1) update guarantee depends on analytical posterior updates when absorbing a regulatory change. This requires that the prior and likelihood are conjugate pairs (Bernoulli‑Binomial, Beta‑Binomial, Gaussian‑Gaussian). The engineer must recognize when conjugacy holds and derive the posterior parameters.
  - **Quick check question:** If a rule’s compliance prior is \(\text{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)\) and you observe \(k\) compliant entities out of \(n\) applicable ones, what is the posterior distribution for \(c_i\)? How would you compute its mean and variance?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** 
  1. Input layer: Raw observations \(D\) (noisy, partial) and regulatory rules \(R\) (structured as parameter templates).
  2. Prior construction module: Encodes each rule \(r_i\) into factors \(P(a_i)\), \(P(c_i|a_i)\), \(P(\delta_i|a_i)\) using hyperparameters \((\pi_i, \alpha_i, \beta_i, \sigma_i)\).
  3. Likelihood module: For each observation \(d_j\), computes \(P(d_j|S)\) by summing over applicable rules, using rule‑specific likelihood functions (e.g., log‑Gaussian noise for declared vs. theoretical VAT).
  4. Variational inference engine: Mean‑field coordinate ascent: iteratively updates \(Q(a_i)\), \(Q(c_i)\), \(Q(\delta_i)\) using closed‑form conjugate updates until ELBO convergence.
  5. Posterior summarization: Outputs \(P(a_i=1|D)\), \(E[c_i|D]\pm\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[c_i|D]}\), \(E[\delta_i|D]\) for each rule.
  6. Regulatory update handler: On rule change \(U_k\), multiplies the current posterior kernel by \(\rho_k = P(s'_k|\Theta'_k)/P(s_k|\Theta_k)\) and re‑normalizes analytically.
- **Critical path:** From data ingestion → likelihood computation → VI loop (5–10 iterations) → posterior. The update handler shortcuts this path for regulatory changes.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Zero‑shot vs. accuracy: RSI forgoes training on labeled compliance data (zero‑shot) at the cost of lower F1/AUC vs. supervised baselines. Tradeoff is justified in low‑label regimes.
  - Factorized prior vs. dependencies: Assuming rule independence (\(P(S)=\prod P(s_i)\)) enables O(1) updates and analytical VI but ignores potential correlations (e.g., VAT and CIT compliance may be linked). Future work could add Belief Propagation.
  - Simple likelihoods vs. realism: Segment‑specific likelihoods (TPU, VAT, CIT) require domain expertise to design. Overly simplistic likelihoods bias posteriors; overly complex ones risk non‑conjugacy.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Poor prior specification: If \(\pi_i\) or \(E[c_i]\) are far from institutional reality, posteriors may be mis‑calibrated even with much data (Thm 2 only guarantees asymptotic recovery).
  - Non‑conjugate updates: If custom likelihoods are added without conjugate priors, O(1) update guarantee breaks; VI may need stochastic gradients, losing monotonic ELBO.
  - Violated independence: If rules are highly correlated (e.g., VAT and turnover‑based CIT), mean‑field approximation error increases, potentially misestimating joint compliance states.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Overall performance (EXP‑1): Compare RSI (zero‑shot) vs. deterministic RBS and supervised XGBoost/MLP on F1, AUC, recall. Shows RSI outperforms RBS despite no labels, but lags supervised methods.
  2. O(1) Adaptability (EXP‑2): Apply the VAT threshold change (60M→100M FCFA). Measure RSI update time (<1 ms) vs. XGBoost retraining (683–1082 ms). Validates Theorem 1’s speedup.
  3. ELBO Convergence (EXP‑5): Plot ELBO vs. VI iteration. Should show monotonic increase and convergence within ~7 iterations, confirming Theorem 3. Also checks robustness to missing data (EXP‑4) and posterior uncertainty trends (EXP‑3).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How does RSI perform in real‑world tax administration settings with genuinely noisy, strategically manipulated data, as opposed to the controlled synthetic benchmark?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors explicitly state in the Discussion: “Future work will focus on pilot deployments with tax administrations to validate performance on real‑world data streams,” acknowledging the limitation of synthetic validation.
- **Why unresolved:** The synthetic dataset, while grounded in real rules, may not capture all idiosyncratic noise, adversarial behavior, or data integration challenges of live administrative systems. Performance on synthetic data (F1=0.519) may not translate to operational utility.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Pilot studies conducted with tax authorities (e.g., Togo’s OTR) using historical real data, comparing RSI’s posterior inferences against known audit outcomes or expert assessments, and measuring operational impacts like audit selection accuracy or revenue recovery.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How sensitive is RSI’s posterior inference to misspecification in the segment‑specific likelihood functions, which currently require domain expertise to design?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper notes that “segment‑specific likelihood functions currently require domain expertise; future work will explore amortized inference for likelihood learning.” This implies the current fixed likelihoods are a simplifying assumption that may not perfectly match real data‑generating processes.
- **Why unresolved:** Inaccurate likelihood models (e.g., wrong noise distribution for declared taxes) could bias posterior estimates of compliance rates (\(c_i\)) and drift (\(\delta_i\)), especially with limited data. The theoretical guarantees (T2, T3) assume correct model specification.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic sensitivity analysis where the true synthetic likelihood is known (via the dataset generator), then deliberately misspecified likelihoods (e.g., wrong variance, wrong distribution family) are used in inference to measure degradation in posterior accuracy (e.g., TV distance to true latent states).

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can RSI scale to full national tax codes with hundreds of interdependent rules while preserving O(1) update complexity and interpretable posteriors?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper suggests scaling via “factor graph extensions via Belief Propagation” but does not implement or test this. The current mean‑field VI assumes rule independence (factorized prior), which may be violated in complex tax codes where rules interact (e.g., deductions affecting multiple tax bases).
- **Why unresolved:** Rule dependencies could break the factorization \(P(S)=\prod P(s_i)\), requiring more complex approximate inference that may lose O(1) update guarantees or analytical update simplicity. Interpretability might also suffer with entangled posterior marginals.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Extend RSI to a factor graph encoding known rule dependencies (e.g., from legal texts), implement belief propagation or structured VI, and test on a larger synthetic tax code (e.g., 100+ rules) measuring: (a) update time for a regulatory change, (b) posterior accuracy against ground truth, (c) clarity of per‑rule posterior summaries.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Can RSI be extended to sequential, time‑series compliance tracking where latent rule states (\(a_i, c_i, \delta_i\)) evolve over time, leveraging temporal coherence?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The Conclusion lists “sequential RSI for longitudinal compliance tracking” as future work, and the Discussion notes a “connection to Kalman Filtering,” suggesting a temporal model is a natural extension.
- **Why unresolved:** The current RSI is episodic: each period’s inference is independent given priors. A sequential model would need a process model (e.g., random walk for drift \(\delta_i\), Markov chain for activation \(a_i\)) and could better handle gradual changes, but would complicate the O(1) update property and require new theoretical guarantees.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Develop a state‑space RSI where priors at time \(t\) depend on posteriors at \(t-1\) via a temporal model (e.g., \(\delta_{i,t} = \delta_{i,t-1} + \eta\)). Evaluate on multi‑period synthetic data (e.g., 5 years) measuring: (a) accuracy of inferred state trajectories, (b) ability to detect abrupt regulatory changes vs. gradual drift, (c) computational cost per time step.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Does RSI’s zero‑shot compliance assessment generalize across jurisdictions with different legal families, administrative structures, and data infrastructures?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The Conclusion proposes “multi‑country extensions across the ECOWAS fiscal zone” as future work, implying a need to test beyond Togo’s Francophone African context.
- **Why unresolved:** The Togolese instantiation uses specific rule definitions, prior hyperparameters (Table 4), and segment likelihoods tailored to that system. Different countries may have different rule logics, compliance cultures, and data availability patterns, potentially requiring non‑trivial adaptation of the prior structure or likelihood design.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Apply RSI to tax benchmarks from other countries (e.g., Senegal, Ivory Coast within ECOWAS) using their respective tax codes. Measure zero‑shot performance (F1, AUC) without retraining likelihoods or priors, and analyze which components (priors vs. likelihoods) need re‑parameterization for cross‑jurisdiction transfer.

## Limitations
- **Unspecified likelihood details:** Exact mathematical forms of the segment‑specific likelihood functions (VAT, CIT, TPU) and the definition of “compliance signals” used in updates are not fully described, blocking faithful replication.
- **Synthetic evaluation:** Results are on a synthetic benchmark grounded in Togolese rules; performance may not generalize to real‑world tax administrations due to unmodeled noise, adversarial behavior, and data integration challenges.
- **Assumption sensitivity:** Theoretical guarantees (O(1) updates, posterior consistency, monotone ELBO) rely on strong assumptions: rule independence, conjugate priors, correct likelihood specification, and identifiability. Violations (e.g., correlated rules, misspecified likelihoods) could degrade performance or break guarantees.

## Confidence
- **O(1) regulatory update:** Medium confidence (theoretically sound under independence/conjugacy; empirical validation on single benchmark; not tested across diverse rule structures).
- **Bernstein‑von Mises posterior consistency:** Medium confidence (proof depends on regularity conditions; discrete states and potential rule correlations may be fragile).
- **Monotone ELBO convergence:** High confidence (standard mean‑field VI result; hinges on correct conjugate updates which are described but not fully verified in implementation).
