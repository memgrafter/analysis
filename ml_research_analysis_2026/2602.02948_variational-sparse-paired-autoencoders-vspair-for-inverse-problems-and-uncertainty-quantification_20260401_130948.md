# Variational Sparse Paired Autoencoders (vsPAIR) for Inverse Problems and Uncertainty Quantification

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.02948
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02948
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: vsPAIR achieves fast amortized inference with structured uncertainty quantification, yielding 95% sparsity and competitive reconstruction on blind MNIST inpainting and CT reconstruction.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces Variational Sparse Paired Autoencoders (vsPAIR), a framework for solving inverse problems with amortized inference and interpretable uncertainty. vsPAIR couples a sparse variational autoencoder (sVAE) for the unknown quantity of interest with a standard VAE for observations, linked by a learned latent mapping. The sVAE employs a spike-and-slab prior and a beta hyperprior to automatically control sparsity. Experiments on blind MNIST inpainting and CT reconstruction demonstrate that vsPAIR achieves high sparsity (95%) and reconstruction accuracy comparable to or exceeding baseline methods, while providing pixel-wise uncertainty estimates that highlight corrupted regions.

## Method Summary
vsPAIR addresses inverse problems by modeling the joint distribution of the quantity of interest (QoI) \(x\) and observations \(y\). The architecture comprises two VAEs: a sparse VAE (sVAE) for \(x\) with a spike-and-slab prior on its latent variables, and a standard VAE for \(y\). A learned mapping \(M\) (implemented as an MLP) transforms the latent parameters of \(y\) \((\mu_y, \log\sigma_y^2)\) into preliminary parameters for \(x\) \((\mu_x, \log\sigma_x^2, \log\omega_x)\), where \(\omega_x\) governs the slab inclusion probability. Training optimizes a combined loss that includes the ELBOs of both VAEs, an MSE loss between the mapped and true sVAE latent parameters, and a regularization term for the beta hyperprior on \(\omega_x\). The Bernoulli mask for the spike-and-slab is relaxed using a straight‑through estimator to enable end‑to‑end differentiation. At inference time, given a new \(y\), the VAE encoder produces \((\mu_y, \log\sigma_y^2)\), which \(M\) maps to \((\mu_x, \log\sigma_x^2, \log\omega_x)\); these define an approximate posterior from which latent samples are drawn and decoded to obtain posterior samples of \(x\). This amortized approach yields fast inference and naturally provides uncertainty through posterior sampling, with sparsity ensuring that only relevant latent dimensions influence reconstruction.

## Key Results
- Blind MNIST inpainting: 95% sparsity, single‑sample MSE \(7.62\times10^{-2}\), 30‑sample average MSE \(6.43\times10^{-2}\), and a 5× higher variance on missing pixels than intact ones.
- CT reconstruction: vsPAIR matches or exceeds the performance of LPD and FBP baselines at high noise levels, while producing interpretable uncertainty maps that reflect reconstruction confidence.
- Latent analysis: Perturbing consistently active latent dimensions selectively alters corrupted regions, demonstrating structured control.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The spike‑and‑slab prior imposes a discrete-continuous mixture that drives many latent dimensions to zero, resulting in a compact representation that naturally explains only the uncertain aspects of the inverse problem. The beta hyperprior eliminates the need to manually set the sparsity level; instead the data determine an appropriate inclusion probability. By pairing a standard VAE for observations with a sparse VAE for the QoI and learning a deterministic mapping between their latent spaces, vsPAIR amortizes the posterior inference: once trained, the mapping directly produces a full approximate posterior for \(x\) given \(y\), avoiding costly optimization per instance. The inclusion of both ELBOs ensures that both VAEs learn faithful generative models of their respective domains, while the mapping MSE loss aligns the two latent representations. The straight‑through estimator allows gradients to flow through the Bernoulli mask, making end‑to‑end training feasible. Finally, generating multiple posterior samples via the sVAE decoder yields a predictive distribution whose variance serves as an uncertainty measure; because the latent mask is sparse, uncertainty is concentrated on dimensions that affect the corrupted regions, leading to higher variance in missing areas.

## Foundational Learning
- **Variational Autoencoder (VAE)**  
  *Why needed*: Provides the foundational generative framework for both the observation and QoI models, enabling tractable posterior approximation via reparameterization.  
  *Quick check*: Train a standard VAE on MNIST; verify that test reconstructions are recognizable and that the ELBO decreases steadily.

- **Spike‑and‑Slab Prior**  
  *Why needed*: Introduces sparsity in the latent space by mixing a point mass at zero with a continuous distribution; essential for structured uncertainty and compact representations.  
  *Quick check*: Sample from a spike‑and‑slab prior with inclusion probability 0.2; confirm that approximately 20% of dimensions are exactly zero.

- **Amortized Inference**  
  *Why needed*: Replaces per‑input optimization with a learned inference network, enabling fast predictions for new data—a critical requirement for inverse problems.  
  *Quick check*: After training, feed a test image through the VAE encoder and check that the output latent parameters are produced in a single forward pass (no iterative steps).

- **Beta Hyperprior**  
  *Why needed*: Places a prior on the inclusion probability \(\omega\) so that sparsity level is data‑driven; hyperparameters \(\alpha_0,\beta_0\) control the strength of regularization.  
  *Quick check*: Monitor the posterior mean of \(\omega\) during training; it should converge to a stable value between 0 and 1, not collapse to 0 or 1 prematurely.

- **Straight‑Through Estimator**  
  *Why needed*: Allows backpropagation through discrete Bernoulli sampling by using the identity function in the forward pass and a straight‑through gradient in the backward pass.  
  *Quick check*: Compute \(\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial \text{logits}\) after a sampling step; it should be non‑zero even though the forward pass used a binary mask.

- **Uncertainty Quantification via Posterior Sampling**  
  *Why needed*: Inverse problems are ill‑posed; a single solution is insufficient. Multiple samples from the approximate posterior provide a predictive distribution, and pixel‑wise variance serves as an uncertainty map.  
  *Quick check*: For a test input, draw at least 30 posterior samples and compute the standard deviation per pixel; verify that variance is higher in regions known to be corrupted.

## Architecture Onboarding

*Component map*  
Training data flow:  
`x -> sVAE_encoder -> (μ_x_true, logσ²_x_true, logω_x_true)`  
`y -> VAE_encoder -> (μ_y, logσ²_y)`  
`(μ_y, logσ²_y) -> Mapping_MLP -> (μ_x_hat, logσ²_x_hat, logω_x_hat)`  
Losses:  
- `L_sVAE` on `x` using `(μ_x_true, logσ²_x_true, logω_x_true)`  
- `L_VAE` on `y`  
- `L_M` = MSE between `(μ_x_hat, logσ²_x_hat, logω_x_hat)` and `(μ_x_true, logσ²_x_true, logω_x_true)`  
- `L_ρ` (beta hyperprior regularization)

Inference (amortized) flow:  
`y -> VAE_encoder -> (μ_y, logσ²_y) -> Mapping_MLP -> (μ_x, logσ²_x, logω_x) -> sVAE_sampler -> z -> sVAE_decoder -> x_hat`

*Critical path*  
The inference pipeline `VAE_encoder → Mapping_MLP → sVAE_sampler → sVAE_decoder` is the core amortized inference chain; it is executed repeatedly for fast prediction.

*Design tradeoffs*  
1. **Observation VAE simplicity** – Using a standard VAE for \(y\) keeps the model modular but may limit representation power if \(y\) has complex structure.  
2. **Spike‑and‑slab relaxation** – The Bernoulli relaxation temperature \(c\) balances gradient magnitude and discreteness; too high yields biased gradients, too low causes training instability.  
3. **Mapping capacity** – The MLP mapping approximates a potentially complex relationship; increasing depth/width improves fit but risks overfitting and memory usage, especially on high‑resolution CT data.  
4. **Beta hyperprior hyperparameters** – \(\alpha_0,\beta_0\) control the prior concentration; extreme values can force sparsity to collapse or become overly dense, requiring careful tuning.

*Failure signatures*  
- **NaN loss** – Often originates from KL divergence explosion or numerical overflow in the spike‑and‑slab term (e.g., \(\omega\) approaching 0). Check clamping of \(\omega\) and log‑variance ranges.  
- **Zero sparsity** – If active dimensions remain near full capacity, consider increasing sparsity pressure by lowering \(\alpha_0/\beta_0\) or raising the hyperprior weight \(\lambda_\rho\).  
- **Poor reconstruction** – Discrepancy between predicted and true latents (high \(L_M\)) indicates the mapping has not learned; try increasing MLP capacity or adjusting \(\lambda_3\).  
- **Uncertainty miscalibration** – If variance does not concentrate on missing regions, verify that the sVAE posterior is properly trained (sufficient KL weight \(\gamma_x\)) and that enough posterior samples are drawn.

*Three first experiments*  
1. **Baseline MNIST reproduction** – Train with hyperparameters from Table 9 (100 epochs, batch 64). Verify ≥95% sparsity and single‑sample MSE ≤ 0.0762.  
2. **Uncertainty sanity check** – For a corrupted MNIST test image, generate 30 posterior samples, compute per‑pixel variance, and check that the variance in the five missing 5×5 patches is at least 3 × higher than in intact areas.  
3. **Ablation of beta hyperprior** – Train a variant with fixed \(\omega\) prior (e.g., Bernoulli(0.5)) and compare sparsity stability and reconstruction quality to the full model; this demonstrates the adaptive benefit.

## Limitations

- The mapping between CT hyperparameters (high‑fidelity vs. strong sparsity) and the corresponding figures is described in the text but not clearly tabulated, risking mis‑configuration.
- The CT model contains approximately 1.1 billion parameters, demanding substantial GPU memory and compute resources, which may limit accessibility for many researchers.
