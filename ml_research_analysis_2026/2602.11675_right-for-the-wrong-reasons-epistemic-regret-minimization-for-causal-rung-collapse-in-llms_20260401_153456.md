# Right for the Wrong Reasons: Epistemic Regret Minimization for Causal Rung Collapse in LLMs
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.11675
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11675
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** LLMs exhibit persistent Rung Collapse; Epistemic Regret Minimization recovers 53–59% of entrenched errors where outcome-level feedback fails.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates "Rung Collapse" in large language models—the conflation of associative reasoning (P(Y|X)) with interventional causal reasoning (P(Y|do(X)))—which leads to entrenched flawed models when outcome-based learning reinforces "Aleatoric Success". The authors propose Epistemic Regret Minimization (ERM), a belief revision objective that penalizes causal reasoning errors independently of task success, implemented within a three-layer architecture that leverages a Physical Grounding Theorem and AGM belief revision to update an external causal graph using interventional data from physical actions.

Experiments on 1,360 causal trap scenarios across six frontier LLMs show that Rung Collapse persists even in reasoning-enhanced models (e.g., GPT-5.2: 3.7%, Claude Sonnet 4.5: 0.9%). Advanced models exhibit "Epistemic Stubbornness" against generic feedback; however, targeted ERM correction recovers 53–59% of entangled errors, demonstrating the necessity of epistemic interventions for safe AGI.

## Method Summary
The study uses the CausalT5K benchmark, specifically its L2 subset containing 1,360 causal trap scenarios across five domains. Six LLM API versions (GPT-3.5 Turbo, GPT-4 Turbo, GPT-5.2, Gemini 2.5 Flash, Claude Sonnet 4.5, Llama 3.3 70B) are evaluated zero-shot with greedy decoding. The core experiments are: (A) measuring baseline Rung Collapse rate via a single prompt; (B) for failures ("Wolf Cases"), applying either Standard Correction (generic "Are you sure?") or ERM Correction (specific critique from the benchmark's "wise refusal") and measuring correction rates. The ERP simulation uses the wise refusal text as a proxy for the epistemic error signal Δ to update an external causal graph and failure registry, though the full three-layer belief revision loop is not fully operational in the offline test.

## Key Results
- Rung Collapse persists in frontier models: GPT-5.2 (3.7%), Claude Sonnet 4.5 (0.9%).
- Epistemic Regret Minimization recovers 53–59% of entrenched errors where generic outcome-level feedback fails.
- The History domain shows particularly high stubborn error rate (79% of stubborn cases).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
1. **Physical Grounding Theorem**  
   Claim: Physical actions satisfying actuator independence instantiate Pearl’s do‑operator, providing valid interventional data for causal learning.  
   Mechanism: The theorem proves that when an actuator sets a variable X=x independently of X’s parents and exogenous noise, the resulting distribution equals P(Y|do(X=x)), bridging action languages and do‑calculus.  
   Core assumptions: independent mechanisms (structural equations are autonomous) and actuator independence.  
   Evidence: Theorem 1 and its proof in Section 3.2.  
   Break condition: Fails if actuator independence is violated (e.g., action indirectly influences X via its parents).

2. **Epistemic Regret Minimization (ERM)**  
   Claim: ERM prevents Aleatoric Entrenchment by penalizing causal errors even when task outcomes are correct.  
   Mechanism: ERM adds an epistemic loss term λR_ep(t) to the training objective, where R_ep is the KL divergence between the model’s predicted interventional distribution and the observed distribution. This provides a gradient signal when standard task loss is zero.  
   Core assumptions: the agent can perform interventions and observe outcomes; an external causal graph G_t exists and can be revised.  
   Evidence: Definition in Section 3.3 and Theorem 2.  
   Break condition: requires accurate observation of Y post‑intervention; noisy or missing outcomes degrade the corrective signal.

3. **Failure Mode Taxonomy and Cross‑Domain Guards**  
   Claim: A failure mode taxonomy with domain‑independent guards enables cross‑domain transfer of causal corrections.  
   Mechanism: ERM classifies high‑regret errors into structural failure modes (e.g., CONFOUNDERBLIND). When a mode’s frequency exceeds a threshold, a guard (a meta‑prompt constraint) is injected into the LLM’s reasoning for all future queries. Guards are falsifiable and retracted via AGM contraction if they increase regret.  
   Core assumptions: the same structural reasoning error recurs across unrelated domains; guards can be expressed as effective natural language constraints.  
   Evidence: Abstract and Section 4, Table 4.  
   Break condition: taxonomy is incomplete or guards are too generic or too domain‑specific.

## Foundational Learning
- **Pearl’s Causal Hierarchy** (Association, Intervention, Counterfactuals). *Why needed:* Distinguishes L1 (P(Y|X)) from L2 (P(Y|do(X))) reasoning, central to identifying Rung Collapse. *Quick check:* "What is the difference between P(Y|X) and P(Y|do(X))? Provide an example where they diverge due to confounding."
- **AGM Belief Revision** (Alchourrón, Gärdenfors, Makinson). *Why needed:* ERM is framed as an AGM‑compliant belief revision operator using interventional evidence. *Quick check:* "State two of the AGM postulates for belief revision (e.g., Success, Inclusion)."
- **Independent Mechanisms Assumption**. *Why needed:* Underlies the Physical Grounding Theorem and the modularity of causal models, enabling valid interventions. *Quick check:* "What does it mean for structural equations to be independent? Why is this important for intervention?"

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** The architecture consists of the following components: LLM (frozen), External Causal Graph G_t, Failure Registry F_t, Causal Transaction Log (CTL), ERM Revision Loop, and Routing Component.

**Critical path:**
1. Task decomposition into subtasks.
2. For each subtask, LLM generates hypothesis H conditioned on G_t and active guards from F_t.
3. Execute action a to obtain outcome Y.
4. Compare predicted Ŷ with Y to compute Δ.
5. If |Δ| > ε:
   - Layer 1: Update G_t via edge contraction/strengthening based on CTL support/refutation.
   - Layer 2: Classify failure mode f_k and update F_t; if frequency high, inject new guard.
6. Layer 3: Monitor residual regret per task class; trigger routing if above threshold.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- External vs internal knowledge: External G_t avoids forgetting and allows inspection but adds serialization latency.
- Feedback specificity: Generic outcome feedback yields sycophancy; ERM requires accurate identification of failed causal claim.
- Guard generality: Domain-independent guards enable transfer but may miss domain-specific nuances; overly broad guards may hinder performance.

**Failure signatures:**
- High epistemic regret R_ep despite zero task loss (Aleatoric Success).
- Repeated Rung Collapse on same trap types across domains.
- Model resists generic correction but responds to named bias feedback (e.g., “consider survivorship bias”).
- Layer 1 corrections fail repeatedly for a pattern → likely missing Layer 2 guard.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Reproduce Experiment A:** Evaluate zero-shot Rung Collapse rate on CausalT5K L2 subset across models. Confirm persistent collapse (e.g., GPT-5.2 at 3.7%).
2. **Reproduce Experiment B:** On Wolf cases (Exp A failures), apply Standard Correction (outcome-based) vs ERM Correction (epistemic). Expect reasoning models (GPT-4/5.2) show inverse scaling: low generic correction rate but high ERM recovery.
3. **Guard injection test:** Select a failure mode (e.g., CONFOUNDERBLIND), disable its guard for a domain, measure failure rate, then reactivate guard and verify cross-domain reduction in that error pattern.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Does ERM prevent Aleatoric Entrenchment in embodied/long-horizon agents?** The paper notes that testing on physical agents or long‑horizon software agents remains a critical next step. Empirical validation was limited to a static text benchmark; full belief revision loop not tested in dynamic environments with latent/continuous variables. Resolution would require deploying the three‑layer ERM architecture on embodied tasks and measuring convergence of G_t and stability under distribution shift.
2. **Are high failure rates in the History domain due to pre‑training bias or fundamental limitations?** The white‑box analysis attributes History’s difficulty to counterfactual inaccessibility, narrative coherence bias, and training distribution alignment. It remains unclear whether failures would persist if narratives were de‑biased or if the problem is intrinsic to reasoning about unique, non‑repeatable events. Resolution: construct a controlled benchmark variant with de‑biased historical narratives or test on a simulated socioeconomic world where interventions are possible.
3. **Do ERM‑corrected causal models exhibit durable belief revision?** The paper did not assess long‑term retention or cross‑domain transfer of corrections; Experiment B only evaluated immediate steerability. LLMs might comply temporarily without deep epistemic change. Resolution: longitudinal experiments testing retention over many turns and transfer to unrelated domains after correction.
4. **Can the “systematic doubt” and explicit causal vocabulary of Claude Sonnet 4.5 be induced in other models?** The superior performance is speculated to stem from an alignment objective penalizing unsupported causal claims. It is unknown whether this is an artifact of specific training or can be engineered. Resolution: prompt other models with “Chain-of-Doubt” instructions mimicking Claude’s style or analyze Claude’s training signals and replicate via fine‑tuning.
5. **Does ERM generalize to novel, unanticipated causal errors?** The current failure‑mode taxonomy was derived from CausalT5K and may not cover new error types. Real‑world errors could involve new biases (e.g., overadjustment for colliders). Resolution: test ERM on held‑out scenarios designed to exhibit errors not in the taxonomy, or implement an automated taxonomy extension mechanism.

## Limitations
- **Dataset and annotation accessibility:** The CausalT5K benchmark and its “wise refusal” annotations are required for replication but may not be publicly available.
- **Model version and API volatility:** The experiments rely on unreleased or future‑specified model versions (e.g., GPT-5.2, Claude Sonnet 4.5) that may be inaccessible or changed.
- **Simulation proxy and sycophancy risks:** ERM feedback is simulated using a static critique rather than the full belief‑revised loop; correction rates may be inflated by sycophantic compliance.
