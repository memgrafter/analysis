# HEART: A Unified Benchmark for Assessing Humans and LLMs in Emotional Support Dialogue

## Quick Facts

- **arXiv ID**: 2601.19922  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.19922  
- **Reference count**: 23  
- **Primary result**: Frontier LLMs achieve Elo scores near or exceeding average humans in perceived empathy and consistency, with human-model agreement at 78.7%.

## Executive Summary

HEART introduces the first unified benchmark for directly comparing human and language model performance in multi-turn emotional support dialogues. It addresses the lack of standardized evaluation by collecting both human-written and model-generated completions for 300 dialogue histories derived from the ESConv dataset, and then assessing them through blinded pairwise preferences across five interpersonal dimensions. The evaluation aggregates preferences into Elo ratings and also measures strategy diversity, response latency, and model behavior on adversarial turns.

Key findings show that state‑of‑the‑art LLMs reach near‑human Elo in perceived empathy and consistency, with human‑model agreement matching inter‑human reliability. However, humans retain advantages in adaptive reframing and handling adversarial situations. The benchmark also reveals a trade‑off between supportive quality and response latency, with most high‑quality models being slower—except for Polaris 4, which achieves frontier performance with sub‑500 ms latency, making it suitable for real‑time voice interaction.

## Method Summary

The benchmark task is to complete short emotional support dialogues with a single supportive utterance. The dataset consists of 300 curated contexts (280 regular, 20 adversarial) from ESConv, filtered for “response potential” and safety. For each context, three independent human contributors and 16 target LLMs produce completions under deterministic sampling. Evaluation is performed in two ways: (1) five human judges per response pair provide blinded, forced‑choice preferences (no rubric); (2) an LLM‑as‑judge ensemble scores each response on the five HEART dimensions using a provided rubric. The primary metric is an Elo rating derived from a Bradley‑Terry fit to all pairwise preferences. Secondary analyses include per‑dimension percentile ranks, strategy usage diversity (via an LLM classifier over a 15‑category taxonomy), and median time‑to‑first‑token latency. The design also incorporates adversarial seeker turns to probe robustness.

## Key Results

- Frontier models (e.g., GPT‑4, Claude 3) achieve Elo scores at or above the human average in perceived empathy and consistency.
- Human‑model agreement reaches 78.7%, matching the inter‑human agreement rate measured on the same pairs.
- Humans maintain advantages in adaptive reframing and handling adversarial turns, while LLMs excel at surface empathy through stylistic warmth; LLMs show lower strategy diversity than humans.
- High supportive‑dialogue quality correlates with increased latency, but Polaris 4 defies this trend with near‑frontier Elo and sub‑500 ms response time.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

HEART works because it evaluates responses in a realistic, multi‑turn setting using holistic human judgments that capture the nuances of emotional support. The blinded pairwise preference design eliminates absolute‑score biases and focuses on relative quality, which aligns with how people choose between alternatives. By aggregating many preferences with the Bradley‑Terry model, it produces stable Elo ratings that are directly comparable across humans and models. The inclusion of five well‑defined interpersonal dimensions and an adversarial subset ensures both breadth and depth of assessment, while the separation of generation and evaluation prevents leakage of model‑specific cues.

## Foundational Learning

**1. Emotional Support Dialogue**  
- *Why needed*: Defines the domain and core task—providing comfort and practical aid to a distressed person.  
- *Quick check*: Given a dialogue, can you identify the seeker’s emotional state and the supporter’s intent?

**2. Pairwise Preference Evaluation**  
- *Why needed*: Avoids scale inconsistencies and yields reliable relative rankings.  
- *Quick check*: For two responses to the same context, which would a typical human judge prefer? Compare your choice to majority vote.

**3. Bradley‑Terry Model**  
- *Why needed*: Transforms many pairwise comparisons into a single, interpretable score (Elo).  
- *Quick check*: Fit a BT model on a small mock preference matrix and verify the ordering of scores matches intuition.

**4. Support Strategy Taxonomy**  
- *Why needed*: Enables analysis of the diversity and appropriateness of supportive techniques (e.g., reflection, validation).  
- *Quick check*: Classify a short response into the correct strategy category using the rubric.

**5. Adversarial Turns**  
- *Why needed*: Stress‑tests robustness by escalating the seeker’s distress in controlled ways.  
- *Quick check*: Does a given response effectively de‑escalate an adversarial turn? Mark Yes/No.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
ESConv dataset → Dialogue History Selection → Human/LLM Response Generation → Response Pool → Blinded Pairwise Presentation → Human Judges + LLM‑Judge Ensemble → Preference Matrix → Bradley‑Terry Fitting → Elo Scores; Parallel: Response Pool → Strategy Classifier → Strategy Diversity Metrics; Parallel: Response Pool → Latency Measurement → TTFT analysis.

### Critical Path
The core pipeline starts with context selection, proceeds through independent response generation (human and LLM), then to blinded pairwise evaluation, and ends with Elo computation. Secondary analyses (strategy diversity, latency) branch off the generated responses.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Human vs. LLM judges**: Humans provide authentic preferences but are costly; LLM judges are cheap and scalable but may exhibit biases (e.g., toward verbosity).
- **Deterministic sampling**: Ensures reproducibility but limits exploration of model creativity; adding temperature would increase variance.
- **Blinded evaluation**: Prevents model identity bias but requires careful UI to avoid leakage of style cues.

### Failure Signatures
- Low inter‑rater reliability (Fleiss’ κ < 0.6) among human judges indicates unclear instructions or ambiguous tasks.
- High correlation between preferences and response length suggests judge bias toward verbosity.
- Large variance in latency measurements across runs (>20%) signals caching issues or inconsistent network conditions.
- Adversarial transforms that fail to follow the intended escalation profile produce invalid test cases.

### First Three Experiments
1. **Human baseline validation**: Compute Elo scores for human‑human pairs and measure Fleiss’ κ; expect κ ≥ 0.6 and stable Elo distribution.
2. **Judge ensemble calibration**: Compare LLM‑judge preferences to human judgments on a held‑out subset; quantify agreement and identify systematic biases.
3. **Strategy diversity analysis**: Using the LLM classifier, compute coverage, entropy, and standard deviation of strategy usage across humans and models; verify humans show higher diversity.

## Limitations

- Human contributor pool details (screening, demographics, clinician proportion) are unspecified, limiting generalizability of the human baseline.
- The LLM‑as‑judge ensemble configuration (specific checkpoints, prompts, bias safeguards) is undisclosed, affecting reliability of dimension‑level scores.
- The adversarial transformation pipeline (LLM used, prompt structure, validation metrics) lacks reproducible documentation, risking inconsistent data generation.

## Confidence

- Frontier LLMs achieve Elo near human average in perceived empathy: **Medium**
- Human‑model agreement (78.7%) matches inter‑human agreement: **High**
- Humans retain advantages in adaptive reframing and adversarial handling: **Medium**
- High supportive‑dialogue quality correlates with increased latency: **Low**
- LLMs excel at surface empathy while humans show greater strategy diversity: **Medium**

## Next Checks

1. Replicate pairwise comparisons with a larger, demographically stratified human judge pool to confirm Elo stability and agreement metrics.
2. Perform an ablation of the LLM‑as‑judge ensemble—test individual models and prompt variants—to quantify bias and identify optimal single‑judge configuration.
3. Validate strategy labels with expert human annotators on a response subset; measure agreement with the LLM classifier to assess labeling accuracy.
