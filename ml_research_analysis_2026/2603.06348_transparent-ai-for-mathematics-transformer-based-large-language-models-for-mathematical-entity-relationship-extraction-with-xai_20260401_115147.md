# Transparent AI for Mathematics: Transformer-Based Large Language Models for Mathematical Entity Relationship Extraction with XAI

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.06348
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06348
- Reference count: 38
- Primary result: Fine-tuning BERT on a custom dataset yields 99.39% accuracy for extracting mathematical entity relations, with SHAP providing token-level explanations.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles Mathematical Entity Relationship Extraction (MERE) by framing operands as entities and operators as relations in mathematical text. A BERT-based model is fine-tuned on a novel dataset comprising 3,284 English statements derived from Bangla MER and Somikoron. The model achieves state-of-the-art performance (accuracy 99.39%, micro F1 99.36%). Explainability via SHAP reveals that predictions are driven predominantly by operation-specific keywords (e.g., “divided” for Division), offering transparent insights into the model’s decision-making. The work demonstrates that contextual language models can accurately extract mathematical relations when combined with post-hoc explainability.

## Method Summary
The authors construct a custom dataset by merging Bangla MER and Somikoron sources, manually labeling entity spans and their associated operator relations among six classes (Addition, Subtraction, Multiplication, Division, Square Root, Factorial). After preprocessing (cleaning, lemmatization, stemming), they fine-tune `bert-base-uncased` with a classification head, using 80% of the data for training and 20% for testing. Hyperparameters include a learning rate of 2e-4, batch size 12, and 40 epochs. To interpret predictions, they apply SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) in a model-agnostic, perturbation-based manner, computing token contributions to each predicted relation. Evaluation employs standard metrics (accuracy, micro/macro F1, confusion matrix) and SHAP visualizations.

## Key Results
- Accuracy: 99.39% on test set.
- Micro F1: 99.36%; Macro F1: 99.27%.
- SHAP analysis shows that operation-specific semantic keywords (e.g., “divided”, “square”, “root”) are the primary drivers of predictions, while numerical values contribute minimally.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The paper attributes its success to three intertwined mechanisms:

1. **BERT Fine-Tuning for Contextual Mathematical Semantics**  
   Fine-tuning `bert-base-uncased` on the custom MERE dataset enables the model to learn context-dependent representations linking lexical cues to relation classes. The bidirectional self-attention captures dependencies between tokens; gradient updates adjust pretrained weights to prioritize features correlated with the six operators. The core assumption is that mathematical relations are reliably signified by specific lexical patterns within short problem statements, consistently captured by BERT’s embeddings. Evidence: [abstract] states best performance; [section 3.2.1] details fine-tuning; [section 4/Figure 12] shows SHAP keyword-driven predictions.

2. **SHAP as a Post-Hoc Feature Attribution Framework**  
   Applying model-agnostic SHAP (perturbation-based) yields faithful, instance-level explanations by computing Shapley values—each token’s marginal contribution to a predicted class. Perturbing input tokens (replacing with baselines) and measuring output changes reveals the additive nature of feature influence. The assumption that a complex model’s prediction can be decomposed linearly holds for BERT when using perturbation. Evidence: [abstract] mentions SHAP reveals keyword dominance; [section 3.3] describes `shap.Explainer`; [section 4/Figures 10 & 11] present SHAP plots.

3. **Dataset Construction via Entity-Relation Alignment**  
   The curated dataset provides unambiguous training signals: each mathematical statement has two operand phrases (entities) and an operator (relation). The pipeline ensures consistency: collecting texts, removing duplicates, extracting entity spans, aligning equations, and manual labeling. This produces a clean `(text, entity1, entity2, relation)` structure. Evidence: [abstract] cites source; [section 3.1] details construction; [section 3.1/Table 1] shows examples and distribution.

Break conditions: performance degrades on novel lexical variants, paraphrased problems without explicit keywords, or tasks requiring numerical reasoning beyond surface pattern matching. Limitations: dataset covers only six basic operations and short, straightforward texts.

## Architecture Onboarding
*Component map:*  
Data Pipeline → Model Core → Explainability Module → Evaluation Module

*Critical path:*  
Data Collection & Labeling → Preprocessing → Model Initialization & Fine-Tuning → Evaluation → SHAP Analysis on unseen examples

*Design tradeoffs:*  
- Accuracy vs. Interpretability: Using BERT yields high accuracy (99.39%) but requires SHAP for transparency, adding computational overhead. Simpler models (e.g., SVM) would be more interpretable but far less accurate.  
- Dataset Specificity vs. Generalization: High performance comes from a narrow, simple dataset; the tradeoff is brittleness on out-of-distribution or more complex problems.  
- SHAP Approximation Cost: A model-agnostic perturbation explainer is faithful but scales quadratically with sequence length; gradient-based methods would be faster but model-specific.

*Failure signatures:*  
1. Confusion between Addition and Subtraction/Multiplication: e.g., 8 Addition→Subtraction errors; model relies on ambiguous cues like “total”.  
2. Dependence on Explicit Keywords: Fails on paraphrased problems where the operator is implied (e.g., “combined weight” without “plus” or “sum”).  
3. SHAP Interpretation Limits: For misclassified instances, SHAP may show strong contributions from “wrong” operation keywords, diagnosing what pushed the error but not why the association was learned.

*First 3 experiments:*  
1. **Keyword Sufficiency Test:** Fine-tune BERT only on synthetic “X [keyword] Y is Z.” sentences for each operation. Matching paper accuracy confirms keyword sufficiency; a significant drop indicates need for broader context.  
2. **Keyword Ablation via SHAP:** For correctly classified test instances, mask the top SHAP-attributed token and re-predict. True class probability should drop >50% to validate SHAP’s critical feature identification.  
3. **Numerical Invariance Check:** Replace all numbers with placeholders (e.g., “[NUM]”) and re-evaluate. A minor accuracy drop (<2%) supports the claim that numerical values are not primary drivers.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1**  
*How can the proposed MERE approach be extended to handle more complex mathematical problems (e.g., longer texts, multi-step operations) beyond the six basic operations and short statements?*  
Basis: The dataset is limited to six basic equations and straightforward short texts; future work aims to address more complex problems with longer sequences (Limitation section). Why unresolved: Model generalization to longer, more complex sequences is untested. Evidence needed: Evaluation on a dataset with longer texts and varied operations, reporting accuracy and SHAP interpretability metrics.

**Open Question 2**  
*How does the model’s performance and interpretability hold when applied to mathematically incorrect or paraphrased problem statements?*  
Basis: The model was not evaluated on paraphrased or incorrect statements due to lack of existing data; future research plans such evaluation (Model Evaluation subsection). Why unresolved: Robustness to linguistic variations and inaccuracies is critical but unassessed. Evidence needed: A dataset of paraphrased correct problems and intentionally incorrect statements, testing accuracy, error rates, and SHAP explanations.

**Open Question 3**  
*What optimization techniques can reduce the computational overhead of integrating SHAP with transformer models without sacrificing interpretability or accuracy?*  
Basis: The limitation section notes the combined BERT+SHAP model is computationally heavy; they aim to optimize it. Why unresolved: SHAP inference for transformers is expensive, hindering deployment; no efficiency solutions provided. Evidence needed: Experiments comparing SHAP variants (e.g., GradientExplainer, approximations), model distillation, or pruning, measuring runtime, memory usage, and trade-offs with explanation fidelity and accuracy.

**Open Question 4**  
*To what extent does the proposed approach generalize to other mathematical domains (e.g., algebra, geometry, calculus) and can it be effectively integrated with automated theorem proving or knowledge graph construction?*  
Basis: Future work mentions expanding to other mathematical areas and connecting with advanced tools like automated theorem proving and knowledge graphs. Why unresolved: The model is trained only on basic word problems; applicability to structurally different domains and higher-level reasoning systems is unexplored. Evidence needed: Developing/adapting datasets from diverse domains, evaluating transfer performance, and building prototypes that combine MERE outputs with downstream applications.

## Limitations
- Dataset Scope: The high performance is benchmarked on a narrow, curated set of short, simple English math statements (six basic operations). Generalization to complex, nested, or implicit problems is unproven.
- Reproducibility Gaps: Exact preprocessing steps (NLTK versions), SHAP parameters (background set size, samples), and random seeds are unspecified, risking implementation variance.
- Explainability Scope: SHAP confirms reliance on operation keywords but does not guarantee the model has learned mathematically correct semantics; spurious correlations may persist.

## Confidence
- **High**: BERT fine-tuning achieves 99.39% accuracy on the specific test set (performance claim). Internal evidence consistent, but scope-bound.
- **Medium**: SHAP identifies keyword-driven decisions; the method is valid, but unknown implementation details and approximations may affect attribution stability.
- **Low**: Findings generalize beyond the simple, keyword-reliant mathematical statements. Break conditions (paraphrasing, numerical reasoning) limit broader applicability.
