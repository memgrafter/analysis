# SeedFlood: A Step Toward Scalable Decentralized Training of LLMs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.18181
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18181
- **Reference count:** 21
- **Primary result:** SeedFlood reduces communication to ~400 KB while matching or exceeding gossip-based methods, narrowing the gap to first-order decentralized training to 4–6%.

## Executive Summary
Decentralized training of large language models suffers from high communication costs and slow consensus due to information decay over network hops. SeedFlood addresses these issues by substituting traditional gossip with a flooding-based dissemination of zeroth-order updates, where each update is a seed–scalar pair enabling reconstruction of the perturbation vector via shared randomness. This makes the communication payload constant and independent of model size. Flooding ensures all clients receive every update within the network diameter, achieving perfect consensus regardless of topology. To handle the resulting volume of updates efficiently, SeedFlood employs a low-rank shared subspace (SubCGE) that aggregates all received perturbations via vectorized operations, keeping computational cost linear in the number of clients. In experiments fine-tuning 1.3B-parameter models across up to 128 clients, SeedFlood reduces total communication from hundreds of GB (DSGD) to a few hundred KB, matches or exceeds the accuracy of gossip-based methods, and narrows the accuracy gap to first-order methods to 4–6%, demonstrating scalable, topology-invariant learning with negligible communication overhead.

## Method Summary
SeedFlood operates in a decentralized setting where clients hold local data and models, and communicate over a static connected graph. The core idea is to use zeroth-order (ZO) gradient estimation: each client perturbs its model along a random direction generated from a shared seed, queries the loss, and computes a directional derivative. Instead of transmitting the full high-dimensional direction, the client sends only the seed (used to reconstruct the direction on any client) and the scalar derivative. All clients share a synchronized random number generator, allowing deterministic reconstruction of the same perturbation from the seed. These seed–scalar updates are disseminated via flooding: each client forwards each new update to all neighbors exactly once, guaranteeing that after D steps (where D is the network diameter) every client has received all updates from the current iteration. Because each update is tiny (≈8 bytes), the communication cost is constant per update, independent of model dimension.

The volume of received updates grows with the number of clients, so SeedFlood introduces Subspace Canonical-basis Gradient Estimation (SubCGE) to aggregate them efficiently. Clients share a low-rank subspace (matrices Uℓ, Vℓ) for each layer, refreshed globally every τ steps. Each seed maps to a canonical coordinate (i,j) within this subspace; the update's scalar coefficient is added to a small r×r coefficient matrix Aℓ. After collecting all updates for an iteration, the aggregated model perturbation is computed as Uℓ Aℓ Vℓ⊤ for each layer, which can be applied with cost O(rd) instead of O(nd). This combination yields a method that is communication-efficient, topologically robust, and computationally scalable.

## Key Results
- **Communication reduction:** Total bytes communicated drop from >500 GB (DSGD) to ~400 KB, a >6-order-of-magnitude improvement.
- **Perfect consensus:** Flooding ensures all clients have identical model updates after D steps, independent of network topology, avoiding the multi-hop decay seen in gossip.
- **Accuracy:** On fine-tuning tasks, SeedFlood matches or outperforms gossip-based baselines (ChocoSGD, DZSGD) and achieves within 4–6% of first-order decentralized training (DSGD), even surpassing it in sparse networks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Constant communication via seed–scalar updates
Clients share a synchronized RNG. A local ZO computation yields a scalar directional derivative α and a seed s. The direction z is deterministically reconstructed from s using the shared RNG and the current low-rank basis. By sending (s, α) instead of z, the message size is constant (~8 bytes) regardless of model dimension d. This breaks the linear dependence of communication cost on d, which plagues both full-gradient and compressed gossip methods.

Break condition: RNG desynchronization (e.g., missing seeds, client failures) prevents exact reconstruction, corrupting gradient aggregation.

### Mechanism 2: Perfect topology-invariant consensus via flooding
Instead of iterative gossip averaging, each new update is flooded (forwarded once to all neighbors). After D steps (network diameter), every client has received all updates from iteration t, exactly as if an all-gather had been performed. This eliminates the coefficient decay and information loss that occur with multi-hop gossip, ensuring consistent models across the network regardless of graph structure.

Break condition: Unknown or changing diameter, asynchronous delays, or packet loss cause missing updates, breaking consensus.

### Mechanism 3: Linear computational cost via SubCGE
Perturbations are restricted to a shared low-rank subspace (UℓVℓ⊤). Each seed maps to a canonical coordinate (i,j) in this basis. Received updates are aggregated by summing their α coefficients into a small r×r matrix Aℓ for each layer. The full update is then Uℓ Aℓ Vℓ⊤, which requires O(rd) operations per layer instead of O(nd), where n is the number of clients. This makes applying many received updates tractable.

Break condition: Subspace rank r too small relative to model curvature introduces bias; inaccurate sharing of Uℓ, Vℓ desynchronizes reconstruction.

## Foundational Learning
### Zeroth-Order (ZO) Gradient Estimation
**Why needed:** SeedFlood relies on ZO to produce scalar directional derivatives instead of full gradients, enabling tiny messages. Understanding the two-point estimator’s bias and variance is key to why many ZO samples are needed and how SubCGE’s subspace restriction adds additional bias.  
**Quick check:** How does increasing the number of zeroth-order gradient samples affect the bias and variance? Why might SubCGE’s low-rank approximation increase bias?

### Decentralized Consensus Protocols (Gossip vs. Flooding)
**Why needed:** SeedFlood replaces gossip with flooding to avoid multi-hop decay and the large number of reconstructions per update. Contrasting the two highlights why flooding is computationally superior when updates are seed-reconstructible.  
**Quick check:** In gossip, a single gradient update from client A must be reconstructed at every intermediate hop. In flooding, how many times is that same update reconstructed and applied at a distant client? Why does this matter?

### Low-Rank Matrix Factorization for Efficient Aggregation
**Why needed:** SubCGE’s efficiency hinges on representing updates in a factored basis (UℓAℓVℓ⊤). Grasping how summing rank-1 outer products leads to batched matrix multiplication clarifies the O(n+rd) cost.  
**Quick check:** Given n updates with coefficients α_k, how does SubCGE compute the total layer update without reconstructing each full z_k? What is the storage and computation trade-off compared to storing all z_k?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Shared RNG & Seed Manager → Local ZO Estimator → Flooding Engine → SubCGE → Model  
Shared RNG & Seed Manager → SubCGE (basis U,V)  
Topology Handler → Flooding Engine (neighbors, D)

### Critical Path
At iteration t:
1. If t mod τ == 0, refresh subspace matrices U, V from a global RNG seed.
2. Each client samples a local seed s, computes ZO scalar α, forms update (s, η_t α / n), and adds it to its send set R_i.
3. For d = 0 to D−1:
   - Broadcast current R_i to all neighbors.
   - Receive neighbors’ messages R, compute new updates R_new = R \ S_i (unseen seeds).
   - Update S_i ← S_i ∪ R (mark seen).
   - For each new (s, v) in R_new, reconstruct z = RNG_S(s, U, V) and apply θ ← θ − v·z.
4. After D steps, set θ_{t+1} = θ and proceed.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Communication vs. Computation:** Near-zero communication per update is traded for higher per-client computation (applying O(n) low-rank updates).
- **Subspace rank r vs. Accuracy:** Larger r reduces approximation bias but increases O(rd) cost; τ balances subspace stability.
- **Flooding steps k vs. Latency:** Full flooding (k=D) gives perfect consensus but may be impractical in dynamic networks; partial flooding (k<D) introduces bounded staleness.
- **ZO vs. FO:** ZO enables tiny messages but requires many local iterations, impacting wall-clock time relative to first-order methods.

### Failure Signatures
- **Convergence poor despite low communication:** SubCGE rank r likely too small; increase r or τ (see Figure 6).
- **High consensus error across clients:** Flooding incomplete; check that all clients eventually receive the same set of seeds. Likely diameter D underestimated or message loss.
- **GPU runtime dominated by “Perturbation” or “RV generation”:** SubCGE not properly used; client still reconstructs full vectors. Ensure RNG_S maps seeds to canonical coordinates and updates are accumulated in A_ℓ, not directly applied.
- **Performance collapse on sparse topologies (if using partial flooding):** Ensure k ≥ ceil(D/2) roughly; degradation below gossip indicates excessive staleness.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Seed reconstruction sanity check:** With shared U,V, have client 1 send (s,α). Client 2 reconstructs z = RNG_S(s,U,V) and verifies bitwise equality with client 1’s original z.
2. **Flooding correctness test:** In a 4‑client ring (D=3), inject a unique seed from client 1. After 3 flooding rounds, all clients must have that seed; log |S_i| per round to confirm convergence.
3. **SubCGE vs. naive aggregation benchmark:** For a large layer (d≈1e8), receive n updates (e.g., n=64). Time SubCGE (update A_ℓ then apply UℓAℓVℓ⊤) against naive loop that reconstructs each z_k and adds. Expect >100× speedup (per Figure 5).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Minimum flooding steps in dynamic networks:** What is the smallest number of flooding steps k that still maintains performance comparable to full flooding under realistic asynchronous delays and topology churn? The paper shows degradation for very small k but provides no systematic guideline.
   - *Resolution evidence:* Simulations across dynamic network models (random delays, loss, unknown diameters) to relate k, D, and performance. Theoretical bounds on staleness error.
2. **Adaptive subspace rank r:** How does the required r scale with model size, task complexity, and data heterogeneity? The paper fixes r=32 or 64 without adaptation.
   - *Resolution evidence:* Empirical studies linking task metrics (gradient variance, feature dimensionality) to optimal r. Development of an adaptive criterion (e.g., based on validation loss) to adjust r or τ during training.
3. **Fundamental accuracy gap of ZO:** Is the 4–6% gap between SeedFlood and first-order methods inherent to zeroth-order estimation, or can better estimators close it?
   - *Resolution evidence:* Ablations varying perturbation design, batch sizes, and aggregation schemes within SeedFlood. Theoretical analysis of estimation error vs. curvature.
4. **RNG synchronization tolerance:** How robust is the method to partial asynchrony or RNG desynchronization among clients? A single desync breaks perfect reconstruction.
   - *Resolution evidence:* Controlled injection of RNG offsets or skipped calls to measure performance collapse. Design of lightweight resynchronization or error-correcting mechanisms.

## Limitations
- RNG desynchronization across clients corrupts gradient reconstruction and breaks consensus.
- Flooding assumes a static, connected graph with known diameter; performance in dynamic or asynchronous networks remains unvalidated.
- SubCGE’s low‑rank approximation introduces a bias that may limit accuracy; the choice of rank r and refresh period τ lacks theoretical guidance and may not generalize to larger models or diverse tasks.

## Confidence
- **Constant communication size:** High
- **Topology‑invariant perfect consensus:** Medium (proven for static graphs, but real‑world dynamics untested)
- **Linear computational cost via SubCGE:** Medium (empirically shown, but low‑rank bias may affect generality)

## Next Checks

3. **SubCGE speedup:** For a layer with d>1e8, aggregate n updates via SubCGE vs. naive reconstruction; expect >100× runtime reduction.
