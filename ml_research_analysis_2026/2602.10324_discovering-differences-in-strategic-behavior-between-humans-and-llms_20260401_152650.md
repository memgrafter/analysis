# Discovering Differences in Strategic Behavior Between Humans and LLMs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.10324
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10324
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Frontier LLMs surpass humans in iterated rock-paper-scissors win rates and learning speed, a gap traced to higher-dimensional opponent modeling via automated program synthesis.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how large language models (LLMs) differ from humans in repeated strategic interactions. Using iterated rock-paper-scissors (IRPS) as a testbed, the authors apply AlphaEvolve—an automated program discovery tool—to extract interpretable behavioral models from gameplay data of humans (from prior work) and frontier LLMs (Gemini 2.5 Pro/Flash, GPT 5.1, GPT OSS 120B). They find that LLMs achieve higher win rates and learn faster, and that the simplest yet best-fitting programs reveal a key structural difference: frontier LLMs employ more complex opponent models (e.g., 3×3 or 3×3×3 matrices) compared to humans and smaller LLMs (3-dimensional frequency vectors). AlphaEvolve models outperform classical baselines, demonstrating that automated synthesis can uncover mechanistic distinctions without imposing human-centric assumptions.

## Method Summary
The authors gathered matched IRPS datasets: human data from Brockbank & Vul (2024) and new LLM data collected under identical conditions (300 rounds × 20 games per bot across 15 bot strategies). They then used AlphaEvolve, an evolutionary loop where an LLM proposes Python program edits, to search for behavioral models that balance predictive likelihood (optimized via SGD on training splits) and code simplicity (Halstead effort). The fitness function guided the creation of a Pareto frontier of programs; the simplest-but-best (SBB) program with negligible likelihood sacrifice was selected for each agent type. Performance was evaluated via two-fold cross-validated normalized likelihood against baselines (Nash, CS-EWA, RNN) and through a cross-generalization matrix. The SBB programs were inspected to compare underlying mechanisms such as value update rules and opponent model structure.

## Key Results
- Frontier LLMs (Gemini 2.5 Pro/Flash, GPT 5.1) achieve significantly higher aggregate win rates than humans, particularly against adaptive bots, and converge to high win rates more quickly.
- AlphaEvolve-discovered models consistently outperform baselines, achieving better predictive fit on held-out gameplay data for all agent types.
- Structural analysis of SBB programs shows all agents use value-based learning and opponent modeling, but frontier LLMs utilize higher-dimensional opponent models (3×3 or larger) while humans and GPT OSS 120B use simpler 3-dimensional frequency trackers; this difference explains the performance gap.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Opponent Model Dimensionality Drives Performance**  
The primary structural distinction is the size of the opponent model. Agents maintain internal representations to predict the opponent’s next move. Frontier LLMs use conditional transition matrices (e.g., 3×3 or 3×3×3) that capture how the opponent’s move depends on joint histories, whereas humans and smaller LLMs rely on simple frequency vectors (3-dimensional). This richer state space allows the former to detect and exploit more subtle patterns, yielding higher win rates especially against complex bots. Evidence: The SBB programs for Gemini and GPT 5.1 contain 3×3 or larger opponent models, while human SBB uses a 3-dimensional vector ([section 4.3.3]).

**Mechanism 2: Faster Convergence via Efficient Learning Updates**  
LLMs not only start stronger but also improve more rapidly. Their SBB programs employ asymmetric learning rates (different updates for positive vs. negative rewards) and counterfactual updates, which may enable more effective credit assignment and exploration. This intrinsic property of their update rules leads to quicker adaptation from early-game feedback, as observed in faster convergence trajectories ([section 4.1], [section 4.3.3]). The core assumption is that learning speed stems from the agent’s algorithmic choices rather than initial conditions.

**Mechanism 3: Automated Program Synthesis Unlocks Mechanistic Discovery**  
AlphaEvolve successfully discovers predictive and interpretable behavioral models without relying on pre-specified human hypotheses. Its evolutionary search, guided by an LLM to generate program variants, explores a diverse space of algorithms. The multi‑objective fitness (likelihood + simplicity) yields a Pareto frontier where the simplest-but-best programs generalize well and offer clear structural insights. The method’s superiority over CS‑EWA and RNN baselines ([section 4.2]) validates the approach.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
IRPS gameplay logs (human & LLM) -> AlphaEvolve (evolutionary program search with LLM editor) -> Parameter fitting via SGD -> Multi‑objective scoring (likelihood + Halstead effort) -> Pareto frontier -> Simplest‑but‑best (SBB) selection -> Evaluation (cross‑validation, cross‑generalization) -> Structural analysis (Q‑table size, opponent model dimensions)

**Critical path:**  
1. Collect or obtain IRPS datasets.  
2. Implement AlphaEvolve loop: initialize with template, use LLM (Gemini Flash) to propose code edits, evaluate each candidate by fitting parameters on training split, compute fitness, maintain Pareto frontier.  
3. After evolutionary run, extract SBB program from frontier.  
4. Evaluate predictive fit on held‑out games via two‑fold cross‑validation.  
5. Perform cross‑generalization: re‑fit each SBB to other agent datasets and compare off‑diagonal performance.  
6. Inspect SBB semantics: identify Q‑table structure, opponent model, and update rules.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Predictive fit vs. interpretability:* Multi‑objective optimization balances likelihood and simplicity; overly complex programs may overfit but offer more expressive power.  
- *Aggregate vs. per‑subject modeling:* The analysis fits a single parameter set across all subjects (humans) or all games (LLMs), potentially masking individual variation.  
- *Program representation freedom:* Unconstrained search risks degenerate or non‑causal models; templates impose plausible structure but could bias discovery.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Overfitting: large gap between training and evaluation likelihood; Pareto frontier does not include low‑complexity options.  
- Cross‑generalization failure: off‑diagonal scores high, indicating models capture dataset‑specific quirks.  
- Semantically incoherent programs: generated code violates game rules (e.g., illegal moves) or contains dead parameters.  
- Pareto frontier collapse: few distinct programs, suggesting search stagnation or insufficient diversity.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Replicate aggregate win rates (Figure 2):** Verify that frontier LLMs win more and converge faster than humans against nonadaptive bots.  
2. **Baseline comparison (Figure 3):** Confirm AlphaEvolve outperforms CS‑EWA and matches/surpasses RNN in normalized likelihood on all datasets.  
3. **SBB program validation (Figure 5, Table 2):** Check cross‑generalization matrix shows strong diagonal dominance, confirming each SBB fits its originating agent type best; extract and compare opponent model dimensions across agents.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can LLM strategic behavior be aligned more closely with human behavior in repeated social interactions?  
2. Do expert human players develop opponent models with complexity comparable to frontier LLMs, or do they employ fundamentally different strategies?  
3. Do the identified structural differences generalize to other repeated strategic games beyond iterated rock-paper-scissors?  
4. What specific architectural or training factors cause some LLMs (e.g., GPT OSS 120B) to exhibit declining performance over time, while others improve?  
5. Are the AlphaEvolve‑discovered behavioral models mechanistic explanations of decision‑making, or merely predictive approximations that may not reflect actual cognitive processes?

## Limitations
- **Dataset access:** The human data from Brockbank & Vul (2024) is not publicly shared; replication depends on author cooperation.
- **AlphaEvolve hyperparameters:** Key details (population size, generations, LLM temperature, mutation rates) are omitted, hindering exact reproduction of the discovery process.
- **Baseline implementation fidelity:** Insufficient specification for training CS‑EWA and RNN baselines could lead to performance discrepancies, affecting comparative claims.

## Confidence
- Opponent model dimensionality explains performance gap: Medium (strong internal evidence, limited external validation).
- LLMs learn faster than humans: Low (based on aggregated trajectories; raw curves unavailable for scrutiny).
- AlphaEvolve uncovers interpretable, predictive models: Medium (superior to baselines, but meaning of "simplest‑but‑best" relies on subjective interpretability).
