# Lexicalized Constituency Parsing for Middle Dutch: Low-resource Training and Cross-Domain Generalization
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.07008
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.07008
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Joint training with auxiliary languages using a Benepar transformer parser and historical Dutch BERT achieves up to 86.21 F1 in-domain; cross-domain adaptation yields gains from just 10 examples and exceeds 70 F1 with 100 examples per domain, with feature separation further improving to over 74.7 F1 after ~200 examples.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the challenge of constituency parsing for Middle Dutch, a low-resource historical language with heterogeneous texts. The authors adapt the Benepar transformer parser by incorporating historical Dutch BERT and employ joint training with auxiliary languages such as Early New High German and Middle Low German. Their results show significant improvements in in-domain parsing accuracy, with F1 scores increasing by up to 0.73 to reach 86.21. For cross-domain generalization, simple strategies like fine-tuning and data-combined retraining prove effective even with minimal target data (detectable gains from 10 examples, >70 F1 with 100). Feature separation techniques provide additional benefits when more target data (~200+ examples) is available, pushing F1 above 74.7. The neural approach consistently surpasses the traditional statistical Bikel parser across all evaluated conditions, including zero-shot scenarios.

## Method Summary
The proposed method builds on Benepar, a transformer-based lexicalized constituency parser. The authors replace its default encoder with a Dutch BERT pretrained on historical texts to better capture Middle Dutch linguistic characteristics. For low-resource training, they jointly train the parser on Middle Dutch and several auxiliary languages (Old High German, Middle Low German, etc.), using separate parsing modules for each language to accommodate language-specific syntactic variations while sharing the underlying transformer representations. To address cross-domain issues (different text genres or dialects), they experiment with three adaptation strategies: fine-tuning the parser on a small set of target domain sentences, data-combined retraining (mix source and target data from scratch), and a feature separation approach that introduces domain-specific feature layers. Performance is measured using labeled F1 (LF1) on held-out test sets.

## Key Results
- Auxiliary joint training lifts in-domain F1 by up to 0.73 to 86.21.
- Cross-domain adaptation shows gains with only 10 target examples; F1 exceeds 70 at 100 examples and reaches >74.7 with feature separation after ~200 examples.
- The neural parser outperforms the statistical Bikel parser across all evaluated conditions, including zero-shot.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept 1: Transformer-based Constituency Parsing**
  - Why needed: Enables modeling of long-range dependencies and hierarchical structure with a pretrained language model.
  - Quick check: Benepar uses a transformer encoder and a recursive neural network to score constituency spans.
- **Concept 2: Historical BERT**
  - Why needed: Captures orthographic, morphological, and syntactic phenomena specific to Middle Dutch, which differ from modern language.
  - Quick check: Dutch BERT trained on historical corpora (e.g., ~14th–16th century texts) provides contextual embeddings suited to the target domain.
- **Concept 3: Cross-Lingual Transfer via Joint Training**
  - Why needed: Leverages syntactically similar auxiliary languages to compensate for scarce Middle Dutch annotations.
  - Quick check: Separate language-specific modules share the transformer encoder; training on multiple languages improves representations for Middle Dutch.
- **Concept 4: Domain Adaptation with Limited Data**
  - Why needed: Middle Dutch texts exhibit heterogeneity; models trained on one domain may not generalize.
  - Quick check: Fine-tuning, data-combined retraining, and feature separation allow efficient adaptation with few target examples.
- **Concept 5: Feature Separation**
  - Why needed: Isolates domain-specific parameters to avoid overfitting when target data is extremely scarce.
  - Quick check: Extra feature layers are added for each domain; they only become effective when enough target examples are present (~200).

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Input text -> BERT encoder (historical Dutch) -> Language-specific parsing module -> Constituency tree

For auxiliary training: each language (Middle Dutch, ENHG, MLG) uses its own parsing module but shares the BERT encoder; during inference, the Middle Dutch module is activated.

**Critical Path**  
1. Tokenize input sentence.  
2. Obtain contextual embeddings via BERT.  
3. Parsing module scores potential spans and predicts tree structure (e.g., via CKY algorithm).  
4. Loss computed against gold tree; gradients update both encoder and module.  
For joint training, batches contain mixed languages; encoder updates are pooled, while module parameters are language-specific.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- Separate modules preserve language identity but increase parameter count; shared encoder encourages cross-lingual transfer but risks interference.  
- Feature separation adds capacity and reduces overfitting in low-target-data regimes but may delay convergence and require more data to be beneficial.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Sudden drop in performance on source domain after fine-tuning indicates catastrophic forgetting.  
- No improvement from feature separation with <200 examples suggests underfitting due to insufficient target data to train extra layers.  
- Poor zero-shot transfer from auxiliary languages if they are typologically distant.

**First Experiments**  
1. Train Benepar with historical Dutch BERT on Middle Dutch only (baseline).  
2. Add joint auxiliary training (separate modules) and compare in-domain F1.  
3. Apply fine-tuning with increasing amounts of target domain data (10, 100, 200 examples) to evaluate cross-domain adaptation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: Does continued pretraining of BERT on raw Middle Dutch text, followed by fine-tuning on the parsed corpus, improve the performance of neural constituency parsing in low-resource settings compared to using only the parsed data?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The conclusion explicitly states that "future work includes exploiting unlabeled Middle Dutch data through continued pretraining (cf. Li et al., 2020, 2022; see also Gururangan et al., 2020; Grobol et al., 2022)" due to the lack of unlabeled data in the current study.
- Why unresolved: The study did not have access to a sufficiently large corpus of raw Middle Dutch text to perform continued pretraining, leaving its impact unmeasured.
- What evidence would resolve it: Collect a large raw Middle Dutch corpus, continue pretraining the historical Dutch BERT on this corpus, then train the Benepar parser on the parsed data with and without this step. Compare in-domain and cross-domain F1 scores to quantify improvement.

### Open Question 2
- Question: How does the effectiveness of domain adaptation strategies (fine-tuning, data-combined retraining, feature separation) change as the amount of target domain data increases beyond 200 sentences per domain?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper finds that feature separation techniques only enhance cross-domain performance with approximately 200+ examples per domain, and the conclusion calls for "deepening exploration of domain adaptation with ongoing development of parsed and unparsed texts," implying that scaling trends with more data remain unknown.
- Why unresolved: Experiments were limited to 10, 100, and 200 examples per target domain; performance with larger datasets (e.g., 500, 1000) was not evaluated, so the saturation point and comparative scaling of methods are unclear.
- What evidence would resolve it: Incrementally add more annotated sentences from the target domains to the training set (e.g., in steps of 200 up to 1000) and measure F1 for each adaptation method (fine-tuning, combined, feature separation) to plot performance curves and identify optimal methods at different data scales.

### Open Question 3
- Question: Can multilingual training objectives, such as a shared tagset or joint representation learning across auxiliary languages and Middle Dutch, yield further gains in historical constituency parsing compared to the separate-module approach used?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The conclusion explicitly mentions "exploring multilingual objectives for historical languages" as future work, noting that the current approach uses separate parsing modules per language.
- Why unresolved: The study tested auxiliary language joint training with dedicated modules but did not experiment with shared tagsets, unified encoders, or other multilingual objectives that might better transfer cross-lingual knowledge.
- What evidence would resolve it: Implement alternative multilingual setups (e.g., unified tagset across all languages, shared encoder with language identifiers, or multilingual BERT) and compare their Middle Dutch parsing F1 (in-domain and cross-domain) against the separate-module baseline to assess improvements.

### Open Question 4
- Question: What specific linguistic factors (e.g., morphological case, word order flexibility) explain why temporally and geographically closer auxiliary languages provide greater transfer benefits for Middle Dutch parsing?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper empirically shows that languages like ENHG and CHLG (closer in time and geography) yield the largest gains, but it does not analyze which linguistic features drive this effect, only correlating proximity with performance.
- Why unresolved: The study does not isolate or measure the contribution of specific linguistic properties (e.g., inflectional morphology, V2 word order) to transfer success; thus, the underlying mechanisms remain speculative.
- What evidence would resolve it: Conduct controlled ablation experiments where auxiliary language data are systematically altered (e.g., remove case markings, flatten word order) to see which modifications reduce transfer gains. Alternatively, correlate transfer improvements with quantitative linguistic distances (e.g., from typological databases) to identify key predictive features.

## Limitations
- Insufficient reporting of data splits, sizes, and annotation quality; unknown parser error profile on Middle Dutch.
- No statistical significance tests or confidence intervals; observed F1 differences may be unstable.
- Cross-domain thresholds (10, 100, 200 examples) are based on limited experiments and lack validation across diverse domains.
- Feature separation's benefit only after ~200 examples is anecdotal; no ablation of feature types.
- Evaluation only versus Bikel parser; lack of modern neural baselines.
