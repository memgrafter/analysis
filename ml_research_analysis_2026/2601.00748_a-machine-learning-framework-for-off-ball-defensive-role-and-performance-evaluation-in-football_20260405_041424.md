# A Machine Learning Framework for Off Ball Defensive Role and Performance Evaluation in Football

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.00748
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.00748
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Corner recipient prediction model achieves 48.07% top‑3 accuracy vs 23.75% human baseline; introduces Group Coverage Advantage (GCA) for role‑conditioned counterfactual evaluation.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the lack of metrics for evaluating off‑ball defensive performance in football, particularly during corner kicks, where traditional statistics fail to capture coordinated movements and counterfactual analysis lacks tactical context. The authors propose a label‑free covariate‑dependent Hidden Markov Model (CDHMM) that infers time‑resolved man‑marking and zonal defensive assignments directly from player tracking data. Validated by professional analysts, the model reveals team‑specific zonal structures and dynamic marking behaviors. A corner recipient predictor built on these assignments attains 48.07% top‑3 accuracy, well above the 23.75% human expert baseline. The framework also introduces the Group Coverage Advantage (GCA) metric, which quantifies a defender’s positioning quality against a role‑aware counterfactual baseline. The CDHMM enables interpretable, context‑aware evaluation of individual defensive contributions and offers a foundation for extending analysis to open play.

## Method Summary
The core of the work is a covariate‑dependent HMM trained per team and corner delivery type (inswing/outswing). Input sequences span from 1 s before the corner kick to 2 s after delivery or the next on‑ball event. Defender roles (man‑marking a specific attacker or occupying a zonal region) are treated as latent states. Emission distributions tie a defender’s observed position to the role: for man‑marking, a bivariate Gaussian centred on a weighted combination of the assigned attacker’s location and the goal centre; for zonal states, a Gaussian with learned mean and covariance. Transition probabilities depend on contextual covariates (distance, convergence, velocity, etc.) and are regularised via L2 penalties. The model is estimated by Expectation‑Maximisation (15 iterations). Inferred state sequences yield behavioural metrics (e.g., switch rate) and feed a counterfactual ghosting generator. A pre‑trained reception probability model (a GNN) scores observed and ghost positions to compute Group Coverage Advantage (GCA), which compares the actual total reception probability of the attacker group against the minimum achievable under any feasible man‑marking assignment. The framework is completed by visualisation tools for analyst validation.

## Key Results
- CDHMM infers interpretable man‑marking and zonal assignments that align with coaching intuition.
- Corner recipient predictor (canonicalised GNN) achieves 48.07% top‑3 accuracy, exceeding the 23.75% human baseline and rivaling prior attention‑based methods.
- Group Coverage Advantage (GCA) provides a quantitative, role‑aware measure of off‑ball defensive contribution, validated through spatial case studies and analyst feedback.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim**: The CDHMM can infer time‑resolved man‑marking and zonal assignments directly from tracking data without manual labels.  
**Mechanism**: Latent states represent tactical roles (either man‑marking a specific attacker or defending a fixed zonal region). For man‑marking, the defender’s location is modelled as a weighted combination of the assigned attacker’s position and the goal centre, with bin‑specific weights (Γ). For zonal states, a Gaussian distribution with learned mean and covariance encodes typical positioning for that team and corner type. Parameters are learned from data, making the states correspond to interpretable behaviours.  
**Core assumption**: Given the latent role, a defender’s position is conditionally independent of other defenders and follows the specified emission distribution.  
**Evidence**: Abstract states the model “infers time‑resolved man‑marking and zonal assignments directly from player tracking data.” Section “CDHMM Definitions” mathematically defines the emission models, and Section “Inferred Defensive Behaviors from the CDHMM” visualises assignments matching coaching intuition.  
**Break condition**: If movement patterns show no clear separation (e.g., extremely noisy or random trajectories), emission distributions may fail to converge to distinct states, yielding mixed or uninterpretable assignments.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim**: Covariate‑dependent transition probabilities allow the model to adapt to game context, capturing dynamic changes in defensive roles over the corner’s duration.  
**Mechanism**: Transitions between latent states are governed by logistic/softmax functions over covariate vectors (distance, relative velocity, convergence, heading alignment, etc.). Separate weight vectors (β_m, β_z, β_s) control self‑transitions (staying in the same man‑mark or zone) and switches (changing attacker or moving from zone to man). The model learns these weights, so the probability of maintaining a man‑mark increases when defender and attacker have similar velocities.  
**Core assumption**: The likelihood of a role change depends on observable physical relationships.  
**Evidence**: Abstract notes the model “captures the dynamic nature of set‑play behavior.” Section “Covariate‑Dependent Transition Model” details the parameterisation and equations for p_m, p_z, p_s. Figure 5 shows learned continuation probability varying spatially with defender speed, confirming context‑sensitivity.  
**Break condition**: Poor covariate selection or insufficient data can cause overfitting, making transition probabilities reflect noise rather than tactical logic and producing erratic state sequences.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim**: Role‑conditioned ghosting (e.g., Group Coverage Advantage) yields a counterfactual baseline that respects team‑specific tactics, enabling meaningful attribution of off‑ball defensive value.  
**Mechanism**: The CDHMM generates a distribution of feasible defender positions for each man‑marking role by sampling from the role’s emission distribution. These “ghosts” are fed into an outcome model (e.g., a pre‑trained GNN reception predictor). For a defender responsible for a set of attackers K, GCA compares the observed total reception probability of K to the minimum (optimal) expected probability achievable under any feasible man‑marking assignment within K. This isolates the defender’s contribution relative to the best plausible alternative role.  
**Core assumption**: The average role‑specific behaviour (as modelled by the emission distribution) is an appropriate baseline for evaluating a defender’s positioning within the same tactical context.  
**Evidence**: Abstract mentions “role‑conditioned ghosting method for counterfactual analysis.” Section “Evaluation Of Off‑ball Defensive Contributions” formally defines GCA and illustrates it with spatial case studies (Figure 9). Section “Counterfactual Metrics Through CDHMM Generated Ghosts” contrasts point‑ghost vs expected‑ghost baselines and explains the preference for expected ghosts.  
**Break condition**: Mis‑specified emission distributions (too narrow) would produce unrealistic ghosts and biased GCA scores. An overly permissive feasible set K could lead to an unattainable optimum, reducing interpretability.

  *Why needed*: The CDHMM builds on the classical HMM framework to represent defender roles as hidden states and their temporal evolution, requiring understanding of HMM fundamentals (forward‑backward, Viterbi, EM) to train from unlabeled tracking data.

  *Why needed*: The paper’s credit attribution relies on comparing observed outcomes to counterfactual scenarios where a defender occupies a role‑specific “average” position, necessitating understanding of how to generate and evaluate such counterfactuals without confounding.  
  *Quick check*: How does an “expected ghost” differ from a simple “point ghost”, and why is the former preferred for non‑linear outcome models?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**: Data ingestion -> Preprocessing -> CDHMM core -> Inference engine -> Behavioural metrics -> Outcome models -> Counterfactual generation -> GCA calculation -> Visualization & validation  
**Critical Path**: Raw data -> canonicalisation -> CDHMM training -> per‑corner state inference -> compute metrics & generate ghosts -> feed ghosts into outcome model -> compute GCA -> produce analyst‑facing visualisations  
**Design Tradeoffs**:
- **Unsupervised vs supervised**: Avoids costly manual role labels but may lose alignment with coach taxonomies; chosen for scalability.
- **Team‑specific models**: Captures unique defensive schemes but requires minimum corners per team/delivery; no sharing across teams.
- **Covariate‑dependent transitions**: Adds flexibility to model dynamics but introduces many parameters; mitigated with L2 regularisation.
- **Gaussian zonal emissions**: Simple and tractable, but may not capture skew; future work could consider mixture models.
- **Prohibited man→zone transitions**: Informed by coaches; reduces state‑space complexity but might miss rare role re‑assignments.
**Failure Signatures**:
- **Zonal instability**: Large pairwise zonal disagreement across training runs indicates non‑convergence or insufficient data.
- **Poor recipient‑prediction accuracy**: Top‑3 accuracy near random or below human baseline suggests learned roles not predictive.
- **Degenerate GCA distribution**: All GCA values near zero or extreme outliers suggest feasible set K or emission distributions are mis‑specified.
- **Excessively high switch rates**: Many defenders always switching may indicate transition weights over‑fitting to noise.
- **Visualization mismatch**: Coaches consistently rejecting inferred assignments as tactically implausible.
**First 3 Experiments**:
1. Train a single‑team CDHMM on all inswinging corners from one Premier League team. Visualise the 10 zonal Gaussians and a frame‑wise assignment plot. Confirm with a set‑piece coach that the zones align with the team’s known defensive scheme.
2. Recipient prediction benchmark: Train the canonicalised GNN on the same team’s data (or pooled data) and evaluate top‑3 accuracy on a held‑out set. Verify that accuracy exceeds the human baseline (23.75%) and is comparable to the TacticAI reimplementation (~48%).
3. GCA sanity check: Compute GCA for a diverse sample of corners. Correlate GCA values with expert ratings (if available) or with simple heuristics (e.g., distance of defenders to nearest attacker). Expect higher (more positive) GCA for well‑positioned defences and a significant difference between inswinging and outswinging distributions (per the KS test).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Uninvolved state**: How to extend CDHMM to include an “uninvolved” latent state for defenders far from the play? The absence forces incorrect assignments, distorting metrics. Resolution: Add the state and show improved coherence and validation.
- **Duration modeling**: Can the geometric duration assumption of HMM be relaxed to capture sustained stability (e.g., longer zonal holds)? The memoryless property may cause spurious switches. Resolution: Implement a Hidden semi‑Markov Model (HSMM) and show better alignment with tactical patterns.
- **Velocity in emissions**: Does incorporating defender and attacker velocities into emission distributions improve accuracy? Velocity cues distinguish active marking from passive holding. Resolution: Ablate with velocity features, demonstrating higher log‑likelihood and expert‑rated plausibility.
- **Global optimality**: How does defender positioning compare to a globally optimal policy that minimizes opponent threat? Current GCA only compares to the best feasible man‑marking assignment within CDHMM roles. Resolution: Develop a separate optimization (e.g., RL or exhaustive search) to compute minimal threat and quantify deviations.
- **Generalization to open play**: Can the framework generalize to open‑play scenarios where zones are dynamic? Current static zonal Gaussians and fixed man‑marking references are unsuitable. Resolution: Redesign CDHMM with relative zones and extended emission models; validate on a new labelled open‑play dataset.

## Limitations
- **Unknown alignment pipeline**: The exact procedure for synchronizing event logs with tracking data to extract corner sequences is unspecified, risking temporal misalignment.
- **Black-box outcome model**: The reception probability model (Pr(atk|x)) used for GCA is not described; its features, training data, or architecture are missing, blocking GCA replication.
- **Weak corpus engagement**: Neighbor papers show low citation impact (average 0.0) and moderate similarity (average FMR 0.446); the claim of outperforming attention‑based methods lacks direct comparative validation.
