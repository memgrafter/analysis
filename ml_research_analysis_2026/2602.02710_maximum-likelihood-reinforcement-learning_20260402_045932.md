# Maximum Likelihood Reinforcement Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.02710
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02710
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** MaxRL Pareto-dominates GRPO and RLOO across reasoning tasks, achieving up to 20× test-time scaling efficiency and matching cross-entropy on ImageNet with sufficient rollouts.

## Executive Summary
Standard reinforcement learning with binary rewards optimizes a first-order Taylor approximation of the maximum likelihood objective, which underweights low-success inputs and leads to poor coverage. MaxRL introduces a compute-indexed family of objectives derived from a truncated Maclaurin expansion of the log-likelihood, interpolating between RL and exact ML as rollout compute increases. Its on-policy estimator, which averages score functions only over successful rollouts, yields an unbiased estimate of the truncated ML gradient. Empirically, MaxRL substantially improves pass@k over prior methods on mazes, GSM8K, and large reasoning models, with favorable scaling and reduced overfitting.

## Method Summary
MaxRL modifies standard RL with a truncated Maclaurin expansion objective \( J_{\text{MaxRL}}^{(T)} = -\sum_{k=1}^{T} \frac{(1-p)^k}{k} \), where \( T \) equals the number of rollouts \( N \) per prompt. The algorithm samples \( N \) trajectories per prompt, computes binary rewards via a verifier, and calculates the gradient as the average of score functions \( \nabla_\theta \log m_\theta(z_i|x) \) only for successful rollouts, normalized by the mean reward \( \mu \) and divided by \( \mu \) (i.e., advantage \( (r_i - \mu)/\mu \)). If no successes occur (\( \mu=0 \)), the gradient contribution is zero. This on-policy estimator is unbiased for the \( T=N \)-order truncated ML gradient, and increasing \( N \) directly improves the optimized objective.

## Key Results
- On 17×17 mazes, MaxRL achieves 84.4 Pass@1 vs. GRPO’s 43.6.
- On GSM8K, MaxRL reaches 33.2 Pass@1 and 75.0 Pass@128 vs. GRPO’s 29.1 and 45.8.
- On Qwen3-1.7B/4B models, MaxRL matches or exceeds GRPO Pass@1 while substantially boosting Pass@k, achieving up to 20× test-time scaling efficiency.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: RL as First-Order ML Approximation
- **Claim:** Binary-reward RL optimizes only a first-order Taylor approximation of the ML objective, underweighting low-success inputs.
- **Mechanism:** The RL gradient \( \nabla_\theta J_{\text{RL}} = \mathbb{E}[\nabla_\theta p_\theta(x)] \) (expected pass@1) corresponds to the \( k=1 \) term from the ML gradient’s Maclaurin series \( \nabla_\theta J_{\text{ML}} = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{k} \nabla_\theta \text{pass@}k(x) \). This first-order approximation assigns weight \( w(p)=1 \) to all inputs regardless of success probability \( p \). When \( p \) is small for hard tasks, higher-order terms that emphasize low-\( p \) inputs are omitted, causing the RL gradient to vanish while the ML gradient scales as \( 1/p \).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [Abstract] "Binary-reward RL optimizes expected pass@1, a first-order approximation of the maximum likelihood objective"
  - [Section 3.1] Eq. (5) shows the infinite series gradient; RL corresponds to \( T=1 \).
  - Weak corpus support; neighboring papers focus on likelihood thresholds and model transfer, not gradient expansion.
- **Break condition:** Fails when \( p_\theta(x) \to 0 \) for a significant fraction of tasks, as RL gradient vanishes for hard inputs.

### Mechanism 2: Compute-Indexed Interpolation via Truncated Expansion
- **Claim:** MaxRL introduces a compute-indexed family that interpolates between RL and exact ML via truncated Maclaurin expansion.
- **Mechanism:** For truncation level \( T \), the objective is \( J_{\text{MaxRL}}^{(T)}(x) = -\sum_{k=1}^{T} \frac{(1-p)^k}{k} \). As \( T \) increases (more rollout compute), higher-order terms proportional to \( \nabla_\theta \text{pass@}k \) are added, recovering ML as \( T \to \infty \). Finite rollout budget \( N \) limits estimation of \( \text{pass@}k \) for large \( k \); truncation makes estimation feasible while approximating ML better with more compute.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [Abstract] "compute-indexed family via truncated Maclaurin expansion... interpolating between RL and exact ML as \( T \to \infty \)"
  - [Section 3.2] Defines \( J_{\text{MaxRL}}^{(T)} \) and its gradient in Eq. (6)-(7).
  - Minimal direct corpus support; neighbors discuss maximum likelihood thresholds and sequential mixing, not RL interpolation.
- **Break condition:** If \( p_\theta(x) \) is extremely small, estimating \( \text{pass@}k \) for moderate \( k \) may require infeasible rollouts, leading to high variance.

### Mechanism 3: Unbiased On-Policy Estimator via Success-Only Averaging
- **Claim:** The estimator \( \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i} \frac{r_i}{K} \nabla_\theta \log m_\theta(z_i|x) \) (averaging scores only over successes) is unbiased for the \( T=N \) order truncated ML gradient.
- **Mechanism:** Conditioning on \( K \ge 1 \) (at least one success), successful samples are from the success-conditioned distribution. Theorem 2 shows \( \mathbb{E}[\text{estimator}] = \nabla_\theta J_{\text{MaxRL}}^{(N)} \). Thus, increasing rollouts \( N \) improves the *objective being optimized* (not just variance). The practical variant drops gradient when \( K=0 \), introducing a bias but reducing variance.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [Section 4.1] Theorem 2 establishes estimator–objective equivalence.
  - [Section 4.3] Algorithm 1 implements this with \( \text{br}(x)>0 \) condition.
  - [Section 6] Empirical scaling: Pass@k improves systematically with more rollouts in Figures 3, 5.
- **Break condition:** If \( K=0 \) frequently (very low \( p \)), gradient updates vanish for that task, slowing learning on hardest inputs.

## Foundational Learning
- **Policy Gradient Estimation (REINFORCE)**
  - **Why needed here:** The core gradient estimator \( \frac{1}{K} \sum r_i \nabla_\theta \log \pi \) generalizes REINFORCE; understanding \( \mathbb{E}[r_i \nabla_\theta \log \pi] = \nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}[r] \) is prerequisite.
  - **Quick check question:** Why does dividing by \( K \) (successes) instead of \( N \) (total rollouts) change the population objective from \( \mathbb{E}[p] \) to a harmonic series of \( \text{pass@}k \)?
- **Maclaurin Expansion of Log Probability**
  - **Why needed here:** Deriving \( \nabla_\theta J_{\text{ML}} = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{k} \nabla_\theta \text{pass@}k \) requires expanding \( \log p = -\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{(1-p)^k}{k} \) and differentiating.
  - **Quick check question:** Show that \( \frac{d}{dp} \sum_{k=1}^{T} \frac{(1-p)^k}{k} = -\sum_{k=1}^{T} (1-p)^{k-1} \) equals \( \frac{1 - (1-p)^T}{p} \).
- **Weight-Function Perspective (Population Objectives)**
  - **Why needed here:** Section 5 shows all methods reduce to \( w(p) \nabla_\theta p \); choosing \( w(p) \) determines optimization dynamics.
  - **Quick check question:** Given \( w_T(p) = \frac{1-(1-p)^T}{p} \), what limiting behavior makes \( w_T \to 1 \) (RL) and \( w_T \to 1/p \) (ML)?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  rollout_engine -> reward_fn -> advantage_calculator -> optimizer_step
- **Critical path:**
  1. For each batch of prompts, sample \( N \) rollouts per prompt.
  2. For each prompt \( x \), compute rewards \( r_i \) and scores \( S_i = \nabla_\theta \log m_\theta(z_i|x) \).
  3. Compute mean reward \( \mu = \frac{1}{N} \sum r_i \). If \( K = \sum r_i > 0 \), advantage = \( \frac{1}{N} \sum \frac{(r_i - \mu) S_i}{\mu} \); else zero.
  4. Average advantages across prompts, take gradient step.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Variance vs. bias*: Dropping gradient when \( \mu=0 \) avoids noisy updates but introduces bias on hardest tasks.
  - *Compute allocation*: Larger \( N \) increases \( T=N \) order but reduces viable prompts per step; typically keep \( N \) moderate (16–128).
  - *Baseline choice*: Using mean reward \( \mu \) yields weight \( w(p) = \frac{1-(1-p)^N}{p} \) unlike GRPO’s std-normalization.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Pass@k degradation over training → objective still favors easy inputs; likely \( N \) too small or \( \mu \) too low on hard prompts.
  - Gradient norms concentrated on medium-\( p \) tasks → check if \( \mu \) near zero for many prompts, making advantage division unstable.
  - No improvement on low-\( p \) tasks despite compute → verify \( T=N \) large enough; consider curriculum or \( N \) increase.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Reproduce weight functions:** For synthetic \( p \) values, compute population \( w(p) \) for RL (\( w=1 \)), GRPO (\( w=1/\sqrt{p(1-p)} \)), MaxRL\( _T \) (\( w=(1-(1-p)^T)/p \)), ML (\( w=1/p \)). Validate truncation approximation.
  2. **Scaling rollout count:** Train on maze or GSM8K subset with \( N \in \{4,8,16,32,64\} \); plot Pass@1 and Pass@128 vs. \( N \). Expect monotonic improvement for MaxRL, plateau for RL/GRPO.
  3. **Gradient norm analysis:** For a fixed checkpoint, compute per-prompt gradient L2 norm vs. \( p_\theta(x) \) under each objective. MaxRL should show increasing norm as \( p \to 0 \); GRPO peaks at \( p \approx 0.5 \).

## Limitations
- Hyperparameter details for the largest Qwen3 experiments are missing (learning rate, KL coefficient, batch size), risking irreproducibility.
- The “perfect verifier” for math benchmarks is underspecified; parsing/execution rules may vary and affect Pass@k.
- The theoretical unbiasedness relies on on-policy data and using all rollouts; in practice the algorithm drops updates when no successes occur (\( K=0 \)), which could bias learning on very hard tasks.

## Confidence
- **RL as first-order ML**: High (direct derivation).
- **MaxRL as truncated Maclaurin expansion**: High (theoretically sound, scaling experiments confirm).
- **Unbiased estimator for \( T=N \)**: Medium (theorem assumes no \( K=0 \) drops; real implementation may deviate).

## Next Checks
1. **Rollout scaling**: Train MaxRL on mazes with \( N=4,8,16,32,64,128 \). Verify Pass@1 and Pass@128 improve monotonically with \( N \) while RL/GRPO plateau.
2. **Unbiasedness test**: On a synthetic Bernoulli reward task, compare analytic gradient (Derivation Eq.6) to Monte‑Carlo average of MaxRL estimator across many seeds; expect <5% error for \( N \le 32 \).
3. **Zero‑success ablation**: Modify algorithm to never drop gradients (e.g., add \( \epsilon \) to mean reward) and compare learning curves on mazes. If variant accelerates progress on hardest prompts, the drop‑gradient heuristic introduces bias.
