# \$OneMillion-Bench: How Far are Language Agents from Human Experts?

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.07980
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07980
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Claude-Opus-4.6 achieves an Expert Score of 55–56% (Pass Rate 35–36%) on 400 expert-curated, economically-valued professional tasks; web search improves this to 63–64% (Pass Rate 43–48%), but degrades performance for some weaker models.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces **\$OneMillion-Bench**, a benchmark of 400 high-stakes professional tasks across Law, Finance, Healthcare, Natural Science, and Industry, each assigned a real-world monetary value exceeding \$1M in aggregate. Designed to overcome the limitations of existing benchmarks, the tasks require multi-step reasoning, authoritative retrieval, rule application, and constraint decisions. Evaluation uses a rubric-based protocol scoring factual accuracy, logical coherence, practical feasibility, and professional compliance. Testing 35 language models reveals that even top agents like Claude-Opus-4.6 achieve only modest performance without tools; web search yields gains for strong models but harms many weaker ones. Finance is the most challenging domain, while formatting and instruction following are relatively easier than factual or analytical reasoning.

## Method Summary
The benchmark comprises 400 tasks (200 English, 200 Chinese) curated by domain experts. Each task includes a prompt, a reference answer, and a detailed rubric with weighted criteria (positive scores −20 to +10) and negative penalties. The task’s **economic value** is computed from the estimated completion time for a senior professional multiplied by a region-specific market wage. Tasks undergo **adversarial validation**: they are retained only if multiple frontier models fail to surpass a predefined passing threshold (Expert Score ≥ 0.7). A three-stage peer review ensures quality and consensus. Agents are evaluated in three configurations: **Vanilla** (no tools), **Search Agents** (with web search), and **Deep Research Agents** (specialized systems). Responses are scored via a rubric-based “LM-as-a-judge” protocol, producing an **Expert Score** (0–1) and a binary **Pass Rate**.

## Key Results
- Claude-Opus-4.6 leads all models but scores only 55–56% (Expert Score) and 35–36% (Pass Rate) in the Vanilla setting; with search, scores rise to 63–64% and 43–48%.
- Web search acts as an **efficacy amplifier**: it improves performance for top models but degrades scores for many weaker agents (e.g., Hunyuan-2.0, Step-3.5-Flash).
- **Domain difficulty** is consistent: Finance is the hardest, while Natural Science and Industry are comparatively easier. Factual information retrieval and analytical reasoning are the most challenging rubric types; formatting and instruction following are the easiest.
- All models perform significantly worse on **temporally sensitive** questions, with the gap largest for the strongest models.
- Increasing the number of generation samples (`k`) raises `pass@k` but degrades `pass^k`, indicating high output variance on complex tasks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Economic valuation of tasks ensures they reflect real-world professional workloads and stakes, motivating realistic task design and evaluation.
- **Mechanism:** Each task is assigned a monetary value based on estimated completion time for a senior expert and market hourly wage, sourced from authoritative data (e.g., U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics). This valuation influences task difficulty, complexity, and domain coverage.
- **Core assumption:** Monetary value correlates with task difficulty and professional relevance; higher-value tasks require more expertise and have greater economic consequences.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "Each task is assigned a real-world monetary value, calculated by the estimated time required for task completion of a senior professional and its prevailing market hourly wage." - **Claim:** Rubric-based multi-dimensional evaluation reliably differentiates expert-level performance by capturing multiple facets of professional work beyond surface correctness.
- **Mechanism:** Domain experts design rubrics with weighted criteria (factual accuracy, logical coherence, practical feasibility, professional compliance) and negative penalties for violations. Scores aggregate into an Expert Score (0–1) and a binary Pass Rate (≥0.7), enabling fine-grained analysis.
- **Core assumption:** Rubrics validly and reliably measure quality of professional deliverables, and weighted aggregation appropriately reflects overall expertise.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "We adopt a rubric-based evaluation protocol scoring factual accuracy, logical coherence, practical feasibility, and professional compliance." [section] Section 2.2 defines Expert Score and Pass Rate; Section 3.1 describes rubric design with asymmetric weights (−20 to 10) and negative penalties. - **Claim:** Adversarial validation and multi-stage expert peer review ensure tasks are discriminative, valid, and of high quality, preventing trivial or impossible tasks.
- **Mechanism:** New tasks are tested against frontier language agents; only tasks that multiple agents fail to pass a predefined threshold are retained. A second expert peer-reviews the task, rubrics, and reference answer; a third expert resolves unresolved disputes.
- **Core assumption:** Tasks that challenge current SOTA agents are useful for measuring progress, and expert peer review guarantees task clarity, fairness, and domain alignment.

## Foundational Learning

### Concept: Economic valuation of cognitive tasks
- **Why needed here:** The benchmark’s core novelty is grounding task difficulty in real‑world monetary value; misunderstanding this obscures why tasks are selected and how results are interpreted (e.g., Economic Value metrics).
- **Quick check:** If a task takes a senior lawyer 10 hours at \$200/hour and a data scientist 2 hours at \$100/hour, what is the merged economic value, and what does the difference imply about required expertise?

### Concept: Rubric-based multi‑dimensional scoring
- **Why needed here:** Agent performance is reported via Expert Score and Pass Rate, which rely on rubrics. Without grasping how rubrics are constructed (weights, negative penalties, tags), one cannot design fair evaluations or interpret score distributions.
- **Quick check:** Design a rubric for a medical diagnosis task that includes both factual accuracy (positive weight) and a penalty for omitting contraindications; explain why negative rubrics matter.

### Concept: Adversarial task validation
- **Why needed here:** The benchmark’s discriminative power stems from retaining only tasks that stump current agents. Understanding this process is crucial for extending the benchmark or diagnosing why new tasks may be too easy/hard.
- **Quick check:** What are the risks of setting the adversarial passing threshold too low or too high, and how would you calibrate it?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Task Curation Interface -> Economic Valuation Module -> Adversarial Validation Engine -> Peer Review Workflow -> Rubric Scoring Service -> Agent Execution Scaffold -> Results Aggregator

### Critical path
Task creation → adversarial validation → peer review → resolution → task inclusion → agent runs → response collection → rubric scoring → score aggregation → leaderboard generation.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Manual curation vs. scalability:** Expert involvement ensures quality but limits size; hybrid approaches (semi‑automated rubric generation) risk validity.
- **Rubric complexity vs. reliability:** More dimensions capture nuance but increase subjectivity; negative penalties improve alignment but require careful calibration.
- **Economic grounding vs. portability:** Region‑specific wages add realism but complicate cross‑regional comparisons; a single global wage may obscure local market differences.
- **Adversarial threshold sensitivity:** Too lenient → tasks become trivial; too strict → tasks become unsolvable, wasting curation effort.

### Failure signatures
- Low inter‑rater agreement on rubric scores (e.g., Cohen’s κ < 0.6).
- High variance in agent Pass Rate across domains without clear rationale (e.g., Finance consistently hardest).
- Economic value not correlating with task difficulty metrics (e.g., question length, rubric count).
- Web search degrading performance for most models, indicating poor scaffold design or retrieval integration.
- Over 20% of tasks achieving near‑perfect scores from all agents, suggesting insufficient discrimination.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Pilot reliability test:** Have two experts independently score 50 sample agent responses using the rubrics; compute inter‑rater reliability and revise ambiguous rubrics.
2. **Adversarial threshold sweep:** Run 20 candidate tasks with 3 frontier agents at varying passing thresholds (e.g., Expert Score ≥0.5, 0.6, 0.7) to find a level that yields ~30–50% pass rate, ensuring discrimination.
3. **Rubric weighting sensitivity:** For a subset of tasks, randomly perturb rubric weights by ±20% and measure changes in model rankings; if rankings shift significantly, weights need stabilization.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** How can \$OneMillion-Bench be expanded to additional high-value domains (e.g., energy, climate science, public policy) while maintaining task quality and rubric discriminative power?
- **Open Question 2:** Can the rubric-based evaluation in \$OneMillion-Bench be automated to scale beyond manual expert scoring without sacrificing reliability, and what machine learning architectures are most effective for assessing intermediate reasoning steps, evidence citation, and professional compliance?
- **Open Question 3:** Why does web search consistently improve the performance of top-tier models on \$OneMillion-Bench but often degrade the performance of weaker models, and how can retrieval-augmented generation be made more robust to noisy or conflicting evidence?
- **Open Question 4:** What architectural or training modifications can reduce the performance gap between temporally agnostic and time-sensitive questions in \$OneMillion-Bench, thereby improving model robustness to evolving information?
- **Open Question 5:** Why does test-time scaling (increasing the number of generation samples) increase the pass@k metric but degrade pass^k on \$OneMillion-Bench, and how can output consistency be improved for complex, long-horizon tasks?

## Limitations
- **Dataset Access:** The 400 tasks, rubrics, and reference answers are not publicly released, preventing external validation of task design, difficulty distribution, and rubric fidelity.
- **Evaluation Reliability:** Rubric scoring uses “LM-as-a-judge” with unreported prompt templates, temperature settings, and calibration procedures; no inter-judge agreement metrics are provided.
- **Adversarial Bias:** Retaining tasks that stump current frontier models may overfit to present weaknesses, creating a moving target that poorly measures long-term progress or general expertise.
