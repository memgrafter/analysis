# NAAMSE: Framework for Evolutionary Security Evaluation of Agents

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.07391
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07391
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: The full NAAMSE system achieves a mean fitness score of 79.76 over 10 iterations, significantly outperforming exploration-only (42.86) and mutation-only (54.79), and discovers high-severity jailbreaks scoring 100.

## Executive Summary
Current AI agent security evaluations rely on manual red-teaming and static benchmarks, which cannot capture adaptive, multi-turn adversarial behavior. NAAMSE reframes robustness evaluation as a feedback-driven evolutionary optimization problem. A single autonomous agent iteratively selects prompts from a hierarchically clustered corpus, queries the target agent, scores responses with an asymmetric fitness function that penalizes both harmful compliance (on adversarial prompts) and over-refusal (on benign prompts), and mutates prompts using a set of genetic operators. Over ten iterations, the full system (combining exploration and mutation) systematically uncovers vulnerabilities missed by one-shot methods, achieving a mean fitness of 79.76 versus 42.86 (exploration-only) and 54.79 (mutation-only). It also discovers jailbreaks with the maximum score of 100. Cross-model ablations and external validation confirm the approach’s superiority, demonstrating that adaptive, synergistic search yields a more realistic and scalable robustness assessment.

## Method Summary
NAAMSE treats security evaluation as an evolutionary process. The system maintains a corpus of ~128K adversarial prompts (from benchmarks like JailbreakBench, AdvBench, HarmBench) and ~50K benign prompts (from Rasa, CLINC150, WildGuardMix), embedded using All-MiniLM-L6-v2 and organized via recursive K-means clustering into a hierarchy. An autonomous agent runs a four-phase lifecycle for each iteration: (1) select a prompt from the corpus using a policy that balances random exploration and similarity-based selection; (2) send the prompt to the target agent (e.g., Gemini 2.5 Flash) and record the response; (3) compute a fitness score (0–100) using a judge LLM to classify harm (RA/HA categories) and a PII detector, with asymmetric penalties to discourage both jailbreak compliance and unnecessary refusals; (4) based on the score, apply a mutation operator (choose from ten types spanning research-derived, community-sourced, and baseline strategies) or reintegrate the prompt into the corpus. Ablations compare the full “explore+similar+mutation” configuration against exploration-only and mutation-only variants. The primary metric is mean fitness over 10 iterations; secondary metrics include the number of perfect-score jailbreaks discovered.

## Key Results
- Full NAAMSE (exploration + mutation) achieves a mean fitness of 79.76, far exceeding exploration-only (42.86) and mutation-only (54.79) over ten iterations.
- The system discovers high‑severity jailbreaks that attain the maximum fitness score of 100.
- Cross‑model ablations and external validators confirm consistent superiority across frontier LLMs, validating the approach’s generalizability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
NAAMSE’s effectiveness stems from its synergistic combination of structured exploration, adaptive mutation, and an asymmetric fitness function. The hierarchical clustering guides the agent toward semantically diverse regions of the prompt space, preventing premature convergence. Mutation operators—ranging from simple paraphrasing to complex adversarial techniques—inject novelty and exploit discovered weaknesses, effectively simulating an evolving attacker. The fitness score simultaneously rewards evasion of safety filters (by penalizing refusal on benign prompts) and penalizes harmful compliance (by penalizing compliance on adversarial prompts), eliminating the incentive to simply refuse all queries. This dual pressure pushes the agent to find prompts that genuinely bypass safeguards while remaining functionally relevant. The iterative feedback loop allows the system to build upon past findings, amplifying subtle vulnerabilities that one‑shot evaluations miss. Together, these elements yield a more realistic, scalable, and automated robustness assessment.

## Foundational Learning
- **Evolutionary Algorithms**: *Why needed*: Provide an automated, iterative mechanism to search the vast space of adversarial prompts without human intervention. *Quick check*: Does the system maintain population-like structures and apply selection/variation operators?
- **Hierarchical Clustering**: *Why needed*: Organizes a large corpus into a tree to balance exploration (traversing disparate clusters) and exploitation (deep diving into promising regions). *Quick check*: Are prompts embedded and grouped recursively?
- **Asymmetric Fitness Scoring**: *Why needed*: Prevents reward hacking by penalizing both over-refusal (benign prompts) and harmful compliance (adversarial prompts), ensuring a meaningful measure of robustness. *Quick check*: Does the score combine harm and refusal metrics with opposite signs?
- **LLM-as-Judge**: *Why needed*: Automates evaluation of target responses for safety and alignment at scale. *Quick check*: Are judge LLMs used to assign RA/HA labels and compute penalties?
- **Mutation Operators**: *Why needed*: Generate diverse adversarial variants, mimicking tactics of real attackers and helping bypass static defenses. *Quick check*: Are multiple mutation types (e.g., role-play, cipher, few-shot) available and selected based on fitness thresholds?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Corpus (Hierarchical Clusters) → Selection Policy → Target Agent (LLM) → Response → Fitness Engine (Judge LLM + PII Detector) → Mutation Operator → Reintegration → Corpus

**Critical path**:  
1. Prompt selection from cluster tree.  
2. Query target agent and receive response.  
3. Evaluate response with fitness engine.  
4. Apply mutation if score in refinement range; else select new cluster.  
5. Add (mutated) prompt back to corpus under nearest centroid.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- *Exploration vs. Exploitation*: Clustering provides structured exploration; mutation adds exploitation. Over‑emphasis on either reduces diversity or stalls progress.  
- *Single‑agent vs. Population*: A single mutating agent simplifies tracking but may lack parallel diversity; population could improve coverage at higher compute cost.  
- *Static vs. Dynamic Corpus*: The corpus evolves with mutated prompts, increasing relevance but risking semantic drift if mutations become noisy.

**Failure signatures**:  
- *Clustering collapse*: Few oversized clusters reduce diversity → monitor cluster size distribution.  
- *Judge inconsistency*: High variance in fitness scores → cache and compare judge outputs on control set.  
- *Invalid mutations*: Malformed prompts yield no response → log and filter mutations by basic syntax checks.  
- *Fitness asymmetry breakdown*: Over‑refusal or over‑compliance not penalized → verify scoring on known benign/adversarial pairs.

**First three experiments**:  
1. **Full system**: Run NAAMSE with all components (explore+similar+mutation) for 10 iterations; record mean fitness and jailbreaks found.  
2. **Exploration‑only**: Disable mutations; select prompts only via hierarchical random sampling; compare fitness.  
3. **Mutation‑only**: Start from a fixed seed prompt and apply mutations without cluster‑guided exploration; compare fitness.  
These ablations isolate the contribution of exploration and mutation synergy.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper does not explicitly list open questions or future research directions. However, the limitations identified (see Limitations section) point to several areas requiring further clarification and investigation, such as the exact clustering parameters, mutation operator selection probabilities, and judge LLM scoring details.

## Limitations
- Exact parameters for recursive K‑means clustering (number of clusters per level, split criteria) and cluster annotation are unreported, hindering faithful replication of the exploration mechanism.  
- Mutation operator selection probabilities and the precise LLM prompt templates for instantiating operators (e.g., “game theory attack”) are unspecified, potentially leading to arbitrary behavior.  
- The judge LLM’s scoring prompts and thresholds for RA/HA classification and PII detection are absent; only scaling matrices are provided, making it difficult to verify the asymmetric fitness design.

## Confidence
- **Overall evolutionary framework design**: High  
- **Empirical superiority claims (mean fitness, jailbreak discovery)**: Medium  
- **Exact numerical reproducibility**: Low  

## Next Checks
1. **Judge prompt fidelity test**: Reconstruct the scoring prompts from the described RA/HA matrices and validate that they produce the expected asymmetry on a held‑out set of adversarial and benign responses.  
2. **Mutation operator output audit**: For each mutation class, generate 10 sample prompts and manually assess semantic validity and adversarial relevance (e.g., ciphers decodable, role‑plays coherent).  
3. **Clustering reconstruction**: Implement recursive K‑means with heuristic defaults (depth = 4, `n_clusters` = √N per level); compare cluster size distributions and centroid annotations against the paper’s implicit patterns.
