# Learning What Matters Now: Dynamic Preference Inference under Contextual Shifts

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.22813
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22813
- **Reference count:** 32
- **Primary result:** DPI infers contextual preference weights from state history and conditions a policy via an on-policy envelope operator, achieving significantly higher post-shift success rates (e.g., 59.0% in Maze) than fixed-weight and heuristic baselines.

## Executive Summary
Agents operating in non-stationary environments must adapt their behavior when multi-objective trade-offs shift. This paper introduces Dynamic Preference Inference (DPI), a framework where an agent treats latent preference weights as variables to be inferred from recent state history. A variational Bayesian module approximates a posterior distribution over plausible preferences given the context. The policy is conditioned on preferences sampled from this posterior, with an envelope operator selecting the most promising candidate at each step. Additional regularization aligns inferred preferences with observed returns and stabilizes learning. Evaluated on simulated tasks with abrupt objective changes, DPI demonstrates effective adaptation and superior performance compared to methods with fixed or heuristic preferences, while producing interpretable preference trajectories.

## Method Summary
DPI comprises two coupled modules: (1) a **Value Appraisal Module** that uses a recurrent encoder (GRU) over a window of recent states to output a Gaussian posterior distribution over latent preference variables; (2) an **Action Selection Module** with a preference‑conditioned actor‑critic. At each decision step, multiple preference candidates are sampled from the posterior. The candidate maximizing the scalarized value estimate ⟨ω, V(s,ω)⟩ is selected to condition the actor. The preference encoder is trained with an ELBO plus two regularizers: **Directional Alignment** (penalizing preferences misaligned with the observed return vector) and **Self‑Consistency** (encouraging the encoder’s mean to match the envelope‑selected preference). This allows the agent to dynamically infer and act upon contextually appropriate trade‑offs without external supervision.

## Key Results
- DPI achieves a mean episodic return of 30.16 and a success rate of 59.0% in the Maze environment after shifts, while baselines like ENVELOPE score near 0% success.
- In the Queue task, DPI attains a success rate above 40%, compared to less than 11% for the best baseline.
- Ablation studies confirm that removing any component of the regularization suite (KL prior, directional alignment, self‑consistency) degrades post‑shift performance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** DPI adapts to shifting objectives by treating preference weights as latent variables inferred from recent state history via variational Bayesian updates.
- **Mechanism:** A recurrent encoder processes a history window of states to approximate a posterior distribution q_ϕ(ω_t | s_{t-H+1:t}) over the preference vector ω_t. Sampling from this posterior provides a distribution over plausible trade‑offs, enabling the agent to explore alternative value configurations before acting.
- **Core assumption:** The agent's current preference state is partially observable and non‑stationary; short‑term perceptual history contains sufficient evidence to update beliefs about the prevailing trade‑offs.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 3.1 describe the variational module approximating the posterior with a Gaussian.
- **Break condition:** If the recent history contains no informative signal about shifting objectives, the posterior collapses to the prior, leading to random or static preference sampling and failure to adapt.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Conditioning the policy on sampled preferences via an envelope operator enables context‑appropriate action selection without committing to a single inferred preference.
- **Mechanism:** At decision time, K preference candidates are sampled from the appraisal posterior. The candidate ω̂_t that maximizes the predicted scalarized value ⟨ω^(i)_t, V_θ(s_t, ω^(i)_t)⟩ is selected to condition the actor. This on‑policy envelope selection approximates the optimal policy across the current belief over preferences.
- **Core assumption:** The value function V_θ(s, ω) accurately estimates the scalarized return for any reasonable preference ω; the envelope over a finite set of samples is a good proxy for the true optimum over the continuous simplex.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 3.2 detail the envelope selection process.
- **Break condition:** If K is too small, the sampled set may not include a high‑performing preference. If V_θ is inaccurate early in training, envelope selection becomes noisy, leading to unstable policy conditioning.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Additional regularization (KL prior, directional alignment, self‑consistency) stabilizes preference learning, prevents degenerate solutions, and aligns inferred preferences with task semantics.
- **Mechanism:** The ELBO balances evidence fit (β E[⟨ω, G⟩]) with KL to a Gaussian prior. Directional alignment L_dir penalizes preferences pointing away from the observed return vector G. Self‑consistency L_stab anchors the encoder's mean prediction to the envelope‑selected ω̂_t.
- **Core assumption:** These auxiliary losses steer the posterior towards preferences that are (i) not overly confident without evidence (KL), (ii) directed towards attainable outcomes (directional alignment), and (iii) consistent with the policy's effective choice (self‑consistency).
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 3.3 introduces the regularizers; Section 4.7 ablation shows removing any component degrades performance.
- **Break condition:** If λ (directional weight) is too high, the agent may ignore evidence contradicting a "safe" direction, hindering adaptation. If γ (self‑consistency) is too low, the policy and preference inference may diverge.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Variational Inference**
  - *Why needed here:* To approximate an intractable posterior over latent preferences from perceptual evidence without explicit supervision of ω_t.
  - *Quick check question:* Why use a reparameterized Gaussian posterior instead of directly outputting a deterministic ω_t? (Answer: Provides uncertainty estimates and enables gradient flow via the reparameterization trick.)
- **Concept: Preference‑Conditioned Policies (MORL)**
  - *Why needed here:* The environment's vector rewards encode trade‑offs; conditioning the policy on ω allows a single network to represent a family of policies for different trade‑offs.
  - *Quick check question:* How does conditioning on ω at inference time differ from scalarizing the reward during training? (Answer: Scalarization pins a single trade‑off; conditioning enables runtime adaptation by varying ω.)
- **Concept: On‑Policy Envelope Selection**
  - *Why needed here:* To select an effective preference instance from the posterior without prematurely committing to a single sample; uses the same critic for all candidates to ensure fair comparison.
  - *Quick check question:* Why not just sample one ω_t from the posterior and act? (Answer: A single sample may be suboptimal; the envelope over K samples approximates maximizing over the full posterior.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** State Encoder -> History Memory (GRU) -> Preference Encoder (Gaussian) -> Sampler (K candidates) -> Action Selector (envelope) -> Actor‑Critic (π_θ, V_θ). Regularization heads compute L_dir and L_stab from selected ω̂_t and return G_t.
- **Critical path:**
  1. Environment step stores transition (s, a, r⃗).
  2. Build history s_{t‑H+1:t} -> GRU -> (µ_t, logσ_t).
  3. Sample K z_t -> ω_t; evaluate V_θ; select ω̂_t = argmax ⟨ω_t, V_θ(s,ω_t)⟩.
  4. Act a_t ~ π_θ(·|s_t, ω̂_t).
  5. Compute vector GAE G_t using ω̂_t.
  6. Update θ with PPO + critic loss (A_t = ⟨ω̂_t, A⃗_t⟩).
  7. Update ϕ with L_ELBO + λ L_dir + γ L_stab.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - K (envelope samples): Larger K improves coverage but increases compute; typical K=8 balances both.
  - H (history window): Too small loses context; too large introduces irrelevant past; performance plateaus beyond H≥9.
  - Regularization balance: λ too high stifles adaptation; γ too low decouples inference from policy.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Misalignment: Cosine similarity ⟨ω̂_t, r⃗_t⟩ near zero/negative → directional alignment failed.
  - Posterior collapse: σ_t → 0 prematurely → KL over‑regularized; agent ignores evidence.
  - Policy divergence: Success rate plummets while MER may stay moderate → policy exploits a poor ω̂_t consistently.
- **First three experiments:**
  1. **Queue:** Validate dynamic reweighting (morality ↔ energy) under discrete events; expect DPI SR >40% vs. <11% baselines.
  2. **Maze:** Test pixel observations, navigation, multiple events; check event→preference alignment via cosine similarity and PS@K.
  3. **Continuous Control (HalfCheetah):** Scale to high‑dim actions; verify DPI handles vector rewards and dynamic constraints without oracle signals.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1:** How can DPI be scaled to high‑dimensional, continuous state spaces such as those encountered in 3D embodied agents and multi‑agent systems?
  - *Basis:* A key limitation is that environments remain controlled and simulated; future work will focus on scaling to 3D embodied and multi‑agent settings, and on more expressive inference for long‑horizon and socially coupled preference dynamics.
- **Question 2:** How can DPI handle long‑horizon preference dynamics and socially coupled preferences where multiple agents influence each other's value trade‑offs?
  - *Basis:* Future work includes developing more expressive inference mechanisms for long‑horizon and socially coupled preference dynamics.
- **Question 3:** Does DPI effectively adapt to gradual or continuous preference shifts, or is it primarily tuned to abrupt event‑driven changes?
  - *Basis:* All experimental environments use event‑driven changes; no analysis is provided for scenarios where the preference distribution drifts slowly over time.
- **Question 4:** Is the diagonal Gaussian posterior assumption sufficiently expressive to capture complex belief structures over preferences, such as multi‑modality or correlations between objective weights?
  - *Basis:* The authors choose a simple diagonal Gaussian q_ϕ with a unit Gaussian prior but do not justify this against more flexible alternatives.

## Limitations
- **Implementation gaps:** Exact event parameters (e.g., energy rates, hazard probabilities), training schedule specifics, and full network layer dimensions beyond diagrams are omitted, hindering faithful reproduction.
- **Scalability unproven:** Evaluation is restricted to three simulated low‑dimensional environments; performance in high‑dimensional, real‑time, or embodied settings remains unknown.
- **Corpus novelty:** The work has not yet been cited; external validation and comparative follow‑up studies are pending.

## Confidence
- *Dynamic preference inference from short‑term history*: **Medium** (required by ablations, but sufficiency of a fixed window across diverse contexts is not fully established).
- *Envelope operator for preference selection*: **Medium** (ablations show benefit; sensitivity to K and value function accuracy needs scrutiny).
- *Regularization suite (KL, directional, self‑consistency)*: **High** (removing any term consistently degrades performance).
- *Superiority over baselines under shifts*: **High** (DPI achieves substantially higher post‑shift success rates across all tasks).

## Next Checks
1. Conduct a hyperparameter sensitivity sweep for history window H, envelope samples K, and regularization coefficients on Maze and Queue, recording post‑shift performance and failure signatures (posterior collapse, misalignment).
2. Track alignment between selected preference ω̂_t and instantaneous reward r_t over time; test whether alignment significantly increases after known event onsets, confirming contextual inference.
3. Isolate the envelope operator’s impact by comparing DPI (K=8) with a single‑sample variant (K=1) and random ω conditioning; ensure reported gains are not solely from the variational module.
