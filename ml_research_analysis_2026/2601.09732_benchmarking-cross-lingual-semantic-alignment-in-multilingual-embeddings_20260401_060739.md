# Benchmarking Cross-Lingual Semantic Alignment in Multilingual Embeddings

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.09732
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.09732
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Introduces Semantic Affinity (SA), a bounded [0,1] metric showing training objective (not scale/architecture) determines cross-lingual alignment; top BERT models with translation supervision achieve SA ≥ 0.60, while MLM-only models fail (SA < 0.50).

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the absence of reliable metrics to differentiate multilingual embedding models that achieve genuine cross-lingual semantic alignment from those that rely on language-specific patterns. It proposes Semantic Affinity (SA), a scale-invariant ratio metric based on cosine distance, and integrates it with PHATE manifold visualization in the Semanscope framework. Benchmarking 13 models across four datasets reveals a clear three-tier performance hierarchy: models with explicit translation-pair supervision (e.g., LaBSE) top the list, large language models plateau at moderate alignment regardless of scale, and masked-language-model-only architectures (e.g., mBERT, XLM-R) fail to align. The central finding is that the training objective—specifically, whether it enforces translation equivalence—is the decisive factor, not model size or architecture. An exploratory test using ancient Oracle Bone script suggests models capture corpus-specific patterns rather than universal semantic concepts.

## Method Summary
The Semanscope framework computes Semantic Affinity (SA) as `SA = D_intra / (D_intra + D_inter)`, where `D_intra` is the average intra-lingual cosine distance (semantic diversity within a language) and `D_inter` is the average inter-lingual cosine distance between translated equivalents. Cosine distance is used for magnitude invariance across models. For qualitative validation, PHATE diffusion manifold learning projects embeddings into 2D, with language interleaving indicating good alignment. The pipeline loads parallel datasets, generates embeddings (with per-word caching for efficiency), computes spreads and SA, estimates bootstrap errors, and produces PHATE plots with SA overlays.

## Key Results
- Semantic Affinity (SA) is introduced as a bounded [0,1] metric that measures cross-lingual alignment relative to within-language dispersion, using cosine distance for stability.
- Benchmark across 13 models and 4 datasets shows a three-tier structure: Tier 1 (supervised BERT models, SA ≥ 0.60), Tier 2 (LLMs, SA 0.55–0.61), Tier 3 (MLM-only models, SA < 0.50).
- Training objective—not architecture or scale—is the primary determinant of alignment; explicit translation supervision is essential for high SA.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Semantic Affinity (SA) quantifies cross-lingual alignment by comparing inter-lingual to intra-lingual spread using cosine distance.  
**Mechanism:** Intra-lingual spread measures semantic diversity within a language (normalization baseline). Inter-lingual spread measures cosine distances between translated equivalents across languages. SA is the ratio `D_intra / (D_intra + D_inter)`, bounded [0,1], where higher values indicate tighter cross-lingual clustering relative to within-language dispersion.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 3 define SA and justify cosine distance’s magnitude invariance; Appendix A.5 shows cosine stability vs Euclidean volatility.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Cross-lingual semantic alignment is determined primarily by training objective (explicit translation-pair supervision or semantic similarity objectives), not model architecture or scale.  
**Mechanism:** Models trained with translation pairs (LaBSE) or semantic similarity (USE, S-BERT) enforce direct cross-lingual binding. MLM-only models (mBERT, XLM-R) learn language-specific patterns without explicit alignment. LLMs via next-token prediction achieve weak incidental alignment that plateaus with scale.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract states the three-tier finding; Section 5.1 presents average SA scores; Section 6.1 contrasts LaBSE vs mBERT (same architecture, +40% SA difference due to training).

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** PHATE manifold visualization combined with SA metric provides diagnostic validation: visual language interleaving correlates with high SA, while language separation correlates with low SA.  
**Mechanism:** PHATE preserves local and global manifold structure; well-aligned embeddings show intermixing of translations in 2D projection. SA legend overlay gives quantitative confirmation.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract describes PHATE for qualitative validation; Section 4.3 details PHATE configuration; Section 5.3 and Figure 3 show PHATE plots with clear correlation to SA scores.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Cosine distance as magnitude-invariant similarity**  
  *Why needed here:* SA relies on cosine distance to ensure scores are comparable across models with different embedding scales. Euclidean distance conflates alignment with absolute magnitude, causing volatile SA scores.  
  *Quick check:* Why would two models with the same semantic alignment but different embedding norms yield different Euclidean SA scores but similar cosine SA scores?

- **Concept: Translation-equivalence assumption**  
  *Why needed here:* The benchmark tests whether translated sentences occupy the same region in vector space. Models violating this cannot support true cross-lingual tasks.  
  *Quick check:* If a model achieves high retrieval scores on a multilingual benchmark but low SA, what might be the reason?

- **Concept: Manifold learning (PHATE) vs linear projection (PCA)**  
  *Why needed here:* PHATE preserves both local neighborhoods and global structure, crucial for visualizing whether languages form separate manifolds or interleaved clusters. PCA might fail to reveal subtle separation.  
  *Quick check:* Why would t-SNE be less suitable than PHATE for diagnosing cross-lingual alignment?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Dataset loading -> Embedding generation (with per-word cache) -> Compute intra-lingual spreads -> Compute inter-lingual spreads -> Calculate SA (cosine primary) -> Bootstrap error estimation -> Generate PHATE -> Overlay SA legend -> Export results/plot.
- **Critical path:** Load parallel dataset → Generate embeddings (cached) → Compute D_intra and D_inter → Compute SA → Estimate bootstrap error → Run PHATE → Add SA legend → Save outputs.
- **Design tradeoffs:** Per-word caching (327 unique vs 9,397 phrase embeddings) gives ~30× efficiency but requires handling multi-meaning expansions. Loky parallelization avoids GIL (4.6× speedup) but increases memory. Cosine SA is stable but less intuitive than Euclidean; PHATE parameters (knn=15, decay=40) are fixed for sample sizes 1k–4k to ensure comparability. Automatic SA legend simplifies diagnostics but may clutter at extremes.
- **Failure signatures:** Average SA < 0.50 indicates alignment failure (Tier 3). Euclidean SA volatility (Δ > 0.1 across datasets) signals scale-dependent pathology. PHATE showing clear language clusters (e.g., Chinese left, English right) indicates spatial separation. Embedding collapse (max distance < 1e-6) yields degenerate outputs. Cache miss explosion negates efficiency gains.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Sanity check with LaBSE on DS1: Expect SA_cosine ≈ 0.80, PHATE with tight interleaving.  
  2. Failure case: mBERT on DS1: Expect SA_cosine ≈ 0.50, PHATE with language separation.  
  3. Dataset difficulty gradient: Run all models on DS1–DS4; expect SA drop from DS1/DS2 to DS4, verifying robustness differences.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How does SA perform on extremely low-resource language pairs or typologically diverse families (e.g., Sino-Tibetan vs Austronesian)?
- Can MLM-only models achieve SA ≥ 0.60 with enough data or novel objectives, or is explicit supervision irreplaceable?
- What is the theoretical upper bound of SA for LLMs under next-token prediction, and does it converge at larger scales (e.g., >70B)?
- Does the Oracle Bone experiment’s finding—that models learn corpus patterns rather than universal concepts—generalize beyond ancient Chinese script?

## Limitations
- SA assumes similar intra-lingual diversity across languages; this may not hold for typologically diverse pairs.
- PHATE hyperparameters (knn=15, decay=40) are fixed; sensitivity analysis across a range is missing.
- Oracle Bone probe is limited to ancient Chinese script; conclusions may not generalize to other language families or modern concepts.
- Model coverage excludes newest LLMs (e.g., GPT‑4, Claude) and low-resource pretraining schemes, limiting scalability and inclusivity claims.

## Confidence
- SA metric definition and boundedness: **High** (mathematically sound, stable across models/datasets per Appendix A.5).
- Training objective as primary determinant of alignment: **Medium** (consistent 13‑model comparison but could be confounded by data volume/quality).
- LLM alignment plateau at 0.55–0.61 SA: **Medium** (observed for 0.6B–8B models; larger models untested).
- Claim that models learn corpus patterns rather than universal cognitive concepts: **Low** (Oracle Bone probe is indirect and culturally specific).

## Next Checks
1. **Language diversity expansion:** Compute SA on 10+ low‑resource languages across 3 new parallel datasets to verify robustness of the three‑tier pattern beyond high‑resource languages.
2. **Direct objective intervention:** Fine‑tune mBERT on high‑quality translation pairs and compare SA change to a control fine‑tuned on monolingual data, isolating the effect of explicit cross‑lingual supervision.
3. **PHATE parameter sweep:** Vary knn (5–50) and decay (10–100) for all models; assess whether language interleaving patterns remain consistent and correlate with SA stability.
