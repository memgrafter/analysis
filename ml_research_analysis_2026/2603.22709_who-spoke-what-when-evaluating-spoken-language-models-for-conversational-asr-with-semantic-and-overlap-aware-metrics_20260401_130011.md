# Who Spoke What When? Evaluating Spoken Language Models for Conversational ASR with Semantic and Overlap-Aware Metrics

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.22709  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22709  
- **Reference count:** 0  
- **Primary result:** Task-specific LLMs match modular systems in two-speaker cases but falter with more speakers and overlap; modular pipelines remain robust with overlap causing ~90% of errors.

## Executive Summary
This paper evaluates spoken language models (LLMs) and modular pipelines for conversational automatic speech recognition (CASR) on multi-speaker recordings with overlapping speech. It introduces overlap-aware metrics (tcpWER decomposition) and a semantic error rate (tcpSemER) based on sentence embeddings. Across three CHiME-8 datasets (2–7 speakers, 14–29% overlap), modular systems (e.g., DiCoW, NTT CHiME-8) consistently outperform task-specific LLMs (e.g., VibeVoice) as speaker count and overlap increase, with LLMs showing high deletion errors in overlapped regions. LLMs achieve lower tcpSemER, indicating their errors are often superficial (e.g., disfluency differences) rather than semantically significant. A simple multi-channel voting scheme (MOVER) improves LLMs but not enough to close the gap. The study highlights the robustness of explicit separation in modular pipelines and the need for native multi-channel LLM approaches.

## Method Summary
The study compares CASR systems on CHiME-8 datasets: Mixer-6 (2 speakers, ~14% overlap), NOTSOFAR-1 (3–7 speakers, ~29% overlap), and DiPCo (4 speakers, ~25% overlap). Systems include modular single-channel (diarization + target‑speaker ASR), modular multi‑channel (with source separation and TS-VAD), task‑specific LLMs fine‑tuned for multi‑speaker transcription, and a general multimodal LLM (Gemini) via prompting. Evaluation uses time‑constrained permutation WER (tcpWER, collar=5 s), its decomposition into overlap vs. non‑overlap errors, and a semantic error rate (tcpSemER) computed with MiniLM-L12v2 sentence embeddings. A simple multi‑channel voting method (MOVER) combines per‑channel outputs.

## Key Results
- Task‑specific LLMs (VibeVoice) achieve comparable tcpWER to modular systems on the 2‑speaker MX6 dataset but degrade significantly on higher‑speaker datasets (NSF1, DiPCo).
- Overlap accounts for over 80% of errors across all systems; LLMs suffer disproportionately high deletion rates in overlapped regions.
- LLMs produce lower tcpSemER than modular systems, suggesting their errors are more often superficial (e.g., filler word differences) rather than semantically altering.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Modular pipelines gain robustness via explicit speaker separation before ASR.  
**Mechanism:** These systems decompose the problem: diarization identifies speaker segments, separation isolates overlapping speech, and single‑speaker ASR transcribes each stream. This reduces interference during recognition, which is optimized for single‑speaker conditions.  
**Evidence:** Modular systems show lower tcpWER in overlap regions; performance remains stable as speaker count increases.  
**Break condition:** If overlap is minimal, the separation front‑end may introduce diarization errors and provide no advantage, potentially allowing simpler end‑to‑end models to match performance.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Task‑specific LLMs degrade with more speakers/overlap because they rely on implicit separation within a monolithic transformer.  
**Mechanism:** LLMs process long‑form mixed audio directly, forcing the model to perform diarization and separation internally without dedicated modules (e.g., time‑frequency masking). This implicit handling is not optimized for concurrent speech.  
**Evidence:** LLMs have high deletion errors in overlap; tcpWER norm rises sharply in overlap regions, especially on high‑speaker datasets.  
**Break condition:** Low overlap allows LLMs to match modular performance, as seen on the 2‑speaker MX6.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** LLMs yield lower tcpSemER because their outputs are normalized and fluent, reducing penalty for lexical variations that preserve meaning.  
**Mechanism:** LLMs fine‑tuned for transcription often produce cleaned‑up text, diverging verbatim but preserving semantics. Modular pipelines produce verbatim outputs including disfluencies, which WER penalizes heavily, but also commit semantic errors when separation fails.  
**Evidence:** On MX6, VibeVoice has higher tcpWER but lower tcpSemER than the NTT modular system.  
**Break condition:** Applications requiring verbatim transcription (e.g., legal) would not benefit from tcpSemER’s reweighting.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Inputs (multi‑channel/single‑channel audio) → Systems (modular single‑channel DiCoW; modular multi‑channel NTT CHiME‑8; task‑specific LLMs VibeVoice/Voxtral; general LLM Gemini; MOVER integration) → Outputs (speaker‑labeled, timestamped word sequences) → Metrics (tcpWER, tcpSemER, DER, overlap decomposition) → Integration (MOVER combines channel‑wise outputs).

**Critical path:**  
For a given system and dataset: Audio is processed by the system to produce a hypothesis transcript with speaker IDs and timestamps. Time‑constrained speaker permutation alignment (Hungarian algorithm on tcpWER costs) maps reference and hypothesis speakers. Error counts (insertions, deletions, substitutions) are aggregated; for tcpSemER, sentence embeddings compute semantic similarity over aligned segments.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Modular vs. E2E:* Modular pipelines offer robustness via specialized components (especially for overlap) but suffer error propagation. E2E LLMs allow joint optimization and long‑context modeling but lack explicit separation, hurting overlap handling.
- *Single vs. Multi‑channel:* Multi‑channel processing (beamforming, separation) improves SNR and overlap robustness but requires specific microphone arrays. Single‑channel or voting (MOVER) is more flexible but less effective.
- *Metric selection:* tcpWER measures verbatim accuracy; tcpSemER measures semantic fidelity. Using only tcpWER may over‑penalize LLM normalization; only tcpSemER may miss disfluency errors important for some applications.

**Failure signatures:**
- *LLM‑based systems:* Surge in deletion errors in overlapped regions; high DER indicating poor speaker separation; performance collapse on high‑speaker sessions (e.g., VibeVoice on DiPCo).
- *Modular single‑channel:* Increased insertion errors with more speakers (likely from diarization oversegmentation); degradation in reverberant/noisy single‑channel conditions (DiPCo).
- *General LLM (Gemini):* Catastrophically high errors due to diarization failure (DER >74%), causing massive misalignment.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Baseline Single‑Channel Comparison: Run DiCoW, VibeVoice, and Gemini on MX6, NSF1, DiPCo. Record cpWER, tcpWER, tcpSemER, DER. Expect LLMs competitive on MX6, degraded on others; Gemini fails everywhere.
2. Overlap‑Aware Analysis: Using tcpWER alignment, compute normalized tcpWER for overlap vs. non‑overlap regions on NSF1. Verify >80% of errors originate in overlap for all systems; LLMs have disproportionately high overlap error rates.
3. Multi‑channel Integration Test: Apply MOVER to channel‑wise outputs of VibeVoice and DiCoW on MX6 and NSF1. Measure improvement in tcpWER. Expect MOVER narrows gap to multi‑channel modular on MX6 but insufficient on NSF1/DiPCo.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **How to design effective native multi‑channel LLM‑based CASR systems?** Current LLMs are single‑channel; simple voting gives limited gains; native multi‑channel processing remains unexplored.
- **What specific improvements enable general‑purpose multimodal LLMs to handle diarization for CASR?** Gemini 3.0 Flash showed poor diarization; underlying reasons and fixes are unknown.
- **How well does tcpSemER align with human semantic judgments and downstream task performance?** Without validation, the metric’s practical advantage over WER‑based metrics is uncertain.

## Limitations
- **Reproduction blockers:** Unknowns in Gemini prompting, MOVER combination rules, exact text normalization, and array‑specific preprocessing prevent faithful replication.
- **Confounders:** Systems differ in architecture, training data, and model size; performance differences may not be solely due to separation design.
- **Metric validation:** tcpSemER’s sensitivity to normalization and embedding choice lacks benchmarking against human semantic error judgments.

## Confidence
- Performance comparison (LLMs match modular on 2‑spk but degrade with more speakers/overlap): **High**
- Overlap as primary error source: **High**
- LLM semantic error rate advantage (lower tcpSemER): **Medium**
- Mechanistic explanations (explicit separation modular, implicit separation LLM, semantic vs. surface errors): **Medium**

## Next Checks
1. **Separated input ablation:** Run VibeVoice on oracle single‑speaker audio (from reference) for NSF1. Compare tcpWER/tcpSemER to its mixed‑audio performance and to DiCoW on separated audio. Expect LLM to improve dramatically if separation is the key bottleneck.
2. **Normalization invariance:** Apply the CHiME‑8 normalization script uniformly to all hypothesis outputs (including originally normalized ones) and recompute metrics. Verify whether the tcpSemER gap between LLMs and modular systems persists.
3. **Diarization‑ASR correlation:** Compute per‑session DER and tcpWER; within overlapped segments, measure the fraction of ASR errors occurring in regions where the reference speaker is misattributed. A strong positive correlation would directly link diarization failures to ASR errors.
