# When Should Humans Step In? Optimal Human Dispatching in AI-Assisted Decisions

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.13688
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13688
- **Reference count:** 18
- **Primary result:** Selective human dispatch reduces mean absolute error from ~0.70 (AI-only) to below 0.55 using only 2–3 human queries per paper (20–30% of full review effort).

## Executive Summary
The paper presents a decision-theoretic framework for optimally allocating costly human review effort to correct noisy AI-generated factor-level assessments. The core idea is to selectively acquire human judgments for those factors where they are expected to most reduce prediction error under squared loss. The optimal selection criterion simplifies to maximizing a reward function that measures the variance in the final decision explained by the human signal. Analytical solutions are derived for linear models, and a two-stage orthogonal estimation procedure is proposed for nonparametric settings. Empirical evaluation on 3,400 papers shows that the dispatching method achieves accuracy comparable to full human review while using only 20–30% of the human evaluation budget, reducing mean absolute error from ~0.70 to below 0.55 with merely 2–3 queries per paper. Notably, simple linear-model-based rules perform nearly as well as complex nonparametric rules but at a fraction of the computational cost.

## Method Summary
The method formulates the human dispatch problem as a constrained optimization: given a budget on the number of human queries, select a subset of factor-level assessments to be replaced by human judgments so as to minimize expected squared error of the final decision. Under squared loss and assuming unbiased, conditionally independent human signals, the optimal selection rule reduces to picking factors that maximize the expected variance reduction, which is proportional to the product of the AI’s error variance and the factor’s weight in the decision rule. For linear outcome models (Y = Σβ_i Z_i + ε), this yields a closed-form selection: choose the factors with largest β_i²·Var(ε_i). In nonparametric settings where the outcome function is unknown, the authors employ a two-stage procedure: first estimate the conditional expectation of the outcome given factors (using any machine learning method), then compute generalized residual importance scores via orthogonalization to avoid regularization bias, and finally select the top scores subject to the budget. The approach is model-agnostic and applicable to any factor-level assessment system.

## Key Results
- The dispatching method achieves mean absolute error below 0.55 with 2–3 human queries per paper, compared to ~0.70 for AI-only, matching the performance of full human review.
- Human effort is reduced to 20–30% of that required for full review while maintaining comparable accuracy.
- Linear-model-based selection rules are nearly as effective as nonparametric rules but with significantly lower computational overhead.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The method’s effectiveness stems from the variance‑based reward function, which directly approximates the marginal benefit of acquiring a human correction for a factor. Factors that contribute more to the outcome variance and where the AI is noisier yield higher expected improvement. By directing the limited human budget toward these high‑impact factors, the method maximizes accuracy gain per query. The squared loss assumption makes the optimal rule separable across factors, enabling efficient computation even with many factors. Moreover, the linear analytical solution captures the bulk of the benefit because in practice (e.g., peer review) decision aggregation is often approximately linear in factor scores, and human error variances across factors tend to be similar.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
AI Factor Assessment → Dispatching Module → Human Review Queue → Integration Module → Final Decision

**Critical path**  
1. AI generates noisy factor scores for a new instance.  
2. Dispatching evaluates each factor’s expected variance reduction and selects top‑K.  
3. Selected factor queries are sent to human reviewers.  
4. Human responses replace corresponding AI scores.  
5. Final decision score is computed (e.g., weighted sum) and thresholded.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- *Computational simplicity vs. adaptability*: Linear rules are cheap and nearly optimal; nonparametric rules capture complex interactions but require more computation and careful cross‑validation.  
- *Query budget vs. accuracy*: Increasing K improves performance but with diminishing returns; the method optimizes this tradeoff under a fixed budget.  
- *Assumption of independent human noise*: The derivation assumes human errors are independent across factors; correlated noise could degrade performance.

**Failure signatures**  
- If AI noise variance is underestimated, high‑impact factors may be deprioritized.  
- If the outcome model is highly nonlinear and factor interactions matter, the linear selection rule may mis‑rank factors, leading to suboptimal allocation.  
- Human review latency could break the sequential path; a fallback strategy (e.g., use AI scores if human does not respond in time) may be needed.

**Exactly 3 first experiments**  
1. *Baseline comparison*: Evaluate MAE of AI‑only, full human review, and dispatching with varying query budgets (1–10 per paper).  
2. *Linear vs. nonparametric*: Compare performance and runtime of the analytical linear selection against the two‑stage nonparametric method on the peer review dataset.  
3. *Robustness to noise*: In simulation, vary AI error variance across factors and test whether the variance‑based selection adapts appropriately.

## Limitations
- Domain scope: Evaluation is limited to AI‑assisted peer review; generalizability to other decision domains is uncertain.
- Human cost assumptions: The model assumes a fixed cost per query, whereas real‑world costs may vary with expertise, urgency, or factor complexity.
- Statistical transparency: The results lack confidence intervals and significance tests, making it difficult to assess the reliability of reported improvements.

## Confidence
- Theoretical optimality under squared loss: **High**
- Empirical effectiveness (effort reduction, comparable accuracy): **Medium**
- Linear‑model efficiency and near‑optimal performance: **Medium**
