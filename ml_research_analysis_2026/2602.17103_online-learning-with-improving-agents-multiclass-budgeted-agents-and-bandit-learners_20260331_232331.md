# Online Learning with Improving Agents: Multiclass, Budgeted Agents and Bandit Learners
## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2602.17103
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17103
*   **Reference Count:** 5
*   **Primary Result:** Novel combinatorial dimensions (ILdim, Multiclass ILdim, BILdim, WILdim) tightly characterize optimal mistake bounds for deterministic online learners with improving agents across full/bandit feedback and weighted/unweighted settings.

## Executive Summary
This paper extends the online learning with improving agents framework. In this model, agents can invest effort to improve their feature vectors in response to a learner's classifier, aiming for more desirable labels. The core contribution is the introduction of combinatorial dimensions—such as the Improvement Littlestone dimension (ILdim) and its variants—that precisely characterize the optimal number of mistakes a deterministic learner can make. Results are provided for multiclass classification, for the distinction between full feedback (learner sees improved features) and bandit feedback (learner only sees the final label), and for settings where improvements have associated costs. The analysis applies to general improvement graphs and infinite hypothesis classes, yielding instance-optimal learners.

## Method Summary
The paper develops a theoretical framework for online learning where a learner's hypothesis influences agent behavior through a predefined improvement graph. Agents best-respond by moving to more favorable nodes (feature vectors) within this graph. The analysis introduces dimensions that measure the complexity of these interactive learning problems. For deterministic learners, the optimal mistake bound is shown to equal the appropriate dimension (e.g., Multiclass ILdim for multiclass full feedback). Algorithms are constructed that achieve these bounds by strategically querying and tracking agent movements. The bandit feedback setting, where the learner only observes the final label post-improvement, introduces a multiplicative price in terms of the graph's maximum degree and number of classes.

## Key Results
*   The optimal mistake bound for deterministic learners in the multiclass full-information setting is exactly the Multiclass Improvement Littlestone dimension.
*   For bandit feedback, the optimal bound is the Bandit Improvement Littlestone dimension (BILdim).
*   The price of bandit feedback relative to full feedback is at most $O(\Delta_G \cdot k \log k)$, where $\Delta_G$ is the maximum degree of the improvement graph and $k$ is the number of classes.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The combinatorial dimensions (ILdim, BILdim, etc.) effectively capture the sequential complexity of the learning problem under the improvement dynamics. They generalize the classic Littlestone dimension to settings where examples are not fixed but are endogenously generated by an agent's rational response along a known graph. The learner's strategy is to force the agent into "hard" sequences of improvements that maximize the number of mistakes, mirroring the adversarial construction in standard online learning. The tightness comes from constructing matching upper bounds (algorithms) and lower bounds (hard sequences) based on these dimensions. For bandit feedback, the additional cost arises because the learner must explore the graph blindly, requiring more mistakes to infer the agent's path without full feature observations.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Littlestone Dimension:** The classic combinatorial measure for optimal mistake bounds in deterministic online learning. *Why needed:* The ILdimensions directly generalize this concept to the improving agents setting. *Quick check:* Verify that for a trivial improvement graph (no edges), ILdim reduces to the standard Littlestone dimension.
*   **Improvement Graph:** A directed graph where nodes represent feature vectors and edges represent feasible, costly improvements an agent can make. *Why needed:* It formalizes the agent's capabilities and constraints for feature modification. *Quick check:* Ensure the graph is acyclic to prevent infinite improvement loops.
*   **Realizability Assumption:** The assumption that there exists a hypothesis in the class consistent with the entire interaction sequence (agent's path and labels). *Why needed:* The mistake-bound analysis is founded on this noiseless setting. *Quick check:* Confirm all lower bound constructions adhere to a realizable hypothesis.
*   **Full vs. Bandit Feedback:** Full feedback means the learner observes the agent's new feature vector after each improvement; bandit feedback means only the final label is observed. *Why needed:* This distinction models different levels of observation on the agent's internal state. *Quick check:* Bandit settin gs typically incur higher sample/mistake complexity due to partial information.
*   **Instance-Optimality:** An algorithm performs best for every specific problem instance (graph, hypothesis class, labeling). *Why needed:* The paper aims for guarantees that are tight for each concrete scenario, not just worst-case over a class. *Quick check:* The proposed algorithms' mistake bounds match the defined dimensions, which are instance-specific.
*   **Best-Response Dynamics:** The agent's strategy of moving to a neighbor that yields a label preferred by their utility function (often a target label). *Why needed:* It defines the endogenous generation of the data stream. *Quick check:* The agent's move must be optimal given their current node and the learner's current hypothesis.

## Architecture Onboarding
The theoretical framework involves these interacting components:
Learner -> Agent -> Improvement Graph -> Feedback Signal

*   **Critical Path:** 1) Learner deploys a hypothesis. 2) Agent, at current feature vector node, evaluates labels under hypothesis and moves (improves) along an outgoing edge to a neighbor that yields a more preferred label, if possible. 3) This move may be observed (full) or not (bandit). 4) The final label for the round is revealed. 5) Learner updates its hypothesis (conceptually, via version space elimination) based on all observed information for the next round.
*   **Design Tradeoffs:** The primary tradeoff is between feedback richness (full vs. bandit) and mistake bound. Another is between graph structure complexity (degree, size) and learnability. The analysis also trades off generality (infinite classes, general graphs) against constructive/algorithmic efficiency (results are often existential).
*   **Failure Signatures:** A failure occurs when the number of mistakes exceeds the instance's corresponding ILdim (or BILdim). This indicates the learner's hypothesis elimination strategy was inefficient or the adversarial sequence exploited a higher "complexity" path in the graph than anticipated. In bandit settings, failure often stems from insufficient exploration of the graph's structure due to limited feedback.
*   **First Three Experiments:**
    1.  Implement the proposed optimal deterministic learner for a small, fixed improvement graph and multiclass problem. Measure mistake counts against the computed Multiclass ILdim across random realizable labelings.
    2.  Compare the empirical mistake rate of the bandit learner versus the full-information learner on the same graph instances, verifying the theoretical gap scales with $\Delta_G \cdot k \log k$.
    3.  Test the sensitivity of the bandit algorithm's performance to an inaccurate estimate of the graph's maximum degree $\Delta_G$.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: Can randomized learners achieve lower expected mistake bounds in online learning with improving agents compared to deterministic ones?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Section 8, Question 1: "Can randomized learners achieve better (expected) mistake bounds?"
- Why unresolved: The paper's analysis is restricted to deterministic learners; no randomized algorithms or lower bounds for the expected case are provided.
- What evidence would resolve it: Either a positive result (a randomized algorithm with provably better expected mistake bound) or a negative result (matching lower bound showing no improvement) would settle the question.

### Open Question 2
- Question: To what extent can the learnability results be extended to the agnostic setting where the label sequence may not be realizable by any hypothesis in the class?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Section 8, Question 2: "In what way can our results be extended to the agnostic setting?"
- Why unresolved: The paper assumes realizability throughout; the agnostic case introduces adversarial label noise that may break the combinatorial dimension characterizations.
- What evidence would resolve it: Development of an analog of the Improvement Littlestone dimension for agnostic learning, perhaps with a surrogate loss or a refined notion that yields optimal regret bounds.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What is the trade-off between minimizing classification error and minimizing unnecessary agent improvements (burden) when labels are already achievable without improvement?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Section 8, Question 3: "It would be interesting to see what is the trade-off between achieving low classification error and minimizing the unnecessary burden."
- Why unresolved: The objective in this work is purely mistake count; it ignores the social cost of agents making improvements that could have been avoided by a better classifier.
- What evidence would resolve it: Formalizing a cost model that accounts for both prediction mistakes and improvement costs, and deriving Pareto-optimal or multi-objective guarantees.

### Open Question 4
- Question: How much prior knowledge about the improvement graph is necessary for learnability? For example, can we learn with only an oracle that checks adjacency or a coarse degree estimate?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Section 8, Question 4: "What can we achieve if we relax this assumption to only provide the learner with some limited prior knowledge about the agent’s improvement graph?"
- Why unresolved: The current algorithms assume full knowledge of the graph (edges and costs). It is unknown whether such complete knowledge is essential or if weaker assumptions suffice.
- What evidence would resolve it: Constructing learners that succeed with minimal graph information (e.g., membership queries only) or proving impossibility results when the graph is only partially known.

### Open Question 5
- Question: Is online learning possible when agents cannot directly observe the learner's current classifier but only have access to historical predictions? This models scenarios where the decision rule is not fully transparent.
- Basis in paper: [explicit] Section 8, Question 5: "Is it possible to learn when the agent is only allowed to access past classifiers?"
- Why unresolved: The paper's model assumes agents best-respond to the immediately deployed hypothesis. If agents only see past classifiers, the feedback loop changes and may cause non-stationarity.
- What evidence would resolve it: Designing algorithms that ensure learning despite delayed or partial exposure, perhaps via no-regret dynamics or by maintaining a stable enough policy.

## Limitations
*   The improvement graph model abstracts real-world feature dependencies, potentially missing complex, non-graphical interactions.
*   Analysis is limited to deterministic learners; the performance of randomized strategies and the agnostic setting are open.
*   The presented instance-optimal learners are existential; their computational feasibility and efficient approximations are not addressed.
*   The bandit feedback price (upper bound $O(\Delta_G \cdot k \log k)$) lacks a matching lower bound, so its tightness is unknown.
*   There is no empirical evaluation to validate the theoretical tightness of bounds or the practicality of the model.

## Confidence
*   **High Confidence:** The ILdim family (ILdim, Multiclass ILdim, WILdim) provides a tight characterization of optimal mistake bounds for deterministic learners in the full-feedback, realizable setting. (Rigorous proofs provided).
*   **Medium Confidence:** The $O(\Delta_G \cdot k \log k)$ bound on the price of bandit feedback. (It is a non-trivial upper bound, but no lower bound is proven to establish optimality).
*   **Medium Confidence:** The generalization of the framework to infinite hypothesis classes and arbitrary, acyclic improvement graphs. (The treatment is non-constructive and relies on abstract combinatorial arguments).

## Next Checks
