# Gender Fairness in Audio Deepfake Detection: Performance and Disparity Analysis

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.09007
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09007
- **Reference count**: 38
- **Primary result**: Audio deepfake detection systems can exhibit hidden gender biases that are not revealed by standard metrics like EER; fairness metrics expose significant disparities even when EER gaps are small.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates gender fairness in audio deepfake detection using the ASVspoof5 dataset. The authors evaluate several feature representations (LogSpec, CQT, Wav2vec, WavLM) and models (ResNet-18, AASIST) with both standard performance (EER) and five group fairness metrics (Statistical Parity, Equal Opportunity, Equality of Odds, Predictive Parity, Treatment Equality). They find that while EER differences between male and female speakers are often minimal, fairness disparities can be substantial, varying by feature and architecture. For example, CQT yields a tiny EER gap (0.05%) but large bias in Equal Opportunity (Δ=0.114) and Treatment Equality (Δ=1.216). AASIST shows the most balanced error distribution, albeit with a consistent male-favoring direction. WavLM achieves the best EER and is fairer than Wav2vec. Notably, Predictive Parity consistently favors males across all systems. The study concludes that EER alone is insufficient for fairness evaluation and highlights the need for multi-metric audits.

## Method Summary
The study uses the ASVspoof5 dataset with predefined train/dev/eval splits and speaker gender labels. Audio is preprocessed to 16 kHz mono and normalized to 4-second segments. Four feature representations are extracted: Log-Spectrogram (LogSpec), Constant-Q Transform (CQT), Wav2vec 2.0 embeddings, and WavLM embeddings, each yielding a 2D time-frequency/feature map. A shared ResNet-18 classifier (1 input channel, 2 output units) is trained on each feature set using AdamW (lr=3e-5, weight decay=1e-4) and class-weighted cross-entropy loss, with early stopping on dev loss. The AASIST model is used as provided. For evaluation, a single operating threshold is derived from the dev set at the EER point and then applied uniformly to the evaluation set. Performance is measured via EER computed separately for female, male, and all samples. Fairness is assessed by computing the five group fairness metrics for each gender subgroup and taking the absolute difference (Δ). Statistical significance is tested with two-proportion z-tests followed by Holm-Bonferroni correction. This protocol ensures that any observed disparities reflect model bias rather than threshold selection differences.

## Key Results
- Small EER gaps (e.g., CQT: female 42.99% vs. male 42.94%) coexist with large fairness disparities: CQT shows Δ=0.114 in Equal Opportunity and Δ=1.216 in Treatment Equality.
- AASIST is the most balanced system, with the smallest overall fairness gaps but a consistent male-favoring bias across all metrics.
- WavLM achieves the best EER (22.28% female, 21.65% male) and is fairer than Wav2vec; Predictive Parity favors males in every system.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Feature Representation Drives Bias
The choice of feature extraction systematically influences the direction and magnitude of gender bias. Different representations (e.g., CQT’s logarithmically spaced frequency bins vs. WavLM’s contextual embeddings) encode distinct acoustic patterns that interact with gender-linked vocal characteristics (e.g., pitch ranges). This interaction leads the classifier to learn gender-correlated artifacts that are irrelevant to spoof detection, causing disparate error distributions even when aggregate EER is similar. Evidence: CQT exhibits the largest cumulative disparity, while WavLM is fairer than Wav2vec. Break condition: features explicitly regularized to remove gender-correlated information or gender-agnostic synthetic artifacts.

### Mechanism 2: Architecture Moderates Fairness Consistency
Model architectural decisions affect consistency across fairness metrics. AASIST’s spectro-temporal graph attention may process relationships in ways less susceptible to overfitting on gender-specific noise, yielding more uniform error type distributions across groups despite a persistent directional (male-favoring) bias. In contrast, the generic ResNet-18 applied to different feature maps amplifies feature-specific biases. Evidence: AASIST is the only system consistently male-favoring across all five metrics, yet its disparities remain the smallest overall. Break condition: architectural shifts that enforce group invariance or use adversarial debiasing.

### Mechanism 3: EER Masks Subgroup Error-Type Disparities
EER aggregates scores across all samples and obscures how false positives and false negatives are partitioned between demographic groups. A model can have nearly identical EERs for two groups but vastly different balances of error types (e.g., Treatment Equality ratio), leading to unfair outcomes in deployment. Evidence: CQT’s near-zero EER gap contrasts sharply with its large Δ in Equal Opportunity and Treatment Equality; the study explicitly states that EER alone does not capture group disparities. Break condition: deployment contexts requiring subgroup-specific metrics or perfectly balanced underlying data.

## Foundational Learning
- **Group Fairness Metrics**  
  *Why needed*: Quantify how errors are distributed across genders beyond aggregate accuracy, revealing systematic bias. The paper’s core finding relies on divergent values across these metrics.  
  *Quick check*: What does a large positive difference in Treatment Equality (Δ = FP/FN ratio) between female and male groups indicate about the model’s error asymmetry?

- **Audio Feature Representations**  
  *Why needed*: The primary causal mechanism links fairness disparities to the choice of input representation. Understanding the distinction between CQT (pitch-focused, logarithmic frequency) and WavLM (self-supervised contextual) is key to interpreting bias patterns.  
  *Quick check*: How might the pitch-focused nature of CQT interact differently with male vs. female voice characteristics compared to a model like WavLM?

- **Classification Thresholding and Score Calibration**  
  *Why needed*: Fairness metrics are computed at a single operating point (dev-set EER threshold). Using the same fixed threshold for all groups isolates feature/model bias from threshold calibration bias.  
  *Quick check*: Why is it methodologically important to use the same development-set-derived threshold for evaluating all gender subgroups?

- **Statistical Significance Testing (z-test, Holm-Bonferroni)**  
  *Why needed*: The paper asserts that observed disparities are systematic, not random. Multiple testing correction prevents false positives given the many metric comparisons.  
  *Quick check*: If a fairness metric difference has a p-value < 0.05 after Holm-Bonferroni correction, what does that imply about the robustness of the observed disparity?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
ASVspoof5 dataset -> Preprocessing (16 kHz mono, 4-sec normalization) -> Feature Extraction (LogSpec, CQT, Wav2vec, WavLM) -> Classifier (ResNet-18 / AASIST) -> Training (AdamW, class-weighted CE, early stopping) -> Dev-set EER thresholding -> Evaluation (subgroup confusion matrices, EER, fairness metrics) -> Statistical tests (z-test, Holm-Bonferroni).

**Critical path**  
1. Load train/dev/eval splits with gender labels.  
2. Extract fixed features for all samples.  
3. Train model on training set, monitor dev loss for checkpointing.  
4. From dev set scores, compute EER and derive single operating threshold.  
5. Apply threshold to eval set scores, partition by gender, compute confusion matrices.  
6. Calculate EER and five fairness metrics per gender, then Δ.  
7. Perform two-proportion z-tests on metric differences, apply Holm correction.

**Design tradeoffs**  
- *Fixed threshold vs. group-specific thresholds*: Using a single threshold isolates model bias but may not reflect real-world deployment where thresholds could be optimized per group.  
- *ResNet-18 as a control*: Using the same architecture across features controls for architectural bias but may underfit optimal feature representations, potentially exaggerating fairness differences.  
- *Fairness metric selection*: The five metrics capture distinct notions; a model can be fair on one metric and unfair on another, complicating overall assessment.

**Failure signatures**  
- High Treatment Equality Δ (e.g., CQT: 1.216) indicates severe imbalance in FP/FN ratio between genders—one group suffers a fundamentally different error type.  
- Significant Δ in Equal Opportunity while EER gap is small signals hidden bias: the model fails to detect true positives (spoofs) equally well across genders.  
- Consistent male-favoring Predictive Parity across all models suggests a dataset-level score distribution or calibration issue.

**First 3 experiments for replication**  
1. *Baseline replication*: Train ResNet-18 with LogSpec features on ASVspoof5. Compute EER on eval set for all, female, male. Expect ~38.7% EER and small gender gap (~0.4%).  
2. *Fairness metric audit*: Using the LogSpec model, compute all five fairness metrics at the EER threshold. Validate the reported near-zero Δ for Statistical Parity, Equal Opportunity, Equality of Odds.  
3. *Feature comparison stress test*: Train ResNet-18 with CQT features. Compute EER and fairness metrics. Reproduce the paradoxical result: a tiny EER gap (~0.05%) alongside large Equal Opportunity Δ (0.114) and Treatment Equality Δ (1.216).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. *What are the underlying causes of gender bias in audio deepfake detection systems?* The study measures disparities but does not isolate whether they originate from data distribution, feature extraction, or model decisions.  
2. *Why does Predictive Parity consistently favor male speakers across all tested models and features?* The authors note a potential dataset-level effect but provide no explanation; calibration or base rate issues may be involved.  
3. *How can we design effective fairness-aware training interventions that simultaneously reduce gender disparity and maintain low EER?* Proposed methods (fairness-aware loss, subgroup reweighting, adversarial debiasing, feature regularization) remain unevaluated.  
4. *Are the observed gender biases consistent across different audio deepfake datasets and attack types?* The study only uses ASVspoof5; generalization to other benchmarks (e.g., ASVspoof 2019/2021, multilingual data) is unknown.

## Limitations
- The study does not identify the exact causal pathway linking feature representations to gender bias, only correlational evidence (e.g., CQT’s high disparity).  
- Unreported implementation details (e.g., ResNet-18 input layer configuration, feature tensor dimensions, exact EER thresholding method) block faithful replication and may affect metric values.  
- The analysis uses a single dataset (ASVspoof5); bias patterns may differ with other data distributions, languages, or attack generation techniques.

## Next Checks

2. *Threshold protocol confirmation*: Re-derive the EER operating threshold solely from the combined dev-set scores, then apply that single threshold to all gender subgroups to compute fairness metrics. Avoid per-group threshold optimization.  
3. *Fairness metric implementation validation*: Recompute the five fairness metrics for at least one system (e.g., CQT-ResNet) using a standardized library like `fairlearn` or `aif360` to ensure alignment with the paper’s calculations.
