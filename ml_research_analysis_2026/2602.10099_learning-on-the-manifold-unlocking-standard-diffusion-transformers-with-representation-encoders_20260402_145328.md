# Learning on the Manifold: Unlocking Standard Diffusion Transformers with Representation Encoders

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.10099
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10099
- **Reference count**: 17
- **Primary result**: Standard diffusion transformers fail to converge on hyperspherical representation spaces due to geometric interference; the proposed Riemannian Flow Matching with Jacobi Regularization (RJF) keeps trajectories on manifold geodesics and corrects curvature-induced error propagation, enabling convergence without width scaling and achieving FID 3.37 (with guidance) on DiT-B using DINOv2-B features.

## Executive Summary
Diffusion transformers operating on features from representation encoders (e.g., DINOv2) struggle to train because these features lie on a hypersphere, and Euclidean linear interpolation (used in standard flow matching) cuts through the interior, creating off-manifold trajectories. This geometric interference forces the model to learn invalid velocity fields. The authors propose Riemannian Flow Matching with Jacobi Regularization (RJF), which replaces linear interpolation with spherical linear interpolation (SLERP) to stay on geodesics and weights the loss by a curvature-dependent Jacobi field term to correct error propagation. As a result, standard DiT architectures converge without requiring width scaling, attaining state-of-the-art results on ImageNet: FID 3.37 (with guidance) and 4.95 (without) with DiT-B (131M parameters), and FID 3.62 with DiT-XL (677M) without guidance after only 80 epochs. RJF also generalizes to other encoders (SigLIP, MAE), dramatically improving FID from >50 to ~10-20.

## Method Summary
RJF trains a diffusion transformer (LightningDiT) to generate samples on the hyperspherical manifold defined by frozen encoder features. It enforces manifold constraints via three components: (1) **SLERP**: constructs conditional paths *xₜ* via spherical linear interpolation, ensuring the trajectory follows the geodesic between data and noise and keeping all points on the sphere; (2) **tangent projection**: the predicted velocity is projected onto the tangent space at *xₜ* to eliminate radial components; (3) **Jacobi regularization**: the loss is weighted by *λ(t, Ω) = sinc²((1-t)Ω)*, where *Ω* is the geodesic distance, to account for curvature-induced error magnification near the noise endpoint. Training minimizes the Riemannian flow matching objective with Adam, gradient clipping, and EMA. Sampling integrates the learned velocity field using geodesic updates and finally rescales the output norm (e.g., to ~45 for DINOv2-B) to match the decoder’s training distribution.

## Key Results
- Standard DiT-B (131M) with DINOv2-B features converges under RJF, achieving FID 3.37 (with guidance) and 4.95 (without) after 200 epochs; prior methods fail to converge.
- Gains scale to larger models: DiT-XL (677M) reaches FID 3.62 without guidance in just 80 epochs.
- RJF generalizes to other hyperspherical encoders (SigLIP, MAE), improving FID drastically from >50 to ~10-20.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Geometric Interference from Euclidean Chords**  
Standard Euclidean flow matching interpolates linearly (chords) between data *x* and noise *ϵ*. On a hypersphere (e.g., DINOv2 features), the chord path collapses the norm at intermediate points (t=0.5), venturing into the low-density interior. The resulting MSE loss penalizes radial errors (invalid magnitude changes) and forces the model to learn undefined off-manifold velocity fields, causing divergence. Theoretically, the norm collapse is ∥xₜ∥² ≈ (1-t)²∥x∥² + t²∥ϵ∥². Ablations show that masking radial loss components restores convergence, confirming the interference is geometric.

**Mechanism 2: SLERP Keeps Trajectories on Geodesics**  
SLERP replaces linear interpolation with great-circle arcs, guaranteeing ∥xₜ∥ = 1 for all *t*. The target velocity derived from differentiating SLERP lies entirely in the tangent space *T_{xₜ}M* and is orthogonal to *xₜ*. This eliminates radial interference entirely. Ablations demonstrate that adding SLERP (with tangent projection) reduces FID from 24.32 to 7.06, proving the geodesic path is essential.

**Mechanism 3: Jacobi Regularization Corrects Curvature Error Propagation**  
On a positively curved sphere, small velocity errors integrate non-uniformly: errors near the noise endpoint (*t*≈1) are magnified because geodesics converge. The Jacobi field solution yields a scaling factor *λ(t, Ω) = sinc²((1-t)Ω)* that weights the loss by expected endpoint error amplification. Weighting by *λ* forces the model to prioritize accuracy at late times, where it matters most. Adding Jacobi to SLERP further improves FID (7.06 → 6.77), and the combination ultimately yields ~3.4.

## Foundational Learning
- **Hyperspherical Manifolds & LayerNorm Geometry**: Pre-trained vision encoders (e.g., DINOv2) apply LayerNorm to patch features, constraining them to near-zero radial variance and placing them on a hypersphere. Recognizing this geometry explains why Euclidean chords fail.

- **Geodesics vs. Euclidean Chords**: On a sphere, the shortest path between two points is the great-circle arc (geodesic), not the straight line through the interior. The latter is shorter in Euclidean distance but leaves the manifold.  
  *Quick check*: For two points on a sphere, which path is longer: the chord or the geodesic? Which stays on the manifold?  
- **Tangent Space Parameterization of Flows**: A velocity field staying on the manifold must be tangent at every point, i.e., orthogonal to the position vector. This constraint ensures the flow does not depart from the manifold.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Frozen Encoder (DINOv2) → Unit-normal Projection → SLERP (geodesic *xₜ*) → Target Tangent Velocity *uₜ* → DiT (predicts *v_θ*) → Tangent Projection (*v_proj*) → Jacobi-weighted MSE Loss → Backprop

**Critical path**:  
1. Normalize data *x* and noise *ϵ* to unit sphere.  
2. Compute geodesic distance *Ω = arccos(x·ϵ)* and generate *xₜ* via SLERP.  
3. Compute target tangent velocity *uₜ = d(SLERP)/dt*.  
4. Feed (*xₜ*, *t*, optional class label) to DiT to obtain *v_θ*.  
5. Project *v_θ* to tangent space: *v_proj = v_θ - (v_θ·xₜ) xₜ*.  
6. Compute weight *λ(t, Ω) = [sin((1-t)Ω)/((1-t)Ω)]²* and loss *L = λ ||v_proj - uₜ||²*.  
7. Optimize with Adam, clip gradients (norm 1.0), apply EMA (0.9995).  
8. Sampling: integrate using geodesic updates (exponential map) and rescale final norm by *R* (≈45).

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- SLERP uses trigonometric operations and may be unstable when *Ω≈0*; but it is geometrically correct.  
- Jacobi weighting adds negligible cost and biases learning toward late-time precision, improving final sample fidelity.  
- Tangent projection is mandatory; omitting it reintroduces radial interference.  
- Inference radius scaling (*R*) is a heuristic to match the decoder’s training distribution; its optimal value is dataset-dependent.

**Failure signatures**:  
- Training does not converge: verify SLERP implementation and tangent projection.  
- Poor FID despite training: check Jacobi weight computation (use *(1-t)Ω*, not *tΩ*) and confirm features are truly hyperspherical.  
- Numerical NaNs: guard against division by zero in SLERP/Jacobi for small *Ω*.  
- Blurry images: sweep inference radius *R* (e.g., 20–60) to find the best FID.

**First 3 experiments**:  
1. Single‑image overfit: train on one ImageNet image’s DINOv2 features. Euclidean FM should fail; RJF should converge quickly and reconstruct the image.  
2. Radial vs. angular loss decomposition: train a narrow model (width < token dim) with full loss vs. masked radial loss. The masked version should converge, confirming geometric interference.  
3. SLERP ablation: train with RJF but replace SLERP with linear interpolation while keeping tangent projection and Jacobi weighting. FID should worsen significantly compared to full RJF.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Why does scaling the feature norm at inference improve quality?** Scaling the output radius from DINOv2’s natural ~27.7 to ~45 reduces FID, but the reason is speculative. Understanding the decoder’s norm dependence could yield a more principled approach.  
2. **Can RJF extend to non‑hyperspherical manifolds?** The Jacobi regularization is derived for constant positive curvature (sphere). Its applicability to hyperbolic spaces, tori, or arbitrary manifolds is unknown.  
3. **How do time shift (from RAE) and Jacobi weighting interact?** Both adjust the time distribution of the loss, but their combined effect—whether synergistic or redundant—is not studied.

## Limitations
- Assumes perfectly hyperspherical features; slight radial variance in real encoders might degrade performance.  
- Jacobi regularization is sphere‑specific; its generalization to other curved manifolds is uncertain.  
- Optimal inference radius (*R*) is decoder‑specific and determined empirically; lacking theoretical guidance.

## Confidence
- **Geometric interference (chord problem)**: High – strong theory and ablations.  
- **RJF convergence and FID gains**: High – consistent across DiT‑B/XL and multiple encoders.  
- **Generality to other encoders**: Medium – tested on SigLIP and MAE but manifold properties not fully verified.  
- **Jacobi regularization contribution**: Medium – gains are modest; may be secondary to SLERP.  
- **No width scaling needed**: High – direct comparison with wider baselines confirms.

## Next Checks
1. **Radial variance robustness test**: Inject controlled radial noise into DINOv2 features and measure RJF’s FID degradation to quantify tolerance to manifold deviations.  
2. **Curvature reweighting ablation**: Vary effective curvature (by scaling feature norms) and compare Jacobi weighting against fixed temporal weighting to isolate curvature‑specific effects.  
3. **Non‑hyperspherical encoder trial**: Apply RJF to an encoder that does not produce norm‑constrained features (e.g., ResNet‑50 pool5) and assess convergence and final FID.
