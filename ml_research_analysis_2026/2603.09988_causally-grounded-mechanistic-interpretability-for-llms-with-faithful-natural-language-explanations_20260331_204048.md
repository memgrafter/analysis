# Causally Grounded Mechanistic Interpretability for LLMs with Faithful Natural-Language Explanations

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.09988  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09988  
- **Reference count:** 16  
- **Primary result:** A pipeline that converts mechanistic circuit analysis into faithful natural-language explanations, evaluated on IOI with GPT-2 Small (124M). Six heads account for 61.4% of logit difference; LLM-generated explanations achieve 99% quality versus 60% for templates (+66% improvement), but faithfulness shows high sufficiency (100%) and low comprehensiveness (22%).

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles a central challenge in mechanistic interpretability: translating quantitative circuit analyses into natural-language explanations that remain faithful to the model’s actual causal mechanisms. The authors propose a pipeline that (1) identifies causally important attention heads using activation patching (effect recovery), (2) generates explanations via templates or LLM prompting, and (3) evaluates faithfulness by adapting token-level ERASER metrics (sufficiency, comprehensiveness) to the circuit level. Tested on the Indirect Object Identification (IOI) task with GPT‑2 Small, the method finds that a fixed set of six heads explains 61.4% of the logit difference. LLM‑based explanations significantly outperform template versions in quality, yet both exhibit a large gap between sufficiency and comprehensiveness, revealing robust distributed backup mechanisms. The work also shows no correlation between model confidence and explanation faithfulness, highlighting the need for reporting faithfulness metrics alongside explanations.

## Method Summary
The pipeline consists of four stages: (1) **Circuit identification** uses corruption patching (swapping names in IOI prompts) to compute each head’s *effect recovery*—the proportion of clean logit difference recovered when its activations are patched into a corrupted run. Heads are ranked globally by average effect and the top‑k (six) are selected as the circuit. (2) **Explanation generation** formats these heads and their statistics either via fixed templates or by prompting an LLM to produce 1–2 sentences. (3) **Faithfulness evaluation** adapts ERASER metrics: *Sufficiency* sums the cited heads’ individual contributions relative to the clean logit difference; *Comprehensiveness* measures the drop in logit difference when the cited heads are ablated from a clean run. (4) **Quality scoring** uses rule‑based criteria (e.g., e‑SNLI) or an LLM to judge clarity, correctness, etc. The pipeline is run over 50 IOI prompts, and metrics are aggregated.

## Key Results
- Six attention heads account for 61.4% of the logit difference on the IOI task.
- LLM‑generated explanations achieve 99% quality versus 60% for templates (+66% improvement).
- Faithfulness metrics show perfect sufficiency (100%) but low comprehensiveness (22%), indicating the identified heads are sufficient but not necessary due to backup mechanisms.
- Model confidence correlates negligibly with comprehensiveness (r = 0.009).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
*Claim:* The pipeline can generate causally faithful natural‑language explanations by grounding them in activation‑patching results rather than raw attention.  
*Mechanism:* First, causally important heads are identified via effect recovery (activation patching against corrupted prompts). Second, these mechanistic signals (head names, contribution percentages) are formatted into explanations via templates or LLM prompting. Faithfulness is then evaluated by adapting token‑level ERASER metrics (sufficiency/comprehensiveness) to the circuit level, measuring how well the cited heads account for/are necessary for the prediction.  
*Core assumption:* Activation patching isolates causally relevant components; translating these quantitative attributions into qualitative descriptions preserves fidelity if the explanation accurately lists and contextualizes the cited components.  
*Evidence anchors:*  
– Abstract: “identifying causally important attention heads via activation patching… generate explanations… evaluating faithfulness using adapted ERASER metrics”  
– Section 3.2: “measure each head’s causal importance via effect recovery”  
– Corpus: Weak direct support; neighboring papers focus on different aspects.

### Mechanism 2
*Claim:* Adapted ERASER metrics (sufficiency/comprehensiveness) provide a meaningful faithfulness signal for circuit‑level explanations.  
*Mechanism:* Sufficiency (Eq. 2) sums the contributions of cited heads relative to clean logit difference; comprehensiveness (Eq. 3) measures prediction change when cited heads are ablated. A faithful explanation should score high on both. The F1 harmonic mean balances them.  
*Core assumption:* Circuit contributions are additive enough that summing individual head effects approximates the collective causal effect of the cited set. Ablating the cited set isolates their necessity.  
*Evidence anchors:*  
– Section 3.4: Full definitions of Suff and Comp equations.  
– Section 5.2: Results show 100% sufficiency but 22% comprehensiveness.  
– Corpus: No direct anchor; neighboring papers use different faithfulness notions.

### Mechanism 3
*Claim:* The sufficiency–comprehensiveness gap (100% vs. 22%) is a revealing property of the model’s distributed computation, not a failure of the explanation method.  
*Mechanism:* High sufficiency means the six cited heads can *recover* the behavior when patched into a corrupted run. Low comprehensiveness means ablating them from a clean run does not destroy the behavior, because other heads (backups) take over. This indicates robust, redundant circuit design.  
*Core assumption:* The model implements the IOI task with a primary circuit plus distributed fallbacks; the top‑6 heads by average effect are the primary path but not the only one.  
*Evidence anchors:*  
– Abstract: “100% sufficiency but only 22% comprehensiveness, revealing distributed backup mechanisms.”  
– Section 5.3: Failure analysis cites “Distributed Computation” and “Redundant Head Activity” as categories.  
– Corpus: No direct anchor; related work notes redundancy but does not quantify it via this metric pair.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Activation Patching (Causal Mediation Analysis)**  
  *Why needed:* It isolates which components causally influence the IOI prediction; without it, one might only observe correlation.  
  *Quick check:* If patching head *h*’s activations from a clean prompt into a corrupted prompt recovers the correct IOI logit difference, what does that imply about *h*’s role?

- **Concept: ERASER Metrics (Sufficiency/Comprehensiveness)**  
  *Why needed:* They provide a standardized, quantitative way to evaluate whether an explanation is *faithful* to the underlying mechanism, not just plausible. Adapted from token rationales to circuit components.  
  *Quick check:* If an explanation’s cited heads have 100% sufficiency but 20% comprehensiveness, what does that say about the model’s reliance on those heads?

- **Concept: Mechanistic Circuit (e.g., IOI circuit)**  
  *Why needed:* Provides the ground‑truth structure (Name Mover, S‑Inhibition heads) against which the automatic pipeline’s output can be compared. Understanding this prior work is essential to interpret the paper’s findings.  
  *Quick check:* In the standard IOI circuit, what is the hypothesized role of an “S‑Inhibition head”?

## Architecture Onboarding
Circuit Identification → Explanation Generator → Faithfulness Evaluator → Quality Scorer

**Critical path:**  
1. Select prompt set (50 IOI prompts).  
2. Run circuit ID: compute effect recovery for all heads; select top‑k (6).  
3. Generate explanation (template or LLM) using head list and statistics.  
4. Compute faithfulness metrics (sufficiency, comprehensiveness) via patching/ablation on the cited head set.  
5. Score explanation quality (rule‑based or LLM).  
6. Aggregate & analyze (e.g., failure categories).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Fixed vs. Adaptive Circuit:* Fixed global top‑6 is simple but may miss instance‑specific important heads; adaptive per‑prompt selection would be more precise but less stable.  
- *Template vs. LLM Generator:* Templates are cheap/consistent but generic; LLM generators are expensive/stochastic but produce nuanced explanations (66% quality gain).  
- *Global vs. Local Attributions:* Dataset‑average head importance may not reflect per‑prompt critical heads, contributing to the comprehensiveness gap.

**Failure signatures:**  
1. Low comprehensiveness (< 15%): Indicates the cited circuit is not necessary; likely distributed computation or missing instance‑specific heads.  
2. High sufficiency + low comprehensiveness: Canonical “backup mechanisms” signature; the circuit can recover behavior but is not relied upon exclusively.  
3. LLM explanation mentions heads not in circuit: May indicate hallucination or that circuit ID missed contextually important heads.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Replicate circuit identification: On 50 IOI prompts, compute effect recovery for all 144 heads. Verify top‑6 heads match Table 1 and account for ~61% of logit difference.  
2. Compare explanation methods: For fixed top‑6 circuit, generate 50 template and 50 LLM explanations. Score quality via the 5 criteria. Expect LLM ~99%, template ~60%. Faithfulness metrics should be identical since cited heads are the same.  
3. Probe the comprehensiveness gap: For prompts with very low comprehensiveness (< 10%), inspect per‑head contributions for that specific prompt. Check whether a different head (e.g., L10H10) has high contribution but is not in the global top‑6.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Does the pipeline generalize beyond the IOI task to other mechanistic circuits (e.g., Greater‑Than, induction heads) and larger models (e.g., GPT‑2 Medium/Large)?  
2. Are the LLM‑generated explanations actually useful and comprehensible to human users for understanding model behavior or debugging errors?  
3. Can adaptive, per‑instance circuit selection improve comprehensiveness and reduce explanation‑mechanism divergence compared to fixed global circuits?  
4. How can the three identified failure categories (distributed computation, missing cited heads, redundant activity) be mitigated to produce more comprehensive and faithful explanations?  
5. What alternative metrics or signals can reliably indicate explanation quality to end‑users, given that model confidence shows no correlation with faithfulness?

## Limitations
- Activation patching may not yield a stable, minimal circuit; head contributions can be prompt‑dependent.  
- Adapted ERASER metrics assume additive head effects; strong non‑additivity would break sufficiency.  
- Global top‑6 selection may miss instance‑specific heads, causing low comprehensiveness and misattributed backup.  
- Explanation quality evaluation uses an unspecified LLM; generalizability is uncertain.  
- The near‑zero confidence‑faithfulness correlation could be task‑specific; broader validity unknown.

## Confidence
- **Mechanism 1 (causal faithfulness via patching):** Medium  
- **Mechanism 2 (ERASER metrics):** Medium  
- **Mechanism 3 (sufficiency–comprehensiveness gap reveals backup):** Medium  

## Next Checks
1. **Per‑prompt circuit analysis:** Compute effect recovery for all heads on each of the 50 IOI prompts. Measure overlap of top‑6 heads across prompts and correlate with per‑prompt comprehensiveness. Tests whether low scores stem from missing instance‑specific heads.  
2. **Additivity test:** Ablate the six heads individually and sum their effects on the logit difference; compare to the effect of ablating all six jointly. Significant deviation indicates non‑additivity, threatening the sufficiency metric.  
3. **Cross‑task replication:** Re‑run the pipeline on at least two other mechanistic circuits (e.g., subject‑verb number agreement) with at least two LLM generators (e.g., GPT‑4, Claude). Verifies whether LLM‑based explanations consistently outperform templates and whether the faithfulness gap persists.
