# ACES: Accent Subspaces for Coupling, Explanations, and Stress-Testing in Automatic Speech Recognition

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.03359
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03359
- **Reference count:** 28
- **Primary result:** Imperceptible perturbations aligned with an extracted accent subspace increase WER disparity by 50% (from 21.3 to 31.8 percentage points), and partially removing that subspace degrades both overall WER and disparity, indicating deep entanglement between accent encoding and recognition-critical features.

## Executive Summary
Accent-based performance disparities in automatic speech recognition (ASR) remain poorly understood. This paper introduces ACES, a framework that extracts linear accent-discriminative subspaces from model representations and uses them as audit instruments. ACES comprises three stages: extracting a discriminative subspace via ridge probing, launching subspace-constrained adversarial attacks to amplify disparity, and performing projection-based removal tests to assess feature entanglement. Evaluated on Wav2Vec2-base over seven English accents from Common Voice, accent-subspace attacks widen the WER gap by nearly 50% while random-subspace controls do not. Moreover, attenuating the accent subspace harms recognition accuracy, suggesting that accent and phonetic information are deeply intertwined. The study positions accent subspaces as powerful tools for fairness auditing but cautions against simple erasure.

## Method Summary
ACES operates in three stages. First, it learns a linear subspace \(U\) that captures accent-discriminative directions from mean-pooled hidden states of a pretrained ASR encoder (Wav2Vec2-base). A ridge probe trained to predict accent labels on held-out data yields the projection matrix; the chosen layer (3) and subspace dimension (8) balance accuracy and stability. Second, it performs untargeted Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) attacks on the waveform with an L2 budget (\(\varepsilon=0.01\)), adding a penalty \(\beta\|\Pi_U(\Delta h)\|^2\) to drive representation shifts along \(U\). The resulting perturbations are imperceptible (~60 dB SNR) but disproportionately increase WER for high-baseline accents, amplifying disparity. A coupling metric \(m(x)=\|\Pi_U(\Delta h)\|\) verifies alignment. Third, a forward hook applies \(e' = e - \alpha UU^\top e\) (\(\alpha=0.5\)) to partially remove the subspace at inference; the resulting degradation in WER and increased disparity indicate that accent-related features are entangled with those needed for accurate recognition.

## Key Results
- **Disparity amplification:** Accent-subspace attacks increased WER disparity by 50% (from 21.3 to 31.8 percentage points), significantly exceeding random-subspace and unconstrained attacks.
- **Specificity control:** Using a permuted-label subspace eliminated the disparity gap, confirming the effect tied to genuine accent structure.
- **Entanglement evidence:** Partial removal of the accent subspace worsened overall WER (by +0.3 pp) and disparity (+0.4 pp), with greater harm to accents that already had higher baseline WER.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Subspace Extraction as a Discriminative Instrument
**Claim:** Accent-discriminative information in Wav2Vec2-base representations is approximately captured by a low-dimensional linear subspace in early layers.  
**Mechanism:** A ridge probe learns a projection matrix \(U\) from mean-pooled encoder hidden states that maximizes accent classification accuracy on hold-out data. The column span of \(U\) forms the accent subspace; validity is checked via probe accuracy and stability (principal angles across data splits).  
**Core assumption:** Accent-relevant variance is separable via a linear transformation and is more salient in mid-early layers (here layer 3) before linguistic abstractions dominate.  
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "extracts linear accent-discriminative subspaces"; [section 3.1] details of probing and validation; [corpus] related accent adaptation works indirectly support.  
**Break condition:** If accent information is fundamentally nonlinear or no low-rank subspace achieves validation accuracy above chance, or if subspaces are highly unstable (large principal angles), extraction fails.

### Mechanism 2: Subspace-Constrained Adversarial Amplification of Disparity
**Claim:** Imperceptible perturbations constrained to the accent subspace disproportionately degrade ASR performance for high-baseline-WER accents, widening the WER disparity gap.  
**Mechanism:** During PGD, the objective adds a term \(\beta \|\Pi_U(\Delta h)\|^2\) to encourage representation shifts along \(U\), while keeping waveform perturbation \(\delta\) within an L2 budget (\(\varepsilon = 0.01\), ~60 dB SNR). The coupling metric \(m(x) = \|\Pi_U(\Delta h)\|\) confirms preferential alignment.  
**Core assumption:** The model has directional vulnerabilities along accent-sensitive dimensions; forcing perturbations there activates these more for accents already struggling with phonetic decoding.  
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "imperceptible perturbations (~60 dB SNR) along the accent subspace amplify the WER disparity gap by nearly 50%"; [section 4.2.2] Table 2 and Figure 3 show larger absolute WER increases for high-baseline accents and a consistent per-accent gap over random-subspace; [corpus] related robustness work does not test subspace-constrained attacks.  
**Break condition:** If the accent subspace does not confer a consistent per-accent advantage over random-subspace controls (mean gap ~0), or if coupling \(m(x)\) is not significantly higher for accent-subspace attacks, the perturbation is not preferentially engaging accent-sensitive directions.

### Mechanism 3: Project-Out Failure as Evidence of Deep Entanglement
**Claim:** Attenuating the accent subspace at inference degrades overall WER and often increases or leaves unchanged disparity, indicating that accent-discriminative and recognition-critical features share directions.  
**Mechanism:** A forward hook applies \(e' = e - \alpha UU^\top e\) (\(\alpha=0.5\)) to mean-pooled embeddings. Comparing WER/disparity with/without this projection tests whether removing accent directions harms recognition.  
**Core assumption:** If accent and phonetic features are orthogonal, project-out should reduce disparity with minimal WER increase; if entangled, removal blurs phonetic boundaries.  
**Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "partially removing the accent subspace degraded both overall WER and disparity"; [section 4.2.3] Table 3 shows clean WER increases and disparity increases, with degradation concentrated on high-WER accents; [corpus] concept erasure methods aim for separation, ACES observes the opposite.  
**Break condition:** If project-out consistently reduces disparity with negligible WER increase, accent and recognition features are separable, and linear erasure could be viable.

## Foundational Learning
- **Linear subspace methods (PCA, LDA, ridge probing):** Used to extract a low-dimensional basis that captures variance predictive of accent labels. Ridge probing (regularized linear classification) provides a stable estimator.  
  *Quick check:* How does ridge regression differ from PCA in seeking directions that are predictive of a label versus directions of maximum variance? What role does regularization play?
- **Adversarial attacks with representation constraints (PGD):** Enables testing whether forcing perturbations along the accent subspace exacerbates disparity. The PGD objective includes a subspace alignment penalty in representation space while bounding the waveform perturbation.  
  *Quick check:* In PGD, why is the subspace constraint added to the loss rather than as a hard constraint on \(\delta\)? What does this imply about the interaction between waveform budget and representation shift?
- **Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) loss and WER:** Wav2Vec2 is trained with CTC; performance is evaluated via WER from greedy decoding. Understanding CTC’s alignment properties helps interpret how continuous representation shifts affect discrete outputs.  
  *Quick check:* How does CTC loss handle variable-length sequences and blank tokens? Why might WER be a noisy metric for correlating with continuous representation shifts \(m(x)\)?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Common Voice (7 accents) → Wav2Vec2-base encoder → Mean-pooled embeddings → Ridge probe (learns subspace U) → PGD attack engine (with subspace constraint) → Forward hook (project-out) → CTC decoder → WER evaluation

**Critical path:**  
1. Prepare multi-accent manifest (audio_path, accent, text).  
2. For candidate layers (2–6) and subspace sizes k∈{4,8,16}, extract subspaces via ridge probe and validate using probe accuracy and stability (principal angle).  
3. Select layer ℓ* (3) and k (8) balancing high probe accuracy and moderate stability (~50°).  
4. Run PGD attacks (clean, unconstrained, random-subspace, accent-subspace) on the test set; compute coupling m(x) and WER disparity.  
5. Repeat attack comparison with a permuted-label subspace to test specificity.  
6. Apply project-out (α=0.5) at layer ℓ* during inference on clean and attacked audio; compare WER and disparity.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Layer selection:* Early layers capture acoustic features (better accent decodability) but may contain more task-critical information; later layers may have abstracted away accent. Choice balances probe accuracy and risk of damaging recognition.  
- *Subspace dimension k:* Larger k captures more accent variance but risks including recognition-critical directions; smaller k may miss relevant directions. ACES used k=8 based on validation.  
- *Perturbation budget ε:* Must be imperceptible (~60 dB) but sufficient to trigger degradation; larger ε risks unconstrained effects dominating.  
- *Projection strength α:* Partial projection (0.5) tests effect; full projection (1) likely catastrophically degrades WER.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Subspace extraction yields low probe accuracy (<80%) or unstable subspaces (principal angle >>50°).  
- Attacks show no consistent gap between accent-subspace and random-subspace WER across accents, or coupling m(x) not significantly higher for accent-subspace.  
- Project-out reduces disparity or decreases overall WER, indicating accent and recognition features are separable.  
- Permuted-label control still shows accent-subspace advantage, suggesting artefactual structure.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Subspace extraction & validation:** Sweep layers 2–6 and k∈{4,8,16}; plot probe accuracy, WER–projection correlation, and stability; confirm early-layer peak and stable subspace at ℓ*=3, k=8.  
2. **Epsilon ablation:** For ε ∈ {0.005, 0.01, 0.05}, compute disparity and mean WER for all attack conditions; expect accent-subspace disparity to remain elevated as ε grows while random-subspace disparity drops.  
3. **Permuted-label specificity:** Learn U_perm on shuffled accent labels; repeat attack comparison; expect accent-subspace vs. random gap to vanish (mean ~0).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** Why does partial projection-out of the accent subspace worsen overall WER and increase disparity for some accents, rather than improving fairness? The paper hypothesizes an acoustic–phonetic overlap but does not prove what specific features are being removed.
- **Open Question 2:** To what extent are the findings generalizable beyond Wav2Vec2-base and the seven English accents studied? The authors note the limitation of evaluating a single model and language; it is unknown whether the subspace geometry, disparity amplification, and negative project-out hold for other architectures or multilingual settings.
- **Open Question 3:** Is the weak per-utterance correlation between coupling metric m(x) and ΔWER solely due to noise from CTC decoding, or does it indicate that the accent subspace influences disparity through aggregate, non-linear effects not captured by the linear metric? The paper attributes it to CTC noise but does not rule out other explanations.
- **Open Question 4:** Does the accent subspace contain directions that are causally necessary for accurate recognition on high-WER accents, or is the observed degradation after project-out merely a side-effect of reducing representational capacity? The project-out test is correlational; causality remains unresolved.

## Limitations
- The linear subspace assumption may oversimplify accent encoding; potential nonlinear structure is not probed.
- Hyperparameter choices (ℓ*, k, β, α) were guided by validation and may overfit; full sensitivity analysis is lacking.
- Evaluation is restricted to Wav2Vec2-base on seven accents from Common Voice, limiting generalizability.
- Some implementation details (PGD step size, hook location) are unspecified, hindering exact replication.

- **Disparity amplification via subspace attacks:** High
