# MAD-SmaAt-GNet: A Multimodal Advection-Guided Neural Network for Precipitation Nowcasting

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.04461
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04461
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: MAD-SmaAt-GNet reduces MSE by 8.9% (0.4299 vs 0.4721) relative to SmaAt-UNet and improves F1-score (0.6078 vs 0.5675) and MCC (0.5279 vs 0.4839).

## Executive Summary
MAD-SmaAt-GNet is a deep learning model for precipitation nowcasting that enhances the efficient SmaAt-UNet architecture. It incorporates a multimodal encoder to ingest additional atmospheric variables (temperature at 300 m, air pressure, relative humidity, and wind components at 300 m) and integrates an advection-based evolution network to enforce physical consistency. Trained on filtered KNMI HARMONIE data (Netherlands region, 2019–2023), the model predicts four future rain images at hourly intervals. Experimental results show that MAD-SmaAt-GNet significantly outperforms the SmaAt-UNet baseline, the persistence model, and single-component ablations, demonstrating the complementary benefits of multimodal data fusion and physics-guided learning for accurate short-term precipitation forecasting.

## Method Summary
MAD-SmaAt-GNet extends SmaAt-UNet by adding two key modules. First, a multimodal encoder processes five atmospheric variables (temperature, pressure, humidity, U-wind, V-wind) at four timesteps (20 channels in total) using the same encoder structure as the rain branch. Its multi-resolution features are fused into the main decoder at every upsampling level via Spatially-Adaptive Denormalisation (SPADE) layers, allowing the decoder to modulate rain features based on atmospheric context. Second, an advection-based evolution network (derived from NowcastNet) takes the latest rain image and predicts motion fields plus an intensity residual; these are used to generate physically evolved rain frames. Intermediate features from this network are injected into the decoder’s bottleneck and the first/last upsampling stages. The full model is trained with mean squared error (MSE) loss using Adam (lr = 0.001), ReduceLROnPlateau (patience = 5), and early stopping (patience = 15). The evolution network is pre‑trained but remains unfrozen during joint training. All encoder/decoder channel counts are halved relative to the original SmaAt-UNet to manage complexity.

## Key Results
- **MSE reduction**: 0.4299 vs 0.4721 (8.9% improvement) over SmaAt-UNet.
- **Classification gains**: F1-score 0.6078 vs 0.5675; MCC 0.5279 vs 0.4839.
- **Ablation insights**: Multimodal input stream significantly boosts 1‑3 hour forecasts; advection component consistently improves all lead times (including the 4th hour). The full model surpasses all benchmarks.
- **Complementarity**: Combined multimodal and advection modules outperform each single-component variant.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Multimodal atmospheric context**: A dedicated encoder extracts features from temperature, pressure, humidity, and wind at multiple timesteps. These features are fused into every decoder level via SPADE, enabling the model to modulate rain‑related features based on surrounding atmospheric conditions. This improves intensity prediction for short lead times (1–3 h) because the auxiliary variables carry predictive signals about near‑future precipitation formation and dissipation. The benefit decays beyond 3 h, likely due to decreasing temporal relevance of the atmospheric state for advection‑dominated evolution.

**Advection‑based physical consistency**: The evolution network decomposes precipitation change into a motion field (optical flow) and an intensity residual. By advecting the latest rain image with the predicted motion and adding the residual, the model enforces a 2D continuity constraint that stabilizes long‑term trajectory forecasting. This component consistently enhances accuracy across all forecast horizons, including the fourth hour, by capturing the dominant transport dynamics of rain systems.

**Synergistic fusion**: The full model combines the intensity cues from the multimodal stream with the motion information from the evolution network. The decoder receives three conditioning streams: rain skip connections (structural detail), multimodal features (atmospheric context at all levels), and evolved rain features (physical trajectory at bottleneck and early/late stages). SPADE gates allow these to interact, yielding predictions that are both intense and correctly displaced. The complementary nature of the two modules explains why the combined model outperforms each variant individually.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Rain Stream Input → [Evolution Network] & [Rain Encoder] & [Multimodal Encoder] → Decoder (SPADE fusion: multimodal at all levels, evolution features at bottleneck + first and final upsampling) → Output Layer (1×1 conv)

**Critical path**: The latest rain frame is processed by the Evolution Network to generate evolved frames (not used directly) and by the Rain Encoder to extract structural features. The Multimodal Encoder processes the five auxiliary variables. The Decoder combines these three streams to predict four future rain images.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- *Evolution integration depth*: Using evolution features at every decoder level caused training instability; restricting to three levels (bottleneck + first/last upsampling) stabilizes training.
- *Parameter efficiency*: Channel counts in rain encoder/decoder and evolution network are halved compared to the original SmaAt‑UNet to counter overfitting from the smaller input sequence (4 frames) and added complexity.
- *Pre‑training evolution*: Best results when the evolution network is pre‑trained but not frozen, allowing joint calibration while preserving learned physical biases.

**Failure signatures**:
- Divergence or poor convergence when evolution features are injected at all decoder levels.
- Degraded 4‑hour performance when only the multimodal stream is used (MSE exceeds baseline at hour 4).
- Persistent blur in predictions due to reliance on MSE loss, regardless of architecture.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. Reproduce SmaAt‑UNet baseline on the filtered KNMI dataset (4‑in/4‑out, 0.5 mm/h threshold) and verify MSE ~0.4721.
2. Add the evolution network features (“SmaAt‑UNet + Evo‑Net”) and compare per‑timestep MSE against baseline; expect improvement especially at later lead times (3–4 h).
3. Add the multimodal encoder (“SmaAt‑UNet + 2‑stream”) and compare per‑timestep MSE; expect improvement at early lead times (1–3 h) but possible deterioration at 4 h.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Temporal decay of multimodal predictive power**  
   *Basis*: The multimodal extension improves only 1‑3 h forecasts, not the 4th hour, while the advection component remains effective.  
   *Why unresolved*: The authors did not analyze how the relevance of each atmospheric variable evolves over time.  
   *What evidence would resolve*: Compute time‑lagged mutual information or correlations between each auxiliary variable and precipitation across lead times; perform a sequential variable ablation per horizon.

2. **Instability from full evolution integration**  
   *Basis*: Including evolution network features at all decoder levels made the model large and unstable, forcing a restricted design.  
   *Why unresolved*: The root cause (e.g., gradient conflict, feature mismatch) is not diagnosed.  
   *What evidence would resolve*: Progressive integration experiments (adding one decoder level at a time) while monitoring training dynamics (loss, gradients, activations); test stabilizations like adaptive weighting or attention gating.

3. **Impact of dataset filtering on generalizability**  
   *Basis*: The dataset was filtered to samples where >20 % of the first rain image exceeds 0.1 mm/h, discarding many light‑rain events.  
   *Why unresolved*: No evaluation on light‑rain or unfiltered data; the filtering’s bias across precipitation intensities is unknown.  
   *What evidence would resolve*: Train and test on datasets with varying rain‑intensity thresholds; stratify metrics by intensity bins (e.g., <0.5 mm/h, 0.5–2 mm/h, >2 mm/h) to quantify bias and optimal filtering.

## Limitations
- **Dataset constraints**: The HARMONIE data is not publicly available; filtering criteria and normalization specifics are not fully detailed, hindering exact replication.
- **Fusion ambiguity**: The paper specifies where SPADE conditioning occurs but not precisely how evolution and multimodal features are combined at each layer, leaving implementation details open.
- **Generalizability limits**: Evaluation is restricted to the Netherlands region and filtered heavy‑rain events; performance on lighter precipitation or other climatic zones remains unverified.

## Confidence
- Multimodal inputs improve short‑term (1‑3 h) forecasts: **Medium**
- Advection component improves all lead times: **Medium**
- Combined model outperforms single‑component variants: **High**
- Overall superiority over persistence and SmaAt‑UNet: **High**

## Next Checks
1. **Data replication**: Obtain a public radar‑plus‑multivariable dataset (e.g., KNMI composites), apply the described filtering and normalization, and verify that the SmaAt‑UNet baseline reproduces an MSE of ~0.4721.
2. **Fusion strategy ablation**: Systematically vary the SPADE conditioning (e.g., remove multimodal fusion, replace with concatenation, adjust depth of evolution feature injection) to quantify effects on per‑timestep MSE and training stability.
3. **Geographic transfer**: Train and test MAD‑SmaAt‑GNet on a distinct region (e.g., Germany or Denmark) to assess robustness to different meteorological patterns and precipitation distributions.
