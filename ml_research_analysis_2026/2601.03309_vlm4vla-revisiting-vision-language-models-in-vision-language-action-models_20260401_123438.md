# VLM4VLA: Revisiting Vision-Language-Models in Vision-Language-Action Models

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.03309
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03309
- Reference count: 22
- Primary result: Pretrained VLM initialization consistently improves VLA success rates, but general VQA performance does not predict robotic manipulation performance.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates how the choice of pretrained Vision-Language Models (VLMs) affects Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model performance in robotic manipulation. The authors propose VLM4VLA, a minimal adaptation pipeline that converts any VLM into a policy by adding a single learnable token and a small MLP head, training all parameters with imitation learning. Across 24 VLMs and three simulation benchmarks (Calvin, SimplerEnv, Libero), VLM initialization outperforms training from scratch. However, a VLM's performance on standard VQA benchmarks is a poor predictor of its VLA success, with correlations ranging from weak to negative. Ablations reveal the vision encoder as the primary bottleneck; freezing it harms performance, while injecting control-relevant supervision into it yields gains. Fine-tuning VLMs on auxiliary embodied tasks does not reliably improve VLA results, highlighting a domain gap between VLM pretraining and embodied action planning.

## Method Summary
The VLM4VLA pipeline starts with a pretrained VLM backbone. A special `<ActionQuery>` token is appended to the input sequence, which combines image tokens (from a single RGB frame) and text instruction tokens formatted to match the VLM's pretraining style. The final hidden state of this token is extracted from the VLM's output and fed into a small multilayer perceptron (MLP) policy head that predicts a chunk of continuous actions (e.g., end-effector pose and gripper state). The entire model—VLM parameters plus the new MLP—is trained end-to-end on robot demonstration data using a combination of Huber loss for continuous actions and binary cross-entropy for discrete gripper control. Training uses consistent hyperparameters across all VLM backbones (learning rates around 2e-5 to 5e-5, batch sizes 128–512, 30k–50k steps). Evaluation measures success rate on held-out tasks from three benchmarks: Calvin ABC-D (tabletop sequence completion), SimplerEnv BridgeV2 (pick/stack scenes), and Libero-Long (long-horizon tasks).

## Key Results
- VLM initialization consistently outperforms training from scratch across all 24 tested VLMs and three benchmarks.
- Standard VQA benchmark scores are poor predictors of VLA success, showing weak or negative correlations except on Calvin.
- Ablations identify the vision encoder as the performance bottleneck; freezing it causes drastic drops, while the language module is less critical.
- Fine-tuning VLMs on auxiliary embodied tasks (e.g., pointing, VQA) does not guarantee VLA improvement and often harms performance.
- Injecting control-relevant supervision (e.g., FAST token pretraining) into the vision encoder yields consistent gains, indicating a domain gap in visual representations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The success of VLM4VLA stems from transferring rich multimodal representations from large-scale pretraining to the control domain, but with critical caveats. The vision encoder emerges as the key bottleneck because VLM pretraining objectives (e.g., image-text matching) do not emphasize the low-level spatial and physical reasoning required for robotic manipulation. Freezing the vision encoder prevents adaptation to control-relevant features, leading to failure. Conversely, injecting supervision like affordance or spatial reasoning during VLM pretraining aligns the visual representation with control needs, enabling even a frozen encoder to support VLA. The language module plays a lesser role, possibly because instructions are already well-structured in the pretraining data. Auxiliary fine-tuning fails because tasks like VQA or pointing do not directly train the representations needed for action prediction; they may even introduce conflicting gradients. Thus, the core mechanism is: VLM initialization provides a strong starting point, but the vision representation must be either fine-tuned on control data or pretrained with control-relevant objectives to bridge the domain gap.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept 1: Vision-Language Models (VLMs)**  
*Why needed:* VLMs provide pretrained multimodal representations that can be adapted to embodied tasks, reducing the need for large-scale robot data.  
*Quick check:* Can the VLM answer questions about an image? If yes, it has basic visual grounding.

**Concept 2: Imitation Learning**  
*Why needed:* VLA models learn from human demonstrations without hand-coded rewards.  
*Quick check:* Does the training objective minimize error between predicted and demonstrated actions? (e.g., MSE on poses)

**Concept 3: Action Chunking**  
*Why needed:* Predicting multiple future actions at once improves temporal coherence and reduces error accumulation.  
*Quick check:* Is the policy output a sequence of actions (e.g., 10 steps) rather than a single step?

**Concept 4: Special Token Augmentation**  
*Why needed:* Adding tokens like `<ActionQuery>` allows the VLM to produce action-conditioned outputs without altering its architecture.  
*Quick check:* Is there a new token whose final hidden state is used for action decoding?

**Concept 5: Domain Gap in Representation**  
*Why needed:* VLM pretraining (web data) differs from embodied control (robot interactions), causing poor transfer if not addressed.  
*Quick check:* Does freezing the VLM's vision encoder hurt performance? If yes, there's a gap.

**Concept 6: Evaluation Benchmarks**  
*Why needed:* Standardized robotic manipulation suites (Calvin, SimplerEnv, Libero) enable fair comparison across methods.  
*Quick check:* Are success rates measured on simulated robot tasks with consistent metrics?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Pretrained VLM (vision encoder + LLM) -> Input sequence: [<image_tokens>, <text_prompt_tokens>, <ActionQuery_token>] -> VLM forward pass -> Extract last_hidden_state of <ActionQuery_token> -> MLP policy head -> Action chunk

Critical path: The `<ActionQuery>` token's hidden state must be correctly extracted from the VLM's output, and the MLP must map it to the action space. The VLM must process the image and text in its native format; any mismatch causes failure.

Design tradeoffs: (1) Freezing vs unfreezing VLM: freezing reduces compute but hurts performance if vision encoder is bottleneck; unfreezing all parameters increases cost but allows adaptation. (2) Action chunk length: longer chunks improve temporal coherence but require more accurate long-horizon prediction. (3) VLM size: larger VLMs have more capacity but may need careful learning rate tuning to avoid instability.

Failure signatures: (a) Success rate near zero: likely input format error (wrong tokenization, misplaced `<ActionQuery>`). (b) Training loss NaN or collapse: may be due to incorrect loss function (should be Huber+BCE, not diffusion) or poor MLP initialization relative to feature scale. (c) Freezing vision encoder causes >20% drop: confirms vision encoder is bottleneck.

First three experiments:  
1. Tokenization fidelity test: For one VLM (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL-3B), generate the exact input sequence (image tokens, prompt tokens, `<ActionQuery>`), print token IDs and attention mask lengths; verify that the `<ActionQuery>` index matches extraction code and that image tokens are correctly processed.  
2. Hyperparameter sensitivity probe: Train the same VLM on Calvin with two learning rates (e.g., 2e-5 and 5e-5) and with/without gradient clipping; record final success rate to see if a single config works uniformly.  
3. Vision encoder freezing ablation: Train three runs: (a) all parameters trainable, (b) only MLP head trainable, (c) only vision encoder trainable; compare success rates to confirm which components are critical.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1**  
- Question: Which concrete VLM skills (beyond general VQA) correlate with VLA success?  
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors find weak or negative correlation between standard VQA benchmarks and VLA performance, showing existing metrics are poor predictors.  
- Why unresolved: The specific visual or multimodal abilities that matter for control are unknown; no alternative predictive framework exists.  
- What evidence would resolve it: Large-scale correlation analysis between VLA outcomes and fine-grained VLM capabilities (e.g., spatial reasoning, video grounding) across many models.

**Open Question 2**  
- Question: Why does fine-tuning on auxiliary embodied tasks not improve VLA performance?  
- Basis in paper: [inferred] Empirically, auxiliary fine-tuning (pointing, VQA, etc.) often harms or does not help VLA results.  
- Why unresolved: The mismatch between auxiliary objectives and control needs is unexplained; could be due to dataset mismatch, interference, or misaligned representations.  
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments varying auxiliary task design, data distribution, and measuring representation overlap with control tasks.

**Open Question 3**  
- Question: How can VLM pretraining objectives be changed to produce vision features suitable for robotic manipulation without extensive fine-tuning?  
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The vision encoder is the main bottleneck, and adding control-related supervision (e.g., FAST tokens) during VLM training improves VLA performance, indicating a domain gap.  
- Why unresolved: Standard web-scale pretraining optimizes for multimodal understanding, not for low-level action planning; suitable pretraining tasks are unknown.  
- What evidence would resolve it: Pretrain VLMs on tasks like affordance detection, action anticipation, or dense spatial reasoning, then test VLA with frozen encoders on multiple benchmarks.

## Limitations
- Input format specifics (token concatenation, special tokens, masking) for each VLM family are omitted, risking implementation drift.
- Complete hyperparameter sweep details (optimizer, scheduler, gradient clipping) are not reported; the "unified" configuration may be suboptimal for some VLMs.
- Exact dataset splits, trajectory windowing, and image augmentations are unspecified, hindering exact replication.
- All evaluations are in simulation; generalizability to real-world robots is unknown.

## Confidence
- VLM initialization outperforms scratch training: **High** (consistent across 24 VLMs, 3 benchmarks).
- VQA scores do not predict VLA success: **Medium** (correlation analysis supports on Calvin but weaker elsewhere).
- Vision encoder is the primary bottleneck: **High** (freezing causes drastic drops; adding supervision helps).
- Auxiliary fine-tuning does not improve VLA: **Medium** (evidence limited to few tasks).
- Control-relevant visual supervision helps: **Medium** (gains shown on one benchmark; needs validation on others).

## Next Checks
1. Tokenization fidelity test: For 2–3 VLMs, generate exact input sequences using presumed format, compare attention masks, and measure performance deviation vs. reported numbers.
2. Hyperparameter sweep replication: On a representative VLM set (e.g., 3B, 7B, 13B), reproduce the sweep to identify optimal LR/weight decay; verify if a single config works uniformly.
3. Sim-to-real transfer experiment: Deploy the top-performing VLM4VLA model on a real robot for a subset of Libero tasks; compare success rate with simulation.
