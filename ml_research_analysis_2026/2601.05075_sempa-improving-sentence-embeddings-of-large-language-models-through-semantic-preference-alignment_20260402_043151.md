# SemPA: Improving Sentence Embeddings of Large Language Models through Semantic Preference Alignment

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.05075  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05075  
- **Reference count**: 25  
- **Primary result**: SemPA achieves state-of-the-art semantic textual similarity performance (average Spearman's ρ of 77.69 on LLaMA2-7B and 78.10 on LLaMA3-8B) while preserving or enhancing generative capabilities across reasoning and knowledge benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
SemPA addresses the challenge of improving sentence embeddings from generative large language models (LLMs) without compromising their text generation abilities. Existing methods either degrade generation (e.g., contrastive learning) or require architectural modifications. SemPA introduces sentence-level semantic preference alignment using Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) on paraphrase preference pairs constructed from natural language inference datasets. The approach aligns the LLM to prefer semantically equivalent outputs while retaining its original generative proficiency, aided by low-rank adaptation (LoRA) for efficient fine‑tuning. Empirical results show state-of-the‑art or competitive scores on semantic textual similarity benchmarks, and importantly, generative performance on tasks like GSM8K and MMLU is maintained or even improved, contrasting with the large drops observed under contrastive learning.

## Method Summary
SemPA constructs training data from NLI triplets (premise, entailment, contradiction) by treating the premise as an anchor, the entailment hypothesis as a positive paraphrase, and the contradiction as a negative. The anchor is wrapped in a paraphrase‑promoting instruction (e.g., “Keep the same meaning of this sentence: “{anchor}”, while making some changes.”). The LLM is fine‑tuned with the DPO objective, which directly optimizes the model to assign higher probability to the positive output than the negative one, relative to a frozen reference model. LoRA adapters (rank = 8, scaling = 32) are applied to all linear layers except the LM head, updating only a tiny fraction of parameters (0.3%) to avoid catastrophic forgetting. After training, sentence embeddings are extracted using the PromptEOL template (“This sentence: “{s}” means in one word:”) by taking the last token’s final‑layer hidden state.

## Key Results
- **STS improvement**: SemPA outperforms prompting and architecture‑modification baselines on multiple STS benchmarks, reaching average Spearman’s ρ of 77.69 (LLaMA2‑7B) and 78.10 (LLaMA3‑8B) with 40–80K NLI samples.
- **Generative capability preserved**: Unlike contrastive learning, which degrades average generative performance by up to 17.71 points on LLaMA3‑8B, SemPA maintains or improves results on GSM8K, MMLU, HellaSwag, DROP, and TruthfulQA.
- **Parameter efficiency**: With only 0.3% of parameters trainable via LoRA, SemPA achieves comparable or better embeddings than full fine‑tuning approaches.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Sentence‑level Preference Alignment via DPO
SemPA applies DPO to paraphrase generation: given an anchor and two candidate outputs (positive paraphrase, negative non‑paraphrase), the model learns to prefer the positive one conditioned on the anchor. This operates at the sentence level via the output distribution, encouraging the internal representations to capture semantic equivalence without being forced to generate the exact paraphrase token‑by‑token. The core assumption is that discrimination between semantically equivalent sentences at the distribution level induces better semantic features in hidden states. Evidence includes the strong STS gains reported and the fact that the model does not degenerate into a pure paraphraser (section 4.2). Break condition: excessive training data or steps (>80K samples) may cause over‑alignment and hurt general generation (Figure 3).

### Mechanism 2: Theoretical Unification under Plackett‑Luce
The paper shows that both DPO and contrastive learning can be expressed as specific cases of the Plackett‑Luce ranking model; DPO uses a log‑likelihood ratio scoring function while contrastive learning uses similarity. This connection explains why DPO, though operating on the output distribution, still optimizes a ranking objective similar to contrastive learning but at the sentence level, thus improving embeddings without token‑level interference. The derivation (section 4.4, Table 2) provides the theoretical justification. Break condition: violations of Plackett‑Luce assumptions (e.g., intransitive preferences) could weaken the link and reduce effectiveness.

### Mechanism 3: LoRA‑based Efficient Fine‑Tuning
LoRA restricts weight updates to low‑rank matrices, allowing the model to adapt to the DPO task while preserving the majority of original weights. This prevents catastrophic forgetting of generative abilities that full fine‑tuning or token‑level contrastive learning might cause. Evidence: Table 6 shows that contrastive learning causes large drops on generative benchmarks, while SemPA with LoRA maintains or improves them. Core assumption: the necessary semantic adjustments lie in a low‑rank subspace. Break condition: if LoRA rank is too low, the model may lack capacity to learn the preference alignment; if too high or too many layers are updated, generative performance may degrade.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
NLI triplets → Preference pair construction → DPO+LoRA training → Fine‑tuned LLM → PromptEOL embedding extraction.

**Critical path**:
1. Load the base LLM and tokenizer; create a frozen reference copy.  
2. Download NLI data (e.g., SNLI/MNLI) and convert each valid triplet into an (anchor, positive, negative) pair, formatting the anchor with a paraphrase instruction template.  
3. Add LoRA modules (rank = 8, alpha = 32) to all linear layers except the LM head.  
4. Train with the DPO loss: for each batch, compute log probabilities of the positive and negative completions under both the reference and policy models, compute the loss (log‑sigmoid of the difference), and update LoRA parameters with AdamW (lr ≈ 1e‑4, batch size = 256).  
5. For inference, apply the PromptEOL template to a new sentence, pass it through the fine‑tuned model without generation, and extract the last token’s hidden state as the sentence embedding.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- DPO vs. contrastive learning: DPO operates at the sentence level via output distributions, avoiding token‑level interventions that degrade generation.  
- LoRA vs. full fine‑tuning: LoRA reduces memory and preserves generative ability but may limit capacity; rank must be chosen carefully.  
- Paraphrase template: Different templates yield similar gains, but some (e.g., “Rewrite the sentence…”) may be less stable on older models like LLaMA2.  
- Training data size: Performance peaks around 40–80 K samples; more data risks over‑alignment (Figure 3).

**Failure signatures**:
- *STS does not improve*: Check for noisy preference pairs, incorrect template, β too high, or embedding extraction using a generated token instead of the last input token.  
- *Generative benchmarks drop*: Indicates over‑fitting to the paraphrase task; reduce training steps, samples, or LoRA rank.  
- *Embeddings are highly anisotropic*: Suggests over‑alignment; try smaller data or earlier checkpoint.  
- *DPO loss oscillates or diverges*: Lower learning rate or adjust β.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Baseline replication**: Evaluate PromptEOL on the base model to confirm expected STS numbers (e.g., ~70.0 on LLaMA2‑7B).  
2. **Sanity check**: Train SemPA with 1 K NLI samples for 1 epoch; expect modest STS gain (+2‑3 points) and minimal change (<1 point) on generative benchmarks.  
3. **Ablation on LoRA rank**: Compare ranks 4, 8, 16; higher rank should increase STS gains but watch for degradation on generation; rank 8 typically gives the best trade‑off.

## Limitations
- The exact source and filtering procedure for the `nli_for_simcse.csv` dataset are unspecified, introducing potential reproducibility issues and risk of data leakage into STS test sets.  
- Critical hyperparameters (e.g., DPO β value, precise training sample count used for main results) are not reported, making exact replication difficult.  
- Over‑alignment to paraphrase generation may occur with excessive data or training steps, as suggested by performance saturation beyond 80 K samples.

## Confidence
- DPO preference alignment improves embeddings: **High**  
- Theoretical unification of DPO and contrastive learning: **Medium**  
- LoRA sufficient for preserving generative ability: **Medium**  
- Overall claim (better embeddings without sacrificing generation): **High**
