# LightMover: Generative Light Movement with Color and Intensity Controls

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.27209
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27209
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: State-of-the-art light editing (PSNR 20.38, DINO 81.27, CLIP 91.85) with 41% token reduction via adaptive pruning.

## Executive Summary
LightMover is a generative model for precise light manipulation in single images. It reformulates light editing—adjusting position, color, and intensity—as a sequence-to-sequence task using a video diffusion transformer. The method encodes all control signals (reference image, object crop, movement map, color, intensity) as pseudo‑video frames, and denoises an output frame token sequence under these conditions. Key innovations include a multi‑signal positional encoding (MSPE) to disentangle spatial, temporal, and semantic aspects of each frame, and an adaptive token‑pruning mechanism that reduces control sequence length by 41% with minimal fidelity loss. A scalable rendering pipeline generates diverse training pairs with physically disentangled lighting. LightMover achieves SOTA results on light movement benchmarks and supports compositional control, while also generalizing to object movement tasks.

## Method Summary
LightMover adapts a pre‑trained video diffusion transformer (e.g., SVD) to generate relit images from a single input. Each condition—including the reference image, cropped light source, movement map (source/target regions), color and intensity frames—is converted into a latent token grid by a video VAE and arranged sequentially as a pseudo‑video. The transformer iteratively denoises the noisy output frame tokens conditioned on this control sequence. MSPE injects four orthogonal positional encodings (spatial 2‑D, temporal index, condition‑type, and frame‑role) into every token, enabling the model to interpret multiple modalities correctly. Adaptive token pruning downsamples tokens for spatial controls based on object size (via a bounding box area ratio τ) and uses learnable ratios for non‑spatial signals, significantly reducing sequence length without sacrificing quality. Training employs a flow‑matching objective on a mixed dataset of synthetic (Blender‑rendered) and real image pairs, with resolution augmentation and task‑balanced sampling.

## Key Results
- PSNR 20.38, DINO score 81.27, CLIP score 91.85 on light movement task, outperforming LLM‑based editors and ObjectMover.
- Adaptive token pruning reduces control sequence length by 41% while maintaining editing fidelity.
- Supports compositional control (e.g., simultaneous movement, color, intensity adjustments) and generalizes to object movement tasks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Sequence-to-Sequence Formulation with Video Diffusion
Claim: Light editing is reformulated as a sequence-to-sequence prediction in visual token space, repurposing a video diffusion transformer to generate physically plausible illumination changes from a single image.  
Mechanism: The input image, object crop, movement map, color control, intensity control, and noisy output are each encoded as pseudo‑video frames by a video VAE. These frames are concatenated into a token sequence and processed by a diffusion transformer trained with a flow‑matching objective. The transformer predicts the clean output frame from the noisy one, conditioning on the control tokens. The model leverages the video prior’s understanding of temporal coherence and scene dynamics to produce consistent lighting changes.  
Core assumption: Video diffusion models contain useful priors about illumination dynamics that can be transferred to single‑image editing when controls are framed as video frames.  
Break condition: If the video prior lacks diversity in illumination scenarios, the model may fail for unseen lighting interactions; also, representing all controls as frames may become inefficient at high resolutions or with many lights.

### Mechanism 2: Multi‑Signal Positional Encoding (MSPE)
Claim: MSPE integrates four orthogonal positional subspaces (spatial, temporal, condition‑type, frame‑role) to enable the diffusion transformer to disentangle and correctly interpret multiple control modalities within a single token sequence.  
Mechanism: Each latent patch token receives: (1) 2‑D sine‑cosine encoding for spatial coordinates within its frame; (2) a temporal index reflecting the sequence order; (3) a discrete identifier for condition type (e.g., reference, movement, color, intensity); (4) a binary flag indicating whether the token belongs to an input or output frame. These are projected, additively combined, and modulated with RoPE‑style rotation. NTK‑aware interpolation allows processing at different resolutions.  
Core assumption: Separating positional information into orthogonal subspaces preserves spatial relationships while allowing semantic distinction of control signals and their roles.  
Break condition: Interference between subspaces (e.g., condition‑type colliding with spatial frequencies) could cause control confusion; poor NTK interpolation may distort spatial layouts at non‑trained resolutions.

### Mechanism 3: Adaptive Token Pruning
Claim: Adaptive token pruning reduces the control sequence length by 41% by downsampling tokens based on spatial content, maintaining editing fidelity while improving computational efficiency.  
Mechanism: For spatial controls (movement map), the bounding box area ratio τ=0.2 determines downsampling: if the ratio is below τ, full resolution is kept; otherwise tokens are downsampled proportionally. For non‑spatial controls (color, intensity), learnable downsampling ratios are optimized to decide the token count. This yields a 41% reduction in overall sequence length.  
Core assumption: Non‑spatial signals do not require fine‑grained spatial layouts, and spatial signals can be compressed in background regions without losing critical details for light manipulation.  
Break condition: Over‑aggressive pruning may discard small‑scale details (e.g., narrow shadows); poorly learned ratios for color/intensity might strip essential control information.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Video VAE -> token grids for each frame -> Adaptive Token Pruning -> concatenation -> Multi‑Signal Positional Encoding (MSPE) -> Diffusion Transformer (denoising) -> VAE decoder -> edited image. The rendering pipeline (training only) generates data: Blender scene -> render with varied light parameters -> post‑process.

### Critical path
Inference flow:  
1. Prepare pseudo‑video frames: reference image, object crop, movement map (optional), color/intensity frames (optional), noisy output.  
2. Encode each frame with video VAE → latent token grids.  
3. Apply adaptive token pruning: movement map downsampled based on bounding box area ratio; color/intensity frames downsampled by learned ratios.  
4. Concatenate pruned token sequences and add MSPE.  
5. Diffusion transformer iteratively denoises output tokens conditioned on the control sequence.  
6. Decode final output tokens with VAE → edited image.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Frame‑based vs. embedding‑based control**: Frames preserve spatial structure for all conditions but increase sequence length; embeddings are compact but lose spatial semantics (ablation in Table 5 shows frame‑based outperforms learnable embeddings).
- **Pruning aggressiveness**: The threshold τ and learnable ratios balance efficiency vs. fidelity; over‑pruning loses detail, under‑pruning wastes compute.
- **Multi‑task data mixing**: Joint training on movement, color, intensity, removal, insertion improves generalization but requires careful sampling ratios (6:3:3:3:1:1) to avoid one task dominating.

### Failure signatures
- **Over‑pruning**: When movement map bounding box area > τ, downsampling may blur small lights or narrow shadows; check by comparing output detail at different pruning thresholds.
- **MSPE confusion**: If condition‑type or frame‑role encodings are misaligned, the model may apply the wrong control (e.g., treat color as intensity); manifests as incorrect color casts or intensity changes.
- **Video prior mismatch**: For light movements with large discontinuities (e.g., through occluders) or extreme color shifts, the model may produce inconsistent shadows or highlights because the video prior lacks such training examples.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Light movement on real data**: Run on LightMove‑A benchmark; report PSNR, DINO, CLIP. Verifies basic functionality and physical plausibility.
2. **Ablation of adaptive pruning**: Compare full model against (a) no pruning, (b) uniform downsampling, (c) removal of object frame; measure metrics and runtime to confirm 41% length reduction without fidelity loss.
3. **Compositional control test**: On LightMove‑B, vary movement + color/intensity simultaneously; evaluate each attribute independently to ensure disentanglement and combined PSNR/DINO/CLIP scores.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1: Extending to large‑scale ambient lighting changes
The paper explicitly notes that LightMover struggles with relocating outdoor sunlight due to limited training coverage (Sec. E. Limitations). The training data is primarily synthetic indoor scenes and a small set of real pairs, lacking outdoor illumination scenarios with complex global light transport (e.g., atmospheric scattering, dynamic shadows from terrain/structures). Extending the rendering pipeline to include diverse outdoor HDRI environments and varying sun positions, then evaluating on a new outdoor benchmark, would be needed to address this limitation.

### Open Question 2: Achieving temporal consistency in video editing
LightMover currently targets single‑image editing and may produce temporally inconsistent results when applied frame‑by‑frame to video (explicitly mentioned in Sec. E.). The model is trained on independent image pairs without temporal constraints, so applying it per frame ignores temporal coherence in lighting dynamics (e.g., smooth shadow motion, consistent color temperature drift). A video extension would require incorporating temporal attention or recurrent modules and training on synthetic video sequences with smooth light trajectories, with evaluation using temporal metrics (e.g., temporal LPIPS, shadow boundary consistency).

### Open Question 3: Integrating explicit 3D geometry for complex light transport
The approach inherits the assumptions of 2D diffusion‑based editing and does not recover explicit 3D geometry or materials, which can lead to physically implausible results in scenes with complex light transport such as multiple refractions or inter‑reflections (explicit in Sec. E.). The video diffusion prior captures some implicit 3D awareness but cannot fully model occlusions, view‑dependent effects, or light bounces that require a 3D scene representation. Hybridizing LightMover with a lightweight 3D estimator (e.g., monocular depth or normal maps) as additional conditioning and then evaluating on geometrically challenging scenes could improve physical accuracy.

### Open Question 4: Pruning’s effect on subtle illumination gradients
Although adaptive token pruning reduces sequence length by 41% while maintaining overall metrics, it is not analyzed per lighting scenario. The spatially‑aware pruning for movement maps downsamples based on object bounding box ratio, which may not account for subtle illumination gradients beyond the object region (e.g., color bleeding, soft shadows). Non‑spatial controls (color, intensity) use fixed learnable downsampling ratios that might under‑represent spatial detail in indirect lighting. An ablation with per‑attribute token budgets and evaluation on subsets of LightMove‑B with varying illumination complexity (e.g., single point light vs. multiple colored lights) would clarify where pruning introduces artifacts.

### Open Question 5: Synthetic‑to‑real domain gap for diverse real‑world light sources
The training data mixes synthetic rendering (with idealized white emissive lights and linear RGB processing) and real captures in a 10:1 ratio. Synthetic scenes assume simplified light sources and tone mapping, which may not cover real‑world variations such as non‑white light spectra, mixed lighting, or camera sensor noise. This domain gap could cause color shifts or intensity mismatches when editing real photos. To resolve this, a real‑world benchmark with radiometric measurements under diverse light sources (LED, incandescent, mixed) is needed, and domain adaptation techniques (e.g., cycle consistency on unpaired real images) should be explored.

## Limitations
- Missing implementation details: the exact video VAE architecture, transformer configuration, and pruning implementation are not fully specified, hindering exact replication.
- Synthetic‑real domain gap: heavy reliance on limited synthetic indoor data may not generalize to outdoor scenes or real images with complex light transport and sensor characteristics.
- Video prior suitability: video diffusion may not adequately model illumination dynamics for large light movements or extreme color shifts, leading to inconsistent shadows or highlights.
