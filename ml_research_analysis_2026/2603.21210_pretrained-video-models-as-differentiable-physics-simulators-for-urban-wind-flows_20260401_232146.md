# Pretrained Video Models as Differentiable Physics Simulators for Urban Wind Flows

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.21210  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21210  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** WinDiNet generates 112-frame wind field rollouts in ~0.3 seconds (~1000× faster than CFD) with test VRMSE 0.520 and enables gradient-based layout optimization reducing dangerous winds from 2.57% to 0.22%.

## Executive Summary
Urban wind comfort design relies heavily on computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations, which are computationally expensive and limit the exploration of design alternatives. This paper introduces WinDiNet, a differentiable surrogate model that repurposes the pretrained video diffusion model LTX-Video for fast urban wind flow prediction. WinDiNet is fine‑tuned on a dataset of 10,000 2D CFD simulations over procedurally generated building layouts, using a physics‑informed VAE adaptation and scalar conditioning on inlet speed and domain size. The model produces 112-frame wind field rollouts in approximately 0.3 seconds—about 1000 times faster than traditional CFD—while achieving a test variance‑normalized RMSE of 0.520, outperforming neural PDE solvers. Moreover, the differentiability of WinDiNet allows gradient‑based inverse optimization of building layouts, significantly reducing dangerous wind speeds (>15 m/s) from 2.57% to 0.22% and uncomfortable winds (>5 m/s) from 49.67% to 12.80%, as confirmed by ground‑truth CFD.

## Method Summary
The authors construct WinDiNet by fully fine‑tuning the pretrained video diffusion model LTX‑Video. Training data consists of 13,000 incompressible Euler simulations (10k train, 1k val, 2k test) on 256×256 grids, generated from procedural layouts: random rectangles placed within a circular city domain, with varied inlet speeds (0.1–20 m/s) and domain sizes (300–800 m). Each simulation is encoded as a video where RGB channels represent horizontal velocity (u), vertical velocity (v), and a fluid mask; values are normalized to [‑1,1]. The VAE decoder is adapted using physics‑informed losses (divergence penalty, no‑penetration at walls, distance‑weighted MSE) to ensure physical consistency, while the encoder remains frozen. The DiT transformer backbone is fine‑tuned with rectified flow matching, using scalar Fourier embeddings for inlet speed and domain size as conditioning. Inference uses 2 denoising steps and classifier‑free guidance weight 1.0. For inverse design, a differentiable rasterizer translates building center positions into soft occupancy masks; the resulting occupancy, together with inlet speed and domain size, forms the conditioning frame. The surrogate’s gradients then optimize layout parameters (rigid or morphable buildings) with regularization terms to promote feasible designs, dramatically improving wind comfort metrics.

## Key Results
- WinDiNet produces 112‑frame wind field rollouts in ~0.3 seconds, ≈1000× faster than CFD.
- Test VRMSE = 0.520, 7.6% lower than the best neural PDE baseline (RNO’s 0.563).
- Gradient‑based inverse layout optimization reduces dangerous winds (>15 m/s) from 2.57% to 0.22% and uncomfortable winds (>5 m/s) from 49.67% to 12.80%, validated on ground‑truth CFD.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
WinDiNet’s effectiveness arises from three key factors. First, LTX‑Video’s pretrained DiT backbone already encodes strong spatiotemporal correlations, allowing it to model complex fluid dynamics with relatively limited data. Second, the VAE decoder is fine‑tuned with divergence and boundary penalties, ensuring that reconstructed and generated velocity fields remain solenoidal and respect no‑penetration conditions—critical for physical plausibility. Third, scalar conditioning via Fourier embeddings enables smooth interpolation across inlet speeds and domain sizes, while the rectified flow matching objective provides stable training with few denoising steps. The fully fine‑tuned DiT captures subtle flow features like vortices and shear layers, and the entire pipeline (from rasterizer to decoder) remains differentiable, enabling gradient‑based inverse layout design.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Pretrained Video Diffusion Models**  
   *Why needed:* Leverage rich spatiotemporal priors from video generation to model complex fluid flows without massive physics datasets.  
   *Quick check:* Generate a rollout with a constant condition; verify coherent motion patterns and temporal smoothness.

2. **Physics‑Informed VAE Adaptation**  
   *Why needed:* Enforce solenoidal velocity fields and wall boundary conditions in the latent space, ensuring physical plausibility of reconstructions.  
   *Quick check:* Compute the divergence of decoded velocity fields; it should be near zero everywhere.

3. **Scalar Conditioning via Fourier Embeddings**  
   *Why needed:* Provide a smooth, high‑frequency representation of physical parameters (inlet speed, domain size) that can be ingested by the DiT.  
   *Quick check:* Interpolate between two embedded scalars and observe monotonic changes in generated flow speed.

4. **Rectified Flow Matching**  
   *Why needed:* Stabilize diffusion training by learning a deterministic velocity field that transports noise to data, allowing few‑step generation.  
   *Quick check:* Use 2 denoising steps; compare sample quality against more steps—should be similar with lower compute.

5. **Differentiable Rasterization for Inverse Design**  
   *Why needed:* Convert continuous building positions into soft occupancy masks that can be differentiated, enabling gradient‑based layout optimization.  
   *Quick check:* Perturb a building center slightly and confirm the occupancy mask changes smoothly.

6. **Procedural CFD Dataset Generation**  
   *Why needed:* Create a diverse, large‑scale training set of physically accurate urban wind simulations without manual annotation.  
   *Quick check:* Run a few simulations and validate that they are divergence‑free and respect boundary conditions.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Input (building binary mask M, inlet speed uin, domain size L) → Conditioning Frame Builder (t=0 frame with u=uin on fluid cells, v=0, B=fluid mask) → VAE Encoder (frozen) → latent z₀ → DiT (full fine‑tune, conditioned on Fourier embeddings of uin & L) → latent video sequence (112 frames) → VAE Decoder (fine‑tuned) → velocity fields (u,v) & fluid mask.  
Inverse design loop: building positions → Differentiable Rasterizer (soft occupancy) → Conditioning Frame Builder → … → Comfort loss (fraction of pixels above speed thresholds) → backpropagation to positions.

**Critical Path:** The differentiable chain for inverse design is: Rasterizer → Conditioning Frame → VAE Encoder → DiT Denoising → VAE Decoder → Loss Computation. All components must be differentiable and numerically stable for gradient‑based layout optimization.

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- *Full DiT fine‑tuning vs. LoRA:* Full fine‑tuning captures subtle flow features but requires more compute and risks overfitting; LoRA would be cheaper but possibly less expressive.  
- *Denoising steps:* 2 steps with rectified flow achieve a good speed‑accuracy trade‑off; more steps improve quality marginally but increase latency.  
- *Physics loss weighting:* Divergence and no‑penetration penalties are essential for physical fidelity but overly large weights can degrade reconstruction quality.  
- *Scalar conditioning:* Fourier embeddings provide a principled way to encode continuous parameters, but the exact number of frequencies and MLP depth are heuristic choices.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- Validation VRMSE > 0.7 suggests underfitting or poor conditioning.  
- Generated flows exhibit non‑zero divergence or velocities inside buildings → VAE or physics loss failure.  
- Inverse optimization does not improve CFD‑verified comfort metrics → surrogate gradients are noisy or the model lacks extrapolation capability.  
- Training instability (loss spikes, NaNs) often indicates too aggressive physics penalties or an unsuitable learning rate.

**First Experiments:**  
1. **Dataset sanity check:** Generate 100 procedural layouts and run a simple 2D Euler solver; visualize velocity fields to confirm divergence‑free behavior and plausible boundary layers.  
2. **VAE decoder fine‑tuning:** Train with physics losses; monitor reconstruction VRMSE and divergence; aim for VRMSE < 0.07.  
3. **Conditioning verification:** Train a minimal DiT (e.g., 2 layers) on a tiny subset; test that varying uin scales flow speed linearly and that the conditioning frame is correctly incorporated (first frame matches conditioning).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Autoregressive video diffusion models and error accumulation**  
   The paper only evaluates OFormer as a baseline; it remains unknown whether truly autoregressive models (e.g., MAGI‑1) suffer from error buildup during rollout that would impair gradient‑based inverse optimization.  
   *Resolution:* Train an autoregressive video diffusion model on the same CFD dataset, run the inverse design pipeline, and compare surrogate‑vs‑ground‑truth loss alignment, convergence speed, and final layout quality against WinDiNet.

2. **Extending WinDiNet to 3D**  
   The authors suggest a 3D extension is straightforward but expect the data bottleneck to be the main obstacle, as generating diverse 3D urban CFD simulations is computationally far more expensive. No 3D implementation exists.  
   *Resolution:* Construct a modest‑scale 3D CFD dataset, adapt the VAE and conditioning to handle height information, train a prototype 3D WinDiNet, and evaluate accuracy, speed, and memory usage.

3. **Real‑world performance on actual cities**  
   All quantitative validation uses synthetic CFD; real urban environments introduce additional complexities (terrain, vegetation, atmospheric stability) that may degrade WinDiNet’s predictions.  
   *Resolution:* Conduct a field campaign with a dense sensor network or remote sensing (e.g., lidar) to collect high‑resolution wind data; use these measurements to quantitatively evaluate WinDiNet’s speed, direction, and statistical predictions across diverse cities.

4. **Improving robustness to out‑of‑distribution conditions**  
   Training data covers inlet speeds up to 20 m/s and domain sizes ≥ 300 m; VRMSE increases for more extreme values or densely built areas. The paper does not explore remedies.  
   *Resolution:* Test data augmentation that includes extreme winds, incorporate dimensionless parameters (e.g., Reynolds number) into conditioning, or apply curriculum learning; evaluate each on held‑out extreme test cases and measure extrapolation error.

## Limitations
- **CFD solver details omitted** – The exact 2D Euler solver (discretization, pressure solver, boundary conditions) is not described, hindering faithful dataset reproduction and possibly leading to mismatched data distributions.  
- **Missing training hyperparameters** – DiT fine‑tuning specifics (learning rate, optimizer, scheduler, gradient clipping, weight decay) are not reported, risking instability or divergence when reproducing the model.  
- **Conditioning and VAE configuration unspecified** – Fourier embedding dimensions, injection mechanism into DiT, and VAE decoder learning rate/batch size are unknown, which may affect conditioning quality and latent stability.  
- **Evaluation limited to synthetic 2D flows** – Real‑world urban wind environments introduce complexities (e.g., terrain, atmospheric stability) not present in the training data; no field measurements are used to verify transferability.

## Confidence
- **Speedup (≈1000×):** High – Direct timing comparison, hardware‑independent metric.  
- **Accuracy (VRMSE 0.520):** Medium – Depends on hyperparameter fidelity; missing details could shift the result.  
- **Inverse design (wind reduction):** Medium – Relies on surrogate accuracy and optimization stability; architectural uncertainties affect reproducibility.
