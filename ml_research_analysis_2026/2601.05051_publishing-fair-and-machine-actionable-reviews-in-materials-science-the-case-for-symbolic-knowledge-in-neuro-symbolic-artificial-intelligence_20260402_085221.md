# Publishing FAIR and Machine-actionable Reviews in Materials Science: The Case for Symbolic Knowledge in Neuro-symbolic Artificial Intelligence

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.05051
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05051
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Structured ORKG comparisons enable perfect symbolic querying (100% F1) and improve LLM performance on review tables from 61.7% to 74.2% RMS F1, demonstrating the necessity of a curated symbolic layer.

## Executive Summary
Scientific review articles in materials science contain valuable insights in tabular form, but these tables are typically embedded in PDFs, making the data difficult to query and reuse. This study addresses the problem by converting 18 review tables from atomic layer deposition and etching (ALD/ALE) into structured, machine-actionable knowledge graph comparisons using the Open Research Knowledge Graph (ORKG). The researchers evaluated the approach through domain expert surveys and a benchmarking framework comparing three question-answering regimes: deterministic SPARQL queries over the ORKG graph, large language models (LLMs) given raw PDF-extracted text, and LLMs provided with the ORKG tables as CSV input. Expert ratings confirmed the structured comparisons are meaningful (average 4.54/5) and useful (4.08/5). The results show SPARQL querying achieves perfect precision and recall (100% F1), while LLMs on PDFs perform poorly (best RMS F1 61.7%). When grounded with the ORKG tables, LLM performance improves substantially (best RMS F1 74.2%), yet still falls short of symbolic perfection. The work concludes that a curated symbolic knowledge layer is indispensable for reliable, exact querying, with LLMs serving best as complementary, natural-language interfaces that leverage the structured data.

## Method Summary
The authors curated 18 review tables from nine ALD/ALE review papers into ORKG comparisons. Each original table row becomes a `contribution` entity, and each column header becomes a typed `property` in the knowledge graph, preserving provenance via linked source publications. This yields a structured dataset with stable identifiers. To benchmark querying, the team formulated 33 natural-language questions and authored corresponding SPARQL queries. Three experimental settings were evaluated: (1) symbolic SPARQL execution over the ORKG graph (gold standard); (2) LLMs (both proprietary and open-weights) provided with PDF-extracted text and tables; (3) LLMs provided with the ORKG comparisons exported as CSV. LLM-generated answer tables were compared to the SPARQL gold standards using the Relative Mapping Similarity (RMS) F1 metric, derived from DePlot. Additionally, an online survey with domain experts assessed the quality of the structured comparisons.

## Key Results
- Expert evaluation rated the ORKG comparisons as highly meaningful (4.54/5) and useful (4.08/5).
- Symbolic SPARQL querying achieved perfect 100% F1 on all benchmark questions, demonstrating lossless, deterministic retrieval.
- LLMs operating solely on PDFs reached a best RMS F1 of 61.7%; when provided with the ORKG-CSV tables, performance improved to 74.2% RMS F1, confirming the value of symbolic grounding but also revealing persistent reasoning gaps.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Structured Representation Enables Precise Querying
Converting static PDF review tables into ORKG comparison objects transforms each row into a typed contribution and each column into a property, explicitly encoding the data as subject-predicate-object triples within a knowledge graph. This lossless representation, with stable identifiers and units, allows exact filtering and aggregation. The core assumption is that tabular scientific insights can be modeled as comparable entities under a fixed schema, with provenance linked to source publications. Evidence includes the detailed mapping of Tables 2–5 from Ghazy et al. into ORKG predicates (e.g., P180031 for host material matrix) and the community traction of knowledge graphs like MatKG and ORKG. The approach breaks if the original table lacks consistency in row structure or property definitions, requiring manual curation beyond the demonstrated workflow.

### Mechanism 2: Symbolic Querying Provides Deterministic Gold Standard
SPARQL queries over the explicit graph structure execute deterministically, filtering and aggregating based on exact predicate matches and numeric comparisons. This guarantees reproducible results for a fixed dataset snapshot. In contrast, LLMs—even given the same CSV tables—must probabilistically parse the prompt and data, leading to stochastic errors in row selection, value copying, and multi-constraint logic. The core assumption is that for tasks demanding exact numeric fidelity and complete recall (e.g., "all processes with GPC ≥1 Å at ≤250°C"), the transparency and determinism of symbolic querying are irreplaceable by current neural text generators. Evidence includes the perfect 100% F1 achieved by SPARQL versus lower scores for LLMs, and the expert validation of the SPARQL-derived tables. If future neuro-symbolic architectures achieve error-free table reconstruction, the exclusive reliance on external SPARQL might be questioned, but currently the gap remains substantial.

### Mechanism 3: LLMs as Symbolically Grounded Exploratory Interfaces
When LLMs receive the structured ORKG comparison as context (Setting 3), their performance improves significantly over PDF-only querying (Setting 2). This indicates that the symbolic layer grounds the neural model, reducing hallucination and under-coverage. However, residual errors in numeric filtering, aggregation, and cross-table joins persist, showing that LLMs approximate rather than replicate symbolic execution. The core assumption is that the value of LLMs lies in natural language interaction, hypothesis generation, and explaining trends *based on* a reliable symbolic core, not in replacing that core. Evidence includes the performance jump from 61.7% to 74.2% RMS F1 and the authors' observation that symbolic grounding does not make all questions easy; small mistakes in filtering, grouping, or averaging quickly translate into missing or incorrect rows. If LLMs evolve to natively and reliably execute set-based operations, their role could shift, but current technology does not support that.

## Foundational Learning
### Knowledge Graphs (RDF Triples)
- **Why needed**: The ORKG representations are fundamentally RDF graphs; understanding entities (papers, materials, processes), properties (predicates), and values is essential to grasp how SPARQL queries traverse the data.
- **Quick check**: How would you represent the statement "Al2O3 ALD uses TMA and H2O with a GPC of 1.1 Å/cycle" as a set of RDF triples in the ORKG context?

### SPARQL Query Logic
- **Why needed**: The paper's gold standard and the claim of deterministic querying depend on SPARQL; one must understand SELECT, WHERE, FILTER, and OPTIONAL to see why it is precise and how LLMs struggle to mimic its logic.
- **Quick check**: Write a SPARQL query fragment to find all contributions where the deposition temperature is between 200 and 300°C and the GPC is greater than 0.5 Å.

### LLM Stochasticity & Hallucination
- **Why needed**: The performance gap hinges on LLMs being non-deterministic and prone to generating plausible but incorrect or misaligned table entries, especially when required to perform multi-step numeric reasoning from unstructured inputs.
- **Quick check**: Why might an LLM, given a CSV table of ALD processes, generate a row with a plausible-sounding precursor but an incorrect numeric value for temperature when asked for "all processes below 250°C"?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Source PDFs -> Curation Interface (ORKG) -> ORKG Backend (Graph Store) -> SPARQL Engine; Source PDFs -> PDF Extractor -> LLM Inference API; ORKG Backend -> CSV Export -> LLM Inference API; All LLM outputs -> Evaluation Harness -> RMS scores.

**Critical path**: The overall accuracy depends first on the integrity of the curation step (Curation Interface). A poorly defined property or mis-linked contribution breaks all downstream queries. The evaluation path runs: SPARQL result (gold) vs. LLM input (PDF-extracted text or ORKG-CSV) -> LLM output table -> RMS score.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Determinism vs. Flexibility: SPARQL offers exact, reproducible results for well-formed queries but requires learning a query language. LLMs offer natural language interaction but introduce variability.
- Curation Effort vs. Automation: Manual ORKG comparison modeling is labor-intensive per table but yields high-quality, reusable data. Fully automated PDF extraction is scalable but currently inaccurate for precise scientific details.
- Context Length: Providing full PDFs ensures no data loss but may introduce noise; RAG focuses context but risks missing key table rows if retrieval fails.

**Failure signatures**:
- Setting 1 (SPARQL): Fails only if the underlying ORKG comparison is incorrectly modeled or the query logic is flawed; errors are deterministic bugs.
- Setting 2 (LLM on PDF): Fails via misreading table structures, hallucinating numbers, omitting qualifying rows, or misinterpreting unit scales; errors are stochastic.
- Setting 3 (LLM on ORKG-CSV): Fails when the model mis-parses CSV headers, misapplies filters (e.g., ≤250°C as <250), or aggregates incorrectly; errors are less frequent than Setting 2 but still present.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Replicate SPARQL Baseline**: Execute a provided SPARQL query (e.g., Q.2) against the ORKG SPARQL endpoint for one comparison (e.g., R1469158) and verify the output matches the paper's gold-standard table.
2. **PDF-Only LLM Query**: Using the open-weights pipeline, extract PDF text/tables for a review paper, feed a detailed natural-language query to an LLM (e.g., `llama-3.3-70b-instruct`) in full-context mode, and compute RMS against the SPARQL gold; expect low scores due to table parsing issues.
3. **ORKG-CSV LLM Query**: Export an ORKG comparison as CSV, provide it with the same query to the same LLM, and compute RMS; expect substantially higher RMS than Experiment 2, though likely not 100%.

## Limitations
- Domain specificity: The study focuses exclusively on atomic layer deposition/etching review tables; transfer to other materials subfields or other scientific domains remains unvalidated.
- Curation scalability: Manual ORKG comparison modeling, while accurate, is labor-intensive and may not scale to large volumes of review literature.
- Model dependence: The reported performance metrics for LLMs are tied to specific architectures and prompting strategies; results may vary with different models or future iterations.

## Confidence
- **High**: The structured symbolic layer (SPARQL over ORKG) yields perfect deterministic querying for the curated benchmark, confirming lossless representation of the 18 review tables.
- **Medium**: LLMs benefit from symbolic grounding (improved RMS F1), but the exact performance gap (61.7% → 74.2%) is model-specific and may not generalize across all LLMs or prompting techniques.
- **Low**: Broader claims about the applicability to "all review tables" or general scientific reviews are speculative; the approach's effectiveness outside the narrow ALD/E domain and specific curation workflow is unproven.

## Next Checks
1. **Replicate SPARQL Gold Standard**: Execute the provided SPARQL queries against the published RDF dumps for at least three ORKG comparisons to confirm the gold-standard tables match those reported in the paper.
2. **Test Generalization to New Domain**: Apply the same curation pipeline to review tables from a distinct materials subfield (e.g., battery electrolytes), then benchmark an LLM (e.g., `llama-3.3-70b`) on both PDF-only and ORKG-CSV settings to see if the ~12.5% RMS F1 improvement holds.
3. **Analyze Residual LLM Errors in Setting 3**: For the rows that the best LLM still misses or misreports on ORKG-CSV, manually categorize errors (numeric threshold misinterpretation, aggregation mistakes, column misalignment, omitted rows) to pinpoint unsolved reasoning gaps.
