# Tracking the emergence of linguistic structure in self-supervised models learning from speech

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.02043
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02043
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Self-supervised speech models acquire acoustic, phonetic, syllabic, lexical, and syntactic representations in a temporally ordered sequence, with the pre-training objective's abstraction level determining the parallelism of these learning trajectories.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates when and how different levels of linguistic structure emerge during the self-supervised training of speech models. By training six Wav2Vec2 and HuBERT models on 831 hours of Dutch speech and probing their internal representations across training checkpoints, the authors find that linguistic structures develop in a consistent temporal order: acoustic alignment improves first, followed by phones, syllables, and words, with syntax emerging last. The design of the pre-training objective—specifically, the use of iteratively refined, higher-order pseudo-labels in HuBERT-I2—induces a more parallel development across structural levels compared to contrastive or lower-level objectives. Most linguistic representations plateau after ~100k steps, except for syntactic dependencies which continue improving. All speech-trained models significantly outperform a non-speech baseline, confirming that the learned representations encode genuine linguistic structure.

## Method Summary
The authors trained three families of base-sized Transformer models (Wav2Vec2, HuBERT-I1, HuBERT-I2; two random seeds each) on a combined 831-hour Dutch speech corpus (CGN, MLS, CommonVoice). HuBERT variants used iterative pseudo-labeling: HuBERT-I1 with MFCC-derived labels, HuBERT-I2 with labels from HuBERT-I1's intermediate layer. Checkpoints were saved at regular intervals up to 200k steps. A suite of representational probes—LDA clustering (silhouette score), Representational Similarity Analysis (Pearson's r), ABX discrimination (accuracy), and a linear structural probe (UUAS)—was applied to mean-pooled model embeddings for nine linguistic units (acoustic, phone, syllable form/type, word form, homophones, semantic, POS, syntax). Analysis used a single male speaker's subset of MLS with time-aligned annotations. Probe scores were aggregated across layers (CNN output through 12 Transformer layers) and training steps, with best-layer scores tracked over time and fitted with sigmoid curves to characterize emergence dynamics.

## Key Results
- Linguistic structures exhibit distinct layerwise organizations: acoustic/phone information peaks in early layers, while lexical/syntactic information peaks in middle layers for Wav2Vec2 and HuBERT-I1, but earlier for HuBERT-I2. Final layers show a drop for most structures, suggesting specialization for the acoustic pre-training objective.
- The pre-training objective controls learning parallelism: HuBERT-I2, with higher-order iteratively refined pseudo-labels, shows tightly coupled emergence trajectories across structural levels, unlike the more sequential patterns in Wav2Vec2 and HuBERT-I1.
- Learning trajectories are consistent across seeds: acoustic alignment saturates within ~10k steps, followed by phones/syllables/words (~10k–30k), with syntax emerging last (~50k). Most structures plateau by 100k steps; only syntactic dependency parsing (UUAS) continues improving beyond that.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Sequential Emergence Driven by Abstraction and Integration
**Claim:** Linguistic structures emerge in a temporally ordered sequence (acoustic → phonetic → syllabic → lexical → syntactic) during self-supervised training.
**Mechanism:** The model must first establish a stable acoustic alignment (mapping raw waveforms to consistent features) before higher-order categorical structures can be reliably decoded. This creates an optimization dependency where early learning is dominated by reducing acoustic error.
**Evidence anchors:** Acoustic alignment scores rise sharply in the first 10k steps; syntactic information emerges last (~50k steps). This pattern holds across multiple seeds and probe types.
**Break condition:** Interventions that disrupt early acoustic learning (e.g., freezing initial layers or altering the acoustic objective) should prevent or delay the development of higher-level structures.

### Mechanism 2: Objective Abstraction Level Controls Trajectory Parallelism
**Claim:** The abstraction level of the pre-training target (e.g., MFCCs vs. higher-layer representations) determines whether learning trajectories are sequential or parallel.
**Mechanism:** Iterative pseudo-label refinement (HuBERT-I2) uses targets that already incorporate some acoustic abstraction, compressing the learning signal and prompting the model to optimize for multiple structural levels simultaneously rather than sequentially.
**Evidence anchors:** HuBERT-I2 shows markedly greater parallelism across probing curves for phones, syllables, words, and syntax, diverging from the sequential patterns in Wav2Vec2 and HuBERT-I1.
**Break condition:** Using lower-level pseudo-labels (pure MFCCs) reverts trajectories to sequential; using even more abstract targets (e.g., word embeddings) might force tighter coupling.

### Mechanism 3: Late-Layer Specialization for Acoustic Objectives
**Claim:** The final Transformer layers specialize for the pre-training loss (e.g., contrastive or masked prediction), causing a drop in linguistic probe scores for abstract categories.
**Mechanism:** The loss function imposes a representational constraint favoring acoustically predictive features, which conflicts with optimal encoding of symbolic categories like syntax or semantics, leading to a performance dip in the last layers.
**Evidence anchors:** Lexical and syntactic probes peak in middle layers and decline in final layers for Wav2Vec2/HuBERT-I1; HuBERT-I2 maintains high scores for lower-level units but still drops for semantics/syntax.
**Break condition:** A multi-task loss explicitly rewarding linguistic structure (e.g., including a syntactic parsing objective) should mitigate or reverse the late-layer drop.

## Foundational Learning
- **Self-Supervised Learning (SSL) Objectives**
  - *Why needed:* The paper’s core comparison hinges on how different SSL losses (contrastive vs. masked prediction with varying pseudo-label quality) shape internal representations.
  - *Quick check:* What is the key difference between Wav2Vec2's contrastive loss and HuBERT's masked prediction loss, and how does iterative pseudo-label refinement in HuBERT-I2 change the prediction target?
- **Representational Probing**
  - *Why needed:* All conclusions derive from probing (clustering, RSA, ABX, structural probe), which requires understanding each probe’s measurement (e.g., silhouette vs. UUAS) and limitations.
  - *Quick check:* How does an LDA clustering probe for syllable types differ in what it measures from an RSA probe for acoustic alignment to MFCCs?
- **Linguistic Hierarchy (Timescale & Abstraction)**
  - *Why needed:* The paper frames hypotheses around a hierarchy (acoustic < phone < syllable < word < syntax) based on integration window and symbolic abstraction.
  - *Quick check:* Why might syntactic dependency structure require a longer integration timescale than word-form clustering in a sliding-window Transformer?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Raw audio -> 7-layer CNN encoder -> 12-layer Transformer backbone (T1–T12) -> Pre-training head (varies: contrastive quantizer or masked prediction over pseudo-labels). Probes attach to frozen embeddings from CNN output, projection layer, and each Transformer layer.

**Critical path:**
1. Load a pre-trained checkpoint and analysis dataset (time-aligned phones/syllables/words/sentences).
2. Feed audio through model, extract mean-pooled embeddings per linguistic unit.
3. For each probe (e.g., LDA phone clustering), train on a held-out training split, evaluate on test split; repeat per layer and checkpoint.
4. Aggregate scores across 5 data folds; repeat for all models/checkpoints.
5. Fit sigmoid curves to best-layer scores over time to characterize emergence trajectories.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Probe sensitivity vs. specificity:** Clustering probes may conflate speaker variance with phonetic categories; RSA may capture non-linguistic correlations (e.g., word length). Multiple probes are used for triangulation.
- **Layer analysis granularity:** Analyzing all 14 layers (CNN + 12 Transformer) is essential because peak locations differ by architecture; averaging would obscure patterns.
- **Training duration:** Most structures plateau by 100k steps; extended training (200k) yields minimal gains except syntax, representing a computational trade-off.
- **Language specificity:** All models trained on Dutch; findings may not generalize to typologically diverse languages without testing.

**Failure signatures:**
- **Unstable training:** Diverging loss curves or degrading WER past 100k steps (as seen in one HuBERT-I2 seed) invalidates trajectory comparisons.
- **Probe confusion:** If a "semantic alignment" probe (FastText RSA) tracks word-form scores, it may reflect orthographic similarity rather than semantics. The paper attempts controls (within-POS, excluding same-word pairs).
- **Non-speech baseline failure:** The baseline should show near-chance scores for all linguistic probes. If it shows high syntactic UUAS, the probe may be capturing sequential regularities, not syntax.
- **Layerwise pattern inversion:** Syntax peaking in layer 1 would indicate training failure or probe invalidity.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Validate training stability** by plotting train/valid loss curves for all six models; flag unstable seeds (e.g., HuBERT-I2 seed 1).
2. **Replicate layerwise pattern** at 100k steps for one model (e.g., Wav2Vec2 seed 1): compute all 9 probes per layer; expect early peaks for acoustic/phone, middle peaks for lexical/syntax, final-layer drop. Confirm non-speech baseline is lower for all linguistic probes.
3. **Track emergence order** by plotting best-layer scores over checkpoints (1k–100k) for the same model; fit sigmoids; verify acoustic rises first (~10k), phones/syllables/words next (~10k–30k), syntax last (~50k). Repeat for a second seed to confirm consistency.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Cross-linguistic generalization:** Does the observed hierarchy of emergence (acoustic → syntax) generalize to languages with diverse phonological and morphosyntactic properties? The current study is limited to Dutch.
- **Causal dependencies:** What is the causal relationship between lower-level (word form) and higher-level (syntax) representation acquisition? The study reports correlational trajectories; interventions (e.g., suppressing word-form encoding) are needed.
- **Top-down interactions in HuBERT-I2:** In iteratively refined models, do higher-layer contextual representations facilitate encoding of lower-level units in later layers? This remains unexplored.

## Limitations
- **Single-language/speaker bias:** Analysis focuses on one male speaker from MLS; generalizability across languages and speaker diversity is uncertain.
- **Probe confounds:** Clustering probes may capture speaker/channel variance; RSA may reflect non-linguistic correlations; the structural probe (linear UUAS) may not capture full hierarchical syntax.
- **Simplified dynamics:** Sigmoid curve fitting may oversimplify complex, potentially non-monotonic learning trajectories, especially for syntax beyond 100k steps where data is sparse.

## Confidence
- **Temporal emergence order (acoustic → phones → syntax):** High. Consistent across seeds, models, and probe types.
- **Parallelism effect of HuBERT-I2 objective:** Medium. Clear correlational pattern, but not causally isolated from other implementation differences.
- **Layerwise specialization & late-layer drop:** Medium-Low. Pattern is robust, but interpretation is confounded by probe limitations and the possibility that late layers encode information in ways inaccessible to linear probes.

## Next Checks
1. **Multilingual replication:** Repeat the trajectory and layerwise analysis on at least two typologically diverse languages (e.g., tonal vs. analytic) to test universality of the acoustic→syntax order and parallelism effects.
2. **Speaker adversarial probing:** Re-run categorical clustering probes after applying a speaker-adversarial loss to embeddings to test whether reported category separability persists after removing speaker variance.
3. **Causal ablation of pseudo-label abstraction:** Train controlled HuBERT variants where iterative refinement halts at different layers (e.g., T3, T6, T9) while keeping other hyperparameters identical, to directly test whether target abstraction alone drives parallelism.
