# SCOPE: Selective Conformal Optimized Pairwise LLM Judging

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.13110
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13110
- **Reference count**: 32
- **Primary result**: SCOPE provides finite-sample guarantees on error rates for accepted pairwise LLM judgments while accepting up to 2.4× more judgments than naive baselines at the same risk level.

## Executive Summary
SCOPE tackles the unreliability of LLM judges in pairwise evaluation, which suffer from miscalibration and position bias. It introduces a conformal calibration framework that sets an acceptance threshold to guarantee the error rate among accepted judgments is bounded by a user-specified level α under exchangeability. A core innovation is Bidirectional Preference Entropy (BPE), a bias‑neutral uncertainty signal derived by querying the judge in both response orders and aggregating the probabilities. Experiments across MT‑Bench, RewardBench, and Chatbot Arena with Qwen and Llama models show that BPE improves uncertainty quality, enabling SCOPE to achieve empirical risk tightly around the target (e.g., 0.097–0.099 at α=0.10) while maintaining high coverage, substantially outperforming baseline methods.

## Method Summary
SCOPE operates in three stages: First, for each pairwise instance (query *q*, responses *r_A*, *r_B*), it computes a bias‑neutral preference estimate *p̄* by querying the judge twice—once in forward order *(q, r_A, r_B)* and once in reverse *(q, r_B, r_A)*—and averaging the extracted probabilities *p_fwd* and *p_rev* for preferring *r_A*. Second, it defines an uncertainty score *s(x)* as the binary entropy of *p̄*. Third, it calibrates an acceptance threshold *λ̂* on a held‑out set by solving a constrained optimization: choose the largest *λ* such that the summed linearized loss ∑ *S(x_i,λ)*(*E(x_i)*−α) ≤ −1, where *S* indicates acceptance and *E* is the error indicator. At test time, a judgment is accepted if *s(x)* ≤ *λ̂*, and the final prediction is the sign of *p̄*−0.5. The conformal procedure guarantees that, under exchangeability, the conditional error rate of accepted predictions does not exceed α.

## Key Results
- SCOPE achieves empirical risk tightly aligned with the target α (e.g., 0.097–0.099 at α=0.10) across all benchmarks and model scales.
- Coverage is substantially higher than heuristic baselines: up to 2.4× more judgments accepted on MT‑Bench with Qwen‑7B under the same risk constraint.
- BPE outperforms predictive probability, verbalized confidence, and simulated annotators in uncertainty quality (higher AUROC/AUPRC, lower ECE) on allJudge/model combinations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Conformal Risk Control with Linearized Loss
SCOPE transforms the goal of controlling the false discovery rate (FDR) into a budget‑balancing optimization over a calibration set. The constraint ∑ *S(x_i,λ)*(*E(x_i)*−α) ≤ −1 ensures that, if the calibration and test data are exchangeable, the expected conditional error among accepted instances is ≤ α. The linearized loss treats each correct accepted prediction as contributing a safety margin of −α and each error as consuming 1−α, while the finite‑sample correction of −1 accounts for the randomness in the estimated threshold. This yields a distribution‑free, finite‑sample guarantee (Theorem 2.1). The method breaks if exchangeability fails (e.g., significant distribution shift) or if the binary error indicator assumption is violated.

### Mechanism 2: Bias-Neutral Uncertainty via Bidirectional Preference Entropy (BPE)
Position bias in LLM judges—where the order of presented responses influences the verdict—is mitigated by enforcing permutation invariance. For a given pair, SCOPE queries the judge in both orders, producing *p_fwd* (probability of preferring *r_A* when it is presented first) and *p_rev* (probability of preferring *r_A* when it is presented second). The aggregated preference *p̄* = (*p_fwd* + *p_rev*)/2 neutralizes any symmetric positional effect: if the judge is unbiased or biased in a fixed direction, *p̄* converges to the true underlying preference; inconsistency across orderings (e.g., *p_fwd* high, *p_rev* low) pushes *p̄* toward 0.5. The uncertainty score *s(x)* = Entropy(*p̄*) is high when *p̄* ≈ 0.5, signalling that the judge’s net preference is weak due to bias rather than genuine ambiguity. BPE fails if position bias is not the dominant nuisance or if other biases (length, self‑preference) remain unchecked.

### Mechanism 3: BPE as a Strong Selection Signal for Conformal Calibration
Because BPE’s entropy better correlates with true judgment error than baselines (as shown by AUROC/ECE improvements), it provides a more discriminative scoring function *s(x)*. During conformal calibration, this superior ranking allows the threshold *λ̂* to be set lower (i.e., more permissive) while still satisfying the finite‑sample risk constraint. Consequently, SCOPE accepts a larger fraction of judgments at a fixed α. The coverage gain is most pronounced for weaker judges (e.g., Qwen‑7B), where position bias is likely more severe and the baseline uncertainty scores are more miscalibrated. If BPE’s ranking quality degrades in a new domain, the coverage advantage diminishes.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Conformal Prediction & Risk Control**  
  *Why needed*: Provides the statistical machinery for finite‑sample, distribution‑free error guarantees on selectively accepted predictions. SCOPE adapts the linear expectation constraint approach to pairwise judging.  
  *Quick check*: How does reformulating FDR control as a linearized loss constraint enable a finite‑sample sufficient condition?

- **Concept: Exchangeability**  
  *Why needed*: The core validity guarantee (Theorem 2.1) requires that the calibration and test sequences are exchangeable—a weaker assumption than i.i.d. but still critical.  
  *Quick check*: Why is exchangeability sufficient for the proof, and what practical scenarios might violate it?

- **Concept: Pairwise Preference Aggregation & Permutation Invariance**  
  *Why needed*: To neutralize position bias, the uncertainty score must be invariant to which response is presented first. BPE achieves this by averaging preferences from both orderings.  
  *Quick check*: How does aggregating *p_fwd* and *p_rev* into *p̄* ensure the final preference and entropy are order‑invariant?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:  
  LLM Judge Core -> Bidirectional Scorer -> Calibrator -> Selective Predictor  
  Data flow: Input pair -> [Bidirectional Scorer: compute *p_fwd*, *p_rev*, *p̄*, *s(x)*] -> (Calibration: [Calibrator] solves for *λ̂*; Testing: compare *s(x)* to *λ̂*) -> Decision (accept/abstain + label)

- **Critical path**: The bidirectional scoring step is foundational; its output feeds both calibration (to learn *λ̂*) and test‑time selection. The calibrator requires ground‑truth labels; the selective predictor relies on the calibrated *λ̂* and the computed *s(x)*.

- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - BPE cost vs. bias mitigation: two forward passes versus one. The tradeoff is justified by improved uncertainty quality and coverage.  
  - Coverage vs. risk: controlled by α; SCOPE’s optimization maximizes coverage subject to the risk budget.  
  - Calibration set size: larger *n* stabilizes *λ̂* but leaves fewer samples for testing.

- **Failure signatures**:  
  1. Empirical risk > α on test set: likely exchangeability violation or off‑by‑one error in the constraint.  
  2. Very low coverage across all α: BPE scores uninformative or judge uniformly poor.  
  3. High variance in coverage/risk across splits: calibration set too small or uncertainty signal unstable.  
  4. BPE does not improve AUROC/ECE: position bias not dominant in the dataset.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Extending beyond binary pairwise settings**: How can SCOPE’s selective guarantees be adapted to multi‑response ranking, rubric‑based scoring, or interactive critique? The current binary preference formulation does not trivially generalize to ordinal outcomes or multi‑dimensional scores.

2. **Black‑box applicability**: Can BPE be approximated when only textual verdicts (no token probabilities) are available? BPE requires logits for *p_fwd* and *p_rev*; APIs that return only final labels or verbalized confidence break the core mechanism.

3. **Sensitivity to exchangeability violations**: How do distribution shifts between calibration and test data (e.g., new domains, judge drift) affect the finite‑sample risk guarantee? The paper assumes exchangeability but does not analyze robustness under shift.

4. **Neutralizing other biases**: Beyond position bias, how to address length bias, self‑preference, or verbosity? BPE only handles order permutation; complementary techniques are needed for other systematic errors.

## Limitations
- SCOPE’s guarantee depends on exchangeability; performance may degrade under distribution shift (e.g., cross‑benchmark evaluation).
- BPE specifically targets position bias; other biases (length, self‑preference) remain unaddressed.
- Empirical validation is limited to Qwen/Llama judges on three pairwise benchmarks; generalizability to other models and tasks is uncertain.
- The paper lacks precise tokenization details for extracting "A"/"B" probabilities, hindering exact replication.

## Confidence
- **SCOPE’s risk control**: High – built on established conformal theory; empirical risk consistently near target α.
- **BPE uncertainty quality**: Medium‑High – strong AUROC/ECE gains on tested setups, but not yet benchmarked against diverse bias‑mitigation alternatives.
- **Coverage gains**: Medium‑High – consistent improvements across experiments, though dependent on BPE’s superiority.
- **General applicability**: Medium – mechanism is general, but empirical support is narrow (few models, pairwise only).
