# On the Complexity of Optimal Graph Rewiring for Oversmoothing and Oversquashing in Graph Neural Networks

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.26140  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26140  
- **Reference count:** 25  
- **Primary result:** Exact graph rewiring to mitigate oversmoothing (minimizing the second eigenvalue of the normalized propagation matrix) and oversquashing (maximizing conductance) is NP‑complete via reduction from Minimum Bisection, justifying the use of heuristic methods.

## Executive Summary
This paper studies the computational complexity of optimizing graph topology to address two fundamental challenges in graph neural networks (GNNs): oversmoothing, where node representations become indistinguishable as depth increases, and oversquashing, where information from distant nodes fails to propagate effectively. The authors formalize these as two optimization problems: GROC (minimizing the second eigenvalue of the normalized propagation matrix) and GROS (maximizing graph conductance). They prove both problems are NP‑complete by providing polynomial‑time reductions from the Minimum Bisection problem, a known NP‑complete problem. The reductions use expander graph embeddings to preserve the relationship between a graph’s bisection width and the target spectral or conductance measures. Since exact optimization is intractable in the worst case, the results provide a theoretical foundation for the widespread use of polynomial‑time heuristic rewiring methods such as SDRF and DIGL in practice.

The work also establishes that these problems belong to NP because a proposed rewiring (with a bounded number of edge modifications) and the corresponding objective value can be verified efficiently via standard algorithms (e.g., computing conductance with max‑flow or isolating polynomial roots for eigenvalues). The hardness holds even when no edges may be modified (K=0), reinforcing the intrinsic difficulty of structure optimization. Overall, the paper delineates fundamental limits on improving GNN performance via graph rewiring and points to the necessity of approximation or heuristic algorithms.

## Method Summary
The methodological core is a reduction from Minimum Bisection to both GROC and GROS. Starting from any graph H, the authors apply an instance scaling to ensure the bisection bound B′ satisfies certain size constraints (B′ = O(n′)). They then embed H′ into a 3‑regular graph G by attaching a set U of |V(H′)| vertices and connecting U to V(H′) using a strong expander. This “expander embedding” ensures that any bisection of H of size ≤ B′ corresponds to a cut in G with very low conductance (or high second eigenvalue), and conversely any low‑conductance cut yields a small bisection in H after balancing.

To establish NP‑completeness, the authors also show that the problems are in NP: given a candidate set of edge additions/deletions (within budget K), one can compute conductance via polynomial‑time min‑cut algorithms or determine the second eigenvalue by isolating roots of the characteristic polynomial and comparing to a rational threshold τ. Together, the reduction and membership arguments yield NP‑hardness even for the decision versions of GROC and GROS.

## Key Results
- GROC and GROS are NP‑complete, even when no edge modifications are allowed (K=0).  
- The reductions rely on expander graph embeddings, showing that bisection properties can be encoded via conductance or spectral gap.  
- These hardness results justify polynomial‑time heuristics (e.g., SDRF, DIGL) and indicate the need for approximation algorithms.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: Reduction from Minimum Bisection**  
- *Claim:* GROC and GROS are NP‑hard because they can encode the Minimum Bisection problem.  
- *Mechanism:* Transform an instance (H, B) into a graph G via an expander embedding such that G has low conductance (or high second eigenvalue) iff H has a bisection of size ≤ B.  
- *Core assumption:* Minimum Bisection is NP‑complete; the expander preserves bisection width within constant factors.  
- *Evidence anchors:* Theorem 4 (GROC) and Theorem 6 (GROS) detail the reductions; Lemma 2 (Expander Embedding) and Lemma 3 (Instance Scaling) provide the construction.  
- *Break condition:* The embedding must use a sufficiently strong expander; otherwise the correspondence between cuts in H and conductance/spectral gap in G fails.

**Mechanism 2: Expander Embedding Properties**  
- *Claim:* Expander embeddings translate combinatorial cuts into spectral/conductance guarantees.  
- *Mechanism:* For H with n vertices, construct a 3‑regular graph G by adding n vertices U and connecting V and U via a strong expander. Cuts that separate V into two parts while including either all or none of U have conductance ≤ c₁·|δH(S)|/n + c₁. Conversely, any cut with conductance ≤ ε yields a cut in H of size ≤ c₂·ε·n after balancing.  
- *Core assumption:* The expander’s high expansion forces low‑conductance cuts to primarily involve vertices from V.  
- *Evidence:* Lemma 2 states the construction and its properties.  
- *Break condition:* If the expander is not sufficiently expanding, a low‑conductance cut might include many U vertices, breaking the link to a small bisection in H.

**Mechanism 3: Membership in NP**  
- *Claim:* GROC and GROS are in NP because conductance and the second eigenvalue of the propagation matrix can be verified efficiently.  
- *Mechanism:* Conductance via polynomial‑time min‑cut; the second eigenvalue of the normalized propagation matrix via root isolation of its characteristic polynomial and comparison to a rational threshold τ.  
- *Core assumption:* Standard graph‑algorithmic tools suffice for unweighted undirected graphs.  
- *Evidence:* Theorem 4 uses min‑cut; Theorem 6 uses root isolation.  
- *Break condition:* Weighted or directed graphs would complicate verification, but the paper restricts to unweighted undirected cases.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Input graph G=(V,E) with budget K and threshold (τ or ϕ₀) → preprocessing (instance scaling) → core transformation (expander embedding) → threshold computation → verification module (conductance/min‑cut or eigenvalue/root isolation).  
- **Critical path**:
  1. Scale Minimum Bisection instance (H,B) to (H′,B′) with B′ = O(n′) and B′ ≥ 2n′ (Lemma 3).  
  2. Embed H′ into a 3‑regular graph G using a fixed strong expander (Lemma 2).  
  3. Set K=0 and compute threshold: for GROC, ϕ₀ = 1 − c₁·(B′+n′)/n′; for GROS, τ = 1 − 2c₁·(B′+n′)/n′ − ε.  
  4. Prove equivalence: (H′,B′) has a bisection ≤ B′ ⇔ ϕ(G) < ϕ₀ (NO instance) or λ₂(P) > τ.  
  5. The reverse direction uses the balancing property of the embedding and Cheeger’s inequality.  
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Expander strength: stronger expanders yield smaller constants (c₁,c₂,c₃) but may increase graph size; constants must satisfy c₁ < 1/6, c₂c₃ < 1/2, c₃ < 1.  
  - K=0 vs K>0: Hardness holds even without edge modifications; allowing K>0 may ease practical optimization but worst‑case hardness persists.  
  - Objective choice: Conductance (global bottleneck) vs spectral gap (diffusion rate). They are linked via Cheeger but not equivalent; spectral‑gap reduction needs tighter bounds.  
  - Approximation: Known inapproximability for conductance (Ω(√log n)) and spectral gap (no constant factor) suggests heuristics are necessary.  
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Instance scaling fails to keep B′ within required range (B′ < n′/2), causing ϕ₀ ≤ 1/2 and breaking forward direction.  
  - Expander embedding does not guarantee every low‑conductance cut corresponds to a small bisection; balancing step might require increasing edge count significantly.  
  - Eigenvalue verification might suffer numerical instability on huge graphs, though theoretically polynomial.  
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Implement the reduction for small graphs (n ≤ 10). Enumerate all bisections of H, construct G using a 3‑regular Ramanujan expander, exhaustively search all K‑edge modifications, and verify the equivalence between Minimum Bisection and GROC/GROS.  
  2. Apply heuristics SDRF and DIGL to barbell graphs (known bottlenecks). Measure improvement in conductance and spectral gap after a fixed budget K; compare against theoretical optimum from Experiment 1.  
  3. Study the tradeoff: for a given graph, vary K and record (ϕ, λ₂) pairs. Plot the Pareto frontier and assess how closely heuristics approach it; also test sensitivity to expander strength by using different expander families in the reduction.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper does not explicitly enumerate open questions or future work. However, the hardness results naturally suggest several directions for further investigation. For instance, extending the NP‑completeness to directed or weighted graphs remains an open problem. Moreover, the lack of efficient exact algorithms motivates the design of approximation algorithms with provable guarantees for GROC and GROS. Finally, understanding the practical impact of these hardness results on specific families of graphs (e.g., planar graphs, trees) could guide the development of more effective heuristics.

## Limitations
- The reduction relies on expander embeddings with specific constants; explicit constructions may not achieve required bounds for finite graphs.  
- The argument that any improvement must target the hidden bisection cut is delicate; rewiring could create new bottlenecks.  
- Results apply only to undirected unweighted graphs; directed or weighted rewiring is not covered.
