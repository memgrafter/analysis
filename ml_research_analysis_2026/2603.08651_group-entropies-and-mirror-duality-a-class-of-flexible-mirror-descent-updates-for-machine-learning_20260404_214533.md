# Group Entropies and Mirror Duality: A Class of Flexible Mirror Descent Updates for Machine Learning

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.08651
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08651
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Dual Mirror Descent (DMD) achieves near-perfect sparsity recovery (IoU=1.0) in 2–15 iterations on large-scale simplex-constrained quadratic programming, outperforming standard and generalized Exponentiated Gradient, with robust convergence under noise and ill-conditioning due to bounded potential function curvature.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a flexible family of Mirror Descent (MD) algorithms by leveraging group entropies and formal group theory. It defines group-theoretical link functions—generalized logarithms and exponentials—parameterized by multi-variable formal group laws, and proposes “mirror duality” to interchange a link function with its inverse via learning rate scaling. The resulting Dual Mirror Descent (DMD) algorithm uses the convex group exponential branch as its primary update. On large-scale simplex-constrained quadratic programs with planted sparse optima, DMD recovers support almost instantly, reduces the Frank-Wolfe duality gap orders of magnitude faster than EG and GEG, and remains stable under high noise and extreme ill-conditioning. Theoretical analysis attributes this to DMD’s uniformly bounded curvature (condition number ≤ e) on the simplex, whereas GEG’s curvature diverges near the boundary, causing small step sizes and oscillation.

## Method Summary
The focus is on simplex-constrained quadratic programming (SCQP): minimize a quadratic loss over the probability simplex. Three MD variants are compared: (1) standard Exponentiated Gradient (EG) using the Shannon entropy geometry (log/exp), (2) Generalized EG (GEG) that replaces the link with a Tsallis-type group logarithm (concave), and (3) the proposed Dual Mirror Descent (DMD) which uses the corresponding group exponential (convex) as the primary link, falling back to the logarithm only when the dual update’s argument becomes negative. All algorithms use a centered gradient (subtracting the weighted average) and an ℓ₁-normalization step. The key innovation is the use of formal group laws to generate a broad family of link functions and the mirror duality principle that mathematically relates the concave and convex branches under a specific learning-rate scaling, allowing a seamless switch between them.

## Key Results
- DMD achieves perfect support recovery (IoU=1.0) within 2–15 iterations on planted sparse SCQP, while EG fails to recover sparsity and GEG converges slowly.
- DMD reduces the relative Frank-Wolfe duality gap by orders of magnitude faster than EG and GEG; its convergence is nearly independent of problem dimension (n up to 5×10⁴) and condition number (κ up to 10⁷).
- DMD remains robust under high noise (SNR >5 dB) and extreme ill-conditioning (κ≤10⁷), whereas EG deteriorates significantly; theoretically, DMD’s potential function has condition number ≤ e on the simplex, while GEG’s smoothness constant becomes unbounded near the boundary.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1:** Group entropies supply infinitely many multi-parametric, strictly concave/convex “link” functions via formal group theory, enabling flexible adaptation of the Bregman divergence geometry in Mirror Descent. A commutative formal group law Φ(x,y) defines a group logarithm log_G through the functional equation log_G(xy)=Φ(log_G(x),log_G(y)); its inverse, the group exponential exp_G, is constructed via the Lazard series. Setting f(w)=∇F(w) with F(w)=∑ h(w_i) and h′=log_G (or exp_G) yields the MD update. The multi-parameter nature (e.g., Tsallis q, Kaniadakis κ) decouples interior and boundary behavior. Core assumption: log_G is strictly increasing and concave; exp_G is strictly increasing and convex on w_i>0. Evidence: the paper’s Definition 4 and abstract; follow-on work using specific group logarithms. Break condition: if log_G loses concavity (violating a_k > (k+1)a_{k+1}) or is not strictly increasing in the chosen regime, F is no longer strictly convex and MD derivation fails.

**Mechanism 2:** Mirror duality permits interchanging the link function with its inverse (log_G ↔ exp_G) in the MD update, subject to a learning rate scaling, thereby swapping the geometric curvature (concave vs. convex) while preserving the solution path. The MD update is w^{t+1}=f^{-1}(f(w^t)−η∇L(w^t)). If f=exp_G (convex), using a step size η scaled by a factor derived from the Legendre duality between the generating functions of log_G and exp_G is mathematically equivalent to using f=log_G (concave). This swap changes whether the Bregman divergence’s curvature grows or decays near the simplex boundary. Core assumption: the scaling relation η_dual = c·η_primal (with c from the duality) must hold exactly. Evidence: abstract; Section 4.2 describing DMD’s two branches; Section 6.2 contrasting curvature. Break condition: violating the scaling constraint causes the “dual” algorithm to diverge or converge to a different point than the “primal” version.

**Mechanism 3:** DMD’s use of the convex group exponential branch yields a potential function with uniformly bounded Hessian curvature on the simplex (condition number ≤ e), allowing a fixed large step size and stable convergence. In contrast, GEG’s concave logarithm potential has unbounded curvature as w_i→0+, forcing its step size to shrink proportionally to (min w_i)^q and causing oscillations under noise. For Tsallis q∈(0,1), F_dual''(w)=d/dw exp_q(w) is bounded on [0,1], while F_primal''(w)=w^{-q}→∞ as w→0+. Bounded curvature ⇒ local Lipschitz constant L_F finite everywhere ⇒ η~1/L_F is safe; unbounded curvature ⇒ necessary η→0 as some weight approaches zero, stalling progress. Core assumption: optimization strictly over the probability simplex; separable F; diagonal Hessian. Evidence: Theorem 1 (DMD κ_F ≤ e) and Theorem 2 (GEG L_F→∞) in Section 6.2; experiments show DMD iterations nearly independent of n and κ, while GEG’s performance degrades. Break condition: if the true solution has many very small non-zero weights, GEG’s required η becomes numerically zero; DMD handles this gracefully. Conversely, if the problem prefers soft boundary behavior, DMD’s aggressive thresholding (via [·]_+) might prematurely prune active weights.

## Foundational Learning
**Formal Group Laws & Lazard Series:** Provide the algebraic template Φ(x,y) to generate all admissible group logarithms/entropies. Without this, the family of link functions would be ad-hoc. Quick check: For Φ(x,y)=x+y+αxy, the associated group logarithm is the Tsallis q-logarithm with 1−q=α (log_q(x)=(x^{1−q}−1)/(1−q)); the exponential is exp_q(x)=[1+(1−q)x]^{1/(1−q)}.

**Bregman Divergence & Mirror Map:** The core primitive in MD; the link function f(w)=∇F(w) and its properties (convexity, curvature) directly determine algorithm stability and convergence rates. Quick check: For F(w)=∑(w_i^q−w_i)/(q(q−1)), q∈(0,1), f(w)=w^{q−1} and the Bregman divergence is the Tsallis α‑divergence.

**Sparsity & Simplex Geometry:** Experiments target simplex‑constrained quadratic programs with sparse optima; the link function’s behavior near w_i=0 (curvature singularity vs. boundedness) is the key differentiator for support recovery. Quick check: Why does the standard exponential link (f(w)=ln(w), EG) fail to achieve exact sparsity on the simplex? Because exp(ln(w_i)−ηg_i)>0 for all w_i>0 and finite g_i; it only asymptotically approaches zero, never setting weights exactly to zero.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Problem Spec (L(w), Δ_n) → Link Function Selector (choose group parameters e.g. q and type: GEG or DMD) → Update Engine (compute w^{t+1}=f^{-1}(f(w^t)−η̂∇L(w^t)) with centered gradient) → Normalizer (w ← w/‖w‖₁) → Step Size Controller (GEG: may scale η∝(min w_i)^q; DMD: fixed η~1 typically stable).

**Critical path:** Define optimization problem → select link function based on expected sparsity/noise → initialize w⁰ uniform → iterate: compute gradient → apply MD update (choose GEG or DMD branch) → normalize → check convergence (FW gap or IoU).

**Design tradeoffs:** Curvature vs. sparsity: Concave log_G (GEG) has high curvature near zero, promoting sparsity but requiring tiny, problem‑dependent step sizes; convex exp_G (DMD) has bounded curvature, enabling large fixed steps and driving small weights exactly to zero. Single‑ vs. multi‑parameter: a single q couples boundary and interior behavior; multi‑parameter chains (e.g. log_q∘exp_κ) allow decoupling. Duality switch: DMD’s convex primary update with a concave fallback adds a branch check but improves robustness by handling domain violations gracefully.

**Failure signatures:** GEG divergence: weights w_i→0+ cause η_effective→0 or gradient explosion (h''(w_i)=w_i^{-q}→∞); symptoms: oscillations, NaNs, increasing gaps. DMD domain violation: exp_G(w_i)−η∇L_i may become ≤1, triggering the [·]_+ clip (set to zero); excessive clipping on active weights leads to premature, incorrect sparsity. Mirror duality break: using a “DMD” step size with the GEG formula (or vice versa) without correct scaling causes non‑convergence or convergence to a wrong point.

## Limitations
- Evaluation restricted to synthetic simplex‑constrained quadratic programs; performance on real‑world tasks (e.g., deep learning, portfolio optimization) is unknown.
- Theoretical analysis assumes separable mirror maps and diagonal Hessians; non‑separable constraints or non‑convex losses may invalidate curvature bounds.
- Implementation details (orthogonal transform construction, random seeds, exact initialization) are underspecified, risking reproducibility.
- Hyperparameter sensitivity (group parameters q, κ) is only superficially explored.

## Confidence
- Group entropies yield flexible multi‑parametric link functions: **High** (rigorous formal group construction; follow‑on work).
- Mirror duality permits interchange of log_G and exp_G: **Medium** (theory sound but experimental validation indirect; scaling constraints may be fragile in practice).
- DMD outperforms EG/GEG due to bounded curvature: **High** for synthetic SCQP (strong empirical/theoretical alignment), **Medium** for broader problem classes.

## Next Checks
1. **Curvature replication:** For Tsallis, Kaniadakis, and Tempesta groups, compute the condition number κ_F(w) of the potential function over the entire simplex; confirm κ_F ≤ e for DMD (exp_G) and κ_F → ∞ for GEG (log_G) as w_i → 0.
2. **Real‑world benchmarks:** Test DMD, GEG, and EG on portfolio optimization (Markowitz with transaction costs) and LDA (simplex‑constrained maximum likelihood); compare convergence rates and solution quality.
3. **Duality ablation:** On a simple quadratic problem, run DMD with step size η and GEG with η′ = c·η (c derived from Legendre duality); verify generated iterates match within numerical precision, confirming the duality condition.
