# How unconstrained machine-learning models learn physical symmetries

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.24638
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24638
*   **Reference count:** 0
*   **Primary result:** Unconstrained transformer models trained with random rotations learn approximate rotational equivariance; their internal symmetry content and equivariance errors can be quantified and improved via metrics like character projection (B_α) and post-hoc readout purification.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces a framework to diagnose and improve how unconstrained machine learning models (those without built-in symmetry enforcement) learn physical symmetries like rotational equivariance. It defines two key metrics: the equivariance error \(A_\alpha\), which measures deviation from exact symmetry in model outputs, and the character projection \(B_\alpha\), which decomposes internal feature representations into irreducible components of the symmetry group. Applied to transformer-based atomistic (PET) and particle trajectory (PoLAr-MAE) models, the analysis reveals that standard architectures initially bias toward low angular momentum channels, causing delays in learning targets requiring high-\(\lambda\) or pseudoscalar (\(\sigma=-1\)) components. The study demonstrates that targeted architectural modifications (e.g., injecting high-\(\lambda\) descriptors) or a simple post-hoc readout purification can significantly reduce equivariance errors while preserving accuracy, providing a practical path to embed physical fidelity into scalable, unconstrained models.

## Method Summary
The core methodology involves computing two complementary metrics across model layers and outputs. The **equivariance error \(A_\alpha\)** quantifies the variance of an output (or feature) under group transformations relative to its norm, with lower values indicating better equivariance. The **character projection \(B_\alpha\)** uses group convolution to measure the fraction of a feature's norm residing in each irreducible representation (irrep) \(\alpha\) (e.g., labeled by angular momentum \(\lambda\) and parity \(\sigma\) for O(3)). Models are trained with standard data augmentation (random rotations). For analysis, outputs and internal features are evaluated over a group-averaged test set using numerical quadrature (Lebedev grid) for O(3). The framework is applied to PET (for energies, forces, stresses) and PoLAr-MAE (for particle trajectory classification). Interventions include modifying the geometry embedder to use solid spherical harmonics (SSH) up to a specified \(\lambda_\text{emb}^\text{max}\) and performing post-hoc readout purification by retraining the final linear layer with a loss combining prediction error and an \(A_\alpha\) penalty.

## Key Results
*   Both PET and PoLAr-MAE achieve high approximate equivariance (\(A_\alpha\) an order of magnitude below model error), but exhibit a **spectral bottleneck**: initial embeddings favor low-\(\lambda\) channels, delaying emergence of high-\(\lambda\) and pseudoscalar (\(\sigma=-1\)) components during training.
*   Standard PET fails to learn targets requiring high-\(\lambda\) (e.g., \(\lambda=8\) electron density) or pure pseudoscalar symmetries, but **injecting higher-\(\lambda\) SSH descriptors** in the geometry embedder enables successful learning.
*   A **simple post-hoc readout purification** halves the equivariance error for stress tensor components without sacrificing prediction accuracy, confirming that correct equivariant information is often present but mixed in the last-layer features.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
*   **Claim:** Data augmentation with random rotations enables unconstrained models to learn approximate equivariance.
*   **Mechanism:** Repeatedly presenting rotated versions of inputs during training penalizes outputs that do not transform consistently under the group action. The loss function drives the network to develop internal representations and readouts that are approximately equivariant on the training distribution.
*   **Core assumption:** The training dataset provides sufficiently diverse orientations to cover the group orbit of each sample.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [abstract]: Models learn "an approximate equivariant behavior with a simple data augmentation strategy."
    *   [section II]: Equivariance error \(A\) quantifies deviation; models like PET are trained with random rotations.
    *   [corpus]: Neighboring works on unconstrained MLIPs confirm rotation augmentation is standard for symmetry learning.
*   **Break condition:** Non-uniform group sampling (e.g., only discrete rotations) may lead to discrete rather than continuous equivariance, especially for non-compact groups.

### Mechanism 2
*   **Claim:** The initial bias of geometry embeddings toward low angular momentum (\(\lambda\)) channels creates a spectral initialization bottleneck that delays learning of high-\(\lambda\) or pseudo (\(\sigma = -1\)) targets.
*   **Mechanism:** Standard embeddings encode edge distances (\(\lambda=0\)) and vectors (\(\lambda=1\)). Nonlinearities can generate higher-\(\lambda\) components, but pseudoscalar (\(\lambda=0, \sigma=-1\)) and high-\(\lambda\) (\(>2\)) signals are initially absent. Learning these requires coordinated higher-order interactions across edges via attention, leading to a delayed activation of these channels during training.
*   **Core assumption:** The model's expressivity is sufficient to generate any symmetry channel, but optimization dynamics are influenced by the spectral content of early-layer features.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [section III.B]: Heatmaps show scalar (\(\lambda=0\)) dominance in randomly initialized PET; pseudotensors (\(\sigma=-1\)) are nearly zero and emerge only after many epochs.
    *   [section III.D]: Training on a geometric pseudoscalar target (\(Q\)) shows two-phase learning: initial stagnation with only proper (\(\sigma=+1\)) channels active, followed by sudden pseudoscalar activation and rapid error drop.
    *   [section III.E]: Standard PET fails on a \(\lambda=8\) electron density channel; only when initial embeddings include SSH up to \(\lambda_\text{emb}^\text{max} \geq 8\) does learning succeed.
*   **Break condition:** If the target requires symmetry channels completely absent from the initial feature space (e.g., pseudoscalars in standard PET), the model may converge to a trivial solution (e.g., predicting zero) rather than learning correct transformation properties.

### Mechanism 3
*   **Claim:** A linear readout layer can purify equivariance errors post-hoc by explicitly penalizing variance under group transformations during a brief retraining phase.
*   **Mechanism:** The readout weights \(\theta\) are optimized to minimize a combined loss: mean-square error plus a penalty on the equivariance error \(A_\alpha\). This forces the linear combination of last-layer features to align with the target's irreducible representation, effectively filtering out contaminating symmetry components.
*   **Core assumption:** The last-layer features already contain the correct equivariant information, but it is mixed with other irreps; a linear readout is expressive enough to separate them.
*   **Evidence anchors:**
    *   [section III.C]: Equation (5) defines the purification loss \(L = L_\mu + \gamma L_\sigma\). Retraining only the readout halves the equivariance error for stress without sacrificing accuracy.
    *   [abstract]: "A simple post-hoc readout purification further reduces equivariance errors (e.g., halved for stress) without sacrificing accuracy."
*   **Break condition:** If the required equivariant information is not present in the last-layer features (due to earlier architectural bottlenecks), purification cannot generate it and may only marginally improve \(A_\alpha\).

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Group representation theory (irreducible representations, characters)**
    *   **Why needed here:** The metrics \(A_\alpha\) and \(B_\alpha\) decompose model behavior according to irreps of the symmetry group (e.g., O(3) labeled by \((\lambda, \sigma)\)). Understanding irreps is essential to interpret which symmetry components are active or missing.
    *   **Quick check:** Given a symmetric rank-2 tensor target, which O(3) irreps should be present, and what would \(B_\alpha\) show for a perfectly equivariant model?
*   **Concept: Equivariance vs. invariance**
    *   **Why needed here:** The paper distinguishes scalar (invariant) targets from vector/tensor (equivariant) ones. The metrics measure how well outputs transform; invariance is a special case where the representation is trivial.
    *   **Quick check:** For forces (vectors), what transformation should apply under rotation, and how would a violation appear in \(A_\alpha\)?
*   **Concept: Transformer architectures on point clouds**
    *   **Why needed here:** PET and PoLAr-MAE use attention over edge/token features. The analysis tracks how symmetry channels propagate through embedding layers, attention, and message passing.
    *   **Quick check:** In PET, which module first enables pseudotensorial (\(\sigma=-1\)) features to appear, and why?
*   **Concept: Data augmentation as an implicit regularizer**
    *   **Why needed here:** The core mechanism relies on random rotations during training to encourage equivariance without explicit architectural constraints.
    *   **Quick check:** If rotations are applied only to inputs but not to targets during training, what implicit assumption about the loss function is being made?
*   **Concept: Spherical harmonics and angular momentum channels**
    *   **Why needed here:** Irreps of O(3) correspond to angular momentum quantum number \(\lambda\); embeddings using spherical harmonics \(Y_\lambda\) directly initialize specific channels.
    *   **Quick check:** How does using raw distance and vector embeddings (standard PET) differ from using solid spherical harmonics (SSH) up to \(\lambda\) in terms of initial \(\lambda\)-channel content?
*   **Concept: Linear readout purification**
    *   **Why needed here:** A post-hoc method to enforce equivariance by retraining the final linear layer with an equivariance penalty, leveraging already-learned features.
    *   **Quick check:** Why is purification expected to work only if the required irrep is already present (non-zero \(B_\alpha\)) in the last-layer features?

## Architecture Onboarding
GE -> EE -> Transformer (first pseudotensor emergence) -> GNN -> BBF -> LLF -> Readout.
*   **Critical path:** The spectral bottleneck originates in the Geometry Embedder (GE), which initializes features with only \(\lambda=0,1\) and \(\sigma=+1\). The Edge Embedder (EE) and first Transformer layers are where pseudotensors (\(\sigma=-1\)) can first be synthesized via cross-edge nonlinearities. The Last-Layer Features (LLF) spectral composition directly determines the final output's equivariance error \(A_\alpha\) before readout purification.
*   **Design tradeoffs:**
    *   *Expressivity vs. symmetry bias:* Unconstrained models maximize flexibility but risk symmetry violations; constrained models guarantee symmetry but may limit scalability/expressivity.
    *   *Initialization vs. learning:* Injecting high-\(\lambda\) SSH in GE provides a minimal inductive bias for high-\(\lambda\) targets while preserving transformer scalability.
    *   *Post-hoc vs. training-time regularization:* Readout purification is cheap (one pass) but cannot fix missing information in LLF; training with an equivariance penalty influences the entire backbone but costs more.
*   **Failure signatures:**
    *   High \(A_\alpha\) for targets with high-\(\lambda\) or \(\sigma=-1\) components, even with low model error.
    *   \(B_\alpha\) for target shows contamination from wrong irreps (e.g., pseudoscalar leaking into energy prediction).
    *   Training curves show prolonged stagnation or two-phase learning (e.g., pseudoscalar target).
    *   LLF and BBF spectra lack required high-\(\lambda\) or \(\sigma=-1\) channels despite deep layers.
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1.  **Spectral audit:** For a trained model, compute \(B_\alpha\) across all layers and for outputs. Identify whether desired irreps are present in LLF; if absent, failure likely stems from GE/EE bottleneck.
    2.  **Inductive bias injection:** For high-\(\lambda\) targets, replace standard GE with SSH up to \(\lambda_\text{emb}^\text{max} \geq \lambda_\text{target}\). Retrain and monitor \(A_\alpha\) and \(B_\alpha\) for the target channel.
    3.  **Readout purification:** With a trained model, collect LLF over a group-averaged dataset, solve closed-form purification (Eq. (95) in SI) for readout weights, and evaluate \(A_\alpha\) on the test set. A drop in \(A_\alpha\) without accuracy loss indicates correct equivariant information was present in LLF.

## Limitations
*   **Dataset dependence on rotation diversity:** The augmentation mechanism assumes uniform group orbit coverage; biased orientation distributions may yield only discrete or approximate equivariance, a break condition not systematically tested.
*   **Architectural scope:** Findings are restricted to transformer-based models (PET, PoLAr-MAE). Other architectures (e.g., invariant GNNs, CNNs) may exhibit different spectral dynamics, which is unexplored.
*   **Metric assumptions:** \(A_\alpha\) and \(B_\alpha\) require known group representations (O(3) irreps). For unknown or learned symmetries, character projections cannot be defined. Numerical integration convergence criteria are also not fully specified.

## Confidence
*   **High:** \(A_\alpha\) and \(B_\alpha\) reliably quantify rotational equivariance and internal symmetry content in tested transformer models; post-hoc readout purification consistently reduces \(A_\alpha\) without accuracy loss when target irreps are present in last-layer features.
*   **Medium:** The spectral bottleneck hypothesis (initial bias toward low-\(\lambda\) channels delaying high-\(\lambda\)/pseudoscalar learning) is empirically supported via heatmaps and two-phase training curves, but causal isolation from other optimization factors is incomplete.
*   **Low:** Claims about generality to other symmetry groups (e.g., E(3), discrete point groups) or non-transformer architectures are unsupported; the framework may not extend directly without representation-theoretic adaptations.

## Next Checks
1.  **Augmentation distribution stress test:** Train PET on a dataset with intentionally biased rotation sampling (e.g., only 90° increments) and evaluate continuous \(A_\alpha\). Expect elevated \(A_\alpha\) for targets requiring high-\(\lambda\) components, confirming break condition in Mechanism 1.
2.  **Cross-architecture spectral audit:** Apply the \(B_\alpha\) analysis to an invariant GNN (e.g., SchNet) and a CNN-based model on the same atomistic tasks. Compare spectral evolution; if high-\(\lambda\) channels fail to emerge, the bottleneck may be architecture-specific.
3.  **Extension to E(3) with translational augmentation:** Modify PoLAr-MAE to include random translations (forming E(3)) and compute \(A_\alpha\) for equivariance under combined rotations+translations. If translational equivariance remains high, the mechanism extends to non-compact groups; if not, augmentation alone may be insufficient.
