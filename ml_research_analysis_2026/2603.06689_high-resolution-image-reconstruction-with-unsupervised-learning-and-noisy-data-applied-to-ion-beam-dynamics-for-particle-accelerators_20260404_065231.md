# High-Resolution Image Reconstruction with Unsupervised Learning and Noisy Data Applied to Ion-Beam Dynamics for Particle Accelerators

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.06689
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06689
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Achieved robust denoising and high-fidelity reconstruction, extending measurable beam amplitudes beyond seven standard deviations (7σ), enabling unprecedented halo resolution.

## Executive Summary
Particle accelerator beam diagnostics suffer from severe image degradation due to low signal-to-noise ratios, particularly hindering the detection of low-intensity beam halo that is critical for controlling beam losses. Traditional denoising tools fail in such regimes, limiting measurable beam amplitudes to about 5σ. This paper introduces an unsupervised deep learning method based on Deep Image Prior (DIP) that reconstructs high-fidelity beam images from single noisy acquisitions without requiring clean training data. The method enhances DIP with optimized early-stopping strategies (K-Fold CDSC and Expected Moving Variance) and a composite loss function (MSE, MAE, Total Variation, Gradient Difference Loss) to prevent overfitting while preserving fine halo structures.

The approach achieves robust denoising and extends measurable beam amplitudes beyond 7σ, revealing previously unobserved halo features. It operates efficiently on standard CPU hardware (under 4 minutes per image) with a low carbon footprint. By eliminating the need for paired clean-noisy datasets, the method opens a practical path to improving beam characterization in existing accelerator facilities.

## Method Summary
The method employs a U-Net-like convolutional encoder-decoder network, randomly initialized, to model a single noisy beam image. Training minimizes a composite loss on the noisy input, encouraging the network to first capture the underlying signal structure before fitting noise, thanks to the network's implicit bias toward natural image statistics. To avoid overfitting without ground truth, two unsupervised early-stopping (ES) criteria are used: K-Fold CDSC monitors reconstruction error on held-out pixel subsets (pseudo-validation), while Expected Moving Variance (EMV) tracks the stability of successive outputs. The loss function combines pixel-wise losses (MSE, MAE) with structural regularizers (Total Variation for smoothness, Gradient Difference Loss for edge preservation), weighted to balance noise removal and halo detail retention. Optimization uses Adam with a fixed learning rate, and the final output is the network's state at the ES iteration determined by the hybrid criteria. The entire process runs on CPU, taking a few minutes per typical beam image.

## Key Results
- Extended measurable beam amplitudes beyond 7σ, enabling unprecedented halo resolution.
- Detected a previously unobserved halo in beam diagnostic images.
- Achieved efficient processing: under 4 minutes per image on standard CPU hardware with low carbon footprint.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Deep Image Prior Implicit Regularization
The Deep Image Prior (DIP) architecture implicitly regularizes toward natural image statistics during optimization on a single noisy image, enabling blind denoising. The encoder-decoder CNN with skip connections captures multi-scale structural redundancies (self-similarities) inherent in the target beam image. During gradient-based fitting of the noisy input, the network's inductive bias causes it to first model the underlying clean signal structure before eventually fitting noise. Early stopping halts this process before noise capture. The core assumption is that the beam emittance image, despite noise, shares fundamental statistical properties (e.g., sparse gradients, piecewise smoothness) with natural images that the CNN architecture is biased to represent, while the noise is statistically distinct. Evidence: Section 2.4 states DIP exploits inherent statistical structure encoded in the CNN; Section 4.1 details the U-Net architecture. A break condition: if the beam distribution itself is highly noise-like or lacks spatial correlations, the prior may fail to distinguish signal from noise, leading to over-smoothing or poor reconstruction.

### Mechanism 2: Optimized Early-Stopping Strategies
The K-Fold CDSC and Expected Moving Variance (EMV) provide reliable proxies for the optimal stopping point in an unsupervised setting with no ground truth. K-Fold CDSC creates pseudo-validation sets by masking different pixel subsets across folds, monitoring reconstruction error on held-out pixels to detect the onset of overfitting. EMV tracks the stability of successive output images, identifying a variance peak where reconstruction quality stabilizes before noise reinjection. The core assumption is that error on randomly masked pixels or inter-iteration output variance correlates with true signal reconstruction quality and the point where the model starts fitting noise. Evidence: Section 6 introduces ES-WMV and ES-EMV as adaptations; Section 7 links ES iteration count to beam area evolution, showing strong correlation and that ES prevents noise reinjection. Break condition: if noise is spatially correlated with signal or beam structure is extremely sparse, the heuristics could stop too early or too late.

### Mechanism 3: Composite Loss Function
A composite loss (MSE, MAE, TV, GDL) balances pixel-level fidelity with structural preservation, guiding optimization toward a physically plausible beam distribution. Weighted MSE/MAE forces output to match input intensities (retaining signal), while Total Variation (TV) penalizes high-frequency noise promoting smoothness, and Gradient Difference Loss (GDL) explicitly aligns edges/gradients with the input to preserve halo boundaries. The weights tune this trade-off. Core assumption: the beam's true intensity distribution has coherent spatial structure where edges are meaningful and should be preserved, whereas noise appears as unstructured high-frequency variation. Evidence: Section 4.2 defines each loss component and its intended effect; Section 7 attributes successful alignment of statistics and physics and stable beam area estimation to this loss, noting initial attempts without proper tensor formulation failed. Break condition: if the halo is extremely diffuse with no clear gradient, GDL/TV might over-smooth the features; incorrect weights could either leave noise or erase fine halo structure.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Deep Image Prior & U-Net architecture**
  - Why needed: The entire method is an instantiation of DIP. Understanding that a randomly initialized CNN contains a prior for natural/structured data is key to trusting unsupervised single-image denoising.
  - Quick check: Why does fitting a randomly initialized CNN to a noisy image tend to recover structure before noise? (Answer: The architecture's bias for spatial coherence and multi-scale features acts as an implicit regularizer).
- **Concept: Early-stopping as regularization in unsupervised learning**
  - Why needed: No validation set exists. The paper's core innovation is adapting ES via pseudo-validation (K-Fold) and variance tracking (EMV). This is non-standard and critical for preventing overfitting.
  - Quick check: How can you detect overfitting when you have only a noisy input image and no clean reference? (Answer: By monitoring performance on held-out pixels from the same noisy image or the stability of the output itself across iterations).
- **Concept: Beam emittance and halo physics**
  - Why needed: The ultimate goal is not a "pretty" image but an accurate 2D phase-space distribution to compute RMS emittance and resolve low-intensity halo. The loss and ES criteria must align with this physical metric.
  - Quick check: Why is measuring beam halo at >7σ considered unprecedented, and what does this imply about the signal-to-noise challenge? (Answer: Halo intensity can be <10⁻⁴ of core, so standard tools fail; achieving this requires extreme denoising that preserves ultra-low-density tails without creating artifacts).

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:
  1. Input: Single-channel float32 tensor (normalized beam image ~0-1), shape [1, H, W].
  2. DIP Core: U-Net-like encoder-decoder with skip_n33d=32, skip_n33u=32, skip_n11=2 filters, leaky ReLU, reflection padding, bilinear upsampling; random input noise tensor z fed at start.
  3. Loss Engine: Computes L_total = w_mse*MSE + w_mae*MAE + w_tv*TV + w_gd*GDL on training pixels only (masked by K-Fold strategy).
  4. ES Monitors: (a) K-Fold CDSC: average loss on current fold's held-out pixels; (b) EMV: running variance of output images over sliding window.
  5. Optimizer: Adam with fixed lr=0.01, updating θ to minimize L_total.
  6. Output: Denoised float32 tensor, later converted to uint8.
- **Critical path**:
  1. Convert raw scanner .dat to normalized float32 tensor I0.
  2. Initialize random network fθ and noise z.
  3. For each iteration until ES triggers:
     a. Generate K-Fold mask for this iteration.
     b. Compute output Î = fθ(z).
     c. Compute L_total only on unmasked (training) pixels.
     d. Backpropagate and optimizer step.
     e. Compute pseudo-validation loss on masked pixels (for K-Fold CDSC).
     f. Update EMV from current Î.
     g. Check ES conditions (K-Fold loss plateau & EMV peak).
  4. Final output Î at ES iteration is the denoised image.
  5. Compute physical metrics (beam area via RMS ellipse or DBSCAN) on Î.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Frugality vs. Speed: Chose CPU execution (<4 min/image) over GPU speed for accessibility/low carbon footprint, accepting slower iteration.
  - Per-image vs. Dataset Training: DIP retrains per image (no generalization) but avoids need for large paired datasets, which are impossible to acquire here.
  - Loss Complexity: Composite loss carefully tuned to balance noise removal (TV) with halo edge preservation (GDL, MAE). Simpler MSE alone would over-smooth.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Overfitting: EMV continues rising after peak; pseudo-validation loss decreases then increases; beam area starts increasing (noise reinjection).
  - Under-denoising: Pseudo-validation loss decreases steadily without plateauing within max iterations; beam area remains small/erratic; entropy stays high.
  - Blurring/Detail Loss: GDL loss remains high; Tenengrad/Laplacian variance decreases; halo edges become indistinguishable from core.
  - Numerical Instability: Total loss oscillates wildly; gradients explode (often from w_tv/w_gd too high).
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Baseline DIP:** Train with only MSE loss, no ES (fixed 1000 iterations). Verify basic denoising on a medium-SNR image. Expected: Over-smoothed core, halo lost. Metric: Beam area will be too small.
  2. **ES Method Comparison:** On same image, run with (a) K-Fold CDSC only, (b) EMV only, (c) hybrid. Compare ES iteration count and final beam area. Expected: Hybrid aligns best with physical beam area optimum.
  3. **Loss Weight Sweep:** Systematically vary w_tv and w_gd (e.g., [0.1, 1, 10]) while keeping w_mse=1, w_mae=0.5. Monitor beam area, GDL, and visual halo definition. Expected: Find weights that maximize beam area without increasing pseudo-validation loss.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Variational study with synthetic data:** How can a variational study be designed to generate a synthetic dataset of beam images, and how effective are supervised deep learning models trained on such data compared to the unsupervised DIP method for halo detection and emittance reconstruction? Basis: The paper explicitly states this as a future work direction. Why unresolved: No supervised models have been evaluated due to lack of ground-truth data. Evidence needed: Simulate beam images with known parameters and noise, train supervised denoisers, compare on real images.
2. **Comprehensive benchmarking framework:** What constitutes a comprehensive benchmarking framework for beam image denoising, and which standard datasets and metrics should be adopted to enable fair comparison across methods? Basis: Paper motivates systematic analyses and a benchmarking framework. Why unresolved: Existing evaluations are method-specific and lack common standards. Evidence needed: Community consensus on benchmark images, metrics (PSNR, SSIM, halo preservation, computational cost), and evaluation protocol.
3. **Computational efficiency improvement:** How can the computational efficiency of the DIP-based denoising be improved to handle large-scale beam images from modern diagnostics without compromising quality? Basis: Paper notes scaling to large datasets introduces challenges. Why unresolved: Current implementation takes several minutes per image; for high-throughput or real-time applications faster processing is needed. Evidence needed: Develop and test optimized versions (e.g., reduced network size, approximate inference, parallelization) and measure performance on images of increasing resolution while monitoring quality.

## Limitations
- Exact hyperparameters (loss weights, early-stopping parameters) are unspecified, hindering exact replication.
- Core assumption that beam images share natural image statistics with CNN prior is not validated for ultra-low-intensity halo structures.
- Correlation between unsupervised early-stopping metrics and true reconstruction error is inferred but not quantified without ground truth.

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (Deep Image Prior implicit regularization): High
- Mechanism 2 (Optimized early-stopping strategies): Medium
- Mechanism 3 (Composite loss function): Low-Medium

## Next Checks
1. **S Prior Stress Test:** Generate synthetic beam images with controlled noise (Poisson, Gaussian, spatially correlated) and known halo structures. Apply the method and measure if reconstruction quality degrades predictably when the halo's spatial statistics deviate from "natural" smoothness.
2. **ES Proxy Correlation:** On a small set of images with approximate "clean" references (e.g., high-SNR acquisitions or physics-based simulations), plot iteration-wise values of K-Fold CDSC loss and EMV against the true MSE to the clean reference. Quantify the correlation coefficient.
3. **Loss Ablation for Halo:** Conduct a systematic sweep of w_tv and w_gd weights on a challenging low-SNR halo case. Evaluate not just overall beam area, but radial profile signal-to-noise at >5σ. Optimal weights should maximize detectable halo signal relative to residual noise variance in the tail.
