# SectEval: Evaluating the Latent Sectarian Preferences of Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.12768
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12768
- **Reference count:** 26
- **Primary result:** LLMs exhibit systematic language-induced reversals in sectarian bias: models favoring Shia in English flip to Sunni in Hindi, and geographic prompting shifts advanced models' alignment.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces SectEval, a benchmark of 88 expert-curated binary-choice questions to evaluate latent sectarian (Sunni vs. Shia) preferences in LLMs across languages and contexts. By testing 15 models in English and Hindi, often with geographic cues, the study reveals that theological outputs are neither neutral nor consistent. Key findings include systematic reversals (e.g., DeepSeek-V3 from 56.2% Shia in English to 59.7% Sunni in Hindi), strong geographic steering (Claude-3.5-Sonnet from 9.1% Sunni for Iran to 64.4% for Saudi), and a scale-dependent “majority collapse” in smaller Hindi models that ignore context. The work highlights that multilingual models internalize language-specific sectarian biases, challenging assumptions of theological neutrality and underscoring the need for culturally aware evaluation.

## Method Summary
SectEval comprises 88 binary-choice questions in English and Hindi, each with one Sunni-aligned and one Shia-aligned option. Evaluation is performed zero-shot without fine-tuning: prompts are constructed by presenting the question and options, and models generate a short answer (A/B). The response is mapped to a sect based on the predefined option-sect pairing. For geographic context, a phrase like “I am from Iran” is prepended. Primary metrics are the percentage of Sunni- vs. Shia-aligned responses per model/language/context, along with statistical tests (McNemar’s) to detect systematic shifts between languages. Analysis also breaks down results by topic (e.g., history, ritual) and model scale.

## Key Results
- Language induces bias reversal: top English-Shia models (DeepSeek-V3 56.2% Shia, GPT-4o 51.9% Shia) become Sunni-majority in Hindi (59.7%, 55.8% Sunni).
- Geographic prompting shifts advanced models: Claude-3.5-Sonnet Sunni alignment drops to 9.1% for Iran and rises to 64.4% for Saudi (55.3% gap).
- Model scale matters: smaller Hindi models (<8B) show rigid Sunni majority views and ignore geographic context, while frontier/MoE models exhibit both strong baseline shifts and contextual adaptability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Language Context Reverses Sectarian Alignment
- **Claim:** The language of the prompt (English vs. Hindi) systematically reverses sectarian alignment.
- **Mechanism:** Distinct language embedding spaces reflect different training data distributions. English corpora may contain more diverse or Shia-inclusive theological discourse, while Hindi corpora are dominated by Sunni-majoritarian South Asian texts, leading to a “majority collapse” toward Sunni interpretations.
- **Core assumption:** LLMs internalize language-specific latent spaces shaped by the statistical prevalence of sectarian viewpoints in their training data for that language.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 4.2 report reversals for DeepSeek-V3 (56.2% Shia→59.7% Sunni) and GPT-4o (51.9% Shia→55.8% Sunni). McNemar’s test shows significant shifts for Llama models (p<0.05).
- **Break condition:** If models trained on perfectly balanced, parallel multilingual theological corpora with equal sectarian representation per language, the reversal would not occur.

### Mechanism 2: Geographic Prompting Steers Sectarian Alignment
- **Claim:** Explicit geographic prompts (“I am from X”) cause advanced LLMs to adapt their sectarian alignment to the user's regional context.
- **Mechanism:** The model activates region-specific knowledge patterns associated with the dominant sect of the named country (Shia for Iran, Sunni for Saudi), effectively profiling the user and conforming to expected local normative views.
- **Core assumption:** LLMs can map geopolitical entities to their associated sectarian majorities and use this as a steering signal, overriding default language-induced bias.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section 4.3 show Claude-3.5-Sonnet Sunni alignment 9.1% for Iran vs. 64.4% for Saudi. Smaller Hindi models ignore context, while frontier models adapt.
- **Break condition:** If the model's alignment mechanisms are frozen or if the geographic entity lacks a strong distinct sectarian association in training data, adaptation would not occur.

### Mechanism 3: Model Scale Determines Susceptibility to Majority Collapse vs. Adaptability
- **Claim:** Smaller parameter models (<8B) default to rigid Sunni majority views, while larger/MoE models show stronger baseline shifts and geographic adaptability.
- **Mechanism:** Capacity governs the trade-off between memorizing dominant data statistics and learning flexible, context-sensitive rules. Smaller models lack capacity to store nuanced conditional mappings and fall back to frequent patterns; larger models can access diverse pathways.
- **Core assumption:** Larger models’ increased parameters allow them to internalize more complex, context-dependent behavior.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 4.4 shows small models biased Sunni, while frontier/MoE models demonstrate counter-intuitive Shia shifts in English. Section 4.3 shows Llama-3.2-3B stuck at ~77.8% Sunni despite context, while Grok-4-fast adapts.
- **Break condition:** If smaller models are explicitly fine-tuned on diverse, balanced sectarian data with geographic prompts, they might show adaptability; if larger models are heavily regularized toward a single viewpoint, they would show rigidity.

## Foundational Learning

### Intra-religious vs. Inter-religious Bias
- **Why needed:** The paper innovates by measuring bias *within* Islam (Sunni vs. Shia), distinct from prior work on bias *against* Muslims. This requires recognizing that discrimination can occur even without an out-group target.
- **Quick check:** What is the methodological difference between evaluating a model for “anti‑Muslim sentiment” and for “Shia vs. Sunni preference”?

### Binary-Choice Forced Elicitation
- **Why needed:** SectEval uses binary options grounded in each sect’s doctrine, forcing the model to express a latent preference rather than evading. This makes bias measurable.
- **Quick check:** Why might a standard multiple‑choice benchmark with one correct answer fail to detect sectarian bias, whereas a binary choice with two valid answers succeeds?

### McNemar’s Test for Paired Categorical Shifts
- **Why needed:** The study uses McNemar’s test to determine if the switch in model answers between English and Hindi is statistically systematic, not random.
- **Quick check:** What does a significant (p<0.05) McNemar’s test result for a model indicate about its language‑induced bias?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map (plain text):**  
Dataset (SectEval) -> Prompt Generator -> Model Inference Layer -> Response Scorer -> Analysis Pipeline

**Critical path:**  
Dataset construction → Prompt template definition → Batch inference for all model/condition combinations → Label mapping → Aggregation & statistical testing → Topic-wise/scale-wise breakdowns.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Binary choice simplifies scoring but flattens nuance (e.g., hybrid views).  
- 88 questions is manageable but may not cover all intra‑Islamic nuances; generalizability limited.  
- Focus on Sunni‑Shia excludes other schools (e.g., Ibadi, Zahiri), a noted limitation.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High variance in responses across random seeds (instability).  
- Models refusing to choose (need fallback handling).  
- Geographic prompt having no effect on mid/low‑tier models (expected) but also no effect on claimed “adaptable” models (bug).  
- Topic analysis showing no coherent pattern (e.g., ritual questions not leaning Sunni), suggesting dataset flaws.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Replicate Language Reversal:** Run DeepSeek‑V3 and GPT‑4o on the full SectEval in English and Hindi. Confirm >5% absolute shift in alignment direction (Shia→Sunni). Check McNemar’s p>0.05 for these models (non‑significant systematic shift as per paper).  
2. **Test Geographic Steering:** For Claude‑3.5‑Sonnet, run prompts with “I am from Iran” and “I am from Saudi Arabia” in English. Measure Sunni alignment drop to ~9% for Iran and rise to ~64% for Saudi. Validate the ~55% point gap.  
3. **Validate Topic‑wise Pattern:** Group questions by provided categories (History/Faith, Ritual, etc.). For any model, verify that “History and Faith” shows higher Shia % than “Roza, Hajj, Nikah” in both languages, and that the Hindi gap for “Namaz” is larger than the English gap.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Question 1: Expansion to Other Islamic Schools
**Question:** How can SectEval be expanded to cover a more comprehensive and representative set of Islamic theological and jurisprudential divergences, including other major schools (Madhabs) and denominations beyond the Sunni‑Shia binary?  
**Basis in paper:** The authors acknowledge limitations: the dataset of 88 questions is limited in quantity and may not reflect all of Islamic jurisprudence; the current research limits the theological landscape to a simple comparison of Sunni and Shia, excluding many other schools of thought and denominations.  
**Why unresolved:** Without broader coverage, evaluations risk missing biases related to other significant traditions (e.g., Ibadi, Zahiri, intra‑Sunni Madhabi differences) and may not generalize to the full spectrum of Islamic knowledge.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** A significantly larger, multi‑phase expert‑curated dataset that systematically includes questions from all major historical and contemporary Islamic schools of theology (Aqeedah) and jurisprudence (Fiqh), validated through a multi‑scholar review process across different regional and sectarian expertise.

### Question 2: Root Causes of Language‑Induced Reversal
**Question:** What specific aspects of the training data (e.g., source language distribution, topic prevalence, regional or sectarian authorship) cause the systematic language‑induced reversal in sectarian bias?  
**Basis in paper:** The paper documents the reversal and hypothesizes about Hindi being shaped by Sunni legal principles and English allowing more diverse outputs, but does not investigate precise mechanisms.  
**Why unresolved:** Understanding the root cause is critical for mitigation. The reversal indicates that model representations are hijacked by language‑specific data biases. Without identifying whether this stems from the volume of Sunni‑majority South Asian sources in Hindi pre‑training data, translation quality, or multilingual alignment failures, corrective measures cannot be designed.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Granular analysis of pre‑training corpora for the affected models, quantifying sectarian orientation (e.g., via scholar‑annotated source tagging or proxy metrics) of English vs. Hindi subsets. Complementary probing of model representations (e.g., via concept activation vectors) for sectarian signals before and after fine‑tuning/alignment in each language would isolate the stage where reversal emerges.

### Question 3: Disparate Geographic Steerability
**Question:** Why do some high‑capacity models (e.g., Claude‑3.5‑Sonnet, Grok‑4‑fast) exhibit strong geographical steerability while others (e.g., DeepSeek‑V3 in Hindi, Llama models) show no change or fixed Sunni bias, especially in Hindi?  
**Basis in paper:** Section 4.3 shows stark differences: Claude shifts from 9.1% to 64.4% Sunni alignment between Iran and Saudi contexts, while Llama‑3.2‑3B in Hindi remains ~79% Sunni regardless of context. The authors note this as a critical reliability hazard but do not analyze underlying reasons.  
**Why unresolved:** This difference points to a fundamental divergence in how models internalize and apply demographic context. Understanding if this is due to RLHF data composition, multilingual SFT strategies, or architectural differences in handling context is key to building reliable, context‑aware AI for sensitive religious domains.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments isolating the effect of: (a) geographic prompt’s position and phrasing; (b) the model’s instruction‑tuning mixture (analyzing for region/culture‑sensitive examples); (c) probing the model’s internal “user profile” representations when given geographic cues; (d) testing if steerability gap closes with explicit chain‑of‑thought prompting or retrieval augmentation focused on region‑specific jurisprudence.

### Question 4: Topic‑Wise Variation in Bias
**Question:** What drives the topic‑wise variation in sectarian bias (e.g., historical/theological questions leaning Shia in English, ritual/legal questions leaning Sunni, with Hindi amplifying the latter), and is this pattern consistent across model families and sizes?  
**Basis in paper:** Table 2 shows clear topic‑dependent shifts: “History and Faith” averages 62.2% Shia in English, while “Roza, Hajj, Nikah” averages 66.4% Sunni in Hindi. The authors speculate about Shia‑oriented historical discussions in English but lack substantiation. The topic analysis is averaged across all 15 models without per‑model breakdown.  
**Why unresolved:** Topic‑sensitive bias indicates that models’ “knowledge” of sectarian differences is not uniformly distorted. Some topics may have more readily available or canonically agreed‑upon sources in one sect’s tradition within certain linguistic corpora. Without knowing if the pattern holds for all model families, we cannot determine if it’s a data artifact or modeling phenomenon. This affects trustworthiness in specific religious domains.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** A breakdown of topic‑wise results by model category (size, open vs. closed, MoE vs. dense) to see if the pattern is universal or specific to certain training regimes. Coupled with analysis of the source texts used to generate training data for these topic areas (e.g., identifying which online forums, textbooks, or encyclopedias are predominantly referenced for “Hajj rulings” vs. “Historical Caliphate debates” in English vs. Hindi), this would clarify whether the bias is corpus‑driven.

## Limitations
- Dataset option‑to‑sect mapping is unclear, risking mis‑scoring.  
- Prompting details (API type, temperature, max tokens) not specified, hindering replication.  
- Hindi tokenization/normalization not addressed; could bias responses.  
- Scope limited: binary choice oversimplifies, only 88 questions, excludes non‑Sunni/Shia schools.

## Confidence
- Language reversal (Mechanism 1): Medium  
- Geographic steering (Mechanism 2): High  
- Scale‑dependent bias (Mechanism 3): Medium
