# Truth as a Trajectory: What Internal Representations Reveal About Large Language Model Reasoning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.01326
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01326
- Reference count: 14
- Primary result: TaT significantly outperforms linear probing in out-of-distribution generalization, achieving up to 12% gains and exceeding few-shot performance on commonsense reasoning, with 84.2% toxicity detection accuracy on ToxiGen.

## Executive Summary
Existing explainability methods for large language models analyze hidden states as static points, causing linear probes to latch onto superficial lexical patterns due to polysemanticity. This paper introduces Truth as a Trajectory (TaT), which reimagines transformer inference as a geometric trajectory of layer-wise displacement vectors—differences between consecutive hidden states. An LSTM classifier processes these displacement sequences to isolate reasoning dynamics from static content. Evaluated on commonsense reasoning (ARC, OpenBookQA), factuality, and toxicity detection across dense and Mixture-of-Experts models (Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-14B/32B), TaT demonstrates superior out-of-distribution generalization. For example, on Llama-3.1-8B, TaT achieves an average OOD accuracy of 79.3% compared to 70.5% for linear probes, with improvements up to 12%, and even surpasses the model's few-shot performance. On ToxiGen, TaT reaches 84.2% toxicity detection accuracy versus 79.6% for probes. These results highlight the efficacy of modeling reasoning as a trajectory, with zero-shot applicability during evaluation.

## Method Summary
Truth as a Trajectory (TaT) shifts the focus from static hidden states to the dynamic evolution of representations across layers. For each input token, the method computes displacement vectors as the difference between hidden states at consecutive layers. These vectors form a sequence that encodes how the representation transforms through the network. An LSTM-based classifier processes this sequence to make predictions, thereby capturing patterns that are invariant to the specific lexical content. The approach is evaluated on multiple benchmarks across different model architectures, showing consistent improvements over traditional linear probing on static states.

## Key Results
- TaT significantly outperforms linear probing in out-of-distribution generalization, achieving average OOD accuracy of 79.3% on Llama-3.1-8B versus 70.5% for probes, with gains up to 12%.
- TaT exceeds the model's own few-shot performance on commonsense reasoning tasks.
- On the ToxiGen toxicity detection benchmark, TaT reaches 84.2% accuracy compared to 79.6% for linear probes.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
By focusing on the changes between layers, TaT captures the process of reasoning as it unfolds, rather than the final state. Displacement vectors are less influenced by the specific words used (which can cause polysemantic interference in static states) and more reflective of the model's internal inference steps. The LSTM classifier can then learn to recognize these dynamic patterns, which are more generalizable across different contexts. This separation of dynamics from static content explains why TaT generalizes better out-of-distribution.

## Foundational Learning
- **Transformer hidden states**  
  *Why needed*: Represent intermediate features at each layer; static analysis often uses these.  
  *Quick check*: Do hidden states contain both task-relevant and irrelevant information?
- **Linear probing**  
  *Why needed*: A standard method to evaluate what information is encoded in representations by training a linear classifier.  
  *Quick check*: Can a linear classifier achieve high accuracy on the target task using frozen representations?
- **Polysemanticity**  
  *Why needed*: Neurons in neural networks often respond to multiple unrelated features, making interpretation difficult and causing probes to latch onto spurious correlations.  
  *Quick check*: Is a single neuron's activation interpretable as a single concept?
- **Displacement vectors**  
  *Why needed*: The core innovation of TaT; they represent the change between layers, hypothesized to capture reasoning steps.  
  *Quick check*: Do displacement vectors differ systematically between correct and incorrect reasoning?
- **LSTM classifier**  
  *Why needed*: Processes the sequential displacement vectors, capturing temporal dependencies that linear models cannot.  
  *Quick check*: Does an LSTM outperform a linear model on the displacement sequences for the task?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Input tokens -> Embedding -> Transformer layers -> Displacement vectors (Δ_i = h_i - h_{i-1}) -> LSTM -> Prediction

Critical path: The critical path begins with tokenization and embedding, followed by sequential processing through each transformer layer. Hidden states from each layer are collected, and displacement vectors are computed as the difference between states from layer i and i-1. These displacement vectors are fed into an LSTM, whose final hidden state is used by a classifier to produce the final prediction. This path emphasizes the temporal evolution of representations.

Design tradeoffs:
- Displacements vs static states: Displacements highlight changes but may discard absolute information that could be useful for some tasks.
- LSTM vs linear classifier: LSTM captures sequence dynamics but introduces more parameters and computational cost; linear probes are simpler and faster.
- Zero-shot evaluation: The classifier is trained on a source task and applied to new tasks without retraining, which improves efficiency but may limit adaptation to task specifics.

Failure signatures:
- If the reasoning process does not involve significant layer-to-layer changes, displacement vectors may be uninformative.
- High dimensionality of displacement vectors may cause the LSTM to overfit, especially with limited training data.
- The approach may fail when static content is crucial for the prediction, as displacements ignore absolute states.

First experiments:
1. Benchmark TaT against linear probing on static states across the same tasks to measure performance improvement.
2. Ablate the displacement computation by feeding static hidden states directly into the LSTM to confirm the benefit of using differences.
3. Evaluate TaT on models of different sizes (e.g., 8B, 14B, 32B) and architectures (dense vs MoE) to test scalability.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Can TaT's trajectory representations be validated on synthetic datasets where reasoning content is separated from surface form, to confirm their invariance properties?
- How does TaT perform across a wider range of model architectures and languages, given the current evaluation limited to three models?
- What is the theoretical explanation for the superior out-of-distribution generalization of displacement-based features compared to static states?
- Under what circumstances does the exclusion of absolute state information via displacement vectors become a limitation, and how can it be mitigated?
- Are there systematic biases in TaT's predictions, particularly in toxicity detection, and how can they be addressed?

## Limitations
- Limited model coverage: Evaluation restricted to three models (Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-14B/32B), limiting conclusions about broader architectural generalizability.
- Insufficient reproducibility details: OOD data splits and hyperparameter settings are not specified, making exact replication difficult.
- Validation gaps: The claim that TaT captures reasoning invariants is not tested on controlled synthetic datasets where surface forms and reasoning content are disentangled; also, potential biases in toxicity detection are not addressed.

## Confidence
- OOD performance superiority: Medium
- Isolation of reasoning dynamics: Low
- Zero-shot applicability: Medium
