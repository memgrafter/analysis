# MemGuard-Alpha: Detecting and Filtering Memorization-Contaminated Signals in LLM-Based Financial Forecasting via Membership Inference and Cross-Model Disagreement

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.26797
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26797
- **Reference count**: 30
- **Primary result**: MemGuard-Alpha boosts Sharpe ratio by 49% (to 4.11) and yields 7× higher returns for clean vs. tainted signals (14.48 bps vs 2.13 bps).

## Executive Summary
LLM-generated financial trading signals can suffer from look-ahead bias when models memorize training data, inflating in-sample accuracy but collapsing out-of-sample. MemGuard-Alpha addresses this by detecting and filtering contaminated signals using two complementary components: the MemGuard Composite Score (MCS), which combines membership inference attack features with temporal proximity to quantify memorization probability, and Cross-Model Memorization Disagreement (CMMD), which exploits exogenous variation in training cutoffs across models to isolate clean consensus. Evaluated on seven open-weight LLMs (124M–7B parameters) and 50 S&P 100 stocks (2019–2024), CMMD improves the Sharpe ratio from 2.76 to 4.11 and produces a 7× return gap between clean (14.48 bps) and tainted (2.13 bps) signals.

## Method Summary
The framework begins with a data pipeline that retrieves daily prices for 50 S&P 100 constituents and constructs prompts containing ticker, date, and recent price context. Seven LLMs with known training cutoffs generate directional signals (−1, 0, +1) and confidence scores. For each prompt‑model pair, five membership inference scores are computed: average loss, Min‑K%, Min‑K%++, zlib ratio, and reference model loss ratio. A logistic regression model, trained on in‑sample/out‑of‑sample labels defined by each model’s cutoff, combines these MIA scores with a normalized temporal distance feature τ to produce the MCS—a calibrated probability of memorization. For each stock‑date, models are ranked by MCS and split at the median into a clean set (lower MCS) and a tainted set (higher MCS). The final trading signal is the mean prediction of the clean models. A daily equal‑weight portfolio is constructed from these signals, with 15 bps round‑trip transaction costs, and performance is measured via Sharpe ratio and average daily return.

## Key Results
- Sharpe ratio improves by 49%: 4.11 (CMMD) vs 2.76 (unfiltered).
- Clean signals yield 14.48 bps average daily return, tainted signals only 2.13 bps (7× difference).
- A striking crossover emerges: in‑sample accuracy rises with contamination (40.8% → 52.5%) while out‑of‑sample accuracy falls (47% → 42%).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: The MemGuard Composite Score (MCS) reliably separates memorization‑contaminated signals from genuine reasoning by combining multiple membership inference attack (MIA) features with temporal proximity.
- **Mechanism**: MCS trains a logistic regression on six features: five MIA scores (loss, Min‑K%, Min‑K%++, zlib ratio, reference model ratio) plus a normalized temporal distance feature τ(t, c_k). This produces a calibrated probability p(memorized | features). The temporal feature dominates separation (Cohen’s d=17.8) because IS/OOS status is cutoff‑determined, while MIA features add model‑specific granularity (d=0.39–1.37).
- **Core assumption**: Temporal proximity is a strong proxy for contamination, and MIA features capture within‑date, cross‑model variation in memorization intensity.
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] "MCS... combining five MIA methods with temporal proximity via logistic regression, achieving Cohen's d = 18.57 for contamination separation." [section 3.5] "MCS outputs a calibrated p(memorized | features)... temporal proximity accounts for 96% of the composite difference." [corpus] Corpus evidence is weak; neighboring papers focus on general MIA or financial forecasting, not composite scoring for debiasing.
- **Break condition**: If temporal cutoffs are unknown (e.g., for closed‑source API models) or if MIA features are miscalibrated across architectures, MCS separation degrades. The paper notes that with only MIA features (no temporal), d drops to 0.39–1.37, and for API models only two features (loss, zlib) may yield d=0.2–0.5.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Cross‑Model Memorization Disagreement (CMMD) filters contaminated signals by exploiting exogenous variation in training cutoffs across models, using median‑split partitioning on MCS scores to isolate clean consensus.
- **Mechanism**: For each stock‑date, models are ranked by MCS. The median splits models into "clean" (≤ med) and "tainted" (> med) groups. The final signal is the mean prediction of the clean group. The key insight: if memorization biases tainted models (e.g., toward bullish recall of past gains), the clean‑tainted disagreement δ(s,t) estimates the bias. Median split maximizes harmonic mean of group sizes for minimum‑variance estimation, adapting to changing MCS distributions without fixed thresholds.
- **Core assumption**: Training cutoffs are exogenous (unrelated to financial data characteristics) and create valid within‑date, across‑model variation in contamination. Clean models' consensus reflects genuine reasoning.
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] "CMMD exploits variation in training cutoff dates across LLMs to separate memorized signals from genuine analytical reasoning." [section 3.6] "The median‑split yields the largest possible harmonic mean... creating a comparable situation to a Fuzzy Regression Discontinuity Design." [corpus] No direct corpus support; neighboring papers on MIA scaling (paper ID 181751) discuss attack success but not cross‑model filtering.
- **Break condition**: If all models share similar cutoffs (no temporal diversity) or if clean/tainted groups do not disagree directionally (e.g., memorization bias is neutral), CMMD provides no filtering benefit. The paper shows 40.1% of pairs have directional conflict >0.5, but this depends on model ensemble diversity.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: The signal accuracy crossover (in‑sample accuracy rising with contamination while out‑of‑sample falls) directly evidences that memorization inflates apparent model quality at the cost of generalization.
- **Mechanism**: As contamination (MCS) increases, models rely more on memorized ground truth within their training window, boosting in‑sample accuracy. However, this memorized knowledge does not generalize, causing out‑of‑sample accuracy to drop. The crossover point (around Q3) marks where memorization harm outweighs any benefit. This pattern validates MCS as a contamination measure and justifies filtering.
- **Core assumption**: In‑sample performance can be inflated by memorization of outcomes, and out‑of‑sample performance reflects true predictive reasoning.
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] "A striking crossover pattern emerges: in‑sample accuracy rises with contamination (40.8% to 52.5%) while out‑of‑sample accuracy falls (47% to 42%)." [section 5.3] Figure 5 visualizes the crossover; the text explains it via bias‑variance tradeoff: high contamination reduces bias (correct memorized answers) but increases variance (poor future forecasts). [corpus] Unaddressed in corpus; neighboring papers on financial LLM bias (e.g., paper ID 85799 on multi‑period learning) do not examine this pattern.
- **Break condition**: If the crossover does not appear in new datasets (e.g., different asset classes, market regimes), it may indicate that memorization effects are context‑dependent or that MCS is mis‑calibrated. The paper acknowledges evaluation over a mostly bullish 2019–2024 period.

## Foundational Learning
### Membership Inference Attack (MIA)
- **Why needed**: Provides sequence‑level features to quantify memorization. MCS relies on MIA scores.
- **Quick check**: Why does a lower average loss on a prompt suggest the model memorized it, and how does Min‑K% amplify this signal for entity‑specific tokens?

### Temporal Cutoff and Point‑in‑Time (PIT) Awareness
- **Why needed**: The entire framework relies on knowing each model's training data end‑date to define IS/OOS status and compute τ(t, c_k). This is the ground truth for training MCS and enabling CMMD's cross‑model comparison.
- **Quick check**: If two models have different cutoffs, how does the same prompt for a 2022 date become IS for one model but OOS for another? What does this allow?

### Signal Construction and Portfolio‑Level Evaluation
- **Why needed**: LLMs generate categorical directions (bullish/bearish/neutral) with confidence. Understanding how these are aggregated into tradeable signals (e.g., equal‑weighted portfolio) and evaluated (Sharpe, daily bps) is essential to see why filtering matters.
- **Quick check**: How does the paper turn an LLM's "bullish" prediction into a daily return? What costs are subtracted, and why?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Data Pipeline -> Alpha Generation -> MIA Scoring Engine -> MCS Module -> CMMD Filter -> Portfolio Simulator

**Critical path**: Prompt generation → Alpha + MIA scoring → MCS inference → CMMD partitioning → Portfolio backtest. MIA scoring is the computational bottleneck (~4.5h for 299.6k pairs).

**Design tradeoffs**:
- **MCS**: Uses temporal feature for strong separation but requires known cutoffs. Without cutoffs (API models), rely on MIA‑only with weaker d.
- **CMMD vs. Thresholding**: CMMD avoids arbitrary thresholds by using cross‑model disagreement; thresholding (e.g., MCS > 0.5) removes all high‑contamination signals regardless of direction, destroying signal‑to‑noise ratio.
- **Model selection**: Needs ≥3 models with ≥2 distinct cutoffs. More models improve partitioning but increase MIA cost linearly. Open‑weight models required for per‑token log‑probs; API models limit MIA methods.

**Failure signatures**:
- MIA scores show no IS/OOS separation (KS p > 0.05) for a model → that model's MCS is unreliable.
- CMMD Sharpe < Raw Alpha Sharpe → either clean/tainted disagreement is not directional, or contamination does not bias signals consistently.
- Threshold sensitivity plot (Figure 8) shows no single percentile improves Sharpe → confirms threshold fragility.
- High correlation between CMMD and Raw Alpha (~0.98) is expected; if CMMD becomes uncorrelated, it may be over‑filtering.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **MIA Separation Validation**: For each model, compute all 5 MIA scores on known IS vs. OOS prompts. Expect KS test p < 0.001 for most models/methods (Table 3). If not, check prompt tokenization or model loading.
2. **CMMD vs. Raw Alpha Backtest**: Run 6 strategies (Raw Alpha, Debiased Alpha, CMMD, Buy‑Hold, Momentum, Random) on 93‑day sample. Expect CMMD Sharpe > Raw Alpha Sharpe (~4.11 vs 2.76) and clean signals' daily return >> tainted signals' (14.48 vs 2.13 bps).
3. **Signal Accuracy Crossover**: Bin signals by MCS quintile. Compute directional accuracy IS and OOS. Expect IS accuracy rising with contamination (Q1→Q5) and OOS accuracy falling, with crossover ~Q3 (Figure 5). If pattern is absent, MCS may not capture memorization.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1**
- Question: Is MIA‑based contamination detection reliable across different MIA algorithms, LLM families, and model scales?
- Basis in paper: RQ1 in Section 1.1 explicitly asks about the reliability of MIA scores and their sensitivity to these factors.
- Why unresolved: While Table 3 shows significant IS/OOS separation for most model‑method combinations, four cases (e.g., Qwen models with Min‑K% methods) have non‑significant t‑tests. The study evaluates only seven models (124M–7B) and five MIA methods, limiting generalizability.
- What evidence would resolve it: Test MIA across a broader range of LLMs (including frontier 70B+ models), more MIA variants, and diverse financial datasets and tasks to establish consistent reliability metrics.

**Open Question 2**
- Question: Does filtering alpha signals with contamination scores enhance out‑of‑sample portfolio returns, and is CMMD superior to threshold‑based debiasing?
- Basis in paper: RQ2 in Section 1.1 questions the impact on portfolio performance and CMMD's advantage over thresholds.
- Why unresolved: CMMD improves Sharpe (4.11 vs 2.76), but the bootstrap difference has p=0.054 due to only 93 trading days, reducing statistical confidence. Comparisons are limited to simple thresholding, not other debiasing approaches.
- What evidence would resolve it: A longer backtest (e.g., 2–5 years) with higher statistical power, and benchmarking against alternative debiasing methods (e.g., retraining, anonymization) in varied market conditions.

**Open Question 3**
- Question: How does memorization vary with model scale, architecture, and training data recency, and do larger models memorize more?
- Basis in paper: RQ3 in Section 1.1 addresses scale and temporal effects on contamination.
- Why unresolved: Results show size increases separation within GPT‑2, but recency dominates (TinyLlama > GPT‑2 Large). Models are capped at 7B; frontier models may differ due to advanced training, and only four architectures are tested.
- What evidence would resolve it: Evaluate frontier LLMs (70B+), more architectures (e.g., Mixtral, Llama 3), and varied training data recencies to confirm scaling patterns in financial memorization.

**Open Question 4**
- Question: Are CMMD's debiasing benefits consistent across contamination thresholds and market regimes, justifying its threshold‑free design?
- Basis in paper: RQ4 questions robustness to thresholds and justifies CMMD's approach.
- Why unresolved: Simple thresholding is fragile (Figure 8), but CMMD's evaluation uses one model ensemble and 93 days. Its robustness to different model mixes, cutoff variations, and market cycles (e.g., bear markets) is not fully tested.
- What evidence would resolve it: Test CMMD with diverse model ensembles (varying cutoffs and sizes) across multiple market cycles (bull, bear, high volatility) to confirm consistent Sharpe improvement and signal accuracy.

## Limitations
- MCS logistic regression hyperparameters (regularization, feature standardization) and exact prompt templates are not disclosed, limiting exact replication.
- The evaluation period (2019‑2024) covers a predominantly bullish market; performance in bear or high‑volatility regimes remains unknown.
- The framework requires known training cutoffs for each model, making it difficult to apply to closed‑source API models without surrogate information.

## Confidence
- MCS separation (Cohen’s d=18.57): **Medium** – temporal proximity dominates; MIA contribution minimal; hyperparameters unverified.
- CMMD Sharpe improvement (2.76→4.11): **Medium** – single‑period backtest; median split may be over‑fitted.
- Clean vs. tainted return gap (14.48 vs 2.13 bps): **Medium** – derived from MCS; circular dependency threatens validity.
