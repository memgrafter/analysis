# Do Multilingual VLMs Reason Equally? A Cross-Lingual Visual Reasoning Audit for Indian Languages

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.26742  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26742  
- **Reference count**: 6  
- **Primary result**: Cross-lingual transfer to Indian languages yields consistent accuracy drops of 9.8–25 percentage points; Dravidian languages lag 3.0–13.2 pp behind Indo-Aryan; chain-of-thought harms several languages.

## Executive Summary
The paper exposes a critical gap in multilingual visual reasoning: current vision-language models (VLMs) perform substantially worse when questions are posed in Indian languages compared to English. By translating 980 visual reasoning items from MathVista, ScienceQA, and MMMU into six Indian languages (Hindi, Tamil, Telugu, Bengali, Kannada, Marathi), the authors conduct a broad audit of eight VLMs. Results reveal steep accuracy declines (9.8–25 pp) and a systematic Dravidian underperformance. Notably, chain-of-thought prompting—typically a boost—backfires in Bengali and Kannada, and even a model trained on 23 languages (Aya-Vision-8B) suffers large drops. The study highlights the English-centric bias of multimodal pretraining and calls for genuinely inclusive multilingual evaluation.

## Method Summary
The audit uses a fixed set of 980 images and corresponding questions from three established English-only visual reasoning benchmarks. Questions are automatically translated into six Indian languages using IndicTrans2 while preserving mathematical symbols and numeric labels. Eight VLMs (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL, Aya-Vision, Gemma 3) are then prompted with the image and the translated text without any fine‑tuning. Accuracy is measured as exact match for multiple‑choice or numeric answers, with tolerant matching for numbers. In addition to standard prompting, two ablation conditions are explored: removing the image and appending a chain‑of‑thought (CoT) instruction. Statistical significance is assessed via McNemar’s test, and cross‑lingual consistency is computed to gauge stability across language variants.

## Key Results
- All eight VLMs exhibit consistent accuracy drops of 9.8–25 percentage points when switching from English to Indian languages.
- Dravidian languages (Tamil, Telugu, Kannada) underperform Indo‑Aryan languages (Hindi, Bengali, Marathi) by 3.0–13.2 pp across models; the gap persists even when controlling for script (Marathi vs. Hindi).
- Chain‑of‑thought prompting degrades accuracy in Bengali (−14.4 pp) and Kannada (−11.4 pp), contrasting with modest or positive effects in English and Marathi.
- Aya‑Vision‑8B, trained on 23 languages, suffers a 28.5 pp drop on Dravidian scripts, indicating that broad multilingual pretraining does not ensure reasoning transfer.
- Gemma 3‑27B shows the smallest average drop (9.8 pp) and a near‑zero Dravidian–Indo‑Aryan gap, suggesting that model scale and targeted alignment can mitigate the disparity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The authors propose three interconnected mechanisms for the observed cross‑lingual performance collapse.

**Mechanism 1: English‑centric representation bottleneck**  
Models pretrained predominantly on English multimodal data produce weaker textual encodings for Indian languages. The fusion module, tuned to align strong English text features with visual cues, receives a degraded signal, harming the entire reasoning chain. Evidence: every model degrades from English to Indian languages; removing the image harms English more (−15.5 pp) than Indian languages (−4.9 to −9.7 pp), suggesting the latter already under‑utilize visual input. Break condition: a model with no significant drop across all Indian languages would falsify this.

**Mechanism 2: Dravidian underperformance due to data scarcity and script divergence*  
Dravidian languages have lower representation in pretraining corpora and use non‑Devanagari scripts. Their tokenizers and text encoders, optimized for Latin/Devanagari, produce noisier embeddings. Even Aya‑Vision‑8B, which emits native scripts (low English token leak), shows a large gap; Marathi (Devanagari) still lags behind Hindi, implicating data volume. Evidence: Dravidian gap of 3.0–13.2 pp; Marathi’s 8.5 pp lower accuracy than Hindi despite shared script. Break condition: balanced Dravidian data would erase the gap.

**Mechanism 3: Chain‑of‑thought backfires because reasoning pathways are locked to English**  
VLMs’ internal CoT generation is reinforced during English‑centric training. When forced to produce step‑by‑step reasoning in a low‑resource language, coherence collapses (e.g., code‑switching, nonsensical logic), contaminating the final answer. Evidence: CoT degrades Bengali (−14.4 pp) and Kannada (−11.4 pp) while barely affecting English (−1.8 pp). The exception of Marathi (+3.3 pp) suggests script alignment can sometimes preserve CoT utility.

## Foundational Learning
- **Cross‑lingual transfer failure**  
  *Why needed*: Explains why strong English VLM performance does not generalize.  
  *Quick check*: If a model is trained on 90% English and 1% Tamil data, would you expect equal performance on a visual reasoning task in both languages? Probably not; the weak Tamil signal would limit transfer.

- **Linguistic typology and script divergence**  
  *Why needed*: Interprets the Dravidian vs. Indo‑Aryan gap; isolates script from language-family effects.  
  *Quick check*: Two languages share a script (e.g., Hindi/Marathi) but belong to different families. If one performs significantly worse, what factor besides script is likely dominant? Training data volume.

- **Chain‑of‑thought as a double‑edged sword**  
  *Why needed*: Accounts for the counterintuitive CoT degradation, showing its benefit is contingent on native‑language reasoning fluency.  
  *Quick check*: A model gives correct answers in Language X but produces nonsensical “step‑by‑step” text when CoT is prompted. What does this suggest about where the model performs its “true” reasoning? Likely internally in a high‑resource language (English), not in the output language.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**  
1. Text Encoder – tokenizes and embeds the question; sensitive to script and language frequency.  
2. Vision Encoder – processes the image; language‑agnostic but requires alignment.  
3. Multimodal Fusion Module – cross‑attention or projections that connect vision and text features.  
4. Language Model Decoder – generates the final answer conditioned on fused context.

**Critical path**  
Input (Image + Translated Text) → Text Encoder (weak signal for Indian languages) → Fusion Module (poor text‑visual alignment) → Decoder → Output (often incorrect).

**Design tradeoffs**  
- Scale vs. Efficiency: Larger models reduce but do not eliminate the gap, at higher compute cost.  
- Multilingual Breadth vs. Reasoning Depth: Broad coverage (Aya‑Vision‑8B) does not guarantee deep reasoning transfer.  
- Prompt Engineering vs. Model Capability: CoT helps only when underlying reasoning fluency exists in the prompt language.

**Failure signatures**  
- High English token leak (e.g., >30%): model defaults to English internally.  
- Low cross‑lingual consistency: answers change dramatically across language variants.  
- Disproportionate drop on math‑heavy datasets (MathVista).  
- Elevated “language confusion” rate in verbose responses (wrong script).

**First 3 experiments**  
1. **Baseline Audit**: Run the full 7‑language benchmark (980 Q × 7) on the target VLM; compute per‑language accuracy, family gap, and cross‑lingual consistency.  
2. **Ablation – Image Removal**: For a subset (e.g., 200 Q), evaluate with no image. Compare the accuracy loss from Full→No‑Image between English and an Indian language (e.g., Tamil). A smaller Tamil loss suggests the model already under‑used the image due to weak text.  
3. **Ablation – Chain‑of‑Thought**: Append the target‑language equivalent of “Think step by step” to the same subset. Compare Standard→CoT accuracy; a large negative delta indicates CoT backfiring. Also measure response length and English token leak to observe reasoning corruption.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Translation quality impact**: How do IndicTrans2 errors, especially on technical content, contribute to the observed gaps? Human evaluation is needed to separate translation noise from inherent language difficulty.  
- **CoT degradation cause and remedy**: Why does CoT harm several Indian languages, and how can prompting be adapted (e.g., English CoT with translated answer) to preserve its benefits?  
- **Gemma 3‑27B’s robustness**: What properties (e.g., multilingual alignment, data balancing) give Gemma a tiny Dravidian gap and a negative ScienceQA drop? Can these be transferred to other models?  
- **Intrinsic language difficulty vs. data scarcity**: Are Dravidian languages harder due to linguistic/script features (agglutinativity, non‑linear script), or is the gap purely a data‑representation issue? Controlled studies are required.

## Limitations
- **Translation fidelity**: Automatic translation of technical questions may introduce errors; only six Indian languages were tested, limiting broader generalizability.  
- **Model selection bias**: Evaluation focused on eight specific VLMs from 2024‑2025; newer architectures or proprietary models might behave differently.  
- **Speculative mechanisms**: The “reasoning lock” hypothesis for CoT failure is inferred, not directly probed via internal model analysis.

## Confidence
- **Consistent cross‑lingual accuracy drop (9.8–25 pp)**: High (robust across all models and datasets, with statistical significance).  
- **Dravidian underperformance relative to Indo‑Aryan**: Medium (pattern is clear, but causal attribution to data+script remains inferential).  
- **Chain‑of‑thought degradation in Bengali/Kannada**: Low (observation solid, but mechanistic explanation is post‑hoc; alternatives not ruled out).

## Next Checks
1. **Human Translation Audit**: Professionally re‑translate a stratified sample (n=200) per language; assess semantic equivalence and technical accuracy. If error rate >5%, re‑run core evaluations to quantify impact.  
2. **Evaluate Next‑Gen Multimodal Models**: Test GPT‑4o, Gemini 2.0 Flash, and Claude 3.5 Sonnet on the same benchmark; check whether gaps shrink and the Dravidian–Indo‑Aryan disparity persists.  
3. **Probe Text Encoder Representations**: For a baseline model (e.g., Qwen2.5‑VL‑7B), extract final text‑encoder states for English vs. Tamil questions. Compute representation similarity (cosine) and image‑text alignment (CCA). Significant degradation for Tamil would directly support the proposed mechanisms.
