# Learning to maintain safety through expert demonstrations in settings with unknown constraints: A Q-learning perspective

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.23816
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23816
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Reduces safety violation costs by 30%–92% compared to SAC baseline on four Safety-Gymnasium benchmarks and consistently outperforms ICRL and VICRL in safety-reward trade-offs.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces SafeQIL, an inverse constrained reinforcement learning algorithm that learns a safe policy from expert demonstrations without explicit knowledge of the safety constraints. SafeQIL operates in constrained MDPs where constraints are unknown but task rewards are observable. The algorithm mixes task rewards with a safety signal derived from a discriminator that estimates the probability of a state being safe (i.e., within the expert demonstration distribution). For states outside the demonstrated support, SafeQIL enforces pessimistic upper bounds on Q-values by referencing the nearest demonstrated state via cosine similarity. On four Safety-Gymnasium environments, SafeQIL significantly lowers safety violations compared to unconstrained SAC and also surpasses prior ICRL methods in balancing safety and task performance. Ablations confirm the importance of each component, and the method shows robust performance across varying dataset sizes, avoiding catastrophic failures seen in some baselines.

## Method Summary
SafeQIL is an inverse constrained RL method that leverages expert demonstrations to learn a safe policy without prior access to constraint functions. The core idea is to shape the Q-function using a mixture of task rewards $r_d$ and a safety reward $r_s$, where $r_s(s)=\log \phi_\omega(s)$ is derived from a discriminator $\phi_\omega$ that classifies states as belonging to the expert demonstration distribution. States within the demo support ($I_S(s)=1$) are updated using standard Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) losses, while out-of-support states are subjected to an additional constraint: their Q-values are upper-bounded by the Q-target of the most similar demonstrated state (found via cosine similarity). In parallel, the discriminator is trained with a logistic loss and gradient penalty to maintain a smooth decision boundary. This combination encourages the policy to stay within regions deemed safe by the demonstrations while still exploring to maximize task reward.

## Key Results
- SafeQIL reduces safety violation costs by 30%–92% relative to the unconstrained SAC baseline across four Safety-Gymnasium benchmarks.
- It consistently outperforms ICRL and VICRL in the safety-reward trade-off, achieving lower costs for comparable or higher task rewards.
- Ablating the constraint term or the cosine similarity component leads to increased cost variance, confirming the necessity of each design choice.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** SafeQIL learns a policy that prioritizes safety by shaping Q-values to reflect a mixture of task rewards and state-level safety assessments, encouraging actions leading to safe states.

**Mechanism:** The Q-function is defined as $Q^\pi(s_t,a_t) = \mathbb{E}\big[\sum_i \gamma^i R_{t+i}\big]$, where $R_t = I_S(s_t)\cdot r_d(s_t,a_t) + (1-I_S(s_t))\cdot r_s(s_t)$. $I_S(s_t)$ indicates whether $s_t$ is in the expert demonstration support, $r_d$ is the environment reward, and $r_s(s_t) \le 0$ is a safety penalty. Thus, Q-values for safe state-action pairs accumulate positive rewards over time, while Q-values for unsafe states accumulate penalties, making safe actions more promising. (Section 3)

**Core assumption:** The expert demonstrations cover a set of state-action pairs known to be safe; states outside this support are treated as uncertain/unsafe unless the policy recovers to safe states.

**Evidence:** The abstract mentions mixing expectations in terms of rewards and safety. Section 3 formally defines the Q-function and provides Theorem 3 bounding Q-values for out-of-support states. Ablations removing the constraint term lead to higher cost variance, underscoring its role.

**Break condition:** If the discriminator poorly estimates $I_S(s)$ (e.g., high false positive/negative rates), the safety reward $r_s(s)$ becomes miscalibrated, causing Q-values to misrepresent safety and potentially leading to excessive conservatism or unsafe exploration.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** A discriminator network $\phi_\omega(s)$ estimates the probability that a state is in the expert demonstration distribution, providing a soft, data-driven safety signal that gates Q-value updates.

**Mechanism:** The discriminator is trained via a logistic loss with gradient penalty (DAC-style) to distinguish states from the expert buffer $D$ versus the agent’s replay buffer $B$. Its output $\phi_\omega(s) \in [0,1]$ is mapped to a safety reward $r_s(s) = \log(\phi_\omega(s))$, which is negative when $\phi_\omega(s) < 1$. This reward is used in the Q-loss for samples where $I_S(s_t)=0$ (out-of-demonstration-support). (Section 4.2)

**Core assumption:** The state distribution of expert demonstrations reliably indicates safety; states rarely seen in demonstrations are likely unsafe.

**Evidence:** The abstract states that SafeQIL mixes task rewards and safety via a discriminator that estimates state safety probability. Section 4.2 details the training objective and reward mapping, building on DAC [7].

**Break condition:** If expert demonstrations are stochastic or multi-modal, the discriminator may produce high uncertainty or conflicting estimates for similar states, causing unstable Q-value updates and degraded safety performance.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** For state-action pairs outside the demonstration support ($s \notin \text{supp}(D)$), SafeQIL enforces an upper bound on their Q-values relative to the most similar demonstrated state-action pairs, preventing overestimation and unsafe exploitation.

**Mechanism:** During critic updates, for each $(s_B, a_B)$ from the agent’s buffer $B$ with $s_B \notin \text{supp}(D)$, SafeQIL finds the closest demonstration state $s^*_D$ using cosine similarity and retrieves its stored Q-target: $\hat{Q}^{\min}(s_B,a_B) = r_d(s^*_D,a^*_D) + \gamma \cdot \min_i \bar{Q}_{\bar{\phi}i}(s'^*_D,a'^*_D)$. The loss term $\max(Q(s_B,a_B), \hat{Q}^{\min}) - \hat{Q}^{\min}$ effectively clamps $Q(s_B,a_B) \le \hat{Q}^{\min}$. (Section 4.1)

**Core assumption:** State similarity (cosine similarity in raw state space) is a reasonable proxy for proximity in terms of safety and task progress; the closest demonstrated state provides a locally valid upper bound.

**Evidence:** Section 4.1 defines the loss, and Section 4.2 describes the nearest-neighbor retrieval. The appendix ablates the cosine similarity component; removing it increases cost variance, confirming its importance.

**Break condition:** If the state space exhibits perceptual aliasing (similar raw states but different safety properties), cosine similarity may retrieve irrelevant demonstration bounds, leading to either over-conservatism or unsafety.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Policy network πθ (actor) -> stochastic policy. Critic networks Qϕ1, Qϕ2 & target critics Q¯ϕ1, Q¯ϕ2 -> Q-value estimation. Discriminator φω -> state safety probability. Buffers: D (demonstration), B (agent replay).

### Critical Path
1. Interact with environment using πθ, store transitions in B.
2. Per update, sample batches from B and D.
3. For each (sB,aB) ∈ B with sB ∉ supp(D):
   - Retrieve nearest demo state s*D via cosine similarity.
   - Compute bound target ˆQmin = rd(s*D,a*D) + γ·min_i Q¯ϕi(s′*D,a′*D).
   - Compute clamping loss: max(Q(sB,aB), ˆQmin)−ˆQmin and safety-penalty TD error.
4. For (sB,aB) with sB ∈ supp(D): use standard SAC TD error.
5. For all samples from D: standard TD error.

### Design Tradeoffs
- State similarity metric: Cosine similarity in raw state space is simple but may not capture meaningful proximity; a learned metric or embedding could improve bound selection.
- Pessimism scope: SafeQIL applies pessimism at the state level (all actions from OOD state are bounded), which is conservative but avoids action-space overestimation; however, it may hinder exploration near safe states.
- Discriminator as safety proxy: Assuming demo states are safe could fail if demonstrations contain unsafe states; miscalibrated discriminator harms Q-value shaping.

### Failure Signatures
- Rising cost/reward variance as dataset size grows (Appendix D) due to human demonstrator drift leading to multi-modal demonstrations.
- Catastrophic reward collapse in complex tasks (e.g., VICRL in SafetyCarButton1-v0); SafeQIL mitigates but still shows reward degradation in interaction-heavy environments.
- Discriminator saturation (outputs near 0 or 1 for many states) causing extreme rs(s) and gradient issues.

## Limitations
- The binary safety indicator threshold $I_S(s)$ is unspecified; sensitivity to this threshold remains unknown.
- State preprocessing for cosine similarity (normalization, embedding) is not detailed, which may affect bound selection in practice.
- Nearest-neighbor search per batch can be computationally heavy; the paper does not clarify whether approximate methods (e.g., FAISS) are used.

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (Q-value mixing): High
- Mechanism 2 (Discriminator as safety proxy): High
- Mechanism 3 (Pessimistic bound via cosine similarity): Medium
- Overall empirical superiority: High
