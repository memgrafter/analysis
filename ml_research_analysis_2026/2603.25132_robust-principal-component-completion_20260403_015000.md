# Robust Principal Component Completion
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.25132
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25132
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: RPCC yields a hard classifier via added noise, achieving near‑optimal recovery on synthetic tensors (median RRSE < 2.5e‑4, IoU = 1) and substantially outperforming RPCA methods on video foreground extraction (AUCF1 up to 0.72) and hyperspectral anomaly detection (AUCF1 up to 0.73).

## Executive Summary
Robust principal component completion (RPCC) addresses the problem of separating a low‑rank background from a sparse foreground in tensor data. Unlike traditional RPCA, which assumes an additive foreground, RPCC directly models occlusion: the sparse component replaces entries of the low‑rank component within a blockwise support Ω. The method combines a Bayesian CP factorization with sparsity‑inducing priors and introduces a small Gaussian noise perturbation to enable tractable variational Bayesian inference. As the noise variance σ tends to zero, the variational posterior for the block assignments becomes binary, providing a hard classifier without any post‑hoc thresholding. Extensive experiments on synthetic tensors, the CDnet video dataset, and four hyperspectral datasets demonstrate state‑of‑the‑art performance, with significant improvements in recovery error and detection metrics. The approach scales to tensors of arbitrary order with polynomial computational complexity.

## Method Summary
RPCC formulates a Bayesian generative model for the observed tensor Y. A Gaussian noise E ~ N(0, σI) is added to Y to obtain bY, which facilitates variational inference. The low‑rank component L is expressed as a CP factorization L = CP{A^(n)}; each factor matrix A^(n) is assigned a Gaussian‑Gamma hierarchical prior, where the Gamma prior on precisions λ_r promotes sparsity and automatic rank determination. The sparse foreground S is defined by a blockwise support Ω: each block bY[K]_k is modeled as a mixture—if the latent Bernoulli z_k = 0, the block comes from the low‑rank part plus noise; if z_k = 1, it comes from an independent sparse component plus noise. Sparse block precisions bτ_k follow truncated inverse‑Gamma priors with threshold σ. Variational Bayesian inference employs a mean‑field factorization q(Θ) and iteratively updates the posterior distributions of the CP factors (Gaussian), λ (Gamma), bτ_k (truncated inverse‑Gamma), z_k (Bernoulli with mean ¯z_k), and the mixture weight η (Beta). Expectations for the truncated inverse‑Gamma are computed via asymptotic approximations valid for small σ. After convergence, the hard support estimate is bΩ = {k : ¯z_k > 0}, the low‑rank part bL is built from the factor posterior means, and the sparse part is bS = P_bΩ[Y].

## Key Results
- Synthetic tensors: RPCC attains a median relative root squared error (RRSE) below 2.5×10⁻⁴ and intersection‑over‑union (IoU) exactly 1, indicating near‑optimal recovery.
- CDnet video foreground extraction: RPCC achieves AUCF1 up to 0.72 and AUCIoU up to 0.57, far surpassing the best RPCA competitor (0.39 and 0.28, respectively).
- Hyperspectral anomaly detection (four datasets): RPCC yields the highest AUCF1 (≤ 0.73) and AUCIoU (≤ 0.57) while also reducing false‑alarm area (AUCFa) compared to RPCA‑based and state‑of‑the‑art methods.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Blockwise Support Modeling
- **Claim:** RPCC directly models occlusion by treating the sparse support Ω as an optimization variable, ensuring sparse elements replace background in those locations rather than adding to it.
- **Mechanism:** The constraint P_Ω⊥[Y] = P_Ω⊥[L] forces the low‑rank component to match observations in non‑support regions, while S = P_Ω[Y] captures the foreground. This avoids the additive mismatch in RPCA where S is superimposed on L.
- **Core assumption:** The sparse foreground occupies contiguous blocks (e.g., entire pixels in color images) and the support is blockwise, not elementwise.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract: "directly models the occlusion scenario Y = P_Ω⊥(L) + S"; Section 4.1: "RPCC avoids underfitting the blockwise pattern... every pixel (which is a block of three elements) is either included in or excluded from S as a whole."
- **Break condition:** If the sparse component does not exhibit blockwise structure (e.g., isolated single‑element outliers), the blockwise assumption may underfit and degrade performance.

### Mechanism 2: Noise‑Induced Hard Classification via VBI
- **Claim:** Manually adding Gaussian noise with small variance enables tractable variational Bayesian inference (VBI) and yields a hard classifier for support as σ → 0.
- **Mechanism:** The observation is perturbed to bY = Y + E, with E ~ N(0, σ). The variational posterior for block assignment z_k is Bernoulli with probability ¯z_k. As σ → 0, ELBO terms drive ¯z_k to 0 or 1 (Proposition 4.1), eliminating post‑hoc thresholding.
- **Core assumption:** The original observation Y is noiseless (consistent with RPCA); σ is chosen sufficiently small to approach hard classification but not so small that all blocks become foreground.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract: "with manually added Gaussian noise to enable tractable inference without over‑parameterization." Section 4.2.1: "we assume the original observation to be noiseless... the added noise provides the randomness necessary for BSTF without introducing additional unknown variables." Section 4.4: "the variational parameter ¯zk... converges unequivocally to either 0 or 1 as we take σ toward 0."
- **Break condition:** If σ is not small enough, ¯z_k may remain intermediate (soft classifier). If σ is too small such that E[(by[K]_k - l[K]_k)ᵀ(...)] = o(σ), all blocks may be classified as foreground (per Proposition 4.1 proof).

### Mechanism 3: Bayesian CP Factorization with Sparsity Priors
- **Claim:** Bayesian CP decomposition with Gamma hyperpriors on factor precisions induces sparsity in factors, enabling low‑rank representation for tensors of arbitrary order while automatically determining rank.
- **Mechanism:** The low‑rank component L = CP{A^(n)} where each A^(n) has a Gaussian‑Gamma hierarchical prior. The Gamma prior on precision λ_r encourages many entries to zero under implicit automatic relevance determination, effectively pruning unnecessary factors.
- **Core assumption:** The low‑rank component can be well approximated by a CP decomposition; over‑parameterization (R larger than true rank) is acceptable because sparsity priors select the effective rank.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract: "Bayesian sparse tensor factorization using a CP decomposition." Section 4.2.3: "To capture such sparsity, a Gaussian‑Gamma conjugate distribution pair is assigned to {A^(n)}." Corpus: Strong, with papers on Bayesian CP factorization.
- **Break condition:** If the true low‑rank structure is better captured by Tucker or tensor‑train decompositions, CP may require very large R, increasing complexity and possibly failing to model the background accurately.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** The algorithm takes a tensor Y and hyperparameters (CP rank R, block sizes {J_n}, noise variance σ, hyperpriors a0,b0,c0,d0,α0,β0). It first adds Gaussian noise E ~ N(0, σI) to form bY. Next, a non‑Bayesian CP decomposition on bY provides initial factor estimates and variational parameters. The main loop performs coordinate‑ascent updates for the posteriors of CP factors (Gaussian), precision λ (Gamma), sparse block precisions bτ_k (truncated inverse‑Gamma), block assignments z_k (Bernoulli with mean ¯z_k), and mixture weight η (Beta). After convergence, the low‑rank component bL is constructed from the CP factors, support bΩ = {k : ¯z_k > 0} is extracted, and the sparse component is bS = P_bΩ[Y].

**Critical path:** The most computationally intensive step is updating CP factor covariances, which involves matrix inverses with complexity O(R²(N+K)N + R³∑K_n). Updates for bτ_k and z_k are inexpensive but depend on accurate expectations from the CP factors. The hard classifier emerges from the z_k updates as σ → 0.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Block size selection:* Smaller blocks yield finer support but may miss correlated anomalies; larger blocks enforce coherence but risk including background.  
- *CP rank R:* Larger R improves low‑rank fit but increases computational cost; over‑parameterization is mitigated by sparsity priors.  
- *Noise variance σ:* Must be small enough for near‑hard classification yet large enough to avoid all‑foreground collapse; typically tuned on validation data.  
- *Hyperpriors:* Non‑informative values (e.g., a0=b0=c0=d0=10⁻⁵, α0=β0=1) work, but adaptation may help specific applications.

**Failure signatures:**  
- All ¯z_k collapse to 0 or 1 (indicative of σ mis‑tuning).  
- Poor low‑rank recovery (high RRSE) suggests insufficient CP rank R.  
- VBI non‑convergence (stagnant ELBO) may stem from poor initialization or unsuitable hyperpriors.  
- High false‑alarm rate in anomaly detection often relates to overly sensitive sparse component (small bτ_k) or mismatched block size.

**First three experiments:**  
1. Synthetic tensor recovery with varying sparsity and true rank, measuring RRSE and IoU.  
2. Foreground extraction on the CDnet color video dataset, comparing against RPCA competitors using AUC of F1, IoU, and false‑alarm rate.  
3. Hyperspectral anomaly detection on four datasets, evaluating AUCF1, AUCIoU, and AUCFa against RPCA‑based and specialized detectors.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What specific conditions on the low‑rank tensor L (e.g., incoherence parameters, blockwise sparsity ratio of Ω) guarantee unique recovery of (L, Ω) in RPCC?  
- **Basis:** [explicit] Section 7 lists "Identifiability Analysis" as the first follow‑up study, noting that "it is now meaningful to understand under what conditions RPCC can identify the true posterior distribution of the low‑rank component and its support, and up to what precision."  
- **Why unresolved:** The paper provides empirical evidence of near‑optimal recovery but no theoretical guarantees for identifiability under the RPCC formulation (4.2), analogous to recovery theory for RPCA.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A theoretical proof establishing sufficient conditions (e.g., tensor incoherence, restricted isometry properties for blockwise decompositions) under which RPCC recovers (L, Ω) with high probability, supported by simulations that validate these conditions across varying tensor dimensions and sparsity levels.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Does replacing CP decomposition with Tucker or tensor‑ring decomposition in BCP‑RPCC improve low‑rank component recovery while maintaining hard classification, and for what types of real‑world data?  
- **Basis:** [explicit] Section 7 states the current model's "poor low‑rank representation capability of CP decomposition" motivates research into "enhancing model performance by replacing CP decomposition with more powerful alternatives, such as tensor singular value decomposition, Tucker decomposition, tensor ring decomposition."  
- **Why unresolved:** The paper uses CP due to its straightforward Bayesian treatment, but acknowledges that CP requires a very large rank R for accurate background reconstruction, often infeasible on common devices.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Development of BCP‑RPCC variants with alternative decompositions, theoretical analysis of their recovery properties (e.g., rank determination, conditioning), and empirical comparisons on benchmark datasets (video, hyperspectral) measuring both hard classification accuracy and low‑rank reconstruction error.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How does RPCC's hard classification empirically compare to optimally thresholded soft RPCA methods across diverse hyperspectral anomaly detection scenarios, considering both detection accuracy and operational practicality?  
- **Basis:** [explicit] Section 7 notes that RPCC enables "a new chapter of hyperspectral 'hard' anomaly detection, which eliminates the need for threshold selection and thus offers greater pragmatism." Section 5.3 also remarks that standard ROC‑AUC metrics do not apply to hard classifiers.  
- **Why unresolved:** The experimental comparison in Section 5.3 only includes RPCA‑based methods that produce soft classifiers; it does not benchmark RPCC against soft RPCA methods with oracle‑optimal thresholds chosen per dataset.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Comprehensive experiments on multiple hyperspectral datasets where soft RPCA methods are evaluated at their optimal thresholds (determined via ground truth), and comparisons use metrics suitable for both hard and soft outputs, including F1‑score, IoU, and false‑alarm rate, to quantify the trade‑off between eliminating threshold selection and any peak‑performance loss.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Can the noise variance σ in RPCC be analytically tied to recovery error bounds, enabling automatic rather than heuristic hyperparameter selection?  
- **Basis:** [inferred] The paper manually sets σ ("the variance of the added noise") and states it "must be tuned carefully" (Sec. 5.2). The conclusion suggests that σ "is strongly correlated with the fitting error of the low‑rank component" and "opens the possibility of determining σ theoretically," but no such theory is developed.  
- **Why unresolved:** Selection of σ currently relies on grid search (Fig. 5) without theoretical guidance on how σ relates to the underlying signal properties (e.g., rank, sparsity) or the posterior uncertainty.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Derivation of error bounds for the estimated L or support that explicitly depend on σ, perhaps via the evidence lower bound (ELBO) or posterior variance analysis. This could lead to an automatic selection rule (e.g., minimizing an information criterion or cross‑validation loss) validated on synthetic data where ground truth is known.

## Limitations
- The coarse CP initialization method is unspecified; the choice of algorithm and random restarts may lead to suboptimal convergence or local minima.
- The evidence lower bound (ELBO) convergence tolerance is chosen ad‑hoc; no recommended threshold or theoretical stopping criterion is provided.
- The truncated inverse‑Gamma expectation approximations assume σ → 0; for finite σ they may be inaccurate and can cause numerical instability when σ is extremely small.

## Confidence
- Blockwise support modeling (direct occlusion model): Medium  
- Noise‑induced hard classification (σ→0 yielding binary support): Medium  
- Bayesian CP factorization with sparsity priors (automatic rank determination): High
