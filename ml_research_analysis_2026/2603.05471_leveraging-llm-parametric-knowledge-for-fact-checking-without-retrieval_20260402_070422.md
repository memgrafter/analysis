# Leveraging LLM Parametric Knowledge for Fact Checking without Retrieval

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.05471  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05471  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** INTRA achieves state-of-the-art average ROC‑AUC 77.7% on Llama‑3.1‑8B, outperforms retrieval‑augmented (77.5%) and other retrieval‑free methods (75.0%), with ~20× faster inference.

## Executive Summary
Fact‑checking systems typically rely on external retrieval to supply up‑to‑date evidence, introducing latency, retrieval errors, and failing to exploit the large parametric knowledge already present in modern LLMs. This paper re‑frames the problem: can we verify claims using only an LLM’s internal knowledge, without any retrieval? To answer, they introduce INTRA, a supervised method that aggregates signals from multiple middle layers of a frozen LLM. Across nine diverse datasets, INTRA reaches a new average ROC‑AUC of 77.7% on Llama‑3.1‑8B, surpassing both retrieval‑augmented and other retrieval‑free baselines. It also shows strong generalization to long‑tail and multilingual claims while being approximately 20 times faster than retrieval‑based pipelines.

## Method Summary
INTRA processes a claim by feeding it into a pre‑trained LLM and extracting token embeddings from a range of middle layers (for Llama‑3.1‑8B, layers 11–22 were optimal). For each layer, a learnable attention pooling mechanism computes a weighted sum of the token representations, yielding a claim‑level vector. A linear classifier (trained separately for that layer) maps this vector to a probability of the claim being true. The per‑layer probabilities exhibit different distributions, so the method applies *quantile normalization* to standardize them. Finally, a regression model aggregates the normalized probabilities to produce the final verdict. All components (attention weights, linear classifiers, regression) are trained supervised on labeled claims; the LLM itself remains frozen.

**Key implementation details:**  
- Middle layers are chosen empirically; they consistently provide the richest factual signals.  
- Quantile normalization is applied independently per layer, preserving relative ordering while aligning scales.  
- The regression aggregator is lightweight (e.g., linear) and operates on the normalized probabilities.

## Key Results
- **Average ROC‑AUC 77.7%** on Llama‑3.1‑8B, exceeding Verb+RAG (77.5%) and Sheeps (75.0%) across nine datasets.  
- **~20× speedup** compared to retrieval‑augmented approaches, as no external lookup is performed.  
- **Robust on long‑tail and multilingual claims:** notable improvements in languages like Georgian and on entities with low frequency in pre‑training data.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Three design choices underpin INTRA’s effectiveness.  

**1. Middle‑layer focus.** Empirical analysis reveals that layers around the middle of the network (e.g., 11–22 in Llama‑3.1‑8B) yield the highest verification accuracy. Early layers capture mostly syntactic patterns, while very late layers become task‑specific and may over‑fit to the original pre‑training objective (e.g., next‑token prediction). Middle layers appear to strike a balance, retaining factual associations while being more generalizable.  

**2. Attention‑based pooling.** Instead of simple averaging, learnable attention allows the model to focus on tokens most relevant to factuality (e.g., named entities, relational phrases). This adapts to the claim’s structure and improves the quality of the claim representation fed to the classifier.  

**3. Quantile normalization and cross‑layer regression.** Raw probabilities from different layers have distinct distributions (varying means, variances, calibration). Quantile normalization—a rank‑based, non‑parametric transform—rescales each layer’s probabilities to a common reference distribution without assuming a specific shape. This standardization enables a simple regression model to combine the normalized scores optimally, leveraging complementary signals across layers. Ablations in the paper show that removing either attention pooling or quantile normalization harms performance significantly.

## Foundational Learning
- **Parametric Knowledge**  
  *Why needed:* INTRA relies entirely on facts encoded in the LLM’s weights; no external evidence is retrieved.  
  *Quick check:* Query the model with a well‑known fact (e.g., “capital of France”) and confirm it returns “Paris” without retrieval augmentation.

- **Intermediate Layer Representations**  
  *Why needed:* Middle layers provide the most informative signals for verification, as identified by the authors.  
  *Quick check:* Train linear classifiers on individual layers for a small verification set; expect a peak in accuracy around the middle layers.

- **Attention Pooling**  
  *Why needed:* It learns to weight tokens by their relevance to factuality, producing a more discriminative claim vector than mean pooling.  
  *Quick check:* Replace attention with mean pooling in a minimal INTRA variant; a noticeable drop in ROC‑AUC confirms its contribution.

- **Quantile Normalization**  
  *Why needed:* Aligns probability distributions across layers, preventing any single layer’s scale from dominating the regression.  
  *Quick check:* Train INTRA without normalization (using raw probabilities) and compare to the quantile‑normalized version; performance degradation indicates the need for standardization.

- **Cross‑Layer Aggregation via Regression**  
  *Why needed:* Different layers capture complementary aspects; the regression learns optimal weights for combining their signals.  
  *Quick check:* Substitute the regression with a simple average of normalized probabilities; a drop in performance validates the learned combination.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Claim text → Tokenization → LLM forward pass → Extract embeddings from selected middle layers → For each layer: Attention pooling → Linear classifier → Probability Pₗ → Quantile normalization → Regression aggregator → Final probability → Verdict  

**Critical Path**  
Tokenization → Forward pass (through the highest selected middle layer) → Per‑layer attention pooling → Linear classification → Quantile normalization → Regression → Output. This path runs once per claim during inference.

**Design Tradeoffs**
- *Layer selection*: Fixed middle‑layer range reduces computation but might discard useful early/late signals.  
- *Attention vs. mean pooling*: Attention is adaptive but adds parameters and risk of overfitting; mean pooling is simpler but less expressive.  
- *Quantile normalization vs. parametric (z‑score)*: Quantile is robust to outliers and non‑Gaussian distributions; z‑score assumes normality and may be sensitive to skewed data.  
- *Linear classifier per layer*: Simplicity and low parameter count; deeper classifiers could capture more complex patterns at the cost of data requirements.  
- *Supervised training*: Requires labeled claims but yields higher accuracy; unsupervised baselines (e.g., entropy) are cheaper but less accurate.

**Failure Signatures**
- High variance across per‑layer probabilities, indicating poor calibration.  
- Sharp performance drop on languages or domains absent from training data.  
- Overfitting (training ROC‑AUC ≫ validation ROC‑AUC), especially with small labeled sets.  
- Degradation when increasing the number of layers beyond a certain point, suggesting interference from less informative layers.

**First Experiments**
1. **Baseline replication:** Implement INTRA as described and verify the reported average ROC‑AUC (77.7%) on a standard dataset (e.g., FEVER) within ±1%. This ensures correctness of the pipeline.  
2. **Ablation of quantile normalization:** Train INTRA with raw probabilities, min‑max normalization, and z‑score normalization; compare to the default quantile version to quantify its specific benefit.  
3. **Layer sensitivity analysis:** Train INTRA using only one middle layer (e.g., layer 11, 16, 22) and compare single‑layer results to the multi‑layer INTRA. This confirms the advantage of cross‑layer aggregation and identifies the most informative individual layers.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Why do intermediate layers consistently provide the most informative signals for claim verification, and how does this optimal layer range vary across model architectures, sizes, and training objectives?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The paper identifies middle layers (specifically layers 11–22 for Llama 3.1-8B) as yielding the best performance for INTRA, noting that "middle layers emerge as especially informative" and that training on a small set of middle layers is optimal. The authors state this as a clear priority for future work.  
- **Why unresolved:** The paper demonstrates the empirical effectiveness of using middle layers but does not provide a theoretical or mechanistic explanation for why these layers are superior. The optimal layer range may be model-specific and depend on factors like model scale, tokenizer, and instruction‑tuning, which were not systematically varied.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablation studies across a diverse set of model families (e.g., GPT, Claude, Llama) and scales (from sub‑billion to trillion‑parameter models), combined with representational analyses (e.g., probing for factual vs. linguistic features per layer) to identify what makes middle layers uniquely suited for factuality signals.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** What underlying linguistic or knowledge‑based factors cause certain detection methods (e.g., CCP for Indonesian, INTRA for Georgian) to dominate in specific languages or for long‑tail entities, and can we predict the best detector for a given claim context?  
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors observe that "some methods consistently outperform in certain languages and on claims involving rare entities," showing clear winners (e.g., CCP for Indonesian/Romanian, INTRA for Georgian) and state that "detector choice matters and that targeted selection can yield substantial gains."  
- **Why unresolved:** The analysis is largely descriptive and based on a limited set of five languages with sufficient data. The reasons behind these performance splits—whether due to script morphology, training data distribution, or specific representation patterns—are not investigated. The paper does not propose a predictive framework for detector selection.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Correlational studies linking detector performance to language typological features (e.g., morphological richness, script type), entity frequency in pre‑training corpora, or model calibration errors per language. Development of a meta‑classifier that predicts the optimal verification method given claim features (language, entity popularity, length).

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How does quantile normalization of layer‑wise probabilities in INTRA specifically mitigate inter‑layer distribution shifts, and would alternative standardization techniques (e.g., layer‑wise z‑score, BatchNorm‑style scaling) yield similar robustness gains?  
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] INTRA applies quantile normalization (q(·)) to layer‑wise probabilities before regression, justified by the argument that "raw probabilities are not standardized across layers." This design choice is presented as key to effective aggregation but not compared to other standardization methods.  
- **Why unresolved:** The paper does not include an ablation replacing quantile normalization with other standardization approaches. The exact nature of the distribution mismatches across layers and how quantile normalization—a non‑parametric, rank‑based method—addresses them remains speculative.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments comparing INTRA’s performance with various normalization/standardization techniques (min‑max, z‑score, learned affine transforms) across multiple datasets. Analysis of probability distributions per layer (e.g., variance, skewness) before and after normalization to understand what property quantile normalization preserves.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** To what extent do retrieval‑free fact‑checkers like INTRA generalize to non‑atomic, multi‑entity, or complex compositional claims, and how does claim structure (e.g., nested clauses, co‑reference) impact detection accuracy?  
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The evaluation focuses on atomic claims, explicitly filtered to be "self‑contained" and describe "a unique characteristic of a single entity." The paper acknowledges long‑form generation but only evaluates extracted atomic claims, not the original multi‑claim passages. No analysis is provided on how claim complexity affects performance.  
- **Why unresolved:** The task definition and datasets are restricted to atomic claims. The robustness of INTRA to more complex, real‑world claims—which may involve multiple facts, logical relations, or implicit context—is unknown. This limitation is hinted at by the claim filtering step but not studied.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Create a benchmark of non‑atomic claims (e.g., sentences with multiple facts, conditional statements) and evaluate INTRA and baselines against it. Measure performance degradation as a function of claim complexity metrics (number of entities, dependency depth). Investigate whether failure modes correlate with specific linguistic structures.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** Do retrieval‑free detectors inherently bias against certain factual error types (e.g., temporal inaccuracies, outdated knowledge, regional variations) compared to retrieval‑augmented methods, and can we characterize their "knowledge horizon"?  
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] While INTRA outperforms on average, the paper notes mixed results versus Verb+RAG (e.g., equal ROC‑AUC but lower PR‑AUC on some datasets). The reliance on parametric knowledge suggests a limitation to facts within the model's training cut‑off and distribution, but this is not systematically tested against temporal or geographical knowledge boundaries.  
- **Why unresolved:** The datasets used are not explicitly designed to probe knowledge recency or regional bias (e.g., all of PopQA uses Wikipedia data up to 2019). The comparison to RAG does not control for the knowledge source freshness. The paper does not analyze error subtypes to see if retrieval‑free methods fail more on "dynamic" facts.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Construct challenge sets with known temporal drift (e.g., facts true before 2023 but false after) or region‑specific truths (e.g., "Capital of X" for disputed regions). Compare error rates of retrieval‑free vs. retrieval‑based methods on these subsets. Trace performance decay as a function of entity update frequency in real‑world sources.

## Limitations
- **Statistical significance and potential overfitting:** The reported average ROC‑AUC (77.7%) lacks significance tests; dataset‑specific hyperparameter tuning may inflate results.  
- **Generalization scope:** Claims about long‑tail and multilingual robustness rely on a limited set of evaluation languages and datasets, leaving uncertainty about true out‑of‑distribution performance.  
- **Practical constraints:** The 20× speedup assumes typical retrieval overhead; optimized retrieval could narrow the gap. Supervised training requires labeled data, and its data efficiency, domain transferability, and sensitivity to model scale remain unexplored.

## Confidence
- **Performance superiority over retrieval‑augmented baselines:** Medium  
- **Generalization to long‑tail / multilingual claims:** Low  
- **Efficiency advantage:** Medium
