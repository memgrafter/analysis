# Text-to-Stage: Spatial Layouts from Long-form Narratives

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.17832
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17832
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: 8B Spatializer-GRPO attains 88.5% macro deterministic score on PDNC, outperforming open‑source (83.4%) and proprietary (82.0%) baselines, with strong human preference alignment (ρ=0.95).

## Executive Summary
Converting long-form narratives into explicit stage layouts—a dramaturgical process—poses a challenge for LLMs due to sparse spatial cues. This paper introduces Text-to-Stage, a framework that infers scene boundaries, speaker positions, movements, and quote attribution from novel‑length text. Their method employs a deterministic, theater‑principles‑inspired evaluation suite and a two‑stage training pipeline: rejection supervised fine‑tuning (SFT) via Best‑of‑N sampling from a teacher model, followed by reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). On classic English novels (PDNC), the resulting 8B Spatializer‑GRPO achieves state‑of‑the‑art 88.5% macro deterministic score, with particularly strong global staging (Stage Position 92.5%, Character Positioning 98.2%) and high text grounding (Quote Attribution 91.8%, Alias Resolution 99.1%). The evaluator correlates highly with human judgments (ρ=0.95, 93.3% rank accuracy), confirming that rule‑based rewards can capture expert staging preferences.

## Method Summary
The system processes 4096‑token narrative windows. A powerful teacher model (Llama‑4‑Maverick) generates N=64 candidate stage‑layout JSONs per window using a detailed dramaturgy prompt. A deterministic evaluator scores each candidate across verifiable components: structural validity, text grounding (quote attribution, alias resolution), spatial composition (stage position, character positioning), movement coherence, and scene transitions. The highest‑scoring valid candidate becomes the target for rejection SFT: a base LLM (e.g., Qwen3‑8B) is fine‑tuned on (window, target JSON) pairs. Subsequently, GRPO is applied: starting from the SFT checkpoint, the policy samples G=8 rollouts per window, computes deterministic rewards, forms group‑relative advantages, and updates the policy with a PPO‑style clipped objective and a KL penalty to a frozen reference model. This directly optimizes the rule‑based score, pushing performance beyond the SFT demonstrations.

## Key Results
- 8B Spatializer‑GRPO achieves 88.5% macro deterministic score on PDNC, surpassing open‑source (83.4%) and proprietary (82.0%) baselines.
- Notable per‑component scores: Stage Position 92.5%, Character Positioning 98.2%, Movement Coherence 57.4%, Quote Attribution 91.8%, Alias Resolution 99.1%.
- Deterministic evaluator strongly correlates with human pairwise preferences (Spearman ρ=0.95, rank accuracy 93.3%) and predicts choices (AUC 0.701).
- Ablations confirm each reward component contributes uniquely; GRPO enhances robustness to prompt variations compared to SFT alone.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** A deterministic, dramaturgy‑inspired reward function provides a verifiable training signal that aligns with human spatial reasoning preferences for stage layouts.
- **Mechanism:** The reward decomposes staging quality into rule‑based components (structural validity, text grounding, spatial composition, movement coherence, scene transitions). Each component operationalizes theatrical principles (e.g., proxemics, balance, movement economy) into checkable constraints and scores. During training, this signal guides model selection (Best‑of‑N) and policy updates (GRPO).
- **Core assumption:** Human expert preferences for plausible, coherent stage blocking can be sufficiently captured by a fixed set of hand‑crafted, verifiable rules that are computable from the model's structured JSON output and the source text.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "dramaturgy‑inspired deterministic evaluation suite... directly optimizing the deterministic score."
  - [section 3.2] Detailed description of the five reward components and their grounding in dramaturgical principles.
  - [section 5.2] The deterministic evaluator shows ρ = 0.95 Spearman correlation with human pairwise preferences and 93.3% rank accuracy, substantially outperforming LLM‑as‑judge variants.
  - [corpus] Strong alignment with human experts on spatialized audio renderings (Cohen's κ = 0.62), but note the evaluation is still on a narrow corpus (PDNC classic English novels).
- **Break condition:** If the rule set fails to capture nuanced, context‑dependent staging preferences that humans recognize but rules cannot encode (e.g., subtle emotional subtext driving movement), or if the rules incentivize "reward hacking" by over‑optimizing for explicit constraints at the expense of implicit dramatic quality.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Rejection Supervised Fine‑Tuning (SFT) using Best‑of‑N sampling from a teacher model, filtered by the deterministic reward, initializes the student model with high‑quality demonstrations that already satisfy core dramaturgical constraints.
- **Mechanism:** For each training passage, a strong teacher model generates N candidate stage layouts. The deterministic evaluator selects the highest‑scoring valid candidate. The student model is fine‑tuned on these (passage, selected layout) pairs. This bootstraps the student with examples that already exhibit rule‑compliant spatial reasoning.
- **Core assumption:** A sufficiently powerful teacher model can generate a non‑trivial fraction of plausible stage layouts for a given narrative passage, and the deterministic reward is a reliable enough selector to identify these high‑quality candidates from the teacher's distribution.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.3] Explicit description of "rejection SFT" using Best‑of‑N sampling and reward‑based filtering.
  - [section 5.1] The Spatializer‑SFT (trained this way) significantly outperforms zero‑shot baselines (84.8% vs. ~83% best zero‑shot).
  - [appendix B] Best‑of‑N selection with the deterministic oracle (reward) provides the largest gains in candidate quality compared to LLM‑judge selectors.
  - [corpus] The teacher model ("Llama‑4‑Maverick") and dataset construction process are described, but the intrinsic diversity and quality of the teacher's raw outputs before filtering are not quantified.
- **Break condition:** If the teacher model's capability ceiling is too low (rarely producing layout candidates with > threshold reward), the filtered dataset will lack examples of complex or nuanced staging, limiting the student's learning potential. Overfitting to the teacher's stylistic biases could also occur.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) further refines the model beyond SFT by directly optimizing the deterministic reward, improving robustness and pushing performance on the most challenging components (global staging, movement coherence).
- **Mechanism:** Starting from the SFT model, GRPO samples multiple candidate layouts per passage, computes rewards with the deterministic evaluator, and updates the policy to increase the likelihood of outputs that score higher relative to a group baseline, while regularizing with a KL penalty to a frozen reference model. This on‑policy optimization fine‑tunes the model's internal decision‑making to better satisfy the rule‑based objectives.
- **Core assumption:** The deterministic reward, despite being a proxy, provides a dense and directional enough signal for policy gradient methods to improve long‑horizon, multi‑faceted spatial reasoning (e.g., coherent movement over many dialogue turns) that may be poorly learned from static SFT demonstrations alone.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Trained... followed by RL from verifiable rewards with GRPO... achieved 88.5% macro deterministic score, surpassing... baselines."
  - [section 3.4] GRPO objective description with group‑relative advantages and KL regularization.
  - [section 5.1] Spatializer‑GRPO (88.5%) beats Spatializer‑SFT (84.8%) and all baselines. Gains are especially strong in Stage Pos. (92.5% vs 77.7% SFT) and Char. Pos. (98.2% vs 96.1% SFT).
  - [section 6] GRPO model shows far less prompt sensitivity than SFT (88.50% -> 87.82% vs 84.80% -> 58.33%), indicating improved robustness and internalization of principles.
  - [corpus] Ablation in Table 2 shows all reward components contribute uniquely; removing movement coherence or scene transition terms specifically harms those metrics, suggesting GRPO is effectively optimizing these nuanced aspects.
- **Break condition:** If the reward signal is sparse, noisy, or mis‑specified for certain critical aspects (e.g., scene transitions remain the lowest‑scoring component in Table 1), RL may overfit to easier‑to‑optimize components or destabilize without clear gradients for the hardest aspects.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Proxemics & Stage Composition Principles**
  - Why needed: The task requires understanding non‑verbal communication through space (e.g., front stage = prominence, balanced composition = visual clarity). The model must internalize these theatrical conventions to generate plausible layouts from sparse textual cues.
  - Quick check: Given a heated argument between two characters, how would proxemic theory suggest placing them on a 3x3 stage grid to reflect their relationship?

- **Concept: Structured Output Generation with Schemas**
  - Why needed: The output is a complex JSON object with specific fields (scene splits, quote attribution, stage positions). The model must generate syntactically valid JSON while maintaining semantic consistency across fields (e.g., a character's position updated correctly across quotes).
  - Quick check: If a character enters a scene in "back stage left" and later moves to "front stage center" after a dramatic revelation, how should the JSON reflect this temporal change without breaking the schema?

- **Concept: Long‑Horizon Temporal Consistency**
  - Why needed: Layouts must be coherent across a contiguous narrative window (4096 tokens). Positions and movements need to form a logical sequence (e.g., no teleporting, consistent entrances/exits). This is a state‑tracking problem over many dialogue turns.
  - Quick check: If Character A exits in quote #10 and re‑enters in quote #50, what constraints should their new position satisfy relative to their last known position and the current scene's other occupants?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Input Processor -> Teacher Model (Best‑of‑N) -> Deterministic Evaluator (selection) -> Rejection SFT Filter -> SFT Trainer -> GRPO Rollout Sampler -> GRPO Advantage Calculator -> Policy Updater

**Critical path:** The deterministic evaluator is the single most critical component. Its design dictates what the model learns. All training stages (SFT filtering, GRPO reward) and evaluation depend on it. Flaws or mis‑specifications here propagate to the entire system.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Discrete vs. Continuous Stage: Discrete 3×3 grid simplifies verification and rule design but sacrifices expressive power (no smooth trajectories). Chosen for verifiability.
- Rule‑Based vs. Learned Reward: Hand‑crafted rules provide clear, grounded, and noise‑free signals but may miss subjective nuances. LLM‑as‑judge is more flexible but noisier and more expensive; they used it only for validation.
- GRPO vs. Standard PLO: GRPO uses a group baseline (no value network), reducing memory/compute for long outputs. It may have higher variance but works well here with a reliable per‑output reward.

**Failure signatures:**
- High text grounding (Quote Attr/Alias Res) but low movement coherence: Model attributes speakers correctly but produces chaotic, unmotivated movements (e.g., constant flipping between front/back).
- Valid JSON but poor scene transitions: Model splits scenes incorrectly (e.g., mid‑dialogue) or fails to handle entrances/exits logically (new characters appear downstage without justification).
- Prompt sensitivity collapse: Performance drops drastically with a simplified prompt (as seen in SFT model in Table 4), indicating the model hasn't internalized principles and is relying on prompt engineering.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Reward Component Ablation:** Train GRPO models with one reward component removed at a time (Table 2). Validates that each principle (e.g., movement coherence) contributes unique, non‑redundant learning signal.
2. **Prompt Robustness Test:** Compare model performance under full "dramaturgy" prompt vs. "minimal" prompt (Table 4). Tests whether RL has internalized principles or just exploits prompt cues.
3. **Backbone Transferability:** Apply the same SFT+GRPO recipe to a different base model (LLaMA 3.1 vs. Qwen3, Figure 3b). Checks generalizability of the training recipe across model families.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
How can the discrete 3×3 stage grid and turn‑level position updates be extended to model continuous 3D trajectories and more expressive movement grammars? The current representation intentionally simplifies spatial layout into a coarse grid to ensure verifiability and ease of evaluation, but this limits physical realism and expressivity for downstream applications requiring fluid motion. Developing a hybrid framework that retains verifiable constraints while outputting continuous waypoints or Bézier curves would be a first step, followed by validation via new deterministic checks for trajectory smoothness and plausibility correlated with human perception.

### Open Question 2
What multimodal signals (e.g., prosody, speaker identity, environmental sound) most effectively improve spatial layout inference from narrative text, and how should they be integrated into the training pipeline? The paper’s model operates solely on text, ignoring rich audiobook production cues that strongly influence real‑world spatialization decisions. Collecting a dataset of audiobooks with aligned transcripts, speaker diarization, and prosodic annotations, then training a multimodal variant of Spatializer‑GRPO, could measure improvements in human preference studies and deterministic movement coherence scores.

### Open Question 3
Why does scene transition quality remain the lowest‑scoring component, and what architectural or reward‑design changes can improve long‑horizon consistency across scene boundaries? The deterministic reward includes scene‑structure checks, but they may be insufficiently shaped or fail to capture subtle narrative continuities. The model may also lack a persistent scene state mechanism. Potential directions include redesigning the scene‑transition reward (e.g., add explicit carry‑over consistency penalties, entrance/exit logic) or augmenting the architecture with a scene‑memory module; evaluation would use targeted diagnostics on long passages and human judgements on scene‑flow naturalness.

### Open Question 4
To what extent do the learned spatialization patterns generalize beyond classic English novels to modern dialogue styles, other genres, and non‑English narratives? The PDNC and FanFic training/evaluation datasets are centered on classic English literature, raising potential domain‑shift concerns. Creating cross‑domain test sets (e.g., contemporary fiction, non‑English translations, film scripts) and measuring drops in deterministic scores and human preference alignment would probe this, as would investigating domain‑adaptive fine‑tuning.

### Open Question 5
What explains the observed uneven transfer of GRPO improvements across model backbones (e.g., Qwen3 vs. LLaMA), and how can RL with verifiable rewards be made more architecture‑agnostic? Under the same recipe, Qwen and LLaMA backbones do not benefit equally, possibly due to differences in base model priors (instruction‑following strength, inherent spatial reasoning) or sensitivity to reward shaping. A controlled study varying model scale and family, measuring per‑component reward improvements, KL trajectories, and output diversity, could identify predictive factors and lead to more robust RL recipes.

## Limitations
- The rule‑based evaluator may not fully capture subtle, context‑dependent staging preferences that humans recognize.
- The teacher model’s (Llama‑4‑Maverick) capability ceiling is unknown and may limit the quality of SFT seed data.
- The training corpus (PDNC) is narrow, focusing on classic English novels; generalization to other genres, modern dialogue, or non‑English narratives is unproven.
- The discrete 3×3 stage grid sacrifices expressive power for verifiability, potentially limiting the realism of generated movements.

## Confidence
- Deterministic reward alignment with human judgments: **High** (ρ=0.95, rank accuracy 93.3%).
- Rejection SFT utility: **Medium** (improves over zero‑shot but depends on teacher quality).
- GRPO refinement: **High** (consistent gains and improved robustness across prompts).

## Next Checks
1. **Rule‑completeness audit:** Evaluate model outputs on a curated set of complex staging scenarios (e.g., emotional subtext, symbolic movements) via expert review to identify missing constraints.
2. **Teacher ceiling measurement:** Compute the maximum achievable reward from the teacher’s N=64 samples per example; low median top‑reward would indicate weak SFT seed data.
3. **Cross‑genre robustness:** Test on a held‑out modern novel/screenplay corpus; a significant drop would indicate overfitting to PDNC conventions and motivate domain‑adaptive training.
