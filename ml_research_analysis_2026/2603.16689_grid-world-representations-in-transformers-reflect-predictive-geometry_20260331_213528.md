# Grid-World Representations in Transformers Reflect Predictive Geometry

## Quick Facts
* **arXiv ID:** 2603.16689
* **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16689
* **Reference count:** 28
* **Primary result:** Decoder-only transformers trained on constrained random walks develop internal representations that are linearly aligned with the 2D analytic sufficient statistic for next-step prediction.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether transformers learn internal world models that correspond to the predictive geometry of a data distribution. Using a simplified, analytically solvable task—constrained random walks on a 2D grid with a fixed endpoint—the authors derive that optimal prediction depends solely on a low-dimensional sufficient vector (position relative to target scaled by remaining time). They train small decoder-only transformers on exact data from this process and find that activations across layers, especially in LayerNorm modules, show strong linear alignment with these sufficient vectors via probing and centered kernel alignment (CKA). The final LayerNorm collapses to a 2D manifold matching the predictive geometry, with alignment improving alongside decreasing loss. This demonstrates that even simple predictive tasks can induce geometry-aligned world-model-like structures in transformers.

## Method Summary
The study uses a Markov process where a random walker on a 2D grid must reach a fixed endpoint `p` in exactly `T` steps. The analytic solution shows the optimal next-step distribution depends only on a 2D sufficient statistic `v_t = (x_t - p)/(T-t-1)`. Transformers (4 layers, `d_model=128`) are trained via next-token cross-entropy on prefixes from the exact distribution. After training, activations from various layers (residual streams, LayerNorms) are extracted for all prefix sequences. These are compared to the analytic sufficient vectors (or their 4D centered log-prob equivalent) using two metrics: (1) linear probe `R²` with inverse-degeneracy weighting, and (2) weighted centered kernel alignment (lCKA). Dimensionality is assessed via weighted PCA on final LayerNorm activations. Training dynamics of alignment and loss are tracked to correlate representation formation with predictive performance.

## Key Results
* Transformer activations show high linear alignment (`R²` and lCKA approaching 1) with the analytic sufficient vectors, with final LayerNorm demonstrating the strongest alignment.
* The final LayerNorm's representation occupies a near-perfect 2D manifold (99% variance explained by two principal components), matching the dimensionality of the predictive geometry.
* Alignment improves monotonically with training alongside decreasing loss, indicating the predictive geometry is learned within the network's internal structure.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Predictive Geometry Guides Representation Learning
The training objective forces the network to internalize the minimal sufficient statistic `v_t` for next-step prediction. The data's predictive manifold is intrinsically low-dimensional (2D), and the transformer's architecture can discover a linear mapping to this manifold via gradient descent. Evidence: Abstract and Section 4.1 report high `R²`/lCKA across layers; Section 4.1 shows final LayerNorm converges to the 2D manifold. Break condition: A high-dimensional or non-linear predictive manifold would yield poor linear probe alignment.

### Mechanism 2: Low-Dimensional Manifold Emergence in LayerNorm
LayerNorm layers, particularly the final one, collapse onto a 2D manifold that matches the predictive geometry. The normalization likely acts as a stabilization bottleneck, and since final LayerNorm precedes the logits, its geometry is directly tied to the Softmax output parameterization. Evidence: Section 4.1 shows PCA on final LayerNorm reveals ~2 principal components; lCKA is highest for LayerNorm. Break condition: Removing LayerNorm might prevent this low-dimensional collapse.

### Mechanism 3: Grammar as Predictive Stochastic Constraints
The walker's formal rules (syntax: must reach `p` in `T` steps) and stochastic generation (semantics: random moves) are encoded together in `v_t`. Its length encodes remaining time (stochastic freedom), and its angle encodes position relative to target (syntactic constraint). Learning optimal prediction inherently captures both. Evidence: Section 3.1 derivation shows `v_t` condenses spatial and temporal aspects; Section 4.3 discusses this duality. Break condition: Removing the fixed endpoint constraint would alter the sufficient statistic and potentially separate these components.

## Foundational Learning
* **Sufficient Statistics:** A minimal statistic that captures all information about future observations. Needed to define the target geometry (`v_t`). Quick check: For a symmetric random walk to a fixed endpoint, the sufficient statistic depends on current position and remaining time—derive why `(x_t - p)/(T-t-1)` is sufficient.
* **Computational Mechanics / Causal States:** Predictive states (future distributions) rather than belief states (hidden state posteriors). Justifies searching for low-dimensional geometries. Quick check: What is the difference between a belief state and a predictive state? Which is lower-dimensional here and why?
* **Information Geometry of Softmax:** The logit space is linear in `v_t`; Softmax maps this to the probability simplex. Links linear algebra of displacements `D` to probability geometry. Quick check: If logits are `-D v_t` (4×2 matrix), what is the dimension of the logit manifold? How does Softmax transform it?

## Architecture Onboarding
* **Component map:** Input Embedding -> Residual Stream (d_model=128) -> (Multi-Head Attention (H=4, d_head=32) -> LayerNorm (post-attn) -> MLP (d_mlp=512, ReLU) -> LayerNorm (pre-FFN)) x L=4 -> Final LayerNorm -> Output Logits (linear to vocab=4)
* **Critical path:** Forward pass through L layers to final LayerNorm; loss is cross-entropy. Post-training, activations from each layer type are extracted for prefix sequences. Targets (`v_t` or 4D centered log-prob) are computed analytically per prefix. Linear probes (weighted least squares) yield `R²`; weighted lCKA measures similarity.
* **Design tradeoffs:** Linear probes test readable geometry; nonlinear probes risk overfitting. 4D centered log-prob vs. 2D `v_t` tradeoff: 4D is complete gauge-invariant target, 2D is interpretable. Inverse-degeneracy weighting prevents bias from overrepresented paths. LayerNorm focus: normalized space may be more stable; final LayerNorm directly precedes logits.
* **Failure signatures:** `R²`/lCKA near zero or degrading with training; alignment only in final layer (not distributed); PCA shows persistent high dimensionality; alignment degrades for shifted/long-horizon walkers without expected changes.
* **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Replicate main finding: train on T=20, p=(0,0); compute lCKA (weighted) between activations and 4D targets per layer; expect increasing lCKA, highest in LayerNorms, ~1 for final LayerNorm.
  2. Dimensionality check: weighted PCA on final LayerNorm activations; compute PCs for 99% variance; expect ~2.
  3. Training dynamics: track final LayerNorm lCKA and validation loss vs. steps; expect concurrent improvement; also monitor cross-validated `R²`.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
* Does alignment with predictive vectors predict generalization to out-of-distribution sequences (e.g., different T or endpoints)? Current work only evaluates in-distribution validation loss.
* Do transformers still align when the generative process has fundamentally hidden states (e.g., non-observable HMMs)? The grid walker has fully observable position.
* Is the alignment robust across architectures (CNNs, RNNs, SSMs) and objectives (masked LM, diffusion)? Only one decoder-only transformer with next-token prediction was tested.
* Do transformers on more complex formal languages (e.g., context-free grammars) exhibit alignment with their predictive geometries? The current regular-language case may not generalize to higher Chomsky hierarchy levels.

## Limitations
* Synthetic task is far simpler than real sequential data, limiting generalizability claims.
* Linear probes may miss non-linear representational structure.
* Inverse-degeneracy weighting scheme could bias results; alternatives unexamined.
* LayerNorm's specific role in inducing low-dimensional manifolds is not isolated from other architectural factors.

## Confidence
* **High:** Alignment with sufficient statistics in this task, supported by consistent CKA/R² results and training dynamics.
* **Medium:** That final LayerNorm collapses to a 2D manifold, based on PCA for one architecture/task.
* **Low:** That this reveals a general "world model" formation mechanism in transformers for more complex, real-world sequences.

## Next Checks
1. **Ablate LayerNorm:** Remove or replace LayerNorm with identity; re-evaluate alignment and dimensionality to test its necessity.
2. **Scale complexity:** Increase grid size (e.g., 10×10) and horizon (T=50); test if alignment remains high and manifold stays 2D despite larger state space.
3. **Nonlinear probing & seed stability:** Compare linear vs. MLP probe performance; test linear probe transfer across random seeds to assess linearity and reproducibility.
