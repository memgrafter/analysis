# Scalable Kernel-Based Distances for Statistical Inference and Integration
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.21846
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21846
- **Reference count**: 0
- **Primary result**: Introduces optimally-weighted MMD, conditional Bayesian quadrature, and kernel quantile discrepancies to achieve scalable, efficient, and powerful kernel-based statistical inference.

## Executive Summary
This thesis advances the theory and practice of kernel-based distances for statistical inference and integration. It presents three main contributions: (1) optimally-weighted maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) estimators that exploit simulator smoothness to reduce simulation counts in simulation-based inference; (2) conditional Bayesian quadrature, a method for estimating conditional expectations with improved data efficiency and principled uncertainty quantification; and (3) kernel quantile discrepancies (KQDs), a new family of probability metrics based on directional quantiles in reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces, which satisfy metric properties under weaker conditions than the MMD and often achieve higher test power at lower computational cost. The work combines rigorous statistical analysis with practical algorithms, demonstrating improvements on synthetic and real-world datasets.

## Method Summary
The thesis evaluates the proposed kernel quantile discrepancies (KQDs) as a novel tool for two-sample hypothesis testing. The core method, e‑KQD2, uses a Gaussian reference measure on the reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) unit sphere to generate random projection directions. For each direction, it computes the quantiles of the projected data and aggregates discrepancies across quantiles and directions. The implementation involves sampling reference points $z_{1:M}$, computing feature vectors via the kernel, generating random feature weights $\lambda_{1:M}\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$, and normalizing to obtain unit vectors $u_l$. The test statistic is the average weighted absolute difference between sorted projected samples from the two distributions. The method is compared against baseline kernel‑based tests (MMD, MMD‑Multi, MMD‑Lin, sliced Wasserstein) using permutation‑based thresholds. Experiments cover synthetic distributions (multivariate g‑and‑k, two moons, fractional Brownian motion) and real image datasets (Galaxy MNIST, CIFAR‑10 vs. CIFAR‑10.1). The evaluation measures test power at significance level $\alpha=0.05$, Type I error control, and computational runtime across varying sample sizes $N$ and dimensions $d$.

## Key Results
- **Optimal weighting for MMD**: Reduces the number of simulations required in simulation‑based inference by leveraging smoothness of the simulator via Bayesian quadrature weights.
- **Conditional Bayesian quadrature**: Enables efficient estimation of conditional expectations with principled uncertainty quantification, outperforming standard BQ in data efficiency.
- **Kernel quantile discrepancies**: Defines probability metrics under weaker conditions than MMD; empirical results show equal or superior power in two‑sample tests with lower computational cost than fast MMD approximations.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Optimally‑Weighted MMD Estimation
The method replaces the standard V‑statistic average in MMD estimation with Bayesian quadrature weights that minimise an upper bound on the MMD error. These weights exploit smoothness of the simulator $G_\theta$ when using Sobolev kernels of order $s > d/2$, assuming a bounded base measure density. The bound (Theorem 9) guarantees a rate improvement under these conditions. The approach fails if the simulator is non‑smooth, the kernel is not characteristic, or the Gram matrix $k(G_\theta(u_n),G_\theta(u_m))$ is near‑singular. Evidence: abstract and Section 3.2.

### Conditional Bayesian Quadrature
Two‑stage hierarchical Gaussian process: first fit independent GPs to $f(x,\theta_t)$ for each $\theta_t$, integrate to obtain BQ estimates $I_{BQ}(\theta_t)$ with uncertainties; second regress $\theta \mapsto I(\theta)$ using these estimates as noisy observations in a heteroscedastic GP. This yields improved data efficiency and principled uncertainty quantification. Key assumptions: $f$ smooth in $x$ and $\theta$ (Sobolev spaces $W^{s_f,2}$, $W^{s_I,2}$) and Sobolev kernels of matching smoothness. Convergence rate $O(N^{-s_x/d+\varepsilon} + T^{-1/4})$ (Theorem 12). Break conditions: high‑dimensional inputs, poor kernel choice, heteroscedastic noise mis‑specification.

### Kernel Quantile Discrepancies
KQDs embed a distribution $P$ via directional quantiles $\rho^\alpha_{u,P}$ of the projected measure $u_\#P$ in the RKHS, for directions $u$ on the RKHS unit sphere. A discrepancy aggregates differences over quantiles $\alpha$ (with measure $\nu$) and directions $u$ (with measure $\gamma$). Under a separating continuous kernel and mild conditions on $\nu,\gamma$, the e‑KQD and sup‑KQD are probability metrics (Theorems 24 and 27), requiring weaker assumptions than the characteristic property for MMD. Estimators based on sorting projected samples achieve near‑linear complexity. Break conditions: non‑separating kernels, degenerate direction measure $\gamma$, unstable quantile estimation for heavy‑tailed projections. Evidence: abstract, Section 6.2, corollary on kernel quantile‑characteristic property.

## Foundational Learning

**Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS)**
- **Why needed**: All three mechanisms rely on the RKHS: MMD and BQ use kernel mean embeddings; KQDs use quantiles of projected measures in the RKHS.
- **Quick check**: For a kernel $k$, what is the RKHS norm of $f(x)=k(x,x_0)$ and why does the reproducing property matter?

**Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD)**
- **Why needed**: MMD is the baseline kernel‑based distance; the thesis improves its estimation (optimal weighting) and introduces alternatives (KQDs).
- **Quick check**: When is MMD a metric (i.e., characteristic kernel), and what does that mean for distinguishing distributions?

**Sobolev Spaces & Smoothness**
- **Why needed**: Smoothness assumptions (Sobolev kernels, smooth simulators/integrands) drive convergence rates in the optimally‑weighted MMD and conditional BQ, and guide kernel selection.
- **Quick check**: If $f \in W^{s,2}$, what does $s$ indicate about its derivatives, and how does this relate to RKHS norms for Matérn kernels?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**: Simulation engine → Kernel integrator → Optimiser → Discrepancy calculator → Inference module.  
The simulation engine draws latent inputs $u\sim U$ and generates $(θ, x, f(x,θ))$ via simulator $G_θ$. The kernel integrator computes kernel matrices and closed‑form integrals (e.g., for Gaussian/Matérn kernels). The optimiser determines optimal weights (Mechanism 1), hyperparameters (via ML/CV), or fits the GP over $θ$ (Mechanism 2). The discrepancy calculator evaluates the chosen kernel distance (MMD, e‑KQD, sup‑KQD). The inference module applies the discrepancy in tasks like ABC, MDE, or two‑sample testing.

**Critical path**: Data generation → Kernel embedding/computation → Optimisation/aggregation → Output. Dependencies: BQ requires kernel mean integrals; CBQ stage‑2 depends on stage‑1 outputs; KQD projection sampling needs the reference measure $γ$.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- *Kernel choice*: Smoothness (Matérn vs. Gaussian) vs. closed‑form integrals; characteristic property vs. computational convenience.
- *Sample allocation*: Mechanism 1: $N$ per $θ$ with optimal weights; Mechanism 2: trade‑off $N$ (samples per $θ$) vs. $T$ (parameters); Mechanism 3: $L$ (directions) vs. $N$ (data points).
- *Stability*: Adding a nugget $λ$ improves matrix inversion but slows convergence; cross‑validation may be slower but yields better‑calibrated uncertainty.

**Failure signatures**:
- *Numerical*: Gram matrix near‑singular (ill‑conditioned weights); unstable hyperparameter optimisation (e.g., ML oversmoothing).
- *Statistical*: Posterior variance mis‑calibrated (BQ/CBQ); low test power for KQD (directed quantiles not capturing differences); inflated Type I error.
- *Implementation*: Slow GP regression for large $T$; memory blow‑up from $N^2$ kernel matrix for large $N$.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. *Synthetic convergence test*: Use a smooth integrand (e.g., Gaussian process sample) with known integral to verify CBQ rate $N^{-s/d}$.
2. *Simulation‑based inference benchmark*: Apply optimal‑weighted MMD on a toy simulator (e.g., Gaussian location model) and compare V‑statistic vs. weighted error.
3. *Two‑sample test on real data*: Compute e‑KQD vs. MMD on binary image datasets (e.g., MNIST 0 vs. 1) to confirm calibration and power.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **Optimal parameterisation for optimally‑weighted MMD**: How to choose the simulator map $G_θ$ and base measure $U$ to minimise rate constants? Section 3.5 notes the constants depend intricately on kernel, simulator smoothness, and $U$, making theoretical guidance difficult. Evidence: explicit in Section 3.5. Resolution would require explicit bounds or empirical studies linking simulator properties to optimal choices.

2. **Active learning for Conditional Bayesian Quadrature**: Can adaptive selection of $N$, $T$, and evaluation points $\theta_{1:T}, x^n_{1:N}$ improve data efficiency? Section 4.5 suggests this as a direction. Currently CBQ uses fixed or random points; no principled acquisition function exists. Resolution would involve defining and testing acquisition strategies (e.g., variance reduction) on benchmarks.

3. **Extension of BQ asymptotic calibration**: Do the asymptotic guarantees for the Brownian motion kernel extend to more general kernels (e.g., Matérn), higher dimensions, and noisy observations? Section 5.7 identifies this gap; results rely on tridiagonal precision structure. Resolution requires sharp lower bounds and spectral analysis for other kernels.

4. **Optimal kernel choice for Kernel Quantile Discrepancies**: How to select kernel family and hyperparameters to maximise test power? Section 6.5 mentions this as analogous to kernel learning for MMD. The current work uses fixed kernels (median heuristic). Resolution would involve developing a kernel alignment or deep kernel framework for KQDs and evaluating on benchmarks.

## Limitations

- **Implementation gaps**: Missing details for sliced Wasserstein max‑SW (optimizer hyperparameters) hinder fair baseline comparison.
- **Dataset ambiguity**: Exact preprocessing and splits for Galaxy MNIST and CIFAR‑10.1 are not specified, risking irreproducibility of real‑data results.
- **Theoretical assumptions**: Smoothness, characteristic kernels, and full‑support reference measures may not hold in practice; break conditions are not extensively stress‑tested, especially in high dimensions.

## Confidence
- **Optimally‑weighted MMD**: Medium confidence. The derivation is rigorous but empirical evaluation is limited; rate improvement depends on smoothness assumptions that may be violated.
- **Conditional Bayesian quadrature**: Medium confidence. Convergence rate proved, but empirical scope is narrow; robustness of heteroscedastic noise model is untested.
- **Kernel quantile discrepancies**: Medium‑High confidence. Theoretical conditions are weaker than MMD and proofs appear sound; initial empirical results are positive, but high‑dimensional stability remains uncertain.
