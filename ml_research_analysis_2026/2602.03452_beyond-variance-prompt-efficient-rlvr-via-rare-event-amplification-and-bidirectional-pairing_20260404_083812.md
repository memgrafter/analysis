# Beyond Variance: Prompt-Efficient RLVR via Rare-Event Amplification and Bidirectional Pairing

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.03452
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03452
- **Reference count:** 36
- **Primary result:** A two‑prompt RLVR approach with Weighted GRPO achieves comparable performance to training on 1209 prompts, improving AIME 2025 Pass@8 from 16.8 to 22.2 and AMC23 Pass@64 from 94.0 to 97.0 on Qwen2.5‑Math‑7B.

## Executive Summary
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) offers a promising path to improve LLMs on tasks with automatic binary feedback, such as mathematical problem solving. However, selecting effective training prompts in low-data regimes remains challenging; variance‑based methods often yield unstable updates. This paper introduces positive–negative pairing: choose one hard‑but‑solvable prompt (low success rate) to amplify rare correct responses as strong positive signals, and one easy‑but‑brittle prompt (high but imperfect success rate) to amplify rare failures as sharp negative penalties. Combined with Weighted Group Relative Policy Optimization (WGRPO), which normalizes advantages per group and scales them inversely with √(p(1-p)), the method amplifies tail events without needing reward shaping. On Qwen2.5‑Math‑7B, using just two carefully selected prompts matches or exceeds models trained on over a thousand prompts, raising AIME 2025 Pass@8 by 5.4 points and AMC23 Pass@64 by 3 points while maintaining stability across base and instruct checkpoints.

## Method Summary
The method consists of an offline probing phase and a lightweight RLVR training loop. During probing, a candidate pool of prompts is evaluated to estimate empirical success rates under the current policy. One prompt with rate near 1/G (hard‑but‑solvable) and one with rate near 1–1/G (easy‑but‑brittle) are selected, where G is the group size (e.g., 8). In training, for each update the policy generates G responses per selected prompt. Rewards are binary: 1 for exact answer match, 0 otherwise. These are mapped to weighted outcomes (+1 correct, –λ_neg incorrect, λ_neg=100) before advantage computation. WGRPO then computes group‑relative advantages by subtracting the group mean and dividing by the group standard deviation (with small ε for numerical stability), which automatically amplifies rare events (high positive advantage when a hard prompt is solved, high negative when an easy prompt fails). The policy is updated with a clipped PPO objective plus a KL penalty to stay close to the reference policy. The entire process uses the same two prompts throughout training, drastically reducing rollout cost while maintaining informative signals.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Rare-event amplification via group normalization
- **Claim:** Group normalization in GRPO automatically amplifies rare events (successes on hard prompts, failures on easy prompts) by scaling advantages inversely with √(p(1-p)).
- **Mechanism:** For a group of G rollouts with success rate p, the normalized advantage for a correct trajectory is A⁺ ~ (1-p)/√(p(1-p)) and for incorrect is A⁻ ~ -p/√(p(1-p)). As p→0, A⁺ becomes large positive; as p→1, |A⁻| becomes large.
- **Core assumption:** The group is non‑degenerate (0 < k < G) and ε_std is small relative to the signal.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "WGRPO applies group‑normalized advantages to reweight binary outcomes and thereby implements rare‑event amplification."
  - [Section 3.1]: Derivation of closed‑form advantages (Eq. 7) showing the dependence on p.
  - [corpus]: Neighbor "Shrinking the Variance: Shrinkage Baselines for Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards" addresses variance in RLVR, but our amplification explicitly exploits tail‑event scaling.
- **Break condition:** If all rollouts are correct or incorrect (p=0 or 1), advantages collapse to zero; also if ε_std is too large, it drowns the amplification.

### Mechanism 2: Bidirectional teaching signals via prompt pairing
- **Claim:** Selecting one hard‑but‑solvable prompt (p≈1/G) and one easy‑but‑brittle prompt (p≈1-1/G) ensures each minibatch provides both a strong positive anchor (from rare successes on the hard prompt) and a strong negative penalty (from rare failures on the easy prompt), stabilizing updates.
- **Mechanism:** The two prompts operate in complementary regimes of the amplification curve. Under WGRPO, the hard prompt yields large A⁺ when a rare success occurs, while the easy prompt yields large |A⁻| when a rare failure occurs. Combining them gives bidirectional supervision.
- **Core assumption:** The success rates of the selected prompts remain within the target intervals throughout training, preserving amplification.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "positive–negative pairing: at each update, we sample a hard‑but‑solvable q⁺ and an easy‑but‑brittle prompt q⁻..."
  - [Section 3.2]: Defines selection criteria (Eq. 8, 9) and probing procedure.
  - [corpus]: Neighbor "No Prompt Left Behind: Exploiting Zero‑Variance Prompts in LLM Reinforcement Learning via Entropy‑Guided Advantage Shaping" focuses on zero‑variance prompts (all correct/incorrect), whereas our method uses extreme but non‑zero variance prompts.
- **Break condition:** If the hard prompt becomes too hard (p≈0) so successes never occur, or the easy prompt becomes too easy (p≈1) so failures never occur, the corresponding signal vanishes. Also, if prompts are unrepresentative, generalization may suffer.

### Mechanism 3: Weighted outcome mapping with λ_neg for robustness
- **Claim:** Mapping binary outcomes to +1 and -λ_neg before group normalization provides a knob to control signal magnitude and prevents degenerate updates, while still enabling amplification.
- **Mechanism:** The weighting factor λ_neg scales outcomes before normalization. In degenerate cases (all correct/incorrect), group mean equals outcome value and std=0, yielding zero advantage after normalization, avoiding spurious updates. For near‑degenerate groups, λ_neg influences σ_q’s scale, making normalization less sensitive to ε_std.
- **Core assumption:** λ_neg is chosen sufficiently large (e.g., 100) to ensure numerical stability and effective degeneracy handling.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [Section 3.1]: Equation (1) defines weighted mapping; Appendix B.2 discusses degenerate groups and λ_neg’s role.
  - [Table 3]: Empirical sweep shows stable performance for λ_neg≥50, but drop for λ_neg=1, confirming importance.
  - [corpus]: Neighbor "Asymmetric REINFORCE for off‑policy reinforcement learning: Balancing positive and negative rewards" also considers asymmetric reward weighting, supporting the idea that imbalance can be beneficial.
- **Break condition:** λ_neg too small (e.g., 1) weakens negative signal and degeneracy handling; extremely large values may cause numerical overflow or over‑downweighting.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept: Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)**  
- *Why needed here:* The paper operates entirely within RLVR, where automatic verifiers provide binary outcome rewards (e.g., 1 for correct, 0 for incorrect) for deterministic tasks like math problems. This setting eliminates reward hacking and avoids training separate reward models.  
- *Quick check:* What typical reward format is used in RLVR for mathematical reasoning, and how is it computed? (Binary: exact answer match yields 1, else 0; sometimes cast to ±1.)

**Concept: Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**  
- *Why needed here:* WGRPO extends GRPO. GRPO’s group normalization is the core mechanism that enables rare‑event amplification; understanding its advantage computation is essential.  
- *Quick check:* In GRPO, for a given prompt, we sample G responses. How is the advantage for each response computed? (Subtract the group mean of rewards, divide by the group standard deviation, then broadcast to token level.)

**Concept: Prompt difficulty characterization by empirical success rate**  
- *Why needed here:* The paper’s prompt selection explicitly targets success rate regimes (low ≈1/G for hard, high ≈1-1/G for easy). This concept underpins positive‑negative pairing.  
- *Quick check:* If a prompt’s empirical success rate is 0.125 with G=8 rollouts, which regime does it belong to (hard‑but‑solvable or easy‑but‑brittle)? (Hard‑but‑solvable, because p≈1/8.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Candidate Prompt Pools -> Success Rate Estimation (Probing) -> Hard/Easy Prompt Pair Selection -> Training Loop (Rollout Generation -> Reward Assignment -> WGRPO Advantage Normalization -> Policy Update)

**Critical path:**  
1. **Pre‑training probing:** For each candidate prompt, sample M groups (e.g., M=1) of size G to estimate success rate. Discard prompts with rates ≤ δ or ≥ 1-δ (δ=1/G). Select q⁺ (hard) with rate closest to 1/G and q⁻ (easy) closest to 1-1/G.  
2. **Training iterations:** For each step, use the same two prompts. Generate G responses per prompt. Compute binary rewards (exact match). Pass rewards to WGRPO to obtain token‑level advantages.  
3. **Policy update:** Optimize π_θ via the clipped objective L_WGRPO (Eq. 4) with AdamW. Repeat for up to 500 steps; optionally checkpoint and evaluate.  
4. (Optional) If performance degrades monotonically, early‑stop and restore best checkpoint.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Data efficiency vs. diversity:** Using only two prompts drastically reduces rollout cost but may limit diversity; however, amplification compensates by focusing on informative tail events.  
- **Group size (G):** Larger G reduces chance of degenerate groups but increases per‑update compute. Paper uses G=8.  
- **λ_neg and ε_std:** λ_neg controls negative weighting; too small (e.g., 1) hurts performance (Table 3). ε_std (1e-6) ensures numerical stability when σ_q→0.  
- **Fixed prompt selection:** Simplicity and stability; re‑probing during training could adapt to policy shifts but adds overhead and may destabilize.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **Degenerate groups frequent:** If one prompt’s success rate drifts to near 0 or 1, many groups become degenerate (all correct/incorrect), yielding zero advantage and stalling learning. Monitor per‑prompt success rates.  
- **Amplification regime lost:** If the hard prompt becomes unsolvable (p≈0) or easy prompt becomes perfect (p≈1), the corresponding amplified signal disappears. Re‑probe periodically.  
- **Distribution mismatch:** If selected prompts are not representative of evaluation benchmarks (e.g., they are from a narrow subdomain), transfer may be poor. Ensure candidate pools cover the intended task distribution.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **WGRPO unit test:** Create synthetic groups with known p (e.g., p=1/8, 7/8) and binary outcomes. Verify that computed advantages match theoretical A⁺≈2.65, A⁻≈-0.38 for p=1/8 (with λ_neg=100, ε_std=1e-6) and swapped for p=7/8. Confirm degenerate groups (all correct/incorrect) yield zero.  
2. **Two‑prompt baseline comparison:** Implement variance‑based prompt selection (pick two highest‑variance prompts from a pool) with standard GRPO, and our easy+hard selection with WGRPO. Train both on Qwen2.5‑Math‑7B for 100 steps on a small math subset (e.g., 50 problems). Expect WGRPO+easy+hard to show higher Pass@k on a held‑out set.  
3. **λ_neg ablation:** Run WGRPO+easy+hard with λ_neg ∈ {1, 50, 100, 200} on the same small setup. Expect λ_neg=1 to underperform significantly, while larger values yield similar improvements, confirming robustness above a threshold.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** What is the minimal number and optimal composition of training prompts required for RLVR to achieve stable and near‑optimal performance on mathematical reasoning, and how does this minimum vary with model scale and benchmark difficulty?  
- **Open Question 2:** Does the positive–negative pairing principle generalize to non‑mathematical, deterministic‑outcome tasks (e.g., code generation, factual QA) and to models with different architectural biases or pre‑training corpora?  
- **Open Question 3:** Can the prompt selection process be made adaptive during training (e.g., cycling or dynamically updating the easy/hard prompts) to maintain informative rare‑event signals as the policy improves, rather than using a fixed pair selected at initialization?  
- **Open Question 4:** How does the choice of group size *G* in WGRPO interact with the "rare‑event" design, and what is the optimal *G* for different levels of data scarcity (i.e., when scaling beyond two prompts)?  
- **Open Question 5:** What is the relationship between the difficulty distributions of the positive and negative candidate pools (e.g., AIME vs. DeepScaleR‑sub) and the robustness of the selected prompt pairs? Could a single unified pool yield equally effective pairs, or is explicit separation into "hard" and "easy" pools necessary?
