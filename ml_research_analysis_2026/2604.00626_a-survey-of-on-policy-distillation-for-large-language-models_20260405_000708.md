# A Survey of On-Policy Distillation for Large Language Models
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2604.00626
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00626
- Reference count: 22
- Primary result: OPSDC reduces chain-of-thought token count by 57–59% while improving accuracy by up to 16% on math benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
On-policy distillation (OPD) addresses the exposure bias problem in off-policy knowledge distillation for large language models by having the student generate its own trajectories and receive teacher feedback on these self-generated outputs, grounded in interactive imitation learning. OPD methods are classified along three axes: feedback signal (logit-based, outcome-based, self-play), teacher access (white-box, black-box, self-distillation), and loss granularity (token, sequence, hybrid). Primary outcomes include significant efficiency gains (e.g., OPSDC reduces CoT token count by 57-59% while improving accuracy by up to 16% on math benchmarks), the ability for students to surpass teachers via reward-guided OPD, and the adoption of on-policy distillation in state-of-the-art models like DeepSeek-R1 and Qwen3. Key challenges remain in establishing scaling laws, developing uncertainty-aware objectives to prevent echo chambers, and extending OPD to multi-turn agentic interactions.

## Method Summary
On-policy distillation (OPD) trains a student model by iteratively generating responses from its current policy and computing a divergence loss between the student and teacher distributions over those self-generated tokens. The core procedure: sample prompts from a dataset, generate full autoregressive responses from the student, and for each token position, obtain the teacher's conditional distribution (via white-box logits, black-box signals, or self-teacher with privileged information). The loss is an f-divergence (e.g., KL, JSD) aggregated over tokens and batch. Optional mixing with off-policy data via a coefficient λ allows a gradual transition to pure on-policy training. This mimics interactive imitation learning (DAgger) and mitigates exposure bias by aligning the training distribution with the inference distribution.

## Key Results
- OPSDC reduces chain-of-thought token count by 57–59% while improving accuracy by up to 16% on math benchmarks.
- Reward-guided OPD enables students to surpass teacher performance (e.g., ExOPD).
- On-policy distillation is a core component in state-of-the-art models like DeepSeek-R1 and Qwen3.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: On-Policy Sampling Eliminates Exposure Bias
- Claim: Training the student on its own generated rollouts reduces error compounding from quadratic O(T²) to linear O(T) by aligning the training distribution with the inference distribution.
- Mechanism: The student samples trajectories from its current policy πθ and receives teacher feedback on these self-generated states. This mimics interactive imitation learning (DAgger), where the expert (teacher) is queried on the learner’s visited states, preventing distribution shift.
- Core assumption: The teacher’s conditional distribution p_T(·|s) provides accurate supervision even for out-of-distribution prefixes generated by the student. If the teacher itself is miscalibrated on such prefixes, the gradient may be misleading.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [abstract]: “On-policy distillation (OPD) addresses this by letting the student generate its own trajectories and receive teacher feedback on these self-generated outputs, grounded in interactive imitation learning.”
  - [section 2.3]: “Exposure bias arises from the inequality d_D(s)≠d_πθ(s)… OPD resolves this by changing the training expectation from E_{s∼d_D} to E_{s∼d_πθ}. By generating responses online, the student confronts its own mistakes… reducing error accumulation from O(ϵT²) to O(ϵT).”
  - [corpus]: Neighbor “Revisiting On-Policy Distillation” identifies failure modes when teacher guidance is unreliable on student-generated prefixes (weak anchor; the paper itself provides stronger evidence).
- Break condition: If the teacher’s feedback becomes noisy or biased on student-generated prefixes (e.g., due to distributional mismatch), the student may be pulled into incorrect modes, exacerbating errors rather than reducing them.

### Mechanism 2: Adaptive f-Divergence Selection Balances Mode Behavior
- Claim: Using a fixed divergence (Forward KL, Reverse KL, JSD) globally harms performance; adaptive per-token switching based on teacher confidence captures both precise imitation (Reverse KL on confident tokens) and diversity preservation (Forward KL on uncertain tokens).
- Mechanism: At each token, compute a weight ω_t from teacher-student log-prob ratio or teacher entropy. Combine Forward and Reverse KL losses with that weight. This lets the student strictly follow high-confidence teacher predictions while exploring plausible alternatives where the teacher is uncertain.
- Core assumption: Teacher confidence (low entropy) correlates with correctness; when teacher is uncertain, multiple outputs are valid and should be preserved.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 4.1.1]: “ToDi adaptively combines Forward and Reverse KL at each position using a sigmoid-based weighting derived from the teacher–student log-probability ratio.” “Entropy-Aware OPD gates between Reverse KL and Forward KL based on teacher entropy.”
  - [corpus]: “X-KD” uses AVRIL to learn in a reconstructed teacher environment, implicitly adapting to the teacher’s reward structure.
- Break condition: If the teacher’s confidence is miscalibrated (e.g., overconfident on wrong answers), adaptive weighting may amplify errors by over-weighting Reverse KL on incorrect high-confidence tokens.

### Mechanism 3: Privileged Information Self-Distillation Breaks Saturation
- Claim: In self-distillation without external teachers, saturation occurs due to mode collapse. Using privileged information (available only during training, e.g., ground-truth answers or source documents) provides a fresh supervision signal that prevents the student from reinforcing its own flawed distributions.
- Mechanism: The same model acts as both teacher (conditioned on privileged info) and student (without it). The student generates rollouts; the teacher, with access to privileged context, provides supervision. This breaks the self-play loop’s echo chamber.
- Core assumption: The privileged information is sufficiently informative to provide a high-quality supervisory signal that the student cannot trivially game.
- Evidence anchors:
  - [section 5.2.2]: “OPSD… The teacher policy conditions on both the problem x and the ground-truth answer y⋆, while the student conditions only on x.” “GATES uses consensus-based gating… distillation proceeds only when the tutor demonstrates high agreement.”
  - [abstract]: “self-play” is listed as a feedback signal.
- Break condition: If the privileged information is ambiguous or the consensus threshold is too high, the distillation signal becomes too sparse to sustain learning; or if the model learns to exploit the privileged context without generalizing, test-time performance degrades.

## Foundational Learning

- **Why needed**: It is the core problem that on-policy distillation solves; understanding the train-test distribution mismatch explains why OPD is necessary.

- **Why needed**: The choice between Forward KL, Reverse KL, and JSD determines whether the student covers all teacher modes or seeks a single mode, directly affecting stability and output quality.

## Architecture Onboarding
#### Component Map
The pipeline consists of: a student model that generates responses autoregressively; a teacher model that provides conditional token distributions (white-box: full logits; black-box: scalar/preference; self: conditioned on privileged info); a divergence calculator that computes token-level f-divergence; a loss aggregator that sums over tokens; and an optimizer that updates the student. Optional components include a curriculum scheduler, a reward model for outcome-based feedback, and a discriminator for black-box settings.

#### Critical Path
1. Student generates rollout y ∼ p_θ(·|x) autoregressively.
2. Teacher provides feedback: white-box gives logits for each prefix; black-box gives scalar reward or preference; self-teacher gives conditional logits with privileged info.
3. Compute divergence loss D_f(p_T, p_θ) over rollout tokens (token-level or sequence-level).
4. Backpropagate and update student parameters θ.
5. Repeat.

#### Design Tradeoffs
- Compute cost: On-policy generation is expensive vs off-policy caching; requires careful memory management.
- Diversity vs stability: Forward KL preserves diversity but risks hallucination; Reverse KL is stable but may drop modes.
- Teacher reliance: White-box gives dense signal but needs full teacher memory; black-box reduces memory but provides coarse signal.
- Curriculum design: Which prompts to sample? Uniform vs difficulty-weighted affects learning efficiency.

#### Failure Signatures
- Teacher miscalibration on student-generated prefixes (leads to bad gradients)
- Mode collapse (especially with pure Reverse KL)
- Self-play saturation (performance plateaus or degrades after few iterations)
- High variance in sequence-level methods (REINFORCE instability)
- Distribution shift if on-policy sampling is insufficient (still exposure bias)

#### First 3 Experiments
1. Baseline comparison: Train a small student (e.g., 1.5B) with off-policy SFT (teacher-forcing on fixed teacher traces) vs on-policy GKD (mix of student and teacher rollouts) on a simple instruction-following dataset. Measure performance on in-distribution prompts vs paraphrased out-of-distribution prompts to expose exposure bias.
2. Divergence ablation: Using the same on-policy setup, train students with Forward KL, Reverse KL, JSD, and an adaptive method (e.g., Entropy-Aware OPD). Evaluate on reasoning (GSM8K) and open-ended generation (MT-Bench) to see how divergence choice affects accuracy and output diversity.
3. Self-distillation with privileged info: Compare SPIN (self-play without PI) vs OPSD (self-distillation with ground-truth answer as PI) on a math reasoning benchmark. Track performance over iterations to observe saturation in SPIN and whether OPSD breaks through.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: What are the precise scaling laws for on-policy distillation, quantitatively relating student model size, teacher model size, and on-policy token budget to downstream performance?
- **Basis**: The paper explicitly states the absence of scaling laws for on-policy distillation and conjectures a joint power-law form involving student size, teacher size, and on-policy tokens. It notes that practitioners currently rely on costly trial-and-error.
- **Why unresolved**: Without validated scaling laws, compute allocation (e.g., choosing between a larger teacher with fewer tokens vs. a smaller teacher with more tokens) remains an empirical art, hindering efficient resource use.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A large-scale controlled study independently varying student scale, teacher scale, and on-policy token count across multiple tasks, fitting a parametric model to predict final utility and verifying or refining the conjectured form.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: How can we design computationally efficient, uncertainty-aware distillation objectives that prevent the "echo chamber" effect when students explore out-of-distribution states?
- **Basis**: The paper identifies "uncertainty-aware feedback to prevent echo chambers" as a key open problem. It notes that current methods (e.g., Entropy-Aware OPD, GATES) use heuristics like teacher entropy or consensus gating but fail to decompose epistemic vs. aleatoric uncertainty.
- **Why unresolved**: Teacher overconfidence in student-generated prefixes can mislead the student, amplifying errors. Existing approaches do not efficiently distinguish between reducible epistemic uncertainty (where the teacher is ignorant) and irreducible aleatoric uncertainty (where multiple answers are valid), leading to either suppressed useful signals or amplified noise.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A novel OPD objective that integrates a lightweight, efficient estimate of teacher epistemic uncertainty (e.g., via ensembles or MC dropout) into the loss weighting, demonstrating improved robustness and calibration on distribution-shifted benchmarks compared to entropy- or consensus-based methods.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: How can on-policy distillation be fundamentally re-architected for multi-turn agentic interactions, where credit assignment is long-horizon, environments are non-stationary, and safety constraints are critical?
- **Basis**: The paper explicitly lists "extension of OPD to multi-turn agentic interactions" as an open problem. It highlights core challenges: environment non-stationarity (making teacher counterfactuals necessary), tool-use combinatorics (requiring abstraction beyond tokens), and safety-critical credit assignment (precluding exploration of catastrophic actions).
- **Why unresolved**: Current OPD methods optimize single-turn or linear chain-of-thought generation. Agentic settings involve dynamic state changes, delayed rewards, and irreversible actions, breaking assumptions of existing token-level or trajectory-level objectives.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A new OPD framework for agents that (a) conditions teacher feedback on the student's actual visited state (handling non-stationarity), (b) operates at the tool-call or sub-episode level (addressing combinatorics), and (c) incorporates safety constraints that limit exploration in high-risk action spaces, validated on interactive benchmarks like WebArena or ToolBench with safety failure metrics.

## Limitations
- Scaling laws for OPD (data size, model size, on-policy ratio) are unknown.
- Teacher reliability on student-generated prefixes is unverified; no uncertainty-aware losses to downweight bad feedback.
- Extending OPD to multi-turn agentic interactions remains an open challenge.
