# GLUScope: A Tool for Analyzing GLU Neurons in Transformer Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.23826
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23826
- **Reference count**: 11
- **Primary result**: GLUScope enables sign-combination-specific analysis of neurons in GLU-based models, revealing hidden patterns and a correlation between weight cosine similarity and gate sign frequency.

## Executive Summary
GLUScope addresses the lack of interpretability tools for models with gated activation functions (e.g., SwiGLU, GEGLU), where each neuron operates in four distinct sign modes based on the gate and input activations. The authors release a large-scale activation dataset for OLMo-7B on a 20 M‑token Dolma subset, accompanied by a website that visualizes per‑neuron statistics and top‑16 examples for each sign combination. Two main findings demonstrate the tool’s utility: (1) a strong negative correlation between the cosine similarity of a neuron’s input and output weights and the frequency of positive gate activations; (2) a case study where a low‑magnitude sign‑specific pattern (“once again”) was uncovered that would have been missed by conventional analysis. All artifacts are open‑source.

## Method Summary
The core method involves running a model with GLU activations (OLMo-7B) on a 20 M‑token corpus and instrumenting the forward pass to capture intermediate activations: `x_gate`, `Swish(x_gate)`, `x_in`, and the final output. For each neuron, the system computes the frequency of the four sign combinations (gate vs. input), aggregates statistical distributions of each activation, and retrieves the top‑16 highest absolute examples within each combination. The resulting dataset is hosted on a static website where users can browse neurons by layer and index to see these statistics and highlighted context snippets.

## Key Results
- A strong negative Pearson correlation between `cos(w_in, w_out)` and the frequency of `x_gate > 0` across all neurons.
- For neuron 31.9634, the `gate-_in-` combination (17.34% of activations) consistently activates on phrases like “once again”—a pattern invisible to top‑overall activation analysis due to lower magnitudes.
- The full activation dataset and visualization website are publicly released, facilitating further research.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Gated activation functions introduce four distinct behavioral modes per neuron based on the sign of the gate (`x_gate`) and input (`x_in`), which can have semantically different functionalities.
- **Mechanism**: For a neuron computing `Swish(x_gate) * x_in`, the sign of the product depends on the signs of both `x_gate` and `x_in`. Each sign combination (`gate+_in+`, `gate+_in-`, `gate-_in+`, `gate-_in-`) can be triggered by different contexts, leading to disparate interpretable roles for the same neuron.
- **Core assumption**: The neuron’s interpretable function is not monolithic but partitions along sign boundaries; ignoring this partition risks conflating distinct behaviors.
- **Evidence**: The authors state in the abstract that both the gate and input activation can be positive or negative, creating four possible sign combinations with different functionalities. Section 2.1 derives SwiGLU as `Swish(⟨w_gate, x⟩)⟨w_in, x⟩`, explicitly showing dependence on two scalar signs. A case study in Section 5.2 shows that the `gate-_in-` combination for neuron 31.9634 fires on “once again,” a pattern missed by traditional tools.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: By separately analyzing and providing examples for each sign combination, GLUScope can discover neuron behaviors invisible to monolithic (positive‑only or overall top‑k) analysis.
- **Mechanism**: The tool’s dataset and website compute the frequency of each sign combination and retrieve the top‑16 text examples *within each combination* for four intermediate activations. This isolates contexts where the neuron operates under a specific sign regime.
- **Core assumption**: High‑activation examples within a constrained sign combination are more likely to reveal a coherent pattern than high‑activation examples drawn from the overall distribution, which may be dominated by a frequent but less informative combination.
- **Evidence**: Section 4.2 describes recording detailed information per sign combination; Section 4.3.2 explains that the website shows examples for each combination; Section 5.2 notes that the `gate-_in-` activations for neuron 31.9634 are much smaller (at most 1.58) and would have been missed by traditional tools that only look at the largest overall activations (~3.48 and -5.12).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: The activation dataset enables model‑wide analyses that correlate neuron‑level activation statistics (e.g., sign combination frequency) with weight‑based properties, revealing regularities.
- **Mechanism**: The dataset provides precomputed per‑neuron summary statistics (e.g., frequency of `x_gate > 0`) that can be correlated with other metrics (e.g., `cos(w_in, w_out)`) across all neurons.
- **Core assumption**: Systematic relationships exist between the distribution of a neuron’s internal activations and its role in the computational graph, possibly reflecting training dynamics or design principles.
- **Evidence**: Section 5.1 uses the dataset to discover a strong negative correlation between `cos(w_in, w_out)` and `freq(x_gate>0)`. Section 4.2 notes the dataset’s dual use for visualization and experiments.

## Foundational Learning
- **Gated Linear Unit Mechanics**  
  Why needed: To understand why a single neuron has four operational modes; a GLU neuron computes `Swish(⟨w_gate, x⟩) * ⟨w_in, x⟩`, so the sign of the gate output and the product depends on the signs of the two inner products.  
  Quick check: If `⟨w_gate, x⟩ > 0` (so `Swish` > 0) and `⟨w_in, x⟩ < 0`, what is the sign of the neuron’s final output?

- **Neuron Analysis in Transformers**  
  Why needed: GLUScope is a neuron interpretability tool; a neuron is a single hidden dimension in an MLP layer, activation datasets are built by recording per‑token activations, and interpretability involves finding strongly activating texts.  
  Quick check: In a Transformer MLP with SwiGLU, how many input weight matrices (`W_gate`, `W_in`) and one output weight matrix (`W_out`) correspond to the computation for a single neuron?

- **Activation Distribution Analysis**  
  Why needed: The tool partitions activations by sign; interpreting results requires comparing statistical summaries (frequencies, max/mean) across partitions and understanding confounding factors like magnitude differences.  
  Quick check: If a neuron’s `gate+_in-` combination has a lower maximum activation than its `gate+_in+` combination, does that necessarily mean the former pattern is less important? What could be a confounding factor?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Model (OLMo-7B) + Text Dataset (Dolma subset) -> `b_activations.py` (hooks & recording) -> Summary Dataset (per‑neuron frequencies, stats, top‑16 indices) -> Visualization Script -> Static HTML Pages -> GLUScope Website. An auxiliary Weight Analysis Script can correlate with the Summary Dataset.

**Critical path**: 1) Select model and dataset. 2) Run `b_activations.py` to generate activation dataset (GPU‑intensive). 3) Run visualization script for chosen neurons. 4) Deploy HTML to web server or view locally. 5) For model‑wide analysis, load the Summary Dataset and compute correlations.

**Design tradeoffs**: Dataset size (20 M tokens) balances pattern diversity and cost; Top‑k=16 balances pattern recognizability and scanning effort; Truncation to two following tokens focuses on immediate context but discards longer dependencies; Model choice (OLMo‑7B) due to openness and SwiGLU use, though tool is model‑agnostic.

**Failure signatures**: Activation dataset generation: OOM (reduce batch size), slow runtime (check hooks), missing sign statistics (verify sign logic). Visualization: misaligned token highlighting (check index mapping), empty example sections (rare combination). Analysis: claims based on very low‑frequency combinations (<1%) may be noisy; correlations may not replicate across models/layers.

**First 3 experiments**:  
1. **Sign combination sanity check**: For random neurons, verify that the four reported frequencies sum to ~100% and that `gate+_in+` + `gate+_in-` ≈ `freq(x_gate>0)`.  
2. **Example retrieval validation**: For a neuron with a claimed `gate-_in-` pattern, inspect top‑16 examples for that combination; confirm pattern consistency and that other combinations show different patterns.  
3. **Reproduce model‑wide correlation**: Run the code from Section 5.1 on the provided Summary Dataset to confirm the negative correlation between `cos(w_in, w_out)` and `freq(x_gate>0)`.

## Limitations
- Findings are based solely on OLMo‑7B with SwiGLU and a 20 M‑token Dolma subset; generalizability to other GLU variants, model scales, or training data is unknown.
- Low‑frequency sign combinations (<5%) may yield noisy top‑k examples, limiting reliable interpretation.
- The observed weight‑activation correlation is observational; its causal origin and consistency across layers/models remain uncertain.

## Confidence
- **High**: The existence of four operational modes due to GLU sign interactions is mathematically sound and supported by the case study.
- **Medium**: The partitioned analysis approach’s value in discovering meaningful patterns is demonstrated for one neuron but needs broader validation.
- **Low**: The robustness of the weight‑activation correlation across models, layers, and datasets is highly uncertain.

## Next Checks
1. **Replication case study sweep**: Apply the `gate-_in-` pattern identification method to a random sample of 50+ neurons where that combination exceeds 10% frequency; report the success rate of finding coherent patterns.
2. **Correlation generalizability test**: Compute `cos(w_in, w_out)` vs. `freq(x_gate>0)` correlations for every MLP layer in OLMo‑7B and for at least one other SwiGLU model (e.g., Llama‑2); analyze variation by layer depth.
3. **Monolithic vs. partitioned analysis benchmark**: For neurons with diverse sign distributions, compare interpretability (blinded human evaluation of pattern coherence) between overall top‑16 activations and top‑16 within the rarest non‑zero sign combination; quantify the improvement.
