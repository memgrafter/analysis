# PRISMA: Reinforcement Learning Guided Two-Stage Policy Optimization in Multi-Agent Architecture for Open-Domain Multi-Hop Question Answering

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2601.05465
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05465
*   **Reference Count:** 40
*   **Primary Result:** PRISMA with Qwen3-4B achieves state-of-the-art Exact Match on MuSiQue (30.6% vs. 19.4%) and NaturalQuestions (38.6% vs. 22.6%) with a 29% inference speedup from the Memoizer.

## Executive Summary
PRISMA addresses core failures in open-domain multi-hop QA—retrieval collapse and reinforcement learning instability—through a decoupled multi-agent system (Planner, Retriever, Inspector, Solver, Memoizer) and a novel two-stage RL training protocol. The architecture isolates subquestion planning and answer solving to prevent credit assignment collapse. A specialized Inspector is then trained to detect residual errors in the Planner-Solver trajectory and trigger targeted recovery actions. This design yields substantial accuracy gains on ten benchmarks and a significant inference speedup via a semantic caching mechanism.

## Method Summary
PRISMA employs a five-component pipeline: the Planner decomposes a question into subquestions; the Retriever executes a three-stage cascade (dense → hybrid → reranker) for each subquestion; the Solver generates an answer conditioned on retrieved evidence; and the Inspector monitors both Context (evidence quality) and Reasoning (answer logic), triggering expansions or solver retries upon error detection. A Memoizer caches question-similar trajectories to avoid redundant computation. Training uses Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) in two stages: Stage I independently optimizes Planner and Solver with task-specific rewards (format, count, judge, accuracy, relevance). Stage II freezes these experts and trains the unified Inspector with Observation-Aware Residual Policy Optimization (OARPO), where the policy observes the full prior trajectory (question, plan, evidence, solver answer) and is rewarded for format, error detection, and concise audit explanations.

## Key Results
*   MuSiQue EM: 30.6% (PRISMA) vs. 19.4% (TIRESRAG-R1) and 22.6% (IRCoT).
*   NaturalQuestions EM: 38.6% (PRISMA) vs. 22.6% (IRCoT).
*   Inference speedup: 29% with the Memoizer (τ=0.85) and minimal accuracy trade-off.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The decoupled architecture prevents the credit assignment collapse that plagues monolithic multi-hop QA models by allowing independent reward shaping for planning and solving. The two-stage RL protocol further stabilizes learning: Stage I calibrates expert policies in isolation, while Stage II’s OARPO trains the Inspector to condition on expert trajectories, enabling it to detect *residual* errors that survive the initial experts. This residual error auditing is key—it shifts the Inspector’s role from generic validation to targeted recovery, which the paper’s failure analysis shows is necessary given that 71.2% of errors are retrieval-related yet most failures remain unrecoverable due to corpus limits or persistent reasoning mistakes.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Planner -> Retriever -> (Context Inspector -> [Rewrite/Expand] -> Retriever) -> Solver -> (Reasoning Inspector -> [Retry Solver]) -> Memoizer (parallel cache check)

**Critical Path:**  
1. Planner generates XML plan with subquestions and hop count.  
2. For each subquestion: Retriever fetches docs; Context Inspector audits evidence, optionally rewrites query/expands docs.  
3. Solver answers with citations.  
4. Reasoning Inspector audits answer logic; may trigger solver retry.  
5. Memoizer checks/saves full subquestion trajectory.  
6. Final answer aggregates subquestion answers.

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
*Decoupling vs. Complexity:* Separating concerns improves training stability but increases inference overhead and system integration burden.  
*Teacher Dependency:* Stage II relies on a high-capacity oracle (Qwen3-Max) for audit labels, raising concerns about bias and cost.  
*Fixed Experts:* Freezing Planner/Solver after Stage I simplifies training but may limit adaptation to new domains.

**Failure Signatures:**  
*Planner:* Invalid XML or hop count mismatch → low fmt/cnt rewards.  
*Retriever:* Low recall on gold documents → triggers Context Inspector expansions.  
*Inspector:* High false-positive/negative rates → misdirected recovery, high unrecoverable failure rate (95.8%).  
*Memoizer:* Cache pollution → incorrect answer reuse, EM drop.

**First Three Experiments:**  
1. **Planner Reward Validation:** Train Planner with GRPO on a small subset; plot per-component rewards (fmt, cnt, judge) to ensure they converge as in Figure 4.  
2. **Inspector Input Format Ablation:** Vary the serialization of τ_P,S (e.g., concatenation order, truncation) and measure error detection accuracy against teacher labels to identify the most effective format.  
3. **Memoizer Threshold Sweep:** Run inference with τ ∈ [0.7, 0.85, 0.95]; measure cache hit rate, EM change, and latency to confirm the claimed 29% speedup at τ=0.85.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:** How can the substantial memory and GPU resource requirements for PRISMA's deployment be reduced while preserving its multi-agent coordination gains, particularly given that the Inspector and Solver dominate runtime?  
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] "Deployment still requires substantial memory and GPU resources... due to the large central structure" (Limitations). Figure 5 shows Inspector and Solver dominate latency.  
**Why unresolved:** The paper identifies resource intensity as a key limitation but provides no analysis of which components (e.g., Memoizer cache, retrieval indexes, agent state management) are most memory-intensive or how to optimize them without breaking the inspection-recovery loop.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Profiling memory usage per component, experiments with model compression (e.g., quantizing Inspector), or alternative caching strategies that trade off semantic similarity thresholds (τ) against hit rate and accuracy.

**Open Question 2:** To what extent does the performance of the trained Inspector depend on the capacity and bias of the oracle Inspector gT used in Stage II, and can weaker (more practical) teachers still yield robust audit policies?  
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] Stage II uses "a high-capacity oracle Inspector teacher gT" (Section 3.3.1, C.3) with Qwen3-Max as teacher (Table 7). The reward ROARPO aligns to gT's audit labels, but no ablation varies teacher strength.  
**Why unresolved:** The reliance on a powerful oracle is a hidden assumption; if gT has systematic biases (e.g., over-retrieval, conservative error detection), the Inspector may inherit them. The paper doesn't analyze sensitivity to teacher quality.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Train Inspectors with teachers of varying scales (e.g., Qwen3-4B vs. Qwen3-30B) and measure audit agreement and end-to-end EM; or replace gT with rule-based heuristics to isolate the benefit of expert trajectory conditioning.

**Open Question 3:** Does freezing the Planner and Solver after Stage I limit PRISMA's adaptability to distribution shifts or novel question types, and would a continuous multi-stage training loop improve robustness?  
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] Stage I "calibrates the Planner and Solver as specialized experts" and Stage II keeps them "fixed" (Section 3.3.1). The two-stage protocol is designed to avoid credit assignment collapse.  
**Why unresolved:** Freezing experts assumes their skills are sufficient for all future inputs, but the failure analysis (Table 13) shows 71.2% retrieval-related errors—many may stem from Planner decompositions that could be improved with further exposure. No experiment revisits Stage I after Stage II or fine-tunes all agents jointly.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Evaluate PRISMA on a held-out domain (e.g., the Chemistry dataset) with and without additional Stage I fine-tuning after Stage II; measure whether periodic re-calibration of Planner/Solver on new data reduces error rates.

**Open Question 4:** Why does inspection-triggered recovery fail to correct errors in 95.8% of failed cases, and what modifications to the recovery actions or Inspector's decision boundaries could increase recoverability?  
**Basis in paper:** [explicit] Table 13: "95.8% [failures] are unrecoverable." The Inspector flags errors (retrieval 69.6%, reasoning 26.0%), but recovery (expanded retrieval, solver retry) rarely helps.  
**Why unresolved:** The paper attributes failures to "hard limits such as corpus gaps/retrieval coverage" but doesn't dissect the unrecoverable cases. For example, are retrieval expansions ineffective because queries are rewritten poorly? Are solver retries failing due to persistent evidence noise? The Inspector's recovery triggers (max iterations Er/Ep) are fixed; perhaps dynamic budgets or alternative recovery actions (e.g., querying external APIs) are needed.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Qualitative analysis of a sample of the 344 unrecoverable cases: categorize whether failure is due to (a) corpus deficiency, (b) inadequate query rewriting, (c) solver's inability to use expanded docs, or (d) Inspector misdiagnosis. Then ablate recovery parameters (Er/Ep, doc limits) or introduce new recovery primitives.

**Open Question 5:** Can PRISMA's decoupled multi-agent architecture and two-stage RL training be extended to multimodal or conversational QA scenarios without fundamental redesign?  
**Basis in paper:** [inferred] PRISMA is evaluated solely on text-based QA over Wikipedia. The human researcher workflow analogy (Figure 2) suggests general applicability, but all tools (Retriever, Solver) are text-centric. No discussion of image/video retrieval or multi-turn dialogue.  
**Why unresolved:** The Planner's subquestion decomposition and Inspector's validation rules assume textual evidence. Multimodal RAG introduces new failure modes (e.g., misaligned visual grounding) that the current Inspector prompts (F.3, F.4) are not designed to handle. The Memoizer's semantic similarity cache also relies on text encoders.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** Adapt PRISMA to a multimodal benchmark (e.g., WebQA) by integrating vision-language models into the Retriever and Solver, then measure whether the two-stage training still stabilizes learning or if new credit assignment challenges arise.

## Limitations
*   **Training data construction not specified:** No method for extracting gold subquestion-answer pairs and supporting documents for Solver training, nor for deriving gold hop counts (n_gold) for Planner's count reward.
*   **Proprietary teacher models:** Reliance on Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct (Judge) and Qwen3-Max (audit teacher gT) hinders faithful replication; no ablation on teacher quality.
*   **Implementation gaps:** Retriever preprocessing (passage segmentation, FAISS details), Memoizer's cascade guard logic, and Inspector's exact input serialization are underspecified.

## Confidence
*   **Architecture & Training Protocol Description:** High – The decoupled design and two-stage GRPO/OARPO are thoroughly described in the main text and supplements.
*   **Performance Claims (EM scores):** Low – Results depend on unresolved data preparation methods and proprietary teacher models; exact replication is currently blocked.
*   **Memoizer Efficiency Gains:** Medium – Caching mechanism and similarity threshold are specified, but integration with Inspector-driven rewrites and accuracy trade-offs require validation.
*   **Failure Analysis (95.8% unrecoverable):** Medium – The statistic is reported, but the underlying causes and potential remedies are not explored.
