# Informative Semi-Factuals for XAI: The Elaborated Explanations that People Prefer

## Quick Facts

- **arXiv ID:** 2603.17534  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17534  
- **Reference Count:** 40  
- **Primary Result:** ISF generates semi-factuals exhibiting a seesaw pattern (key-feature weakening, hidden-feature strengthening) in 89–100% of cases, outperforms SOTA ensembles on distance, sparsity, plausibility, and trustworthiness, and receives 69–80% user preference over bare semi-factuals in studies.

## Executive Summary

Standard semi-factual explanations show that an outcome remains unchanged despite a key feature change, but they fail to explain *why*—omitting hidden factors that compensate. This paper proposes **Informative Semi-Factuals (ISF)**, a multi‑objective algorithm that supplements a maximal change to a key feature with a hidden feature whose marginal contribution strengthens as the key feature’s weakens along the perturbation path. Using NSGA‑II optimization, ISF balances distance, sparsity, and plausibility while explicitly enforcing the characteristic “seesaw” trend (measured via Kendall’s τ on SHAP main effects). Experiments on five tabular datasets show that ISF produces valid seesaw patterns in nearly all cases, improves on baseline metrics, and is strongly preferred by users.

## Method Summary

Given a query instance and a trained Random Forest classifier, ISF treats each feature as a potential key‑feature. For each candidate, it solves a constrained multi‑objective problem via NSGA‑II: (1) maximize change in the key feature; (2) minimize total change to other features; subject to (a) preserving the predicted class and (b) satisfying a plausibility constraint derived from a Gaussian copula model of the training data. The Pareto set of semi‑factual candidates is then evaluated: for each candidate, ten linearly interpolated steps between query and semi‑factual are created, and the SHAP main effect (diagonal of the interaction matrix) is computed at each step. Kendall’s τ trend is calculated for every feature. The semi‑factual with the strongest negative trend for the key‑feature (τ < –0.3) and the most positive trend among non‑key features (the hidden‑feature) is selected. The final explanation states: “Even if [key‑feature] changed to [value], outcome same, because [hidden‑feature] is [value].”

## Key Results

- The seesaw pattern (key‑feature weakening, hidden‑feature strengthening) appears in 89–100% of generated semi‑factuals.  
- ISF significantly outperforms state‑of‑the‑art ensembles on distance, sparsity, plausibility, and trustworthiness metrics.  
- User studies (N = 30) show 69–80% preference for ISF’s elaborated explanations over bare semi‑factuals and 69–81% for “good” versus “bad” semi‑factuals.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

ISF works because it directly optimizes for the **seesaw pattern** that underlies an *informative* semi‑factual. The key insight is that a semi‑factual’s outcome stability is often maintained by a compensatory change in another (hidden) feature. By quantifying marginal contributions along the perturbation path and enforcing a strong negative trend for the key‑feature together with a positive trend for one other feature, ISF isolates explanations that reveal this hidden compensation. The multi‑objective formulation ensures semi‑factuals are sufficiently different from the query (maximal key‑feature change) while staying close to the data distribution (total change minimized, plausibility constraint). The NSGA‑II search efficiently explores trade‑offs, and the post‑hoc trend analysis selects the candidate that best exhibits the seesaw mechanism.

## Foundational Learning

- **Semi‑factual explanation**: Shows the outcome would not change under a hypothetical modification; ISF extends this by adding *why* via a hidden feature.  
  *Why needed*: Plain semi‑factuals are incomplete; they do not identify hidden factors.  
  *Quick check*: Verify generated semi‑factual preserves the predicted class but differs on the chosen key‑feature.

- **Seesaw pattern**: Anticorrelated trends in feature marginal contributions along the perturbation path; key‑feature weakens (τ < –0.3) while a hidden‑feature strengthens (τ > 0).  
  *Why needed*: This pattern captures the hidden feature’s compensatory role.  
  *Quick check*: Compute Kendall’s τ for each feature’s SHAP main effects; check τ_key < –0.3 and τ_hidden > 0.

- **Multi‑objective optimization (NSGA‑II)**: Generates a Pareto front of semi‑factual candidates balancing competing objectives.  
  *Why needed*: No single best solution; trade‑offs between change magnitude and plausibility must be explored.  
  *Quick check*: Ensure Pareto front contains feasible individuals (satisfy constraints) and shows diversity.

- **Plausibility constraint**: Uses a Gaussian copula to ensure semi‑factuals lie in high‑density regions of the data distribution.  
  *Why needed*: Implausible semi‑factuals erode trust.  
  *Quick check*: Compute log‑PDF of candidate via trained copula; confirm within threshold (µ ± θσ, θ=1.5).

- **SHAP main effects (diagonal of interaction matrix)**: Provides feature‑wise marginal contributions for each interpolated instance.  
  *Why needed*: Quantifies each feature’s influence on the model output as the semi‑factual is approached.  
  *Quick check*: Obtain `shap_interaction_values` from `shap.TreeExplainer`; extract diagonal; validate sum equals total SHAP value.

- **Kendall’s τ**: Rank‑based correlation metric to detect monotonic trends in feature contributions.  
  *Why needed*: Robust to scale; ideal for assessing monotonic weakening/strengthening.  
  *Quick check*: Compute τ on a monotonic sequence; should be near –1 or +1.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**  
Query → (Key‑feature selection) → NSGA‑II candidate generation → Interpolation → SHAP effect computation → Trend analysis → Selection → Explanation

**Critical Path**  
1. NSGA‑II must find at least one feasible semi‑factual satisfying class preservation and plausibility.  
2. SHAP computation must succeed for all interpolated points without memory/time overflow.  
3. Trend analysis must identify at least one candidate with τ_key < –0.3; otherwise no explanation is produced.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *NSGA‑II population/generations* vs. computational cost: larger populations yield more diverse fronts but increase runtime.  
- *Interpolation steps*: more steps (e.g., 10) give smoother trends but multiply SHAP calls.  
- *SHAP background size*: smaller speeds up but may reduce effect stability.  
- *Plausibility threshold θ*: stricter yields more realistic candidates but may reduce feasible set.

**Failure Signatures**  
- No feasible semi‑factual in final Pareto front (infeasible constraints).  
- SHAP calls exceed memory or take > minutes per candidate.  
- All candidates have τ_key ≥ –0.3; seesaw pattern not detected.  
- Selected hidden‑feature shows no meaningful value change from query (e.g., identical or near‑identical).

**First Experiments**  
1. **Feasibility test**: Run ISF on one dataset with default parameters; confirm ≥ 1 feasible semi‑factual exists. If none, relax plausibility θ or increase population.  
2. **SHAP extraction check**: For a single candidate, compute `shap_interaction_values` on interpolated points; extract diagonal and compare with `shap_values`. Plot effects sequences for key and candidate hidden features.  
3. **Pattern validation**: Pre‑define a query and key‑feature where a seesaw is expected (e.g., via manual reasoning); run ISF and verify the selected semi‑factual has τ_key < –0.3 and a positive τ for the hidden‑feature.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can ISF’s computational efficiency be improved to scale effectively to high‑dimensional feature spaces or non‑tabular data types (e.g., images, time series), and does the seesaw pattern generalize to these domains?  
- **Basis in paper:** The authors note in the Discussion that “a notable limitation of our work is its computational efficiency when there are large number of features” and propose exploring “other domains such as images, and time series” as future work.  
- **Why unresolved:** The current ISF method relies on feature‑wise perturbation and marginal contribution analysis along linear interpolation paths, which may become computationally intractable or conceptually invalid for high‑dimensional or structured data.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Extending ISF with feature selection or dimensionality reduction for high‑dimensional tabular data; developing domain‑specific perturbation strategies (e.g., saliency‑based for images) and re‑evaluating the seesaw pattern’s prevalence; benchmarking computational cost against SOTA semi‑factual methods.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** To what extent do ISF’s explanatory preferences (e.g., favoring elaborated semi‑factuals with hidden features) generalize beyond the controlled loan‑scenario user studies to diverse real‑world applications, user demographics, and decision contexts?  
- **Basis in paper:** The user studies were limited to a single adapted dataset (German Credit) with five features, specific outcome labels, and recruited participants from English‑speaking Western countries; no cross‑domain validation.  
- **Why unresolved:** Preference might be context‑dependent (e.g., medical vs. loan decisions), vary with user expertise, cultural background, or trust in AI. Scenarios were static, not addressing dynamic decisions.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Multi‑domain user studies (healthcare, criminal justice) with diverse participants; testing in dynamic environments; measuring comprehension, trust calibration, and behavioral outcomes.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How do different feature characteristics (continuous vs. categorical, correlated vs. independent, actionable vs. non‑actionable) influence the detectability and strength of the seesaw pattern, and does ISF robustly identify valid hidden‑feature strengthening across these variations?  
- **Basis in paper:** ISF uses linear interpolation and SHAP main effects assuming additive, independent effects; no analysis of how feature types or dependencies affect pattern emergence.  
- **Why unresolved:** Categorical features with non‑ordinal values may not interpolate meaningfully; correlated features could cause spurious seesaws; actionable constraints were not explicitly modeled.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablation across datasets with varied feature types; analyzing failure cases; incorporating correlation‑aware perturbation or causal graph constraints; user validation of psychological meaningfulness.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What are the long‑term cognitive effects of repeatedly receiving informative semi‑factuals on users’ mental models of the AI’s decision‑making, such as changes in causal reasoning, regret mitigation, or detection of spurious correlations?  
- **Basis in paper:** The user studies measured immediate preference, not deeper psychological impacts. Related work cites that semi‑factuals weaken causal relations and reduce regret, but ISF’s elaboration could modify these effects.  
- **Why unresolved:** Preference does not equate to improved understanding; repeated exposure might create over‑reliance or misunderstanding of model logic.  
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Longitudinal studies tracking causal attributions, trust, and task performance; cognitive interviews probing inferred decision rules; comparisons with bare semi‑factuals or counterfactuals on mental model accuracy and bias detection.

## Limitations

- **Mixed‑data plausibility:** Gaussian copula requires continuous marginals; the treatment of encoded categorical features for joint PDF estimation is unclear and may compromise the plausibility constraint.  
- **SHAP main effect isolation:** Diagonal of SHAP interaction matrix may not equal pure main effects, potentially biasing trend computation.  
- **Scalability:** NSGA‑II with SHAP recomputation per candidate is computationally expensive, limiting real‑time or high‑dimensional use.
