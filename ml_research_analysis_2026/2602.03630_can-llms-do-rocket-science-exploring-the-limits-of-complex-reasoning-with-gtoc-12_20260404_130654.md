# Can LLMs Do Rocket Science? Exploring the Limits of Complex Reasoning with GTOC 12
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.03630
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03630
- Reference count: 35
- Primary result: LLMs achieve an 88% increase in strategic viability scores on GTOC 12 (from 9.3 to 17.2–21.15/26) but consistently fail implementation due to unit inconsistencies and debugging loops, acting as facilitators rather than autonomous engineers.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates large language models (LLMs) on the GTOC 12 asteroid mining challenge, a complex orbital mechanics problem, using an adapted MLE-Bench framework and the AIDE agent architecture. Solutions are assessed via a 26-criteria expert rubric applied by an LLM judge. Results show modern LLMs excel at conceptual strategy formulation—correctly framing objectives and mission architectures—but repeatedly stumble on precise implementation, primarily due to physical unit errors, boundary condition mistakes, and inefficient iterative debugging. The work concludes that while LLMs are powerful domain facilitators, they are not yet capable of fully autonomous engineering for high-fidelity physics-constrained tasks.

## Method Summary
The authors transform the static GTOC 12 optimization problem into an interactive agent task by embedding the official trajectory validator within a Docker environment (MLE-Bench adaptation). An AIDE-based agent with domain-specific prompt engineering and debugging handlers iteratively generates and refines mission solution files. After a fixed number of drafts, a separate LLM judge (Gemini 2.5 Pro) evaluates each draft’s strategic viability using a detailed 26-criteria rubric covering five structural categories, decoupling conceptual soundness from numerical validity.

## Key Results
- Strategic viability scores rose 88% from GPT-4-Turbo (9.3/26) to top models (17.2–21.15/26).
- LLMs demonstrate strong conceptual understanding (e.g., mission architectures, gravity assists) but fail implementation due to unit inconsistencies, boundary errors, and unproductive debugging loops.
- No model produced a single physically valid trajectory, positioning LLMs as facilitators rather than autonomous engineers.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The adapted MLE-Bench framework successfully converts GTOC 12 into a closed-loop, sandboxed agent task by integrating the official validator as a subprocess and reformatting problem specifications. This provides immediate, ground-truth feedback on trajectory feasibility rather than prediction accuracy, assuming the validator’s deterministic rules capture all constraints. Evidence includes the direct embedding of the GTOC validator and reconstruction of the environment with necessary astrodynamics libraries.

Modifying AIDE’s tree-search with domain-specific prompts, automated entry-point fixes, and unit warnings partially redirects the agent’s inductive bias from ML hyperparameter tuning to physics debugging. The best-first search maintains multiple drafts for backtracking, but the agent often introduces regressions via “buggy debug” patterns where changes compound errors. This assumes AIDE’s structure can generalize to engineering domains by overriding its internal monologue.

The 26-criteria expert rubric, applied by an LLM judge, isolates strategic understanding from implementation flaws. Segmentation reveals models score highly on conceptual framing (e.g., mothership usage) but poorly on execution-sensitive criteria (e.g., building feasible transfer sequences). High intra-rater reliability (ICC=0.937) and low self-preference bias support score validity, assuming the rubric correctly identifies factors that predict actual GTOC success.

## Foundational Learning
- **GTOC 12 Trajectory Optimization**: Understanding the problem’s constraints (15-year window, low-thrust propulsion, dynamic fleet caps) is essential to interpret failure modes and rubric criteria. Quick check: What physical parameter causes the fleet size to dynamically cap, and how does it incentivize high-yield asteroids?
- **AIDE’s Tree-Search vs. Linear Agents**: AIDE’s best-first search over solution drafts enables iterative refinement and backtracking, unlike single-turn retry loops. Quick check: How does AIDE’s branching differ from simple retries, and why is this critical for complex engineering?
- **LLM-as-a-Judge Validation Metrics**: Reliability metrics (ICC, self-preference delta) are crucial for trusting comparative scores. Quick check: What does ICC(2,k)=0.937 indicate about judge reliability, and why is near-zero self-preference delta desirable?
- **Physical Unit Propagation in Astrodynamics**: Mixed units (MJD, km, m, AU) and library defaults cause pervasive errors. Quick check: If a Lambert solver expects km but receives AU, what validator symptom appears?
- **Low-Thrust vs. High-Fidelity Trajectory Methods**: The rubric distinguishes fast feasibility checks (e.g., Edelbaum) from high-fidelity optimization (e.g., Sims-Flanagan). Quick check: Why must preliminary filtering use low-fidelity methods, and what happens if high-fidelity is used for all candidates?

## Architecture Onboarding
Docker environment (astrodynamics libraries, GTOC data) -> AIDE agent (tree search, modified prompts) -> GTOC validator subprocess (feedback loop) -> LLM judge (rubric scoring)

Critical path:
1. Clone `GTOC-Agent-Bench` and `aide-gtoc`; build Docker image with required packages and copy data files.
2. Launch AIDE with a target LLM, system prompt, and iteration limits.
3. After each draft, agent POSTs `Result.txt` to validator; parser extracts feedback for next iteration.
4. Post-run, feed final draft to Gemini 2.5 Pro with rubric prompt; parse True/False into 26-point score.

Design tradeoffs:
- Fixed judge (Gemini 2.5 Pro) ensures consistency but may embed model-specific biases.
- Prompt modularity (`prompts.txt`) eases domain switching but risks injection if unsanitized.
- Direct validator subprocess calls are fast but brittle to malformed input (e.g., trailing newlines).

Failure signatures:
- Unit errors: validator reports position/velocity mismatches; agent widens launch windows instead of converting units.
- Formatting crashes: trailing newline in `Result.txt` crashes validator; agent repeatedly appends `\n`.
- Debugging loops: agent modifies multiple unrelated sections per iteration, causing regressions.
- Rubric mismatch: judge penalizes oversimplified strategies (e.g., o1’s numbered comments) as non-viable.

First 3 experiments:
1. Baseline replication: Run 100 drafts with GPT-4-Turbo; expect average score ~9.3/26 and zero valid trajectories; catalog validator error distribution.
2. Unit-warning ablation: Remove unit-consistency advice from prompts; quantify increase in unit-related rejections to isolate debugging utility.
3. Judge calibration: Compute ICC for 20 solutions across 5 judge inference cycles; if ICC < 0.7, rephrase rubric criteria.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **What is baseline performance of LLMs on complex, multi-stage engineering tasks?** The study provides provisional baselines for specific models and agent setups, but rapid model evolution and architectural variations prevent generalization. Resolution requires a comprehensive, reproducible benchmark suite across diverse LLMs, agents, and prompting strategies.
2. **What are the primary failure modes in high-dimensional, physics-constrained optimization?** The paper identifies unit errors, boundary violations, and debugging loops, but the taxonomy may be GTOC12-specific. Cross-domain analysis (e.g., structural design, fluid dynamics) is needed to test generalizability.
3. **Do LLMs fail due to flawed strategy or implementation?** The paper concludes implementation is the barrier, based on rubric decoupling. However, the separation may be artificial; implementation flaws could reflect deeper strategic gaps. Resolution requires controlled experiments where strategy or implementation is externally fixed.
4. **Can LLM agents autonomously iteratively refine solutions?** The study’s debugging attempts showed regression, possibly due to AIDE design. More advanced reasoning models or alternative architectures (e.g., evolutionary search) might achieve self-correction; evidence requires agents with systematic debugging tools and long-horizon improvement measurement.
5. **How do prompting, agent architecture, and tools collectively affect performance?** The study uses a single agent architecture, so interactions between these factors are not disentangled. A factorial experimental design varying prompting, search algorithm, and toolset while holding the LLM constant is needed to isolate main effects and interactions.

## Limitations
- Unreleased model details (GPT-5, Claude 4.5, etc.) prevent direct score comparisons.
- Hidden hyperparameters (reasoning effort, seeds, score post-processing) limit reproducibility.
- Validator brittleness (ambiguous errors, unit inconsistencies, trailing-newline crashes) induces agent loops and may inflate failure rates.
- Rubric may reward “plausible-sounding” plans that remain mathematically invalid.

## Confidence
- MLE-Bench adaptation: Medium (well-described but validator fragility challenges closed-loop assumption).
- AIDE modifications: Medium (tree-search helps but “buggy debug” patterns persist).
- Rubric decoupling: High (ICC=0.937, low bias; modest risk of misalignment with true GTOC success).
- Overall “facilitator” thesis: High (88% strategic gain with zero valid solutions strongly supports limited autonomy).
