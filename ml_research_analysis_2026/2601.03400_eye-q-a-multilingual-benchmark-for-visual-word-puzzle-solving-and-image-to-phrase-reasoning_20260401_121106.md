# Eye-Q: A Multilingual Benchmark for Visual Word Puzzle Solving and Image-to-Phrase Reasoning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.03400
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03400
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** State-of-the-art VLMs achieve at most 60.27% on English and much lower on Arabic (19.15%) and cross-lingual (29.15%) puzzles, revealing significant gaps in complex visual reasoning.

## Executive Summary
Eye-Q is a new multilingual benchmark designed to evaluate visual word puzzle solving—a task requiring the discovery of implicit cues in dense images and mapping them to non-literal phrases. The benchmark comprises 1,343 puzzles across English, Persian, Arabic, and cross-lingual Persian–English settings. Evaluation employs a human-aligned protocol with iterative refinement and optional character hints. Results from testing six state-of-the-art vision-language models show poor performance, with the best model reaching only 60.27% accuracy on English and substantially lower on Arabic (19.15%) and cross-lingual (29.15%) puzzles. These findings highlight critical limitations in current VLMs' ability to perform abstract, associative reasoning beyond literal grounding and simple retrieval.

## Method Summary
The task is to solve cue-implicit visual word puzzles: given an image and a short rule explanation, the model must output a single word or phrase that non-literally captures the scene. The dataset contains 1,343 instances split into English (300), Persian (671), Arabic (50), and cross-lingual (322) puzzles. Images are deliberately dense with distractors, and puzzles range from monolingual wordplay to cross-lingual phonetic/semantic bridging. Evaluation uses exact-match accuracy after minimal cleanup. Four prompting variants are evaluated: basic (length hint only), few-shot chain-of-thought, iterative refinement (up to three attempts with feedback), and partial character reveal (25% of characters shown). Six VLMs—including GPT-5.2, Gemini 2.5 Flash/Pro, Grok 4.1 Fast, Llama 4 Scout 17B, and Qwen3 VL-235B—are tested in a zero-training setting.

## Key Results
- SOTA VLMs peak at 60.27% (English), 43.03% (Persian), 19.15% (Arabic), 29.15% (cross-lingual).
- Iterative refinement yields only modest improvements: average accuracy rises from 11.53% to 15.63%.
- Partial character reveal boosts scores but does not close the gap, indicating models struggle beyond output-space constraints.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Implicit Cue Discovery Failure
Current VLMs fail primarily because they cannot identify and prioritize subtle, implicit visual cues within distractor-rich scenes, instead relying on salient surface features. Successful solving requires compressing a perceptually dense image into a sparse set of functional clues while ignoring irrelevant objects. Evidence includes the abstract’s emphasis on implicit cues, Section 3.2’s description of conceptually dense scenes, and Section 5.5’s observation that models miss which elements constitute the right cues. The mechanism is bypassed when cues are made explicitly textual or highlighted.

### Mechanism 2: Weak Hypothesis Revision Loop
Iterative refinement provides only marginal gains because models lack a robust internal mechanism to generate and verify hypotheses. With feedback on failed attempts, a capable solver would re-weight attention or explore alternative interpretations, but Eye-Q results show models cannot effectively diagnose why an initial guess was wrong, as indicated by low semantic similarity between successive wrong answers (Section 5.5). The loop adds value only when the initial hypothesis is close to correct.

### Mechanism 3: Cross-Lingual Bridging Deficiency
Cross-lingual puzzles (English↔Persian) expose a specific failure: models cannot bridge between languages via phonetic, semantic, or cultural wordplay. Solving requires mapping a visual cue to a concept in one language and then transforming it—e.g., via homophony or transliteration—to the target language. The large performance drop on Arabic and cross-lingual subsets (Section 5.1) suggests models rely on direct grounding and lack the associative network for non-standard cross-lingual mappings.

## Foundational Learning
**Visual Abstraction & Cue Selection**  
*Why needed:* Models must selectively attend to task-relevant elements while ignoring high-salience distractors, compressing a dense scene into a few functional clues.  
*Quick check:* Given an image of a "cat on a mat" with extra furniture, which elements should be selected as primary clues if the answer is a pun on "catnap"? What distractors would you ignore and why?

**Non-Literal Image-to-Phrase Mapping**  
*Why needed:* The target phrase is often an idiom, pun, or cultural expression requiring non-literal connections (phonetic, orthographic, semantic) between visual signs and words.  
*Quick check:* If an image shows a USB port labeled "GHAL," what linguistic mechanism links this to the Persian answer "پورتغال" (Portugal)?

**Hypothesis Space Navigation under Partial Feedback**  
*Why needed:* Solvers must generate candidate answers, evaluate them against evidence, and use failure feedback to revise their hypothesis, maintaining a dynamic search process.  
*Quick check:* After an initial guess "Battery" fails for a bat-on-tree image, what specific aspect of the visual cue might you re-examine to pivot toward "Bat-trick"?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Visual Encoder → Cue Extraction Head → Hypothesis Generator → Verification/Refinement Loop → Output Decoder  
For iterative refinement: Output → Feedback → Hypothesis Generator

### Critical Path
Image → Visual Encoder → Cue Extraction → Hypothesis Generation → (Optional: Verification) → Final Answer.  
With iterative refinement, the cycle repeats: Output → Feedback → Hypothesis Generator (with re-weighted cues).

### Design Tradeoffs
- *Depth vs. Noise:* Deeper multi-hop reasoning over visual features risks amplifying distractor noise; shallow paths may miss abstract connections.
- *Generative vs. Retrieval:* Pure generation risks invalid phrases; constrained retrieval from a known answer space (given hints) is easier but less general.
- *Monolingual vs. Cross-lingual Lexicon:* Supporting cross-lingual puns requires a shared phonetic/semantic embedding space, not just parallel corpus alignments.

### Failure Signatures
- **Cue Selection Failure:** Attending to dominant objects (e.g., largest animal) rather than subtle functional ones (e.g., keyhole).
- **Abstraction Failure:** Producing a literal description instead of the required transformed phrase.
- **Cross-lingual Mapping Failure:** Translating cues directly instead of recombining via phonetic/semantic bridges.
- **Refinement Loop Failure:** Iterative attempts are minor paraphrases rather than exploring new hypotheses.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Baseline vs. Hint Impact:** Compare basic setup with partial character reveal on a single subset (e.g., English) to isolate output-space brittleness.
2. **Cue Implicitness Ablation:** For solved puzzles, create original cue-implicit and cue-explicit variants (highlighting key elements), then evaluate model performance. A large drop for implicit scenes would confirm cue-discovery as the bottleneck.
3. **Cross-lingual Phonetic Bridge Test:** For cross-lingual puzzles, measure correlation between model accuracy and the strength of phonetic similarity between the English cue and the Persian answer (e.g., "share"→"shar-ket" vs. direct loanword). This isolates the phonetic bridging requirement.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Scaling Multilingual Puzzle Creation**  
   How can the creation of visual puzzles be systematized to scale to low-resource languages while preserving cultural authenticity and consistent difficulty? Current reliance on fluent contributors limits scalability. A semi-automated pipeline with native speaker validation could provide a solution.

2. **Arabic Performance Disparity**  
   Why do VLMs perform dramatically worse on Arabic puzzles (19.15% vs. 60.27% in English)? Potential factors include script complexity, morphological richness, and cultural specificity of puzzles. Controlled experiments are needed to isolate the cause.

3. **Limited Gains from Iterative Refinement**  
   Why does allowing up to three answer attempts improve accuracy only modestly (from 11.53% to 15.63%)? The paper speculates that models lack internal critique mechanisms to use failure feedback effectively. Richer feedback or fine-tuning on revision tasks might help.

4. **Dominant Failure Mode Identification**  
   Which subskill—cue selection among distractors, relational abstraction, or linguistic wordplay mapping—is the primary bottleneck? Without diagnosing per-skill weaknesses, future work cannot target improvements effectively. Skill-stratified evaluation or diagnostic subtasks are required.

## Limitations
- **Small sample sizes:** Especially only 50 Arabic puzzles, which may not fully represent complexity and could lead to overfitting or statistical instability.
- **Exact-match metric:** Strict equality may undercount near-miss answers that are valid paraphrases or transliterations, potentially understating model capabilities.
- **Reproducibility hurdles:** Unreleased exact few-shot examples, changing API endpoints, and unknown random seeds for partial-reveal masks hinder faithful replication.

## Confidence
- Implicit cue discovery failure: High
- Iterative refinement marginal gains: Medium
- Cross-lingual bridging failure: Medium

## Next Checks
1. **Fixed demonstration replication:** Obtain or reconstruct the exact few-shot examples used by the authors and re-run the evaluation to measure variance and absolute scores against reported numbers.
2. **Cue implicitness ablation:** For a random subset (e.g., 100 puzzles), generate both original and cue-explicit versions, then compare model performance. A significant drop for implicit scenes would directly confirm the cue-discovery bottleneck.
3. **Cross-lingual phonetic correlation:** Compute phonetic distances (e.g., IPA-based) between English cues and Persian answers across cross-lingual puzzles, then test correlation with model accuracy. A strong correlation would isolate the phonetic bridging requirement.
