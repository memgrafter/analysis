# Detecting Autism Spectrum Disorder with Deep Eye Movement Features

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.05812  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05812  
- **Reference count:** 26  
- **Primary result:** DSTS achieves superior detection performance on eight eye-tracking datasets, with accuracy and F1-score gains over state-of-the-art methods, while having lower computational cost and parameter count.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) detection using eye movement sequences. It demonstrates that Transformer-based models with global attention are ineffective for this data, which primarily contains short-term, localized dependencies. The proposed Discrete Short-Term Sequential (DSTS) framework employs 1D convolutional layers to capture local temporal patterns, enhanced by a Class-aware Representation (CaR) mechanism using Multi-Similarity Loss and an Imbalance-aware (Ia) weighting mechanism to handle skewed class distributions. Evaluated on eight eye-tracking datasets, DSTS consistently outperforms state-of-the-art methods in accuracy and F1-score while being significantly more efficient in terms of parameters and computation. Ablation studies confirm the necessity of both CaR and Ia components.

## Method Summary
The DSTS framework processes discrete fixation sequences (multivariate time series of coordinates, durations, etc.). A stack of 1D convolutional layers with small kernels extracts local temporal patterns (e.g., rapid saccade-fixation transitions). Max pooling over time aggregates these features into a fixed-length embedding. This embedding feeds into two parallel loss heads: Multi-Similarity Loss (CaR) shapes the embedding space to tighten intra-class clusters and separate inter-class clusters, while weighted cross-entropy (Ia) assigns higher misclassification penalties to minority-class (typically TD) samples to counteract imbalance. The final classifier is a fully connected layer on the embedding. Training optimizes a weighted sum of these losses, jointly learning robust representations and balanced decision boundaries.

## Key Results
- DSTS outperforms several Transformer-based baselines (Informer, CrossFormer, MPTSNet, EmMixFormer, Detach-Rocket) across all eight eye-tracking datasets in accuracy and F1-score.
- DSTS uses far fewer parameters and less computational resources than Transformer alternatives, demonstrating superior efficiency.
- Ablation studies show that removing either the Class-aware Representation or Imbalance-aware mechanism degrades performance, confirming both contributions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

#### Mechanism 1: Convolutional Layers Capture Short‑Term Dependencies
- **Claim:** 1D convolutional layers effectively capture the short‑term, localized dependencies inherent in discrete eye movement sequences, whereas global attention (Transformers) introduces noise.
- **Mechanism:** Small sliding receptive fields detect local patterns (e.g., a saccade followed by a fixation). Stacked layers build hierarchical detectors. Max pooling creates a fixed‑length summary of short‑term dynamics.
- **Core assumption:** Discriminative signals for ASD/TD classification reside in micro‑patterns of adjacent fixations/saccades, not in long‑range temporal relationships.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract and Section III.A state that eye movements have short‑term dependencies and global attention yields limited benefits; Section III.A notes adjacent points reflect short‑term perceptual continuity.

#### Mechanism 2: Class‑Aware Representation Enhances Embedding Separability
- **Claim:** The CaR mechanism via Multi‑Similarity Loss explicitly shapes the embedding space to tighten within‑class clusters and separate between‑class clusters, making subtle gaze pattern differences more detectable.
- **Mechanism:** MS Loss pulls same‑class embeddings closer and pushes different‑class embeddings apart using a learnable margin λ, applied to the pooled embedding before the classifier.
- **Core assumption:** Gaze patterns from the same diagnostic class share a latent structure that can be compactly represented, while patterns from different classes occupy separable regions in a learned metric space.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section III.C and Equation 4 define MS Loss with positive/negative pair sets and parameters α, β, λ.

#### Mechanism 3: Imbalance‑aware Weighting Mitigates Majority Bias
- **Claim:** The Ia mechanism via weighted cross‑entropy prevents the model from being biased toward the majority ASD class, improving recall for the minority TD class.
- **Mechanism:** Class frequencies from the training set compute inverse‑frequency weights; misclassifying a minority sample incurs a higher loss penalty, forcing more balanced decision boundaries.
- **Core assumption:** Performance drop on the minority class is primarily due to optimization bias, and loss‑level re‑weighting can correct this without severely harming majority‑class performance.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section III.D and Equation 5 describe weighted cross‑entropy; Table I shows consistent class imbalance across all datasets.

## Foundational Learning
- **Short‑Term Temporal Dependencies in Time Series**  
  *Why needed here:* DSTS’s design rests on the premise that relevant signals are in *local* patterns (e.g., 3‑4 consecutive fixations), not global sequence order. Engineers must model this with CNN kernels of small size versus long‑range dependencies.  
  *Quick check:* For a fixation sequence `[F1, S, F2, S, F3]` (F=Fixation, S=Saccade), which pattern is most likely discriminative: the relationship between F1 and F3 (span 4 steps), or the transition `F2→S→F3` (span 2 steps)?

- **Class Imbalance in Medical Diagnosis Datasets**  
  *Why needed here:* All datasets have more ASD than TD participants. Without Ia, a model could achieve >70% accuracy by always predicting “ASD”. Engineers must understand why accuracy is misleading and how loss weighting changes optimization.  
  *Quick check:* If a dataset has 900 ASD and 300 TD samples, what weight should be applied to a TD sample’s loss using inverse frequency? What about an ASD sample?

- **Metric Learning with Multi‑Similarity Loss**  
  *Why needed here:* CaR is a metric‑learning loss that shapes the embedding space *before* the final linear layer, not just a classifier tweak. Engineers must grasp this representation‑learning aspect.  
  *Quick check:* In MS Loss, what is the practical effect of the margin parameter λ on the push/pull forces? If λ is set too high, what might happen to the embedding space?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Input (multivariate fixation sequence S) → CNN Encoder (1D conv layers) → Pooling (max over time) → Dual Loss Heads (MS Loss + Weighted CE) → Classifier Head (FC layer)
- **Critical path:** Raw sequence → CNN layers (local pattern extraction) → MaxPool (summary) → MS Loss / WCE Loss (simultaneous representation shaping & imbalance handling) → FC layer → prediction.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - CNN vs. Transformer: CNN chosen for parameter efficiency and inductive locality bias; forgoes long‑range dependency capture (deemed irrelevant) for lower compute (Fig. 3).  
  - CaR integration: Added *on top of* standard classifier loss, increasing objective complexity but aiming for more separable features.  
  - Loss weighting λ: Fixed at 0.25; a tradeoff between CaR and Ia without per‑dataset tuning.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - **Pattern mismatch:** If test data contains sequences where long‑range dependencies are key, CNN‑based DSTS may underperform vs. Transformers.  
  - **Imbalance handling failure:** If λ=0.25 is suboptimal, Ia may not compensate enough, hurting minority (TD) recall. Check per‑class F1/recall.  
  - **Representation collapse:** Over‑aggressive MS Loss could force all embeddings of one class into a tiny cluster, harming generalization; monitor intra‑class variance in embeddings during training.  
  - **Data leakage in Ia:** Weights must be computed *only* from the training set; using validation/test frequencies invalidates the mechanism.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Baseline comparison:** Replicate Table II – train/evaluate DSTS and transformer baselines on all 8 datasets; expect DSTS to win on ACC/F1 while being most efficient (Fig. 3).  
  2. **Ablation study:** Replicate Tables III & IV – create variants without CaR (I) and without Ia (II); full DSTS should consistently rank 1st/2nd.  
  3. **Short‑term dependency verification:** Artificially shuffle local windows (swap adjacent fixations) vs. globally randomize sequences; CNN‑DSTS should degrade more on local shuffling, while a Transformer might degrade more on global shuffling.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. Why do Transformer‑based architectures with global self‑attention mechanisms fail to capture the discriminative patterns in discrete, short‑term dependent eye movement data for ASD detection, despite their success on other sequential data? The paper hypothesizes that the discrete fixation points and short‑term dependencies reduce the utility of global attention, but lacks rigorous analysis (e.g., attention weight visualization, synthetic data probing) to isolate the exact cause.
2. Can a single, unified model architecture be developed that adaptively learns optimal local versus global temporal patterns across diverse eye‑tracking paradigms (e.g., social stimuli vs. motor tasks) without manual configuration? The conclusion identifies this as future work; DSTS’s fixed CNN backbone is not shown to dynamically adjust to new paradigms.
3. What is the precise relationship between the severity of class imbalance (ASD:TD ratio) and the individual contribution of the proposed Class‑aware Representation (CaR) and Imbalance‑aware (Ia) mechanisms to model robustness and fairness? The paper uses both but does not vary imbalance levels or dissect their roles under different imbalance spectrums.
4. Are the superior performance and efficiency of the CNN‑based DSTS framework over Transformers an intrinsically data‑driven phenomenon specific to the discrete, short‑term structure of eye movements, or could similar architectural biases (local over global) be empirically derived for other discrete event‑sequence data (e.g., clickstreams, medical event logs)? The paper suggests broader implications but does not test or formalize this hypothesis.
5. To what extent does the DSTS framework’s performance depend on the specific preprocessing and discretization of continuous raw eye‑tracking signals into fixation‑saccade sequences, versus the model architecture itself? The paper does not detail the preprocessing pipeline; gains attributed to CNN capturing local patterns could be partially due to preprocessing choices that inherently smooth or segment data in a CNN‑friendly way.

## Limitations
- The eight datasets may not represent broader ASD populations; external validity is untested.
- Loss weighting λ=0.25 is fixed without tuning, possibly suboptimal for some imbalance levels.
- The core premise that only short‑term dependencies matter may be too restrictive; some ASD signals could be long‑range.
- No independent clinical validation; risk of overfitting to specific eye‑tracking setups.

## Confidence
- **CNN captures short‑term patterns; global attention ineffective:** Medium (empirical on provided data, but not proven universal).
- **Class‑aware Representation (MS Loss) enhances separation:** Medium (ablation supportive, but metric learning can be fickle).
- **Imbalance‑aware weighting handles class skew:** High (standard method, clear imbalance, strong ablation results).
- **Overall SOTA performance across 8 datasets:** Medium (needs external replication and statistical significance testing).

## Next Checks
1. **Replicate main results:** Train/evaluate DSTS and all transformer baselines on the 8 datasets using identical splits; report ACC, F1, and model size to verify claims.
2. **Ablation of CaR and Ia:** Remove each mechanism individually and jointly; quantify performance changes to confirm their contributions (cf. Tables III/IV).
3. **Short‑term vs. long‑term perturbation:** Artificially shuffle local windows (adjacent fixations) vs. globally randomize sequences; compare robustness of DSTS (CNN) and a transformer baseline to directly test the short‑term dependency hypothesis.
