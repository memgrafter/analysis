# Coupling Local Context and Global Semantic Prototypes via a Hierarchical Architecture for Rhetorical Roles Labeling

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.03856
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03856
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Prototype-based methods (PBR and PCM) consistently improve Macro-F1 on rhetorical role labeling across legal, medical, and scientific benchmarks. PCM achieves state-of-the-art on 4 of 7 tasks, with gains up to +4.4 Macro-F1 on low-frequency roles (e.g., SCOTUS Steps: 46.70% → 54.03%), and offers a favorable accuracy-efficiency trade-off compared to fine-tuned LLMs.

## Executive Summary
Rhetorical Role Labeling (RRL) aims to assign functional labels to sentences in a document, but hierarchical models often fail to capture global, corpus-level semantic patterns. This paper introduces two prototype-based approaches: Prototype-Based Regularization (PBR) aligns sentence embeddings with learnable soft prototypes via an auxiliary loss, while Prototype-Conditioned Modulation (PCM) injects precomputed corpus-wide prototypes during encoding. Evaluated on seven benchmarks spanning legal, medical, and scientific domains, both methods yield consistent improvements over a strong Hierarchical Sequential Labeling Network (HSLN) baseline. PCM delivers the best results on four tasks, with particularly large gains on fine-grained legal roles and low-frequency categories. The study also releases SCOTUS-LAW, the first U.S. Supreme Court corpus annotated at three rhetorical levels. By requiring far fewer parameters than fine-tuned large language models, prototype methods present an effective and efficient solution for global feature integration in RRL.

## Method Summary
The authors build upon the Hierarchical Sequential Labeling Network (HSLN), which uses a sentence encoder (domain-specific BERT followed by a Bi-LSTM and attention pooling) to create sentence vectors, then a document-level Bi-LSTM to capture context, and finally a CRF classifier for sequential labeling. Two prototype-based enhancements are proposed.

Prototype-Based Regularization (PBR) introduces a set of learnable prototype vectors (one per role or a flexible number). During training, an auxiliary loss encourages each sentence’s contextual representation to stay close to its nearest prototype (proximity loss) while pushing prototypes apart from each other (diversity loss). This geometric regularization shapes the latent space into well-separated, coherent clusters for each rhetorical function, complementing the standard cross-entropy loss.

Prototype-Conditioned Modulation (PCM) precomputes a prototype for each rhetorical role by averaging sentence embeddings from the training corpus. These fixed vectors are then used to modulate the sentence representations inside the hierarchical encoder: for a given sentence, its closest prototype is retrieved and fused with its contextual vector (e.g., by concatenation followed by a linear projection). This injects global semantic priors directly into the encoding process, helping the model disambiguate roles based on corpus-wide evidence. Both methods are evaluated on seven datasets, including the newly introduced multi-level SCOTUS-LAW corpus.

## Key Results
- PBR and PCM consistently outperform the HSLN baseline on all seven benchmarks (legal, medical, scientific).
- PCM achieves the best results on 4 of 7 tasks, with the largest improvement seen on the finest-grained SCOTUS Steps (+7.33 Macro-F1) and on low-frequency roles in legal datasets (e.g., SCOTUS Steps: 46.70% → 54.03%).
- Prototype methods provide a favorable accuracy‑efficiency trade‑off, surpassing fine‑tuned LLMs while using far fewer parameters.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Prototype-Based Regularization (PBR) Structures Latent Space
PBR jointly learns a set of soft prototypes and penalizes sentence embeddings for deviating from their nearest prototype. The auxiliary loss comprises a proximity term (minimizing the distance to the closest prototype) and a diversity term (maximizing pairwise prototype distances). This direct geometric constraint forces the model to organize the latent space into well‑separated, coherent clusters corresponding to rhetorical roles, thereby reducing confusion between semantically similar functions. The approach assumes that each role has a corpus‑wide semantic core that can be captured by a centroid. Evidence: PBR improves Macro‑F1 on SCOTUS RF from 61.36% to 65.75% and boosts the minority role “Stating the Court’s reasoning” from 57% to 60.35%. t‑SNE visualizations show clearer role separation. Ablation studies reveal that overly strong regularization (e.g., λ=10.0) degrades performance, highlighting the need for balanced hyperparameters.

### Mechanism 2: Prototype‑Conditioned Modulation (PCM) Injects Global Priors During Encoding
PCM constructs a fixed prototype per role by averaging embeddings of training sentences, then uses these vectors to condition the hierarchical encoder. For each sentence, the most similar prototype is retrieved and fused with its contextual representation (via strategies like Linear Fusion or Gated Addition). This injects a global semantic prior directly into the flow of information, allowing the model to leverage corpus‑level knowledge during sentence encoding rather than relying solely on local context. The core assumption is that precomputed prototypes reliably capture the central semantics of each role. Evidence: PCM yields the top score on SCOTUS Steps (46.70% → 54.03%), the finest granularity, indicating global cues are most valuable for subtle distinctions. Oracle experiments using gold prototypes achieve near‑perfect scores (e.g., 99.7% on CS‑ABSTRACTS), confirming the approach’s high potential when retrieval is accurate. The choice of injection strategy is domain‑dependent: Linear Fusion excels on legal texts, while Gated Residual Addition works slightly better on biomedical abstracts.

### Mechanism 3: Multi‑Level Annotation Highlights Where Global Cues Are Essential
The SCOTUS‑LAW corpus’s three‑level annotation (category, rhetorical function, step) reveals that as label granularity increases, the benefit from prototype methods grows. Finer‑grained steps involve more semantically overlapping roles (e.g., “Recalling” vs. “Stating the Court’s reasoning”), making them harder for local sequential models. Global prototypes provide stable, corpus‑wide anchors that help resolve these ambiguities. The assumption is that rhetorical role ambiguity intensifies at specific levels where definitions rely on subtle pragmatic nuances. Evidence: The marginal gain from the baseline to PCM increases with granularity: SCOTUS Category (+1.74 mF1), RF (+5.17), Steps (+7.33). Expert evaluation identifies the most difficult sentences as those involving confusions among step‑level roles within the Analysis category—precisely the level where PCM delivers the largest improvements.

## Foundational Learning
- **Hierarchical Sequential Labeling Network (HSLN)**
  - Why needed here: It is the backbone architecture for RRL, processing sentences with BERT and Bi‑LSTM, then aggregating document‑level context. Its limitation in modeling global features is what the prototype methods aim to address.
  - Quick check question: What is the key limitation of HSLN that prototype methods aim to address? (Answer: Its inability to model global, corpus‑level features, focusing only on intra‑document dependencies.)

- **Prototype‑Based Learning**
  - Why needed here: Both PBR and PCM are variations of this paradigm, where each class is represented by a centroid in embedding space and distances to centroids are used for regularization or conditioning.
  - Quick check question: What is the fundamental difference between PBR (soft, learned prototypes as regularizers) and PCM (hard, precomputed prototypes as conditioning inputs)? (Answer: PBR’s prototypes are trainable parameters optimized with the model; PCM’s are fixed vectors computed from the corpus and injected.)

- **Rhetorical Role Labeling (RRL) Task & Annotation Granularity**
  - Why needed here: The problem setting, with its label space and challenges (imbalance, ambiguity), defines the need for global prototypes. The multi‑level annotation (category/function/step) is central to the paper’s analysis of when prototypes help most.
  - Quick check question: Why might labeling a sentence as “Stating the Court’s reasoning” be more ambiguous than labeling it as “Analysis”? (Answer: Because “Stating the Court’s reasoning” is a specific function within the Analysis category, requiring finer discrimination from related functions like “Recalling” or “Accepting arguments.”)

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Input (Document) -> Sentence Encoder (BERT + Bi‑LSTM + Attention Pooling) -> Sentence vectors (v_ij) -> Contextual Encoder (Bi‑LSTM) -> Contextual vectors (c_ij) -> Classifier (CRF) -> Labels.  
Additionally:  
- **PBR branch**: Learnable prototypes P are stored; from c_ij, compute L_prox (mean min distance to P) and L_div (mean pairwise distance among prototypes). These losses are added to the task loss during training; no change at inference.  
- **PCM branch**: Precomputed role prototypes pr are looked up (e.g., nearest to v_ij). Before or during the Contextual Encoder, pr is fused with the sentence representation (e.g., Linear Fusion: [c_ij; pr] -> linear layer -> modulated c_ij') to produce a conditioned vector that proceeds through the rest of the encoder and classifier.

### Critical Path
1. Train the base HSLN model until convergence to establish a baseline.
2. For **PBR**: Initialize Q learnable prototypes. Jointly train the HSLN and prototypes with total loss L = L_task + λ_prox * L_prox − λ_div * L_div. Tune Q, λ_prox, λ_div on the development set (recommended values: Q=16, λ_prox=λ_div=0.9).
3. For **PCM**: Using a frozen sentence encoder (BERT+BiLSTM+Pooling), encode all training documents to obtain v_ij. Compute a corpus‑level prototype for each rhetorical role by averaging v_ij of all sentences with that label (using a sampling strategy such as Full, Random, or Supervised K‑Means). Freeze these prototypes. Insert the injection module (e.g., Linear Fusion) into the second Bi‑LSTM level. Train the modified model (injection module + second Bi‑LSTM + CRF) with the standard CRF loss, using the precomputed prototypes.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **PBR vs. PCM**: PBR is simpler, end‑to‑end trainable, and incurs no extra inference cost, but learns prototypes from scratch which may be noisy. PCM introduces a strong corpus‑level prior and can be more powerful, but requires careful prototype extraction and adds computation for retrieval and fusion.
- **Injection strategy**: Linear Fusion is straightforward and effective on legal data, but may dilute the signal; Conditional Layer Norm and Gated Addition offer more nuanced control; Cross‑Attention is the most flexible but also the most expensive. The optimal choice depends on the dataset (e.g., Linear Fusion for SCOTUS, Gated Residual for PUBMED).
- **Prototype quantity (PBR)**: More prototypes (larger Q) can represent multimodal role distributions but risk overfitting and increased memory; too few may not capture intra‑role variance.

### Failure Signatures
- **PBR**: Prototype collapse (all prototypes become nearly identical) if λ_div is too low; over‑compression of the embedding space if λ_prox is too high; divergence of auxiliary losses. Monitor prototype pairwise distances and embedding variance.
- **PCM**: Performance drop relative to baseline indicates poor prototype quality or mismatched injection. Check cosine similarity of sentences to their assigned prototype; a low average similarity suggests prototypes are not representative.
- **General**: If improvements occur only on majority roles, the method may be reinforcing dominant patterns without aiding minority/ambiguous cases. Always examine per‑role F1 scores.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
Can prototype‑based RRL be extended to multi‑label classification to handle sentences expressing multiple rhetorical functions? The current single‑label formulation may not capture the semantic complexity of long sentences. Modifying PBR/PCM to support multi‑label objectives (e.g., binary relevance, label powerset) and evaluating with multi‑label metrics would be needed.

### Open Question 2
How can semantic prototyping be effectively adapted to multilingual RRL, given language‑specific rhetorical conventions and potential label mismatches? All experiments are in English; extending to other languages would require parallel corpora with consistent annotation and careful handling of cross‑lingual embedding alignment.

### Open Question 3
What improvements in prototype retrieval and construction are most critical for PCM’s effectiveness, particularly on domains with low structural variation like medical abstracts? Simple averaging may not suffice; exploring clustering‑based prototypes, contrastive learning for embeddings, or more sophisticated retrieval (e.g., attention) could yield gains.

### Open Question 4
For highly ambiguous role pairs (e.g., ACCEPTING vs. REJECTING arguments), what additional linguistic or structural signals beyond global prototypes can further disambiguate? Prototypes capture coarse semantics but may miss pragmatic cues (e.g., modality, attribution verbs). Incorporating argumentative discourse units or sentiment features might help.

## Limitations
- Hyperparameter dependency: PBR’s (Q, λ) and PCM’s (sampling, injection) require per‑dataset tuning; guidance for new domains is limited.
- Prototype misassignment risk in PCM: The nearest‑prototype search may fail for ambiguous sentences, potentially limiting gains, especially in structurally simple domains.
- Evaluation scope restricted to English corpora; cross‑lingual and cross‑system generalization remain unexplored.

## Confidence
- PBR latent space regularization improves clustering and low‑frequency role performance: **High**  
- PCM global prior injection yields state‑of‑the‑art on most fine‑grained tasks: **Medium**  
- Increasing benefit with annotation granularity (multi‑level annotation): **High**  
- Favorable accuracy‑efficiency trade‑off versus fine‑tuned LLMs: **High**

2. **Cross‑lingual robustness test**: Apply PBR and PCM to a non‑English legal dataset (e.g., German court rulings) to evaluate generalization across languages and legal systems.
