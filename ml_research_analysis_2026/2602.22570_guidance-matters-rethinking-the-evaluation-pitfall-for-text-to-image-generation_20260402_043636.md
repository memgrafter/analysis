# Guidance Matters: Rethinking the Evaluation Pitfall for Text-to-Image Generation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.22570
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22570
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Human preference models (HPS v2, ImageReward) are biased toward large guidance scales, rewarding oversaturation; GA-Eval reveals most advanced guidance methods offer no real benefit over simply scaling CFG.

## Executive Summary
This paper uncovers a critical evaluation pitfall in text-to-image generation: commonly used human preference models (e.g., HPS v2, ImageReward) exhibit a strong bias toward large classifier-free guidance (CFG) scales, preferring oversaturated and artificially enhanced images over genuinely higher-quality outputs. As a result, recent guidance methods that implicitly increase effective guidance strength appear superior under these metrics, but their claimed improvements are often illusory. To address this, the authors introduce the Guidance-Aware Evaluation (GA-Eval) framework, which calibrates each method’s effective guidance scale (ω_e) by decomposing its noise update into components parallel and orthogonal to the standard CFG direction. This enables fair comparison by matching guidance strength. Applying GA-Eval to eight state-of-the-art guidance methods across multiple diffusion models shows that nearly all suffer severe performance degradation when guidance strength is equalized, with only Z-Sampling maintaining a modest advantage. The work concludes that scaling CFG alone can match or beat most recent methods, highlighting the need for more robust evaluation protocols and truly orthogonal guidance innovations.

## Method Summary
GA-Eval provides a systematic way to compare any diffusion guidance method against standard CFG on an equal footing. The core idea is to compute an effective guidance scale ω_e for a given method by analyzing its per-timestep noise update during sampling. For each timestep, the method’s final noise prediction ˜ϵ* is compared to the unconditional noise ϵ_uncond. The difference vector (˜ϵ* - ϵ_uncond) is projected onto the canonical CFG direction Δϵ = ϵ_cond - ϵ_uncond. The ratio of the projected magnitude to ‖Δϵ‖ yields a per-timestep effective scale ω_e_t; averaging over all timesteps gives ω_e. This ω_e represents the equivalent CFG scale that would produce a similar parallel effect. To evaluate a method fairly, one generates a reference image set using plain CFG with ω = ω_e (the “e-CFG” set). Pairwise preferences are then computed between the method’s outputs and both the default CFG (with the method’s original hyperparameters) and the e-CFG set. The difference in winning rates (Δη = η_CFG - η_e-CFG) quantifies how much the method relied on an inflated effective guidance scale rather than orthogonal improvements. The framework handles methods that modify latent updates by reformulating them into an equivalent noise update using the scheduler’s update equations.

## Key Results
- Human preference models (HPS v2, ImageReward, PickScore, CLIPScore) show a monotonic increase in score as CFG scale ω grows from 5.5 to 20, even though visual quality degrades due to oversaturation.
- After GA-Eval calibration, seven of the eight studied guidance methods exhibit severe winning rate degradation (Δη > 10% on HPS v2) when compared to e-CFG, indicating their gains were primarily from increased effective guidance.
- Only Z-Sampling retains a modest superiority (η_e-CFG > 55% on average) after calibration, suggesting it provides some orthogonal benefit.
- Simply increasing the CFG scale of the baseline can match or outperform most advanced guidance methods under conventional (biased) evaluation.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Human preference models are trained on datasets where images with bright, saturated colors and strong prompt alignment are often preferred by human annotators. Increasing CFG scale ω pushes the predicted noise more aggressively toward the conditional direction, producing images with amplified color saturation and contrast. These visual attributes correlate with patterns in the preference training data, causing models like HPS v2 and ImageReward to assign higher scores despite a decline in true aesthetic quality or fidelity. This creates a systematic evaluation bias: any method that inadvertently or deliberately increases the effective guidance strength will appear better under these metrics, even if it introduces oversaturation artifacts.

GA-Eval circumvents this by mathematically decomposing any guidance method’s effect into two parts relative to standard CFG: a parallel component (proportional to the CFG direction Δϵ) and an orthogonal component. The effective guidance scale ω_e is computed as the average per-timestep projection coefficient of the method’s noise deviation onto Δϵ. This isolates the portion of the method’s influence that is equivalent to simply using a larger CFG scale. By then comparing the method against a CFG baseline with ω = ω_e, the evaluation levelizes the playing field—any remaining advantage must come from the orthogonal component, which represents true innovation beyond stronger guidance.

The degradation results arise because most advanced guidance methods implicitly amplify the conditional signal, leading to ω_e values that far exceed their nominal guidance parameters or the standard CFG scale used in baseline comparisons. When fairer comparison is made via e-CFG, these methods lose their apparent edge, revealing that their reported improvements were largely illusory and driven by the same saturation bias that affects the metrics.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG)**
  - **Why needed:** CFG is the reference mechanism in GA-Eval; its standard update rule ϵ = ϵ_uncond + ω(ϵ_cond - ϵ_uncond) defines the parallel direction Δϵ.
  - **Quick check:** If ω = 0, the prediction reduces to unconditional noise ϵ_uncond.
- **Concept: Diffusion Sampling (DDPM/DDIM)**
  - **Why needed:** GA-Eval operates on predicted noise during sampling; the relationship between latent updates and ϵ depends on the scheduler.
  - **Quick check:** The DDIM update is: x_{t-1} = √α_{t-1} · (x_t - √(1-α_t)ϵ) / √α_t + √(1-α_{t-1})ϵ.
- **Concept: Human Preference Models (HPS v2, ImageReward)**
  - **Why needed:** These are the metrics exhibiting the saturation bias; they predict overall preference based on text-image alignment and aesthetics, not pixel-level fidelity.
  - **Quick check:** They do not directly measure no-reference image quality; they are trained on human rankings.
- **Concept: Vector Projection and ℓ₂‑norm**
  - **Why needed:** Computing ω_e requires projecting a vector onto the CFG direction using dot products and norms.
  - **Quick check:** The scalar projection of a onto b is ⟨a, b⟩ / ‖b‖.
- **Concept: Effective Guidance Scale**
  - **Why needed:** This is the key calibrated quantity that equates a non‑CFG method to an equivalent CFG strength.
  - **Quick check:** If a method’s ω_e = 12 while its native ω = 5, it amplifies conditional influence by a factor of 2.4 beyond nominal, likely causing oversaturation.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Diffusion model loader → generates ϵ_uncond, ϵ_cond
  Guidance method wrapper → applies custom sampling, records intermediate quantities
  CFG baseline generator → runs standard CFG with adjustable ω
  Effective scale estimator → computes ω_e via vector projection per timestep
  Evaluation engine → applies preference models (HPS v2, ImageReward, etc.)
  Comparison and reporting module → calculates η_CFG, η_e-CFG, Δη

- **Critical path:**
  1. For each prompt, run the guidance method, storing timestep‑wise predictions or latent updates.
  2. Compute Δϵ = ϵ_cond – ϵ_uncond from the same run.
  3. Reconstruct the method’s noise-equivalent update ˜ϵ*_t (directly or via Eq.10 for latent modifiers).
  4. Compute per‑timestep ω_e_t = (⟨˜ϵ*_t - ϵ_uncond, Δϵ⟩) / ⟨Δϵ, Δϵ⟩ and average to get ω_e.
  5. Generate a CFG reference image set with ω = ω_e (same seeds, scheduler, steps).
  6. Evaluate all sets (method, e‑CFG, default CFG) using preference models.
  7. Compute winning rates η_CFG (method vs default CFG) and η_e-CFG (method vs e‑CFG).
  8. Report degradation Δη = η_CFG – η_e-CFG.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Speed vs fidelity: Storing all timestep data increases memory; sampling every k steps reduces cost but may approximate ω_e less accurately.
  - Metric choice: HPS v2/ImageReward favor saturation; including AES/FID balances view but may not reflect prompt alignment.
  - Generality: Reformulating latent updates to noise‑equivalents (Eq.10) works for common schedulers but may fail for exotic updates, limiting out‑of‑the‑box coverage.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - Extremely high ω_e (>15) with visibly oversaturated outputs indicates the method is effectively just increasing guidance strength.
  - Low Δη (<5%) suggests genuine orthogonal benefit; high Δη (>10%) indicates reliance on bias.
  - High variance of ω_e across timesteps points to unstable guidance; averaging may hide anomalies.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Bias confirmation: Generate 50 prompts with CFG at ω = 5, 10, 15, 20 on SD‑XL. Plot HPS v2 and AES vs ω; expect HPS v2 to rise monotonically while AES peaks and declines.
  2. Effective scale extraction: Compute ω_e for Z‑Sampling, CFG++, TDG on 20 prompts; confirm most ω_e exceed nominal ω by 2‑3×.
  3. Full GA‑Eval benchmark: Run pipeline on Pick‑a‑Pic (100 prompts) for all eight methods; reproduce Table 1, showing only Z‑Sampling maintains η_e‑CFG > 55%.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How can we develop human preference models robust to the saturation bias induced by large guidance scales while accurately assessing both prompt alignment and aesthetic quality?
- What specific orthogonal effects (i.e., components perpendicular to the CFG direction) in diffusion guidance methods contribute to genuine quality improvements, and how can we systematically design new guidance techniques that leverage these orthogonal components without merely increasing the guidance scale?
- Does the GA-Eval framework with its effective guidance scale calibration fully disentangle all confounding factors in diffusion guidance evaluation, or are there remaining biases (e.g., due to architecture, noise scheduling, or non‑linear interactions) that could still inflate method performance?
- How can we modify the training data or objectives of large‑scale human preference models to eliminate the saturation bias while maintaining their correlation with true human judgments across a broader range of image qualities?

## Limitations
- GA-Eval assumes a linear decomposition of the noise update relative to CFG; non‑linear interactions or scheduler‑specific effects may confound the effective scale calculation.
- The framework’s validity for methods with extreme or discontinuous modifications (e.g., certain adaptive schedulers) is not proven; reformulating latent updates may introduce errors.
- Evaluation relies on existing human preference models that are themselves biased; while GA-Eval calibrates for one bias, other metric flaws may remain.
