# On the Power of Source Screening for Learning Shared Feature Extractors

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.16125
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16125
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Training on a carefully selected subset of sources achieves the minimax optimal rate and improves classification accuracy.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the problem of selecting a subset of heterogeneous data sources to learn a shared linear subspace efficiently. It introduces the notion of an admissible subpopulation, characterized by a well‑conditioned diversity matrix of local coefficients and a size proportional to $k$ times the smallest eigenvalue of the global covariance. Leveraging the Bourgain–Tzafriri theorem, the authors prove that such a subset always exists when the standardized coefficient matrix has sufficiently large stable rank. They then propose an efficient algorithm—both genie‑aided (with true parameters) and empirical (using split‑sample averages)—to identify this subpopulation.

Theoretically, training on the selected subset attains the minimax optimal estimation rate, matching the lower bound. Empirically, on synthetic data, the method yields lower principal angle errors than using all sources, especially when the local coefficients form clusters. On real‑world datasets (ACSIncome and CelebA), the screened subpopulation improves classification accuracy by up to 1.5% compared to baselines.

## Method Summary
The goal is to learn a shared $k$‑dimensional linear subspace from $M$ heterogeneous sources, each providing local data with parameters $\alpha_i \in \mathbb{R}^k$. Instead of using all sources, the method finds a subset $S$ such that the matrix of stacked $\alpha_i$ (for $i\in S$) has bounded condition number. The procedure first estimates a proxy matrix $\tilde{A}$ by splitting each source’s samples into two halves and forming sufficient statistics; their sum serves as a rotation‑adjusted stand‑in for the true parameter matrix. From $\tilde{A}$, stable rank and the smallest eigenvalue $\lambda_{\min}$ are computed. An iterative Bourgain–Tzafriri‑inspired algorithm then samples random column subsets of size $s = \lceil c^* \cdot \text{stable rank}(\tilde{A}) \rceil$, tests whether the subset’s Gram matrix is close to identity (via $\|\cdot\|_{\infty\to1}$), and if so, extracts its columns. The loop runs at most $\lceil \lambda_{\min} \rceil$ times; the union of all extracted sets yields the admissible subpopulation $S$. A subspace estimator (e.g., split‑averaging) trained on $S$ achieves the theoretical guarantee.

## Key Results
- The selected subpopulation attains the minimax optimal estimation rate, matching the lower bound.
- Synthetic experiments show reduced principal angle error compared to using all sources, with significant gains in clustered coefficient settings.
- Real‑world classification accuracy on ACSIncome and CelebA improves by up to 1.5% over baseline methods (random, power‑of‑choice, balanced).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

#### Mechanism 1: Balanced subpopulation selection for minimax optimality
- **Claim:** A subpopulation with balanced representation of latent directions (low condition number of the diversity matrix) and size $\Theta(k \cdot \lambda_{\min}(AA^\top))$ achieves the minimax optimal estimation rate.
- **Mechanism:** By restricting training to sources whose local parameters $\alpha_i$ span the shared subspace with bounded condition number, bias from imbalanced sources is eliminated. The statistical rate then depends on the eigenvalue $\lambda_k$ of the diversity matrix $D$, which is maximized when the subpopulation is balanced.
- **Core assumption:** There exists a subset $S$ satisfying $\kappa(\sum_{i\in S} \alpha_i \alpha_i^\top) = \Theta(1)$ and $|S| = \Theta(k \cdot \lambda_{\min}(AA^\top))$.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions minimax optimality; Theorem 2 provides the rate $O(\sqrt{d/(N\lambda_k)})$; the clustered analysis in Section 4.1 shows error reduction $\Omega(\sqrt{d/(N\beta_k)})$ when $\beta_k = \lambda_k$.
- **Break condition:** If the stable rank $\text{st.rank}(A) = \omega(1)$ fails, the Bourgain–Tzafriri argument cannot guarantee a subset with condition number $\le 3$; the algorithm may return an empty set or a poorly conditioned subset.

#### Mechanism 2: Stable rank guarantees existence of well‑conditioned subsets
- **Claim:** If the standardized matrix $A = [\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_M]$ has sufficiently large stable rank $\text{st.rank}(A) = \|A\|_F^2 / \|A\|^2$, then there exists a subset of $O(\text{st.rank}(A))$ columns whose Gram matrix has condition number $\le 3$.
- **Mechanism:** The proof relies on the Bourgain–Tzafriri theorem: a random subset of size $s = \lceil c \cdot \text{st.rank}(A) \rceil$ yields a submatrix with condition number $\le 3$ with positive probability. The algorithm iteratively removes such subsets to build the admissible subpopulation.
- **Core assumption:** $\|A\|_2 \lesssim M/k$, which is mild when $A$ is standardized and columns are not too aligned.
- **Evidence anchors:** Theorem 4 and its proof (Appendix G) derive the subset size $s$ in terms of stable rank; the condition $\|A\|_2 \lesssim M/k$ is stated in Theorem 3.
- **Break condition:** If $\text{st.rank}(A)$ is too small (e.g., all $\alpha_i$ nearly identical), the required subset size $s$ may be less than 1, making the construction vacuous.

#### Mechanism 3: Empirical estimation via split‑sample concentration
- **Claim:** When true $\alpha_i$ are unknown, split‑sample averages can be used to form a proxy matrix $\tilde{A}$ that concentrates around $A$ up to rotation, preserving stable rank and $\lambda_{\min}$ with high probability.
- **Mechanism:** For each source, split local data into two halves; compute per‑source averages $\bar{z}_i$ and $\dot{z}_i$ from each half. Stack into $\bar{Z}$ and $\dot{Z}$. Under sub‑Gaussian covariates and noise, these matrices concentrate around $\mathbb{E}[\bar{Z}] = B^\star A$ and $\mathbb{E}[\dot{Z}] = B^\star A$. Thus $\tilde{A} = \bar{Z} + \dot{Z}$ approximates $A$ after rotation by $B^\star$, preserving eigenvalues and stable rank.
- **Core assumption:** Per‑source sample size $n$ is sufficiently large for concentration; the error $\|\tilde{A} - A\|_2$ is small relative to $\lambda_{\min}(AA^\top)$.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 4.3 shows $\mathbb{E}[\bar{Z}] = B^\star A$ etc.; Algorithm 2 is the empirical version replacing $A$ with $\tilde{A}$.
- **Break condition:** If $n$ is too small, $\tilde{A}$ may not concentrate, leading to incorrect stable rank/$\lambda_{\min}$ estimates and potentially selecting a non‑admissible subset.

## Foundational Learning

### Principal angle distance
- **Why needed:** It quantifies the alignment between the estimated subspace $\hat{B}$ and the ground truth $B^\star$, serving as the primary error metric in theory and experiments.
- **Quick check:** "What does a principal angle distance of 0.5 between two $k$‑dimensional subspaces in $\mathbb{R}^d$ indicate about their alignment?" (Approximately a 30° largest principal angle, indicating moderate misalignment.)

### Condition number of a covariance matrix
- **Why needed:** An admissible subpopulation requires $\kappa(\sum_{i\in S} \alpha_i \alpha_i^\top) = \Theta(1)$. This condition number measures how balanced the representation of latent directions is across the selected sources; a high condition number indicates domination by a few directions.
- **Quick check:** "For a positive semi‑definite matrix, what does $\kappa = 1$ imply about its eigenvalues?" (All eigenvalues are equal, i.e., the matrix is a scalar multiple of the identity, meaning perfectly isotropic spread.)

### Stable rank
- **Why needed:** Stable rank determines the subset size in the Bourgain–Tzafriri argument. Unlike algebraic rank, it is robust to small perturbations and captures the effective energy spread of the columns, enabling the existence proof for well‑conditioned subsets even when the matrix is near low‑rank.
- **Quick check:** "If a matrix has all columns unit‑norm but highly correlated, would its stable rank be closer to 1 or to $M$? Why?" (Closer to 1, because $AA^\top$’s eigenvalues are dominated by the first few principal components; $\|A\|_F^2 \approx M$ while $\|A\|^2$ equals the top eigenvalue, yielding a small ratio.)

## Architecture Onboarding

#### Component map
Split data → Compute per‑source split averages ($\bar{z}_i$, $\dot{z}_i$) → Form proxy matrix $\tilde{A} = \bar{Z} + \dot{Z}$ → Compute stable rank $\&$ $\lambda_{\min}(\tilde{A}\tilde{A}^\top)$ → Iterative random subset selection (size $s$): test condition $\|H\|_{\infty\to1} \le s/8$ → Grothendieck factorization $\to$ column extraction (small diagonal) → Aggregation into $S$.

#### Critical path
The algorithm begins by splitting each source’s data into two halves. From each half we compute the sufficient statistics $\bar{z}_i$ and $\dot{z}_i$. These are stacked to form $\bar{Z}$ and $\dot{Z}$, and their sum $\tilde{A}$ serves as a proxy for the true parameter matrix $A$. Next, we compute the stable rank of $\tilde{A}$ and the smallest eigenvalue $\lambda_{\min}$ of $\tilde{A}\tilde{A}^\top$. Using these, we determine the subset size $s = \lceil c^* \cdot \text{stable rank}(\tilde{A}) \rceil$. Then, for $t$ from $1$ to $\lceil \lambda_{\min} \rceil$ (the number of rounds), we check whether the current residual matrix $A_t$ still has stable rank $\ge k/(2e c^*)$. If not, we stop. Within each round, we repeatedly draw random column subsets $eS_t$ of size $s$ from $A_t$. For each, we compute $H = A_{eS_t}^\top A_{eS_t} - I_s$ and evaluate its $\infty\to1$ norm. If $\|H\|_{\infty\to1} \le s/8$, we factor $H = D T D$ via Grothendieck factorization (with $D$ diagonal non‑negative, $\text{trace}(D^2)=1$, $\|T\| \le 2\|H\|_{\infty\to1}$). We then select the columns $S_t = \{ j \in eS_t : d_{jj}^2 \le 2/s \}$. Those columns are removed from $A_t$ to form $A_{t+1}$. After all rounds, the union $S = \bigcup_t S_t$ is the final admissible subpopulation.

#### Design tradeoffs
- **Subset size $c^*$:** A smaller $c^*$ reduces the subset size per round, potentially lowering the condition number bound (Theorem 5 requires $320(c^*+\sqrt{2c^*}) \le 0.5$). However, if $c^*$ is too small, $s$ may become $1$, making the Grothendieck test degenerate.
- **Number of rounds:** The loop runs at most $\lceil \lambda_{\min} \rceil$ times. Larger $\lambda_{\min}$ allows more rounds and thus a larger total subpopulation, but each removal reduces stable rank, possibly causing early termination.
- **Random subset trials:** Each round attempts $\lceil \log(8/7 / \delta) \rceil$ random subsets to achieve failure probability $\le \delta$. More trials increase the chance of finding a good subset but add computational overhead.

#### Failure signatures
- **Early termination:** The algorithm stops with an empty or very small $S$ if the initial stable rank is below $k/(2e c^*)$ or drops below this threshold during iterations.
- **Condition number blow‑up:** After removing a subset, the remaining matrix’s condition number may increase, violating the stable rank requirement and preventing further rounds, leaving an inadmissible subset.
- **Grothendieck factorization errors:** If $H$ is nearly singular or ill‑conditioned, the factorization may yield inaccurate $D$, causing incorrect column selection (e.g., too many or too few columns with small diagonal).
- **Proxy matrix misestimation:** With small per‑source sample size $n$, $\tilde{A}$ may not concentrate, leading to erroneous stable rank or $\lambda_{\min}$ estimates and consequently a poor subpopulation.
- **Empirical performance regression:** The selected subset might yield higher principal angle error or classification accuracy than using all sources, indicating a failure to meet the theoretical conditions.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** Can source screening theory be extended to nonlinear representation learning (e.g., deep nets) to retain statistical optimality? The conclusion suggests this as future work. It remains unresolved because current theory only covers linear subspaces; empirical gains in nonlinear regimes are observed but unexplained. Evidence for resolution: derive minimax rates for a nonlinear shared representation model (e.g., using NTK) and prove that a screened subset achieves them.
- **Open Question 2:** Does the empirical subpopulation selection algorithm (Algorithm 2) provably identify an admissible subset with finite samples? Section 4.3 notes that Algorithm 1 is provable while Algorithm 2 is a heuristic without guarantees. It is unresolved because its sample complexity and error probability are unknown. Evidence for resolution: show that the split‑sample matrix concentrates sufficiently so that the selected columns satisfy Definition 2 with high probability, yielding an explicit finite‑sample bound.
- **Open Question 3:** How does heterogeneity in client sample sizes ($n_i$ varying) impact the existence and size of admissible subpopulations? Throughout the theory, $n_i = n$ is assumed. It remains unresolved because the condition $\kappa(\sum \alpha_i \alpha_i^\top) = \Theta(1)$ may no longer hold or may require a larger subset when sample sizes differ; the scaling of subset size with $n_i$ is unclear. Evidence for resolution: generalize the analysis to a weighted aggregation $\sum n_i \alpha_i \alpha_i^\top$ and derive necessary/sufficient conditions on the $n_i$ distribution.

## Limitations
- **Rate ambiguity:** The paper states the minimax rate as $O(d/(N\lambda_k))$ in the abstract, while the detailed analysis suggests $O(\sqrt{d/(N\lambda_k)})$. The correct expression is unclear and may reflect a difference between error and squared‑error metrics.
- **Assumption sensitivity:** The existence of an admissible subpopulation relies on strong assumptions: bounded condition number of the diversity matrix, $\|A\|_2 \lesssim M/k$, and sufficiently large stable rank. Violations (e.g., highly correlated $\alpha_i$) can cause the algorithm to fail (empty set, poorly conditioned subset).
- **Algorithmic uncertainty:** The empirical algorithm (Algorithm 2) replaces unknown $A$ with a split‑sample proxy $\tilde{A}$, which lacks finite‑sample guarantees. Moreover, the constant $c^*$ is not explicitly provided; solving $320(c^*+\sqrt{2c^*}) \le 0.5$ yields a very small value ($\approx 0.000577$) that may lead to $s=1$ in practice, undermining the Grothendieck factorization.
