# CLEF HIPE-2026: Evaluating Accurate and Efficient Person-Place Relation Extraction from Multilingual Historical Texts
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.17663
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17663
- Reference count: 38
- Primary result: Pilot study shows GPT-4o achieves up to 0.8 agreement on *at* relation; quadratic candidate pair processing is a key scalability bottleneck.

## Executive Summary
The CLEF HIPE-2026 shared task focuses on extracting person-place relations from multilingual historical texts, specifically distinguishing between two temporal scopes: *at* (presence at any time before publication) and *isAt* (presence around publication time). This requires systems to perform temporal and geographical reasoning over noisy entity pairs. The evaluation comprises three profiles: accuracy (macro-Recall), accuracy-efficiency (balancing performance with computational cost), and generalization to a surprise literary domain. A pilot study demonstrated high inter-annotator agreement (Cohen’s κ 0.7–0.9 for *at*, 0.4–0.9 for *isAt*) and found that GPT-4o aligns with gold *at* labels up to 0.8, though *isAt* results were lower and more variable. The quadratic complexity of candidate pair generation was highlighted as a major scalability challenge.

## Method Summary
The task provides documents with pre-identified person and place entity mentions. Systems must consider every possible person-place pair within a document and classify each pair for both relations: *at* (true/probable/false) indicating whether the person was ever at that place before publication, and *isAt* (+/-) indicating presence around publication time. Input data includes multilingual historical newspapers (French, German, English, Luxembourgish) from the 19th–20th centuries and a surprise test set of French literary texts from the 16th–18th centuries (for *at* only). The primary metric is macro-Recall averaged over relation types and languages. An additional "accuracy-efficiency" profile considers model size and computational cost, encouraging efficient solutions. The "generalization" profile evaluates performance on the surprise literary domain. The pilot study used GPT-4o in a zero-shot setting, but the task is open to any approach, including supervised classifiers and LLMs.

## Key Results
- High inter-annotator agreement: Cohen’s κ 0.7–0.9 for *at* and 0.4–0.9 for *isAt*.
- GPT-4o achieves up to 0.8 alignment with gold *at* labels, but *isAt* results are lower and more variable (0.2–0.7).
- Quadratic computational complexity of candidate pair processing is identified as a key scalability bottleneck.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The task's design with explicitly labeled relation types (*at* and *isAt*) grounded in temporal scopes provides a clear, learnable objective for systems.
- Mechanism: By formally separating "ever been at" (unbounded past) from "located at publication time" (immediate temporal context), the task forces models to distinguish between general historical association and temporally-localized presence. This binary refinement of *at* creates a structured output space that can be targeted by classifiers or prompting.
- Core assumption: Human annotators can consistently apply these temporal distinctions to noisy text, as validated by inter-annotator agreement.

- Break condition: If the temporal distinction proves unintelligible to models (e.g., they cannot learn to separate general past association from publication-time presence), the core task formulation fails.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The three-fold evaluation profile (accuracy, efficiency, generalization) drives the development of practically viable systems for large-scale historical processing.
- Mechanism: Beyond pure accuracy (macro-Recall), the "accuracy-efficiency" profile explicitly penalizes high computational cost, encouraging innovations in candidate pruning, model distillation, or efficient architectures to address the quadratic growth of person-place pairs. The "generalization" profile with a surprise literary domain tests robustness to domain shift, preventing overfitting to the newspaper format.
- Core assumption: Practical deployment on digitized historical corpora requires balancing performance with computational feasibility and cross-domain robustness.

## Foundational Learning
**Temporal Distinction (*at* vs *isAt*)**  
Why needed: The core task requires differentiating between a person's historical association with a place (any time before publication) and their presence specifically around the publication time. This temporal reasoning is fundamental to correct classification.  
Quick check: Consider a document about a historical figure: if it says "Napoleon died in Saint Helena", does that imply *at*? What about "the author currently resides in Paris" for a newspaper article?  

**Multilingual and Noisy Historical Text**  
Why needed: The data spans multiple languages and centuries, with OCR errors, spelling variations, and archaic language. Models must be robust to such noise and handle cross-lingual patterns.  
Quick check: What challenges arise when processing 19th-century German newspapers? How might spelling variations affect named entity recognition?  

**Quadratic Candidate Pair Complexity**  
Why needed: For a document with N person mentions and M place mentions, there are N×M pairs to classify. This quadratically growing cost makes naive processing infeasible for long documents.  
Quick check: If a document contains 50 persons and 100 places, how many pairs must be evaluated? What strategies can reduce this number without losing recall?  

**Multi-Profile Evaluation (Accuracy, Efficiency, Generalization)**  
Why needed: Real-world applications require not only high accuracy but also computational efficiency and robustness to new domains. The three profiles ensure systems are practically viable.  
Quick check: How would you measure "efficiency" in this task? Why is a surprise literary domain used for generalization?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**:  
Document -> Entity Mentions -> Candidate Pair Generator -> Context Extractor -> Relation Classifier -> Predictions

**Critical Path**: The system must correctly generate all person-place pairs, extract relevant context (including temporal cues), and apply a classifier that respects the temporal scopes. Any failure in pair completeness, context representation, or temporal reasoning will degrade metrics.

**Design Tradeoffs**:  
- Accuracy vs. Efficiency: Using large LLMs improves accuracy but increases computational cost; pruning candidate pairs improves efficiency but risks missing true relations.  
- Multilingual vs. Per-language Models: A single multilingual model simplifies deployment but may underperform on low-resource languages; separate models allow specialization but increase maintenance.  
- Heuristic vs. Learned: Hand-crafted rules for temporal reasoning are interpretable but brittle; learned models adapt to noise but require training data.

**Failure Signatures**:  
- Very low recall on *isAt* relative to *at*, indicating poor handling of fine-grained temporal windows.  
- High variance across languages, suggesting inadequate multilingual modeling.  
- Exponential runtime growth with document length, pointing to unpruned candidate pairs.

**First Experiments**:  
1. **Heuristic Baseline**: For each pair, if the person and place appear within the same sentence and the document date is within a small window of any explicit time expression, predict *isAt* true; otherwise predict *at* true. This establishes a simple rule-based benchmark.
2. **Supervised Small Transformer**: Fine-tune a lightweight multilingual model (e.g., XLM-RoBERTa base) on the development set using a concatenation of the two entity mentions and surrounding text as input. Report macro-Recall and inference time per document.
3. **Candidate Pruning Impact**: Implement a geographic filter: only consider pairs where the place is among the top-K most frequently mentioned in the document, or where the person and place co-occur within a window of 50 tokens. Measure the tradeoff between recall and number of pairs processed.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The paper notes that GPT-4o shows significantly lower and more variable agreement on the *isAt* relation (0.2–0.7) compared to *at* (up to 0.8), but does not analyze why. The underlying reason—whether due to the stricter temporal window, the need for subtle contextual cues, or model limitations—remains unresolved. An error analysis comparing model failures on the two relation types, especially probing sensitivity to fine-grained temporal expressions and evidential thresholds, would help clarify.

## Limitations
- Baseline evaluation is incomplete: only GPT-4o was tested on a subset; performance of other model families is unknown.
- Scalability solutions are unproven: the quadratic complexity is identified but no concrete pruning or efficient architecture has been validated.
- Generalization to the surprise literary domain is prospective: no empirical results exist yet; systems may fail on the domain shift.
- Multilingual consistency details are sparse: cross-lingual annotation agreement and model performance per language are not thoroughly reported.

## Confidence
- Task design & annotation validity: High (supported by strong inter-annotator agreement).
- Efficiency challenge & need: Medium (quadratic complexity is theoretically sound but real-world impact not quantified).
- Generalization to surprise domain: Low (entirely prospective, no data or results yet).
