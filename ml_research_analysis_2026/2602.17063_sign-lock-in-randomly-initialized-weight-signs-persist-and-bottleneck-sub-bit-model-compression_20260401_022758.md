# Sign Lock-In: Randomly Initialized Weight Signs Persist and Bottleneck Sub-Bit Model Compression

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.17063  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17063  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Randomly initialized weight signs persist through training, creating a bottleneck for sub-bit compression; gap initialization and outer-drift regularization achieve a flip rate of ~10⁻³ with only ~1 point perplexity increase, enabling sign-template compression to outperform direct SVD.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates sign lock-in in deep neural networks, where the signs of weights (positive/negative) are largely determined at random initialization and resist change during training. This persistence creates a bottleneck for sub-bit model compression, as the sign bit consumes storage but is difficult to compress due to its apparent randomness (spectrally indistinguishable from Rademacher noise). The authors develop a theoretical framework based on stopping-time analysis, showing that under bounded updates, the number of effective sign flips follows a geometric tail with a rare re-entry condition. Guided by this, they propose two simple interventions: gap initialization (ensuring minimum initial magnitude) and outer-drift regularization (log-barrier penalty to keep weights away from zero). These reduce the flip rate to approximately 10⁻³ with minimal impact on task performance (~1 point perplexity increase). For compression, they use a fixed low-rank sign template and compress only magnitudes via truncated SVD and quantization, achieving superior performance in the sub-bit regime compared to direct SVD on raw weights. Empirical results across Transformers, CNNs, and MLPs confirm the theory, and large-scale experiments indicate that lock-in strengthens with model size.

## Method Summary
The core methodology revolves around separating weight matrices into sign and magnitude components: \(W = S \odot A\), where \(S = \text{sign}(W)\) and \(A = |W|\). To study sign dynamics, the authors define an “outer” region (\(|W| \ge \rho = 10^{-3}\)) and a boundary neighborhood (\(|W| \le \epsilon = 10^{-4}\)); they track sign flips using stopping times when weights cross the boundary. Their theoretical analysis models each weight as an independent stochastic process under bounded gradient updates, proving that the effective number of flips follows a geometric distribution with a rare re-entry condition. To suppress flips, they introduce gap initialization (sampling from a two-sided truncated Gaussian with minimum magnitude \(a_{\text{init}}\)) and outer-drift regularization (log-barrier penalty \(\mathcal{R}_{\text{LB}}\) applied during an initial warmup and then decayed). For sub-bit compression, they generate a low-rank sign template \(T = \text{sign}(GH^\top)\) from a fixed random seed, initialize weights as \(W = T \odot |Z|\) with \(Z\) from gap initialization, and after each training step enforce the template via hard projection \(W \leftarrow T \odot |W|\). Magnitudes are compressed using truncated SVD and 4-bit symmetric uniform quantization; the sign bits are stored implicitly by the template (zero cost in \(bpw_{\text{eff}}\)). The method is evaluated on language modeling (CharLM, Text8-Char), classification (DBPedia14), and vision tasks, with baselines including direct SVD, HashedNets, OneBit, pruning, and quantization-aware training.

## Key Results
- Signs are highly persistent: most signs never flip after initialization, and sign matrices are spectrally indistinguishable from i.i.d. Rademacher noise, yet they resist low-rank compression.
- The number of effective sign flips follows a geometric tail under bounded updates; interventions (gap init + outer-drift) reduce flip rate to ~10⁻³ with only ~1 point perplexity increase.
- Using a fixed low-rank sign template with magnitude-only SVD+quantization consistently outperforms direct SVD on raw weights in the sub-bit compression regime, especially as model size increases.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Sign lock-in emerges because gradient updates are typically bounded and weights tend to move away from zero after initialization; once a weight's magnitude exceeds a threshold, the probability of crossing zero (and flipping sign) becomes exponentially small. The theoretical analysis formalizes this via stopping-time arguments, showing that the effective number of flips \(K_{\text{eff}}\) has a geometric tail: \(P(K_{\text{eff}} > k) \le (1-p)^k\) for some \(p>0\), under a rare re-entry condition (weights rarely re-enter the boundary region after exiting). The interventions enhance this natural tendency: gap initialization starts weights already outside the boundary (large initial magnitudes), and outer-drift regularization adds a penalty that pushes magnitudes further from zero during early training, making re-entries even rarer. Consequently, sign flips are suppressed to near-zero rates without harming optimization, because the magnitudes can still adapt. For compression, the sign template exploits the fact that signs are essentially random and persistent; by fixing them to a low-rank pattern, the sign bits become highly compressible (stored as a small seed). Since magnitudes are the dominant source of information, compressing only magnitudes via low-rank SVD and quantization yields significant savings while preserving accuracy.

## Foundational Learning
- **Sign-Magnitude Decomposition**  
  *Why needed:* Allows independent treatment of signs and magnitudes; signs can be locked and templated, magnitudes compressed.  
  *Quick check:* For a weight matrix \(W\), verify \(W = S \odot A\) element-wise; try reconstructing \(W\) from its sign and absolute value.
- **Low-Rank Approximation (SVD)**  
  *Why needed:* Captures the dominant spectral components of weight matrices, enabling storage reduction.  
  *Quick check:* Compute the SVD of a random matrix, reconstruct with top‑\(r\) singular values, and measure Frobenius error vs rank.
- **Sub-Bit Quantization**  
  *Why needed:* Achieves extreme compression by exploiting redundancy in sign bits.  
  *Quick check:* Calculate effective bits per weight (\(bpw_{\text{eff}}\)) when signs are stored via a template (0 bits) and magnitudes are quantized to \(b\) bits per element.
- **Stopping-Time Analysis**  
  *Why needed:* Provides theoretical guarantee that flips are rare under bounded updates.  
  *Quick check:* Simulate a simple random walk with absorbing boundaries and estimate distribution of number of boundary crossings before absorption.
- **Log-Barrier Regularization**  
  *Why needed:* Encourages weights to stay in the outer region, reducing sign flips.  
  *Quick check:* Minimize a simple quadratic with a log-barrier constraint; observe that solution stays away from boundary.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Gap Initialization → Outer-Drift Regularization → Hard Sign Projection (template enforcement) → Magnitude Compression (SVD+quantization) → Reconstruction (template × compressed magnitudes) → Evaluation.

**Critical path:**  
1. Train a model with gap initialization and outer‑drift regularization to reduce sign flips.  
2. Use a fixed low‑rank sign template (generated from a seed) and enforce it via hard projection throughout training.  
3. After training, compress magnitudes using truncated SVD and quantization; reconstruct the model using the template.  
4. Compare compression‑adjusted performance (bpw_eff vs perplexity/accuracy) against baselines.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Gap initialization* (\(a_{\text{init}}\)): Larger values suppress flips more but may hinder optimization.  
- *Outer‑drift weight* (\(\lambda\)): Higher \(\lambda\) further reduces flips but risks over‑constraining magnitudes.  
- *Template rank* (\(r\)): Lower \(r\) increases compressibility but may sacrifice expressivity if signs are not truly low‑rank.  
- *Quantization bits*: More bits preserve magnitude fidelity but increase \(bpw_{\text{eff}}\).  
- Goal: minimal performance degradation (≈1 point) for high compression.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High flip rate (\(K_{\text{eff}}\)): gap init/outer‑drift ineffective.  
- Large performance drop (>1 point): over‑regularization or excessive \(a_{\text{init}}\).  
- Template compression underperforms direct SVD: insufficient sign lock‑in or template rank too low.  
- Inconsistent results across seeds: non‑deterministic template seeding or unstable optimization.

**Exactly 3 first experiments:**  
1. **Baseline sign lock‑in:** Train CharLM on Tiny Shakespeare; track flips and compute low‑rank compressibility of sign vs magnitude matrices; verify spectral similarity to Rademacher.  
2. **Flip suppression:** Apply gap init (sweep \(a_{\text{init}}\)) and outer‑drift (sweep \(\lambda\)) to CharLM; measure \(K_{\text{eff}}\) and validation perplexity; fit zero‑inflated geometric model.  
3. **Template compression:** For CharLM/Text8‑Char/DBPedia14, train with gap init and outer‑drift (chosen from Exp2), using a fixed low‑rank sign template (\(r=2\), fixed seed) and hard projection; compress magnitudes via SVD+4‑bit quantization; compare \(bpw_{\text{eff}}\) vs performance against direct SVD and other baselines.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Alternative sign‑flip interventions:** The paper only explores gap initialization and outer‑drift; other strategies (e.g., adaptive regularizers, architectural constraints) remain unexplored.
- **Representational impact of fixed signs:** Constraining signs to a low‑rank template may limit model expressivity; the paper does not analyze the function classes realizable under such constraints.
- **Sign floating mode:** Under extremely strong magnitude regularization, signs can enter a floating regime with frequent boundary re‑entries and random sign changes; its natural occurrence and potential exploitation are unknown.
- **Sign correlations:** The theory assumes independent weight signs; real networks may have correlations that could be exploited for further compression or might invalidate the independent stopping‑time analysis.

## Limitations
- Incomplete hyperparameters for billion‑scale sweeps (e.g., optimizer betas, gradient clipping) hinder full replication.
- Template seeding algorithm is not fully specified, risking non‑deterministic reconstruction across implementations.
- Validation limited to Transformer/CNN/MLP models with AdamW; generalizability to other architectures or optimizers is unclear.
