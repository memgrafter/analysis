# Same Claim, Different Judgment: Benchmarking Scenario-Induced Bias in Multilingual Financial Misinformation Detection

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.05403
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05403
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Significant, systematic behavioral biases across all 22 evaluated LLMs in multilingual financial misinformation detection, with biases amplified in low-resource languages and larger models showing smaller magnitudes.

## Executive Summary
This research introduces MFMD-Scen, a benchmark designed to evaluate scenario-induced behavioral biases in large language models (LLMs) for multilingual financial misinformation detection. The benchmark integrates persona, region, and identity scenarios with a curated dataset across English, Chinese, Greek, and Bengali. By comparing model judgments under scenario-augmented prompts versus neutral baselines, the study reveals that contextual scenario information consistently overrides claim-level signals, leading to unstable truth assessments for the same misinformation claim. Biases are most pronounced for retail-investor roles, herding personality cues, and emerging Asian market contexts, and are further amplified in low-resource languages (Greek, Bengali). Larger models exhibit reduced bias magnitudes, highlighting the influence of model scale on robustness.

## Method Summary
The study conducts a pure evaluation of 22 LLMs using the MFMD-Scen benchmark, which combines a multilingual financial misinformation dataset (502 claims across four languages) with three scenario types (persona, region, identity). For each claim, models are queried twice: once with a scenario-augmented prompt and once with a neutral baseline prompt. The primary bias metric is the absolute F1-score difference (`Biasscen = |F1_scenario - F1_base|`), computed per model, per scenario subtype, and per language. Aggregation across models yields mean direction (AM) and mean absolute magnitude (MAV). No model training is performed; the focus is on measuring existing behavioral biases under controlled contextual priming.

## Key Results
- All 22 LLMs exhibit significant, systematic scenario-induced biases, causing the same claim to receive different truth judgments across simulated stakeholder and market contexts.
- Bias magnitudes are largest for retail-investor personas, herding personality cues, and scenarios set in emerging Asian markets.
- Effects are amplified in low-resource languages (Greek, Bengali), while larger models show systematically smaller bias magnitudes.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Scenario Context Overrides Claim Signals
Scenario descriptors (e.g., "retail investor," "emerging Asian market") act as high‑salience credibility priors that shift the effective decision boundary. LLMs do not evaluate claims in isolation but through the lens of provided role, region, or identity cues, triggering learned stereotypical associations from training data (e.g., retail investors = less sophisticated → more skepticism). Evidence shows consistent F1‑score shifts across all models and non‑zero biases, with related work on anchoring bias supporting contextual overriding effects.

### Mechanism 2: Linguistic Resource Scarcity Amplifies Bias
In low‑resource languages (Greek, Bengali), weaker linguistic signals and poorer domain calibration cause models to rely more heavily on contextual scenario heuristics. The claim‑level signal is noisier, so the model falls back on stronger learned scenario‑prior associations. Evidence includes a clear gradient in bias magnitude across languages (BN/EL > ZH > EN) and correlation with resource levels, consistent with findings on language‑based performance disparities.

### Mechanism 3: Intersectional Identity‑Role Interactions
Bias arises from non‑additive interactions between identity cues (ethnicity/faith) and role, not independent main effects. The same identity group can show reversed bias direction under different roles (e.g., "Chinese" identity paired with "retail investor" yields skepticism, but with "company owner" may reduce or reverse it). This suggests LLMs encode intersectional stereotypes activated only when multiple contextual dimensions combine. Evidence includes role‑dependent reversals for ethnic/religious groups and support from studies on complex social bias scenarios in LLMs.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Scenario Generator -> Multilingual Claim Database -> LLM Inference Engine -> Bias Quantifier -> Aggregation & Visualization
- **Critical path:** Select claim (gold label) -> Generate scenario variant -> Construct prompt (scenario + claim or claim only) -> Query LLM -> Parse prediction -> Compute F1 per condition -> Calculate `Biasscen` per model -> Aggregate across models/items.
- **Design tradeoffs:** Scenario complexity vs. generalizability (specific prompts trigger strong but narrow priors); translation faithfulness vs. naturalness (post‑edited MT balances cost/quality); metric choice (F1‑difference may miss systematic consistent errors).
- **Failure signatures:** Scenario insensitivity (near‑zero MAV); random effects (mixed sign bias across models); language confound (translation quality driving effects); data imbalance (false‑class dominance inflating metrics).
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Baseline Stability Check: Run all 22 models on base dataset; verify reasonable F1 scores (>0.8 on false, ~0.5–0.7 on true).
  2. Single‑Axis Bias Isolation: Test MFMD‑persona on English claims; compute MAV per role/personality; expect Retail+Herding yields largest positive MAV.
  3. Cross‑Lingual Amplification: Compare same persona scenario (e.g., Retail+Herding) across all four languages; expect MAV(BN/EL) > MAV(ZH) > MAV(EN).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Balanced dataset construction:** The paper notes an imbalance (majority false claims) due to Snopes sourcing. A balanced multilingual dataset with comparable true/false claims, validated by experts, would improve bias evaluation robustness.
2. **Human annotator reliability:** Only two annotators were available for some regions due to resource constraints. Increasing annotators per region (e.g., 5–10) and measuring inter‑annotator agreement would yield more representative gold standards.
3. **Bias mitigation techniques:** The paper evaluates existing models but tests no debiasing methods (e.g., prompt engineering, fine‑tuning). Concrete mitigation strategies must be developed and measured via reduced `Biasscen`.
4. **Mechanisms for amplification in low‑resource languages:** The correlation between resource scarcity and larger bias is observed but not causally explained. Controlled experiments comparing models trained on varying low‑resource data or probing internal representations are needed.

## Limitations
- **Translation fidelity:** Post‑edited GPT translations may introduce subtle language‑specific artifacts that confound resource scarcity effects.
- **Scenario template coverage:** Predefined prompts cannot exhaust real‑world variability; unmeasured contextual cues might drive observed biases.
- **Metric blind spots:** F1‑difference may miss systematic consistent errors (e.g., always wrong under a scenario), only capturing instability relative to baseline.

## Confidence
- **Scenario context overrides claim signals:** High (robust across 22 models, multiple axes, significance testing).
- **Linguistic resource scarcity amplifies bias:** Medium (clear gradient across languages but cannot fully disentangle data scarcity from script/typological factors).
- **Intersectional identity‑role interactions:** Medium (statistically detected interactions but template design limits ecological validity).

## Next Checks
1. **Translation ablation:** Replicate key multilingual comparisons using human‑only translations (no MT) to isolate language resource effects from translation noise.
2. **Scenario boundary test:** Add neutral/control persona/region prompts (e.g., "analyst" instead of "retail investor") to verify bias magnitude diminishes toward baseline, confirming non‑linear priming.
3. **Consistency audit:** Compute prediction consistency (same label across base/scenario) per claim, not just F1‑shift, to detect whether bias produces systematic misclassification versus random vacillation.
