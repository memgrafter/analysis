# What Makes a Good Example? Modeling Exemplar Selection with Neural Network Representations

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.03144
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03144
- Reference count: 24
- Primary result: Human exemplar selection for teaching is best captured by a joint representativeness objective, with transformer-based ViT representations aligning more closely than ResNet.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates the computational principles underlying how adults select examples to teach a novel visual category. Using three synthetic categories (dax, vep, bem) defined along a morph continuum, participants chose one to three exemplars to convey the category structure. The authors modeled these choices with subset selection criteria derived from features of pretrained vision models (ResNet-50 and ViT-B/16). The criteria—prototypicality (typicality), representativity (coverage of the full distribution), diversity (mutual dissimilarity), and their combination—were evaluated by comparing model-predicted scores to human selections via mean absolute error. The results indicate that the representativity criterion, alone or combined with diversity, most closely matches human behavior. Notably, ViT-based representations yielded consistently lower alignment errors than ResNet, suggesting that holistic, attention-based visual representations better capture human pedagogical intuitions.

## Method Summary
The study employed three novel visual categories, each consisting of a set of images varying continuously along a single morphological scale. Adult participants (N=24) were asked to select 1, 2, or 3 exemplars that would best teach the category. Feature vectors were extracted from the penultimate layer of pretrained ResNet-50 and ViT-B/16 models and normalized to unit length. Four subset selection criteria were implemented: (1) *Prototypicality*: pick the most central items by average similarity to all category members; (2) *Representativity*: maximize the facility location function, i.e., sum over all items of their maximum similarity to the selected subset; (3) *Diversity*: maximize total pairwise distance within the subset; (4) *Combined*: sum of representativity and diversity. For each category and quota, the optimal subset under each criterion was identified (exhaustively for small N). The chosen subsets were then converted into two scalar scores—prototypicality (average absolute scale distance from the midpoint) and diversity (maximum scale distance among selected exemplars)—and compared to the corresponding human-derived scores using mean absolute error (MAE). This allowed a quantitative assessment of how well each computational model captured human exemplar preferences.

## Key Results
- Representativity (joint representativeness) alone, or combined with diversity, best captured human exemplar selections across quotas, outperforming pure prototypicality or diversity strategies.
- Transformer-based ViT representations consistently achieved lower MAE than CNN-based ResNet, indicating better alignment with human similarity judgments.
- For ViT, the combined criterion produced the lowest errors: prototypicality MAE = 0.066 and diversity MAE = 0.062.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Joint Representativeness Optimizes Coverage
*Claim:* Human exemplar selection for teaching is best captured by a joint representativeness objective, which chooses a subset that collectively covers the entire category distribution.  
*Mechanism:* The facility location criterion (sum of max similarities of each data point to the selected subset) is maximized. This implicitly rewards subsets that include points summarizing different regions, discouraging redundancy.  
*Core assumption:* Teachers intuitively aim to provide a condensed summary of the category rather than merely pick the most typical or most extreme examples.  
*Evidence anchors:* The representativity criterion alone achieved low errors in both prototypicality and diversity scores, and was competitive with the combined approach.  
*Break condition:* If participants consistently selected only the single most central or most extreme exemplars regardless of quota, the representativity model would fail, as it tends to choose intermediate points for coverage.

### Mechanism 2: Hybrid Objective Balances Summarization and Breadth
*Claim:* Adding an explicit diversity term to the representativity objective further improves alignment with human judgments, especially under ViT representations.  
*Mechanism:* The combined linear objective adds a pairwise diversity component (sum of cosine distances within the subset) to the representativity score, encouraging the selection of stimuli that both cover the distribution and are mutually dissimilar.  
*Core assumption:* Human teachers simultaneously optimize multiple pedagogical goals—summarizing the category while highlighting its breadth—and the trade-off may depend on the number of examples allowed.  
*Evidence anchors:* For ViT, the combined criterion yielded the lowest MAE for both prototypicality (0.066) and diversity (0.062), outperforming representativity alone.  
*Break condition:* If human diversity scores were high and completely uncorrelated with representativeness scores across quotas, the fixed linear combination would be insufficient, suggesting a more complex or nonlinear trade-off.

### Mechanism 3: Transformer Representations Reflect Human Similarity Judgments
*Claim:* Transformer-based vision representations (ViT) align more closely with human exemplar selection than convolutional ones (ResNet).  
*Mechanism:* ViT's global self-attention produces a representational geometry where similarity relations along the morph continuum more closely match human intuitions about typicality and diversity.  
*Core assumption:* Human category representations are holistic and relation-focused, which aligns with the attention mechanism's ability to integrate information globally.  
*Evidence anchors:* Across all criteria and quotas, ViT-based models achieved lower MAE relative to human judgments than ResNet-based models.  
*Break condition:* If a broader set of architectures (e.g., various CNNs, other transformers) eliminated the consistent ViT advantage, the claim would reduce to a model-specific artifact rather than a general representational principle.

## Foundational Learning
- **Facility Location (Submodular Maximization)**  
  *Why needed:* It formalizes the joint representativeness objective as a monotone submodular function, guaranteeing near-optimality via greedy selection and providing a theoretical foundation for coverage-based subset selection.  
  *Quick check:* Why does maximizing the sum of max similarities discourage selecting two very similar exemplars? Because if two exemplars are nearly identical, their contribution to the sum of max similarities for other points is redundant, reducing the total compared to picking more distinct points that collectively cover more of the distribution.

- **Feature Space Cosine Similarity**  
  *Why needed:* All selection criteria (prototypicality, representativity, diversity) are defined in terms of pairwise cosine similarities between normalized feature vectors, making similarity the core metric for "representative" and "diverse".  
  *Quick check:* If features are not normalized, how would the interpretation of the representativity objective change? Unnormalized features scale with vector magnitude; cosine similarity would mix angular and magnitude information, potentially conflating semantic similarity with feature intensity, and the facility location function could be dominated by high-magnitude features rather than angular closeness.

- **Model-Human Alignment via Score MAE**  
  *Why needed:* The evaluation compares continuous prototypicality and diversity scores derived from model selections to human-derived scores, quantifying fit without requiring exact exemplar matches.  
  *Quick check:* Why compare scores rather than exact selected exemplars? Because multiple subsets can produce the same score (e.g., different central exemplars), and human judgments exhibit variability; comparing continuous scores captures the underlying preference distribution more sensitively than a discrete match metric.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Stimulus Generator → Feature Extractor → Selection Engine → Scorer → Comparator
- **Critical path**: The stimulus images are processed by the feature extractor (ResNet or ViT), producing normalized feature vectors. The selection engine then applies one of the four criteria to choose an optimal subset of size M. The scorer computes prototypicality and diversity scores for that subset. Finally, the comparator calculates MAE against human scores.

- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Representativity vs. Diversity: Pure representativity risks clustering central points; pure diversity tends to pick extremes; the combined objective seeks a balance that may shift with quota M.
  - Architecture choice: ViT yields better human alignment but is more computationally expensive than ResNet; the trade-off involves biological plausibility versus engineering efficiency.
  - Quota sensitivity: The optimal weighting of diversity in the combined criterion likely varies with M (e.g., more weight for diversity when M is larger), but the paper uses a fixed equal weight.

- **Failure signatures**:
  - High MAE for diversity score under the diversity criterion indicates that humans do not push mutual distance to the maximum possible; they prefer a moderate spread.
  - High MAE for prototypicality score under the prototypicality criterion for M>1 indicates that humans do not simply select multiple most typical items; they incorporate diversity considerations.
  - Consistently higher errors for ResNet across all criteria suggest a fundamental representational mismatch with human similarity judgments.

- **First three experiments**:
  1. **Reproduce Table 1**: Extract the scale values of the exemplars selected by each model-criterion for quotas 1–3 and verify that representativity picks near-midpoints for M=1 and mixes central and peripheral points for M=2,3.
  2. **Replicate Figure 4**: Compute MAE between model-derived scores and human mean scores across categories; confirm that representativity (and combined for ViT) achieve the lowest errors.
  3. **Conduct Fine-Grained Quota Analysis**: Calculate MAE separately for each quota condition (1, 2, 3) and test whether representativity/combined show particularly low errors in the M=2 and M=3 conditions, where human diversity behavior is informative.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **What specific representational properties of transformer architectures (e.g., ViT) cause them to align more closely with human exemplar selection judgments compared to convolutional networks (e.g., ResNet)?**  
   *Basis in paper*: Results consistently show ViT models achieve lower mean absolute error than ResNet models. The discussion suggests global self-attention may better capture human similarity relations, but the precise underlying geometry is unclear.  
   *Why unresolved*: Only two architectures were compared; the responsible inductive bias (e.g., locality, attention patterns) remains unidentified.  
   *Resolving evidence*: Systematic study across a wide range of architectures (varying attention scope, convolutional depth, patch size) with analyses linking specific representational geometric properties (e.g., manifold smoothness, centroid bias) to human selection patterns.

2. **Can a more interpretable, lower-dimensional representational model (e.g., a linear psychological space or a small set of semantic features) capture human exemplar selection behavior as well as or better than deep neural network features?**  
   *Basis in paper*: The discussion notes neural network representations are complex and difficult to interpret directly; developing smaller or more interpretable models is of interest.  
   *Why unresolved*: The current approach uses black-box features; it does not reveal which perceptual or semantic dimensions humans rely on.  
   *Resolving evidence*: Construct continua from a small set of perceptually meaningful dimensions; derive a psychological feature space from human similarity judgments and exemplar choices; compare its predictive power to deep network models.

3. **How do individual differences in human exemplar selection strategies (e.g., "prototype-focused" vs. "diversity-focused" learners) relate to the computational models, and what factors (e.g., cognitive style, working memory) predict these differences?**  
   *Basis in paper*: The human results show heterogeneity, especially in the 1-exemplar condition; the paper suggests subsequent work might model individual learners.  
   *Why unresolved*: The analysis collapses across participants, masking potential subgroups; group-level model fit does not indicate whether the same criterion applies to all.  
   *Resolving evidence*: Cluster participants based on their exemplar choices to identify distinct strategy profiles; fit each computational model (or its parameters) to individuals; correlate best-fitting models/weights with independent cognitive measures (e.g., working memory, need for closure).

4. **At what developmental stage does the adult-like balance between representativeness and diversity in teaching emerge, and how does this trajectory relate to the development of the underlying representational system?**  
   *Basis in paper*: The discussion cites prior work showing younger children prioritize typicality over diversity and proposes examining how such strategies emerge over development.  
   *Why unresolved*: The current study only tests adults; it is unknown whether the shift in children is due to changes in representations, optimization criteria, or implementation ability.  
   *Resolving evidence*: Replicate the paradigm with children of various ages (e.g., 4, 6, 8, 10 years). Use age-appropriate neural network correlates or derive representations from children’s own perceptual data. Compare the best-fitting objective (prototypicality, representativity, combined) across age groups; test mediation by representation quality.

5. **Can synthetic exemplars generated via modern dataset distillation techniques (optimized for machine learners) effectively teach human learners a novel visual category, and how does their efficacy compare to human-selected or prototypical/distributed real exemplars?**  
   *Basis in paper*: The final discussion paragraph explicitly proposes testing whether synthetic exemplars optimized to convey category structure could serve as effective teaching tools for humans.  
   *Why unresolved*: The paper uses distillation only as a computational model, not as a generator of actual teaching stimuli; no learning outcomes are measured.  
   *Resolving evidence*: Human participants are randomly assigned to learn a novel category from (1) human-chosen exemplars, (2) model-optimized exemplars (using ViT-based representativity/diversity), (3) synthetic images generated by dataset distillation to match the distribution, and (4) random sets. Learning is assessed via classification accuracy, generalization, and reaction times to compare efficacy.

## Limitations
- The study uses a small set of three synthetic categories, and the exact stimulus images and raw human response data are not publicly available, limiting reproducibility and generalizability.
- Key methodological details (normalization constants, exact subset optimization method) are ambiguously described, creating uncertainty in exact replication.
- The combined objective employs an arbitrary equal weight, and the comparison is restricted to ResNet-50 and ViT-B/16, preventing conclusions about the optimal balance or architectural generality.

## Confidence
- **Representativity as best single criterion**: Medium (results are internally consistent but may not generalize beyond the specific morph continua).
- **Combining representativity and diversity**: Medium-Low (the observed improvement may be sensitive to the fixed equal weighting; no weight tuning was performed).
- **ViT superiority over ResNet**: Low (only one CNN and one transformer were tested; differences could be due to pretraining data or model size rather than architectural inductive biases).
