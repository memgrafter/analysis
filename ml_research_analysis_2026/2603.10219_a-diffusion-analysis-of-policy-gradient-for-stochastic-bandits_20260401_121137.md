# A Diffusion Analysis of Policy Gradient for Stochastic Bandits

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.10219
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10219
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** With η = O(Δ_min² / log n), expected regret is O(k log k log n / η); for k>2, a construction shows linear regret Ω(n Δ_min²) unless η = O(Δ_min²).

## Executive Summary
The paper studies the policy gradient algorithm for k-armed stochastic bandits by means of a continuous-time diffusion approximation. Discrete policy updates are replaced by a stochastic differential equation driven by Brownian motion, enabling the use of Itô calculus and other SDE tools to analyze expected regret. Under a learning rate η = O(Δ_min² / log n), the resulting expected regret bound is O(k log k log n / η). Complementing this upper bound, a lower bound construction demonstrates that when k > 2 and the learning rate is not scaled properly (η = Ω(Δ_min²)), there exists a bandit instance (with k = Θ(log n) arms) that incurs linear regret Ω(n Δ_min²). This reveals an essential distinction between the two-armed and multi-armed settings: the dynamics of policy gradient become unstable for more than two arms unless the step size is sufficiently small relative to the smallest reward gap.

## Method Summary
The method revolves around a continuous-time diffusion approximation for policy gradient in stochastic bandits. The discrete algorithm (Algorithm 1) updates the logit vector θ_t via θ_{t+1} = θ_t + η (e_{A_t} - π_t) Y_t, where π_t is the softmax policy. The continuous counterpart (Algorithm 2) replaces these discrete steps with the SDE dθ_t = η (I - π_t 1^T) dX_t, where dX_t = diag(π_t) μ dt + diag(√π_t) Σ^{1/2} dB_t models the cumulative rewards under a k-dimensional Brownian motion B_t. This SDE is the primary object of theoretical analysis. Regret is decomposed as the integral of the instantaneous gap times the probability of not choosing the optimal arm. The upper bound (Theorem 6) constructs a Lyapunov-like function ψ(θ_{t,1}) that grows when π_{t,1} is small, and using Itô’s formula and concentration inequalities, shows that under η ≤ c Δ_min² / log n, the quantity ∫_0^n R_t dt is bounded by O(k log k log n / η) in expectation. The lower bound (Theorem 10) builds a specific bandit instance with μ = (0, Δ_2, 1, …, 1) and Σ = diag(1,1,0,…,0) and k = Θ(log n) arms; for η = Ω(Δ_min²), the continuous process (and its discrete counterpart) suffers linear regret. Together these results highlight that for k>2 the learning rate must be O(Δ_min²) to avoid catastrophic failure.

## Key Results
- **Upper bound:** If the learning rate satisfies η ≤ c Δ_min² / log n, then the expected regret after n rounds is O(k log k log n / η).
- **Lower bound:** There is a bandit instance with k = Ω(log(n/Δ_min²)) arms such that for any η = Ω(Δ_min²), the expected regret is Ω(n Δ_min²).
- **Fundamental difference:** For k=2, policy gradient remains stable even with larger η; for k>2, the dynamics may collapse onto a suboptimal arm unless η scales with Δ_min².

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Replacing discrete policy gradient updates with a continuous-time stochastic differential equation (SDE) driven by Brownian motion simplifies the analysis of regret.
- **Mechanism:** The discrete random action selections and reward noise are approximated by a continuous diffusion process. The policy parameters \(\theta_t\) evolve according to an SDE where the drift matches the expected policy gradient update and the diffusion term captures the stochasticity. This allows applying tools from SDE theory (e.g., Itô's formula, time-changes) to bound the expected regret directly, avoiding intricate discrete-time martingale arguments.
- **Core assumption:** The continuous-time process accurately approximates the discrete-time algorithm's behavior, especially for small learning rates \(\eta\). The paper states this is "believed (but not proven)" to be a high-quality approximation.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "replaces discrete updates with a stochastic differential equation driven by Brownian motion, simplifying analysis through SDE theory."
  - [section 2]: Defines Algorithm 2 (continuous-time policy gradient) as the primary object of study.
  - [corpus]: Weak direct support; neighbors focus on discrete-time analysis. The mechanism is novel to this paper.
- **Break condition:** The approximation may degrade for large \(\eta\) or in highly discrete settings, as suggested by the lower bound construction which reveals behaviors not captured by the continuous-time upper bound analysis.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The learning rate \(\eta\) must scale as \(O(\Delta_{\min}^2 / \log n)\) to achieve \(O(k \log(k) \log(n)/\eta)\) regret for \(k > 2\).
- **Mechanism:** Small \(\eta\) ensures the SDE drift for the difference \(Z_{t,a} = \theta_{t,1} - \theta_{t,a}\) (between optimal and suboptimal arms) remains positive with high probability. Lemma 8 shows that if \(\eta = O(\Delta_a^2 / \log n)\), then \(Z_{t,a}\) likely stays above \(-\Delta_a/2\), preventing the policy from collapsing onto a suboptimal arm. This maintains sufficient exploration.
- **Core assumption:** The minimum gap \(\Delta_{\min}\) governs the separation needed between arm parameters. The logarithmic term arises from concentration arguments using stopping times and Brownian motion maximal inequalities (Proposition 16).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3, Theorem 6]: Formal statement: if \(\eta \leq \Delta_{\min}^2 / (8 \log(2n^2))\), then \(\mathbb{E}[\text{Reg}_n] = O(k \log(k) \log(n)/\eta)\).
  - [section 3, Lemma 8]: Proof hinges on comparing \(Z_t\) to an SDE with positive drift when \(Z_t \geq -\Delta_a/2\), requiring \(\eta \leq c \Delta_a^2 / \log(n)\).
- **Break condition:** If \(\eta = \Omega(\Delta_{\min}^2)\) the construction in Theorem 10 shows linear regret \(\Omega(n \Delta_{\min}^2)\) can occur for \(k \geq C \log(n / \Delta_{\min}^2)\). The drift of \(Z_t\) can become negative when \(\pi_{t,1} < \pi_{t,a}\), causing premature convergence to a suboptimal arm.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** For \(k > 2\), policy gradient exhibits fundamentally different dynamics than for \(k=2\), necessitating smaller learning rates.
- **Mechanism:** With \(k=2\), the drift of \(Z_t = \theta_{t,1} - \theta_{t,2}\) is always positive (proportional to \(\pi_t(1-\pi_t)\Delta^2\)). For \(k>2\), the drift of \(Z_{t,a}\) includes a term \( (\pi_{t,1} - \pi_{t,a}) R_t \) (Lemma 7). If the policy misassigns probability (\(\pi_{t,1} < \pi_{t,a}\)) and regret \(R_t\) is large, this term is negative, potentially overwhelming the positive \(\pi_{t,a}\Delta_a\) term. The lower bound construction (Theorem 10) exploits this by designing an instance where two arms are nearly tied (\(\Delta_2 \approx 0\)) and others are very bad, causing the algorithm to effectively "choose" between arms 1 and 2 randomly early on, then commit suboptimally.
- **Core assumption:** The presence of more than two arms introduces coupling through the policy probabilities, making the drift sign non-trivial.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 4, Theorem 10]: Constructs an instance with \(k = \Theta(\log(n / \Delta_2^2))\) arms and \(\mu = (0, \Delta_2, 1, \dots, 1)\) that yields linear regret unless \(\eta = O(\Delta_2^2)\).
  - [section 3, Lemma 7]: Explicit SDE for \(dZ_{t,a}\) showing the problematic drift term.
  - [corpus]: Neighbor "A Lyapunov Analysis..." (paper_id 272509) adapts the continuous-time analysis to discrete time, reinforcing the multi-arm difficulty.
- **Break condition:** The analysis fails (regret becomes linear) if \(k > 2\) and \(\eta\) is not small enough relative to \(\Delta_{\min}^2\), especially when some gaps are very small relative to others.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Stochastic Differential Equations (SDEs) and Itô Calculus**
  - **Why needed here:** The core analysis transforms the discrete algorithm into an SDE and uses Itô's formula to derive dynamics for quantities like \(\psi(\theta_{t,1})\) (Theorem 6 proof) and \(Z_{t,a}\) (Lemma 8, Theorem 10). Understanding drift, diffusion, time-changes, and martingale properties is essential.
  - **Quick check question:** Given \(dX_t = a(X_t) dt + b(X_t) dW_t\), how would you apply Itô's formula to \(f(X_t) = \log(1 + e^{X_t})\) to understand the evolution of a softmax probability?
- **Concept: Policy Gradient for Bandits & Softmax Policy**
  - **Why needed here:** The algorithm updates \(\theta_t\) using an unbiased estimator of the gradient of \(\langle \pi(\theta), \mu \rangle\). The softmax parameterization \(\pi_a(\theta) \propto e^{\theta_a}\) and the gradient structure \((diag(\pi) - \pi\pi^\top)\mu\) are used throughout (Eq. 1, Lemma 7).
  - **Quick check question:** Why does the update \(d\theta_t = \eta (I - \pi_t 1^\top) dX_t\) correspond to a policy gradient step in continuous time?
- **Concept: Regret Decomposition and Gap \(\Delta\)**
  - **Why needed here:** Regret is decomposed as \(\text{Reg}_n = \Delta_a \int_0^n (1 - \pi_{t,a}) dt\) or summed over gaps. The minimum gap \(\Delta_{\min}\) sets the difficulty. The analysis bounds the integral of \(1 - \pi_{t,1}\) or related quantities.
  - **Quick check question:** In the two-armed proof (Proposition 4), how does the identity \(\text{Reg}_n = \Delta^2 \int (\pi_t(1-\pi_t) + (1-\pi_t)^2) dt\) lead to separate bounds using exponentials of \(Z_t\)?
- **Concept: Concentration for Brownian Motion & Martingales**
  - **Why needed here:** Bounds like Proposition 16 (infimum of an SDE with positive drift) and maximal inequalities for \(M_t = e^{\pm Z_t}\) are used to show \(Z_{t,a}\) stays above \(-\Delta_a/2\) (Lemma 8) and that \(\theta_{t,a}\) does not become too negative (Lemma 2).
  - **Quick check question:** Why is the process \(M_t = e^{-2a X_t}\) a martingale when \(dX_t = a dt + dW_t\), and how does that give \(\mathbb{P}(\inf X_t \leq -\epsilon) \leq e^{-2a\epsilon}\)?

## Architecture Onboarding
Parameters: θ_t ∈ ℝᵏ (logits, sum to zero by Lemma 1), policy π_t = softmax(θ_t). Processes: X_t (cumulative reward vector, SDE-driven), B_t (k-dim Brownian motion), R_t = μ_1 - ⟨π_t, μ⟩ (instantaneous regret). Update rule: dθ_t = η (I - π_t 1^⊤) dX_t (Algorithm 2). Key derived quantities: Z_{t,a} = θ_{t,1} - θ_{t,a}, S_t = θ_{t,1} + θ_{t,2} (for lower bound), C_t = η π_{t,3+}.

Critical path:
1. Initialize θ_0 = 0 (uniform policy).
2. Observe dX_t = diag(π_t) μ dt + diag(√π_t) Σ^{1/2} dB_t.
3. Update θ_t continuously.
4. Monitor Z_{t,a}. If any Z_{t,a} ≤ -Δ_a/2 or θ_{t,a} too negative (Lemmas 2,8), algorithm enters a "bad" regime.
5. For upper bound, construct Lyapunov-like function ψ(θ_{t,1}) that grows when π_{t,1} is small, ensuring ∫ R_t dt is bounded.

Design tradeoffs:
- η size: Small η (e.g., O(Δ_min² / log n)) stabilizes Z_{t,a} but slows learning. Large η (e.g., Ω(Δ_min²)) risks linear regret for k>2.
- Time horizon vs. arms: The upper bound's log(k) factor is mild, but the lower bound shows that for k = Θ(log(n/Δ_min²)), any η can fail. The continuous-time setting may hide discrete-time nuances.
- Variance structure: Lower bound uses Σ = diag(1,1,0,…,0) (only first two arms noisy). General Σ ⪯ I is allowed for upper bound but complicates lower bound.

Failure signatures:
- Linear regret: Occur if η = Ω(Δ_min²) and k is sufficiently large (logarithmic in n/Δ_min²). Evidenced by Theorem 10 and Fig. 1 (discrete simulation).
- Collapse to suboptimal arm: Signaled by Z_{t,a} ≪ 0 for some a ≠ 1, meaning π_{t,a} > π_{t,1}. The SDE drift for Z_{t,a} becomes negative when π_{t,1} < π_{t,a} and R_t large.
- θ_{t,a} unbounded below: Controlled by Lemma 2; if violated, π_{t,a} becomes negligible and updates stall.

First 3 experiments:
1. Validate upper bound conditions: Run Algorithm 2 with η = c Δ_min² / log n on a k=10 Gaussian bandit. Plot ∫_0^n R_t dt vs. n; should grow as O(k log k log n / η).
2. Reproduce lower breakdown: Implement the Theorem 10 instance (μ = (0, Δ_2, 1, …, 1), Σ = diag(1,1,0,…,0)) with k ≈ 4 log_2(n) and Δ_2 = 0.01. Vary η around Δ_2². For η ≥ 0.5 Δ_2², observe π_{t,1} + π_{t,2} ≈ 1 early and π_{t,1} converges to ≈ 0.5 or 0, yielding linear regret. For η ≪ Δ_2², should see slower but sublinear regret.
3. Two-armed vs. multi-armed comparison: Run identical η = Δ² for k=2 and k=5 with same Δ for suboptimal arms. For k=2, expect O(log n) regret (near-optimal). For k=5, expect much worse (likely linear unless η reduced), illustrating the phase transition.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
Open Question 1
- Question: Does the lower bound construction extend to instances with a linear number of arms, yielding Ω(k n Δ²) regret?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper states: "An interesting direction is to extend the lower bound construction to introduce a linear dependence on k." (Discussion)
- Why unresolved: The current lower bound uses only logarithmically many arms; it is unknown if the same catastrophic failure occurs when k scales linearly.
- What evidence would resolve it: Construct an explicit bandit instance with k = Θ(n) and prove that for η not scaling as Δ², the expected regret is Ω(k n Δ²).

Open Question 2
- Question: Does the lower bound in Theorem 10 hold for k=3, or does the catastrophic failure require more arms?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] "You may also wonder if the lower bound might hold with k=3. We believe the answer is no..." (Page 7)
- Why unresolved: The construction relies on many suboptimal arms to drive down π_{3+}; with only three arms the dynamics might differ. No formal proof exists.
- What evidence would resolve it: Analyze the SDE for k=3 with the same mean structure or run extensive discrete-time simulations to check for linear regret.

Open Question 3
- Question: Can the logarithmic factor in the learning rate condition η = O(Δ² / log n) in the upper bound be removed?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] "One may wonder if this logarithmic term in the denominator can be dropped. The argument in Lemma 8 is quite crude..." (Discussion)
- Why unresolved: Lemma 8 uses a coarse bound; a sharper analysis might show η = O(Δ²) suffices.
- What evidence would resolve it: Improve the probability bound in Lemma 8, perhaps via a tighter supermartingale or comparison principle, to eliminate the log(n) dependence.

Open Question 4
- Question: Does the upper bound on regret for continuous-time policy gradient carry over to the discrete-time algorithm?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] "We believe our proof technique for the upper bound is likely to translate to the discrete time setting..." (Discussion)
- Why unresolved: The continuous-time model removes discretization effects; discrete updates may introduce additional errors.
- What evidence would resolve it: Adapt the proof to discrete time by handling random time steps, e.g., using Azuma-Hoeffding, and derive the same O(k log(k) log(n)/η) bound.

## Limitations
- The diffusion approximation linking the discrete algorithm to the continuous SDE is not formally justified; its error may depend on η and n in unquantified ways.
- The derived η threshold (η = O(Δ_min² / log n)) stems from the continuous analysis and may be either loose or require extra constraints (e.g., η ≤ c/k) in practice.
- The lower bound construction uses a very specific bandit instance (some arms have zero variance, a near‑tied suboptimal arm, and k = Θ(log(n/Δ²))). It is unclear how broadly this “catastrophic failure” scenario applies.

## Confidence
- **Continuous-time upper bound:** High.
- **Discrete-time transfer:** Medium.
- **Lower-bound existence:** High.
- **Fundamental k>2 vs. k=2 dichotomy:** Medium.
