# Partial Causal Structure Learning for Valid Selective Conformal Inference under Interventions
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.02204
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02204
- Reference count: 30
- Primary result: A task-driven partial causal learning approach for selective conformal inference under interventions, with a contamination-robust coverage guarantee and empirical validation on synthetic and CRISPRi data.

## Executive Summary
Selective conformal prediction improves efficiency by restricting calibration points to those exchangeable with the test under a known invariance (e.g., non-descendants under an intervention). However, this invariance structure is often unknown. This paper proposes learning only the necessary partial structure—binary descendant indicators for intervention-target pairs—enabling valid selective inference without full causal graph recovery. Two algorithms are presented: descendant discovery via set intersections of differentially affected gene sets across perturbations, and a local invariant causal prediction method that estimates causal distances. A key theoretical contribution is a contamination-robust coverage theorem that explicitly bounds coverage loss as a function of the fraction δ of misclassified calibration interventions. Synthetic experiments confirm the bound and show that correcting the conformal level by subtracting g(δ,n) maintains nominal coverage under contamination, while widening intervals by a factor of 1.2–1.8. On real CRISPRi data (Replogle K562), the corrected method achieves coverage 0.906 (nominal 0.9), outperforming baselines, demonstrating practical utility despite imperfect exchangeability.

## Method Summary
The method operates as follows: For each intervention a, estimate a differentially affected set S_a (e.g., genes with significant expression changes) from interventional versus observational data. Algorithm 1 identifies candidate descendants by computing ddesc(a) = S_a ∩ (∩_{b∈U(a)} S_b), where U(a) are interventions for which a appears in their affected set. This intersection prunes false positives under assumptions of detectability and upstream diversity. Optionally, Algorithm 2 (local ICP) estimates a distance bd(a,i) to allow weighted calibration. For a test target i and intervention a* (presumed non-descendant), the selective calibration set is bA(i,a*) = {a∈A_cal : bZa,i=0}. A conformal quantile is computed from absolute residuals on bA, and the nominal level α is adjusted to α' = α − g(δ,n) based on an estimated contamination fraction δ (where g(δ,n)=δn/((1-δ)n+1)). The resulting interval enjoys a lower bound on coverage even if δ is inaccurate, and infinite intervals are returned if α'≤0.

## Key Results
- **Contamination-robust coverage theorem:** Theorem 1 gives a finite-sample lower bound 1−α−g(δ,n) for coverage when a fraction δ of selected calibration points are actually descendants.
- **Synthetic evaluation:** In linear SEMs (p=200, |A|=150), uncorrected selective coverage declines from 0.905 to 0.867 as δ grows to 0.30; the corrected method maintains ≥0.95 coverage at all δ, with intervals 1.2–1.8 times wider.
- **Real data application:** On the Replogle K562 CRISPRi dataset, the corrected method achieves 0.906 coverage (nominal 0.9), while uncorrected selective, pooled, and other baselines fall below 0.9, indicating robustness to imperfect exchangeability in practice.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** The contamination-robust coverage theorem quantifies how misclassifying "unaffected" calibration interventions degrades selective conformal coverage.
**Mechanism:** If a fraction δ of selected calibration interventions are actually descendants (i.e., affect the target), then coverage is lower-bounded by 1−α−g(δ,n) where g(δ,n)=δn/((1−δ)n+1). The proof considers an adversarial placement of "bad" scores and relies on exchangeability among truly unaffected scores. Core assumption: scores from unaffected interventions are exchangeable with the test; misclassification introduces contamination that violates this exchangeability for those points.
**Break condition:** When δ is large enough that g(δ,n)≥α, the bound becomes trivial (negative). Also, if the number of truly unaffected calibration points m is very small, the bound weakens (g(δ,n)≈δ/(1−δ) for large n).

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Task-driven partial causal learning reduces the problem to estimating binary descendant indicators, avoiding full graph recovery.
**Mechanism:** Only the binary relation Za,i = 1{i∈desc(a)} is needed. False positives (non-descendants labeled as descendants) directly increase δ and threaten coverage; false negatives merely reduce the calibration set size (safer but less efficient). The asymmetric cost justifies focusing on controlling false positives.
**Break condition:** In dense networks where the prior probability of non-descendants π0 is small, even a moderate false positive rate yields a large δ, causing significant coverage loss. Additionally, a classifier that ignores causal distance may include many distal descendants that still contaminate the selective set.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** The descendant discovery algorithm (Algorithm 1) controls false positives under upstream diversity and detectability assumptions.
**Mechanism:** For each intervention a, compute U(a) = {b∈A : a ∈ S_b}. Estimate descendants as ddesc(a) = S_a ∩ (∩_{b∈U(a)} S_b). Intersection removes variables that appear in S_a only due to false positives, because true descendants should appear in all upstream affected sets S_b for b∈U(a). This leverages transitivity.
**Break condition:** Upstream diversity (Assumption 3) can fail if ancestors of a have nested descendant sets, leaving common false positives unpruned. High false negative rates in S_a (ϵ_fn) increase FNR (safe for coverage) but reduce efficiency.

## Foundational Learning
- **Selective (Mondrian) Conformal Prediction:** Standard CP uses all calibration data; selective CP splits calibration points into groups (strata) and applies CP separately within each group, improving efficiency when the test belongs to a small stratum. ## Architecture Onboarding
#### Component map
1. Differentially Affected Set Estimation: From raw interventional and observational data, compute binary indicator vectors S_a (e.g., genes with statistically significant changes via two-sample t-tests and Benjamini–Hochberg correction).
2. Descendant Discovery (Algorithm 1): Input {S_a}; for each a, find upstream set U(a) = {b : a ∈ S_b}; output ddesc(a) = S_a ∩ (∩_{b∈U(a)} S_b). This yields estimated binary descendant matrix bZ.
3. Optional Distance Estimation (Algorithm 2): Local invariant causal prediction estimates a distance bd(a,i) (e.g., path length) by testing residual invariance across subsets of interventions; this can be used for weighted calibration.
4. Selective Calibration with Correction: For test pair (i, a*) with assumed Za*,i=0, form bA = {a ∈ A_cal : bZa,i=0}. Compute absolute residuals on these a, take the (⌈(|bA|+1)(1−α)⌉)-th order statistic as the conformal quantile after adjusting α to α' = α − g(δ,|bA|) with estimated δ. If α'≤0, return infinite interval.
5. Theoretical Monitoring: Use Propositions 1–2 to bound expected δ under detectability and upstream diversity; ensure calibration split yields enough points to avoid infeasible corrections.

#### Critical path
Raw data → (1) DEG sets → (2) Algorithm 1 → bZ → (4) bA selection → α' adjustment → conformal quantile. Algorithm 2 can replace step 2 to provide distances for weighted calibration.

#### Design tradeoffs
- *Conservatism vs. efficiency:* Larger correction (smaller α') widens intervals but protects coverage; the tradeoff is tuned by how aggressively one estimates δ.
- *Binary classification vs. continuous distance:* Hard bZ is simple but may discard useful similarity information; using bd allows finer-grained weighting but requires reliable distance estimates from Algorithm 2.
- *Calibration set size vs. purity:* A selector with low false negative rate yields a large bA (good quantile accuracy) but may increase false positives and δ; a conservative selector yields a purer bA but risks too few points, inflating quantile variance.

#### Failure signatures
- *Coverage below nominal:* Indicates δ larger than accounted for, likely due to high FPR in bZ or hidden exchangeability violations within bA.
- *Excessive infinite intervals:* α'≤0; caused by overestimated δ or too few calibration points after selection (small n).
- *No width improvement over pooled:* Selector misclassifies many unaffected as affected (high FNR), making bA≈A_cal; correction negligible.
- *High variance in coverage across test interventions:* Heterogeneous δ across (i,a*) pairs, suggesting inconsistent bZ quality.

#### First 3 experiments
1. **Synthetic SEM with controlled δ injection:** In simulated linear SEMs, artificially replace a fraction δ of unaffected calibration scores with affected scores. Empirically verify that coverage of the uncorrected method follows g(δ,n) and that the corrected method maintains ≥0.95 coverage.
2. **Real CRISPRi data application:** Apply the full pipeline to Replogle K562 data, using DEGs as S_a. Compare corrected selective, uncorrected selective, and pooled conformal intervals in terms of coverage and width.
3. **Recovery conditions ablation:** Vary detectability (false positive/negative rates of S_a) and upstream diversity in synthetic graphs to test Propositions 1–2, measuring resulting FPR (and thus δ) and coverage.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Tightening the contamination bound under benign models.** The current bound is worst-case; tighter bounds might be possible if affected scores stochastically dominate unaffected ones (e.g., larger residuals). *What evidence would resolve:* Derive a bound under a stochastic ordering assumption and validate on synthetic data where affected scores are indeed larger.
2. **Consistency and efficiency of local ICP distance estimation.** Algorithm 2 is heuristic; its theoretical properties and impact on weighted conformal coverage are unclear. *What evidence would resolve:* Prove consistency of bd(a,i) under suitable assumptions (faithfulness, sufficient interventions) and compare weighted vs. hard selective intervals in simulation.
3. **Scalability to larger real perturbation datasets.** The real-data evaluation used a small calibration set (~40 perturbations) and a proxy oracle, limiting confidence. *What evidence would resolve:* Apply the method to a larger CRISPR screen (hundreds of interventions) with a biologically validated oracle (e.g., curated GRN) to assess coverage and feasibility broadly.
4. **Active experimental design for minimal δ.** Can we sequentially select interventions to most effectively reduce δ? *What evidence would resolve:* Develop an acquisition function based on uncertainty in descendant indicators, analyze sample complexity, and test in simulation, showing fewer experiments achieve a target δ.
5. **Extension to covariate-dependent causal structures.** Biological networks often vary across cell types; the current static Za,i may not hold. *What evidence would resolve:* Define context-specific descendant indicators and strata; derive coverage guarantees; evaluate on multi-context perturbation data (e.g., Perturb‑seq across cell lines).

## Limitations
- **Real-data oracle uncertainty:** The lack of ground-truth descendant relationships forces the use of heuristic proxies (e.g., top log‑fold‑change genes), making δ estimates and coverage conclusions approximate.
- **Algorithm 2 underspecification:** Details for local ICP (parent candidate pools, invariance tests, frontier expansion) are omitted, preventing full implementation and replication.
- **Synthetic generation ambiguity:** Key parameters (DAG connection probability, exact edge weight distribution) are not fully described, hindering exact reproduction of the synthetic experiments.

## Confidence
- Theorem 1 (contamination-robust coverage guarantee): **High** – proof is provided and synthetic experiments corroborate.
- Task-driven partial causal learning formulation: **Medium** – conceptually sound but relies on accurate estimation of descendant indicators.
- Algorithm 1 false positive control (under Assumptions 2–3): **Medium** – theoretical conditions are given; real-world satisfaction remains to be demonstrated.
- Empirical results (synthetic and real): **Medium** – synthetic results are convincing; real-data evidence is limited by oracle quality and small calibration size.
