# Dual-Space Knowledge Distillation with Key-Query Matching for Large Language Models with Vocabulary Mismatch

## Quick Facts
- ArXiv ID: 2603.22056
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22056
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: DSKD-CMA-GA achieves an average +0.37 ROUGE-L gain over DSKD-CMA on out-of-distribution data, narrowing the cross-tokenizer distillation gap.

## Executive Summary
Addressing knowledge distillation between LLMs with mismatched tokenizers, this paper analyzes DSKD-CMA, a state-of-the-art method that uses cross-model attention to implicitly align teacher and student representations. Manual chunk-alignment probing reveals that DSKD-CMA captures coarse, chunk-level correspondences but produces noisy token-level mappings. To improve alignment, the authors propose DSKD-CMA-GA (generative adversarial) and DSKD-CMA-CT (conditional transport), which explicitly match the distributions of teacher keys and student queries. Experimentation with a GPT-2 student and Qwen1.5 teacher on text generation shows DSKD-CMA-GA consistently outperforms the baseline, with an average ROUGE-L improvement of +0.37 on OOD data and a reduced gap to same-tokenizer distillation.

## Method Summary
The base method DSKD-CMA projects student embeddings into teacher space as queries and normalizes teacher embeddings as keys. Scaled dot-product attention computes a soft alignment matrix, which weights teacher values to produce an aligned teacher state. This state is fed through the teacher's prediction head to obtain a distribution that the student is trained to mimic via an f‑divergence loss, combined with the student's own cross‑entropy loss. Proposed extensions add a key‑query matching term: DSKD‑CMA‑GA introduces a discriminator network that distinguishes teacher keys from student queries, leading to an adversarial min‑max loss that aligns their distributions; DSKD‑CMA‑CT uses a conditional transport critic to minimize a transport cost between the two distributions. Both extensions are added to the base loss. Evaluation uses the DataBricks Dolly dataset (11K train, 1K validation) with GPT‑2 (124 M, 50K vocab) as student and Qwen1.5 (1.8 B, 150K vocab) as teacher, measuring text generation quality via average ROUGE‑L across in‑distribution and multiple out‑of‑distribution test sets.

## Key Results
- DSKD‑CMA‑GA yields an average ROUGE‑L improvement of +0.37 over DSKD‑CMA on OOD data (peaking at +1.04 on Self‑Instruct).
- The adversarial approach (GA) outperforms conditional transport (CT) and works best with a skewed KL divergence (SKL).
- With GA, cross‑tokenizer KD approaches the performance of same‑tokenizer KD, significantly narrowing the gap.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Implicit alignment via cross‑model attention.** DSKD‑CMA treats student embeddings as queries and teacher embeddings as keys; attention weights implicitly learn token‑level associations despite different tokenizations. Evidence includes the formulation in Section 3.1, the abstract, and empirical results (Table 1). The assumption is that dot‑product similarity can connect corresponding tokens; this fails if sequence lengths or granularities differ drastically.

**Mechanism 2: Coarse chunk‑level alignment but noisy token‑level mapping.** Manual probing constructs binary chunk‑alignment matrices from character offsets and compares them to the learned attention matrices. Ablations show that replacing attention with uniform chunk projection (CLP) performs similarly to DSKD‑CMA, while restricting attention within chunks (CLA) degrades performance. This indicates that DSKD‑CMA successfully captures coarse spans but is sensitive to noisy local mappings. Evidence: Section 3.2 and 5.1, visualizations in Figure 5. The ground‑truth alignment itself may become ambiguous with repeated tokens.

**Mechanism 3: Aligning key‑query distributions improves attention quality.** The gap in cross‑tokenizer KD persists because teacher keys and student queries come from mismatched distributions. DSKD‑CMA‑GA adds an adversarial objective where a discriminator tries to separate teacher keys from student queries; the student’s query projector is updated to fool the discriminator, thereby aligning the distributions. Evidence: Abstract, Section 3.3, Table 5.2 where GA gives consistent ROUGE‑L gains, especially with SKL divergence. Potential instability arises if the architectures are too dissimilar.

## Foundational Learning
**Knowledge Distillation (KD)**  
- *Why needed:* KD is the overarching paradigm; the paper uses white‑box KD that transfers internal representations instead of only outputs, requiring understanding of f‑divergence losses and their trade‑offs.  
- *Quick check:* What are the trade‑offs between logit‑based and representation‑based KD in terms of knowledge transferred and computational cost?

**Tokenizer Mismatch & Chunk Alignment**  
- *Why needed:* Different tokenizers cause misaligned sequences; the paper’s analysis builds explicit chunk alignments as a probe for evaluating implicit attention, necessitating a grasp of why token‑level alignment is fragile and why aggregating over text spans is more robust.  
- *Quick check:* Why might aggregating token embeddings over a text span (a “chunk”) be more reliable for alignment than a one‑to‑one token mapping?

**Attention as an Implicit Alignment Mechanism**  
- *Why needed:* DSKD‑CMA’s novelty is repurposing cross‑model attention to learn alignments without explicit token mapping; understanding queries, keys, and values in this cross‑model context is central.  
- *Quick check:* In cross‑model attention, why are student embeddings used for queries and teacher for keys? What would happen if the roles were reversed?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Raw text → Teacher/Student tokenizers (T_t, T_s) → Token IDs → Embedding lookup (e_t, e_s) →  
Student branch: [e_s_input, e_s_target] → Query projector P_Q → Q  
Teacher branch: [e_t_input, e_t_target] → Key normalizer N_std. → K  
Q, K → Scaled dot‑product + sigmoid → Attention matrix A →  
Teacher values: V = P_{t→s}(N_std.(h^t) + e^t_target) → Aligned state h^{t→s} = A V →  
h^{t→s} → Teacher prediction head → Distribution q^{t→s}  
(Symmetric s→t branch produces q^{s→t})  
Loss: L = L_CE(s) + 0.5*(L_KD^{s→t} + L_KD^{t→s}) + λ L_KQ (if GA/CT)  
Analysis pipeline: Character offset tracking → Chunk matrix M → Compare to A → Visualizations; CLP (A←M) and CLA (A masked by M) ablations.

**Critical path:**  
*Training:* Student forward pass → compute Q → detach teacher K → compute A → fetch V from teacher → h^{t→s} → q^{t→s} → L_KD^{t→s}. Mirror for s→t. Sum L_CE, f‑divergences, and (optionally) L_KQ → backward pass.  
*Analysis:* On held‑out batch, cache A; compute M via character offsets; produce heatmaps; run CLP/CLA by replacing/masking A, generate outputs, and compute ROUGE‑L to gauge alignment impact.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *CMA vs. CLP:* CLP gives noise‑free alignment but needs precomputed M each step (I/O overhead); CMA is implicit and cheaper but yields diffuse attention.  
- *f‑divergence choice:* Divergence interacts with alignment method (SRKL best for vanilla CMA, SKL for GA, KL for CT); hyperparameter tuning required.  
- *GA vs. CT:* GA is empirically stronger but introduces a discriminator, risking instability and extra compute; CT is simpler but consistently underperforms.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Heatmaps showing highly diffuse attention on sequences with many repeated tokens (e.g., numbers).  
- Sharp performance drop when using CLA, indicating the model relies on coarse alignment and is harmed by overly fine‑grained token matching.  
- Adversarial training instability: discriminator accuracy quickly reaches 100% and stays there, or L_KQ fails to decrease.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Replicate DSKD‑CMA baseline: Train GPT‑2 student on Dolly with Qwen1.5 teacher using original DSKD‑CMA (SRKL divergence). Verify ROUGE‑L ≈ 24.6 on in‑distribution data (Table 1).  
2. Implement chunk‑alignment probe: For a validation batch, compute M via character‑offset matching; visualize A and M as heatmaps ( Figure 5); confirm attention broadly respects chunk boundaries but is noisy within chunks.  
3. Integrate DSKD‑CMA‑GA: Add adversarial key‑query matching loss (Eq 14) and train with SKL divergence. Expect ROUGE‑L gains from +0.15 (in‑dist) to +1.04 (Self‑Instruct) over baseline; monitor discriminator loss/accuracy for stability.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

- Do other adversarial alignment frameworks (e.g., Wasserstein GAN, moment matching) offer advantages over GA and CT for cross‑tokenizer KD in diverse settings?  
- Why does generative adversarial alignment (GA) empirically outperform conditional transport (CT) for key‑query matching under vocabulary mismatch?

## Limitations
- Missing critical training hyperparameters (learning rates, λ weight, batch size, scheduler) hampers exact replication.  
- The character‑offset chunk alignment used as ground truth may be ambiguous for texts with repeated tokens or special formatting, affecting analysis validity.  
- Evaluation is restricted to a single teacher‑student pair (GPT‑2 ↔ Qwen1.5), limiting confidence in generalizability across tokenizers, model sizes, and tasks.  
- Lack of statistical significance testing makes it hard to judge the robustness of observed gains.

## Next Checks
- Hyperparameter ablation: Systematically vary the λ weight and learning rates for the GA/CT modules (and discriminator/critic) to determine stable, optimal configurations and establish performance upper bounds.  
- Gold‑standard alignment evaluation: Manually annotate token alignments for a validation set, compute alignment metrics (e.g., precision, recall) for DSKD‑CMA and its variants, and correlate alignment quality with ROUGE‑L.  
- Cross‑tokenizer generalizability: Replicate the main experiments with at least two additional tokenizer pairs (e.g., BPE vs. SentencePiece, English vs. multilingual vocabularies) to verify that the observed improvements hold across diverse mismatches.
