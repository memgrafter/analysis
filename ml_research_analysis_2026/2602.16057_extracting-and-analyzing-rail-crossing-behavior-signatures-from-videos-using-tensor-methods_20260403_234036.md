# Extracting and Analyzing Rail Crossing Behavior Signatures from Videos using Tensor Methods

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.16057
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16057
- **Reference count:** 11
- **Primary result:** A multi-view tensor framework segmented crossing videos into Approach, Waiting, and Clearance phases, used TimeSformer embeddings, applied non-negative CP decomposition, and discovered that crossing location strongly determines behavior patterns, with the approach phase being highly discriminative, enabling scalable grouping for targeted safety interventions.

## Executive Summary
Railway crossing safety poses significant challenges, and understanding driver behavior at these locations is critical. This paper introduces a multi-view tensor framework to analyze videos of crossing events. The method first segments each video into three temporal phases (Approach, Waiting, Clearance), extracts visual-kinematic embeddings using a pre-trained TimeSformer model, and constructs a similarity tensor with one slice per phase. Non-negative symmetric CP decomposition is then applied to discover latent behavioral components, each characterized by a phase-specific signature and a set of videos that express it. Evaluated on 31 videos from four locations in Lincoln, NE, the analysis reveals that crossing location is the dominant factor shaping behavior, outweighing time-of-day effects. The approach phase emerges as the most discriminative, and t-SNE visualizations show clear location-based clustering. The resulting components provide an interpretable basis for grouping crossings and designing targeted safety interventions.

## Method Summary
The pipeline begins with collecting video recordings of railway crossing events. Each video is manually (or automatically) segmented into three phases: Approach (from warning activation to fully lowered gates), Waiting (gates down until train passes), and Clearance (train clears to gates raised). For every phase, multiple short clips are sampled according to phase duration, and each clip is processed by a TimeSformer model pre-trained on Kinetics-400 to yield a 768-dimensional embedding; the mean of clip embeddings becomes the phase's representation. Cosine similarity is computed between all phase embeddings within each phase, producing three 31×31 symmetric matrices (one per phase). These matrices are stacked into a 31×31×3 tensor. Non-negative symmetric CP decomposition factorizes this tensor into a sum of rank-1 components, each with a non-negative phase loading vector (indicating which phases define the component) and a non-negative video loading vector (indicating which videos express the component). Rank selection employs CORCONDIA (for ranks ≤3), reconstruction error elbow, and holdout validation (10% masked entries, RMSE). The final decomposition (chosen rank = 4) enables analysis of phase contributions and video similarities, with t-SNE used to visualize the video loading matrix.

## Key Results
- The approach phase is highly discriminative: Component 4 exhibits strong dominance of the approach-phase (loading: 1.52), indicating that initial driver response to crossing warnings provides a particularly distinctive behavioral signature.
- Crossing location strongly determines behavior patterns, outweighing time-of-day effects; location-based clusters are evident in the decomposed component space.
- t-SNE visualization of video loadings confirms clear separation by crossing location.
- Rank 4 was selected as the optimal trade-off between reconstruction fidelity and interpretability, enabling scalable grouping of crossings for targeted safety interventions.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Segmenting crossing events into discrete temporal phases (Approach, Waiting, Clearance) enables the capture of phase-specific behavioral patterns that differ in their discriminative power for grouping crossing locations.  
**Mechanism:** The framework isolates three functionally distinct periods and constructs separate similarity matrices for each phase. The subsequent tensor factorization treats each phase as a separate “view,” allowing latent components to weight each phase differently. This reveals which phase(s) are most characteristic of a given behavioral cluster.  
**Core assumption:** Behavior meaningful for location grouping is temporally localized and can be separated into these three canonical phases defined by the crossing hardware state.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] “captures behavioral similarities across three distinct temporal phases (approach, waiting, clearance)”  
- [section III-B] “We focus our analysis on phases A, B, and C, as these represent the constrained event period during which the crossing mechanisms influence driver behavior.”  
- [section IV-C] “Component 4 exhibits strong dominance of the approach-phase (1.52), indicating that the initial driver response to crossing warnings provides a particularly discriminative behavioral signature.”  
**Break condition:** If phase boundaries are incorrect or driver behavior is not meaningfully segmented by gate state, the phase-specific similarity tensors would not exhibit distinct structures, and phase loadings would show no dominant pattern.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Using a pre-trained general video understanding model (TimeSformer) to extract embeddings from phase clips provides a sufficiently rich, high-dimensional representation of behavioral dynamics for cross-location comparison, even without domain-specific fine-tuning.  
**Mechanism:** TimeSformer, pre-trained on Kinetics-400 for action recognition, processes short clips distributed across a phase. The mean embedding over multiple clips is used as a fixed vector representation of that phase’s visual-kinematic activity. The cosine similarity between these embeddings across videos forms the basis of the tensor. The mechanism assumes that the space of learned features from a broad action dataset contains subspaces relevant to distinguishing constrained driving behaviors like hesitation, acceleration, or stopping at crossings.  
**Core assumption:** The manifold of human/vehicle actions in railway crossing scenarios overlaps sufficiently with the general action categories in Kinetics-400, such that embedding distances correlate with behavioral similarity in this specific domain.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [section III-C] “We extracted 768-dimensional embeddings using TimeSformer (facebook/timesformer-base-finetuned-k400), a transformer-based video understanding model pre-trained on Kinetics-400.”  
- [section III-C] “This resulted in a total of 93 phase embeddings… capturing behavioral dynamics across the entire phase rather than a single moment.”  
- [section VI-Discussion] “While this transfer learning approach successfully captures behavioral patterns sufficient for location-based clustering, fine-tuning on annotated railway crossing data could potentially improve sensitivity…”  
**Break condition:** If the pre-trained model’s features are entirely irrelevant to crossing behavior (e.g., focusing on background scenery or non-relevant actions), the resulting similarity matrices would be noisy or random, and no meaningful location-based clustering would emerge in the decomposed component space.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** Non-negative symmetric CP decomposition on the multi-view similarity tensor yields an interpretable, parts-based representation where each latent component corresponds to a behavioral “signature” characterized by a specific phase weighting and a specific set of videos (locations/times) that express it.  
**Mechanism:** The tensor’s symmetric structure (two identical modes for video pairs) and the application of non-negativity constraints force the decomposition to produce additive, non-negative components. A component’s phase loading vector (`a_r`) indicates which temporal phase(s) define its signature (e.g., Approach-dominant). Its video loading vector (`u_r`) indicates which crossing events strongly exhibit that signature. Because the outer product `λ_r * a_r ◦ u_r ◦ u_r` reconstructs similarity, high `u_r` values for a pair of videos mean they are similar *in the phase pattern defined by `a_r`*. This makes the latent space directly interpretable for grouping.  
**Core assumption:** The observed behavioral similarities across the dataset can be modeled as a non-negative combination of a small number (`R`) of latent, phase-specific prototypes.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] “applying non-negative symmetric CP decomposition… discover latent behavioral components with distinct temporal signatures.”  
- [section III-F] “The non-negativity constraint ensures all factor loadings are non-negative, making each video interpretable as a non-negative mixture of behavioral components—a representation more intuitive to interpret than standard CP decomposition’s bipolar structure.”  
- [section IV-C] “Component 4 exhibits strong dominance of the approach-phase (1.52)… NW 12th Street forms a distinct behavioral cluster (Component 1 dominance).”  
- [section IV-D] t-SNE visualization shows “location-based clustering patterns.”  
**Break condition:** If the true generative process of behavioral similarity is not well-approximated by a low-rank, non-negative symmetric CP model, the components will be uninterpretable (e.g., all phases loading equally on all components, video loadings showing no coherent grouping) or the decomposition will be unstable across random restarts.

## Foundational Learning

### Tensor Decomposition (CP/PARAFAC)
**Why needed here:** The core analytical engine that factors the 3-way tensor into a sum of rank-1 components, each with a phase signature and a video signature, discovering latent behavioral prototypes.  
**Quick check question:** What does a component’s phase loading vector (`a_r`) tell you about that component’s behavioral signature?

### Non-Negativity Constraints in Decomposition
**Why needed here:** Enforces a “parts-based” representation where behavioral signatures are additive and all contributions are non-negative, making it easier to interpret a video’s profile as a mixture of distinct, positively defined behavioral types.  
**Quick check question:** How does non-negativity change the interpretation of a component’s video loading (`u_r`) compared to a standard (unconstrained) CP decomposition?

### Phase-Wise Similarity Tensors (Multi-View Representation)
**Why needed here:** The construction method that creates the tensor. Having one slice per phase allows the decomposition to explicitly model how the pattern of similarity changes over time, crucial for identifying phase-dominant components.  
**Quick check question:** If all three phase similarity matrices were identical, what would you expect the phase loadings (`a_r`) for all components to look like?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
1. **Input:** Raw video files + metadata (location, time-of-day)  
2. **Phase Segmentation Module:** Manual/automatic labeling of frames into Approach, Waiting, Clearance. Outputs: lists of frame ranges per video per phase.  
3. **Embedding Extraction Module:** For each (video, phase) pair, sample distributed clips → pass each 8-frame clip through TimeSformer → average clip embeddings → final 768-D phase embedding.  
4. **Tensor Construction Module:** For each phase `p`, compute 31×31 cosine similarity matrix from all phase embeddings. Stack three matrices into tensor `X` (31×31×3).  
5. **Decomposition Module:** Apply non-negative symmetric CP decomposition to `X` with selected rank `R`. Outputs: component weights `λ_r`, phase loadings `a_r` (R × 3), video loadings `u_r` (R × 31).  
6. **Analysis & Visualization Module:** Compute t-SNE of video loadings matrix (31 × R). Plot colored by location/time. Examine phase loading bar charts and video loading scatter/bar plots.

**Critical path:** Phase Segmentation → Embedding Extraction → Tensor Construction → Rank Selection → Decomposition → Component Interpretation. A failure in phase segmentation or embedding extraction propagates irreversibly.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Rank (`R`) Selection vs. Interpretability:** Higher rank fits data better but risks components that are difficult to distinguish or overfit to noise. Paper chose R=4 as a balance, sacrificing some reconstruction error for clearer separation.  
- **Non-Negativity vs. Expressiveness:** Non-negativity improves interpretability but may prevent the model from capturing patterns that require subtractive relationships. Could explore semi-non-negative variants.  
- **General-Purpose Embeddings vs. Domain Specificity:** Using pre-trained TimeSformer is fast & works for clustering, but may miss subtle, domain-specific behaviors. Fine-tuning costs more data/time.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **Tensor Construction:** Phase similarity matrices appear random (no block structure). Indicates embeddings are not phase-discriminative.  
- **Decomposition:** Very low CORCONDIA (<<80%) for all R>1. Indicates tensor lacks valid CP structure; maybe symmetric CP is inappropriate.  
- **Component Output:** Phase loadings (`a_r`) are near-uniform for all components. Indicates decomposition failed to find phase-specific patterns.  
- **Visualization:** t-SNE shows no clustering by location or time. Indicates learned component space does not separate meaningful groups.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Sanity Check Phase Separation:** Compute average within-phase cosine similarity vs. cross-phase similarity for all video embeddings. Expect: within-phase > cross-phase. If not, phase segmentation or embedding extraction is flawed.  
2. **Validate Rank Selection Logic:** Re-run decomposition for R=1..5. Plot: (a) CORCONDIA, (b) Reconstruction Error, (c) Holdout RMSE (as in paper Figs 2-4). Confirm the “elbow” and validity range match paper’s reasoning for choosing R=4.  
3. **Component Stability Check:** For chosen R=4, run decomposition with 50 random restarts. Check that the component phase loadings (`a_r`) are stable (e.g., using Procrustes analysis). If components permute or change drastically, the solution is unstable and not reliably interpretable.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How do crossing characteristics (e.g., geometry, signage, traffic volume) explain the location-based behavioral clusters, and can integrating these data enable more targeted interventions? The authors lack crossing characteristic data; future work should incorporate such metadata to link patterns to design factors.  
2. Does fine-tuning TimeSformer on railway crossing-specific annotations improve its ability to capture domain-relevant behaviors (e.g., gate violations, near-misses) compared to the general pre-trained model? The study used off-the-shelf embeddings; fine-tuning could enhance sensitivity to domain-specific events.  
3. Is the finding that crossing location dominates over time-of-day in shaping behavior patterns generalizable beyond the four locations studied? The dataset is small and location-imbalanced; validation across a larger, diverse set of crossings is needed.  
4. What specific behavioral cues within the approach phase make it so discriminative, and can these cues be automatically extracted from the video embeddings? The paper treats TimeSformer embeddings as a black box; post-hoc explainability could identify the discriminative features.

## Limitations
- Dataset and exact segmentation criteria are not public, hindering replication and limiting external validity.
- Hyperparameters (holdout masking pattern, ALS initialization, convergence tolerance, regularization) are under-specified, making results sensitive to implementation choices.
- Small and location-imbalanced sample (31 videos from 4 sites, heavily skewed to one location) may affect generalizability of the discovered clusters.

## Confidence
- Phase-specific discriminative patterns: High
- Location dominates over time-of-day: Medium
- Approach phase most discriminative: High
- Non-negative CP yields interpretable components: High
