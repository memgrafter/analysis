# Chain-of-Sanitized-Thoughts: Plugging PII Leakage in CoT of Large Reasoning Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.05076
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05076
- **Reference count:** 4
- **Primary result:** For high-capacity LRMs, prompt engineering reduces PII leakage in CoT (e.g., +5.53 Privacy Score) with minimal utility impact; weaker models require supervised fine-tuning for substantial privacy gains (e.g., +25.21 for LLaMA) while preserving utility.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles a critical privacy vulnerability in Large Reasoning Models (LRMs): personally identifiable information (PII) can leak through chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning traces even when final answers are properly sanitized. To address this, the authors introduce PII-CoT-Bench, a dataset containing privacy-aware CoT annotations for medical and financial queries, and evaluate two practical interventions—prompt engineering (PE) and supervised fine-tuning (SFT)—aimed at embedding “privacy-first” reasoning. Their findings reveal a capability-dependent pattern: state-of-the-art models like GPT-OSS, Phi-4, and QwQ achieve significant privacy improvements via PE (e.g., Privacy Score gains up to +19.49) with only minor utility reductions (Utility Score drops ≤ -2.43). In contrast, weaker models (LLaMA, DeepSeek-R1-Qwen) require SFT on PII-CoT-Bench to attain comparable privacy gains (e.g., +25.21 for LLaMA) and maintain utility. Both methods lower Total Leakage Rate and Normalized Exposure across six evaluation categories, with SFT providing stronger robustness under adversarial framing. The study concludes that private CoT reasoning can be realized without substantial performance trade-offs.

## Method Summary
The core methodology revolves around the creation and use of PII-CoT-Bench, a supervised dataset of 350 samples from medical and financial domains. Each sample includes a query, a ground-truth answer, and a privacy-preserving CoT trace that either redacts, abstracts, or obfuscates sensitive information while maintaining correct reasoning. Two interventions are examined: (1) Prompt Engineering (PE), which employs a carefully designed system prompt instructing the model to reason privately (e.g., "never think about original PII"); (2) Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), which applies LoRA (low-rank adaptation) on PII-CoT-Bench to update model parameters toward private reasoning behavior. Evaluation is conducted on a separate, category-balanced test set covering six scenarios (Incidental PII, Task-Critical PII, Adversarial Framing, Cross-Domain, Superficially Relevant, and Compositional PII). Privacy and utility are quantified through a combination of deterministic metrics (Total Leakage Rate, Normalized Exposure) and LLM-as-a-judge scores (Privacy Score and Utility Score) using GPT-4o-mini. The experiment pipeline profiles baseline performance, applies PE, and then SFT for five open-weight LRMs (GPT-OSS-20B, Phi-4, DeepSeek-R1-Qwen-7B, LLaMA-3.3-70B, QwQ-32B) all quantized to 4-bit for efficiency.

## Key Results
- High-capability models (GPT-OSS, Phi-4, QwQ) achieve Privacy Score improvements of +5.53 to +19.49 via PE with Utility Score decreases ≤ -2.43.
- Weaker models (LLaMA, DeepSeek-R1-Qwen) require SFT for substantial privacy gains: LLaMA +25.21 Privacy Score, DeepSeek-R1-Qwen +22.34, with negligible utility loss (<1 point).
- Both interventions reduce Total Leakage Rate and Normalized Exposure across all six evaluation categories; SFT demonstrates superior robustness, especially in adversarial scenarios.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** High-capability reasoning models (e.g., GPT-OSS, Phi-4, QwQ) can internalize privacy constraints via prompt engineering alone, achieving substantial PII leakage reduction with minimal utility loss.  
**Mechanism:** These models possess sufficiently robust instruction-following and latent knowledge of privacy norms from pre-training and RLHF. A carefully crafted system prompt activates this latent capability, steering the model’s reasoning process to abstract or omit PII without altering weights.  
**Core assumption:** State-of-the-art LRMs have developed a generalizable "privacy-aware" reasoning pattern that can be elicited through natural language directives.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract reports Privacy Score +5.53 for GPT-OSS via PE with Utility Score -2.29; Section 5.1/Table 1 shows PE yields gains for GPT-OSS (+5.53), Phi-4 (+14.44), QwQ (+19.49) with small utility drops.  
**Break condition:** If the model’s instruction hierarchy is weak, prompt constraints may be overridden by the drive to answer correctly or by adversarial prompting, leading to negligible privacy gains.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Weaker reasoning models (e.g., LLaMA, DeepSeek-R1-Qwen) require supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on privacy-aware CoT data to achieve meaningful leakage reduction, as prompt engineering is insufficient.  
**Mechanism:** SFT on the PII-CoT-Bench dataset provides direct supervision on *how* to reason privately, updating model parameters to form a dedicated "private-first" reasoning pathway. This compensates for the model’s lack of innate instruction adherence or latent privacy representations.  
**Core assumption:** Privacy-preserving reasoning is not a natural byproduct of general pretraining for mid-tier models; it must be explicitly taught via weight updates.  
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract reports LLaMA Privacy Score +25.21 via SFT vs. +13.37 via PE; Section 5.1 shows DeepSeek-R1-Qwen improves +22.34 via SFT vs. +19.99 via PE, with SFT also reducing Normalized Exposure more.  
**Break condition:** If the fine-tuning data is too narrow or misaligned, the model may fail to generalize private reasoning to the diverse categories in the evaluation set.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** The structure and content of the PII-CoT-Bench training dataset is a causal enabler for both PE and SFT interventions, as it defines the target "private-first" reasoning behavior.  
**Mechanism:** The dataset provides concrete examples of CoT traces that correctly handle PII (e.g., using placeholders, refusing to reason on sensitive details, abstraction). For SFT, it directly shapes model weights. For PE, it implicitly informs the design of the system prompt by enumerating PII types and required behaviors.  
**Core assumption:** Effective privacy-preserving reasoning requires a clear, supervised specification of what "thinking privately" looks like, which is absent in base models.  
**Evidence anchors:** Section 4.1 describes PII-CoT-Bench as designed for training models to produce privacy-preserving traces; Section 5.1 notes the SFT system prompt is derived from the dataset’s philosophy and the PE prompt lists explicit rules mirroring the annotations.  
**Break condition:** If the dataset’s definition of PII or its "private" reasoning traces are inconsistent, incomplete, or biased, both SFT and PE will learn or enforce flawed privacy behaviors.

## Foundational Learning

### Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Privacy Attack Surface
**Why needed:** The core problem is that LRMs expose PII in intermediate reasoning even when final outputs are safe. Understanding why CoT creates a distinct attack surface (longer traces, logging, RAG integration) is fundamental to evaluating mitigation strategies.  
**Quick check:** Why might a model that correctly sanitizes its final answer still leak PII in its CoT?  
*Answer:* The model may internally reference or restate sensitive context during reasoning steps without explicit instruction to suppress it.

### Capability-Dependent Intervention Efficacy
**Why needed:** The paper’s key finding is that the optimal intervention (PE vs. SFT) depends on the model’s inherent reasoning and instruction-following strength. This is a non-obvious design principle for deploying privacy controls.  
**Quick check:** For a given model, how would you decide whether to invest in prompt engineering or supervised fine-tuning to reduce PII leakage?  
*Answer:* Assess the model’s general reasoning/instruction adherence capability from standard benchmarks; higher capability suggests PE may suffice, lower capability suggests SFT is needed.

### Privacy-Utility Trade-off Metrics
**Why needed:** The paper uses multiple metrics (Total Leakage Rate, Normalized Exposure, Privacy Score, Utility Score) to quantify the nuanced impact of interventions. Understanding what each metric captures is essential for interpreting results.  
**Quick check:** What is the difference between "Total Leakage Rate" and "Normalized Exposure," and why might they move in opposite directions after an intervention?  
*Answer:* Leakage Rate measures the proportion of tokens that are PII; Normalized Exposure weights PII by sensitivity. An intervention might reduce the number of leaked tokens (Leakage Rate ↓) but those remaining could be highly sensitive, or vice-versa.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Open-weight LRMs (GPT-OSS-20B, Phi-4, DeepSeek-R1-Qwen-7B, LLaMA-3.3-70B, QwQ-32B) are the base. Two intervention pathways: Prompt Engineering (system prompt) and Supervised Fine-Tuning (LoRA on PII-CoT-Bench). Evaluation flow: Evaluation Prompts -> Model (Baseline/PE/SFT) -> CoT/Answers -> Metrics (Total Leakage Rate, Normalized Exposure, Privacy Score, Utility Score) with GPT-4o-mini as judge. Supporting tooling: Unsloth for LoRA, 4-bit quantization.

### Critical path
1. Prepare PII-CoT-Bench (350 samples) and the category-balanced evaluation dataset.
2. Run baseline inference on all models to establish natural PII leakage levels.
3. Apply the PE system prompt to each model and re-evaluate on the same dataset.
4. Perform SFT on each model using LoRA on PII-CoT-Bench, then evaluate the tuned model.
5. Aggregate metrics globally and per-category, compute changes (Δ) from baseline for PE and SFT.
6. Analyze capability-dependent trends (e.g., correlate benchmark scores with Δ-Privacy for PE vs. SFT).

### Design tradeoffs
- **PE vs. SFT:** PE is lightweight, reversible, and low-cost but brittle to prompt injection and less effective for weaker models. SFT is more robust and effective for mid-tier models but requires training data, compute, and risks utility degradation if overfit.
- **Quantization & LoRA:** Enables SFT on consumer hardware but may limit the magnitude of behavioral change compared to full fine-tuning.
- **Evaluation scope:** The six categories cover broad scenarios but may not capture all real-world PII types or long-context, multi-turn leakage dynamics.
- **Judge model:** Using GPT-4o-mini for automated scoring is scalable but inherits its biases and may not perfectly align with human judgment on nuanced privacy violations.

### Failure signatures
- High Leakage Rate and Normalized Exposure in the "Adversarially Framed" category across all interventions, indicating susceptibility to prompt injection.
- A large drop in Utility Score (e.g., >5 points) after SFT, suggesting over-correction into excessive refusal or flawed reasoning.
- For a "high-capability" model (e.g., GPT-OSS), negligible Δ-Privacy from PE, indicating the base prompt was already sufficient or the PE design was ineffective.
- SFT on a weak model yielding minimal Δ-Privacy but high Δ-Utility, indicating training instability or catastrophic forgetting.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Baseline Capability Profiling:** Run all models (no intervention) on the full evaluation dataset. Record global Leakage Rate, Normalized Exposure, Privacy Score, Utility Score. Also run a standard reasoning benchmark (e.g., MATH) to establish a "capability baseline" for later correlation.
2. **Prompt Engineering Efficacy Test:** For each model, apply the defined PE system prompt and re-evaluate on the same dataset. Compute Δ-metrics (PE - Baseline). Analyse per-category results, especially "Adversarial" and "Task-Critical."
3. **SFT Training & Evaluation:** For each model, perform LoRA SFT on PII-CoT-Bench (e.g., 3 epochs, learning rate 2e-4). Evaluate the tuned model. Compute Δ-metrics (SFT - Baseline). Compare SFT Δ-Privacy to PE Δ-Privacy. Validate that utility is preserved (Δ-Utility ≈ 0).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can RLHF or GRPO frameworks be effectively adapted to incentivize privacy-preserving reasoning during training, and how would their privacy-utility trade-offs compare to supervised fine-tuning?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors explicitly state in the Conclusion and Future Work: "A natural next step is to extend our framework to privacy-first RLHF (Christiano et al., 2023) or GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), where privacy leakage is explicitly penalized at the trajectory level during reasoning."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper only evaluates supervised fine-tuning and prompt engineering; RLHF/GRPO could offer stronger incentives for privacy but their effectiveness and trade-offs are unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct experiments training models with RLHF/GRPO objectives that penalize PII leakage in CoT, then compare privacy and utility metrics against SFT and PE baselines across multiple models.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How do inherent model properties—such as architecture, scale, pretraining data, and prior alignment—determine a model's baseline PII leakage and its responsiveness to privacy interventions like PE or SFT?
- **Basis in paper:** In the Conclusion, the authors note: "we plan to investigate privacy at model level, aiming to understand how different architectures and pretraining regimes encode, surface, or suppress sensitive information during reasoning." Also, the capability-dependent trend observed suggests model capability matters.
- **Why unresolved:** The study compares several open-source models but doesn't systematically isolate architectural or pretraining factors; correlations exist but causal insights are lacking.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments varying model architectures (e.g., decoder-only sizes), pretraining data diversity, and alignment histories, while measuring baseline leakage and intervention efficacy.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What changes in the model's internal representations (e.g., attention patterns, neuron activations, latent space) are induced by prompt engineering versus supervised fine-tuning to suppress PII, and do these changes reflect true internalization of privacy constraints?
- **Basis in paper:** The Limitations state: "our analysis treats privacy leakage as an observable surface phenomenon... without direct access to internal representations, limiting our ability to draw causal conclusions about how models internally encode and suppress sensitive information." This gap is critical for understanding why PE works for stronger models but SFT for weaker ones.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper only analyzes outputs; internal mechanisms are speculative. Without probing representations, we cannot confirm if models truly "think privately" or just mimic it superficially.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Use probing techniques (e.g., linear probes, activation analysis) on models before and after intervention to identify changes in representations related to PII concepts, and correlate with leakage metrics.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How effective are current privacy mitigation strategies (PE and SFT) in scenarios involving long, complex reasoning chains or multi-turn interactions, and does reasoning length exacerbate PII leakage despite interventions?
- **Basis in paper:** Limitations: "we primarily study short to medium-length reasoning traces; leakage dynamics in very long or multi-turn reasoning remain underexplored." Also, the paper mentions longer CoT tends to increase leakage, but interventions were tested on shorter traces.
- **Why unresolved:** Real-world applications often involve extended reasoning; if leakage increases with length, interventions may not scale.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Evaluate interventions on datasets requiring long CoT (e.g., multi-step math problems, lengthy analysis) and multi-turn dialogues, measuring leakage as a function of CoT length and turn count.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** How reliable are automated evaluation metrics (e.g., LLM-as-a-Judge) for assessing privacy and utility in CoT, and how do they correlate with human judgments and more rigorous privacy definitions?
- **Basis in paper:** Limitations: "we employ an independent large language model, GPT-4o-mini, as a judge... these scores inherit biases and calibration issues from the judge model itself and should be interpreted as relative rather than absolute measures." Also, deterministic metrics may miss PII.
- **Why unresolved:** Without human validation or ground truth, we cannot trust that automated scores truly reflect privacy compliance or utility. Biased judges could mislead conclusions.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct human evaluation studies on a subset of model outputs, comparing human privacy/utility scores with LLM-as-a-Judge scores and deterministic metrics, and analyze discrepancies.

## Limitations
- Unreleased datasets (PII-CoT-Bench and evaluation prompts) block exact replication and hinder verification of privacy definitions.
- Unspecified SFT hyperparameters (LoRA rank, learning rate) and PII sensitivity weights reduce reproducibility and may affect results.
- Reliance on GPT-4o-mini as judge and limited evaluation scope (short CoT, synthetic prompts) may not capture real-world privacy dynamics or human judgment.

## Confidence
- **PE efficacy for high-capability models:** Medium
- **SFT necessity for weaker models:** Medium
- **Dataset as causal enabler:** Low
