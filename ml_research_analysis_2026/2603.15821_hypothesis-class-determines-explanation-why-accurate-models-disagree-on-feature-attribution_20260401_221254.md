# Hypothesis Class Determines Explanation: Why Accurate Models Disagree on Feature Attribution

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.15821  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15821  
- **Reference count:** 13  
- **Primary result:** Prediction-equivalent models from different hypothesis classes disagree on SHAP explanations (cross‑class mean ρ=0.415, intra‑class ρ=0.676); an explanation lottery (ρ<0.5) occurs in 35.4% of cross‑class pairs and persists asymptotically.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates whether models that produce identical predictions necessarily yield the same feature attribution explanations. Training tree, linear, and neural classifiers on 24 binary classification datasets, the authors focus on instances where all models agree on the output. They compute SHAP values and measure pairwise Spearman rank correlations. They find that while models within the same hypothesis class (e.g., all trees) agree strongly, models from different classes disagree substantially, even though they are functionally equivalent on the selected instances. An "explanation lottery"—pairs with low correlation—appears in over a third of cross‑class model pairs. A controlled experiment with fixed training splits shows the gap is not a training artifact but a structural property of the hypothesis class. Theoretically, the gap is shown to arise from feature interactions: tree models represent them directly, while linear models cannot, leading to divergent attributions. The effect persists asymptotically. To flag unreliable explanations, the authors propose the Explanation Reliability Score R(x), which measures consensus across models; values below 0.5 indicate high lottery risk.

## Method Summary
The study trains multiple classifiers (XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost, RandomForest, Logistic Regression, and an MLP) on 24 tabular binary classification datasets using default hyperparameters and three random seeds. For each dataset and seed, an 80/20 stratified train‑test split is created. After training, the test set is filtered to retain only instances where all models produce the same prediction. SHAP values are computed via TreeSHAP for tree models and KernelSHAP (with 1000 samples) for the linear model and MLP. For each surviving instance, pairwise Spearman rank correlations between SHAP vectors are calculated. These are aggregated to produce mean intra‑class (e.g., tree‑tree) and cross‑class (e.g., tree‑linear) correlations, as well as the lottery rate (fraction of pairs with ρ<0.5). To isolate the effect of hypothesis class from training variance, a same‑split experiment fixes one split and compares multiple models trained on it. Theoretically, the authors analyze how SHAP decomposes for linear and tree models, showing that feature interactions introduce terms that diverge across classes. The Explanation Reliability Score R(x) is defined as the mean pairwise ρ across an ensemble of models for a given instance, used to identify cases where explanations are unstable.

## Key Results
- Cross‑class SHAP agreement (mean ρ=0.415) is much lower than intra‑class agreement (ρ=0.676), leading to an explanation lottery (ρ<0.5) in 35.4% of cross‑class pairs.  
- A same‑split control experiment isolates the hypothesis‑class effect: the gap persists with Δ=0.631 and a lottery rate of 61.9%.  
- Disagreement heavily involves top‑3 decision‑relevant features: 76.8% of cross‑class pairs differ on at least one such feature. The introduced Reliability Score R(x) flags unstable explanations (R<0.5).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Different hypothesis classes have inherently different representational capacities, leading to structurally divergent SHAP attribution patterns even when predictions are identical.  
- **Mechanism:** SHAP values are a deterministic functional of the model's prediction function. For linear models, the linearity axiom forces SHAP values to collapse to weight‑scaled deviations (`φ_j = w_j(x_j - E[X_j])`), completely blind to feature interactions. Tree models, through axis‑aligned splits, implicitly encode interactions; their SHAP values include cross‑product terms (`φ_i ≈ α_ij(x_i - x̄_i)(x_j - x̄_j) + β_i(x_i - x̄_i)`). When the true data‑generating process `f*` contains interactions, trees can represent them directly while linear models must distribute attribution across main effects and correlated proxies. This structural difference in the attribution formula persists even when both models achieve the same input‑output mapping.  
- **Core assumption:** SHAP values are a well‑defined, deterministic function of the conditional expectation `E[M(X)|X_S = x_S]`.  
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract identifies hypothesis class as the structural driver; Section 4.1 Lemmas 1–2 formally derive the divergent SHAP formulas for linear and tree models; related work shows within‑class multiplicity but not cross‑class divergence.  
- **Break condition:** The divergence disappears only if `f*` is purely additive (no interactions) or if the hypothesis classes are functionally equivalent in `L²(p)`. In practice, with correlated features, interactions are nearly ubiquitous.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Feature interactions in the data‑generating process `f*` are the engine that creates explanation disagreement across hypothesis classes. The strength and prevalence of interactions determine the magnitude of the Agreement Gap.  
- **Mechanism:** When `f*` contains an interaction `(i,j)` (`∂²f*/∂x_i∂x_j ≠ 0`), the Bayes‑optimal tree model will utilize splits on both features, causing its SHAP values for `i` and `j` to include a term proportional to `(x_i - x̄_i)(x_j - x̄_j)`. The linear model's SHAP values lack this term. This creates a set of instances `X_flip` where the sign or rank of the attribution for feature `i` differs between the two model types. The measure of this set is bounded below by a constant times the interaction strength `|α_ij|`. This rank‑reversal directly reduces the Spearman correlation `ρ`.  
- **Core assumption:** The true data‑generating process `f*` contains at least one non‑zero feature interaction.  
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states the gap is driven by feature interactions; Section 4.1 Theorem 1, Claim 4 shows `∂E[∆]/∂I(f*) ≥ 0`; Section 5.1 shows a negative correlation between agreement and dimensionality (r=-0.251, p<0.001).  
- **Break condition:** If `f*` is perfectly additive (`I(f*)=0`), then `α_ij=0` for all pairs, and tree and linear SHAP values can converge.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The Explanation Lottery is not a finite‑sample or training artifact; it is an asymptotic, population‑level property of crossing hypothesis class boundaries. More data or better tuning does not resolve it.  
- **Mechanism:** As training size `|D|→∞`, both models within a hypothesis class converge to their respective Bayes‑optimal functions `f*_H` (Lemma 3). For linear models, `f*_linear` is the best linear predictor; for trees, `f*_tree` is the best piecewise‑constant function. If `f*` contains interactions, `f*_tree` will generally be closer to `f*` than `f*_linear`, but both remain different functions. Their induced SHAP value functions `φ_*tree(x)` and `φ_*linear(x)` thus converge to two different, fixed functions. The rank correlation between these fixed functions is bounded strictly below 1 by the interaction structure (Lemma 4). Therefore, the Agreement Gap `∆` converges to a positive constant `c'>0`. The same‑split experiment eliminates all training variance, confirming this asymptotic gap (`∆=0.631`).  
- **Core assumption:** PAC learning guarantees hold for the chosen hypothesis classes; i.e., they are learnable and converge to their population optima.  
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 4.1 Theorem 1, Claims 2 and 3 (Asymptotic Persistence, Split Invariance); Section 5.3 same‑split experiment (within‑class ρ=1.000±0.000, cross‑class ρ=0.369±0.321, lottery rate 61.9%).  
- **Break condition:** The gap persists unless the two hypothesis classes have the same closure in `L²(p)` (i.e., they can represent the same set of functions). This is generally false for tree vs. linear classes.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: SHAP Values (Shapley Additive Explanations)**  
  - **Why needed:** SHAP is the explanation metric used to measure disagreement. Understanding its computation (via conditional expectations and Shapley axioms) is key to seeing why linear models yield additive, interaction‑blind attributions while tree models can capture interactions through path‑dependent marginal contributions.  
  - **Quick check:** For a linear model `M(x)=w^T x`, why does SHAP for feature `j` simplify to `w_j(x_j - E[X_j])`? (Answer: The coalition value function `v(S)=E[M(X)|X_S]` is additively separable, so all marginal contributions are independent of the coalition `S`.)
- **Concept: Prediction Equivalence (`M1 ∼_D M2`)**  
  - **Why needed:** This is the experimental control. By holding predictions constant, the paper isolates the effect of hypothesis class on explanations, ruling out that disagreement is simply due to different models making different predictions.  
  - **Quick check:** What does `M1 ∼_D M2` mean, and why is it stronger than similar test accuracy? (Answer: It means `M1(x)=M2(x)` for all `x` in dataset `D`. It ensures the models are functionally identical on the observed data, so any explanation difference cannot be due to different classifications.)
- **Concept: Hypothesis Class (e.g., `H_tree`, `H_linear`)**  
  - **Why needed:** The paper's central claim is that the *class* of models (its representational constraints) determines explanation stability, not the specific algorithm or training run.  
  - **Quick check:** How does the paper demonstrate that the tree‑linear gap is a property of the hypothesis class and not a specific algorithm? (Answer: By showing that all tree variants (XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost, RandomForest) agree highly among themselves, all linear variants (Ridge, ElasticNet, SVM) agree among themselves, but any tree vs. any linear pair shows the gap. It persists across regularization and loss functions.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Models: XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost, RandomForest → `H_tree`; Logistic Regression (L2), Ridge, ElasticNet, LinearSVM → `H_linear`; MLP → `H_neural`.  
  Explanation: SHAP (TreeSHAP for trees, KernelSHAP for linear/neural); LIME for validation.  
  Metrics: Pairwise Spearman ρ between SHAP vectors; Lottery Rate L(τ) with τ=0.5; Agreement Gap Δ = ρ_intra - ρ_inter; Reliability Score R(x) (mean pairwise ρ).  
  Data: 24 tabular binary classification datasets from OpenML and COMPAS.
- **Critical path:**  
  1. Train models for each dataset/seed using default hyperparameters and identical train‑test splits.  
  2. Identify test instances where all models make the same prediction (`D_agree`).  
  3. Compute SHAP values for each model on `D_agree`.  
  4. For each model pair and each instance, compute Spearman ρ between SHAP vectors.  
  5. Aggregate ρ by same‑class and cross‑class; compute Δ and lottery rate.  
  6. Compute per‑instance R(x) as mean pairwise ρ across the ensemble; flag R(x)<0.5.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Controlling for prediction equivalence drastically reduces the number of usable instances (mean retention 68.3%) but is necessary for a clean causal claim.  
  - SHAP implementation differs (TreeSHAP exact vs KernelSHAP approximate); the phenomenon holds for both SHAP and LIME, but LIME's local linear approximation attenuates the gap.  
  - Default hyperparameters avoid optimization bias; the trade‑off is suboptimal individual accuracy, but the goal is explanation comparison.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Within‑class ρ far below 1 (especially in same‑split) suggests training instability or incorrect SHAP method (e.g., using KernelSHAP for trees).  
  - Cross‑class Δ near 0 may indicate the data has no interactions or that the linear model includes polynomial features.  
  - R(x) not discriminating high‑lottery instances suggests ensemble is too small or not diverse enough.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Replicate same‑split control:** On 2–3 datasets, fix a single split. Train 3 tree models and 3 linear models. Compute within‑class ρ (should exceed 0.99) and cross‑class ρ (should be below 0.5). Confirm Δ > 0.5 and lottery rate > 50%.  
  2. **Validate R(x) utility:** For the trained ensemble, compute R(x) per instance. For instances with R(x)<0.5, inspect top‑3 SHAP features across models. Expect >75% to have at least one top‑3 feature change.  
  3. **Stress test with neural networks:** Add an MLP to the ensemble. Verify: (a) neural‑neural ρ is lower than tree‑tree/linear‑linear due to optimization variance; (b) neural vs. tree/linear ρ is in the lottery range (<0.5), confirming cross‑class boundary effect generalizes.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How does hypothesis class choice affect algorithmic recourse and fairness auditing?
- **Basis in paper:** Explicitly stated: "This connection between hypothesis class choice and recourse equity has not been studied, and our findings motivate it as a direction for future work."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper measures explanation disagreement but does not investigate how it translates into different recourse recommendations or fairness assessments.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematically generate counterfactuals from prediction‑equivalent models across hypothesis classes on high‑stakes datasets and compare the resulting suggested actions and fairness metrics.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Does the Explanation Lottery generalize to non‑tabular data and transformer‑based models?
- **Basis in paper:** Limitations: "Extending the analysis to large language models and transformers is the most important open direction."
- **Why unresolved:** The study is confined to tabular binary classification; transformers have different inductive biases and hypothesis class geometry, so the cross‑class gap may not hold.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicate the same experimental pipeline (prediction‑equivalent models, SHAP/LIME) using transformer architectures (e.g., BERT, ViT) versus simpler baselines on standard NLP or vision tasks.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Is the observed lottery rate an artifact of SHAP, or does it occur with other attribution methods?
- **Basis in paper:** Limitations: "LIME results are directionally consistent but smaller in magnitude, which raises the question of whether the Lottery rate is partly a SHAP‑specific phenomenon."
- **Why unresolved:** Theoretical results rely on SHAP axioms; other methods (Integrated Gradients, attention rolls) might yield different disagreement patterns.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct a large‑scale comparison across multiple attribution methods on the same set of prediction‑equivalent models and datasets, measuring lottery rates for each method.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** What explains the elevated within‑class explanation disagreement for neural networks (34.4% lottery rate)?
- **Basis in paper:** Discussion: "The neural within‑class variance complicates the hypothesis class account and points toward a source of instability our theorem does not address."
- **Why unresolved:** Theorem 1 predicts near‑perfect within‑class agreement asymptotically, but neural networks show substantial variance even with fixed splits, suggesting unaddressed optimization factors.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Isolate contributions from initialization, optimizer choice, and architectural hyperparameters via controlled experiments on neural networks, and extend the theory to incorporate non‑convex optimization effects.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** How can the Explanation Reliability Score R(x) be reliably calibrated for new deployment contexts?
- **Basis in paper:** Acknowledged: "The thresholds (0.5 and 0.7) are derived from our datasets and have no formal statistical guarantees; applying them to a new deployment setting requires care."
- **Why unresolved:** Thresholds are data‑dependent and may not generalize; no theoretical guidance exists for setting them in unseen distributions.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Evaluate R(x) on a held‑out, diverse set of datasets not used in threshold derivation to assess calibration; develop a theory linking R(x) distribution to dataset properties (e.g., interaction density) for adaptive thresholding.

## Limitations
- The study is restricted to tabular binary classification tasks; it is unclear whether the explanation lottery generalizes to image, text, or multi‑class problems.  
- The theoretical analysis depends on SHAP's axioms and the specific forms of linear and tree models; different attribution methods may yield different patterns of disagreement.  
- The reliability thresholds for R(x) were derived empirically from the studied datasets and lack formal statistical guarantees for new deployments.
