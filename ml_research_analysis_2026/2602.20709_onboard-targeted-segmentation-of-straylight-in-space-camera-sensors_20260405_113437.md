# Onboard-Targeted Segmentation of Straylight in Space Camera Sensors

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.20709  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20709  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Developed an AI method for onboard semantic segmentation of straylight (solar flares) in space camera imagery to enable fail‑operational navigation, achieving mIoU 0.873 and Per‑artifact Recall 0.991 after fine‑tuning on a proprietary dataset.

## Executive Summary
This paper presents an AI‑based approach for onboard semantic segmentation of straylight (solar flares) in space camera imagery, aiming to enable fail‑operational navigation by masking corrupted pixels. The method employs a DeepLabV3 model with a MobileNetV3 backbone, leveraging pre‑training on the large and diverse Flare7k++ dataset to capture generic flare features, followed by fine‑tuning on a proprietary space‑specific dataset. To better reflect system‑level impact, the authors propose artifact‑centric evaluation metrics (Per‑artifact Recall, Precision, and mIoU) that assess the detection of entire straylight events, complemented by Gaussian smoothing of ground‑truth masks to address fragmentation. After fine‑tuning, the model achieves a per‑artifact recall of 0.991 and an mIoU of 0.873, effectively identifying faulty regions for exclusion from navigation pipelines. The architecture is designed for deployment on resource‑constrained spacecraft hardware, balancing segmentation accuracy with computational efficiency.

By enabling onboard real‑time straylight segmentation, the system can autonomously prevent navigation failures caused by solar flare corruption, increasing mission resilience and reducing reliance on ground intervention.

## Method Summary
The proposed method consists of a two‑stage training process. First, a DeepLabV3 model with a MobileNetV3‑Large backbone is pre‑trained for 100 epochs on the Flare7k++ dataset (7,962 images of 512×512 pixels) to learn general flare representations. Second, the model is fine‑tuned for 500 epochs on a proprietary dataset containing 1,000 space‑camera images (1024×1024 pixels) with synthetically injected straylight. The loss function is binary cross‑entropy, optimized with Adam (learning rate 0.0001). For evaluation, in addition to standard pixel‑wise precision, recall, and mean Intersection‑over‑Union (mIoU), the authors introduce artifact‑centric metrics: Per‑artifact Recall (PaR), Precision (PaP), and mIoU (PamIoU). Because ground‑truth straylight masks are often fragmented, these metrics require applying Gaussian smoothing (σ=1, truncate=5) to the masks prior to extracting connected components. The model design emphasizes low computational cost (output stride 16, atrous rates 6,12,18) to meet spacecraft constraints. The inference output is a binary mask that excludes straylight‑affected pixels from downstream navigation processing.

## Key Results
- After fine‑tuning on the proprietary dataset, the model achieves mIoU = 0.873 and Per‑artifact Recall = 0.991.
- The artifact‑centric metrics demonstrate near‑perfect detection of straylight events, indicating reliable masking for navigation safety.
- The DeepLabV3‑MobileNetV3 architecture meets onboard resource constraints while providing high segmentation accuracy, making it suitable for deployment on spacecraft.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Pre‑training on Flare7k++ provides generic flare features
- **Claim:** Pre‑training on the Flare7k++ dataset provides generic flare features that transfer to space straylight segmentation, improving robustness to diverse textures.
- **Mechanism:** The model learns low‑level edges and high‑level shapes common to flares from a large, varied collection of synthetic and real images. Fine‑tuning then adapts these features to the specific appearance of space‑camera straylight.
- **Core assumption:** Visual characteristics of flares in everyday scenes share enough similarity with space straylight for transfer learning to succeed.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "emphasizes generalization across diverse flare textures, leveraging pre‑training on a public dataset (Flare7k++)"
  - [section 3.2] Details the pre‑training process and shows visual improvement after fine‑tuning.
  - [corpus] Weak evidence; neighboring papers do not cover Flare7k++ specifically, but pre‑training is common in segmentation (e.g., SIS‑Challenge, BEVCar).
- **Break condition:** If the domain gap between Flare7k++ and space imagery is too large, pre‑training may degrade performance or fail to improve beyond training from scratch.

### Mechanism 2: Fine‑tuning on proprietary space dataset
- **Claim:** Fine‑tuning on the proprietary space‑specific dataset adjusts the model to accurately segment straylight as rendered for the mission scenario.
- **Mechanism:** Gradient‑based updates on space examples shift the pre‑trained weights to better match the target distribution, accounting for specific flare injection and background characteristics.
- **Core assumption:** The proprietary dataset, despite being synthetic, adequately represents real straylight distribution for effective adaptation.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "fine‑tuned on a proprietary space‑specific dataset"
  - [section 3.2] "Fine‑tuned model shows consistent improvement" and Table 3 (mIoU 0.188 → 0.873).
  - [corpus] Neighbor paper *Addressing Camera Sensors Faults in Vision‑Based Navigation* (arxiv:2507.02602) documents the dataset’s creation and scope.
- **Break condition:** Overfitting to dataset biases (e.g., unrealistic flare rendering) or insufficient data quantity leads to poor generalisation to real mission imagery.

### Mechanism 3: Efficient architecture for onboard deployment
- **Claim:** The DeepLabV3 + MobileNetV3 architecture meets the stringent computational and memory limits of spacecraft hardware while maintaining required segmentation accuracy.
- **Mechanism:** MobileNetV3 uses depthwise separable convolutions and inverted residuals to minimise FLOPs and parameters; DeepLabV3’s ASPP aggregates multi‑scale context at low cost; output stride 16 balances detail vs speed.
- **Core assumption:** The onboard computer (e.g., radiation‑hardened FPGA) can execute the model within the real‑time frame budget (e.g., processing each image before the next arrives).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "model design targets deployment on resource‑constrained spacecraft hardware"
  - [section 3.1] Model selection criteria: reduced memory footprint and FLOPs.
  - [section 2] Reviews embedded‑compatible segmentation models and cites space‑hardware studies.
  - [corpus] Limited direct evidence; some neighbors explore embedded AI (e.g., performance evaluation on Zynq), but not this exact model.
- **Break condition:** Inference latency exceeds the frame interval; memory usage surpasses available RAM; or accuracy drops below safety thresholds, making the model unsuitable for onboard use.

## Foundational Learning

### Semantic segmentation
**Why needed here:** The task is to classify every pixel as either straylight or nominal, which is a binary semantic segmentation problem.  
**Quick check question:** How does semantic segmentation differ from instance segmentation?  
Answer: Semantic segmentation assigns class labels without distinguishing individual object instances; instance segmentation separates each object instance.

### Transfer learning
**Why needed here:** Real space straylight data is extremely scarce. Pre‑training on a large external dataset (Flare7k++) provides a strong initialisation that can be adapted to the target domain with limited proprietary samples.  
**Quick check question:** Why is a lower learning rate typically used during fine‑tuning compared to pre‑training?  
Answer: To avoid overwriting the generic features learned from the large dataset; fine‑tuning makes only small, task‑specific adjustments.

### Resource‑constrained deep learning
**Why needed here:** Spacecraft have tight power, memory, and compute budgets, often lacking GPUs. The model must be efficient enough to run on radiation‑hardened but low‑performance hardware in real‑time.  
**Quick check question:** Name two architectural techniques used in MobileNetV3 to reduce computational cost.  
Answer: Depthwise separable convolutions and inverted residual blocks; also hard‑swish activation and squeeze‑and‑excite modules.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Flare7k++ dataset -> Pre‑training (100 epochs) -> Fine‑tuning (500 epochs) on proprietary dataset -> DeepLabV3‑MobileNetV3 model -> Evaluation (standard metrics & custom metrics with Gaussian smoothing) -> Binary mask output -> Integration into navigation pipeline (pixel exclusion, FDIR) -> Deployment on space‑qualified hardware.

### Critical path
1. Acquire/prepare Flare7k++ and proprietary datasets.
2. Pre‑train model on Flare7k++ (~100 epochs).
3. Fine‑tune on proprietary data (~500 epochs).
4. Evaluate on test set using both standard and custom metrics; apply Gaussian smoothing to ground truth for custom metrics.
5. Optimise model for target hardware (quantisation, pruning).
6. Integrate inference module into onboard navigation software.
7. Perform hardware‑in‑the‑loop testing with realistic image streams.

Dependencies: Dataset availability is the initial bottleneck; hardware specs must be known before optimisation.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Accuracy vs. speed:** Larger backbones or higher output stride improve segmentation detail but increase latency. MobileNetV3 stride=16 was chosen as a compromise.
- **Pre‑training source:** Flare7k++ provides texture diversity but introduces a domain gap; relying solely on space data would limit generalisation.
- **Metric alignment:** Optimising for per‑pixel mIoU does not guarantee system‑level availability. Custom metrics prioritise recall (detecting all artifacts) even at some precision cost.
- **Ground‑truth preprocessing:** Gaussian smoothing repairs fragmentation for reliable artifact‑centric evaluation but artificially inflates the ground‑truth area, invalidating standard metrics – an accepted trade‑off for system‑focused assessment.

### Failure signatures
- High false negative rate (low PaR): Undetected straylight corrupts navigation data, risking erroneous state estimation.
- High false positive rate (low PaP): Excessively many valid pixels are masked, starving the navigation filter and reducing accuracy.
- Overfitting: Training loss decreases while validation (custom) metrics stagnate or drop; indicates dataset bias or insufficient diversity.
- Deployment bottleneck: Inference time exceeds the real‑time budget (frame interval) or memory overflow on target hardware.
- Integration bug: Misalignment between mask and image coordinates leads to incorrect pixel exclusion, nullifying benefits.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Pre‑training benefit:** Train a model from scratch on the proprietary dataset and compare it to the fine‑tuned model (from Flare7k++). Expect the latter to show superior generalisation, especially on unseen flare textures, as measured by both standard and custom metrics.
2. **Architecture efficiency:** Replace MobileNetV3 with a heavier backbone (e.g., ResNet50) and measure FLOPs, memory use, and inference time on representative hardware. Quantify the accuracy‑vs‑speed trade‑off to justify the lightweight choice.
3. **Custom metric robustness:** Vary Gaussian smoothing parameters (sigma, truncate) and recompute PaR, PaP, PamIoU. Ensure metrics are stable; also compare raw vs. smoothed ground truth to demonstrate the necessity of smoothing for meaningful artifact‑centric evaluation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1:** How does the model's segmentation performance transfer from synthetic and publicly available flare datasets to real‑world space mission imagery, given the identified domain shifts in flare textures and background scenes?  
*Basis:* The future work explicitly states the need to "validate the model’s robustness against domain shift by transitioning from synthetic to real‑world mission imagery."  
*Why unresolved:* The current evaluation uses a proprietary dataset with synthetically injected flares and simulated backgrounds. The model's ability to generalize to actual in‑flight camera data remains unverified.  
*What evidence would resolve:* Empirical results from testing on a held‑out set of real, in‑flight space camera images containing straylight, with performance compared against the synthetic test set.

**Open Question 2:** What are the actual computational resource requirements (e.g., inference latency, memory footprint, power consumption) and real‑time processing capability of the proposed DeepLabV3‑MobileNetV3 model when deployed on target space‑qualified hardware?  
*Basis:* Future work prioritizes "hardware‑in‑the‑loop deployment on space‑qualified FPGAs to verify real‑time processing capabilities within strict computational constraints."  
*Why unresolved:* The paper details model design choices for onboard compatibility but presents only software‑based performance metrics; no benchmark on the specific, radiation‑hardened, resource‑constrained FPGA platforms intended for flight.  
*What evidence would resolve:* A hardware deployment report measuring end‑to‑end inference time, CPU/FPGA utilization, and memory usage on the specified space‑grade boards, along with a demonstration of meeting the strict timing budget.

**Open Question 3:** Does the integration of the straylight segmentation model into the onboard navigation pipeline measurably improve system‑level metrics such as navigation state estimation accuracy, fault recovery time, and overall mission availability during straylight events?  
*Basis:* Future work calls for the interface to be "functionally demonstrated" with evaluation to "specifically quantify system availability and resilience in the fail‑operational state."  
*Why unresolved:* The paper proposes a processing scheme and artifact‑centric metrics that *reflect* system impact, but the actual closed‑loop performance of the navigation system with and without the segmentation module is not evaluated.  
*What evidence would resolve:* A hardware‑in‑the‑loop or high‑fidelity simulation study where a vision‑based navigation algorithm processes image streams with injected straylight. System performance would be compared between scenarios using the segmentation model's mask and scenarios using ground truth or no mask.

**Open Question 4:** Can the proposed artifact‑centric evaluation metrics be reliably applied to other fault classes (e.g., Dust on Optics, Broken Pixels/Lines) and do they consistently correlate with the downstream impact on navigation performance across different fault types?  
*Basis:* The custom metrics were developed specifically for straylight and evaluated only on that class; their generalisation is not addressed.  
*Why unresolved:* The rationale for artifact‑centric over pixel‑based metrics is that missing an entire artifact is more critical, but this criticality may vary by fault type. The metrics' sensitivity and predictive power are not validated beyond straylight.  
*What evidence would resolve:* Applying the same artifact segmentation and smoothing pipeline to other fault classes, computing the custom metrics, and correlating these scores with navigation system performance to establish a consistent relationship across fault modalities.

## Limitations
- **Proprietary dataset:** Not publicly available, preventing independent verification of fine‑tuning results and generalization claims.
- **Missing training details:** Batch size, learning rate schedule, data augmentation, and normalization are unspecified, hindering exact replication.
- **No hardware benchmark:** The model has not been tested on actual spacecraft‑grade processors; real‑time feasibility remains speculative.

## Confidence
- Pre‑training transfer: Medium
- Fine‑tuning effectiveness: Medium
- Onboard hardware suitability: Low
- Artifact‑centric metrics validity: Medium
