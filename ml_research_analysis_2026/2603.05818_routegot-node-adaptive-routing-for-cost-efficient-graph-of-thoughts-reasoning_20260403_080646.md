# RouteGoT: Node-Adaptive Routing for Cost-Efficient Graph of Thoughts Reasoning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.05818
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05818
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: RouteGoT improves average accuracy by 8.1 percentage points and reduces output tokens by 79.1% compared to AGoT.

## Executive Summary
RouteGoT introduces node-adaptive routing for Graph of Thoughts reasoning to address the inefficiency of using heavy language models uniformly across all sub-tasks. By predicting sub-task difficulty and dynamically assigning small, medium, or large models, and by integrating a global token budget scheduler to control graph expansion, RouteGoT achieves a superior cost–accuracy trade-off. Evaluated on seven benchmarks, it significantly outperforms AGoT and other routing baselines, maintaining robust performance under varying budget constraints.

## Method Summary
RouteGoT operates in a Graph of Thoughts (GoT) framework where reasoning is structured as a graph of sub-tasks. The system trains a router from a large offline dataset of 20,000 instances across 12 datasets, using three Qwen3 model sizes (4B, 8B, 30B) to execute three strategies: IO (small), CoT (medium), Decompose (large). The router comprises three learned components: a Success Predictor (estimates likelihood of each strategy succeeding), a Budget Predictor (ordinal difficulty tier based on required tokens), and a PolicyNet (selects the best feasible action given the budget tier). During inference, a global budget scheduler tracks accumulated costs, reserves tokens for final synthesis, adapts branching width based on remaining budget, and triggers a plan-guided fallback when decomposition would exceed the budget. The graph is built incrementally: the root is decomposed by the large model, children are enqueued, and each node is processed by assigning the cheapest viable strategy while respecting a per-node budget cap. After all leaves are solved or the budget is exhausted, a large model synthesizes the final answer from collected results.

## Key Results
- Average accuracy gain of 8.1 percentage points over AGoT.
- Output token reduction of 79.1% compared to AGoT.
- Superior cost–accuracy trade-off across all evaluated baselines (IO, CoT, ToT, GoT*, AGoT, Random, KNN, RouteLLM, EmbedLLM, RTR).
- Robustness to varying total token budgets, maintaining stable accuracy and cost.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### 1. Dynamic Node-Level Model Assignment
RouteGoT reduces token consumption while preserving accuracy by assigning smaller models to sub-tasks that do not require heavy reasoning. The router predicts each node’s difficulty and success probability using the Success Predictor and Budget Predictor, then selects an action (IO, CoT, Decompose) bound to a specific model scale. Actions whose estimated cost exceeds a per-node budget cap are masked. The core assumption is that nodes exhibit heterogeneous difficulty; many intermediate sub-tasks are simpler than the root problem and can be solved accurately by smaller models. Evidence anchors include the abstract statement that RouteGoT “prioritizes strong models for planning and synthesis, while dynamically allocating lightweight models and cost-effective strategies to leaf subtasks based on predicted difficulty,” and Section 3.4 detailing the predictors and policy network. Inaccurate difficulty predictions or insufficient capability of smaller models can break this mechanism.

### 2. Global Budget Scheduler with Adaptive Branching and Fallback
Explicit budget control prevents token overrun and shapes graph expansion to stay within user-specified limits, enabling predictable trade-offs. The scheduler reserves tokens for final synthesis, tracks accumulated cost, and computes remaining budget. It adapts branching width based on the remaining budget ratio and restricts recursive decomposition to nodes marked Hard. When a Decompose plan’s estimated subtree cost would exceed the remaining budget, a plan-guided fallback converts it into a local solve using the plan as context. The core assumption is that graph expansion (depth/branching) is the primary driver of token variability; controlling expansion yields predictable costs without sacrificing necessary reasoning depth. Evidence anchors include the abstract mention of “explicit budget constraints into a global inference scheduler to control graph expansion” and Section 3.5 describing synthesis reserve, depth constraints, and fallback. Inaccurate subtree cost estimates or classification errors can cause premature fallback or budget overrun.

### 3. In-Graph Budget-Aware Policy Learning
Learning a policy that jointly considers success likelihood and token cost, conditioned on budget tier, yields better utility under constraints than heuristic or static routers. PolicyNet inputs the success logits for each action and the predicted budget tier, and outputs a distribution over actions. Training uses a soft target favoring successful actions within the feasible set (cost ≤ node’s budget tier) and preferring lower-cost actions among them. At inference, actions are masked if their estimated cost exceeds the node’s effective budget. The core assumption is that the relationship between node features and optimal action under a budget constraint can be learned from offline logs of the three strategies. Evidence includes the formalization in Section 3.4.3 and the experimental results showing lowest mean utility regret (0.087) and highest oracle match rate (40.0%) among routers. Limitations include potential distribution shift between training logs and test queries, and reliance on accurate cost logging.

**Why needed:** The core innovation of RouteGoT is explicit budget controllability. One must understand how total token budget (B_total) is partitioned into expansion budget and synthesis reserve, and how remaining budget affects node processing and graph growth.

### Concept: Model Routing and Strategy Selection

**Quick check question:** What model scales are bound to the IO, CoT, and Decompose actions in the implemented system?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Router (Success Predictor, Budget Predictor, PolicyNet) -> Global Budget Scheduler -> Executor -> Graph Manager; feedback loops: Executor returns tokens and results to Graph Manager; Global Budget Scheduler updates accumulated cost and remaining budget for next node.

### Critical path
1. Root node created from input question.
2. Root decomposed by Large model (Decompose action) producing K sub-tasks and a summary.
3. Sub-tasks enqueued.
4. While queue not empty and remaining budget > 0: pop node, predict success and budget, select action with cost masking.
   - If action = Decompose and node depth allowed: generate children and branch summary; estimate subtree cost; if within remaining budget, enqueue children; else fallback to SolveWithPlan and mark node solved.
   - Else: execute chosen action (IO/CoT) and mark node solved.
5. After loop, if all leaves solved, synthesize answer using Large model; otherwise include UNSOLVED markers.

### Design tradeoffs
- Budget vs Accuracy: Tighter budgets force cheaper actions, potentially lowering accuracy; RouteGoT uses large models only when necessary.
- Prediction reliability: Router and scheduler rely on learned predictors; distribution shift reduces performance.
- Overhead: Routing adds small model calls and planning; system caps depth (3) and nodes (50) to bound worst-case.
- Fallback cost: Decomposition tokens are spent even when fallback prevents expansion, risking token waste.

### Failure signatures
- Excessive token usage: Router over-selects Decompose or cost estimates underestimate.
- Low accuracy on complex tasks: Router under-selects Decompose for hard nodes or small models fail on subtasks.
- Frequent fallbacks: Budget predictor too conservative or subtree estimator too high.
- Early budget exhaustion: Scheduler too aggressive in reserving tokens or branching/depth too restrictive.

### First 3 experiments
- **Baseline comparison:** Run AGoT and RouteGoT on 5–10 examples from HotpotQA. Record token counts and accuracy. Verify routing decisions for node-level model assignment.
- **Ablation of Budget Predictor:** Replace the ordinal Budget Predictor with a continuous cost regressor (as in the w/o BP variant). Compare token usage and accuracy; expect higher consumption without accuracy gain.
- **Budget sweep:** For a fixed dataset (GPQA), vary total token budget (e.g., 1k–50k tokens). Plot accuracy vs. tokens for RouteGoT and baseline RTR; expect RouteGoT to maintain a more stable frontier.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### 1. Hierarchical Routing Pipeline
**Question:** How would a hierarchical routing pipeline, where a lightweight gateway first decides between "skip graph" vs. "construct graph" and then applies node-level routing only for complex queries, impact the overall cost-accuracy trade-off?  
**Basis:** The conclusion proposes this direction but does not implement it.  
**Why unresolved:** The gateway adds overhead and risk of misclassification; its net benefit is unknown.  
**Resolving evidence:** Build a binary classifier gateway and compare its accuracy/cost curve against always-constructing-a-graph (current RouteGoT) on a mix of simple and complex queries.

### 2. Granular Cost Estimation Beyond Ordinal Tiers
**Question:** Can the ordinal, tier-based budget prediction be replaced or augmented with a more granular, uncertainty-aware cost estimator to further reduce token waste while maintaining robust budget adherence?  
**Basis:** Ablation shows removing BP increases token usage massively, but cost prediction MAE remains ~500-600 tokens.  
**Why unresolved:** It is unclear if a continuous predictor with risk-aware constraints (e.g., CVaR) could achieve similar safety with finer control.  
**Resolving evidence:** Compare the current BP against a Bayesian/quantile cost predictor measuring overrun/underrun rates and the resulting Pareto frontier.

### 3. Dynamic Depth Allocation
**Question:** To what extent does performance depend on the fixed maximum graph depth (3), and would a learned, dynamic depth allocation per node/task type improve robustness on problems requiring deeper reasoning?  
**Basis:** The paper caps depth at 3 and restricts deeper recursion to 'Hard' nodes; no analysis on depth sensitivity.  
**Why unresolved:** It is unknown if depth=3 is optimal across tasks; deeper needs might be cut off.  
**Resolving evidence:** Sweep depth limits (2–5) per benchmark; optionally learn a depth budget predictor and compare.

### 4. Interpretable Features for IO Pruning
**Question:** What precise linguistic or semantic features cause the router to select the "IO" action for pruning, and can these be distilled into an interpretable rule set replicating the learned router’s efficiency?  
**Basis:** The case study shows one example (spatial inconsistency) but no systematic analysis.  
**Why unresolved:** The router is a black-box; the underlying signals for low-difficulty decisions are not identified.  
**Resolving evidence:** Apply post-hoc explanation tools (SHAP/LIME) to router inputs on a validation set, extract dominant features, and test a rule-based proxy.

### 5. Model Size Spectrum and Generalization
**Question:** How does performance scale with the size and capability gap between Small, Medium, and Large models? Is the Qwen3 4B/8B/30B configuration optimal, or would a different capability-cost spectrum yield a better trade-off?  
**Basis:** Actions are bound to fixed scales; the paper does not study sensitivity to different model families or gaps.  
**Why unresolved:** The router is tuned for a specific triplet; it may not generalize if the capability hierarchy changes.  
**Resolving evidence:** Swap one or more backbone models (same family different sizes or different architecture), re-tune router if needed, and compare the Pareto frontier.

## Limitations

- **Implementation gaps:** Missing details on model variants, token counting, and key hyperparameters (λ_rank, Budget Predictor weights, synthesis reserve) hinder exact replication.
- **Baseline fidelity:** Insufficient description of some baselines (AGoT, RTR) raises risk of unfair comparisons.
- **Cost estimator noise:** The online subtree cost estimator is underspecified; inaccuracies could lead to routing errors and fallback inefficiencies.
- **Distribution shift:** Some evaluation benchmarks (e.g., Crosswords) may be out-of-distribution relative to the training pool, limiting generalizability.
