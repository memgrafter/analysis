# Humans and transformer LMs: Abstraction drives language learning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.17475
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17475
- Reference count: 22
- Primary result: Transformer language models acquire linguistic categories through an abstraction-first process, where class-level generalizations emerge earlier than item-specific learning, and different syntactic constructions are learned sequentially.

## Executive Summary
The study examines whether transformer language models (GPT-2) learn linguistic categories by first generalizing over abstract classes or by memorizing individual exemplars. Using Jensen‑Shannon divergence to track the evolution of next‑token predictions across 450 training checkpoints, the authors find that class‑level behavioral distinctions emerge before lexical item‑specific divergence for various verb classes and syntactic constructions. Moreover, the order of acquisition follows a sequence: syntactic subcategorization, then semantic argument structure, and finally non‑local dependencies. An exemplar‑first baseline fails to reproduce these patterns, supporting an abstraction‑driven account of language model learning.

## Method Summary
The researchers trained a GPT‑2 small model from scratch on a large English corpus, capturing checkpoints throughout pretraining. For each checkpoint, they evaluated the model on held‑out test sets containing verbs from different classes (e.g., to‑dative vs. motion verbs) and syntactic structures (transitivity, argument structure, filler‑gap dependencies). They computed Jensen‑Shannon divergence between the model’s next‑token probability distributions for items within a class, between classes, and against a baseline. This divergence trajectory reveals when the model begins to treat items differently. To compare with an exemplar‑first hypothesis, they implemented a simple count‑based model that predicts based on memorized item frequencies. By contrasting the learning dynamics of the two models, they assessed whether abstraction or exemplar memory better explains the observed patterns.

## Key Results
- Class‑level divergence (among members of a verb class) appears earlier in training than item‑level divergence (individual verbs diverging from the class average).
- Different syntactic constructions are acquired in a fixed sequential order, with simpler abstractions (subcategorization) preceding more complex ones (filler‑gap dependencies).
- The exemplar‑first baseline does not show the class‑first pattern, indicating that the abstraction‑first dynamics are not trivially explained by memorization.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Jensen‑Shannon divergence provides a continuous, symmetric measure of how different two probability distributions are. By applying it to the model’s next‑token predictions for various linguistic stimuli, the authors can quantify behavioral change in a fine‑grained, temporally resolved way. The finding that class‑averaged distributions diverge earlier than individual item distributions suggests the model first discovers a shared representation for a class and only later refines predictions for specific members. Similarly, the sequential emergence of divergence peaks for different constructions reflects an inductive bias toward acquiring simpler, more frequent abstractions before more complex ones. Together, these patterns support a learning mechanism where abstraction drives language acquisition, mirroring theories of human language learning.

## Foundational Learning
- **Jensen‑Shannon divergence**  
  *Why needed*: Quantifies similarity between probability distributions, enabling tracking of predictive behavior over time.  
  *Quick check*: Is it bounded between 0 (identical) and 1 (maximally different) and symmetric?  

- **Transformer language models (GPT‑2)**  
  *Why needed*: The architecture under study; predicts tokens via self‑attention.  
  *Quick check*: Does it use a decoder‑only stack with masked attention?  

- **Verb classes (to‑dative, motion verbs)**  
  *Why needed*: Test cases for abstract category formation.  
  *Quick check*: Do members share syntactic properties (e.g., “give” vs. “throw”)?  

- **Abstraction vs. exemplar learning**  
  *Why needed*: Theoretical framework for interpreting acquisition trajectories.  
  *Quick check*: Does the model show early convergence across a class before item‑specific differentiation?  

- **Syntactic constructions (transitivity, argument structure, filler‑gap)**  
  *Why needed*: Varying complexity to test sequential learning hypothesis.  
  *Quick check*: Are these constructions distinct in their grammatical properties?  

- **Checkpoint analysis**  
  *Why needed*: Captures dynamics of learning rather than only final performance.  
  *Quick check*: Are checkpoints frequent enough to observe transitional phases?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Input tokens -> Token embeddings -> Positional encoding -> Transformer layers (multiple) -> Layer normalization -> LM head (linear projection) -> Softmax -> Next‑token distribution

### Critical path
1. Preprocess and tokenize a large English corpus.  
2. Initialize GPT‑2 small and train with next‑token prediction objective.  
3. Every few thousand steps, save a checkpoint.  
4. For each checkpoint, run held‑out test sets for each linguistic construct, collecting next‑token probabilities.  
5. Compute Jensen‑Shannon divergence between distributions (class‑level, item‑level, cross‑class).  
6. Plot divergence over training steps, identify emergence order.  
7. Compare with exemplar‑first baseline trained on the same data.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Model scale**: Using GPT‑2 small allows dense checkpointing but may not scale to larger models where dynamics differ.  
- **Divorce of abstraction from frequency**: Class‑level averages may be influenced by member frequencies; matching frequencies is needed to isolate abstraction.  
- **Linguistic coverage**: Focusing on a few well‑understood constructions yields clear signals but may miss broader patterns.  
- **Baseline simplicity**: The exemplar‑first baseline is a rudimentary count model; more sophisticated exemplar theories remain untested.

### Failure signatures
- No consistent ordering of construction emergence (e.g., filler‑gap before transitivity).  
- Class‑level divergence not preceding item‑level divergence for multiple verb classes.  
- Exemplar‑first baseline replicates the abstraction‑first pattern, undermining the claim.  
- High variance in divergence trajectories that obscures clear breakpoints.

### First three experiments
1. **Class vs. item divergence** – Replicate Experiment 1: measure Jensen‑Shannon divergence for to‑dative vs. motion verb classes across checkpoints.  
2. **Sequential acquisition** – Replicate Experiment 2: test the order of emergence for transitivity, argument structure, and filler‑gap dependencies.  
3. **Baseline comparison** – Train the exemplar‑first count model and compare its divergence trajectories to the transformer’s.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How generalizable are the observed abstraction‑first learning dynamics across different language model architectures, parameter scales, and languages?  
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors explicitly state as a limitation that their study "is limited by its focus on a single model architecture trained and evaluated on a single language" and suggest that testing "across languages" and on "different models" and "different sizes" is needed.  
- **Why unresolved**: The paper only analyzes GPT‑2 small in English. It is unknown if the sequential, class‑first emergence pattern holds for other architectures (e.g., decoder‑only models of different designs, encoder‑decoders) or morphologically richer languages where abstraction might operate at different levels.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Replicating the divergence‑based trajectory analysis (Experiments 1 & 2) on a diverse suite of models (e.g., LLaMA, GPT‑Neo, mBERT) and in multiple languages. A consistent pattern would support the claim that abstraction‑first is a general inductive bias of standard pretraining.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: What is the relative contribution of construction frequency versus inherent cognitive/architectural biases in determining the sequential order in which linguistic abstractions are learned?  
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors note that "acquisition order may in principle also arise from differences in construction frequency" and that "the role of frequency effects and abstraction‑driven learning biases in determining acquisition order must be teased apart in future work."  
- **Why unresolved**: The paper establishes the order (syntactic subcategorization → semantic argument structure → non‑local dependencies) but cannot disentangle whether this sequence is driven by (a) the model’s architectural preference for simpler abstractions first, (b) the statistical prevalence of simpler constructions in the training corpus, or (c) an interaction of both.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Controlling for surface frequency and complexity in the training data (e.g., constructing a synthetic corpus where construction frequencies are counterbalanced) and observing if the learning order remains stable. Alternatively, analyzing the internal representations (e.g., probing for abstract category representations) at the predicted breakpoints could show if simpler representations become stable before more complex ones.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Are the abstractions learned by LMs best characterized as stable, feature‑based categories (e.g., a “to‑dative verb” prototype) or as dynamic, construction‑specific schemas that are sensitive to context?  
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors conclude by asking a core question their dataset may help answer: "are LM abstractions best understood at the word‑level or a more dynamic construction‑level?" They contrast learning classes like “transitive verbs” with potentially more flexible, usage‑based generalizations.  
- **Why unresolved**: Their metrics (e.g., average divergence across a verb class) treat class membership as static. They do not test if the model’s notion of a “to‑dative verb” changes depending on the surrounding discourse or if the abstraction is a rigid feature. The exemplar‑first account they contrast against often involves graded, context‑sensitive generalizations.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Designing experiments that probe the stability of class‑level representations across varying linguistic contexts (e.g., does the model treat “give” identically in a literal vs. metaphorical “to” dative?). Analyzing attention patterns or neuron activations for class‑representative units could reveal whether representations are invariant (abstraction‑as‑category) or variable (abstraction‑as‑schema).

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Can LMs learn abstract morphological categories (e.g., the derivational suffix *‑ness* creating nouns from adjectives) with an abstraction‑first trajectory analogous to syntactic constructions?  
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] In the limitations, the authors state: "abstraction occurs at different levels of linguistic representation, including below the word level. Derivational suffixes… is another example of abstraction… Whether LMs can productively combine and use such suffixes and whether this displays the same abstraction‑first learning… would be an interesting avenue for future work."  
- **Why unresolved**: The study focused on phrasal/syntactic verb classes. It is an open question if the same learning dynamics apply to sub‑word morphological productivity, which may rely on different representational mechanisms (e.g., character‑level or morpheme‑level processing) and could be more or less abstraction‑prone.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Extending the divergence‑based trajectory method to morphological classes (e.g., tracking predictions for stems that take *‑ness* vs. those that do not, across a range of base adjectives). Comparing the onset of class‑level divergence to item‑specific divergence for individual stems would test the abstraction‑first hypothesis at the morphological level.

### Open Question 5
- **Question**: To what extent can alternative exemplar‑based learning mechanisms (e.g., retrieved exemplar similarity, connectionist pattern completion) produce learning trajectories that mimic the “abstraction‑first” pattern observed in GPT‑2?  
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The authors implement a simple count‑based, exemplar‑first baseline that fails to replicate the class‑first pattern. However, they note that modern LLMs’ high parameter count "in principle allows them to memorize," and critics suggest LMs might be exemplar‑based. The paper’s baseline is a simplified exemplar model; more sophisticated exemplar or hybrid models might still align with the data.  
- **Why unresolved**: The test of the exemplar‑first account was limited to one operationalization (co‑occurrence vectors). More nuanced exemplar theories (e.g., Bybee’s schema‑based exemplars, or memory‑augmented neural networks that retrieve similar instances) could potentially generate smooth, class‑level divergences early if many overlapping exemplars for a class are encountered. The paper does not rule out these models.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Implementing and training other exemplar‑inspired architectures (e.g., k‑NN augmented LMs, networks with explicit memory buffers) on the same data and applying the same divergence analysis. If such models exhibit a class‑first trajectory, it would challenge the strong conclusion that abstraction‑first is necessary; it would suggest that certain forms of exemplar storage can produce macro‑level patterns resembling abstraction.

## Limitations
- Single‑model (GPT‑2 small) and single‑language (English) focus limits generalizability across architectures and linguistic diversity.  
- Jensen‑Shannon divergence may conflate statistical similarity with true abstraction, and class‑level frequency advantages could drive earlier emergence.  
- The exemplar‑first baseline is simplistic, and the set of syntactic constructions examined is limited.

## Confidence
- Class‑level precedes item‑level divergence: **High**  
- Sequential acquisition of constructions: **Medium** (frequency confounds possible)  
- Class‑generalization always precedes item‑specific learning: **High**

## Next Checks
- **Scale and architecture variation**: Replicate the divergence analysis on models of different sizes (GPT‑2 small/large, GPT‑3, LLaMA) and architectures (encoder‑decoder) to test universality.  
- **Frequency‑controlled corpus**: Train a model on a synthetic corpus where each verb class and its members have matched total token frequency to disentangle abstraction from frequency effects.  
- **Higher‑resolution tracking**: Increase checkpoint frequency (e.g., every 0.1% of training) to capture finer‑grained transitions and verify the sharpness of abstraction onset.
