# Evaluating Large Language Models' Responses to Sexual and Reproductive Health Queries in Nepali
## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.22291
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22291
*   **Reference Count:** 40
*   **Primary Result:** Only 35.1% of LLM responses to 14,132 Nepali sexual and reproductive health queries met all criteria to be considered "proper" (accurate, usable, and safe).

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates ChatGPT's (GPT-3.5) performance on a large dataset of real-world Nepali sexual and reproductive health (SRH) queries. Researchers developed the LEAF framework—a multi-criteria rubric assessing accuracy, language, usability (relevance, adequacy, cultural appropriateness, length, updatedness), and safety—to manually annotate responses. While 62.1% of responses were factually accurate, most had significant usability or safety deficits, with inadequacy being the most common gap. A small sample showed GPT-4 improved overall "proper" response rates, but the findings underscore that high accuracy is insufficient for safe, effective deployment in sensitive, low-resource linguistic contexts.

## Method Summary
The authors constructed an end-to-end evaluation pipeline. First, they iterated with SRH experts to define the LEAF framework's 11 criteria. They built a web platform for data collection (user chat interface), model serving (TGI server interfacing with OpenAI/Anyscale APIs), and expert annotation. The RAG variant used 22 curated Nepali SRH documents. Field data comprised 14,132 multi-turn conversations from 9,035 Nepali users. Four SRH experts then manually annotated each valid LLM response against every LEAF criterion using a custom labeling interface.

## Key Results
*   A mere 35.1% of all responses were scored as "proper" (meeting all positive criteria).
*   Although 62.1% of responses were accurate, 43.8% of these accurate responses still contained critical usability or safety gaps; inadequacy (failure to fully address the query) accounted for 74.0% of all gaps.
*   A GPT-4 sample increased the proper response rate by 12 percentage points (from 1% to 13%) compared to GPT-3.5, and showed reductions in inaccuracy and irrelevance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The LEAF framework's strength lies in its multi-dimensional, expert-informed assessment for a sensitive domain. It moves beyond a singular accuracy metric by decomposing "usability" and "safety" into specific, actionable gaps (e.g., cultural misalignment, insufficient detail). This granularity reveals that even factually correct answers can be practically harmful or useless if they are irrelevant, inadequate, or culturally inappropriate. The use of domain experts (SRH specialists) for annotation is critical for capturing nuances—especially cultural appropriateness and safety—that automated metrics would miss in a low-resource language context like Nepali.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Multi-Criteria Evaluation Frameworks (LEAF):** Why needed: Sensitive domains (e.g., health) require more than factual correctness; usability and safety are paramount. Quick check: Can your rubric identify responses that are accurate but culturally tone-deaf or medically incomplete?
*   **Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) for Low-Resource Languages:** Why needed: LLMs have limited knowledge of niche, local-language health information. RAG grounds responses in verified documents. Quick check: Does adding a Nepali SRH corpus improve factual coverage without introducing retrieval errors or irrelevance?
*   **Expert Annotation for Cultural & Domain Nuance:** Why needed: Automated metrics fail on subtle criteria like "cultural appropriateness." Native-speaking domain experts are essential. Quick check: Do your annotators have the SRH and cultural expertise to judge sensitivity and appropriateness?
*   **Script Variation Handling (Devanagari vs. Romanized Nepali):** Why needed: User queries may be in either script; models may perform inconsistently or respond in the "wrong" script. Quick check: Is your evaluation stratified by input script? Does model performance drop significantly on Romanized input?
*   **Evaluating in Low-Resource Language Contexts:** Why needed: Most LLM benchmarks are in high-resource languages. Evaluation must account for linguistic diversity, data scarcity, and local context. Quick check: Are your test queries representative of real user language (mix of scripts, dialects, colloquialisms)?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**
User -> Web Chat Interface -> TGI Server (LLM API) -> Model Response -> Labeling Interface -> SRH Expert Annotators (per LEAF criterion) -> PostgreSQL Database (Stores Scores & Metadata)

**Critical Path:** Query ingestion -> LLM response generation -> Expert multi-criterion annotation -> Metric aggregation (e.g., % proper, gap prevalence).

**Design Tradeoffs:**
*   *Manual vs. Automated Scoring:* Accuracy, language, and length can be auto-checked; relevance, adequacy, cultural appropriateness, and safety require expert human judgment, creating a bottleneck.
*   *RAG Implementation:* Using a small, curated document set (22 files) improves factuality but may limit coverage and add retrieval latency.
*   *API vs. Local Model:* Using OpenAI/Anyscale APIs simplifies deployment but introduces cost, potential data privacy concerns, and dependency on external model versions.

**Failure Signatures:**
*   Low inter-annotator agreement (Cohen’s κ < 0.6) on adequacy, relevance, or cultural appropriateness.
*   Significant performance degradation on Romanized Nepali queries compared to Devanagari.
*   High "safe but irrelevant/ inadequate" rates, indicating the model prioritizes safety over usefulness.

**First Three Experiments:**
1.  **Pilot Annotation Reliability:** Have 3 SRH experts annotate the same 200 responses. Compute Cohen’s κ for each criterion; target >0.7. If κ is low for adequacy/cultural, revise the rubric with concrete examples.
2.  **Script Sensitivity Test:** Run the same model on 500 paired queries (Romanized and Devanagari versions). Measure the delta in "proper" response rate. A >15% drop on Romanized input confirms a critical handling flaw.
3.  **Baseline Replication with Open-Source Model:** Evaluate a publicly available model (e.g., Llama 3) on a 1,000-query subset using the LEAF rubric. Compare its accuracy and gap profile to the reported GPT-3.5 baseline to assess generalizability beyond ChatGPT.

## Limitations
*   The full 14,132-query dataset and the exact 22-document RAG corpus are not public, preventing exact replication of topic distribution and retrieval-augmented results.
*   The detailed LEAF annotation codebook, decision rules, and conflict-resolution protocol are unpublished, introducing subjectivity—especially for nuanced gaps like "cultural appropriateness."
*   The evaluation is restricted to ChatGPT (GPT-3.5/4); performance on open-source or locally-deployable models for Nepali SRH is unknown.
*   The rarity of safety issues (<1%) may lead to under-counting; critical harmful incidents could be missed in a dataset of this size.

## Confidence
*   **Proper response rate (35.1%):** *Medium* (large N provides strength, but dependent on subjective multi-criteria annotation).
*   **Usability gap prevalence (inadequacy at 74.0%):** *Low-Medium* (high subjectivity in "adequacy" scoring without standardized guidelines).
*   **GPT-4 improvement vs. GPT-3.5:** *Medium-High* (direct comparison on same queries, though magnitude affected by annotation subjectivity).
*   **"Accuracy alone is insufficient":** *High* (robustly supported by the finding that 43.8% of accurate responses had usability/safety deficits).

## Next Checks
1.  Conduct an **interannotator reliability audit** on a shared 200-pair subset with 3+ Nepali SRH experts; report Cohen’s κ per criterion. κ<0.6 for adequacy/relevance confirms rubric subjectivity.
2.  Perform a **script-stratified performance test**: re-run GPT-3.5/4 on 500 matched Romanized vs. Devanagari queries. A >15% drop in proper responses on Romanized inputs validates the script-handling concern.
3.  Execute an **external framework validation**: have a different team of Nepali SRH experts apply the LEAF rubric to a fresh 500 LLM-generated responses. Discrepancies >20% in proper-rate or gap distribution challenge framework robustness.
