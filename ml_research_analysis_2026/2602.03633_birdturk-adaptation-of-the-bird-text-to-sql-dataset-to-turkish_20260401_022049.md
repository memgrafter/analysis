# BIRDTurk: Adaptation of the BIRD Text-to-SQL Dataset to Turkish

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.03633
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03633
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Turkish causes consistent performance degradation; DIN-SQL achieves 49.02% EX compared to 43.16% for direct prompting, while Qwen2.5-Coder fine-tuning reaches up to 15.38% EX; mT5 fine-tuning remains near-zero.

## Executive Summary
BIRDTurk presents the first large-scale Turkish adaptation of the English BIRD Text-to-SQL benchmark. Using a controlled translation pipeline, the authors preserve database schemas and SQL execution semantics while localizing natural language and schema identifiers to Turkish. The pipeline includes deterministic AST-based SQL rewriting and automated execution equivalence checks, ensuring that translated queries remain semantically identical to the originals. Translation quality is statistically validated at 98.15% accuracy. The paper evaluates Turkish Text-to-SQL performance across direct prompting (in-context learning), agentic reasoning (DIN-SQL), and supervised fine-tuning with multilingual models. Results show that Turkish induces a consistent drop in execution accuracy compared to English, highlighting the challenges posed by Turkish’s agglutinative morphology and SOV word order. Agentic pipelines demonstrate stronger cross-lingual robustness, and architecture choices—particularly tokenizer efficiency and instruction tuning—significantly impact fine-tuning scalability.

## Method Summary
The adaptation process begins by mapping all database identifiers (tables, columns) to Turkish ASCII snake_case using a large language model (Gemini 2.5 Flash) with collision resolution. The mapping is then applied to standardize identifiers in the evidence text. Natural language questions and evidence are jointly translated into Turkish while freezing backticked identifiers and preserving constants. The original English SQL queries are parsed into an abstract syntax tree (AST), and only identifier nodes are rewritten according to the Turkish mapping; no neural SQL generation is used. An automated verifier executes both the English and Turkish SQL on the corresponding databases to ensure complete equivalence (EXECEQUAL). Any mismatches are flagged for human review via an LLM-as-a-judge system. Quality is further validated by statistically sampling 974 examples, yielding 98.15% translation accuracy. The resulting BIRDTurk dataset includes training and development splits and is used to benchmark Turkish Text-to-SQL under three paradigms: zero-shot prompting (ICL), the multi-stage DIN-SQL agentic pipeline, and supervised fine-tuning of mT5 and Qwen2.5-Coder models.

## Key Results
- Direct prompting EX drops from 58.21% (English) to 43.16% (Turkish).
- DIN-SQL agentic reasoning declines from 60.89% to 49.02%, showing a larger relative improvement over direct prompting in Turkish.
- Supervised fine-tuning with mT5 models yields <2% EX, while Qwen2.5-Coder scales effectively to 15.38% EX.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Turkish causes consistent performance degradation in Text-to-SQL due to structural linguistic divergence and model pretraining imbalance.
- **Mechanism:** Turkish's agglutinative morphology and Subject-Object-Verb (SOV) word order disrupt the linear token-to-SQL construct alignment that English-centric LLMs leverage. This forces models to perform more complex compositional reasoning across suffix-heavy tokens, exacerbated by tokenizers that over-segment Turkish, increasing input length and sparsity. Underrepresentation of Turkish in pretraining corpora limits exposure to these patterns.
- **Core assumption:** The performance gap is attributable primarily to language-inherent properties and data imbalance, not dataset construction artifacts.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "Turkish introduces consistent performance degradation—driven by both structural linguistic divergence and underrepresentation in LLM pretraining." [section 5.5] Explicitly links degradation to SOV order, agglutination, and pretraining scarcity. [corpus] Neighbors like TURSpider and Multispider document similar tokenization and structural challenges for Turkish and other typologically divergent languages.
- **Break condition:** If a model with perfect Turkish representation and morphology-aware architecture achieved parity with English on BIRDTurk, the primary causal attribution would shift away from linguistic divergence.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Agentic reasoning pipelines (e.g., DIN-SQL) demonstrate stronger cross-lingual robustness by reducing reliance on surface-level token alignment.
- **Mechanism:** Multi-stage decomposition (schema linking, intent classification, self-correction) enforces intermediate grounding in the schema and execution semantics. This bypasses the need for direct, linear mapping from Turkish surface forms to SQL syntax, mitigating issues from word order divergence and suffix fragmentation.
- **Core assumption:** The relative improvement of DIN-SQL over direct prompting in Turkish is due to the pipeline's structure, not just model scaling.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "agentic reasoning demonstrates stronger cross-lingual robustness." [section 5.4] Notes larger relative gains for DIN-SQL in Turkish vs. English, suggesting decomposition compensates for linguistic mismatch. [corpus] Related work (e.g., APEX-SQL, SQL-Trail) posits that multi-step, interactive exploration helps in complex, ambiguous settings—a principle that extends to cross-lingual ambiguity.
- **Break condition:** If agentic reasoning showed no relative improvement for Turkish compared to a linguistically aligned language, the mechanism would be undermined.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Supervised fine-tuning effectiveness on Turkish Text-to-SQL scales with model architecture (instruction tuning, tokenizer efficiency), not just multilingual coverage.
- **Mechanism:** Models like Qwen2.5-Coder combine instruction-following training (better adherence to structured generation tasks) with a tokenizer that better handles agglutinative suffixes, reducing the "multilingual tokenization tax." This preserves semantic integrity within the fixed context window, making the supervised signal more learnable. Standard mT5 lacks these properties, leading to near-zero learning.
- **Core assumption:** The observed scaling in Qwen results is causally linked to architecture and tokenization, not merely parameter count.
- **Evidence anchors:** [section 5.2] mT5 models show negligible gains (<2%) while Qwen scales from 1.24% to 15.38% EX. [section 4.1.2] Explicitly connects Qwen's tokenizer efficiency for Turkish (per Hacıfazlıoğlu et al., 2024) to its superior performance. [corpus] Papers on Turkish tokenization (Toraman et al., 2023) and model efficiency (TurkBench) support the importance of suffix handling.
- **Break condition:** If an mT5-sized model with Qwen's tokenizer and instruction tuning achieved comparable performance, the attribution to specific architecture choices would be confirmed.

## Foundational Learning

### Agglutinative Morphology & Tokenization
**Why needed here:** Turkish forms words by attaching multiple suffixes to a stem (e.g., "kullanıcıları" = user + plural + accusative). This creates many low-frequency surface forms, causing sparsity in subword tokenizers that are typically trained on English-centric data. The paper evidences this via increased Type-Token Ratio (+122%) and token count changes.
**Quick check:** How does adding the suffix "-lar" (plural) and "-ı" (accusative) to "kitap" (book) change its subword tokenization in a model like mT5 vs. one optimized for Turkish?

### Cross-Lingual Structural Divergence
**Why needed here:** English (SVO) and Turkish (SOV) have different canonical word orders. The paper illustrates how the SQL-logic-bearing verb ("getir") appears sentence-final in Turkish, breaking direct sequential alignment with the SELECT clause. This requires models to perform non-local reasoning to map intent to syntax.
**Quick check:** Translate the English query intent "Find cities with population greater than 1 million" into Turkish. Identify which morphemes hold the "greater than" logic and where the main verb ("find") appears relative to the subject and object.

### Execution-Centric Evaluation & Semantic Preservation
**Why needed here:** The primary validity criterion is execution equivalence (`Ren = Rtr`), not textual similarity of SQL strings. The pipeline uses AST rewriting to ensure only identifiers are changed, preserving logical structure. This is a core design principle to isolate the effect of language from dataset noise.
**Quick check:** If a Turkish query's SQL string differs syntactically from its English counterpart but returns the same result set on the database, is it considered a valid translation? What does this imply for evaluation?

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Schema Mapping Module -> Evidence Standardizer -> Joint Translator -> AST SQL Localizer -> Automated Verifier -> Statistical Validator -> LLM-as-a-Judge

### Critical Path
Schema Mapping (`M` creation) → Evidence Standardization → Joint Translation → AST Rewrite → Execution Equivalence Check. A failure in execution equivalence flags the item for the judge/correction loop, potentially requiring `M` update.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Translation vs. Native Authoring:** Chose controlled translation for cross-lingual control, accepting limitations in capturing naturally occurring Turkish phrasing (see Limitations).
- **AST Rewrite vs. Neural SQL Generation:** opted for deterministic rewrite to guarantee execution consistency, avoiding drift in complex SQL logic.
- **CLT Sampling vs. Exhaustive Annotation:** traded perfect per-sample certainty for scalable, statistically confident dataset-level quality assurance.

### Failure Signatures
1. **Execution mismatch (`Ren != Rtr`):** Indicates a broken SQL semantics during localization. Pipeline automatically flags.
2. **Schema hallucination:** Turkish SQL references a column not in `M` or the DB. Caught by structural integrity check.
3. **Backtick violation:** Translator modifies a frozen identifier. Caught by schema alignment check pre-translation.
4. **Low CLT sample accuracy (<98%):** Would suggest systemic translation flaws requiring pipeline revisitation.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Validate Pipeline Integrity:** Randomly sample 100 items post-AST-rewrite. Verify 100% execution equivalence with original English SQL. Confirms core preservation mechanism.
2. **Baseline Capillary Test:** Run `gemini-2.5-flash-lite` with the ICL prompt on 50 BIRDTurk dev items. Expect ~40-50% EX. Documents baseline degradation.
3. **Agentic Pipeline Sanity Check:** Run DIN-SQL (translated prompts) on same 50 items. Expect a relative gain (e.g., +5-10% EX over ICL). Confirms mechanism 2's local effect before full-scale evaluation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
**Question:**  
How does a *fully native* Turkish Text-to-SQL dataset—where questions and database contents are authored by Turkish speakers for Turkish use cases—compare to a translation-based benchmark like BIRDTurk in terms of linguistic naturalness, query distribution, and model performance?

**Basis in paper:**  
*Explicit*: The authors state BIRDTurk is translation-based and may not reflect "naturally occurring Turkish query formulations" (Limitations). They propose constructing a "fully native Turkish Text-to-SQL benchmark" as a future direction (Conclusion).

**Why unresolved:**  
Translation may impose English-centric phrasing or omit culturally specific query patterns. A native dataset could reveal different error modes, ambiguity patterns, or domain-specific challenges not captured by translation.

**What evidence would resolve it:**  
- Collect a native Turkish dataset parallel to BIRDTurk’s schema and complexity.
- Compare distributional shifts in question phrasing, entity references, and reasoning depth.
- Evaluate state-of-the-art models on both datasets to measure performance gaps and error correlations.

### Open Question 2
**Question:**  
What specific linguistic or structural properties of Turkish (e.g., SOV word order, agglutination, suffix-driven semantics) most significantly disrupt direct token-to-SQL alignment, and can targeted prompt engineering or model architectures mitigate these?

**Basis in paper:**  
*Explicit*: The authors attribute performance degradation to Turkish’s "structural linguistic divergence" (Abstract) and detail how SOV and agglutination break linear alignment (Section 1). They show agentic reasoning reduces the gap but do not isolate which linguistic factors contribute most.

**Why unresolved:**  
The paper demonstrates the *existence* of a gap but does not decompose it into constituent causes (e.g., word order vs. morphology vs. tokenization). Without this, interventions remain coarse.

**What evidence would resolve it:**  
- Controlled ablation studies: Synthetically generate Turkish queries with graduated linguistic perturbations (e.g., force SVO order, strip suffixes) and measure EX changes.
- Error analysis categorized by SQL construct (JOIN, WHERE, GROUP BY) and Turkish linguistic feature (case marking, verb finality).
- Test model variants with Turkish-specific tokenization or added positional encodings to see which factors are most addressable.

### Open Question 3
**Question:**  
Does the observed advantage of agentic reasoning (DIN-SQL) over direct prompting in Turkish stem primarily from (a) better handling of morphological ambiguity via intermediate decomposition, or (b) from the reinforcement of schema grounding that bypasses tokenization issues?

**Basis in paper:**  
*Explicit*: The paper notes DIN-SQL’s larger relative gains in Turkish (Section 5.4) and suggests it reduces "reliance on surface-level token alignment." However, it does not disentangle whether the benefit comes from step-wise semantic parsing (handling SOV/morphology) or from repeated schema linking (avoiding token fragmentation).

**Why unresolved:**  
Both mechanisms are plausible but not tested separately. The agentic pipeline includes multiple stages; the critical component for Turkish robustness is unknown.

**What evidence would resolve it:**  
- Ablate DIN-SQL components: run versions with/without schema linking, with/without self-correction, on Turkish vs. English.
- Measure alignment quality between intermediate representations (e.g., schema links, intent classifications) and the final SQL, comparing to direct prompting.
- Analyze whether corrections in DIN-SQL predominantly fix schema hallucination (tokenization issue) or mis-parsed relational logic (morphology issue).

### Open Question 4
**Question:**  
For supervised fine-tuning, why do standard multilingual models (mT5) fail to learn from BIRDTurk while instruction-tuned models (Qwen2.5-Coder) scale effectively? Is the primary factor (a) tokenizer efficiency for agglutinative languages, (b) code-specific pretraining, or (c) instruction-following capability?

**Basis in paper:**  
*Explicit*: The authors note mT5 remains near-zero (<2% EX), while Qwen scales to 15.38% EX (Section 5.2). They hypothesize Qwen’s tokenizer "achieves superior compression" for Turkish (citing Hacıfazlıoğlu et al., 2024) and that instruction-tuning helps.

**Why unresolved:**  
The paper selects Qwen for two reasons (tokenization + instruction-tuning) but does not isolate their contributions. mT5 is multilingual but not code-focused; Qwen is both code-focused and Turkish-efficient. The independent effect of each factor is unclear.

**What evidence would resolve it:**  
- Fine-tune matched model families (e.g., mT5 vs. code-mT5; Qwen base vs. instruct) on BIRDTurk.
- Control for tokenization: apply the same Turkish-optimized tokenizer to mT5 and measure change.
- Control for instruction-tuning: take a non-instruction Qwen model and compare to its instruct-tuned version.
- Measure learning curves and failure modes (e.g., schema hallucination, SQL syntax errors) across conditions.

### Open Question 5
**Question:**  
How does BIRDTurk’s "dirty data" and enterprise-scale complexity (inherited from BIRD) interact with Turkish linguistic properties? Do certain types of "external knowledge" reasoning (e.g., numeric calculation, domain terminology) become disproportionately harder in Turkish?

**Basis in paper:**  
*Explicit*: BIRDTurk inherits BIRD’s realistic databases ("dirty data", external knowledge). The authors state Turkish causes "consistent performance degradation" but do not break down whether degradation is uniform across query types or concentrated in reasoning-heavy categories (e.g., aggregation over ambiguous terms, multi-hop joins with numeric filters).

**Why unresolved:**  
BIRD’s design tests content-grounded reasoning. If Turkish models struggle more with numeric comparisons (e.g., "older than 20" → "yaşı 20’den büyük") or domain terms (e.g., movie ratings), it suggests language-specific bottlenecks in semantic parsing beyond syntax.

**What evidence would resolve it:**  
- Error analysis stratified by BIRD’s known difficulty factors: presence of external knowledge (e.g., unit conversion), aggregation complexity, number of joins.
- Compare per-category EX drop (English → Turkish) to see if some reasoning types (e.g., comparative logic) degrade more than others.
- Test if providing English translations of key domain terms in the prompt recovers performance, isolating the "terminology" vs. "structure" problem.

### Open Question 6
**Question:**  
To what extent does the translation pipeline introduce systematic biases in question phrasing or evidence wording that do not reflect how Turkish users would naturally ask database questions?

**Basis in paper:**  
*Explicit*: The authors acknowledge translation may not capture "naturally occurring Turkish query formulations" (Limitations) and use a single LLM (Gemini), risking "systematic stylistic biases." They also standardize phrasing (e.g., "How many" → "Kaç ... vardır?").

**Why unresolved:**  
Standardization forces formal, possibly unnatural Turkish. Real users might use colloquial ellipsis, pragmatic inferences, or varied phrasing that the pipeline does not generate. This could affect model generalization to real-world use.

**What evidence would resolve it:**  
- Collect a set of naturally occurring Turkish questions for BIRDTurk’s databases (e.g., from Turkish-speaking data analysts).
- Compare distribution of linguistic features (e.g., pronoun use, verb forms, question structure) between translated and native questions.
- Evaluate models trained on BIRDTurk on native questions—does performance drop further?

### Open Question 7
**Question:**  
Does the performance gap between English and Turkish primarily reflect (a) pretraining data imbalance (Turkish underrepresentation) or (b) inherent linguistic divergence? Can data augmentation (e.g., back-translation, synthetic Turkish data) close the gap without additional Turkish pretraining?

**Basis in paper:**  
*Explicit*: The authors attribute the gap to both "structural linguistic divergence and underrepresentation in LLM pretraining" (Abstract and Section 5.5). They do not disentangle these factors or test data-centric interventions.

**Why unresolved:**  
If pretraining imbalance is the main driver, then scaling Turkish data might help. If linguistic divergence is primary, architectural changes may be needed. The paper does not experiment with data augmentation or model adaptation beyond fine-tuning.

**What evidence would resolve it:**  
- Augment BIRDTurk training data with synthetic Turkish examples (e.g., paraphrase, back-translation from English) and measure EX gains.
- Compare models with similar capacity but different Turkish pretraining exposure (e.g., mT5 vs. Qwen, or a model further pretrained on Turkish corpora).
- Correlate model performance with the amount of Turkish data in pretraining, controlling for architecture.

### Open Question 8
**Question:**  
How robust are the CLT-based validation results for translation quality? Could systematic errors exist in low-frequency, linguistically complex queries (e.g., heavy ellipsis, pragmatic inference) that the random sampling might miss?

**Basis in paper:**  
*Explicit*: The paper uses CLT to sample 974 examples for human validation, achieving 98.15% accuracy. However, they note "a small subset of linguistically complex or highly implicit queries may still be affected" (Limitations).

**Why unresolved:**  
CLT guarantees dataset-level confidence but not per-stratum coverage. Rare error types (e.g., ambiguous comparatives, implicit temporal logic) might be underrepresented in the random sample, inflating the reported accuracy.

**What evidence would resolve it:**  
- Analyze the 18 flagged/incorrect examples from validation to characterize error types.
- Perform targeted human evaluation on potential high-risk categories (e.g., queries with "top-k", implicit aggregation, complex conditional logic).
- Compute confidence intervals for accuracy within these subpopulations, not just globally.

### Open Question 9
**Question:**  
Does BIRDTurk’s schema-only localization (not translating database cell values) create a mismatch for Turkish queries that reference string literals? Could translating values (e.g., "New York" → "New York" vs. "N York"? ) affect execution or model learning?

**Basis in paper:**  
*Explicit*: The pipeline "does NOT translate database cell values" to "avoid introducing a separate value-linking problem" (Section 3.1). This is a design choice to preserve executability but may reduce linguistic naturalness.

**Why unresolved:**  
Turkish users might expect localized values (e.g., city names in Turkish, translated categories). Leaving values in English could bias models toward code-switching or create unnecessary tokenization burdens. The trade-off between executability and naturalness is untested.

**What evidence would resolve it:**  
- Create a variant of BIRDTurk with selectively translated value literals (where unambiguous and culturally appropriate).
- Compare model performance on both variants, especially for string-matching conditions (WHERE name = 'X').
- Survey Turkish users on whether they would expect database values to be in Turkish or English in a real interface.

### Open Question 10
**Question:**  
Can the agentic reasoning advantage in Turkish be distilled into a more efficient architecture or prompting strategy that retains robustness without the full multi-stage overhead?

**Basis in paper:**  
*Explicit*: DIN-SQL improves over direct prompting in Turkish but is computationally heavier. The paper does not explore efficiency-robustness trade-offs or lightweight alternatives.

**Why unresolved:**  
Agentic pipelines are slower and more complex. If the benefit stems from specific intermediate checks (e.g., schema grounding), a simpler modification (e.g., constrained decoding, token-level alignment losses) might suffice.

**What evidence would resolve it:**  
- Ablate DIN-SQL stages to identify minimal effective set for Turkish.
- Experiment with single-pass models that include explicit schema-linking or self-correction tokens.
- Measure the correlation between intermediate step quality (e.g., schema link F1) and final EX in Turkish vs. English.

## Limitations
- Controlled translation may not capture natural Turkish phrasing; dataset is limited to the BIRD domain.
- Missing reproduction details: schema serialization, exact DIN-SQL prompts, fine-tuning hyperparameters, Gemini parameters, BIRD version, and evaluation script nuances.
- LLM-as-a-judge for translation quality may introduce bias; only 1.85% flagged cases were manually reviewed.

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (Turkish degradation due to linguistic divergence & pretraining imbalance): Medium
- Mechanism 2 (Agentic reasoning cross-lingual robustness): High
- Mechanism 3 (SFT scaling with architecture): Medium
- Translation quality (98.15% ±3%): High
