# Nonlinear multi-study factor analysis
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.18128
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.18128
- **Reference count**: 31
- **Primary result**: MSSVAE with spike-and-slab priors achieves high synthetic factor recovery (≥0.86 across metrics) and reveals biologically enriched shared and disease-specific factors in platelet RNA-seq.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the problem of separating shared and study‑specific latent factors in multi‑study high‑dimensional data, with platelet gene expression across diseases as a motivating example. The proposed method, MSSVAE, is a nonlinear variational autoencoder that incorporates spike‑and‑slab priors on decoder weights to enforce sparsity and implicitly select the number of factors. Under anchor feature assumptions, theoretical identifiability is proved. Synthetic experiments yield near‑perfect scores in consensus, relevance, recovery, and disentanglement. Application to a real cohort (1,463 samples, 5,000 genes, 6 disease groups) shows that the majority of factors are enriched for Gene Ontology biological processes, recovering known platelet functions and disease‑relevant pathways.

## Method Summary
MSSVAE extends the VAE framework to multi‑study settings by modeling each study’s data as a combination of shared latent factors (common to all studies) and study‑specific latent factors. The decoder is a neural network whose output weights are governed by spike‑and‑slab priors, encouraging each factor to influence only a sparse subset of features. This sparsity yields interpretable factor loadings and allows the model to automatically determine the number of active factors, effectively performing implicit model selection. Amortized variational inference is used to learn an encoder that maps data to approximate posterior distributions over the latent factors. The method’s identifiability relies on the presence of anchor features—variables that depend monotonically on a single latent factor—as formalized in Theorem 1 and Theorem 2.

## Key Results
- **Synthetic performance** (Gaussian data): Consensus 0.99, Relevance 0.86, Recovery 0.93, Disentanglement 0.95.
- **Real data** (platelet RNA‑seq, 6 disease groups): 96% of shared factors and 56% of disease‑specific factors are enriched for Gene Ontology biological processes, capturing known platelet functions and disease pathways.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
MSSVAE succeeds because it combines (1) anchor features that break rotational invariance and guarantee each latent factor has a unique, identifiable signature; (2) a flexible nonlinear decoder that can model intricate relationships between factors and features; and (3) spike‑and‑slab priors that produce sparse, interpretable factor loadings while implicitly controlling factor cardinality. The variational objective jointly optimizes reconstruction fidelity and a sparsity‑inducing regularizer, leading to factors that are both accurate and parsimonious. Theoretical guarantees under anchor assumptions provide a solid foundation for the empirical results.

## Foundational Learning
- **Variational Autoencoder (VAE)**: A generative model that encodes data into a latent representation and decodes back, trained by maximizing a variational lower bound (ELBO). *Why needed*: provides a framework for nonlinear latent factor modeling with efficient amortized inference. *Quick check*: How does the reparameterization trick enable backpropagation through stochastic nodes?
- **Spike-and-Slab Prior**: A discrete mixture prior with a point mass at zero (“spike”) and a diffuse distribution (“slab”) to induce sparsity in model coefficients. *Why needed*: selects relevant feature‑factor associations, yielding interpretable factors and automatic factor count. *Quick check*: What is the effect of the slab’s variance on the posterior inclusion probabilities?
- **Anchor Feature**: A feature whose mapping to a latent factor is strictly monotonic and unique, used to break identifiability ambiguities. *Why needed*: establishes identifiability of latent factors in nonlinear ICA‑type models. *Quick check*: Why can’t identifiability be achieved without anchor features?
- **Multi‑Study Factor Analysis**: Decomposing data from multiple studies into shared and study‑specific factors. *Why needed*: captures common biological signals while accounting for study‑specific variations (e.g., batch effects, disease subtypes). *Quick check*: How would ignoring study‑specific factors bias the shared factors?
- **Implicit Factor Selection**: Determining the number of latent factors automatically through sparsity‑inducing priors rather than explicit model selection criteria. *Why needed*: avoids overfitting and manual tuning of factor dimensionality. *Quick check*: How does the spike‑and‑slab prior lead to a data‑driven factor count?
- **Amortized Variational Inference**: Learning an encoder network to map data directly to variational parameters, sharing inference across samples. *Why needed*: scales to large datasets and provides fast posterior approximation at test time. *Quick check*: What are the trade‑offs between amortized inference and per‑data‑point optimization?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map** (data flow):
Input -> Encoder -> Latent Shared & Study-specific Factors -> Spike-and-Slab Decoder -> Reconstructed Data

**Critical Path** (training loop):
1. Forward pass: encode each study’s data to distributions over shared (z_s) and study‑specific (z_i) latents.
2. Sample latents via reparameterization.
3. Decode combined latents through a neural network decoder with spike‑and‑slab priors on its output weights.
4. Compute ELBO: reconstruction loss (e.g., MSE) plus KL divergences for latents and weight priors.
5. Backpropagate gradients and update encoder and decoder parameters with Adam.
6. Repeat until convergence, optionally pruning factors with near‑zero inclusion probabilities.

**Design Tradeoffs**:
- Nonlinearity vs Interpretability: Deep decoder captures complex patterns but reduces linear interpretability; mitigated by sparse decoding.
- Sparsity Strength: Stronger sparsity yields more interpretable factors but risks discarding weakly associated features; requires tuning of slab variance or prior inclusion probability.
- Computational Cost: Spike‑and‑slab requires sampling discrete inclusion variables or using continuous relaxations, increasing time/memory; amortization helps but may still be heavy for very high dimensions.

**Failure Signatures**:
- Collapse of shared factors (e.g., all samples map to similar shared latent codes).
- Overestimation of factor number due to insufficient sparsity pressure, leading to many inactive or redundant factors.
- Poor disentanglement: shared factors capturing study‑specific variation, indicating anchor failure.
- Training instability: KL divergence dominating reconstruction, causing posterior collapse.

**First Experiments**:
1. **Synthetic validation**: Train MSSVAE on Gaussian data with known ground‑truth factors (varying anchor strength, noise) and compute consensus, relevance, recovery, disentanglement.
2. **Real data application**: Apply to the platelet RNA‑seq dataset, inspect GO enrichments of top factors, and compare study‑specific factors to known disease pathways.
3. **Ablation on anchors**: Disable anchor assumptions (or use weaker anchors) to assess impact on identifiability and factor recovery metrics.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How robust is MSSVAE to violations of the anchor feature assumption, and can we detect when anchor features are absent in real data?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The identifiability proof (Theorem 1) relies on Assumption 1, requiring anchor features with strictly monotone mappings to each latent factor.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper proves identifiability under these assumptions but does not evaluate performance when anchors are missing, which may be common in genomic data where genes seldom depend on single pathways.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Simulations with varying anchor strengths; empirical methods to identify anchor features in real datasets; robustness analysis measuring factor recovery as anchor prevalence decreases.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Does the implicit factor number selection via spike‑and‑slab priors in MSSVAE consistently recover the true number of latent factors, and how does it compare to explicit model selection approaches?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] In synthetic RNA experiments (Section 4.2), MSSVAE overestimates the true number of shared factors (e.g., Figure 3c), suggesting the implicit sparsity penalty may not optimally control factor dimensionality.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper does not assess factor number accuracy or compare with techniques like BIC or cross‑validation; the interplay between sparsity and factor count remains unexplored.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Systematic simulations varying true factor counts and signal strengths, tracking estimated K; benchmarks against methods with explicit factor selection; analysis of posterior distributions for factor inclusion probabilities.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: For multi‑study data with factors shared across subsets of studies (e.g., diseases with overlapping biological pathways), can MSSVAE capture partial sharing, or does it force an all‑or‑nothing shared/study‑specific dichotomy?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] Theorem 2 constructs the shared matrix W(S) from columns equal across all studies, implying study‑specific matrices have disjoint supports and no intermediate sharing.
- **Why unresolved**: Real applications like disease cohorts may have factors shared among some studies (as noted for Grabski et al., 2023), but MSSVAE as formulated cannot represent such structures.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Simulations with nested sharing patterns (e.g., factors shared by 2 out of 3 studies); application to datasets with known partial sharing; extension of MSSVAE to allow group‑specific factor allocation.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: What is the computational scalability of MSSVAE to large‑scale multi‑study data (e.g., thousands of studies or millions of samples), and can approximations improve efficiency without sacrificing interpretability?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The method is applied to 6 studies and 5000 genes using VAEs with amortized inference, but no scalability analysis is provided.
- **Why unresolved**: Neural network‑based VAEs with spike‑and‑slab priors may have high memory and time costs; for larger datasets (common in genomics), feasibility is unclear.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Empirical runtime and memory usage experiments with synthetic data of increasing size; comparison with linear multi‑study methods; exploration of stochastic variational inference or subsampling for scalability.

## Limitations
- **Anchor assumption**: Requires pre‑specified anchor variables; no principled way to select them without external knowledge, limiting real‑world use.
- **Narrow validation**: Only one synthetic scenario (Gaussian data) and no comparisons to other multi‑study methods; generalizability is uncertain.
- **Indirect real‑data evidence**: 44% of disease‑specific factors lack GO enrichment; enrichment analysis is biased and scalability remains unaddressed.
