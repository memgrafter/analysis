# FEKAN: Feature-Enriched Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.16530
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16530
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: FEKAN adds non-trainable feature enrichment (e.g., Fourier features) to KAN, improving accuracy and convergence without extra trainable parameters; reduces L2 error by an order of magnitude on high-frequency functions and by over 50% on Helmholtz PDE.

## Executive Summary
FEKAN enhances Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KAN) by prepending a fixed feature enrichment layer (e.g., Fourier sinusoids) to the input. This simple modification increases representational capacity and mitigates spectral bias while keeping the number of trainable parameters unchanged. Across function approximation, physics-informed PDE solving (PI-FEKAN), and neural operator learning, FEKAN consistently outperforms baseline KAN variants. Key results include a tenfold reduction in relative L2 error for high-frequency discontinuous functions, over 50% error reduction for the 2D Helmholtz equation, and improved training stability for challenging bases like Chebyshev. Theoretical analysis via an extended Kolmogorov superposition theorem and Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) spectra confirms that feature enrichment enlarges the family of representable functions and slows eigenvalue decay, enabling more efficient learning of high-frequency components.

## Method Summary
FEKAN augments the standard KAN architecture with a non-trainable feature enrichment layer applied to input coordinates. This layer maps raw input \(x\) to a higher-dimensional vector \(\gamma(x)\) using fixed basis functions such as Fourier sinusoids, polynomials, or radial basis functions. The enriched vector is then fed into the KAN backbone, which comprises layers of nodes where each edge implements a learnable univariate function (e.g., splines, RBFs). The network remains fully differentiable; only the enrichment layer is frozen. This design shifts the burden of generating high-frequency patterns from the KAN’s learnable functions to the explicit enrichment, improving sample efficiency and convergence. The method is evaluated in three settings: standalone function approximation, physics-informed neural networks for PDEs (PI-FEKAN), and neural operators (DeepOFEKAN), showing consistent gains over baseline KANs across various internal bases (spline, Fourier, Chebyshev, etc.).

## Key Results
- On high-frequency discontinuous function approximation, FEKAN reduces relative L2 error by an order of magnitude compared to KAN.
- For the 2D Helmholtz equation, PI-FEKAN lowers relative L2 error by over 50% with comparable computational cost and stabilizes training for Chebyshev bases that otherwise diverge.
- FEKAN also improves continual learning in sequential PDE tasks and enhances operator learning accuracy, e.g., achieving ~10× lower error in high-frequency bubble dynamics.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Feature enrichment mitigates spectral bias, enabling faster learning of high-frequency components.
- **Mechanism:** The non-trainable feature map (e.g., Fourier features) lifts inputs into a higher-frequency-enriched space before the KAN backbone. This pre-encodes high-frequency information, reducing the burden on the KAN’s learnable univariate functions to generate such structure from scratch. In the NTK framework, this leads to a slower decay of eigenvalue spectra, allowing higher-frequency modes to be learned more efficiently.
- **Core assumption:** The target function contains high-frequency components that are poorly represented by the native basis of the KAN (e.g., splines). The enrichment map must be chosen to align with these components (e.g., Fourier for periodicities).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "...increases representational capacity and mitigates spectral bias without increasing the number of trainable parameters... FEKAN reduces relative L2 error by an order of magnitude compared to KAN" for high-frequency discontinuous functions.
  - [section 2.2] NTK analysis shows FEKAN has slower eigenvalue decay than KAN for spline and Fourier bases, correlating with improved high-frequency learning.
  - [corpus] Neighboring papers (e.g., KANICE, ChebyshevKAN) focus on architectural variants but do not study feature enrichment; thus, the corpus provides weak direct evidence for this specific mechanism. The mechanism is primarily evidenced within the submitted paper.
- **Break condition:** The enrichment map is misaligned with the target function's dominant frequencies (e.g., using polynomial features for purely periodic data), or the number of enrichment terms is insufficient, leading to representational bottlenecks.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Feature enrichment enlarges the family of representable functions and reduces approximation complexity for certain targets.
- **Mechanism:** By augmenting the input vector with continuous nonlinear features \(u_j(x)\), the effective dimensionality of the Kolmogorov representation increases from \(n\) to \(n+m\). The Feature-Enriched Kolmogorov Superposition Theorem (Theorem 1) guarantees that any continuous function \(f(x, u_1(x),..., u_m(x))\) can be represented. This structural enlargement means functions that are complex in the original coordinates may become simpler in the augmented space, requiring fewer outer functions \(\phi_i\) or inner functions \(\psi_q\) for the same approximation error.
- **Core assumption:** The additional features \(u_j(x)\) are informative transformations of the original inputs that align with the nonlinear structure of the target function \(f\). The function \(f\) must be representable in the augmented space.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 2.1] Theorem 1 formally extends the Kolmogorov superposition to feature-enriched inputs. Theorem 2 proves structural enlargement (\(\mathbb{R}^n \subsetneq \mathbb{R}^{(n,m)}\)) and demonstrates a construction where a highly oscillatory function \(F(x)=g(\sin(N\sum x_i))\) becomes trivially representable (Comp=1) with the feature \(u_1\), whereas many components are needed without it.
  - [abstract] "...theoretical analysis confirms that feature enrichment enlarges the family of representable functions and reduces approximation complexity."
  - [corpus] The survey paper [183765] discusses KAN's basis function expressivity but does not address feature enrichment; evidence is absent from the corpus.
- **Break condition:** The chosen features \(u_j\) are constant, linearly dependent on existing inputs, or otherwise fail to capture the essential nonlinearities of \(f\), providing no representational advantage.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Feature enrichment improves training stability and suppresses catastrophic forgetting in sequential tasks.
- **Mechanism:** For PDE solving and continual learning, the feature-enriched input \(\gamma(x)\) creates a more structured and separable representation in the early layers. This can reduce overlap between activation supports for different regions of the input domain (e.g., different boundary segments in phase-wise training). The locality of KAN's spline activations combined with a richer initial encoding means parameter updates for one task/region interfere less with others, mitigating forgetting. For unstable bases like Chebyshev, enrichment may regularize the input space, smoothing the optimization landscape.
- **Core assumption:** Catastrophic forgetting in KAN is driven by support overlap between tasks (per Rahman et al. [46]). The feature map \(\gamma(x)\) must decorrelate or separate task/region manifolds in the enriched space.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.2.4] Phase-wise Helmholtz BVP experiments show PI-FEKAN maintains boundary condition accuracy across sequential phases while PI-KAN exhibits unbounded fluctuations and forgetting. Figures 22-23 and 24 demonstrate superior boundedness and retention.
  - [section 3.2.1] PI-FEKAN stabilizes training for the unstable Chebyshev basis across PDEs; Table 7 shows ChebyKAN diverges (NaN) while FE-ChebyKAN does not.
  - [corpus] The neighboring paper on catastrophic forgetting in KANs [paper_id: 106872] investigates the phenomenon but does not test feature enrichment, so direct corpus evidence is lacking.
- **Break condition:** The feature map \(\gamma(x)\) fails to disentangle task manifolds (e.g., using a linear map for tasks that are nonlinearly mixed in the original space), or the support overlap is intrinsically high and cannot be reduced by input transformation alone.

## Foundational Learning

**Kolmogorov-Arnold Representation Theorem (KAT)**  
Why needed here: FEKAN is built upon KAN, which directly implements KAT via learnable univariate functions on spline/RBF/etc. bases. Understanding KAT explains why KAN decomposes multivariate functions into sums of 1D functions and why adding features (\(u_j\)) fits naturally into this framework (Theorem 1).  
Quick check question: If you add a feature \(u(x)=\sin(x)\) to the input of a KAN, how does the form of the Kolmogorov superposition change? (Answer: The inner sum now includes \(\psi_{n+1}(\sin(x))\), increasing the number of \(\psi\) functions from \(n\) to \(n+m\).)

**Spectral Bias in Neural Networks**  
Why needed here: A key motivation for FEKAN is to overcome spectral bias—the tendency of standard (and KAN) networks to learn low-frequency components first. The mechanism relies on enriching inputs with high-frequency bases (Fourier) to make these components explicit.  
Quick check question: Why might a network with a standard spline-based KAN architecture struggle to learn a function with a sharp discontinuity? (Answer: Spline bases have limited high-frequency support and smoothness; learning discontinuities requires many spline segments and is slow due to spectral bias.)

**Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)**  
Why needed here: The paper uses NTK to analyze training dynamics and spectral bias. The NTK eigenvalue spectrum's decay rate predicts a model's ability to learn high frequencies. FEKAN's slower decay explains its superior high-frequency learning.  
Quick check question: In the NTK framework, if a kernel matrix has eigenvalues that decay very rapidly (e.g., \(\lambda_i \propto 1/i^2\)), what does this imply about the model's training dynamics for a target function with high-frequency components? (Answer: High-frequency components align with small eigenvalues, so they will be learned very slowly or not at all before training converges.)

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:** Raw Input -> Feature Enrichment (γ) -> KAN Backbone -> Output
- **Critical path:** Raw input → Feature Enrichment (fixed map) → KAN Backbone (trainable) → Output. The enrichment *must* precede the KAN layers; integrating it later (e.g., between KAN layers) is a design variant not evaluated.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Enrichment map choice: Fourier features excel for periodic/high-frequency content but add many parameters (2m+1 per dim). Polynomial features can capture interactions but cause combinatorial explosion in dimensionality. Random Fourier features (sampling \(a \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)\)) reduce hyperparameter sensitivity to a single \(\sigma\).
  - Number of enrichment terms (m): More terms increase representational capacity and reduce bias but risk overfitting and higher compute in the KAN backbone due to larger input dimension. There is a "sweet spot" (e.g., 5–7 terms in experiments) before error plateaus or rises.
  - Basis function pairing: FEKAN's benefit is agnostic to the KAN's internal basis (spline, Chebyshev, ReLU, etc.), but some pairings are more effective. Spline+FE is universal; Chebyshev+FE gains stability; DoG wavelet+FE still underperforms for PDEs.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Divergence/NaNs: Particularly with Chebyshev bases in KAN. FEKAN often suppresses this, but if enrichment introduces extreme values (e.g., using very high-frequency Fourier terms without scaling), it can destabilize training.
  - Lack of improvement: If the relative L2 error of FEKAN is comparable to KAN across basis functions and tasks, the enrichment map may be misaligned (e.g., low-frequency target with high-frequency enrichment) or m may be too small/large.
  - Catastrophic forgetting persists: In phase-wise PDE tasks, if boundary condition accuracy degrades in later phases, feature enrichment may be insufficient to separate task manifolds; consider increasing grid size G for splines or adjusting enrichment.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Baseline Function Approximation: Train KAN and FEKAN (with Fourier enrichment, m=5) on the high-frequency discontinuous test function (Eq. 14) using a spline basis (k=2, G=15). Metric: relative L2 error and convergence rate. Expect FEKAN error ~1e-3 vs. KAN ~1e-2, with faster convergence.
  2. PDE Solving - Helmholtz: Solve the 2D Helmholtz equation with PI-KAN and PI-FEKAN using spline basis ([n,7,7,1], k=3, G=5). Enrich with 7 Fourier terms per spatial dim. Metric: relative L2 error and sec/iter. Expect >50% error reduction for FEKAN and more stable Chebyshev training.
  3. Operator Learning - High-Frequency Bubble Dynamics: Use two-step DeepOKAN vs. DeepOFEKAN. Enrich both trunk and branch networks with Fourier features. Metric: relative L2 error on radius prediction at 2 MHz. Expect branch-enriched DeepOFEKAN to show ~10× lower error than DeepOKAN.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1**  
- Question: How can the selection of feature enrichment parameters (e.g., Fourier frequencies, polynomial degrees) be automated to minimize manual tuning?  
- Basis in paper: The paper identifies that selecting suitable feature enrichment mappings may require prior knowledge and careful hyperparameter tuning, and that suboptimal choices diminish representational benefits. They partially address this with random Fourier features but note that further research is needed.  
- Why unresolved: Current practice relies on manual selection or simple random sampling, which may not generalize across problems; an automated method would improve usability and performance.  
- What evidence would resolve it: A systematic study comparing automated selection methods (e.g., Bayesian optimization) against manual tuning across a suite of PDE benchmarks, measuring accuracy, convergence, and computational overhead.

**Open Question 2**  
- Question: What regularization techniques are most effective at preventing overfitting when the enriched feature space becomes high-dimensional?  
- Basis in paper: The authors state: "Expanding the feature space increases model capacity, which can lead to overfitting... requires careful regularization strategies and judicious feature selection."  
- Why unresolved: The paper does not investigate specific regularization methods (e.g., dropout, weight decay) in the context of FEKAN; this remains an open design question.  
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments applying various regularization methods to FEKAN with varying enrichment dimensions, measuring generalization error on held-out data and comparing to baselines.

**Open Question 3**  
- Question: Can the feature enrichment process be made adaptive (i.e., learnable) during training to further enhance representational capacity and efficiency?  
- Basis in paper: The paper uses fixed Fourier features, while earlier work (Sun et al.) introduced adaptive sinusoidal positional encoding with learned frequencies. The authors note that "selecting or learning suitable feature enrichment mappings" is a limitation, implying that learnable enrichment could be beneficial.  
- Why unresolved: The current FEKAN employs non-trainable enrichment; making these parameters trainable could improve adaptability but may introduce instability. The trade-offs are unexplored.  
- What evidence would resolve it: Develop a FEKAN variant where enrichment parameters (e.g., frequencies) are updated via gradient descent, test on benchmark PDEs, and compare stability, convergence, and accuracy with fixed enrichment.

**Open Question 4**  
- Question: How does FEKAN scale to high-dimensional PDEs and large-scale scientific simulations in terms of computational efficiency and memory consumption?  
- Basis in paper: The paper evaluates FEKAN up to 3D PDEs using separable architecture (SPI-FEKAN) and reports computational costs. They also remark that KANs have high computational overhead and training cost exceeds that of MLPs. The combinatorial explosion of enrichment terms with dimensionality is a concern.  
- Why unresolved: The paper does not investigate scaling beyond moderate dimensions (e.g., 3D) or with massive datasets; the computational complexity of enrichment in high dimensions may limit practicality.  
- What evidence would resolve it: Benchmarks of FEKAN on higher-dimensional problems (e.g., 4D–10D) and comparisons with alternative architectures in terms of time, memory, and accuracy; theoretical analysis of complexity scaling with dimension.

**Open Question 5**  
- Question: What theoretical guarantees exist for the approximation error of FEKAN with specific enrichment families (e.g., Fourier, Chebyshev) when solving PDEs?  
- Basis in paper: The paper provides theoretical analysis (Theorems 1–3) showing that feature enrichment enlarges representable functions and reduces approximation complexity for general continuous functions. However, explicit error bounds for PDE solutions are not derived.  
- Why unresolved: While empirical improvements in PDE solving are shown, a rigorous error analysis linking enrichment to solution accuracy (e.g., in Sobolev norms) is lacking, which would strengthen the theoretical foundation.  
- What evidence would resolve it: Derive a priori error estimates for FEKAN approximating solutions of various PDE classes, supported by numerical experiments that verify the predicted convergence rates.

## Limitations
- Corpus evidence is weak; neighboring KAN variants do not address feature enrichment.
- Theoretical guarantees assume perfect alignment of enrichment features with target structure; misalignment may nullify benefits.
- Hyperparameter selection for enrichment (number of terms, frequencies) lacks systematic study.

## Confidence
- Spectral Bias Mitigation: Medium
- Representational Enlargement: High
- Stability & Forgetting Reduction: Medium
