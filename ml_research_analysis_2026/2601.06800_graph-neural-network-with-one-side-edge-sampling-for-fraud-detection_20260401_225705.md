# Graph Neural Network with One-side Edge Sampling for Fraud Detection

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.06800
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06800
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** One-Side Edge Sampling (OES) improves F1-scores by ~8-9% (e.g., GIN+EU: 50.96% → 59.47%) and reduces training time by ~10-20% on the IBM AML dataset.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles slow training and over-smoothing/over-fitting in deep Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) for financial fraud detection. It introduces One-Side Edge Sampling (OES), a method that uses predictive confidence from an edge classification task to stochastically remove high-confidence, correctly predicted edges during early training epochs. This adaptive edge dropout regularizes the model, reduces computational cost, and delays feature homogenization. Experiments on the IBM AML dataset with GIN, GIN+EGO, and GIN+EU backbones show consistent improvements in F1-score and training efficiency, supported by theoretical analysis linking edge removal to increased over-smoothing thresholds and reduced information loss.

## Method Summary
OES is applied during the first *n* training epochs. For each epoch, the model predicts edge labels (fraud/not fraud) and computes predictive confidence *c(e)* = max(ŷ_pos, ŷ_neg) for every edge. A percentile threshold *P(p)* is calculated from all confidences. Edges are filtered to those where *c(e) ≥ P(p)* **and** the prediction is correct. A ratio *r* of these filtered edges is then sampled to form a reduced edge set *E_sampled*. The training graph for the next epoch is rebuilt as *G_sampled = (V, E_sampled)*, replacing the original graph. After epoch *n*, training reverts to the full graph. This process creates a noisier, sparser training signal that mitigates over-reliance on fully confident patterns.

## Key Results
- OES improves F1-score on HI_Small: GIN (53.79% → 58.36%), GIN+EGO (51.11% → 58.85%), GIN+EU (50.96% → 59.47%).
- Training time is reduced: GIN (185.4 → 158.1 min), GIN+EGO (217.2 → 179.2 min), GIN+EU (232.9 → 189.9 min) on HI_Small.
- OES increases the theoretical over-smoothing threshold and reduces information loss, enabling deeper effective models.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** OES reduces computational cost and mitigates over-fitting by stochastically removing edges the model already predicts correctly with high confidence during training.
- **Mechanism:** By filtering the input graph to a subset of high-confidence, correctly predicted edges for a fixed number of early epochs, OES creates a noisier, sparser training signal. This prevents the model from over-relying on fully confident patterns in the full graph, acting as an adaptive edge dropout that regularizes the model and reduces the number of message-passing operations per epoch.
- **Core assumption:** Edges with high predictive confidence early in training represent "easier" or redundant relational patterns that contribute less to learning robust, generalizable representations for fraud detection.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "The approach leverages predictive confidence in an edge classification task to sample edges... reduce training duration as well as the effects of over-smoothing and over-fitting."
  - [section III.C] Details the algorithm: filter edges where `c(e,t) >= P(p)` *and* `y_pred == y_true`, then sample a ratio `r` to form a new graph `G_sampled`.
  - [section V.A, Table 2] Shows consistent ~10-20% reduction in training minutes across backbone models.
- **Break condition:** If the confidence threshold `p` is set too low (e.g., 80th percentile), too many edges—including uncertain or incorrect ones—are retained, negating the regularization effect and potentially harming performance.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** OES increases the theoretical threshold for over-smoothing by increasing effective resistance between nodes, thus delaying the convergence of node representations to a non-informative stationary point in deep GNNs.
- **Mechanism:** Removing edges (especially high-confidence ones) increases the effective resistance `R_st` between node pairs. Theorem 1 links this increased resistance to a lower second-largest eigenvalue `λ` of the graph Laplacian. Since the over-smoothing threshold `l_w(M,ε)` is inversely related to `λ`, raising `λ`'s lower bound via edge removal raises the threshold, allowing more layers before representations collapse.
- **Core assumption:** Over-smoothing is fundamentally a spectral graph theory problem where excessive message passing causes feature homogenization; perturbing the graph topology (via edge removal) can alter the spectral properties to delay this homogenization.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section III.E, Theorem] "Removing edges from graph will only increase R_st... when R_st = ∞, λ will decrease and eventually equal to one... l_w(M,ε) will increase."
  - [section III.E] "OES is able to increase the relaxed ε-smoothing: l_w(M,ε) ≤ l_w(M_OES,ε)."
- **Break condition:** If edge removal is too aggressive (very high `p` and/or `r`), graph connectivity may fracture into disconnected components (`R_st = ∞`), causing a complete loss of cross-node information flow and catastrophic failure for node-level tasks.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** OES reduces the information loss inherent in the over-smoothing process by increasing the dimensionality (`dim(M)`) of the subspace in which node features are averaged.
- **Mechanism:** As edges are removed and components disconnect, the dimension `M` of the invariant subspace `M` (where features converge during over-smoothing) can increase. The information loss is quantified as `N - dim(M)`, where `N` is the feature dimension. A higher `dim(M)` means the converged features retain more of the original space's expressivity.
- **Core assumption:** Over-smoothing causes features to collapse onto a low-dimensional manifold; preserving or increasing the dimensionality of this manifold reduces the distortion of the original feature space.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section III.E] "When R_st = ∞, the value of M will increase by one as when enough edges are dropped, the component will separate into two parts. Hence, N-M will decrease."
  - [section III.E] "OES is able to decrease the information loss... N-dim(M) ≥ N-dim(M_OES)."
- **Break condition:** This mechanism relies on controlled disconnection. If edge sampling is random or too sparse, the graph may fragment randomly, increasing `M` in an uncontrolled way that destroys meaningful structural information.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Message Passing in GNNs.
  - **Why needed here:** OES explicitly modifies the message-passing graph during training. Understanding how node features are aggregated from neighbors is essential to see how removing edges changes the aggregation set and thus the learned representations.
  - **Quick check question:** If you remove all incoming edges to a node `v` in one training epoch via OES, what happens to `v`'s representation update in the next layer, and why might this sometimes be beneficial?
- **Concept:** Over-smoothing.
  - **Why needed here:** OES is primarily motivated as a solution to this. One must grasp that in deep GNNs, repeated aggregation causes node features to converge to a constant (or low-rank) value, making depth useless. The paper links this to spectral properties.
  - **Quick check question:** Why does a GNN with many layers (e.g., 16) often perform worse than a shallow one (e.g., 2) on node classification tasks, according to the over-smoothing phenomenon?
- **Concept:** Edge Classification & Confidence.
  - **Why needed here:** OES's core signal is the *predictive confidence* from an edge classification task. This requires understanding that the GNN is trained to predict edge labels, and its output probability can be interpreted as confidence.
  - **Quick check question:** If an edge from account A to B has a predicted fraud probability of 0.98 and the true label is fraud, would OES likely include or exclude this edge from the sampled graph at a 99th percentile threshold? Why?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Backbone GNN (e.g., GIN, GIN+EU) -> Edge Classifier Head -> Confidence Calculator -> OES Filter & Sampler -> Graph Rebuilder
- **Critical path:** During training loop for epoch `t` (if `t <= n`): (a) Forward pass on current graph `G_t` -> get edge predictions. (b) Compute confidences & filter/sample to get `E_sampled`. (c) Rebuild graph `G_{t+1} = (V, E_sampled)`. (d) Continue training on `G_{t+1}`. After epoch `n`, revert to using the full graph `G` for validation/testing.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Parameter Sensitivity:** `p`, `r`, and `n` are highly interdependent. High `p` + high `r` risks graph fragmentation. Low `p` + low `r` yields negligible effect. Requires dataset/backbone-specific tuning.
  - **Backbone Compatibility:** Works best with architectures that natively support edge features/classification (like GIN+EU). For simpler backbones (standard GIN), the edge classifier head is an add-on, introducing minor overhead.
  - **Temporal Integrity:** OES is applied only during training. Validation/test graphs remain the full, unsampled historical graph, ensuring fair evaluation without data leakage.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Performance Collapse:** F1-score drops to near 0% (seen with `p=80%` in sensitivity analysis). Indicates too many critical edges were removed, destroying the fraud pattern.
  - **No Improvement on Deep Models:** Observed for GIN+EU/EGO with 16 layers. Suggests the model's internal complexity already mitigates over-smoothing effectively, leaving little room for OES; or current `p,r,n` are insufficient.
  - **Increased Variance:** Higher standard deviation in F1 scores across seeds. Indicates the sampling is too stochastic or threshold too unstable, harming training consistency.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Reproduce baseline: Train GIN, GIN+EU, GIN+EGO on IBM AML without OES for 100 epochs. Record final F1 and training time.
  2. Apply OES with defaults: Integrate OES (`p=99, r=10, n=20`) into each backbone. Train identically. Compare F1 and training time against baselines.
  3. Parameter sweep on `p`: On GIN backbone, vary `p` in {95, 90, 80} while keeping `r=10, n=20`. Plot F1 vs. `p`. Expect sharp performance degradation as `p` decreases.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How does OES scale to larger, more complex graph datasets and deeper GNN architectures beyond the IBM AML Small datasets and 16-layer models tested?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state: "Due to constraints on computer resources, the experiments cannot be conducted on a more complex model or larger data."
- **Why unresolved:** Computational limitations prevented empirical validation on larger-scale, real-world financial graphs or extremely deep architectures.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Benchmarking OES on standard large-scale graph fraud detection datasets (e.g., full IBM AML, Yelp Fraud) with modern hardware.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can the theoretical relationship between edge removal (via OES), effective resistance, and over-smoothing thresholds be empirically validated on diverse graph topologies?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The proof relies on resistance increasing with edge removal, but no empirical spectral analysis is provided.
- **Why unresolved:** Real financial graphs may have community structures or heterogeneous edge types that affect resistance and smoothing differently.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Measure changes in Laplacian eigenvalues and effective resistance before/after OES across multiple real-world graphs, correlating with representation rank collapse.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Are OES's hyperparameters optimal across different GNN backbones, or can a dynamic, adaptive scheduling scheme improve robustness?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Sensitivity analysis uses fixed defaults; interplay between backbone complexity and OES parameters is not systematically studied.
- **Why unresolved:** Hyperparameters were manually set; the paper hints current settings may be insufficient for complex models.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Comprehensive hyperparameter search across backbones/datasets or development of an adaptive controller that adjusts parameters based on training dynamics.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Does OES inadvertently bias the training process against discovering low-confidence but potentially fraudulent edge patterns, especially in highly imbalanced fraud detection scenarios?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] OES samples edges with high predictive confidence *and* correct predictions. This may remove minority-class fraud edges with low confidence early in training.
- **Why unresolved:** The paper focuses on overall F1-score improvement but does not analyze per-class metrics or track sampled edge label distributions.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablation studies measuring fraud recall/precision at varying imbalance levels and visualization of sampled edge label distributions over epochs.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** How does OES perform when the graph structure evolves temporally (e.g., new accounts, changing transaction flows), as is common in real-time fraud detection systems?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The training graph is static per epoch; no discussion of handling continuous graph streams or concept drift.
- **Why unresolved:** The method description and experiments assume a static training graph per epoch.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Extending OES to a streaming setting with temporal decay or drift detection, tested on temporally split datasets.

## Limitations
- **Training hyperparameters missing:** Exact settings (hidden dimension, learning rate, optimizer, weight decay, total epochs) not specified; exact reproduction impossible.
- **Dataset and split details incomplete:** Precise temporal split timestamps and node/edge feature preprocessing pipeline (e.g., normalization, multi-edge handling) are not provided.
- **High parameter sensitivity:** OES performance is highly sensitive to `p`, `r`, and `n`; defaults may not transfer across backbones or datasets, risking performance collapse if misconfigured.
