# Do MLLMs Really Understand Space? A Mathematical Reasoning Evaluation

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.11635
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11635
- Reference count: 22
- Primary result: Fine-tuning Qwen2.5-VL-7B with MathSpatial-SRT yields competitive accuracy on MathSpatial-Bench and reduces reasoning token consumption by 25%.

## Executive Summary
Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) exhibit a significant gap in mathematical spatial reasoning compared to humans, who exceed 95% accuracy while most models fall below 60%. This paper introduces MathSpatial, a comprehensive framework comprising a 2K-problem benchmark (MathSpatial-Bench), an 8K-problem training corpus (MathSpatial-Corpus), and a structured reasoning trace method (MathSpatial-SRT) that decomposes problems into Correlate, Constrain, and Infer operations. By fine-tuning Qwen2.5-VL-7B on the corpus with SRT supervision, the authors achieve competitive performance on the benchmark and a 25% reduction in reasoning token consumption. The framework disentangles perception from reasoning and provides the first large-scale resource for systematically evaluating and improving spatial reasoning in MLLMs.

## Method Summary
The authors construct MathSpatial-Bench, a benchmark of 2K clean geometric problems designed to isolate reasoning difficulty from perceptual noise, and MathSpatial-Corpus, an 8K-problem training set with verified solutions sourced from educational materials. They propose MathSpatial-SRT, a structured reasoning trace that explicitly breaks down each problem into three operations: Correlate (align features across views), Constrain (apply geometric rules), and Infer (deduce the answer). Supervised fine-tuning of a base MLLM (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL-7B) on the corpus using these traces yields improved accuracy on the benchmark and a 25% reduction in token consumption, demonstrating both effectiveness and efficiency.

## Key Results
- Human performance on MathSpatial-Bench exceeds 95%, while most evaluated MLLMs perform below 60%.
- Fine-tuning Qwen2.5-VL-7B with MathSpatial-SRT achieves competitive accuracy on the benchmark.
- The fine-tuned model reduces reasoning token consumption by 25% compared to baseline.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** MathSpatial-Bench isolates mathematical spatial reasoning from perceptual noise, enabling a genuine measurement of reasoning capability.
- **Mechanism:** By using clean geometric problems with minimal background and texture, the benchmark forces models to rely on internal spatial relationships rather than low-level visual pattern matching. The problem design focuses on textbook-style tasks where the core challenge is parsing and manipulating 2D/3D relations.
- **Core assumption:** Errors on this benchmark predominantly stem from reasoning failures, not perception errors. This is assumed because the visual stimuli are simplified.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "...designed to isolate reasoning difficulty from perceptual noise."
  - [section 3.1 & 3.3]: The benchmark "employs clean geometric problems with minimal background and texture" and covers "three major categories... eleven subcategories" to assess specific reasoning skills like "cross-view correspondence."
  - [corpus]: Weak direct anchor; the corpus itself is for training, not validation of this mechanism's claim. The claim of isolation is supported by the benchmark design in the paper, not by corpus metadata.
- **Break condition:** If follow-up studies show that minor, realistic variations in texture or lighting (even within this "clean" design) cause significant performance drops, the claim of successful isolation would be weakened. The mechanism breaks if perceptual confounds are not effectively minimized.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** MathSpatial-Corpus provides the necessary large-scale, high-quality, and diverse training data to overcome the data bottleneck for improving MLLM spatial reasoning.
- **Mechanism:** The corpus supplies a substantial volume (8K) of educational geometry problems with verified solutions. This addresses the scarcity of dedicated training data. Its source from systematic textbooks and problem banks ensures diversity across difficulty levels and spatial task types (e.g., unfolding, projection, property calculation), providing a rich and structured supervision signal for model learning.
- **Core assumption:** Scaling quantity and diversity of training data, while maintaining solution correctness, will lead to improved model performance on the targeted reasoning skills.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "To address data scarcity, we construct MathSpatial-Corpus, a large-scale dataset of 8K additional problems with verified solutions... covering a wide range of tasks."
  - [section 3.2 & 3.4]: Details the "semi-automated pipeline" for sourcing from textbooks and the "multi-stage validation pipeline" including "geometric consistency checking" and "solution verification" by trained annotators.
  - [corpus]: The corpus metadata shows it combines bilingual support, a spatial focus, and a train set, which is unique compared to other benchmarks listed in Table 1.
- **Break condition:** If fine-tuning on this corpus consistently fails to improve performance on MathSpatial-Bench or similar OOD tasks, the claim that this corpus is sufficient for systematic improvement is challenged. The mechanism also breaks if the "verified" solutions contain unreported systematic errors.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** MathSpatial-SRT's structured decomposition into Correlate, Constrain, and Infer operations provides an interpretable and effective supervision signal that improves both accuracy and efficiency.
- **Mechanism:** The structured trace forces the model to explicitly perform three separable cognitive steps: 1) establish correspondences (aligning features across views), 2) apply geometric rules (enforcing consistency), and 3) perform deduction (synthesizing to an answer). This scaffold likely prevents "reasoning gaps" and reduces token consumption by avoiding verbose, unfocused chains of thought. The atomicity also enables precise error diagnosis.
- **Core assumption:** Spatial reasoning can be reduced to this minimal, orthogonal set of operations and that learning this explicit structure leads to more robust and efficient internal reasoning processes.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "MathSpatial-SRT... decomposes problems into Correlate, Constrain, and Infer operations... reducing reasoning token consumption by 25%."
  - [section 3.5]: Explicitly defines the three primitives, claims "minimal sufficient set," and provides Propositions 1 & 2 on coverage and minimality (formal proofs in Appendix A.4.1).
  - [section 4.2 & 4.4]: Shows improved accuracy and token reduction for fine-tuned models. Error analysis indicates SRT-trained models have fewer "feature errors" and "geometry violations," suggesting the Constrain operation is effective.
  - [corpus]: Weak anchor; corpus provides the data, but SRT's efficacy is an experimental result from the paper's analysis, not a property of the corpus itself.
- **Break condition:** If the formal proof of minimality (Proposition 2) is found flawed or if experiments show that removing any single operation does not degrade performance on a significant subset of tasks, the core assumption of orthogonality and necessity is invalidated. The mechanism also fails if the structured traces introduce new, systematic logical errors not present in free-form CoT.

## Foundational Learning
- **Geometric Consistency Rules (Orthographic Projection, Hidden/Visible Line Conventions)**  
  Why needed here: The "Constrain" operation in SRT explicitly applies these deterministic rules. Without understanding these foundational geometric laws, a model cannot correctly validate or generate spatial configurations, leading to "Geometry Violation" errors (33% of errors in Fig 6b).  
  Quick check question: Given a front view and top view of a simple block, which edges in the side view must be dashed according to standard projection rules?

- **Cross-View Correspondence**  
  Why needed here: The "Correlate" operation's purpose is to establish which features (points, edges) in one view map to which in another. This is fundamental to holistic recognition tasks (e.g., C3M, 3VM). Failure here causes "Projection Error" and "Feature Error."  
  Quick check question: If an edge appears as a point in the top view, what must be true about its orientation in the 3D object?

- **Structured Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Supervision**  
  Why needed here: MathSpatial-SRT is a specific form of structured CoT. Moving beyond free-form text to a constrained schema (operation type + arguments) is key to reducing noise, improving interpretability, and achieving the reported efficiency gains. This is a prerequisite for implementing the SRT training pipeline.  
  Quick check question: Why might a model trained on free-form CoT generate inconsistent reasoning traces compared to one trained on a constrained operation sequence?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Data Ingestion & Pipeline: Sources educational materials → standardizes to JSON schema → applies geometric rule validators → human verification for solution correctness.  
  Structured Reasoning Trace (SRT) Generator: Uses a frozen, powerful MLLM (GPT-4o) under strict role-playing and grounding to an official solution to produce `corr/cons/infer` sequences. Includes an automated dual-agent (Reviewer/Checker) validation loop.  
  Fine-Tuning Engine: Takes a base MLLM (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL-7B) and fine-tunes it on the corpus, where each sample is `(problem image, text, structured SRT, final answer)`. The loss is applied to the full auto-regressive sequence.  
  Evaluation Harness: Runs the fine-tuned model on MathSpatial-Bench with a constrained output format to extract the final answer. Computes accuracy and token count.

- **Critical path:** Data Collection → Standardization & Geometric Validation → SRT Generation & Validation → Model Fine-Tuning → Benchmark Evaluation → Error Analysis. The SRT generation is a critical gating step; its quality directly impacts training signal quality.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  Scale vs. Interpretability: The corpus (8K) is smaller than massive web-crawled datasets but is traded for high geometric validity and explicit traces.  
  Generality vs. Focus: By focusing on clean, educational geometry, the framework loses direct applicability to messy real-world scenes but gains the ability to isolate the reasoning skill.  
  Automation vs. Human Labor: The pipeline is semi-automated, relying heavily on human annotators for geometric validation and solution verification. This ensures quality but limits scaling speed.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  High "Reasoning Gap" errors (34.4%): Model fails to produce a coherent multi-step trace, often jumping to conclusions.  
  High "Geometry Violation" errors (33.0%): Model applies incorrect projection rules or misinterprets line conventions, indicating weak "Constrain" operation learning.  
  Poor performance on Abstract Deduction tasks (GPC, PPCD): Suggests the "Infer" operation, especially for numeric property calculation, is not being mastered.  
  Token reduction fails: If fine-tuned models do not show shorter reasoning traces, the SRT's efficiency claim is unsupported; the model may be learning to mimic the format without internalizing the concise operations.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Baseline Benchmark Evaluation: Run a suite of closed-source (GPT-4, Gemini, Claude) and open-source (Qwen, Llama, InternVL) models on MathSpatial-Bench with zero-shot or few-shot prompting. Establish the human performance upper bound (~96%) and the significant model-human gap (~<60%).  
  2. SRT Fine-Tuning & Ablation: Fine-tune a base model (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL-7B) on MathSpatial-Corpus with SRT. Run an ablation comparing: (a) no fine-tuning, (b) fine-tuning with final answer only, (c) fine-tuning with free-form CoT, (d) fine-tuning with MathSpatial-SRT. Measure accuracy and average token count on the benchmark.  
  3. External Generalization Test: Evaluate the SRT-fine-tuned model on out-of-distribution benchmarks (SOLIDGEO, GeoEval, 3DSRBench-Real) to test if the learned spatial reasoning skills transfer to perception-heavy or different distribution tasks, as reported in Table 3.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can the MathSpatial-SRT framework, designed for static educational geometry, generalize to real-world, perception-heavy spatial reasoning tasks involving natural scenes, dynamic changes, and multi-object interactions?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors explicitly state their dataset's focus on "clean geometric problems with minimal background" as a design choice to isolate reasoning, and acknowledge in Limitations that this may limit generalization to "real-world scenarios involving natural scenes and complex environments" and "dynamic scene understanding." Their OOD experiments on SOLIDGEO/GeoEval/3DSRBench show positive transfer but these remain geometric.
- **Why unresolved:** The framework's core operations (Correlate, Constrain, Infer) were validated on a specific, simplified problem distribution. Their sufficiency for complex, noisy, or temporally-varying spatial cognition remains an open empirical question.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Developing and evaluating on a new benchmark of dynamic, real-world spatial tasks (e.g., video-based causal spatial reasoning, cluttered scene manipulation planning) and analyzing whether SRT traces remain coherent and effective.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Is the set of three atomic operations (Correlate, Constrain, Infer) in MathSpatial-SRT both necessary and sufficient for capturing the full spectrum of human-like mathematical spatial reasoning, or are there missing primitives (e.g., for analogical reasoning, uncertainty handling, or topological intuition)?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors propose Proposition 1 (normal-form coverage) and Proposition 2 (minimality) to argue the triad is sufficient and minimal **within the formalized problem space of MathSpatial**. They state: "We posit that this triad constitutes the minimal sufficient set... any task in MathSpatial can be decomposed."
- **Why unresolved:** The proof of minimality and coverage is specific to their curated dataset's problem space. It does not rule out the existence of other fundamental operations needed for spatial reasoning outside that domain (e.g., in continuous space, with fuzzy boundaries, or for creative construction). The error analysis (34.4% reasoning gaps) suggests current models still struggle even with these operations.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A formal analysis comparing SRT traces against detailed think-aloud protocols from human experts solving diverse spatial problems, looking for recurring reasoning steps that don't fit the Correlate/Constrain/Infer schema. Alternatively, ablation studies where a candidate new operation is added to the set, measuring if performance on a held-out set of *complex* spatial tasks improves.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** To what extent does the performance gain from MathSpatial-SRT fine-tuning stem from the structured trace format itself (providing a inductive bias for decomposable reasoning) versus simply the increased quantity and quality of supervised spatial reasoning data (the 8K MathSpatial-Corpus), independent of trace structure?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper compares "Base-CoT" (free-form CoT) and "SRT CoT" (structured), showing SRT CoT yields a +2.6% gain over Base-CoT. However, both use the same underlying corpus. The authors attribute the gain to structured supervision, but the experiment doesn't fully isolate the *format's inductive bias* from other factors like potentially more consistent step-by-step supervision within the SRT generation pipeline.
- **Why unresolved:** The ablation in Figure 8 compares baseline vs. Base-CoT vs. SRT CoT, but Base-CoT and SRT CoT may differ in more than just structure (e.g., SRT generation might enforce more rigorous step validation via the dual-role system). The unique contribution of the *operational decomposition* is confounded with the data curation process.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A controlled experiment where the same set of problems has two versions of solution traces: (a) high-quality free-form CoT, and (b) the SRT decomposition of the *same* solution. Fine-tuning on these two corpora (matched for problem set and solution correctness) would isolate the effect of trace structure.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Does the reliance on GPT-4o to generate MathSpatial-SRT traces introduce subtle biases or errors that propagate to the fine-tuned model, given that GPT-4o itself exhibits significant spatial reasoning deficits on the same benchmark (only 19.6% accuracy)?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The SRT generation uses GPT-4o under a "constrained schema" and "grounded by the authoritative official solution." However, GPT-4o's poor performance on MathSpatial-Bench (Table 2) raises a critical question: can a model that fails on these problems itself generate correct, generalizable *reasoning traces* for them, even when guided? The dual-role validation (Reviewer/Checker) also uses GPT-4o, relying on it to spot errors in its own or its peer's traces.
- **Why unresolved:** The pipeline assumes GPT-4o can act as a competent *formatter* and *validator* of correct solutions despite being a poor *solver*. The risk is that GPT-4o might produce traces that are syntactically correct but semantically misaligned with robust spatial reasoning, or that its validation misses subtle flaws due to its own weaknesses.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A human audit of a stratified sample of the final MathSpatial-Corpus SRTs, focusing on cases where GPT-4o performed poorly on the benchmark. Check if traces for those problems contain reasoning errors not caught by the automated validation. Alternatively, train a model on traces generated by a different, more capable supervisor (if available) and compare performance.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** What is the precise mechanism by which MathSpatial-SRT leads to a 25% reduction in reasoning token consumption? Is it because the structured trace format teaches the model more compact reasoning, or because it filters out verbose, inconsistent "reasoning noise" from the model's native generation?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state: "Fine-tuning Qwen2.5-VL-7B with this approach achieves competitive performance on the benchmark while reducing reasoning token consumption by 25%." They also show in Table 2 that fine-tuned models (e.g., MathSpatial-Qwen2.5-VL-7B: 351.9 tokens) use far fewer tokens than the base model (465.3 tokens) and much fewer than closed-source models (>676 tokens).
- **Why unresolved:** The paper demonstrates the correlation (improved accuracy + fewer tokens) but does not diagnose the causal process. Is the model learning to reason more efficiently by following a concise template? Or is the supervision effectively suppressing redundant or erroneous generation steps that the base model would produce? The token count is measured at inference; the internal process isn't analyzed.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Analyzing the base model's vs. the fine-tuned model's free-form outputs (without SRT forcing) on a sample of problems, quantifying verbosity and redundancy. Additionally, probing whether the token reduction holds when the model is prompted to generate *unstructured* reasoning after SRT fine-tuning—if reduction persists, it suggests internal efficiency gains rather than just format compression.

## Limitations
- The datasets (MathSpatial-Corpus and MathSpatial-Bench) are not yet publicly released, limiting reproducibility and independent verification.
- The SRT generation pipeline relies on GPT-4o and undisclosed prompts, which may introduce subtle biases or errors due to GPT-4o's own spatial reasoning limitations.
- The benchmark’s clean, synthetic geometry tasks may not generalize to real-world, noisy environments, restricting ecological validity.

## Confidence
- Isolation of reasoning: Medium
- Corpus-driven improvement: Medium
- SRT structured supervision: Medium
