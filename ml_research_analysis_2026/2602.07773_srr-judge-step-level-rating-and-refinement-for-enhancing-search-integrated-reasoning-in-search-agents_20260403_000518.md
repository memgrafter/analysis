# SRR-Judge: Step-Level Rating and Refinement for Enhancing Search-Integrated Reasoning in Search Agents
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.07773
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07773
- Reference count: 37
- Primary result: SRR-Judge (QwQ-32B) improves search agent performance: +3.8–7.3 pass@1 via inference-time refinement and >10% absolute gain with iterative RFT on deep-search benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
Deep search agents rely on outcome-only supervision, which provides sparse feedback and leads to error accumulation in long-horizon reasoning tasks. This paper introduces SRR-Judge, a step-level evaluator that rates and refines each thought-action pair within a ReAct framework, enabling denser supervision and inference-time correction. SRR-Judge is distilled from DeepSeek-V3.1 and filtered using point-biserial correlation to ensure its step ratings predict final correctness.

The authors evaluate SRR-Judge (based on QwQ-32B) on challenging deep-search benchmarks. The judge achieves a strong average point-biserial correlation of 0.479, outperforming larger teacher models. Integrating SRR-Judge into a rate-and-refine inference loop yields immediate gains: on QwQ-32B, pass@1 increases by +3.8 on BrowseComp (5.4→9.1), +5.8 on BrowseComp-ZH (23.4→29.2), and +7.3 on Xbench-DeepSearch (46.0→53.3). Furthermore, iterative rejection-sampling fine-tuning (RFT) with SRR-annotated trajectories produces over 10% absolute improvement relative to outcome-only supervision across all benchmarks. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of step-level supervision and iterative refinement for enhancing search agents.

## Method Summary
SRR-Judge is trained in a two-stage process. First, trajectories are collected from multiple models (DeepSeek-V3.1, Qwen3-235B-A22B, QwQ-32B, Qwen3-8B) on a large set of training queries (6,050 QA pairs). Each step in these trajectories is annotated with a numeric rating (1–5) using DeepSeek-V3.1 under a self-consistency protocol (five independent runs); one run is randomly selected to provide an explanation and a refined thought-action pair. To remove noisy annotations, only trajectories where the point-biserial correlation between the average step rating and the binary outcome (correct/incorrect) exceeds 0.7 are retained. The filtered dataset is then used to fine-tune a QwQ-32B model via standard supervised fine-tuning (SFT) for one epoch, yielding the final step-level evaluator.

Inference with SRR-Judge follows a rate-and-refine ReAct loop. For each step, the base policy (e.g., QwQ-32B) generates N candidate thought-action pairs. SRR-Judge assigns a rating to each candidate; the highest-rated is selected. If the top rating is below a threshold (4), SRR-Judge is prompted to refine that candidate, and new candidates are generated from the refinement. This cycle repeats until a candidate meets the threshold or the step budget is exhausted. For online deployment, N=1; for offline data generation, N=5 and the best trajectory (by averaged step ratings) is kept. The offline trajectories serve as training data for iterative rejection-sampling fine-tuning (RFT): the current policy generates new trajectories using rate-and-refine (N=5), only correct final answers are kept, and the policy is fine-tuned on these high-quality examples. This generate-and-refine cycle is performed for two iterations, yielding progressive improvements as the policy climbs the step-quality gradient.

## Key Results
- SRR-Judge (QwQ-32B) achieves average point-biserial correlation 0.479 with final correctness, outperforming larger teacher models.
- Rate-and-refine inference improves QwQ-32B pass@1 on deep-search benchmarks: +3.8 on BrowseComp (5.4→9.1), +5.8 on BrowseComp-ZH (23.4→29.2), +7.3 on Xbench-DeepSearch (46.0→53.3).
- Iterative RFT with SRR-annotated data yields over 10% absolute gain compared to outcome-only supervision across benchmarks (e.g., BrowseComp 13.9→16.2, BrowseComp-ZH 32.8→38.3, Xbench-DeepSearch 56.5→61.3).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** SRR-Judge delivers reliable step-level evaluation by distilling knowledge from a large teacher model and filtering noisy annotations via point-biserial correlation with final correctness.
- **Mechanism:** Trajectories from multiple models are annotated with step-level ratings using DeepSeek-V3.1 and self-consistency. The average rating per trajectory is correlated with binary outcome; trajectories with correlation below 0.7 are discarded. The filtered data fine-tunes a smaller model (QwQ-32B) to serve as an efficient judge.
- **Core assumption:** Step-level ratings from a strong teacher are informative but contain noise; filtering based on outcome correlation removes trajectories where step ratings are poor predictors of success, yielding a higher-quality training set.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "SRR-Judge is a step-level evaluator distilled from DeepSeek-V3.1 and filtered via point-biserial correlation."
  - [Section 2.2] Details the annotation and filtering procedure.
  - [corpus] Neighboring works on process reward models (e.g., "PRISM", "ConfProBench") explore similar distillation and filtering ideas, but direct evidence for this specific combination is limited to the paper itself.
- **Break condition:** If the teacher’s step-level judgments do not correlate with outcome in the target domain, distillation will not transfer reliable evaluation ability. Overly aggressive filtering may leave too little training data.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The rate-and-refine inference workflow uses SRR-Judge to correct thought-action pairs on the fly, improving search trajectories without weight updates.
- **Mechanism:** At each ReAct step, the agent generates *N* candidate thought-action pairs. SRR-Judge assigns a rating (1–5) to each; the highest-rated is selected. If no candidate exceeds a threshold (4), the highest-rated is refined by SRR-Judge. This loop continues until an answer is produced or the step budget is exhausted.
- **Core assumption:** Local errors in reasoning or search queries can be detected and fixed by a reliable step-level judge, preventing error accumulation in long-horizon search.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Integrated into a rate-and-refine ReAct workflow, it enables inference-time correction..."
  - [Section 2.3] Describes the rate-and-refine loop and parameters.
  - [corpus] Neighbors such as "Watch Every Step! LLM Agent Learning via Iterative Step-Level Process Refinement" and "Search-R2: Enhancing Search-Integrated Reasoning via Actor-Refiner Collaboration" demonstrate the broader promise of step-level refinement.
- **Break condition:** Misaligned ratings (e.g., high scores for low-quality steps) will propagate errors. The paper shows that applying SRR-Judge to a much stronger model (DeepSeek-V3.1) degrades performance (Table 4), indicating the judge must be competent relative to the policy.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Iterative rejection sampling fine-tuning (RFT) with SRR-annotated trajectories yields substantial gains over outcome-only supervision by aligning the policy with step-level quality.
- **Mechanism:** The current policy generates trajectories using the rate-and-refine workflow with *N*=5. The best trajectory (by step ratings) is selected and used to fine-tune the policy via rejection sampling. This process repeats for multiple iterations, each time generating higher-quality data.
- **Core assumption:** Step-level supervision provides a denser, more informative training signal than binary outcome, and iterative refinement allows the policy to climb a step-quality gradient.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "Iterative RFT with SRR-annotated data yields over 10% absolute gain compared to outcome-only supervision."
  - [Section 2.4] Outlines the iterative RFT procedure.
  - [Section 4.3] Table 3 shows SRR-RFT consistently outperforming ORM-RFT.
  - [corpus] Neighbors like "PRISM" and "S^3cMath" explore iterative process refinement, supporting the general method.
- **Break condition:** Gains may saturate after a few iterations; if SRR-Judge’s ratings fail to improve alongside the policy, annotation quality could deteriorate, limiting further progress.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Base Policy (Search Agent) -> Rate-and-Refine Loop (orchestrates candidate generation, calls SRR-Judge for step rating/refinement) -> improved ReAct trajectory; RFT Pipeline: Rate-and-Refine Loop (N=5) -> filtered correct trajectories -> SFT -> updated Base Policy.

**Critical path:**
1. Judge training: Collect multi-model trajectories → annotate with DeepSeek-V3.1 (self-consistency) → filter via point-biserial → fine-tune QwQ-32B.
2. Inference: For each query, run rate-and-refine ReAct with SRR-Judge (N=1) and measure pass@1; also run Best-of-N (N=5) to generate high-quality trajectories.
3. Data generation: Use rate-and-refine with N=5 to produce high-quality trajectories for a training query set.
4. Iterative RFT: Start from QwQ-32B-SFT (SFT on DeepSeek-V3.1 correct trajectories). For two iterations: generate 6K new trajectories using rate-and-refine (N=5), filter for correct final answers, perform SFT, evaluate pass@1. Compare against outcome-only RFT baseline.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Judge size: 32B balances capability and cost; larger teachers (e.g., DeepSeek-V3.1) are more accurate but prohibitively expensive for per-step evaluation.
- Filtering threshold: point-biserial >0.7 ensures quality but may reduce data quantity.
- Best-of-N *N*: Larger *N* improves candidate quality but increases inference compute; *N*=5 for offline data generation, *N*=1 for online.
- RFT iterations: Two iterations used; more may yield diminishing returns or require careful judge re-evaluation.

**Failure signatures:**
- Low point-biserial correlation (<0.4) on held-out trajectories indicates an unreliable judge.
- Performance drop when applying SRR to a model stronger than the judge (as in Table 4).
- No improvement after RFT or overfitting to SRR-judge quirks (e.g., repetitive refinements).
- Extreme rating distributions (e.g., >80% rating=5) suggest judge is not discriminative.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Correlation validation: Compute point-biserial correlation of SRR-Judge on a held-out set; target >0.45.
2. Inference-time refinement test: Compare base agent vs. base+SRR on a dev set (e.g., 100 queries); expect +3–8% absolute pass@1.
3. Single-iteration RFT: Generate SRR-annotated trajectories, fine-tune base agent, evaluate vs. SFT with outcome-only data; expect >5% absolute gain on benchmarks.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **1. Robustness to stronger agents**  
  *Question:* How can SRR-Judge be made robust enough to supervise agentic models significantly stronger than itself without causing performance degradation?  
  *Basis in paper:* The Limitation section states that using SRR-Judge to refine DeepSeek-V3.1 reduces performance (Table 4), and “further advances will be needed to make SRR-Judge robust enough to handle such scenarios.”  
  *Why unresolved:* Current SRR-Judge is distilled from DeepSeek-V3.1 but fine‑tuned on a smaller model (QwQ‑32B); it may not capture the reasoning patterns of much stronger agents, leading to mis‑guidance.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Develop techniques such as adaptive judge scaling, knowledge distillation from stronger to weaker judges, or ensemble judges that maintain accuracy across model strengths; evaluate by measuring whether the judge’s step‑level ratings still correlate with correctness and whether refinement improves rather than harms the stronger model’s performance.

- **2. Higher correlation evaluator**  
  *Question:* Can we design a step‑level evaluator that achieves higher point‑biserial correlation with final answer correctness, approaching the theoretical upper bound?  
  *Basis in paper:* Section 4.1 reports that all judges remain below 0.5 correlation and notes “judge–answer correlation is inherently upper‑bounded, even for strong evaluators.”  
  *Why unresolved:* SRR‑Judge evaluates only reasoning and action quality, ignoring external factors like environment returns and stochastic exploration that also affect final correctness.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Incorporate additional signals (e.g., tool execution outcomes, environment dynamics) into the judge’s input; train judges on richer data that includes these factors; compare correlation improvements on held‑out trajectories.

- **3. Iteration saturation**  
  *Question:* Does iterative rejection‑sampling fine‑tuning (RFT) with SRR‑Judge continue to yield performance gains beyond two iterations, or does it plateau/decline?  
  *Basis in paper:* Inferred from Section 4.3 shows monotonic improvement from SFT → Iter1 → Iter2, but only two iterations are performed.  
  *Why unresolved:* It is unknown whether further iterations would provide additional benefits or cause overfitting or degradation in model performance.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Run RFT for 3–5 iterations on the same benchmarks, tracking pass@1 and data quality metrics; analyze whether gains diminish or reverse.

- **4. Generalization beyond search**  
  *Question:* Can the SRR‑Judge framework be effectively generalized to non‑search agentic tasks (e.g., robot control, code generation) with minimal domain adaptation?  
  *Basis in paper:* Inferred; the paper targets search‑integrated reasoning; its four criteria (Clarity, Logical Structure, Query Appropriateness, Coverage) are tailored to web search, and the method is not evaluated on other domains.  
  *Why unresolved:* Different agentic tasks have distinct action spaces and reasoning patterns; the current criteria may not transfer directly, requiring redesign of the judge’s prompt and training data.  
  *What evidence would resolve it:* Adapt SRR‑Judge to a new domain (e.g., code generation) by redefining evaluation criteria, collecting domain‑specific step‑level annotations (possibly via the same distillation pipeline), and demonstrating that (a) the judge’s ratings correlate with task success and (b) integrating the judge into a rate‑and‑refine workflow improves the agent’s performance on that domain.

## Limitations
- Uncertainty in exact training data splits and class-balancing details may affect reproducibility of SRR-Judge training.
- Missing hyperparameters for SRR-Judge and RFT fine-tuning (learning rate, batch size, optimizer) could lead to implementation drift.
- Long-term judge staleness across RFT iterations: as the policy improves, the static judge may become misaligned, limiting further gains.

## Confidence
- Step-level judge reliability: Medium
- Inference-time refinement gains: Medium
- Iterative RFT improvements: Medium-High

## Next Checks
1. **Rating distribution analysis:** Compute entropy of SRR-Judge’s step ratings on a held-out set; expect uniform-ish spread (min entropy >0.5). Concentrated ratings (e.g., >80% =5) indicate poor discrimination.
2. **Filtering ablation:** Train SRR-Judge without point-biserial filtering and compare correlation (target >0.4) and downstream pass@1 gains; large drop (>0.1) confirms filtering necessity.
3. **Iteration saturation test:** Run iterative RFT for 4–5 iterations, tracking pass@1 and judge-policy alignment (e.g., judge correlation on new trajectories). Look for plateaus or degradation to identify staleness.
