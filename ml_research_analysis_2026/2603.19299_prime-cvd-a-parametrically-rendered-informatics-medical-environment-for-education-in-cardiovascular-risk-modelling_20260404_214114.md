# PRIME-CVD: A Parametrically Rendered Informatics Medical Environment for Education in Cardiovascular Risk Modelling
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.19299
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19299
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Introduces two publicly released, privacy-safe synthetic assets for teaching cardiovascular risk modelling, derived from a causal DAG parameterised with Australian population statistics, enabling practice in EMR cleaning, harmonisation, and Cox regression.

## Executive Summary
PRIME-CVD addresses the critical lack of accessible, privacy-safe electronic medical record (EMR) data for teaching cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk modelling. It provides two complementary synthetic assets: (1) a clean, analysis-ready cohort of 50,000 simulated adults, and (2) a relational EMR-style database containing the same cohort but engineered with realistic structural and lexical heterogeneity, missingness, and unit inconsistencies. Both assets are generated from a user-specified causal directed acyclic graph (DAG) parameterised with publicly available Australian statistics and published effect estimates. Pedagogical validation demonstrates that learners can successfully reconstruct the relational cohort, perform socioeconomic stratification, and fit multivariable Cox models with expected risk gradients, making it a valuable resource for education in epidemiology and informatics.

## Method Summary
The generation pipeline is deterministic and non‑machine‑learning. First, a causal DAG encodes relationships among socioeconomic status (IRSD), demographics, behaviours (smoking), anthropometrics (BMI), chronic conditions (diabetes, CKD, atrial ﬁbrillation), biomarkers (HbA1c, eGFR, SBP), and the composite CVD outcome. Exogenous variables are sampled from population distributions (e.g., ABS data). Child nodes are generated via conditional logistic or linear models using coefficients derived from published odds ratios and effect sizes. This produces Data Asset 1: a flat, clean cohort table. Data Asset 2 is then created by a deterministic transformation that splits the cohort into three relational tables (`PatientMasterSummary`, `PatientChronicDiseases`, `PatientMeasAndPath`) and injects realistic EMR artefacts: non‑sequential IDs, missing values (e.g., smoking status for non‑smokers), heterogeneous diagnosis labels, unit inconsistencies (HbA1c in % vs mmol/mol), and non‑uniform measurement dates. All parameters and transformation rules are publicly documented, enabling full reproducibility.

## Key Results
- Data Asset 1 yields a plausible Australian adult cohort: median age 49.6 years, 73.1% non‑smokers, diabetes 7.4%, CKD 0.7%, AF 0.7%, median BMI 28.3 kg/m², SBP 123.1 mmHg, eGFR 82.9 mL/min/1.73 m², and a composite CVD event rate of ~4% over a mean 4.8‑year follow‑up.
- Data Asset 2 replicates the same cohort within a relational EMR schema containing realistic heterogeneity; learners can reconstruct the underlying clean cohort using standard cleaning and linkage techniques.
- Pedagogical exercises confirm the assets support learning objectives: cohort reconstruction (matching disease cohorts within ~1% error), socioeconomic stratification (IRSD gradient in CVD incidence), and multivariable Cox regression (hazard ratios align with the DAG’s ground truth, e.g., diabetes HR ~4.15, CKD HR ~1.08).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Synthetic cohort generation from a user-specified causal DAG parameterized with public statistics yields a privacy-safe, epidemiologically coherent dataset.
- **Mechanism:** Variables are sampled along a DAG where each node's distribution is conditioned on its parents. Exogenous nodes (IRSD quintile, age) use population distributions from the Australian Bureau of Statistics. Child nodes (smoking, BMI, diseases, biomarkers) use logistic/linear models with coefficients derived from published effect estimates (e.g., ORs from AIHW). This ensures internal consistency without referencing real individuals.
- **Core assumption:** Published effect estimates and population statistics are sufficient to approximate the joint distribution of cardiovascular risk factors in the target population.
- **Break condition:** If parameterization (e.g., age-specific diabetes OR) does not align with the intended population, resulting gradients will be implausible, undermining pedagogical realism.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Transforming the clean cohort into a relational EMR-style dataset introduces realistic data engineering challenges (heterogeneity, missingness, linkage) without compromising privacy.
- **Mechanism:** A deterministic pipeline redistributes variables across three tables and injects lexical heterogeneity (multiple diagnosis labels), unit inconsistency (HbA1c % ↔ mmol/mol), patterned missingness (non-smokers), and non-sequential IDs. Each artifact mimics a real EMR quirk while remaining reproducible from the source cohort.
- **Core assumption:** The injected distortions accurately reflect typical primary-care EMR messiness and are learnable via standard cleaning/harmonization techniques.
- **Break condition:** If injected noise patterns are unrealistic or too severe, cohort reconstruction may become ambiguous or fail, reducing educational utility.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Pedagogical alignment is achieved through two complementary data assets supporting progressive learning objectives (data cleaning → causal modeling).
- **Mechanism:** Data Asset 1 enables direct survival modeling and stratification exercises. Data Asset 2 requires relational reconstruction first, teaching EMR-specific workflows. Both assets retain known ground-truth risk gradients (e.g., diabetes → CVD), allowing learners to validate model outputs against expected effect sizes.
- **Core assumption:** The simulated risk relationships (hazard ratios from the DAG) are plausible for cardiovascular risk modeling and match published Australian studies.
- **Break condition:** If hazard ratios deviate significantly from literature, learners may question model validity, though this could itself be a teaching moment about model specification vs. data truth.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Causal directed acyclic graphs (DAGs)**
  - **Why needed here:** The dataset is generated from a DAG; understanding parent–child relationships (e.g., IRSD → BMI → Diabetes) explains variable dependencies and is essential for correct model specification (avoiding adjustment bias).
  - **Quick check question:** In the PRIME-CVD DAG, is smoking a confounder, mediator, or collider for the effect of IRSD on CVD? How should it be handled in a Cox model?
- **Concept: Survival analysis with censored time-to-event data**
  - **Why needed here:** The primary outcome is 5-year composite CVD, requiring Cox proportional hazards modeling to account for variable follow-up and right-censoring (Data Asset 1).
  - **Quick check question:** Why is `cvd_time` continuous rather than a fixed 5-year binary indicator? How does the Cox model handle administrative censoring at "2022-12"?
- **Concept: EMR data heterogeneity and linkage**
  - **Why needed here:** Data Asset 2 deliberately replicates real-world messiness: free-text diagnosis labels, non-sequential IDs, unit inconsistencies, missingness. Learners must reconstruct a clean cohort before analysis.
  - **Quick check question:** In `PatientMeasAndPath`, why are HbA1c values sometimes in "%" and sometimes in "mmol/mol"? How would you standardize them for analysis?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  DAG engine -> Relational transformer -> Validation notebooks
- **Critical path:**
  1. Start with Data Asset 1 to understand the clean cohort structure and verify basic epidemiologic gradients (e.g., diabetes prevalence ~7.4%).
  2. Move to Data Asset 2; practice reconstructing a patient-level table by linking `PatientMasterSummary`, `PatientChronicDiseases`, and `PatientMeasAndPath`.
  3. Fit a Cox model on the reconstructed cohort and compare hazard ratios to Table 9; discrepancies reveal reconstruction errors or overadjustment.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Realism vs. privacy:** The DAG approach eliminates re-identification risk but simplifies correlations (only DAG-encoded edges exist). Real datasets like MIMIC offer more complex correlations but restrict access.
  - **Clean vs. messy:** Two assets serve different pedagogy: clean data teaches statistical modeling; messy data teaches engineering. Neither is "truer" to real EMRs, but together they scaffold skills.
  - **Parameter transparency vs. completeness:** All parameters are public (AIHW, ABS), but the DAG omits some real-world confounders (e.g., genetics, detailed comorbidities). The included variables are assumed sufficient for CVD risk modeling.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Cohort summary statistics deviate >5% from reported values → parameter mis-specification.
  - Reconstruction exercise yields poor sensitivity/specificity for disease cohorts (e.g., missing >10% of diabetics) → label dictionaries are incomplete.
  - Cox model fails to converge or yields NaN HRs → perfect separation or missing data in key covariates post-reconstruction.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Reproduce Table 1:** Load Data Asset 1 and compute all descriptive statistics. Compare to paper's Table 1; any discrepancy suggests data version mismatch or generation error.
  2. **Reconstruct CKD/T2DM cohorts:** Using only Data Asset 2, implement the regex-based matching from Appendix D.2. Visualize IRSD distributions. Failure indicates flawed label dictionaries or linkage logic.
  3. **Fit Cox model on Data Asset 1:** Using `lifelines` or `survival`, replicate Table 9 and Figure 6. Check that HRs for age, diabetes, HbA1c are >1 and statistically significant; CKD HR near 1.0 is expected. Divergence suggests incorrect formula implementation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How generalizable is the PRIME-CVD approach to non-Australian populations, and what modifications are needed to reparameterize the DAG for other demographic contexts?
- **Basis in paper:** PRIME-CVD uses Australian Bureau of Statistics and AIHW data to parameterise its causal DAG, aligning with Australian population structures. The authors do not explore whether this parameterisation would produce valid synthetic cohorts for other countries or ethnic groups.
- **Why unresolved:** Risk factor prevalences, effect sizes, and even causal pathways can differ across populations. Applying the same parameters without recalibration may yield unrealistic distributions and relationships, potentially misleading learners about global CVD epidemiology.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Studies that reparameterise the DAG using publicly available statistics from diverse regions (e.g., Europe, Asia, North America) and then compare synthetic cohort characteristics to real-world reference datasets. Educational pilots could assess whether learners correctly interpret population-specific patterns.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** To what extent can synthetic datasets like PRIME-CVD support advanced methodological training (e.g., high-dimensional machine learning, causal mediation analysis) beyond basic survival modeling, and what are the limits of realism in the underlying DAG?
- **Basis in paper:** The authors state that "PRIME-CVD is not a substitute for real clinical data, nor are models trained on it clinically deployable" and note in Appendix E that "only relationships explicitly encoded in the DAG manifest as correlations, while unmodelled or subtle dependencies remain absent." Pedagogical validation is limited to cohort reconstruction, stratification, and Cox modeling.
- **Why unresolved:** The synthetic data's utility for sophisticated tasks—such as handling complex missing data, time-varying confounding, or high-dimensional feature selection—has not been demonstrated. The simplified DAG may fail to reproduce the intricate correlation structure of actual EMR data, which could affect the transferability of methods learned on PRIME-CVD.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Development of new teaching modules targeting advanced methods, evaluation of student performance on these tasks using PRIME-CVD versus real or more complex synthetic data, and qualitative feedback on whether the data challenges adequately mimic real-world EMR complexities.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** How can PRIME-CVD be extended to incorporate richer social determinants of health (e.g., individual-level education, income, neighborhood environment) and their dynamic interplay with clinical risk factors to improve teaching about health inequities?
- **Basis in paper:** PRIME-CVD currently uses only the area-level Index of Relative Socioeconomic Disadvantage (IRSD) as a socioeconomic measure. The authors discuss teaching about socioeconomic gradients and note that real EMRs often suppress rare high-risk strata, limiting fairness education. However, PRIME-CVD does not model individual-level SES or intersectionality (e.g., race × SES).
- **Why unresolved:** Using a single area-level index restricts learners' ability to explore nuanced equity questions, such as how individual resources or place-based effects interact with clinical factors to shape CVD risk. Critical dimensions of health disparity may be omitted.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Integration of additional synthetic variables (e.g., personal education, income, racial/ethnic identity) calibrated to appropriate population data, coupled with curriculum that guides students through intersectional analyses. Validation could involve expert review of the synthetic distributions and student assessments of equity concepts.

## Limitations
- Exact reproducibility is limited by missing `RNG_SEED` and the precise lexical variant lists for EMR artifacts.
- The DAG’s parameterization relies on published effect estimates that may not perfectly reflect the current Australian population (e.g., temporal trends, heterogeneity).
- The simplified correlation structure (only DAG-encoded edges) may omit real‑world complexities, potentially affecting external validity of models.
- No empirical evaluation of learning outcomes; pedagogical claims rest on expert design and technical feasibility.

## Confidence
- **Privacy‑safety and generation reproducibility:** High (synthetic by design, deterministic pipeline).
- **Epidemiologic coherence (within Australian context):** Medium (parameters from authoritative sources but not directly validated against a contemporaneous real cohort).
- **EMR artifact realism and pedagogical utility:** Medium (technical reconstruction works, but realism and actual learning impact are assumed).

## Next Checks
1. **Australian epidemiology benchmark:** Compare key statistics from PRIME‑CVD (age‑specific diabetes prevalence, BMI distribution, CVD incidence) to the latest Australian National Health Survey or AIHW report. Discrepancies >5% would question parameter choices.
2. **EMR artifact representativeness:** Analyze a sample of real Australian primary‑care EMRs (e.g., from MedicineInsight) to characterize actual label variability and missingness. Use statistical tests (e.g., chi‑square) to compare these distributions to the injected artifacts in Data Asset 2.
3. **Learning outcomes trial:** Conduct a pre‑post study with students using PRIME‑CVD for a CVD risk module. Primary outcomes: accuracy of cohort reconstruction, Cox model specification, and interpretation of hazard ratios. Compare to a control group using a different dataset.
