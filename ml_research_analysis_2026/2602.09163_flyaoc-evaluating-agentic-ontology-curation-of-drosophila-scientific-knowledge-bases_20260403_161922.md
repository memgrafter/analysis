# FlyAOC: Evaluating Agentic Ontology Curation of Drosophila Scientific Knowledge Bases

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.09163
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09163
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A multi-agent architecture that partitions paper reading to manage context achieved the best performance at higher paper budgets, reaching 53.1% semantic recall@20 for Gene Ontology, 52.8% semantic recall@10 for expression, and 57.3% exact recall@20 for synonyms.

## Executive Summary
FlyAOC introduces a benchmark for evaluating end-to-end AI agent performance on scientific knowledge curation tasks using a corpus of 16,898 full-text Drosophila papers and 7,397 expert annotations. The study tests four agent architectures across three curation tasks: Gene Ontology assignment, expression pattern annotation, and synonym extraction. The key finding is that a multi-agent design, which delegates full-text reading to subagents to keep the orchestrator's context small, significantly outperforms other architectures at larger paper budgets. Architectural innovations like context partitioning yield larger gains than scaling the backbone language model. However, all methods leave substantial room for improvement, and agents primarily use retrieval to confirm existing parametric knowledge rather than discover novel information.

## Method Summary
The benchmark defines a curation task where, given a Drosophila gene symbol, an agent must query a corpus of full-text papers and produce structured annotations for three outputs: Gene Ontology terms (with semantic similarity scoring), expression patterns (anatomy and stage, also semantically evaluated), and historical synonyms (exact match). The ground truth consists of expert-curated annotations from FlyBase. Four agent architectures are evaluated: a memorization baseline (parametric knowledge only), a fixed pipeline (no feedback), a single-agent with iterative tool use (all retrieved papers in context), and a multi-agent system (an orchestrator delegates per-paper reading to subagents). Retrieval uses BM25 over the corpus, and a shared ontology resolution tool maps natural language descriptions to controlled vocabulary IDs. Performance is measured by recall@k, with the primary metric being micro-averaged recall on corpus-grounded annotations.

## Key Results
- Multi-Agent architecture achieves the highest recall at the largest paper budget (16 papers/gene): 53.1% (GO), 52.8% (expression), 57.3% (synonyms).
- Scaling the backbone model (GPT-5 vs. GPT-5-mini) yields only marginal improvements (+2-4%), while the gap between Single-Agent and Multi-Agent exceeds 10 points.
- Agents primarily use retrieval to confirm parametric knowledge; the memorization baseline (0 papers) already achieves ~49% recall on Gene Ontology, with retrieval adding only a modest gain.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Multi-Agent outperforms Single-Agent at higher paper budgets by avoiding context overflow.
- **Mechanism:** The orchestrator delegates full-text reading to subagents, each processing one paper in isolation. The orchestrator only receives structured outputs, keeping its context small and relevant, which prevents dilution of attention across many documents.
- **Core assumption:** LLM retrieval degrades when context is filled with many documents, especially when only a small fraction is relevant.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract notes Multi-Agent's gains at 16 papers/gene. Section 5.2 describes Single-Agent's performance decrease beyond a peak due to context overflow, which Multi-Agent avoids via partitioning. The neighboring paper "CurateGPT" also highlights context management importance.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Agents use retrieval mainly to confirm parametric knowledge rather than discover new information.
- **Mechanism:** LLM pre-training encodes substantial biological knowledge for well-studied genes; retrieval serves to validate or provide evidence for these known facts but rarely uncovers novel annotations absent from training.
- **Core assumption:** LLMs have encoded significant biological knowledge from their pre-training corpora.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states agents use retrieval for confirmation. Section 5.2 shows memorization (0 papers) achieves 49% on GO, and retrieval at 16 papers adds only 4 points. The neighboring paper "Automating Intervention Discovery" focuses on discovery, contrasting FlyAOC's observation.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Architectural choices (e.g., multi-agent, feedback) have a larger impact than scaling the backbone model.
- **Mechanism:** Sophisticated agent architectures enable effective tool use, iterative refinement, and context management; larger models only yield marginal reasoning improvements, so design innovations are more cost-effective.
- **Core assumption:** After a baseline capability threshold, architectural improvements yield greater gains than parameter increases for this task.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract notes scaling yields only +2-4% gains. Section 5.3 shows a 10-point gap between Single-Agent and Multi-Agent versus a 2-4 point gain from model scaling. Neighboring paper "Agentic Publications" also stresses framework design over scale.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Input module -> Retrieval tool (BM25) -> Paper reading module (Single-Agent or Multi-Agent orchestrator/subagents) -> Extraction module (LLM per paper) -> Ontology resolution tool -> Aggregation module -> Output formatter.
- **Critical path:**  
  1. Given a gene, retrieval fetches top-k full-text papers (budget-limited).  
  2. Each paper is read (by subagent in Multi-Agent, or sequentially in Single-Agent) and annotations (GO terms, expression, synonyms) are extracted.  
  3. Natural language descriptions are resolved to ontology IDs.  
  4. The orchestrator or main agent aggregates resolved annotations across papers.  
  5. Aggregation deduplicates, filters by confidence, and ranks predictions.  
  6. Output JSON with ranked lists per task is produced.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Multi-Agent vs Single-Agent: Partitioning avoids context overflow but adds coordination overhead and risks information loss; Single-Agent retains context but degrades at scale.  
  - Pipeline vs Agentic: Fixed pipeline is simpler/cheaper but cannot correct errors; agentic with feedback is flexible but costly.  
  - Retrieval budget: More papers increase recall but also noise/cost; optimal budget depends on literature density.  
  - Model size vs architecture: Larger models give modest gains; architecture improvements are more cost-effective.  
  - Precision vs recall: Curation favors high recall; agents over-generate, lowering precision.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Single-Agent: Recall drop when paper budget exceeds 4–8 papers indicates context overflow.  
  - Pipeline: Low precision (<30%) and high duplicate rate due to rigid batch resolution without feedback.  
  - Multi-Agent: Large gap between subagent extraction quality and final recall suggests orchestrator aggregation failure.  
  - General: Very few predictions produced indicates retrieval/extraction failure; matching memorization performance suggests retrieval ineffectiveness.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Compare Memorization, Pipeline, Single-Agent, and Multi-Agent across paper budgets (1,2,4,8,16) to test scaling and context management.  
  2. Test backbone scaling (GPT-5-mini, GPT-4o, GPT-5) using Multi-Agent at 16 papers to isolate model size impact.  
  3. Evaluate agent handling of GO terms missing from the ontology by measuring recall on that subset and analyzing natural language description use.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1:** How can agents effectively extract and integrate information from scientific figures (e.g., microscopy images for expression patterns) to improve annotation quality?  
  Basis: Section 6.1 notes figures contain critical information but evaluation was text-only.  
  Why unresolved: Multimodal extraction was out of scope; requires new models and frameworks.  
  Evidence needed: Integrate visual tools and measure gains on expression annotation vs. text-only.
- **Question 2:** How can agents mitigate limited corpus coverage (~30% of relevant papers available) on annotation completeness?  
  Basis: Section 4.2 notes corpus covers ~24% of FlyBase-referenced papers, up to 30–40% for recent literature.  
  Why unresolved: Paywall access restrictions limit expansion; partnerships needed.  
  Evidence needed: Evaluate on corpus subsets (recent vs. old, open vs. paywalled) to gauge sensitivity.
- **Question 3:** How to reliably evaluate agent performance when ground truth (FlyBase) is incomplete, potentially missing correct predictions?  
  Basis: Section 6.1 acknowledges agents may produce correct annotations absent from ground truth.  
  Why unresolved: Current recall metrics conflate extraction errors with ground-truth gaps; human validation is expensive.  
  Evidence needed: Large-scale expert review of agent predictions not in FlyBase to estimate true recall.
- **Question 4:** How can multi-agent architectures balance context efficiency with information fidelity during corpus-scale synthesis?  
  Basis: Section 5.2 case studies show Multi-Agent avoids Single-Agent's overflow but suffers "information loss in subagent."  
  Why unresolved: Tradeoff present; no optimal coordination strategy identified.  
  Evidence needed: Ablation studies varying subagent output detail (raw snippets vs. summaries) and measuring per-annotation win rates.
- **Question 5:** How can agents be encouraged to propose novel terminology or concepts (missing terms) not present in the ontology?  
  Basis: Section 5.3 (missing-term setting): agents produced only 49 natural language descriptions for 144 missing terms, with low semantic similarity (mean 0.20).  
  Why unresolved: Agents lack willingness/ability; current prompting and tool constraints favor conservative outputs.  
  Evidence needed: Modified prompting (reward specificity) or training on data with novel concepts, measuring proposal rate/accuracy.

## Limitations
- Exact prompts and tool parameters (BM25 configuration, ontology search) are not detailed, risking replication drift.
- Benchmark is Drosophila-specific; findings may not generalize to other organisms or domains.
- Evaluation uses semantic similarity for GO/expression but exact match for synonyms, which may undercount valid synonyms with minor variations.

## Confidence
- Multi-Agent context partitioning effectiveness: **High** (gain consistent across budgets, but magnitude sensitive to prompts).
- Retrieval primarily for confirmation: **Medium** (modest improvement over memorization suggests limited discovery, but temporal data split unknown).
- Architecture innovations > model scaling: **High** (10-point architecture gap dwarfs 2-4% scaling gain, observed within same model family).
