# Overview of the TREC 2025 Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG) Track

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.09891
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09891
- **Reference count:** 12
- **Primary result:** Over 150 submissions were evaluated with a multi‑layered framework; automated relevance and support metrics strongly correlate with human judgments (Kendall’s τ ∼0.8–0.9), while fine‑grained relevance labeling shows low human‑LLM agreement (fraction ∼0.3, κ ∼0.0–0.2).

## Executive Summary
The TREC 2025 Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG) Track addressed the need for robust evaluation of systems that combine retrieval and generation to answer complex, long‑form information needs. It introduced multi‑sentence narrative queries derived from clustered real queries, employed the MS MARCO v2.1 segmented corpus, and defined a multi‑layered evaluation scheme: relevance scoring (0–4) based on sub‑narrative coverage, response completeness via vital nuggets and sub‑narrative coverage, and sentence‑level attribution support (Full/Partial/No). With more than 150 submissions, the track demonstrated that large language model ensembles can automate much of the evaluation with high run‑level agreement with human assessors, though automating fine‑grained relevance labels remains challenging.

## Method Summary
The track comprised four tasks: pure Retrieval (R), Augmented Generation with fixed retrieval (AG), end‑to‑end RAG, and a Relevance Judgment (RJ) task. Test data included 105 multi‑sentence narratives created by clustering real queries and expanding them with GPT‑4.1. The MS MARCO v2.1 segmented corpus provided fine‑grained passages. Each narrative was decomposed into atomic sub‑narratives and annotated with vital/okay nuggets. Evaluation combined manual NIST judgments and automated LLM assessments. Relevance was scored 0–4 based on the number of sub‑narratives adequately covered; response quality used strict vital nugget recall and sub‑narrative coverage; citation support was judged at the sentence level (Full/Partial/No). An ensemble of LLMs automated relevance, nugget extraction, and support evaluation, achieving high correlation with human scores at the run level, while the RJ task—where participants labeled documents—suffered from low human‑LLM agreement.

## Key Results
- Run‑level Kendall’s τ between automated and manual relevance scores reached ∼0.8–0.9, indicating strong correlation.
- GPT‑OSS 120B achieved τ∼0.8 for support evaluation, demonstrating stable performance in citation alignment.
- The Relevance Judgment task showed low human‑LLM agreement (fraction ∼0.3, κ ∼0.0–0.2), highlighting difficulty in automating fine‑grained relevance labeling.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Narrative Generation from Query Clusters
- **Claim:** Converting clusters of short queries into multi‑sentence narratives creates realistic, multi‑faceted information needs that stress retrieval and generation components.
- **Mechanism:** Queries are grouped by topic; a language model (GPT‑4.1 nano then GPT‑4.1) expands the cluster into a coherent 2–3 sentence narrative while preserving distinct aspects.
- **Core assumption:** Queries in the same cluster represent a single broader information need, and an LLM can fuse them into a natural‑language narrative that retains all essential facets.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - Abstract: “introduces long, multi‑sentence narrative queries to better reflect the deep search task…”
  - Section (Page 2): “the automated narrative generation process transformed clusters of related queries into coherent, multi‑sentence narratives…”
  - Corpus: The MS MARCO v2.1 segmented corpus provides fine‑grained passages that can address multiple angles.
- **Break condition:** If clustering merges unrelated intents, the narrative becomes incoherent or overly broad, weakening evaluation validity.

### Mechanism 2: Sub‑Narrative Decomposition for Structured Relevance
- **Claim:** Breaking complex narratives into atomic sub‑narratives and judging documents by the number of sub‑narratives they fully cover yields a consistent, interpretable relevance scale (0–4) for multi‑faceted queries.
- **Mechanism:** Human assessors (or LLMs) enumerate sub‑narratives capturing distinct aspects of the main narrative; a retrieved segment’s relevance is the count of fully addressed sub‑narratives.
- **Core assumption:** The set of sub‑narratives is exhaustive and non‑overlapping; covering more sub‑narratives correlates with greater utility for answering the overall information need.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - Abstract: “relevance scoring (0–4) based on sub‑narrative coverage.”
  - Section (Page 3): “Relevance assessment… begins by decomposing each narrative into a set of sub‑narratives… assign relevance labels to segments using a five‑point scale based on coverage.”
  - Corpus: The segmented corpus’s passage‑level granularity aligns with sub‑narrative granularity.
- **Break condition:** Poorly defined sub‑narratives (redundant, missing key aspects, overlapping) produce noisy relevance labels.

### Mechanism 3: LLM Ensemble for Scalable, High‑Correlation Evaluation
- **Claim:** Employing an ensemble of large language models (GPT‑4.1, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Qwen3 Thinking 32B, GPT‑OSS) to automate relevance, nugget, and support judgments achieves run‑level Kendall’s τ ≈ 0.8–0.9 compared to human assessments, enabling efficient evaluation of hundreds of submissions.
- **Mechanism:** Each LLM independently produces scores or extracts information; final metrics are derived via majority voting or aggregation (e.g., median), aligning strongly with manual NIST judgments at the run level.
- **Core assumption:** LLMs can consistently simulate human judgment on these specific IR evaluation tasks when their outputs are combined, despite individual model noise.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - Abstract: “Results showed strong run‑level Kendall’s τ (∼0.8–0.9) between automated and manual relevance scores, with GPT‑OSS 120B providing stable support evaluation (τ∼0.8).”
  - Section (Page 6): “Automatic Assessment… an ensemble of cutting‑edge large language models… is utilized to generate and cross‑validate relevance scores.”
  - Corpus: The MS MARCO v2.1 corpus served as the common test collection, with NIST manual judgments as gold standard.
- **Break condition:** The approach shows limited agreement for the fine‑grained Relevance Judgment task (fraction ≈ 0.3, κ ≈ 0.0–0.2), indicating not all subtasks are equally amenable to LLM automation.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Narrative Loader -> Retriever -> (optional Reranker) -> Generator -> Formatter

**Critical path:**  
- For end‑to‑end RAG: minimal pipeline is Retriever (top‑100) -> Generator.  
- For AG: Generator only (retrieval fixed via provided reference lists).  
- For R: Retriever only.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Retrieval:* Sparse (BM25) is fast and interpretable; dense (e.g., Arctic‑Embed‑L) may improve recall but requires embedding storage. Larger k increases coverage risk of noise.  
- *Generation:* Extractive (copying sentences) guarantees citation fidelity but lacks synthesis; abstractive (using an LLM) can integrate sources but risks hallucination. Prompt design strongly influences citation accuracy.  
- *Reranking:* Improves relevance but adds latency; may be unnecessary if first‑stage retrieval is already high‑quality.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Very low sub‑narrative coverage (<0.2) – the answer misses most key aspects.  
- Weighted support <0.4 – citations do not align with answer content (hallucinations or missing citations).  
- Large gap between automated and manual scores on the same run – possible overfitting to the automated judge or systematic bias.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Baseline: BM25 retrieval (k=100) + extractive generation (top snippet copy) with trivial citation (first retrieved segment). Record sub‑narrative coverage and support to establish a floor.  
2. Swap extractive generator for an abstractive LLM (e.g., GPT‑4.1) with a citation‑forcing prompt; keep BM25 retrieval. Measure change in coverage and support.  
3. Add a reranker (e.g., RankQwen3‑32B) between retrieval and generation. Compare improvements in relevance metrics (nDCG) and downstream coverage/support.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question:** Why do large language models (LLMs) exhibit such low agreement with human assessors on the fine‑grained relevance labeling task for complex, multi‑sentence narratives (fraction ∼0.3, κ ∼0.0–0.2), despite strong run‑level correlations in other evaluation components?  
**Basis:** [explicit] The abstract and Section 4.3 explicitly state that the new Relevance Judgment task yielded low human‑LLM agreement, highlighting challenges in automating fine‑grained relevance labeling.  
**Why unresolved:** The paper identifies this as a key challenge but does not analyze the root causes, such as whether the complexity of sub‑narrative coverage or the 0‑4 scale is inherently difficult for LLMs to judge consistently without deeper contextual understanding.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** A detailed error analysis comparing human and LLM judgments, investigating failures on specific sub‑narrative types or narrative complexities. Controlled studies with simplified labeling schemes or iterative LLM refinement prompts could isolate the source of disagreement.

### Open Question 2
**Question:** Does the semi‑automated pipeline for generating sub‑narratives from clustered queries introduce scalability bottlenecks or systematic biases that limit the consistency and coverage of the evaluation framework?  
**Basis:** [inferred] Section 2 describes sub‑narrative generation as a "semi‑automated" process using GPT‑4.1 nano and manual post‑editing, but provides no analysis of inter‑annotator agreement among human editors or the cost/throughput of this step.  
**Why unresolved:** The paper treats this process as a practical solution but does not evaluate its reliability or whether the human‑in‑the‑loop step creates subjective variability that could affect downstream metrics like nugget extraction and relevance scoring.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** A study measuring the consistency of sub‑narratives generated by different human editors or across different random seeds in the initial LLM step. An ablation comparing fully automated sub‑narrative generation against the current pipeline would reveal its impact on final system rankings.

### Open Question 3
**Question:** What explains the high run‑level Kendall’s τ (∼0.8–0.9) but increased scatter at the individual narrative level in automated relevance assessment (using an LLM ensemble), and does this variability correlate with specific narrative characteristics?  
**Basis:** [inferred] Section 4.1 and Figure 6 note that while run‑level correlation is strong, "scatter becomes more pronounced" at the narrative‑avg and all‑narratives levels, suggesting noise in localized patterns. The paper does not investigate why this occurs.  
**Why unresolved:** This pattern is reported but not analyzed. It is unclear whether certain narratives (e.g., by topic, length, or number of sub‑narratives) inherently produce noisier automated judgments, which would affect the reliability of evaluation for those queries.  
**What evidence would resolve it:** A post‑hoc correlation analysis between narrative‑level disagreement (human vs. automated scores) and metadata features of the narratives (e.g., number of vital sub‑narratives, lexical diversity, cluster size). Controlled experiments on a held‑out set of narratives with known difficulty could validate the findings.

## Limitations
- The full dataset (105 narratives, query clusters, manual qrels) is not publicly available, limiting independent verification.
- Exact model versions and hyperparameters for components like "GPT‑4.1 nano" and "GPT‑OSS 120B" remain ambiguous.
- The Relevance Judgment task shows low inter‑rater agreement, indicating that automated fine‑grained relevance labeling for complex narratives is still challenging.

## Confidence
- Narrative generation validity: Medium
- Sub‑narrative decomposition: High
- LLM ensemble evaluation (relevance/support): High
- LLM ensemble evaluation (Relevance Judgment task): Medium
- Overall track metrics robustness: High
