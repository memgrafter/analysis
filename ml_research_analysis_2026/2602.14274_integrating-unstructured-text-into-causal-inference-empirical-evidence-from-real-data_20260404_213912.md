# Integrating Unstructured Text into Causal Inference: Empirical Evidence from Real Data

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.14274
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14274
- Reference count: 28
- Primary result: Causal estimates from unstructured product descriptions align with those from structured numerical features, demonstrating that text can substitute for tabular data in causal inference.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles a key limitation in causal inference: the dependence on structured tabular data, which is often missing or delayed (e.g., for new products). The authors propose a framework that uses unstructured text—product descriptions—to directly estimate causal effects. A decoder-only transformer (Mistral-3B) is fine‑tuned to predict potential outcomes and propensity scores, and doubly robust estimation with cross‑fitting ensures valid comparison with traditional gradient boosting models on numerical features.  

Empirical results on a large e‑commerce dataset (~430k products) show that text‑based causal estimates are highly consistent with numeric ones: ATE and ATET confidence intervals overlap, group‑level effects (GATE) correlate at 0.92, and individual effects (CATE) correlate at 0.60. This suggests that unstructured text can reliably support causal inference when tabular data are unavailable, enabling faster business decisions.

## Method Summary
The authors compare two modalities—text and numeric—using a T‑learner with K‑fold cross‑fitting. For each modality, separate models predict the potential outcomes under treatment and control, plus the propensity score. The text model fine‑tunes a Mistral‑3B decoder‑only transformer via LoRA, with a multi‑task loss combining regression for outcomes (weighted by treatment prevalence) and binary cross‑entropy for propensity. The numeric model uses gradient boosting, selected based on out‑of‑sample prediction performance. Cross‑fitted predictions feed a doubly robust estimator to compute ATE, ATET, GATE, and CATE. The two modalities are then compared through overlapping confidence intervals, Pearson correlations, and lift curves.

## Key Results
- ATE: text = -1.1, numeric = -0.94; overlapping 95% CIs. ATET: text = -1.2, numeric = -1.1; overlapping CIs.
- GATE across 20 product groups: Pearson r = 0.92; rankings aligned.
- CATE: Pearson r = 0.60; text‑based lift curve achieves 84% of the area under the optimal numeric‑based curve.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** A decoder‑only transformer language model (Mistral‑3B), fine‑tuned in a multi‑task fashion, can learn to predict potential outcomes \( \tilde{g}_0(Z^{text}), \tilde{g}_1(Z^{text}) \) and the propensity score \( \tilde{\mu}(Z^{text}) \) directly from unstructured product descriptions.
- **Mechanism:** The model ingests raw text \( Z^{text}_i \) (product descriptions). Its final hidden state is passed through a shared autoencoder to produce three outputs: the predicted outcome if untreated, if treated, and the probability of treatment. The loss function (Eq. 4) weightedly combines regression losses (using only the relevant treated/control observed outcome) and a propensity classification loss. This forces the model's internal representations to encode information predictive of both the outcome and the treatment assignment mechanism from text alone.
- **Core assumption:** The relevant causal information (confounders, effect modifiers) for the counterfeit reduction outcome is sufficiently present in the semantic and lexical content of the product descriptions. The model architecture and fine‑tuning process can extract this information.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 2.2 & 3.2] Describes the model architecture (Mistral‑3B, LoRA, autoencoder head) and multi‑task loss formulation explicitly.
  - [section 4] Reports out‑of‑sample correlation for the text‑based outcome models (0.64/0.58), comparable to gradient boosting on structured features (0.75/0.70), indicating the text model learns predictive signal.
  - [corpus] Neighbor papers like "Counterfactual Causal Inference in Natural Language with Large Language Models" and "TRIALSCOPE" explore similar premises of using language models for causal tasks from text, providing conceptual alignment but not direct evidence for *this* paper's empirical claim.
- **Break condition:** If product descriptions are extremely sparse, templated, or omit key attributes (e.g., sales history, material quality) that are captured in the structured numerical features, the text model’s learned representations will be uninformative for the causal task, leading to poor outcome/propensity prediction and, consequently, biased causal estimates.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The doubly robust (DR) estimator, constructed from the text‑model predictions \( (\tilde{g}_1, \tilde{g}_0, \tilde{\mu}) \), yields a causal effect estimate \( \tilde{\theta} \) that remains consistent if *either* the outcome model *or* the propensity score model is correctly specified, even if the other is biased.
- **Mechanism:** The DR label \( \tilde{c}_{\eta}(Z_i) = \tilde{g}_1(Z_i)-\tilde{g}_0(Z_i) + (Y_i - \tilde{g}_{T_i}(Z_i))\tilde{H}_i \) (Eq. 3) combines the naive CATE estimate with an outcome‑model residual weighted by the inverse propensity tilting factor \( \tilde{H}_i \). The final CATE estimate for an individual is the Best Linear Predictor (BLP) obtained by regressing \( \tilde{c}_{\eta} \) on \( (1, \tilde{\theta}(Z_i)) \) (Eq. 4). Theorem A.2 proves the bias terms \( \text{bias}_1, \text{bias}_2 \) vanish if either \( \tilde{\mu} \approx \mu \) or \( \tilde{g}_t \approx g_t \).
- **Core assumption:** The standard causal identifying assumptions hold (unconfoundedness, overlap, SUTVA). Additionally, the cross‑fitting procedure ensures the DR label is an out‑of‑sample prediction, mitigating overfitting bias in the final regression step.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 2.2 & Appendix A] Defines the DR estimator and provides the theoretical theorem (A.2) demonstrating the double robustness property via bias decomposition.
  - [section 2.3] Implements cross‑fitting (Algorithm 1), which is necessary for the DR estimator to be valid in practice with machine learning models, as it prevents the same data from being used to train the ML models and compute the final DR estimate.
  - [corpus] The neighbor "Proximal Causal Inference With Text Data" addresses confounding from text, a related but distinct challenge. The DR mechanism itself is a standard econometric/statistical technique; the paper's contribution is its *application* within a text‑based ML pipeline.
- **Break condition:** If *both* the text‑based outcome models \( (\tilde{g}_0, \tilde{g}_1) \) and the propensity model \( \tilde{\mu} \) are severely mis‑specified (e.g., text has no signal for either), the DR estimator’s bias terms do not cancel, and the final causal estimates will be inconsistent. The "double robustness" is a theoretical safeguard, not a guarantee against complete model failure.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Cross‑fitting, combined with the T‑learner architecture and DR estimation, enables a valid and low‑variance comparison between causal effect estimates derived from text (\( \tilde{\theta}^{text} \)) and those from numerical data (\( \tilde{\theta}^{num} \)).
- **Mechanism:** The data is partitioned into K folds. For each fold k, models are trained on the out‑of‑fold data \( I_{-k} \) for each modality (text and num). These models predict on the held‑out fold \( I_k \) to generate out‑of‑sample predictions for \( g_0, g_1, \mu \). These fold‑specific predictions are aggregated to compute the final ATE, ATET, GATE, and individual CATE estimates for the entire sample. This ensures that for every unit i, the models used to generate its causal estimate were trained on data *excluding* i, which is crucial for the theoretical properties of the DR estimator and for an apples‑to‑apples comparison (both modalities use the same cross‑fitting procedure and data splits).
- **Core assumption:** The data is independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.). The cross‑fitting splits are random and balanced enough that the training sets \( I_{-k} \) are representative of the full population.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 2.3] Algorithm 1 explicitly details the cross‑fitting procedure applied identically to both text and numerical model training and prediction.
  - [section 4] The ATE/ATET confidence intervals and GATE/CATE correlations are computed from these cross‑fitted estimates. The reported consistency (overlapping CIs, r=0.92 for GATE, r=0.60 for CATE) is the empirical validation of this comparative mechanism.
  - [corpus] Cross‑fitting is a standard technique in modern causal ML (e.g., in the cited Chernozhukov et al. papers). The corpus neighbors do not challenge this methodological choice.
- **Break condition:** If the data has strong temporal or spatial autocorrelation (violating i.i.d.), or if the product populations in different folds are systematically different (e.g., one fold contains only new products), the cross‑fitted estimates for each fold may target different causal parameters, inflating variance or introducing bias in the aggregated comparison.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Interactive Regression Model (IRM)**
  - **Why needed here:** The paper's entire formalism (Eqs. 1-2, definitions of ATE/ATET/GATE/CATE) is built on the IRM, which decomposes outcome \( Y \) into a deterministic function of treatment \( T \) and covariates \( Z \), plus an independent noise \( U \). This structural model is the basis for defining potential outcomes \( g_0(Z), g_1(Z) \) and the target estimands.
  - **Quick check question:** In the IRM \( Y = g(T, Z) + U \), if \( E[U|Z,T]=0 \), what does this imply about the relationship between observed \( Y \) and the unobserved potential outcomes \( Y(0), Y(1) \)?
- **Concept: Unconfoundedness (C ignorability)**
  - **Why needed here:** This is the central identifying assumption for causal inference from observational data. It states \( (Y(0), Y(1)) \perp T \mid Z \), meaning all common causes of treatment and outcome are captured in \( Z \). The paper's framework assumes this holds for the chosen \( Z \) (either text or num). The validity of the comparison between text and num estimates hinges on both representations satisfying this assumption to a similar degree.
  - **Quick check question:** If a product's "brand reputation" affects both its likelihood of enrollment (T) and its complaint rate (Y) but is only mentioned in the text description and not captured in the numerical features, how would this threaten unconfoundedness for the text‑based analysis?
- **Concept: Doubly Robust (DR) Estimation**
  - **Why needed here:** The paper uses the DR estimator (Eq. 3–4) as the core aggregation method for ATE/ATET/GATE/CATE. Its double robustness property is critical for the validity of the estimates derived from potentially mis‑specified machine learning models (both text‑based and num‑based). It ensures the final estimate is consistent if *either* the outcome regression *or* the propensity model is correct.
  - **Quick check question:** In the DR formula \( \tilde{c} = \tilde{g}_1-\tilde{g}_0 + (Y-\tilde{g}_T)\tilde{H} \), what is the role of the term \( (Y-\tilde{g}_T)\tilde{H} \) and why does it help correct bias?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Data Input Layer → Model Backbone → Multi‑Task Prediction Heads → Cross‑Fitting Controller → Aggregation & Estimation Module. For text: raw product description tokenized → Mistral‑3B (LoRA) → shared autoencoder heads for \( \tilde{g}_0, \tilde{g}_1, \tilde{\mu} \). For numeric: engineered features → Gradient Boosting → separate models for \( \tilde{g}_0, \tilde{g}_1, \tilde{\mu} \). Cross‑fitting controller orchestrates K‑fold splits and out‑of‑sample prediction aggregation.
- **Critical path:** 1) Preprocess text and numerical features, ensure identical K‑fold splits. 2) For each fold k: train text‑model (Mistral+heads) and num‑model (GBM) on \( I_{-k} \) using multi‑task loss (text) or separate losses (num). 3) Generate predictions \( (\tilde{g}_0, \tilde{g}_1, \tilde{\mu}) \) for held‑out fold \( I_k \) from both modalities. 4) After loop, concatenate all fold predictions. 5) Compute DR terms and naive CATEs. 6) Calculate ATE/ATET/GATE via sample averages; individual CATEs via OLS regression of DR label on naive CATE (BLP). 7) Compare metrics (CI overlap, correlations, lift curve).
- **Design tradeoffs:** Decoder‑only LLM vs. Gradient Boosting: LLM leverages semantic text but requires GPUs; GBM is efficient but inapplicable to raw text. Single multi‑task model vs. separate models: joint training shares representations and reduces cost but risks negative transfer; separate models avoid this but triple inference cost. LoRA vs. full fine‑tuning: LoRA reduces memory/time drastically (1% params) at slight performance cost; full fine‑tuning may be infeasible.
- **Failure signatures:** Text model underfitting: out‑of‑sample outcome correlations significantly lower than numeric (e.g., <0.4). Propensity mismatch: distributions of \( \tilde{\mu}^{text} \) and \( \tilde{\mu}^{num} \) differ (KS test p<0.01). CATE discrepancy: Pearson correlation between \( \tilde{\theta}^{text} \) and \( \tilde{\theta}^{num} \) low (<0.3) or lift curve near 50%. ATE/ATET CI non‑overlap: direct red flag of inconsistent population estimates.
- **First 3 experiments:** 1) **Baseline Prediction Check:** Train text and num models on full training set (ignoring cross‑fitting); compute out‑of‑sample \( R^2 \) and correlation for predicting observed Y in treated/control groups. Goal: text model achieves non‑trivial performance (Corr T/C > 0.5) comparable to num. 2) **Propensity Score Balance Diagnostics:** After cross‑fitting, compute standardized mean difference (SMD) for each numerical feature after weighting by \( 1/\tilde{\mu}^{text}(Z) \) (treated) and \( 1/(1-\tilde{\mu}^{text}(Z)) \) (control). Repeat for num‑based \( \tilde{\mu} \). Goal: both modalities yield similar covariate balance (mean absolute SMD < 0.1). 3) **Sensitivity to Text Truncation:** Train text model with different max sequence lengths (128, 256, 512 tokens). Re‑run full pipeline and compute Pearson correlation between \( \tilde{\theta}^{text} \) and \( \tilde{\theta}^{num} \). Goal: Identify if performance drops sharply at shorter lengths, indicating loss of critical causal information.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How can the proposed framework be extended to handle continuous or multi‑treatment settings?** The current method is designed for a single binary treatment, limiting applicability to scenarios with varying treatment intensity or combinations. Resolving this would require novel model architectures or loss functions validated on data with continuous/multi‑treatment structures.
2. **Does joint multi‑modal training on both unstructured text and structured numerical features improve predictive accuracy and statistical power?** The paper only compares separate unimodal models; a unified model ingesting both modalities might yield superior estimates (e.g., narrower confidence intervals, higher correlation with gold standards).
3. **What specific semantic or lexical features in unstructured product texts are most predictive of causal treatment effects?** The black‑box LLM leaves interpretability unexamined; understanding which text aspects drive predictions is needed for actionable business insights and alignment with numeric feature importances.
4. **How does the choice of transformer architecture (decoder‑only vs. encoder‑decoder, model size, pre‑training corpus) impact text‑based causal inference?** The paper uses a 3B decoder model for practicality but does not compare architectures or scales, leaving open the optimal design for this task.
5. **To what extent do the findings generalize beyond the e‑commerce anti‑counterfeit context?** Validation on a single domain leaves unknown whether text‑based causal inference transfers to other fields (e.g., healthcare, marketing) with different text genres (clinical notes, social media).

## Limitations
- Missing details on data preprocessing, feature engineering, and control matching hinder exact replication.
- Hyperparameter choices (LoRA configuration, loss weighting, training epochs) are not fully specified, risking performance variability.
- Generalizability is limited to a single domain (e‑commerce anti‑counterfeit campaign); transfer to other settings is untested.

## Confidence
- Consistency of text vs numeric causal estimates: Medium
- LLM‑based nuisance learning from text: Medium
- Double robustness and cross‑fitting validity: High

## Next Checks
1. Replicate outcome and propensity prediction performance: Using the same fold splits, train both models and report out‑of‑sample correlations for treated/control outcomes and propensity AUC. Expected: text model correlations ≥0.60 and AUC ≥0.75.
2. Balance diagnostics via weighted SMD: After applying DR weights from both modalities, compute average absolute standardized mean differences across all numerical features. Target: mean SMD < 0.1 for both, indicating adequate covariate balance.
3. Truncation sensitivity test: Vary maximum sequence length (128, 256, 512 tokens) and re‑estimate CATE correlation between text and numeric modalities. A drop below 0.5 at shorter lengths would indicate loss of critical causal information in text.
