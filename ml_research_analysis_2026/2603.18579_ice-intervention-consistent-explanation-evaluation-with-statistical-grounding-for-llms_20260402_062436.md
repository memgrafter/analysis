# ICE: Intervention-Consistent Explanation Evaluation with Statistical Grounding for LLMs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.18579
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18579
- **Reference count:** 33
- **Primary result:** Faithfulness evaluation is highly operator-dependent; deletion inflates scores on short texts but reverses on long texts; nearly one-third of configurations exhibit anti-faithfulness; zero correlation with human plausibility.

## Executive Summary
ICE proposes a statistically rigorous framework for evaluating the faithfulness of feature attribution explanations in large language models. It introduces matched random baselines and randomization tests across multiple intervention operators (deletion, retrieval infill) to compute win rates with confidence intervals, controlling for artifacts and chance. Empirical evaluation on seven models, four English tasks, and six languages reveals that faithfulness estimates vary dramatically by operator, with gaps up to 44 percentage points. Deletion tends to overestimate faithfulness on short texts but underestimates it on long texts. Surprisingly, in almost one-third of settings, explanations perform worse than random (anti-faithfulness). Faithfulness scores show no correlation with human-rated plausibility, suggesting they capture different aspects. Multilingual results indicate strong model-language interactions not explained by tokenization alone.

## Method Summary
ICE evaluates an explanation method by comparing its token importance rankings against a random baseline under various intervention operators (deletion, retrieval infill). For each operator, the input text is perturbed many times; each perturbation removes or replaces a token, and the model’s prediction change is measured. The explanation’s ranking is then compared to a random ranking via a permutation test: over M=50 random permutations, the fraction of permutations where the explanation outperforms the random baseline yields the win rate, with a binomial confidence interval. This process is repeated across operators, tasks, and languages to assess operator dependence and statistical reliability.

## Key Results
- Operator-dependence: faithfulness estimates differ dramatically between deletion and retrieval infill, with gaps as large as 44 percentage points.
- Deletion inflation reversal: on short texts, deletion typically inflates faithfulness; on long texts, the trend reverses, suggesting artifacts related to text length.
- Anti-faithfulness: nearly one-third of configurations produce win rates below 50%, indicating explanations are less reliable than random baselines.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
ICE achieves statistical grounding by using matched random baselines that preserve the distribution of tokens used in interventions, thereby controlling for potential biases such as out-of-vocabulary artifacts or the influence of high-frequency tokens. Randomization tests provide a distribution under a null hypothesis of no genuine attribution signal, allowing the computation of win rates and confidence intervals that reflect whether the explanation is reliably better than chance. By evaluating multiple intervention operators, ICE reveals operator-specific biases that could inflate or deflate faithfulness estimates, leading to more robust conclusions. The pairwise comparison to random permutations avoids assumptions about the shape of the attribution score distribution and directly measures discriminative power.

## Foundational Learning
- **Randomization Test**: A nonparametric method to assess whether an observed effect (explanation vs. random) is statistically distinguishable from random variation. *Why needed*: It provides a distribution under the null hypothesis without parametric assumptions. *Quick check*: Verify that win rates are computed over M permutations and that confidence intervals exclude 0.5 for clear effects.
- **Intervention Operators**: Functions (e.g., deletion, retrieval infill) that perturb the input text to measure the effect on model predictions. *Why needed*: Different operators probe different aspects of token importance and expose potential artifacts. *Quick check*: Apply both deletion and retrieval infill on a sample and ensure they produce varied perturbation patterns.
- **Matched Random Baseline**: A random ranking that uses the same pool of tokens as the intervention to ensure fairness. *Why needed*: Prevents mismatched distributions from skewing comparisons (e.g., OOV tokens). *Quick check*: Confirm that baseline draws tokens from the same set as those used in the perturbed inputs.
- **Win Rate**: The proportion of random permutations where the explanation’s token ranking yields a better perturbation effect than the random baseline. *Why needed*: It summarizes the explanation’s discriminative ability in a scale-invariant way. *Quick check*: Compute win rate for a known good explanation (oracle) – should be close to 1, and for a random explanation – should be near 0.5.
- **Confidence Interval**: An interval estimate for the win rate based on binomial uncertainty given M trials. *Why needed*: Quantifies statistical uncertainty due to finite permutations. *Quick check*: Ensure that reported CIs shrink as M increases and do not include 0.5 when the effect is strong.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**:  
ExplanationGenerator -> TokenScores -> InterventionOperator(Deletion/RetrievalInfill) -> PerturbedTexts -> ModelPredictions -> ComparisonEngine -> PermutationTest -> WinRate & ConfidenceInterval

**Critical Path**:  
1. Receive input text and a pretrained model.  
2. Generate token importance scores via the explanation method.  
3. For each intervention operator, generate many perturbations.  
4. For each perturbation, compute model output change (e.g., drop in target log probability).  
5. For each operator, compare the explanation’s ranking to M random rankings using the same set of perturbed texts; compute the fraction of permutations where explanation beats random (win rate).  
6. Derive binomial confidence intervals and report operator-specific win rates.

**Design Tradeoffs**:  
- *Number of permutations (M)*: Larger M yields tighter confidence intervals but increases computational cost. The paper uses M=50; scaling to larger models may require fewer permutations or early stopping.  
- *Operator choice*: Deletion is simple but can create out-of-distribution inputs; retrieval infill uses in-distribution tokens but may retain task-relevant signal. Choice biases results and must be reported.  
- *Baseline matching*: Strict matching (same token pool) avoids distributional shifts but may not be feasible for all operators.

**Failure Signatures**:  
- Win rates vary widely across operators without clear pattern → operator instability.  
- Win rates consistently below 50% → anti-faithfulness, possibly due to mismatched baselines or explanation corruption.  
- Very wide confidence intervals (e.g., ±0.2) → insufficient permutations or high variance in explanation performance.  
- Strong negative correlation between win rate and text length might indicate length-related artifacts.

**First Experiments**:  
1. Operator comparison on a single English task (SST-2) using a small model (e.g., GPT-2) to validate that win rates differ between deletion and retrieval infill.  
2. Permutation count sweep: vary M ∈ {10, 30, 50, 100} and measure win rate stability and CI width to ensure M=50 is sufficient for the given model/task.  
3. Random explanation sanity check: replace token scores with random noise and confirm that win rate hovers around 50% (no systematic bias).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How to efficiently scale ICE to models beyond 8B parameters?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "scaling to larger LLMs (70B+) requires either subsampling or adaptive early stopping, which we have not yet validated."
- **Why unresolved:** The M=50 design is computationally prohibitive for 70B+ models, preventing faithfulness evaluation on state-of-the-art systems.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Systematic comparison of fewer permutations, variance reduction, or early stopping on models up to 70B, with metrics of statistical reliability and computational savings.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Which linguistic properties (morphology, syntax, script) drive the observed model-language interactions in faithfulness?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "languages with complex morphology beyond Turkish (e.g., Finnish, Japanese) and tonal languages (e.g., Vietnamese) remain untested."
- **Why unresolved:** The study only covered 6 languages; the specific linguistic factors causing variation are unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Evaluate ICE across a diverse set of languages with controlled tokenization, using typological features as predictors in a regression analysis.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can we develop methods to explain why attributions are (un)faithful at a mechanistic level?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "behavioral evaluation remains the most scalable approach for billion-parameter models but does not explain why attributions are or are not faithful."
- **Why unresolved:** ICE measures correlation of tokens with prediction changes but offers no insight into internal reasoning processes that cause faithfulness.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Combine ICE with mechanistic interpretability probes (e.g., activation patching) to identify circuits responsible for faithful vs. unfaithful attributions.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Under what conditions does retrieval infill overestimate or underestimate faithfulness compared to deletion?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "replacement tokens drawn from the same distribution may preserve some task-relevant signal, potentially underestimating faithfulness. The GPT-2 Hindi case (retrieval exceeding deletion) suggests this concern is language- and model-dependent rather than systematic."
- **Why unresolved:** Retrieval infill is intended to mitigate OOD artifacts, but its bias direction varies (e.g., Hindi retrieval > deletion, while on short text deletion inflates), making it hard to choose a reliable operator.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments where task-relevant signals are inserted into replacement tokens, measuring operator bias across text lengths, languages, and model architectures; development of a predictive model for operator bias.

## Limitations
- Scope limited to 7 LLMs, 4 English tasks, and 6 languages; findings may not generalize to other models, tasks, or languages.
- Operator dependence is based on three interventions; other operators (e.g., substitution, attention ablation) remain untested, potentially revealing further biases.
- Tokenization analysis is partial; other linguistic factors (e.g., morphology, script) could drive the observed model-language interactions.

## Confidence
- **Operator-dependence and large gaps (44pp)**: High
- **Deletion inflates faithfulness on short texts but reverses on long texts**: Medium
- **Anti-faithfulness (~1/3 of configurations)**: Medium
- **Zero correlation with human plausibility (|r| < 0.04)**: High
- **Model-language interactions not explained by tokenization alone**: Low

## Next Checks
1. Expand operator set: Evaluate additional interventions (e.g., synonym replacement, attention ablation) to test generalizability of operator-dependence.
2. Broaden human evaluation: Collect plausibility judgments for explanations across varied tasks and languages to reassess correlation with faithfulness.
3. Deep dive into tokenization: Conduct ablation studies with character-level or tokenization-free models to isolate tokenization effects on multilingual performance.
