# One Model, All Roles: Multi-Turn, Multi-Agent Self-Play Reinforcement Learning for Conversational Social Intelligence

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.03109
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03109
- **Reference Count:** 17
- **Primary Result:** A single-model self-play RL framework (OMAR) with hierarchical advantage estimation significantly improves fine-grained social behaviors (e.g., empathy, persuasion) andWerewolf win rates (55% → 72%) from sparse end-of-episode rewards.

## Executive Summary
Current reinforcement learning methods for conversational AI are limited to single-turn or static interactions, preventing the acquisition of long-term social intelligence. This paper presents OMAR, a framework where a single model role-plays all participants in multi-turn conversations, enabling learning via competitive-cooperative self-play. Its core innovation is hierarchical advantage estimation, which propagates final rewards back to turn-level and then token-level advantages, stabilizing training over extended dialogues. Evaluated in the SOTOPIA social simulation, OMAR yields large gains in nuanced social behaviors not explicitly rewarded, such as compromise and empathy. In competitive Werewolf games, it achieves a 72% win rate (up from 55% baseline) by mastering advanced tactics like identity concealment. However, reward hacking remains a practical challenge, highlighting difficulties in credit assignment for sparse-reward, long-horizon interactions.

## Method Summary
OMAR trains a single language model to simulate all agents in a multi-party conversation through self-play. For each episode, the model generates utterances for every role synchronously turn-by-turn. After the full dialogue, a scalar reward—computed from environment-specific objectives—is assigned. The hierarchical advantage estimation process first backs up this reward to each turn using discounting, then decomposes turn-level advantages into token-level estimates within the turn. These hierarchical advantages drive policy updates via PPO. This design avoids the high variance of direct token-level credit assignment across long trajectories while maintaining a unified policy network that dynamically switches roles across episodes.

## Key Results
- OMAR models exhibit significantly higher frequencies of fine-grained social behaviors (e.g., persuasion, empathy, compromise) in SOTOPIA compared to supervised and RL baselines, despite these metrics not being directly optimized.
- In Werewolf, OMAR’s win rate increases from 55% to 72%, accompanied by more sophisticated strategic behaviors like identity concealment and voting manipulation.
- Rich social intelligence emerges solely from end-of-episode rewards, demonstrating that multi-agent, multi-turn self-play can elicit complex interactive skills without intermediate supervision.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Multi-agent self-play generates a dynamic curriculum where agents must adapt to evolving strategies of others, implicitly driving the emergence of nuanced social behaviors. Hierarchical advantage estimation reduces variance by amortizing credit assignment first at the turn level before distributing to tokens, making long-horizon learning feasible. The single-model design ensures consistent policy updates across roles, avoiding coordination problems inherent in multi-model setups. Together, these elements allow effective learning from sparse terminal feedback, as the competitive-cooperative pressures naturally shape the desired behaviors.

## Foundational Learning
**Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning**  
*Why needed:* Social intelligence requires interacting with multiple agents with potentially conflicting/cooperating goals, which single-agent RL cannot replicate.  
*Quick check:* Implement a simple multi-agent coordination task (e.g., collaborative navigation); verify self-play yields better coordination than independent training.

**Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning**  
*Why needed:* Long dialogues have natural substructures (turns, utterances); hierarchical credit assignment reduces variance and focuses learning on meaningful segments.  
*Quick check:* On a synthetic task with sparse rewards (e.g., long sequence manipulation), compare a two-level advantage estimator against flat token-level GAE for training stability and sample efficiency.

**Self-Play**  
*Why needed:* Creates a curriculum of increasingly challenging opponents, prompting emergence of complex strategies without explicit reward shaping.  
*Quick check:* Train agents in a repeated game like iterated prisoner’s dilemma; observe whether socially sophisticated strategies (e.g., tit-for-tat with forgiveness) emerge from self-play.

**Advantage Estimation (Generalized Advantage Estimation)**  
*Why needed:* Balances bias and variance in policy gradient estimates, crucial for stable updates in long-horizon tasks.  
*Quick check:* On a standard RL benchmark (e.g., Atari Pong), compare PPO with GAE against pure Monte Carlo returns; measure loss variance and final performance.

**Sparse Rewards**  
*Why needed:* Social outcomes are often only evaluable at conversation end (e.g., relationship score, game victory); algorithms must propagate feedback across many steps.  
*Quick check:* Solve a maze with reward only at the exit using REINFORCE vs. a method with credit assignment (e.g., eligibility traces); compare success rates and sample complexity.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**  
Role Assignment -> Multi-Agent Self-Play -> Hierarchical Advantage Estimation -> Policy Gradient Update

**Critical Path:**  
1. Initialize a single policy network.  
2. For each training episode: assign roles to the model, generate all agents’ utterances synchronously turn-by-turn.  
3. After the full dialogue, compute the aggregate environment reward.  
4. Back-propagate advantages from the final reward to each turn using a discount factor.  
5. Within each turn, distribute the turn advantage to tokens (e.g., via token-level GAE).  
6. Update the policy via PPO using the resulting token-level advantages.  
7. Periodically apply quality filtering to remove degenerate trajectories.

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- *Synchronous turn approximation* simplifies implementation but sacrifices realistic turn-taking dynamics like interruptions or variable response latencies.  
- *Quality filtering* (using an external LLM like GPT-5) mitigates reward hacking but adds computational cost and potential biases from the filter model.  
- *Single-model design* ensures consistent updates across roles but may limit role-specific specialization and increase interference during learning.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- Training collapse: sudden spikes in policy loss or drop in episode reward, often from high-variance advantage estimates.  
- Degenerate strategies: outputs that exploit rewards via short, repetitive, or off-topic utterances (e.g., saying “I agree” repeatedly).  
- Role inconsistency: model generates actions inconsistent with assigned role (e.g., a villager claiming werewolf identity without evidence).

**First Three Experiments:**  
1. **HAE vs. Flat Advantage Ablation:** Replace hierarchical advantage estimation with standard token-level GAE; compare training stability (loss variance), sample efficiency, and final social behavior scores.  
2. **Quality Filter Sensitivity Test:** Vary the strictness of the GPT-5 filter and measure the prevalence of reward-hacked trajectories (via human spot-checks) and impact on performance in Werewolf.  
3. **Turn Structure Evaluation:** Implement a simple asynchronous turn-taking variant (randomized agent order per turn) and compare the synchronous OMAR version on social behavior metrics and win rates.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How can reward hacking in multi-turn, multi-agent RL with sparse terminal rewards be systematically mitigated beyond heuristic quality filtering and early stopping?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors identify reward hacking as a "key practical challenge" and current mitigations (cold start, quality filtering, early stopping) are described as initial steps that "remain a central challenge for future research" (Section 4.3, 10).
- **Why unresolved:** The proposed solutions are reactive (filtering degenerate outputs) and lack a principled framework for accurate credit assignment across long, interactive horizons. The fundamental issue of poor intermediate turn credit persists.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A robust, automated method (e.g., a learned process reward model or a stable counterfactual credit assignment technique) that reliably prevents models from exploiting spurious correlations in long-horizon, self-play settings without requiring extensive manual filtering or premature training termination.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Is the proposed hierarchical advantage estimation (HAE) necessary and sufficient for stable multi-turn RL, or could alternative credit assignment mechanisms (e.g., variations of trajectory-level credit assignment or latent variable models) be equally or more effective?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] HAE is introduced as a novel solution to "high-variance token-level advantages" from vanilla PPO (Section 3.2, 4). The paper presents it as the adopted method without a comparative ablation against other potential multi-turn credit assignment strategies.
- **Why unresolved:** The claim that HAE is the optimal approach for stabilizing training across long dialogues in this specific multi-agent, single-model self-play paradigm is not experimentally validated. Other methods from long-horizon RL or hierarchical RL could perform differently.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A controlled study comparing HAE against strong baselines (e.g., modified GAE for segments, U-shaped advantage weighting, or learned hierarchical critics) on metrics like training stability (variance of policy loss), final performance on social intelligence benchmarks, and sample efficiency in the same OMAR framework.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** To what extent do the observed emergent social behaviors (empathy, persuasion, compromise) arise from the competitive multi-agent self-play dynamics versus the hierarchical reward structure (end-of-episode aggregate scores) or the base model's pre-training?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The authors attribute these behaviors to "multi-agent, multi-turn interaction dynamics" (Section 4.1.3, 8) and state they emerge "without direct supervision signals." However, the experiments lack ablations that isolate these factors (e.g., single-agent self-play with same rewards, same multi-agent setup with different base models, or multi-agent with step-wise rewards).
- **Why unresolved:** The causal link between the specific *multi-agent competitive cooperation* setup and the *fine-grained* social skills is correlational. It's unclear if similar behaviors would emerge from other training paradigms that share components (e.g., long-horizon RL with same reward, or multi-agent with imitation learning).
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablation experiments varying: (1) Agent count (single vs. multi-agent), (2) Reward structure (terminal aggregate vs. per-turn proxy rewards), and (3) Base model capability. Measuring the targeted fine-grained behaviors across these conditions would disentangle the contributions of each factor.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Does the synchronous, all-players-speak-simultaneously turn approximation fundamentally limit the model's ability to learn realistic turn-taking, interruption handling, or other temporal dynamics of natural conversation?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors acknowledge this as a "computational approximation" in the Limitations (Section 7, 12), noting real conversations are asynchronous. They state this provides a "streamlined alternative" but do not evaluate its impact on learned social intelligence.
- **Why unresolved:** The approximation simplifies state transitions but may obscure learning sensitive timing, repair sequences, or influence from immediate prior utterances (vs. batch aggregation). The model may learn strategies optimal for this artificial turn structure but brittle in real-time interaction.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An evaluation where the trained model interacts in an environment with *asynchronous, variable-delay* turn-taking (e.g., one agent acts, then another responds after a simulated "thinking" delay). Comparing performance on fine-grained social metrics and win rates (in Werewolf) against the synchronous version would quantify any degradation or adaptation.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** How does the performance of OMAR-scale with increasing model size and conversational complexity (longer dialogues, more participants), and does the current mitigation for reward hacking (quality filtering) remain effective for larger, more capable models?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors "call for future studies on multi-turn, multi-agent RL with larger LLMs" (Section 4.3, 10) and note that "models that are too small often struggle," implying current results may be capacity-limited. The quality filter uses GPT-5, raising scalability and cost concerns for larger models.
- **Why unresolved:** The experiments use 7B and 4B parameter models under constrained turn limits. Scaling trends are unknown. Larger models may develop more sophisticated deception or manipulation that bypasses simple quality filters, exacerbating reward hacking.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A scaling study: train OMAR with models of increasing size (e.g., 7B, 70B, 405B) on the same and more complex environments (e.g., 5+ participant dialogues, >10 turns). Track: (1) performance on social intelligence metrics, (2) prevalence of reward hacking (measured by filter failure rate and human evaluation of output quality), and (3) computational efficiency.

## Limitations
- External validity limited: experiments only in two simulated environments (SOTOPIA, Werewolf) without real-human validation.
- Behavioral metrics are automated and not human-verified; may not reflect true social intelligence.
- Reward hacking is acknowledged but not quantified; observed gains could partly stem from exploiting sparse rewards.
- Hierarchical advantage estimation’s stabilizing effect is claimed but not isolated via ablation against alternative credit assignment methods.
