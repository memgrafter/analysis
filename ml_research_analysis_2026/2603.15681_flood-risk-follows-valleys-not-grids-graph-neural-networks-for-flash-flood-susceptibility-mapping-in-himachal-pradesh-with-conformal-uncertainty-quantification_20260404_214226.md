# Flood Risk Follows Valleys, Not Grids: Graph Neural Networks for Flash Flood Susceptibility Mapping in Himachal Pradesh with Conformal Uncertainty Quantification

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.15681
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15681
- **Reference count**: 10
- **Primary result**: A GraphSAGE model on a directed watershed graph achieved an AUC of 0.978 ± 0.017, significantly outperforming the best pixel-based model (AUC = 0.881) and providing the first statistically guaranteed (90%) uncertainty intervals for susceptibility mapping.

## Executive Summary
This study introduces a Graph Neural Network (GraphSAGE) framework for flash flood susceptibility mapping that explicitly encodes river network connectivity. By treating sub-watersheds as nodes in a directed graph, the model propagates hydrological influence upstream to downstream, overcoming a key limitation of traditional pixel-based methods that ignore connectivity. Trained on a six-year SAR flood inventory and 12 environmental variables in Himachal Pradesh, the model achieved a 0.097 AUC improvement over the best baseline under rigorous spatial cross-validation. The integration of split-conformal prediction produced the first susceptibility maps with finite-sample 90% coverage guarantees, though empirical coverage varied across risk classes, highlighting challenges with label noise.

## Method Summary
The methodology involves constructing a directed watershed connectivity graph from a DEM (460 sub-watersheds, ~1,700 edges), where nodes have area-weighted averages of 12 static environmental factors and binary flood labels from Sentinel-1 SAR. A three-layer GraphSAGE network is trained on this graph. Model performance is evaluated using strict leave-one-basin-out spatial cross-validation to avoid inflation from spatial autocorrelation. To quantify uncertainty, split-conformal prediction is applied post-training, using a calibration set to derive prediction intervals with a statistical 90% coverage guarantee. Final susceptibility maps are generated by rasterizing node-level predictions and intervals.

## Key Results
- The GraphSAGE model achieved an AUC of 0.978 ± 0.017 under leave-one-basin-out cross-validation, a +0.097 gain over the best pixel-based stacking ensemble (AUC = 0.881).
- Conformal prediction produced prediction sets with an 82.9% empirical coverage on a held-out 2023 temporal test set, meeting but not exceeding the target 90%.
- Coverage was heterogeneous across risk classes, with Very High susceptibility zones showing only 45–59% coverage, likely due to SAR label noise or distribution shift.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Graph-Based Upstream-Downstream Propagation
**Claim**: Encoding watershed connectivity via a directed graph allows the model to learn that high flood risk upstream increases risk downstream, a signal unavailable to pixel-based models.
**Mechanism**: GraphSAGE performs neighborhood aggregation: each sub-watershed node updates its representation by sampling and combining features from upstream neighbors (via directed edges). This propagates hydrological influence (e.g., high TWI/slope upstream) to downstream nodes during training. The model learns that a downstream node's flood label depends not only on its local terrain but also on aggregated upstream conditions, mimicking discharge accumulation.
**Core assumption**: Flood occurrence in a sub-watershed is conditionally dependent on flood occurrences and environmental conditions in upstream connected sub-watersheds. This assumes the 1:700km² sub-watershed resolution adequately captures connectivity without excessive aggregation.
**Evidence anchors**:
- [abstract]: "The GNN achieved AUC=0.978±0.017, outperforming... the +0.097 AUC gain confirms river connectivity provides critical predictive signal."
- [section 5.1]: "by aggregating information from upstream sub-watersheds... the GNN learns that high-runoff upstream nodes co-predict flooding in downstream nodes even when the downstream node’s own local conditioning factors would not indicate high susceptibility."
**Break condition**: If the watershed graph edges do not accurately represent true hydrological flow paths (e.g., due to DEM errors in flat valleys), message-passing propagates misleading signals, degrading performance relative to pixel models.

### Mechanism 2: Distribution-Free Uncertainty Calibration
**Claim**: Split-conformal prediction produces susceptibility intervals with a finite-sample statistical guarantee (≥90% coverage) for any base model, enabling trustworthy risk communication where traditional point estimates fail.
**Mechanism**: After training the base GNN, a held-out calibration set computes non-conformity scores (1 − predicted probability for true labels). The (1−α)-quantile of these scores defines a threshold; any prediction with non-conformity below this threshold includes the true label in the interval. This creates prediction sets that cover true outcomes at least 1−α of the time, regardless of the base model's internal calibration, under exchangeability.
**Core assumption**: The calibration and test data are exchangeable (drawn from the same distribution). This requires that temporal splits (2018–2022 train, 2023 test) do not introduce systematic distribution shifts beyond what the model can handle, and that SAR label noise is consistent across sets.
**Evidence anchors**:
- [abstract]: "Conformal prediction produced the first HP susceptibility maps with statistically guaranteed 90% coverage intervals; these achieved 82.9% empirical coverage..."
- [section 3.7]: Explicit algorithm steps and coverage guarantee statement: "Coverage was empirically verified... guarantees that P(y ∈ C(X)) ≥ 1−α..."
**Break condition**: Violations of exchangeability (e.g., 2023 being an extreme flood year not represented in calibration) or significant miscalibration of the base model's probability estimates (e.g., from SAR label noise) can cause empirical coverage to fall below the nominal level, as seen in High/Very High zones (45–59% coverage).

### Mechanism 3: Spatial Block Cross-Validation for Unbiased Evaluation
**Claim**: Leave-one-basin-out spatial block CV provides an unbiased estimate of model generalisation to new geographic regions, avoiding the 5–15% AUC inflation from random splits due to spatial autocorrelation.
**Mechanism**: The 460 sub-watersheds are clustered into five geographically contiguous blocks (one per major river basin). In each fold, all points from one block are held out as test data; the model trains only on the other four blocks. This enforces that training and test observations are spatially separated, preventing the model from leveraging local spatial autocorrelation (e.g., similar terrain in adjacent pixels) to artificially inflate accuracy.
**Core assumption**: The major river basins represent distinct enough hydrological and morphological regimes that a model trained on four will generalize poorly to the fifth if it relies on region-specific patterns rather than generalizable processes. Assumes no cross-basin leakage of infrastructure or extreme events.
**Evidence anchors**:
- [abstract]: "All models were evaluated with leave-one-basin-out spatial block cross-validation to avoid the 5–15% accuracy inflation common in random train-test splits."
- [section 3.6]: Detailed CV procedure and reference to Valavi et al. (2019) on spatial autocorrelation inflation.
**Break condition**: If the k-means spatial blocks inadvertently create test folds that are not truly out-of-distribution (e.g., if a block contains only a small, unrepresentative sub-watershed sample), the AUC estimate may remain biased.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Graph Neural Networks (GraphSAGE)**
  - Why needed: The core innovation is replacing pixel-based ML with a GNN that operates on a watershed connectivity graph. Understanding node feature aggregation, neighborhood sampling, and message-passing is essential to grasp how upstream information influences downstream predictions.
  - Quick check: *How does a GraphSAGE layer combine features from a node's direct neighbors versus its own features during message-passing?*
- **Concept: Watershed Hydrology & Connectivity**
  - Why needed: The premise is that flood risk propagates downstream via river networks. Without understanding contributing area, flow accumulation, and sub-watershed delineation, the graph construction rationale is opaque.
  - Quick check: *Why is a directed edge from sub-watershed A to sub-watershed B interpreted as A influencing B's flood risk?*
- **Concept: Conformal Prediction**
  - Why needed: The paper's key contribution is uncertainty quantification with guarantees. Understanding split-conformal, non-conformity scores, and coverage guarantees is necessary to interpret the uncertainty maps.
  - Quick check: *What is the role of the calibration set in split-conformal prediction, and how is the prediction interval threshold derived?*
- **Concept: Spatial Autocorrelation & Validation Bias**
  - Why needed: The paper's rigorous evaluation hinges on avoiding inflated metrics. Understanding why random splits are invalid for spatial data and how block CV mitigates this is critical to evaluating the reported AUC gains.
  - Quick check: *Why does a random train-test split typically overestimate AUC in spatial flood susceptibility modeling?*
- **Concept: SAR-Based Flood Detection**
  - Why needed: The training labels come from Sentinel-1 SAR composites. Knowing the principles of backscatter change detection, limitations in mountainous terrain (shadows, soil moisture vs. inundation), and the -3dB threshold is key to assessing label quality and its impact on model performance.
  - Quick check: *What terrain-based filters were applied to the SAR-derived flood inventory to reduce false positives from non-inundation features?*

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**:
  SAR inventory -> Conditioning factors -> Watershed graph builder -> GraphSAGE GNN training -> Spatial CV wrapper (LOBO) -> Conformal wrapper (MAPIE) -> Susceptibility maps + uncertainty overlay -> Infrastructure exposure analysis.
- **Critical path**: Acquire SAR and terrain data -> Delineate sub-watersheds and build directed graph -> Compute node features/labels -> Train GNN and pixel baselines -> Perform spatial CV evaluation -> Apply split-conformal -> Rasterize and map results with infrastructure.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Graph resolution: Smaller sub-watersheds capture local processes but increase graph size and may overfit; chose 150km² minimum to balance.
  - Aggregation: GraphSAGE uses mean aggregation for efficiency; a Graph Attention Network (GAT) could learn edge weights but is more computationally expensive and riskier with limited data.
  - Conformal set size: Used 20% of training data; smaller sets yield wider intervals, larger sets reduce training data. Tradeoff between interval tightness and model accuracy.
  - Static vs. dynamic factors: Used static/long-term averages for tractability; sacrificing temporal dynamics (e.g., antecedent soil moisture) limits seasonal forecasting.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Spatial CV failure: If GNN's AUC advantage vanishes under LOBO but not random CV, the gain is likely due to spatial leakage, not genuine graph benefit.
  - Conformal miscalibration: Empirical coverage <<90% in high-susceptibility zones indicates label noise or distribution shift; coverage >>90% in low zones suggests over-conservative intervals.
  - Poor graph construction: GNN performance sensitivity to DEM resolution or minimum catchment area threshold indicates hydrologically implausible edges.
  - Infrastructure overlay mismatch: High-susceptibility zones not aligning with known flood-prone corridors suggests model or inventory error.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Graph validity check**: Visualize the 460 sub-watersheds and directed edges over DEM hillshade and river network. Confirm edges follow valley lines and no impossible upstream connections exist. Compute Strahler stream order consistency between graph and DEM-derived network.
  2. **Conformal coverage stratification**: After calibration, compute empirical coverage separately for each susceptibility quartile on the 2023 test set. Expect ~90% overall; if high-susceptibility coverage is low (<70%), inspect SAR labels in those areas for systematic noise (e.g., shadows misclassified as flood).
  3. **Ablation of graph structure**: Train a GraphSAGE variant where edge directions are randomized or set to undirected only. Compare LOBO AUC to the original directed graph model. A significant drop (e.g., >0.05 AUC) confirms directed connectivity is the key signal.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1**: Can Graph Attention Networks (GATs) improve flash flood susceptibility prediction, especially for GLOF-triggered events?
  - Basis: "Future work should explore GNN variants better suited to GLOF contexts, such as graph attention networks (GATs)" (p.18).
  - Why unresolved: GraphSAGE uses uniform aggregation; GAT could assign different attention to upstream nodes based on learned importance, potentially capturing the distinct signal from glacial areas.
  - Resolution: Train a GAT model on the same watershed graph, compare AUC and coverage against GraphSAGE; analyze attention weights to confirm they highlight glacial-proximate sub-watersheds.
- **Question 2**: How can we quantify and reduce label noise in the SAR-derived flood inventory to improve model calibration, especially in high-susceptibility zones?
  - Basis: "Future work should cross-validate SAR-derived labels against optical-imagery flood extents for individual events to quantify label error rates" (p.20); also "conformal undercoverage in high-risk areas likely reflects SAR label noise" (p.13-14).
  - Why unresolved: SAR backscatter changes can be caused by factors other than inundation; current plausibility filters are insufficient in steep terrain.
  - Resolution: Use high-resolution optical imagery (e.g., Sentinel-2) to validate a random sample of SAR-detected flood pixels; compute error rates; re-train the model with corrected labels; assess improvements in conformal coverage and AUC.
- **Question 3**: Do mechanism-specific models (monsoon vs. GLOF vs. cloudburst) outperform the unified flood susceptibility model?
  - Basis: "Future work should stratify the inventory by trigger mechanism and assess whether mechanism-specific models outperform the unified approach for Trans-Himalayan catchments" (p.20).
  - Why unresolved: Different triggers have different spatial patterns and controlling factors; mixing them may obscure trigger-specific signals.
  - Resolution: Label each flood point by trigger using historical records and event attributes; train separate models or a multi-task model; compare predictive performance on held-out test sets for each trigger type.
- **Question 4**: Can dynamic antecedent soil moisture and snowmelt proxies improve the predictive accuracy of flash flood susceptibility models compared to static conditioning factors?
  - Basis: "A dynamic susceptibility model incorporating GRACE-FO soil moisture or GPM soil moisture product would be a valuable extension" (p.21); also note that current factors are static (p.20).
  - Why unresolved: Soil moisture and snowmelt are critical but vary temporally; their omission may limit the model's ability to capture pre-flood conditions.
  - Resolution: Integrate time-series soil moisture products (e.g., SMAP, GRACE-FO) and snow cover data as time-varying features; implement a temporal model (e.g., LSTM, temporal GNN); evaluate improvement in temporal validation (2023) and spatial block cross-validation.
- **Question 5**: How can we construct more physically realistic edge weights for the watershed graph using measured streamflow data, and does this improve model performance?
  - Basis: "Edge weights based on catchment area ratio are a first-order approximation; more accurate edge weights would incorporate measured streamflow data, which are not freely available for HP" (p.20).
  - Why unresolved: Current weights approximate discharge contribution but ignore actual flow dynamics influenced by rainfall, soil, and channel characteristics.
  - Resolution: Obtain stream gauge data (if available) or simulate discharge with a rainfall-runoff model; compute empirical flow contributions at confluences; replace area-ratio weights; compare model performance and interpret edge importance changes.

## Limitations
- **Graph accuracy**: DEM-based flow routing may misrepresent connectivity in flat areas, risking signal corruption.
- **SAR noise**: Thresholding Sentinel-1 backscatter can misclassify shadows/soil moisture, especially in mountains, likely causing undercoverage in high-risk zones.
- **Temporal shift**: The 2023 test year may differ from 2018–2022 (e.g., extreme events), violating conformal exchangeability assumptions.

## Confidence
- **Connectivity gain (+0.097 AUC)**: High — Robust LOBO CV design; ablation expected to confirm.
- **Conformal coverage (82.9% overall)**: Medium — Meets target but heterogeneous; high-susceptibility undercoverage suggests label noise/shift.
- **Spatial CV validity**: High — Rigorous basin-left-out design aligns with best practices; risk is non-representative block splits.
- **Graph construction hydrologic plausibility**: Medium — No independent validation of flow directions against field evidence; depends on DEM quality.

## Next Checks
1. **Ablate graph structure**: Randomize edge directions or make undirected; expect large AUC drop if connectivity is key.
2. **Stratify conformal coverage**: Compute coverage by susceptibility decile on 2023 test; link undercoverage to terrain (e.g., steep slopes) or SAR acquisition parameters.
3. **Validate SAR labels**: Manually check a subset of 2023 flood points against external data (news, optical) to quantify noise; refine inventory if needed.
