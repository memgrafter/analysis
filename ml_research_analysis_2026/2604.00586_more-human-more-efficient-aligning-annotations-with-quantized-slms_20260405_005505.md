# More Human, More Efficient: Aligning Annotations with Quantized SLMs
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.00586
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00586
- **Reference Count:** 32
- **Primary Result:** A 4-bit quantized 1.7B SLM fine-tuned with rubric-based scoring and data augmentation achieves Krippendorff’s α of 0.5774 on a Q&A dataset, outperforming GPT-5-mini (α=0.2462) and nearly doubling GPT-4o's accuracy on emotion classification.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses biases, reproducibility issues, and privacy concerns associated with using proprietary large language models (LLMs) for data annotation. The authors propose a method that fine-tunes a small, efficient language model (Qwen3-1.7B) quantized to 4-bits on a limited set of human-annotated examples. The core innovation lies in aligning the model to a multi-dimensional human rubric via targeted data augmentation (paraphrasing, component permutation, token dropout). Evaluated on a specialized Q&A dataset and an emotion classification task, the approach demonstrates significantly higher agreement with human annotators (measured by Krippendorff’s α) and better classification performance than leading proprietary LLMs. The results position task-specific alignment of quantized small models as a deterministic, cost-effective, and privacy-preserving alternative for high-quality annotation.

## Method Summary
The method involves fine-tuning a 4-bit quantized 1.7B parameter SLM (Qwen3-1.7B) using Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (LoRA). Training data consists of human-annotated examples where each instance is scored across six criteria on an ordinal scale from -2 to 2, based on a defined rubric. To prevent overfitting on the small dataset, three augmentation strategies are applied: paraphrasing the input text, permuting the order of context/question/answer components, and randomly dropping tokens. The model is trained to generate the score vector as a completion, learning the evaluation logic intrinsic to the task-specific rubric. This pipeline yields a deterministic, locally deployable annotator.

## Key Results
- On a specialized Q&A dataset, the finetuned SLM achieves a Krippendorff’s α of **0.5774**, surpassing the best proprietary LLM baseline (GPT-5-mini, α=0.2462) by **0.3312 points**.
- For emotion classification on the GoEmotions dataset, it reaches **81.63% accuracy** and **63.80% Macro-F1**, nearly **doubling GPT-4o's accuracy**.
- The augmentation pipeline is critical; ablations show full fine-tuning without augmentation yields substantially lower agreement (α=0.4304).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Task-specific fine-tuning on a small, high-quality dataset yields better alignment with human annotators than zero-shot prompting of large proprietary LLMs.
- **Mechanism:** The SLM internalizes a multi-dimensional rubric by directly generating human-provided score vectors, learning evaluation logic specific to the task rather than relying on pre-training biases or generic reasoning.
- **Core assumption:** A well-defined rubric and limited expert annotations are sufficient to steer a base SLM toward human-like evaluative judgment for that domain.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "task-specific alignment and efficient 4-bit quantized fine-tuning provide superior open-source alternative"; [section 2.2] Uses Krippendorff’s α to measure agreement with preserved human label diversity; [corpus] Neighbors corroborate the efficacy of task-specific alignment.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Data augmentation (paraphrasing, permutation, token dropout) is critical for preventing overfitting on small datasets and improving model generalization to novel prompt formulations.
- **Mechanism:** Augmentations force the model to focus on invariant evaluation criteria (e.g., accuracy, relevance) rather than superficial prompt patterns, position cues, or lexical artifacts.
- **Core assumption:** Without augmentation, a small dataset leads the model to memorize prompt-specific phrasing instead of learning the underlying assessment task.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "simple augmentation and regularization techniques" lead to higher agreement; [section 2.3] Explicitly describes three augmentation strategies; [section 3] Table 1 shows full finetuning without augmentation (α=0.4304) underperforms the augmented method (α=0.5774).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** 4-bit quantized PEFT (LoRA) on a 1.7B SLM provides a deterministic, privacy-preserving, and cost-effective alternative to proprietary LLM APIs for annotation.
- **Mechanism:** Quantization and PEFT drastically reduce memory/compute requirements, enabling local deployment; deterministic generation eliminates API non-determinism; local processing ensures data never leaves the user's environment.
- **Core assumption:** The base SLM (Qwen3-1.7B) possesses sufficient latent knowledge of language quality to support fine-tuning for evaluation tasks, and quantization loss is tolerable.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "4-bit quantized Small Language Model...deterministic evaluator and annotator" resolving "reproducibility...data privacy concerns"; [section 2.4] Uses Unsloth for 4-bit quantized PEFT on a single GPU; [section 3] Finetuned models are run once due to deterministic nature.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Krippendorff’s α for ordinal multi-rater agreement.
  - **Why needed here:** Measures inter-annotator agreement accounting for magnitude of disagreement on an ordinal scale (-2 to 2), crucial when human annotators disagree on rubric boundaries.
  - **Quick check:** When would you prefer Krippendorff’s α over simple percent agreement, and what does an α of 0.5774 indicate about annotator reliability?
- **Concept:** Multi-dimensional rubric design.
  - **Why needed here:** Decomposes abstract qualities (naturalness, quality) into concrete, scoreable dimensions (completeness, clarity, etc.), enabling consistent annotation and model training.
  - **Quick check:** How does a -2 to 2 ordinal scale differ from a 5-point Likert scale in evaluation rubric design, and why might the former be chosen?
- **Concept:** Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) with LoRA and quantization.
  - **Why needed here:** Adapts a large base model to a specific task with minimal added parameters (LoRA rank=16) and reduced memory via 4-bit quantization, making fine-tuning feasible on limited hardware.
  - **Quick check:** What is the trade-off between using full fine-tuning vs. LoRA with 4-bit quantization in terms of memory, speed, and potential accuracy loss?
- **Concept:** Deterministic text generation.
  - **Why needed here:** Annotation requires reproducible outputs; proprietary LLM APIs often introduce non-determinism via sampling, making consistent scoring impossible without multiple runs.
  - **Quick check:** Why is deterministic generation a critical requirement for an automated annotation system, and how is it achieved in this pipeline?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input (Context, Question, Answer) -> Prompt Templater (with rubric instructions) -> Augmentation Module (paraphrase, component permutation, token dropout) -> 4-bit Quantized Qwen3-1.7B (PEFT with LoRA) -> Output (6 integer scores -2 to 2)
- **Critical path:** 1) Curate limited human-annotated data with multi-rater scores. 2) Define rubric boundaries. 3) Implement augmentation pipeline. 4) Fine-tune SLM with Unsloth (4-bit, LoRA r=16) using SFT on score completions. 5) Evaluate against proprietary LLMs via Krippendorff’s α (specialized) or accuracy/Macro-F1 (classification).
- **Design tradeoffs:** Quantization (4-bit) vs. fidelity: Smaller bits reduce memory but may lose nuanced rubric distinctions. Dataset size vs. augmentation: Very small datasets rely heavily on augmentation, but over-augmentation can distort semantics. Determinism vs. creativity: Deterministic output is desired for annotation, but reduces explorative output diversity.
- **Failure signatures:** 1) Validation loss diverges from training loss early -> augmentation too severe or learning rate too high. 2) α/accuracy near zero on held-out set -> rubric definitions ambiguous or model capacity insufficient. 3) Inconsistent scores for near-identical inputs -> token dropout rate too low, model overfits to prompt quirks.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Ablation on augmentation:** Train without augmentation and compare loss curves and α/accuracy to confirm regularization effect.
  2. **Cross-task generalization:** Apply the *same* fine-tuned model (without retraining) to GoEmotions to test if learned evaluation logic transfers, as in Section 4.
  3. **Augmentation sensitivity sweep:** Systematically vary token dropout probability (e.g., 0.1, 0.2, 0.3) and paraphrase intensity to find optimal regularization without semantic drift.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- To what extent does the proposed multi-dimensional rubric generalize to annotation tasks beyond Q&A and emotion classification, such as evaluating factual consistency or creative generation?
- What is the minimum quantity and diversity of human-annotated data required for the finetuning pipeline to achieve comparable performance, and how does data efficiency compare to larger proprietary models?
- How does 4-bit quantization specifically affect the model's annotation fidelity compared to full-precision or higher bit-width finetuning, and are there trade-offs in rubric adherence or bias mitigation?
- Do the observed performance advantages over proprietary LLMs (e.g., GPT-5-mini) hold when evaluated against a wider range of commercial and open-source models, including those with specialized annotation fine-tuning?
- How robust is the multi-dimensional rubric to annotator disagreement and ambiguous cases, and does the ordinal scoring scheme (-2 to 2) introduce or mitigate systematic biases in the finetuned SLM?

## Limitations
- **Domain generalization unknown:** Claims are validated only on a specialized Q&A dataset and GoEmotions; unclear if rubric/augmentation transfers to other annotation domains (e.g., biomedical, legal).
- **Rubric specificity:** The multi-dimensional rubric design is task-specific; its effectiveness likely depends on precise human consensus during curation, which may not scale to ambiguous tasks.
- **Quantization trade-offs:** While 4-bit PEFT enables efficiency, potential nuance loss is not systematically analyzed; the claim assumes quantization is neutral or beneficial.

## Confidence
- **Task-specific alignment superiority (α=0.5774 vs. GPT-5-mini α=0.2462):** Medium-High. Supported by ablation and consistent metric, but limited to two tasks.
- **Augmentation necessity (token dropout, paraphrasing, permutation):** Medium. Ablation shows benefit, but optimal hyperparameters are task-specific and not thoroughly swept.
- **4-bit quantized SLM as deterministic, private alternative:** Medium. Technical implementation is clear, but "sufficient latent knowledge" assumption rests on Qwen3-1.7B's pre-training scope.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-domain replication:** Apply the *identical* fine-tuning pipeline (same rubric structure, augmentations, LoRA config) to at least two new annotation tasks (e.g., named entity recognition, product review) and measure Krippendorff’s α against human annotators and GPT-4 with standardized prompts.
2. **Rubric robustness test:** Deliberately introduce ambiguity in rubric definitions for the same dataset and quantify the drop in α for both the SLM and human annotators to assess sensitivity to rubric clarity.
3. **Quantization-fidelity sweep:** Re-train at 2-bit, 4-bit, 8-bit, and full-precision on the same data; plot α and Macro-F1 against memory usage to empirically locate the efficiency frontier for ordinal scoring tasks.
