# Training In-Context and In-Weights Mixtures Via Contrastive Context Sampling

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2604.01601
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01601
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Contrastive-Context fine-tuning consistently maintains both in-context learning (ICL) and in-weights learning (IWL) across diverse tasks and models, outperforming random and similar context baselines, with up to 3 COMET point gains in high-similarity translation scenarios.

## Executive Summary
Fine-tuning large language models (LLMs) for continuous adaptation often sacrifices either in-context learning (ICL) or in-weights learning (IWL). Standard IC-Train with random contexts leads to IWL dominance and loss of ICL, while using only highly similar contexts encourages blind copying and impairs IWL. This paper proposes Contrastive-Context, a method that mixes random and similar examples within each training context, varies target-context similarity across batches, and adds synthetic paraphrased examples to ensure high-similarity exposure. Theoretically, analysis of a minimal transformer shows such contrast yields optimal stationary points enabling the model to switch between ICL and IWL. Empirically, across four LLMs (1B–8B) and multiple tasks—machine translation, Text-to-SQL, multilingual semantic parsing, and synthetic reasoning—Contrastive-Context consistently outperforms baselines across the full similarity spectrum. Diagnostic probes confirm a robust ICL-IWL mixture: high in-weights performance, stable in-context accuracy, and low propensity for blind copying.

## Method Summary
Contrastive-Context constructs training contexts by sampling a mixture of random examples and highly similar examples (or their paraphrases) for each target input. Specifically, with probability \(1-p\), a context consists of \(k\) random examples; with probability \(p/2\), it uses the \(k\) most similar examples; and with probability \(p/2\), it uses one highly similar example plus \(k-1\) random examples. If naturally similar examples are scarce (similarity below a threshold), a paraphrase of the target is generated (with probability \(\epsilon\)) to serve as the highly similar example. Models are fine-tuned using LoRA on attention projection matrices, with a standard cross-entropy loss on the target output. Performance is evaluated by binning test cases by target-context similarity and measuring accuracy (e.g., COMET for translation), and by diagnostic probes: IWL-score (prediction stability with random vs. empty context), ICL-score (accuracy with one-similar context), and Copy-score (propensity to copy mismatched context labels).

## Key Results
- Contrastive-Context significantly outperforms Random-Context and Similar-Context baselines across 32 model/task/test configurations in machine translation, 11 Text-to-SQL databases, multilingual semantic parsing, and synthetic reasoning.
- For machine translation, it achieves COMET score gains up to 3 points in high-similarity cases while avoiding the performance drops seen in low-similarity scenarios with other methods.
- Diagnostic probes confirm a robust mixture: high IWL-score (comparable to Random-Context), stable ICL-score (comparable to Similar-Context), and low Copy-score (avoiding collapse into blind copying).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Contrastive-Context prevents degenerate copying by mixing dissimilar and similar examples within each context, forcing the model to learn selective attention based on input similarity.
- **Mechanism:** During training, every context contains both random examples (low similarity to the target) and at least one highly similar example (or its paraphrase). The model’s attention mechanism must learn to assign high weights only to tokens whose inputs are similar to the query. The intra-context contrast creates a discriminative signal: the random examples act as negatives, while the similar example acts as a positive. This teaches the model to copy labels only when the context input matches the target, rather than indiscriminately.
- **Core assumption:** The transformer’s attention can represent and learn to weight by similarity, and the random examples are sufficiently dissimilar to provide a meaningful contrast.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: “mix of similar and random examples within a context to evolve a correct form of ICL”
  - [section 3]: The analysis of the One-Near-Context regime (Case 3) shows optimal parameters drive attention to the single near example. In contrast, Similar-Context alone leads to blind copying when all examples are similar (Case 2b: θ₁+θ₂→∞ causes copying from random labels if context is dissimilar).
  - [corpus]: Neighbor paper *The Transient Nature of Emergent In-Context Learning in Transformers* discusses ICL degradation; our mechanism specifically targets degradation into blind copying via intra-context contrast.
- **Break condition:** If the random examples are not sufficiently dissimilar (e.g., due to bursty data where all examples share some similarity), the contrast weakens and the model may still copy indiscriminately. Also if model capacity is too low to learn attention weights.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Varying target-context similarity across training batches (mixing random and similar contexts at the batch level) promotes a stable ICL-IWL mixture by preventing specialization to a single mode.
- **Mechanism:** A fraction (1−p) of training instances use random contexts (driving IWL), while fraction p use contexts with a similar example (driving ICL). This inter-context contrast forces the attention parameters to adapt such that they can switch: for random contexts, θ₂→−∞ suppresses context reliance; for contexts with a near example, θ₁→∞ spikes attention on the relevant label. Theoretically, the optimal stationary point for such a mixture (Section 3, Case 5) enables correct behavior in both regimes.
- **Core assumption:** The model encounters both regimes during training and can learn a representation that gates between them; the mixture proportions p are non-extreme.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: “varying grades of similarity across contexts to evolve ICL-IWL mixtures.”
  - [section 2]: “We create contrast across batches by choosing a fraction 1−p with random context, and fraction p with similar context.”
  - [section 3]: Case 5 (Contrastive-Context) derives optimal parameters (θ₁+θ₂→∞, θ₂→−∞, θ₁≫θ₃) that yield correct behavior for both Random-Context (IWL) and One-Near-Context (ICL).
  - [corpus]: *Predictability Shapes Adaptation: An Evolutionary Perspective on Modes of Learning in Transformers* frames ICL/IWL as strategies shaped by predictability, aligning with the idea that varying similarity drives mixture.
- **Break condition:** If p is too close to 0 or 1, the model overcommits to IWL or ICL, respectively. Also if the training distribution lacks diversity in similarity, the model may not learn to switch.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Synthetic highly similar examples via paraphrasing ensure robust ICL performance even when natural similar examples are scarce in the training pool, by artificially creating high-similarity contexts.
- **Mechanism:** For each training target, with probability ϵ, a paraphrase of the target (same meaning, different surface form) is injected as a context example with the correct label. This guarantees exposure to a high-similarity context regardless of the training pool’s natural distribution. The model learns to harness such examples, and at test time it can utilize any truly similar user-provided feedback.
- **Core assumption:** The paraphrasing model produces semantically equivalent variants; task semantics are robust to surface variation.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: “adds paraphrased synthetic examples for high similarity.”
  - [section 2]: “When the labeled pool is small, there may not be examples close enough to the target during fine-tuning, causing the model to loose the capability of harnessing highly related context examples. We therefore augment ϵ fraction of the training instances with synthetic highly similar example by small perturbation of x∗.”
  - [section 4] ablations: Figure 5(c) shows that removing paraphrases (ϵ=0) hurts accuracy in the high-similarity range; Figure 5(b) shows performance is stable across paraphrasing models (gemini-2.0-flash-lite, gemini-2.5-flash-lite, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct).
  - [corpus]: Limited direct evidence; neighbor *ReasonCACHE: Teaching LLMs To Reason Without Weight Updates* also uses ICL but not paraphrasing. The assumption about paraphrase quality is critical and supported by our ablations.
- **Break condition:** If paraphrasing alters task semantics (e.g., changes key entities or relations), the synthetic example becomes misleading and degrades performance. Too high ϵ may reduce diversity and hinder IWL development.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: In-context learning (ICL)**
  - **Why needed here:** The paper’s core goal is to preserve ICL while developing IWL. ICL is the ability to adapt to new tasks or examples provided in the input context without updating model parameters.
  - **Quick check question:** What is the key difference between in-context learning and in-weights learning in terms of how knowledge is stored and applied?

- **Concept: Transformer attention mechanism**
  - **Why needed here:** The theoretical model and empirical method rely on attention to weight context examples. Understanding how attention scores are computed from query-key similarity is essential to grasp why contrast works.
  - **Quick check question:** In a transformer’s self-attention, how does the dot product between a query and a key vector influence the contribution of that key’s value to the output?

- **Concept: Example selection for in-context learning**
  - **Why needed here:** The paper compares context sampling strategies (random, similar, contrastive) based on similarity to the target. This requires understanding metrics like BM25 or embedding cosine and the trade-offs between diversity and relevance.
  - **Quick check question:** What are the potential drawbacks of using only nearest-neighbor examples as context during fine-tuning?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Data Sampler -> Paraphrase Generator (when ϵ>0) -> Fine-tuning Engine -> Evaluation Harness & Probe Modules

**Critical path:**
1. Prepare data: Split dataset into training pool D and test set. Precompute similarity indices (e.g., BM25) for fast nearest-neighbor lookup.
2. Sampling for each batch: For each target (x*, y*), with probability 1-p sample k random examples from D. With probability p/2 sample k most similar. With probability p/2 sample 1 most similar + k-1 random. If max similarity < threshold for "similar", generate paraphrase using gemini-2.0-flash-lite and treat as highly similar.
3. Training: Feed the constructed prompt (context + target) to the LLM. Compute loss on target output. Update parameters (LoRA or full).
4. Validation: Periodically evaluate on a fixed validation set with multiple context types (random, similar, mixed) to track performance across similarity spectrum.
5. Probing: After training, run the three probes on a fixed probe set to measure ICL, IWL, and copying tendencies. Expect high IWL-score, stable ICL-score, low copy-score for successful Contrastive-Context.
6. Hyperparameter tuning: Adjust p, ϵ, LoRA rank, learning rate based on validation trade-offs.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Paraphrase model: Stronger models (e.g., gemini-2.5-flash-lite) may preserve semantics better but are costlier/slower; weaker open-source models may suffice but risk meaning drift.
- Similarity metric: BM25 is fast and lexical; embedding cosine captures semantics but requires encoding overhead and may be less interpretable.
- Paraphrase injection (ϵ): Too low (<0.1) may not sufficiently boost high-similarity performance; too high (>0.5) may reduce diversity and hurt IWL development.
- Mixture proportion (p): Typically around 0.5 in experiments; lower p emphasizes IWL training, higher p emphasizes ICL. May need task-specific adjustment.
- LoRA target modules: Training only attention projections (as in main experiments) is parameter-efficient; adding MLP layers may improve performance but increases risk of overfitting and compute cost.

**Failure signatures:**
- IWL collapse: Validation accuracy on high-similarity contexts (with a near neighbor) does not improve over zero-context baseline; IWL-score is high but ICL-score remains low.
- Blind copying: Copy-score is elevated; model outputs labels from context even when context examples are irrelevant (low similarity). Accuracy degrades on low/medium similarity contexts.
- Overfitting to paraphrases: Performance drops when evaluating with natural similar examples compared to paraphrased ones. Indicates paraphrase quality issue or ϵ too high.
- Training instability: Loss spikes or diverges when mixing contexts; may indicate too aggressive contrast (e.g., p extreme) or poor initialization of attention parameters.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Baseline replication: Train Zero-Context, Random-Context, and Similar-Context on a small subset (e.g., one language pair) to reproduce the trade-offs shown in Figure 3. Verify that Random-Context loses ICL (bad on high-similarity) and Similar-Context loses IWL (bad on low-similarity).
2. Ablation of ϵ and p: Train Contrastive-Context with varying ϵ (0, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5) and p (0.3, 0.5, 0.7). Evaluate across similarity bins to see impact of paraphrases and mixture proportion. Expect high-similarity gain with higher ϵ, medium-similarity stability with p~0.5.
3. Probe validation: After training, compute the three probe scores on a fixed probe set. Contrastive-Context should show: IWL-score comparable to Random-Context, ICL-score comparable to Similar-Context, and copy-score lower than Similar-Context. This confirms a true ICL-IWL mixture without collapse into copying.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **What explains the observed pattern where model accuracy is lower at medium target-context similarity but higher at both low and high similarity levels?**  
   - Basis: In Appendix D.2, the authors directly ask about this non-monotonic behavior.  
   - Why unresolved: This pattern is noted in ablation results but not analyzed or explained.  
   - Needed evidence: Controlled experiments varying the proportion of similarity levels during training, combined with probing of attention weights and gradient flows to understand why medium-similarity contexts are less beneficial.

2. **Why does training with Random-Context lead to qualitatively different learning outcomes compared to other context sampling strategies, and what mechanisms drive this divergence?**  
   - Basis: The authors reference this in the ablation section.  
   - Why unresolved: Although theory suggests Random-Context should yield IWL dominance, the exact reasons for its distinct empirical performance and its interaction with model capacity are not fully elucidated.  
   - Needed evidence: Track attention parameters during training on diverse models, comparing empirical stationary points with theoretical predictions, and test whether the "different behavior" persists across architectures and data distributions.

3. **How well do the theoretical findings from the minimal two-layer transformer model transfer to large, deep language models used in practice?**  
   - Basis: The theoretical analysis relies on a simplified architecture; applicability to modern LLMs is an assumption.  
   - Why unresolved: The minimal model may omit factors like multi-head attention, layer normalization, or deeper nonlinearities that could alter learning dynamics.  
   - Needed evidence: Extend analysis to multi-layer transformers with realistic components, or perform large-scale empirical validation measuring whether attention parameters in fine-tuned LLMs converge to the predicted regimes under Contrastive-Context.

4. **To what extent does Contrastive-Context depend on the quality of external paraphrasing for generating synthetic similar examples, and can the approach be made robust or independent of such tools?**  
   - Basis: The method uses gemini-2.0-flash-lite for paraphrasing when natural similar examples are scarce.  
   - Why unresolved: High-quality paraphrases are crucial for creating intra-context contrast; sensitivity threshold and potential substitutes are unknown.  
   - Needed evidence: Evaluate performance using paraphrasers with controlled quality degradation, and investigate other augmentation methods (e.g., back-translation, learned perturbations) to replace external paraphrasing.

## Limitations
- Theoretical analysis assumes a minimal transformer; real LLMs with depth, normalization, and complex attention may deviate from predicted optimal stationary points.
- Paraphrase generation is critical but underspecified (prompt, decoding settings), risking semantic drift that weakens intra-context contrast.
- Probe definitions lack concrete choices (e.g., sentence encoder, COMET variant), hampering replication and comparison.
