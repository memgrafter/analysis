# RAMP: Reinforcement Adaptive Mixed Precision Quantization for Efficient On Device LLM Inference

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.17891
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17891
- **Reference count:** 23
- **Primary result:** A reinforcement learning policy learns transferable per-layer bit allocations, achieving a 5.54 WikiText-2 perplexity at 3.68 GB on Llama-2-7B (3.65 effective bits), outperforming AWQ-4 in size (6% smaller) and quality (1% higher), with zero-shot generalization to larger models.

## Executive Summary
RAMP introduces a reinforcement learning (RL) framework to solve the accuracy-efficiency trade-off in post-training quantization (PTQ) of large language models. It replaces uniform bit-width allocation with a learned, per-layer mixed-precision strategy. A Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) agent observes an 11-dimensional embedding of each layer's features and assigns optimal bit-widths under a global budget. A preconditioning technique called Scale Folding migrates activation outliers into weights, enabling stable sub-4-bit quantization. The resulting mixed-precision model is exported via the HALO pipeline to the standard GGUF format for kernel-free inference. On Llama-2-7B, RAMP achieves a superior Pareto frontier of perplexity versus model size. Notably, a policy trained on one model generalizes zero-shot to other decoder-only Transformer architectures (e.g., Llama-2-13B, Mistral-7B), often matching or exceeding the performance of a model-specific policy.

## Method Summary
RAMP formulates mixed-precision quantization as a sequential decision-making problem. For each linear layer in a Transformer model, an RL agent (SAC) selects a bit-width from {3,4,5,6} based on an 11-dimensional normalized state embedding derived from activation statistics, weight properties, and structural descriptors (e.g., layer type, normalized depth). The reward combines a quality penalty (derived from a fast perplexity estimate after a trial quantization) and a penalty for exceeding the target bit budget. Before quantization, Scale Folding is applied: per-channel scaling factors derived from activation statistics are applied to projection weights, while preceding normalization layers (RMSNorm) are inversely scaled. This mathematically preserves the forward pass but smooths activation distributions for low-bit quantization. After the policy assigns bits, each layer is quantized with asymmetric per-group affine quantization (group size 128). Finally, the HALO exporter maps each layer's nominal bit allocation to the nearest supported GGUF quantization type (e.g., Q3_K_M) for deployment with llama.cpp.

## Key Results
- On Llama-2-7B, RAMP achieves 5.54 WikiText-2 perplexity at 3.68 GB (3.65 effective bits), compared to AWQ-4's 5.60 at 3.90 GB.
- A policy trained on Llama-2-7B generalizes zero-shot to Llama-2-13B and Mistral-7B, often performing on par with or better than a policy trained specifically on the target model.
- The HALO pipeline exports the quantized model to GGUF format, enabling hardware-agnostic inference that retains 99.5% of the FP16 model's commonsense reasoning (accuracies on PIQA, HellaSwag, etc.).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Transferable RL Policy via Normalized Embeddings
A Soft Actor-Critic policy trained on per-layer 11-dimensional embeddings learns to assign bit-widths that generalize across Transformer architectures. The embeddings include normalized activation statistics (e.g., `max(|X|)/n`), weight properties, and structural descriptors (layer type, position). Normalization by layer width and other factors creates approximate invariance to model scale and parameter count. This allows a policy trained on a 7B model to be applied zero-shot to a 13B model or a different model family (Mistral) because the fundamental sensitivity patterns of layers (e.g., embeddings and output projections are sensitive) are preserved. Evidence includes high Pearson correlations (>0.93) of embeddings between source and target models and the strong zero-shot transfer results reported.

### Mechanism 2: Scale Folding for Stable Sub-4-Bit Quantization
Scale Folding enables stable quantization below 4 bits by relocating activation outliers into the weight distribution. It computes per-channel scaling factors from activation statistics (e.g., for Q/K/V projections), multiplies the corresponding weight matrices by these factors, and compensates by dividing the preceding RMSNorm layer by the same factors. This operation is mathematically equivalent but transfers large activation magnitudes into the weights, which can be handled by per-group quantization scales. The smoothed activations are then less prone to clipping or saturation in low-bit formats. Evidence is provided through ablations showing that without Scale Folding, 3-bit quantization fails or yields much higher perplexity.

### Mechanism 3: Hardware-Agnostic Deployment via GGUF Mapping
The HALO pipeline bridges the gap between the continuous bit allocations produced by the RL policy and the discrete, standardized quantization types in the GGUF format (used by llama.cpp). It maps each layer's target bit-width to the nearest available GGUF type (e.g., a target of 3.7 bits maps to Q3_K_M, ~3.9 bpw). This allows the mixed-precision model to be executed with llama.cpp's existing, highly optimized per-type kernels, avoiding the need for custom kernels. The portability is evidenced by the minimal drop in commonsense reasoning accuracy (retaining 99.5% of FP16 performance).

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) for Sample-Efficient RL.** SAC is an off-policy algorithm that maximizes a reward plus an entropy term, encouraging exploration. It is crucial here because each episode requires a full forward pass of a billion-parameter model to compute perplexity—an extremely costly operation. SAC's reuse of past experiences via a replay buffer (~30k transitions) allows training with only ~120-150 episodes, whereas on-policy methods like PPO would require thousands. *Quick check:* Why does entropy regularization prevent the policy from collapsing to a single bit-width prematurely?
- **Concept: Transformer Layer Sensitivity Patterns.** Different layers in a Transformer have varying sensitivity to quantization. For example, embedding layers, attention output projections (`o_proj`), and FFN down-projections are typically more sensitive than query/key projections or intermediate FFN activations. RAMP's embedding design encodes this structural role (via layer type and position). *Quick check:* Why are `o_proj` and FFN down-projections often more sensitive than `q_proj`/`k_proj` and FFN up-projections?
- **Concept: Post-Training Quantization (PTQ) with Per-Group Affine Quantization.** The base quantization primitive in RAMP is asymmetric per-group quantization (group size 128), where each group has its own scale and zero point. This is standard in GPTQ and AWQ. Per-group quantization offers a better accuracy-size trade-off than per-channel for large models but has higher overhead. *Quick check:* What is the primary trade-off between per-group and per-channel quantization in terms of reconstruction error and memory overhead?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Calibration & Feature Extractor -> SAC Agent (Actor/Critics) -> Quantization Engine (Scale Folding + Per-Group Quantization) -> HALO Exporter (GGUF Mapping) -> Inference Runtime (llama.cpp kernels).
- **Critical path:** The dominant cost is the full-model evaluation (WikiText-2 perplexity) at the end of each RL episode, taking 5-10 minutes. Calibration (2 min/layer) and quantization per layer are also significant. The total training time for a policy spans days to weeks on a single high-end GPU.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Sample efficiency vs. policy quality:* Training for more episodes (200-250) can improve the final perplexity but linearly increases compute cost. Early stopping at 120 episodes is shown to be near-optimal.
  - *Hardware portability vs. peak throughput:* Using GGUF and llama.cpp sacrifices ~10-20% throughput compared to custom, model-specific kernels (e.g., ExLlama) but ensures universal deployment across CPU/GPU hardware.
  - *Embedding simplicity vs. expressivity:* The 11D feature set enables cross-model transfer but may not capture fine-grained, channel-wise sensitivity patterns. Allocating bits at the head level could improve accuracy but lacks widespread kernel support.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - *High final perplexity (>5.70 on Llama-2-7B):* Policy is too aggressive on low bits. Check reward function calibration (asymmetric penalty slopes) or increase the bit budget.
  - *Unstable training (reward oscillates wildly):* Likely due to too high learning rate or insufficient entropy coefficient α. Reduce LR or increase α. A replay buffer smaller than 30k transitions can also cause instability.
  - *Export/Inference errors:* GGUF type mismatch (e.g., policy assigns 3.1 bits but only Q3_K_M exists). Verify the bit-to-type mapping table and ensure the target llama.cpp version supports all required types.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Calibration sanity check:** Extract 11D embeddings for Llama-2-7B. Verify that the normalized depth feature correlates perfectly with layer index (ρ ≈ 1) and that activation max values are bounded between 0 and 1 after normalization.
  2. **Policy transfer test:** Train a policy on Llama-2-7B. Apply the resulting bit allocations zero-shot to Llama-2-13B and quantize it. The perplexity difference compared to a policy trained directly on the 13B model should be ≤0.1, confirming transferability.
  3. **Scale Folding ablation:** Perform a 3-bit average budget quantization on Llama-2-7B with and without Scale Folding. Without folding, the model should yield NaN/Inf or perplexity >5.58. With folding, it should achieve the reported ~5.54.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Extending to other architectures:** Does RAMP's zero-shot transferability extend to encoder-decoder models (e.g., T5) and non-Transformer models (e.g., CNNs)? The current 11D embedding is tailored to decoder-only Transformers; other architectures may require different features.
- **Integration with QAT:** Can combining RAMP with quantization-aware training (QAT) enable stable sub-3-bit quantization? QAT could adapt the model to extreme low-bit regimes but at significant additional training cost.
- **Multi-objective hardware-aware optimization:** How can the reward function be extended to jointly optimize for latency, power consumption, and memory bandwidth? The current formulation only considers perplexity and bit budget. Hardware-specific surrogates would be needed.

## Limitations
- **Scale Folding implementation details are sparse.** The exact calculation of "activation-importance scores" and the compensation formula for RMSNorm lack full pseudocode, increasing risk of implementation error.
- **SAC hyperparameters are unreported.** Key parameters like the initial entropy coefficient α, its automatic tuning schedule, and the specifics of action space clipping are missing, hindering exact replication.
- **Transferability is only validated on similar decoder-only Transformers.** Generalization to encoder-decoder (T5) or mixture-of-experts models is not tested. The claim of architectural invariance is therefore limited.

## Confidence
- **RL policy and cross-model transferability:** **Medium.** Strong empirical zero-shot results and high embedding correlations support the claim, but the evaluation is restricted to closely related decoder-only models. Robustness to calibration set variation is unknown.
- **Scale Folding for enabling sub-4-bit quantization:** **Low.** The concept is plausible and supported by related work on activation smoothing. However, the paper lacks a clean ablation isolating Scale Folding's effect (all experiments use it), and implementation ambiguities prevent independent verification.
- **HALO/GGUID export and inference quality:** **High.** GGUF is a mature, widely used format. The reported near-FP16 commonsense scores are consistent with the modest average bit-width (~3.7bpw). The portability claim is solid.
