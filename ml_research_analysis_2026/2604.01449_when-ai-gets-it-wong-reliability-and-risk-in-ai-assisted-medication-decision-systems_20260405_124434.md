# When AI Gets it Wong: Reliability and Risk in AI-Assisted Medication Decision Systems

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2604.01449
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01449
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Aggregate accuracy metrics obscure critical failure behaviors and their asymmetric clinical consequences, necessitating risk-aware evaluation focused on error type and severity rather than overall performance.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses a critical gap in evaluating AI systems for medication decision support: standard performance metrics (e.g., accuracy) fail to capture the real-world reliability risks that can lead to patient harm. Through a controlled simulation of ten medication scenarios—covering drug interactions and dosage decisions—the authors manually introduced and classified errors into three types: false negatives (missed harmful interactions), false positives (false risk alerts), and incorrect dosage recommendations. The error distribution revealed false negatives as the most frequent and dangerous failure mode, as they evade human detection and can cause severe adverse drug reactions. The study concludes that deployment safety requires shifting from aggregate accuracy to risk-aware evaluation that explicitly weights error type and clinical severity.

## Method Summary
The study employed a scenario-based evaluation framework. Ten simulated medication decision points were constructed (e.g., specific drug pairs, dose extremes) with ground truth judgments based on standard clinical knowledge. A baseline medication safety classifier (unspecified) generated predictions for each scenario. Errors were then manually injected to produce clear instances of false negatives, false positives, and dosage errors. Each output was classified by error type and mapped to potential clinical consequences (e.g., adverse reaction severity). This method isolates system failure modes but relies on a generic model, synthetic error injection, and a small, non-generalizable scenario set, limiting ecological validity.

## Key Results
- False negatives (n=3) were the most frequent failure mode, followed by dosage errors (n=2) and false positives (n=1).
- False negatives pose the highest patient risk because they provide no warning for dangerous combinations, enabling severe adverse drug events without trigger for human vigilance.
- Aggregate metrics like accuracy collapse these distinct error types into a single score, obscuring the asymmetry in clinical harm and misrepresenting system safety.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Aggregate Metrics Obscure Risk Asymmetry
- **Claim:** Standard aggregate performance metrics (e.g., accuracy, precision, recall) systematically collapse distinct error types into a single scalar, hiding the asymmetric clinical consequences where certain errors (undetected harmful interactions) pose far greater patient harm than others.
- **Mechanism:** By treating all misclassifications as equal losses, these metrics fail to differentiate a false negative (missed danger) from a false positive (unnecessary alert) in terms of realized risk, thereby misrepresenting safety for deployment.
- **Core assumption:** Clinical harm severity is not linearly related to generic error rates and must be modeled separately.
- **Evidence anchors:** 
  - [abstract] "aggregate accuracy metrics obscure critical failure behaviors and their asymmetric clinical consequences, necessitating risk-aware evaluation focused on error type and severity."
  - [section 2] "Metrics such as accuracy, precision, or recall provide an overall indication of model performance but do not capture how systems behave when they fail."
- **Break condition:** If an evaluation regime explicitly weights errors by a validated clinical harm severity scale and reports failure-mode distributions as primary metrics, the obscuring effect of aggregates is mitigated.

### Mechanism 2: False Negatives as Highest-Risk Errors
- **Claim:** False negatives (missed harmful drug interactions) represent the most dangerous error class because they generate a false sense of safety and are less likely to be caught by human oversight.
- **Mechanism:** A false negative directly contradicts clinical safety intent (warn of danger → no warning), providing no trigger for clinician review. This contrasts with false positives, which at least prompt scrutiny, and dosage errors, which may be caught by secondary checks. The harm mechanism is the absence of an alert in a dangerous context.
- **Core assumption:** The *detectability* of an error by a human supervisor is a primary determinant of its realized clinical risk; undetected errors are maximally hazardous.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3] "False negatives... are particularly dangerous because they can lead to severe adverse drug reactions without any warning, giving both the system and the user a false sense of safety."
  - [section 5] "The simulated evaluation showed that false negatives were the most frequent error type (n = 3)... This highlights the increased risk associated with undetected harmful interactions."
- **Break condition:** If real-world monitoring data shows that false negatives are consistently caught by human workflow checks at a rate approaching that of false positives, the assumed risk asymmetry diminishes.

### Mechanism 3: Simulation-Based Validation Uncovers Latent Failures
- **Claim:** A controlled, scenario-based evaluation with manually injected error types is a valid and necessary method to surface failure distributions and their hypothetical clinical impacts, which are invisible in standard retrospective testing on curated datasets.
- **Mechanism:** By constructing a minimal set of representative, high-stakes decision points and forcing the system to output both correct and incorrect responses, the evaluation isolates *how* the system fails rather than just *how often* it is correct, creating a map of failure modes to potential outcomes.
- **Core assumption:** Carefully constructed simulation scenarios have ecological validity for the *type* of failure (if not exact frequency) that would occur in real pharmacy workflows.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 5] "Through a series of controlled, simulated scenarios... we analyse different types of system failures... This approach allows for focused examination of system behavior under failure conditions."
  - [section 5] "The results of this simulated evaluation highlight that even a small number of incorrect outputs can lead to significant clinical risks."
- **Break condition:** If subsequent prospective, real-world validation studies find that the error type distribution observed in simulation (e.g., false negatives dominant) is significantly different from the distribution observed in actual clinical use with human oversight.

- *Quick check:* In the context of a drug interaction checker, what is the real-world difference between a false positive (flagging a safe combination) and a false negative (missing a dangerous combination), and why is the latter more immediately hazardous?

**Concept: Risk-Aware vs. Performance-Oriented Evaluation**
- *Why needed:* The paper advocates shifting from measuring "accuracy" to measuring "reliability under failure." This requires learning to evaluate systems based on the severity and nature of their mistakes, not just their aggregate correctness.
- *Quick check:* Why might a medication AI system with 99% accuracy still be considered unsafe for deployment in a pharmacy, according to the paper's argument?

- *Why needed:* The paper's methodology relies on creating specific, challenging test cases to provoke and study failures. This contrasts with typical validation on large, clean, historical datasets.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**
Input Parser & Normalizer -> (Interaction Risk Assessor & Dosage Calculation Engine) -> Confidence & Uncertainty Estimator -> (Decision Logger & Failure Classifier | Final Recommendation Formatter) -> Explanation Generator

**Critical path:** 
The critical path begins with the Input Parser, which feeds both the Interaction Risk Assessor and Dosage Calculation Engine in parallel. Their outputs are aggregated and assessed by the Confidence & Uncertainty Estimator. Depending on context, the system routes to either the Decision Logger & Failure Classifier for evaluation or the Final Recommendation Formatter for clinical output. Finally, an optional Explanation Generator may produce a rationale to support human review. A mandatory human oversight checkpoint exists after the combined assessment but outside the automated pipeline.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- **Sensitivity vs. Specificity:** Tuning the interaction assessor toward high sensitivity reduces false negatives (missed dangers) but increases false positives (alert fatigue). The paper implies sensitivity must be prioritized in high-risk contexts.
- **Interpretability vs. Complexity:** A simple, interpretable model (e.g., rule-based) may reduce obscure false negatives but could lack nuance, causing other errors. A complex black-box model may have higher aggregate accuracy but higher-risk failure modes.
- **Speed vs. Safety Checks:** Adding redundant checks (e.g., a second rule-based verifier for dosage) increases latency but may catch certain failure types.

**Failure signatures:**
- **Signature A (False Negative):** Interaction Assessor outputs "Safe" for a known high-risk pair (e.g., Warfarin + NSAID). No alert flag generated.
- **Signature B (Wrong Dosage):** Dosage Engine outputs a value >20% outside standard clinical range for the drug/indication, especially for narrow-therapeutic-index drugs (e.g., Digoxin, Warfarin).
- **Signature C (Silent Overoverride):** System modifies a human's prescribed dose in a direction that increases risk (e.g., increases dose for a patient with renal impairment).

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Replication & Error Distribution Confirmation:** Run the paper's 10 simulated scenarios on the candidate system. Primary metric: Count of each error type. Goal: Confirm if false negatives are the predominant failure mode.
2. **Edge Case Stress Test for False Negatives:** Create 20 new scenarios involving high-risk, common drug interactions not in the original set (e.g., SSRIs + MAOIs, ACE inhibitors + Potassium-sparing diuretics). Measure false negative rate specifically on this high-severity subset.
3. **Human-in-the-Loop Detection Audit:** Present a mix of system outputs (correct, false positive, false negative, wrong dosage) to clinical pharmacists. Measure: (a) detection rate for each error type, (b) time to detection, (c) stated confidence. Goal: Quantify the "undetected" risk window for false negatives vs. false positives.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

**Open Question 1:** How do specific AI failure types (false negatives, false positives, dosage errors) in medication decisions correlate with quantifiable adverse clinical outcomes in real-world pharmacy practice?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper states that errors "can lead to adverse drug reactions, ineffective treatment, or delayed care" but uses simulated scenarios without real outcome data.
- **Why unresolved:** The study manually introduced errors into a small set of hypothetical cases (n=10), so the actual frequency and severity of clinical harm remain unmeasured.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Large-scale prospective studies or retrospective analyses linking AI decision logs to patient safety incident reports, emergency visits, or hospitalization rates.

**Open Question 2:** Under what patient or clinical contextual factors (e.g., polypharmacy, comorbidities) does the prevalence of false negatives—the most risky error type—increase in AI-assisted medication systems?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper identifies false negatives as most frequent (n=3) and most dangerous but does not analyze why they occur more often; it notes complex regimens increase risk but doesn't test this.
- **Why unresolved:** The simulated evaluation used generic scenarios without varying patient complexity systematically, so drivers of false negatives are unspecified.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Multivariate analysis of error logs from deployed AI systems across diverse patient populations, identifying covariates associated with missed interactions.

**Open Question 3:** What specific design features in AI interfaces (e.g., uncertainty visualization, explanation quality) most effectively reduce automation bias and improve clinicians' detection of AI errors in medication workflows?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper warns that "over-reliance on AI recommendations" and "limited transparency" increase risk but offers no empirical evaluation of mitigation strategies.
- **Why unresolved:** Recommendations for transparency and human oversight are suggested without testing how implementation affects user behavior or error rates.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Randomized controlled trials comparing clinician performance (error detection, trust calibration) with different AI explainability or confidence-display designs in simulated pharmacy tasks.

**Open Question 4:** Can reliability-focused evaluation metrics (e.g., severity-weighted error scores, failure-mode distributions) be validated as better predictors of real-world safety than traditional accuracy metrics for medication AI systems?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper argues aggregate metrics "obscure critical failure behaviors" and calls for "risk-aware evaluation" but does not propose or test alternative frameworks.
- **Why unresolved:** The simulated study only illustrates error distribution; it does not compare predictive validity of new vs. traditional metrics against actual harm data.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Comparative studies correlating various evaluation metric outputs (accuracy, precision-recall, failure-type breakdowns) with adverse event rates in live clinical deployments of medication AI.

## Limitations
- **Unspecified AI system:** The paper does not name or describe the evaluated AI model(s), preventing replication and raising questions about whether observed failure distributions generalize beyond the authors' chosen (or simulated) system.
- **Ground truth ambiguity:** The "standard clinical knowledge" used to define true/false errors lacks specificity (e.g., which databases, guidelines, or expert panels), introducing potential subjectivity in error labeling and severity assignment.
- **Simulation-to-reality gap:** Findings rely on 10 manually crafted scenarios with intentionally injected errors. The ecological validity—whether this error type distribution (false negatives dominant) reflects actual deployment with clinician oversight—remains unproven.

## Confidence
- **Aggregate metrics obscure risk asymmetry:** High (logical argument is sound and well-articulated)
- **False negatives as highest-risk error:** Medium (mechanism is plausible but assumes low human catch rates without empirical validation)
- **Simulation as necessary validation method:** Low (approach is described but its ability to predict real-world distributions is a foundational assumption without evidential support from the paper)
