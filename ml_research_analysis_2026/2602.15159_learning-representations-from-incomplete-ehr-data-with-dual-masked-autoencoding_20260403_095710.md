# Learning Representations from Incomplete EHR Data with Dual-Masked Autoencoding

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.15159  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15159  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** AID-MAE achieves state-of-the-art performance on multiple clinical prediction tasks (mortality, length of stay, acute kidney injury) without prior imputation, improving AUROC by up to 1.3 percentage points on MIMIC-IV and demonstrating strong cross-dataset transferability.

## Executive Summary
Incomplete electronic health record (EHR) time series pose significant challenges due to irregular sampling, heterogeneous missingness, and high sparsity. Existing self-supervised methods often rely on prior imputation or focus solely on reconstruction quality, limiting their ability to learn robust, generalizable representations. This paper introduces AID-MAE (Augmented-Intrinsic Dual-Masked Autoencoder), a novel framework that learns directly from incomplete EHR data. AID-MAE employs two complementary masks: an intrinsic missingness mask that identifies naturally missing values, and an augmented mask that stochastically hides observed entries during training. The encoder processes only unmasked tokens, while the decoder reconstructs both unmasked and augmented-masked values, explicitly excluding intrinsically missing positions from the loss. This design encourages the model to infer missing values from context without biasing toward missingness patterns.

Experiments on MIMIC-IV and PhysioNet Challenge 2012 datasets show that AID-MAE consistently outperforms strong baselines, including XGBoost and DuETT, on downstream tasks such as in-ICU mortality, length of stay, and acute kidney injury prediction. The method also yields clinically meaningful embeddings that naturally cluster patient cohorts by ICU type and group laboratory features into coherent clusters. With strong transferability across datasets and robust performance even with limited labeled data, AID-MAE offers a promising approach for real-world EHR applications where data is often sparse and irregular.

## Method Summary
AID-MAE transforms each patient's EHR into a fixed-length token grid with regular time bins (e.g., daily rows). For each bin, features (labs, vitals, vasopressors) are represented as tokens embedding both value and timestamp. The grid is padded to a uniform length per batch, and an intrinsic missingness mask (M) denotes truly unobserved entries. During training, an augmented mask (A) randomly hides a subset of observed tokens (default 25%). The encoder processes only the intersection of observed tokens (R) and non‑masked tokens (R∖A), while the decoder receives the full sequence with masked tokens (both A and M) replaced by a learned `[MASK]` token. Positional encodings convey time bin and feature column information.

The model is trained with a mean squared error reconstruction loss computed solely on the augmented‑masked tokens (A) and the unmasked observed tokens (R∖A); intrinsically missing tokens (M) are excluded. This dual‑mask objective forces the encoder to learn representations capable of predicting any observed value while remaining invariant to the intrinsic missingness pattern. Architecture uses a Transformer encoder (depth 8) and decoder (depth 4) with combined value‑time embeddings (dim 64). Training employs AdamW with cosine annealing. The resulting embeddings can be used for downstream tasks via linear probing or full fine‑tuning, achieving strong performance even with minimal labeled data.

## Key Results
- On **MIMIC‑IV**, AID‑MAE achieves AUROC 87.7% for in‑ICU mortality (vs. DuETT 86.4%) and AUROC 77.6% for length of stay < 72h (vs. DuETT 77.2%).
- On **PhysioNet Challenge 2012**, it obtains AUROC 78.2% for mortality and AUROC 77.3% for acute kidney injury.
- **Linear probing** demonstrates superior transfer: pretrained on MIMIC‑IV, it reaches AUROC 73.1% on PhysioNet mortality (vs. DuETT 70.9%). Learned embeddings also naturally stratify patients by ICU type and cluster laboratory features into clinically coherent groups.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
AID‑MAE’s effectiveness arises from three intertwined mechanisms:

- **Invariance to Intrinsic Missingness:** By explicitly excluding intrinsically missing values from the reconstruction loss and using augmented masking to simulate missingness on observed entries, the model learns representations that depend on observed signals rather than missingness patterns. This avoids bias from imputation errors and allows handling of missing‑not‑at‑random (MNAR) scenarios indirectly.
- **Cross‑Temporal and Cross‑Feature Context:** The fixed‑grid representation with positional encodings enables self‑attention to capture interactions between features and over time. The dual reconstruction objective forces the model to build coherent context to predict held‑out values, leading to embeddings that reflect relationships among variables (e.g., hemoglobin and hematocrit cluster together).
- **Missingness Augmentation for Robustness:** Stochastic augmented masking during pretraining exposes the model to diverse sparsity patterns, acting as domain randomization. This improves invariance to missingness distribution shifts and enables effective transfer across datasets.

## Foundational Learning
**Masked Autoencoding**  
*Why needed:* Provides a self‑supervised pretraining signal from unlabeled EHR data by reconstructing masked inputs, forcing the model to learn rich representations.  
*Quick check:* If a token is in \(R\setminus A\), it is observed and not augmented‑masked. What must the model do to reconstruct it correctly?  
Answer: It must infer the true value from the encoded context of other unmasked tokens.

**Handling Missing Data (Intrinsic vs. Augmented)**  
*Why needed:* EHRs contain two types of missingness: truly unobserved (intrinsic) and artificially created for pretext tasks (augmented). Distinguishing them ensures the model does not try to reconstruct non‑existent values while still benefiting from masked reconstruction.  
*Quick check:* In the loss function \(L\), why is the summation over \(R\setminus A\) and \(A\) but not \(M\)?  
Answer: Because \(M\) (intrinsic missing) has no ground truth to compare against; including it would require imputation or guessing, which defeats the purpose of learning from actual data.

**Tabular Time Series Discretization & Positional Encoding**  
*Why needed:* Irregular EHR measurements must be aligned to a regular grid to enable sequence modeling. Positional encodings provide the model with information about the time bin and feature column, preserving temporal and feature context.  
*Quick check:* If two vital signs are measured at the same hour, how does their shared time position get encoded relative to a lab from the previous day?  
Answer: They receive the same time positional embedding for that hour, while the lab receives a different time embedding for its day; the feature‑specific positional component distinguishes the two vitals from each other.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
EHR data → Fixed‑length token grid → Intrinsic mask (M) & Augmented mask (A) → Combined value‑time embedding + positional encoding → Encoder (processes R∖A) → Decoder (full sequence with `[MASK]` at A∪M) → Prediction head (linear) → Reconstruction loss (MSE on R∖A ∪ A)

**Critical Path**  
Raw data → Grid → Intrinsic mask → Augmented mask → Encoder on (R∖A) → Decoder on full → Loss (on R∖A ∪ A) → Parameter update.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- Fixed sequence length L simplifies batching but pads short sequences and may discard long histories.  
- Excluding intrinsic missing positions (M) from the loss avoids noisy gradients but discards potentially informative missingness patterns when M is MNAR.  
- Using only observed and non‑masked tokens (R∖A) in the encoder reduces compute but limits the context available during encoding.

**Failure Signatures**  
- Poor linear probing performance indicates embeddings are not generative or discriminative.  
- Decoder reconstruction loss on augmented‑masked tokens (A) significantly higher than on unmasked observed tokens (R∖A) suggests over‑fitting or masking too aggressively.  
- UMAP visualization shows no clinically meaningful clustering (e.g., features not grouped by type).  
- Transfer performance (e.g., on PhysioNet) drops substantially compared to in‑dataset results, indicating lack of robustness to missingness distribution shifts.

**First 3 Experiments**  
1. **Pretraining:** Train AID‑MAE on MIMIC‑IV with default 25% augmented masking on observed tokens; monitor convergence of dual loss and its two components (R∖A and A). Ensure both losses decrease and are roughly balanced.  
2. **Linear Probing:** Freeze encoder, train logistic regression on CLS embeddings using 1%, 10%, and 100% of labeled training data. Compare AUROC against DuETT frozen encoder on mortality and LOS tasks. Expect AID‑MAE to outperform, especially at low label rates.  
3. **Ablation on Masking Strategy:** Compare random masking (a=0, b=0.25) with logit‑based reweighted masking using different (a,b) parameters from Table 4. Check if enabling a>0 (upweighting frequent features) improves LOS AUROC, confirming that frequent features serve as reliable reference points.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
The authors identify the following unresolved questions:

**Open Question 1**  
- Question: Can AID‑MAE's dual‑mask mechanism be extended to explicitly model missing‑not‑at‑random (MNAR) mechanisms rather than relying on implicit invariance, and would this improve performance on clinical tasks where missingness itself carries prognostic information?  
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors state in the conclusion: "Informative missingness is currently modeled implicitly. Explicitly capturing missing‑not‑at‑random mechanisms remains an important direction for future work, even though the learned representations are encouraged to be invariant to the missingness distribution."  
- Why unresolved: The paper acknowledges that missingness in EHRs can be informative (MNAR), but AID‑MAE treats all intrinsic missingness uniformly. This could limit performance in scenarios where the pattern of missingness directly relates to patient condition or care processes.  
- What evidence would resolve it: A modified AID‑MAE variant that explicitly incorporates missingness mechanism labels (e.g., via auxiliary tasks predicting why a value is missing) or models missingness as a latent variable. Comparison on datasets with validated MNAR patterns, using metrics that capture representation quality for both observed and missing data distributions.

**Open Question 2**  
- Question: Is there a fundamental trade‑off between optimizing for imputation quality versus learning general‑purpose representations in masked autoencoders for EHRs, and does AID‑MAE's dual‑loss design partially resolve this trade‑off?  
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The introduction criticizes prior methods for focusing "solely for imputation quality" which "reduces their capacity to efficiently learn representations that support clinical downstream tasks." AID‑MAE uses a dual loss but still includes reconstruction objectives. The paper shows strong downstream performance but does not directly analyze whether imputation quality correlates with representation quality.  
- Why unresolved: While AID‑MAE outperforms imputation‑focused methods on downstream tasks, it is unclear whether its gains come from improved imputation or from learning representations that are transferable beyond reconstruction. The paper reports single‑value reconstruction results (Appendix A.2) but does not correlate these with downstream task performance.  
- What evidence would resolve it: Controlled experiments varying the reconstruction loss weight while measuring both imputation metrics (NRMSE, R²) and downstream task performance. Analysis of whether high‑quality imputation is necessary, sufficient, or even detrimental for optimal downstream transfer.

**Open Question 3**  
- Question: What is the optimal strategy for augmented masking in EHR time series given heterogeneous feature‑specific missingness rates, and does logit‑based proportional reweighting provide the best trade‑off between frequent and rare features?  
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The paper introduces a logit‑based reweighting scheme and tests various (a,b) parameters in Table 4, showing small gains. However, they note: "frequently observed variables may provide reliable supervision... making them both clinically important for downstream tasks and useful reference points for reconstructing other features." The optimal balancing remains open.  
- Why unresolved: The ablation study (Table 4) shows marginal differences across masking ratios and reweighting schemes. The paper does not provide a principled derivation or strong empirical justification for the chosen logit transform, and the performance gains are small, suggesting the problem is not fully resolved.  
- What evidence would resolve it: Systematic evaluation of reweighting strategies (inverse probability, uniform, random, learned weighting) across diverse EHR datasets with different missingness profiles, measuring both representation quality (linear probing) and computational efficiency. Theoretical analysis of how masking distributions affect gradient signals for features with different observation frequencies.

**Open Question 4**  
- Question: Does AID‑MAE's architecture (fixed‑length grids with carry‑forward imputation) fundamentally limit its ability to model very long‑term temporal dependencies or irregular sampling intervals compared to set‑based representations, and would hybrid designs improve performance on tasks requiring long‑range context?  
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The paper contrasts AID‑MAE with set‑based methods (STraTS, EBCL) and argues that tabular grids "align features with time, enabling learning contextual embeddings that capture temporal–feature dependencies." However, they acknowledge that grid‑based approaches require imputation (even if implicit via masking) and may lose fine‑grained timing information. The paper does not compare against set‑based methods on tasks where long‑range dependencies matter.  
- Why unresolved: The introduction positions tabular grids as advantageous for cross‑temporal and cross‑feature context, but the experiments focus on first‑24‑hour predictions. Tasks requiring longer horizons (e.g., predicting outcomes after several days) or precise timing of events are not evaluated. The relative merits of grid vs. set representations remain an open empirical question.  
- What evidence would resolve it: Direct comparison between AID‑MAE and set‑based methods on tasks with longer observation windows or where exact event timing is predictive. Ablation studies varying the grid resolution (hourly vs. daily) or incorporating continuous time encodings beyond fixed positional embeddings.

**Open Question 5**  
- Question: Can AID‑MAE's dual‑mask pretraining be effectively transferred to entirely different healthcare modalities (e.g., clinical notes, medical images, or sensor data) without modality‑specific architectural changes, and would this yield a true multimodal foundation model for healthcare?  
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The conclusion mentions: "extending AID‑MAE to multimodal inputs, such as clinical text and medical images" as a future direction. The paper demonstrates cross‑dataset transfer within tabular EHRs (MIMIC‑IV to PhysioNet), but multimodal extension is not attempted.  
- Why unresolved: Multimodal healthcare data have heterogeneous structures (discrete tokens for text, pixels for images, time series for sensors). AID‑MAE's current design is specific to tokenized numerical sequences with time stamps. It is unclear whether the dual‑mask principle (intrinsic + augmented missingness) generalizes to other modalities where "missingness" may be defined differently (e.g., masked regions in images, masked words in text).  
- What evidence would resolve it: Development of a unified architecture that can process different modalities with modality‑specific encoders but shared dual‑mask pretraining objectives. Evaluation on multimodal downstream tasks (e.g., using images + labs to predict sepsis) comparing modality‑specific pretraining vs. joint pretraining. Analysis of whether representations from different modalities align in a common space.

## Limitations
- Hyperparameter choices for augmented masking (ratio, reweighting parameters) are not fully specified in the paper, hindering exact reproduction.  
- The model assumes that intrinsic missingness is non‑informative; if missingness is MNAR and directly predictive of outcomes, assumptions break down and performance may degrade.  
- Fixed‑length grid with carry‑forward imputation may lose fine‑grained temporal resolution; current evaluation is limited to short‑term (first 24 h) prediction tasks.

## Confidence
- **Claim 1** (Learns effective representations without prior imputation): *Medium‑High*  
- **Claim 2** (Captures cross‑temporal and cross‑feature contextual dependencies): *Medium*  
- **Claim 3** (Invariant to missingness distribution shifts; robust transfer): *Medium*
