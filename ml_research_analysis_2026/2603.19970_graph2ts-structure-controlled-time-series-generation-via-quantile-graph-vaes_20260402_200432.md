# Graph2TS: Structure-Controlled Time Series Generation via Quantile-Graph VAEs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.19970  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19970  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: Graph2TS generates time series with superior distributional fidelity, temporal alignment, and manifold coverage compared to TimeGAN and DiffusionTS by using a quantile‑graph conditioned VAE that separates deterministic structure from stochastic residuals.

## Executive Summary
Graph2TS addresses a key limitation of existing time‑series generative models—their tendency to blur global temporal structure with local variability—by introducing a compact quantile‑based transition graph that explicitly captures global dynamics. This graph conditions a variational autoencoder, which decouples a deterministic structural backbone (derived from the graph) from a stochastic residual modeled by a latent variable. Evaluated on four datasets (CHB‑MIT EEG, Sunspot, Electricity, ECG), Graph2TS achieves state‑of‑the‑art performance on multiple metrics, including lower Wasserstein distance and Kolmogorov‑Smirnov statistic, better autocorrelation and power‑spectrum alignment, and higher manifold coverage. Ablation studies confirm that both the graph‑based structural conditioning and the residual component are essential.

## Method Summary
The method begins by converting a univariate time series into a discrete sequence of quantile states via uniform binning of the value range. From this discrete sequence, a first‑order Markov transition graph is built, where nodes represent quantile states and edge weights denote transition probabilities. This graph serves as a compact representation of the global temporal structure. A variational autoencoder is then trained to generate the time series: the encoder maps the original series to a latent distribution that captures stochastic deviations from the deterministic structure; the decoder reconstructs the series conditioned on the graph’s structural embedding (e.g., obtained via a graph neural network). At generation time, a new graph (from a training example or a designed one) is encoded, a latent vector is sampled, and the decoder produces a series that respects the global dynamics encoded in the graph while exhibiting realistic local fluctuations.

## Key Results
- On three datasets (CHB‑MIT EEG, Sunspot, Electricity) Graph2TS attains the lowest Wasserstein distance and Kolmogorov‑Smirnov statistic among compared baselines.
- Generated series exhibit significantly lower ACF MAE and PSD discrepancy, indicating superior temporal alignment.
- Manifold coverage metrics show that Graph2TS explores the data distribution more thoroughly than TimeGAN and DiffusionTS.
- Ablation experiments demonstrate that removing either the graph conditioning or the stochastic residual markedly degrades performance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Graph2TS succeeds because it cleanly separates the ingredients of a time series: the quantile transition graph encodes the repeatable, deterministic patterns that govern large‑scale temporal evolution, while the VAE’s latent variable captures the irreducible noise and fine‑grained variability. This decoupling prevents the model from conflating structure with randomness—a common pitfall of GANs and diffusion models—allowing the generator to reproduce the true data distribution more faithfully. The graph also provides an interpretable and controllable handle on global dynamics, enabling users to steer generation by supplying different graphs.

## Foundational Learning
- **Quantile Discretization**: Converts continuous values into discrete states (bins) based on quantiles.  
  *Why needed*: Enables construction of a graph with a finite set of nodes; preserves the marginal distribution.  
  *Quick check*: Compare histograms and autocorrelation of original vs. discretized series; they should be similar.
- **Transition Graph**: A directed weighted graph where nodes are quantile states and edges represent first‑order transition probabilities.  
  *Why needed*: Compactly summarizes global temporal dynamics; provides a structural prior for generation.  
  *Quick check*: Verify that row‑stochastic transition matrix is sparse and sums to one.
- **Variational Autoencoder (VAE)**: A generative model that learns a latent distribution and uses it to reconstruct data.  
  *Why needed*: Offers a probabilistic framework to model stochastic residuals and enable sampling.  
  *Quick check*: Ensure reconstruction loss decreases and latent interpolations yield smooth transitions.
- **Structural Conditioning**: The decoder receives the graph embedding as additional input.  
  *Why needed*: Forces generated series to follow the same global transition dynamics as the training data.  
  *Quick check*: Generate multiple series from the same graph but different latents; check that aggregate ACF/PSD matches the graph’s statistics.
- **Stochastic Residual Modeling**: The latent variable accounts for deviations from the deterministic graph‑based backbone.  
  *Why needed*: Captures local irregularities and noise that the graph cannot explain.  
  *Quick check*: Compare distribution of residuals (original minus deterministic prediction) to samples from the latent prior.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**  
Quantile Discretizer → Transition Graph Builder → Graph Encoder → VAE Decoder → Generated Time Series

**Critical Path**  
1. Build quantile graph from training time series.  
2. Encode the graph into a fixed‑size structural embedding (e.g., via GNN).  
3. Sample a latent noise vector from the prior.  
4. Decode the embedding + latent to produce a synthetic time series.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- *Graph size (number of quantiles)*: More bins yield finer representation but increase sparsity and computational cost.  
- *Markov order*: First‑order graphs are simple but may miss longer‑range dependencies; higher‑order graphs capture more context at the expense of blow‑up in node count.  
- *Deterministic vs. stochastic balance*: Over‑reliance on the graph can make generation too rigid; too much stochasticity may lose global structure. The weighting is tuned via training objectives.

**Failure Signatures**  
- High ACF/PSD discrepancy → graph does not capture essential dynamics (maybe too few quantiles or wrong order).  
- Low manifold coverage (e.g., generated samples are near‑duplicates) → latent space under‑utilized or decoder too deterministic.  
- High distributional distance (Wasserstein) despite good alignment → residual modeling inadequate (e.g., encoder/decoder capacity insufficient).

**First Experiments**  
1. **Ablate graph conditioning**: Train a VAE without the graph input; expect a significant drop in temporal alignment metrics.  
2. **Vary number of quantiles**: Train with 5, 10, 20 bins; observe trade‑off between graph sparsity and generation fidelity.  
3. **Compare Markov orders**: Construct a second‑order graph (states = pairs of consecutive quantiles) and compare against first‑order on datasets with longer memory (e.g., Sunspot).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How can Graph2TS be extended to handle multivariate time series?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The conclusion explicitly states: "Future work will explore extensions to multivariate time series..."
- **Why unresolved**: The current model uses a univariate quantile graph and conditioning; multivariate data require capturing dependencies across variables, which may necessitate a different graph construction (e.g., multi‑dimensional quantization, cross‑variable edges) or an adapted architecture.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A well‑defined multivariate extension of the quantile graph, combined with experimental validation on multivariate datasets (e.g., sensor networks, multivariate EEG) showing improved fidelity and coherence over existing multivariate generative baselines.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: What richer structural graph representations (beyond first‑order quantile transitions) could further improve generation quality?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The future work mentions exploring "richer structural graph representations."
- **Why unresolved**: The current quantile graph only encodes first‑order transition probabilities; higher‑order dynamics or alternative graph constructions (e.g., visibility graphs, recurrence plots) might capture more complex temporal patterns and further improve structural fidelity, especially for signals with long‑range dependencies.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Systematic comparison of different graph encodings (e.g., second‑order Markov, weighted paths, topological features) within the Graph2TS framework, measuring gains in temporal alignment and distributional fidelity across diverse datasets.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Can the cross‑modal generation paradigm (graph‑to‑time series) be applied to other data modalities or tasks?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The conclusion notes the promise of "broader cross‑modal generative settings."
- **Why unresolved**: Graph2TS demonstrates that structure‑conditioned generation can decouple global patterns from local noise, but it is unclear whether this principle generalizes to other modalities (e.g., text, images) or to tasks like imputation, forecasting, or anomaly detection.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Development of analogous structure‑conditioned models for at least one other modality (e.g., graph‑to‑image, graph‑to‑text) with empirical evidence of improved fidelity and controllability compared to unimodal generative baselines.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: To what extent does the first‑order Markov assumption in the quantile graph limit the representation of temporal structure, and would higher‑order models yield benefits?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The paper constructs the quantile graph under a first‑order Markov assumption, but does not justify why higher‑order dependencies are unnecessary. The ablation study shows that structural conditioning matters, but the order of the Markov chain remains unexplored.
- **Why unresolved**: Real‑world time series often exhibit memory beyond one step; a first‑order graph might oversimplify, especially for signals with slow dynamics or long periods. Higher‑order graphs could preserve more context but increase complexity and sparsity.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Comparative experiments varying the Markov order (e.g., first vs. second vs. third) while controlling for graph size and sparsity, evaluating trade‑offs in metrics like ACF error, PSD alignment, and generation stability.

## Limitations
- Evaluation is restricted to four datasets, with no inclusion of recent baselines (e.g., TimeFlow) and limited domain diversity.
- The paper relies solely on automatic metrics (Wasserstein, KS, ACF MAE, PSD) without human validation or rigorous statistical testing, risking metric bias.
- Hyperparameter sensitivity of the quantile‑graph construction (number of quantiles, edge thresholds) is not investigated, leaving robustness open.
