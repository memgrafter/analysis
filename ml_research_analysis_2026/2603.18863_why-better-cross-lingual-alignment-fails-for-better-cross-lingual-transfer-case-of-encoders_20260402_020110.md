# Why Better Cross-Lingual Alignment Fails for Better Cross-Lingual Transfer: Case of Encoders

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.18863
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18863
- Reference count: 14
- Primary result: Improved cross-lingual embedding alignment via contrastive learning often fails to enhance zero-shot transfer due to gradient conflicts between alignment and task objectives, especially for token-level tasks.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates why better cross-lingual alignment, typically measured by reduced embedding distances, does not reliably improve zero-shot transfer performance on downstream NLP tasks. By analyzing XLM-R encoders fine-tuned for POS tagging and sentence classification after contrastive alignment, the authors find that alignment and task gradients are frequently orthogonal or negatively correlated, meaning the optimization objectives conflict. Token-level tasks suffer the most, as alignment objectives can actively hinder task learning. Moreover, reduced embedding distances are poor predictors of task performance gains. The effectiveness of alignment varies with language relatedness and task type, and linear-layer fine-tuning proves more robust than full-model fine-tuning.

## Method Summary
The study uses XLM-R base, aligning it on parallel sentence pairs from OPUS/MultiCCAligned for language pairs (de-en, en-kk, ja-kk, kk-ko) via a modified HiCTL loss combining sentence-level (S), word-level (W), and masked language modeling (M) objectives in seven configurations. After alignment training (1 epoch, ~6000 steps), checkpoints are fine-tuned on English POS tagging or sentence classification either with full-model updates or linear-only (frozen backbone). Zero-shot evaluation is performed on nine target languages. Analyses compute changes in accuracy, layer-wise embedding distances (cosine similarity on MUSE/FLORES), and cosine similarity between alignment and task gradients.

## Key Results
- Alignment and task gradients are often orthogonal or negatively correlated, particularly for POS tagging, indicating objective conflict.
- Reductions in cross-lingual embedding distances do not correlate with downstream performance improvements; better alignment often coincides with worse task accuracy.
- Linear-layer fine-tuning is more robust to alignment than full fine-tuning, reducing negative transfer.
- Sentence-level tasks show more positive gradient alignment than token-level tasks, benefiting more from alignment.
- The benefit of alignment depends on language typology and the specific loss combination (e.g., sentence loss helps sentence tasks; word loss often harms).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Better cross-lingual alignment can harm downstream task performance because the optimization objectives for alignment and the task often conflict, producing orthogonal or negatively correlated gradients.
- **Mechanism:** Contrastive alignment objectives and task-specific objectives update model parameters in different directions; when gradients are orthogonal, optimizing one does not help the other; when negative, they actively work against each other, especially for token-level tasks.
- **Core Assumption:** Parameter updates induced by different losses can be meaningfully compared via gradient similarity metrics (cosine similarity).
- **Evidence:** The paper reports gradient cosine similarities near zero or negative for POS tagging (Table 3) and more positive values for sentence classification. Abstract: "alignment and task gradients are often close to orthogonal, indicating that optimizing one objective may contribute little to optimizing the other."
- **Break Condition:** If gradient similarity between alignment and task objectives becomes strongly positive (>0.5), conflict diminishes and alignment may benefit the task; this is task- and configuration-dependent.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Reductions in cross-lingual embedding distances are unreliable predictors of downstream performance improvements.
- **Mechanism:** Alignment objectives reduce distances in certain layers (e.g., layer 8, language-agnostic), but these representational changes do not necessarily preserve or enhance the linguistic features (e.g., syntax for POS) critical for the task. The model's representational space is altered in ways orthogonal to task needs.
- **Core Assumption:** Token- and sentence-level cosine distances are valid proxies for "alignment quality" and that this quality should correlate with task performance if alignment is beneficial.
- **Evidence:** Figure 1 shows aligned models have reduced distances in layer 8 but task performance often degrades (e.g., POS with S0W1M0). Token-level distances in fine-tuned models increase in lower layers but shrink in middle/upper layers; aligned models show reduced distances mid-model but increases in final layer, indicating complex, non-uniform changes.
- **Break Condition:** If a specific alignment loss configuration (e.g., including sentence-level loss for sentence classification) produces both reduced distances and performance gains, the metric could be predictive for that task-loss pair; not universal.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Effectiveness of cross-lingual transfer after alignment depends on linguistic relationship between alignment languages and target language, as well as task type.
- **Mechanism:** Alignment on typologically similar or shared-script language pairs creates a representational space more compatible with the linguistic structure of related target languages. For token-level tasks requiring fine-grained lexical/syntactic mapping, alignment on a distant language pair provides less useful scaffolding.
- **Core Assumption:** Linguistic relatedness (shared script, language family) influences degree to which aligned representational space transfers to a new language.
- **Evidence:** Abstract: "downstream benefits from alignment vary substantially across languages and task types." Section 5.1 tables show grouped results: aligned languages with English sometimes show smaller gains/larger losses than aligned pairs without English. Sentence classification linear fine-tuning shows larger gains for aligned pairs (e.g., de-en, en-kk). These patterns illustrate task-type and language-dependency.
- **Break Condition:** When alignment languages are typologically close to target language and task representation level matches alignment level (e.g., sentence-level alignment + sentence classification), transfer is more likely to succeed.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Cross-Lingual Alignment (Weak vs. Strong)**
  - **Why needed:** The paper operationalizes alignment as a weak correlation between semantic similarity and embedding similarity, not strict equivalence. This definition underpins all metric interpretations (cosine distance).
  - **Quick Check:** Does a reduction in average cosine distance between translated sentence pairs necessarily imply better downstream task performance on a token-level task? Why or why not based on the paper's findings? *Answer: No, because alignment may alter representations in ways that are orthogonal to task-specific features; the paper shows decoupling between distance reduction and POS performance.*

- **Concept: Gradient Conflict in Multi-Objective Learning**
  - **Why needed:** The core causal mechanism is orthogonality/negativity of alignment and task gradients.
  - **Quick Check:** If the gradient cosine similarity between two losses is -0.1, what does that imply about simultaneously optimizing them? *Answer: It indicates negative correlation; gradient steps oppose each other, making simultaneous optimization difficult.*

- **Concept: Zero-Shot Cross-Lingual Transfer**
  - **Why needed:** The paper's evaluation is zero-shot transfer; alignment is expected to improve it.
  - **Quick Check:** In this context, what does "zero-shot" mean for the target language (e.g., Kazakh when fine-tuned on English POS), and why is alignment expected to improve it? *Answer: The model is trained only on source language (English) and evaluated on target language without any target-language training data. Alignment is expected to make representations across languages more similar, enabling the source-trained model to generalize to the target.*

- **Concept: Contrastive Learning for Alignment**
  - **Why needed:** The alignment objective uses contrastive losses (InfoNCE) at sentence and word levels.
  - **Quick Check:** What is the effect of a word-level contrastive loss (L_W) on token representations, based on the paper's results? *Answer: L_W often harms performance and increases gradient conflict; it is less beneficial than sentence-level loss for most tasks.*

- **Concept: Layer-wise Analysis of Representations**
  - **Why needed:** The paper examines distances at different layers (4, 8, 12) to understand where alignment happens.
  - **Quick Check:** Which layer showed the most consistent reduction in cross-lingual distances after alignment, and why is that significant? *Answer: Layer 8, associated with language-agnostic processing; its alignment suggests mid-level representations become more language-independent.*

- **Concept: Full vs. Linear-Only Fine-Tuning**
  - **Why needed:** A key practical finding is that linear-only fine-tuning is more robust to alignment.
  - **Quick Check:** Why might linear-only fine-tuning suffer less from gradient conflict compared to full fine-tuning? *Answer: Because only the classification head is updated; the backbone remains fixed, preserving the alignment and avoiding conflict-induced overwriting.*

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Phase I (Alignment Tuning): XLM-R base -> Modified HiCTL loss (combinations of Sentence S, Word W, MLM M) -> Aligned checkpoint (for a given language pair).  
Phase II (Task Fine-tuning): Aligned checkpoint -> Fine-tuning mode (full-model or linear-only) + Task head (linear layer) -> Fine-tuned model.  
Phase III (Zero-shot Evaluation): Fine-tuned model -> Evaluation on multiple target languages -> Metrics (accuracy, distances).  
Analysis Layer: Parallel computation of embedding distances (MUSE/FLORES), gradient cosine similarity (between alignment and task losses), and retrieval recall.

### Critical Path
1. Choose language pairs (L_ALIGN) and loss configurations (7 combos of S, W, M).
2. Run Phase I alignment training for each (pair, config) for 1 epoch (~6000 steps, batch 16, lr 1e-5). Save checkpoints at 3k and 6k steps.
3. For each checkpoint, run Phase II fine-tuning on English downstream tasks (POS tagging, sentence classification) in both full and linear-only modes (15 epochs, hyperparams from Tables 5/6).
4. For each fine-tuned model, run Phase III zero-shot evaluation on all 9 target languages.
5. For each (pair, config, fine-tune mode, task), compute: (a) Δ accuracy vs. XLM-R base, (b) Δ embedding distances at layers 4, 8, 12, (c) gradient cosine similarity between alignment loss and task loss.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Full vs. Linear-only fine-tuning:** Full updates risk overwriting alignment with task-specific "catastrophic forgetting" and amplify gradient conflict; linear-only is more robust but limits expressivity.
- **Loss Configuration:** Adding S (sentence loss) helps sentence tasks; adding W (word loss) often harms all tasks (S0W1M0 detrimental). Including MLM (M) sometimes mitigates conflict.
- **Layer Focus:** Focusing only on final layer (12) misses critical mid-model (layer 8) alignment changes; the 8th layer is key for language-neutral representations.

### Failure Signatures
- **Primary:** Negative or near-zero gradient cosine similarity between alignment and task objectives (Table 3), especially for POS tagging.
- **Secondary:** Significant drop in zero-shot accuracy for non-aligned or distant languages despite improved embedding distances (Table 1, e.g., en-kk POS full fine-tuning).
- **Tertiary:** Word-loss-only (S0W1M0) configuration consistently degrades distances and performance.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Replicate Gradient Conflict Analysis:** For a new language pair (e.g., fr-zh) and task (e.g., NER), compute gradient cosine similarity between a chosen alignment loss (e.g., S1W0M0) and the task loss during first 100 fine-tuning steps. Expect near-zero/negative values for token-level task.
2. **Linear vs. Full Fine-tuning A/B Test:** Take an aligned checkpoint (e.g., XLM-R ja-kk with S1W1M0). Fine-tune for POS tagging in full and linear-only modes. Compare zero-shot accuracy on Korean (kk) and French (fr). Expect linear-only to show more stable/positive Δ for kk (aligned) and potentially less degradation for fr (non-aligned).
3. **Layer-wise Distance vs. Performance Correlation:** For all aligned checkpoints, compute average Δ cosine distance at layer 8 (token and sentence) on FLORES. Plot against corresponding Δ accuracy on sentence classification. Expect weak/no correlation (R² < 0.3), confirming Mechanism 2.

## Limitations
- Exact parallel corpus sources per language pair (OPUS vs. MultiCCAligned) unspecified, risking variability in alignment quality.
- Ambiguity in the word-level contrastive loss (L_W) implementation details (query formulation, negative sampling) critically affects gradient dynamics.
- Checkpoint selection (3k vs. 6k steps) for final analysis is unclear, potentially missing optimal alignment states; the specific SIB-200 dataset variant for sentence classification is not itemized.

## Confidence
- **High:** Gradient conflict (orthogonal/negative similarity) as core mechanism for token-level tasks—directly measured and consistently reported.
- **Medium:** Decoupling of embedding distance reduction and task performance—evident in layer-wise analyses but may not generalize beyond tested tasks/layers.
- **Medium-Low:** Claims about linguistic relationship dependencies—supported by grouped results but limited to 4 language pairs and specific tasks.

## Next Checks
1. Resolve L_W ambiguity: Systematically vary query representation (mean-pooled sentence vs. token-wise) and negative sampling strategies (in-batch vs. vocab-based) during alignment. Re-measure gradient cosine similarity with POS tagging to test robustness of conflict finding.
2. Temporal gradient analysis: For a canonical pair (e.g., de-en), compute alignment-task gradient similarity at every 500 steps during Phase I training. Correlate with downstream performance at each saved checkpoint to identify optimal alignment stage and validate if conflict persists throughout.
3. Expand language typology: Replicate core experiments for 10+ diverse language pairs (e.g., covering Indo-European, Semitic, Dravidian families) with both POS and NER tasks. Test whether language-family proximity predicts reduction in gradient conflict and improvement in zero-shot transfer.
