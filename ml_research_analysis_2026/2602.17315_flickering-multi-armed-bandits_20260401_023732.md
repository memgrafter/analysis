# Flickering Multi-Armed Bandits

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.17315
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17315
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A two-phase algorithm using a lazy random walk achieves high-probability and expected sublinear regret $O(n \log(nT)/\Delta_{\min}^2)$ with additive costs for mixing, navigation, and burn-in, and its exploration cost matches an information-theoretic lower bound up to logarithmic factors.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the Flickering Multi-Armed Bandit (FMAB) problem, where the set of available actions (arms) changes each round and depends on the previously chosen action. The availability is modeled by a random graph process—either i.i.d. Erdős–Rényi or Edge-Markovian—restricting the learner to local moves. The proposed solution is a two-phase algorithm: first, a lazy random walk explores uniformly for a predetermined duration to sample all arms; second, the same walk navigates to the empirically best arm and then commits. The approach yields sublinear regret guarantees for both graph models, with exploration cost confirmed as near-optimal.

## Method Summary
The algorithm operates in two distinct phases. During exploration, a lazy random walk (which with probability 1/2 stays put and with probability 1/2 moves uniformly to a neighbor in the current availability graph) is run for a carefully chosen number of steps $T_0$. This ensures each arm is visited sufficiently many times to estimate its mean reward accurately. After exploration, the arm with the highest empirical mean is selected as the target. In the exploitation phase, the lazy walk continues until it hits the target arm, at which point the learner commits to that arm for all remaining rounds. The walk uses only local neighborhood information, making it suitable for constrained environments where global knowledge is unavailable. The analysis decomposes total regret into exploration error, navigation time, and (for Edge-Markovian graphs) a burn-in cost, with $T_0$ scaled to graph connectivity parameters like spectral gap or effective number of arms.

## Key Results
- Expected cumulative regret is $O(n \log(nT)/\Delta_{\min}^2)$ with high probability, where $n$ is the number of arms, $T$ the horizon, and $\Delta_{\min}$ the smallest reward gap.
- Additive costs include: $O(n \log n)$ for mixing, $O(\log T/p)$ for navigation in Erdős–Rényi graphs, and $O(1/(\alpha+\beta) + n)$ burn-in for Edge-Markovian graphs (with $\alpha,\beta$ edge flip rates).
- The exploration cost is near-optimal, matching an information-theoretic lower bound up to logarithmic factors.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Uniform Exploration via Lazy Random Walk
The lazy random walk’s symmetric transition kernel (in dense homogeneous graphs) and constant spectral gap ensure rapid mixing ($O(\log n)$ time). During a predetermined exploration length $T_0$, each arm receives visits proportional to its stationary probability $\pi(a)$, which is roughly $1/n$ in well-connected graphs. Concentration guarantees (e.g., on visitation counts) ensure every arm gets at least $s_0 = O(\log(n/\delta)/\Delta_{\min}^2)$ samples with high probability, enabling accurate mean estimation. This fails if the graph is disconnected or the spectral gap vanishes (e.g., sparse Erdős–Rényi with $np \to 0$), or in Edge-Markovian graphs if the edge-flip rate $\beta$ is too large ($\beta = \Omega(1/n)$), causing the walk’s distribution to drift too far from stationarity.

### Near-Optimal Exploration Cost
The information-theoretic lower bound for best-arm identification under local moves requires $\Omega((n-1) \mathrm{kl}(\delta,1-\delta)/\Delta_{\min}^2)$ samples. The lazy walk’s stationary mass on the least-visited arm is $\Omega(1/n)$ under typical conditions (connected graph, constant spectral gap, $\min$-degree $\Omega(\log(nT))$). Setting $T_0 = \Theta(n \log(n/\delta)/\Delta_{\min}^2)$ makes the smallest sample count match the lower bound up to constants and logs. If the graph is heterogeneous with highly skewed degrees, the effective number of arms $n_{\text{eff}}$ may exceed $n$, inflating the required $T_0$.

### Handling Edge-Markovian Dynamics via Burn-In and Stickiness
For Edge-Markovian graphs, the algorithm first undergoes a burn-in phase of length $O(\log(nT/\delta)/(\alpha+\beta))$ to allow the graph process to mix to its stationary Erdős–Rényi distribution with edge probability $p_\infty = \alpha/(\alpha+\beta)$. After burn-in, concentration ensures all degrees are near $(n-1)p_\infty$ and the lazy walk has a uniform spectral gap $\gamma_0 = \Theta(1)$. The stationary distribution drift per step is bounded by $\varepsilon_{\max} = O(\zeta_0/p_\infty)$, where $\zeta_0$ is the fraction of flipped edges. If $\varepsilon_{\max}/\gamma_0 < \pi_0/2$ (equivalent to $\zeta_0 = O(p_\infty/n)$ and thus $\beta = O(1/n)$), the effective visitation probability $\pi_{\text{eff}} = \Omega(1/n)$ remains positive, enabling uniform coverage during exploration. The total regret then adds the burn-in cost and still achieves sublinear growth. If $\beta$ is larger, the walk cannot track the moving stationary law, leading to poor coverage and possible misidentification.

## Foundational Learning

### Multi-armed bandits
**Why needed here:** FMAB extends the classic MAB framework by adding dynamic, local constraints on action availability. Understanding the exploration–exploitation trade-off and regret definitions is essential.
**Quick check:** In standard stochastic MAB with $n$ arms and horizon $T$, what is the minimax regret order? (Answer: $\Omega(\sqrt{nT})$.)

### Random walk on graphs
**Why needed here:** The algorithm’s core operation is a lazy random walk on a time-varying graph. Its mixing time, spectral gap, and stationary distribution determine coverage uniformity and navigation efficiency.
**Quick check:** For a lazy random walk on an undirected, connected, non-bipartite graph, what is the stationary distribution in terms of node degrees? (Answer: $\pi(v) \propto \deg(v)$.)

### Concentration inequalities (Hoeffding, Chernoff, Freedman)
**Why needed here:** The analysis relies on these to bound deviations of empirical means, visitation counts, and graph concentration events, ensuring high-probability guarantees.
**Quick check:** What is the typical form of Hoeffding’s bound for bounded i.i.d. variables? How does Freedman’s inequality differ from Azuma’s? (Answer: Hoeffding: $\Pr(|\bar{X}-\mathbb{E}\bar{X}| \ge t) \le 2\exp(-2nt^2/(b-a)^2)$. Freedman handles martingales with bounded differences and variance, giving exponential decay with variance term.)

### Information-theoretic lower bounds
**Why needed here:** To prove the exploration cost is near-optimal, the paper derives a lower bound using KL-divergence and the local-move constraint, a standard tool for assessing optimality in bandits.
**Quick check:** For best-arm identification in MAB, how does the lower bound on sample complexity scale with $n$ and $\Delta_{\min}$? (Answer: $\Omega((n-1)/\Delta_{\min}^2)$ for equal gaps up to constants.)

### Markov chains and stationary distributions
**Why needed here:** The lazy walk is a reversible Markov chain; in the Edge-Markovian case the kernel evolves. Properties like spectral gap and total variation contraction are used to track how the walk’s distribution follows a drifting stationary law.
**Quick check:** What is the relationship between spectral gap $\gamma$ and mixing time $t_{\text{mix}}$ for a reversible Markov chain? (Answer: $t_{\text{mix}} = O(1/\gamma \log \pi_{\min}^{-1})$ up to constants.)

### Erdős–Rényi and Edge-Markovian graph processes
**Why needed here:** These model how action availability changes over time. Understanding their mixing properties (e.g., edge flip spectral gap $\alpha+\beta$) is crucial for burn-in and stickiness conditions.
**Quick check:** For the Edge-Markovian process, what is the stationary edge existence probability $p_\infty$ in terms of creation rate $\alpha$ and deletion rate $\beta$? (Answer: $p_\infty = \alpha/(\alpha+\beta)$.)

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map:**  
  Graph process (ER or Edge-Markovian) -> Lazy random walk (exploration phase, duration $T_0$) -> Empirical mean estimation $\hat{\mu}(a)$ -> Arm selection $\hat{a}^*$ -> Lazy random walk (navigation until hitting $\hat{a}^*$) -> Commitment to $\hat{a}^*$.

- **Critical path:**  
  1. Initialize: set $t=1$, arbitrary starting arm $a_0$.  
  2. Exploration loop (t=1 to T₀): observe graph Gₜ, sample aₜ ~ Uniform(Lₜ(aₜ₋₁)), receive reward rₜ, update visitation count ϕₜ(a) and cumulative reward Sₜ(a).  
  3. Identification: compute $\hat{\mu}(a) = S_{T_0}(a)/\phi_{T_0}(a)$ (use 0 if unvisited), set $\hat{a}^* = \arg\max_a \hat{\mu}(a)$.  
  4. Exploitation loop (t=T₀+1 to T): observe Gₜ; if aₜ₋₁ ≠ $\hat{a}^*$, continue lazy walk; else set aₜ = $\hat{a}^*$.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - **T₰ sizing:** Too small → insufficient samples → misidentification; too large → wasted exploration. The choice balances $\Delta_{\min}$, graph spectral gap, and desired confidence.  
  - **Walk choice:** Lazy random walk is simple and local but may have high navigation cost in sparse graphs; more efficient navigation (e.g., shortest path) is impossible without global knowledge.  
  - **Burn-in for Edge-Markovian:** If omitted, initial samples are non-stationary and may bias estimates; adding explicit burn-in mitigates this at the cost of additive regret.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - **Misidentification:** Some arms receive fewer than $s_0$ samples due to small stationary probability (e.g., low-degree nodes in heterogeneous graphs) or too short $T_0$.  
  - **Navigation failure:** The lazy walk fails to hit $\hat{a}^*$ within reasonable time if the graph is disconnected or $\hat{a}^*$ lies in a poorly connected component.  
  - **Burn-in under-coverage:** In Edge-Markovian case, if $\beta$ is too large or $\alpha+\beta$ too small, the post-burn-in walk’s distribution may not be uniform, leading to uneven coverage.  
  - **Parameter mismatch:** Using $n$ instead of effective $n_{\text{eff}}$ in $T_0$ leads to under-exploration in graphs with skewed degree distributions.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Regret scaling:** Vary $n$ and $\Delta_{\min}$ for fixed $T$ in homogeneous Erdős–Rényi graphs (fixed $p$). Plot $R(T)/T$ vs. $T$ to confirm sublinear convergence and $O(n \log(nT)/\Delta_{\min}^2)$ trend.  
  2. **Edge-Markovian burn-in and stickiness:** Test different $(\alpha,\beta)$ pairs with fixed $p_\infty$. Vary $\beta$ while keeping $p_\infty$ constant, measure identification time and regret; expect blow-up when $\beta$ exceeds $\approx 1/n$. Also measure sensitivity of regret to $\alpha+\beta$ (burn-in cost).  
  3. **Isolated navigation cost:** Set $T_0$ large enough for near-perfect identification, then measure distribution of navigation time $T_{\text{nav}}$ as function of edge probability $p$ in Erdős–Rényi graphs. Validate the $O(1/p)$ tail and $O(n \log n)$ typical cost.

## Limitations
- Requires the (expected) availability graph to be connected with a constant spectral gap; fails in sparse or disconnected graphs.
- For Edge-Markovian graphs, the success condition $\beta \le O(1/n)$ (stickiness) is restrictive; faster edge flips break uniform coverage.
- Navigation cost $O(1/p)$ in sparse Erdős–Rényi graphs can be large, potentially making the algorithm impractical for very sparse regimes.
