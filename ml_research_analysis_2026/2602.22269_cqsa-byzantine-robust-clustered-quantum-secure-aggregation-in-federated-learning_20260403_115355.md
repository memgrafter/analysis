# CQSA: Byzantine-robust Clustered Quantum Secure Aggregation in Federated Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.22269  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22269  
- **Reference count**: 25  
- **Primary result**: CQSA partitions clients into small random clusters to use high-fidelity GHZ states and applies Byzantine-robust verification on cluster aggregates, significantly improving state fidelity and ensuring stable model convergence under depolarizing noise.

## Executive Summary
CQSA introduces a clustered approach to quantum secure aggregation (QSA) in federated learning, simultaneously addressing the exponential decay of GHZ state fidelity with increasing qubits and the lack of Byzantine robustness in existing QSA schemes. Instead of a single global GHZ state, clients are divided into small random clusters, each performing local quantum aggregation with a high-fidelity small GHZ state. The server then aggregates the cluster-level phase sums using classical Byzantine-robust statistics (e.g., Euclidean distance, cosine similarity) to detect and reject malicious clusters. Simulations under a depolarizing noise model show that CQSA achieves substantially higher state fidelity than global QSA, especially for cluster sizes around *k*=5, and maintains stable convergence even in the presence of hardware noise. The modular design localizes errors and aligns with NISQ-era constraints.

## Method Summary
CQSA comprises three phases. First, clients compute local updates and report their maximum weight magnitude to the server, which determines a global scaling factor *S*. Second, for each randomly formed cluster, the server prepares a *k*-qubit GHZ state and distributes it. Each client applies a phase rotation proportional to its scaled update (*θ* = *S·w*) to its qubit and returns it. The server decodes the aggregate phase sum *Θ*<sub>j</sub> for each cluster. Third, the server treats the *M* cluster aggregates as classical vectors and applies a Byzantine-robust aggregation function (e.g., Krum, Multi‑Krum, FLTrust, MESAS) to select or weight honest clusters. The verified aggregates are summed and decoded to produce the final global update. This structure reduces quantum resource demands, preserves fidelity, and enables statistical detection of malicious contributions.

## Key Results
- **Fidelity advantage**: CQSA’s total state fidelity scales as (*F*<sub>cluster</sub>)<sup>*N/k*</sup>, which is exponentially higher than global QSA’s (*1−ε*)<sup>*N*</sup> for small *k* under depolarizing noise.
- **Byzantine resilience**: Statistical verification at the cluster level identifies and discards malicious clusters, protecting convergence even when some clients are compromised.
- **Noise tolerance**: Simulations confirm CQSA maintains high fidelity across varying noise levels and client counts, with error localization suitable for NISQ devices.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Clustered GHZ States for Fidelity Preservation
- **Claim**: Partitioning clients into small random clusters mitigates exponential fidelity decay in large‑scale GHZ states.
- **Mechanism**: Instead of one global *N*-qubit GHZ state, CQSA creates *M* independent *k*-qubit GHZ states. Overall *F*<sub>total</sub> ≈ (*F*<sub>cluster</sub>)<sup>*N/k*</sup> is exponentially higher than *F*<sub>*N*</sub> ≈ (*1−ε*)<sup>*N*</sup> under depolarizing noise.
- **Core assumption**: Decoherence (per‑gate depolarizing noise) dominates; cluster‑level fidelity scales independently.
- **Evidence**: Abstract and Section 4 derive the scaling; Figures 2 & 3 in Section 5 show CQSA (solid) outperforms global QSA (dashed).
- **Break condition**: If even small clusters cannot achieve sufficient fidelity due to high error rate *ε* or coherence‑time overflow for given *k*.

### Mechanism 2: Phase Encoding Preserves Geometric Properties for Verification
- **Claim**: Linear phase encoding (*θ* = *S·w*) preserves Euclidean norms and cosine similarities, enabling Byzantine‑robust statistics on cluster aggregates.
- **Mechanism**: Scaling factor *S* = π/(*k·w*<sub>max</sub>) ensures encoded phases stay within [−π, π] to avoid wrapping. This transformation yields ∥*θ*∥ = *S*·∥*w*∥ and CosSim(*θ*<sub>*a*</sub>, *θ*<sub>*b*</sub>) = CosSim(*w*<sub>*a*</sub>, *w*<sub>*b*</sub>). Thus, statistical measures on {*Θ*<sub>*j*</sub>} reflect relationships between original weight vectors.
- **Core assumption**: Global bound *w*<sub>max</sub> is accurate and broadcast; no individual update exceeds it; encoding is perfectly linear and synchronized.
- **Evidence**: Section 4 contains derivations for proportionality and invariance; abstract mentions Euclidean/cosine verification; Section 4 notes many schemes use these statistics.
- **Break condition**: If a malicious client submits *w*<sub>*i*</sub> with |*w*<sub>*i*</sub>| > *w*<sub>max</sub>, *S* becomes invalid, causing phase wrapping and distorting geometry.

### Mechanism 3: Inter‑Cluster Verification for Byzantine Detection
- **Claim**: Multiple independent cluster aggregates allow the server to apply classical Byzantine‑robust schemes to detect malicious clusters.
- **Mechanism**: The server collects {*Θ*<sub>1</sub>, …, *Θ*<sub>*M*</sub>}. Since each *Θ*<sub>*j*</sub> encodes a sum of its members’ updates, statistical relationships between aggregates (e.g., pairwise Euclidean distances, cosine similarity to a reference) reveal clusters containing poisoned updates. Outlier clusters are discarded before forming the final sum.
- **Core assumption**: More than (*M* − *f*) clusters are honest (majority); clustering is random and dynamic per round, limiting adversary control.
- **Evidence**: Abstract states server applies Byzantine‑robust verification; Section 4 discusses outlier rejection and tailoring to schemes like Krum, Multi‑Krum, FLTrust, MESAS; Section 4 also argues random stochastic cluster formation prevents collusion.
- **Break condition**: If adversary corrupts enough clients to form ≥ ⌊*M*/2⌋ entirely malicious clusters, inter‑cluster statistics are skewed, allowing malicious clusters to be selected or honest ones rejected.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
1. **Server**: Generates/distributes *k*-qubit GHZ states per cluster, performs intra‑cluster decoding, computes *w*<sub>max</sub> and scaling *S*, conducts inter‑cluster Byzantine verification, forms final global update.  
2. **Client**: Computes local gradient, sends *w*<sub>max</sub> to server, receives *S*, encodes *θ* = *S·w* via Rz on its GHZ qubit, returns qubit.  
3. **Quantum Channels**: For GHZ state distribution (server → clusters) and qubit return (clusters → server).  
4. **Classical Channels**: For *w*<sub>max</sub> broadcast, coordination, and optional verification metadata.

### Critical Path
1. Server collects *w*<sub>max</sub> from selected clients → computes global *w*<sub>max</sub> & *S* → broadcasts *S*.  
2. For each cluster: Server prepares *k*-qubit GHZ state → distributes to cluster members → waits for encoded qubits → decodes to get *Θ*<sub>*j*</sub>.  
3. After all *M* clusters decoded, server applies chosen Byzantine‑robust function on {*Θ*<sub>*j*</sub>} → selects/weights cluster aggregates → sums verified *Θ*<sub>*j*</sub> → decodes to obtain aggregate update.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Cluster size *k***: Smaller *k* → higher fidelity, lower coherence‑time need, but lower privacy threshold (fewer parties per secret) and more clusters *M*, increasing classical coordination overhead. Paper suggests *k*=5 as a sweet spot.  
- **Verification scheme**: Stronger schemes (e.g., MESAS) may discard more clusters, harming utility if honest clusters are misidentified. Weaker schemes may allow malicious clusters through. Trade‑off between robustness and convergence guarantee.  
- **Randomization frequency**: Re‑clustering every round maximizes security but adds classical overhead. Less frequent reduces cost but allows targeted attacks.

### Failure Signatures
- **High systematic error in final aggregate**: Likely phase‑wrapping due to *S* misconfiguration (*w*<sub>max</sub> inaccurate) or insufficient decoding precision.  
- **Server rejects many clusters**: Could indicate high hardware noise (fidelity too low, aggregates divergent), overly aggressive Byzantine threshold, or a coordinated poisoning attack across multiple clusters.  
- **Client dropout within a cluster**: If a client fails to return its qubit, the cluster’s GHZ state is broken and that cluster’s aggregate is lost; protocol tolerates up to one dropout per cluster (e.g., *k*=5 expects one loss).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How can CQSA detect and isolate malicious clients within a single quantum cluster while preserving the information‑theoretic privacy of honest participants?  
- **Basis in paper**: Section 5 (Discussion) explicitly states: “malicious detection is performed at the level of clusters, and thus, the protocol cannot directly identify or isolate malicious participants within a cluster.”  
- **Why unresolved**: Intra‑cluster GHZ aggregation only reveals the sum; identifying a malicious contributor would require breaking quantum privacy or adding trusted hardware.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Design a verifiable intra‑cluster aggregation scheme (e.g., via auxiliary quantum commitments) that allows detection without revealing individual updates, and prove its security and effectiveness in simulations.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: What is the optimal cluster size for CQSA under heterogeneous NISQ hardware to maximize fidelity, Byzantine resilience, and FL convergence stability?  
- **Basis in paper**: Conclusion lists “optimizing cluster sizing under heterogeneous hardware” as future work.  
- **Why unresolved**: Current analysis assumes uniform depolarizing noise and fixed size; real hardware has varying coherence times, gate errors, and client dynamics that affect trade‑offs.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Develop a theoretical model incorporating per‑client error rates and update magnitudes; validate via simulations with diverse noise profiles and real quantum hardware benchmarks to derive adaptive sizing strategies.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: How does the computational and communication overhead of CQSA compare to classical secure aggregation protocols (e.g., HE, MPC) in realistic FL deployments?  
- **Basis in paper**: Conclusion mentions “comparing computational overload between the proposed CQSA and present classical secure aggregation protocols” as future work.  
- **Why unresolved**: The paper lacks empirical overhead metrics; quantum operation costs (GHZ generation, measurement) are hardware‑dependent and not quantified against classical baselines.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Implement CQSA and classical SA on a common FL benchmark (e.g., MNIST), run on hybrid quantum‑classical simulators with realistic latency models, and compare wall‑clock time, energy, and bandwidth.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Can Byzantine‑robust statistical verification (e.g., Euclidean distance, cosine similarity) reliably detect malicious clusters when quantum noise distorts the aggregate phase distributions?  
- **Basis in paper**: The paper applies classical metrics to cluster aggregates but does not analyze robustness under quantum noise; simulation focuses on fidelity, not attack detection accuracy. (Inferred)  
- **Why unresolved**: Noise may cause honest clusters to appear as outliers or mask malicious ones; the claim that geometric properties are preserved may not hold under high noise.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Simulate adaptive Byzantine clients crafting poisoned updates that exploit noise patterns, measure false positive/negative rates under varying noise levels; conduct formal analysis of metric distributions under depolarizing channels.

### Open Question 5
- **Question**: How effective is random cluster reshuffling in preventing collusion attacks where Byzantine clients strategically target specific clusters over multiple rounds?  
- **Basis in paper**: Paper argues randomization mitigates collusion (Page 5) but lacks formal security analysis; threat model does not cover adaptive colluders coordinating across rounds. (Inferred)  
- **Why unresolved**: Randomization may fail if adversaries influence cluster assignment (e.g., via dropout) or constitute a large fraction; security guarantees are informal.  
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Game‑theoretic modeling of adaptive adversaries with bounded Byzantine fraction, proving random reshuffling limits cumulative damage; simulations with coordinated Byzantine strategies over many FL rounds.

## Limitations
- **Fidelity model oversimplification**: The analysis assumes only depolarizing noise and uses an extrapolation for large GHZ states without validation; real NISQ devices also suffer T1/T2 decay, crosstalk, and measurement errors that could erode the predicted gains.
- **Geometric invariance vulnerability**: The linear phase encoding requires an accurate global bound *w*<sub>max</sub>. Heterogeneous gradient magnitudes may cause phase wrapping (*θ* ∉ [−π, π]), breaking Euclidean and cosine similarity preservation.
- **Byzantine detection unverified**: The inter‑cluster robustness relies on classical statistical schemes without empirical evaluation under adaptive attacks; random clustering might be predictable, and the majority‑honest‑cluster assumption may fail in practice.

## Confidence
- **Clustered GHZ states mitigate fidelity decay**: Medium  
- **Linear phase encoding preserves Euclidean and cosine metrics**: Medium  
- **Inter‑cluster statistics detect malicious clusters**: Low
