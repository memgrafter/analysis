# Beyond Accuracy: A Decision-Theoretic Framework for Allocation-Aware Healthcare AI

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.06161
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06161
- Reference count: 4
- Primary result: Allocation-aware AI policies, which explicitly optimize for constrained utility, outperform conventional risk-threshold policies by 18–25% in realized utility, even with identical predictive accuracy.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles the "allocation gap" in healthcare AI: despite improvements in predictive accuracy, patient outcomes often do not improve due to resource scarcity. It redefines AI as decision infrastructure that estimates patient utilities within a constrained stochastic allocation framework, rather than as an autonomous decision-maker. By formulating care delivery as an integer program maximizing total expected utility subject to capacity limits, the authors show that allocation-aware policies (e.g., greedy marginal utility selection) significantly outperform conventional risk-threshold rules. A synthetic triage simulation with fixed predictive accuracy (AUROC 0.85) demonstrates an 18–25% gain in realized utility, with the advantage growing as constraints tighten. This work provides a principled foundation for evaluating healthcare AI beyond task-level metrics, emphasizing the importance of aligned allocation logic under scarcity.

## Method Summary
The methodology combines theoretical formulation, analytical reasoning, and a synthetic simulation. Theoretically, the authors model healthcare allocation as a constrained integer program: maximize ∑ᵢ uᵢⱼ xᵢⱼ over binary allocation variables xᵢⱼ, subject to ∑ᵢ xᵢⱼ ≤ Cⱼ for each resource j. AI models supply estimates ûᵢⱼ of expected utility for each patient-resource pair. The Expected Value of Information (EVI) is defined to quantify the benefit of improved utility estimation under scarcity. For empirical evaluation, they construct a synthetic triage environment where patients arrive with severity and resource needs, and three policies are compared: (1) risk-threshold (treat if predicted risk exceeds a threshold), (2) allocation-aware greedy (select highest marginal utility per resource until capacity full), and (3) random. Realized utility incorporates severity-weighted outcomes and penalties for untreated patients. Capacity constraints are varied to examine performance across different scarcity levels.

## Key Results
- Allocation-aware policies achieve 18–25% higher realized utility than risk-threshold policies, even with identical predictive accuracy (AUROC 0.85).
- The relative advantage of allocation-aware policies increases monotonically as the capacity-to-demand ratio decreases (tighter constraints).
- The Expected Value of Information (EVI) is non-trivial and positively correlated with constraint tightness, indicating that modest predictive improvements yield large system gains under scarcity.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Allocation Gap and Constrained Optimization
**Claim:** The primary value of AI in healthcare arises from improving allocation decisions under binding resource scarcity, not from isolated predictive accuracy gains.  
**Mechanism:** By formalizing care delivery as a constrained integer program that maximizes the sum of expected utilities subject to capacity limits, the framework makes explicit that predictions only matter insofar as they alter the ranking of marginal utilities under competition for scarce resources.  
**Core assumption:** Resource constraints are routinely binding, creating zero-sum competition; allocation decisions are irreversible and state-dependent.  
**Evidence:** The paper presents the full optimization formulation (Eq. 1) and demonstrates in a synthetic triage simulation that allocation-aware policies outperform risk-threshold policies despite identical predictive accuracy.  
**Break condition:** If capacity constraints are non-binding (abundant resources), the optimization collapses to independent patient decisions and the allocation advantage vanishes.

### Mechanism 2: Value of Information Under Scarcity
**Claim:** The Expected Value of Information (EVI) is non-trivial and positively correlated with constraint tightness; even modest predictive improvements can produce large system-level utility gains when resources are scarce.  
**Mechanism:** EVI = E[maxₐ E[u(a|x)]] – maxₐ E[u(a)] captures the value of refining utility estimates. Under scarcity, the inner maximization (selecting the best allocation) is highly sensitive to small changes in estimated utilities because the choice among competing patients hinges on subtle ranking differences.  
**Core assumption:** The allocation policy is optimal given the model's estimates, and utility estimates are imperfect and competitive.  
**Evidence:** The paper derives the EVI expression and explains theoretically that scarcity amplifies its value; the simulation shows that the performance gap between allocation-aware and risk-threshold policies widens as capacity declines.  
**Break condition:** When capacity is abundant relative to demand, EVI approaches zero because nearly all patients receive care regardless of prioritization.

### Mechanism 3: Dominance of Allocation-Aware Policies
**Claim:** Allocation-aware policies that solve the constrained optimization (e.g., greedy marginal utility selection) systematically dominate conventional risk-threshold policies under identical predictive accuracy, with the advantage increasing as constraints tighten.  
**Mechanism:** Risk-threshold policies rank patients by predicted risk alone and ignore severity-weighted marginal utility per resource and capacity constraints. Utility-aware policies explicitly consider scarcity and severity weights when selecting allocations.  
**Core assumption:** The predictive model's risk estimates are fixed and identical across policies; differences in realized utility stem solely from allocation logic.  
**Evidence:** The synthetic triage simulation, with fixed AUROC=0.85, shows that allocation-aware policies achieve 18–25% higher realized utility than risk-threshold policies. Figure 3 illustrates that the relative advantage increases monotonically as the capacity-to-demand ratio decreases.  
**Break condition:** If the predictive model's accuracy varies between policy comparisons (confounding estimation quality with allocation logic), the observed difference cannot be attributed purely to allocation awareness.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Constrained Stochastic Allocation**  
  *Why needed here:* Understands healthcare as a system of finite, competing resources under uncertainty—not a series of independent predictions.  
  *Quick check:* "In the integer program formulation (Eq. 1), what does a binding capacity constraint force the optimizer to do that a per-patient threshold rule does not?"

- **Concept: Expected Value of Information (EVI)**  
  *Why needed here:* Quantifies the marginal benefit of improved AI estimates specifically under scarcity; explains why accuracy gains matter more in constrained settings.  
  *Quick check:* "Why does EVI increase as capacity becomes scarcer, even if prediction error variance stays constant?"

- **Concept: Utility vs. Risk Prioritization**  
  *Why needed here:* Distinguishes estimating 'risk of adverse event' from estimating 'marginal benefit of allocating a specific resource to this patient'—the latter is allocation-relevant.  
  *Quick check:* "Give an example where a lower-risk patient might be prioritized over a higher-risk one under a utility-aware policy."

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map:**  
Feature extractor → Utility estimator → Allocation optimizer → Evaluation module  
(Constraint monitor provides real-time capacity to Allocation optimizer)

**Critical Path:**  
Real-time pipeline: Patient arrival → feature extraction → utility estimation → optimizer with current constraints → allocation decision → resource consumption update → repeat. For sequential decisions (MDP), also include belief state update and action-value evaluation.

**Design Tradeoffs:**  
- Optimality vs. latency: Greedy allocation is fast but suboptimal; full constrained MDP solving is optimal but may be intractable for large N.  
- Utility maximization vs. fairness: Raw utility maximization may disadvantage protected groups; requires explicit fairness constraints (e.g., demographic parity in allocation rates) which reduce total utility.  
- Model complexity vs. interpretability: Complex models may estimate ûᵢⱼ better but are harder to audit for allocation bias.

**Failure Signatures:**  
- Realised utility consistently below random allocation (optimizer failure).  
- Frequent capacity breaches (constraint monitor failing to feed accurate Cⱼ).  
- High variance in realised utility across similar demand patterns (instability in optimization under uncertainty).  
- Fairness metrics (e.g., allocation disparity) worsen despite utility gains.

**First 3 Experiments:**  
1. Replicate synthetic triage: Fix predictive accuracy (AUROC 0.85), implement risk-threshold and utility-aware policies, verify 18–25% utility gap and monotonic advantage with tighter constraints (Fig. 2, 3).  
2. Stress test optimizer: Introduce stochastic capacity fluctuations (Cⱼ varies by ±10%) and measure degradation in realised utility and capacity breach rate for greedy vs. more robust optimizers.  
3. Fairness-utility tradeoff: Add a constraint that allocation rate for a subgroup must exceed a threshold; plot Pareto frontier of total utility vs. subgroup fairness, demonstrating the required utility sacrifice.

## Limitations
- Synthetic simulation may not reflect real-world complexity, including heterogeneity, dynamic arrivals, and capacity errors.
- The analysis assumes fixed predictive accuracy across policies; in practice, utility model quality may vary and confound results.
- The framework does not incorporate fairness constraints, risking exacerbated disparities, and the greedy optimizer may be suboptimal for large-scale or sequential allocation problems.

## Confidence
- Allocation gap & value: Medium
- EVI significance: Low
- Policy superiority: Medium
