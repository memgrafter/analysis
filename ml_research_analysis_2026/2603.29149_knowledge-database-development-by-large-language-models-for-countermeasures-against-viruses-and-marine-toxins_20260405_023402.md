# Knowledge database development by large language models for countermeasures against viruses and marine toxins

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.29149
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.29149
- Reference count: 0
- Primary result: Created two structured, updatable knowledge databases (for five viruses and 16 marine toxin families) via a scalable LLM-driven approach, including agentic AI workflows for evidence-based ranking.

## Executive Summary
This study addresses the critical lack of comprehensive, curated databases for medical countermeasures against specific viruses and marine toxins. Employing two large language models (ChatGPT and Grok) under high-level human supervision, the authors identified relevant public databases, iteratively collected and cross-validated information, and generated interactive web portals. The outcome is two structured, updatable knowledge databases covering therapeutic countermeasures for five high-priority viruses (LASV, MARV, EBOV, NiV, VEEV) and 16 families of marine toxins. The system integrates agentic AI workflows that automatically rank potential countermeasures based on evidence of mortality reduction, demonstrating a scalable and maintainable approach for organizing complex biomedical knowledge.

## Method Summary
The curation process began with a human-defined scope and a list of authoritative public databases (e.g., ClinicalTrials.gov, ChEMBL, PubMed). ChatGPT acted as the primary LLM, using retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) to query these sources and extract data into a predefined JSON schema. Grok independently performed cross-validation on the same sources; discrepancies were iteratively resolved under human oversight. The validated JSON data served as the single source of truth. ChatGPT then generated Python/FastAPI code to render hierarchical web interfaces (TRx Explorer and Marine Toxin Explorer). Finally, a two-agent workflow—using OpenAI GPT-5.2—was integrated: a researcher agent supplements the JSON with additional internet searches, and a decision-maker agent ranks countermeasures by mortality reduction evidence, providing rationales and evidence grades. The entire pipeline is designed for monthly automated updates.

## Key Results
- Two structured, updatable knowledge databases: therapeutic countermeasures for LASV, MARV, EBOV, NiV, VEEV; and 16 marine toxin families.
- Interactive hierarchical web portals (TRx Explorer, Marine Toxin Explorer) enabling intuitive navigation from virus/toxin family down to detailed treatment information.
- Agentic AI workflow (researcher + decision-maker) that automatically ranks countermeasures based on evidence of mortality reduction, with traceable rationales and evidence grades.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Dual-LLM cross-validation improves data accuracy and completeness for biomedical databases.
- **Mechanism:** One LLM (ChatGPT) performs primary data curation from specified public databases and literature via RAG, while a second LLM (Grok) independently reviews the same sources. Discrepancies or omissions identified during this iterative cross-checking are resolved, reducing hallucination and oversight risks.
- **Core assumption:** Independent LLMs, guided by identical source instructions, will produce sufficiently overlapping outputs that meaningful validation occurs, and any divergence flags potential errors or gaps.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "employ two large language models (LLMs) of ChatGPT and Grok... iteratively cross-validate the collected information"
  - [section 3] "ChatGPT was employed as the primary LLM to collecting the relevant information... while Grok was employed for cross-validation... ensuring the information collected... was consistent... and no relevant information was overlooked"
  - [corpus] Weak relevance. Neighboring papers discuss RAG and knowledge base construction but not multi-LLM validation for biomedical countermeasure databases.
- **Break condition:** Cross-validation fails if both LLMs systematically miss the same critical data (e.g., due to shared blind spots in source database querying) or if their outputs remain consistently divergent without human resolution, undermining the consistency check.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) grounded in authoritative, enumerated public databases curates structured, source-attributed data.
- **Mechanism:** The LLMs are prompted to consult a pre-defined, human-provided list of public databases (e.g., ClinicalTrials.gov, ChEMBL, PubMed) to extract specific data fields (e.g., survival rates, mechanisms of action). This constrains the LLM's generative space to retrieved snippets, mitigating hallucination and ensuring traceability to a source.
- **Core assumption:** The enumerated public databases are reasonably comprehensive and up-to-date for the target domains (five viruses, 16 marine toxin families), and the LLM can accurately interpret and extract from their content.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "identify public databases containing data... collect relevant information from these databases and the literature, iteratively cross-validate"
  - [section 4] "prompted the LLM to consult databases listed in Main Data 1 and publications in... PubMed and Europe PMC"
  - [corpus] Strong relevance. Neighboring papers (e.g., "Knowledge Base Construction for Knowledge-Augmented Text-to-SQL") describe RAG for structured data extraction but not this specific biomedical countermeasure application.
- **Break condition:** RAG fails if source databases lack key information (e.g., no clinical trial data for a virus), if database APIs change or become inaccessible, or if the LLM mis-parses retrieved content, leading to incorrect JSON population.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A two-agent "researcher-decision-maker" workflow enables evidence-based ranking of countermeasures by mortality reduction.
- **Mechanism:** A 'researcher' agent scans the curated database and supplements it with targeted internet searches to gather efficacy evidence. A 'decision-maker' agent then evaluates this compiled evidence against a rubric (implied: strength of mortality reduction data) to produce a ranked list with rationales and evidence grades.
- **Core assumption:** The 'decision-maker' agent's rubric—inferred from the prompt to rank based on "extents of mortality reductions"—can be operationalized consistently by the LLM, and the 'researcher' agent fetches relevant, high-quality supplementary evidence.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "design agentic AI workflows (consisting of two AI agents for research and decision-making) to rank potential countermeasures... based on evidence of mortality reduction"
  - [section 7] "‘researcher’ agent... may collect additional information from reliable internet sources... ‘decision-maker’ agent evaluated the information... ranked the countermeasures... based on their extents of mortality reductions"
  - [corpus] Moderate relevance. Neighboring papers on LLM applications (e.g., for materials science) lack this specific dual-agent ranking design for biomedical countermeasures.
- **Break condition:** Ranking fails if the 'researcher' agent retrieves low-quality or biased sources, if the 'decision-maker' agent's rubric is opaque or inconsistent (e.g., favoring preclinical over clinical data arbitrarily), or if mortality reduction data is absent/ambiguous for most countermeasures, forcing superficial ranking.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept:** Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)
  - **Why needed here:** To anchor LLM data extraction in verifiable, external sources (the listed public databases) and reduce fabrication of nonexistent countermeasures or data points.
  - **Quick check question:** If an LLM extracts a survival rate for a drug from a PubMed abstract via RAG, what metadata would you require to verify that extraction later?
- **Concept:** Agentic Workflow Design (Specialized LLM Agents)
  - **Why needed here:** To decompose the complex task of ranking countermeasures into sequential, constrained sub-tasks (research then decision), imposing structure that improves reliability over a single monolithic LLM query.
  - **Quick check question:** What is a key risk if the 'researcher' and 'decision-maker' agents are not given distinct, non-overlapping prompts and output constraints?
- **Concept:** Hierarchical Data Modeling for Web Interfaces
  - **Why needed here:** To translate flat JSON data into navigable, multi-level web portals (e.g., Virus → Category → Treatment → Details), which requires understanding how to map data relationships to UI components.
  - **Quick check question:** For the marine toxin portal, why might the designers have chosen a central circle expanding to 16 toxin families (Figure 3A) instead of a simple list?

## Architecture Onboarding
- Component map: Human-Supervision Layer → Dual-LLM Curation Engine → JSON Data Store → Web Portal Generator → Agentic Ranking Module
- Critical path: Human defines scope & sources → Dual-LLM iterative curation → JSON creation/update → Web code generation → Agent integration → Deployment.
- Design tradeoffs:
  - *Accuracy vs. Speed:* Using two LLMs for cross-validation improves accuracy but doubles curation time and cost.
  - *Generality vs. Control:* LLM-generated web/agent code is flexible but may produce inefficient or non-standard implementations; human code review is implied but not detailed.
  - *Automation vs. Safety:* Monthly LLM-driven updates enable scalability but risk incorporating erroneous new data without robust automated validation beyond the dual-LLM check.
- Failure signatures:
  - Curation: Persistent JSON schema mismatches between viral and toxin data; identical omission errors in both LLM outputs.
  - Web Portal: Broken hierarchical navigation (e.g., clicking a virus shows no countermeasure categories); unhandled errors when JSON fields are missing.
  - Ranking: Agent outputs list countermeasures with identical, vague rationales ("effective based on evidence"); rankings change erratically between queries for the same toxin.
- First 3 experiments:
  1. **Cross-Validation Stress Test:** Intentionally provide both LLMs with a corrupted source database link (e.g., a 404 page). Do they both fail gracefully, or does one produce plausible-but-fabricated data?
  2. **Update Propagation Test:** Add a new, high-impact preclinical study for a VEEV countermeasure to PubMed. Run the monthly update. Does the new study appear correctly in the JSON and on the web portal's treatment detail page?
  3. **Agent Ranking Consistency Test:** Query the Nipah virus ranking agent three times with identical prompts. Measure variance in the top-ranked countermeasure and the stated rationales/evidence strengths. High variance indicates unstable agent reasoning.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can cross-validation between multiple LLMs effectively mitigate hallucination and ensure data accuracy in biomedical database curation without extensive human verification?
- **Basis:** [inferred] The study employs ChatGPT and Grok for iterative cross-validation but does not quantify the accuracy achieved; it acknowledges LLM hallucinations and the need for high-level human supervision.
- **Why unresolved:** The assumption that cross-validation between LLMs suffices for accuracy is untested; no comparison to a gold standard or expert review is provided.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A systematic evaluation where database entries curated via LLM cross-validation (with varying human oversight) are benchmarked against an expert-curated gold standard, measuring precision, recall, and error rates.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How do agentic AI rankings of countermeasures compare to expert consensus, especially when integrating heterogeneous evidence from animal models and clinical trials?
- **Basis:** [inferred] The paper introduces an agentic AI workflow to rank countermeasures based on mortality reduction, but provides no validation of these rankings against human expert assessments or established clinical guidelines.
- **Why unresolved:** Rankings may be used for decision-making; without validation, their reliability and potential biases are unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A comparative study in which the AI-generated rankings for a set of viruses/toxins are evaluated by domain experts; assess correlation, agreement, and identify sources of disagreement.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What is the long-term impact of fully automated monthly updates on the consistency and accuracy of LLM-maintained biomedical databases?
- **Basis:** [inferred] The databases are automatically updated monthly by LLMs without re-verification; the paper does not monitor whether updates introduce new errors or cause content drift over time.
- **Why unresolved:** Continuous automation could amplify initial inaccuracies or introduce new hallucinations, potentially degrading database quality unnoticed.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Longitudinal analysis of database snapshots over multiple update cycles, comparing changes to a stable reference and flagging errors, to quantify update-induced error rates.

## Limitations
- Undocumented Prompts & Protocols: The exact high-level prompts, cross-validation dialogue rules, and agent rubric definitions are unspecified, creating a reproducibility black box.
- Agent Opacity: The decision-maker agent's ranking logic—beyond "mortality reduction"—is not formalized, risking inconsistent or non-reproducible outputs.
- Scalability & Generalization: Success demonstrated only for five viruses and 16 marine toxin families; performance on broader domains or different database structures remains unknown.
