# A zone-based training approach for last-mile routing using Graph Neural Networks and Pointer Networks

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.04705
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04705
- **Reference Count:** 29
- **Primary Result:** A zone-based GNN+PN model reduces MAPE from 215.97% (general model) to 134.57% on the Amazon Last Mile Routing Challenge (Los Angeles), with larger gains on complex, multi-zone routes.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles last-mile routing with asymmetric travel times by proposing a deep learning model with a Graph Neural Network (GNN) encoder and a Pointer Network (PN) decoder. Its core innovation is a zone-based training strategy: the service area is partitioned into geographical zones (via H3 hexagonal clustering), and a separate model is trained per zone. This approach significantly outperforms a single model trained on all data, reducing mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) by over 80 points. The improvement is especially pronounced for routes with many stops or those spanning multiple zones, demonstrating the value of learning localized spatial patterns.

## Method Summary
The authors model routing as a sequential node visitation problem on a directed complete graph where nodes are stops and edge weights are asymmetric travel times. For a given route, its stops are assigned to predefined geographical zones. During training, a distinct GNN+PN model is trained for each zone using only the sub-routes (stops within that zone) extracted from the full training routes. The GNN encoder (three GATv2 layers) generates node embeddings that capture local travel time relationships. The PN decoder sequentially selects the next stop via attention over these embeddings. Models are trained with the REINFORCE policy gradient algorithm to optimize total route time. At inference, a new route's stops are split by zone; each zone's model generates a sub-route, and these sub-routes are concatenated in geographical order to form the final predicted route.

## Key Results
- Average predicted route length decreased from 30,481.52s (general model) to 21,973.6s (zone-based model).
- Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) reduced from 215.97% to 134.57%.
- Maximum error decreased by over 50,000 seconds for the most difficult routes.
- The zone-based approach showed substantially larger improvements for routes with more stops and those spanning a greater number of zones.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Spatial PartitioningReduces Combinatorial Explosion
- **Claim:** Dividing the service area into geographical zones and training separate models per zone significantly improves routing accuracy, especially for long routes covering multiple zones.
- **Mechanism:** Partitioning a large, complex graph into smaller, manageable subgraphs reduces the number of nodes and possible edges the model must sequence at inference. It implicitly learns to optimize sub-routes within homogeneous geographical areas, avoiding improbable long-distance connections between distant stops.
- **Core assumption:** Stops within a geographically coherent zone have more consistent spatial and temporal relationships (e.g., similar traffic, street network) than stops across a large metro area.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "zone-based approach significantly outperforms... improvement is more substantial for routes with more stops and those spanning more zones."
  - [section 5] "The zone-based training strategy helps to partition these large areas, thereby reducing the highest errors by removing connections between stops that are geographically distant... the number of edges is reduced by splitting the main graph into more manageable subgraphs."
- **Break condition:** If routes are very short and contained within a single zone, partitioning adds overhead without benefit. Fails if zone boundaries artificially split a natural, efficient route.

### Mechanism 2: Graph Neural Network Encoder Captures Local Spatial Structure
- **Claim:** The GNN encoder (GATv2 layers) effectively generates node embeddings encoding the asymmetric travel time relationships between stops within a zone.
- **Mechanism:** GATv2 layers perform neighbor aggregation; each stop's embedding is informed by its directed travel times to/from other stops in its local graph. Edge attributes (asymmetric travel times) are used during the attention-based update, making embeddings sensitive to directional cost structure.
- **Core assumption:** A stop's optimal route position is primarily determined by its direct and second-order travel time relationships with geographically proximate stops (within its zone).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "A Graph Neural Network encoder produces node embeddings that captures the spatial relationships between stops."
  - [section 4] "three GATv2 layers... edge attributes are considered during the update process."
- **Break condition:** If a zone is too coarse (contains stops with highly heterogeneous patterns), the GNN's ability to capture coherent local structure is compromised. Fails if graph construction (thresholding edges) removes critical connections.

### Mechanism 3: Pointer Network Decoder Enables Iterative, Context-Aware Route Construction
- **Claim:** The PN decoder with a GRU hidden state provides an effective mechanism to sequentially construct a route by attending to context-summarizing node embeddings.
- **Mechanism:** Starting from the depot, the decoder's GRU maintains a running summary of the partially-built route. At each step, it computes an attention score over *all* unvisited node embeddings, allowing it to "point" to the next stop based on global context (all remaining stops) and current state (sequence already visited).
- **Core assumption:** The optimal next stop can be selected by comparing a query (current GRU state) against static, pre-computed candidate embeddings (the GNN output for all stops in the zone).
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "A Pointer Network decoder... sequentially select the next stops, assigning a probability to each unvisited node as the next destination."
  - [section 3] "The decoder receives as input the node embeddings... a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) to compute a hidden state... The PN decides, in each step, the next node... computes the attention values."
- **Break condition:** If GNN embeddings are poor (failed encoder or zone with insufficient data), the decoder has low-quality candidates. Limited by fixed context window (GRU state); may struggle with very long sequences.

## Foundational Learning
- **Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) for Node Embedding:** Why needed: The routing problem is defined on an irregular graph (stops as nodes, travel times as directed edges). Standard neural networks cannot process this structure. GNNs are the foundational tool to learn embeddings that summarize a node's structural role and relationships. Quick check: If you add a new stop to an existing route, how does a GNN generate an embedding for it that reflects its travel time connections to *all other stops* in the zone, not just its immediate neighbors?
- **Encoder-Decoder Sequence-to-Sequence (Seq2Seq) Models:** Why needed: The task is to generate an ordered sequence (the route) from an unordered set (the stops in a zone). The encoder must digest the full set of stop features and their graph relationships. The decoder must autoregressively generate the permutation. Quick check: Why can't a standard Seq2Seq model with a CNN/RNN encoder be used directly? (Answer: It assumes a fixed, grid-like input structure, not a graph).
- **Reinforcement Learning (Policy Gradient) for Non-Differentiable Objectives:** Why needed: The final performance metric (total route time) is non-differentiable. The REINFORCE algorithm allows training by sampling routes from the model's probability distribution and using the resulting route length as a reward signal to estimate the gradient. Quick check: What is the role of the "baseline" in the REINFORCE loss function? (Answer: To reduce the variance of the gradient estimates, often a moving average of past rewards).

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Input Processor (stop features, travel times) -> Graph Builder (complete directed graph per zone) -> GNN Encoder (3x GATv2 layers) -> Node Embeddings -> Pointer Network Decoder (GRU + attention) -> Sub-route Sequence. Separate path: Training Loop (per-zone filtering -> REINFORCE loss -> zone-specific model update).
- **Critical path:** Data Preparation -> Zone Clustering (H3 + k-Means) -> Per-Zone Model Training -> Inference (Zone Splitting -> Sub-route Generation per zone -> Concatenation by geographical order). The critical novel step is the per-zone training loop.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Zone Granularity:* Finer zones = smaller graphs, easier learning, but more models and risky inter-zone transitions. Coarser zones = fewer models but larger, harder problems.
  - *Model Capacity vs. Zone Size:* Fixed model size must handle varying stop counts per zone; very large zones may exceed capacity.
  - *Generalization vs. Specialization:* Zone-specific models may overfit to local idiosyncrasies but capture local patterns better; a single model must generalize across all patterns.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **High MAPE on long, multi-zone routes in zone-based model:** Zones still too large or insufficient model capacity for zone's stop count.
  - **High MAPE on short, single-zone routes:** Fundamental GNN/PN architecture or training regime flawed.
  - **Assembly artifacts:** Sudden jumps in predicted route time when concatenating sub-routes, indicating locally optimal but globally incompatible zone solutions.
  - **Inconsistent performance across zones:** Data imbalance (some zones sparse) or geographical heterogeneity within a zone.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Sanity Check - Single Zone, Small Graph:** Train one model on routes confined to a single tiny H3 cell (<20 stops). Expect near-zero MAPE to confirm baseline GNN/PN functionality.
  2. **Isolate the Partitioning Effect:** Compare (a) general model on full test graphs vs. (b) zone-based models with an *Oracle Assembler* (perfect inter-zone order). Any improvement in (b) confirms partitioning value even with perfect assembly.
  3. **Zone Granularity Sweep:** Repeat pipeline with different k for k-Means (e.g., 30, 57, 100 zones). Plot test MAPE vs. number of zones to identify the optimal trade-off (likely U-shaped).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1:** How does increasing the training batch size from 32 to larger values (e.g., 64, 128, 512) affect predictive accuracy and convergence stability?
  - Basis: Authors state GPU VRAM limited batch size to 32; prior work used up to 512.
  - Why unresolved: Hardware prevented empirical testing.
  - Resolution: Train identical architecture with varying batch sizes on sufficient hardware, compare loss curves and MAPE.
- **Question 2:** To what extent would incorporating additional features (e.g., real-time traffic, Euclidean inter-stop distances) improve accuracy?
  - Basis: Authors acknowledge dataset lacks such features and hypothesize enhancement.
  - Why unresolved: Dataset did not include them; no experiments conducted.
  - Resolution: Augment dataset with simulated/real features, retrain, statistically compare MAPE and error distributions.
- **Question 3:** Does replacing the Pointer Network decoder with a Multi-Head Attention mechanism yield significantly lower MAPE?
  - Basis: Authors propose Multi-Head Attention could outperform PN but did not implement due to computational constraints.
  - Why unresolved: No direct comparison within their framework.
  - Resolution: Implement Multi-Head Attention decoder with identical encoder/hyperparameters, train both models, perform statistical tests on test MAPE.
- **Question 4:** What is the optimal zone granularity (number and size of partitions) for balancing localized specialization and global coherence?
  - Basis: Paper uses fixed 57 zones (H3 res 7, k-Means) without investigating variation.
  - Why unresolved: No ablation study on zone granularity; choice of 57 is arbitrary.
  - Resolution: Train for different zone counts (e.g., 20, 57, 100, 200), evaluate per-zone and combined MAPE, identify optimal trade-off.

## Limitations
- Exact specification of 16-dimensional positional encoding and zone embedding per node is not detailed, hindering faithful reproduction.
- Critical training hyperparameters (REINFORCE baseline details, precise learning rate schedule) and the zone sub-route assembly ordering logic are unspecified.
- The chosen zone count (57) is not justified, and no sensitivity analysis on zone granularity is provided.

## Confidence
- **Zone-based training superiority:** High (clear MAPE reduction, consistent across route lengths)
- **GNN captures local spatial structure:** Medium-High (architecture is sound, but dependent on unspecified feature fidelity)
- **Partitioning reduces combinatorial complexity:** Medium-High (supported by error breakdowns on multi-zone routes)
- **PN decoder effectiveness:** Medium (well-established in corpus, but no ablation against alternatives in this work)

## Next Checks
1. **Architecture sanity check:** Train a single model on routes confined to one tiny H3 cell (<20 stops). Expect near-zero MAPE to confirm the base GNN+PN can learn a simple instance.
2. **Oracle assembly isolation:** Run zone-based models but concatenate sub-routes using the true inter-zone order from ground truth. Compare MAPE to the general model; any remaining gap isolates intra-zone modeling error from assembly loss.
3. **Zone granularity sweep:** Repeat the full pipeline with different zone counts (e.g., k=30, 100). Plot overall test MAPE vs. zone count to identify the optimal granularity and validate the robustness of the chosen k=57.
