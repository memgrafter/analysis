# Automatic Image-Level Morphological Trait Annotation for Organismal Images

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2604.01619  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01619  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: A scalable pipeline using sparse autoencoders and vision‑language prompting generated 80K trait annotations (BIOSCAN‑TRAITS) for 19K insect images and improved zero‑shot species classification by +0.93 percentage points.

## Executive Summary
Morphological trait extraction from biological images is traditionally slow and relies heavily on expert annotators, limiting its scalability in ecology. This paper introduces a fully automated pipeline that combines sparse autoencoders (SAEs) trained on DINOv2 features with multi‑image prompting of a large multimodal language model. The SAE discovers monosemantic, spatially coherent neurons that correspond to morphological parts. A species‑contrastive filter selects latents that are both frequent within a target species and rare among its congeners, ensuring diagnostic relevance. Bounding boxes derived from SAE decoder masks are shown to multiple specimen images, and the MLLM is prompted to describe traits common across them, reducing hallucinations. Applied to the BIOSCAN‑5M insect dataset, the method produced BIOSCAN‑TRAITS (80K trait annotations for 19K images). Human evaluators rated the output highly (mean 3.91 vs. 3.15 for MLLM‑only), demonstrating biological plausibility. Fine‑tuning BioCLIP on these annotations improved zero‑shot species classification on an in‑the‑wild benchmark by +0.93 pp (56.23% vs. 55.3%). The approach offers a scalable path to large‑scale morphological trait annotation.

## Method Summary
The pipeline begins by extracting patch embeddings from input images using DINOv2. A sparse autoencoder trained on these embeddings learns a dictionary of monosemantic latents, each representing a reusable visual pattern (e.g., wing, antenna) with a corresponding spatial mask from the decoder. Per image, latents with activation above a threshold are retained. To focus on diagnostically useful morphology, latents are aggregated over all images of a given species and its genus; only those with normalized activation frequency higher in the species than in the genus (*f_s > f_g*) and exceeding a minimum frequency (*t_freq*) are kept. The SAE decoder masks then provide bounding boxes for the selected latents. For each species, up to three images exhibiting the latent are sampled and their bounding boxes are fed to a multimodal LLM (Qwen2.5‑VL 72B) with a prompt that asks for traits common to all images. The resulting natural‑language descriptions constitute the trait annotation. Optionally, the annotated pairs are used to fine‑tune BioCLIP, yielding improved zero‑shot classification.

## Key Results
- **BIOSCAN‑TRAITS dataset**: 80,232 trait annotations across 19,073 insect images.
- **Human evaluation**: SAE‑guided multi‑image MLLM achieved a mean rating of 3.91 (on a 5‑point scale) compared to 3.15 for MLLM‑only, confirming biological plausibility.
- **Downstream improvement**: Fine‑tuning BioCLIP on the generated traits increased zero‑shot species classification accuracy on an in‑the‑wild benchmark by +0.93 percentage points (56.23% vs. 55.3%).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Sparse autoencoders (SAE) trained on DINOv2 features yield monosemantic, spatially coherent neurons that consistently activate on meaningful morphological parts (e.g., wings, antennae).
- **Mechanism**: The SAE learns a dictionary of latent representations under sparsity (L0/L1) and reconstruction constraints. This forces each latent to capture a single reusable visual pattern. The decoder weights can be interpreted as spatial masks, localizing the region where the latent fires.
- **Core assumption**: DINOv2’s pretrained features already encode morphology in a linearly decomposable way; the SAE isolates these factors.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  * [abstract] "...sparse autoencoders trained on foundation-model features yield monosemantic, spatially grounded neurons..."  
  * [section 4.4] Figure 5: neurons 4852 (wings) and 13860 (antennae) show clear, repeatable activation patterns across insect species.  
  * [corpus] Weak: neighboring papers (e.g., "Static Segmentation by Tracking") explore alternative segmentation methods but do not directly validate SAE for morphological parts.
- **Break condition**: If sparsity coefficient (α) is too high, latents become polysemantic or fail to reconstruct; if too low, latents capture background clutter. Mis‑set activation thresholds also degrade coherence.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Species‑contrastive ranking selects SAE neurons that are both common within a focal species and rare in its genus, highlighting diagnostically useful morphological traits.
- **Mechanism**: For each latent *z*, compute normalized activation frequency *f_s*(*z*) for species *s* and *f_g*(*z*) for its genus *g*. Retain latents where *f_s*(*z*) > *f_g*(*z*) and *f_s*(*z*) exceeds a minimum threshold *t_freq*.
- **Core assumption**: The most salient traits for distinguishing a species from its close relatives are those consistently expressed in that species but less prevalent in congeners.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  * [section 3.2] Algorithm 1 explicitly computes species‑ and genus‑frequency and selects traits satisfying *f_s* > *f_g* and threshold.  
  * [section 4.3] Table 3 shows that varying *t_freq* directly trades recall for precision, confirming the filter’s role.  
  * [corpus] Minimal; the concept of taxonomic contrastive filtering is novel to this work.
- **Break condition**: If a species shares many traits with its congeners (cryptic diversity), contrastive scores may be low, causing under‑selection. Sparse species data also yields noisy genus estimates.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: Multi‑image prompting of a multimodal LLM (MLLM), with SAE‑derived bounding boxes, generates more accurate and consistent morphological trait descriptions than single‑image or MLLM‑only baselines.
- **Mechanism**: For a given species, the pipeline samples multiple specimen images, overlays high‑confidence SAE masks, and prompts the MLLM to describe traits *common* across all images. This forces consensus, filtering out instance‑specific noise, background, and hallucinations.
- **Core assumption**: True morphological traits are invariant across individuals of a species; requiring agreement across images suppresses spurious cues.
- **Evidence anchors**:  
  * [abstract] "Incorporating latent‑specific patches significantly improves the quality of trait descriptions… multi‑image… leads to improved precision."  
  * [section 4.3] Table 1: mean human rating rises from 3.84 (1 image) to 3.91 (3 images) for MLLM+SAE.  
  * [section 4.3] Figure 4: single‑image output overfits, combining multiple body parts; multi‑image isolates consistent leg traits.  
  * [corpus] No direct parallel in neighbor papers; multi‑image prompting for trait consensus is a novel design choice.
- **Break condition**: If images differ substantially in pose/lighting, consensus may discard valid variation; small MLLMs (e.g., 7B) still hallucinate even with multi‑image input.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component map
Species‑labeled images → DINOv2 feature extractor → SAE encoder (sparse latent) → activation thresholding (*t<sub>activation</sub>*) → taxonomic aggregation (compute *f_s*, *f_g*) → species‑contrastive filtering (*f_s* > *f_g*, *t<sub>freq</sub>*) → bounding box generation (using SAE decoder masks) → multi‑image sampling (≤3 images) → MLLM prompting (Qwen2.5‑VL) → trait descriptions. Optional: fine‑tune BioCLIP on (image, trait) pairs.

### Critical path
1. Compute DINOv2 patch embeddings for an image.  
2. Encode with SAE → sparse latent vector.  
3. Threshold latents; record which patches activated.  
4. Aggregate activations over all images of each species to compute *f_s* and *f_g*.  
5. For each species, filter latents by contrastive score and *t<sub>freq</sub>*.  
6. Use SAE decoder weights to draw bounding boxes for selected latents.  
7. Pick up to 3 images per species that exhibit the latent; overlay boxes.  
8. Prompt MLLM with multi‑image + boxes; parse response into trait list.  
9. Store trait descriptions; repeat for all species.

### Design tradeoffs
- **SAE sparsity (α)**: Lower α → more latents per image (higher recall) but may include noise; higher α → cleaner, monosemantic latents (higher precision) but risk missing subtle traits.  
- **Frequency threshold (*t<sub>freq</sub>*)**: Lower threshold → more traits (recall) but includes rare/erroneous activations; higher threshold → only stable, taxonomically informative traits (precision).  
- **Number of images per prompt**: More images (e.g., 3) improves consistency and reduces hallucinations but increases token cost; 1 image is cheaper but less robust.  
- **MLLM scale**: 72B models produce more reliable descriptions and avoid hallucinated traits vs. 7B, but are slower and more expensive.

### Failure signatures
- **SAE latent quality**: Decoder masks that are large, diffuse, or cover background → reduce sparsity (lower α) or improve SAE training data diversity.  
- **Low human‑rated quality** (e.g., <3.0): Likely MLLM hallucinations; switch to larger MLLM or increase *t<sub>freq</sub>* for stricter latent selection.  
- **No traits extracted for a species**: Check that the species has enough images and that *t<sub>freq</sub>* isn’t too high; inspect genus‑level activation distributions.  
- **Poor downstream transfer**: Overfitting to dataset biases; ensure fine‑tuning uses trait‑only prompts and appropriate regularization/early stopping.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Baseline comparison**: Run MLLM‑only (single‑ and multi‑image) vs. MLLM+SAE (multi‑image) with 1,000 images; human‑rate 30 samples each; expect rating >3.8 for SAE approach (Table 1).  
2. **Single vs. multiple images**: For MLLM+SAE, compare 1‑image vs. 3‑image prompts; measure rating improvement and token usage; expect rating ↑ from ~3.84 to ~3.91 (Table 1).  
3. **SAE hyperparameter sweep**: Vary α∈{2e‑4,4e‑4,8e‑4} and *t<sub>freq</sub>*∈{3e‑3,1e‑2}; compute rating; expect lower α (e.g., 2e‑4) and moderate *t<sub>freq</sub>* (3e‑3) to perform best (Tables 2–3, Figure 6).

## Limitations
- SAE neuron monosemanticity is shown only via a few examples; lacks large‑scale validation.  
- Species‑contrastive filtering relies on heuristic thresholds; may under‑select traits for cryptic or data‑poor species.  
- Multi‑image prompting assumes trait invariance; sexually dimorphic or polymorphic species may lose valid traits.

## Confidence
- SAE monosemantic neurons: Medium  
- Species‑contrastive filtering: Medium  
- Multi‑image MLLM prompting: High  
- Downstream classification gain: High  

## Next Checks
1. **Large‑scale neuron monosemanticity test**: Sample 1,000 SAE latents, compute IoU with expert part masks (e.g., wings, antennae) on 500 images; report mean and distribution.  
2. **Cross‑organism transfer test**: Apply pipeline to a plant dataset (e.g., iNaturalist Plants) and evaluate traits via expert review and downstream few‑shot classification; compare to insect results.  
3. **Intra‑species variation robustness**: For a sexually dimorphic insect species, vary number of images per prompt (1–5) and *t<sub>freq</sub>*; analyze whether generated traits cover both morphs and align with taxonomic literature.
