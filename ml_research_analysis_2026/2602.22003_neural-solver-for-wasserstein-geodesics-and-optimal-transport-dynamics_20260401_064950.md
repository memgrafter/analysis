# Neural solver for Wasserstein Geodesics and optimal transport dynamics
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.22003
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22003
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** A neural solver for dynamic optimal transport that learns Wasserstein geodesics and velocity fields from samples using an adversarial min‑max formulation and a two‑phase training procedure.

## Executive Summary
Dynamic optimal transport seeks time‑dependent maps that move one distribution to another while minimizing a chosen cost, often interpreted as a fluid‑dynamic system on a geodesic. Exact computation from samples is intractable; this paper proposes a neural framework that recasts the problem as an unconstrained min‑max objective. In Phase 1, a transport trajectory \(G_\theta(t;z) = z + t F_\theta(z,t)\) is learned by adversarial training: a critic \(\phi_\omega\) with approximate Lipschitz constraint enforces terminal distribution matching, while the actor \(F_\theta\) minimizes the expected Lagrangian cost along the path. In Phase 2, the full velocity field \(v(x,t)\) is recovered via supervised regression on velocities sampled from the converged trajectories. The method is validated on synthetic 2D/10D Gaussians (including mixtures) and MNIST digit translation, and it theoretically extends to general convex Lagrangians. Results show successful learning of transport maps and velocity fields, but terminal matching sometimes fails (Synthetic‑2) and Phase 2 velocity errors do not vanish, pointing to limitations inherent in the two‑phase scheme.

## Method Summary
The method consists of two phases. Phase 1: the transport trajectory is reparameterized as \(G_\theta(t;z)=z+t F_\theta(z,t)\), where \(F_\theta\) outputs a time‑varying velocity increment. The terminal constraint \(G_\theta(1;\cdot)_\#\rho_a = \rho_b\) is replaced by the Kantorovich‑Rubinstein duality: a critic \(\phi_\omega\) (approximated to be 1‑Lipschitz via spectral normalization) yields the loss
\[
\min_\theta \max_\omega \; \mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\rho_a}[L(dG_\theta/dt)] + \lambda\bigl(\mathbb{E}[\phi_\omega(G_\theta(1;Z))] - \mathbb{E}[\phi_\omega(Y)]\bigr),
\]
where \(Y\sim\rho_b\). Alternating updates (e.g., RMSprop) are performed for several iterations. Phase 2: after freezing \(F_{\theta^*}\), a fresh dataset of pairs \((Z,U)\) with \(U\sim\text{Uniform}(0,1)\) is generated; the trajectory \(G_{\theta^*}(U;Z)\) and its derivative \(dG_{\theta^*}/dt\) provide training samples. A new network \(v_\eta\) is trained by minimizing the mean squared error between its prediction \(v_\eta(G_{\theta^*},U)\) and the sampled derivative. The overall structure accommodates general Lagrangians \(L(v,x,t)\) simply by modifying the Phase 1 loss term.

## Key Results
- Transport maps and velocity fields are successfully learned on synthetic Gaussians (2D/10D, mixtures) and on MNIST digit translation (9→6).
- Phase 1 attains near‑analytic Wasserstein‑2 costs on simple Gaussian pairs and reduces the Wasserstein‑1 distance for most cases; however, for multimodal targets (Synthetic‑2) the distance fails to converge.
- Phase 2 velocity field loss consistently decreases but does not reach zero, indicating that the learned velocity deviates from the true optimal field due to inaccuracies in Phase 1 trajectories.
- The framework extends to general Lagrangians in principle, though empirical validation beyond the kinetic‑energy case remains limited.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Adversarial saddle‑point formulation
**Claim:** By reformulating the constrained dynamic OT problem as an unconstrained min‑max objective, terminal distribution matching is enforced without explicitly constructing a continuity equation.
**Mechanism:** The reparameterization \(G_\theta(t;z)=z+t F_\theta(z,t)\) embeds the initial condition and turns the continuity equation into a kinematic constraint. The terminal equality \(G_\theta(1;\cdot)_\#\rho_a = \rho_b\) is replaced by its Kantorovich‑Rubinstein dual, introducing a critic \(\phi_\omega\) with bounded Lipschitz norm (approximated by spectral normalization). The resulting minimax objective absorbs the Lagrange multiplier into the critic’s Lipschitz constant.
**Core assumption:** The saddle‑point problem is equivalent to the original constrained problem under a unique solution (Theorem 2.2, Appendix A).
**Evidence anchors:** Abstract states “recast the constrained optimization as a minimax problem, using deep neural networks to approximate the relevant functions”; Section 2.2 derives (2.6) from (2.4) via duality; related work on Wasserstein GANs provides context but the specific dynamic OT formulation is novel.
**Break condition:** Poor Lipschitz enforcement (e.g., spectral norm not strictly controlled) or insufficient critic capacity can cause the terminal matching term to stagnate, as seen in Synthetic‑2 where Wasserstein‑1 distance fluctuates.

### Mechanism 2: Two‑phase velocity recovery
**Claim:** The optimal velocity field \(v(x,t)\) is obtained by supervised regression on velocity samples derived from the learned trajectories of Phase 1.
**Mechanism:** After Phase 1 converges to \(G_{\theta^*}(t;z)\), its time derivative \(dG_{\theta^*}/dt\) supplies approximate velocity values along the transport. A new network \(v_\eta\) is trained to minimize \(\|v_\eta(G_{\theta^*}(U;Z),U) - dG_{\theta^*}/dt\|^2\) over random \(Z\) and \(U\sim\text{Uniform}(0,1)\).
**Core assumption:** The samples \((G_{\theta^*}(U;Z), U, dG_{\theta^*}/dt)\) accurately represent the true optimal velocity, which requires Phase 1 to have found a near‑optimal transport.
**Evidence anchors:** Section 3.1 defines the two‑phase procedure and regression loss (3.1); Remark 3.1 notes that Phase 2 loss vanishing does not imply \(v_\eta \to v^*\) because Phase 1 solutions are approximate; Appendix B shows decreasing Phase 2 loss but non‑zero velocity MSE on synthetic benchmarks.
**Break condition:** Inaccuracies in Phase 1 trajectories bias the velocity samples, causing the learned \(v_\eta\) to deviate from the true field; Phase 2 MSE typically plateaus above zero.

### Mechanism 3: Extension to general Lagrangians
**Claim:** The framework naturally accommodates arbitrary convex, superlinear Lagrangians \(L(v,x,t)\) without requiring explicit Hamiltonian forms or additional Lipschitz constraints.
**Mechanism:** The Phase 1 minimax structure (2.7) remains unchanged; only the Lagrangian inside the expectation is replaced by the general \(L(dG_\theta/dt, G_\theta, t)\). This allows modeling time‑ or position‑dependent transport costs while preserving adversarial training.
**Core assumption:** The theoretical equivalence (Theorem 2.2) extends to strictly convex, superlinear \(L\), as indicated in Section 3.2.
**Evidence anchors:** Section 3.2: “Our method extends naturally to time‑dependent optimal transport problems… by replacing the simple kinetic energy with the more general Lagrangian”; Section 1.3 contrasts with Hamiltonian‑based approaches, highlighting this advantage; the corpus suggests novelty in adversarial handling of general Lagrangians.
**Break condition:** For non‑convex or irregular \(L\), the min‑max problem may lose convex‑concave properties, leading to instability or suboptimal saddles; the paper does not explore such cases.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
\(F_\theta(z,t)\) (actor/velocity increment) → produces trajectory \(G_\theta(t;z)=z+tF_\theta(z,t)\); critic \(\phi_\omega(x)\) (Lipschitz network) → evaluates terminal discrepancy; after Phase 1, \(v_\eta(x,t)\) (velocity regressor) → trained on samples \((G_{\theta^*}(U;Z), U, dG_{\theta^*}/dt)\).

**Critical path (training procedure):**  
1. Initialize \(F_\theta\) and \(\phi_\omega\).  
2. Sample \(Z\sim\rho_a\), \(Y\sim\rho_b\), \(U\sim\text{Uniform}(0,1)\) (often \(m\) equally spaced time points).  
3. Compute \(G_\theta(t;z)\) and \(dG_\theta/dt = F_\theta(z,t) + t\cdot \nabla_zF_\theta(z,t)\) (via autograd).  
4. Update \(\phi_\omega\) to maximize \(\mathcal{L}_\omega = \mathbb{E}[\phi_\omega(G_\theta(1;Z))] - \mathbb{E}[\phi_\omega(Y)]\).  
5. Update \(F_\theta\) to minimize \(\mathcal{L}_\theta = \mathbb{E}[L(dG_\theta/dt)] + \lambda_{\text{term}}\,\mathbb{E}[\phi_\omega(G_\theta(1;Z))]\).  
6. Repeat steps 2‑5 for several thousand iterations until Phase 1 losses stabilize.  
7. Freeze \(F_{\theta^*}\). Generate a fresh dataset of \((Z,U)\) and compute \(G_{\theta^*}(U;Z)\) and \(dG_{\theta^*}/dt\).  
8. Train \(v_\eta\) by minimizing \(\mathbb{E}\|v_\eta(G_{\theta^*}(U;Z), U) - dG_{\theta^*}/dt\|^2\) for a few thousand iterations (Phase 2).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Lipschitz enforcement:* Approximated by spectral normalization and Leaky ReLU (slope 0.2); output scaling allows the critic to absorb the Lagrange multiplier but needs tuning to avoid vanishing gradients.
- *Sample reuse:* Fresh samples for Phase 2 are recommended to prevent overfitting; synthetic tests showed little difference but fresh sampling is more robust.
- *Time sampling:* Equally spaced \(t\) values (e.g., \(m=10\) or \(20\)) yield more stable gradients than i.i.d. uniform draws, at the cost of possibly missing early/late dynamics.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Phase 1: Wasserstein‑1 distance between pushed‑forward and target samples does not decrease; critic loss oscillates without clear trend.
- Phase 2: Velocity field loss (MSE) plateaus at a non‑zero value; visualizations (e.g., quiver plots) show mismatch with ground truth.
- General: Min‑max instability (large swings in both losses); degraded performance on high‑dimensional data (e.g., blurry MNIST transports).

**First three experiments:**  
1. **Synthetic‑1 (2D Gaussians):** Source \(\mathcal{N}(0,I_2)\), target \(\mathcal{N}([6,6],\Sigma)\) with correlated covariance. Validates Phase 1 recovers near‑analytic \(W_2\) cost and Phase 2 reduces velocity MSE (though not to zero).  
2. **Synthetic‑2 (Mixture):** Two‑mode source and target. Demonstrates difficulty: Wasserstein‑1 fails to converge, indicating limited critic capacity or optimization hardness for multimodal cases.  
3. **Synthetic‑3 (10D Gaussians):** Ten‑dimensional isotropic normals, testing scalability. Trajectories are roughly aligned in projection, but velocity error remains appreciable, highlighting the curse of dimensionality.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How can the neural solver achieve perfect terminal distribution matching in Phase 1 for all distribution types?
2. Why do residual velocity errors persist in Phase 2 despite Phase 1 convergence, and can we eliminate this dependency?
3. What causes training instability in Phase 1, and how can we ensure stable convergence for the min‑max problem?
4. How sensitive is the method to neural network hyperparameters, and what guidelines can ensure robust performance?
5. Can the framework be extended to scenarios with only source distribution samples, e.g., for unsupervised generative modeling?

## Limitations
- Phase 1 often fails to achieve exact terminal matching for challenging distributions (e.g., multimodal Synthetic‑2), likely due to limited critic capacity or imperfect Lipschitz enforcement.
- Velocity field errors in Phase 2 persist because learned trajectories are only approximate; there is no guarantee that regressing on these samples yields the true optimal velocity.
- Critical training details (network widths, learning rates, batch sizes, time discretization) are omitted, hindering reproducibility.
- Empirical validation of extensions to general Lagrangians and high‑dimensional real data remains limited.

## Confidence
- Mechanism 1 (Adversarial saddle‑point): Medium
- Mechanism 2 (Two‑phase velocity recovery): Low
- Mechanism 3 (Extension to general Lagrangians): Low
