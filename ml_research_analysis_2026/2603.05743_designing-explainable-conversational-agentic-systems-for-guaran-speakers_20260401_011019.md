# Designing Explainable Conversational Agentic Systems for Guaraní Speakers

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2603.05743
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05743
*   **Reference Count:** 30
*   **Primary Result:** Proposes an oral-first multi-agent architecture that decouples Guaraní language understanding from conversation state and governance agents, with four novel evaluation metrics targeting multi-turn repair, shared context, perceived data sovereignty, and culturally-aligned latency.

## Executive Summary
This paper identifies a critical mismatch: prevailing text-first conversational AI paradigms fail to support the primarily oral, multi-turn interaction patterns of low-resource languages like Guaraní, where features like repair, interruptions, and community-governed data are essential. It introduces a conceptual "oral-first" architecture using multiple specialized agents. A dedicated Guaraní Understanding Agent handles speech-to-intent, while separate agents manage conversation state, permissions (sovereignty), and action execution. The design explicitly models turn-taking and shared context to move beyond single-turn command execution. The work aims to operationalize indigenous data sovereignty (by separating governance from execution) and address linguistic diglossia, proposing a new evaluation framework centered on conversational coherence, error recovery, and user trust rather than just task completion.

## Method Summary
The core method is a conceptual systems architecture analysis. The authors deconstruct the requirements for a conversational system serving an oral, low-resource language with strong community data governance norms. They identify the need to separate the "understanding" component (specific to Guaraní's phonology and grammar, including code-switching) from the "management" components (handling multi-turn state, turn-taking rules, and permission workflows). This decoupling is presented as necessary to enable explainability (each agent's role is clear) and sovereignty (governance logic is distinct and potentially community-controlled). The paper does not present an implemented system or empirical user study; it outlines the architectural principle and its associated evaluation metrics.

## Key Results
*   A multi-agent framework where a Guaraní-specific Understanding Agent feeds abstract intents to separate State, Permission, and Action Agents, enabling modular governance and explanation.
*   Four new evaluation metrics: Task Success Rate (multi-turn coherence), Repair Success Rate (handling misunderstandings), Perceived Sovereignty (user trust in data control), and Latency (alignment with conversational tempo).
*   A design mandate to prioritize community-led data collection for spontaneous, multi-turn conversational speech with annotations for repair, interruptions, and context.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The proposed mechanism works by isolating concerns that are typically bundled in standard conversational AI stacks. The *Guaraní Understanding Agent* is solely responsible for interpreting oral Guaraní (and Jopará) speech into a language-agnostic intent representation. This isolates the low-resource, culturally-specific language challenge. The *State Agent* tracks the multi-turn dialogue context and turn-taking status, the *Permission Agent* enforces community-defined data access and consent rules (operationalizing sovereignty), and the *Action Agent* executes approved intents. This separation allows each component to be optimized, explained, and—crucially for sovereignty—governed independently. For example, the Permission Agent's logic could be audited or configured by community representatives without needing to understand the nuances of Guaraní speech recognition. This structure directly addresses the identified failure modes of text-first systems, which collapse turn-taking, context, and command execution into a single, opaque pipeline that cannot support repair or community governance.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Oral-First Design:** Prioritizes spoken, multi-turn interaction patterns over text-based commands. *Why needed:* Guaraní is primarily oral; systems must support natural turn-taking, overlap, and repair. *Quick check:* Does the system model and respond to conversational timing cues (e.g., pauses, backchannels)?
*   **Indigenous Data Sovereignty (IDS):** The principle that indigenous communities have the right to govern data about themselves. *Why needed:* To ensure AI systems align with community values of control, consent, and benefit-sharing, moving beyond extractive data practices. *Quick check:* Is there a clear boundary where community-defined rules can intercept or modify system behavior?
*   **Multi-Agent Systems for Dialogue:** Using multiple specialized software agents to manage different aspects of a conversation. *Why needed:* No single monolithic agent can effectively handle the distinct demands of low-resource language understanding, complex state tracking, and ethical governance. *Quick check:* Can agents be developed, updated, or replaced independently?
*   **Linguistic Diglossia:** The coexistence of a formal,Written variety and an informal, spoken variety within a language community. *Why needed:* Systems must serve the daily spoken form (Guaraní/Jopará) while respecting formal domains, avoiding the common pitfall of only supporting a written standard. *Quick check:* Is the speech model trained on and evaluated with spontaneous, code-switched conversation?
*   **Turn-Taking & Repair:** The conversational mechanisms for managing speaking rights and recovering from misunderstandings. *Why needed:* These are fundamental to natural oral dialogue; their absence makes AI interaction feel brittle and unnatural. *Quick check:* Does the system have a protocol for detecting and handling failed understanding across multiple turns?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**
User (Spoken Guaraní) -> Guaraní Understanding Agent (Speech-to-Intent) -> [State Agent <-> Permission Agent] -> Action Agent -> System Response (Spoken Guaraní)
*   State Agent manages dialogue context and turn status.
*   Permission Agent consults governance rules before forwarding intent.
*   Interaction between State and Permission Agents can determine if a repair sequence is needed.

**Critical Path:** The most frequent path is: Speech -> Understanding -> State Update -> Permission Check (if new action/topic) -> Action -> Response. A repair path diverts after a failed Understanding or Permission check, involving the State Agent to manage clarification with the user.

**Design Tradeoffs:**
1.  **Latency vs. Governance:** Querying a community-governed Permission Agent may introduce delay versus a local, simple rule check. The system must balance responsiveness with respect for sovereignty protocols.
2.  **Complexity vs. Explainability:** More agents increase system complexity but improve modular explainability ("The permission agent denied that based on rule X"). Simpler monolithic systems are opaque but easier to build.
3.  **General Intent Space vs. Coverage:** A small, fixed set of abstract intents simplifies the Permission Agent's task but may limit expressiveness. A rich, open intent space increases functionality but makes governance rules harder to define and manage.

**Failure Signatures:**
1.  **Brittle Understanding:** High failure on code-switched (Jopará) utterances or accented speech, causing immediate breakdown without repair.
2.  **Timing Violations:** System responses that consistently preempt or lag behind Guaraní conversational turn-taking norms, creating a disjointed "robot" feel.
3.  **Sovereignty Bypass:** Permission checks are skipped or overridden by the Action Agent during time-critical tasks, undermining the core governance model.
4.  **Context Loss:** State Agent fails to track references across turns ("that one," "the first thing"), leading to incoherent multi-turn task execution.

**First Three Experiments:**
1.  **Wizard-of-Oz Prototype:** Implement a prototype where a human "wizard" plays the roles of State and Permission Agents behind a functional Understanding Agent. Test with Guaraní speakers on multi-turn tasks to gather initial data on natural turn-taking, repair initiation, and user reactions to permission prompts.
2.  **Metric Piloting:** Run a controlled study comparing the prototype against a baseline single-agent text-first system (if available) or a rule-based script. Measure the four proposed metrics (Task Success Rate, Repair Success Rate, Perceived Sovereignty via interview, objective Latency) to establish initial benchmarks and feasibility.
3.  **Governance Model Co-Design Sprint:** Partner with a Guaraní community to design the simplest possible Permission Agent interface and rule specification. Test whether users understand the data control model and whether it enhances their sense of trust ("Perceived Sovereignty") compared to a black-box system.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- Question: How can multi-turn conversational speech datasets for Guaraní be collected and annotated to include turn-taking, repair, and shared context, given current data scarcity?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors state in Section 4.2: "While data scarcity is a common constraint, oral-first systems face a more specific gap: the lack of conversational audio capturing turn-taking, interruptions, repair, and shared context... Community-led data collection efforts are therefore essential, and future datasets should prioritize conversational speech over read or isolated utterances."
- Why unresolved: Existing resources like Mozilla Common Voice are not conversational; there is no annotated corpus of natural multi-turn Guaraní dialogue for training and evaluating systems.
- What evidence would resolve it: A publicly available, community-annotated corpus of spontaneous Guaraní conversations with rich turn-level labels for repair, intent shifts, and contextual references.

### Open Question 2
- Question: What community-centered, ethnographic methods can reliably measure "Perceived Sovereignty" in oral-first AI systems?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] In Section 3.2, the authors propose "Perceived Sovereignty" as an evaluation metric but note it "requires community-centered, ethnographic evaluation methods." In Section 4.3, they link this to indigenous data governance frameworks like CARE.
- Why unresolved: Current conversational AI evaluation lacks validated qualitative methods for assessing user trust in data control, especially in low-resource indigenous contexts where sovereignty concerns are acute.
- What evidence would resolve it: A set of co-designed evaluation protocols (e.g., interview guides, scenario-based probes) validated through iterative testing with Guaraní-speaking communities to produce reproducible scores for perceived data control.

### Open Question 3
- Question: What are the turn-taking timing parameters (e.g., gap thresholds, overlap tolerance) in Guaraní conversation, and how should they guide system response latency?
- Basis in paper: [inferred] The Latency metric (Section 3.2) requires alignment with "Guaraní conversational tempo," and the authors cite cross-cultural variation in turn-taking (Stivers et al.). However, they provide no Guaraní-specific timing data.
- Why unresolved: Without empirical norms for Guaraní turn transitions, systems cannot avoid violating conversational timing expectations (preempting or lagging), which disrupts natural flow.
- What evidence would resolve it: Quantitative analysis of audio from natural Guaraní dialogues to establish typical gap durations, overlap frequencies, and repair timing, followed by A/B testing of system latency settings against user comfort metrics.

### Open Question 4
- Question: How can the Guaraní Understanding Agent be designed to robustly interpret code-switched utterances (Jopará) given limited training data and high variation?
- Basis in paper: [explicit] The authors note that daily use frequently involves "jopará and other forms of code-switching" (Section 4.1) and that the Understanding Agent must map "Guarani and Jopará speech to abstract intents" (Section 3). They also highlight the data bottleneck for conversational speech (Section 4.2).
- Why unresolved: Code-switching introduces lexical and syntactic variability; existing resources are scarce and not focused on spontaneous mixed speech, making intent recognition fragile.
- What evidence would resolve it: A benchmark dataset of annotated Jopará conversational utterances and/or a model that achieves high intent classification accuracy on such data through techniques like multilingual pretraining or data augmentation tested in multi-turn dialogue simulations.

## Limitations
*   **Purely Conceptual Proposal:** The architecture and evaluation metrics are theoretical, with no implemented prototype or empirical validation reported with Guaraní-speaking communities.
*   **Unverified Community Alignment:** The efficacy of the design for addressing diglossia, supporting authentic multi-turn repair, and meaningfully enhancing indigenous data sovereignty is assumed rather than demonstrated through ethnographic or participatory design work.
*   **Missing Linguistic Grounding:** The paper does not provide or cite specific empirical data on Guaraní conversational turn-taking norms, repair sequences, or code-switching patterns that would be necessary to implement the system's timing and understanding components correctly.

## Confidence
*   **Architecture Potential (Oral-First, Multi-Agent):** Medium. The conceptual separation of language understanding, state, governance, and action is sound for the identified problems but remains unproven in this domain.
*   **Proposed Evaluation Metrics (Task/Repair/Sovereignty/Latency):** Low. The metrics propose valuable constructs beyond completion rates, but their operational definitions, measurability, and validity for Guaraní oral interaction are entirely speculative.
*   **Addressing Indigenous Data Sovereignty & Diglossia:** Low. The asserted link between this technical design and profound socio-technical goals (self-determination, linguistic equity) is inspirational but lacks any evidence of technical feasibility, community uptake, or measured impact.
