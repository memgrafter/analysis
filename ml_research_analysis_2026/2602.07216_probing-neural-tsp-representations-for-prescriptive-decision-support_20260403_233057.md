# Probing Neural TSP Representations for Prescriptive Decision Support

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.07216
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07216
- **Reference count:** 36
- **Primary result:** Frozen embeddings from neural TSP solvers transfer to sensitivity ranking tasks; probe accuracy correlates with solver quality and surpasses baselines (65% top-1 for node-removal, 73% for edge-forbid).

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether internal representations learned by neural TSP solvers are transferable to prescriptive decision-support tasks beyond tour construction. The authors extract frozen encoder embeddings from trained attention-based TSP policies and train lightweight probes to rank nodes by removal impact and tour edges by forbiddance impact—both NP-hard sensitivity tasks requiring expensive optimal re-solves for labels. Probes achieve strong performance and their accuracy improves monotonically with solver quality across training checkpoints and model scales, suggesting that stronger TSP solvers yield more transferable representations. Controls confirm that gains stem from learned representations, not probe capacity or raw geometry.

## Method Summary
The authors train an AttentionModel TSP solver using REINFORCE on Euclidean TSP100 instances. After training, they freeze the encoder and extract node embeddings. They then train lightweight probes (linear, DeepSets, Transformer) on two downstream tasks: node-removal (identify node whose removal most reduces optimal tour length) and edge-forbid (identify tour edge whose forbiddance most increases length). Labels are generated by repeatedly solving TSP instances with Concorde under perturbations. The probes use candidate-specific features derived from the frozen embeddings (e.g., node embeddings, edge embeddings from endpoints). They compare against geometry-only baselines and ensembles with heuristics. To study transfer, they correlate probe accuracy with solver quality across training checkpoints and model sizes.

## Key Results
- For node-removal: transformer probe ensemble with geometric features reaches 65% top-1 accuracy vs. 58% for the best non-tour baseline.
- For edge-forbid: ensemble with a 2-opt repair proxy reaches 73% top-1 vs. 67%.
- Probe accuracy improves monotonically with solver quality across training checkpoints and model scales; gains continue even after tour-cost improvements plateau.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The encoder learns contextualized embeddings that implicitly encode global sensitivity structure (which nodes/edges most affect optimal tour length under perturbations).
- **Mechanism:** Training the TSP policy via RL forces the encoder to develop representations that distinguish nodes and edges by their contribution to tour quality. These same features prove accessible to lightweight probes for sensitivity ranking, even without decoder involvement.
- **Core assumption:** Optimization for tour construction induces representations that capture interdependencies relevant to what-if queries.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "probes achieve strong performance... probe accuracy improves monotonically with solver quality"
  - [section 5.2] Table 2 and Figure 3 show positive correlation between solver suboptimality and probe top-1 accuracy across checkpoints and model scales.
  - [corpus] Neighbors include "Probing Neural Combinatorial Optimization Models" (Zhang et al., 2025a) indicating community focus on probing NCO, but this paper's specific prescriptive tasks and correlation analysis are novel.
- **Break condition:** If the encoder is randomly initialized, probe performance drops to near chance (e.g., linear probe on edge-forbid: 0.13 top-1 vs. 0.41 with trained encoder; Table 1), indicating learned weights are essential.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Permutation-invariant set processing via attention enables the encoder to produce candidate features suitable for sensitivity scoring without positional bias.
- **Mechanism:** The Transformer encoder operates on unordered node sets; probes (DeepSets, Set Transformer) then score candidates in a permutation-equivariant manner. This matches the nature of sensitivity tasks where node/edge order is arbitrary.
- **Core assumption:** Sensitivity ranking is a set prediction problem; architectural inductive biases for sets are beneficial.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.3] "For node-removal... we use node embeddings hi directly"; for edge-forbid, "edge features from endpoint embeddings via concatenation with absolute difference" – no positional encodings used.
  - [section 5.1] DeepSets/Transformer probes substantially outperform linear probes, showing the value of set-aware decoders.
  - [corpus] Weak; neighboring works explore different architectures (e.g., diffusion models, mixture-of-experts) but do not evaluate set-probing for sensitivity.
- **Break condition:** Introducing explicit positional encodings tied to input order might harm performance on these set tasks, though the paper does not test this (assumption based on design).

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Training dynamics exhibit a curriculum where early learning captures local geometry, while later stages develop global structural features that transfer better to sensitivity tasks.
- **Mechanism:** Probe accuracy continues to improve even after tour-cost gains plateau (Figure 3), suggesting representation refinement beyond immediate solution quality. Larger models with more capacity achieve stronger transfer.
- **Core assumption:** The encoder's feature space evolves to encode finer-grained optimization-relevant distinctions as training progresses.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "probe accuracy improves monotonically with solver quality across training and model scale"
  - [section 5.2] "probe accuracy can continue to improve even after tour-cost improvements largely plateau"
  - [corpus] Neighbors like "Probing Neural Combinatorial Optimization Models" also analyze training dynamics but focus on different probing targets; this paper's correlation with solver quality is distinctive.
- **Break condition:** If training is stopped early, probe accuracy would be lower even if tour quality is acceptable; conversely, overfitting might eventually degrade transfer, but not observed in their runs.

## Foundational Learning
- **Probing**
  - *Why needed here:* To evaluate what information is encoded in frozen representations without altering the solver, isolating transfer potential.
  - *Quick check:* What is the key difference between training a probe and fine-tuning the full model on a downstream task?
- **Attention Mechanisms**
  - *Why needed here:* The TSP solver uses a Transformer encoder; understanding self-attention is necessary to interpret how node embeddings are contextualized globally.
  - *Quick check:* How does self-attention allow the model to consider relationships between non-adjacent nodes in a TSP instance?
- **Reinforcement Learning for Combinatorial Optimization**
  - *Why needed here:* The TSP policy is trained with REINFORCE because tour length is non-differentiable; grasping this explains why embeddings are learned via policy gradients rather than supervised targets.
  - *Quick check:* Why can't standard backpropagation be used to train the TSP solver directly?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  - TSP Encoder (Transformer) → Candidate Featurizer → Probe Decoder.
  - Baseline Modules: Geometry-only features, tour-based heuristics.
- **Critical path:**
  1. Generate labeled dataset by solving TSP100 with Concorde and re-optimizing under node-removal/edge-forbid to obtain sensitivity labels.
  2. Train TSP policies with REINFORCE; save checkpoints.
  3. For each checkpoint, extract frozen encoder embeddings for all instances.
  4. Train lightweight probes (linear, DeepSets, Transformer) on standardized embeddings and candidate features; tune hyperparameters on validation.
  5. Evaluate top-k accuracy and Spearman rank correlation on test sets.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Probe capacity: Simpler probes generalize better with limited data; complex probes risk overfitting.
  - Candidate set size: Edge-forbid restricts to tour edges (O(n)) to avoid O(n²) scaling; node-removal is inherently O(n).
  - Label cost: Exact labels require many Concorde solves (~50s/instance); using heuristics (2-opt) trades exactness for speed.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - Probe top-1 accuracy near chance (<0.2 for 100-node node-removal).
  - No monotonic correlation between probe performance and solver quality across checkpoints.
  - High variance across random seeds (>0.05 std in top-1) indicating unstable training or insufficient probe capacity.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Baseline comparison: Train geometry-only deep models and compare probe performance against hand-crafted baselines (nearest-neighbor, detour).
  2. Training dynamics: For a fixed model size, extract embeddings from multiple checkpoints; plot probe accuracy vs. policy suboptimality to confirm monotonic improvement.
  3. Random-init control: Extract embeddings from a randomly initialized encoder; train identical probe and verify dramatic accuracy drop.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Transfer to larger TSP/VRP with additional constraints?** The study was limited to Euclidean TSP100; extending to larger instances and richer constraints (time windows, capacities) may require approximate labeling or different evaluation protocols.
2. **Can we predict edge-forbid sensitivity for arbitrary (non-tour) edges efficiently?** The paper restricted candidates to tour edges to avoid O(n²) scaling; predicting for all edges is an open scaling challenge.
3. **How robust are the findings to different interdiction modeling choices?** They used Big-M cost inflation; real disruptions may involve partial unavailability or time-dependent effects, potentially altering representation utility.
4. **Do other NCO architectures yield more transferable representations?** Only AttentionModel was studied; comparing to POMO, DACT, etc., controlled for solver performance would test generalizability.

## Limitations
- Oracle label generation via Concorde is computationally expensive (~50s/instance), limiting dataset scale and potentially introducing heuristic bias (e.g., 2-opt proxy).
- Training details for the RL4CO solver (e.g., baseline EMA, data augmentations) are unreported, hindering exact replication.
- Probe hyperparameter selection process and validation reuse may overfit to evaluation; seed variance reporting is incomplete.

## Next Checks
1. Replicate training dynamics using fully specified hyperparameters (e.g., baseline EMA, augmentations) and verify monotonic probe accuracy vs. validation suboptimality across multiple seeds.
2. Ablate positional bias in probes by randomizing candidate order during training/inference; a significant drop would confirm set-equivariance as key.
3. Isolate geometry vs. learned features by training a linear probe on raw coordinates and simple geometric features (nearest neighbor, density) using the same labeling protocol; compare accuracy to the paper's geometry-only deep model.
