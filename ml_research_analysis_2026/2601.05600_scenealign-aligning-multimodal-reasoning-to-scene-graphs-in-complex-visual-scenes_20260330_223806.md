# SceneAlign: Aligning Multimodal Reasoning to Scene Graphs in Complex Visual Scenes

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2601.05600
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05600
- **Reference count**: 39
- **Primary result**: SceneAlign uses scene-graph perturbations to create contrastive reasoning pairs, improving MLLM faithfulness and accuracy by 3–5% on reasoning-intensive benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles unfaithful reasoning in multimodal large language models (MLLMs) when processing complex visual scenes. Existing preference-based methods often let models rely on language priors instead of visual grounding. The proposed SceneAlign framework leverages scene graphs—structured representations of entities, attributes, and relations—to identify reasoning-critical elements. It perturbs these elements through four strategies (swap, replace, shorten, overthink) to generate hard negative rationales that are linguistically plausible but visually inaccurate. These contrastive pairs are used in Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) to steer models toward fine-grained, structure-faithful reasoning. Evaluated on seven visual reasoning benchmarks, SceneAlign consistently boosts both answer accuracy and grounding fidelity, outperforming strong baselines and prior CoT-based preference methods.

## Method Summary
SceneAlign begins by extracting a scene graph (entities, attributes, relations) from the image and question using GPT-4o. A positive chain-of-thought (CoT) is generated by the MLLM and verified to be grounded in the scene graph. The subgraph referenced in the CoT is then isolated. Four perturbation operators are applied to this subgraph to create negative scene graphs: swapping attributes/relations, replacing nodes, shortening (removing elements), and overthinking (adding spurious details). Each negative graph generates a negative CoT via the MLLM. Negatives are filtered by Jaccard overlap with the positive CoT to avoid trivial or irrelevant samples, then diversity-sampled to ensure a challenging yet balanced set. Finally, DPO trains the MLLM using these preference pairs, optimizing it to prefer reasoning faithful to the true scene structure.

## Key Results
- Average improvements of 3–5% on reasoning-intensive tasks across seven benchmarks.
- Gains on vision-centric benchmarks (e.g., MME-RealWorld, GQA), demonstrating broader applicability.
- Outperforms both standard supervised fine-tuning and prior CoT-based preference construction methods.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: SceneAlign improves grounding faithfulness by exposing fine-grained structural errors through scene-graph perturbations.
- **Mechanism**: Perturbations (swap, replace, shorten, overthink) simulate realistic grounding failures, creating contrastive CoTs that train the model to distinguish visually grounded reasoning from plausible but incorrect alternatives.
- **Core assumption**: Scene graphs capture all relevant elements for reasoning; perturbations mimic actual failure modes.
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] "identifying reasoning-critical nodes and perturbing them through four targeted strategies that mimic typical grounding failures"; [section] "perturb the graph through controlled interventions... to simulate realistic grounding failures".
- **Break condition**: Incomplete scene graph extraction (missing critical entities/relations) prevents realistic failure simulation.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Diversity-based sampling improves negative quality by selecting medium-difficulty, structurally inconsistent samples.
- **Mechanism**: After Jaccard overlap filtering, a max-min criterion selects negatives that are semantically coherent yet structurally divergent from the positive, providing strong contrastive supervision.
- **Core assumption**: Hard negatives that are neither too similar nor too distant yield optimal learning signal.
- **Evidence anchors**: [section] "apply diversity-based sampling... select m diverse negatives via a max-min criterion"; [section] "challenging yet balanced set of counterfactuals".
- **Break condition**: Sampling fails to capture structurally relevant negatives, weakening contrastive signal.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: DPO with scene-graph grounded pairs steers the model toward structure-faithful reasoning.
- **Mechanism**: DPO optimizes the MLLM to assign higher likelihood to reasoning chains consistent with the true scene graph while penalizing grounding errors.
- **Core assumption**: Reference model and preference pairs sufficiently inform the desired behavior.
- **Evidence anchors**: [abstract] "contrastive pairs are used in Direct Preference Optimization to steer models toward fine-grained, structure-faithful reasoning"; [section] DPO objective formula.
- **Break condition**: Poorly constructed pairs or inappropriate DPO coefficient β cause misalignment.

## Architecture Onboarding

Component map: Scene graph generation (GPT-4o) -> Scene graph extraction -> CoT generation (MLLM) -> Perturbation operators -> Negative CoT generation -> Diversity sampling -> DPO training -> Optimized MLLM. Reference model π_ref -> Preference pairs P.

Critical path:
1. Generate scene graph from image+question.
2. Generate positive CoT grounded in the scene graph.
3. Extract the CoT-grounded subgraph.
4. Apply perturbations to create negative scene graphs.
5. Filter negatives by Jaccard overlap and perform diversity sampling.
6. Apply DPO using the resulting preference pairs.

Design tradeoffs:
- Perturbation complexity vs. computational cost (more operators increase negatives but raise cost).
- Overlap thresholds (γ_ℓ, γ_u) vs. negative quality (too strict yields few samples; too loose yields weak supervision).
- Number of negatives per instance vs. training efficiency (diminishing returns beyond 3–4).

Failure signatures:
- No improvement on reasoning benchmarks: likely poor scene graph quality or ineffective perturbations.
- Degraded performance on vision-centric tasks: possible over-alignment to structure at the expense of perception.
- Training instability: indicates issues with preference pair construction or DPO hyperparameters.

First 3 experiments:
1. Validate scene graph extraction quality on 100 random samples from A-OKVQA.
2. Test each perturbation operator independently to verify semantic coherence of negatives.
3. Run DPO with only one negative per instance to establish a baseline before scaling.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Multi-image/video generalization**: The framework focuses on single-image reasoning; static scene graphs may not capture temporal or cross-view consistency. Evidence from video or multi-image benchmarks is needed.
2. **Sensitivity to scene graph quality**: The paper acknowledges occasional redundant nodes but does not evaluate robustness to errors or missing elements in the extracted graphs. Systematic corruption experiments would clarify this.
3. **Human vs. automatic evaluation**: Reliance on GPT-based automatic evaluation may miss nuanced human judgments of reasoning quality. Human studies are required.
4. **Optimal number of negatives**: Testing 1–4 negatives shows diminishing returns beyond 3, but higher counts (5–10) and intermediate points remain unexplored for cost-benefit analysis.

## Limitations
- **Scene graph quality dependency**: The entire pipeline hinges on accurate GPT-4o extraction; missed entities/relations break perturbation realism.
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: Key DPO settings (learning rate, β) are not fully specified, risking non-reproducible performance.
- **Generalization trade-offs**: Potential over-alignment to structured reasoning could impair performance on vision-centric or perceptual tasks.

## Confidence
- **High**: The core mechanism of using scene-graph perturbations to expose grounding failures is well-supported by ablations and benchmark results.
- **Medium**: Diversity-based sampling effectively selects medium-difficulty negatives, but thresholds may need dataset-specific tuning.
- **Low**: Long-term stability and out-of-distribution generalization remain untested; behavior on novel visual concepts is uncertain.

## Next Checks

2. Isolate each perturbation operator on a small subset to confirm semantic coherence and individual impact.
3. Establish a DPO baseline with one negative per instance, then systematically increase negatives to find the optimal trade-off point.
