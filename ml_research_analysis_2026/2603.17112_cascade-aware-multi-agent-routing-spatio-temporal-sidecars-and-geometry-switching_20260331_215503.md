# Cascade-Aware Multi-Agent Routing: Spatio-Temporal Sidecars and Geometry-Switching

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.17112
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17112
- Reference count: 36
- Primary result: A 133-parameter spatio-temporal sidecar improves multi-agent routing win rate from 50.4% to 87.2% (+36.8 pp) on 250 real-world scenarios.

## Executive Summary
Current multi-agent schedulers are geometry-blind: they optimize local load and agent fitness without modeling how failure propagation differs across graph topologies. As a result, they cannot distinguish between tree-like structures where failures cascade exponentially and cyclic/dense graphs where cascades self-limit. The authors introduce a lightweight spatio-temporal sidecar that reads the live execution graph and failure history. It computes two risk scores—a Euclidean diffusion model for dense/cyclic subgraphs and a hyperbolic expansion model for tree-like subgraphs—and blends them using a compact learned gate (133 parameters). On 250 Genesis 3 scenarios, the sidecar raises the scheduler win rate from 50.4% to 87.2%, with gains up to +68 pp in tree-like regimes. The learned gate achieves held-out AUC 0.9247, and a structural baseline without learning generalizes perfectly to synthetic graphs. The work demonstrates that coupling temporal failure bursts with spatial topology is essential for robust routing under stress.

## Method Summary
The sidecar augments an existing scheduler. For each candidate route, it extracts nine structural features from the current directed execution graph (including shell-growth slope, cycle rank, and Poincaré curvature). These features feed two fixed risk scorers: a Euclidean diffusion model that accounts for adaptive recovery and bottlenecks, and a hyperbolic expansion model that captures geodesic spread in tree-like graphs. A small MLP (9–12–1) takes the same features and outputs a blend weight π trained on binary labels indicating which geometry yields a larger separation margin. The final risk score R = π·RHyp + (1−π)·REuc supplements the scheduler’s native score. The system also incorporates temporal failure intensity via a point process with burst detection. Ablations confirm that spatial structure is the decisive signal and that temporal modeling alone harms performance.

## Key Results
- Geometry-blind native scheduler win rate: 50.4%; sidecar (learned gate) win rate: 87.2% (+36.8 pp) on 250 Genesis 3 scenarios.
- Regime-dependent gains: up to +48 to +68 pp in tree-like stress regimes; more modest improvements elsewhere.
- The learned gate achieves held-out AUC 0.9247 (accuracy 86.4%). A structural baseline with zero learned parameters generalizes perfectly to BA, WS, ER graphs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Geometry-blind schedulers fail because they lack a model of how failure propagation differs between tree-like (exponential cascade) and cyclic (self-limiting) execution graphs.
- **Mechanism:** They score routes using only aggregated node load and agent fitness, treating all graph topologies equivalently if those aggregates match, ignoring structural differences that dictate cascade reach.
- **Core assumption:** Failure spread is fundamentally shaped by graph topology, not just local node conditions.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states the gap; Section 2 notes schedulers carry no model of graph shape; ablation shows temporal-only modeling underperforms native.
- **Break condition:** If the execution graph never shifts between structural regimes (pure tree or pure cycle), a fixed-geometry model would suffice.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** A spatio-temporal sidecar closes the observability gap by coupling temporal failure history with spatial graph structure to predict the appropriate propagation geometry.
- **Mechanism:** The sidecar extracts a 9-dimensional feature vector from the live execution graph and candidate route. It computes two independent risk scores (Euclidean and hyperbolic) and blends them via a gate.
- **Core assumption:** Neither temporal failure clustering nor static graph structure alone is sufficient; their joint analysis distinguishes supercritical from self-limiting cascades.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract describes the sidecar; Section 3.3 explains joint necessity; Section 8.4 shows temporal-only model yields worse than native win rate (48.0%).
- **Break condition:** If failure events are temporally independent and uncorrelated with topology, the temporal component adds no signal and may add noise.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** A compact learned geometry gate can reliably detect the current topological regime from live structural features and select the correct propagation model.
- **Mechanism:** A 9–12–1 MLP outputs a blend weight π trained on binary labels indicating which geometry yields larger route-separation margin. The risk score is R = π·RHyp + (1−π)·REuc.
- **Core assumption:** Geometry preference is a deterministic function of observable topological features like shell-growth slope and cycle rank.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract notes the 133-parameter gate; Section 5.2 gives the MLP formula; Section 8.5 reports held-out AUC 0.9247 and accuracy 86.4%.
- **Break condition:** If the execution graph distribution is highly homogeneous (always tree-like), the gate output collapses to a constant and provides no adaptive benefit over a fixed prior.

## Foundational Learning
### Geometric Graph Learning
- **Why needed:** Hyperbolic space naturally represents tree-like graphs with exponential volume growth, while Euclidean space suits dense/cyclic graphs with linear growth. This justifies maintaining two separate propagation models.
- **Quick check:** Why does a hyperbolic embedding model exponential cascade reach in a delegation tree?

### Spatio-Temporal Point Processes & Burst Detection
- **Why needed:** Temporal failure intensity and burst statistics capture clustered failure events, which when combined with spatial structure inform cascade risk.
- **Quick check:** What is the operational difference between a temporally clustered failure on a leaf node versus on a high-degree hub?

### Multi-Agent Orchestration & Execution Graphs
- **Why needed:** The problem arises in systems where tasks are routed through dynamic graphs of specialized agents; current schedulers lack propagation modeling.
- **Quick check:** In a system like LangGraph or AutoGen, what does a "route" represent, and what signals does a native scheduler typically use?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Execution Graph Snapshot (Gt) and Failure History (F) -> Feature Extractor (ϕ) -> Euclidean Scorer (REuc) & Hyperbolic Scorer (RHyp) -> Geometry Gate (π) -> Blender (R = π·RHyp + (1−π)·REuc) -> Native Scheduler (uses R alongside its existing score).

### Critical Path
For a candidate route r at time t: (1) Extract subgraph Gt[r] and compute structural features ϕ. (2) Compute REuc and RHyp using Gt and F. (3) Obtain π from gate using ϕ. (4) Blend scores to get R(r,t). (5) Native scheduler ranks routes using R as an additional signal.

### Design Tradeoffs
- Accuracy vs. Overhead: Learned gate adds ~305 µs and 5 KB, negligible vs. LLM inference, but requires training data. Structural baseline is free but less accurate.
- Fixed vs. Adaptive Geometry: Fixed hyperbolic prior excels only in tree-like regimes; adaptive switching preserves win rate across regimes but introduces calibration burden.
- Interpretability vs. Performance: Simple threshold on cycle-rank is transparent but underperforms learned gate by ~4.8 pp.

### Failure Signatures
- High native win rate in non_tree/mixed regimes: May indicate attack protocol confound; validate with random attack protocol (Table 9).
- Gate degradation on BA/ER graphs: Sign of distribution shift; fine-tune gate on target graph family needed.
- Temporal-only model underperforming native: Confirms spatial structure is prerequisite; temporal alone adds noise.
- Low gate AUC in churn regime (0.726, ECE 0.120): Volatility makes geometry preference unstable; may need more data or robust features.

### First 3 Experiments
1. Overall Benchmark (Table 2): Native vs. structural baseline vs. learned gate on 250 Genesis 3 scenarios; validates geometry-aware modeling lift.
2. Regime-Specific Evaluation (Table 4): Disaggregate by topology stress regime; shows largest gains in tree-like regimes.
3. Component Decomposition (Table 5): Ablates spatial vs. temporal components; confirms spatial signals are decisive and temporal modeling alone is harmful.

## Limitations
- Missing parameter values: Exact temporal half-life, burst decay, and scorer weights are unreported, hindering exact replication.
- Data access barrier: Genesis 3 real traces are not released; only synthetic graphs are available, limiting full reproducibility.
- Generalization concerns: Learned gate degrades on out-of-distribution graph families and volatile regimes, indicating possible overfitting.

## Confidence
- Geometry-blind scheduler failure: Medium (ablation evidence and lift support claim, but causal link indirect).
- Spatio-temporal sidecar effectiveness: High (empirical +36.8 pp win rate increase on 250 scenarios with consistent ablations).
- Learned geometry gate reliability: High for in-distribution (AUC 0.9247); Medium for out-of-distribution (degradation observed on BA/WS/ER).

## Next Checks
1. Parameter sweep: Vary temporal half-life h (0.5–5 s) and burst decay δ (0.1–0.9) to assess robustness of win-rate improvement.
2. Cross-framework test: Integrate the sidecar into a widely-used multi-agent system (e.g., LangGraph or AutoGen) and evaluate on a standard routing benchmark.
3. Rule-based vs learned gate: Replace MLP gate with deterministic threshold on cycle-rank and compare win rate, overhead, and calibration error (ECE).
