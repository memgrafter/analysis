# Bielik-Q2-Sharp: A Comparative Study of Extreme 2-bit Quantization Methods for a Polish 11B Language Model

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.04162  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04162  
- Reference count: 12  
- Primary result: Six 2‑bit quantization methods compared on Polish Bielik‑11B; QuIP# E8P12 nearly matches community baseline (71.92% vs 72.07%) with ~3.26 GB size; rotation‑based methods fail autoregressive generation despite good MC scores.

## Executive Summary
This study systematically compares six state‑of‑the‑art post‑training quantization (PTQ) methods for extreme 2‑bit compression of the Polish 11B‑parameter model Bielik‑11B‑Instruct. Using a shared Polish calibration set (CulturaX‑PL), the authors evaluate each method across 22 Polish benchmarks, revealing a critical evaluation gap: rotation‑based methods (SpinQuant, ButterflyQuant) preserve multiple‑choice log‑likelihood scores but fail during autoregressive text generation. The QuIP# variant (E8P12) reaches near parity with the community IQ2_XXS baseline (71.92% vs 72.07% average) with a modest size increase. The QTIP method demonstrates superior per‑bit efficiency, matching VPTQ quality at 35% smaller size. The work highlights the importance of full generative evaluation and provides all artifacts publicly at a total cost of ~$285.

## Method Summary
The authors quantize Bielik‑11B‑Instruct (Mistral architecture) to true 2‑bit precision using six PTQ methods: QuIP# (E8 lattice vector quantization with random Hadamard rotations), SpinQuant+GPTQ (learned rotations with scalar quantization), ButterflyQuant+E8P (Kronecker butterfly rotations with E8 lattice), QTIP (trellis‑coded quantization with e2e finetuning), VPTQ (residual vector quantization), and AQLM (additive multi‑codebook quantization). All methods use the same Polish calibration corpus (CulturaX‑PL) to generate Hessian matrices for weighted rounding. The base model’s embedding and LM head layers are retained in FP16. QTIP undergoes additional 4‑epoch end‑to‑end finetuning on FineWeb‑Edu to improve generation quality. Evaluation covers 22 Polish tasks, split into multiple‑choice (MC) and generative, with normalized scores computed against official baselines.

## Key Results
- QuIP# (E8P12) achieves 71.92% average on 22 Polish benchmarks, nearly matching the IQ2_XXS community baseline (72.07%) with a model size of 3.26 GB.
- QTIP yields the best per‑bit efficiency (79.4% normalized MC accuracy at ~2.4 bpw) while matching VPTQ’s quality at 35% smaller size (3.27 GB).
- A critical failure mode: rotation‑based methods (SpinQuant, ButterflyQuant) maintain MC scores but produce incoherent autoregressive generation, exposing an evaluation gap in prior work.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: MC‑Generation Dissociation via Missing Runtime Transforms
Rotation‑based quantization methods (SpinQuant, ButterflyQuant) preserve log‑likelihood (multiple‑choice) scores but fail catastrophically during autoregressive generation because essential runtime transforms (R3 for KV cache, R4 for FFN) are omitted. MC evaluation computes log‑probabilities in a single forward pass; errors from missing transforms affect all options equally, preserving relative rankings. Autoregressive generation compounds errors token‑by‑token: each new token relies on prior outputs that already contain uncorrected quantization drift, causing rapid coherence collapse. The learned rotations (R1, R2) are fused into weights at compile time, but auxiliary transforms must be applied at every decoding step and are unsupported by standard inference engines. Evidence: Section 6.1 explicitly describes the dissociation; neighbor papers (SpinQuant, ButterflyQuant) describe the transforms but do not report this failure, implying an implementation gap.

### Mechanism 2: E8 Lattice Vector Quantization Exploits Weight Correlations
QuIP# uses an E8 lattice codebook for 8‑dimensional vector quantization, capturing inter‑weight correlations to approach scalar quantization’s information‑theoretic limits at 2 bits. Weight matrices are first decorrelated via a random Hadamard transform (RHT). Then blocks of 8 weights are quantized jointly to the nearest lattice point in E8 (optimal sphere packing in 8D). This vector quantization reduces distortion by sharing bits across correlated weights. The lattice codebook is tiny (~1 KB) and fits in L1 cache. Evidence: Section 3.3 describes the pipeline; neighbor paper QuIP# introduces the lattice.

### Mechanism 3: Trellis Coded Quantization Achieves Per‑Bit Efficiency via Computational Codebooks
QTIP replaces fixed lattice codebooks with trellis‑coded quantization (TCQ) in 256 dimensions, using codevectors generated on‑the‑fly as sums of basis vectors along a trellis. Only the small basis set needs storage, enabling a high‑dimensional codebook with minimal size overhead and improving the rate‑distortion tradeoff. The end‑to‑end finetuning further aligns the quantized model to the original distribution. Evidence: Section 3.6 describes TCQ and computational codebooks; neighbor paper QTIP introduces the method.

## Foundational Learning
- **Post‑Training Quantization (PTQ)**: PTQ quantizes a pre‑trained model without additional training, offering a low‑cost path to compression. It differs from quantization‑aware training (QAT) which requires retraining with simulated quantization. Why needed: All six methods are PTQ, balancing speed, data requirements, and fidelity. Quick check: What are the main tradeoffs between PTQ and QAT?
- **Vector Quantization (VQ)**: VQ quantizes groups of weights jointly, beating scalar quantization’s information‑theoretic limits by exploiting correlations. Why needed: QuIP#, QTIP, VPTQ, AQLM all use VQ at block, trellis, residual, or additive levels to achieve extreme 2‑bit performance. Quick check: Why can VQ achieve lower distortion than scalar quantization at the same average bitrate?
- **Rotation‑Based Outlier Suppression**: Orthogonal rotations (e.g., Hadamard) spread outlier energy across weight blocks, making quantized values more robust. Why needed: Several methods (QuIP#’s RHT, SpinQuant, ButterflyQuant) rely on rotations to handle the extreme dynamic range of 2‑bit quantization. Quick check: What is the intuition behind using random or learned rotations to improve quantization robustness?

## Architecture Onboarding
Base model: Bielik‑11B‑v2.3‑Instruct (Mistral architecture, 50 layers, 4096 hidden dim, 14336 intermediate). Calibration data: CulturaX‑PL (512 sequences × 4096 tokens). Hessians: 200 files (25.6 GB) per‑layer second‑order statistics. Quantization pipelines: six variants (A–F). Evaluation: Open Polish LLM Leaderboard (22 tasks) + eq_bench. Infrastructure: Cloud H200 GPUs (vast.ai) for heavy compute; local RTX 5070 for prototyping.

Component map:
Bielik‑11B‑v2.3‑Instruct -> CulturaX‑PL -> Hessians -> Quantization variants (A–F) -> Evaluation harness

Critical path:
1. Generate shared Hessians from CulturaX‑PL (~40 min on H200).
2. Apply method‑specific quantization (handle Mistral→Llama patches; watch for OOM with rotations).
3. Evaluate MC tasks first, then generative tasks (expensive, ~80% of eval time); ensure consistent baselines.

Design tradeoffs:
- Size vs. quality: True 2‑bit (~2.4 bpw) vs higher effective bitrate (VPTQ ~3.58 bpw).
- Rotation complexity: Learned rotations (SpinQuant) may improve MC but require custom inference; random rotations (QuIP#) are simpler.
- Calibration language: Polish Hessians likely capture morphology better but limit generalizability.
- Evaluation completeness: MC‑only tests miss generation failures; full evaluation must include both.

Failure signatures:
- High proxy error (e.g., >0.01 in Layer 0) indicates poor weight reconstruction.
- Good MC scores but incoherent generation: missing R3/R4 transforms (rotation methods).
- OOM during rotation learning (SpinQuant) or butterfly storage (ButterflyQuant).
- Mismatched baselines: using Hessians from base model instead of Instruct.

First 3 experiments:
1. Reproduce IQ2_XXS baseline with llama.cpp to establish reference score on the 22‑task Polish leaderboard.
2. Generate a single Hessian file from CulturaX‑PL using the quip‑sharp pipeline and validate its shape/format (e.g., [4096, 4096] for attention).
3. Run QuIP# E8P12 on one layer of Bielik‑11B, evaluate one MC task (e.g., piqa), and verify the score is within 1% of the reported layer‑wise proxy error.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
What is the root cause of the MC‑generation dissociation in rotation‑based methods (SpinQuant, ButterflyQuant), and can implementing the missing runtime transforms fully restore generation quality? The dissociation is documented (Section 6.1) and proposed as a solution (Future Work #3), but it is unclear if implementing R3/R4 will yield generation quality matching MC performance, or if rotation inherently harms long‑context generation. Evidence: Variant B fails due to missing transforms; Variant C fails even after decompression to FP16, suggesting additional issues.

### Open Question 2
Does using a Polish‑language calibration corpus significantly improve 2‑bit quantization quality for a Polish LLM compared to an English corpus, and is this effect attributable to morphological richness? The study used Polish calibration but provided no ablation. Future Work #2 calls for a formal Polish vs. English comparison. Without controlled comparison, the claimed morphology benefit is speculative.

### Open Question 3
Is the ~79% MC accuracy ceiling observed across four methods an inherent limit for extreme 2‑bit compression of Bielik‑11B, or does it generalize to other Slavic language models and architectures? The ceiling (78.1–79.4%) is found on a single model (depth‑upscaled Mistral) and language (Polish). It may be model‑specific. Future Work #6 asks if it holds for other Slavic models.

### Open Question 4
How do generative capabilities compare under uniform evaluation, and is knowledge distillation (e2e finetuning) necessary for coherent generation at 2‑bit? The paper lacks a complete generative comparison: AQLM not evaluated generationally; VPTQ skipped; QTIP results from finetuned model while others are not. Thus, unclear which methods preserve generative coherence or whether finetuning closes the gap.

## Limitations
- Reproduction gaps: Exact Hessian generation CLI, evaluation harness commands, and Mistral→Llama patch diffs are unspecified.
- Generalizability: Findings rely on Polish‑specific calibration; performance on other languages is unknown.
- Rotation method failures: The MC‑generation dissociation is attributed to missing runtime transforms, but this is inferred, not formally ablated.

## Confidence
- **High**: Reported benchmark scores, model sizes, and qualitative generation failures for rotation methods.
- **Medium**: Causal explanation (missing R3/R4) for MC‑generation dissociation; per‑bit efficiency claims for QTIP.
- **Low**: The assumption that implementing R3/R4 would fix rotation methods; extrapolation of Polish calibration benefits to other morphologically rich languages.
