# Understanding Unreliability of Steering Vectors in Language Models: Geometric Predictors and the Limits of Linear Approximations

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.17881
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17881
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Steering reliability for a given behavior is predictable from the geometric relationship between individual activation differences and the mean steering vector (directional agreement) and the separability of positive and negative activations along that direction (d').

## Executive Summary
Steering vectors, which adjust model behavior by adding a learned bias to activations, often work unreliably across individual samples. This study investigates why some behaviors are more steerable than others using Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA) on 36 diverse datasets with Llama 2-7B-Chat. Two geometric properties of the training activations strongly predict steering success: (1) the consistency of individual activation differences with the final steering vector (directional agreement), and (2) how well-positive and negative activations separate when projected onto the steering direction (discriminability index d'). Higher directional agreement and larger d' correlate with larger average steering effects and fewer cases where steering backfires ("anti-steerable" samples). Experimenting with different prompt formats to generate steering vectors shows that while the vectors themselves can differ substantially, their overall efficacy across datasets is highly correlated. This suggests that unreliability is an inherent property of certain behavioral representations, likely because the linear function class of CAA fails when the true latent behavior direction is non-linear or requires a higher-rank transformation.

## Method Summary
The study employs Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA) to steer a Llama 2-7B-Chat model across 36 binary-choice Model-Written Evaluations (MWE). For each dataset, a steering vector is computed at layer 13 as the mean difference between activations from "positive" (behavior-matching) and "negative" (behavior-non-matching) training prompts. The primary evaluation uses 500 held-out base prompts. Steering effectiveness is measured by the per-sample change in the logit difference between the two answer tokens (ΔmLD), the fraction of samples that move in the wrong direction (anti-steerable), and a steerability rank derived from the slope of mean mLD over varying steering strengths λ. Two geometric predictors are calculated from the training set: (1) directional agreement—mean cosine similarity between individual activation differences and the steering vector; (2) separability—the discriminability index d' obtained by projecting activations onto the difference-of-means line. Additional experiments vary the prompt type used to generate training examples (prefilled, instructed, few-shot) to assess the impact of data collection on the steering vector's direction and efficacy.

## Key Results
- Higher directional agreement between individual activation differences and the mean steering vector strongly predicts more reliable steering (Spearman ρ ≥ 0.76 with effect size, ρ ≤ -0.78 with anti-steerable fraction, p < 1e-6).
- Better separability of positive and negative training activations along the steering direction (higher d') correlates with greater steerability (ρ ≈ ±0.74 with key metrics).
- Steering vectors derived from different prompt types point in diverse directions (cosine similarities as low as 0.07), yet their mean steering effects across datasets are highly correlated (Pearson r ≥ 0.80), indicating that unreliability is an inherent characteristic of the behavior's representation geometry rather than an artifact of the specific training prompts.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Higher directional agreement between individual activation differences and the steering vector leads to more reliable steering.
- **Mechanism**: CAA assumes the behavior is encoded linearly. The steering vector is the mean of activation differences. When individual differences consistently point in similar directions, this mean reliably captures a true linear feature direction. Adding this vector then consistently shifts representations toward the intended state. Scattered differences mean the mean approximates no single direction, causing inconsistent effects.
- **Core assumption**: The behavior is linearly representable in the activation space.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [Abstract] “First, I find that higher directional agreement between individual activation differences predicts more reliable steering.”
  - [Section 5.2] Figures 8–9 show significant negative correlation (ρ = -0.75) between steerability rank and mean cosine similarity, also positive correlation with effect size (ρ = 0.76).
  - [Corpus] Related work on non-identifiability notes that misaligned activation differences prevent unique recovery of a steering direction.
- **Break condition**: Mean cosine similarity below ~0.2 leads to erratic effects and many anti-steerable samples.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Better separability of positive and negative activations along the steering direction leads to more reliable steering.
- **Mechanism**: The discriminability index d' measures separation when activations are projected onto the steering direction (μ₊ – μ₋). A high d' means narrow, well-separated distributions. A constant offset λ moves most negative samples across the decision boundary. Overlap due to within-class variation leaves some samples negative or even pushes them further negative.
- **Core assumption**: The output logits follow a roughly linear classifier along that direction.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [Abstract] “Second, I observe that better separation along the steering direction is more reliably steerable.”
  - [Section 5.5] Figure 12 shows high-steerable datasets (d' ≈ 8.94) vs. low-steerable (d' ≈ 1.25). Figure 13 reports significant Spearman correlation of d' with steerability rank (ρ = -0.74), effect size (ρ = 0.74).
- **Break condition**: d' < 1.5 correlates with >40% anti-steerable samples.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: CAA's linear function class fails when latent behavior representations are non-linear, and this failure is inherent to the behavior, not the prompt engineering.
- **Mechanism**: CAA uses a one-dimensional offset f(a)=a+λs. If the true transformation from negative to positive states is non-linear (e.g., curved manifold, requires multiple directions), no single s works reliably. Different prompts yield distinct s (low cosine), yet their efficacy correlates, implying the limitation lies in the representation geometry, not the training data.
- **Core assumption**: Some behaviors require non-linear or higher-rank updates.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [Abstract] “The results suggest that CAA’s linear function class fails when latent behavior representations are non-linear.”
  - [Section 5.7] Figure 20 shows diverse cosine similarities (0.07–0.86) between vectors from different prompt types, but Figure 21a shows correlated mean logit differences across datasets (Pearson r ≥ 0.80). Figure 19 confirms all prompt types retain high variance.
- **Break condition**: When both directional agreement (cosine < 0.3) and d' (< 2) are low, consider richer function classes (affine, learned MLP steering).

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Prompt Generator -> Activation Collector -> Steering Vector Module -> Steering Applier -> Evaluation Module
- **Critical path**: Generate training prompts (positive/negative) -> Record activations at layer 13 -> Compute mean difference s -> Apply s·λ during test inference -> Compare steered vs. unsteered logit differences.
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Layer choice: Mid-layers (e.g., l=13 for Llama-2-7B) often optimal.
  - Training size: ~250 samples needed for directional convergence.
  - Steering strength λ: Default 1; may need calibration per dataset.
  - Prompt type: Affects vector direction but not mean efficacy; prefilled prompts may slow convergence.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Mean cosine similarity between training Δ and s < 0.2.
  - d' < 1.5 indicating overlapping clusters.
  - Large variance in per-sample ΔmLD.
  - Anti-steerable fraction >30%.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. Convergence analysis: Measure cosine similarity between steering vectors computed from increasing training subset sizes and the full-data vector.
  2. Directional agreement vs. steerability: Correlate mean cosine similarity with steerability rank, mean ΔmLD, and anti-steerable fraction across datasets.
  3. Separability vs. steerability: Compute d' for each dataset and correlate with the same steerability metrics.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: Do the geometric predictors (directional agreement and separability) generalize to other language model architectures and steering methods?
- **Basis**: The study only evaluates Llama 2-7B-Chat with CAA. Future work is needed for cross-architecture validation and for more expressive methods like MiMiC or ACE.
- **Why unresolved**: No evidence yet from other models or non-linear steering techniques.
- **What evidence would resolve**: Systematic replication across diverse models and steering methods, testing if the same correlations hold.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Can we causally improve steering reliability by shaping activation geometry during training (e.g., curating data to maximize directional agreement)?
- **Basis**: Findings are correlational; no intervention has shown that increasing directional agreement or separability causes improved steerability.
- **Why unresolved**: It is unclear if observed geometry is a cause or merely an indicator of an underlying, hard-to-modify representation.
- **What evidence would resolve**: Interventional studies where training datasets are explicitly constructed to maximize directional agreement or separability, with steering reliability compared to controls.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: Can we predict a behavior's steerability before training, using only a behavioral description or a small set of unlabeled prompts?
- **Basis**: Current methodology requires training a vector and evaluating many test samples to measure steerability.
- **Why unresolved**: A pre-training predictor would enable efficient decision-making about whether to use CAA or more complex methods.
- **What evidence would resolve**: A model that takes a text description or few exemplars of a target behavior and outputs a score that strongly correlates with empirically observed steerability across many behaviors and models.

## Limitations
- **Model scope**: Findings are restricted to a single model (Llama 2-7B-Chat); generalization to other architectures is unproven.
- **Dataset scope**: Evaluated only on 36 binary-choice MWE datasets; broader behavioral domains remain unexplored.
- **Method scope**: Analysis focuses on CAA; results may not apply to more sophisticated steering techniques that use affine transformations, sparse edits, or learned functions.

## Confidence
- **High**: Core correlations between directional agreement/d' and steerability metrics (ρ ≥ 0.74, p < 1e-6) are robust across 36 datasets.
- **Medium**: Claim that unreliability is inherent to the behavior, not data collection, based on correlated efficacy across diverse prompt types; however, exact prompt templates are missing, limiting full replication.
- **Medium**: Inference that CAA fails due to a mismatched linear function class; supported by geometry but not by direct comparison to non-linear alternatives.
- **Low**: Generalizability to other models and steering methods—no evidence provided.
