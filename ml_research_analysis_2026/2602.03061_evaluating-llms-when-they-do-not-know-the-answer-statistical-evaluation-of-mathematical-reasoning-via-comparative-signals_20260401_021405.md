# Evaluating LLMs When They Do Not Know the Answer: Statistical Evaluation of Mathematical Reasoning via Comparative Signals

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2602.03061
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03061
- **Reference count**: 6
- **Primary result**: One‑step EIF estimator reduces variance of LLM accuracy estimates by up to 12 percentage points on small benchmarks (e.g., AIME 2025 with 15 samples).

## Executive Summary
Large language models (LLMs) exhibit high variance when evaluated on mathematical reasoning benchmarks, making model rankings unreliable, especially with few samples. This paper proposes a novel variance‑reduction technique that repurposes the target model’s own pairwise comparisons between auxiliary reasoning chains as control variates. By deriving the Efficient Influence Function (EIF) within a semiparametric framework, the authors construct a one‑step estimator that attains the semiparametric efficiency bound, guaranteeing optimal asymptotic variance. Evaluated on GPQA Diamond, AIME 2025, and GSM8K, the method consistently outperforms the naive sample mean, with absolute error reductions of up to 12 points on AIME 2025 when only 15 samples are used.

## Method Summary
For each problem instance, the data consists of a prompt \(X\), the target model’s response \(Y\), and the ground truth \(G\). Additionally, two auxiliary reasoning chains \(W_1, W_2\) are generated by separate auxiliary models, and the target model is asked to prefer one chain, yielding a binary signal \(V\). The tuple \(Z=(W_1,W_2,V)\) constitutes the auxiliary signal. The target parameter is \(\theta = \mathbb{E}[\phi(Y,G)]\), where \(\phi\) is the correctness indicator.

The estimator leverages the conditional expectation \(\tau(X,Z) = \mathbb{E}[\phi(Y,G) \mid X,Z]\) as a control variate. Since \(\tau\) is unknown, it is estimated from data as \(\hat\tau\). For small datasets (\(N\le 50\)), \(\hat\tau\) is obtained by prompting a strong LLM (e.g., Gemini‑3‑Flash) to output the probability that the target answer is correct given \(X,W_1,W_2,V\). For larger datasets, a logistic regression classifier is trained on frozen embeddings of \(X,W_1,W_2\) and the binary \(V\) to predict \(\phi\). The conditional mean \(\hat m(X)=\mathbb{E}[\hat\tau(X,Z)\mid X]\) is approximated by Monte Carlo averaging over \(M=10\) independently sampled auxiliary triples per \(X\).

Cross‑fitting with \(K=5\) folds is employed to avoid overfitting: for each fold, \(\hat\tau\) is trained on the other folds and used to predict \(\tau\) for the held‑out instances. The final one‑step estimator is
\[
\hat\theta = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \left( \hat m(X_i) + \phi(Y_i,G_i) - \hat\tau(X_i,Z_i) \right),
\]
where \(Z_i\) is a designated auxiliary sample (e.g., the first one). Under consistency of \(\hat\tau\) and \(\hat m\) and the assumption that \(p(Z\mid X)\) is known (approximated by the Monte Carlo sampling), this estimator achieves the semiparametric efficiency bound, i.e., it has the smallest possible asymptotic variance among regular estimators.

## Key Results
- The one‑step estimator yields lower variance than the naive sample mean on all three benchmarks, with gains increasing under higher noise.
- On AIME 2025 with only 15 samples, the absolute error of the accuracy estimate is reduced by up to 12 percentage points compared to the naive estimator.
- The method produces more reliable model rankings (e.g., higher Kendall’s Tau) in small‑sample regimes.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The variance reduction stems from the correlation between the auxiliary preference signal \(V\) and the latent correctness of the target response. The quantity \(\tau(X,Z)=\mathbb{E}[\phi(Y,G)\mid X,Z]\) acts as an optimal (minimum variance) linear predictor of \(\phi\) given the available auxiliary information. The correction term \(\hat\tau(X_i,Z_i)-\hat m(X_i)\) has zero expectation by construction but is correlated with \(\phi(Y_i,G_i)\). Subtracting it from the observed \(\phi(Y_i,G_i)\) therefore cancels a portion of the random noise, leading to a lower‑variance estimate of \(\theta\). The Efficient Influence Function (EIF) provides the exact form of the estimator that achieves the semiparametric efficiency bound: it is the debiased version of the naive estimator, where the bias arises from the estimation of the nuisance \(\tau\) and \(m\). Cross‑fitting ensures that the estimated nuisances are (nearly) independent of the residuals required for the asymptotic linearity, preserving the double‑robustness property—i.e., if either \(\hat\tau\) or \(\hat m\) is inconsistent but the other is consistent, the estimator remains consistent. Under correct specification, the estimator’s asymptotic variance equals the Cramér–Rao lower bound for the semiparametric model, making it statistically efficient.

## Foundational Learning
- **Semiparametric efficiency bound**: The theoretical lower bound on the variance of any regular estimator for a given statistical functional. *Why needed*: Guarantees that the proposed one‑step estimator is optimal in the sense of having the smallest possible variance. *Quick check*: What does it mean for an estimator to attain the efficiency bound?
- **Efficient Influence Function (EIF)**: A function that characterizes the first‑order behavior of any estimator of the target functional; its variance defines the efficiency bound. *Why needed*: The EIF directly yields the one‑step estimator with desirable asymptotic properties. *Quick check*: Derive the EIF for the mean of a response under a known conditional distribution.
- **Control variates**: Auxiliary random variables correlated with the target, used to reduce estimation variance. *Why needed*: The auxiliary preferences \(V\) (and the chains \(W_1,W_2\)) serve as control variates that provide a proxy for the unknown correctness indicator. *Quick check*: If \(\mathrm{Cov}(\phi, Z) \neq 0\), how does using \(Z\) as a control variate change the variance of \(\phi\)?
- **Cross‑fitting**: A technique that uses out‑of‑sample predictions when estimating nuisance functions to maintain independence from the estimating equations. *Why needed*: Prevents overfitting of \(\hat\tau\) and \(\hat m\) that could bias the one‑step estimator, especially in small samples. *Quick check*: In a \(K\)‑fold cross‑fitting scheme, how are the nuisance functions trained and applied to each fold?
- **Monte Carlo approximation**: Estimating an expectation by averaging over random samples. *Why needed*: Approximates \(m(X)=\mathbb{E}[\tau(X,Z)\mid X]\) using \(M\) additional auxiliary draws per instance. *Quick check*: How does the number of Monte Carlo samples \(M\) affect the bias and variance of \(\hat m(X)\)?
- **Logistic regression with embeddings**: A flexible model for predicting a binary outcome from high‑dimensional features, often built on frozen LLM embeddings. *Why needed*: Provides a data‑driven way to estimate \(\tau(X,Z)\) for larger datasets where semantic LLM prompting is too costly. *Quick check*: What are the advantages and disadvantages of using frozen embeddings compared to training a classifier from scratch?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Raw benchmark instances (X, G) → target model generates Y → auxiliary models generate W1, W2 → target model preference V → store (X,Y,G,W1,W2,V) → for each X, draw M additional auxiliary triples Z_j → split data into K folds → for each fold, train τ̂(X,Z) on other folds using either semantic LLM or logistic regression → compute m̂(X) = (1/M) Σ_{j=2}^{M+1} τ̂(X, Z_j) → for each instance i, compute ψ_i = m̂(X_i) + ϕ(Y_i,G_i) - τ̂(X_i, Z_i1) → average ψ_i to obtain θ̂.

### Critical Path
The longest poles are: (1) generating auxiliary reasoning chains and preferences, which requires multiple LLM calls per problem; (2) estimating τ, either via repeated expensive LLM prompts (semantic regime) or by training a classifier on embeddings (data‑driven regime); (3) Monte Carlo averaging for m̂. Failures in any of these steps directly impact estimator quality.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Semantic vs. data‑driven τ**: Semantic regression (LLM as universal approximator) is flexible but prompt‑sensitive and costly; data‑driven logistic regression is cheaper and scalable but depends on feature quality and may underfit in very small N.
- **Monte Carlo samples M**: Larger M reduces the approximation error of m̂ but increases auxiliary generation cost; M=10 is used as a default.
- **Cross‑fit folds K**: Higher K (e.g., 10) reduces bias from nuisance estimation but increases computational overhead of training multiple models; K=5 is chosen as a compromise.

### Failure Signatures
- **Weak control variate**: Correlation between (τ̂ - m̂) and ϕ(Y,G) is near zero → variance not reduced. Diagnostic: compute empirical correlation on held‑out data.
- **Poor τ calibration**: R² of τ̂ against true correctness falls below 0.1 → likely introduces bias or eliminates variance gains. Diagnostic: evaluate on a subset with known ground truth.
- **Unstable rankings**: Model rankings (e.g., Exact Match or Kendall’s Tau) vary significantly across different random seeds → hints at high stochasticity in V or insufficient M.

### First Experiments
1. **Baseline variance comparison**: On a fixed small subset (N=15) of a benchmark, compute both the naive sample mean and the one‑step estimator. Use bootstrap resampling to estimate their variances; confirm the one‑step estimator has lower variance.
2. **Prompt sensitivity for V**: Implement three distinct wording templates for querying the target model’s pairwise preference (e.g., “Which reasoning chain is more likely to lead to the correct answer?” vs. “Select the better chain.”). For each, compute the correlation between (τ̂ - m̂) and ϕ, and compare the resulting variance reduction. Identify the most robust template.
3. **τ estimation quality check**: For both semantic (LLM) and logistic approaches, measure the R² of τ̂ against true correctness on a held‑out set. If R² < 0.2, iterate on prompt engineering or feature representation. Verify that higher R² correlates with larger gains in estimator variance.

## Limitations
- Prompt templates for auxiliary reasoning generation and the pairwise comparison (V) are not specified, so their exact impact on signal quality is unknown.
- The semantic regression setup for small N lacks details on input formatting and output parsing, risking poor calibration of τ estimates.
- The theoretical assumption that \(p(Z\mid X)\) is known is approximated via Monte Carlo with finite M; the resulting finite‑sample bias is not quantified.

## Confidence
- **Semiparametric efficiency bound and asymptotic theory**: High
- **Empirical results on the three benchmarks**: Medium
- **Replication of the reported 12‑point error reduction on AIME 2025**: Low
