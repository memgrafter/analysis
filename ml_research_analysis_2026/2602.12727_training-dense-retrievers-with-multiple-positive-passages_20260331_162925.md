# Training Dense Retrievers with Multiple Positive Passages

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.12727
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12727
- **Reference Count:** 40
- **Primary Result:** LSEPair loss consistently achieves state-of-the-art dense retrieval performance (e.g., 77.01% Acc@20 on NQ, 39.49% avg NDCG@10 on BEIR) and demonstrates robustness to heterogeneous positive quality, outperforming other multi-positive objectives.

## Executive Summary
Dense retrievers are typically trained with a single positive passage per query due to annotation constraints, leading to false-negative noise when other relevant passages are incorrectly treated as negatives. The proliferation of LLMs enables cheap generation of multiple positive passages per query. This paper systematically studies how to optimally incorporate these multiple positives within a contrastive learning framework. It unifies several loss functions—Joint Likelihood (JointLH), Summed Marginal Likelihood (SumMargLH), Log-Sum-Exp Pairwise (LSEPair), and random sampling (Rand1LH)—and analyzes their distinct gradient behaviors in allocating probability mass across positives. Empirical results across Natural Questions, MS MARCO, and BEIR benchmarks show that LSEPair is the most robust and effective objective, as its gradient prioritizes lower-scoring (harder) positives and enforces strict positive-negative separation. In contrast, JointLH and SumMargLH are highly sensitive to the quality distribution of positives.

## Method Summary
The study frames multi-positive integration as extensions of the standard InfoNCE contrastive loss. For a query *q*, a candidate set *D* consists of a set of positive passages *D⁺* (size *M*) and negatives *D⁻*. The core approaches differ in how they aggregate the loss over the multiple positives:
- **JointLH** averages the per-positive InfoNCE losses, implicitly driving each positive toward a uniform probability of 1/|*D⁺*|.
- **SumMargLH** computes a single loss based on the sum of positive scores relative to all scores, which focuses gradient on the highest-scoring positives.
- **LSEPair** sums over all positive-negative pairs a log-sum-exp term `exp(s(q,d⁻) - s(q,d⁺))`, enforcing pairwise separation for every positive.
- **Rand1LH** randomly samples one positive per query and applies the standard single-positive InfoNCE loss, serving as a simple baseline.

All methods use a dual-encoder architecture (e.g., BERT) with dot-product similarity. Training batches are constructed in *groups* of *G* queries to provide in-batch negatives, and the maximum number of positives per query (*M*) is controlled. The paper provides theoretical gradient derivations revealing each loss's inductive bias regarding which positives receive stronger learning signals.

## Key Results
- LSEPair achieves state-of-the-art results on in-domain (NQ: 77.01% Acc@20, 85.62% Acc@100; MS MARCO: 39.7% MRR@10) and out-of-domain (BEIR: 39.49% avg NDCG@10) evaluations with homogeneous LLM-annotated positives.
- Under heterogeneous positive quality (mix of human gold and LLM silver labels), LSEPair remains robust, while JointLH and SumMargLH suffer significant drops in top-rank metrics like MRR@10.
- Rand1LH is a consistently strong and simple baseline, while JointLH and SumMargLH exhibit high sensitivity to positive quality distribution and positive-to-negative ratio.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Uniform Probability Equalization (JointLH)
- **Claim:** JointLH implicitly enforces a uniform distribution of probability mass across all positive documents within a query's candidate set.
- **Mechanism:** The gradient update for a positive document's score is `∂L/∂s = P(d⁺|q,D) - 1/|D⁺|`. This drives each positive's assigned probability toward the equilibrium value of `1/|D⁺|`. Consequently, if one positive already has a high probability (P > 1/|D⁺|), its score is pushed down, while lower-probability positives are pushed up.
- **Core assumption:** Positive documents are conditionally independent given the query, allowing the joint likelihood to be a product of individual probabilities.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "...JointLH implicitly enforces uniform probability allocation across all positive documents."
  - [section 3.3] The explicit gradient derivation in Equation (10) and accompanying text: "This formulation drives the optimization toward an equilibrium in which each positive receives probability mass 1/|D⁺|."
- **Break condition:** Performance degrades when positive quality is heterogeneous (e.g., mixing human gold labels with LLM silver labels). The uniform target suppresses high-quality positives while over-promoting weaker ones, harming top-ranking effectiveness. This is empirically shown in Table 4 (heterogeneous setting) where JointLH has the lowest MRR@10.

### Mechanism 2: Dominant Positive Emphasis (SumMargLH)
- **Claim:** SumMargLH implicitly re-weights gradient updates by focusing supervision on the highest-scoring positive documents within the set, potentially neglecting lower-scoring but valid positives.
- **Mechanism:** The gradient factorizes into a global error term and a local weight `exp(s_i)/Σ_{d∈D} exp(s(d))`. The local weight is the positive's share of the total exponentiated score in the candidate pool. Higher-scoring positives thus receive disproportionately larger gradient magnitudes, directing optimization primarily toward them.
- **Core assumption:** The summed marginal likelihood is a valid relaxation of the joint likelihood, trading strictness for robustness to noise by not requiring every positive's likelihood to be maximized individually.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "...SumMargLH concentrates on a small number of dominant positives."
  - [section 3.3] The gradient analysis in Equation (11) and text: "Consequently, the highest-scoring positive dominates the gradient update, suppressing the influence of low-scoring or noisy positives."
  - [section 5.1] Empirical result: SumMargLH shows inconsistent performance and is "consistently weaker" than LSEPair on homogeneous LLM data (Table 2).
- **Break condition:** Highly sensitive to positive quality distribution. In homogeneous settings (all positives similar quality), it underutilizes supervision. In heterogeneous settings with a clear "easiest" positive (e.g., a human label), it may overfit to that single signal, as seen in its improved Dev MRR@10 but collapsed Recall@1000 in Table 4.

### Mechanism 3: Hard Positive Prioritization (LSEPair)
- **Claim:** LSEPair enforces strict separation between *every* positive-negative pair by prioritizing updates on lower-scoring (harder) positives, leading to more robust representation learning.
- **Mechanism:** The gradient magnitude for a positive `d_i⁺` is proportional to `exp(-s(q,d_i⁺))` (Equation 12). This means positives with lower relevance scores receive stronger gradient updates, forcing the model to improve the ranking of the most challenging relevant documents. It aggregates all `|D⁺| x |D⁻|` pairwise constraints.
- **Core assumption:** Optimizing all pairwise score differences (`s(q,d⁻) - s(q,d⁺) > 0`) is a sufficient and stronger criterion for retrieval effectiveness than optimizing aggregated softmax probabilities.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "...LSEPair enforces strict separation between every positive–negative pair, yielding a stronger optimization criterion."
  - [section 3.3] The gradient factorization: "This shows that the gradient scales with exp(-s(q,d_i⁺)), meaning lower-scoring positives receive stronger gradients."
  - [section 5.1 & 5.2] Consistent SOTA or near-SOTA performance across in-domain (NQ, MS MARCO) and out-of-domain (BEIR) evaluations under both homogeneous and heterogeneous positive conditions.
  - [section 6.2] Ablation shows variants focusing only on max-positive (LLSEPair_maxP) perform worst, confirming the value of comprehensive aggregation.
- **Break condition:** The paper finds LSEPair to be the most robust. A potential sensitivity could be to an extremely high positive-to-negative ratio in the training batch, as noted in Section 3.3, but this is mitigated by its pairwise nature compared to listwise losses. No empirical break is shown in the experiments.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Contrastive Learning Framework (InfoNCE)**
  - *Why needed here:* All multi-positive objectives are extensions of the standard single-positive InfoNCE loss. Understanding its formulation—maximizing the softmax-normalized score of a positive against negatives—is prerequisite to grasping how the extensions differ in aggregating multiple positives.
  - *Quick check question:* Given `s(q,d⁺)` and a set of negative scores `{s(q,d⁻)}`, what does minimizing `-log( exp(s⁺) / (exp(s⁺) + Σ exp(s⁻)) )` optimize for in the embedding space?
- **Concept: Softmax Probability Interpretation**
  - *Why needed here:* The core of JointLH, SumMargLH, and the denominator in all losses relies on the softmax over the candidate set `D`. The gradient behaviors are derived from how changes in `s(d)` affect `P(d|q,D)`.
  - *Quick check question:* If you increase `s(d_i)` while keeping all other scores fixed, what happens to `P(d_i|q,D)` and `P(d_j|q,D)` for `j≠i`?
- **Concept: Gradient Magnitude as Learning Signal**
  - *Why needed here:* The paper's central theoretical insight is that different losses produce different gradient magnitudes for the same positive document based on its current score relative to others. This explains their distinct inductive biases (e.g., focusing on hard vs. easy positives).
  - *Quick check question:* In a loss where the gradient for a positive is proportional to `exp(-s)`, what happens to the learning signal for a positive that the model currently ranks very highly vs. one it ranks poorly?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Dual-Encoder (Query & Document) -> Dot Product Similarity -> Candidate Pool Constructor (D⁺ ∪ D⁻) -> Loss Module (SingleLH/Rand1LH/JointLH/SumMargLH/LSEPair)
- **Critical path:**
  1. Input: A batch of queries `{q_i}` with their positive sets `{D⁺_i}` and pre-mined hard negatives.
  2. Encoding: Run all queries and all documents in `{D_i}` through the encoders to get embeddings.
  3. Scoring: Compute similarity matrix `S` where `S[i,j] = s(q_i, d_j)`.
  4. Loss Computation: For each query `i`, slice its candidate scores `S[i, :]` and apply the selected loss function to compute `L_i`. Average over the batch.
  5. Backpropagation: Compute gradients w.r.t. encoder parameters and update.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Positive-Negative Ratio:** Increasing *M* (max positives per query) enriches positive signal but dilutes negative signal and shifts training distribution. LSEPair and Rand1LH are more robust to this shift than listwise losses (JointLH, SumMargLH).
  - **Loss Function Choice:** LSEPair offers best robustness and performance but computes gradients over all `|D⁺| x |D⁻|` pairs (slightly higher compute). Rand1LH is simplest and maintains the standard 1-positive vs. many negatives ratio per step. JointLH/SumMargLH are more sensitive and require careful positive quality curation.
  - **Annotation Cost vs. Coverage:** Under a fixed label budget, covering more queries with fewer positives is more cost-effective for top-rank metrics (MRR). However, if annotation is cheap (LLM), increasing both coverage and per-query depth yields best overall performance.
- **Failure signatures:**
  - **JointLH:** Sudden drop in top-rank metrics (MRR@10, Acc@20) when positive quality is heterogeneous or when *M* is increased. Recall metrics may remain high.
  - **SumMargLH:** Stagnant or declining recall metrics (R@100, R@1000) indicating failure to learn to rank all relevant documents, only the easiest.
  - **General:** Performance collapse when *M* is set too high (e.g., *M=8, G=16* in Fig 1) across all losses, due to excessive positive dilution.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Establish a SingleLH (InfoNCE) baseline on your dataset (e.g., MS MARCO) with one positive per query; evaluate in-domain and on BEIR.
  2. For each multi-positive objective (Rand1LH, JointLH, SumMargLH, LSEPair), train a model using the LLM-annotated multi-positive version of the same dataset with identical hyperparameters; compare all metrics to baseline.
  3. Create a heterogeneous positive set (1 human-quality + 3 lower-quality LLM positives per query) and train with each objective; measure performance degradation on top-rank metrics (MRR@10) to validate gradient sensitivity claims.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Open Question 1:** Can the multi-positive objectives studied be effectively extended and adapted to traditional learning-to-rank frameworks beyond dense retrieval, such as gradient-boosted decision trees or listwise neural rankers?
  - *Basis in paper:* Explicitly stated in "Conclusion and Future Work."
  - *Why unresolved:* Paper's analysis is confined to contrastive learning in dual-encoder dense retrievers; theoretical gradients and inductive biases may differ in other frameworks.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* A systematic study replicating the gradient analysis for LTR losses on standard LTR datasets with multi-positive judgments, followed by ranking metric comparisons.
- **Open Question 2:** How do multi-positive training objectives perform and generalize in multilingual or cross-lingual retrieval scenarios, where the quality and consistency of LLM-generated relevance judgments may vary significantly across languages and scripts?
  - *Basis in paper:* Explicitly stated in "Conclusion and Future Work."
  - *Why unresolved:* Experiments are limited to English datasets; LLM annotation quality and loss robustness across languages are unvalidated.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Replication using multilingual datasets (e.g., BEIR non-English, XOR-QA) with LLM-annotated positives in target languages, measuring in-language and cross-lingual zero-shot performance.
- **Open Question 3:** What is the theoretical and empirical threshold for the positive-to-negative ratio in a training batch beyond which the optimization of all multi-positive objectives (especially listwise ones like JointLH) degrades, and can adaptive sampling strategies mitigate this?
  - *Basis in paper:* Inferred from sensitivity discussion in §3.3 and empirical results in §6.1 showing collapse at high *M*.
  - *Why unresolved:* Precise breaking point and interplay with negative hardness, positive quality variance are not modeled; adaptive strategies are suggested but not tested.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Theoretical analysis of gradient variance as a function of |*D⁺*|/|*D⁻*|; empirical sweep over finer-grained *M* values with metrics on training stability (loss curvature, gradient norms); testing adaptive heuristics (e.g., clipping positives by score).
- **Open Question 4:** To what extent do the comparative strengths and weaknesses of multi-positive objectives depend on the specific LLM used for annotation, the prompting strategy, and the inherent noise in the generated positive labels, versus being inherent properties of the loss functions themselves?
  - *Basis in paper:* Inferred from reliance on a single LLM (Qwen3-32B) and pipeline; robustness claims are based on one heterogeneity scenario.
  - *Why unresolved:* Not disentangled whether observed rankings (LSEPair > Rand1LH > others) hold across varied annotation error profiles from different LLMs or prompts.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Controlled study using multiple LLMs/prompting strategies to annotate the same queries; train all objectives on each annotation set; correlate objective performance with annotation set statistics (e.g., positive precision, intra-query score variance).
- **Open Question 5:** Can the theoretical gradient analysis explaining LSEPair's success—its emphasis on low-scoring positives via a gradient proportional to `exp(-s(d⁺))`—be directly linked to specific retrieval behaviors (e.g., improved recall of tail queries, better handling of ambiguous queries), and is there a principled way to interpolate between the LSEPair and SumMargLH/JointLH gradients to balance top-rank precision and recall?
  - *Basis in paper:* Inferred from gradient derivation (§3.3) and empirical recall/precision trade-offs (§5.1, §5.2), but no causal analysis or hybrid loss exploration.
  - *Why unresolved:* No direct evidence that hard-positive emphasis *causes* broad recall gains; no exploration of continuous loss mixtures or parameterized trade-offs.
  - *What evidence would resolve it:* Fine-grained error analysis by query difficulty; synthetic dataset experiments manipulating positive difficulty; development and evaluation of a parametric loss family (e.g., `α*LSEPair + (1-α)*SumMargLH`) to find optimal α for different metric trade-offs.

## Limitations
- **Reproduction gaps:** Exact LLM annotation pipeline (prompts, candidate retrievers) and hard negative mining procedures are unspecified, preventing exact positive/negative set replication.
- **Untested extremes:** Theoretical break conditions (e.g., very high positive-to-negative ratios, non-English data) lack empirical validation beyond evaluated configurations.
- **Scope:** Findings are based on BERT-based dual-encoders; generalizability to other architectures (e.g., cross-encoders, rerankers) or multilingual settings is not established.

## Confidence
- **High:** JointLH uniform allocation (gradient derivation + heterogeneous degradation evidence), SumMargLH dominant positives (factorized gradient + inconsistent performance), LSEPair hard-positive prioritization (consistent SOTA + inverse gradient-score relationship), overall LSEPair robustness (comprehensive homogeneous/heterogeneous experiments).
- **Medium:** Claims about optimal positive count (*M*=4) and group size (*G*=8) are based on a limited sweep (Fig 1); other configurations may differ.
- **Low:** Extensions beyond dense retrieval, multilingual scenarios, and precise threshold for positive-negative ratio are explicitly open questions with no evidence provided.
