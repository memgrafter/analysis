# Aletheia tackles FirstProof autonomously

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.21201
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21201
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** An autonomous math research agent solved 6 of 10 research-level problems from the FirstProof challenge with expert-validated correct proofs, using a no-human-intervention pipeline.

## Executive Summary
Aletheia, an autonomous mathematics research agent built on Gemini 3 Deep Think, attempted the inaugural FirstProof challenge—a set of 10 unsolved, research-level mathematics problems. Using a fully automated pipeline that copy-pasted problem statements without modification and applied a fixed verification/extraction prompt, the agent produced candidate solutions for six problems. A majority of expert assessors (with one non-unanimous case) judged these solutions correct for Problems 2, 5, 7, 8, 9, and 10. Inference costs for solved problems were substantially higher than a prior benchmark (Erdős-1051), especially for Problem 7, suggesting a correlation with difficulty. The agent self-filtered and produced no output for four problems, reflecting a design that prioritizes reliability over raw output volume. The authors frame this as progress toward autonomous mathematical discovery, while noting interpretational caveats about autonomy criteria and the informal nature of the FirstProof benchmark.

## Method Summary
The authors deployed Aletheia in a two-stage, fully autonomous pipeline. First, the raw problem statement was sent to the base model (Gemini 3 Deep Think) without any human preprocessing or prompt engineering. The model's raw output was then passed through a single, predetermined verification/extraction prompt. This prompt either extracted a LaTeX-formatted proof tagged `[CORRECT]` or requested a revision (`[FIXABLE]`); no manual intervention or reformatting occurred at any point. Two agent variants (AletheiaA and AletheiaB, using different model snapshots) were run per problem, and a human “best-of-2” selection was made from their outputs. Correctness was assessed by domain experts against the standard “publishable after minor revisions.” Inference cost (total tokens processed) was recorded as a proxy for problem difficulty from the agent’s perspective.

## Key Results
- Correct proof candidates were generated and expert-validated for Problems 2, 5, 7, 8, 9, and 10.
- Problem 7 required an order of magnitude more inference cost than the Erdős-1051 benchmark, indicating high difficulty.
- The agent self-filtered (produced “No output”) for Problems 1, 3, 4, and 6, demonstrating a reliability-focused design choice.
- Expert assessment was not unanimous for Problem 8 (5 out of 7 experts rated Correct), highlighting ambiguity in the correctness threshold.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** The autonomous pipeline (problem copy-paste + verification/extraction prompt) can generate candidate solutions without human intervention for research-level mathematics problems.
- **Mechanism:** Aletheia runs a base model with the raw problem statement as input. Its output is passed through a pre-determined verification/extraction prompt that either extracts a LaTeX proof tagged `[CORRECT]` or requests a revision (`[FIXABLE]`). This two-stage process ensures the final output requires no manual reformatting or content alteration.
- **Core assumption:** The verification prompt accurately judges solution correctness according to “publishable after minor revisions” standards, and the base model can generate correct proofs within its inference budget.
- **Evidence anchors:** The abstract and Section 3.1 describe the pipeline; the design is echoed in neighboring work on other AI agents but not this exact architecture.
- **Break condition:** If the verification prompt misclassifies solutions (e.g., marks incorrect ones `[CORRECT]` or fails to extract valid proofs from revisions), the autonomy claim fails. Evidence: P8 assessments were not unanimous (5/7 Correct), showing subjectivity in thresholds.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Self-filtering (producing “No output”) is a deliberate design trade-off that favors reliability over raw problem-solving capability, reducing false positives.
- **Mechanism:** For some problems (P1, P3, P4, P6), Aletheia explicitly returned no solution or timed out. The authors interpret this as the agent recognizing its inability to produce a minimally correct proof, avoiding generation of plausible-but-incorrect outputs that would waste expert verification time.
- **Core assumption:** The agent’s internal confidence or verification-stage rejection can reliably distinguish unsolvable problems within the timeframe, and false negatives are preferable to false positives for expert bandwidth.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 3.1 states self-filtering was a key design principle; Section 2 frames autonomy as “absolutely no human intervention” for generation, implying internal filtering is part of autonomous decision-making.
- **Break condition:** If self-filtering is arbitrary or misjudges solvable problems as unsolvable (e.g., over-conservative thresholds), it artificially lowers measured performance. No direct evidence, but the design is inferred from author interpretation.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Inference cost (computation expended) correlates with problem difficulty from the agent’s perspective, with some problems (P7) requiring an order of magnitude more compute than a prior benchmark (Erdős-1051).
- **Mechanism:** The agent’s internal orchestration (likely involving multiple subagent calls, search, or verification loops) adapts its compute budget per problem. Higher difficulty triggers more Generator subagent attempts and more Verifier interactions, increasing total tokens processed.
- **Core assumption:** The agent’s compute allocation is a meaningful proxy for its perceived problem difficulty, and the architecture allows dynamic scaling of inference-time resources.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section 3.1 reports inference cost as a rough proxy; P7 was previously an open problem (Weinberger, 2023), providing external validity. Neighboring work describes Aletheia’s architecture but does not validate the cost-difficulty correlation.
- **Break condition:** If higher inference cost is caused by architectural inefficiencies (e.g., loop overhead, poor search strategy) rather than problem difficulty, the cost metric is misleading. The paper acknowledges comparison is “not on equal footing” due to different base models.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Equivariant Stable Homotopy Theory (for Problem 5)**  
  Why needed: Problem 5 concerns an “incomplete transfer system” and asks for a characterization of O-slice connectivity via geometric fixed points. Understanding slice filtrations, transfer systems, and the geometric fixed point functor is essential to evaluate the proof.  
  Quick check: What is the relationship between the O-index of a subgroup J and the connectivity bound for Φ^J(X) in Theorem 6?

- **Concept: Uniform Lattices in Semi-Simple Lie Groups & Rational Acyclicity (for Problem 7)**  
  Why needed: Problem 7 asks if a uniform lattice with 2-torsion can be the fundamental group of a compact manifold with rationally acyclic universal cover. The proof uses symmetric spaces, Borel’s equivariant cohomology, and the Strong Novikov Conjecture.  
  Quick check: Why does the existence of a 2-torsion element force a contradiction between mapping degree parity (even) and signature rigidity (±1)?

- **Concept: Canonical Polyadic (CP) Tensor Decomposition & Khatri-Rao Products (for Problem 10)**  
  Why needed: Problem 10 involves an alternating optimization subproblem for a CP decomposition with an infinite-dimensional mode (RKHS-constrained). The linear system uses Kronecker and Khatri-Rao products, and the solution must avoid O(N) operations.  
  Quick check: How does the agent’s proposed preconditioner (block-Jacobi) exploit the structure of the Khatri-Rao product to achieve sub-O(q) iteration cost?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Problem Statement -> Base Model (Gemini 3 Deep Think) -> Verification/Extraction Prompt -> Final LaTeX Proof. Orchestration Layer runs two agent variants (AletheiaA, AletheiaB) and selects best-of-2. Post-Processing: none; final output is the verification prompt’s result.
- **Critical path:**  
  1. Input raw problem text to agent → generate initial solution text.  
  2. Initial solution → fixed verification/extraction prompt → final LaTeX proof (or `[FIXABLE]` revision).  
  3. If `[FIXABLE]`, agent autonomously generates revised output.  
  4. Human experts assess final proofs against “minor revisions” standard.  
  5. Best solution selected from the two agent variants per problem.
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Reliability vs. Raw Output: Self-filtering (no output) is prioritized to avoid false positives, accepting false negatives.  
  - Compute vs. Accuracy: Higher inference cost may yield correct proofs but is expensive; architecture allows dynamic compute scaling.  
  - Autonomy vs. Expert Selection: Generation is fully autonomous, but best-of-2 selection uses human judgment.  
  - Prompt Simplicity vs. Flexibility: A single fixed verification prompt is used for all problems; no problem-specific prompting or iterative human-AI interaction.
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - No Output: Agent times out or states “No solution found” (P1, P3, P4, P6); may indicate capability limit or over-conservative filtering.  
  - High Inference Cost: Orders of magnitude above baseline (P7) suggests struggle, possibly long runtimes.  
  - Non-Unanimous Expert Assessment: P8 had 5/7 experts rating Correct; indicates ambiguity in “minor revisions.”  
  - Agent-Specific Flaws: AletheiaA’s P7 solution was Critically Flawed while AletheiaB’s was Correct, showing model-dependent reliability.  
  - Pre-Deadline Evaluation Errors: Internal pre-deadline assessments were inaccurate for P5, P7, P8, P10, highlighting difficulty of self-evaluation.
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Problem 2 (Representation Theory):** Both agents produced correct solutions; unanimous expert verdict (4/4). Tests basic Whittaker model and Rankin-Selberg integral knowledge.  
  2. **Problem 7 (Geometry/Topology):** Only AletheiaB succeeded; AletheiaA failed due to a fallacy about Euler characteristic divisibility. High inference cost (10× Erdős-1051). Tests advanced geometric reasoning and correct use of finiteness assumptions.  
  3. **Problem 8 (Symplectic Geometry):** AletheiaB’s solution deemed Correct by 5/7 experts; AletheiaA’s Inadequate. Expert disagreement centered on completeness of edge smoothing details. Tests ability to handle polyhedral Lagrangian smoothing with precise flux arguments.

## Limitations
- Autonomy scope is diluted by human “best-of-2” selection and expert evaluation, conflicting with a fully autonomous claim.
- FirstProof’s 10 problems are an informal, non-standardized benchmark with unknown difficulty distribution, limiting generalizability.
- Inference cost metric is reported relatively without clear definition (e.g., FLOPs, token count, monetary cost), preventing accurate difficulty comparisons.
- Self-filtering thresholds are undisclosed; risk of false negatives that understate the agent’s true capability.
- Subjectivity in correctness: P8 had mixed expert judgments, and the “minor revisions” standard is informal.
