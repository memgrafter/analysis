# Is there "Secret Sauce'' in Large Language Model Development?

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.07238
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07238
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: At the frontier (top-performing models), training compute explains 80–90% of performance variation on MMLU-Pro.

## Executive Summary
The paper analyzes a dataset of 809 LLMs released between 2022 and 2025 to understand the drivers of performance differences. Using regression with release-date and developer fixed effects, the authors find that at the performance frontier, higher training compute accounts for 80–90% of variation, indicating that scaling is the primary engine of frontier progress. Away from the frontier, developer-specific efficiency advantages (14–18% of overall variation) and shared algorithmic progress substantially reduce the compute needed for a given capability level, with some developers achieving up to 61× greater compute efficiency. The results suggest that while compute access is central for frontier leadership, proprietary and shared efficiencies are crucial for developing smaller, more cost-effective models below the frontier.

## Method Summary
The study constructs a model-level dataset (N=809) combining open and proprietary LLMs, with performance measured by logit-transformed MMLU-Pro scores and compute estimated as FLOPs = 6 × parameters × training tokens. The core specification is a linear regression: `logit(score) ~ log(FLOPs) + period dummies + developer dummies`, where periods group release dates into three broad bins and developer dummies capture major firms and size-based groups for others. To disentangle contributions, the authors compute Shapley values from the regression R², assigning shares to training compute, shared algorithmic progress (period effects), developer-specific advantages ("secret sauce"), and residual model-specific factors. They also translate coefficients into multiplicative compute factors (exp(effect/β_c)) to interpret efficiency differences.

## Key Results
- At the frontier, 80–90% of performance differences are explained by training compute; scaling contributed over 100% of the observed score increase from 2022 to 2025.
- Developer-specific efficiency accounts for 14–18% of overall performance variation, with the most efficient firms producing models up to 61× more compute-efficient than the baseline.
- Shared algorithmic progress increased effective compute by a factor of 7.5× during the observation period, and the compute required to reach modest capability thresholds fell by up to 8,000×.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** At the performance frontier, increases in training compute explain 80–90% of benchmark score variation.
- **Mechanism:** Frontier models follow log-linear scaling laws; progress comes from moving further along this trajectory rather than shifting the law itself. The regression's scaling coefficient β_c captures the elasticity; the upper envelope of the data cloud is dominated by compute.
- **Core assumption:** The logit-transformed performance is a linear function of log compute with a constant slope β_c across the sample.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] states frontier compute share; Figure 4(a) shows scaling contribution rising from 77% to 88% as compute grows ~5,000×; robustness tests find no significant time trend in β_c.
- **Break condition:** Frontier progress would become driven by shifts in β_c (a new scaling exponent) rather than compute scale; absence of such a trend supports the mechanism.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Developer-specific efficiency advantages ("secret sauce") account for 14–34% of overall performance variation and are more pronounced below the frontier, enabling some firms to produce models up to 61× more compute-efficient.
- **Mechanism:** Developer fixed effects ν_j capture persistent proprietary techniques that shift the entire scaling law upward multiplicatively for that firm (effective compute multiplied by exp(ν_j/β_c)).
- **Core assumption:** Developer identity proxies for a bundle of time-invariant technological choices yielding a constant multiplicative gain.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] reports 14–18% share and up to 61× efficiency; Figure 2(c) shows large dispersion in developer factors (e.g., Microsoft ~60.5× vs. baseline); Figure 2(d) hints at a negative relationship between model size and secret sauce.
- **Break condition:** If developer effects were primarily due to omitted time-varying shocks (one-time breakthroughs), the fixed effect would be mis-specified. The persistence within companies and residual model-specific variation (41× gap) suggest the effect is not purely time-invariant, but the fixed effect still captures a significant share.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Shared algorithmic progress, independent of any single developer, increases effective compute by a factor of ~7.5× over the observation window, lowering compute needs for fixed capability levels below the frontier.
- **Mechanism:** Period fixed effects δ_t capture industry-wide improvements that shift all models upward at a given compute level, representing diffusion of public innovations (better architectures, data processing, training recipes).
- **Core assumption:** Publicly documented advances adopted quickly will manifest as a common time effect distinct from firm-specific secrets.
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] states 7.5× compute factor; Figure 2(b) visualizes leftward shift of compute-performance curves between first and last periods; Appendix Figure E.10 confirms robustness to finer time granularity.
- **Break condition:** If "shared" progress actually reflects early proprietary adoption by leading firms that later diffuses, period dummies may absorb some firm-specific trends. The residual significance of developer effects after controlling for time suggests a distinct proprietary channel.

## Foundational Learning
### Scaling Laws (Power-Law Relationship)
- **Why needed here:** The analysis assumes performance improves predictably with compute, providing the baseline log-linear functional form. Without this, the decomposition would lack a coherent scaling trajectory.
- **Quick check:** If training compute increases by 10×, the predicted logit(MMLU-Pro) increases by β_c × log(10). With β_c ≈ 0.79, that is ≈ 0.79 × 1 ≈ 0.79 units on the logit scale.

### Variance Decomposition (Shapley Value)
- **Why needed here:** To fairly allocate the regression's total explanatory power (R²) among the three sets of predictors (compute, period, developer), acknowledging that their contributions are not orthogonal.
- **Quick check:** In the full sample, what share of variance remains in the residual (model-specific factors)? The Shapley decomposition shows that after accounting for scaling, shared progress, and developer secrets, the residual accounts for the remainder (around 30–40% in some specifications).

### Effective Compute & Multiplicative Factors
- **Why needed here:** The paper translates regression coefficients into intuitive "compute efficiency" ratios (e.g., a developer effect of 2 means the firm's models perform as if they had twice the compute). This bridges statistical output and practical implications.
- **Quick check:** A period effect δ_t is converted into a compute factor M = exp(δ_t / β_c). If δ_t = β_c × log(2), then M = 2, meaning effective compute doubled in that period.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Data Ingestion (assemble model dataset) -> Regression Engine (OLS estimation) -> Factor Conversion (transform coefficients to compute factors) -> Decomposition (Shapley R² allocation) -> Visualization & Comparison (generate figures, counterfactuals).

**Critical path:** Data collection (manual token counts, merging sources) → Specification choice (logit transform, period grouping) → Regression estimation → Factor conversion → Decomposition → Robustness checks. A failure in data merging or transformation cascades.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- *Excluding reasoning models*: Necessary because inference-time compute is unobserved; inclusion would bias frontier compute estimates downward at the cost of losing reasoning-specific insights.
- *Period aggregation*: Coarse periods (3 bins) ensure stable δ_t estimates but may miss short-term trends; robustness to quarterly specs mitigates.
- *Developer grouping*: "Main developers" defined by size/fame; arbitrary cutoffs could alter ν_j estimates; robustness checks explored alternatives.

**Failure signatures:**
- *Omitted variable bias*: If a confounder (e.g., dataset quality) correlates with both compute and developer dummies, β_c and decomposition shares become biased.
- *Mis-scaled time effects*: If shared progress is not constant across developers, period dummies may absorb some firm trends, understating secret sauce.
- *Non-linear scaling*: The log-linear assumption implies constant elasticity β_c; if β_c changes with scale, factor conversions are approximate.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Replicate Figure 1, Panel (a): Run the base regression on all 809 models and compute the Shapley decomposition. Verify scaling ~32%, secret sauce ~18%, shared alg ~3% (full sample shares).
2. Replicate Figure 4, Panel (a): Identify frontier models in first and last periods; use counterfactual decomposition to show scaling accounts for >100% of score increase.
3. Replicate Figure 2, Panel (c): Plot developer compute factors (exp(ν_j/β_c)) against model-level residuals. Confirm Microsoft's factor ~60× and substantial within-company dispersion (e.g., OpenAI points spanning a wide range).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How large is the scope for compute efficiency improvements in LLM development?
- **Basis in paper:** Explicitly posed in introduction; paper quantifies historical gains (7.5× shared, up to 61× developer-specific) but not theoretical limits.
- **Why unresolved:** No analysis of optimal algorithmic bounds or long-term efficiency trends; evidence would require projections or experiments probing diminishing returns.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Will slowing compute growth become a binding constraint on frontier AI advances, or can algorithmic efficiency sustain past progress rates?
- **Basis in paper:** Directly asked; paper shows compute drove 80–90% of frontier variation but does not forecast future compute availability or assess compensatory efficiency gains.
- **Why unresolved:** Requires projections of compute infrastructure and empirical tests of algorithms that decouple performance from scale.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Will efficiency improvements diffuse widely to democratize AI, or concentrate rents among a few proprietary firms?
- **Basis in paper:** Explicitly raised; paper finds significant developer-specific effects and within-company heterogeneity but does not study diffusion or market concentration.
- **Why unresolved:** Need evidence on technology transfer rates, open-source vs. proprietary adoption, and economic impacts on accessibility and firm power.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Do the paper's findings on compute scaling and proprietary efficiency generalize to reasoning models and specialized task performance?
- **Basis in paper:** Discussion notes focus on non-reasoning models; extensions to reasoning systems and task-specific evaluations are future work.
- **Why unresolved:** Reasoning models excluded due to missing inference compute data; applicability to reasoning benchmarks (e.g., MATH) and specialized tasks remains unexamined.

## Limitations
- **Data accessibility:** The core dataset (809 models) is not public; hand-collected token counts for proprietary models involve judgment calls, making exact replication difficult.
- **Developer grouping:** The set of "main developers" is defined descriptively (≥3 models, top performance); alternative thresholds could redistribute variance shares.
- **Omitted variable bias:** Unmeasured confounders (e.g., dataset quality, architectural innovations) may correlate with compute and developer dummies, biasing estimates.

## Confidence
- **Frontier progress is compute-driven (80–90% share):** High. Robust to alternative specifications; scaling exponent β_c shows no significant time trend. Limitation: excludes reasoning models.
- **Developer-specific "secret sauce" explains 14–18% of variation:** Medium. Fixed-effects assume time-invariance; observed within-firm variation (41×) suggests the effect is not perfectly captured by a single dummy.
- **Shared algorithmic progress yields a 7.5× compute factor:** Medium. Period dummies may conflate early proprietary adoption and diffusion; coarse binning may mask uneven adoption.

## Next Checks
1. Re-run the decomposition using an independent, merged benchmark dataset (e.g., combining LMSys, Open LLM Leaderboard, and BIG-Bench Hard submissions) to validate the frontier compute share and developer effect magnitudes.
2. Test robustness of developer fixed effects to alternative developer groupings (e.g., by market cap, publication venue, or open-source policy) and compare Shapley shares for ν_j.
3. Correlate estimated developer fixed effects with external proxies of algorithmic secret sauce (e.g., citations of key methodology papers, data curation effort, or pipeline uniqueness) to assess alignment with measurable practices.
