# LLMs Do Not Grade Essays Like Humans
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.23714
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23714
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Out-of-the-box LLMs exhibit weak agreement with human essay scores (QWK < 0.34) and employ divergent grading criteria focused on surface features.

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates whether large language models (LLMs) can score student essays consistently with human raters. Testing six LLMs (GPT and Llama families) on two standard datasets (ASAP and DREsS), the authors find poor alignment across key metrics—Quadratic Weighted Kappa (QWK) below 0.34, Pearson correlation ≤ 0.57, and higher Mean Absolute Error than between human raters. LLMs systematically compress score ranges, assigning relatively higher scores to short, underdeveloped essays and lower scores to longer essays with minor grammatical errors. However, LLM-generated scores are internally consistent with the sentiment of their explanatory feedback: essays receiving more praise tend to score higher, while those with more criticism score lower. This suggests LLMs follow a coherent but fundamentally different grading logic from humans, making them better suited for generating supportive feedback than for fully automated scoring.

## Method Summary
The research uses a prompt-only approach without task-specific fine-tuning. Six LLMs (GPT-3.5, GPT-4, GPT-5-mini, Llama-2-70B, Llama-3-70B, Llama-4-Scout) are given standardized prompts containing the essay prompt, rubric, and essay text, and asked to produce a numeric score and a concise explanation. Human-LLM agreement is measured on ASAP Task 1 (1,783 essays, scores 1–6), ASAP Task 7 (1,569 essays, trait and overall scores), and DREsS_New (1,917 essays, trait and overall scores) using QWK, Pearson correlation, and normalized MAE. Essay-level features—length (token count), readability (Flesch Reading Ease), and error counts (via LanguageTool)—are extracted to analyze scoring patterns. LLM feedback is processed with aspect-based sentiment analysis (ABSA) using a DeBERTa-v3 model to identify praise and criticism mentions per rubric trait. SHAP values from an XGBoost proxy model then quantify each trait's contribution to the LLM-assigned score.

## Key Results
- LLMs show weak agreement with human scores: QWK < 0.34, Pearson ≤ 0.57, and MAE exceeding human rater differences across all models and datasets.
- LLMs compress score distributions, assigning higher scores to short/underdeveloped essays and lower scores to longer essays with minor grammatical or spelling errors.
- Feedback sentiment consistently aligns with assigned scores: praise frequency increases with score, criticism decreases; however, trait emphasis varies substantially across models (e.g., GPT-4 focuses on ideas, GPT-3.5 on mechanics, Llama on style).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

**Mechanism 1: LLMs compress essay scores toward the middle of the scale, favoring short essays and penalizing longer ones with minor errors.**  
- **Mechanism:** LLMs overweight surface-level text properties (e.g., token count, error detection) relative to holistic development. This biases scores: brief essays that address the prompt avoid penalties for lack of depth, while well-developed essays are downgraded for minor language imperfections tolerated by humans.  
- **Core assumption:** Out-of-the-box LLMs rely on lexical and syntactic heuristics rather than deeper rhetorical or argumentative coherence when scoring.  
- **Evidence:** Abstract states LLMs assign higher scores to short/underdeveloped essays and lower to longer essays with minor errors; Section 4.5 shows LLMs give higher scores than humans to low-scoring essays and lower scores to high-scoring ones.

**Mechanism 2: LLM scores are internally consistent with the sentiment of their feedback; praise predicts higher scores, criticism predicts lower.**  
- **Mechanism:** Scoring logic is a weighted aggregation of trait-specific signals extracted from the feedback. The shared attention mechanisms between feedback generation and score prediction make the numeric output a differentiable function of the feedback's sentiment distribution.  
- **Core assumption:** The model's score reflects a coherent, feedback-anchored evaluation where evaluative language in explanations systematically maps to the numeric output.  
- **Evidence:** Abstract notes consistency between scores and feedback; Section 4.5 demonstrates monotonic increase in praise and decrease in criticism with rising scores; trait-specific praise/criticism mentions have predictable directional effects on scores.

**Mechanism 3: Different LLMs emphasize different rubric traits, leading to divergent grading criteria even when feedback-score consistency holds.**  
- **Mechanism:** Trait importance emerges from model-specific heuristics shaped by architecture and training data, not the rubric. For example, some LLMs prioritize "ideas" while others focus on "conventions," resulting in varied score distributions and human agreement levels.  
- **Core assumption:** Internal representations of writing quality differ across LLM families and versions, causing implicit, non-uniform trait weighting.  
- **Evidence:** Abstract reports trait importance varies across models and datasets; Section 4.5 details GPT-4's skew toward idea-related praise, GPT-3.5's reliance on mechanics criticism, and Llama's style focus; Section 5 notes praise concentrates on few traits while criticism is broader.

## Foundational Learning
- **Quadratic Weighted Kappa (QWK):** Measures agreement for ordinal ratings, weighting disagreements by their squared distance. *Why needed:* Primary metric for LLM-human alignment; accounts for chance and penalizes larger score discrepancies more heavily. *Quick check:* Why use QWK instead of accuracy for essay scoring, and what does a QWK of 0.34 imply about alignment?
- **Aspect-Based Sentiment Analysis (ABSA):** Extracts fine-grained sentiment (positive/negative) tied to specific aspects (e.g., rubric traits) from text. *Why needed:* Enables linking feedback sentiment to score decisions by identifying which traits are praised or criticized. *Quick check:* How does ABSA differ from general sentiment analysis here, and why is trait-level granularity necessary?
- **SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations):** Quantifies each feature's contribution to a model's prediction using Shapley values. *Why needed:* Applied via a proxy model to estimate how much each trait-specific sentiment feature influences the LLM's score, rendering the black-box scoring logic interpretable. *Quick check:* What does a high absolute SHAP value for "organization_neg" indicate about the model's grading logic?

## Architecture Onboarding
Prompt Engine -> LLM Inference Layer -> Feature Extractor -> Analysis Module

**Critical path:** Prompt design → LLM call → Feature extraction → Agreement metrics → SHAP/trait distribution analysis.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Manual trait-term lists (simpler, interpretable) vs. automatic extraction (broader coverage, noisier).
- Single prompt template (consistency) vs. prompt variants (potential calibration gains, but inconsistent).
- Proxy model for SHAP (actionable insight) vs. direct LLM interpretation (black-box, infeasible).

**Failure signatures:**
- QWK < 0.3 and MAE > 0.2 normalized across datasets.
- Score compression pattern: positive residuals for low human scores, negative for high.
- Trait distribution heavily skewed (e.g., >70% praise on one trait) with some traits rarely mentioned.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. Baseline scoring: Run the base prompt on ASAP Task 1 for all LLMs. Record scores and compute QWK/Pearson/MAE vs. human ratings. Expect QWK < 0.3.
2. Feature correlation: For each essay, compute length, FRE, error counts. Correlate (LLM-human) score difference with these features. Expect positive correlation with length and positive correlation with errors.
3. Feedback-sentiment link: For each LLM, group essays by assigned score. Compute proportion of essays with positive/negative trait mentions per score level. Expect monotonic increase in praise, decrease in criticism with score.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:** Are human raters more tolerant of minor language errors in high-quality essays than LLMs? *Basis:* Observation 6 posits human tolerance but dataset limitations prevent testing. *Resolution:* Controlled essays with systematic variations in content quality and minor errors; compare score patterns between humans and LLMs.

**Open Question 2:** How do prompt design variations influence alignment between LLM scores and human grades? *Basis:* Three prompt variants (base, student-grade, few-shot) showed only modest, inconsistent improvements. *Resolution:* Large-scale ablation of prompt components across multiple LLMs and datasets, measuring effects on QWK, Pearson, and MAE.

**Open Question 3:** Have successive generations of GPT and Llama models become better aligned with human essay grading? *Basis:* Observation 2 reports inconsistent improvements across model versions. *Resolution:* Evaluate many model checkpoints on a comprehensive, diverse essay benchmark suite, tracking agreement metrics over time.

**Open Question 4:** Does receiving LLM-generated feedback lead to measurable improvements in student writing? *Basis:* Future Work identifies evaluating feedback impact as a key direction. *Resolution:* Randomized controlled trials comparing LLM feedback, human feedback, and control on subsequent essay quality, revision quality, and skill development.

## Limitations
- Parameter sensitivity: Unreported generation settings (temperature, top_p) may affect score distributions and feedback content.
- Feature extraction variability: LanguageTool version and rule set unspecified, so error counts could vary and weaken the error-compression link.
- Generalizability: Findings based on two English academic essay datasets; unknown if patterns hold for other genres, languages, or rubric designs.

## Confidence
- LLMs show weak agreement with human scores (QWK < 0.34): High
- LLMs compress scores, favoring short/error-free essays: High
- LLM scores align with feedback sentiment: High
- Trait importance varies across LLMs and datasets: High
- LLMs rely on surface heuristics rather than holistic development: Medium

## Next Checks
1. Parameter Robustness Check: Re-run scoring with fixed generation parameters (temperature=0, max_tokens=500). Compare QWK and compression metrics to original results. Persistence of patterns increases confidence.
2. Error Detection Standardization: Recompute error counts using a fixed LanguageTool version (e.g., 5.0 with default English rules). Re-analyze correlation between errors and LLM-human score differences. Weakened correlation would challenge the compression mechanism's dependency on error counting.
3. Cross-Dataset Generalization: Apply the same prompt-only protocol to another essay dataset (e.g., TOEFL). If similar low QWK and score compression appear, the conclusion about fundamental LLM-human divergence is strengthened. If not, claims may be dataset-specific.
