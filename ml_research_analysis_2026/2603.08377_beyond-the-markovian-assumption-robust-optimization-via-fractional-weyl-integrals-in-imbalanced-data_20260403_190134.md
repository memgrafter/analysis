# Beyond the Markovian Assumption: Robust Optimization via Fractional Weyl Integrals in Imbalanced Data

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.08377  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08377  
- **Reference count:** 5  
- **Primary result:** Prevents overfitting on Breast Cancer Wisconsin and yields ~40% PR‑AUC improvement over classical gradient descent on Credit Card Fraud (0.172% fraud).

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces the Weighted Weyl Optimizer, a novel gradient‑based method that leverages fractional calculus to incorporate long‑term memory into the optimization process. By replacing the instantaneous gradient with a fractional Weyl integral—a weighted sum over a history of past gradients using a power‑law kernel—the algorithm retains persistent memory of minority‑class updates, effectively shielding them from the noise of the majority class. The approach is evaluated on two binary classification tasks with varying degrees of class imbalance: Breast Cancer Wisconsin and the extreme‑imbalance Credit Card Fraud dataset (0.172% fraud). Results show that the optimizer prevents overfitting on the former and yields a ~40% improvement in PR‑AUC relative to classical gradient descent on the latter, with optimal performance when the fractional exponent α is between 0.4 and 0.8. The study demonstrates the viability of fractional memory for robust optimization under extreme class imbalance.

## Method Summary
The Weighted Weyl Optimizer modifies the standard gradient descent update by replacing the instantaneous gradient \(g(t)\) with an effective gradient \(G(t)\) computed as a truncated fractional Weyl integral over the past \(L\) gradients:

\[
G(t) = \frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)\,\omega(t)} \sum_{\tau=\max(0,t-L)}^{t-1} (\psi(t)-\psi(\tau))^{\alpha-1} \,\omega(\tau)\, g(\tau)\, \psi'(\tau),
\]

where \(\alpha \in (0,1)\) controls memory length, \(\psi\) warps time (e.g., \(\psi(\tau)=\ln(\tau+1)\)), \(\omega\) applies additional decay (e.g., \(\omega(\tau)=(1+c\tau)^{-1}\)), and \(L\) bounds computational cost to \(O(L)\) per step. The algorithm stores past gradients in a circular buffer, computes kernel weights and weighted sums each step, normalizes by \(\Gamma(\alpha)\omega(t)\), and updates parameters \(\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t - \eta\,G(t)\). The paper uses logistic regression as the base model and evaluates on Breast Cancer Wisconsin (569 instances, 30 features) and Credit Card Fraud (284,807 instances, 0.172% fraud). A minimal reproduction involves implementing this optimizer with \(\alpha=0.6\), \(c=0.01\), \(L=50\) and comparing its performance against classical gradient descent using full‑batch training, standard feature scaling, and an 80/20 stratified train/test split. Expected outcomes are smoother loss curves on breast cancer and a ~40% PR‑AUC improvement on fraud detection.

## Key Results
- Prevents overfitting on the Breast Cancer Wisconsin dataset.  
- Achieves approximately 40% improvement in PR‑AUC on the extreme‑imbalance Credit Card Fraud dataset compared to classical gradient descent.  
- The fractional exponent α must lie in (0.4, 0.8) for optimal performance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Long‑term memory via power‑law kernel
The power‑law kernel \((\psi(t)-\psi(\tau))^{\alpha-1}\) decays polynomially, granting non‑trivial weight to older gradients even for moderate \(\alpha\) (0.4–0.8). This allows rare but informative minority‑class updates to accumulate influence across many steps, counteracting the instantaneous dominance of majority‑class gradients. Core assumption: minority gradients are systematically informative and can be distinguished from noise when integrated over sufficiently long history. Evidence: The abstract states the method “retain[s] a persistent, long‑term memory of the minority‑class gradients. This structural memory acts as a shield against the noise of the majority class.” Section 2.2 explains that unlike exponential momentum decay, the fractional Weyl kernel imposes a power‑law decay, preserving memory. Break conditions: \(\alpha < 0.3\) leads to over‑accumulation of noisy ancient gradients, diluting signal; \(\alpha > 0.8\) makes decay nearly exponential, losing long‑memory and returning to Markovian vulnerability.

### Mechanism 2: Truncated window for computational feasibility
Implementing the infinite‑history operator would be infeasible; the paper uses a fixed‑length window \(L\) (Podlubny’s Short‑Memory Principle). Because the power‑law kernel already down‑weights older gradients, truncation at a sufficiently large \(L\) discards only negligibly weighted history, while reducing per‑step complexity to \(O(L)\). Core assumption: The contribution of gradients older than \(L\) is below a significance threshold for appropriately chosen \(L\). Evidence: Section 2.3 notes that bounding the memory horizon reduces complexity to strict \(O(L)\) per update, ensuring scalability. Break condition: If \(L\) is too small relative to \(\alpha\) and data dynamics, the window discards gradients with non‑trivial weight \((\psi(t)-\psi(t-L))^{\alpha-1}\), eroding long‑memory benefits.

### Mechanism 3: Time‑warping scale \(\psi(t)\)
The time‑warping function \(\psi\) (e.g., \(\psi(\tau)=\ln(\tau+1)\)) nonlinearly remaps the age variable, compressing distant past while magnifying recent gradients. This adds a layer of regularization: even if the power‑law exponent grants weight to old gradients, the logarithmic warp ensures their effective contribution diminishes quickly for very old steps. Evidence: Section 2.2 describes \(\psi\) as enabling the optimizer to act as a “high‑resolution magnifying glass for recent, relevant gradients while heavily compressing the distant past into a stable contextual baseline.” Break condition: An inappropriate \(\psi\) (e.g., linear) fails to provide sufficient compression, letting irrelevant ancient gradients retain excessive influence; overly aggressive warping may discard useful long‑term signal.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Fractional Calculus (Weyl Integral)**  
  *Why needed here:* The core innovation is a non‑standard integral operator. Understanding that \(I^\alpha\) is a non‑local averaging operator with a power‑law kernel, not a pointwise operation, is fundamental.  
  *Quick check question:* How does the Weyl integral \(I^\alpha\) differ conceptually from a simple moving average over the last \(k\) steps?

- **Concept: Class Imbalance in Supervised Learning**  
  *Why needed here:* The problem motivation (majority‑class gradient dominance) and evaluation metric (PR‑AUC for 0.172% fraud) are rooted in extreme imbalance. Understanding why accuracy is misleading and PR‑AUC is critical is prerequisite to evaluating the claim.  
  *Quick check question:* Why is PR‑AUC a more meaningful metric than ROC‑AUC for a dataset with 0.17% positive class prevalence?

- **Concept: Momentum & Moving Averages in Optimization**  
  *Why needed here:* The paper positions its method against exponential decay momentum (e.g., Adam). The key contrast is power‑law vs. exponential memory. One must understand the Markovian, finite‑memory limitation of standard optimizers to grasp the proposed non‑Markovian benefit.  
  *Quick check question:* What is the fundamental limitation of an exponentially decaying moving average of gradients in the context of a sudden, persistent change in the underlying data distribution (concept drift)?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Gradient History Buffer → Kernel Compute Module → Weighted Summation Module → Normalization Module → Effective Gradient → Parameter Update (with Hyperparameter Controller providing α, ψ, ω, L).  
- **Critical path:** Buffer write (each step) → Kernel/Weight compute (each step, O(L)) → Effective gradient \(G(t)\) → Parameter update \(\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta\,G(t)\).  
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  * L vs Memory: Larger L preserves more history but increases memory footprint and per‑step compute.  
  * α vs Responsiveness: Lower α gives longer memory but slows adaptation to new regime changes; higher α is more responsive but loses minority‑class accumulation.  
  * ψ(t) complexity vs interpretability: Logarithmic warp is simple and effective; more complex ψ may offer theoretical adaptability but adds tuning burden without clear empirical guidance.  
- **Failure signatures:**  
  1. PR‑AUC on fraud detection < Classical GD: Indicates α too high or L too small.  
  2. Oscillating/diverging training loss: Likely α too low or ω mis‑scaled, causing effective gradient instability.  
  3. No smoothing on Breast Cancer dataset (trajectory still noisy): Suggests α too close to 1, providing negligible memory effect.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Deep networks:** Does the Weighted Weyl Optimizer significantly improve optimization performance in deep neural networks compared to standard methods? The paper only tests logistic regression, leaving non‑convex landscapes unevaluated.  
2. **Adaptive optimizers:** How does the method compare to adaptive optimizers (e.g., Adam) in extreme class‑imbalance scenarios? No empirical comparison is provided.  
3. **Selection principles for ψ and ω:** What are dataset‑agnostic principles for choosing the time‑warping function ψ(t) and weight decay ω(t)? The paper fixes these without exploring alternatives or offering theoretical justification.  
4. **Truncation window trade‑off:** How does the window length L balance memory retention against computational cost in practice? The paper does not vary or optimize L experimentally.

## Limitations
- Hyperparameter values (learning rate, exact L, ω decay c) and critical implementation details (kernel singularity handling, warm‑start for initial steps) are incompletely reported, hindering faithful reproduction.  
- The evaluation is restricted to two datasets (Breast Cancer Wisconsin and Credit Card Fraud) and logistic regression; generalizability to deep networks and other domains remains unknown.  
- The proposed mechanism lacks ablative experiments to isolate the contributions of the power‑law kernel, time‑warping, and weighting functions; their individual effects are not disentangled.

## Confidence
- **Efficacy claim:** Medium – The reported ~40% PR‑AUC improvement is compelling but rests on limited datasets and unreported hyperparameters; replication would be decisive.  
- **Mechanism claim:** Low – The narrative that the fractional Weyl integral’s long‑tail memory shields minority gradients is theoretically plausible but lacks direct ablative evidence.  
- **Optimal α range:** Medium – The parabolic relationship between α and PR‑AUC is based on a single ablation figure; without raw data or variance across seeds, robustness is unclear but the claim is readily testable.
