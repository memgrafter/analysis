# Interpretable Multiple Myeloma Prognosis with Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership Data
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.20341
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20341
- Reference count: 25
- Primary result: Two training-time regularization techniques for five-year survival prediction achieve 0.721 accuracy while steering a neural network to rely on clinically meaningful features (age, LDH) or R-ISS biomarkers (LDH, albumin, B2M), albeit with a trade-off in predictive performance for stage-consistency.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the "black-box" problem in healthcare machine learning by proposing two regularization methods to enforce interpretability in a neural network for five-year survival prediction in multiple myeloma. Using 812 observational health records (OMOP CDM format), the authors add penalty terms to the training loss: one aligns the network's predictions with a simple logistic regression model built on two clinician-selected features (age, LDH), and the other enforces prediction consistency within established R-ISS stages. The auxiliary-alignment approach maintains accuracy comparable to an unregularized network while successfully shifting feature importance toward the targeted variables, as confirmed by SHAP analysis. In contrast, the stage-consistency method robustly increases the influence of R-ISS-defining biomarkers but incurs a significant drop in accuracy and AUC, illustrating a fundamental tension between strict clinical coherence and predictive power.

## Method Summary
The study uses a cohort of 812 multiple myeloma patients. Data is split into three disjoint sets: a small auxiliary set (D_aux, n=122) to train a transparent logistic regression, a main training/validation set (D_kf, n=568) for model selection and final training, and a held-out test set (D_test, n=122) for evaluation. The core model is a feedforward artificial neural network (ANN). Its training objective combines standard logistic loss with one of two regularization penalties: (1) **Auxiliary-Alignment (R_AA)**: the KL divergence between the ANN's predictions and those of the pretrained logistic regression (on a selected feature pair, found via exhaustive search on D_aux), and (2) **Stage-Consistency (R_SC)**: the mean squared error of individual predictions from the mean outcome of their assigned R-ISS stage (computed via clinical rules using albumin, LDH, β2-microglobulin, age, and cytogenetics). The ANN's base architecture and hyperparameters are first tuned on D_kf without regularization (α=0). Final models are then retrained on D_kf for various regularization strengths α (integer values from 1 to 8). Interpretability is assessed post-hoc via SHAP (mean absolute value) to rank feature global importance, with success defined as the targeted features rising to the top ranks.

## Key Results
- Maximum test accuracy achieved: 0.721.
- The auxiliary-alignment regularizer (R_AA) preserves predictive performance (accuracy/AUC near the unregularized baseline) while making age and LDH the top-ranked features in SHAP analysis.
- The stage-consistency regularizer (R_SC) successfully increases SHAP importance for R-ISS-defining biomarkers (LDH, albumin, β2-microglobulin) but causes a noticeable drop in accuracy and AUC, demonstrating a clear accuracy-interpretability trade-off under this constraint.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- Claim: The auxiliary-alignment regularizer encourages the neural network to rely on the same features (age, LDH) as the interpretable logistic regression auxiliary model.
- Mechanism: A KL divergence penalty between the network's output distribution and the auxiliary model's output is added to the loss. This biases the optimization toward solutions where the network's internal representations align with the decision boundary defined by the auxiliary model's selected features.
- Core assumption: The auxiliary model, trained on a small, clinically chosen feature set, captures a meaningful and interpretable prognostic signal; aligning with it yields a model that is both accurate and transparent.
- Evidence anchors: SHAP analysis shows age and LDH become top-ranked features (Table III), and the regularizer value R_AA decreases monotonically as its weight α increases (Figure 1b).
- Break condition: If the auxiliary model is misspecified (e.g., incorrect features or functional form), alignment could force the network toward a suboptimal, clinically irrelevant boundary, harming performance.

### Mechanism 2
- Claim: The stage-consistency regularizer increases the importance of R-ISS biomarkers (LDH, albumin, β2M) in the neural network's predictions.
- Mechanism: The penalty computes the mean squared deviation of a patient's prediction from their R-ISS stage's mean outcome (µ(s)). Minimizing this reduces variance within stages, incentivizing the model to use features strongly correlated with the stage label itself—the defining biomarkers.
- Core assumption: R-ISS stage is a valid proxy for homogeneous survival risk; enforcing consistency with this coarse label is a valid proxy for interpretability.
- Evidence anchors: SHAP rankings for LDH, albumin, and β2M rise monotonically with α (Table IV), and the regularizer term R_SC decreases with training (Figure 2b).
- Break condition: If R-ISS assignments are noisy or within-stage heterogeneity is clinically predictive, the regularizer forces the model to ignore finer-grained signals, leading to the observed accuracy drop.

### Mechanism 3
- Claim: Both techniques embed interpretability as a training-time constraint, shaping the optimization landscape rather than relying on post-hoc explanation.
- Mechanism: Penalty terms R(h) are added to the empirical risk. Gradient descent minimizes this combined objective, directly steering parameters toward solutions satisfying the structural constraint (alignment or consistency). This "interpretability-by-design" approach limits the effective hypothesis class.
- Core assumption: The penalty function is a differentiable, meaningful measure of deviation from the desired structure; the optimization landscape permits a solution balancing primary loss and regularizer.
- Evidence anchors: Section IV describes constructing regularizers to penalize deviations; Figures 1b/2b show R values decreasing with α.
- Break condition: Excessive α weights force the optimizer into a constrained optimum with poor predictive performance, a trade-off explicitly shown in the results.

## Architecture Onboarding
- Component map: Data Input (OMOP records) -> Auxiliary Model Trainer (logistic regression on D_aux) -> Stage Calculator (Algorithm 1) -> Primary Model (ANN) -> Regularization Module (computes R_AA or R_SC) -> Combined Loss -> Optimizer -> Trained ANN -> Evaluation & SHAP on D_test.
- Critical path: 1) Split data into D_aux, D_kf, D_test. 2) On D_aux, train auxiliary logistic regression and select optimal feature pair. 3) Tune ANN hyperparameters on D_kf with α=0. 4) For each α, retrain final ANN on D_kf with chosen regularizer. 5) Evaluate accuracy/AUC on D_test and compute SHAP. 6) Compare SHAP rankings to target features.
- Design tradeoffs: Auxiliary-alignment (R_AA) minimally impacts performance but ties interpretability to the auxiliary model's feature choices. Stage-consistency (R_SC) strongly enforces a clinical narrative (stage homogeneity) but significantly harms accuracy by ignoring within-stage predictive variation.
- Failure signatures: Metric failure: For R_SC, a sharp, monotonic decrease in test AUC/Accuracy with α without recovery. Interpretability failure: SHAP values do not show targeted features rising in rank. Optimization failure: Regularizer term R does not decrease monotonically during training.
- First 3 experiments: 1) Baseline: Train unregularized ANN (α=0), record test metrics and full SHAP ranking. 2) Auxiliary-alignment sanity check: Train with R_AA (α=1); verify SHAP shows age/LDH in top-3 and metrics are within ~2% of baseline. 3) Stage-consistency stress test: Train with R_SC across α ∈ {0,1,2,4,8}; expect downward trend in accuracy/AUC and top-ranked LDH/albumin/β2M at high α.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- What specific factors cause the notable accuracy drop in stage-consistency regularization compared to auxiliary-alignment? The paper suggests coarser stage limits flexibility but does not isolate whether the cause is the adapted stage definitions (missing cytogenetics), the mean-outcome penalty, stage imbalance, or a combination.
- Can the auxiliary-alignment regularizer be enhanced by incorporating more features in the auxiliary model without compromising interpretability or performance? The authors restrict the auxiliary model to two features for simplicity; the trade-off between auxiliary complexity and alignment effectiveness is unexplored.
- Do the explanations from regularized models align with clinical reasoning and gain trust from physicians? The paper relies on SHAP analysis alone; interpretability in healthcare ultimately requires domain expert assessment of plausibility and utility.
- How sensitive are the results to imputation methods and the biomarker measurement time window? The study uses mean imputation and a fixed ±30-day window without sensitivity analysis, which could significantly impact feature attributions in real-world data.

## Limitations
- Model architecture and hyperparameter details are omitted, hindering exact replication.
- The study uses a single-center cohort (812 patients), limiting generalizability and raising overfitting concerns.
- The auxiliary model's exhaustive feature-pair search on D_aux lacks a validation protocol, risking optimistic feature selection.
- Stage-consistency regularization causes a severe accuracy drop, questioning its practical clinical benefit.

## Confidence
- Auxiliary-alignment yields accurate, interpretable models: **Medium** (SHAP shows target features rank high, but auxiliary model quality is unverified).
- Stage-consistency increases R-ISS feature importance in SHAP: **High** (monotonic ranking changes in Table IV).
- Training-time regularization shapes the optimization landscape: **High** (penalty terms decrease with α as shown in Figures 1b, 2b).
