# Best Arm Identification with LLM Judges and Limited Human
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.21471
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.21471
- **Reference count:** 22
- **Primary result:** The paper develops a method that combines a biased LLM judge with selective human audits to identify the best arm with high probability while reducing cost by up to 90% compared to full human audit.

## Executive Summary
The paper tackles fixed-confidence best-arm identification (BAI) where a cheap but biased proxy (like an LLM judge) is available, and expensive human audits can be selectively acquired. The core contribution is an estimator that fuses proxy averages with inverse-propensity-weighted residuals from audited samples, along with anytime-valid confidence sequences built via martingale techniques. These confidence sequences remain valid under adaptive sampling and stopping, enabling δ-correct identification. The algorithm adaptively chooses which arms to pull and when to audit, using a Neyman allocation that directs audits proportionally to residual uncertainty. Theoretical results show impossibility for judge-only selection, guarantee δ-correctness, and prove near-optimal audit efficiency for the Neyman policy. Empirical tests on synthetic data show over 98% coverage (target 95%) and a 48% cost reduction over uniform auditing, with total cost savings up to 90% versus full human audit.

## Method Summary
The proposed algorithm (PP-LUCB) operates in an adaptive loop. Each iteration selects two arms: the current best estimated arm (based on combined proxy and residual estimates) and the arm with the highest upper confidence bound among the rest. For each selected arm, a proxy score (from an LLM judge) is observed. Then, an audit decision is made with probability π_t, computed via a Neyman allocation: π_t is proportional to the estimated square root of the residual second moment g_k(x,f), clipped to [π_min,1] and scaled to achieve a target average audit rate ρ. If audited, the true human outcome Y is observed, and the residual R = Y - F is used to update estimates. Arm means are estimated as θ̂_k = μ̂_F,k + μ̂_R,k, where μ̂_F,k is the proxy average and μ̂_R,k is the inverse-propensity-weighted residual mean. Anytime-valid confidence intervals for each component are constructed using polynomial-stitched martingale boundaries, and the algorithm stops when the lower bound of the best arm exceeds the upper bound of all others. The approach ensures δ-correctness under bounded outcomes, audit ignorability, and positivity assumptions.

## Key Results
- Theoretically, best-arm identification is impossible if relying solely on the biased judge without human audits.
- The algorithm guarantees δ-correct identification with high probability under stated assumptions.
- Neyman allocation achieves near-optimal audit efficiency, leading to 48% cost reduction compared to uniform auditing and up to 90% total cost savings versus full human audit baselines in synthetic experiments.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Prediction-Powered Estimation with IPW Residuals
The algorithm obtains unbiased estimates of arm means by combining biased proxy averages with inverse-propensity-weighted residuals from selectively audited samples. For each arm k, the estimate is θ̂_k = μ̂_F,k + μ̂_R,k. The proxy mean μ̂_F,k is a simple average of observed judge scores F. The residual mean μ̂_R,k is estimated via IPW: (1/N_k)∑_{s≤t: k_s=k} (A_s/π_s)(Y_s - F_s), where A_s is the audit indicator and π_s the audit propensity. This corrects for the selective missingness of Y_s. Core assumptions: audit ignorability (given past proxy, arm, context, and current proxy, audit decision is independent of the potential Y) and positivity (π_t bounded below by π_min>0). Evidence: Section 4.1 defines the estimator and martingale argument for unbiasedness. Break condition: if audit propensities are not logged or violate positivity (π_t=0 in some contexts), the IPW estimator becomes biased or unstable.

### Mechanism 2: Anytime-Valid Confidence Sequences via Martingales
Confidence intervals are constructed using polynomial-stitched boundaries that remain valid for all times under adaptive sampling, auditing, and optional stopping. The centered proxy sums S_F,k(n) = ∑_{i=1}^n (F_{t_i} - μ_F,k) and IPW residual sums S_R,k(n) = ∑_{i=1}^n (R_{t_i} - μ_R,k) form martingales. Using boundary ψ(v;α), widths w_F,k(n) = ψ(V_F,k(n); δ_k/2)/n and w_R,k(n) similarly, with V_F,k(n)=N_k/4 (sub-Gaussian) and V_R,k(n) based on sum of R². The union over arms and times gives simultaneous coverage with probability ≥1-δ. Assumption: bounded outcomes (Y,F∈[0,1]). Evidence: Section 4.2 details the CS construction using Howard et al.'s polynomial stitching. Break condition: variance misestimation or outcome bound violations can lead to under-coverage.

### Mechanism 3: Neyman Allocation for Audit Efficiency
Neyman allocation concentrates audits proportionally to the square root of the conditional residual second moment g_k(x,f)=E[(Y-F)^2 | k, X=x, F=f], minimizing the asymptotic variance of the IPW estimator for a given audit budget. This is derived by minimizing E[R^2/π] subject to E[π]=ρ via Lagrange multipliers, yielding π*_k(x,f) ∝ √g_k(x,f). The plug-in policy uses a clipped, normalized rule: π_t = clip(λ_t * √ĝ_k(X_t,F_t), π_min, 1). Theorem 5.2 proves near-oracle audit efficiency. Assumption: large samples and consistent estimation of g_k. Evidence: Abstract and Section 5. Break condition: if residual variance g_k is highly non-stationary or pilot estimates are noisy, the plug-in policy may misallocate audits, increasing cost.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Inverse Propensity Weighting (IPW)**  
  Why needed: Corrects selection bias from the adaptive, context-dependent audit policy. Without IPW, the residual mean estimator is biased because audited samples are not representative.  
  Quick check: If audit propensity π_t=0.2 for a sample with Y-F=0.8, what weight is given to that residual in the IPW sum? (Answer: 0.8/0.2 = 4)

- **Concept: Anytime-Valid Confidence Sequences (CS)**  
  Why needed: The algorithm stops based on data-dependent conditions; standard fixed-time confidence intervals are invalid under optional stopping. CS maintain coverage simultaneously over all stopping times.  
  Quick check: Why does a traditional 95% confidence interval computed at the stopping time fail to guarantee δ-correctness? (Answer: Because the stopping rule depends on the data, the interval may not have the correct coverage when considering all possible stopping times.)

- **Concept: Neyman Allocation**  
  Why needed: Minimizes variance of the IPW estimator for a fixed audit budget, directly translating to lower total cost in BAI by avoiding wasteful audits.  
  Quick check: If residual variance g(x,f) is constant across contexts, what does Neyman allocation reduce to? (Answer: Uniform allocation, since √g is constant.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Arm Selector -> Audit Policy Module -> IPW Estimator & CS Updater -> Stopping Condition Check (loop back)

**Critical path:**  
1. Initialize: pull each arm once with π=π_min, audit accordingly, update summaries.  
2. Main loop (iteration t):  
   - Compute θ̂_k, L_k, U_k for all arms.  
   - Select b = argmax θ̂_k and c = argmax_{k≠b} U_k.  
   - For each selected arm k ∈ {b, c}:  
     a. Observe proxy F (pay c_F).  
     b. Estimate residual variance ς̂_k from past audits (if none, default 1).  
     c. Adapt λ to achieve target average audit rate ρ (if needed).  
     d. Compute π_t = clip(λ * ς̂_k, π_min, 1).  
     e. Audit with probability π_t; if yes, observe Y (pay c_Y), compute R=Y-F, update sums and ς̂_k.  
   - After processing both arms, check stopping condition: if L_b > max_{k≠b} U_k, output b and stop; else continue.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- Audit cost vs. accuracy: Higher audit rates ρ shrink intervals faster but increase cost; Neyman allocation mitigates by focusing audits where residual uncertainty is high.  
- Adaptive vs. uniform auditing: Adaptive reduces cost by ~48% but requires learning g_k; uniform is simpler but inefficient under heterogeneity.  
- Conservatism of CS: Polynomial stitching provides strong validity guarantees but may be overly conservative, leading to more samples than necessary.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Low coverage (<95%): Likely violation of assumptions (non-bounded outcomes, incorrect π_t logging) or bug in CS calculation.  
- Accuracy < 90% (for easy Δ=0.2): IPW estimator bias or CS too narrow.  
- Excessive audit rate (>> ρ): λ adaptation may be mis-scaled; check λ update.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Validate CS coverage:** Run 1000 single-arm Bernoulli (μ=0.5) sequences, compute CS at each step, check if true μ ever leaves interval. Target: ≤5% violation for δ=0.05.  
2. **Compare Neyman vs Uniform:** For K=4, Δ=0.1 synthetic BAI, compare total cost and accuracy of Neyman allocation against uniform fixed π=0.1. Expect ~48% cost reduction with comparable accuracy.  
3. **Test adaptive auditing value:** Compare "Fixed" (π=0.1 uniform) vs "Adaptive" (uncertainty-weighted heuristic) on homogeneous bias scenario. Expect ~10% cost reduction from adaptivity alone.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
Can we drop the uniform lower bound on audit propensity and allow π_t = 0 in some contexts while still guaranteeing δ‑correctness? The theoretical analysis assumes a global π_min; it is unknown whether confidence sequences remain valid without this bound.

### Open Question 2
Is the Neyman allocation provably optimal for the overall best‑arm identification cost, rather than solely for the IPW estimator’s variance? The paper shows asymptotic variance optimality but lacks a matching lower bound for total cost.

### Open Question 3
Can we develop tighter anytime‑valid confidence sequences for the IPW estimator to reduce the observed over‑coverage and thus lower the total sampling cost? The current polynomial‑stitched boundary is conservative; a tailored concentration inequality might yield tighter intervals.

## Limitations
- Theoretical guarantees rely on bounded outcomes, audit ignorability, and positivity; these may not hold for real LLM-judge/human scenarios.
- Empirical validation is limited to synthetic data; no real-world LLM/human experiments.
- Confidence sequences exhibit over‑coverage (~98% vs target 95%), indicating conservatism that may inflate sample complexity.
- Implementation details (ψ function, λ adaptation, context-aware variance estimation) are ambiguous, hindering exact replication.
