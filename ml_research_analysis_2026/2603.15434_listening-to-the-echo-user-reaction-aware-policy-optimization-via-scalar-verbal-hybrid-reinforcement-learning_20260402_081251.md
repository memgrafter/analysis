# Listening to the Echo: User-Reaction Aware Policy Optimization via Scalar-Verbal Hybrid Reinforcement Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.15434
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15434
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** RAPO significantly outperforms RL baselines, e.g., Qwen-2.5-RAPO improves EmoHarbor overall by 12.4% versus GRPO and achieves SOTOPIA score 3.87 vs. CPO 3.77.

## Executive Summary
Emotional support dialogue systems traditionally rely on static expert-defined scalar rewards, which are sparse, ambiguous, and misaligned with dynamic user reactions, leading to unexplained failures and limited adaptability. RAPO addresses these issues through three key innovations: Hindsight Dialogue Selection, which filters dialogue turns to focus on pivotal interactions that meaningfully alter user emotional trajectories; Generative Hindsight Feedback, which transforms simulated user reactions into contrastive ranking signals and natural-language critiques; and Scalar‑Verbal Hybrid Policy Optimization, which couples Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) for global scalar alignment with on‑policy self‑distillation for token‑level verbal refinement. Evaluations on EmoHarbor, ESC‑Eval, and SOTOPIA show consistent improvements: Qwen‑2.5‑RAPO outperforms GRPO by 12.4% on EmoHarbor and achieves 3.87 on SOTOPIA versus CPO’s 3.77. Human evaluations confirm win rates against ablated variants, and ablations demonstrate the necessity of both user‑reaction modeling and verbal distillation, while maintaining stable training dynamics.

## Method Summary
RAPO (Reaction-Aware Policy Optimization) integrates three components. **Hindsight Dialogue Selection** employs an LLM judge to assign a binary *learn* indicator per turn, retaining only those that exhibit meaningful emotional support skills (e.g., validation, strategy) and discarding low‑signal exchanges. **Generative Hindsight Feedback** samples multiple candidate responses per dialogue, uses a user simulator to generate reactions, and then processes the entire group with a generative reward model (GRM) to produce a unique rank and a natural‑language critique for each candidate, yielding dense, contrastive supervision. **Scalar‑Verbal Hybrid Policy Optimization** combines GRPO, which updates the policy using group‑relative advantages derived from the rankings, with on‑policy self‑distillation (SDPO) that distills the policy toward a teacher distribution conditioned on the user reaction and critique for the lowest‑reward response. The unified objective enables the policy to align with global reward signals while refining responses at the token level based on verbal feedback. This hybrid approach resolves the sparsity and ambiguity of scalar rewards and provides actionable corrective guidance.

## Key Results
- RAPO outperforms strong RL baselines: Qwen‑2.5‑RAPO improves EmoHarbor overall by 12.4% vs. GRPO; SOTOPIA overall score 3.87 vs. CPO 3.77.
- Human evaluation shows consistent win rates against ablated variants (e.g., without user‑reaction modeling or verbal distillation).
- Ablations confirm the necessity of both user‑reaction modeling and verbal distillation, with RAPO achieving superior performance across all benchmarks while maintaining stable training dynamics.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Hindsight Dialogue Selection
Filters dialogue turns to focus training on pivotal interactions that meaningfully alter the user’s emotional trajectory, avoiding overfitting to generic empathy templates (Abstract, Sec. 3.2). It uses a hindsight judge (e.g., GPT‑4o) to assign a binary *learn* indicator per turn, retaining only those exhibiting meaningful support skills (validation, strategy) and discarding low‑signal exchanges (greetings, rote acknowledgments). Core assumption: high‑frequency template turns provide limited learning value and can cause “hallucinated empathy.” If the judge becomes inconsistent or biased, valuable subtle behaviors may be discarded.

### Generative Hindsight Feedback
Converts simulated user reactions into dense, contrastive supervision (ranking scores + verbal critiques) that resolves scalar reward sparsity and ambiguity (Abstract, Sec. 3.3). For a group of candidate responses, a user simulator produces reactions; a generative reward model (GRM) then processes all trajectories together, assigning a unique rank and a natural‑language critique per candidate. This groupwise contrastive setup normalizes rewards and provides explanatory feedback. The approach assumes user reactions are a more direct signal than expert rubrics; however, if the simulator fails to generate realistic reactions or the GRM produces inconsistent critiques, the feedback may misguide updates.

### Scalar‑Verbal Hybrid Policy Optimization
Jointly optimizes a global scalar objective (GRPO) for ranking alignment and a token‑level verbal objective (self‑distillation) for fine‑grained refinement, enabling the model to learn both effective strategies and precise linguistic corrections (Abstract, Sec. 3.4). GRPO updates the policy using group‑relative advantages from scalar ranks. Separately, for the lowest‑reward response in each group, a self‑teacher distribution is constructed by conditioning the policy on the user reaction + critique; the policy is then distilled via on‑policy distillation (SDPO). The final objective combines both terms. The core assumption is that scalar rewards alone cannot explain *how* to fix a suboptimal response; token‑level verbal feedback provides actionable corrections. If the self‑distillation coefficient η is mis‑set, training may diverge or overfit.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Policy model (π_θ) → Generates responses given dialogue history  
Hindsight Dialogue Selector → Filters training turns  
User Simulator → Produces simulated user reactions for candidate responses  
Generative Reward Model (GRM) → Outputs groupwise ranks and critiques  
Hybrid Optimizer → Combines GRPO (scalar) and LSD (verbal) losses  
Self‑teacher construction → Identifies lowest‑reward response and forms feedback‑aware distribution

### Critical Path
Input dialogue → sample G responses → user simulator reactions → GRM rankings/critiques → select pivotal turns → compute GRPO advantages → identify worst response for LSD → update π_θ

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Signal richness vs. computational cost:** User simulation and GRM inference add significant overhead (~13h ESC vs. 9h CPO).  
- **Stability vs. exploration:** Hybrid objective may require careful tuning of η and β; over‑distillation can collapse diversity.  
- **Rubric independence vs. simulator bias:** Moving away from expert rubrics introduces dependence on simulator quality.

### Failure Signatures
- Reward collapse or high variance during GRPO updates.  
- Over‑generation of long, repetitive responses (monitor response length).  
- Degradation in human‑likeness or safety scores (per EmoHarbor/ESC‑Eval).  
- In ablations, removal of URM or SD causing sharp performance drops.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**Open Question 1:** How do different user simulator architectures affect the performance and robustness of RAPO?  
*Basis:* Explicitly noted in the paper’s limitations (Simulator Sensitivity).  
*Why unresolved:* The authors used pre‑trained simulators (ESC‑Role, Qwen‑3‑8B) but did not explore other architectures due to training costs; systematic comparison is lacking.  
*Evidence needed:* Run ablation studies with multiple user simulators (varying base models, fine‑tuning data) on ESC and SOTOPIA, measuring performance stability.

**Open Question 2:** Can RAPO generalize to non‑dialogue sequential decision‑making domains?  
*Basis:* Explicitly stated that validation is confined to dialogue‑based tasks; domain generalizability is unverified.  
*Why unresolved:* RAPO was evaluated only on emotional support and social interaction benchmarks; transfer to robotics, gaming, or control tasks remains untested.  
*Evidence needed:* Apply RAPO to a representative non‑dialogue environment and compare against strong RL baselines.

**Open Question 3:** Can the computational overhead of RAPO be reduced without sacrificing effectiveness?  
*Basis:* Limitation citing “significant computational overhead” and longer training times than CPO due to user simulation and iterative scoring.  
*Why unresolved:* High cost limits scalability; alternative training strategies (e.g., fewer rollouts, offline replay, caching) are unexplored.  
*Evidence needed:* Experiment with varying rollout group size (G), cached reactions, or offline reaction data, quantifying trade‑offs under fixed compute budgets.

**Open Question 4:** Can large generative reward models (GRMs) be replaced by smaller, more efficient reward models while preserving RAPO’s gains?  
*Basis:* Authors identify “Dependency on Heavy Reward Models” and suggest distilling large judges into smaller specialized models.  
*Why unresolved:* Current GRMs (e.g., DeepSeek‑R1) are resource‑intensive; it’s unclear whether compact models can capture necessary contrastive feedback.  
*Evidence needed:* Train smaller reward models via distillation or fine‑tuning on GRM critiques, integrate them into RAPO, and evaluate performance on ESC and SOTOPIA.

## Limitations
- Dependence on LLM‑based user simulator and GRM introduces potential bias and distribution mismatch with real users.
- High computational cost (e.g., ~13 hours for ESC vs. 9 hours for CPO) may limit accessibility.
- Human evaluation uses pairwise win rates only; lacks fine‑grained metrics and larger‑scale user feedback.
