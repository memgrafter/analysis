# Proximal-IMH: Proximal Posterior Proposals for Independent Metropolis-Hastings with Approximate Operators

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2602.21426
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21426
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Proximal-IMH is an independence Metropolis-Hastings sampler that corrects approximate posterior samples via a proximal optimization step, achieving higher acceptance rates and accuracy than existing IMH methods while using computationally cheaper proposals.

## Executive Summary
Bayesian inverse problems often involve expensive-to-evaluate forward models, but cheaper approximate operators may be available. This paper proposes Proximal-IMH, an independent Metropolis-Hastings scheme that generates proposals by applying a proximal map to samples drawn from an approximate posterior. The proximal map balances fidelity to the exact forward model against stability near the approximate sample, effectively correcting bias introduced by the approximation. For linear operators, the map is a closed-form linear transformation; for nonlinear operators, a single Gauss-Newton step suffices.

Theoretically, under linear-Gaussian assumptions, Proximal-IMH yields proposals with smaller expected Kullback-Leibler divergence to the exact posterior and enjoys faster mixing times than existing IMH variants (Approx-IMH, Latent-IMH), with improved robustness to operator perturbations. Across three diverse inverse problem tests (bimodal, MNIST imaging, nonlinear Helmholtz), Proximal-IMH consistently achieves higher acceptance rates, faster convergence to accurate posterior means, and lower relative mean and second-moment errors. It often matches the accuracy of NUTS while benefiting from cheaper, independent proposals. The method is robust to the regularization parameter β, which can be chosen on the order of the noise variance σ².

## Method Summary
Proximal-IMH follows the independent Metropolis-Hastings framework. Given an approximate posterior π_a(x|y) (e.g., via a surrogate forward operator Ã), one first draws a sample ẽ from π_a. A deterministic proximal map g then corrects ẽ to a proposal x = g(ẽ) by solving minₓ ‖A(x) - Ã(ẽ)‖² + β‖x - ẽ‖², where A is the exact forward operator and β is a regularization parameter. For linear A, the solution is a linear transformation K = (AᵀA+βI)⁻¹(AᵀÃ+βI), so x = Kẽ and the proposal density is Gaussian with known covariance. For nonlinear A, one Gauss-Newton step yields x = ẽ - (J(ẽ)ᵀJ(ẽ)+βI)⁻¹ J(ẽ)ᵀ r(ẽ), where J is the Jacobian of A and r(ẽ)=A(ẽ)-Ã(ẽ). The Jacobian determinant ratio in the proposal density is close to 1 and can be neglected in practice. The Metropolis-Hastings acceptance probability uses the exact posterior density and the ratio of proposal densities. Because proposals are independent of the current state, they can be precomputed and evaluated in parallel; the dominant computational cost per accepted sample is evaluating the exact forward operator A.

## Key Results
- Theoretically, for linear models with Gaussian priors and noise, Proximal-IMH achieves smaller expected KL divergence and faster mixing times than Approx-IMH and Latent-IMH, with mixing time scaling more favorably under operator perturbations.
- Numerically, across bimodal, MNIST imaging, and nonlinear Helmholtz tests, Proximal-IMH attains higher acceptance rates, faster convergence to accurate posterior means, and lower relative mean and second-moment errors compared to other IMH methods, often matching NUTS accuracy.
- The method is robust to the choice of regularization parameter β ≈ σ².

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Proximal correction reduces bias from approximate operator.
- **Claim**: Proximal-IMH generates proposals that are closer to the exact posterior than those from the approximate posterior alone by using a proximal step that balances fidelity to the exact forward model and stability around the approximate sample.
- **Mechanism**: Given an approximate posterior sample ẽ ~ π_a(x|y), solve x = argminₓ ‖A(x) - Ã(ẽ)‖² + β‖x - ẽ‖². For linear A, this yields a closed-form linear transform K = (AᵀA+βI)⁻¹(AᵀÃ+βI); for nonlinear A, one Gauss-Newton step is used. The resulting proposal is the push-forward of π_a via this map.
- **Core assumption**: The approximate forward operator Ã is sufficiently accurate such that the correction is a small adjustment; β is chosen on the order of noise variance σ² to prevent overfitting.
- **Evidence**: Abstract and Section 3 (Eqs. 3a–3b, 4, 17); Theorem 3.2 shows smaller expected KL divergence.
- **Break condition**: Over-regularization (β ≫ σ²) degrades performance; under-regularization (β too small) may cause instability, especially under large operator error (Fig. 4).

### Mechanism 2: Push-forward proposal enables independent and precomputable proposals.
- **Claim**: By constructing the proposal as a push-forward of the cheap approximate posterior, Proximal-IMH retains the computational advantages of IMH: proposals are independent of the current state and can be precomputed in parallel.
- **Mechanism**: Approximate posterior π_a can be sampled (e.g., via surrogate). Each proposal is obtained by applying a deterministic map (linear K or nonlinear Gauss-Newton) to each sample. The MH acceptance ratio uses exact posterior and proposal density, which for the push-forward has an analytical expression (linear) or involves a Jacobian determinant (nonlinear, often ≈1).
- **Core assumption**: The push-forward map is invertible or at least we can evaluate its Jacobian determinant; for nonlinear case the Jacobian determinant ratio is close to 1.
- **Evidence**: Abstract; Section 2 on IMH advantages; Section 3.1 (Eqs. 5,6); Section 3.4 neglects Jacobian.
- **Break condition**: Map may not be invertible (loss of dimensions); neglecting Jacobian may cause bias if determinant varies significantly.

### Mechanism 3: Robustness to operator perturbations via regularized pseudoinverse.
- **Claim**: The mixing time of Proximal-IMH depends on ‖I - K⁻¹‖, which is bounded by ‖I - eF⁻¹F‖ (used in Latent-IMH) and is more robust to ill-conditioning arising from squaring the forward operator.
- **Mechanism**: For linear case, K = I + A†(eA - A) where A† is a regularized pseudoinverse (AᵀA+βI)⁻¹Aᵀ. This regularization filters high-frequency errors in operator mismatch. Mixing time bound (Theorem 3.3) shows τ_mix ~ ‖I - K⁻¹‖², whereas Latent-IMH depends on ‖I - eF⁻¹F‖², which can be large if eF is ill-conditioned.
- **Core assumption**: Prior is log-concave, forward operators are linear and invertible (as required by Latent-IMH), and β = Θ(σ²).
- **Evidence**: Theorem 3.3; Figure 3 shows ‖I - K⁻¹‖ stable while ‖I - eF⁻¹F‖ grows under low-rank perturbation.
- **Break condition**: Violation of log-concavity or strong convexity may invalidate bounds; β not scaled with σ² may break bounds; operator perturbation in nullspace of A may render correction ineffective.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
- Approximate posterior sampler: generates samples ẽ from π_a(x|y), often using a surrogate forward model (e.g., coarse discretization, learned operator).
- Proximal solver: computes the proximal map g; for linear A uses matrix factorization of (AᵀA+βI); for nonlinear A performs one Gauss‑Newton step (requires Jacobian of A).
- Exact forward evaluator: computes A(x) for the exact likelihood in the MH acceptance ratio.
- MH acceptance calculator: computes acceptance probability using exact posterior density at proposal and current state, and the proposal density (including Jacobian determinant ratio if needed).
- Parallel proposal batch generator: can precompute many proposals offline in an embarrassingly parallel fashion.

Data flow: Approximate sampler → Proximal solver → (proposal x) → MH acceptance calculator (calls Exact forward evaluator). Batch generator may produce many proposals ahead of time.

### Critical Path
For each Metropolis‑Hastings step:
1. Draw ̃x ∼ π_a.
2. Compute proposal x = g(̃x) via proximal solver.
3. Evaluate exact posterior density at x (using exact forward evaluator) and at current state xt.
4. Compute proposal densities π_a(̃x) and π_a(xt) (precomputed or analytic) and, for nonlinear case, the Jacobian determinant ratio |det J_GN(xt)| / |det J_GN(x)| if included.
5. Accept or reject the proposal according to the Metropolis‑Hastings ratio.

The expensive operation is evaluating A(x) in step 3; proposals are relatively cheap.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Linear vs nonlinear**: Linear case yields closed‑form K and no Jacobian; nonlinear requires solving a local optimization (Gauss‑Newton) and approximating the Jacobian determinant. Multiple Gauss‑Newton steps could improve accuracy but increase cost.
- **Precomputation vs on‑the‑fly**: Precomputing many proposals allows parallel generation and reduces per‑step latency, but requires storing large arrays. On‑the‑fly computation trades memory for time.
- **β selection**: Theory suggests β = Θ(σ²); too large oversmooths and degrades accuracy, too small may cause instability or overfitting, especially when operator error is large. Empirically robust around σ² but may need tuning for specific problems.
- **Neglecting Jacobian**: For nonlinear, ignoring the determinant ratio is convenient but may introduce bias if the map causes significant volume distortion; monitoring log|det J_GN| is recommended.

### Failure Signatures
- Acceptance rate ≪ 10%: indicates poor proposal quality, possibly due to large operator mismatch or inappropriate β.
- High variance in posterior estimates: could stem from insufficient mixing or bias (e.g., from neglected Jacobian determinant).
- Proximal solver divergence: Gauss‑Newton step may fail if initialized too far or residual large; use line search/damping.
- Memory blowup: Precomputing many proposals may exceed memory; process in batches.
- Theoretical assumptions violated: Non‑log‑concave prior or highly nonlinear forward operator may invalidate mixing‑time bounds; rely on empirical validation.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Bimodal test (Section 4.1)** – Linear inverse problem with a bimodal prior; introduces multiplicative, additive, and low‑rank operator perturbations. Measures acceptance rates, mean error, and mode recovery. Expected: Proximal-IMH outperforms Approx-IMH and Latent-IMH, especially under ill-conditioning; validates theoretical KL and mixing predictions.
2. **MNIST imaging (Section 4.2)** – Inverse problem with a VAE decoder prior and forward model linearized from Helmholtz; two levels of approximation error. Measures relative mean and second-moment errors against ground truth. Expected: Proximal-IMH achieves lower error and faster convergence than Approx-IMH, comparable to NUTS; also inspects Jacobian determinant variations.
3. **Nonlinear Helmholtz (Section 4.3)** – Fully nonlinear Helmholtz forward operator with total-variation prior and multiple sources; approximate model on a coarser grid. Measures mean error. Expected: Proximal-IMH outperforms Approx-IMH and matches NUTS accuracy, demonstrating scalability to nonlinear PDE‑based inverse problems.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Effect of neglecting Jacobian determinant in nonlinear Proximal‑IMH**  
   *Basis*: Section 3.4 states that the Jacobian determinant ratio is neglected because it is expected to be close to one.  
   *Unresolved*: The impact on the stationary distribution and mixing rates has not been analyzed; correctness of the Metropolis‑Hastings algorithm requires the exact acceptance ratio.  
   *What evidence*: A theoretical bound showing the determinant ratio is uniformly close to 1 under certain conditions (e.g., small residual, well‑conditioned Jacobian), or numerical experiments comparing the distribution produced with and without the correction.

2. **Extension of mixing time guarantees beyond log‑concave priors and Gaussian noise**  
   *Basis*: The mixing time analysis (Section 3.3) assumes a log‑concave prior and Gaussian noise; the bimodal test violates log‑concavity but lacks theoretical support.  
   *Unresolved*: Many real inverse problems involve multi‑modal or non‑log‑concave posteriors; understanding robustness to such settings is crucial.  
   *What evidence*: Derivation of new bounds under weaker assumptions (e.g., strongly unimodal, bounded curvature) and empirical validation across diverse prior structures.

3. **Optimal number of Gauss‑Newton steps for nonlinear proximal correction**  
   *Basis*: Proximal‑IMH uses a single Gauss‑Newton step to solve the proximal optimization; no exploration of multiple steps.  
   *Unresolved*: The trade‑off between additional computational cost and improvement in proposal quality is unknown; a single step may be suboptimal.  
   *What evidence*: Systematic empirical study comparing varying numbers of Gauss‑Newton steps (1, 2, 5, …) on a set of nonlinear inverse problems, measuring acceptance rate, effective sample size per unit time, and posterior accuracy. Theoretically, linking the number of steps to residual magnitude and local smoothness could bound the KL divergence of the proposal.

## Limitations
- Theoretical guarantees (expected KL, mixing time) are restricted to log‑concave priors and linear operators; no analogous results hold for nonlinear or multi‑modal posteriors.
- The recommended β = Θ(σ²) is derived for linear Gaussian models; its robustness for nonlinear problems is only supported by limited empirical tests and may not generalize.
- Neglecting the Jacobian determinant ratio in the nonlinear case may bias the stationary distribution if the proximal map induces significant volume changes.

## Confidence
- Theoretical optimality (smaller expected KL, mixing time bounds) for linear Gaussian models: **High**
- Empirical superiority over Approx‑IMH and Latent‑IMH across inverse problems: **Medium**
- Robustness of β ≈ σ² and insensitivity to operator error: **Medium**
- Generality to nonlinear operators via one Gauss‑Newton step with negligible Jacobian effects: **Low‑to‑Medium**
