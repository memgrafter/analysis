# When Models Decide and When They Bind: A Two-Stage Computation for Multiple-Choice Question-Answering

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2601.03914
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03914
- **Reference count**: 24
- **Primary result**: Language models first commit to a winning content position in early stages, then later bind that content to the appropriate output symbol during answer generation.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates the internal reasoning process of instruction-tuned language models on multiple-choice question answering (MCQA). Using two models—Llama-3.2-Instruct-3B and Qwen3-8B—the authors apply linear probes to residual activations at option boundaries. They find that per-option correctness is highly decodable from middle-to-late layers. Moreover, probes for the winning content position become accurate immediately after the final option, while probes for the output symbol (e.g., “A” or “B”) emerge only near the answer emission phase. Symbol permutation experiments show that content representations remain robust despite symbol changes, whereas symbol decoding collapses until late layers. Activation patching confirms that these representations are causally involved. Together, the evidence supports a two-stage computation: models first decide which option content is correct, and subsequently route that decision to the correct output symbol during generation.

## Method Summary
The study examines the residual streams of two instruction-tuned transformers on an MCQA benchmark. For each input, activations are extracted at the token corresponding to the end of each answer choice (the “option boundary”). Linear classifiers (probes) are trained on these activations to predict (a) whether a given option is correct and (b) the identity of the winning option. Probe performance (AUC, top-1 accuracy) is tracked across layers. To test the separation of content and symbol, the answer symbols are permuted (e.g., swapping “A” and “B”) while keeping option texts fixed. Additionally, activation patching is used: activations from runs with known correct/incorrect content are inserted into other runs to assess causal impact.

## Key Results
- Per‑option correctness is linearly decodable from residual states at option boundaries with near‑perfect AUC and top‑1 accuracy in middle‑to‑late layers.
- The winning content position is decodable right after processing the final option, while the output symbol becomes decodable only near answer emission, indicating a dissociation.
- Under symbol permutations, content position probes remain accurate early, but symbol probes collapse until late layers; probe‑aligned patching confirms the causal relevance of both signals, supporting a two‑stage computation where content is committed first and then bound to the output symbol.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Linear probing reveals that models encode the correctness of each option in a linearly separable manner by the middle layers. The dissociation between content position and output symbol emerges from the timing of decodability: the model determines *which* option is correct (content) soon after all options have been presented, but only later maps that decision onto the required output token (e.g., “A”). Symbol permutation experiments demonstrate that the content representation is unaffected by changes to the output symbols, confirming that the two signals are distinct. Activation patching shows that intervening on the content representation (e.g., patching a layer where content is strong) alters the model’s final answer, while intervening on the symbol representation also changes the output, establishing causal roles. This two‑stage mechanism allows the model to separate semantic reasoning from the mechanics of answer formatting, which may be more robust to perturbations in symbol assignment.

## Foundational Learning

### 1. Linear Probing
A technique to test whether a specific property (e.g., correctness) is encoded in neural activations by training a simple linear classifier on those activations.  
*Why needed*: The paper uses linear probes to assess decodability of option correctness and winner identity across layers.  
*Quick check*: If a probe achieves high validation accuracy/AUC, the target information is linearly separable in the representation at that layer.

### 2. Residual Stream Activations
The hidden states after each transformer layer’s residual connection; they carry the accumulated representation up to that point.  
*Why needed*: Probes are applied to residual activations at precise token positions (option boundaries) to track the evolution of the model’s internal state.  
*Quick check*: PCA or t-SNE on residual vectors often reveals clusters corresponding to correct/incorrect options, suggesting information presence.

### 3. Option Boundaries
The token indices marking the end of each answer choice (e.g., after “A: …” or after the text of choice B).  
*Why needed*: Selecting a consistent focal point ensures that activations correspond to the representation of each option rather than mixed context.  
*Quick check*: Verify alignment by mapping token indices to the structure of the input prompt; the activations should be extracted exactly at the delimiter token or the last token of each choice.

### 4. Symbol Binding
The process of linking the semantic content of the selected option to the output symbol (e.g., the letter “A”) that the model ultimately generates.  
*Why needed*: The paper’s central finding is a dissociation between representing *what* the correct answer is and *how* it is expressed as an output token.  
*Quick check*: Permuting answer symbols (swapping the letters associated with option texts) should affect symbol decoding but leave content decoding intact.

### 5. Activation Patching (Causal Mediation)
An intervention where activations from one forward pass (the “donor”) are inserted into another pass (the “recipient”) at a specific layer, to test whether those activations are causally necessary for the output.  
*Why needed*: The authors use probe‑aligned patching to confirm that the representations identified by probes are not merely correlated but play a functional role in the decision.  
*Quick check*: If patching a layer’s activation from a run with a known correct content changes the recipient’s answer toward that content, then that activation is causally involved.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Input Text -> Tokenizer -> Token Embeddings -> Transformer Stack (L layers) -> Residual Activations at Option Boundaries -> Linear Probe (train/predict) -> Decodability Metrics (AUC, accuracy).  
Additional path for patching: Source Activation (from donor run) -> Patch Hook (inject at specific layer) -> Modified Recipient Run -> Output Change -> Causal Effect.

### Critical Path
1. Generate MCQA input with structured options.  
2. Perform a forward pass, saving residual activations at the final token of each option.  
3. Train linear probes on these activations to predict per‑option correctness and winner identity.  
4. Track probe performance across layers to identify when each signal becomes decodable.  
5. Repeat with symbol‑permuted inputs to test content‑symbol separation.  
6. Apply activation patching between controlled donor and recipient runs to verify causality.  
7. Synthesize the temporal pattern into the two‑stage hypothesis.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Linear vs. Nonlinear Probes**: Linear probes offer interpretability and efficiency, but may miss nonlinear encodings; nonlinear probes (e.g., MLPs) could be more sensitive but risk overfitting or obscuring the signal.  
- **Boundary Selection**: Using the exact last token of an option ensures clean separation but depends on tokenization; a small shift can mix contexts.  
- **Metric Choice**: AUC reflects ranking ability, while top‑1 accuracy measures exact classification; both provide complementary views.  
- **Model Selection**: Studying only two models keeps analysis tractable but limits generalizability.  
- **Patching Granularity**: Patching whole‑layer activations is coarse; more precise head‑level patching could pinpoint sub‑circuits but increases complexity.

### Failure Signatures
- Probe performance at chance level across layers → no decodable information.  
- No temporal dissociation between content and symbol probes → contradicts two‑stage claim.  
- Symbol permutation does not degrade symbol probe accuracy → symbol signal may be entangled with content.  
- Patching interventions produce no change in outputs → probes may detect incidental correlations, not causal factors.  
- Results highly sensitive to tokenization details → boundary definition may be flawed.

### First Experiments
1. Train and evaluate linear probes on each layer’s option‑boundary activations to map the decodability of per‑option correctness and winner identity.  
2. Introduce symbol permutations and re‑evaluate probes to confirm that content decoding persists while symbol decoding collapses in early layers.  
3. Conduct activation patching: for layers where content or symbol signals peak, patch the corresponding activation from a run with a known correct content (or symbol) into a run with the opposite, and measure changes in the model’s final answer to establish causal relevance.

## Limitations
- Only two instruction‑tuned models were examined; broader generalizability to other architectures or base models remains uncertain.  
- Linear probing may overlook nonlinear encoding of the target properties, potentially under‑estimating the richness of the representations.  
- The experiments were conducted on a single MCQA benchmark; cross‑dataset robustness and variability are not established.
