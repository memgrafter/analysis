# Variational inference via radial transport

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.17525
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17525
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: radVI drastically improves tail approximation in VI, reducing squared 2-Wasserstein distance by up to two orders of magnitude on heavy-tailed targets and correcting underestimation in structured posteriors like Neal's funnel.

## Executive Summary
Variational inference (VI) typically employs Gaussian approximations that struggle with heavy tails and anisotropy, leading to poor radial profile capture and biased estimates. This paper introduces radVI, a method that optimizes variational families over radially symmetric distributions by learning radial transport maps from a standard Gaussian. The approach parameterizes these maps with a piecewise linear basis and performs projected gradient descent directly in the Wasserstein space, yielding convergence guarantees under well‑conditioned assumptions. A whitening procedure combines radVI with existing Gaussian proxies (e.g., Laplace approximation or Gaussian VI) to handle structured posteriors. Empirical results on heavy‑tailed targets (Student‑t, Laplace, logistic) and complex models (Neal’s funnel) show dramatic improvements in tail matching and parameter estimation, with reductions in 2‑Wasserstein distance of up to 100× compared to standard VI in 50 dimensions.

## Method Summary
radVI approximates a posterior π ∝ exp(−V) by the pushforward of a standard Gaussian ρ under a radial map Tλ(x) = (α‖x‖ + Σ_{j=0}^J λ_j Ψ_j(‖x‖)) x/‖x‖, where {Ψ_j} are scaled hat functions on an interval centered at √d. The parameters λ are optimized to minimize KL(Tλ#ρ ∥ π). This objective is shown to be ℓ_V‑strongly convex and smooth with respect to a weighted Euclidean norm induced by the Gram matrix Q = E[⃗Ψ(‖X‖)⃗Ψ(‖X‖)^T] for X∼ρ. Stochastic projected gradient descent (SPGD) updates λ while respecting non‑negativity constraints. To address anisotropy, radVI is used as a post‑processing step: first obtain a Gaussian approximation (m,Σ) via Laplace or Gaussian VI; then whiten the potential as Ṽ(z)=V(Σ^{1/2}z+m); apply radVI to the whitened target; finally compose the maps to form T(z)=Σ^{1/2}z+m∘Tλ(z). The basis size J, interval radius R, and step size h are key hyperparameters, with theoretical guidance provided under log‑smoothness and strong log‑concavity of the (whitened) potential V.

## Key Results
- On heavy‑tailed targets (Student‑t, Laplace, logistic) in d=50, radVI reduces squared 2‑Wasserstein distance by up to two orders of magnitude relative to Gaussian VI and Laplace approximation.
- For anisotropic posteriors, the combination of Laplace/Gaussian VI with radVI corrects severe tail underestimation, as evidenced by radial profile plots and scatter diagrams.
- On Neal’s funnel, radVI accurately estimates key quantities (e.g., E[z^2], tail probabilities) that standard Gaussian VI underestimates by large factors.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: radVI parameterizes radial transport maps via a piecewise linear basis, enabling universal approximation of any sufficiently regular radial profile.
- **Mechanism**: The radial map T(x)=Ψ(‖x‖)x/‖x‖ is expressed as α‖x‖ + Σ_{j=0}^J λ_j Ψ_j(‖x‖), discretizing the scalar function Ψ on an interval [√d−R,√d+R] using hat functions.
- **Core assumption**: The true radial transport map Ψ^* has bounded first two derivatives (Theorem 3.5), which holds when the posterior potential V is log‑smooth and strongly log‑concave.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 4.1]: Theorem 4.1 gives approximation bound ‖Tλ−T^*_rad‖_{L^2(ρ)} ≤ ε/ℓ_V^{1/2} with R ≍ √log(d/ε) and J = e^{Ω(√κ/ε)}.
  - [abstract]: "We make use of the representation of a given radial measure as the pushforward of the standard Gaussian by a radial map."
- **Break condition**: If Ψ^* is highly irregular or requires high frequencies, J may become computationally infeasible. The smoothness assumption ensures polynomial rates in practice.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: radVI optimizes the KL objective over radial maps via projected gradient descent in a weighted Euclidean space, with convergence guarantees under well‑conditioned posteriors.
- **Mechanism**: The KL functional F(λ) = KL(Tλ#ρ ∥ π) is ℓ_V‑strongly convex and eO(J^2 L_V)‑smooth w.r.t. the norm ‖·‖_Q. Standard projected gradient descent then converges linearly.
- **Core assumption**: The (whitened) posterior potential V satisfies L_V‑smoothness and ℓ_V‑strong convexity.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 4.3]: Theorem 4.3 provides iteration complexity K = e^{Ω(κ^5 ε^{−1} log(initial KL/ε^2))} with stepsize h = e^{Θ(ε/(L_V κ^2))}.
  - [section 3]: Proposition 3.4 ensures the radial minimizer inherits strong convexity; Theorem 3.5 gives derivative bounds needed for smoothness.
- **Break condition**: Without strong log‑concavity, F(λ) may not be strongly convex; without smoothness, gradients may be unbounded.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: radVI enhances existing Gaussian VI methods through a whitening procedure, allowing it to correct tail mismatches in anisotropic posteriors.
- **Mechanism**: Given a Gaussian approximation N(m,Σ), define affine map T_{m,Σ}(x)=Σ^{1/2}x+m. Apply radVI to the whitened posterior exp(−V∘T_{m,Σ}), then compose: T_comp = T_{m,Σ}∘T_λ.
- **Core assumption**: The whitened posterior’s radial component is well‑approximated by a radial map, and the Gaussian proxy captures mean and covariance accurately.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [section 4.5]: Algorithm 2 outlines whitening and composition.
  - [section 5.2]: Experiments show LA/GVI fail on tails but LA+radVI/GVI+radVI succeed.
  - [abstract]: "radVI is a cheap, effective add‑on to many existing VI schemes, such as Gaussian VI and Laplace approximation."
- **Break condition**: If the initial Gaussian approximation is poor (multimodal or heavy skew), whitening may not yield a radially symmetric target; radial assumption might break.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Gaussian proxy (LA/GVI) → Whitening → Basis generation → Gram matrix Q → Stochastic gradient estimation → Projected gradient descent → Composite transport map
- **Critical path**:
  1. Obtain Gaussian approximation (m,Σ) via Laplace or Gaussian VI.
  2. Define whitened potential Ṽ(z)=V(Σ^{1/2}z+m).
  3. Run radVI on Ṽ: initialize λ; iterate stochastic gradient computation and projected update in (ℝ_+^{J+1},‖·‖_Q) until convergence.
  4. Return composite map T(z)=Σ^{1/2}z+m ∘ T_λ(z).
- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Basis size J: Larger J reduces approximation error but increases per‑iteration cost (O(J) gradient, O(J²) matrix) and smoothness constant, forcing smaller stepsizes.
  - Interval radius R: Should exceed √d by O(√log d) to cover Gaussian norm concentration; too large increases J and computation.
  - Step size h: Must scale with ε/(L_V κ²) in deterministic case; in stochastic, as ε²/(d κ³ J³ L_V). Tuning via line search recommended.
  - α parameter: Theoretically α=1/√L_V; heuristic 0.01 used in paper. Robustness shown for isotropic targets but ill‑conditioned cases may need proper scaling.
- **Failure signatures**:
  - Tail underestimation persists: radial profile shows T_λ shrinks large radii → increase J or R.
  - Objective plateaus early: may indicate too small J or poor stepsize; check strong convexity.
  - Numerical overflow in log‑det: ensure g_λ(r)>0; use λ≥0 and projection.
  - Whitening backfires: whitened posterior becomes multimodal/non‑radial → visualize radial CDF; switch to full‑cov GVI.
- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Isotropic heavy‑tailed targets** (d=50): compare squared Wasserstein distance and radial profiles for Student‑t, Laplace, logistic.
  2. **Anisotropic distributions**: apply LA/GVI then radVI; inspect scatter plots and sorted radial pushforwards.
  3. **Neal’s funnel**: estimate E[z²], E[x₁²], P(|z|>2); compare to ground truth after GVI+radVI.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Can the convergence guarantees be extended to target distributions that are not strongly log‑concave or not log‑smooth? The analysis relies on the well‑conditioned assumption; many posteriors violate these.
- What are the theoretical approximation guarantees when radVI is used as a preconditioner via whitening (Algorithm 2)? The current theory isolates the radial step but does not bound the total error of the composed map.
- Can the parametrization of radial maps be improved so that the required basis size J scales polynomially rather than exponentially in the target accuracy ε? The current guarantee J = e^{Ω(κ/ε)} may be pessimistic.
- For posteriors that are not radially symmetric even after whitening, what is the theoretical limit of radVI’s improvement, and can we characterize when it will fail to capture important non‑radial features? No theory quantifies the error of the radial projection for general structured measures.

## Limitations
- Theoretical analysis assumes well‑conditioned (log‑smooth, strongly log‑concave) posteriors, while empirical results include heavy‑tailed targets that violate these conditions.
- Approximation guarantee (Theorem 4.1) exhibits exponential dependence on condition number κ and accuracy ε, potentially making the basis size J impractically large for ill‑conditioned or high‑precision problems.
- Whitening relies on an initial Gaussian approximation that may be poor for multimodal or skewed posteriors; the projection step under the weighted norm ||·||_Q is not fully detailed, raising scalability concerns.
