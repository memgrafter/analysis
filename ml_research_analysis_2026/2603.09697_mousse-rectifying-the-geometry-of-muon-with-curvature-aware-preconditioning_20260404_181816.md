# Mousse: Rectifying the Geometry of Muon with Curvature-Aware Preconditioning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.09697
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09697
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Mousse reduces training steps to reach target validation loss by ~12% versus Muon, with negligible (~3%) wall‑clock overhead, while achieving lower final validation loss on language models from 160M to 800M parameters.

## Executive Summary
Mousse is an optimizer that improves upon the recently proposed Muon by addressing its isotropic gradient assumption, which is suboptimal in the ill‑conditioned, heavy‑tailed curvature landscapes of deep neural networks. It reconciles spectral stability with curvature awareness through a two‑step procedure: first, gradients are whitened using Shampoo’s Kronecker‑factored statistics; then, Newton‑Schulz orthogonalization is applied in this preconditioned coordinate system. Extensive experiments on transformer language models ranging from 160M to 800M parameters trained on 20B tokens demonstrate a 12% reduction in required training steps and a modest improvement in final validation loss, all with only a slight (~3%) increase in wall‑clock time.

## Method Summary
Mousse builds on Muon’s isotropic update by introducing curvature awareness without sacrificing spectral stability. For each parameter matrix, the optimizer maintains exponential moving averages of the outer products of activations and gradients, forming Kronecker factors that approximate the local curvature. These factors are used to compute a whitening transformation, i.e., multiplying the gradient by the inverse square roots of the Kronecker matrices. In this whitened space, where the covariance becomes identity, the gradient directions are orthogonalized via a few Newton‑Schulz iterations. This orthogonalized direction is then used as the update, ensuring that the step does not amplify any particular direction excessively and retains stable spectral properties. The overall procedure adds modest computational overhead relative to Muon while providing a more tailored descent direction.

## Key Results
- ~12% fewer training steps needed to reach a target validation loss compared to Muon on language models from 160M to 800M parameters.
- Lower final validation loss across all tested scales (e.g., 0.012 lower on the 800M model).
- Negligible wall‑clock overhead (~3%) and high throughput maintained relative to Muon.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Muon’s isotropic updates treat all gradient dimensions equally, ignoring the fact that deep network loss landscapes often exhibit heavy‑tailed curvature—some directions have much larger eigenvalues than others. Updating isotropically can lead to inefficient progress and oscillations along high‑curvature directions. Shampoo’s whitening aligns the coordinate system with the principal curvatures, effectively normalizing the gradient’s covariance. However, naive application of Shampoo can suffer from spectral instability because the whitening matrix itself may have a large condition number. By following whitening with Newton‑Schulz orthogonalization, Mousse ensures that the update vector lies in a subspace where all directions are orthogonal, preventing any single direction from dominating and thus maintaining spectral stability. This combination yields an update that is both curvature‑aware and spectrally well‑behaved, leading to faster convergence and improved final loss.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map**: Raw gradient → Shampoo whitening (Kronecker factors) → Newton‑Schulz orthogonalization → Parameter update.
- **Critical path**: (1) Compute exponential moving averages of Kronecker factors; (2) Apply whitening via inverse square roots; (3) Perform a fixed number (e.g., 5) of Newton‑Schulz iterations; (4) Execute the parameter update.
- **Design tradeoffs**: Shampoo reduces curvature estimation cost via Kronecker factorization but incurs memory overhead; Newton‑Schulz adds a few matrix multiplications per layer at low relative cost; number of iterations trades orthogonalization accuracy for speed.
- **Failure signatures**: Numerical overflow from near‑singular Kronecker factors; divergence with insufficient Newton‑Schulz iterations (loss increases); performance drop on non‑Kronecker‑friendly architectures (e.g., convolutions).
