# OTI: A Model-free and Visually Interpretable Measure of Image Attackability

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2601.17536  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17536  
- **Reference count:** 8  
- **Primary result:** OTI improves attack success rate by 13.39% on average over random sampling at a 10% sampling rate for single‑surrogate untargeted attacks.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces Object Texture Intensity (OTI), a novel model‑free metric that quantifies the attackability of an image based on the texture strength within its semantic object region. OTI uses a fixed Sobel filter to extract mid‑frequency edge information and a binary object mask derived from segmentation, saliency detection, or Grad‑CAM to isolate the relevant region. The resulting scalar—lower values indicate higher attackability—is both computationally efficient and directly interpretable through its texture map output. The authors motivate OTI theoretically via decision boundary proximity and the predominance of adversarial perturbations in mid‑ and high‑frequency components. Comprehensive experiments across diverse attacks, tasks (classification and segmentation), datasets (ImageNet, Kvasir‑SEG), and models—including adversarially trained ones—demonstrate that ranking images by OTI yields substantial gains in attack success rate over random sampling, particularly at low sampling rates. Ablation studies confirm the synergy of combining object area ratio and texture intensity. OTI’s simplicity and strong performance make it a valuable tool for understanding sample‑level vulnerability.

## Method Summary
OTI is defined as \(\text{OTI}(x) = \frac{1}{C \times H \times W} \| \text{object}(x) \odot (f * x) \|_1\), where \(x\) is an image, \(\text{object}(x)\) is a binary mask of the semantic object, \(f\) is a Sobel filter, \(\odot\) denotes the Hadamard product, and \(\|\cdot\|_1\) is the L1 norm. The Sobel operator captures mid‑frequency edges; masking ensures only the object’s texture contributes. Lower OTI indicates higher attackability. To apply OTI, one first precomputes object masks for the dataset (e.g., using a saliency detector), then computes OTI for every image and sorts them ascendingly. For evaluation, the top‑α% most attackable images are selected, adversarial examples are crafted on a surrogate model (e.g., ResNet‑50) under an \(\ell_\infty\) budget, and transfer attack success rate to target models is measured. The approach requires no target model access and is lightweight—essentially a single convolution and element‑wise multiplication per image.

## Key Results
- At a 10% sampling rate, OTI improves attack success rate by **13.39% on average** over random sampling on ImageNet across eight target models for single‑surrogate untargeted attacks.  
- Ablation studies show that **full OTI** (object‑masked texture) significantly outperforms its individual components (object area ratio and image texture intensity), confirming the benefit of their combination.  
- OTI **generalizes** to medical image segmentation (Kvasir‑SEG), remains effective against various attacks (ANDA, GI‑FGSM, BSR), and works against adversarially trained models, underscoring its broad applicability.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
OTI’s effectiveness rests on three pillars.

**Decision boundary proximity via relative degradation.**  
Images with smaller objects and weaker textures have a smaller input margin (distance to the classification boundary), so fewer perturbations are needed to cross it. OTI approximates this margin by combining object area ratio (OAR) and image texture intensity (ITI) within the object region; a lower score indicates a degraded signal that is easier to corrupt.

**Sensitivity to mid/high‑frequency perturbations.**  
Adversarial noise concentrates in mid‑ and high‑frequency bands. The Sobel filter extracts semantically relevant mid‑frequency edges; weaker texture (lower OTI) implies fewer inherent defenses in these bands, allowing adversarial components to more easily disrupt the semantic signal. This aligns with known frequency characteristics of attacks.

**Visual interpretability through object‑centric texture mapping.**  
The Hadamard product of the mask and Sobel map yields a direct visualization of “attackable texture.” A smooth, low‑texture object produces a low OTI score, giving humans an intuitive grasp of vulnerability—unlike black‑box model‑dependent measures.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Input image → Object Extractor (produces binary mask) → Texture Extractor (Sobel filter) → Isolator (Hadamard product of mask and texture map) → Aggregator (L1‑norm sum normalized by pixel count) → OTI score.

**Critical path:** The accuracy of the Object Extractor is the highest‑risk component; imprecise masks may include background textures or exclude object parts, breaking the link between OTI and the true semantic object’s attackability.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Sobel operator vs. more complex texture descriptors:* Sobel offers efficiency and strong empirical results but may not capture all texture nuances; a learned descriptor could improve accuracy but would reintroduce model dependency.  
- *Segmentation vs. Saliency vs. Grad‑CAM:* Ground‑truth segmentation is ideal but often unavailable; saliency is fast but can include contextual background; Grad‑CAM is model‑dependent yet task‑relevant. The paper accepts any “approximately correct” mask.

**Failure signatures:**  
1. High OTI but vulnerable image: a large, highly textured background mistakenly included in the mask would inflate OTI, misclassifying a vulnerable image as robust.  
2. Low OTI but robust image: a large, smooth object may receive a low OTI despite other robustness factors (e.g., adversarial training), leading to false‑high attackability prediction.  
3. Inconsistent rankings across object extractors: drastic changes when switching mask types indicate OTI’s sensitivity to mask quality and unreliability in practice.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Single‑Surrogate Untargeted Attack Efficacy:** Rank ImageNet by OTI, take top‑α% (e.g., 10%) samples, generate adversarial examples on a surrogate (ResNet‑50/ViT), transfer to eight target models, and compare attack success rate (ASR) to random sampling. Validates that low‑OTI samples yield higher ASR.  
2. **Ablation Study:** Compare OAR‑only, ITI‑only, and full OTI against random sampling under identical attack conditions. Shows the synergistic gain of combining object area and texture via the Hadamard product.  
3. **Cross‑Domain/Task Validation on Segmentation:** Apply OTI to Kvasir‑SEG (medical polyps), rank images, attack U‑Net variants with MI‑FGSM, and measure degradation in precision/F1/IoU. Demonstrates generalizability beyond natural image classification.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Extension to non‑image modalities.** OTI relies on visual concepts (object texture, area); adapting it to audio or text requires new definitions of “semantic object” and “texture.”
- **Robustness to mask inaccuracies.** Real‑world masks (from saliency or imperfect segmentation) may be noisy; the paper does not analyze how such errors affect OTI’s ranking of attackability.
- **Optimality of the Sobel operator.** Sobel is used as a fixed filter; other frequency‑domain (e.g., Fourier, wavelet) or learned texture descriptors might better capture attack‑relevant features.
- **Behavior on multi‑object or cluttered scenes.** OTI assumes a single primary semantic object; its performance when objects are numerous or backgrounds noisy remains unexplored.

## Limitations
- Attack hyperparameters (iterations, step sizes, decay) for MI‑FGSM and BSR are not fully specified, hindering exact replication.  
- Details of the VST saliency detector (checkpoint URL, input resolution, thresholding, morphological ops) are missing, making mask generation ambiguous.  
- OTI’s dependence on accurate object masks introduces sensitivity; mask errors from saliency or Grad‑CAM can invalidate the measure.
