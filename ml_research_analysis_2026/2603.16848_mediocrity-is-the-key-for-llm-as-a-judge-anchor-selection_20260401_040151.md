# Mediocrity is the key for LLM as a Judge Anchor Selection

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2603.16848  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16848  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Anchor choice critically impacts LLM-as-a-judge reliability, with mediocre (mid-ranked) models as optimal anchors and extreme top/bottom models degrading correlation by up to 0.19 due to signal saturation.

## Executive Summary
This paper studies anchor selection in LLM-as-a-judge evaluations, where a single anchor model is compared against all others to reduce cost. Evaluating 22 anchors on Arena-Hard-v2.0 with five different judges, the authors find that anchor quality—measured by correlation with human or full pairwise rankings—follows an inverted U-shape relative to the anchor’s capability rank. Top- and bottom-performing anchors are worst because they produce many saturated comparisons (e.g., the anchor always wins or loses), rendering up to 55% of judgments uninformative. Consequently, standard benchmark sizes (750 samples) are often statistically underpowered, sometimes requiring over 1,300 samples for small effect sizes. The impact of anchor selection is comparable to that of judge selection. Recommendations include preferring mid-ranked anchors for leaderboards, avoiding anchor-based evaluation for very small model pools, and explicitly reporting anchor informativeness.

## Method Summary
The authors conduct a large-scale empirical study using the Arena-Hard-v2.0 benchmark (750 instructions) and a pool of 22 LLMs. For each of 22 potential anchor models, they generate pairwise preference judgments from five different judge LLMs (e.g., GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet) comparing the anchor against every other model on each instruction. Verdicts are collapsed to three values: anchor wins, loses, or ties. They aggregate these into rankings via Bradley-Terry (BT) scores and compute Kendall’s τ against two gold standards: the quadratic ranking from full pairwise comparisons and a human ranking. To quantify saturation, they measure the proportion of comparisons where the anchor’s win-rate is near 0 or 1. Finally, they perform a power analysis to estimate required sample sizes for detecting effect sizes given observed informativeness rates.

## Key Results
- Anchor quality (Kendall’s τ) exhibits a robust inverted U-shape versus anchor rank; mid-ranked anchors (e.g., Gemma 3 27B Instruct) achieve highest correlation, while top/bottom anchors degrade it by up to 0.19.
- Extreme anchors cause severe signal saturation: for o3, ~55% of comparisons are ties or deterministic wins, wasting evaluation budget.
- Standard benchmark size (N=750) is underpowered for small effects when informativeness is moderate; e.g., with 45% informative comparisons, N≈1,372 is needed to detect a +5% win-rate difference.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Signal Saturation with Extreme Anchors
Extreme anchors (much stronger or weaker than the model pool) yield many uninformative comparisons, reducing effective sample size and correlation with gold rankings. When an anchor is extreme, the judge’s verdict is nearly always the same (anchor wins or loses) across instructions, providing no discriminative signal between competing models. This saturation wastes evaluation budget because those comparisons do not help distinguish the other models from each other. The break condition is when the anchor’s win-rate against the pool is near 1 or 0; at that point, informativeness collapses.

### Mechanism 2: Inverted U-Shape of Anchor Quality
Anchor quality correlates with its capability rank in an inverted U-shape: mid-ranked models produce more balanced verdict distributions and higher informativeness, while extremes produce skewed distributions. The gold ranking (quadratic or human) serves as a valid reference, and this relationship holds robustly across different judges and datasets tested. The optimal anchor is one whose win-rate against the pool is near 50%, maximizing the number of non-tie verdicts that differentiate other models. If the model pool is extremely diverse, the curve might shift, but within the studied pool, the shape is consistent.

### Mechanism 3: Sample SizeRequirements Driven by Informativeness
The effective sample size in anchor-based evaluation is the number of informative (non-tie) comparisons. Because informativeness is bounded by anchor position (theoretical maximum ~50% with three-valued verdicts), typical benchmarks may be underpowered. Power analysis accounting for measured informativeness shows that for modest effect sizes (e.g., +5% win-rate), over 1,300 samples are often needed, exceeding the common 750-sample benchmark. This effect size of anchor selection matches the impact of judge choice, highlighting its practical significance.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Anchor-based evaluation**  
  *Why needed:* The paper’s entire setup relies on anchor-based comparison to achieve O(M) instead of O(M²) judgment cost. Understanding this trade-off is essential.  
  *Quick check:* What is the primary scalability advantage of anchor-based evaluation over full pairwise comparison? **Answer: Reduces required judgments from O(M²) to O(M), where M is the number of models.**

- **Concept: Informativeness (I(p,A))**  
  *Why needed:* Informativeness quantifies the fraction of comparisons that yield discriminative (non-tie) verdicts; it directly determines statistical power and ranking accuracy.  
  *Quick check:* How is informativeness mathematically defined in the paper? **Answer: I(p,A) = (1/N)*(M choose 2)^{-1} Σ_{i=1}^N Σ_{a≠b} 1_{v_{i,a} ≠ v_{i,b}}; the average per-sample fraction of model pairs with differing verdicts.**

- **Concept: Bradley-Terry (BT) model**  
  *Why needed:* BT is the aggregation method used to convert pairwise verdicts into scalar scores and rankings for both quadratic and anchor-based evaluations.  
  *Quick check:* Under the Bradley-Terry model, what is P(i beats j)? **Answer: exp(s_i)/(exp(s_i)+exp(s_j)), where s_i is the latent score for model i.**

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Model pool -> Judge model -> Anchor model -> Benchmark dataset -> Response generation -> Verdict collection -> Aggregation (BT/win-rate) -> Ranking -> Evaluation (Kendall’s τ vs gold)

- **Critical path:**  
  1. Select judge LLM and anchor model.  
  2. Generate responses from all models for all benchmark instructions.  
  3. Query judge for each anchor vs. every other model comparison, per instruction.  
  4. Collate verdicts into matrix V.  
  5. Compute BT scores from V.  
  6. Rank models by scores to obtain anchor-based ranking.  
  7. Compute correlation (Kendall’s τ) with gold (quadratic/human) ranking.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Single vs. multiple anchors: single anchor cheap but high variance; multiple anchors improve reliability at linear cost.  
  - Strong vs. mediocre anchor: strong anchors saturate, yielding low informativeness; mediocre anchors improve power but may be less intuitive.  
  - Judge choice: larger/stronger judges align better with humans but cost more; smaller judges may introduce bias.  
  - Aggregation: BT discards magnitude; preserving raw scores might reduce ties but complicate analysis.  
  - Sample size: larger N improves reliability; power analysis guides minimal N given informativeness.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Low informativeness (<50%): anchor too extreme, many ties.  
  - High variance across random subsamples: insufficient sample size.  
  - Large swings when changing judge: judge-specific bias.  
  - Discrepancy between anchor-based and quadratic rankings >0.19: poor anchor choice.  
  - High tie rate (~55% with o3): signal saturation.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Evaluate all 22 anchors on Arena-Hard-v2.0 using 5 judges; compute Kendall’s τ against quadratic and human rankings (Section 4.1; Tables 1,4-7; Figures 12-16).  
  2. Quantify signal saturation by plotting win-rate distributions for extreme (o3) vs. mediocre (Gemma 3 27B) anchors (Section 4.2; Figure 2; Table 9).  
  3. Perform power analysis via simulation to determine required sample sizes for various effect sizes and informativeness rates (Section 4.3; Table 2; Appendix C).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Question 1:** Do the observed inverted U-shape anchor quality patterns generalize to commercial LLM judges such as GPT-4 and Claude?  
  *Basis:* The study uses five non-commercial models as judges; applicability to top commercial models is assumed but unverified.  
  *Why unresolved:* Commercial models may have different biases affecting anchor comparisons.

- **Question 2:** Does preserving the magnitude of judge preferences (e.g., using continuous scores) increase anchor informativeness and reduce required sample sizes?  
  *Basis:* The paper notes BT discards magnitude and that power analysis considered both sign test and Wilcoxon, but main experiments used collapsed verdicts.  
  *Why unresolved:* Magnitude could carry additional signal that reduces ties and improves discrimination.

- **Question 3:** How do intransitive cycles in LLM-as-judge comparisons affect anchor-based ranking reliability, and can detection or correction methods improve results?  
  *Basis:* The paper relies on transitivity but cites prior work showing LLMs violate it; impact on anchor evaluation is not quantified.  
  *Why unresolved:* Intransitivity might introduce systematic errors, especially for extreme anchors.

- **Question 4:** Can we develop a predictive model for anchor informativeness that avoids full benchmarking, enabling efficient anchor selection?  
  *Basis:* The authors show small-sample estimation works but do not explore predictive features; "mediocrity" is relative to the model pool.  
  *Why unresolved:* Full benchmarking is expensive; prediction from model properties would be more practical.

## Limitations
- **Benchmark scope:** Findings derive solely from Arena-Hard-v2.0 (750 English instructions, general-purpose LLMs). Generalizability to other domains (code, math), languages, or specialized model pools is untested.
- **Verdict scale dependency:** Analysis assumes three-valued verdicts {-1,0,1}; magnitude-based judging (e.g., 0–10) likely reduces ties, altering informativeness and sample size requirements.
- **Small-pool extrapolation:** Recommendation to avoid anchors for ≤3 models is inferred from the 22-model pool’s behavior; explicit validation on very small pools (2–4 models) is absent.

## Confidence
- **High:** Saturation effect (up to 55% uninformative comparisons) and inverted U-shape relationship between anchor rank and Kendall’s τ—robust across 5 judges and direct metrics.
- **Medium:** Power analysis conclusions (e.g., 1,372 samples needed) depend on measured informativeness rates and simulation assumptions; external validation pending.
- **Low:** Specific guideline “avoid anchor-based evaluation for ≤3 models” lacks direct empirical tests on such small pools.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-benchmark replication:** Repeat anchor evaluation on a diverse suite (e.g., MT-Bench, multilingual tasks) to validate the inverted U-shape’s universality.
2. **Magnitude-scale experiment:** Compare informativeness and power requirements using fine-grained judge scores (no forced ties) versus three-valued collapse.
3. **Small-pool empirical test:** Directly evaluate anchor selection on leaderboards with 2–4 models, measuring ranking stability against full pairwise gold standards.
