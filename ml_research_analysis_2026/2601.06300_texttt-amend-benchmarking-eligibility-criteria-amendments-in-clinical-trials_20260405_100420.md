# $\texttt{AMEND++}$: Benchmarking Eligibility Criteria Amendments in Clinical Trials

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.06300
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06300
- Reference count: 10
- Primary result: CAMLM improves amendment prediction; on AMEND_LLM, BioBERT_CAMLM achieves AUROC 0.697 (vs 0.687), AUPRC 0.634 (vs 0.621), and accuracy 0.644 (vs 0.619), and LLM-denoised labels have 99.34% human agreement.

## Executive Summary
This paper introduces the novel NLP task of eligibility criteria amendment prediction to address frequent and costly changes in clinical trial protocols. It presents the AMEND++ benchmark suite, comprising the large-scale AMEND dataset and the refined AMEND_LLM subset with high-fidelity labels obtained via an LLM-based denoising pipeline. The core method is Change-Aware Masked Language Modeling (CAMLM), which leverages historical edits by masking unstable spans during pretraining. Experiments demonstrate that CAMLM consistently improves prediction performance, with BioBERT_CAMLM outperforming baseline BioBERT on multiple metrics. The LLM denoising achieves 99.34% agreement with human annotations, confirming label quality.

## Method Summary
The AMEND++ benchmark is built by scraping ClinicalTrials.gov to collect longitudinal eligibility criteria (EC) histories. An LLM-based pipeline decomposes amendments into added, removed, and modified criteria to produce denoised labels for training/validation, while a human-annotated test set ensures reliability. The proposed CAMLM pretraining identifies historically unstable spans (tokens that changed between earlier and final EC versions) and masks them with high probability during MLM, forcing the model to learn representations sensitive to likely amendments. For downstream prediction, a pretrained encoder (e.g., BioBERT_CAMLM) is fine-tuned with a classifier head using initial EC text and metadata. The pipeline proceeds: collect data → denoise labels → pretrain with CAMLM → fine-tune classifier → evaluate on human test set.

## Key Results
- CAMLM yields consistent improvements: on AMEND_LLM, BioBERT_CAMLM surpasses baseline BioBERT by +0.010 AUROC, +0.013 AUPRC, and +2.5% accuracy.
- LLM-denoised labels achieve 99.34% agreement with human annotations, demonstrating high fidelity.
- Ablation studies confirm the contribution of revision-aware masking and long-horizon modeling.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
CAMLM's efficacy stems from three key mechanisms:

**Mechanism 1: Revision-Aware Masking**  
The method computes token-level diffs between earlier and final EC versions to identify "unstable spans" (deletions/substitutions). These spans are masked with high probability (0.8) during MLM pretraining. The core assumption is that historically unstable tokens are more likely to be involved in future amendments. Evidence: Section 3.4 details the unstable span computation and masking strategy. Break condition: If unstable spans are not predictive (e.g., changes are random), CAMLM may not improve performance.

**Mechanism 2: LLM-Based Label Denoising**  
The LLM pipeline decomposes amendment detection into three complementary checks (added, removed, modified criteria) to isolate substantive changes from formatting edits. The OR of outputs yields the final label. The assumption is that LLMs can accurately judge substantive changes per defined rules. Evidence: Table 6 shows 40 mismatches out of 6,000 samples, achieving 99.34% match rate with human annotations. Break condition: If LLM prompts fail to capture domain nuances, label noise may persist.

**Mechanism 3: Long-Horizon Protocol Modeling**  
CAMLM pairs each earlier EC version with the final version, exposing the model to changes that persist across multiple revisions, rather than only adjacent diffs. The assumption is that criteria ultimately amended exhibit instability over multiple steps, providing a stronger signal. Evidence: Section 3.4 describes this pairing strategy, and Section 4.4 compares it to baselines. Break condition: If long-horizon changes include irrelevant reverted edits or are not more predictive, benefits may be limited.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Data Collection → Label Denoising (train/val labels)  
Data Collection → CAMLM Pretraining (pretrained model)  
Label Denoising + CAMLM Pretraining → Downstream Classification (fine-tuned model)  
Downstream Classification → Evaluation (metrics on human test set)

**Critical path:**  
1. Build AMEND_LLM dataset: scrape ClinicalTrials.gov, compute diffs, run LLM prompts, filter denoised subset.  
2. Pretrain BioBERT with CAMLM on training EC histories using unstable-span masking.  
3. Fine-tune the pretrained model on the amendment prediction task using initial EC and metadata.  
4. Evaluate on the human-annotated test set and compare with baselines.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- AMEND_LLM (denoised, high quality) vs. AMEND (larger raw scale).  
- CAMLM (requires version histories and diff computation) vs. standard MLM (simpler).  
- Domain-specific encoder (BioBERT) vs. general (BERT): BioBERT performs better but is less flexible.

**Failure signatures:**  
- No CAMLM gain: Check unstable span computation; histories may be sparse or changes non-semantic.  
- Low human-LLM agreement (<95%): Inspect LLM prompt failures; refine or use a more capable LLM.  
- Overfitting (train/test AUROC gap >0.1): Increase regularization or simplify classifier.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Main result: Fine-tune BioBERT with/without CAMLM on AMEND_LLM/AMEND, showing consistent metric improvements (e.g., AUROC +0.01, accuracy +2.5%).  
2. Pretraining ablation: Compare no pretraining, MLM, Span MLM, CAMLM to isolate the contribution of long-horizon unstable masking.  
3. Label denoising impact: Train on raw CTGov vs. LLM-denosed labels to demonstrate cleaner labels improve downstream accuracy.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Extension to other protocol sections** – The paper focuses exclusively on eligibility criteria; other sections (outcomes, interventions, study design) are also commonly amended but have different linguistic patterns. Resolving this requires building analogous longitudinal datasets and adapting the LLM denoising pipeline.
2. **Potential false negatives in amendment labels** – Unreported changes in ClinicalTrials.gov could bias training data. The prevalence of false negatives is unknown; resolving this would involve manual annotation of label-0 trials or cross-referencing with sponsor records.
3. **Completeness of LLM-based decomposition** – The three-way decomposition (added, removed, modified) may miss complex or nuanced amendments. Error analysis on mismatched cases and exploring alternative decompositions could address this.
4. **Interpretability of unstable spans** – Which characteristics of unstable spans (e.g., numeric thresholds, medical entities) are most predictive? Feature attribution studies and qualitative review could translate findings into actionable design guidelines.

## Limitations
- Limited dataset scope: AMEND++ is sourced from ClinicalTrials.gov, and many trials lack multiple versions, restricting generalizability.
- Residual label noise: Despite 99.34% human-LLM agreement, subtle amendments might still be misclassified.
- Small performance gains: CAMLM improvements are marginal (e.g., AUROC +0.01), raising concerns about practical impact.

## Confidence
