# Attention Retention for Continual Learning with Vision Transformers
## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.05454  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05454  
- **Reference count:** 10  
- **Primary result:** ARCL-ViT mitigates catastrophic forgetting in ViTs by masking attention gradients, achieving up to 3.7% higher average accuracy and 46% reduced forgetting versus sequential fine-tuning on ImageNet-R and CIFAR-100.

## Executive Summary
Catastrophic forgetting in Vision Transformers (ViTs) is often caused by attention drift, where previously learned discriminative regions lose focus during sequential task training. The proposed ARCL-ViT framework addresses this by generating binary masks from prior task attention maps via layer‑wise rollout and adaptive thresholding. These masks zero out gradients within previously salient regions during new task training, preserving learned visual concepts while maintaining optimizer compatibility through scaled updates. Extensive experiments on 10/20‑split ImageNet‑R and CIFAR‑100 show state‑of‑the‑art performance, with up to 3.7% accuracy improvement and 46% forgetting reduction over strong baselines. Ablations validate the necessity of masking and the benefits of the proposed rollout and thresholding strategies.

## Method Summary
ARCL‑ViT proceeds in three stages. After training task *T<sub>t‑1</sub>*, a small set of representative samples is forwarded through the model to collect attention maps from all transformer blocks. A mask generator then applies multiplicative layer‑wise rollout to aggregate multi‑layer attention, extracts class‑specific attention maps, and uses an adaptive threshold derived from the curvature of the sorted attention values to produce a binary mask per layer. During training on task *T<sub>t</sub>*, the gradients of query/key/value projections are element‑wise multiplied by the inverted mask to suppress updates in prior salient regions. To preserve optimizer stability (e.g., Adam), the resulting parameter update is scaled proportionally to the ratio of masked to original gradients. This process repeats for each new task, accumulating masks to continuously protect knowledge without storing raw exemplars.

## Key Results
- Achieves state‑of‑the‑art average accuracy on 10/20‑split ImageNet‑R and CIFAR‑100, improving by up to 3.7% over prior methods.
- Reduces catastrophic forgetting by up to 46% compared to sequential fine‑tuning.
- Ablations confirm the importance of layer‑wise rollout and adaptive thresholding, which together contribute ~1–5% accuracy gains.
- Generalizes robustly across diverse pre‑training schemes (e.g., DINO, iBOT) and long‑sequence scenarios.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Zeroing out gradients in regions corresponding to previous task attention prevents disruption of learned visual concepts, mitigating catastrophic forgetting.
- **Mechanism:** After training task *T<sub>t‑1</sub>*, binary masks derived from its attention maps are applied during *T<sub>t</sub>* backpropagation: element‑wise multiplication of gradients ∇*W<sub>{q,k,v}</sub>* by the inverted mask M̄ suppresses updates in previously salient regions. This directly reduces attention drift—the shift away from earlier task features.
- **Core assumption:** Attention maps are stable indicators of learned discriminative features; altering gradients in these regions degrades performance on prior tasks.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract; Section “Attention Retention via Gradient Masking” Eq. (5); related work on attention mechanisms in ViTs for CL.
- **Break condition:** If masks inaccurately identify prior attention (e.g., due to poor thresholding), irrelevant gradients may be suppressed, harming plasticity.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Layer‑wise rollout combined with adaptive thresholding generates instance‑adaptive masks that reliably capture discriminative regions across inputs and layers.
- **Mechanism:** For each layer *l*, attention maps from all layers are multiplicatively accumulated: Ŝ<sub>l</sub> = ˜S<sub>1</sub>·…·˜S<sub>l</sub>. From this, the class‑attention map U<sub>l</sub> is extracted. An adaptive threshold τ<sub>l</sub> is set at the inflection point of the sorted U<sub>l</sub> curve (minimizing second derivative), binarizing high‑attention pixels.
- **Core assumption:** Layer‑wise rollout better aggregates multi‑layer attention; adaptive thresholding separates salient features from background without manual tuning.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section “Adaptive Mask Generation” (Eqs. 9–11); ablation in Table 2 showing ~1–5% gain over raw/naive rollout and fixed threshold.
- **Break condition:** Uniform attention distributions may lack a clear inflection, causing adaptive threshold to fail; fallback strategies may be needed.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Scaling parameter updates proportionally to the ratio of masked/original gradients maintains optimizer compatibility (e.g., Adam) while applying gradient masking.
- **Mechanism:** Optimizer computes update Δ*W* from raw gradients. Updates are then scaled as Δ*W'* = (∇(W)' / ∇(W)) ⊙ Δ*W*, preserving relative magnitude between masked and original update directions.
- **Core assumption:** Direct use of masked gradients disrupts optimizer state (e.g., Adam moments), leading to divergence; scaling aligns update magnitudes with original gradient flow.
- **Evidence anchors:** Section “Attention Retention via Gradient Masking” (Eqs. 6–8); abstract statement on optimizer compatibility.
- **Break condition:** Near‑zero raw gradients (e.g., early training) cause division instability; epsilon smoothing may be required.

## Architecture Onboarding
Component Map: Attention Extractor -> Mask Generator -> Gradient Modifier -> Parameter Updater.

Critical Path:
1. Train *T<sub>1</sub>* normally on all parameters.
2. Post‑*T<sub>1</sub>*: Extract attention from the *T<sub>1</sub>* model on a small validation set or cached samples → generate masks *M̄<sub>1</sub>*.
3. Train *T<sub>2</sub>*: Forward pass → compute loss → backprop → **modify gradients using *M̄<sub>1</sub>*** → scale optimizer updates → update *W<sub>{q,k,v}</sub>* layers.
4. Repeat: After *T<sub>t‑1</sub>*, update mask set {*M̄<sub>1</sub>*, ..., *M̄<sub>t‑1</sub>*} (averaged per class); during *T<sub>t</sub>* apply all masks sequentially or combined.

Design Tradeoffs:
- **Stability vs. Plasticity:** Stronger masking preserves old knowledge but may hinder new task learning; adaptive thresholding balances this per‑instance.
- **Storage vs. Performance:** Masks are small (size of attention maps) versus storing exemplars (replay methods); no raw data storage, but requires forward passes on representative samples per task.
- **Layer‑specific vs. Shared Masks:** Per‑layer masks improve accuracy; sharing across layers compresses representation at a cost.
- **Optimizer Overhead:** Scaling adds minimal computation; optimizer states (e.g., Adam moments) remain unchanged.

Failure Signatures:
- **High forgetting:** Mask quality degraded (threshold too low/high); check adaptive threshold output distribution.
- **Poor new‑task accuracy:** Over‑masking (too many zeros); verify mask sparsity and layer‑wise rollout consistency.
- **Optimizer divergence:** Scaling factor miscomputed (division by near‑zero ∇(W)); add epsilon to denominator.
- **Attention collapse:** Mask zeros out all regions; inspect *U<sub>l</sub>* histogram—should show a multi‑modal distribution for adaptive thresholding.

First 3 Experiments:
1. **Baseline validation:** Train Seq‑FT (no masking) vs. ARCL‑ViT on 10‑split ImageNet‑R; expect ~37% accuracy gain, 46% forgetting reduction (Table 1).
2. **Ablation of mask components:** Test raw vs. layer‑wise rollout; fixed vs. adaptive threshold (Table 2). Expect layer‑wise + adaptive to be best.
3. **Generalization test:** Use DINO/iBOT pre‑trained weights (Table 4). Expect consistent improvement over Seq‑FT and SOTA, confirming robustness to pre‑training.

## Limitations
- Assumption that attention maps reliably encode learned features may break for visually similar tasks or attention collapse.
- Adaptive thresholding relies on a clear inflection in the attention curve; uniform distributions could cause mask failure.
- Requires representative samples per task for mask generation, adding forward‑pass overhead and potential bias.
- Evaluation limited to 10/20‑split ImageNet‑R and CIFAR‑100; unexplored on detection/segmentation or longer sequences.

## Confidence
- Gradient masking prevents attention drift: **High**
- Layer‑wise rollout + adaptive thresholding: **Medium**
- Optimizer compatibility via scaling: **Medium**
- General robustness across pre‑training schemes: **High**
