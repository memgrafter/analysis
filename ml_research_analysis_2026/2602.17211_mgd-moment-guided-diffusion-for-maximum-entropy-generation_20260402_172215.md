# MGD: Moment Guided Diffusion for Maximum Entropy Generation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.17211  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17211  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** MGD generates maximum entropy samples matching wavelet-scattering moments with O(σ²) computational cost and σ⁻² convergence, applicable to complex multiscale processes.

## Executive Summary
Maximum entropy sampling from moment constraints is hindered by slow mixing in MCMC/Langevin methods, while diffusion generative models lack explicit moment control and can overfit with limited data. Moment Guided Diffusion (MGD) introduces a stochastic differential equation that guides moments—estimated via wavelet scattering—along an interpolating path from noise to data, with tunable volatility σ. Theoretical analysis shows that as σ → ∞, the output distribution converges to the maximum entropy target with KL divergence bounded by Cσ⁻², and a computable entropy lower bound converges at the same rate. Numerical verification on 1D non‑log‑concave and non‑smooth densities confirms the σ⁻² decay. For high‑dimensional real datasets (S&P 500 returns, 2D turbulent vorticity, cosmological dark‑matter fields), MGD generates samples matching wavelet‑scattering moments and yields negentropy estimates. Computationally, MGD scales as O(σ²) and remains insensitive to energy‑barrier height, unlike MCMC which scales exponentially.

## Method Summary
MGD solves the McKean‑Vlasov stochastic differential equation:  
 dX_t = (η_t^⊤ – σ² θ_t^⊤)∇ϕ(X_t) dt + √(2σ) dW_t,  
where η_t and θ_t are drift coefficients determined from the Gram matrix G_t = E[∇ϕ·∇ϕ^⊤] and the target moment path m_t = E[ϕ(I_t)]. The interpolant I_t = cos(α_t)Z + sin(α_t)X (Z∼N(0,I), X data) defines m_t, typically with α_t = πt/2. The coefficients satisfy G_t η_t = dm_t/dt and G_t θ_t = E[Δϕ(X_t)], ensuring exact moment preservation along the trajectory. The SDE is discretized using a predictor‑corrector scheme on a particle ensemble; the number of integration steps scales as O(σ²). Larger σ reduces the gap to the maximum entropy distribution p* ∝ exp(–θ*^⊤ϕ), while the entropy lower bound H_σ* = H(p₀) + ∫ η_t^⊤ dm_t converges at rate O(σ⁻²).

## Key Results
- **Convergence rate:** As σ → ∞, the output distribution p_σ^1 converges to the maximum entropy target p* with KL divergence D_KL(p_σ^1 ‖ p*) ≤ Cσ⁻².
- **Entropy estimation:** A tractable lower bound H_σ* on the entropy converges to H(p*) at rate O(σ⁻²), validated numerically on 1D test densities.
- **Empirical performance & scaling:** On high‑dimensional datasets (S&P 500 returns, 2D turbulent vorticity, cosmological dark‑matter fields), MGD generates samples matching wavelet‑scattering moments and yields negentropy estimates (e.g., S&P 0.05, cosmic web 0.07, turbulence 0.34). Computational cost scales as O(σ²) and is insensitive to energy‑barrier height.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
MGD relies on three interlocking mechanisms:

1. **Moment preservation via coupled drift.** The SDE’s drift coefficients η_t and θ_t are dynamically adjusted to exactly maintain the prescribed moment constraints E[ϕ(X_t)] = m_t along the trajectory. They solve G_t η_t = dm_t/dt and G_t θ_t = E[Δϕ(X_t)], ensuring the instantaneous change in moments matches the target path m_t. Evidence: Theorem 3.1 proves preservation when G_t is invertible. Break condition: G_t becomes near‑singular (e.g., collinear gradients or particle collapse), causing η_t, θ_t to blow up and moment preservation to fail.

2. **Early mode formation through stochastic transport.** Starting from unimodal Gaussian noise, MGD follows a smooth interpolant path I_t that transitions from unimodal to multimodal. Particles are guided into the eventual target modes while the energy landscape is still smooth, avoiding high barrier crossings that slow MCMC. The expressiveness of ϕ determines whether mass transport can form modes early; insufficient ϕ forces reliance on late‑time noise, reverting to exponential scaling. Evidence: Figure 1 illustrates early mode formation; Section 5.1.2 shows slower convergence when ϕ = (x², log p(x)) cannot displace mass early. Break condition: ϕ does not uniquely determine the interpolating path m_t, so mode placement is inaccurate.

3. **Large‑σ convergence to maximum entropy.** In the limit σ → ∞, the Fokker‑Planck equation for p_σ_t reduces to a PDE whose solution is the exponential family p* ∝ exp(–θ*^⊤ϕ). A Taylor expansion yields D_KL(p_σ^1 ‖ p*) ≤ Cσ⁻², and the entropy lower bound H_σ* converges at the same rate. Evidence: Conjecture 4.1 and Proposition 4.3; Section 5 validates σ⁻² decay on 1D non‑log‑concave and non‑smooth densities. Break condition: The maximum entropy solution p* does not exist (incompatible moments) or the expansion fails (e.g., poor ϕ makes p*_t a bad approximant).

## Architecture Onboarding
The MGD pipeline flows as:  
Interpolant I_t generates target moments m_t → Particle ensemble approximates density p_t^σ → Predictor applies drift adjustment based on η_t → Corrector projects to preserve moments using θ_t → Volatility σ determines step count n_σ = O(σ²).

**Critical path:** Accurate moment estimation m_t → correct coefficients η_t, θ_t via Gram inversion → stable dynamics → convergence to p*.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Precomputation vs. memory: Algorithm 1 computes coefficients on‑the‑fly (memory efficient); Algorithm 2 precomputes η_t via SGD on I_t (faster sampling, stores coefficients); Algorithm 3 learns offline (parallel sampling, stores n_σ coefficient sets).
- Gram matrix regularization: Adding δI or discarding vanishing ϕ components prevents singularity, but excessive δ biases coefficients while insufficient δ causes instability.
- Volatility σ: Larger σ improves accuracy but increases O(σ²) cost; trade‑off between precision and runtime.

**Failure signatures:**
- Moment drift: Empirical ϕ‑mean deviates from m_t despite corrector → step size too large or G_t near‑singular.
- Exploding coefficients: Norms of η_t, θ_t grow without bound → regularize Gram matrix or increase particle count.
- No convergence of H_σ*: H_σ* does not plateau as σ increases → ϕ lacks expressivity or constant C is large.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. 1D non‑log‑concave test: Use p(x) ∝ exp(–β(x⁴–5x²–x/2)) with ϕ = (x, x², x³, x⁴). Verify D_KL(p_σ^1 ‖ p) ≤ Cσ⁻² and H_σ* → H(p).
2. Non‑smooth ϕ test: Laplacian p(x) = ½e^{-|x|} with ϕ = (x², |x|). Test corrector‑only approach without computing Δϕ.
3. High‑dimensional scattering: Apply to 2D turbulent vorticity (d = 128²) with scattering moments. Check negentropy convergence and visual fidelity of generated samples.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- What are the necessary and sufficient conditions for MGD to converge to the maximum entropy distribution in full generality? Current proofs rely on regularisation assumptions; a general theory remains an important challenge.
- How does MGD behave when the maximum entropy distribution constrained by the given moments does not exist? This scenario is unanalyzed and could lead to non‑convergence or biased samples.
- How does the choice of moment function ϕ quantitatively affect the convergence rate and accuracy? The dependence of the O(σ⁻²) constant on ϕ’s expressiveness is not quantified, making optimal ϕ selection difficult.
- Can the moment interpolation path m_t be computed directly from target moments m = E[ϕ(X)] and ϕ without requiring samples from the data distribution? This would enable maximum entropy sampling from limited moment information alone.

## Limitations
- **Moment function expressivity:** Inflexible ϕ fails to capture early mode formation, leading to poor convergence or biased maximum entropy approximations.
- **Numerical stability:** Gram matrix inversion requires many particles; high‑dimensional scattering ϕ risks near‑singularity, increasing memory and computational cost.
- **Entropy bound tightness:** The lower bound H_σ* may have a non‑vanishing gap for finite σ; reported negentropy values lack independent validation against known ground truths.

## Confidence
- Theoretical convergence rate (D_KL ≤ Cσ⁻², H_σ* rate O(σ⁻²)): **Medium** (formal asymptotic derivation and 1D validation, but no general proof).
- High‑dimensional sampling accuracy (matching scattering moments, negentropy estimates): **Medium** (empirical results on three datasets, but no ground truth; scattering parameters not fully specified).
- Computational scaling (O(σ²) steps, barrier‑insensitive): **Medium** (step count scales as σ² by design, but constant factors and particle memory needs unquantified; barrier insensitivity based on mechanism, not systematic experiments).

## Next Checks
1. **Ablation on moment expressivity:** Use a synthetic multimodal target (e.g., mixture of Gaussians) with known maximum entropy solution for increasing polynomial moment orders. Measure KL(p_σ^1 ‖ p*) vs. σ; expect error constant C to decrease with richer ϕ.
2. **Scaling vs. barrier height:** For a 1D double‑well with barrier height λ, fix a target KL error and measure MGD’s time (steps × particles) vs. λ. Compare to Langevin dynamics; expect MGD’s complexity to grow polynomially while Langevin’s grows exponentially.
3. **Entropy estimator accuracy:** On distributions with known entropy (Gaussian, Laplace, Student‑t) in dimensions d = 2, 10, 50, compute negentropy via MGD (large σ) and compare to truth and standard estimators (e.g., KSG). Assess convergence of ΔH = d⁻¹(H_gaussian – H_σ*) as σ increases.
