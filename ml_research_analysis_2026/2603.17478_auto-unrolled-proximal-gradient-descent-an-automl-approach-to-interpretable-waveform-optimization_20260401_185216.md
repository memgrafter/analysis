# Auto-Unrolled Proximal Gradient Descent: An AutoML Approach to Interpretable Waveform Optimization

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.17478
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17478
- **Reference count:** 11
- **Primary result:** With only 5 layers and 100 training samples, Auto-PGD achieves 98.8% of the spectral efficiency of a 200-iteration classical PGD solver, a 40× reduction in inference complexity.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the accuracy–computational cost trade-off in 6G waveform optimization by proposing Auto-Unrolled Proximal Gradient Descent (Auto-PGD). The method integrates deep unfolding—converting iterative Proximal Gradient Descent (PGD) into a fixed-depth neural network—with automated machine learning (AutoML via AutoGluon) to optimize hyperparameters such as depth, step-sizes, and layer type. A hybrid layer introduces a learnable linear transformation to pre-condition gradients, while a fixed proximal projection enforces power constraints. The approach achieves near-optimal spectral efficiency with ultra-low inference latency (<0.1 ms), especially in data-scarce regimes, and offers interpretability through per-layer diagnostics and guaranteed feasibility.

## Method Summary
Auto-PGD unfolds the PGD algorithm into a K-layer neural network. Each layer computes a gradient step on the sum-rate objective, optionally applies a learnable linear transformation **A**<sup>(k)</sup> (hybrid variant), scales by a learnable step-size η<sup>(k)</sup>, and then applies a fixed proximal projection onto the power constraint set to ensure feasibility. AutoGluon automates hyperparameter optimization over a structured search space—including depth *K* ∈ [3,25], initial step-size, optimizer, scheduler, layer type, and activation—to maximize validation sum-rate subject to a latency constraint (τ < 0.1 ms). Training uses a sum-loss (not mean-loss) for batch-invariant gradients over 200 epochs. The resulting model replaces a 200-iteration iterative solver with a single forward pass through a compact network, drastically reducing inference cost while preserving the optimization structure.

## Key Results
- Achieves 98.8% of the spectral efficiency of a 200-iteration classical PGD solver using only 5 unrolled layers and 100 training samples, representing a 40× reduction in inference complexity.
- AutoML automatically selects network depth based on training data size (e.g., *K*=5 for *N*=100, *K*=25 for *N*=10,000), outperforming manually-tuned unrolled networks and black-box baselines, particularly in low-data regimes.
- Maintains high interpretability via per-layer diagnostics (e.g., step-size trends) and built-in constraint satisfaction through the proximal projection.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Auto-PGD’s success rests on three synergistic mechanisms. First, deep unfolding compresses many PGD iterations into a shallow network, embedding domain knowledge (gradient steps, projections) into a structured hypothesis space with lower sample complexity than black-box models. Second, AutoGluon’s hyperparameter optimization efficiently discovers the optimal accuracy–latency trade-off, especially depth, which scales with training data size—a nuance manual tuning misses. Third, the hybrid layer’s learnable linear transformation pre-conditions gradients, approximating quasi-Newton steps within the same inference budget, while the proximal projection guarantees feasibility. These rely on assumptions: the unrolled space’s expressiveness, a complete HPO search space, and trainable **A**<sup>(k)</sup>; violations could break the gains.

  *Why needed:* The proximal operator enforces the power constraint at every layer, guaranteeing feasibility regardless of learned parameters—critical for physical-layer constraints.  
  *Quick check:* Given \( g(W)=0 \) if \( \|w_k\|^2 \leq P_{\max} \) else \( \infty \), what is the mathematical form of \( \text{prox}_{\eta g}(Z) \) and why does this simple scaling guarantee feasibility?

  *Why needed:* AutoGluon automates the search over DU-specific hyperparameters (depth, step-size init, layer type) to optimize the accuracy–latency trade-off for varying data sizes.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Channel tensor *H* (real-imag stacked) → Initialize *W*<sup>(0)</sup> (e.g., zero-forcing) → For *k* = 1 to *K*: compute gradient ∇*f*(*W*<sup>(k‑1)</sup>) → [optional linear transform **A**<sup>(k)</sup>] → scale by η<sup>(k)</sup> → apply fixed proximal projection → final beamforming matrix *W*<sup>(*K*)</sup>.

**Critical path:** For each layer *k*: *Z* = *W*<sup>(k‑1)</sup> − η<sup>(k)</sup> · ( **A**<sup>(k)</sup> · ∇*f* ) → *W*<sup>(k)</sup> = (√*P*<sub>max</sub> / max(√*P*<sub>max</sub>, ‖*Z*‖<sub>F</sub>)) · *Z*.

**Design tradeoffs:** Depth *K*: lower *K* reduces latency but may lower optimality; HPO finds a data-dependent sweet spot. Layer type: standard PGD (fewer params, stable) vs. hybrid (more expressive but risk of mislearned **A**<sup>(k)</sup>). HPO budget: more trials improve search but increase compute overhead.

**Failure signatures:** Constraint violation (‖*W*<sup>(*K*)</sup>‖<sub>F</sub> > *P*<sub>max</sub>) indicates proximal projection failure. Poor low-*N* performance despite shallow network suggests HPO converged poorly or hybrid layer overfitted. HPO selects minimal depth even for large *N* hints at search space mis‑specification.

**First three experiments:**  
1. **Unrolling sanity check:** For fixed *K*=5 and hand‑set step‑sizes, verify the DU forward pass exactly matches classical PGD iterates.  
2. **Proximal integrity test:** Generate random pre‑projection matrices *Z* with extreme norms; confirm projection always yields ‖prox(*Z*)‖<sub>F</sub> ≤ *P*<sub>max</sub>.  
3. **HPO depth trend replication:** Run AutoGluon HPO for *N* ∈ {100, 1 000, 10 000} and verify selected depth scales as reported (shallow for low‑*N*, deeper for high‑*N*).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How does Auto-PGD scale to massive MIMO with imperfect CSI, and what is the performance–complexity trade‑off?**  
   *Basis:* Conclusion states future work will extend to massive MIMO and robustness under imperfect CSI.  
   *Why unresolved:* Evaluation limited to M=8, K=4 with perfect Rayleigh fading; scaling introduces computational/generalization challenges, and imperfect CSI violates clean gradient assumptions.
2. **What is the computational overhead of the AutoGluon HPO relative to inference speed gains?**  
   *Basis:* Paper emphasizes low inference latency but omits HPO compute cost.  
   *Why unresolved:* Deployment cost‑benefit analysis is incomplete without accounting for GPU time/trials in the HPO phase.
3. **Do the learned hybrid‑layer transformations **A**<sup>(k)</sup> exhibit structured, interpretable patterns?**  
   *Basis:* Hybrid layer approximates a quasi‑Newton step; interpretability claim hinges on per‑layer diagnostics.  
   *Why unresolved:* Paper analyzes step‑sizes but not **A**<sup>(k)</sup> matrices; their spectral properties or sparsity are unexamined.
4. **What are the fundamental limits of the unrolled PGD hypothesis space **H**<sub>DU</sub> for non‑convex extensions?**  
   *Basis:* Inferred from convex problem formulation; future 6G waveforms may involve non‑convexities.  
   *Why unresolved:* 98.8% result is against a convex PGD baseline; applicability to broader problem classes is an open theoretical/practical frontier.

## Limitations
- AutoGluon version/API specifics and an unrevealed gradient‑normalization fix may impact reproducibility and reported performance.
- The hybrid layer’s benefit is shown only by comparison; a direct ablation isolating the learned linear transform is missing.
- Generalization beyond i.i.d. Rayleigh fading channels remains untested.
