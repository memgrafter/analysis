# SERE: Similarity-based Expert Re-routing for Efficient Batch Decoding in MoE Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.07616  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07616  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** SERE accelerates MoE batch decoding via dynamic expert re‑routing, achieving up to 2.0× speedup with minimal accuracy loss.

## Executive Summary
SERE tackles the inefficiency of batch decoding in Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) models, where the number of distinct experts activated per batch often far exceeds the per‑token budget due to token diversity. It introduces a lightweight dynamic re‑routing mechanism that redirects tokens from secondary experts to similar primary experts, based on a precomputed layer‑wise expert similarity matrix. The approach requires no model retraining and integrates into the vLLM serving engine with a custom CUDA kernel and only a one‑line code change. Evaluated on Qwen1.5‑A2.7B, DeepSeekV2‑Lite, and Qwen3‑30B‑A3B, SERE delivers up to 2.0× speedup in time‑per‑output‑token while retaining most of the original accuracy (e.g., 77.82% on Qwen3‑30B‑A3B with K=2), significantly outperforming prior static and dynamic baselines.

## Method Summary
SERE operates in two phases. Offline, it computes a layer‑wise expert similarity matrix using the Frobenius norm on expert activations from a small calibration dataset (e.g., FineWeb‑Edu). The activations are accumulated across batches and normalized to obtain pairwise similarities. Online, during batch inference, it classifies experts as *primary* (the union of the top‑S experts across all tokens) or *secondary*. Each secondary expert is either re‑routed to the most similar primary (if similarity ≥ ρ) or preserved as a critical expert (if similarity < ρ). The remapping is performed by a custom CUDA kernel that replaces the standard MoE dispatch in vLLM with only a single‑line change. The final set of active experts is reduced, lowering memory traffic and computation without altering model weights.

## Key Results
- Up to **2.0× speedup** in time‑per‑output‑token (TPOT) under high QPS.
- On **Qwen3‑30B‑A3B** (K=2), TPOT drops from 44.4 ms to 32.8 ms (1.35×) while retaining **77.82%** of original task accuracy.
- SERE significantly outperforms dynamic baselines like HC‑SMoE and LYNX, especially under aggressive top‑k settings.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Expert similarity can be precomputed offline and remains stable enough to guide dynamic re‑routing during inference.  
**Mechanism:** A calibration dataset is passed through the MoE model. For each layer, expert activations are collected and pairwise similarities (e.g., Frobenius norm) are accumulated across batches. After normalization, these form layer‑wise similarity matrices that quantify functional redundancy.  
**Core assumption:** Expert behavior, as captured by activation patterns, is consistent across inputs; thus a small, general calibration set can reliably capture expert relationships.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [section 3.1.1] describes the accumulation and normalization process.  
- [section 3.1.2] Figure 4 visualizes similarity matrices, showing clusters of high similarity and distinct low‑similarity “critical” experts.  
- [section 4.4] Table 6 demonstrates that SERE’s accuracy remains stable even with different calibration datasets (FineWeb, C4, WIKI) and volumes, confirming robustness.  
- [corpus] Related work (e.g., HC‑SMoE) also precomputes expert similarity but for static merging; SERE repurposes this offline step for dynamic decisions.  
**Break condition:** Major distribution shifts (e.g., fine‑tuning on a new domain) may invalidate the offline similarity matrix, requiring recalculation.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** Distinguishing primary from secondary experts via router scores and re‑routing secondary tokens to the most similar primary reduces active experts with minimal accuracy loss.  
**Mechanism:** For each batch and layer, SERE defines the primary expert set as the union of the top‑S (e.g., top‑1) experts across all tokens. All other activated experts are secondary. Using the precomputed similarity matrix, each secondary expert is either re‑routed to the most similar primary (if similarity ≥ ρ) or preserved as critical (if similarity < ρ). The final set of active experts is then used for computation.  
**Core assumption:** Router weights correlate with output influence, so primary experts are safe to keep; functionally similar experts produce comparable outputs, making substitution viable; critical experts have unique functions and must be retained.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] explicitly mentions “re‑routing tokens from secondary to similar primary counterparts” and “preserves primary and critical experts”.  
- [section 3.2] Figure 5 shows a long‑tail distribution of router scores, justifying the primary/secondary split.  
- [section 4.4] Table 7 ablates re‑routing strategies: re‑routing to the most similar expert far outperforms random or least‑similar choices.  
- [corpus] Dynamic skipping methods like Lynx use batch‑aware confidence but ignore inter‑expert similarity; SERE’s inclusion of similarity yields better accuracy at similar speedups.  
**Break condition:** If router scores become extremely uniform (e.g., due to aggressive load balancing), the primary set may grow too large, limiting skipping gains or misclassifying redundancies.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** SERE’s re‑routing logic is lightweight and integrates seamlessly into high‑performance serving engines (vLLM) with negligible overhead, enabling practical deployment.  
**Mechanism:** The re‑routing is implemented as a custom CUDA kernel that runs after the router’s top‑k selection. The kernel marks retained primary experts, then for each secondary expert queries the similarity matrix to find the best primary subject to the threshold ρ. Token‑to‑expert indices are rewritten in‑place; only the final distinct experts are loaded and computed. This kernel requires only a single line change in vLLM’s MoE dispatch.  
**Core assumption:** The re‑routing computation is memory‑light and parallelizable; its cost is amortized by the saved expert computation, especially under memory‑bound decoding with large batches.  
**Evidence anchors:**  
- [abstract] states “custom CUDA kernel for SERE, enabling plug‑and‑play use in vLLM with only a single‑line code change”.  
- [section 3.3] and Algorithm 2 provide the kernel pseudo‑code.  
- [section 4.3] Figure 6 shows that the CUDA version out‑performs the PyTorch version by ~1.5×.  
- [section 4.3] Figure 7c breaks down computation cost; SERE’s additional overhead is tiny compared to attention and MLP costs.  
- [corpus] Many MoE acceleration systems require substantial system changes; SERE’s one‑line integration is unusually non‑intrusive.  
**Break condition:** If the MoE layer implementation deviates from the assumed tensor layout (e.g., grouped expert packing) or vLLM’s API changes, the kernel may need modification; also, on non‑CUDA platforms the custom kernel is not available.

## Foundational Learning

- **Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) Sparse Activation**  
  *Why needed:* SERE builds on MoE’s inherent sparsity: each token activates only a small set of experts. Understanding this design reveals why batching inflates total activated experts.  
  *Quick check:* “In an MoE layer, how is the output computed? What is the role of the router? How does top‑k sparsity work in theory vs. batched practice?”

- **Batch‑level Expert Redundancy**  
  *Why needed:* SERE’s goal is to reduce the number of distinct experts activated within a batch, which exceeds the per‑token budget due to token diversity. This phenomenon is the root inefficiency SERE addresses.  
  *Quick check:* “If each token activates k experts, why might a batch of B tokens activate far more than k distinct experts? What hardware effect does this have on decoding?”

- **Expert Functional Similarity**  
  *Why needed:* SERE’s re‑routing relies on the premise that experts can be measured as functionally similar; high similarity allows safe substitution.  
  *Quick check:* “How do you quantify similarity between two experts’ behaviors? What does a similarity matrix tell you about the MoE layer? What are the trade‑offs between activation‑based (e.g., Frobenius) and parameter‑based metrics?”

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Offline similarity estimator → Online re‑router → Integration into MoE layer forward pass (after router top‑k, before expert aggregation).

**Critical path:**  
1. Router produces token‑wise top‑k expert indices \(I^{(l)}\) and weights \(W^{(l)}\).  
2. SERE re‑router remaps these to final active experts \(I'^{(l)}\).  
3. System dispatches to only the distinct experts in \(I'^{(l)}\) and accumulates weighted outputs.  
4. Proceed to next layer. Offline similarity matrices are loaded once into GPU memory.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **S** (primary size): Smaller S increases skipping ratio but may drop useful experts; start with S = K−1.  
- **ρ** (similarity threshold): Higher ρ preserves more experts (better accuracy, lower speedup). Sweep ρ to find inflection point.  
- **Similarity metric:** Frobenius is fastest; CKA may capture subtle semantics but is computationally heavier. Choose based on calibration budget.  
- **Calibration data:** General web text (FineWeb) works robustly; task‑specific data can help under aggressive skipping (K=1) but is not necessary for moderate settings.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **Accuracy collapse on reasoning tasks:** likely over‑skipping of critical experts; check per‑layer active counts and increase ρ.  
- **Speedup lower than expected:** re‑routing overhead too high; profile kernel occupancy, consider using the provided CUDA implementation.  
- **Inconsistent speedup across batch sizes:** SERE benefits grow with batch size; if small batches show no gain, verify that primary‑secondary separation is meaningful.  
- **Non‑deterministic outputs:** ensure the re‑routing is deterministic (no random tie‑breaking).

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. Validate basic integration: run a single forward pass with a fixed batch, record the final expert set size and compare to baseline (original top‑k). Ensure outputs are numerically close (max diff < 1e‑5).  
2. Threshold sweep: fix S (e.g., 2) and vary ρ from 0.0 to 1.0; plot average accuracy (OpenCompass tasks) and TPOT (QPS=16) to locate the knee point.  
3. Scaling check: test batch sizes 16, 32, 64, 128 with S=2, ρ found in experiment 2; verify that speedup grows with batch size and that overhead remains <5% of total MoE time.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. How well does SERE generalize to diverse MoE architectures beyond the three models tested?  
2. Why are activation-based similarity metrics (e.g., Frobenius) superior to parameter-based approaches for capturing expert redundancy in re‑routing?  
3. Can the similarity threshold ρ be dynamically adapted per layer or batch to optimize the speed‑accuracy trade‑off without manual tuning?  
4. How does SERE interact with other inference optimization techniques, such as static expert pruning or quantization, and can they be synergistically combined?
