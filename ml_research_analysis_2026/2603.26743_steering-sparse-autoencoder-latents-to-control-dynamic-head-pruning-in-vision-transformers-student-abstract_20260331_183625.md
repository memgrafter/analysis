# Steering Sparse Autoencoder Latents to Control Dynamic Head Pruning in Vision Transformers (Student Abstract)

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.26743  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26743  
- **Reference count:** 1  
- **Primary result:** Per-class latent steering reduces head usage while preserving or improving accuracy; e.g., for class "bowl", accuracy increases from 76% to 82% and head usage decreases from 0.72 to 0.33.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the limited interpretability and controllability of dynamic head pruning in Vision Transformers (ViTs). The authors propose training a Sparse Autoencoder (SAE) on final-layer residual embeddings to extract sparse, more interpretable latents. By amplifying per-class frequently activated latents before decoding, they steer the pruning decision network to use fewer, class-specific attention heads without sacrificing accuracy. Experiments on CIFAR-100 show significant reductions in head usage for certain classes (e.g., "bowl") along with accuracy gains, suggesting that sparse latents can bridge efficiency and mechanistic interpretability.

## Method Summary
The method integrates a Sparse Autoencoder (SAE) into a pre-trained ViT with dynamic pruning (e.g., AdaViT). The SAE is trained on final-layer CLS token embeddings using a reconstruction objective and top-k sparsity. At inference, for a given input class, the top-k most frequently activated SAE latents (based on training statistics) are selected and amplified by a factor α. The modified latent vector is re-sparsified (top-k), decoded into a steered embedding, and fed to the pruning decision network, which outputs head importance logits. These logits are sampled to binary masks, pruning attention heads. The approach explores per-class, global, and random steering strategies, demonstrating that per-class steering yields compact head subsets and sometimes improves accuracy.

## Key Results
- Per-class steering reduces head usage while preserving or improving class accuracy.
- For class "bowl", accuracy improves from 76% to 82% and head usage drops from 0.72 to 0.33, with pruning concentrated on heads h2 and h5.
- Semantically related classes (e.g., "bowl" and "plate") share similar head usage patterns, indicating class-specific head subsets.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Training a k-sparse autoencoder (SAE) on final-layer ViT residual embeddings disentangles dense, polysemantic representations into sparse, more interpretable latent features.
- **Mechanism:** The SAE uses a top-k activation in the latent layer, forcing the encoder to represent the input using only the k most relevant dimensions from an expanded latent space (n > d). The decoder reconstructs the input from this sparse code. The sparsity constraint yields latents that tend to align with monosemantic concepts.
- **Core assumption:** Disentanglement via SAE sparsity produces latents that are interpretable and correspond to distinct, class-relevant features within the ViT's residual stream.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states the method leverages SAEs' ability to disentangle embeddings; section defines SAE equations and architecture (8x expansion, k=64); appendix shows semantic analysis of latents.
- **Break condition:** Poor reconstruction quality (high MSE) or latents with no semantic coherence (no class-specific activation patterns) would undermine the disentanglement claim.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Amplifying per-class frequently activated SAE latents enriches class-specific signals in the residual embedding, leading the dynamic pruning decision network to select a smaller, class-specific subset of attention heads while preserving accuracy.
- **Mechanism:** For a given input, specific latents S are amplified by α before a final top-k activation. The modified latent vector is decoded to a steered embedding ˜x, which is fed to the pre-trained decision network, producing different head importance logits and thus different pruning masks.
- **Core assumption:** The decision network's pruning logic is sensitive to nuanced shifts in the CLS token representation, and latent steering induces meaningful, class-specific shifts.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract notes per-class steering reduces head usage with accuracy gains; experiments show per-class steering uniquely reduces usage (Figure 2) and head patterns for top classes (Figure 3); appendix shows semantic overlap matches shared head usage.
- **Break condition:** If per-class steering does not produce distinct, class-correlated head usage patterns, or uniformly degrades accuracy across all α, the mechanism linking amplified latents to useful pruning fails.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The observed class-specific pruning behavior bridges efficiency and interpretability by revealing which attention heads are most relevant for specific visual concepts.
- **Mechanism:** The end-to-end process (SAE training → latent steering → decision network inference) maps a semantic class (via its top latents) to a sparse head activation pattern. This serves as post-hoc mechanistic interpretation: heads consistently selected/retained for a class are inferred as critical for processing that class's features.
- **Core assumption:** Head selection patterns induced by per-class steering reflect functional specialization of attention heads for certain visual concepts, not random artifacts.
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract claims sparse latents enable class-specific control bridging efficiency and interpretability; experiments show semantic coherence in head usage across similar classes (e.g., bowl/plate share h2,h5).
- **Break condition:** If head usage patterns show no semantic coherence (e.g., similar classes use disjoint head sets, or all classes converge to the same heads), the interpretability claim collapses.

## Architecture Onboarding

### Component Map
Pre-trained ViT with decision networks -> Extract final-layer CLS token (x) -> SAE Encode (z = TopK(W_enc(x - b_dec))) -> Steering (select S, amplify α) -> z' (after amplification) -> TopK on z' -> ž -> SAE Decode (x̃ = W_dec ž + b_dec) -> Decision Networks (per layer) -> Head importance logits (a^ℓ) -> Sample to binary masks (M^ℓ,i) -> Apply masks to attention heads -> Pruned ViT forward pass -> Output.

### Critical Path
1. Input image passes through ViT up to final layer, yielding CLS token embedding x.
2. x is encoded by SAE: z = TopK(W_enc(x - b_dec)).
3. Latents S (per-class top-k frequent) are amplified: z'_i = z_i + α for i∈S, else z_i.
4. z' is re-sparsified: ž = TopK(z').
5. ž is decoded: x̃ = W_dec ž + b_dec.
6. x̃ is fed to each layer's decision network (instead of x), producing head logits a^ℓ.
7. Logits a^ℓ are sampled (e.g., Gumbel-Sigmoid) to binary mask M^ℓ,i.
8. Masks are applied to attention heads, pruning inactive heads.
9. The pruned forward pass completes, yielding output logits.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **SAE capacity (n, k):** Larger latent size n and smaller k increase sparsity/disentanglement but risk reconstruction loss and loss of pruning-relevant information. Paper uses n=3072 (8× expansion), k=64.
- **Steering strength (α):** Larger α increases effect but may push latents out of decoder's manifold, causing reconstruction artifacts and accuracy drop.
- **Strategy for S (Per-class vs. Global):** Per-class yields interpretable, efficient results but requires pre-computed class statistics; global is simpler but less effective.

### Failure Signatures
1. **High reconstruction MSE (>0.05):** SAE failed to learn meaningful decomposition; steering operates on broken representation.
2. **Accuracy drops >5% with small α (e.g., 0.2) for per-class:** Steering destroys task-relevant information; latent selection S may be noise or misaligned.
3. **No change in head usage ratio across α:** Steering does not influence decision network; amplification too weak or decision network insensitive.
4. **Baseline dynamic pruning fails:** Pruning either too aggressive (accuracy collapse) or too lenient (insufficient sparsity); indicates decision network training issues.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **SAE Fidelity Check:** Replace original final-layer CLS embeddings with SAE reconstructions (no steering) in the pruned ViT pipeline. Measure accuracy delta and head usage delta. Expected: minimal change (<0.2% accuracy, <0.03 head usage).
2. **Baseline Pruning Characterization:** Run unsteered model on validation set for a target class (e.g., "bowl"). Record average head usage per layer and class accuracy. Establishes baseline (e.g., usage ~0.72, accuracy ~76%).
3. **Steering Impact Scan:** For target class, vary α from 0 to 1.5 using per-class S. Plot accuracy vs. head usage (replicating Figure 2). Identify α yielding best efficiency/accuracy trade-off (expected near α=1.2).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **How does the framework generalize to earlier ViT layers and larger foundation models (e.g., ViT-Large, CLIP-ViT)?** The paper explicitly states future work will extend to earlier layers and foundation models, but current experiments are limited to final layer of ViT-Small on CIFAR-100.
- **What is the mechanism by which per-class latent steering alters the pruning decision network's head importance logits?** The paper shows steering changes head usage but does not analyze how steered embeddings affect the decision network's internal computations or the subsequent attention masking.
- **Do the SAE latents encode human-interpretable visual or semantic concepts that align with the class-specific head subsets?** The paper reports semantic class overlap in latent usage but does not demonstrate what individual latents represent or how they relate to specific heads (e.g., h2, h5 for "bowl").

## Limitations
- **Decision network training details unspecified:** Exact hyperparameters (architecture, learning rate, Gumbel-Sigmoid schedule, sparsity regularization) are not provided, hindering faithful replication of baseline dynamic pruning.
- **Steering S selection methodology unclear:** The precise method for computing "frequently activated" latents per class (e.g., activation threshold, k for selection) is ambiguous; the assumed k=64 is plausible but unconfirmed.
- **Input preprocessing mismatch:** CIFAR-100 images are 32x32, but ViT-Small typically expects larger inputs (e.g., 224x224). The paper does not specify patch size or resizing strategy, which critically affects embeddings.
