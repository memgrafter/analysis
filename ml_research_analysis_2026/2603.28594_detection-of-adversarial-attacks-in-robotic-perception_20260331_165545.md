# Detection of Adversarial Attacks in Robotic Perception

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.28594
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.28594
- **Reference count:** 30
- **Primary result:** A feature‑based detection framework using frozen ResNet backbones and statistical metrics (non‑Maximal Entropy, Kernel Density) reliably separates clean from FGSM‑perturbed inputs for robotic semantic segmentation, with ResNet‑50 offering superior stability.

## Executive Summary
This paper addresses the detection of adversarial attacks on semantic segmentation models used in robotic perception. The authors propose a framework that employs pre‑trained ResNet‑18 or ResNet‑50 backbones with all layers frozen, replacing the original classifier head with a new linear layer trained on clean data. During inference, two statistics—non‑Maximal Entropy and Kernel Density—are computed from the model’s output and a threshold is applied to flag adversarial inputs. Evaluated on Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM) attacks, segmentation performance degrades severely (mean IoU drops from 1.00 to 0.10 at ε=0.1), yet the detection metrics maintain clear separation across attack strengths. The deeper ResNet‑50 backbone not only yields better segmentation under attack but also exhibits markedly more stable training (accuracy swings ≤0.3 vs. up to 0.6 for ResNet‑18). The study demonstrates the viability of simple, feature‑based detection for robust robotic perception.

## Method Summary
The method adapts a pre‑trained ResNet‑18 or ResNet‑50 as a frozen feature extractor for a 100‑class semantic segmentation task. The original fully connected head is discarded and replaced by a linear classifier; only this head is trained using stochastic gradient descent (learning rate 0.001, momentum 0.9) and cross‑entropy loss. Input images are resized to 224×224, normalized with ImageNet statistics, and augmented (random crop, horizontal flip) during training. At test time, an image passes through the frozen backbone and the trained head to obtain per‑class probabilities. Detection relies on two metrics derived from these probabilities: (1) non‑Maximal Entropy (non‑ME), the average entropy of all non‑top‑class probabilities over all pixels; and (2) Kernel Density (K‑density), which evaluates the density of the global average‑pooled feature vector under a class‑conditional Gaussian KDE fitted on clean training data. A predetermined threshold on either metric classifies an input as adversarial. The framework is evaluated under FGSM attacks with perturbation budgets ε ∈ {0.02, 0.04, ..., 0.1}.

## Key Results
- FGSM attacks cause severe segmentation degradation: mean IoU drops from 1.00 to 0.48 at ε=0.02 and to 0.10 at ε=0.1.
- ResNet‑50 consistently outperforms ResNet‑18, with gains of 0.04–0.06 across segmentation metrics and markedly more stable training (accuracy swings ≤0.3 vs. up to 0.6 for ResNet‑18).
- The detection metrics effectively distinguish clean from adversarial inputs across a range of attack strengths.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Frozen Backbone as Robust Feature Extractor
Freezing the pre‑trained ResNet backbone layers yields a stable, high‑quality feature extractor that limits the impact of gradient‑based attacks to the trainable classifier head. The fixed low‑level and mid‑level representations (edges, textures, object parts) remain at their robust, ImageNet‑learned states; any perturbation that drastically alters these features would likely be detected by statistical metrics. Evidence: Section 3.2 describes the freezing protocol; Section 4 shows ResNet‑50’s more stable training (accuracy swings ≤0.3 vs. up to 0.6) and superior detection. The core assumption is that the clean ImageNet manifold is hard to distort without producing detectable anomalies. **Break condition:** if an adversary explicitly targets the frozen feature space (e.g., via a meta‑attack simulating the frozen model), this protection fails.

### Mechanism 2: Statistical Metrics Reveal Distributional Shifts
Adversarial examples cause predictable shifts in the classifier’s output distribution, either by becoming over‑confident in incorrect classes or under‑confident and uncertain. The detection uses two complementary metrics: non‑Maximal Entropy (non‑ME) measures the entropy of all non‑top‑class probabilities, while Kernel Density (K‑density) compares the feature vector against a density estimate from clean data. Thresholding these metrics separates clean inputs (typically low non‑ME, high K‑density) from adversarial ones. Evidence: Section 3.1 defines the metrics; the approach builds on prior work [18] that combines thresholding with K‑density. Core assumption: clean outputs occupy a distinctive, learnable region of the probability space that is systematically disrupted by bounded‑norm attacks. **Break condition:** adaptive attacks that explicitly minimize the chosen metric (e.g., produce low non‑ME and high K‑density) can evade detection.

### Mechanism 3: Architectural Depth Enhances Detection Stability
Deeper architectures like ResNet‑50 provide richer hierarchical features, resulting in a more separable and stable distribution of output probabilities for clean inputs. This leads to statistical metrics with lower variance during training and a clearer margin from adversarial distributions. Empirically, ResNet‑50 outperforms ResNet‑18 by 0.04–0.06 in segmentation metrics under attack and exhibits markedly smaller training accuracy swings. Evidence: Section 4 reports these gains and stability. The core assumption is that deeper networks develop more abstract representations less linearly correlated with the input pixel space, making perturbations appear more anomalous. **Break condition:** if greater capacity leads to overfitting on the specific training set, the clean feature distribution may become less general, blurring the separation.

## Foundational Learning
### Adversarial Examples (FGSM Attack)
- **Why needed:** The entire detection framework is designed to counter Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM) attacks. Understanding that these are small, worst‑case perturbations added to maximize loss explains the catastrophic segmentation drops (mIoU 1.00 → 0.10 at ε=0.1) and why the perturbations are imperceptible.
- **Quick check:** Why does an imperceptible perturbation (ε=0.02) cause such a catastrophic drop in mean IoU for segmentation?

### Transfer Learning & Frozen Backbones
- **Why needed:** The method’s core innovation is adapting a pre‑trained classification backbone (ResNet) for segmentation detection by freezing it. This requires understanding the distinction between fixed feature extraction and trainable task‑specific layers.
- **Quick check:** What is the risk of fine‑tuning the entire backbone on the segmentation task versus freezing it for this adversarial detection application?

### Statistical Thresholding for Anomaly Detection
- **Why needed:** The detection decision relies on a simple threshold on non‑Maximal Entropy or Kernel Density. Grasping the trade‑off between false positives and negatives is essential, as well as how the threshold `T` is chosen (e.g., from a validation set).
- **Quick check:** How would changing the detection threshold `T` affect the detector’s precision and recall? What metric would you plot to choose `T`?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:** Input Preprocessor -> Feature Extractor (Frozen ResNet-18/50) -> Segmentation Classifier Head -> Detection Module (non-ME, K-density) -> Decision Engine (Threshold)  

**Critical path:** Preprocess Image → Forward pass through Frozen ResNet → Pass features through Trainable Classifier Head → Get 2‑class output → Compute Detection Metric (non‑ME or K‑density) → Compare Metric to Threshold `T` → Flag as Adversarial if Metric > `T` (or < `T`)  

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- *Frozen vs. Fine‑tuned Backbone:* Frozen ensures feature stability but prevents adaptation to the segmentation domain; fine‑tuning risks forgetting robust ImageNet features and may increase attack susceptibility.  
- *Metric Choice:* Non‑ME is simple and fast; K‑density is more powerful but requires maintaining a clean reference dataset and is computationally heavier.  
- *Threshold Sensitivity:* The threshold `T` is a critical hyperparameter; a value optimal for one attack strength may fail for another.  

**Failure signatures:**  
- *High False Positive Rate:* Clean images are consistently flagged, likely due to an excessively low threshold or distribution shift between training and deployment data.  
- *High False Negative Rate:* Adversarial images are missed, possibly due to an overly high threshold or adaptive attacks that minimize the detection metric.  
- *Unstable Training Curves:* Large accuracy oscillations (e.g., ResNet‑18 swings up to 0.6) indicate the classifier head is failing to learn a stable decision boundary.  

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Baseline Clean Performance:** Train the segmentation head on clean data to establish a normal output distribution and achieve reasonable mIoU (>0.8) on the validation set.  
2. **Attack Detection Efficacy:** Generate FGSM examples at increasing ε values, compute non‑ME and K‑density for clean and adversarial samples, plot separation, and select a preliminary threshold `T`.  
3. **Architecture Comparison:** Repeat Experiment 2 for ResNet‑18 and ResNet‑50; compare detection AUROC, segmentation mIoU drop, and training stability to validate the depth‑robustness hypothesis.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How effective is the proposed detection framework against adaptive adversarial attacks that specifically target the detector itself, rather than just the segmentation model?
- **Basis in paper:** The paper acknowledges in Related Work that “many methods remain vulnerable to adaptive attacks” and uses standard FGSM attacks for evaluation.
- **Why unresolved:** Robustness to attacks that explicitly minimize the detection metrics (e.g., by incorporating non‑ME or K‑density into the adversarial loss) is untested, leaving real‑world security guarantees unclear.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Evaluate detection under adaptive attacks (e.g., Carlini & Wagner) that optimize perturbations to remain undetected, reporting true‑positive and false‑positive rates.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Does freezing all backbone layers limit detection capability compared to fine‑tuning some layers, especially for capturing attack‑specific feature distortions?
- **Basis in paper:** The method freezes all backbone parameters and only trains the classifier head (Section 3.2); this design choice is not compared against alternatives.
- **Why unresolved:** Freezing restricts feature adaptation to the segmentation task and potential attack patterns; fine‑tuning might improve detection by allowing the backbone to extract more discriminative features, but could also degrade robustness or clean‑input performance.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Ablation studies varying the degree of backbone fine‑tuning (e.g., last block only, all layers) and measuring detection accuracy and segmentation robustness.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Can the detection framework generalize to modern segmentation architectures beyond ResNet, such as Vision Transformers or lightweight models for resource‑constrained robots?
- **Basis in paper:** Evaluation is exclusively on ResNet‑18 and ResNet‑50 (Section 4); no other backbones are tested.
- **Why unresolved:** Different architectures have distinct feature hierarchies and inductive biases; the statistical metrics (non‑ME, K‑density) might behave differently on attention‑based models or models with alternative normalization, affecting detection reliability.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Replicate the experiment with at least two non‑ResNet architectures (e.g., a Vision Transformer and a lightweight CNN), comparing detection performance under identical attacks.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** How does the detection method perform under realistic robotic perception conditions, including sensor noise, motion blur, and dynamic environmental changes?
- **Basis in paper:** Evaluation uses standard datasets (implied by Cityscapes reference) with predefined clean/adversarial splits; no real‑world deployment factors are considered.
- **Why unresolved:** In practice, robotic sensors produce noisy data that may overlap with adversarial feature statistics, potentially causing high false positive rates or missed detections. The threshold‑based detector assumes separable clean and adversarial distributions, which may not hold under real‑world noise.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Test the trained detector on a physical robot platform in varied environments (indoor/outdoor, day/night), collecting both clean and adversarially perturbed sensor data; analyze ROC curves and false‑positive/false‑negative rates compared to controlled dataset results.

## Limitations
- **Dataset ambiguity:** The exact 100‑class segmentation dataset is not named, hindering reproducibility and assessment of domain specificity.
- **Detection thresholding:** The method for selecting the threshold `T` on non‑ME and K‑density is unspecified; it is unclear whether a fixed threshold generalizes across attack strengths.
- **Attack scope:** Only FGSM attacks are evaluated; robustness to stronger (e.g., PGD, CW) or adaptive attacks remains unknown.

## Confidence
- Overall detection efficacy: **Medium**
- Frozen backbone stability: **Medium**
- Statistical metrics (non‑ME, K‑density): **Medium**
- Depth advantage (ResNet‑50 > ResNet‑18): **High**
