# Assessing the Impact of Typological Features on Multilingual Machine Translation in the Age of Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2602.03551  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03551  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Target language typological features (morphological complexity, head‑finality) significantly predict translation quality and beam‑search sensitivity in large multilingual models, even after controlling for data resources.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates whether the typological properties of a target language impact machine translation quality in the era of large language models. While earlier studies suggested that linguistic features like morphological complexity and word order affect MT performance, it remained unclear if these effects persist in modern large‑scale multilingual systems such as NLLB‑200 and Tower+. Using the FLORES+ dataset with over 100 target languages, the authors find that morphological complexity (measured by MATTR) and head‑finality are significant negative predictors of chrF++ scores for both encoder‑decoder and decoder‑only models, even after accounting for training data size (via GlotCC) and script similarity. Moreover, widening the beam width from 1 to 7 yields larger improvements (both in chrF++ and model‑averaged generation probability) for languages with these typological traits, indicating that standard narrow beam search is suboptimal for them. Architectural differences are observed: Tower+ shows stronger dependence on data resourcedness, yet typology remains significant. The study also releases fine‑grained typological features for 212 FLORES+ languages.

## Method Summary
The evaluation uses the FLORES+ development set (997 English sentences) across 124+ target languages. Two state‑of‑the‑art multilingual translation models are examined: NLLB‑200 (3.3B parameters, encoder‑decoder) and Tower+ (9B parameters, decoder‑only). Target language properties include a data resourcedness proxy (GlotCC size), URIEL linguistic distances (genetic, geographic, etc.), morphological complexity (MATTR and related measures), and head‑finality derived from Universal Dependencies. For each source‑target pair among seven source languages (EN, AR, IT, NL, TR, UK, VI) with complete features (52 or 53 languages), translations are generated at beam sizes 1, 3, 5, and 7.

Primary translation quality is measured by chrF++ (sacreBLEU). The model’s sentence generation probability is computed as the sum of token log‑probabilities along the decoded path. For each beam, average chrF++ and probability are recorded. To assess the impact of typology, the authors perform Spearman correlations between all language properties and chrF++@k=5 and probability gains (Δ between beam 7 and 1). Then multiple linear regressions are fitted: (1) chrF++ ~ source_id + log(GlotCC) + same_script + genetic distance + target MATTR + target head_finality; (2) log(Δprob) ~ same predictors; for Tower+, an additional binary predictor “is_in_tower” (official language support) is included. Incremental model comparison (likelihood ratio tests) determines the added explanatory power of typology after controlling for other factors.

## Key Results
- Target morphological complexity (MATTR) and head‑finality are significant negative predictors of chrF++ translation quality for both NLLB‑200 and Tower+, even after controlling for training data size (GlotCC) and script similarity.
- The magnitude of quality improvement (ΔchrF) and model probability gain (Δprob) when widening the beam from 1 to 7 is larger for languages with higher MATTR and head‑finality, though ΔchrF itself is not significantly predicted by typology, only Δprob.
- Tower+ exhibits stronger dependence on data resourcedness (is_in_tower, GlotCC) than NLLB‑200, but typological features remain significant in both models, indicating that architectural differences do not eliminate the effect of typology.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
1. **Typology as intrinsic difficulty**  
   Claim: Target language typological properties (MATTR, head_finality) significantly predict translation quality even after controlling for data resources.  
   Mechanism: Morphologically complex languages expand the output vocabulary, increasing uncertainty; head‑final languages have flexible word order, forcing the decoder to maintain more hypotheses. Both make sequence modeling harder, leading to lower quality under standard decoding.  
   Evidence: Spearman correlations (sec 5.1.1) show significant negative relationships; regression coefficients remain significant after adding controls (Table 1).  
   Break condition: If high‑resource morphologically simple/head‑initial languages still perform poorly, or if newer models fully eliminate the typology effect.

2. **Beam search sensitivity**  
   Claim: The improvement in translation quality and model probability from increasing beam width is larger for morphologically complex and head‑final languages.  
   Mechanism: Such languages have higher target distribution entropy, so narrow beams prematurely prune viable hypotheses. Wider beams explore more of the high‑entropy space, leading to higher‑probability outputs and better chrF++.  
   Evidence: Regression on Δprob (Table 2) shows significant positive coefficients for MATTR and head_finality; interaction with ΔchrF not significant.  
   Break condition: If alternative decoding strategies show no interaction with typology, or if probability gains do not correlate with metric gains.

3. **Architectural differences**  
   Claim: The relative influence of typology vs. data resources differs between encoder‑decoder (NLLB‑200) and decoder‑only (Tower+) models, with Tower+ showing stronger dominance of language support/resourcedness but retaining a significant typology effect.  
   Mechanism: Decoder‑only LLMs may develop more specialized representations for covered languages, making data presence more decisive; however, the generative process still confronts the same morphological/word‑order challenges, leaving a residual typology effect.  
   Evidence: In chrF++ regression for Tower+, coefficients for is_in_tower and GlotCC are much larger, but MATTR and head_finality remain significant (Table 3). For Δprob, head_finality remains significant while resource effects vanish.  
   Break condition: If evaluating more decoder‑only models reveals no consistent pattern, or if Tower+ results are driven solely by its limited support set without generalization.

## Foundational Learning
- **Morphological Complexity (MATTR)**: Moving Average Type/Token Ratio quantifies lexical diversity; higher values indicate richer inflection/derivation. Why needed: It captures the expansion of the output space and increased uncertainty in word form selection. Quick check: Why might a higher MATTR make beam search less efficient? (Answer: More possible word forms per lemma increase the number of plausible sequences, making the search space more ambiguous.)
- **Head‑Finality**: Measures the preference for heads (verbs, adpositions) to follow their dependents. Higher head‑finality implies more flexible word order. Why needed: Left‑to‑right autoregressive decoding assumes a linear order; head‑final languages often require the decoder to postpone committing to the head, increasing the number of viable partial hypotheses and the branchiness of the search. Quick check: How does a head‑final structure potentially increase the difficulty of left‑to‑right autoregressive decoding? (Answer: The decoder must keep multiple hypotheses for where the head will appear, as dependents appear earlier, leading to a larger search space.)
- **Beam Search & Probability Gain**: Beam width controls the trade‑off between computational cost and search completeness. Probability gain (Δprob) measures the increase in average sentence log‑probability when widening the beam (e.g., from 1 to 7). Why needed: It isolates whether wider search yields higher‑confidence outputs, independent of final metric (chrF++), allowing analysis of decoding optimality. Quick check: What is the conceptual difference between predicting chrF++ gain and predicting probability gain from beam width? (Answer: chrF++ gain reflects changes in surface‑level n‑gram overlap with the reference; probability gain reflects the model’s internal confidence. They may diverge if the reference uses synonyms or different word orders that the model deems less probable.)

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
FLORES+ dev set (997 EN sentences) → inference with NLLB‑200/Tower+ at beam sizes {1,3,5,7} → compute chrF++ and sentence log‑prob → align with language features (GlotCC, URIEL, MATTR, head_finality) → regression analysis.

**Critical path:**  
1. Filter FLORES+ for languages with complete feature data and model coverage → resulting 52 target languages for regression.  
2. Assemble feature matrix: source_id (categorical), log(GlotCC), same_script (binary), genetic distance, tgt:MATTR, tgt:head_finality (and is_in_tower for Tower+).  
3. Run controlled generation for each (src, tgt) pair and beam size; compute average metrics and Δprob between beam 7 and 1.  
4. Fit incremental linear models (chrF++, log(Δprob)), compare via likelihood ratio tests to assess contribution of typology.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- chrF++ chosen for cross‑lingual consistency; sacrifices semantic nuance compared to COMET or BERTScore.  
- GlotCC size used as proxy for training data; imperfect but correlates with expected resource levels.  
- Beam size capped at 7 to avoid known degradation at very wide beams; optimal width may be larger for some languages.  
- Focused on MATTR and head_finality based on prior hypotheses; other typological dimensions not explored.

**Failure signatures:**  
- Low adjusted R² in ΔchrF regression (e.g., 0.09) suggests other factors dominate quality gains.  
- Persistent source‑language effects indicate unmodeled source‑side variability.  
- Missing feature values reduce language set to ~52; may not represent the full 124+ languages in FLORES+.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Correlation Sweep**: For each source language, compute Spearman ρ between all 22+ language properties and chrF++@k=5; identify features with consistent significant correlations (e.g., MATTR, head_finality).  
2. **Controlled Regression (chrF++)**: Fit chrF++ ~ src_id + log(GlotCC) + same_script + genetic distance; then add tgt:MATTR and tgt:head_finality; test significance via likelihood ratio.  
3. **Probability Gain Analysis**: Fit log(Δprob) ~ same predictors to determine whether typology predicts search optimality independently of metric improvements.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Generalizability of typology effects:** Do typological properties predict MT quality across a wider range of LLMs (both encoder‑decoder and decoder‑only) and more source‑target pairs? The current study covers only two models.  
- **Metric vs. confidence decoupling:** Why do typological features significantly predict probability gains with wider beams but not chrF++ quality gains? This suggests a mismatch between model confidence and metric capture.  
- **Broader model evaluation:** Do the observed effects hold for other multilingual models (e.g., M2M‑100, mBART, GPT series)? The two‑model focus limits confidence.  
- **Decoding strategy adaptation:** Can alternative decoding methods (e.g., diverse sampling, non‑autoregressive decoding) better handle typological diversity, reducing the need for manual beam tuning per language?

## Limitations
- The analysis is restricted to NLLB‑200 and Tower+, so findings may not generalize to other architectures or training regimes.  
- The regression sample comprises only 52–53 languages due to missing typological data, which may bias results and reduce coverage of low‑resource languages.  
- chrF++ is a surface‑form metric that may not reflect semantic adequacy, especially for morphologically rich languages; beam width limited to 7 may miss optimal decoding parameters.

## Confidence
- **Typology predicts translation quality:** High (robust across both models after extensive controls).  
- **Beam search sensitivity correlates with typology:** Medium (significant but low explained variance; ΔchrF not predicted).  
- **Architectural differences affect the balance of typology vs. resource effects:** Medium (based on two models; pattern suggestive but needs replication).

## Next Checks
1. **Language set reconciliation:** Determine the exact intersection of FLORES+ languages with complete property data (GlotCC, URIEL distances, MATTR, head_finality) and model coverage to resolve the discrepancy between 52 and 53 languages reported.  
2. **Probability extraction audit:** Verify the precise method for computing sentence generation probabilities (e.g., length normalization, EOS token handling) by comparing implementations; test whether cumulative log‑prob or length‑normalized log‑prob aligns with reported trends.  
3. **Baseline score verification:** Generate translations for NLLB‑200 and Tower+ at beam 5 for a representative subset of source‑target pairs; confirm that average chrF++ scores match those in Figure 4 to ensure reproducible inference setup.
