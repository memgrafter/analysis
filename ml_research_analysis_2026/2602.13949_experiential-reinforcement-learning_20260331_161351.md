# Experiential Reinforcement Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.13949
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13949
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: ERL improves final performance by up to +81% in Sokoban and +11% in HotpotQA over standard RLVR by using an explicit experience-reflection-consolidation loop.

## Executive Summary
Experiential Reinforcement Learning (ERL) addresses reinforcement learning for language agents in sparse-reward settings. The method introduces an explicit in-episode cycle: after an initial attempt and feedback, the model generates a self-reflection to produce a refined second attempt. Successful reflective revisions are stored in a cross-episode memory and consolidated into the base policy via selective distillation, enabling persistent improvement without requiring reflection during deployment. Experiments on grid control (FrozenLake, Sokoban) and agentic reasoning (HotpotQA) show consistent gains in final performance and learning efficiency.

## Method Summary
ERL augments standard reinforcement learning with a three-stage loop inside each training trajectory. Given a task input `x`, the model first samples an initial attempt `y(1) ~ π(x)` and receives environment feedback `f(1)` and reward `r(1)`. If `r(1)` falls below a failure threshold, the model generates a self-reflection `Δ` conditioned on `x, y(1), f(1), r(1)`, and a memory `m` of past successful reflections. It then samples a refined attempt `y(2) ~ π(x, Δ)` and receives `f(2), r(2)`. Policy updates via a GRPO-style loss are applied to the tokens of `y(1)`, `Δ`, and `y(2)` using their respective rewards. Reflections that yield a sufficient improvement (`r(2) > τ`) are stored in `m`. Finally, successful `y(2)` outcomes are distilled into the base policy via supervised learning on the pair `(x, y(2))` to internalize the improvement. This allows the policy to perform effectively without `Δ` at inference time.

## Key Results
- On sparse-reward grid tasks (FrozenLake, Sokoban), ERL reaches up to **+81%** higher final performance compared to RLVR.
- On agentic reasoning (HotpotQA), ERL yields **+11%** improvement in token-level F1.
- Learning efficiency accelerates across both model scales (Olmo3-7B, Qwen3-4B).
- Ablations confirm the necessity of the reflection step and the benefit of cross-episode memory for most settings; memory removal slightly hurts or is neutral, except Olmo3-7B on Sokoban where no-memory slightly outperforms.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
Reflection converts sparse/delayed feedback into structured intermediate revision signals within each episode, enabling targeted behavioral correction before policy consolidation. The self-reflection `Δ` explicitly models how the initial attempt `y(1)` should be modified based on observed feedback `f(1)` and reward `r(1)`, creating a corrective conditioning that guides `y(2)` toward improved actions within the same episode. Evidence: [abstract] describes embedding an explicit experience–reflection–consolidation stage; [section 4.3] shows post-reflection trajectories achieve higher reward than pre-reflection and exceed RLVR. Break condition: if reflections are unfaithful or counterproductive, post-reflection performance degrades; the no-reflection ablation confirms reflection's necessity.

### Mechanism 2
Cross-episode memory accumulates successful corrective patterns, enabling reuse and propagation of effective strategies across episodes, which stabilizes training and accelerates learning. Reflections `Δ` that lead to sufficiently improved outcomes (`r(2) > τ`) are stored in memory `m` and injected as context in future reflection generations, allowing the model to bootstrap from previously discovered corrections. Evidence: [section 2] describes preserving corrective patterns; [section 4.4] Table 1 shows consistent performance drops when memory is disabled (except one case), and Figure 7 shows slower convergence without memory. Break condition: memory can propagate inaccurate early reflections; indeed, Olmo3-7B-Instruct on Sokoban shows no-memory slightly outperforms full ERL, suggesting memory harm when reflections are frequently incorrect.

### Mechanism 3
Selective distillation internalizes reflection-driven improvements into the base policy, ensuring gains persist at deployment without requiring reflection or memory access. Successful second attempts `y(2)` are distilled via cross-entropy loss, training the policy to reproduce improved behavior directly from the original input `x` alone (bypassing `Δ`). This consolidates corrective knowledge into the core model. Evidence: [section 2] introduces internalization to convert reflection-guided improvements into persistent policy behavior; [section 6] emphasizes pairing reflection-guided revision with selective internalization. Break condition: if `y(2)` exploits spurious correlations or overfits to `Δ`, distillation could lock in brittle behaviors; the gated reflection (only distill when `r(1)<τ`) helps mitigate by focusing on corrections for failures.

## Foundational Learning
**Concept: Policy Gradient Methods (e.g., REINFORCE, GRPO)**  
Why needed: ERL updates the policy using rewards from both attempts via a GRPO-style loss. Understanding how gradient estimates are shaped by reward signals is fundamental to see why sparse rewards are challenging and how ERL's richer trajectory structure helps.  
Quick check: In standard RLVR, how does the policy gradient estimate change when rewards are sparse and delayed? Why might adding a reflection step between action and reward improve gradient signal quality?

**Concept: Knowledge Distillation**  
Why needed: ERL's internalization uses a distillation loss to transfer successful `y(2)` behavior into `π(x)` without `Δ`, differing from standard SFT.  
Quick check: How does `L_distill` (supervised on `y(2)` given `x`) differ from typical distillation minimizing KL divergence to a teacher? Why is it important to condition only on `x`, not `x+Δ`?

**Concept: Experience Replay / Memory in RL**  
Why needed: The cross-episode memory `m` in ERL is a symbolic analog to replay buffers, storing reflections instead of transitions to enable corrective knowledge reuse.  
Quick check: What makes storing `Δ` (a textual reflection) different from storing `(s, a, r, s')` tuples? How might the memory update rule (store only if `r(2) > τ`) act as a quality filter?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
`x` -> `πθ` -> `y(1)` -> `env` -> `f(1)`, `r(1)`  
If `r(1)<τ`: `(x, y(1), f(1), r(1), m)` -> `πθ` -> `Δ` -> `(x, Δ)` -> `πθ` -> `y(2)` -> `env` -> `f(2)`, `r(2)`; if `r(2)>τ`: `Δ` -> `m`; `(x, y(2))` -> `L_distill` -> `πθ` update.  
`L_policy(θ)` updates `πθ` on generated tokens (y(1), Δ, y(2)) using reward-based advantages.

**Critical path**:  
1. Sample task `x`; generate `y(1) ~ πθ(·|x)`; get `f(1), r(1)` from environment.  
2. Update `πθ` on `(x → y(1))` using `r(1)` via `L_policy`.  
3. If `r(1) < τ`:  
   a. Retrieve memory `m`; generate `Δ ~ πθ(·|x, y(1), f(1), r(1), m)`.  
   b. Generate `y(2) ~ πθ(·|x, Δ)`.  
   c. Get `f(2), r(2)`.  
   d. Update `πθ` on `(x → Δ)` and `(x, Δ → y(2))` using `r(2)` via `L_policy`.  
   e. If `r(2) > τ`: append `Δ` to `m`.  
   f. Update `πθ` on `(x → y(2))` via `L_distill` (supervised, no `Δ`).  
4. Loop.

**Design tradeoffs**:  
- **Reflection gating**: Using `τ=1` (reflect only on failure) improves stability but may miss improvement opportunities. Lower `τ` increases reflection frequency but risks noise.  
- **Memory management**: Simple append-only memory is easy but may accumulate low-quality `Δ`. Retrieval-based memory could improve relevance but adds complexity.  
- **Distillation scope**: Supervised `L_distill` only on successful `y(2)` is simple but may narrow policy distribution; on-policy reverse KL preserves diversity but needs tuning.  
- **Compute allocation**: ERL uses 2 attempts per `x`, so fewer distinct rollouts per compute unit vs. RLVR; matching FLOPs requires halving RLVR rollouts.

**Failure signatures**:  
- **Reflection useless**: Mean(`r(2)-r(1)`) ≤ 0 across samples → reflection not improving; check reflection quality manually.  
- **Memory divergence**: Performance drops when memory size grows beyond a threshold → memory pollution. Consider memory pruning (e.g., keep top-K by improvement).  
- **Distillation collapse**: Validation reward declines after internalization, especially on held-out tasks → catastrophic forgetting. Add KL regularization or reduce distillation frequency.  
- **Gating mis-set**: >90% reflections triggered (`τ` too low) or <5% (`τ` too high). Monitor reflection rate; target ~30-70% depending on task sparsity.

**First 3 experiments**:  
1. **Reflection gating sensitivity**: Train with `τ ∈ {0.0, 0.5, 1.0}` on FrozenLake. Plot final reward vs. `τ` and reflection rate. Expect `τ=1` best for sparse rewards.  
2. **Memory quality filter**: Replace append-only memory with "store top-20% best `Δ` by improvement (`r(2)-r(1)`)". Compare to full memory and no-memory on Sokoban. Test hypothesis: quality filtering mitigates memory corruption.  
3. **Distillation objective contrast**: Compare `L_distill` (supervised) vs. on-policy reverse KL (`L_OD`) for internalization. Monitor final reward and policy entropy. Hypothesis: reverse KL maintains more exploration but may be less stable; supervised distillation gives faster initial gains.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
**Question**: Does retrieval-augmented memory for reflections outperform simple stored reflections in ERL, particularly on diverse or long-horizon tasks?  
**Basis**: Authors note that "a natural extension is to replace this mechanism with a more sophisticated agentic memory system" (Appendix A) but evaluate only plain text memory without retrieval.  
**Why unresolved**: No comparison of memory architectures; unknown whether selective recall enhances reuse across varied instances.  
**What evidence would resolve it**: Implement embedding-based retrieval over past reflections and compare against baseline on tasks with high diversity or longer horizons, measuring sample efficiency and final performance.

### Open Question 2
**Question**: When and why does persistent reflection memory degrade performance, and how can reflection quality be controlled?  
**Basis**: Ablations show removing memory improves results in one case (Olmo on Sokoban, Table 1), and text warns that "persistent memory may propagate early inaccurate reflections" (Section 4.4).  
**Why unresolved**: No analysis of reflection content quality or filtering; memory is unconditional and may store ineffective corrections.  
**What evidence would resolve it**: Qualitatively analyze reflection texts for correctness/consistency during training, correlate with performance trends, and test memory-pruning strategies based on reflection utility signals.

### Open Question 3
**Question**: Is ERL's advantage specific to sparse-reward, unknown-dynamics environments, or does it extend to denser-reward or more structured settings?  
**Basis**: Section 4.1 attributes larger gains on Sokoban/FrozenLake to their sparse rewards and unknown dynamics, while HotpotQA's denser feedback yields smaller improvements.  
**Why unresolved**: No controlled experiments vary reward density or dynamics complexity; unclear which factors trigger ERL's benefits.  
**What evidence would resolve it**: Design task variants with systematically adjusted reward sparsity and stochasticity; measure ERL vs RLVR performance across this spectrum to identify boundary conditions.

## Limitations
- **Implementation ambiguity**: Missing details on GRPO formulation (advantage estimation, KL penalty integration) risk unstable replication.
- **Memory storage rule**: Algorithm 2's condition "store if r(2) > τ" is ambiguous; for binary rewards, this likely should be `r(2) > r(1)` or `r(2)==1`. Uncertainty here critically affects memory content and long-term improvement.
- **Reflection quality control**: No mechanism to filter low-quality or hallucinated reflections; memory may propagate poor corrections, sometimes degrading performance.
- **Distillation stability**: Long-term effects of internalization (e.g., catastrophic forgetting) are not assessed; supervised distillation may overfit to specific reflective contexts.

## Confidence
- **Episodic reflection corrects behavior within an episode**: Medium (supported by ablations, but mechanistic evidence weak).
- **Cross-episode memory stabilizes and accelerates learning**: Medium (mostly beneficial, but one counterexample shows potential harm).
- **Selective distillation creates deployable policy without reflection**: Medium (plausible, but long-term stability unknown).
- **ERL consistently outperforms standard RLVR**: High (gains are consistent across tasks and model scales).
