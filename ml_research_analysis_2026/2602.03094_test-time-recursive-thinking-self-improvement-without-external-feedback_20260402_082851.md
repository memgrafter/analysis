# Test-time Recursive Thinking: Self-Improvement without External Feedback

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.03094  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03094  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Test-time Recursive Thinking (TRT) enables LLMs to self-improve reasoning without external feedback, achieving 100% on AIME-25/24 and 10.4–14.8 pp gains on LiveCodeBench’s hardest problems.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates whether large language models can enhance their reasoning at test time without external feedback or weight updates. It identifies two core challenges: generating diverse, high-quality candidate solutions and reliably selecting correct answers without ground-truth supervision. To address these, the authors propose Test-time Recursive Thinking (TRT), an iterative framework where models generate rollouts conditioned on a dynamically updated knowledge list and per-round strategies, self-rank solutions using domain-specific verification (mutual exclusivity for math, test execution for code), and distill insights by contrasting the best solution against others to refine knowledge for subsequent rounds.  

TRT achieves remarkable results: on the AIME-25 and AIME-24 benchmarks, open-source models reach 100% accuracy. On LiveCodeBench’s hardest problems, closed-source models improve by 10.4–14.8 percentage points over strong baselines, even outperforming recursive self-aggregation under comparable compute. The gains are attributed to strategic exploration via knowledge-guided strategy design and self-guided selection via model-generated test execution, demonstrating the potential of test-time compute scaling for self-improvement.

## Method Summary
TRT is an iterative test-time framework that enables an LLM to refine its reasoning across multiple rounds without external supervision. Starting with an empty knowledge list, the process cycles through: (1) strategy synthesis—generating K distinct approaches from the current knowledge; (2) rollout generation—producing K candidate solutions conditioned on these strategies; (3) selection—choosing the best candidate using domain-specific signals (answer mutual exclusivity for math, execution of model-generated tests for code); and (4) reflection—contrasting the selected solution with the others to extract concise “don’t” insights, which are appended to the knowledge list for the next round. The knowledge list is compacted to fit the context window, and the loop runs for T rounds, returning the final selected solution. This design promotes strategic diversity, self-verification, and progressive error avoidance.

## Key Results
- **AIME-25/24:** Open-source models (e.g., GPT-OSS-120B) achieve 100% accuracy, solving all problems.
- **LiveCodeBench (hardest):** Closed-source models (o3, o4-mini) gain 10.4–14.8 percentage points over the baseline, surpassing recursive self-aggregation with equivalent compute.
- Gains are driven by strategic exploration (knowledge-guided diverse rollouts) and self-guided selection (test execution), with test execution alone contributing ~7 pp for o4‑mini.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
**Mechanism 1: Strategic Exploration via Knowledge-Guided, Diverse Rollout Generation**  
Claim: By conditioning each rollout on a unique, knowledge-derived strategy, TRT explores the solution space more efficiently than independent sampling, producing complementary rather than redundant attempts.  
Mechanism: The model maintains a compact knowledge list of distilled failure insights (primarily negative constraints). At each round, it synthesizes K distinct rollout-specific strategies from this knowledge, ensuring each candidate explores a different reasoning direction or algorithmic paradigm. This deliberate diversification increases the likelihood of discovering valid solutions within a fixed compute budget.  
Core assumption: The model can generate meaningful, non-redundant strategies from its knowledge and that these strategies meaningfully influence the generated solution paths.  
Break condition: If the model's strategy generation becomes repetitive or misaligned with the knowledge base (e.g., suggesting approaches already known to fail), exploration efficiency collapses and gains diminish.  
Evidence anchors: abstract: "conditions generation on rollout-specific strategies, accumulated knowledge"; section 3.1: "To ensure the K rollouts explore distinct regions..."; section 4.4, Figure 4: Strategic planning improves pass@k by 2–7 percentage points.

**Mechanism 2: Self-Guided Selection via Domain-Specific Verification**  
Claim: TRT reliably selects the best candidate without ground truth by exploiting structural properties of the domain: mutual exclusivity of answers for math, and execution-based verification for code.  
Mechanism: For math, the model tracks previously self-rejected answers, leveraging that the single correct answer should not have been rejected. For code, the model generates its own test suite and ranks solutions by the number of passed tests, with tie-breakers based on code quality and consistency with accumulated knowledge.  
Core assumption: The model can generate effective verification criteria (tests) or that the answer space has a unique correct answer identifiable by consistency. Self-generated verification provides a meaningful ranking signal though imperfect.  
Break condition: For code: if generated tests are weak (miss critical edge cases), the selection gap remains large; for math: if self-rejection is inconsistent or multiple incorrect answers converge, selection fails.  
Evidence anchors: section 3.2: "For mathematical reasoning: Mutual Exclusivity... For code generation: Execution-Based Self-Verification..."; section 4.4, Table 1: Test execution contributes 7.4 pp improvement for o4‑mini.

**Mechanism 3: Contrastive Insight Distillation for Knowledge Accumulation**  
Claim: By pairwise comparing the selected best solution against all others, TRT extracts actionable, negative insights ("don'ts") that are added to the knowledge list, preventing repeated failures and guiding future strategy design.  
Mechanism: In the Reflect stage, the model analyzes why non-selected rollouts are inferior to the best. These failure modes are distilled into concise constraints and appended to the knowledge list. This list is then used to generate future strategies, creating a feedback loop where each round builds on past mistakes.  
Core assumption: The model can accurately identify comparative failures and that these insights are generalizable beyond the specific solutions compared.  
Break condition: If extracted insights are overly specific, noisy, or redundant, the knowledge list becomes ineffective or harmful, leading to performance plateaus or degradation.  
Evidence anchors: abstract: "distill insights from contrasting the best solution against others"; section 3.1: "the model performs a pairwise analysis comparing the remaining solutions against the best one, distills what to avoid into an updated knowledge list"; section 5.4: Recording failures ("Don'ts") yields higher accuracy than recording successes ("Dos").

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Test-time compute scaling**  
  *Why needed here:* TRT is fundamentally an inference-time approach that trades additional compute (more rounds/rollouts) for better performance without weight updates. Understanding this paradigm is key to appreciating TRT's goal.  
  *Quick check:* How does TRT's use of test-time compute differ from simply sampling more independent chains (e.g., majority@64)?

- **Concept: Self-consistency and majority voting**  
  *Why needed here:* TRT builds upon but significantly extends the parallel sampling baseline (self-consistency). An engineer must understand this baseline to appreciate TRT's recursive, knowledge-guided improvements.  
  *Quick check:* Why is majority voting insufficient for code generation tasks, and how does TRT address this?

- **Concept: Code execution as a verification signal**  
  *Why needed here:* For code generation, TRT relies on the model's ability to generate and execute tests. This is a core design choice that replaces ground-truth labels.  
  *Quick check:* What are the limitations of using model-generated tests for selection, and how does TRT try to mitigate them?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Knowledge Manager (holds knowledge list) -> generates per-round strategies -> Solver Agent (uses problem, strategies, knowledge to produce rollouts) -> Selector (evaluates rollouts; for code, uses Test Generator) -> selects best solution -> passes to Knowledge Manager for reflect (pairwise comparison, insight distillation) -> updates knowledge list -> next round. Context Manager maintains the knowledge list and conversation history to stay within context limits.

### Critical Path
1. Initialize empty knowledge list.  
2. For each round (T rounds):  
   a. **Generate:** Knowledge Manager designs K unique strategies from current knowledge. Solver Agent generates K rollouts using the problem, knowledge list, and respective strategies.  
   b. **Select:** Selector ranks the K rollouts (mutual exclusivity for math; test execution for code) and selects the best solution `r*`.  
   c. **Reflect:** Knowledge Manager performs pairwise analysis of each non-best rollout against `r*`, distills failure insights (“don’ts”), appends them to the knowledge list, then synthesizes new strategies for the next round.  
3. Return the selected solution from the final round (or aggregate via rolling majority).

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Knowledge compression vs. expressiveness:** Storing only distilled “don’ts” keeps context small but may lose nuance; full trajectory storage would exceed context limits.
- **Strategy diversity vs. relevance:** Per-round strategy design aims for diversity but risks generating irrelevant or already-failed strategies if knowledge is poor.
- **Selection grounding:** Code uses test execution (expensive, imperfect tests) while math uses mutual exclusivity (cheap but domain-specific). A generic selector would be weaker.

### Failure Signatures
- **Context explosion:** Knowledge list grows linearly; if compression fails, context window fills, truncating early knowledge and collapsing guidance.
- **Selection gap widens:** Difference between cumulative best and selected solution accuracy remains high (e.g., >10%), indicating poor verification.
- **Non-monotonic performance:** Accuracy decreases in later rounds, suggesting knowledge contamination or strategy degradation.
- **Breakthrough problems remain unsolved:** Problems solved by baseline at pass@k but never by TRT indicate loss of correct solutions during refinement.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Baseline replication:** Implement parallel thinking with majority voting (64 samples) on AIME-25 to establish a baseline (e.g., ~93% for gpt-oss-120b). Verify against reported numbers.
2. **TRT core loop:** Implement TRT with K=1, T=64 on AIME-25. Expect monotonic improvement to 100%. Validate that knowledge list stays compact (<2% context).
3. **Ablation - test execution:** On LiveCodeBench, run TRT with strategy only (no test generation/execution) vs. full TRT. Expect ~7 pp drop (per Table 1), confirming selection mechanism’s contribution.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **What specific properties of problems make them “breakthrough” candidates for TRT?**  
   *Basis:* Section 5.2 identifies 36 breakthrough problems (17.7%) that baseline failed at pass@10 but TRT solved.  
   *Unresolved:* The paper does not analyze common structural or representational characteristics.  
   *Needed evidence:* A controlled study categorizing breakthrough problems by domain, required algorithmic paradigm, etc., and correlating with TRT’s success rate.

2. **Why does recording failures (“don’ts”) as distilled knowledge outperform recording successes (“dos”) for self-improvement in TRT?**  
   *Basis:* Figure 8 shows “don’ts” yield higher accuracy than “dos” on AIME-25 for both models.  
   *Unresolved:* No theoretical or empirical analysis explains why failure-oriented knowledge is more effective.  
   *Needed evidence:* Ablations comparing “don’ts” vs. “dos” across diverse domains, measuring accuracy, strategy diversity, and convergence stability.

3. **To what extent does the model’s self-generated test execution for code generation suffer from blind spots, and how does this impact the robustness of TRT’s gains?**  
   *Basis:* Section 3.2 acknowledges self-verification is imperfect; Table 1 shows a residual selection gap (4.9% for o4‑mini, 3.5% for o3).  
   *Unresolved:* The paper does not quantify mismatch between model-generated test coverage and ground-truth edge cases.  
   *Needed evidence:* Analysis measuring recall of model-generated tests against held-out ground-truth edge cases per problem, and correlating test quality with final accuracy.

4. **How transferable is the distilled knowledge across different problems within the same domain, and could cross-problem knowledge aggregation improve TRT’s sample efficiency?**  
   *Basis:* Knowledge list is reset per problem; future work suggests aggregating across problems.  
   *Unresolved:* Unclear whether insights generalize or if problem-specific failures dominate; cross-problem aggregation risks noise.  
   *Needed evidence:* Experiment where knowledge list is initialized with insights from a training set and evaluated on a test set, measuring convergence speed vs. per‑instance TRT.

5. **What is the computational overhead trade-off of TRT’s iterative depth (T rounds) versus parallel breadth (K rollouts per round), and is there an optimal schedule for different problem difficulties?**  
   *Basis:* Section 4.4 and Figure 6 show increasing K does not raise the performance ceiling, suggesting “depth beats breadth.” Overhead scales linearly with T; the paper does not quantify the trade-off.  
   *Unresolved:* Optimal T may vary by problem complexity; cost of reflection calls not analyzed.  
   *Needed evidence:* Cost-benefit analysis measuring accuracy per unit compute (sampling + reflection) across T and K, possibly learning an adaptive stopping criterion.

6. **How does the model’s ability to generate complementary per-round strategies evolve with accumulated knowledge, and is there a risk of strategy collapse after many rounds?**  
   *Basis:* Section 3.1 describes generating K complementary strategies; Figure 5c shows positive correlation between unique technique clusters and solve rate, but analysis stops at round 8.  
   *Unresolved:* Strategy design mechanism not analyzed; could exhaust meaningful strategies or enter cycles.  
   *Needed evidence:* Tracking strategy diversity metrics (e.g., embedding clustering of strategy descriptions) over many rounds, and whether solve rate plateaus correlate with strategy saturation.

7. **Can TRT’s recursive self-improvement mechanism be transferred to domains without clear execution-based verification, where mutual exclusivity does not apply and self-generated tests are unreliable?**  
   *Basis:* Section 3.2 specifies domain-specific selection; future directions mention verification mechanisms for mathematical proof checking.  
   *Unresolved:* Selection mechanism is critical; without reliable self‑verification, reflection may distill incorrect insights.  
   *Needed evidence:* Implement TRT for a domain like theorem proving using self-generated proof critiques or consistency checks, evaluating whether knowledge accumulation leads to improvement or divergence.

8. **What is the minimal set of architectural or prompting modifications needed to enable TRT in existing models, and does the benefit scale with base model capability?**  
   *Basis:* Paper uses large models (120B+, o3/o4); Appendix A.3 shows benefits on 4B–30B models but gains vary; prompting templates are complex.  
   *Unresolved:* No ablation isolates which components (knowledge distillation, strategy design, test generation) are most responsible or model-size-dependent.  
   *Needed evidence:* Systematic ablation on multiple model scales, simplifying or removing each TRT component, measuring impact on convergence and accuracy.

9. **Does TRT truly achieve “self-improvement” or merely “self-adaptation” within a single problem instance, and can the learned knowledge be generalized beyond the immediate problem context?**  
   *Basis:* Knowledge list is reset per problem; cross-problem aggregation is future work. Current results show per-instance improvement.  
   *Unresolved:* Ambiguous whether process leads to lasting reasoning policy enhancement or just contextual search.  
   *Needed evidence:* Experiment where knowledge list from one set of problems is used as a fixed prefix for a new held-out set, measuring any lift in baseline performance.

10. **How does the size and composition of the knowledge list affect context window utilization and reasoning quality, and what is the optimal pruning strategy?**  
    *Basis:* Section 3.1 mentions pruning “up to one outdated knowledge entry per round”; Figure 7 shows list remains compact (<1.5% context), but pruning selection not specified.  
    *Unresolved:* Pruning could remove critical insights; longevity and impact of different knowledge types not analyzed.  
    *Needed evidence:* Ablation with different pruning strategies (random, oldest-first, least-referenced, conflict-based) measuring convergence speed, final accuracy, and knowledge retention.

## Limitations
- **Strategy relevance & drift:** Model may generate repetitive or irrelevant strategies if knowledge is noisy, leading to exploration collapse.
- **Selection grounding in code:** Self‑generated tests can miss edge cases, leaving a persistent accuracy gap (~7%).
- **Insight generalization:** Contrastive distillation may produce problem‑specific insights that don’t transfer, risking contamination across rounds.
