# Symbol-Equivariant Recurrent Reasoning Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.02193  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02193  
- **Reference count:** 31  
- **Primary result:** SE-RRM achieves 93.73% fully solved rate on 9×9 Sudoku with only 8 augmentations per sample and generalizes zero-shot to larger grids, while matching TRM on ARC-AGI with far fewer parameters.

## Executive Summary
Recurrent reasoning models (RRMs) like HRM and TRM rely heavily on data augmentation to handle symbol permutation symmetries in tasks such as Sudoku and ARC-AGI. This work introduces Symbol-Equivariant RRM (SE-RRM), which enforces permutation equivariance architecturally by introducing a dedicated symbol dimension and applying axial attention across positions and symbols with shared embeddings. SE-RRM (2M parameters) surpasses TRM and HRM on 9×9 Sudoku while using orders of magnitude fewer augmentations, and it generalizes zero-shot to 16×16 and 25×25 Sudokus—sizes where prior RRMs fail entirely. On ARC-AGI benchmarks, it matches TRM’s performance with substantially reduced parameters and augmentation, demonstrating that architectural symmetry bias improves robustness, scalability, and data efficiency in neural reasoning.

## Method Summary
SE-RRM processes inputs as a 3D tensor (feature-dimension × positions × symbols). A shared embedding maps all symbols (except special tokens) to a common vector, and positional information is added via 2D RoPE. The core recurrent block applies axial attention: first over positions (using shared symbol embeddings), then over symbols (mixing across symbol dimension while preserving position), followed by RMSNorm and a position-wise SwiGLU MLP. The model iterates a fixed-point update: \(Z_{t+1} = H(E, Z_t)\), where \(E\) is the encoded input and \(Z_t\) is the constant-initialized state. Deep supervision (loss applied at randomly halted steps) enables multi-step refinement without full backpropagation through time. Training uses minimal augmentations (8 dihedral transforms for ARC/Sudoku) and random halting; inference uses all steps.

## Key Results
- On 9×9 Sudoku, SE-RRM (2M) achieves 93.73% fully solved rate, beating TRM (71.94%) and HRM (63.53%).
- Zero-shot generalization: SE-RRM trained on 9×9 achieves 51.95% grid-point accuracy on 16×16 and 31.49% on 25×25, while prior RRMs fail completely.
- On ARC-AGI-1 and ARC-AGI-2, SE-RRM matches TRM (45.3% and 7.1% pass@2) with far fewer parameters and augmentations.
- Maze-hard task breaks under enforced equivariance, showing the method’s limitation to tasks with interchangeable symbols.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** SE-RRM achieves permutation equivariance architecturally, removing reliance on data augmentations and enabling robust generalization across symbol permutations.
- **Mechanism:** Introduce a dedicated symbol dimension and apply axial attention—first across positions (\(T_{D,I}\)), then across symbols (\(T_{D,K}\))—using shared embeddings for all usual symbols. This guarantees that any permutation of symbols in input yields a corresponding permutation in outputs.
- **Core assumption:** In tasks like Sudoku or ARC‑AGI, symbols are interchangeable; the solution should be equivariant under symbol permutations.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Abstract states "enforce permutation equivariance at the architectural level … guaranteeing identical solutions under symbol or color permutations."  
  - Section 2.3 details axial attention layers and Proposition 2.3 formally proves symbol permutation equivariance.  
  - Maze experiment (symbols not interchangeable) breaks equivariance and achieves higher accuracy (~88.8% vs. 0% when enforced).
- **Break condition:** When symbols have distinct semantics (e.g., Maze walls vs. start/end), enforcing equivariance harms performance.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** The recurrent fixed‑point iteration with deep supervision supports multi-step reasoning and adaptive computation, decoupling reasoning depth from model size.
- **Mechanism:** A recurrent block updates constant‑initialized state \(Z_t\) using task embedding \(E\) and possibly previous states: \(Z_{t+1}=H(E, Z_t)\). Deep supervision applies the loss at selected intermediate steps, detaching gradients between cycles, allowing refinement without full backpropagation through time.
- **Core assumption:** Complex constraint‑satisfaction problems require iterative refinement; additional steps can improve solution quality.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Abstract notes RRMs offer a compact alternative but rely on costly data augmentation for symbol symmetries (implies recurrence).  
  - Section 2.1 formalizes fixed‑point iteration and deep supervision.  
  - Corpus supports recurrence for CSPs and adaptive depth.
- **Break condition:** If the block capacity is insufficient or the problem does not benefit from iteration, recurrence may not improve or could cause instability.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Shared symbol embeddings and axial attention enable zero‑shot generalization to grids with new dimensions and larger symbol sets, where vanilla RRMs fail.
- **Mechanism:** All usual symbols share the same embedding vector \(d\). Symbol permutation equivariance ensures representation does not depend on identity. Positional info via RoPE2D scales to any 2‑D grid. Thus, new symbols (e.g., digits 1–16) are represented by \(d\) and processed via symbol attention.
- **Core assumption:** The relational structure of the task is independent of the particular symbols; only their interactions matter.
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - Abstract: "SE‑RRMs … generalize from just training on 9×9 to smaller 4×4 and larger 16×16 and 25×25 instances, to which existing RRMs cannot extrapolate."  
  - Section 2.2 notes vanilla RRMs cannot extrapolate to \(|C'| > |C|\); Section 3.1 presents generalization results.
- **Break condition:** If symbol identities matter or \(K\) becomes large relative to \(I\), the \(O(I^2K + K^2I)\) cost may be prohibitive.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Input encoder → `EC` (shared symbol embedding) + `EP` (RoPE2D positional) + `ET` (task-type broadcast) → 3D tensor (D×I×K) → Recurrent block `G` (axial attention: `T_D,I` over positions, then `T_D,K` over symbols, each with RMSNorm, residual, SwiGLU MLP) → Constant state `Z_t` updated iteratively → Prediction head (linear layer → logits (I×K) → softmax) → Cross-entropy loss per position.

- **Critical path:**  
  1. Encode puzzle `EG = EC + EP + ET` (shape `D×I×K`).  
  2. Initialize `Z0 ∈ R^{D×I×K}`.  
  3. For `t = 0..T-1`:  
     - Build `H0 = EG + Z_t` (+ `Z_{t-r}` if used).  
     - Run block `G` to obtain `Z_{t+1}`.  
     - With probability `p_stop`, compute loss on `O(Z_t)` and stop early; else continue.  
  4. Final predictions from `Z_T`.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - Axial order (positions then symbols) chosen to preserve both equivariances; reversing may break one.  
  - Shared vs. separate symbol embeddings: shared reduces parameters and enforces equivariance; separate adds capacity for tasks with semantically distinct symbols.  
  - Deep supervision: random halting (simple) vs. learned q‑learning (adaptive but complex).  
  - Positional encoding: RoPE2D handles 2‑D grids and scales; 1‑D RoPE may not generalize across grid sizes.  
  - Memory complexity: \(O(IK)\) due to attention; keep \(I*K\) within hardware limits.

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Very low fully solved rate (<10%) on Sudoku after many steps → check embedding sharing and equivariance enforcement.  
  - Performance collapse on larger \(I\) → confirm RoPE2D and absence of size‑specific biases.  
  - Out‑of‑memory errors → reduce batch size or model dimensions; likely \(I*K\) too large.  
  - Maze performance near zero when equivariance enforced → symbols are not interchangeable; need separate embeddings.  
  - Training instability with random halting (loss spikes) → adjust `p_stop`.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. Train on 9×9 Sudoku with minimal augmentations; report FSR/GPA vs. HRM/TRM.  
  2. Evaluate same model zero‑shot on 4×4, 16×16, 25×25 Sudoku; measure generalization.  
  3. Test on ARC‑AGI‑1/2 and Maze‑hard; verify competitive results with few augmentations.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Scaling with model size:** How does SE‑RRM perform with parameters matching TRM (7M) or HRM (27M)? The paper only uses 2M parameters.
2. **Efficiency for K>>I:** Can we design more efficient symbol‑equivariant architectures that avoid \(O(K^2 I)\) complexity when symbol sets are large?
3. **Larger Sudoku solving:** What modifications are needed to achieve non‑zero fully solved rates on 16×16 and 25×25 Sudokus when trained only on 9×9?
4. **Learned symbol equivalence:** Can the model learn which symbols are interchangeable rather than enforcing uniform equivariance across all symbols?

## Limitations
- Relies on tasks where symbols are truly interchangeable; fails on Maze‑hard unless equivariance is broken.
- Computational complexity \(O(I^2K + K^2I)\) may be prohibitive for very large grids or symbol sets.
- Architecture and training details (e.g., exact augmentation count for Sudoku, dataset preprocessing) are underspecified, risking reproduction hurdles.
- Zero‑shot generalization to larger Sudokus yields grid‑point accuracy but 0% fully solved rate, indicating incomplete constraint learning.

## Confidence
- **Permutation‑equivariant architecture:** High (formal proof in Proposition 2.3; Maze ablation confirms).
- **Recurrent iteration with deep supervision:** Medium (theoretically sound; no direct ablation on depth/training stability).
- **Zero‑shot generalization & minimal augmentation:** Medium (empirical support exists; fidelity of implementation and potential data leakage are unknown).
