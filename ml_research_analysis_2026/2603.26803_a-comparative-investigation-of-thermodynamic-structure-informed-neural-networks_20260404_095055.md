# A Comparative Investigation of Thermodynamic Structure-Informed Neural Networks
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.26803
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26803
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Thermodynamic structure-informed PINNs (LM, HM, OVP, EIT) significantly improve physical consistency, robustness to noise, and interpretability over standard Newtonian PINNs.

## Executive Summary
This study systematically evaluates the integration of thermodynamic formulations—Newtonian, Lagrangian, Hamiltonian, Onsager variational principle, and extended irreversible thermodynamics—into Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) for forward and inverse problems of both conservative and dissipative dynamical systems. While Newtonian residual-based PINNs can reconstruct system trajectories, they fail to reliably recover key physical quantities such as Lagrangian, Hamiltonian, Rayleighian, and entropy. In contrast, structure-preserving formulations (Lagrangian, Hamiltonian, Onsager, EIT) demonstrate superior performance: Lagrangian PINNs enhance robustness in parameter identification under noise; Hamiltonian PINNs enforce energy conservation with high precision; Onsager PINNs stabilize parameter recovery for dissipative systems; and extended irreversible thermodynamics PINNs ensure thermodynamic consistency, accurately recovering entropy and entropy fluxes. Across all tested systems—including ideal mass-spring, pendulum, double pendulum, damped pendulum, 2D diffusion, and Fisher-Kolmogorov equations—the thermodynamic-informed PINNs consistently outperform Newtonian PINNs.

## Method Summary
The study compares five variants of structure-informed PINNs: Newtonian (NM), Lagrangian (LM), Hamiltonian (HM), Onsager variational principle (OVP), and extended irreversible thermodynamics (EIT). Six benchmark systems are used: ideal mass-spring, pendulum, double pendulum (conservative); damped pendulum, 2D diffusion, and Fisher‑Kolmogorov (dissipative). Synthetic training data are generated from analytical solutions or high‑precision numerical solvers, with additive Gaussian noise at levels 0%, 1%, 5%, and 10%. Each PINN uses a neural network to approximate state variables, with automatic differentiation computing required derivatives. The residual loss is specific to each formalism: Euler‑Lagrange for LM, canonical equations for HM, Onsager’s variational principle for OVP, and entropy balance for EIT. The total loss combines a data fidelity term and the physical residual term, weighted by hyperparameters λ. Training is performed using gradient‑based optimization (Adam). Performance is evaluated via relative state errors, parameter identification accuracy (relative absolute error), mean‑squared error of recovered physical quantities (Lagrangian, Hamiltonian, Rayleighian, entropy, entropy flux), variance across random seeds, and the Frobenius norm of the loss‑landscape Hessian. The results show that structure‑preserving PINNs achieve better physical fidelity, noise robustness, and interpretability.

## Key Results
- Newtonian PINNs accurately reconstruct trajectories but fail to reliably recover key invariants (e.g., Lagrangian MSE up to 2.02×10⁻² in mass‑spring).
- Structure‑preserving PINNs significantly improve performance: LM-PINNs offer robust parameter identification under 10% noise (relative absolute error ~4.72×10⁻¹); HM-PINNs enforce energy conservation with Hamiltonian MSE 1.14×10⁻³; OVP-PINNs yield the smallest variance in diffusion coefficient recovery; EIT-PINNs recover entropy with MSE 1.47×10⁻⁴ (vs. 5.75×10⁻² for Newtonian) and maintain non‑negative entropy production.
- Across all benchmark systems, thermodynamic‑informed PINNs consistently exhibit higher physical consistency, robustness to noise, and interpretable learned quantities than Newtonian PINNs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Newtonian PINNs reconstruct system states but fail to recover key physical invariants (e.g., Lagrangian, Hamiltonian) because their residual loss lacks structure-preserving constraints.
- **Mechanism:** The Newtonian residual `∂u/∂t - F[u] = 0` enforces pointwise force balance but does not constrain the solution to satisfy variational principles (e.g., least action) or conserve geometric structures (e.g., symplectic, metric). This leaves the learned solution within a larger, physically inconsistent function space, where invariants emerge only accidentally if at all.
- **Core assumption:** The learned solution's consistency with higher‑order physical structures (energy, entropy) is not guaranteed by minimizing a local residual and can be easily violated under noise or optimization fluctuations.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Abstract: "Newtonian residual-based PINNs accurately reconstruct system trajectories but fail to reliably recover key physical quantities such as Lagrangian, Hamiltonian, Rayleighian, and entropy."
  - Section 3.1.1: NM-PINNs show high MSE for Lagrangian (2.02×10⁻²) and poor phase portrait (q,p) for mass‑spring.
  - Corpus: Weak; neighbors focus on other PINN variants but none directly challenge this mechanism's claim from the paper's own experiments.
- **Break condition:** When the system is conservative or has a clear variational structure and the inverse problem requires recovering potentials or parameters sensitive to the solution's geometric properties.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Lagrangian PINNs improve inverse problem robustness (parameter identification under noise) by embedding the Euler‑Lagrange equation, which imposes first‑order structure on the generalized velocities and coordinates.
- **Mechanism:** The Euler‑Lagrange residual `d/dt(∂L/∂q̇) - ∂L/∂q = 0` couples position and velocity through a variational principle. This creates a loss landscape with stronger, correlated gradients that are less sensitive to noise amplification in second derivatives (as in Newton's `m q̈`) and more directly constrain the dynamical trajectory's geometric properties.
- **Core assumption:** The system is hyperelastic and admits a Lagrangian `L(q, q̇, t)`; the noise primarily affects state measurements, not the structure of the ODE itself.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Abstract: "Lagrangian PINNs improve parameter identification robustness under noise (relative absolute error ~4.72×10⁻¹ at 10% noise for mass‑spring)."
  - Section 3.1.1: "LM‑PINNs demonstrate the strongest robustness" in `k/m` recovery under noise compared to NM‑ and HM‑PINNs.
  - Section 2.3: Explicit formulation of `Loss_res` using Euler‑Lagrange.
- **Break condition:** For systems with strong non‑conservative forces (dissipation), as the Lagrangian formalism for such systems is non‑trivial and may not exist in standard form.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Hamiltonian and EIT PINNs achieve high physical consistency (energy/entropy preservation) by enforcing structure through canonical equations or entropy balance, which are inherently compatible with the system's thermodynamic geometry.
- **Mechanism:** 
  - **Hamiltonian PINNs:** Add `dH/dt = 0` loss, enforcing energy conservation explicitly. The canonical equations `q̇ = ∂H/∂p, ṗ = -∂H/∂q` preserve the symplectic structure, leading to a flatter loss landscape (lower Hessian norm) and accurate recovery of `H(q,p)`.
  - **EIT PINNs:** Enforce `∂S/∂t + ∇·Jₛ - σₛ = 0` using a known entropy function `S`. This directly constraints the entropy production rate `σₛ ≥ 0`, ensuring thermodynamic consistency and accurate flux learning.
- **Core assumption:** 
  - For HM‑PINNs: The system is conservative with a time‑independent Hamiltonian.
  - For EIT‑PINNs: The entropy function `S` and fluxes `Jₛ` are correctly specified or parameterized within the network.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - Abstract: "Hamiltonian PINNs enforce energy conservation with high precision (Hamiltonian MSE 1.14×10⁻³)" and "EIT PINNs excel in thermodynamic consistency, accurately recovering entropy functions and fluxes (entropy MSE 1.47×10⁻⁴ vs. 5.75×10⁻² for Newtonian)."
  - Section 3.1.1: HM‑PINNs' phase diagram and Hamiltonian are accurate; NM‑PINNs fail.
  - Section 3.2: EIT‑PINNs have smallest entropy flux discrepancy and high entropy recovery accuracy.
  - Section 3.3: Loss landscape Hessian norms are lowest for HM‑PINNs (34.07) and EIT‑PINNs (0.71) vs. NM‑PINNs (214.64, 15.25), indicating flatter minima correlated with better generalization for these quantities.
- **Break condition:**
  - HM‑PINNs: Fails for dissipative systems (requires explicit dissipation potential or extension like `dH/dt = -D`).
  - EIT‑PINNs: Suffers if the entropy function form is misspecified or if nonequilibrium variables are poorly chosen.

## Foundational Learning
- **Variational Principles (Euler‑Lagrange, Hamilton’s Canonical Equations)**  
  Why needed here: They provide the structure‑preserving residuals for conservative systems (LM‑, HM‑PINNs). Understanding that these equations derive from extremizing an action or energy functional is key to knowing why embedding them improves robustness.  
  Quick check question: How does the Euler‑Lagrange equation `d/dt(∂L/∂q̇) - ∂L/∂q = 0` differ fundamentally from Newton’s `F = m a` in terms of the variables it couples and the type of invariants it guarantees?

- **Dissipation Potentials and Rayleighian**  
  Why needed here: Central to OVP‑PINNs. The Rayleighian `R = Φ + dU/dt` combines dissipation `Φ(q̇)` and potential change. Minimizing `δR/δ q̇ = 0` yields thermodynamically consistent dynamics for linear irreversible processes.  
  Quick check question: What physical property does the dissipation potential `Φ` (e.g., `½ ξ q̇²`) ensure about the evolution equation derived from `δR/δ q̇ = 0`?

- **Entropy Production and Balance**  
  Why needed here: Foundational for EIT‑PINNs. The requirement `dS/dt + ∇·Jₛ = σₛ ≥ 0` must hold pointwise. Ensuring the learned dynamics satisfy this, via a known `S`, guarantees thermodynamic admissibility.  
  Quick check question: In the damped pendulum example, why does the entropy production `σₛ = λ ω²` being non‑negative impose a constraint on the damping coefficient `λ`?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Neural Network (`uθ(t,x)` or `(qθ(t), pθ(t))`) → Automatic Differentiation → Residual Loss Modules (Loss_newton, Loss_lagrange, Loss_hamilton, Loss_ovp, Loss_eit) + Data Loss → Weighted Total Loss → Optimizer (Adam).  
- **Critical path:**  
  1. Define system and select appropriate thermodynamic formalism (conservative vs dissipative).  
  2. Implement network to output required state variables (e.g., `(q,p)` for HM; `(u,v)` for OVP/EIT).  
  3. Derive analytical expressions for physical quantities (`L`, `H`, `R`, `S`) in terms of network outputs.  
  4. Implement residual loss using autograd to compute necessary derivatives.  
  5. Train with collocation points sampled across the domain plus any available data points.  
  6. Monitor recovery of key invariants (e.g., `H(t)`, `S(t)`).  
- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - *Complexity vs. Robustness:* HM‑PINNs require learning `(q,p)` and an extra `dH/dt` loss, increasing gradient complexity; EIT‑PINNs require correct `S` form; LM‑PINNs are simpler but rely on correct `L`.  
  - *Noise Sensitivity:* NM‑PINNs amplify noise through second derivatives; HM‑PINNs can become unstable under high noise; OVP‑PINNs show smallest variance in parameter recovery.  
  - *Applicability:* NM is universal but physically weak; LM/HM only for conservative systems; OVP for linear dissipative systems with known `R`; EIT for systems with known `S` and fluxes.  
- **Failure signatures:**  
  - Invariant drift: `H(t)` or `S(t)` trends linearly or erratically, indicating violated conservation/dissipation.  
  - High parameter variance: Inferred parameters (e.g., diffusion coefficient `D_x`) show large spread across random seeds.  
  - Phase portrait distortion: `(q,p)` plot exhibits spiraling or area distortion.  
  - Entropy production negative: `σₛ < 0` in some regions for EIT‑PINNs.  
- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Ideal mass‑spring (conservative):** Compare NM, LM, HM. Evaluate `H(t)` recovery and `k/m` inference under 10% noise. Expect LM best for parameter noise robustness; HM best for Hamiltonian preservation.  
  2. **Damped pendulum (dissipative):** Compare NM, OVP, EIT. Evaluate Rayleighian `R(t)` and entropy `S(t)` recovery. Expect EIT best for entropy accuracy; OVP best for stable damping coefficient inference (lowest variance).  
  3. **2D Diffusion (PDE):** Compare NM, OVP, EIT. Evaluate diffusion coefficient `D_x` recovery and entropy flux `Jₛ` discrepancy field. Expect OVP lowest variance in `D_x`; EIT smallest coherent error in `Jₛ`.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Global loss landscape vs. parameter identification:** The current analysis focuses on local flatness (Hessian norm), but parameter recovery may depend on the global topology of the loss surface in parameter space (basin connectivity, multiple minima). Evidence needed: quantitative mapping of loss surfaces via mode connectivity, basin volume estimation, or global optimization landscapes across formulations and noise levels, correlated with recovery metrics.
2. **Integration of GENERIC or broader nonequilibrium thermodynamics:** The conclusion suggests exploring frameworks like GENERIC for multi‑physics systems with coupled conservative‑dissipative dynamics. No implementation or empirical validation of such generalizations is provided. Evidence needed: derivation of GENERIC‑based residual losses, implementation in PINNs, and testing on benchmarks with mixed reversible‑irreversible dynamics (e.g., fluid‑structure interactions).
3. **Scalability to high‑dimensional PDEs and complex geometries:** Experiments are limited to low‑dimensional ODEs and simple 1D/2D PDEs. It remains unclear whether the reported advantages of structure‑preserving PINNs hold for large‑scale engineering problems (e.g., 3D Navier‑Stokes, complex boundaries). Evidence needed: application to such high‑dimensional systems, measuring accuracy, training time, memory usage, and robustness compared to standard PINNs.
4. **Comparative training dynamics:** The paper evaluates solution accuracy but not training efficiency (convergence rates, hyper‑parameter sensitivity, computational overhead). Evidence needed: controlled experiments tracking loss curves, gradient norms, convergence thresholds across optimizers (Adam, L‑BFGS) and architectures, supplemented by ablation studies on loss weighting and network size.

## Limitations
- The paper lacks essential training details (network architecture, optimizer settings, loss weighting coefficients, data sampling strategy, noise distribution). Faithful numerical replication therefore depends on obtaining the code repository, and even then some hyperparameters may be undocumented.
- All experiments are conducted on low‑dimensional synthetic systems; the scalability of the findings to real‑world, high‑dimensional PDEs with complex geometries is not addressed.
- No statistical significance testing is performed; reported improvements could be due to chance, and confidence intervals are not provided.
