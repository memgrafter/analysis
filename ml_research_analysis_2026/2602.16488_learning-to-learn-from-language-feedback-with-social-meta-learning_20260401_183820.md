# Learning to Learn from Language Feedback with Social Meta-Learning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.16488
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16488
- **Reference count:** 23
- **Primary result:** Social Meta-Learning (SML) via online RL (GRPO) enables LLMs to learn from language feedback, cross-domain transfer, and with Q-priming drastically increases clarifying questions on ambiguous tasks.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles a key limitation of large language models (LLMs): their inability to effectively learn from conversational feedback. The authors introduce Social Meta-Learning (SML), a framework that transforms static problem-solving tasks into interactive pedagogical dialogues. In SML, a "teacher" with privileged knowledge (e.g., ground-truth solutions) provides corrective feedback to a "student" model over multiple turns. The core methodological innovation is the use of online reinforcement learning with GRPO, which optimizes the student's policy for successful multi-turn strategies by group-normalizing rewards across sampled trajectories. Experiments on math and coding problems show that online RL substantially outperforms offline supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and single-turn baselines. Models trained with just 4 turns generalize to solve problems over 10 test turns, demonstrating improved in-context learning from feedback. Moreover, the learned skill transfers across domains: math-trained SML models perform better on coding tasks with feedback, and vice versa. Finally, an initial Q-priming stage—where synthetic clarifying questions are injected during pretraining—boosts question-asking rates by over 5× on ambiguous tasks and reduces premature answer attempts. The work provides a proof-of-concept for training LLMs to engage in effective, feedback-driven learning dialogues.

## Method Summary
Social Meta-Learning (SML) converts a static dataset (e.g., math or coding problems) into multi-turn dialogues between a student (the trainable model) and a teacher (a frozen model with access to private knowledge such as the correct solution or test case outputs). For each problem, the teacher initiates the interaction; then for up to N turns (training N=4, evaluation N=10), the student generates an utterance, the teacher responds with feedback based on the private knowledge, and the conversation proceeds. The episode ends when the student produces a correct solution or the turn limit is reached; a sparse binary reward (1 for correct, 0 otherwise) is assigned.

Training proceeds in two main regimes: (1) *Offline SFT*: rollouts are collected, only trajectories ending with correct solutions are retained, and the student is supervised on its own utterances from those successful dialogues. (2) *Online RL with GRPO*: for each problem, a group of trajectories is sampled on-policy; GRPO computes advantages by normalizing the binary rewards within the group and updates the student policy to increase the likelihood of successful trajectories relative to others, using a KL penalty to stay close to a reference. Additionally, a *Q-priming* pretraining stage can be applied: before RL, the student is fine-tuned on data where, whenever a student turn is incorrect, it is replaced with a synthetically generated clarifying question. These questions are produced by prompting the student with the teacher's private knowledge (to make them informative). The probability of injection decays exponentially with turn number (e.g., \(P_Q(t)=0.75^t\)), encouraging early exploration.

## Key Results
- **Online RL (GRPO) superiority:** Models trained with 4-turn online RL show a widening performance gap over longer conversations compared to single-turn RL and offline SFT baselines, demonstrating effective learning of feedback-utilization strategies.
- **Cross-domain transfer:** SML-trained models transfer their feedback-learning skill across domains; e.g., a model trained on math (Omni-MATH) with teacher feedback solves coding problems (LiveCodeBench) more effectively when given feedback, and vice versa.
- **Q-priming effect:** With Q-priming, models are over 5× more likely to ask clarifying questions on ambiguous (sharded) tasks and make far fewer premature answer attempts, despite being trained only on fully-specified problems.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Online RL vs Offline SFT
**Claim:** Online RL (GRPO) is substantially more effective than offline SFT for teaching models to learn from language feedback because it directly optimizes for successful multi-turn conversational strategies via group-normalized rewards.  
**Mechanism:** During training, the student generates a group of conversational trajectories per problem. GRPO computes advantages by normalizing trajectory-level binary rewards across the group, then updates the policy to increase the likelihood of successful trajectories relative to peers within the batch. This on-policy exploration within the dialogue space allows the model to discover and reinforce effective feedback-utilization patterns that might be missed in a static filtered dataset.  
**Core assumption:** The advantage normalization provides a stable learning signal that effectively credits useful conversational moves (e.g., asking clarifying questions, incorporating corrections) even with sparse, conversation-level rewards, and that on-policy data generation is necessary for this discovery.  
**Evidence:** Abstract and Section 6.1 show online RL (4-turn RL) establishing a "widening performance gap" over longer conversations compared to single-turn RL and SFT.  
**Break condition:** If the KL penalty (𝛽) is misconfigured or the group size (𝐺) is too small, the normalization could become unstable, leading to degenerate policies or failure to learn from feedback.

### Mechanism 2: Cross-Domain Transfer
**Claim:** The ability to learn from language feedback, acquired via SML, transfers across domains (e.g., math → code) because SML teaches a domain-agnostic meta-skill of using conversational turns to refine a solution based on external feedback, rather than domain-specific factual knowledge.  
**Mechanism:** Training on interactive dialogues in one domain (e.g., math) forces the model to practice a general loop: propose partial solution → receive corrective/divergent feedback → update internal state → generate improved response. This loop is reinforced regardless of whether the feedback is based on ground truth solutions (math) or test case outputs (code), leading to transferable in-context learning dynamics.  
**Core assumption:** The core skill being learned is the conversational problem-solving loop itself, which is abstracted from the specific content domain by the consistent structure of the teacher-student interaction and the binary success reward.  
**Evidence:** Abstract and Section 6.2 show empirical results where models trained on Omni-MATH (math) generalize to LiveCodeBench (code), and vice versa, with gains in multi-turn performance.  
**Break condition:** If the domains have fundamentally different feedback modalities (e.g., math feedback is logical correction, code feedback is test outputs) and the model overfits to surface patterns in the training domain, the abstract loop may not form.

### Mechanism 3: Q-Priming for Exploration
**Claim:** Q-priming (an initial SFT stage with injected, informative questions) significantly boosts question-asking behavior during subsequent RL training and reduces premature answer attempts on ambiguous tasks, because it establishes a strong prior for exploration/information-seeking before reward shaping can act.  
**Mechanism:** Q-priming creates a dataset where, on incorrect turns, the model's response is replaced with a synthetically generated question formulated using the teacher's private knowledge. The exponential decay injection schedule encourages questioning early in conversations. This pre-trains the policy to associate early-turn uncertainty with generating interrogative utterances. Subsequent online RL then refines this behavior, maintaining the exploratory tendency while optimizing for task success.  
**Core assumption:** Explicitly demonstrating useful questioning behavior in a supervised setting creates a more robust prior for exploration than relying on RL to discover it from scratch, which can be slow or unstable.  
**Evidence:** Abstract and Section 6.4 directly show Q-priming leads to "over 5 times more likely to ask a clarifying question" and fewer premature attempts on Lost-in-Conversation.  
**Break condition:** If the injected questions are not diverse or truly informative (e.g., they rely too heavily on the provided private knowledge without generalization), the learned prior may not transfer to novel ambiguous scenarios where the private knowledge structure differs.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map (plain text):**  
Student Policy (π_θ^S) takes dialogue history c_t and generates student utterance u_S_t. Teacher Policy (π_T) takes updated history c_{t+1} and private knowledge k_t to generate teacher feedback u_T_{t+1}. Private Knowledge k_t (e.g., ground truth solution, test case outputs) is fixed per problem. Rollout Engine manages the interaction loop: sample problem → initialize teacher with k_t → for N turns: student acts → teacher responds. Reward Function G(τ) returns binary 1/0 at episode end. For training: Online RL uses GRPO (group of trajectories, group-normalized advantages, KL penalty). Offline SFT filters successful trajectories and performs supervised learning on student turns. Q-priming Module (optional) replaces incorrect student turns with synthetically generated questions using k_t.

**Critical path:**  
1. Sample a problem and determine its private knowledge k_t.  
2. Teacher generates initial utterance u_T_1 based on (c_0, k_t).  
3. Loop for t=1..N:  
   - Student observes c_t and generates u_S_t.  
   - Teacher observes (c_{t+1}, k_t) and generates u_T_{t+1}.  
   - Check if u_S_t is correct; if so, optionally terminate early.  
4. After termination, assign reward G(τ)=1 if final student solution is correct, else 0.  
5. Update:  
   - Offline SFT: collect all successful trajectories (G=1), extract student turns, perform SFT on π_θ^S.  
   - Online RL (GRPO): for each problem, sample g trajectories, compute group-normalized advantages A_i, apply policy gradient update to π_θ^S with KL penalty to reference policy.  
   - Q-priming: before RL, perform SFT on dataset where, for each turn t, if student turn was incorrect, it is replaced with a question generated by prompting student with (c_t, k_t) with probability P_Q(t) (exponential decay).

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Teacher strength:** Stronger teacher may provide more useful feedback but increases computational cost; paper finds training teacher strength less critical than inference-time teacher strength.  
- **Turn budget (N):** Longer training conversations could teach more complex feedback incorporation but raise compute; 4-turn training generalizes to 10-turn test.  
- **Reward sparsity:** Binary final reward simplifies design but offers no intermediate signal; dense turn-level rewards are future work.  
- **Q-priming injection decay:** Decay rate (e.g., 0.75^t) balances early exploration vs. later task focus; requires tuning.

**Failure signatures:**  
- High rate of premature answer attempts on ambiguous (sharded) tasks.  
- Low frequency of clarification questions even when information is missing.  
- No improvement in loss on correct answer across turns (e.g., base model curve in Figure 3).  
- Online RL fails to improve over single-turn baseline or generates degenerate utterances (reward hacking).  
- Cross-domain transfer absent; model only performs well in training domain.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **In-Domain Efficacy:** Compare SML (4-turn online RL) against single-turn RL and offline SFT on Omni-MATH with N=10 test turns. Expect SML to show rising accuracy over turns while baselines plateau.  
2. **Cross-Domain Transfer:** Train SML on Omni-MATH (math) and evaluate on LiveCodeBench (code) with teacher feedback based on test outputs. Expect improved multi-turn learning curve compared to single-turn baselines.  
3. **Q-Priming Ablation:** Compare SML with Q-priming vs. without on Lost-in-Conversation (sharded math/code). Measure question-asking rate and premature answer attempts. Expect >5× question rate increase and fewer premature attempts with Q-priming.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Can SML be effectively extended to open-ended, subjective tasks (e.g., creative writing, debate) where "correctness" is ambiguous and rewards are less verifiable?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The Limitations & Future Work section states: "Our approach is demonstrated in verifiable domains (math and code)... Future work should extend SML to more subjective, open-ended tasks, which will require use of more nuanced reward models."
- **Why unresolved:** The paper's methodology relies on sparse, binary rewards from objective verifiers (e.g., test cases, ground truth). Subjective tasks lack such clear signals, risking reward hacking or degenerate strategies.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Implement SML on a held-out subjective domain (e.g., essay critique) using a combination of automated metrics (e.g., style classifiers) and LLM-as-judge rewards. Evaluate whether models learn to iteratively improve responses based on nuanced feedback versus fixating on spurious correlations.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** To what extent do denser, turn-level rewards (vs. sparse, conversation-level rewards) improve the sample efficiency and robustness of SML?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The Limitations note: "Exploring denser, turn-level rewards could also improve sample efficiency." The paper exclusively uses sparse rewards, acknowledging this as a constraint.
- **Why unresolved:** Turn-level rewards could provide more frequent learning signals, mitigating credit assignment issues and guiding exploration more precisely. However, they may also incentivize myopic behavior or gaming intermediate metrics.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Run controlled experiments comparing SML with sparse vs. turn-level (e.g., partial correctness, progress metrics) rewards on the same domains. Measure sample complexity (episodes to convergence), final performance, and qualitative analysis of learned conversational strategies.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What mechanisms underlie the observed cross-domain transfer of SML (e.g., math-trained models improving on coding)? Is it due to abstract "learning-from-feedback" skills, shared reasoning patterns, or something else?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] The paper demonstrates transfer (Section 6.2) but does not analyze *why* it occurs. It speculates that "the ability to learn from language feedback is a general skill," but this remains an assumption.
- **Why unresolved:** Understanding the transfer mechanism is key to scaling SML efficiently. If transfer relies on superficial similarities, it may not hold for disparate domains (e.g., math to creative dialogue).
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Conduct probing analyses: (1) Train SML on diverse source domains and test transfer to a novel target domain, controlling for overlap in reasoning patterns. (2) Use representational similarity analysis (RSA) on model activations during feedback processing across domains. (3) Ablate components of SML (e.g., teacher's private knowledge format) to see which are critical for transfer.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Does the Q-priming stage inadvertently make models overly cautious or inefficient in fully-specified problems where clarification is unnecessary?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Q-priming drastically increases question-asking (5x) on ambiguous tasks (Figure 6). However, the paper only evaluates behavior on underspecified "sharded" problems. It does not assess whether this bias persists when problems are fully specified, potentially harming efficiency.
- **Why unresolved:** Real-world interactions often mix ambiguous and clear tasks. A model that habitually asks clarifying questions even when sufficient information is present could frustrate users and waste tokens.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Evaluate SML models (with/without Q-priming) on a mixed dataset of fully-specified and underspecified problems. Measure: (1) question-asking rates on fully-specified tasks; (2) task completion time/token efficiency; (3) user satisfaction ratings in human evaluations.

### Open Question 5
- **Question:** How does SML perform when the teacher's private knowledge or feedback style is non-stationary (e.g., user intent evolves, teacher makes mistakes), and can the student adapt in-context?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The Limitations state: "our experiments assume a static underlying task goal; applying SML to dynamic settings where user intent, knowledge, or preference evolves during conversation remains an important direction." The paper's POMDP formulation allows dynamic \(k_t\), but empirical evaluation uses static ground truth or verifier outputs.
- **Why unresolved:** Real conversational partners change their goals, provide inconsistent feedback, or reveal information selectively. SML's current training assumes a benevolent, consistent teacher with fixed private knowledge.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Create dynamic evaluation benchmarks where: (1) user goals shift mid-conversation; (2) the teacher's private knowledge is updated (e.g., corrected solution); (3) the teacher gives contradictory feedback. Test if SML-trained students detect and adapt to these changes versus base models.

## Limitations
- **Unspecified teacher policy details:** The paper does not provide exact prompts, sampling parameters, or phrasing strategies for the teacher's feedback generation, which hinders exact replication of the training environment.
- **Missing GRPO hyperparameters:** Beyond group size (g=8) and discount (γ=0.7), key hyperparameters (learning rate, PPO clip range, number of steps) are not reported, making it unclear how sensitive online RL results are to these settings.
- **Reliance on LLM-as-judge for behavior metrics:** Behavioral analysis depends on a Gemini-2.5-Pro classifier without human validation, raising questions about the reliability of measured effects like question-asking rates.

## Confidence
*Online RL superiority over SFT: High* (strong internal evidence from multiple experiments, though hyperparameter sensitivity untested).  
*Cross-domain transfer of feedback skill: Medium* (demonstrated for two domains; broader generalizability uncertain).  
*Q-priming boost to question-asking: High* (large effect size reported, though synthetic question quality may limit realism).

## Next Checks
1. **Teacher policy replication:** Specify exact teacher prompts and generation settings; run sensitivity analysis on temperature/prompt variations to assess impact on student learning curves.  
2. **GRPO hyperparameter robustness:** Perform systematic sweep over group size, learning rate, and KL penalty; verify online RL maintains advantage over SFT across a reasonable range.  
3. **Human-annotated behavioral validation:** Collect human labels for turn types on a subset of dialogues; measure agreement with Gemini judge; adjust metrics if needed; re-evaluate Q-priming effect.
