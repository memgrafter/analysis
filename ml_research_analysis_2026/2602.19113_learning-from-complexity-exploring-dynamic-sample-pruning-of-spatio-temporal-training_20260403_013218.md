# Learning from Complexity: Exploring Dynamic Sample Pruning of Spatio-Temporal Training

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.19113
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19113
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Up to 2× per‑epoch speedup and 4–6× total training time reduction with ≤0.5% MAPE degradation; occasional accuracy improvements at extreme pruning.

## Executive Summary
Training spatio‑temporal forecasting models on large datasets is computationally intensive due to redundant samples. ST‑Prune introduces a dynamic sample pruning framework that iteratively selects a subset of training samples based on a complexity‑informed scoring metric, while applying stationarity‑aware gradient rescaling to correct distribution shifts. This approach achieves significant reductions in training time with minimal impact on accuracy and consistently outperforms numerous static and dynamic baselines across diverse datasets and architectures.

## Method Summary
ST‑Prune periodically reevaluates the training set every K=10 epochs (until epoch 90). It scores each sample with H_i = μ_i + λ(σ_space,i + σ_time,i), where μ_i is the mean absolute error on the sample, σ_space,i and σ_time,i capture spatial and temporal heterogeneity, and λ=0.5. Samples with H_i above the mean (S_inf) are always kept; the remainder (S_red) are sampled to meet the target retention rate r. For samples in S_inf, a weight w_i = (1/(1−r))·(δ̄/(δ_i+ε))^α (α=1, ε=1e−8) rescales gradients, where δ_i is the sample’s temporal variance. After epoch 90, pruning stops and full data is used. Training proceeds on the retained subset with weighted MAE loss.

## Key Results
- **Speedup:** 2× faster per‑epoch; 4–6× less total training time at 1% data retention.
- **Accuracy:** ≤0.5% MAPE degradation at typical retention rates (10–70%); +0.91% MAPE improvement on UrbanEV at 1%.
- **Robustness:** Outperforms 15+ baselines across models, optimizers, and tasks.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
ST‑Prune tackles two core issues: (1) the averaging masking effect—using only error (μ_i) overlooks samples with low error but high spatial‑temporal heterogeneity that are critical for learning complex patterns—by adding heterogeneity terms to the scoring metric; (2) distribution shift—pruning alters the stationarity of the training set—by reweighting gradients of retained samples inversely to their temporal variance, thereby preserving the original distribution’s stationary properties. The dynamic reassessment every K epochs adapts to the model’s evolving notion of sample usefulness, while annealing to full data after epoch 90 ensures fine‑tuning on all available information.

## Foundational Learning
1. **Dynamic Sample Pruning:** Adaptively discarding less informative samples during training. *Why needed:* Static pruning cannot respond to model progress; dynamic updates align pruning with current model knowledge. *Quick check:* Track overlap between retained sets across epochs; it should change as model learns.*
2. **Spatio‑Temporal Heterogeneity:** Variation across spatial nodes and time steps within a sample. *Why needed:* High heterogeneity indicates locally complex patterns that might be missed by global error metrics. *Quick check:* Compute per‑sample spatial/temporal standard deviations; high values often correlate with informative samples.*
3. **Stationarity‑Aware Gradient Rescaling:** Adjusting sample weights based on temporal variance to maintain distribution stationarity. *Why needed:* Pruning can remove samples that contribute to the temporal distribution’s stability, causing shift. *Quick check:* Compare temporal variance distributions of pruned vs. retained sets; reweighting should counter skew.*
4. **Complexity‑Informed Scoring:** Composite metric combining error and heterogeneity. *Why needed:* Pure error may undervalue samples that are “easy” globally but contain crucial local variations. *Quick check:* Correlate scores with downstream performance; high‑scoring samples should be disproportionately valuable.*
5. **Annealing Schedule:** Stopping pruning after a fixed epoch and using full data thereafter. *Why needed:* Early pruning accelerates learning; later stages benefit from all data for refinement. *Quick check:* Ablate annealing; removing it typically harms final accuracy.*
6. **Selection Frequency (K):** Interval between pruning updates. *Why needed:* Balances adaptation speed against scoring overhead. *Quick check:* Vary K; lower K increases adaptability but may increase compute cost per epoch.*

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map:**
Full Data Loader → Scoring Module (compute H_i) → Sample Selection (S_inf, S_red) → Retained Set R with Weights → Training Loop (weighted loss) → Model → (repeat every K epochs) → Annealing (switch to full data after epoch 90)

**Critical Path:** The iterative loop: (1) forward pass on full training set to compute per‑sample errors, (2) calculate H_i and select R, (3) train for K epochs on R with weighted loss, (4) repeat until epoch 90, then train on full data.

**Design Tradeoffs:**
- **K (selection frequency):** Lower K adapts faster but increases scoring overhead; higher K reduces overhead but may use outdated scores. Paper uses K=10.
- **r (retention rate):** Lower r gives higher speedup but increases risk of accuracy loss; ST‑Prune aims to maintain accuracy even at very low r.
- **α (reweighting exponent):** α=1 used; higher α amplifies stationarity correction but may cause gradient instability.
- **λ (heterogeneity weight):** λ=0.5 balances error and heterogeneity; optimal value may vary by dataset.

**Failure Signatures:**
- **Excessive scoring overhead:** If time spent in full forward passes dominates training, speedup diminishes. Monitor time per epoch breakdown.
- **Gradient explosion:** Very low r leads to large weights (1/(1−r)); watch for NaN loss or exploding gradients. Gradient clipping may be needed.
- **Accuracy drop:** If |S_inf| > r·N and fallback to top‑r discards many high‑complexity samples, performance may suffer. Monitor fraction of S_inf retained.
- **Non‑stationary retained set:** If temporal variance distribution of R is heavily skewed, reweighting may be insufficient. Compare δ_i distributions before/after pruning.

**First Three Experiments:**
1. **Main benchmark replication:** Evaluate ST‑Prune against 15+ baselines on PEMS08, UrbanEV, and LargeST at r=10,30,50,70% using MAE, RMSE, MAPE. Report speedup and accuracy.
2. **Ablation study:** Remove stationarity‑aware reweighting and complexity scoring individually. Measure impact on accuracy and speedup to quantify each component’s contribution.
3. **Hyperparameter sensitivity:** Vary λ ∈ {0.1,0.5,1.0}, α ∈ {0.5,1,2}, and K ∈ {5,10,20}. Record accuracy and computational overhead to assess robustness.

## Limitations
- **Edge‑case handling unspecified:** The paper assumes |S_inf| ≤ r·N but does not detail fallback when this fails, risking implementation variability.
- **Hyperparameter defaults possibly dataset‑specific:** Fixed λ, α, ε, K may require tuning for new domains; the paper lacks a systematic tuning protocol.
- **Scoring overhead not fully accounted:** Full‑dataset forward passes for scoring can erode speedups on large datasets if not optimized.

## Confidence
- **Speedup claims (2× per‑epoch, 4–6× total training time reduction):** *Medium* – dependent on implementation details and scoring overhead management.
- **Accuracy preservation (≤0.5% MAPE degradation, occasional improvement):** *Medium* – sensitive to reweighting hyperparameters and pruning schedule.
- **Broad superiority over 15+ baselines:** *Low* – baseline configurations and evaluation conditions may differ; exact replication is challenging.
