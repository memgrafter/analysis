# Sliced-Wasserstein Distribution Alignment Loss Improves the Ultra-Low-Bit Quantization of Large Language Models

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.07878
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.07878
- **Reference count:** 6
- **Primary result:** A Sliced-Wasserstein loss added to post-training quantization frameworks consistently recovers significant accuracy loss (e.g., 4.12–20.37% on LLaMA-2-7B) and reduces perplexity in ultra-low-bit (below 4-bit) regimes with minimal computational overhead.

## Executive Summary
Ultra-low-bit post-training quantization (PTQ) of large language models often degrades performance due to distorted activation distributions. This paper introduces a Sliced-Wasserstein (SW) loss that aligns the full output distributions of full-precision and quantized models via random linear projections, complementing the standard mean-squared error loss. Integrated into two state-of-the-art PTQ frameworks (OmniQuant and TesseraQ), the SW loss yields consistent improvements in perplexity and zero-shot accuracy across multiple models (LLaMA-2-7B/13B, OPT-6.7B) and bit-widths, with less than 0.1 hour added calibration time. The method effectively pushes quantization limits by preserving higher-order statistical structure in activations.

## Method Summary
The method augments existing PTQ frameworks with a retraining component by adding a Sliced-Wasserstein loss term during layer-wise block calibration. For each transformer block, full-precision and quantized outputs are reshaped into 2D tensors. The SW loss computes the average 1D Wasserstein-1 distance across multiple random projections: random unit vectors project the outputs onto 1D lines, sorted values yield the distance per projection. This differentiable loss is combined with mean-squared error (e.g., \(L_{\text{block}} = (1 - sw_w) \cdot L_{\text{MSE}} + sw_w \cdot L_{\text{SW}}\)) to optimize quantization parameters (e.g., clipping thresholds) while freezing preceding blocks. Hyperparameters include the number of projections \(n_{\text{proj}}\) and SW weight \(sw_w\), tuned per configuration.

## Key Results
- Recovers 4.12–20.37% of OmniQuant's lost accuracy on LLaMA-2-7B, 0.93–7.65% on OPT-6.7B, and 2.26–6.20% on LLaMA-2-13B across ultra-low-bit settings.
- For TesseraQ, reduces accuracy degradation by 3.63–7.63%.
- Lowers perplexity on WikiText-2/C4 for multiple bit-widths with negligible calibration overhead (<0.1 hour for 7B/13B models).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The Sliced-Wasserstein loss improves ultra-low-bit quantization by addressing limitations of point-wise losses like MSE. First, it aligns the *full empirical distributions* of full-precision and quantized block outputs under random linear projections, capturing higher-order statistics (e.g., skewness, multimodality) that MSE misses, which is critical as quantization artifacts distort these in aggressive bit-widths. Evidence includes the paper's ablation showing consistent gains and the design of projections to approximate Wasserstein distance efficiently. Second, within block-wise calibration, the SW term regularizes optimization of quantization parameters (e.g., LWC, LET) toward solutions preserving activation geometry, avoiding local minima that match only mean/variance. This is supported by improved metrics in Tables 1–3. Third, the computational design—parallelizable projections and sorting—keeps overhead low, as shown in Table 8, making it practical for PTQ pipelines.

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Base PTQ Framework (e.g., OmniQuant) manages layer-wise optimization → Loss Combiner modifies block loss to include SW term → SW Loss Module computes \(L_{\text{SW}}\) from full-precision and quantized block outputs; Hyperparameter Manager sets \(n_{\text{proj}}\) and \(sw_w\).
- **Critical path:** For each transformer block \(i\) from first to last: (a) freeze blocks 1 to \(i-1\); (b) forward pass through block \(i\) with current quantizers to get \(Y_q\); (c) retrieve cached full-precision \(Y_fp\); (d) compute \(L_{\text{block}} = (1-sw_w) \cdot \text{MSE} + sw_w \cdot \text{SW}\); (e) backpropagate to update block \(i\)'s quantizer parameters; (f) proceed to next block.
- **Design tradeoffs:** Balance \(sw_w\) (distribution alignment strength) vs. \(n_{\text{proj}}\) (approximation accuracy)—higher values increase compute but may stabilize gradients. Integration is at block output only; altering other stages (e.g., weights) is unexplored. Generality across PTQ frameworks is high, but gains vary (e.g., smaller for TesseraQ).
- **Failure signatures:** Gradient explosion or NaN during SW-augmented training (reduce \(sw_w\) or increase \(n_{\text{proj}}\)); no improvement if \(sw_w\) is poorly tuned or base method already preserves distributions well; excessive calibration time with very high \(n_{\text{proj}}\).
- **First 3 experiments:** 1. Replicate baseline PTQ (OmniQuant/TesseraQ) on a single model/config to verify implementation. 2. Sweep \(n_{\text{proj}}\) (e.g., 64, 128, 256) and \(sw_w\) (e.g., 0.05, 0.1, 0.2) on a held-out calibration subset to find optimal region. 3. Ablate against baseline and alternative losses (e.g., KL) to confirm SW's consistent gain and stability.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- Can an adaptive mechanism for balancing MSE and SW loss during training yield better performance than fixed weighting?
- Is the SW loss effective for ultra-low-bit quantization of extreme formats like binary (1-bit) or ternary (2-bit) weights?
- Does the SW loss generalize to quantization-aware training (QAT) frameworks, not just PTQ?
- Why does KL divergence calibration yield inconsistent improvements in ultra-low-bit quantization, while SW shows more consistent gains?

## Limitations
- Hyperparameters (\(n_{\text{proj}}\), \(sw_w\)) are not fully tuned for all model-bit pairs; only selective values reported.
- Calibration dataset composition and size (e.g., "mixed" data) are unspecified, affecting reproducibility.
- Gains for TesseraQ are modest; generality to other PTQ frameworks is unclear.
- Instability noted for OPT-6.7B at W4A4 suggests sensitivity in aggressive settings.
- Experiments limited to models up to 13B; larger architectures (e.g., 70B) untested.

## Confidence
- **Effectiveness:** High – robust improvements across perplexity and multiple zero-shot tasks.
- **Mechanism:** Medium – distribution alignment hypothesis is well-motivated but not directly verified via higher-order statistics.
- **Efficiency:** High – negligible added time and memory, empirically validated.
