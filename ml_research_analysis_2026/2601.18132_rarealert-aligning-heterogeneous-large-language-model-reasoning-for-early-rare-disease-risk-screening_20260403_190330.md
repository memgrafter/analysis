# RareAlert: Aligning heterogeneous large language model reasoning for early rare disease risk screening

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.18132
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.18132
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Achieved an AUC of 0.917 on an independent test set for early rare disease risk screening, outperforming all individual LLMs and ML ensembles.

## Executive Summary
Rare diseases are frequently missed or diagnosed late due to inadequate initial screening with limited clinical data. This paper introduces RareAlert, a system that addresses this by aligning the diverse reasoning processes of ten large language models (LLMs) through a machine learning calibration layer, then distilling this aligned knowledge into a single, compact, and locally deployable model. On a retrospective test set, RareAlert demonstrated superior diagnostic accuracy (AUC 0.917) with high specificity (0.935) and positive predictive value (0.818), showing promise for accurate, privacy-preserving universal screening at the first primary care visit.

## Method Summary
RareAlert employs a three-stage pipeline. First, ten diverse LLMs independently process a primary-visit clinical narrative to generate a structured output including a risk score, key weighted features, and an explanation. Second, a machine learning model (CatBoost) is trained to integrate these ten heterogeneous risk signals, learning to weight each model's contribution case-by-case via SHAP analysis to produce a calibrated reference probability. Third, this calibrated signal, combined with the most influential LLM's reasoning path for each specific case (selected by SHAP), is used as the training target to distill the ensemble's knowledge into a single, smaller open-source model (Qwen3-4B) via supervised fine-tuning.

## Key Results
- RareAlert (distilled Qwen3-4B) achieved a test set AUC of **0.917**.
- It attained a **specificity of 0.935** and a **positive predictive value (PPV) of 0.818** at the Youden-optimal threshold.
- It outperformed the best multi-LLM machine learning ensemble (AUC 0.912) and all ten individual foundation models evaluated, including GPT-5 (AUC 0.905).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Leveraging Heterogeneous Medical Reasoning
Integrating outputs from ten diverse LLMs captures a broader set of clinical hypotheses and feature weights than any single model. The low pairwise agreement rates (<20%) confirm substantial reasoning diversity. The core assumption is that complementary, case-specific reasoning pathways from different models, when aligned, contain a superior signal for risk prediction under high uncertainty.
- **Break condition:** If LLM diversity is systematically correlated with shared errors or is purely random noise, alignment would not improve upon the best single model.

### Mechanism 2: Machine Learning for Signal Calibration and Integration
A CatBoost model effectively learns to weight and combine the ten LLM risk scores, producing a stable, calibrated reference probability. SHAP analysis revealed case-specific dominance (e.g., GPT-4.1 dominant in 87.15% of cases), indicating the ML model dynamically trusts different models for different presentations.
- **Break condition:** If LLM signals are highly collinear or the label noise is too high, the alignment model cannot learn an integrable pattern superior to its inputs.

### Mechanism 3: Selective Knowledge Distillation
The distilled Qwen3-4B model is fine-tuned on pairs of (input text, target output), where the target is the calibrated risk score plus the reasoning path from only the top-1 most important LLM for that case (per SHAP). Training with a single, case-specific reasoning path (N=1) yielded the best test performance, matching the teacher ensemble's AUC.
- **Break condition:** If the alignment model's reference scores are miscalibrated or the composite training target is too complex for the small student, distillation would cause a significant performance drop.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: Knowledge Distillation**
  - **Why needed:** To compress the capabilities of a large, expensive multi-LLM ensemble into a single, efficient, locally deployable model without catastrophic performance loss, enabling scalable clinical deployment.
  - **Quick check:** Why might distilling from a *calibrated ensemble's output* be more effective than distilling from a single "best" LLM's output?

- **Concept: SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)**
  - **Why needed:** To provide model-agnostic, case-specific attribution of which LLM's reasoning was most influential in the alignment model's decision, enabling selective and noise-reduced distillation.
  - **Quick check:** How does SHAP's per-case feature attribution directly inform the "Top N reasoning paths" selection during RareAlert training?

- **Concept: Clinical Task Formulation as Universal Screening**
  - **Why needed:** The entire system is built on framing the problem as a binary "high-risk vs. low-risk" triage decision using *only* primary-visit data. This framing dictates the input scope, output (risk score), and the priority on high specificity for a screening tool.
  - **Quick check:** Why is a high specificity (0.935) potentially more critical for a universal screening tool at the primary visit than a high sensitivity?

## Architecture Onboarding
Data Foundation -> Multi-LLM Inference Engine -> Alignment Module -> Distillation Trainer -> Deployable Model (RareAlert) -> Evaluation Harness

**Critical Path:** Data Curation -> Multi-LLM Scoring -> ML Alignment & SHAP -> Training Data Construction with Case-Specific Reasoning Selection -> Distillation Fine-Tuning -> Independent Test Evaluation.

**Design Tradeoffs:**
- **Diversity vs. Cost/Latency:** Using ten LLMs is computationally expensive for the teacher ensemble. This high development cost is traded for a cheap, single-model student.
- **Calibration Complexity vs. Simplicity:** The 3-stage pipeline is complex versus direct fine-tuning. The complexity buys superior performance by leveraging diverse, unlabeled reasoning.
- **Reasoning Selection Strategy:** Using the single top-N (N=1) reasoning path for distillation versus all ten. Evidence favors N=1 for simplicity and reduced conflicting signal.

**Failure Signatures:**
1. High inter-LLM agreement (pairwise <25% expected) suggests failed diversity premise.
2. ML alignment model fails to beat the best single LLM on the dev set, indicating non-integrable signals.
3. Distilled model performance drops significantly vs. teacher ensemble (AUC gap >0.005).
4. Poor SHAP dominance; no single LLM has high "dominant-feature frequency," making case-specific selection noisy.

**First 3 Experiments:**
1. **Validate Reasoning Diversity:** On a 10k subset, quantify average distinct reasoning chains per case and pairwise co-occurrence rates. Expect ~6 chains/case, co-occurrence <25%.
2. **Benchmark ML Alignment:** Train CatBoost on dev set with all 10 LLM scores. Compare its AUC to the best single LLM's AUC on the same dev folds. Expect alignment AUC >= best LLM AUC.
3. **Test Distillation Target Efficacy:** Train three Qwen3-4B models on the dev set: (a) target = only ensemble score, (b) target = score + all 10 LLM reasoning, (c) target = score + top-1 LLM reasoning per case (via SHAP). Expect (c) matches or beats (a) and (b) on test set.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1.  **Question:** Does implementing RareAlert in a prospective, real-world clinical setting improve time-to-diagnosis and clinical outcomes for patients with rare diseases compared to standard care?
    - **Basis:** [explicit] "our evaluation is limited to retrospective benchmarking... Prospective studies will be required to assess how rare disease risk alerts influence clinician behaviour, diagnostic timelines, and resource utilisation."
    - **Why unresolved:** No prospective clinical trial data; only retrospective performance is shown.

2.  **Question:** Under what specific clinical or data conditions does a multi-agent discussion framework fail to improve performance over simple aggregation for early-stage risk screening tasks?
    - **Basis:** [explicit] "applying multi-agent debate in this study did not improve rare disease risk assessment performance. We speculate that early-stage risk screening... is underrepresented in LLM training, such that increasing inference-time deliberation offers limited benefit."
    - **Why unresolved:** The hypothesis is speculative; no systematic experiments varying task difficulty, information completeness, or agent diversity to find the breaking point.

3.  **Question:** How does the performance and generalizability of RareAlert vary across different demographic subgroups and healthcare systems with varying documentation practices?
    - **Basis:** [inferred] Dataset is retrospective, English-language from specific sources. Limitation notes: "Differences in documentation practices, healthcare systems, and patient populations may affect model performance when applied in other settings."
    - **Why unresolved:** No subgroup performance analysis or external validation across diverse populations or health systems presented.

4.  **Question:** Can the distilled RareAlert model's reasoning supervision be effectively leveraged to generate specific, actionable next-step diagnostic recommendations?
    - **Basis:** [explicit] "the present work formulates rare disease identification as a binary risk screening task, without attempting to localise specific disease categories or directly recommend diagnostic tests... translating elevated risk signals into actionable clinical pathways remains an important challenge."
    - **Why unresolved:** System outputs a risk score and general explanations but stops short of an integrated "next-step" recommendation system.

5.  **Question:** What is the fundamental reason for the dominant influence of GPT-4.1 in the SHAP-based alignment model?
    - **Basis:** [inferred] SHAP shows GPT-4.1 was dominant in 87.15% of cases and consistently increased predicted risk, yet its standalone AUC (0.896) was lower than the final ensemble (0.917). The reason for this outsized weight in the *alignment model* is unexplained.
    - **Why unresolved:** Unclear if dominance reflects superior medical reasoning, better score calibration matching the target, or an artifact of the alignment process.

## Limitations
- **Reproduction Blockers:** Exact CatBoost hyperparameters and the final prompt templates used for all ten LLMs are undisclosed, preventing exact replication.
- **External Validity:** Performance relies on the RareBench dataset construction; generalizability to other populations, languages, or EHR formats is unknown and requires external validation.
- **Distillation Signal Integrity:** The assumption that the top-N (N=1) LLM reasoning path is the *correct* path for distillation, not just the most influential for the ML model, lacks qualitative validation.

## Next Checks
1.  **Replicate SHAP Dominance Analysis:** On a held-out 5k-case subset, recompute SHAP values for the trained CatBoost model. Quantify the distribution of "dominant" LLMs. Validate that a meaningful proportion of cases have a clear single dominator (e.g., >70%).
2.  **Prompt Consistency Audit:** For a random sample of 100 cases, run all 10 LLMs with the *exact same* prompt copy-paste. Measure output format compliance (integer score, 5 features) and inter-LLM score correlation. Low compliance or high correlation would undermine the diversity premise.
3.  **Calibration Stress Test:** Evaluate the final RareAlert model's calibration curve (predicted vs. actual risk) on a sub-group with significantly different disease prevalence from the test set (e.g., pediatric if available). A major shift would indicate brittle calibration.
