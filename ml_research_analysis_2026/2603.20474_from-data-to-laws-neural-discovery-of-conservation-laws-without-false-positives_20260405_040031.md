# From Data to Laws: Neural Discovery of Conservation Laws Without False Positives
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.20474
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20474
- Reference count: 22
- Primary result: Achieved perfect F1 score (1.0) with zero false discoveries on all benchmark systems with true invariants and correctly identified systems without invariants.

## Executive Summary
Discovering symbolic conservation laws from data is a fundamental task in scientific machine learning, but existing neural methods often produce spurious invariants or fail on non‑polynomial cases. This paper introduces NGCG, a decoupled neural‑symbolic pipeline that first learns a frozen dynamics model, then employs a multi‑restart variance minimizer to obtain a near‑constant latent representation, followed by system‑specific symbolic extraction and rigorous filtering. On a diverse benchmark of nine dynamical systems (both ODEs and PDEs), NGCG attains flawless performance (DR=1.0, FDR=0.0) on every system that possesses true invariants, while correctly returning “no law” for systems without. It is the only approach to succeed on the notoriously difficult Lotka–Volterra system and achieves constancy values two to three orders of magnitude smaller than the best baseline. The method is robust to moderate noise, sample‑efficient (50–100 trajectories), and runs in under one minute per system, offering a reliable and practical tool for conservation law discovery.

## Method Summary
NGCG proceeds in four stages. **Stage 1** trains a neural ODE model \(f_\theta\) on one‑step prediction losses and freezes it. **Stage 2** independently trains a scalar network \(\phi_\psi\) with the normalized variance loss \(L_\phi = \frac{\text{mean intra‑trajectory variance}}{\text{inter‑trajectory variance} + \epsilon} + \lambda\|\psi\|^2\). To avoid poor local minima, \(R=10\) random restarts are performed and the best checkpoint (lowest validation constancy) is selected. **Stage 3** generates symbolic candidate expressions: for polynomial invariants, a convex Lasso on monomials (degree ≤ 4) using the eigenvector of the mean trajectory covariance; for Lotka–Volterra, a log‑basis library; explicit PDE candidates (spatial mean) are added where applicable; and a PySR fallback runs genetic programming on \((\mathbf{x}, \phi(\mathbf{x}))\) pairs. **Stage 4** applies a strict constancy gate (test relative standard deviation < 0.01) and a diversity filter requiring \(\rho = \frac{\operatorname{std}(\operatorname{mean}_i C_i)}{\operatorname{mean}_i \operatorname{std}_t C_t} > 10\) to reject spurious near‑constants. Accepted expressions are output as conservation laws; otherwise “no law” is returned. The pipeline needs only 50–100 trajectories, tolerates noise \(\sigma=0.1\), and finishes in under a minute per system.

## Key Results
- Perfect precision and recall: DR=1.0, FDR=0.0, F1=1.0 on all four systems with true invariants; correctly outputs “no law” on all five without invariants.
- Exceptional accuracy: constancy values 2–3 orders of magnitude lower than the best baseline.
- Robustness and efficiency: robust to noise (\(\sigma=0.1\)), sample‑efficient (50–100 trajectories), runs < 1 min/system.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Decoupling dynamics and invariant search
- **Claim:** Decoupling prevents interference and catastrophic forgetting.
- **Mechanism:** The dynamics model \(f_\theta\) is trained first on one‑step prediction and then frozen. A separate network \(\phi_\psi\) is subsequently trained to minimize the variance loss, using the frozen dynamics only to generate trajectories for evaluation. This separation ensures the invariant search is not biased by the dynamics model’s training objective or errors.
- **Core assumption:** An accurate dynamics model can be learned without requiring it to encode invariants; the invariant lies in the state space and can be approximated independently.
- **Evidence:** Abstract and Sec. 4.1 explicitly state the decoupling; novelty compared to baselines (HNN, IRAS) which intertwine the two objectives.

### Mechanism 2: Multi‑restart variance minimization
- **Claim:** Multiple independent restarts reliably find a near‑constant latent representation, avoiding poor local minima.
- **Mechanism:** \(\phi_\psi\) is trained \(R=10\) times from different initializations, each minimizing \(L_\phi\). The restart with the lowest validation constancy is chosen. The loss ratio prevents trivial constant solutions; restarts hedge against the non‑convex optimization landscape.
- **Core assumption:** At least one initialization leads to a \(\phi\) that approximates the true invariant’s level sets; multiple trials increase the chance of success.
- **Evidence:** Abstract mentions multi‑restart; Sec. 4.2 and the ablation for Lotka–Volterra (single restart fails, \(R\ge3\) succeeds) confirm its necessity.

### Mechanism 3: System‑specific symbolic extraction and filtering
- **Claim:** Combining tailored symbolic regression with strict filtering eliminates false positives while capturing diverse invariant forms.
- **Mechanism:** Candidates are generated via one of three routes: polynomial Lasso (eigenvector of mean monomial covariance), log‑basis Lasso (for Lotka–Volterra), explicit PDE candidates, or PySR fallback. Every candidate is subjected to (i) a constancy gate (test constancy < 0.01) and (ii) a diversity filter that requires \(\rho>10\), i.e., substantial variation of candidate values across different trajectories.
- **Core assumption:** True invariants are nearly constant within each trajectory and varying across trajectories; spurious near‑constants have low inter‑trajectory variation.
- **Evidence:** Abstract and Sec. 4.3–4.4 detail the filters. Table 2 shows FDR=0 on no‑law systems, while baselines produce false positives. The diversity filter is crucial for rejecting near‑constant expressions on the chaotic Lorenz system.

## Foundational Learning
#### Normalized variance minimization for neural invariants
- **Why needed here:** Provides the learning signal for \(\phi_\psi\) to output values that are nearly constant along each trajectory while remaining sensitive to initial conditions. The loss \(L_\phi = \frac{\text{mean intra‑trajectory variance}}{\text{inter‑trajectory variance} + \epsilon}\) encourages this pattern.
- **Quick check:** Why does dividing by inter‑trajectory variance help avoid the trivial constant solution? What would happen if only intra‑variance were minimized?  
  (Answer: Minimizing intra‑variance alone would allow a globally constant function, which is trivial and often uninformative. The ratio forces the network to produce differences across trajectories, thus biasing towards non‑trivial invariants that take distinct values on different trajectories. The diversity filter further rejects any remaining global constants.)

#### Convex invariant extraction via eigendecomposition of mean trajectory covariance
- **Why needed here:** For polynomial invariants, the “polynomial Lasso” reduces to a convex problem: find \(\mathbf{w}\) minimizing \(\mathbf{w}^\top C_{\text{poly}} \mathbf{w}\), where \(C_{\text{poly}}\) is the average of the monomial feature covariance across trajectories. The eigenvector of \(C_{\text{poly}}\) with smallest eigenvalue yields the linear combination of monomials with minimal total variance, i.e., the most conserved polynomial.
- **Quick check:** How does the eigenvector of \(C_{\text{poly}}\) correspond to the most conserved linear combination of monomials? What property of \(C_{\text{poly}}\) makes this a convex optimization?  
  (Answer: The objective \(\mathbf{w}^\top C_{\text{poly}} \mathbf{w}\) is a quadratic form with positive‑semidefinite \(C_{\text{poly}}\), making it convex. The smallest eigenvector solves the minimization because it points in the direction of minimal quadratic growth, i.e., minimal variance of the combination.)

#### Diversity filter (ρ statistic) for false positive elimination
- **Why needed here:** On chaotic systems like Lorenz, symbolic regression can accidentally produce expressions with extremely low constancy but which are actually near‑constant over the sampled trajectories. The diversity filter \(\rho = \frac{\operatorname{std}(\operatorname{mean}_i C_i)}{\operatorname{mean}_i \operatorname{std}_t C_t}\) quantifies how much the candidate’s mean value varies across trajectories relative to within‑trajectory fluctuations. True invariants have high \(\rho\); spurious near‑constants have low \(\rho\).
- **Quick check:** Why would a spurious near‑constant have a low \(\rho\) value? How does the threshold \(\rho>10\) separate true from false?  
  (Answer: A spurious near‑constant is nearly constant both within each trajectory and across trajectories; thus the inter‑trajectory variation (numerator) is tiny while the average within‑trajectory variation (denominator) is not, yielding \(\rho\approx0\). A true invariant has near‑zero within‑trajectory variance but varying means across trajectories, so \(\rho\) becomes large. The threshold 10 was chosen empirically to reject all spurious cases on the benchmark.)

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:** Trajectories -> Dynamics model \(f_\theta\) -> Frozen -> Invariant network \(\phi_\psi\) -> Symbolic extraction (polynomial/log Lasso or PySR) -> Filters (constancy gate, diversity filter) -> Output (conservation law or “no law”).
- **Critical path:** The core pipeline flows \(\phi_\psi\) training → symbolic extraction → filtering. The quality of \(\phi_\psi\) directly determines whether symbolic candidates can approximate the true invariant; the filters then ensure only globally valid expressions are output.
- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - *Restarts vs runtime:* Using \(R=10\) restarts improves robustness (especially for Lotka–Volterra) but roughly doubles training time; ablations show \(R=3\) often suffices.
  - *Specialized vs generic extraction:* Polynomial and log‑basis Lasso are fast, convex, and guarantee a candidate, but are limited to specific forms. PySR is generic but may need more data and compute. Combining both increases coverage at the cost of complexity.
  - *Filter strictness:* \(\tau=0.01\) and \(\rho>10\) are conservative; they guarantee zero false positives but may reject borderline true invariants. If a few false negatives are acceptable, thresholds could be relaxed.
  - *PDE reduction:* Reducing spatiotemporal fields to three moments (mean, variance, skewness) makes the problem tractable but restricts discovered invariants to integral‑type quantities; local conservation laws are inaccessible.
- **Failure signatures:**
  1. All \(\phi_\psi\) restarts yield high validation constancy → the invariant network failed to find a near‑constant representation; check network capacity, loss hyperparameters, or whether the system truly has an invariant.
  2. Candidates pass constancy gate but fail diversity filter → spurious near‑constant; expected for chaotic or law‑less systems; if unexpected, increase trajectory diversity.
  3. No candidate passes either gate → true invariant may lie outside the monomial/PySR operator bases or the number of trajectories is insufficient.
  4. PySR cannot produce a low‑constancy expression even with a good \(\phi_\psi\) → enrich operator set or increase iterations/population size.
  5. Runtime significantly exceeds 1 min/system → likely due to too many \(\phi_\psi\) restarts or PySR HyperThreads; reduce \(R\), use early stopping, or limit PySR iterations.
- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Mass‑spring system:** Use polynomial Lasso and PySR to recover the quadratic energy. Expect constancy ≪ 0.01 and \(\rho > 10\), yielding DR=1 and FDR=0.
  2. **Lotka–Volterra system:** Test log‑basis Lasso and PySR for the logarithmic invariant. Vary the number of restarts; with R=1 we anticipate failure (F1=0), while \(R\ge3\) should succeed.
  3. **Lorenz system (no law):** Run full pipeline; expect all symbolic candidates to be rejected by the diversity filter, resulting in DR=0 and FDR=0. If any candidate is accepted, the diversity threshold may be too low or insufficient trajectory spread.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** Does NGCG maintain zero false positives on real‑world noisy trajectories with incomplete state measurements?
- **Basis:** The authors state that applying the method to “real‑world data, such as climate time series or mechanical measurements” is necessary to test its “practical utility.”
- **Why unresolved:** Current evaluation uses clean, full‑state synthetic data; real data often include noise, missing variables, and biased sampling that could break the constancy gate or diversity filter.
- **What evidence would resolve:** Systematic application to experimental datasets with independently verified conservation laws, reporting discovery and false discovery rates under varied noise and partial observability conditions.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** Can the diversity filter’s fixed threshold (\(\rho > 10\)) be replaced by a statistically principled, adaptive hypothesis test?
- **Basis:** The discussion acknowledges the threshold is empirical and suggests exploring “a more principled statistical test (e.g., based on the distribution of \(\rho\)).”
- **Why unresolved:** A universal fixed threshold may not be optimal for all systems; a significance test would provide controlled false positive rates and reduce arbitrary hyperparameter choices.
- **What evidence would resolve:** Deriving the null distribution of \(\rho\) under the null hypothesis of no invariant, constructing a test with known type‑I error, and validating that it uniformly rejects spurious candidates across a diverse benchmark.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What determines the sample complexity (trajectories needed) for a given dynamical system, and can it be predicted a priori?
- **Basis:** Results show varying requirements: Lotka–Volterra needs ≥ 100 trajectories while other systems succeed with ≥ 50, but the influencing factors are not analyzed.
- **Why unresolved:** Understanding sample complexity is crucial for data‑limited applications; currently it must be determined empirically for each new system.
- **What evidence would resolve:** A systematic study varying system properties (dimensionality, nonlinearity, Lyapunov exponents) and measuring the trajectory count needed for consistent discovery, followed by a predictive model or scaling law.

## Limitations
- Empirical thresholds (diversity filter \(\rho>10\)) and PySR hyperparameters are not fully justified and may not generalize to unseen systems.
- The pipeline depends on an accurate frozen dynamics model; if \(f_\theta\) is poorly trained or extrapolates incorrectly, invariant discovery can fail.
- PDE systems are reduced to three moments, which restricts discovery to integral‑type invariants and cannot capture local conservation laws.

## Confidence
- Decoupling dynamics from invariant search: **High**
- Multi‑restart variance minimization: **Medium**
- Symbolic extraction with filtering yields zero false positives: **High**
- Overall DR/FDR on the benchmark: **Medium**
- Robustness to noise and sample efficiency: **Medium**
