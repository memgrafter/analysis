# SinhaLegal: A Benchmark Corpus for Information Extraction and Analysis in Sinhala Legislative Texts

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.04854
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04854
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: Created SinhaLegal, a 1,206-document Sinhala legal corpus (~2M words) with post-OCR WER 23.44%, showing domain-specific lexical features and lower perplexity for legal Sinhala.

## Executive Summary
SinhaLegal addresses the critical lack of structured Sinhala legal text resources for NLP. The authors assemble a corpus of 1,206 documents (1,065 Acts, 141 Bills) totaling approximately 2 million words by extracting text from scanned PDFs using OCR (Google Document AI), applying thorough filtration, and manual post-processing by native Sinhala speakers. Evaluation demonstrates high OCR confidence (96.6% > 0.8) and significant error reduction (WER 23.44%, CER 24.06% post-processing). Lexical analysis reveals a Type-Token Ratio of 0.728 and 46.14% hapax legomena, indicating a specialized legal vocabulary distinct from general Sinhala. Topic modeling (LDA) surfaces coherent legal themes (e.g., courts, elections, pensions), and perplexity comparisons across multiple language models confirm that legal Sinhala is more predictable due to its formulaic nature. The corpus enables future work on information extraction, summarization, and other legal NLP tasks in a low-resource language.

## Method Summary
The authors constructed SinhaLegal from a public GitHub repository containing 2,865 PDFs of Sri Lankan Acts and Bills. They applied a multi-stage pipeline: (1) Data acquisition with year-based filtering and duplicate handling; (2) Text extraction via Google Document AI, splitting documents longer than 15 pages into chunks; (3) Filtration using EDA-driven rules, including year range limits (Acts 1981–2014, Bills 2010–2014), exclusion of Acts from 1992/1996/1997 due to double-sided printing issues, removal of documents with >50 pages, and rejection of multi-column or table-heavy layouts; (4) Manual post-processing by native Sinhala speakers to correct OCR errors, strip footers/seals, fix spacing, and delete out-of-context sentences; (5) Organization into type/year folders with per-document metadata in JSON format; and (6) Comprehensive evaluation using lexical statistics (TTR, hapax legomena), rule-based NER, LDA topic modeling, and perplexity sampling across several language models. The process prioritized quality for a low-resource language with complex script and legal orthography, accepting some scalability trade-offs.

## Key Results
- Created SinhaLegal: 1,206 Sinhala legal documents (~2M words) with post-OCR quality (WER 23.44%, CER 24.06%).
- Lexical metrics (TTR 0.728, 46.14% hapax legomena) and topic coherence confirm a specialized legal domain.
- Perplexity analyses show legal Sinhala is more predictable; Llama-3.1-8B and Falcon-7B achieve the lowest scores.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: High-quality OCR + manual post-processing
**Claim:** A structured pipeline of OCR extraction followed by manual, native-speaker post-processing can transform noisy scanned legal PDFs into a machine-readable corpus suitable for NLP.  
**Mechanism:** Google Document AI provides a first-pass text extraction with high confidence; manual intervention by native Sinhala speakers corrects character substitutions, removes non-content artifacts (footers, seals, repeated titles), fixes spacing, and deletes extraneous sentences.  
**Core assumption:** Native linguistic intuition is necessary to accurately reconstruct intended meaning from OCR artifacts in a low-resource language with complex script and legal orthography.  
**Evidence:** [abstract] mentions OCR and extensive post-processing; sections 3.3–3.5 detail the steps; Table 13 shows WER reduction from 26.87% to 23.44% and CER to 24.06% after cleaning.  
**Break condition:** Effectiveness degrades if source PDFs have extremely poor scan quality, multi-column layouts not filtered out, or manual post-processing is inconsistent; the authors excluded >50-page and multi-column documents to mitigate these risks.

### Mechanism 2: Domain-specific lexical and topical patterns
**Claim:** The corpus exhibits lexical and topical properties (high hapax legomena, repetitive functional terms, distinct topic clusters) that confirm its nature as a specialized legal domain resource, distinct from general Sinhala text.  
**Mechanism:** Legal drafting uses a core set of high-frequency function words for formulaic structures alongside a long tail of rare technical terms (unique law names, commissions). Topic modeling (LDA) surfaces recurring institutional themes.  
**Core assumption:** Statistical signatures (TTR, hapax frequency, topic coherence) are valid proxies for identifying a specialized legal corpus.  
**Evidence:** Lexical analysis reported TTR 0.728 and 46.14% hapax legomena; LDA yields coherent topics centered on “Act/Law” (පනත), institutions (සභාව, අධිකරණය), and themes (මුදල්, විශ්ාම); Jensen-Shannon Divergence 0.614 indicates distinct vocabulary from general Sinhala.  
**Break condition:** Low hapax legomenon percentage (<30%) or incoherent topics would suggest insufficient specialization or residual noise; high hapax may partly reflect unique names rather than legal terms.

### Mechanism 3: Legal language predictability
**Claim:** Lower perplexity scores on SinhaLegal vs. a general Sinhala corpus indicate that legal Sinhala text is more *predictable* due to its repetitive, formulaic nature, despite containing complex terminology.  
**Mechanism:** Legal texts rely heavily on conventionalized phrasing and structural templates (e.g., “this Act contains…”). This repetitive pattern reduces uncertainty for next-token prediction in language models compared to diverse general-domain texts.  
**Core assumption:** Perplexity is a meaningful metric for assessing a model’s adaptation to a domain; lower scores imply the model captures the domain’s distributional regularities.  
**Evidence:** All tested models (Llama-3.1-8B, Falcon-7B, Deepseek-1.3B, Mistral-7B, DistilGPT-2, Gemma-2B) show lower perplexity on SinhaLegal; Llama-3.1-8B and Falcon-7B achieve best scores (2.55, 2.61 vs. 3.05, 2.77 on general corpus) (Table 5).  
**Break condition:** Lower perplexity might reflect overfitting to surface-level repetition rather than deep legal reasoning; the mechanism breaks if repetitive structures are artifacts (e.g., unremoved titles) rather than genuine discourse.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: OCR Error Propagation & Post-Processing Trade-offs**
  - *Why needed:* The entire corpus quality depends on mitigating OCR errors from scanned PDFs. Understanding the types of errors (character substitution, layout fragmentation) and the cost/benefit of manual vs. automated correction is crucial.
  - *Quick check question:* What are three specific error types common in legal PDF OCR (see section 3.5/Appendix B), and why might automated rule-based fixing be insufficient for Sinhala?
- **Concept: Lexical Diversity Metrics (TTR, Hapax Legomena)**
  - *Why needed:* These metrics (section 4.2) are primary evidence that the corpus is a specialized domain resource. They help diagnose vocabulary richness vs. repetition, informing vocabulary selection for embeddings or tokenizers.
  - *Quick check question:* If a corpus has a TTR of 0.73 and 46% hapax legomena, what does that suggest about its content compared to a general-language corpus with more even word distributions?
- **Concept: Perplexity as a Domain Adaptation Signal**
  - *Why needed:* Perplexity comparison (section 4.7) is the key experiment showing the corpus has a distinct, more predictable distribution. This guides model selection and hints at the nature of legal language.
  - *Quick check question:* Why might a legal corpus have *lower* perplexity than a general news corpus, even though it contains more specialized terminology?

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map:**  
Data Acquisition -> OCR Engine -> Filtration Module -> Post-Processing Pipeline -> Metadata Generation -> Evaluation Suite

**Critical path:**  
OCR quality → Filtration decisions → Post-processing load → Final corpus quality

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Manual vs. Automated Cleaning: Manual ensures accuracy for Sinhala but doesn’t scale; automated rules (regex) risk over-cleaning legal terms.
- Filtration Strictness: Excluding >50 pages and multi-column docs improved quality but discarded potentially valuable long Acts.
- Metadata Depth: Basic metadata (confidence, pages) is low-cost; deeper structural metadata (section labels) was not attempted.

**Failure signatures:**
- High post-WER/CER (>25%) after manual cleaning indicates intractable source material.
- LDA coherence score low (<0.4) or topics nonsensical suggests residual noise or inadequate preprocessing.
- Perplexity gap between legal/general corpora vanishes → corpus may not be domain-distinct.
- Hapax legomena % unusually low (<30%) → corpus may be too homogenized or missing rare terms.

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **OCR Sanity Check:** Process 10 sample PDFs (varying lengths/layouts) through the full pipeline. Measure raw vs. post-processed WER/CER. Target: post-WER <25%.
2. **Lexical Baseline:** Compute TTR and top-100 word coverage on the pilot set. Validate that top words include expected legal function words (e.g., වන, පනත) and that hapax % >40%.
3. **Perplexity Pilot:** Sample 200 sentences from the pilot corpus. Compute perplexity on 2-3 small LMs (e.g., DistilGPT-2, a Sinhala-specific model if available). Confirm legal subset has lower perplexity than a matched general Sinhala sample.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Expansion to other document types:** How can SinhaLegal be expanded to include Gazettes/Extraordinary Gazettes, and what new NLP tasks (regulatory tracking, cross-document reference resolution) would this enable? Basis: Limitations and Conclusion note the current scope restriction. Unresolved: The corpus excludes other categories; evidence needed: integrated expansion with benchmark performance.
- **Temporal coverage:** What is the impact of extending coverage to post-2014 Acts/Bills on generalizability, especially for diachronic analysis? Basis: Limitations note cutoff at 2014; evidence: extended corpus with updated documents and evaluation of performance shifts.
- **Structural segmentation:** What automated methods can segment Sinhala legal documents into functional units (preamble, definitions, clauses, schedules), and how does segmentation improve IE accuracy? Basis: Limitations state documents are continuous text without consistent segmentation; evidence needed: a segmenter evaluated on manual annotation and its effect on IE tasks.
- **Annotated NER dataset:** How can a gold-standard, manually annotated NER dataset for Sinhala legal texts be created, and to what extent do supervised neural models outperform the current rule-based NER? Basis: Limitations note rule-based approach and manual evaluation infeasible; evidence needed: publicly released annotated corpus with inter-annotator agreement and comparative F1 scores.

## Limitations
- Excluding >50-page and multi-column docs biases corpus toward shorter Acts, omitting complex legislation.
- Manual cleaning improves quality but is not scalable and may introduce inconsistency.
- High hapax legomena (46.14%) may partly stem from OCR artifacts or unique names, not just legal terms.
