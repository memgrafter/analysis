# Detecting AI-Generated Essays in Writing Assessment: Responsible Use and Generalizability Across LLMs

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.02353
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02353
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Perplexity-based GBM detectors achieve high within-model AUC (>0.9) and mutual generalizability among GPT-4, GPT-4o, GPT-o1, and GPT-o3-mini, but fail to generalize to/from GPT-o4-mini and GPT-5; a detector trained on combined data from all models (GPT-all) attains uniformly high AUC across all tested LLMs.

## Executive Summary
This paper evaluates the cross-model generalizability of AI-generated essay detectors for standardized writing assessments. Using GRE prompts, it trains Gradient Boosting Machines on perplexity-derived features (from a fixed GPT-2 reference model) for essays produced by various GPT models and tests them on each other. Key findings reveal that models cluster in the feature space: detectors transfer well within the GPT-4 family but not to newer models like GPT-o4-mini and GPT-5. Training a detector on diverse outputs from all models yields robust performance, underscoring the necessity of varied training data for reliable detection and highlighting responsible use considerations in high-stakes assessment contexts.

## Method Summary
The study generated essays from six GPT models (GPT-4, GPT-4o, GPT-o1, GPT-o3-mini, GPT-o4-mini, GPT-5) and human writers using two GRE prompts. For each essay, it computed token-level perplexity using a fixed GPT-2 reference model, then derived overall and sentence-level statistics (mean, median, min, max, deciles). A Gradient Boosting Machine classifier was trained per model on a balanced dataset (300 AI + 100 human essays) via four-fold cross-validation. Each trained detector was evaluated on test sets from all models (100 human + 100 AI essays per target). A "GPT-all" detector was trained on combined data from all models. Performance was measured by AUC, producing a cross-model generalizability matrix.

## Key Results
- **High within-model detection:** AUC > 0.9 for all models when trained and tested on the same model.
- **Strong mutual generalizability among GPT-4 family:** Pairwise AUC > 0.8 for GPT-4, GPT-4o, GPT-o1, and GPT-o3-mini.
- **Poor generalization to/from GPT-o4-mini and GPT-5:** These models show low AUC with others and vice versa.
- **GPT-all detector effectiveness:** A single detector trained on all models achieves high AUC (>0.85) uniformly across all tested LLMs.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
**Claim:** Perplexity-based features extracted using a fixed reference model (GPT-2) provide a discriminative signal for distinguishing LLM-generated from human-written essays within a standardized prompt context.  
**Mechanism:** GPT-2 assigns higher perplexity to text whose token distribution diverges from its training data. LLM-generated essays, from models with different architectures and training corpora, systematically produce patterns that GPT-2 finds less probable. Summary statistics over sentence-level perplexities capture typical ranges for each source.  
**Core assumption:** The distributional divergence between an LLM's output and GPT-2 is stable across essays from that LLM and differs meaningfully from human essays on the same prompts.  
**Break condition:** The target LLM is fine-tuned to mimic GPT-2's token distribution, or its generation produces perplexity characteristics overlapping the human distribution.

### Mechanism 2
**Claim:** A Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM) classifier can learn a decision boundary that transfers to other LLMs producing text with similar feature distributions.  
**Mechanism:** The GBM ensembles decision trees to model non-linear relationships in the multi-dimensional perplexity feature space. Transferability depends on the target LLM occupying a similar region in this space as the training data.  
**Core assumption:** LLMs form clusters in the feature space; the GBM can capture separating boundaries between clusters.  
**Break condition:** The target LLM's features either overlap significantly with the human cluster or form a novel, isolated cluster not represented in training.

### Mechanism 3
**Claim:** The observed mutual generalizability among GPT-4, GPT-4o, GPT-o1, and GPT-o3-mini—and the isolation of GPT-o4-mini and GPT-5—stems from shared architectural or training similarities within each group, manifesting as consistent GPT-2 perplexity patterns.  
**Mechanism:** Related models produce statistically similar "stylometric fingerprints" as measured by GPT-2's perplexity, allowing transferred detection rules.  
**Core assumption:** GPT-2's perplexity is sensitive to high-level generation patterns conserved across model variants but shifted by major architectural changes.  
**Break condition:** A new LLM employs a fundamentally different training objective or architecture, placing its output in a novel feature region.

## Foundational Learning

**Perplexity as a distributional divergence metric**  
*Why needed:* It is the sole feature source; understanding that it measures surprise relative to a fixed reference (GPT-2), not absolute fluency, is critical. The signal comes from relative differences in how "in-distribution" various LLM outputs are for GPT-2.  
*Quick check:* If an LLM is fine-tuned on data very similar to GPT-2's training set, its GPT-2 perplexity would be lower, potentially making it harder to distinguish from human essays if human writing also aligns with GPT-2's distribution.

**Supervised learning with gradient boosting for tabular data**  
*Why needed:* The GBM handles heterogeneous, non-linear feature relationships (e.g., multiple perplexity percentiles) and learns patterns present in the training distribution without strong extrapolation biases.  
*Quick check:* A GBM trained on GPT-3 may fail on GPT-5 because GPT-5's output distribution (in feature space) may lie outside the training manifold, despite both being GPT models—the GBM cannot extrapolate to unseen regions.

**Cross-model generalizability as a clustering problem in feature space**  
*Why needed:* The core finding is that models cluster; robust detection requires training data spanning expected clusters. This frames the practical strategy of pooling diverse model outputs.  
*Quick check:* A detector trained only on GPT-3 might work poorly on GPT-4 if they form separate clusters. Adding Claude outputs could improve coverage if Claude forms a distinct but overlapping cluster, but might hurt performance if its features overlap heavily with human essays.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**  
Essay Corpus Generator → Reference Perplexity Scorer → Classifier Trainer → Evaluation Engine → Generalizability Matrix Builder

**Critical path:** Essay Generation → Feature Extraction (using fixed reference LM) → Model Training → Cross-Model Evaluation. The fixed reference LM is a singleton dependency for the entire pipeline.

**Design tradeoffs:**  
- **Reference Model Choice:** Using a small, old model (GPT-2) is computationally cheap and provides a stable, invariant reference. Downside: may miss nuances detectable only with a more capable, contemporary reference model. Tradeoff: stability vs. sensitivity.
- **Feature Set:** Using distributional stats (percentiles) over sentence-level perplexity captures within-essay variation, enhancing robustness over a single essay-level score. Tradeoff: feature dimensionality vs. information loss from aggregation.
- **Classifier Choice:** GBM offers good performance on tabular data and some interpretability. An end-to-end fine-tuned LLM classifier (e.g., RoBERTa) might perform better in-distribution but likely generalizes worse to unseen models. Tradeoff: peak in-distribution accuracy vs. cross-distribution robustness.

**Failure signatures:**  
- **Diagonal failure:** Low AUC on same-model pairs implies the feature set or classifier cannot distinguish that model's output from human text. Check reference model compatibility or feature extraction bugs.
- **Cluster failure:** High AUC within a suspected cluster (e.g., GPT-4 variants) but low AUC to/from a new model (e.g., GPT-5) indicates a fundamental shift in the new model's output distribution relative to the reference LM.
- **GPT-all failure:** Even a detector trained on all models performs poorly on a new model, signaling the new model lies outside the convex hull of all previously observed feature distributions.

**First 3 experiments:**  
1. **Within-Model Sanity Check:** Train and test a detector on essays from a single, well-understood LLM (e.g., GPT-4) using human essays as the negative class. Expect AUC > 0.9. Confirms pipeline integrity and that the reference model/perplexity features provide a signal for that specific model.
2. **Cross-Model Cluster Discovery:** Train detectors individually on GPT-4, GPT-4o, GPT-o1, GPT-o3-mini. Test each on all others. Expect a block of >0.8 AUC in the heatmap for these four. Then train on GPT-o4-mini and GPT-5 and test on others. Expect these two to show high mutual AUC but low to the first cluster. Validates the cluster hypothesis.
3. **GPT-all Benchmark:** Train a single GBM on the combined feature set from all seven LLMs (balanced with human data). Test this single detector on every LLM individually. Expect uniformly high AUC (>0.85) across all. Confirms that diverse training data is critical for robustness.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
**1. How should the construct of "writing proficiency" be redefined in educational assessments as AI assistance becomes ubiquitous in human writing processes?**  
*Basis:* AI tools can handle surface-level features like grammar; assessment may need to reconsider the weight of these dimensions.  
*Why unresolved:* Core human-centric competencies to prioritize remain unclear and contested.  
*What evidence would resolve:* Longitudinal studies tracking skill development in AI-normalized environments, combined with expert consensus (e.g., Delphi studies) on revised construct frameworks, validated against real-world writing outcomes.

**2. How does the cross-model generalizability gap observed within the GPT family compare to generalizability between fundamentally different LLM architectures (e.g., GPT vs. Claude vs. Gemini)?**  
*Basis:* Poor generalization observed to/from GPT-o4-mini and GPT-5 within GPT lineage; authors speculate gaps may be larger across corporate lineages but did not test.  
*Why unresolved:* Unknown whether perplexity features capture universal AI-generated signals or vendor-specific fingerprints.  
*What evidence would resolve:* Replication using same methodology on diverse LLMs (Claude, Gemini, Llama) with same GRE prompts.

**3. Under what specific conditions do AI-generated essay detectors exhibit systematic bias against second-language writers, and can such bias be mitigated?**  
*Basis:* Conflicting findings exist; some detectors show higher false-positive rates for non-native speakers, while carefully developed ones may not.  
*Why unresolved:* Precise conditions that nullify or introduce bias are not established.  
*What evidence would resolve:* Large-scale, multi-institutional audit comparing differential performance (e.g., DIF analysis) of detector types across well-characterized L1 vs. L2 corpora, controlling for prompt, genre, proficiency.

**4. What methodological approaches can reliably detect essays that are collaborative products of human and AI (hybrid texts) in open-ended writing tasks?**  
*Basis:* Detecting AI involvement is extremely challenging for mixed-origin texts; no generally applicable method exists for real-world, open-ended tasks.  
*Why unresolved:* Similarity matching works only for constrained, fixed-prompt assessments.  
*What evidence would resolve:* Development and benchmark evaluation of novel feature sets (e.g., hybrid stylometric markers, edit distance trajectories) on authentic human-AI collaborative essays with verified contribution proportions.

**5. What concrete, evidence-based institutional policies and procedures for using AI-detectors in high-stakes assessment minimize harm (e.g., false positives) while maintaining academic integrity?**  
*Basis:* Responsible use requires institutional consensus rather than ad hoc decisions; evaluation designs should combine take-home with proctored tasks.  
*Why unresolved:* Principles are stated, but no implemented policy frameworks or their comparative efficacy have been documented.  
*What evidence would resolve:* Design-based research implementing and comparing governance models (e.g., mandatory AI-disclosure, multi-evidence review panels, threshold-based flagging with human oversight) across settings, measuring outcomes like appeal rates, fairness metrics, learning impact.

## Limitations
- Study limited to GRE prompts; domain generalizability unknown.
- Generation parameters (temperature, max_tokens, etc.) and exact GPT-2 variant unspecified, hindering replication.
- GBM hyperparameters, cross-validation splits, and human essay selection criteria not fully detailed; potential for overfitting or data leakage.
- Clustering explanation is correlational without architectural ablation; causal mechanisms remain unclear.

## Confidence
- **Within-model detection (AUC >0.9):** Medium (plausible but sensitive to implementation choices like reference model and generation parameters).
- **Cross-model generalizability among GPT-4 cluster:** Medium (observed pattern may shift with different settings or reference models).
- **GPT-all detector effectiveness:** Medium (assumes training data spans expected feature space; may fail for future models outside tested set).

## Next Checks
1. **Exact replication:** Generate essays with standardized parameters (e.g., temperature=0.7, max_tokens=500) using provided GRE prompts, extract GPT-2 perplexity features, train GBMs, and reproduce the cross-model AUC matrix to confirm the cluster pattern.
2. **Reference model sensitivity:** Replace GPT-2 with GPT-2-medium and GPT-Neo; re-run full cross-model evaluation. Significant changes in clustering or AUC magnitudes would indicate dependence on the specific reference model's biases.
3. **Cross-domain evaluation:** Test the trained GPT-all detector on essays from a different writing task (e.g., TOEFL independent prompts). A substantial AUC drop would indicate overfitting to GRE-specific patterns and limited generalizability.
