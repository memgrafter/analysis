# Enhancing Physics-Informed Neural Networks with Domain-aware Fourier Features: Towards Improved Performance and Interpretable Results

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2603.02948  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02948  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: Domain-aware Fourier Features (DaFFs) enforce boundary conditions structurally, eliminating explicit boundary loss terms and loss balancing, yielding orders-of-magnitude lower validation errors (e.g., Kirchhoff: 6.42e-18 vs 2.75e-06) and more interpretable LRP attributions.

## Executive Summary
Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) often suffer from training instability due to competing loss terms and lack interpretability. This paper introduces Domain-aware Fourier Features (DaFFs), which encode the geometry and boundary conditions of the domain into the network’s input via eigenfunctions of the Laplace operator. By hard-constraining homogeneous boundary conditions through the input encoding, the need for explicit boundary losses and adaptive weighting is removed, simplifying optimization. Experiments on 2D linear PDEs (Kirchhoff, Helmholtz) show validation errors reduced by 5–6 orders of magnitude and faster convergence. Additionally, applying Layer-wise Relevance Propagation (LRP) to DaFFs reveals physically consistent spectral patterns in feature attributions, enhancing interpretability.

## Method Summary
The method replaces raw coordinate inputs with a truncated set of eigenfunctions of the Laplace operator defined on the problem domain with homogeneous boundary conditions (DaFFs). These basis functions are zero on the boundary and, when fed into a bias-free fully connected network with zero-preserving activations (e.g., tanh, ReLU), ensure that the network output satisfies the boundary conditions by construction. The loss function reduces to only the PDE residual, computed via automatic differentiation. This eliminates the stiffness caused by balancing multiple losses and, combined with the spectral richness of the Fourier basis, mitigates the low-frequency bias typical of vanilla PINNs. For interpretability, LRP is applied to attribute predictions to specific DaFF components, linking the network’s behavior to physically meaningful spatial frequencies.

## Key Results
- **Validation errors** drop by 5–6 orders of magnitude compared to vanilla PINNs and random Fourier features (RFFs): Kirchhoff (6.42e-18 vs 2.75e-06), Helmholtz (2.32e-11 vs 5.48e-05).  
- **Training stability** and convergence speed improve because the loss is simplified to a single PDE residual term, removing the need for adaptive weighting schemes like ReLoBRaLo.  
- **Interpretability** via LRP shows that DaFFs yield physically consistent feature attributions aligned with expected spectral modes, whereas vanilla PINNs and RFFs produce scattered, less meaningful patterns.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Hard enforcement of boundary conditions
DaFFs are constructed from eigenfunctions of the Laplace operator that vanish on the domain boundaries by design. When these features are concatenated and passed through a bias-free network with zero-preserving activations, the zero-values at the boundaries propagate through all layers, forcing the network output to satisfy homogeneous boundary conditions without an explicit loss term. This structural enforcement relies on the completeness of the eigenbasis and the exactness of the eigenvalue solve; any numerical inaccuracies may introduce small boundary residuals.

### Mechanism 2: Loss simplification and gradient stability
Removing boundary loss terms reduces the multi-objective loss to a single PDE residual, eliminating conflicting gradient directions that cause stiffness during optimization. The Fourier basis also enriches the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) spectrum, alleviating the spectral bias toward low frequencies that plagues vanilla PINNs. Consequently, training becomes more stable and converges faster, as evidenced by smoother loss curves and lower final errors.

### Mechanism 3: Interpretable spectral attributions
Each DaFF directly corresponds to a spatial frequency determined by its eigenvalue. LRP redistributes prediction relevance backward through the network; for DaFFs, high relevance for a specific component indicates that the solution depends strongly on that eigenmode. For smooth solutions, low-index modes dominate, which matches theoretical expectations and is confirmed experimentally. In contrast, random Fourier features lack this structured mapping, leading to scattered and less physically meaningful LRP scores.

## Foundational Learning
- **Partial Differential Equations (PDEs) & Boundary Conditions**  
  DaFFs derive from the Laplace eigenvalue problem tailored to the PDE’s domain and boundary conditions (Dirichlet/Neumann/Robin). Correct generation of eigenfunctions requires understanding the PDE form and boundary types.  
  *Quick check*: For a rectangular domain with Neumann boundary conditions on the y‑edges (∂u/∂y = 0 at y=0,b), which harmonic forms (sine or cosine) in the y‑direction satisfy the condition?  

- **Fourier Analysis & Spectral Methods**  
  DaFFs are a truncated expansion of eigenfunctions. Interpreting LRP relevance involves mapping indices (m,n) to spatial frequencies (proportional to m/a and n/b). Low (m,n) correspond to slow, global variations; high (m,n) capture fine details.  
  *Quick check*: For a unit square domain [0,1]×[0,1], what spatial frequency magnitude (cycles/unit length) does DaFF (m=2,n=3) represent relative to (m=4,n=6)?  

- **Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) & Spectral Bias**  
  Vanilla PINNs exhibit a bias toward low‑frequency solutions because the NTK spectrum is dominated by low‑frequency eigenfunctions. DaFFs pre‑wire high‑frequency basis functions into the input, altering the effective NTK and enabling the network to capture higher frequencies.  
  *Quick check*: Why might a deeper vanilla PINN still struggle with high‑frequency solutions even if it has the capacity to approximate them? How do DaFFs change the effective frequency prior?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map**: Domain discretization → Eigendecomposition → DaFF generation → Network training (single loss) → LRP analysis

**Critical path**: 
1. Discretize the domain and compute the Laplace eigenfunctions (analytically for rectangles, numerically via finite difference for complex shapes).  
2. Assemble the DaFF matrix Φ(x) by selecting a fixed number of eigenfunctions (ordered by eigenvalue).  
3. Train a fully connected network (no bias terms) on inputs concatenated with DaFFs, minimizing only the PDE residual loss.  
4. Optionally apply LRP to attribute predictions to individual DaFF components.

The eigendecomposition is a one‑time offline cost; once DaFFs are fixed, training is faster and more stable.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- *Eigendecomposition cost vs. training efficiency*: Numerical eigensolvers scale as O(N³) with grid size but are offline; sparse domains benefit less.  
- *DaFF dimensionality*: Including more eigenfunctions (higher M) increases expressive power but also input size and computational cost. The paper uses modest M (∼10s) for 2D problems.  
- *Generalization*: DaFFs are tightly coupled to the specific domain geometry; changing the domain requires recomputing eigenfunctions.

**Failure signatures**:
- High validation MSE despite low PDE residual → DaFFs lack sufficient basis functions to represent the true solution (increase M or include more harmonic families).  
- Non‑zero predictions on boundaries → accidental introduction of biases, inaccuracies in the eigenfunctions, or insufficient activation zero‑preservation.  
- LRP scores scattered across many high‑frequency DaFFs → network not using the spectral structure effectively; may require deeper architecture or different activation functions.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. **Sanity check**: Train a PINN‑DaFFs on the 1D Poisson equation u'' = f on [0,1] with Dirichlet BCs u(0)=u(1)=0. Verify that û(0) and û(1) are near zero without a boundary loss, and compare accuracy against a vanilla PINN that includes boundary terms.  
2. **Baseline comparison**: Replicate the Kirchhoff experiment with identical network architecture but replace DaFFs with random Fourier features (RFFs) of comparable variance. Expect significantly higher validation error (≥1e‑7) and non‑zero boundary residuals.  
3. **LRP consistency**: For the Helmholtz problem with known solution sin(πx)sin(πy), DaFFs should yield the highest LRP relevance for the (m=1,n=1) component. If LRP highlights high‑frequency modes instead, investigate convergence or DaFF generation errors.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Generalization to non‑homogeneous BCs and complex PDEs**: DaFFs currently assume homogeneous Dirichlet conditions on simple geometries; extending to arbitrary domains and non‑homogeneous boundary operators lacks theoretical and empirical validation.  
2. **Sparse observations and incomplete dynamics**: The paper’s experiments use dense collocation points and full PDE knowledge; it remains unclear how DaFFs perform under data scarcity or with partially unknown physics.  
3. **Reliability of LRP for PINN diagnostics**: While LRP shows promising patterns for well‑trained models, it is uncertain whether LRP can consistently detect biases or failures across diverse PINN setups, as its attributions are heuristic and may be misleading if the network is unstable.

## Limitations
- **Eigendecomposition overhead**: DaFFs require solving a Laplace eigenvalue problem; for complex domains numerical eigensolvers add offline cost and potential discretization errors.  
- **Homogeneous BC assumption**: The current construction targets homogeneous boundary conditions; inhomogeneous cases need additional modifications.  
- **Scope of evaluation**: Experiments are limited to linear 2D PDEs; generalizability to nonlinear equations and 3D geometries remains unexplored.  
- **Interpretability heuristic**: LRP provides insights but lacks a rigorous guarantee that attributions reflect true physical dependencies.
