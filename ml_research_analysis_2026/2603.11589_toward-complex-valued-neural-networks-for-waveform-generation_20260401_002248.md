# Toward Complex-Valued Neural Networks for Waveform Generation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.11589
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11589
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: ComVo introduces a fully complex-valued neural vocoder that achieves state-of-the-art objective metrics and 25% faster training via efficient block-matrix computation.

## Executive Summary
Standard iSTFT-based vocoders treat complex spectrograms as separate real and imaginary channels, losing inherent phase-magnitude structure. ComVo addresses this with a fully complex-valued neural network (CVNN) generator and discriminator, phase quantization regularization, and a novel block-matrix computation scheme that reduces backward graph size. Evaluated on LibriTTS and MUSDB18-HQ, it achieves superior objective metrics (UTMOS, MR-STFT, PESQ) and matches top subjective scores, while training 25% faster. Grad-CAM analysis shows the complex-domain discriminator provides more coherent spectral feedback, yielding cleaner generated spectrograms.

## Method Summary
ComVo proposes a complex-valued generative adversarial network for waveform generation. The generator is a CVNN that directly outputs complex spectrograms, converted to waveforms via inverse STFT. The discriminator, termed complex MRD (cMRD), operates in the complex domain and uses a split-style hinge loss applied separately to real and imaginary parts. Phase quantization—discretizing phase into 128 levels—acts as a regularization technique. To improve efficiency, the authors implement a block-matrix computation scheme that reduces the number of nodes in the backward graph, cutting training time without altering numerical results or output quality.

## Key Results
- Achieves state-of-the-art objective metrics (UTMOS ↑, MR-STFT ↓, PESQ ↑) on LibriTTS and MUSDB18-HQ benchmarks.
- Matches top subjective evaluation scores while reducing training time by 25% via 55% fewer generator backward nodes and 67% fewer discriminator nodes.
- Complex-domain adversarial feedback produces cleaner spectrograms, evidenced by Grad-CAM visualizations highlighting more coherent spectral regions compared to real-valued discriminators.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Complex-valued neural networks preserve the natural phase-magnitude relationships in spectrograms, allowing the discriminator to provide structured feedback that respects complex-domain geometry. Phase quantization regularizes the generator by discretizing phase, preventing overfitting to fine phase details and improving stability. The block-matrix scheme optimizes gradient computation for complex parameters, minimizing unnecessary operations in the backward pass. Together, these elements yield more spectrally accurate waveforms and faster training.

2. **Phase Quantization**: Discretizing continuous phase into discrete bins (e.g., 128 levels). *Why needed*: Acts as regularization, smoothing phase predictions and improving perceptual stability. *Quick check*: Apply uniform quantization to a set of phase angles and measure reconstruction error.
3. **Complex-Domain Adversarial Training**: Extending GANs to complex-valued outputs and discriminators. *Why needed*: Standard hinge losses on split real/imaginary components may not fully exploit complex feedback structure. *Quick check*: Train a toy complex generator with real-valued vs. complex-valued discriminators and compare sample quality.
4. **Block-Matrix Computation**: Organizing complex matrix operations into real block matrices to optimize autograd. *Why needed*: Reduces backward graph size and computational overhead for complex parameters. *Quick check*: Profile backward pass time for a complex linear layer vs. its block-matrix equivalent.
5. **iSTFT Limitations**: Standard spectrogram inversion treats magnitude and phase as separate, often producing artifacts. *Why needed*: Complex spectrograms encode joint information; splitting degrades phase coherence. *Quick check*: Convert a complex spectrogram to magnitude/phase and back, then compute spectral convergence.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**: Complex Spectrogram Input -> CVNN Generator -> Time-Domain Waveform; Complex Spectrogram Input -> Complex Discriminator (cMRD) -> Real/Imag Split Losses

**Critical path**: Input complex spectrogram feeds both generator (for synthesis) and discriminator (for feedback). Generator outputs complex spectrogram, converted via iSTFT to audio. Discriminator processes both real and synthesized complex spectrograms, producing split hinge losses. Adversarial loop updates generator and discriminator.

**Design tradeoffs**: 
- Split hinge loss (real/imaginary separate) vs. unified complex loss: split design simplifies implementation but may not fully leverage complex-domain relationships.
- Phase quantization level (Nq): Higher Nq preserves phase detail but risks instability; Nq=128 was empirically optimal.
- Block-matrix size: Larger blocks reduce graph nodes but may increase memory; implementation is framework-dependent.

**Failure signatures**: 
- Numerical instability in multi-GPU training due to complex autograd or gradient reduction.
- Training divergence when phase quantization is too aggressive (low Nq) or insufficient (high Nq).
- Gradient explosion in complex discriminator if real/imaginary losses are unbalanced.

**First experiments**:
1. Replace split hinge loss with a unified complex-domain hinge loss (e.g., on complex output magnitude) and compare spectral convergence and UTMOS.
2. Perform a hyperparameter sweep over phase quantization levels (Nq = 32, 64, 128, 256) and measure impact on PESQ and phase continuity metrics.
3. Profile distributed training with complex parameters: compare communication overhead and scaling efficiency between real-valued and complex-valued models, testing mixed-precision all-reduce.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: How can the "split-style" discriminator loss design—applying component-wise hinge losses to real and imaginary parts—be improved with a fully unified complex-domain adversarial objective?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The Limitations section notes they "adopt split-style designs" for cMRD losses and will "explore more advanced designs" in future work.
- **Why unresolved**: The current split hinge loss may not fully leverage complex-valued feedback structure; a unified loss could better respect phase-angle relationships.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Implementing and comparing a complex-domain hinge loss (e.g., on |output| or phase) against the current split approach, measuring impact on spectral accuracy and training stability.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Why is phase quantization with Nq=128 optimal in this setup, and can adaptive or learnable quantization further improve quality without sacrificing stability?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] Ablation results show Nq=128 achieves best UTMOS/PESQ trade-off. The paper states phase quantization "acts as a form of regularization" but does not explain the specific level choice.
- **Why unresolved**: The choice of discrete levels is heuristic; adaptive quantization guided by magnitude or a learned codebook might better regularize phase while preserving structure.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Hyperparameter sweep over Nq with adaptive schedules (e.g., magnitude-dependent bins) and evaluation of phase continuity effects on spectrogram errors and perceptual metrics.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: What causes the "numerical issues" and multi-GPU under-optimization observed for complex parameters, and how can they be mitigated for large-scale distributed CVNN training?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The Limitations section reports "occasional numerical issues" and under-optimized multi-GPU performance with complex parameters.
- **Why unresolved**: These issues hinder scalability; root causes (e.g., gradient reduction or precision handling in complex autograd) are not diagnosed.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Profiling distributed training dynamics for complex vs. real-valued models, isolating communication/computation patterns, and testing mixed-precision or custom all-reduce for block matrices.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Do complex-domain adversarial feedback mechanisms provide qualitatively different guidance to the generator compared to real-valued discriminators, and how does this translate to other tasks (e.g., source separation)?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] Grad-CAM visualizations (Figure 3) show cMRD highlights more coherent spectral regions than real-valued MRD, suggesting structured feedback. The conclusion suggests extending CVNNs beyond vocoders.
- **Why unresolved**: The link between discriminator feedback geometry and generator improvements is anecdotal; it is unclear if these advantages transfer to other complex-domain tasks.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Analyzing discriminator gradient statistics (e.g., magnitude/phase alignment) across tasks and measuring performance when swapping discriminators between vocoders and separation models.

### Open Question 5
- **Question**: How does the complex-valued parameterization compare to simply scaling real-valued models in terms of sample efficiency, robustness to noisy data, and generalization across speaker or acoustic conditions?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] Table 10 shows ComVo outperforms a 2× parameter real-valued baseline despite similar memory, indicating benefits beyond scaling. However, tests are limited to LibriTTS/MUSDB.
- **Why unresolved**: The paper does not evaluate robustness to noise, accents, or low-resource settings, leaving open whether complex modeling confers inherent generalization advantages.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Systematic evaluation across diverse noise conditions, speaker variability, and low-data regimes, comparing sample complexity curves and error decomposition between CVNN and scaled RVNN baselines.

## Limitations
- Summary lacks full architectural details, hyperparameters, and data preprocessing steps.
- Benchmarking is limited to two datasets; generalization to other audio domains is unknown.
- Subjective evaluation protocol (listener count, test design, statistical analysis) is unspecified.
- Efficiency claims depend on specific block-matrix implementation and may not hold across all frameworks/hardware.
- Grad‑CAM interpretations for complex‑valued networks are not standardized; cleaner spectrograms need quantitative corroboration.
