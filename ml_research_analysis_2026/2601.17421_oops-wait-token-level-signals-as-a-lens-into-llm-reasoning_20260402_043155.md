# Oops, Wait: Token-Level Signals as a Lens into LLM Reasoning

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2601.17421
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17421
- Reference count: 31
- Primary result: Specific tokens (e.g., "therefore", "wait") strongly correlate with reasoning correctness; these signals vary by training strategy but not model scale, and leveraging token probability gaps improves performance via ensemble filtering.

## Executive Summary
This paper explores token-level signals as a window into LLM reasoning processes. By analyzing next-token probabilities after delimiter tokens like "\n\n" in thinking trajectories, the authors identify tokens that are significantly associated with correct versus incorrect reasoning. They find that these associations are shaped primarily by post-training strategy (e.g., DeepSeek-R1-distill vs. s1) rather than model scale, and that the "wait" token often precedes or follows sharp increases in answer probability—a pattern whose effectiveness depends on training data quality. Interventions such as suppressing incorrect-associated tokens degrade performance, while discarding trajectories with small token probability gaps before majority voting boosts accuracy on AIME24. The study reveals how post-training supervision influences internal reasoning signals and suggests token-centric methods for analyzing and improving LLM reasoning.

## Method Summary
The analysis pipeline generates multiple reasoning trajectories for each evaluation problem (using temperatures 0 and 0.6). Trajectories are segmented by the delimiter "\n\n", and for each segment, the softmax probability of the next token is extracted. Trajectories are labeled correct or incorrect based on final answer exact match. The authors compute average probabilities per token for correct and incorrect sets, identifying tokens with significant differences (t-test p<0.05). To study "wait", they monitor the answer token's probability at 10-token intervals, detect jumps (sharp increases within a sliding window of 4 preceding/following tokens), and classify "wait" occurrences as "rethink" (before a jump) or "recall" (after a jump). They evaluate the impact of masking tokens linked to incorrect reasoning and implement a token-gap ensemble that computes the average probability difference between correct- and incorrect-associated tokens per trajectory, discards the bottom-k samples, and aggregates the remaining answers via majority vote.

## Key Results
- Discourse tokens like "therefore" and "so" have higher probabilities in correct reasoning, while "wait" and "alternatively" are associated with incorrect reasoning.
- Token associations vary substantially across training recipes (e.g., DeepSeek-R1-distill vs. s1.1) but remain stable across model scales (7B, 14B, 32B) within the same recipe.
- In R1-32B, "wait" near probability jumps yields a success rate ~4× higher than in s1.1-32B; s1.1 overuses "wait" (1.5× more) with weaker jump association.
- Token-gap ensemble improves AIME24 results: QwQ-32B achieves 70% vs. 63.3% with standard majority vote.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
Token probability differences after segmenting delimiters (e.g., "\n\n") serve as indicators of reasoning correctness. By averaging the softmax probabilities of tokens generated after "\n\n", the model's internal preference for certain discourse tokens diverges between correct and incorrect reasoning trajectories. This divergence reflects the model's latent reasoning state. The core assumption is that the delimiter "\n\n" naturally segments reasoning steps and the next-token distribution at these points encodes the model's confidence in proceeding. Evidence includes Table 1 showing significant differences (t-test p<0.05) for tokens like "wait" (∆ = -10.4%) and "therefore" (+2.5%). A break condition occurs if the delimiter does not consistently mark reasoning boundaries (e.g., in non-delimited formats), making the signal noisy or absent.

### Mechanism 2
Training strategy is the primary determinant of token-level signal patterns, overriding model scale. Models fine-tuned on different reasoning trajectories develop distinct associations between tokens and correctness because the training data encodes different reasoning styles and supervision signals. Scale variations within the same recipe preserve these patterns. The core assumption is that token-level signals are learned from the distribution of reasoning traces in post-training data, not from pre-training scale alone. Evidence includes Table 3 showing substantial variation in correct/incorrect tokens across R1-32B, s1.1-32B, s1-32B, QwQ-32B despite a shared Qwen2.5 backbone, and Table 4 showing stable token sets across 7B, 14B, 32B within the DeepSeek-R1-distill series. A break condition is if training data is extremely limited or homogeneous, preventing signal stabilization; architectural differences could also dominate.

### Mechanism 3
The "wait" token functions as a dynamic trigger for probability jumps and a self-check cue, but its effectiveness is contingent on training data quality. In correct reasoning, "wait" often precedes or follows a sharp increase in answer probability (a "jump"), indicating a pivotal reasoning step. The model uses "wait" to either rethink (before jump) or recall (after jump). However, small-scale SFT leads to overuse and weaker association with successful jumps. The core assumption is that answer probability jumps reflect moments of insight or correction, and "wait" tokens are causally linked to these jumps. Evidence includes Section 4 defining jumps and classifying "wait"; Figure 3 shows distribution of nearest "wait" to jump; Figure 4 shows R1-32B has higher probability increases after rethink "wait"; Figure 5c shows R1-32B's success rate ~4× higher than s1.1-32B. A break condition is if "wait" appears without subsequent probability change (e.g., in incorrect trajectories), it may be spurious; in domains where self-correction is not needed, "wait" may be irrelevant.

## Foundational Learning
- **Token-level probability analysis in language models**: Interprets model reasoning via internal distributions rather than just generated text. *Why needed*: To uncover latent reasoning states beyond surface output. *Quick check*: How would you compute the average probability of a token after a specific delimiter in a generation?
- **Post-training strategies for reasoning (SFT on teacher trajectories)**: Understands how different post-training recipes (e.g., distillation, SFT) shape model behavior. *Why needed*: The paper compares models trained with different recipes; signal differences stem from these strategies. *Quick check*: What is the difference between DeepSeek-R1-distill and s1 training data sources?
- **Probability jumps as confidence leaps in reasoning trajectories**: Sharp increases in answer probability signal moments of insight or correction. *Why needed*: Central to analyzing the "wait" token's role and its impact on answer correctness. *Quick check*: What is a probability jump and how is it detected algorithmically?

## Architecture Onboarding
Component map: Trajectory collector -> Token probability extractor -> Correctness labeler -> Signal analyzer; Jump detector -> Wait annotator; Suppression module; Token-gap ensemble.

Critical path:
1. Generate multiple reasoning trajectories for evaluation problems (temperature 0 and 0.6).
2. For each trajectory, compute token probabilities after "\n\n".
3. Split trajectories by answer correctness.
4. Compute average probabilities per token for correct and incorrect sets.
5. Identify tokens with significant differences (t-test p<0.05).
6. For "wait" analysis, compute answer probabilities at truncations (every 10 tokens), detect jumps (window size 4), classify "wait" as rethink (before jump) or recall (after jump).
7. Evaluate token suppression by masking associated tokens and measuring performance change.
8. Implement token-gap ensemble: compute correct-incorrect token probability gap per trajectory, filter bottom-k samples, majority vote on remaining.

Design tradeoffs:
- Delimiter choice: "\n\n" works for CoT but may not generalize to other formats (e.g., JSON, bullet lists).
- Token frequency threshold: Paper uses average probability >0.02 and >20 occurrences per question; too low yields noise, too high misses rare meaningful tokens.
- Jump window size: 4 preceding/following tokens balances sensitivity; larger windows smooth noise but may miss sharp jumps.
- Suppression method: Full masking vs. logit bias; full masking may degrade fluency or induce compensatory tokens.
- Ensemble threshold: Bottom 20% (paper) arbitrary; should be cross-validated on held-out set.

Failure signatures:
- No tokens show significant p<0.05 difference → either model lacks reasoning signals or analysis parameters mis-specified.
- "Wait" distribution symmetric around jump → no functional role.
- Suppression of incorrect-associated tokens improves performance → contradicts expectation; maybe token list inaccurate.
- Token-gap correlation with confidence low (Pearson <0.5) → gap not capturing reliability.

First 3 experiments:
1. Replicate Table 1 and 2 on a new model (e.g., Llama-3.1-70B) to validate signal detection pipeline.
2. Reproduce Figure 3 and 4 for R1-32B and s1.1-32B to confirm "wait" patterns.
3. Test token-gap ensemble on AIME24 with QwQ-32B, varying filter percentage (e.g., 10%, 20%, 30%) to find optimal.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
Do small-scale SFT datasets fully inherit token-level reasoning signals from teacher models?  
*Basis*: Introduction explicitly questions whether "small-scale SFT datasets successfully inherit token-level signals... or impose limitations."  
*Why unresolved*: s1.1-32B partially replicates signals (e.g., "wait") but with overuse and lower success rate vs. R1-32B, indicating incomplete transfer; boundary conditions (data scale, quality) unknown.  
*What evidence would resolve it*: Controlled experiments varying SFT dataset size, diversity, and teacher model quality, measuring correlation between token signal metrics (e.g., "wait" success ratio) and reasoning performance.

### Open Question 2
Are token-level signals (e.g., "therefore", "wait") consistent across model architectures and reasoning domains?  
*Basis*: Limitations note reliance on Qwen models and English benchmarks; code-generation may differ.  
*Why unresolved*: Study only covers Qwen-derived models on math/natural language; discourse markers may be less salient in coding or multilingual contexts.  
*What evidence would resolve it*: Replicate analysis on diverse architectures (LLaMA, Mixtral) and domains (code, multilingual QA) to check stability of token associations.

### Open Question 3
Does explicitly supervising token-level signals during post-training improve reasoning performance?  
*Basis*: Discussion conjectures "token-centric supervision... could serve as an effective strategy for reasoning-oriented post-training."  
*Why unresolved*: Paper shows suppression of incorrect tokens hurts performance, suggesting their importance, but no positive reinforcement of correct tokens tested; hypothesis unvalidated.  
*What evidence would resolve it*: Modify training objectives to reward high probability for correct-associated tokens (or penalize incorrect ones), evaluate on reasoning benchmarks and token usage shifts.

### Open Question 4
What training factors cause token-level signals to vary across recipes but remain scale-invariant?  
*Basis*: Results show signals differ by training strategy (s1 vs DeepSeek) but not model size (7B-32B), implying supervision over capacity; underlying drivers not isolated.  
*Why unresolved*: Correlational; cannot distinguish whether data distribution, RL, distillation, or other recipe components cause variations.  
*What evidence would resolve it*: Ablation studies swapping specific training components (e.g., data source, RL algorithm) while holding others constant, measuring resulting token signal changes.

## Limitations
- **Delimiter generalizability**: Analysis hinges on "\n\n" segmenting reasoning steps; this may not hold for other formats (e.g., JSON, bullet lists) or models with different prompting conventions.
- **Model/task scope**: Evidence is concentrated on Qwen-based models (7B–32B) and math benchmarks (AIME24); transfer to non-math reasoning, multilingual, or encoder-decoder architectures is unknown.
- **Causal interpretation of "wait"**: The link between "wait" tokens and probability jumps is correlational; it remains unclear if "wait" *triggers* re-evaluation or merely *reflects* an internal state already in flux.
- **Suppression side effects**: Masking incorrect-associated tokens may degrade fluency or induce compensatory spurious tokens; the paper does not report fluency metrics.

## Next Checks
1. **Cross-architecture replication**: Apply the same pipeline (delimiter-based probability aggregation, jump detection) to Llama-3.1 and Mistral models on math and logical reasoning tasks. Failure to recover similar signal patterns would limit generalizability.
2. **Delimiter robustness test**: Replace "\n\n" with alternative step separators (e.g., "Step:", "→", or newline-only) and measure changes in token-signal strength (effect size Δ). A large drop would confirm dependency on formatting.
3. **Causal intervention on "wait"**: Force-inject or suppress "wait" tokens during generation (via logit bias) in a controlled set of problems, then measure changes in (a) probability jump frequency and (b) final accuracy. If forcing "wait" increases jumps without accuracy gains, its role is likely epiphenomenal.
