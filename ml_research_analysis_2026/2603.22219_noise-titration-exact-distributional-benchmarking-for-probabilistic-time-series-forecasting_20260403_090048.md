# Noise Titration: Exact Distributional Benchmarking for Probabilistic Time Series Forecasting

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.22219
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22219
- Reference count: 27
- Primary result: Fern outperforms state-of-the-art zero-shot foundation models (Chronos, TimesFM) by 40–52× lower MSE on chaotic and non-stationary benchmarks via Noise Titration.

## Executive Summary
The paper identifies a fundamental flaw in time series forecasting evaluation: passive assessment on single historical trajectories renders claims about non-stationarity robustness unfalsifiable. It proposes Noise Titration, an interventionist benchmarking paradigm that injects calibrated Gaussian noise into known dynamical systems, transforming forecasting into an exact distributional inference task with mathematically explicit data-generating processes. The authors extend the Fern architecture to natively output full Gaussian covariance structures via spectral SPD parameterization and train it with a multivariate Gaussian negative log-likelihood objective. Across chaotic (Lorenz, Rössler, Chua) and stochastic (Double Well, OU, SLDS, SAR) benchmarks, Fern achieves order-of-magnitude improvements over foundation models under regime shifts and elevated noise levels, while maintaining statistically sharp calibration. Noise titration experiments precisely quantify each model’s robustness threshold and resolution limit, revealing that foundation models systematically fail due to context-parroting, whereas Fern preserves structural fidelity.

## Method Summary
The method centers on interventionist benchmarking using Noise Titration. Known deterministic dynamical systems (e.g., Lorenz-63, Rössler, Chua) are simulated with high-precision integrators, and i.i.d. Gaussian noise ε∼N(0, σ²I) is injected at varying levels σ∈{0.0,0.25,1.0,2.0}. For each trajectory, a context window of length L=336 is used to predict horizons H∈{64,192}. The Fern architecture is extended to produce a full Gaussian predictive distribution N(µθ, Σθ) by parameterizing Σθ = R Λ² Rᵀ, where R is an orthogonal matrix built from Householder reflections and Λ is a diagonal matrix of positive eigenvalues with soft bounds. Training minimizes the multivariate Gaussian NLL: L = ½∑[logλ²ᵢ + rᵢ²/λ²ᵢ] where r = Rᵀ(y−µθ). The loss is estimated using S=8 training samples to reduce gradient variance. Several zero-shot foundation models (Chronos-Bolt, Chronos-2, TimesFM-2.5) serve as baselines. Evaluation metrics include MSE (point accuracy), CRPS (distributional accuracy), PIT histograms, empirical coverage for 50%/90% intervals, and Shapiro-Wilk tests with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction on whitened residuals. Shock experiments (Wang 2025) further probe non-stationarity by applying abrupt parameter/state changes at a fraction of the trajectory.

## Key Results
- Fern achieves 40–52× lower MSE compared to foundation models on Lorenz-Base and similar chaotic systems.
- Noise titration reveals foundation models’ MSE often decreases with increasing noise (e.g., Chronos-Bolt on Rössler), indicating context-parroting, while Fern’s performance degrades gracefully.
- Fern exhibits calibrated uncertainty quantification: PIT histograms are uniform, empirical coverage matches nominal levels, and BH-FDR-corrected Shapiro-Wilk pass rates are high even under high noise, whereas baselines fail.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
Noise Titration transforms forecasting into an exact distributional inference problem by anchoring the evaluation in a fully known data-generating process (DGP) with controllable observation noise. Since the underlying dynamics are deterministic and the injected noise level σ is known, any stochasticity in the test data arises solely from the perturbation, allowing a precise theoretical distribution for the target variable given the context. Forecasts can be evaluated against this distribution using proper scoring rules (e.g., CRPS) and calibration diagnostics without ambiguity. Fern’s SPD cone parameterization ensures that predicted covariances are positive-definite and that eigenvalues are directly trained via the NLL to match the magnitude of the whitened residuals, yielding statistically sharp uncertainty estimates. The interventionist approach bypasses the limitations of passive backtesting: by systematically varying σ and applying shocks, it exposes how models rely on spurious correlations (context-parroting) versus capturing true dynamics, making robustness claims falsifiable.

## Foundational Learning
- **Probabilistic Forecasting**: Predicting full probability distributions rather than point values. Why needed: Real-world decision-making requires calibrated measures of uncertainty. Quick check: Model outputs both a mean and a covariance (or predictive distribution).
- **Multivariate Gaussian NLL**: Negative log-likelihood loss for a multivariate Gaussian. Why needed: Directly optimizes the probability of the observed data under the predicted Gaussian, encouraging calibrated mean and covariance. Quick check: Loss computed as ½∑[logλ²ᵢ + rᵢ²/λ²ᵢ] for whitened residuals.
- **SPD Cone Parameterization**: Representing a symmetric positive-definite matrix as Σ = UΛ²Uᵀ with orthogonal U and positive diagonal Λ. Why needed: Guarantees Σ is PSD by construction and enables stable gradient flow on the manifold of SPD matrices. Quick check: All eigenvalues λᵢ > 0 after exponentiation.
- **Interventionist Benchmarking**: Injecting calibrated noise into known dynamical systems to create test distributions. Why needed: Converts forecasting into an exact distributional inference problem where ground-truth probabilities are known, enabling falsifiable evaluation. Quick check: Compute CRPS against the known noisy DGP.
- **PIT Histograms**: Histogram of the probability integral transform of observations under the predictive CDF. Why needed: Uniformity indicates that the predictive distribution is calibrated; deviations reveal over/under-dispersion. Quick check: Bin PIT values uniformly; expect flat histogram.
- **FDR Correction (Benjamini-Hochberg)**: Adjusting p-values in multiple hypothesis testing. Why needed: When performing many Shapiro-Wilk tests (e.g., per dimension/horizon), controls false discovery rate to avoid spurious calibrations. Quick check: Sort p-values, compare to (i/m)*q threshold.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:
Time Series Context -> Invertible Neural Net (Affine Coupling Layers) -> Parameter Head (ty, Householder reflections R, Λ) -> Affine Construction -> Predictive Distribution N(µ, Σ)

**Critical path**: The raw context window passes through a series of coupling blocks that maintain invertibility and gradually transform the representation. The final block outputs parameters of an affine transport: a translation ty, a rotation matrix R (assembled from a fixed number of Householder reflections), and a diagonal scaling Λ (with soft bounds). The mean µ is set to ty, and the covariance Σ is formed as R Λ² Rᵀ. This construction ensures Σ is symmetric positive-definite, and the NLL loss can be computed by whitening y−µ with Rᵀ/Λ.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- *Coupling depth vs. stability*: More layers increase expressive power but may cause training instability; the paper uses a moderate depth.
- *Householder reflections*: A fixed number (24) of reflections balances the ability to approximate any orthogonal matrix with computational cost and gradient flow.
- *Eigenvalue bounds*: Soft bounds on the unconstrained scaling parameter (e.g., c∈[−1,4.5] → λ∈[0,5.5]) prevent extreme covariances that could cause numerical issues, but may limit representation of very anisotropic uncertainties.
- *Training sample size (S)*: Using S=8 samples reduces gradient variance and memory, yet may introduce noise; larger S improves gradient quality at the cost of resources.

**Failure signatures**:
- *Covariance collapse*: Eigenvalues near zero or negative after exponentiation → non-SPD Σ → NaN loss.
- *Bound saturation*: Soft bounds on eigenvalues consistently at limits indicates scaling mismatch.
- *Gradient explosion*: Often from poor initialization or too large learning rates; manifested as NaNs or diverging loss.
- *Calibration mismatch*: High Shapiro-Wilk pass rates combined with low empirical coverage suggest overconfident mean predictions with under-dispersed variance.
- *PIT U-shape*: Indicates predicted variance too small (under-dispersion).
- *Context-parroting under noise*: MSE decreasing with increasing σ suggests the model is memorizing noise rather than learning dynamics.
