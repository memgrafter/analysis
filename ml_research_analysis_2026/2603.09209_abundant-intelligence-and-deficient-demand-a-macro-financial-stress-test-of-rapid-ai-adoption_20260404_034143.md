# Abundant Intelligence and Deficient Demand: A Macro-Financial Stress Test of Rapid AI Adoption

## Quick Facts

- **arXiv ID:** 2603.09209
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.09209
- **Reference count:** 12
- **Primary result:** Under rapid AI capability growth (g_A ≥ 0.20) and weak task reinstatement, labor share declines up to 40% within a decade, monetary velocity falls, consumption drops 5–12% (comparable to 2008 crisis), and crisis depth is avoidable via fast, large fiscal transfers.

## Executive Summary

The paper introduces a macro-financial stress testing framework to assess the economic impact of rapid AI adoption. It identifies a fundamental mismatch: AI generates abundance while existing institutions, anchored in human cognitive scarcity, create demand deficiency. The core model is a task-based production economy with logistic AI diffusion, featuring three interconnected mechanisms—a displacement spiral (with competing task reinstatement), “Ghost GDP” (velocity collapse as labor share falls), and intermediation margin compression.

Calibrated with FRED and BLS data, the simulations show that if AI capability grows rapidly (g_A ≥ 0.20) and reinstatement is weak, the labor share could plummet by up to 40% within ten years. This reduces monetary velocity and cuts consumption by 5–12%, a shock comparable to the 2008 financial crisis. The top income quintile, which drives 47–65% of consumption, faces the highest AI exposure and transmits distress to private credit ($2.5T) and mortgage markets ($13T). Crucially, the analysis finds that crisis depth is a policy variable: swift, large fiscal transfers can avert the worst outcomes, indicating that the future is not technologically predetermined.

## Method Summary

The method is a deterministic dynamic system built on a task-based CES production function. AI adoption follows a logistic diffusion curve, characterized by capability growth rate \(g_A\) and automation rate \(d(t)\). Labor share dynamics incorporate direct displacement \(d(t) \cdot f(g_A)\), countervailing task reinstatement \(\rho_t\), and a feedback loop from profit margins \(\beta \cdot \pi_t\). Monetary velocity is linked to consumption through the labor share and fiscal transfers \(\tau_t\). Intermediation margins are modeled as a linear function of information friction \(\phi(A_t)\), which decays exponentially with AI capability. The model is calibrated using parameters from Table 3 and FRED/BLS data. The system of ordinary differential equations is integrated over a 10-year horizon under varying \(g_A\), reinstatement rates, and policy interventions. The analysis yields three regimes (reinstatement-dominated, stable, explosive) and quantifies outcomes for labor share, velocity, consumption, and financial contagion via quintile-specific income shocks.

## Key Results

- **Displacement spiral:** With \(g_A \geq 0.20\) and weak reinstatement, the labor share declines by up to 40% within a decade; the explosive regime threshold is \(g^*_A(\rho) > 0.20\) for baseline \(\rho_0\).
- **Ghost GDP:** The fall in labor share reduces monetary velocity, driving a consumption drop of 5–12%—a magnitude similar to the 2008 crisis.
- **Policy escape:** Fast, large fiscal transfers (\(\tau\)) can neutralize demand deficiency; crisis depth is not an inevitable consequence of AI progress.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** If AI capability growth (g_A) is sufficiently rapid and task reinstatement (ρ) is weak, the displacement spiral becomes self-reinforcing, causing an explosive decline in labor share and aggregate demand.  
- **Mechanism:** Each firm’s rational substitution of AI for labor reduces aggregate labor income, which lowers aggregate demand (due to higher MPC for labor income). This demand shortfall squeezes profit margins for other firms, accelerating their AI adoption—a positive feedback loop. The competing reinstatement effect (new task creation) can absorb displacement, but if displacement outpaces reinstatement, the net feedback is explosive.  
- **Core assumption:** The marginal propensity to consume from labor income (¯c) exceeds that from capital income (1−¯c), so a falling labor share directly reduces aggregate consumption. Firms ignore the aggregate demand externality when adopting AI.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] The abstract identifies the “displacement spiral (with competing task reinstatement)” and derives conditions for self-limiting versus explosive dynamics.  
  - [section] Section 2.2 (pages 7–10) formalizes equation (7) and Proposition 1, showing the threshold \(g_A^*(\rho)\) that separates regimes.  
  - [corpus] Corpus neighbors (e.g., LLM-generated stress scenarios, AI labor impact studies) do not directly address this macro-feedback loop; evidence is weak.  
- **Break condition:** The explosive regime is avoided if reinstatement rate ρ rises sufficiently (e.g., strong institutional adaptation) or if g_A falls below the threshold. Falsification condition: AI-exposed occupations show wage growth at or above average through 2028 (H1) and new AI-complementary job categories emerge at historical rates (H9).

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** When AI-generated output substitutes for labor-generated output, monetary velocity declines monotonically with the labor share absent fiscal transfers, creating a wedge between measured GDP and consumption-relevant income (Ghost GDP).  
- **Mechanism:** The consumption function ties aggregate consumption to labor income with high MPC. As labor share \(s_L\) falls, the consumption-to-output ratio drops, reducing monetary velocity (\(V \propto C/Y\)). This velocity collapse can cause demand deficiency even if output growth remains positive.  
- **Core assumption:** Fiscal transfers (τ) are zero; the consumption function’s MPC gap (\(2\bar{c}-1 > 0\)) is stable.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] Mentions “Ghost GDP: velocity collapse as labor share falls.”  
  - [section] Section 2.3 (pages 11–12) derives Proposition 2, showing \(V_t\) strictly increasing in \(s_{L,t}\) and declining as \(s_{L,t}\) falls.  
  - [corpus] No direct corpus support; velocity collapse is a monetary phenomenon not covered in neighbor papers.  
- **Break condition:** Velocity does not collapse if labor share remains stable (strong reinstatement) or if large fiscal transfers (τ) offset the consumption drop. Falsification: AI-exposed sectors show price deflation exceeding nominal wage compression, raising real incomes (H8), which would mitigate the consumption drop.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** AI agents that reduce information frictions compress intermediary margins (e.g., SaaS, payments, consulting) toward pure logistics costs, triggering repricing and revenue shocks that propagate to financial markets.  
- **Mechanism:** Intermediaries extract margin \(m(\phi) = m_0 + \gamma_m \cdot \phi\), where \(\phi\) is information friction. AI reduces friction exponentially: \(\phi(A_t) = \phi_0 \cdot e^{-\gamma_\phi A_t}\). As AI capability grows, \(\phi\) falls, squeezing margins toward the floor \(m_0\). The revenue at risk is \(\Delta R_t = \gamma_m \cdot (\phi_0 - \phi(A_t)) \cdot Q_t\), where \(Q_t\) is transaction volume.  
- **Core assumption:** The friction-margin relationship is linear and friction declines smoothly with AI capability; regulatory/ switching-cost moats are weak or absent.  
- **Evidence anchors:**  
  - [abstract] Lists “intermediation margin compression” as a key mechanism.  
  - [section] Section 2.4 (pages 13–16) formalizes the mechanism, includes Table 1 on sector exposure, and Proposition 3.  
  - [corpus] No direct corpus anchors; neighbor papers focus on stress testing methodology or AI labor impacts, not intermediation economics.  
- **Break condition:** Margin compression is limited if friction floors \(\phi_{min}\) are high (regulatory barriers, trust requirements) or if AI’s friction-reduction rate \(\gamma_\phi\) is low. Falsification: Payments interchange remains stable and stablecoin settlement stays below 5% of card volume (H10), indicating limited friction reduction in that sector.

## Foundational Learning

- **Concept: Task-based production model**  
  - **Why needed here:** The paper’s core analysis builds on Acemoglu and Autor’s framework, decomposing production into tasks automated by AI or performed by labor. This allows formalizing displacement, reinstatement, and the labor share dynamics.  
  - **Quick check question:** How does the CES aggregation over tasks differ from a standard Cobb-Douglas production function with aggregate labor and capital?

- **Concept: Monetary velocity and the quantity equation**  
  - **Why needed here:** The Ghost GDP mechanism relies on velocity (\(V = PY/M\)) falling when labor share falls, decoupling GDP from consumption. Understanding velocity’s link to the consumption-output ratio is crucial.  
  - **Quick check question:** In \(MV = PY\), if M is fixed and Y grows but V falls, what happens to P? How does a falling V affect real economic activity if prices are sticky?

- **Concept: Intermediation economics (friction-based margins)**  
  - **Why needed here:** The intermediation collapse mechanism treats intermediary margins as a spread over information frictions. This connects AI’s ability to reduce frictions to sector-level revenue shocks and financial stability.  
  - **Quick check question:** Why might intermediary margins not compress to zero even if AI eliminates all information frictions? (Consider regulatory floors, trust, and logistics costs.)

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map:**
- Production block: Task-based CES output \(Y_t\), with automation rate \(s_{eff}(t) = d(t) \cdot s(t)\).  
- AI dynamics: Capability \(A_t\) (\(g_A\)), cost \(c_{AI,t}\) (\(g_c\)), diffusion \(d(t) = \bar{d}/(1+e^{-\kappa(t-t_0)})\).  
- Labor market: Displacement \(d(t) \cdot f(g_A)\), reinstatement \(\rho_t = \rho_0 + \eta \cdot A_t^{\alpha_\rho}\), feedback \(\beta \cdot \pi_t\).  
- Demand: Consumption \(C_t = \bar{c} \cdot s_{L,t} \cdot Y_t + (1-\bar{c})(1-s_{L,t}) \cdot Y_t\); aggregate demand \(D_t = C_t + I_t + G_t\).  
- Profit margin: \(\pi_t = (E_t[D_t] - D_t)/E_t[D_t]\).  
- Velocity: \(V_t \propto C_t/Y_t + \tau_t\).  
- Intermediation: Margin \(m_t = m_0 + \gamma_m \cdot \phi(A_t)\), friction \(\phi(A_t) = \phi_0 \cdot e^{-\gamma_\phi A_t}\).  
- Financial transmission: Income shock by quintile \(\delta_q\), DSCR \(r_i\), default probability \(P_D(r_i)\).  
- Consumption concentration: Top-quintile share \(\chi\), exposure \(\delta_5\).

**Critical path:**
1. Input AI parameters (\(g_A\), \(\kappa\), \(\bar{d}\)) → compute \(s_{eff}(t)\) and direct displacement.
2. Solve labor share \(s_{L,t}\) dynamics (Equation 7) with feedback (\(\beta\)) and reinstatement (\(\rho_t\)).
3. From \(s_{L,t}\), compute velocity \(V_t\) (Proposition 2) and consumption ratio \(C/Y\).
4. For financial contagion, apply income shocks by quintile (\(\delta_q\)) to default model (Proposition 5).
5. For intermediation, compute margin compression \(m_t\) and revenue at risk \(\Delta R_t\).
6. Policy experiments: introduce \(\tau(t)\) with lag \(\ell\) and re-solve.

**Design tradeoffs:**
- Task aggregation ignores within-occupation heterogeneity (Remark 1).
- Consumption function forces MPC_K = \(1-\bar{c}\), which is conservative (strengthens feedback).
- Reinstatement \(\rho_t\) is parametric; exact institutional adaptation not microfounded.
- Friction reduction assumes exponential decay; real frictions may have sticky floors.
- Representative-firm production abstracts from firm-level heterogeneity in AI adoption.

**Failure signatures:**
- Labor share stable despite high g_A → displacement spiral offset (strong ρ or weak β).
- Velocity stable/rising while \(s_L\) falls → Ghost GDP weak (maybe deflation dominant).
- Intermediary margins stable in high-friction sectors (e.g., SaaS, consulting) → intermediation collapse not operating.
- Wage growth in AI-exposed occupations not lower than controls → displacement channel falsified (H1).
- New AI-complementary job growth matches historical automation → strong reinstatement (H9).

**First 3 experiments:**
1. **Baseline validation:** \(g_A=0.05\), \(\rho_0=0.002\), \(\tau=0\). Verify labor share declines gradually (~historical trend), velocity stable, consumption ratio stable.
2. **Rapid scenario:** \(g_A=0.20\), same \(\rho_0\). Check for sharp labor share drop (>20% in 10 years), velocity decline, consumption drop comparable to 2008 crisis (5–12%).
3. **Reinstatement sensitivity:** Under \(g_A=0.20\), vary \(\rho_0\) from 0 to 0.01. Identify threshold where labor share stabilizes (transition from explosive to stable regime). Test Proposition 1’s condition.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question:** What is the causal effect of AI exposure on wage growth and employment, and how can identification challenges be addressed?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] The authors state that "an ideal identification strategy would exploit exogenous variation in AI tool deployment... we note this as a priority for future work" (Section 3.2).
- **Why unresolved:** Current evidence is correlational with pre-trends, preventing causal claims needed for the displacement spiral mechanism.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Natural experiments from staggered AI adoption, using difference-in-differences with firm or geographic variation.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** How does AI impact wage distribution within occupations, and what are the aggregate labor share implications?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Remark 1 notes that within-occupation heterogeneity is an empirical question not resolved by the model, as AI may compress wages without reducing employment.
- **Why unresolved:** The aggregate model assumes homogeneous effects, but experimental evidence shows heterogeneous impacts by worker experience, which could alter displacement predictions.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Micro wage data by occupation, experience, and AI exposure to measure within-job income shifts and aggregate them.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** What is the true magnitude and responsiveness of the task reinstatement rate (ρ) from AI, and under what conditions does it dominate displacement?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] Proposition 1 and Remark 2 state that the regime (reinstatement vs. explosive) depends on ρ, and the authors call the prevailing regime an empirical question.
- **Why unresolved:** Calibration uses historical estimates, but generative AI may differ; institutional factors affecting ρ are uncertain.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Longitudinal tracking of new task creation and job categories in AI-exposed sectors, linked to policy changes, to estimate ρ empirically.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Does AI-induced price deflation offset nominal income losses, and what is the net effect on real consumption by income quintile?
- **Basis in paper:** [inferred] Remark 5 describes deflation as a competing channel that could negate Ghost GDP, but the stress test assumes it does not dominate.
- **Why unresolved:** The consumption outcome hinges on the balance between income compression and deflation, but empirical evidence on AI's price effects is lacking.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Sectoral analysis of price changes in AI-automated industries versus wage data, combined with consumption surveys by income group to measure real income effects.

## Limitations

- **Parameter calibration gaps:** Critical parameters (feedback coefficient β, displacement sensitivity f(g_A), reinstatement baseline ρ₀) lack transparent empirical calibration, risking misclassification of regimes.
- **Exposure measurement uncertainty:** The composite occupation-level AI exposure score is opaque and not reproducible from cited sources, undermining financial contagion predictions.
- **Velocity mechanism realism:** The Ghost GDP link rests on a stable MPC gap and ignores price adjustment; if wages and prices adjust, velocity collapse may not occur as modeled.

## Confidence

- **Displacement spiral & regime threshold:** Medium (theoretically clear but limited empirical validation)
- **Ghost GDP (velocity collapse):** Low (derivation algebraic but restrictive assumptions; no corpus evidence)
- **Intermediation margin compression:** Low (plausible but lacking microfoundation and direct evidence)

## Next Checks

1. **Parameter sensitivity & threshold replication:** Re-run the baseline simulation while systematically varying β and ρ₀ across plausible ranges (β ∈ [0, 0.5], ρ₀ ∈ [0, 0.01]). Map the resulting \(g^*_A(\rho)\) threshold surface and compare to the paper’s reported >0.20 value. A shift of >0.05 in threshold would invalidate the explosive regime claim for \(g_A=0.20\).
2. **Exposure score reconstruction & wage test:** Replicate the occupation exposure index using original Felten et al. (2021) and Eloundou et al. (2023) data with a transparent weighting scheme (e.g., principal component of patent, task, and LLM scores). Regress 2019–2023 wage growth on this reconstructed exposure, controlling for occupation fixed effects. Failure to find a significant negative coefficient (H1) falsifies the displacement channel.
3. **Velocity deflation decomposition:** Using FRED data (1990–2023), estimate a panel regression of \(V_t\) on \(s_{L,t}\), inflation (\(\pi_t\)), and uncertainty indices. Test whether the coefficient on \(s_L\) remains negative and significant when \(\pi_t\) is included. If inflation absorbs the \(s_L\) effect (i.e., coefficient becomes insignificant), the Ghost GDP link is spurious—real activity may be unchanged but nominal variables shift (H8).
