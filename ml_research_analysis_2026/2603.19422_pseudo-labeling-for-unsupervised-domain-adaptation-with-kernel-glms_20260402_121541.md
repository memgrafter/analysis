# Pseudo-Labeling for Unsupervised Domain Adaptation with Kernel GLMs

## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.19422  
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19422  
- Reference count: 40  
- Primary result: The proposed pseudo‑labeling method achieves minimax‑optimal rates for covariate shift in kernel GLMs and demonstrates empirical superiority over naive baselines on synthetic and real data.

## Executive Summary
Unsupervised domain adaptation under covariate shift—where the marginal distribution of features differs between labeled source and unlabeled target domains—is studied for kernel generalized linear models (GLMs) including ridge‑regularized linear, logistic, and Poisson regression. The authors introduce a pseudo‑labeling algorithm that splits the source data into two batches. One batch trains a grid of candidate models; the other trains an imputation model that generates soft pseudo‑labels for the unlabeled target covariates. The candidate minimizing the empirical pseudo‑target risk is selected. Theoretical analysis yields non‑asymptotic excess‑risk bounds featuring an effective labeled sample size \(n_{\text{eff}}\) that captures the alignment between source and target covariance operators in the reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS). The bound attains the minimax optimal rate \(n_{\text{eff}}^{-2\alpha/(2\alpha+1)}\) under smoothness \(\alpha\), and the additional error from pseudo‑labeling is shown to be negligible (order \(1/n_{\text{eff}} + 1/n_0\)). Empirical evaluations on synthetic data yield an error exponent of 0.546, close to the oracle (0.523) and well above the naive baseline (0.439). On the UCI Raisin dataset, pseudo‑labeling reduces mean log‑loss to 0.428 (95% CI [0.412,0.444]) compared to 0.502 [0.463,0.541] for the naive method and 0.373 [0.361,0.385] for the oracle over 100 seeds.

## Method Summary
The core algorithm (Algorithm 1) proceeds as follows. Given labeled source data \(\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^n\) and unlabeled target covariates \(\{x^0_i\}_{i=1}^{n_0}\), the source is randomly split into two subsets \(D_1\) (size \(\lfloor n/2\rfloor\)) and \(D_2\) (the remainder). On \(D_1\) we train a family of kernel GLMs \(\{f_\lambda\}\) for a grid of ridge regularization parameters \(\Lambda\) (geometrically spaced from \(\Theta(n^{-1})\) to \(\Theta(1)\)). Concurrently, on \(D_2\) we fit an imputation model (also a kernel GLM) with a fixed, small regularization \(\tilde{\lambda} \asymp n^{-1}\) (undersmoothing). For each target covariate \(x^0_i\), the imputer produces a soft pseudo‑label \(\tilde{y}^0_i = a'(g(x^0_i))\), where \(a'\) is the derivative of the canonical link function of the GLM (e.g., sigmoid for logistic) and \(g\) is the imputer. Each candidate \(f_\lambda\) is then evaluated on the target set using the empirical pseudo‑target risk \(R_{\text{pseudo}}(f_\lambda) = \frac{1}{n_0}\sum_{i=1}^{n_0} \big( a(f_\lambda(x^0_i)) - \tilde{y}^0_i f_\lambda(x^0_i) \big)\). The candidate minimizing this risk is chosen; optionally the chosen model can be refitted on the full source data. The use of a separate hold‑out set \(D_2\) ensures that the pseudo‑labels are approximately independent of the candidates, which is key for the theoretical guarantees.

## Key Results
- **Theoretical guarantee**: Excess risk is bounded by an order that depends on the effective sample size \(n_{\text{eff}}\), defined via the alignment of source and target covariance operators in the RKHS. The rate \(n_{\text{eff}}^{-2\alpha/(2\alpha+1)}\) matches the minimax optimal rate for smooth functions, and the pseudo‑label error overhead is negligible.
- **Synthetic experiment**: Pseudo‑labeling achieves an error exponent of 0.546, compared to 0.523 for the oracle and 0.439 for the naive baseline.
- **Raisin dataset**: Over 100 seeds, pseudo‑labeling obtains a mean log‑loss of 0.428 (95% CI [0.412, 0.444]), while the naive method yields 0.502 [0.463, 0.541] and the oracle 0.373 [0.361, 0.385].

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The pseudo‑labeling method works because it effectively decouples model selection from estimation error. By training the imputation model on an independent batch \(D_2\), the soft pseudo‑labels serve as unbiased (up to approximation error) estimates of the target conditional expectation \(\mathbb{E}[Y|X=x^0]\). The effective sample size \(n_{\text{eff}}\) emerges from the analysis of the covariance operator alignment: the more the source and target covariate distributions overlap in the RKHS, the larger the effective information contributed by each labeled source example when evaluating risk on the target. The theoretical bound shows that with undersmoothing (\(\tilde{\lambda} \asymp n^{-1}\)), the error due to noise in the pseudo‑labels is of order \(1/n_{\text{eff}} + 1/n_0\), which vanishes as \(n_{\text{eff}}\) grows. Consequently, the risk of the selected candidate essentially matches that of an oracle with access to true target labels, up to the minimax‑optimal estimation rate for the underlying function class. This mechanism also explains why the naive baseline (training directly on source without adaptation) suffers: it ignores the misalignment of covariate distributions, leading to an effective sample size that is simply \(n\) rather than \(n_{\text{eff}}\), which can be much smaller under severe shift.

## Architecture Onboarding
The method can be viewed as a processing pipeline:

Source Data → Split into D1 and D2 → D1 → TrainCandidates (grid Λ) → {f_λ}; D2 → TrainImputer (λ̃) → Imputer; Target Data → Imputer → PseudoLabels → EvaluatePseudoRisk({f_λ}) → SelectBestCandidate → FinalModel.

**Critical path**: Split source → parallel training of candidates and imputer → generation of pseudo‑labels for target → risk evaluation → selection. A failure at any stage (e.g., CG non‑convergence, poor imputer accuracy) propagates to the final model.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Split ratio \(n_1 : n_2\): Larger D1 improves candidate quality; larger D2 improves imputer quality. The analysis uses roughly equal sizes.
- Grid size for \(\Lambda\): Finer grid reduces model selection error but increases computational cost.
- Regularization \(\tilde{\lambda}\) for imputer: Must be small (undersmoothing) to avoid bias in pseudo‑labels, yet not so small that imputation overfits.
- Use of soft pseudo‑labels (canonical link derivative) vs. hard class labels: Soft labels preserve probabilistic information and are more stable.
- Solver choice: Conjugate gradient vs. low‑rank approximations for large n; tradeoffs between accuracy and memory.

**Failure signatures**:
- Imputer over‑smoothing (\(\tilde{\lambda}\) too large): pseudo‑target risk curves become flat or monotonically decreasing, pseudo‑labels cluster near 0/1.
- CG divergence or stagnation: high relative residual, excessive iterations; may indicate ill‑conditioning from very small regularization.
- Model mis‑specification: negligible performance gap between pseudo‑labeling and naive baseline, suggesting the RKHS class cannot represent the true function or the alignment condition fails.

**Three first experiments**:
1. **Synthetic baseline**: Run the algorithm with \(n=8000\), compute excess risk, and verify it outperforms the naive source‑only model.
2. **Imputer regularization sweep**: Vary \(\tilde{\lambda}\) over orders of magnitude to confirm the predicted U‑shaped pseudo‑risk curve and identify a suitable undersmoothed regime.
3. **Conjugate gradient sanity check**: For large \(n\) (e.g., 32,000), monitor CG convergence; adjust preconditioner or λ bounds if residual fails to drop below tolerance.

## Limitations
- The exact conjugate gradient solver configuration (max iterations, tolerance, preconditioner) is not specified, hindering exact reproducibility for large‑scale experiments.
- The Raisin dataset experiment deviates from Algorithm 1 by employing repeated cross‑validation and an unusually small imputation penalty (\(\tilde{\lambda}=10^{-4}\)), which may contribute to the observed gains and limits direct comparison.
- Theoretical assumptions (smoothness exponent \(\alpha\), covariance operator alignment) may be violated in real applications, potentially degrading the promised minimax‑optimal behavior.
