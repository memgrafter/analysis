# What Capable Agents Must Know: Selection Theorems for Robust Decision-Making under Uncertainty

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2603.02491  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02491  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: Low average-case regret on action-conditioned prediction tasks forces internal states to be predictive, yielding quantitative bounds on interventional kernel recovery in fully observed settings and on belief-like state necessity in partially observed settings.

## Executive Summary
The paper investigates the necessary internal structures an agent must possess to make robust decisions under uncertainty. While classical results identify sufficient statistics like belief states for optimal behavior, the converse—what is forced by performance requirements—remains less understood. The authors prove quantitative selection theorems that link an agent’s regret on action‑conditioned prediction tasks to the information encoded in its internal state. Their approach reduces predictive modeling to binary betting and uses a margin‑based regret decomposition to constrain the probability of suboptimal bets. In fully observed environments, low regret implies the internal state approximates the interventional transition kernel with a bound scaling as \( t_\gamma \sqrt{p(1-p)/n} + \bar{\delta}/c(\gamma) + O(1/n) \). Under partial observability, the state must be belief‑like, satisfying a quantitative no‑aliasing condition. For structured task families, low regret selects for modular, regime‑sensitive, and representationally invariant structures. However, only Pearl‑level 2 interventions are recoverable, not full counterfactuals.

## Method Summary
The core method transforms each action‑conditioned prediction into a binary bet on a future observation. The agent’s internal state induces a predictive distribution; the regret measures deviation from the optimal predictor. By decomposing regret with a margin parameter \(\gamma\), the authors bound the probability that the agent makes a suboptimal bet. This bound directly ties predictive accuracy to the distinguishability of histories under different actions. In fully observed MDPs, the analysis forces the state to approximate the interventional transition kernel, with the error dominated by the average normalized regret \(\bar{\delta}\) and a margin constant \(c(\gamma)\). In partially observable settings, the state must separate latent states that yield different future distributions (up to the margin), yielding a quantitative no‑aliasing principle. For families with factored dynamics, the regret minimization implicitly encourages modular decomposition and invariance across task regimes.

## Key Results
- **Fully observed MDPs**: Low average‑case regret forces the internal state to approximate the interventional transition kernel, with mean absolute error bounded by \( t_\gamma \sqrt{p(1-p)/n} + \bar{\delta}/c(\gamma) + O(1/n) \).
- **Partial observability**: The internal state must be belief‑like, satisfying a quantitative no‑aliasing condition that distinguishes latent states leading to different futures.
- **Structured task families**: Regret minimization selects for modular, regime‑sensitive, and invariant representations; only Pearl‑level 2 interventions are recoverable, not level‑3 counterfactuals.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The reduction to binary betting introduces a margin \(\gamma\) that acts as a buffer around the decision boundary. Any internal state that fails to separate outcomes with sufficient margin will cause a non‑negligible probability of a suboptimal bet. The average normalized regret \(\bar{\delta}\) then bounds this probability via a margin‑based decomposition. Consequently, if the agent maintains low regret across many action‑conditioned predictions, its state must partition histories into classes that are nearly separable with respect to the true interventional outcomes. This forces the state to encode sufficient statistics of the transition kernel. In the partially observable case, the state must resolve aliasing by distinguishing latent states that produce different observation distributions under some action. The analysis thus tightly connects performance‑based selection to representational requirements.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component Map**  
Perception & Action Encoder → State Update Module (recurrent or belief update) → Predictive Head (outputs distribution over next observation) → Policy Head (selects action). For modular tasks: State Update may contain separate sub‑modules per factor.

**Critical Path**  
Raw observations and chosen actions are encoded into features, integrated by the State Update to maintain an internal history summary. The Predictive Head uses this state to forecast the next observation under the recent action; the forecast is compared to the true outcome to compute loss/regret. The Policy Head may use the same state to decide actions, closing the loop.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- State capacity vs. generalization: Larger states capture finer distinctions but risk over‑fitting; smaller states may fail to resolve necessary aliasing.  
- End‑to‑end vs. modular: Modular architectures (separate predictor and policy) can improve interpretability and sample efficiency but might hinder joint optimization.  
- Margin \(\gamma\) choice: Larger \(\gamma\) yields safer bounds but looser guarantees; smaller \(\gamma\) tightens bounds but requires more precise state separation.

**Failure Signatures**  
- High prediction error on specific action‑observation pairs despite low overall regret (regional aliasing).  
- Internal state insensitive to observations that should be distinguishable (e.g., failing to change when latent state flips).  
- Absence of modular structure in multi‑task settings (representations entangle independent factors).

**First Experiments**  
1. **Fully observed MDP test**: Train a tabular or neural agent on a small MDP. Extract predictions for all (state, action) pairs and compute mean absolute error against the true transition matrix. Expect error to decrease with training and correlate with accumulated regret.  
2. **Partial observability and aliasing test**: Use a POMDP with known latent state (e.g., a grid with ambiguous observations). After training, decode the latent state from the agent’s internal state (e.g., train a classifier). High accuracy indicates a belief‑like state.  
3. **Modularity test**: Construct a multi‑task environment where dynamics factorize into independent subsystems (e.g., two separate MDPs combined). Train a multi‑task agent and analyze its internal representation (e.g., via PCA or probing). Look for disentangled factors corresponding to the subsystems.

## Limitations
- Assumes a fixed margin parameter \(\gamma\) without practical guidance on setting its value.  
- Theoretical results are restricted to finite MDPs; scalability to continuous/high‑dimensional state spaces remains open.  
- Selection claims for modularity and invariance rely on idealized regret minimization across all actions, which is unrealistic for bounded agents.
