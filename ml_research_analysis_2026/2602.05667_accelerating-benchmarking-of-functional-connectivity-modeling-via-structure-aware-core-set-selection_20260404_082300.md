# Accelerating Benchmarking of Functional Connectivity Modeling via Structure-aware Core-set Selection

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.05667
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05667
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** SCLCS preserves the ground-truth ranking of 130 functional connectivity operators using only 10% of the REST-meta-MDD dataset and outperforms nine state-of-the-art core-set selection methods by up to 23.2% in nDCG@k.

## Executive Summary
Benchmarking hundreds of functional connectivity (FC) modeling methods on large fMRI datasets is computationally prohibitive. This paper introduces Structure-aware Contrastive Learning for Core-set Selection (SCLCS), a framework that selects a small, representative subset of samples to preserve the relative performance ranking of connectivity operators. SCLCS employs an adaptive Transformer encoder to learn each sample's FC structure, a Structural Perturbation Score (SPS) to identify samples with stable structural representations during training, and a density-balanced sampling strategy to ensure diversity. Evaluated on the large REST-meta-MDD dataset (904 subjects), SCLCS maintains the full-data SPI ranking with just 10% of the samples and significantly surpasses existing core-set selection techniques. The work makes large-scale, pre-analysis comparisons of FC models computationally feasible.

## Method Summary
SCLCS begins by training an adaptive Transformer encoder on fMRI samples using contrastive learning supervised by subject identity. The encoder produces a per-sample connectivity structure matrix via multi-head attention with learnable fusion weights. After training, the Structural Perturbation Score (SPS) is computed for each sample by averaging the Frobenius norm differences between its attention matrices across consecutive epochs, quantifying representation stability. For core-set selection, two variants are considered: (1) selecting the top‑k samples with lowest SPS, and (2) first filtering a stable pool (bottom (1‑β) quantile) then applying density‑balanced sampling via kernel density estimation to counteract top‑k bias and promote diversity. The resulting core-set is used to compute a battery of SPIs; their ranking (induced by discriminability scores) is compared to the full‑data ranking via nDCG@k. The entire pipeline enables efficient benchmarking of hundreds of operators.

## Key Results
- SCLCS preserves the full‑dataset ranking of 130 SPIs using only 10% of the REST‑meta‑MDD data (high nDCG@k).
- It outperforms nine state‑of‑the‑art core‑set selection methods by up to 23.2% in ranking consistency (nDCG@k).
- The density‑balanced variant (SCLCS_Dense) further improves performance at moderate core‑set sizes (e.g., 30%) and yields better subject/class balance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
*   **Claim:** An adaptive Transformer encoder can learn a stable, expressive representation of each fMRI sample's functional connectivity (FC) structure suitable for a broad class of SPIs.
*   **Mechanism:** The adaptive multi‑head attention mechanism (Equation 6) learns sample‑specific weights α_h to combine head‑wise attention matrices. This avoids the structural interference caused by uniform averaging (Theorem 1), enabling the model to approximate continuous SPI mappings (Theorem 2). The model is trained via contrastive learning (Equation 17) using subject identity as a supervisory signal to encourage stable, person‑specific "brain fingerprint" representations.
*   **Core assumption:** The space of continuous SPI operators on compact fMRI data domains can be uniformly approximated by the proposed adaptive attention architecture.
*   **Evidence anchors:** [abstract]: "...SCLCS first uses an adaptive Transformer to learn each sample’s unique FC structure... we show this encoder has universal approximation capacity for continuous SPI mappings (Theorem 2)." [section 4.1]: Detailed derivation of the adaptive attention mechanism and proof of Theorem 2 showing it forms a universal approximator for row‑stochastic target matrices. [corpus]: Weak/missing direct evidence. Neighboring work on graph transformers for dynamic fMRI (D‑GATNet) validates the general use of attention for connectivity, but SCLCS's adaptive fusion and specific stability objective are novel.
*   **Break condition:** The approximation guarantee (Theorem 2) holds for continuous targets on compact domains. Discontinuous or thresholded SPI outputs (e.g., binary masks from strict statistical thresholds) are not directly covered, though the paper suggests continuous relaxations may be used.

### Mechanism 2
*   **Claim:** The Structural Perturbation Score (SPS) effectively identifies samples with stable, foundational FC archetypes by quantifying the fluctuation of the learned structural representation across training epochs.
*   **Mechanism:** SPS (Equation 10) computes the average Frobenius norm difference between an input sample's structure matrix A(X) at consecutive training epochs. Samples whose internal representation converges quickly and remains stable (low SPS) are hypothesized to be purer representations of core connectivity patterns (Proposition 1 links lower expected perturbation to a more concentrated mixture of underlying prototypes).
*   **Core assumption:** The sequence of per‑sample structural representations {A^{(e)}(X)} is a stationary and ergodic process with finite mean under the model's training dynamics, allowing the sample mean SPS to consistently estimate its long‑term stability (Lemma 1).
*   **Evidence anchors:** [abstract]: "...introduces a novel Structural Perturbation Score (SPS) to quantify the stability of these learned structures during training, identifying samples that represent foundational connectivity archetypes." [section 4.2]: Defines SPS formally and links its expected value to the Gini impurity of an underlying mixture of prototype structures (Proposition 1). Lemma 1 provides a theoretical consistency guarantee under stationarity/ergodicity. [section 5.3]: Qualitative visualization (Figure 3) showing low‑SPS samples stabilizing quickly versus high‑SPS samples fluctuating. [corpus]: Weak/missing direct evidence. Similar concepts exist in example forgetting (Toneva et al.), but SPS targets *structural* stability in a learned representation space, not classifier confidence.
*   **Break condition:** If the training process does not converge to a stable point for all samples or if the SPS calculation window [1, L] is insufficient for the dynamics to mix, the score may be noisy and not a reliable indicator of long‑term archetype purity.

### Mechanism 3
*   **Claim:** Density‑balanced sampling corrects the representational bias of naive top‑k SPS selection, ensuring the final core‑set is both structurally robust *and* distributionally representative across the dataset's structural diversity.
*   **Mechanism:** After filtering to a pool of stable (low‑SPS) candidates, kernel density estimation (KDE) on the SPS distribution identifies sparse regions. The final subset is sampled with probability inversely proportional to local density (Equation 16). This upweights structurally distinctive samples from low‑density pockets, counteracting the tendency of top‑k to oversample dense clusters of typical patterns (Theorem 3 proving persistent bias).
*   **Core assumption:** The SPS distribution within the stable pool is a meaningful proxy for the diversity of FC structure. Under‑sampled, low‑density regions in SPS space contain samples with structurally unique patterns critical for preserving SPI ranking diversity.
*   **Evidence anchors:** [abstract]: "...further introduce a density‑balanced sampling strategy as a necessary correction to promote diversity, ensuring the final core‑set is both structurally robust and distributionally representative." [section 4.3]: Formalizes the density‑aware scheme using KDE. Theorem 3 mathematically proves that top‑k selection introduces a persistent proportional bias. Theorem 5 provides an expectation bound on ranking preservation error under ε‑coverage. [section 5.2]: Empirical results show `SCLCS_Dense` often outperforms the basic `SCLCS` (top‑k only), especially at moderate ratios (0.3) and on the MDD diagnosis task. [corpus]: Weak/missing direct evidence. The concept relates to core‑set diversity principles (e.g., Sener & Savarese), but is applied here to balance a stability score rather than feature space.
*   **Break condition:** The KDE bandwidth parameter is sensitive. If set too large, distinct structural subtypes merge; if too small, noise may create false sparse regions. The assumption that SPS density correlates with structural diversity may fail if SPS is insensitive to a relevant but latent dimension of variation.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept: Functional Connectivity (FC) & Statistical Pairwise Interactions (SPIs)**  
    *Why needed here:* The entire problem is defined around benchmarking the hundreds of methods (SPIs) used to compute FC matrices from fMRI time series. Understanding that an SPI is a mapping S: X → R^{N×N} from a time series to a connectivity matrix is fundamental.  
    *Quick check question:* If an SPI computes a Pearson correlation matrix, what is the nature of its output (e.g., row‑stochastic, symmetric)? How does this relate to the target class in Theorem 2?

*   **Concept: Core‑set Selection for Model Evaluation (vs. Training)**  
    *Why needed here:* The paper re‑frames core‑set selection from its typical use as a training proxy for *one* model to a tool for preserving the *relative ranking* of *hundreds* of models. This shifts the objective from minimizing loss to preserving rank order (nDCG@k).  
    *Quick check question:* Why would a core‑set selected by a method like AUM (which optimizes for training one classifier) likely fail to preserve the ranking of 100+ different SPIs?

*   **Concept: Rank Preservation Metrics (nDCG@k)**  
    *Why needed here:* The core optimization goal (Equation 1) and all experimental results use nDCG@k to measure the discrepancy between the full‑dataset and core‑set induced SPI rankings. Understanding that this metric rewards placing top‑ranked SPIs highly in the core‑set ranking is key.  
    *Quick check question:* How would a core‑set that perfectly preserves the top‑5 SPI order but scrambles ranks 6‑100 score on nDCG@5 vs. nDCG@20?

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:** Input fMRI sample X → Encoder (linear projections to Q_h, K_h for H heads) → Multi‑head Attention (A_h = softmax(Q_h K_h^T / √d)) → Adaptive Fusion (learnable α_h to combine A_h into final structure A) → (during training: store A per epoch) → SPS Calculator (compute mean Frobenius norm Δ between consecutive A) → Filter low‑SPS pool → Density‑Balanced Sampler (KDE on SPS, sample inversely proportional to density) → Core‑set. Contrastive loss (subject ID pairs) trains the encoder.
*   **Critical path:** Data flow: X → Encoder produces A(X) → during L training epochs, save A^{(e)}(X) for all samples → after training, compute SPS(X) = (1/L) Σ_e ||A^{(e)}(X) − A^{(e−1)}(X)||_F^2 → select core‑set via low‑SPS top‑k or density‑balanced sampling from stable pool.
*   **Design tradeoffs:**  
    *Encoder complexity vs. stability:* More heads increase approximation capacity (Theorem 2) but may increase optimization noise, harming SPS consistency; adaptive fusion α mitigates interference.  
    *SPS computation vs. resolution:* More epochs L give a better stability estimate (Lemma 1) but increase storage/IO; the paper uses L=200.  
    *Top‑k simplicity vs. density correction:* Pure low‑SPS top‑k is simple but theoretically biased (Theorem 3); density balancing improves ranking but introduces KDE bandwidth hyperparameter.
*   **Failure signatures:**  
    *Poor ranking preservation (low nDCG@k):* Encoder failed to learn SPI‑agnostic structure, SPS is not a valid stability proxy, or core‑set is imbalanced. Diagnose by checking contrastive loss convergence, SPS correlations with metadata (e.g., site), and subject/class balance plots.  
    *High variance across seeds:* Indicates SPS ties or unstable KDE; verify bandwidth choice.  
    *Degradation at higher core‑set ratios:* Added samples may be harmful due to top‑k bias pulling in dense clusters of dissimilar‑relative samples; SCLCS_Dense should mitigate.
*   **First 3 experiments:**  
    1. Validate encoder as SPI probe: Train frozen encoder to approximate FC matrices of diverse SPIs (e.g., Pearson, PLV, Transfer Entropy); confirm low test MSE.  
    2. Visualize SPS dynamics: For random samples, plot per‑epoch structural change ||A^{(e)} − A^{(e−1)}||_F; expect low‑SPS samples stabilize quickly, high‑SPS fluctuate. Visualize A^{(e)} at early/mid/late epochs.  
    3. Ablate density balancing: For MDD task, compare SCLCS vs. SCLCS_Dense at ratios 0.1, 0.3, 0.5; measure nDCG@k curves and subject/class balance; expect Dense to improve at 0.3 and provide better balance.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **How does SPS theoretically guarantee preservation of SPI rankings?** The authors state that quantifying how approximation error propagates to ranking preservation is a future direction; the precise link between training dynamics and ranking preservation remains unclear. Evidence: A direct theoretical bound relating SPS to nDCG is absent.
*   **Can an adaptive sampling strategy automatically choose between low‑SPS top‑k and density‑balanced sampling?** The optimal choice is task‑dependent; the authors propose developing heuristics or a meta‑learning framework to select the variant automatically based on dataset characteristics.
*   **Does SCLCS generalize to task‑fMRI and heterogeneous multisite datasets without retraining?** SCLCS was developed on resting‑state DMN‑33 data; task‑evoked dynamics and different acquisition protocols may alter captured structural patterns. Future studies should test on task‑fMRI (e.g., HCP), alternative parcellations (e.g., AAL), and multisite data with varying scanners/TRs.
*   **How do SPI properties (e.g., computational speed, linearity) influence ranking‑preservation difficulty?** The paper evaluated a fixed set of SPIs; it is unknown whether some SPI families are inherently harder to rank from limited data, or whether speed correlates with ranking stability. Systematic simulations varying SPI characteristics are needed.

## Limitations
*   **Hyperparameter sensitivity:** Final values for Transformer dimensions, SPS epoch window L, KDE bandwidth, density filter β, and contrastive temperature are not fully specified and were grid‑searched, blocking exact replication and affecting stability across datasets.
*   **Baseline implementation gaps:** Critical details for reproducing competing core‑set methods (e.g., EL2N epoch range, AUM averaging period, EVA window, BOSS feature similarity) are omitted, and the supervision (subject ID vs. MDD) used for all baselines is ambiguous.
*   **Theoretical assumptions in practice:** Theorems rely on continuous SPI outputs and stationary training dynamics; many SPIs are discontinuous (e.g., thresholded graphs), and SPS consistency (Lemma 1) may fail if training is non‑stationary or the observation window L is too short.

## Confidence
*   SCLCS framework preserves SPI rankings with small core‑sets: **High** (empirically validated on REST‑meta‑MD, outperforms baselines).
*   Adaptive Transformer universal approximation for continuous SPIs: **Medium** (theoretical proof under continuity assumption; not all SPIs are continuous).
*   SPS as stability proxy identifying foundational archetypes: **Medium** (empirical correlation supported, but theoretical link to ranking preservation is open; break conditions exist).
*   Density‑balanced sampling corrects top‑k bias: **High** (theoretical proof of bias and empirical improvements observed).
