# CTS-Bench: Benchmarking Graph Coarsening Trade-offs for GNNs in Clock Tree Synthesis

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.19330
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19330
- **Reference count:** 31
- **Primary result:** Clustering reduces GPU memory by up to 17.2× and speeds training 3×, but catastrophically degrades clock skew prediction (negative R²), while raw graphs preserve accuracy, revealing a fundamental trade‑off.

## Executive Summary
Clock Tree Synthesis (CTS) is a critical and computationally intensive step in VLSI design. Predicting post‑CTS quality‑of‑result (QoR) metrics with machine learning offers the potential for faster design exploration, but Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) operating on raw gate‑level netlists face prohibitive memory and runtime costs. This paper introduces **CTS‑Bench**, a large‑scale benchmark comprising 4,860 designs from five RTL architectures. Each design is provided in two representations: a raw gate‑level graph and a clustered graph produced by a physics‑aware coarsening algorithm that compresses the graph by an average factor of 13.3×. The benchmark evaluates three GNN backbones (GCN, GraphSAGE, GATv2) using a multi‑modal fusion architecture that also ingests placement and CTS knob settings. Results confirm substantial efficiency gains from clustering—up to 17.2× reduction in GPU memory and 3× faster training—while highlighting a severe fidelity collapse for the CTS‑critical metric of clock skew. Clustered models frequently achieve negative R² scores, especially under zero‑shot evaluation on an unseen architecture, whereas raw models maintain robust accuracy. The core finding is that generic graph coarsening, despite its benefits for global metrics and scalability, fundamentally compromises local learning objectives that depend on fine‑grained spatial relationships. The authors release the benchmark to encourage research on task‑aware coarsening methods that co‑optimize efficiency and accuracy.

## Method Summary
CTS‑Bench is generated using an open‑source EDA flow (OpenROAD) with the TritonCTS tool. For each of five RTL designs (PicoRV32, AES, SHA256, EthMAC, Zipdiv), the pipeline first performs placement with randomized parameters to create diverse placement databases. From each placement, 10 CTS variants are generated using randomized CTS knobs. For every placement, a **raw graph** is constructed where nodes represent standard cells (features: coordinates, cell type, activity) and edges represent logical nets. A three‑step clustering algorithm—BFS traversal, spread filtering, and gravity‑merge—produces a **clustered graph** with macro‑nodes (cluster centroid, spread, aggregated activity) and edges between clusters. Static Timing Analysis (STA) on the CTS results yields 15 QoR metrics, including clock skew, total power, and wirelength. The machine‑learning task is to predict these metrics from the graph representation and the placement/CTS knobs. Models use a multi‑modal fusion architecture: a GNN backbone (GCN, GraphSAGE, or GATv2) processes the graph, its global mean pooling is concatenated with MLP embeddings of the knobs, and a fully‑connected head regresses the targets. Training uses an 80/20 split of the first four architectures (seen) and evaluates zero‑shot performance on Zipdiv (unseen). Resource usage (GPU memory, time) and prediction accuracy (MAE, Spatial R²) are measured.

## Key Results
- **Efficiency:** Clustering compresses graphs by ~13.3×, reducing GPU memory by up to 17.2× and training time by 3×.
- **Skew prediction collapse:** On seen designs, clustered models yield negative Spatial R² for clock skew, while raw models achieve ~0.90. On unseen Zipdiv, raw models retain modest positive R², but clustered models plummet further negative.
- **Metric sensitivity:** Power and wirelength MAEs are similar between raw and clustered models, showing global metrics are resilient to coarsening, whereas skew—a local metric—is not.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Graph coarsening reduces computational resource consumption (GPU memory and training time) for GNNs on gate‑level netlists.
- **Mechanism:** The clustering algorithm compresses the raw graph by an average factor of ~13.3×, aggregating nodes into macro‑clusters. This directly reduces the number of graph nodes and edges processed per batch, lowering memory allocation for feature tensors and message‑passing operations, and decreasing the number of neural network computations per forward/backward pass.
- **Core assumption:** The architectural and connectivity information preserved in the clustered macro‑node features and aggregated edges is sufficient for learning global, aggregate design metrics (e.g., total power, wirelength), which are less sensitive to fine‑grained spatial topology.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "...clustering reduces GPU memory usage by up to 17.2× and accelerates training by up to 3×."
  - [section 4.1] Presents Figure 4 quantifying VRAM reduction and time acceleration across GCN, SAGE, GATv2.
  - [corpus] Weak direct evidence; neighboring papers discuss general efficiency gains of coarsening but are not specific to EDA/CTS.
- **Break condition:** The compression ratio is insufficient to enable training on target hardware, or the clustering process itself introduces computational overhead that negates the gains from a smaller graph.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Generic graph clustering catastrophically degrades predictive fidelity for the *local* CTS metric of clock skew, often yielding models worse than a mean baseline (negative R²).
- **Mechanism:** The clustering algorithm merges physically proximate logic gates into macro‑nodes based on electrical compatibility and spatial gravity. This process discards the precise geometric coordinates and individual cell identities of flip‑flops and their immediate logic, which are critical for modeling the point‑to‑point clock path delays that determine skew. The macro‑node centroid and spread features are insufficient to reconstruct these fine‑grained spatial relationships.
- **Core assumption:** Clock skew is a hyper‑local metric strongly dependent on the exact Manhattan distances and topological paths between specific flip‑flop pairs, information irretrievably lost during aggressive node aggregation.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "...for the CTS‑critical metric of clock skew, clustered models consistently exhibit poor fidelity, frequently yielding negative R² scores..."
  - [section 4.2] Reports Spatial R² for Skew dropping below 0 for clustered models on seen data and as low as -2.23 on unseen (Zipdiv), contrasting with ~0.90 for raw models.
  - [corpus] Weak; neighboring papers study coarsening for general GNN scalability, not metric‑specific fidelity collapse in EDA.
- **Break condition:** A clustering method is developed that explicitly preserves pairwise distances or routing topology between critical clock sinks (flip‑flops), leading to measurable improvement in Skew R².

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** The observed fidelity gap is a symptom of a fundamental *task misalignment*; the coarsening objective (compression, preserving global spectral/spatial properties) is not aligned with the learning objective (predicting a local physical design metric sensitive to specific micro‑architectural details).
- **Mechanism:** The clustering heuristic is "physics‑aware" (respecting electrical domains, gravity vectors) but not *task‑aware*. It optimizes for a generic, compressed representation of the placement graph, not for the preservation of the specific subgraph structures and edge features (exact Manhattan distances) that a GNN would need to accurately regress clock skew. The loss of fine‑grained node/edge attributes creates an representational bottleneck that prevents the GNN from learning the underlying CTS dynamics.
- **Core assumption:** Generic clustering objectives (e.g., minimizing conductance, preserving community structure) are not guaranteed to preserve the inductive biases required for a given downstream prediction task. Success requires co‑design of the clustering criterion with the downstream loss function.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract] "...generic graph clustering techniques can fundamentally compromise CTS learning objectives, even when global physical metrics remain unchanged."
  - [section 4.2] The "Representation Struggle" paragraph notes that even Raw graphs have non‑trivial Skew error, but the clustered collapse is worse, implying the representation itself is compromised.
  - [section 5 (Conclusion/Future Work)] Explicitly states the need for "task‑aware and learned graph coarsening methods that optimize clustering for CTS‑relevant objectives."
- **Break condition:** A coarsening method is introduced that uses the prediction loss (or a surrogate) as a regularizer during the clustering/embedding process, demonstrably improving task‑specific accuracy at a given compression ratio.

## Foundational Learning
- **Graph Neural Networks (GNNs)**
  - *Why needed here:* The entire benchmark evaluates GNN backbones (GCN, GraphSAGE, GATv2) for regression on graph‑structured circuit netlists. Understanding message passing, graph convolutions, and readout pooling (global mean pooling) is essential to interpret the model architecture table and performance results.
  - *Quick check:* How does the choice of GNN aggregation function (mean vs. sum vs. attention) potentially impact learning on a graph with highly variable node degrees like a clock tree?

- **Graph Coarsening / Clustering**
  - *Why needed here:* The core trade‑off is defined by the two provided graph representations (Raw vs. Clustered). One must understand the algorithmic process (BFS, spread filtering, gravity‑merge) and its consequences (node/edge reduction, feature aggregation) to analyze the accuracy‑efficiency trade‑off.
  - *Quick check:* What specific structural information is lost when a cluster merges multiple flip‑flops and their logic, and why would that be critical for a skew predictor?

- **Electronic Design Automation (EDA) - Clock Tree Synthesis (CTS)**
  - *Why needed here:* The domain context defines the problem: predicting skew, power, wirelength post‑CTS. Understanding that CTS balances clock distribution under fixed placement constraints explains why placement‑topology‑sensitive metrics (skew) are harder to model than global aggregates (power).
  - *Quick check:* Why is clock skew considered a "local" metric in this context, and how does the physical placement of standard cells influence it?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  Data Generator (OpenROAD/TritonCTS) → Graph Constructor (Raw & Clustered) → Label Extractor (STA, QoR metrics) → Model Trainer (Multi‑Modal Fusion) → Evaluator (MAE, R², VRAM, time)

- **Critical path:** Raw Placement Netlist → Graph Constructor (Raw & Clustered) → Model Trainer → Evaluator → Analysis of MAE/R² vs. VRAM/time trade‑off, focusing on the Skew metric disparity.

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - **Accuracy vs. Efficiency:** Directly quantified: ~17.2× VRAM reduction and 3× speedup from clustering, at the cost of local metric (Skew) fidelity.
  - **Global vs. Local Metrics:** Global metrics (Power, Wirelength MAE) are resilient to clustering; local metric (Skew R²) collapses.
  - **Seen vs. Unseen Generalization:** Both Raw and Clustered models show performance drop on Zipdiv, but Clustered fails catastrophically (negative R²), indicating distribution shift sensitivity magnified by representation loss.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - **Primary:** Negative Spatial R² for clock skew prediction on clustered graphs, especially in zero‑shot (Unseen) evaluation.
  - **Secondary:** A significant gap between Raw and Clustered model MAE for Skew, while MAEs for Power/Wirelength remain comparable.
  - **Systemic:** Inability of any GNN backbone to achieve high Skew R² (>0.5) even on Raw graphs for the Unseen design, indicating potential lack of diverse training data.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. **Reproduce Efficiency Gains:** Train a single GNN backbone (e.g., GCN) on both Raw and Clustered graphs for one design (e.g., PicoRV32). Measure and report peak VRAM and epoch time to confirm ~13.3× node reduction correlates with ~17× VRAM reduction and ~3× speedup.
  2. **Isolate Fidelity Gap:** For the same model/design, evaluate prediction MAE and R² for all three targets (Skew, Power, Wirelength) on the *Seen* validation set. Verify that Clustered graph MAE for Power/Wirelength is comparable to Raw, while Skew MAE is similar or worse but Skew R² drops dramatically.
  3. **Test Zero‑Shot Collapse:** Take the best model from Exp. 2 (trained on 4 seen architectures) and evaluate it on the *entirely held‑out* Zipdiv design (500 data points). Confirm that for Skew, Clustered model R² is significantly negative (e.g., <-1.0), while Raw model R² is low but positive/near‑zero, demonstrating the amplification of representation failure under distribution shift.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **How can graph coarsening techniques be designed to specifically preserve clock skew prediction accuracy while retaining significant memory and runtime benefits?**  
   *Basis:* Section 5 identifies the need for task‑aware and learned graph coarsening that optimizes for CTS‑relevant objectives such as skew and power.  
   *Why unresolved:* Current clustering uses heuristic rules (BFS, gravity) that do not incorporate the downstream skew prediction loss, leading to negative R² scores.  
   *Resolving evidence:* A new coarsening method evaluated on CTS‑Bench that maintains skew R² > 0.5 at a compression ratio comparable to ~13×, demonstrating that task‑specific coarsening can mitigate the trade‑off.

2. **What specific graph‑theoretic or geometric properties of gate‑level netlists are most predictive of clock skew, and how are they altered or discarded by the proposed clustering algorithm?**  
   *Basis:* Section 4.2 reveals that clustered models collapse on skew (R² < 0) despite similar MAE for power/wirelength, and Section 2.2 describes clustering that aggregates logic around flip‑flops, potentially losing fine‑grained spatial relationships.  
   *Why unresolved:* The paper hypothesizes that skew relies on "fine‑grained resolution," but does not analyze which preserved or lost features (e.g., hop counts between flip‑flops, distribution of intermediate logic) are critical.  
   *Resolving evidence:* An ablation study on CTS‑Bench that systematically modifies clustering steps (atomic cluster size, merge thresholds) and measures resulting changes in skew prediction error, pinpointing the exact structural attributes that must be retained.

3. **Why do global metrics (total power, wirelength) remain relatively robust to graph coarsening, while highly local metrics (clock skew) suffer catastrophic fidelity loss?**  
   *Basis:* Section 4.2 shows clustered models achieve Power MAE ~0.10 and Wirelength MAE ~0.10 (close to raw baselines of ~0.06), but Skew MAE degrades slightly (0.17 vs 0.16) with R² collapsing to negative values, indicating a fundamental difference in metric sensitivity.  
   *Why unresolved:* The paper suggests skew requires distinguishing "high‑skew and low‑skew regions" due to local flop‑to‑flop distances, but does not investigate whether this is due to node aggregation, loss of intermediate routing paths, or geometric smoothing during clustering.  
   *Resolving evidence:* A correlation analysis between coarsening‑induced distortions in specific graph features (e.g., edge weight distributions, cluster centroid variance) and the prediction error for each metric, revealing the distinct dependency patterns.

4. **What topological characteristics of circuit designs (e.g., scale, flip‑flop density, hierarchy) most significantly limit zero‑shot generalization of CTS prediction models, and how should benchmarks be varied to stress‑test these factors?**  
   *Basis:* Section 4.3 reports that on the unseen Zipdiv architecture, raw models achieve only R² ≈ 0.0–0.2 for skew, while clustered models fail (R² negative). The paper explicitly states the training set may lack "structural diversity" and that Zipdiv is "much smaller and distinct."  
   *Why unresolved:* The benchmark includes five architectures but does not characterize which topological features (e.g., number of flip‑flops, fan‑out distribution, aspect ratio) drive the generalization gap.  
   *Resolving evidence:* A statistical analysis extracting topological descriptors from all CTS‑Bench designs, correlating them with model performance (especially on Zipdiv), to identify key factors (e.g., flip‑flop‑to‑logic ratio, spatial clustering) that should be expanded in future benchmark versions.

5. **Can placement‑stage features be used to predict the expected degradation in clock skew fidelity caused by graph clustering, enabling placement‑aware coarsening that proactively minimizes accuracy loss?**  
   *Basis:* Section 5 (Future Work) proposes "tighter integration between placement and Clock Tree Synthesis by training fast proxy models that provide clock‑aware feedback during placement," implying the need to forecast clustering impacts.  
   *Why unresolved:* Clustering is currently applied post‑placement without evaluating how placement topology interacts with coarsening to affect skew. The Gap Score measures placement‑CTS overhead but ignores additional loss from clustering abstraction.  
   *Resolving evidence:* A model trained on CTS‑Bench that ingests raw placement features (e.g., flip‑flop coordinates, logic distribution metrics) and predicts the drop in skew R² if a standard clustering algorithm is applied, allowing for adaptive clustering parameter selection based on placement characteristics.

## Limitations
- The clustering algorithm is a specific heuristic; conclusions about "generic" clustering may not hold for methods that explicitly preserve local structure.
- The dataset spans only 5 architectures; zero‑shot failures could stem from insufficient design diversity rather than representation loss alone.
- No empirical validation of task‑aware coarsening; the suggested remedy remains theoretical.

## Confidence
- **Efficiency gains from coarsening:** High
- **Local metric (skew) collapse with clustering:** Medium
- **Task misalignment as root cause:** Medium
