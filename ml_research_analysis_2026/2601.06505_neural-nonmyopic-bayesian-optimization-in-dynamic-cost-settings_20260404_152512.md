# Neural Nonmyopic Bayesian Optimization in Dynamic Cost Settings

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID**: 2601.06505  
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06505  
- **Reference count**: 40  
- **Primary result**: LookaHES significantly outperforms myopic and nonmyopic baselines in dynamic-cost settings, achieving ~2.7 vs. ~1.5 fluorescence units in protein sequence design and consistent gains across nine synthetic benchmarks and two real-world tasks.

## Executive Summary
The paper presents LookaHES, a nonmyopic Bayesian optimization (BO) framework designed for dynamic cost environments where the cost of a query depends on prior actions. By integrating multi‑step H‑Entropy Search (EHIG), neural policy optimization (including large language models), and pathwise sampling, LookaHES enables long‑horizon planning (beyond 20 steps) without exponential computational complexity. Empirical evaluations on synthetic functions (2D–8D), geospatial optimization with NASA night‑light imagery, and constrained protein sequence design demonstrate robust superiority over strong baselines, with dramatic improvements in protein fluorescence under edit‑cost constraints.

## Method Summary
LookaHES models BO as a Markov Decision Process with a history‑dependent cost function. Each iteration updates a Gaussian process surrogate on accumulated data. A neural policy ξ is then optimized to maximize the expected multi‑step H‑information gain (EHIG) minus a cost penalty, using pathwise sampling of GP functions to approximate rollouts with a constant number of restarts. The policy—implemented as an RNN, transformer, or autoregressive LLM—maps the query history to the next action. After training, the policy outputs the next query (optionally with exploration noise). This amortized planning approach avoids the exponential blowup of traditional nonmyopic methods while accounting for dynamic costs such as travel distance or edit distance.

## Key Results
- LookaHES outperforms myopic (EI, KG) and nonmyopic (MSL) baselines on nine synthetic benchmarks (2D–8D) and two real‑world tasks (geospatial optimization, protein design).
- In protein sequence design with a spotlight edit cost, myopic methods plateau at approximately 1.5 fluorescence units, while LookaHES achieves approximately 2.7 units.
- The method scales to 20‑step lookahead horizons and 50‑dimensional problems without exponential computational overhead.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: Long‑Horizon Planning with Constant Complexity
- **Claim:** LookaHES achieves long‑horizon planning (20+ steps) without exponential complexity by combining neural policies with pathwise sampling.  
- **Mechanism:** The neural policy ξ parameterizes the decision mapping from history to next query, reducing optimization variables from exponential in the horizon to constant (policy parameters). Pathwise sampling draws GP function samples and updates them along trajectories, approximating the posterior predictive with a fixed number of restarts instead of an exponential number of rollouts.  
- **Core assumption:** The policy can represent near‑optimal decisions and pathwise sampling provides low‑bias estimates of the expected H‑information gain.  
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “enabling long‑horizon planning (beyond 20 steps) without the exponential complexity of existing nonmyopic methods.”; [section 3.4] “employ a neural network policy as it can reduce the number of optimization parameters to a constant with respect to the horizon.”; [section 3.5] “we use pathwise sampling … to reduce the number of trajectories from exponential to constant.”  
- **Break condition:** If policy capacity is insufficient or pathwise sampling introduces high bias, the scalability advantage collapses and performance degrades.

### Mechanism 2: Dynamic Cost Integration
- **Claim:** LookaHES incorporates dynamic, history‑dependent query costs into the acquisition function, enabling cost‑aware nonmyopic optimization.  
- **Mechanism:** The EHIG acquisition embeds a cost penalty: $\inf_a \{ \mathbb{E}[\ell(f,a)] + \lambda\,c(x_{1:t}, a) \}$, where $c$ depends on the query history (Markovian or non‑Markovian). Lookahead expands the receptive field to plan for future cost‑driven accessibility.  
- **Core assumption:** The cost function $c$ is known and accurately models true costs; the Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$ appropriately trades off reward versus cost.  
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “where evaluation costs vary with prior actions”; [section 3.3] formal definitions of cost structures and receptive field concept; [section 1] examples: travel distance, edit distance; Table 1 cost taxonomy.  
- **Break condition:** Misspecified cost models lead to queries that incur real costs much higher than anticipated, breaking budget constraints or causing suboptimal cumulative regret.

### Mechanism 3: LLM‑Based Policies for Combinatorial Spaces
- **Claim:** LookaHES leverages large language models (LLMs) as policies to navigate structured combinatorial action spaces (e.g., protein sequences) with semantic constraints.  
- **Mechanism:** The policy ξ is instantiated as an autoregressive LLM fine‑tuned via PPO. Given the history (previous sequences and observations), it outputs the next edit. The reward signal comes from the nonmyopic acquisition (EHIG), and dynamic costs are enforced during rollout via regeneration.  
- **Core assumption:** LLMs can model the distribution over valid actions and be fine‑tuned with RL to optimize the acquisition objective; domain constraints can be effectively incorporated.  
- **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] “The key innovation is the integration of neural policies, including large language models, to effectively navigate structured, combinatorial action spaces such as protein sequences.”; [section 3.4] “Predicting the next query is autoregressive, which makes language models suitable policies.”; [section 4.2] protein design with LLaMa‑3.2‑3B, achieving ~2.7 fluorescence vs. ~1.5 for myopic methods.  
- **Break condition:** If the LLM lacks domain knowledge or RL fine‑tuning is unstable, the policy may generate invalid or low‑utility edits, especially under hard constraints.

## Architecture Onboarding
**Component map**:  
Data → Surrogate (GP) → Pathwise sampler → EHIG acquisition (with cost c) → Neural policy ξ ← Optimizer (Adam/PPO) → Next query

**Critical path** (per BO iteration):
1. Update surrogate model with dataset $D_{t-1}$.
2. Optimize policy ξₜ: sample pathwise functions, generate $L$‑step trajectory via ξ, compute EHIG (including cost), backpropagate.
3. Select next query $x_t = ξ_t(\text{history})$ (optionally add exploration noise).
4. Query black‑box, observe $y_t$, update $D_t = D_{t-1} \cup \{(x_t,y_t)\}$.

**Design tradeoffs**:
- Lookahead horizon $L$: longer improves planning but increases rollout cost; pathwise sampling keeps it linear per restart.
- Policy architecture: LLMs capture semantics but need SFT/RL and more compute; smaller RNNs are faster but less expressive.
- Surrogate model: GPs are flexible but $O(n^3)$; Bayesian linear regression scales but may underfit nonlinear landscapes.
- Cost modeling: requires accurate domain knowledge; misspecification leads to budget overruns.
- Exploration: adding noise (e.g., von Mises‑Fisher) avoids local optima but may destabilize training.

**Failure signatures**:
- Invalid actions (e.g., protein edits violating spotlight) → enforce constraints via regeneration.
- Poor surrogate fit → check validation performance; consider kernel choice or more data.
- Short lookahead → myopic stagnation in local optima.
- PPO divergence → adjust learning rate, batch size, or reward scaling.
- Cost overrun → revisit cost model or λ tuning.

**First 3 experiments**:
1. 2D Ackley with Euclidean cost, $L=5$: verify LookaHES matches MSL and outperforms EI/KG.
2. Discrete SynGP (20 categories per dim), spotlight cost, $L=4$: compare LookaHES vs. EI and MSL (random search) to confirm scalability in combinatorial spaces.
3. Protein design with LLaMa‑3.2‑3B, spotlight cost, 12 edits: show fluorescence ~2.7 vs. ~1.5 for myopic methods.

## Limitations
- Exact random seeds and initialization details are undisclosed, preventing precise replication of reported error bars.
- Hyperparameters for the protein task’s SFT and PPO stages (e.g., LoRA rank, PPO clip ratio) are incompletely specified, risking nonidentical performance.
- The method assumes a known accurate cost model; real‑world cost misspecification could break budget constraints or degrade planning.
