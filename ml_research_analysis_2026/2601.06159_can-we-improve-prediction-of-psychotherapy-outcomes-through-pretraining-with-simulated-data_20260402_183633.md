# Can we Improve Prediction of Psychotherapy Outcomes Through Pretraining With Simulated Data?

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2601.06159
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06159
- **Reference count:** 0
- **Primary result:** Pretraining random forests with literature-based simulated data did not consistently improve prediction of OCD psychotherapy outcomes; Study 1 showed a non-significant descriptive improvement, while Study 2 found standard training significantly superior.

## Executive Summary
This study investigated whether pretraining machine learning models with simulated data derived from published summary statistics could enhance the prediction of psychotherapy outcomes for obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD). The authors generated synthetic datasets by sampling from multivariate normal distributions parameterized by means, standard deviations, and correlations extracted from the literature for responder and non-responder groups. These simulated data were used to pretrain a subset of trees in a random forest ensemble, with the remaining trees fine-tuned on real patient data. Two empirical studies were conducted: Study 1 predicted response using a set of six key variables, while Study 2 predicted remission after a systematic literature search that yielded only seven studies for five variables. Results indicated that the pretraining approach did not yield consistent improvements. In Study 1, a pretrained model with 50% simulated weight achieved a descriptively higher balanced accuracy (0.549) than a standard model (0.526), but the difference was not statistically significant. In Study 2, the standard model significantly outperformed all pretrained models (0.65 vs. 0.637). The authors attributed the inconsistent results to scarcity of suitable literature statistics and oversimplified simulation assumptions, such as multivariate normality and lack of nonlinear interactions.

## Method Summary
The methodology centered on creating simulated datasets from summary statistics reported in published studies. For each outcome (response/remission), the authors identified variables with reported means, standard deviations, and correlations for responder and non-responder subgroups. They computed weighted averages across studies to derive population-level parameters, then sampled 500 cases per class from corresponding multivariate normal distributions. Two simulation variants were used: one with only the key variables ("six-features") and another that included all variables present in the real dataset ("all-features"), where non-key variables were simulated from the training set's empirical distributions. The real dataset comprised 463 OCD patients with 504 variables, preprocessed via one-hot encoding, multiple imputation (MICE) on the training split only, and outcome labeling based on reliable change indices or remission criteria. To address class imbalance (72% responders), the training data were balanced using SMOTENC. A random forest classifier was then trained with a portion of trees (20%, 50%, or 100% weight) trained solely on the simulated data ("pretrained trees") and the remainder fine-tuned on the (optionally SMOTE'd) real training data. Hyperparameters (max_features, min_samples_leaf, max_samples) were tuned via grid search with 5-fold cross-validation for each configuration. Model performance was evaluated using balanced accuracy as the primary metric, across 100 Monte Carlo cross-validation (MCCV) iterations with random train/test splits. Statistical comparisons between models were performed using corrected resampled t-tests.

## Key Results
- Study 1 (response prediction): The pretrained model with 50% simulated weight achieved a balanced accuracy of 0.549, compared to 0.526 for the standard model (t(99)=0.89, p=0.19) – a non-significant descriptive improvement.
- Study 2 (remission prediction): The standard model significantly outperformed all pretrained models, achieving a balanced accuracy of 0.65 versus 0.637 for the best pretrained model (corrected resampled t-test, p<0.05).
- In an unbalanced analysis without SMOTE in Study 1, 100% pretrained weight significantly improved balanced accuracy (0.571 vs. 0.516, p=0.02), but this benefit disappeared when SMOTE balancing was applied, suggesting the simulated data primarily aided minority class information.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Noise Averaging and Bias Reduction
Simulated data derived from heterogeneous literature statistics aims to average out noise and reduce sampling bias from the local real dataset. By sampling from multivariate normal distributions parameterized by weighted means and covariances from multiple studies, the model is exposed to a broader, generalized representation of responder/non-responder characteristics, which serves as a prior integrated during fine-tuning. The core assumption is that population-level statistics from diverse studies are transferable to the target OCD population and capture stable signal separate from study-specific noise. Evidence includes the authors' introduction highlighting the integration of heterogeneous samples to average out noise, and interim discussion noting that pretraining provided information about the non-responder class in the unbalanced analysis. The break condition occurs when literature statistics are scarce or non-transferable, causing misrepresentation and performance drop, as seen in Study 2.

### Mechanism 2: Implicit Oversampling for Minority Class
Pretraining with balanced simulated data acts as an implicit, data-driven form of oversampling for the minority class, improving specificity without SMOTE's overfitting risks. The pretrained trees, trained on simulated data free from majority-class dominance, learn decision boundaries that contribute a class-balanced prior to the ensemble, counteracting bias in the fine-tuned trees. The core assumption is that the simulated minority class distributions are sufficiently realistic. Evidence: In Study 1's unbalanced analysis, 100% pretrained weight significantly improved balanced accuracy without SMOTE, but the benefit vanished with SMOTE balancing, and the authors noted the simulated data provided valuable minority-class signal. Break condition: Poorly estimated or non-transferable simulated minority distributions harm performance, evidenced by Study 2 where all pretrained models underperformed the standard model.

### Mechanism 3: Robustness via Weighted Ensembling (Failed)
The authors hypothesized that partial pretraining (low simulated weight) would allow fine-tuned trees to dominate in certain cases while pretrained trees stabilize ambiguous ones by reducing variance. The core assumption was that fine-tuned trees' uncertainty could be estimated and simulated data provides complementary lower-variance signal. However, this hypothesis was not supported: in Study 1, higher weights (50-100%) performed better, and the 20% weight condition was inferior. The mechanism failed, with break conditions including: simulated signal too weak/noisy, poor uncertainty calibration in real-data model, or severe distributional discordance causing pretrained trees to introduce noise even in confident cases.

## Foundational Learning
#### Monte Carlo Cross Validation (MCCV)
Why needed here: Used to assess the stability and significance of performance differences across many competing pretraining configurations (varying features simulated, weight percentages) via 100 random data splits, providing a distribution of metrics for reliable statistical comparison.
Quick check question: How does MCCV differ from k-fold cross-validation in terms of randomness and repetition, and why is that important for comparing many similar model variants?

#### SMOTENC (Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique for Nominal and Continuous features)
Why needed here: The real dataset has a 72/28 responder/non-responder imbalance. SMOTENC was applied during training to balance classes before model fitting, preventing the model from learning a trivial majority-class rule and allowing fair evaluation of pretraining effects on minority-class prediction.
Quick check question: What is the core algorithmic step of SMOTE, and why is a variant like SMOTENC necessary when the dataset contains mixed data types (categorical and continuous)?

#### Simulation from Multivariate Normal Distribution (MVND) from Summary Statistics
Why needed here: The core data synthesis method. Given a vector of means (μ) and a covariance matrix (Σ) for responder and non-responder groups (sourced from literature), new synthetic cases can be generated. This is the only feasible way to create structured data from published descriptive statistics without raw data.
Quick check question: What are the two critical matrices/vectors needed to define an MVND, and how were they constructed from heterogeneous literature sources in this study (e.g., weighting by sample size)?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
Data Sources (Real patient dataset + Literature summary statistics) → Preprocessing Pipeline (One-hot encoding, MICE imputation, label creation) → Simulation Engine (Construct responder & non-responder MVNDs from literature; sample N=500 per class) → Pretraining Controller (Allocate tree count between simulated and real data by weight%) → Hyperparameter Tuner (Grid search with 5-fold CV) → Evaluation Engine (Predict on test, compute metrics, repeat via MCCV)

### Critical path
Real Data Split → Literature Extraction → Simulation Dataset Generation → [For each of 100 MCCV iterations] → Preprocess Real Train/Test → Balance Train with SMOTENC → Tune Hyperparameters on Train → Train Random Forest (split trees per weight) → Predict on Test → Record Metrics → Aggregate over 100 iterations → Statistical Comparison (corrected resampled t-test)

### Design tradeoffs
- Simulation fidelity vs. feasibility: Strict inclusion criteria yield more comparable statistics but drastically reduce usable variables/studies; looser criteria increase variables but risk distributional mismatch.
- Weight allocation: Low simulated weight (20%) minimizes influence of simulated data on confident predictions but may help ambiguous cases; high weight (100%) maximizes simulated signal but risks overriding real patterns if simulation is poor. The optimal weight is data-dependent.
- Feature space restriction: Simulating only key variables simplifies the problem but forces pretrained trees to learn in a lower-dimensional space than fine-tuned trees, potentially harming integration; simulating all variables mitigates this but relies on literature for key variable distributions.

### Failure signatures
1. Severe performance drop from Study 1 to Study 2, indicating insufficient/poor-quality literature statistics (only 7 studies for 5 variables in Study 2).
2. High variance in pretrained model performance across MCCV iterations, signaling unstable simulated signal.
3. Extreme sensitivity/specificity shifts in pretrained models (e.g., 100% weight model in Study 1 had sensitivity 0.420 vs. standard 0.924, but specificity 0.671 vs. 0.128), indicating threshold rebalancing without accuracy gain.
4. Divergence in optimal hyperparameters between simulated and real data, leading to incompatible tree biases when ensembled.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
#### Open Question 1
**Question:** Does increasing the number of variables simulated from literature summary statistics improve the predictive performance of pretrained models, or does simulating too many variables reduce performance due to inclusion of weakly informative or noisy statistics?  
**Basis:** The authors' exploratory analysis in Study 1 showed that adding MADRS yielded descriptively the best performance, and they recommend simulating "rather more than less potentially informative variables." However, systematic testing of variable quantity is lacking.  
**Why unresolved:** It is unclear whether larger variable sets consistently boost performance or introduce noise; the optimal balance between quantity and quality is unknown.  
**What evidence would resolve:** A systematic study comparing models pretrained with varying sets of variables (strict core vs. expanded sets), measuring performance while controlling for number and quality of source studies.

#### Open Question 2
**Question:** Can more realistic simulation methods—such as using empirically derived non-normal distributions (e.g., via kernel density estimation) and modeling nonlinear interactions—outperform the current MVND approach, especially when fewer high-quality studies are available?  
**Basis:** Authors identify multivariate normality as a key limitation and propose using empirically identified distributions (e.g., KDE) and modeling nonlinear interactions.  
**Why unresolved:** The current simplicity is a limitation, but it's unclear whether relaxing normality or adding interactions would improve performance given the scarcity of detailed statistics; more complex simulations require higher-order moments rarely reported.  
**What evidence would resolve:** Comparative studies where simulated data are generated using alternative distributions (estimated from available statistics or target dataset) and including pairwise interaction terms, compared to MVND across different levels of literature data.

#### Open Question 3
**Question:** For which psychological disorders and outcome definitions (e.g., response vs. remission) is the pretraining approach most likely to yield a significant benefit, contingent on the availability of published subgroup summary statistics?  
**Basis:** The approach failed in Study 2 (remission) after a systematic search yielded only 7 studies for 5 variables, while Study 1 (response) used informally gathered stats from more studies. Authors speculate it might work for commonly investigated disorders with more descriptive data.  
**Why unresolved:** Contradictory results suggest success depends on quantity and quality of available literature statistics; thresholds for required studies/variables are unknown and may vary by disorder/outcome.  
**What evidence would resolve:** A meta-analytic investigation mapping volume and type of published subgroup statistics across disorders/outcomes, coupled with simulation studies to estimate minimum information density needed for pretraining to outperform standard training.

#### Open Question 4
**Question:** Under what conditions of real training data scarcity (small sample size, extreme class imbalance) does pretraining with simulated data provide a net benefit, and how does this interact with oversampling techniques like SMOTE?  
**Basis:** Authors state the approach seems best as a substitute for balancing, and in Study 1's unbalanced analysis pretraining significantly improved balanced accuracy, while balancing diminished the improvement. In Study 2, pretraining performed poorly despite likely high imbalance.  
**Why unresolved:** It is unclear whether pretraining is beneficial when real data are both scarce and imbalanced, or if it primarily mitigates oversampling artifacts; the interaction depends on sample size, imbalance ratio, and simulation quality.  
**What evidence would resolve:** Simulation studies systematically varying real training set size and class imbalance, combined with varying quantities of high-quality simulated data, to map parameter space where pretraining yields significant improvement over standard training (with/without SMOTE).

## Limitations
- Inconsistent performance: The approach failed to improve outcomes in Study 2 and showed only non-significant improvement in Study 1, suggesting limited generalizability, especially under data scarcity.
- Simulation fidelity constraints: Key implementation details for simulating non-key features are unspecified, and the assumption of multivariate normality may not reflect true data distributions, limiting the realism of synthetic data.
- Reproducibility gaps: Exact hyperparameter grids for some analyses and random seed strategies are not reported, hindering exact replication of results.

## Confidence
- Core claim (pretraining improves outcomes): Low confidence
- Simulation methodology: Medium confidence
- Reproducibility infrastructure: High confidence
