# HAPS: Hierarchical LLM Routing with Joint Architecture and Parameter Search

## Quick Facts
- **ArXiv ID:** 2601.05903  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05903  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Outperformed strong baselines in 5 of 6 settings on HotpotQA and MMLU, with gains up to 4.33% F1 and 6.00% accuracy.

## Executive Summary
Standard LLM routing selects only the architecture of a model for a given query, overlooking the potential of adapting model parameters. HAPS introduces a hierarchical routing framework that jointly searches over LLM architectures and their low‑rank adaptation (LoRA) parameters. A high‑level router (a LLaMA‑based classifier) chooses candidate architectures, while low‑level routers (per‑candidate MLPs) generate LoRA weights conditioned on the prompt and a shared backbone. The system employs reward‑augmented maximum likelihood estimation, blending policy gradients with MLE to optimize the hybrid discrete‑continuous action space. Experiments on HotpotQA and MMLU show HAPS beating strong baselines in 5 out of 6 configurations, achieving absolute gains of up to 4.33% F1 and 6.00% accuracy, while offering better performance‑cost trade‑offs and extending to mixed open/closed‑source model pools.

## Method Summary
HAPS consists of two routers that share a backbone: a high‑level router outputs a probability distribution over candidate LLM architectures; for each architecture, a low‑level hypernetwork (an MLP head attached to the shared backbone) generates LoRA matrices \(A\) and \(B\) tailored to the input prompt. The generated LoRA is injected into the final projection layer of the selected frozen LLM. During training, multi‑agent interaction trajectories are collected and a task metric (e.g., F1 or accuracy) forms a reward. The loss combines standard cross‑entropy for routing decisions with a reward‑weighted policy gradient, enabling joint optimization of discrete architecture choices and continuous LoRA generation. This hierarchical design enables fine‑grained, task‑specific adaptation while keeping most LLM parameters frozen, thus maintaining efficiency.

## Key Results
- SOTA on HotpotQA/MMLU: HAPS outperforms baselines in 5 of 6 settings.
- Absolute gains: up to +4.33% F1 on HotpotQA and +6.00% accuracy on MMLU.
- Superior performance‑efficiency trade‑offs and successful extension to mixed open/closed‑source model pools.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Jointly routing LLM architectures and their parameters via a hierarchical framework enables fine‑grained, task‑specific adaptation beyond discrete architecture selection alone.
- **Mechanism:** A high‑level router (LLaMA‑based classifier) selects the base LLM architecture for each agent. A low‑level router (per‑model MLP) then generates LoRA parameters tailored to the input prompt and selected architecture. This extends the routing decision space from purely discrete (architecture IDs) to a hybrid of discrete (architecture) and continuous (LoRA weights) choices.
- **Core assumption:** Optimal task performance depends on both the inherent capabilities of an LLM architecture *and* its parameter configuration for the specific query; decoupling these decisions forfeits potential synergy.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [abstract]: "jointly searches over model architectures and parameters... selecting LLM architectures while overlooking parameter settings, which are critical for task performance."
  - [section 3.1 & 3.2]: Formalizes the two‑phase routing and parameter generation network `sk(p;ϕk)`.
- **Break condition:** If the low‑level router's generated parameters fail to generalize to out‑of‑distribution prompts (e.g., due to insufficient training diversity or model heterogeneity), the continuous search space may become noisy, leading to performance collapse worse than fixed‑parameter baselines.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Sharing the backbone parameters `θ` between the high‑level router and all low‑level routers enables mutual enhancement by aligning their feature representations and facilitating co‑adaptation via joint gradients.
- **Mechanism:** The low‑level router `sk` is constructed by appending an MLP head to the high‑level router's backbone. Thus, `ϕk = {θ, γk}` where `θ` is shared. Gradients from the reward‑augmented objective flow through both routers, allowing the parameter generator to learn features relevant to architecture selection, and vice versa.
- **Core assumption:** Architecture selection and parameter generation are complementary sub‑tasks that benefit from a shared latent representation of the routing prompt; isolated optimization cannot capture this interdependence.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.3]: "partial parameters of the low‑level router are directly copied and extended from those of the high‑level router... share parameters between the two routers to mutually enhance their capabilities."
  - [section 4.4]: Ablation shows decoupled training (frozen high‑level router) degrades performance by up to 4.33% F1, confirming the necessity of shared gradients.
- **Break condition:** Severe negative transfer could occur if the optimal features for architecture classification and LoRA generation diverge significantly (e.g., for highly dissimilar model types), where forced parameter sharing harms both tasks.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Reward‑augmented maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) with on‑policy RL optimization effectively navigates the hybrid discrete‑continuous action space by scaling gradients with episode‑level task success.
- **Mechanism:** Instead of standard cross‑entropy loss on routing decisions, the loss `L_rl = -E[ R(ŷ, y) * log P(z, wz | p) ]` weights the log‑probability of chosen architectures `z` and generated parameters `wz` by the reward `R`. This encourages exploration of high‑reward (architecture, parameter) pairs and suppresses low‑reward ones, even when supervision is sparse.
- **Core assumption:** Token‑level log‑probabilities from the multi‑agent interaction can be effectively reused for policy gradient updates; the reward signal is sufficiently informative and low‑variance to stabilize joint training.
- **Evidence anchors:**
  - [section 3.3]: "jointly optimize θ and Γ using reward‑augmented maximum likelihood estimation... promotes architectures and parameters with higher R(ŷ, y)."
  - [algorithm 3]: The RL phase uses `L_rl = -ˆr * Σ log π` where `ˆr` is shaped from the task metric.
- **Break condition:** Noisy or sparse rewards (e.g., binary success/failure on complex tasks) may lead to high‑variance gradient estimates, causing training instability or convergence to poor local optima, especially with large action spaces.

*Corpus evidence note:* Neighboring papers focus on MoE routing, unlearning, and inference optimizations. While they validate the importance of router design, they do not directly address joint architecture‑parameter search or reward‑augmented RL for LLM routing, so corpus anchors are weak for these specific mechanisms.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: LLM Routing**  
  - **Why needed here:** HAPS extends classical routing (which selects a static model from a pool) by making parameters dynamic. Understanding the limitation of "black‑box" routing—where each LLM is treated as a fixed function—is essential to appreciate why parameter generation adds value.  
  - **Quick check question:** What is a key drawback of standard LLM routers like RouteLLM that HAPS aims to overcome?

- **Concept: Parameter‑Efficient Fine‑Tuning (PEFT) with LoRA**  
  - **Why needed here:** HAPS generates LoRA parameters (`A`, `B` matrices) per‑input. Knowing that LoRA injects low‑rank updates (`ΔW = αAB`) into frozen base weights explains why generating these matrices is feasible and efficient compared to full fine‑tuning.  
  - **Quick check question:** How does LoRA reduce the number of trainable parameters compared to full fine‑tuning?

- **Concept: Reinforcement Learning for Policy Optimization**  
  - **Why needed here:** The high‑level router is trained with a reward‑augmented policy gradient. Grasping how an episode reward (`ŷ` vs. ground truth) is used to weight token log‑likelihoods is key to understanding the training dynamics of HAPS.  
  - **Quick check question:** In the REINFORCE‑style loss `-ˆr * Σ log π`, what does each term represent and why multiply them?

- **Concept: Hypernetworks for Parameter Generation**  
  - **Why needed here:** The low‑level router is a hypernetwork that maps prompts to LoRA weights. This concept bridges continuous input conditioning to parameter generation, a core innovation of HAPS.  
  - **Quick check question:** What is a hypernetwork, and why is it suitable for generating model parameters conditioned on an input?

- **Concept: Hierarchical Decision‑Making**  
  - **Why needed here:** HAPS employs a two‑level hierarchy: first choose architecture, then generate its parameters. Understanding how hierarchical policies decompose complex decisions is necessary to analyze the design.  
  - **Quick check question:** In HAPS’s hierarchy, what does the high‑level router decide, and what does the low‑level router produce based on that decision?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**  
  - **High‑level router `r(p;θ)`**: LLaMA‑3.2‑1B‑Instruct backbone, multi‑label classifier outputting assignment vector `z`.  
  - **Low‑level routers `sk(p;ϕk)`**: Per‑candidate MLP (2 layers, 256 hidden) attached to shared `θ` backbone, outputting LoRA `A` and `B` matrices for model `mk`.  
  - **Parameter injection module**: Applies generated LoRA (`αAB`) to final `o_proj` layer of the selected base LLM.  
  - **Reward calculator**: Computes task metric (F1/accuracy) from generated answer vs. ground truth, forms `ŷ` for RL loss.  
  - **Vectorized dynamic LoRA executor**: Batched BMM for per‑sample LoRA application within a bucket (see Appendix E).

- **Critical path:**  
  1. Input prompt `p` (with role/task/problem/model list) enters high‑level router.  
  2. Router `r` classifies architecture indices `z` for Teacher/Student agents.  
  3. For each selected model `m_zi`, its dedicated `s_zi` takes `p` and generates LoRA parameters `wzi`.  
  4. LoRA weights injected into base model `m_zi` (frozen weights + `αAB`).  
  5. Multi‑agent interaction runs with LoRA‑adapted models; token log‑probs recorded.  
  6. Episode reward `R(ŷ, y)` computed; RL loss applied to both routers' parameters.

- **Design tradeoffs:**  
  - **Discrete vs. continuous search space:** Joint search increases expressiveness but complicates optimization (non‑differentiable discrete choice). HAPS uses policy gradients to bridge the gap.  
  - **Parameter sharing:** Sharing `θ` reduces total parameters and aligns features but risks interference if tasks (architecture selection vs. parameter generation) conflict.  
  - **LoRA depth:** Injecting into more layers (l2 vs. l3) can improve adaptation but risks overfitting or instability (see RQ4).  
  - **Inference cost:** Per‑input LoRA generation adds latency mitigated by vectorized BMM and request bucketing (Appendix E).

- **Failure signatures:**  
  - **High variance in validation reward during RL phase:** Indicates unstable policy updates; consider reward smoothing or higher `L` samples in warm‑up.  
  - **Performance drop when increasing LoRA depth beyond l2 for certain model pairs:** Suggests over‑parameterization; default to l2 as robust choice.  
  - **Decoupled variant (w/o parameter sharing) underperforms:** Confirms shared backbone is critical; if degradation is small, tasks may be too similar to benefit from sharing.  
  - **Mixed‑source routing fails to outperform all‑weak baseline:** Indicates generated LoRA for open‑source model cannot compensate for weak proprietary API; check `oz` indicator implementation or reward shaping.

- **First 3 experiments:**  
  1. **Overall performance (RQ1):** Run HAPS and baselines on HotpotQA/MMLU for all candidate pairs (L‑Q, M‑Q, L‑M). Verify HAPS achieves SOTA in 5/6 settings, with gains up to +4.33% F1. This validates the core claim of joint search superiority.  
  2. **Ablation of routers (RQ2):** Remove high‑level router (random/fixed assignment) or low‑level router (fixed parameters). Expect significant drops (e.g., >15% F1 on HotpotQA) confirming both components are necessary.  
  3. **Parameter sharing ablation (RQ3):** Freeze high‑level router after warm‑up, train low‑level router independently. Expect consistent degradation (up to -6.00% Acc on MMLU), proving shared gradient flow is essential for co‑adaptation.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- How does the low‑level router's generalization to out‑of‑distribution prompts depend on the diversity of the training distribution, and can data augmentation techniques improve robustness?
- What is the impact of candidate architecture heterogeneity on the effectiveness of parameter sharing in the hierarchical router? When does negative transfer occur?
- Can alternative reward shaping or auxiliary losses reduce the variance of policy gradients in the hybrid discrete‑continuous action space?
- Can the hierarchical routing framework be extended to dynamically adjust the rank of LoRA matrices based on task complexity?
- What are the theoretical limits of joint architecture‑parameter search in terms of expressive power versus optimization difficulty?

## Limitations
- Potential overfitting of the low‑level router to the training prompt distribution, leading to poor out‑of‑distribution generalization.
- Risk of negative transfer from forced parameter sharing if optimal features for architecture classification and LoRA generation diverge.
- Reward sparsity may cause high‑variance gradients and training instability.

## Next Checks
1. **OOD Generalization Test:** Evaluate HAPS on held‑out prompt distributions (e.g., paraphrased queries, new domains) and compare degradation to fixed‑parameter baselines; larger drop would confirm overfitting risk in low‑level router.
2. **Heterogeneity Stress Test:** Introduce highly dissimilar candidate models (e.g., varying sizes, architectures) into the pool; measure performance delta between shared‑parameter and decoupled‑router ablations to detect negative transfer.
3. **Reward Signal Ablation:** Replace dense task metrics with binary success/failure rewards; compare training stability (reward variance, sample efficiency) against the paper’s shaped reward to assess RL robustness.
