# The Potential of CoT for Reasoning: A Closer Look at Trace Dynamics

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.14903  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14903  
- **Reference count:** 36  
- **Primary result:** The conditional probability of a correct answer given a partial chain‑of‑thought (potential) is often non‑monotonic, and high‑quality reasoning steps from stronger models can be transferred to weaker ones via partial CoT prefixes, unlocking previously unsolvable problems.

## Executive Summary
This paper investigates which segments of chain‑of‑thought (CoT) reasoning actually contribute to correct answers in large language models. It introduces *potential*—the conditional probability of a correct final answer given a prefix of the CoT—and estimates it via Monte Carlo sampling. Applied to competition‑level mathematics and coding tasks, the analysis reveals that potential is frequently non‑monotonic: many traces contain “reasoning insights” (sharp positive spikes) or “reasoning tangents” (sharp drops). Furthermore, providing a weaker model with just 20% of a stronger model’s partial CoT dramatically improves its accuracy on previously unsolvable problems, demonstrating that key reasoning steps are transferable across models. These findings challenge a simple evidence‑accumulation view of CoT and suggest that reasoning quality can be localized to specific, model‑agnostic steps.

## Method Summary
The core method defines **potential** as \( \text{pot}(c_{<t}; x) = P(y = y^* \mid c_{<t}, x) \), the probability that a model produces the correct answer \(y^*\) given input \(x\) and a partial CoT prefix \(c_{<t}\). This conditional probability is estimated by sampling \(N\) continuations from the model conditioned on the prefix and computing the fraction that yield the correct answer (extracted, e.g., via regex `\\boxed{([^}]*)}`). For each problem, a full CoT is generated, split into \(C\) equal‑token chunks, and the potential is evaluated at the start of each chunk. Summary statistics—insight (increase >40% between consecutive chunks), tangent (drop >30% at any point), late spike (final guess), and monotonicity—are aggregated across many traces. Transferability tests then replace the initial portion of a weaker model’s prompt with a partial CoT from a stronger model and measure accuracy improvement.

## Key Results
- Potential curves are often non‑monotonic; only ~42% of CoTs on AIME‑2024 (Qwen2.5‑7B) are monotonic.  
- Approximately 62% of traces contain reasoning insights (sharp positive spikes), while ~9.5% contain reasoning tangents (sharp drops).  
- Late spikes (correct answers appearing only at the end) occur in up to 20% of trajectories, indicating reliance on lucky guesses.  
- Supplying as little as 20% of a strong model’s CoT to a weak model dramatically improves accuracy on unsolvable problems, demonstrating transferability of key steps.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Sharp increases in potential (reasoning insights) correspond to steps where the model acquires crucial problem‑solving knowledge, significantly boosting the likelihood of a correct final answer.  
- **Mechanism:** The conditional probability of success, \(\text{pot}(c_{<t};x)\), rises sharply when the prefix \(c_{<t}\) contains a key insight (e.g., recognizing a symmetry or reducing a complex subproblem). This reflects evidence accumulation: the model's internal state shifts from uncertainty to a more determined solution path.  
- **Core assumption:** The model's future sampling is more likely to succeed if the prefix already contains a correct and relevant insight.  
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions "very sharp but sometimes tough to interpret spikes (reasoning insights)"; Section 5 provides qualitative examples of spikes aligned with human‑interpretable insights; corpus evidence is weak as neighboring papers focus on faithfulness.  
- **Break condition:** If the insight is incorrect or misapplied, the potential may drop later (tangent) rather than spike.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Reasoning tangents (sharp drops in potential) occur when the model commits to an unproductive or incorrect subproblem, actively reducing the probability of eventual success.  
- **Mechanism:** A tangent token or segment steers the model down a path that, while seemingly plausible, leads to a dead end. This is captured by a drop in \(\text{pot}(c_{<t};x)\) because conditional sampling from that prefix yields fewer correct completions.  
- **Core assumption:** The model's generation is not always optimal; it can get distracted by irrelevant or flawed reasoning threads.  
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract mentions "reasoning tangents" causing sharp drops; Section 4 reports quantitative summary (~9.5% of CoTs contain tangents) and figures illustrating potential dips; neighboring works hint at post‑hoc rationalization.  
- **Break condition:** The model may recover from a tangent if later steps correct the course, but the potential dip remains a marker of inefficiency.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Transferability of partial CoT demonstrates that a small fraction (e.g., 20%) of high‑quality reasoning from a stronger model can unlock problems previously unsolvable by a weaker model.  
- **Mechanism:** The key reasoning steps captured in the stronger model's CoT encode problem structure that is model‑agnostic. When used as a prefix, they raise the weaker model's potential from near zero to a solvable level, effectively providing a "cheat code".  
- **Core assumption:** The difficulty of competition‑level problems is concentrated in a few non‑uniform steps; once these are supplied, the remaining steps are within the weaker model's capability.  
- **Evidence anchors:** Abstract states "as little as 20% of partial CoT can 'unlock' the performance"; Section 6 (Figure 8) shows accuracy improvements for Qwen3‑0.6B and Qwen2.5‑7B with partial CoT from stronger models.  
- **Break condition:** If the weaker model cannot follow the provided reasoning style or lacks capacity for the remaining steps, transfer benefits diminish.

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component map** (plain text):  
Prompt (x) → Model (LMθ) → Sampler → Potential estimator (pot) → Summary stats.  
Additional: Chunk divider, Transfer test harness.

**Critical path**:
  1. For each problem (x, c, y*), generate full CoT c.  
  2. Split c into C equal‑token chunks; identify chunk start tokens.  
  3. For each prefix at chunk start (c_{<t}, x), sample N continuations via the model.  
  4. Compute potential = fraction of continuations yielding correct answer y*.  
  5. Compute summary statistics (insight, tangent, late spike, monotonicity) from the potential curve.  
  6. For transferability: take partial CoT from strong model, prepend to prompt for weak model, generate answer, compare accuracy to baseline.

**Design tradeoffs**:
  - Number of samples N: larger N reduces variance but increases compute; original used N=128.  
  - Number of chunks C: larger C gives finer resolution but multiplies sampling cost; trade‑off between detail and efficiency.  
  - Generation length T: must be long enough to capture full reasoning; too long wastes compute on irrelevant tokens.  
  - Model choice: Reasoning models produce longer CoTs but may exhibit more tangents; non‑reasoning models may guess more.

**Failure signatures**:
  - High variance in potential estimates: suggests insufficient N.  
  - No monotonicity even for correct CoTs: could be due to noisy sampling or model instability.  
  - Transfer gains vanish if prefix is too short: indicates key steps are later in the CoT.  
  - Late spikes dominating pass@k: indicates reliance on guessing rather than reliable reasoning.

**First 3 experiments**:
  1. Reproduce potential curves for Qwen2.5‑7B on AIME‑2024; verify non‑monotonic patterns and compute average insight/tangent rates.  
  2. Test transferability: Use Qwen3‑32B partial CoT on Qwen3‑0.6B for AIME‑2025; measure accuracy vs. % of prefix used (e.g., 10%, 20%, 30%).  
  3. Optimize CoT via greedy search (Algorithm 1): attempt to construct monotonic potential curves and compare the resulting reasoning steps against human‑written solutions.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

1. **Can we always find chain‑of‑thought traces where every sub‑step increases the potential (i.e., is contributive) for any given model and problem?**  
   *Basis*: Explicitly raised in Section 4; the authors show that greedy search can produce largely monotonic CoTs but it is unclear if such traces exist universally.  
   *Why unresolved*: Greedy search may be stuck in local optima; dependence on candidate number M not fully understood.  
   *Resolution*: Systematic evaluation across diverse models/problems with varying M; theoretical analysis of conditions for monotonicity.

2. **What specific properties of a CoT segment cause a sharp increase in potential (a "reasoning insight"), and can we automatically detect such segments without estimating potential?**  
   *Basis*: The paper identifies insights via potential spikes but does not characterize their linguistic/logical features.  
   *Why unresolved*: Lack of interpretability; unable to generate insights deliberately.  
   *Resolution*: Annotate insight segments with features (key equations, decompositions, analogies), train a classifier to predict potential increase from content, or analyze model internals during spikes.

3. **Why do language models produce reasoning tangents that decrease potential, and can we reduce their occurrence without harming overall performance?**  
   *Basis*: Tangents are documented as sharp drops in potential, but their causes are not investigated.  
   *Why unresolved*: Unknown whether tangents are inherent to training, due to distribution shift, or decoding strategies.  
   *Resolution*: Correlate tangents with model mistakes, training data, or decoding parameters; interventions like fine‑tuning or decoding penalties could test reducibility.

4. **To what extent are reasoning insights transferable across models of different architectures and sizes, and what makes an insight transferable?**  
   *Basis*: Transferability is shown via partial CoT prefixes, but the paper does not analyze which specific insights transfer and why.  
   *Why unresolved*: Treated as a black box; unclear if transfer is due to insight content, context length, or other factors.  
   *Resolution*: Analyze transferred partial CoTs to identify common patterns that improve performance; test transfer between differently trained models; fine‑tune weaker models on insights.

## Limitations
- **Implementation details missing**: The exact prompt template, answer extraction method, chunk count, and full sampling parameters (e.g., top‑k, repetition penalty) are not disclosed, hindering faithful replication.  
- **Arbitrary thresholds**: Insight/tangent rates rely on fixed percentage thresholds (40% increase, 30% drop), which may not generalize and lack theoretical justification.  
- **High computational cost**: Potential estimation requires many model samples (N=128) across many chunks, making full replication expensive and limiting scalability.  
- **Parsing and answer extraction errors**: The paper does not specify how final answers are parsed; different regexes can affect correctness labels and thus potential estimates.

## Confidence
- *Non‑monotonic potential across models*: **High** (directly observed in experiments).  
- *Sharp spikes/drops correspond to reasoning insights/tangents*: **Medium** (threshold‑dependent, limited human validation).  
- *Partial CoT transfer dramatically improves weak model accuracy*: **Medium** (shown for a few model pairs; unclear if 20% token cut captures key logical steps universally).

## Next Checks
1. **Threshold robustness**: Recompute insight/tangent rates using varied thresholds (e.g., +30%/+50%, -20%/-40%) and different numbers of chunks (10, 20, 50) to assess stability of the main statistics.  
2. **Human annotation**: For a random sample of 100 CoTs, collect human judgments on whether potential spikes/drops align with meaningful reasoning shifts; compute agreement with automated labels.  
3. **Semantic transfer**: Replace token‑based partial CoT with the first segment that yields a significant potential spike (e.g., >20% increase); compare transfer success against a fixed 20% token prefix to test whether the benefit is due to logical content rather than context length.
