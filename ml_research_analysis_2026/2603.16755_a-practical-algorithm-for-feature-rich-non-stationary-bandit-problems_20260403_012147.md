# A Practical Algorithm for Feature-Rich, Non-Stationary Bandit Problems
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.16755
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16755
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: C3 reduces average cumulative regret by 5.7% across four OpenML tabular datasets and achieves a 12.4% click lift on the Microsoft News Dataset (MIND) compared to baseline algorithms.

## Executive Summary
C3 (Conditionally Coupled Contextual Thompson Sampling) addresses contextual bandit problems with dense arm features, nonlinear rewards, and non-stationary coupled arms. It combines an importance-weighted Nadaraya-Watson estimator in a learned embedding space for reward prediction with Thompson sampling using Beta posteriors derived from kernel mass. The algorithm adapts to concept drift by updating a reference dataset with new observations and periodically removing old samples, avoiding expensive retraining. Empirical results on OpenML datasets and the Microsoft News Dataset (MIND) demonstrate a 5.7% reduction in cumulative regret and a 12.4% click lift over baselines. C3 offers a practical approach for real-world feature-rich, non-stationary bandit scenarios while highlighting challenges in theory, numerical stability, and scalability.

## Method Summary
C3 operates in two phases. Offline, it trains an embedding model ϕ to map context-arm pairs into a space where reward smoothness (Lipschitz) holds, using a combination of regression and calibration losses. Online, it maintains a reference dataset D_ref of past embeddings, rewards, and importance weights. For each decision, it estimates expected rewards via importance-weighted kernel regression (Nadaraya-Watson) over D_ref. It then forms Beta distribution parameters α = η·μ̂ and β = η·(1−μ̂) where η is the total kernel mass for the query, samples θ from Beta(α,β) for each arm, and selects the arm with the highest sample. After observing the reward, the new sample is appended to D_ref and all importance weights are recomputed. Every K steps, the oldest 20% of samples are randomly discarded to cope with non-stationarity. This design provides efficient exploitation-exploration and adaptation without retraining.

## Key Results
- Reduced average cumulative regret by 5.7% compared to strong baselines (LinUCB, NeuralUCB, etc.) across four OpenML tabular datasets.
- Achieved a 12.4% lift in click-through rate on the Microsoft News Dataset (MIND) under induced concept drift.
- Demonstrated robust adaptation to non-stationarity via selective memory updates, outperforming methods that rely on fixed windows or discounting.
- Ablation studies validated the contribution of importance weighting, Beta posterior construction, and RBF bandwidth selection.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: Importance-Weighted Kernel Regression for Unbiased Estimation
- **Claim**: C3 uses importance-weighted Nadaraya-Watson kernel regression to estimate reward means without bias from non-uniform sampling, even under concept drift.
- **Mechanism**: For a query embedding `s` (derived from context-arm pair), IWKR averages rewards from reference samples weighted by RBF similarity and an importance weight `w(s_i)=1/∑_j κ(s_i,s_j)`. This downweights samples in densely populated regions, mitigating sampling bias.
- **Core assumption**: The true mean `µ(s)` is Lipschitz continuous over the embedding space, and the reference dataset eventually covers neighborhoods densely enough for local averaging to converge (Theorem 2).
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: “combines an improved Nadaraya-Watson estimator on an embedding space”
  - [section 4.1]: Definition of IWKR (Equation 5) and Theorem 2 (approximation under Lipschitz and sufficient samples).
  - [corpus]: Related works (e.g., KernelUCB, GP-UCB) use standard kernel regression; C3’s importance weighting is a novel correction for bandit sampling bias, not present in the cited corpus.
- **Break condition**: If the RBF bandwidth `σ` is too small (e.g., 0.33 in ablation), neighborhoods become sparse and IWKR fails to approximate; also if importance weights become numerically unstable due to near-zero kernel sums.

### Mechanism 2: Thompson Sampling via Beta Posteriors for Adaptive Exploration
- **Claim**: C3 constructs Beta posterior distributions from IWKR outputs and kernel mass, enabling Thompson sampling that naturally balances exploration and exploitation in continuous spaces without retraining.
- **Mechanism**: For a query `s`, compute kernel mass `η(s)=∑ κ(s,s_i)`. Set `α(s)=η(s)·IWKR(s)` and `β(s)=η(s)·(1-IWKR(s))`. Sampling `θ∼Beta(α,β)` yields a randomized estimate whose mean matches IWKR and whose variance reflects sampling density (well-sampled regions → peaked Beta, exploited; sparse → flat Beta, explored).
- **Core assumption**: Bernoulli rewards conjugate to Beta; the mapping of kernel mass to pseudo-counts preserves information about neighborhood sampling frequency, and Lipschitz continuity ensures local smoothness.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: “Thompson sampling that allows online learning without retraining.”
  - [section 4.2]: Derivation of `α,β` (Equations 7–8), proof that `α+β=η` (Equation 10), and illustration in Figure 2.
  - [corpus]: Thompson sampling for contextual bandits (e.g., LinTS, NeuralTS) uses Gaussian or linear priors; C3’s Beta on kernel mass is distinct and suited for nonparametric IWKR.
- **Break condition**: If `η(s)` is extreme (very large or near zero), Beta degenerates (deterministic or uniform), causing either no exploration or random play; also miscalibrated embeddings (high ECE) bias the Beta mean.

### Mechanism 3: Selective Memory Update for Non-Stationarity
- **Claim**: C3 adapts to non-stationary reward distributions by maintaining a reference dataset that is updated online: new samples are appended and old ones are periodically removed, avoiding costly retraining while leveraging temporal Lipschitz continuity.
- **Mechanism**: After each pull, the tuple `(ϕ(c,a), r)` is added to `D_ref`. Every `K` steps (e.g., 100), ~20% of oldest samples are randomly dropped. Since `µ(c,a,t)` is assumed Lipschitz in time, recent samples are more relevant; the RBF kernel further upweights nearby (in feature space) samples, implicitly biasing toward recent data.
- **Core assumption**: The environment changes slowly (Lipschitz in time), so a sliding window of recent data suffices to approximate current `µ`; also, the removal rate matches the drift magnitude.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: “handles non-stationarity by updating a reference dataset with new samples and removing old ones without retraining.”
  - [section 3.3]: Formulates non-stationary `µ(c,a,t)` with Lipschitz continuity in time.
  - [section 4.1]: “To mitigate the issues presented by non-stationarity without frequent retraining, C3 allows the removal of older samples while appending the latest samples.”
  - [section 5.3]: MIND experiment removes 20% every 100 steps; Figure 5 shows adaptation to drift.
  - [corpus]: Works like Restless-UCB or discounted Thompson sampling handle non-stationarity via restart or discounting; C3’s selective memory is simpler and couples with IWKR.
- **Break condition**: If drift is too abrupt (violates Lipschitz), the sliding window may contain mixed distributions; if removal is too aggressive, the buffer becomes too small for reliable IWKR; random removal may discard informative old samples that remain relevant.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component map
- **Embedding model ϕ**: Neural network mapping (context, arm) → embedding space S. Trained offline via Algorithm 1 to optimize IWKR regression and calibration (BCE+ECE losses).
- **Reference dataset D_ref**: Buffer of tuples (s, r, w) where s=ϕ(c,a), r reward, w importance weight. Updated online: append new (s,r), recompute w for all samples (Theorem 1), and periodically drop oldest random 20%.
- **IWKR estimator**: Given query s, compute RBF similarities κ(s,s_i) to all s_i∈D_ref, multiply by w_i, and return weighted average reward (Equation 5).
- **Beta constructor**: Compute η(s)=∑ κ(s,s_i), then set α(s)=η(s)·IWKR(s), β(s)=η(s)·(1-IWKR(s)).
- **Thompson sampler**: For each valid arm, sample θ_i ~ Beta(α_i,β_i) and pick arm with max θ_i.
- **Removal policy**: Every K steps (e.g., 100), randomly remove ≈20% of oldest samples from D_ref to combat non-stationarity.

### Critical path
1. Offline: Train ϕ on historical (context, arm, reward) data using Algorithm 1 (self-supervised masking, time-partitioned reference sets).
2. Online per round:
   a. Given context c and valid arms {a_i}, compute embeddings q_i = ϕ(c, a_i).
   b. For each q_i, run IWKR on D_ref → μ̂_i, then compute α_i, β_i.
   c. Sample θ_i ~ Beta(α_i, β_i) for all arms; select a_j = argmax θ_i.
   d. Observe reward r.
   e. Append (q_j, r) to D_ref and update all importance weights w in D_ref (O(n) per Theorem 1).
   f. Every K steps, drop oldest random 20% of samples.

### Design tradeoffs
- **Memory vs. recency**: Larger D_ref reduces variance but may include outdated samples; removal rate and window size must match drift magnitude.
- **RBF bandwidth σ**: Small σ → high variance (few neighbors); large σ → high bias (overly smooth). Ablation (Figure 9) shows robustness beyond a minimum threshold.
- **Calibration**: Including ECE loss in ϕ training reduces overconfidence but may slow convergence; without it, Beta means may be misaligned.
- **Embedding dimension**: Higher dimensions capture complex couplings but require more data and increase computational cost for IWKR.
- **Inference time**: O(|D_ref|) per step; for large buffers, approximate nearest-neighbor search may be needed.

### Failure signatures
- **Linear cumulative regret**: No learning; check IWKR estimates or Beta sampling behavior.
- **Degenerate Beta distributions** (α or β near 0/∞): often from numerical instability in importance weights or extreme η(s) due to poor σ or empty neighborhoods.
- **Exploding/vanishing importance weights**: caused by σ too small (kernel sums near zero) or too large (saturation); monitor w_i range.
- **High ECE** (>0.1) after training: embedding ϕ is miscalibrated; may need more training or stronger ECE regularization.
- **Buffer underflow**: after aggressive removal, |D_ref| too small for reliable IWKR (e.g., <50 samples); track buffer size.

### First 3 experiments
1. **Synthetic coupled arms simulation (Section 5.1)**: Generate arms with controlled correlation to an anchor; verify that ϕ embeddings correlate with true coupling (Figure 3). This tests whether ϕ learns the coupling structure.
2. **OpenML tabular datasets (Section 5.2)**: Compare cumulative regret against LinUCB, NeuralUCB, etc., on stationary bandit tasks. Expected: C3 achieves lower regret (5.7% improvement) and handles high-dimensional nonlinearity (MNIST).
3. **MIND news recommendation (Section 5.3)**: Evaluate on non-stationary data with induced concept drift; expect C3 to show a click lift (12.4%) and adapt over time while baselines falter. Validate removal schedule impact.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question**: Can a theoretical regret bound be derived for C3 in non-stationary environments, extending the stationary bound from Theorem 3?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] The authors state in Section 7: "future works should include obtaining a non-stationary bound that extends the stationary regret bound in Theorem 3."
- **Why unresolved**: The current theoretical analysis only applies to stationary settings, while C3 is designed for non-stationary problems with concept drift; without a non-stationary bound, worst-case guarantees are unknown.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A proof that relates the regret to measures of non-stationarity (e.g., total variation of µ over time) and algorithm-specific parameters, possibly by adapting techniques from restless bandit literature.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: How does the numerical instability of importance weight updates affect C3's performance and reliability in long-running deployments?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] From Section 6: "the transductive learning aspect and importance weight updates can result in high numerical instability since it relies on many sum and division operations of floating points. This effectively disallows quantization to be used."
- **Why unresolved**: Numerical issues could accumulate over time, leading to degraded performance or failure, and the lack of quantization support hinders edge deployment.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Empirical stress tests on very long time horizons (e.g., millions of steps) measuring error growth; analysis of condition numbers; development of stable computation methods (e.g., log-sum-exp tricks, online normalization).

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: To what extent does the performance of C3 depend on the choice of the RBF kernel bandwidth and other hyperparameters, and can automatic tuning methods be developed?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] From Section 6: "the hyperparameter tuning of ϕ can be slightly challenging because the RBF kernel used in IWKR can result in vanishing gradients if points are too near or far from some query point, so the choice of bandwidth of the RBF kernel is important."
- **Why unresolved**: Hyperparameter sensitivity increases the barrier to adoption and may require expensive manual tuning per new dataset.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Systematic ablation studies over diverse datasets quantifying the sensitivity; meta-learning approaches to adapt bandwidth based on data properties; dropout or Bayesian optimization for hyperparameters.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: Is the assumption that the degree of coupling between arms is learnable from their features always valid in practical scenarios, especially when arm features may not fully capture underlying correlations?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The problem formulation (Section 3.2) states: "we assume that there are strongly coupled arms that can be exploited. The degree of coupling is learnable from the arm features, conditioned on the context c." This is an assumption not verified.
- **Why unresolved**: If arm features are incomplete or noisy, the learned embeddings may not correctly capture coupling, limiting C3's effectiveness.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Experiments on synthetic datasets where true coupling is known but arm features are perturbed or corrupted; real-world studies comparing feature-based coupling vs. observed reward correlations.

### Open Question 5
- **Question**: Can C3 be efficiently scaled to handle reference datasets with millions of historical samples while maintaining low inference latency?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] From Section 6: "as an algorithm that relies on a dataset for inference, it may not scale to millions of points without some type of sampling if speed is crucial in the use case."
- **Why unresolved**: The inference algorithm (Algorithm 2) has O(n) time per query (n = reference set size), which becomes prohibitive for large n; this limits application in high-volume scenarios.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: Development of approximate nearest neighbor search for kernel computations; theoretical analysis of approximation errors when pruning the reference set; empirical benchmarks on large-scale datasets (e.g., news recommendation with millions of articles).

## Limitations
- Theoretical guarantees for non-stationarity rely on a Lipschitz continuity assumption that may be violated by abrupt or oscillatory drift in practice.
- The heuristic removal policy (randomly dropping 20% every K steps) lacks theoretical guidance for choosing K and the drop rate relative to drift magnitude.
- Inference time scales linearly with the reference set size, and scalability to large buffers (>10⁵ samples) is unproven without approximate nearest-neighbor methods.
