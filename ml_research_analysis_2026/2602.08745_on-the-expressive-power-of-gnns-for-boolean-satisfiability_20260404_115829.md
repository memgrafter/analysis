# On the Expressive Power of GNNs for Boolean Satisfiability

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.08745  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08745  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** GNNs whose expressive power is bounded by the Weisfeiler–Leman hierarchy cannot, in general, distinguish satisfiable from unsatisfiable SAT formulas, a limitation that carries over to sequential variable assignment and is reflected in poor performance on many industrial benchmarks.

## Executive Summary
This paper analyzes the expressive limitations of Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) for SAT solving through the lens of the Weisfeiler–Leman (WL) test. The authors prove that even the full WL hierarchy fails to generally separate satisfiable from unsatisfiable formulas, and this indistinguishability persists when variables are assigned sequentially. Empirically, random SAT instances are mostly distinguishable within a few WL iterations, while many industrial instances from SAT competitions require higher-order WL or remain indistinguishable, indicating that standard, WL‑bounded GNN architectures are insufficient for practical SAT solving.

## Method Summary
The core method is the **WL‑constrained satisfiability test**. Each SAT formula is converted to a Literal‑Clause Network (LCN): a bipartite graph with literal nodes, clause nodes, and colored edges for clause membership (color 0) and literal negation (color 1). The edge‑colored WL algorithm iteratively refines node colors until convergence (recording *r*<sub>converged</sub>). For each iteration *r*, literals in the same color class are forced equal by adding chain equality clauses (e.g., ¬*l*<sub>*n*</sub> ∨ *l*<sub>1</sub>). The resulting formula *f*<sup>*r*</sup> is checked with a SAT solver. The smallest *r* where *f*<sup>*r*</sup> remains satisfiable is *r*<sub>crit</sub>; if none, the instance is labeled “unsat”. This procedure is applied to G4SAT random families and SAT Competition industrial instances.

## Key Results
- The full WL hierarchy cannot distinguish satisfiability in general (Theorem 5.3); thus WL‑bounded GNNs are theoretically limited.
- Indistinguishability under *k*‑WL carries over to sequential assignment of up to Θ(*n*) variables (Corollary 5.5).
- Random SAT formulas are WL‑distinguishable with high probability (Theorem 6.3); empirically, *r*<sub>crit</sub> is typically 3–4.
- Industrial SAT families often resist WL: only 234 of 448 competition instances were solvable by WL, and 38 families contained “unsat” cases (high *r*<sub>crit</sub> or convergence without separation).

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
WL‑bounded GNNs produce node representations invariant under *k*‑WL equivalences. If two literals receive the same WL color after convergence, any GNN with *k*‑WL power must assign them identical representations. For WL‑indistinguishable formula pairs (Theorem 5.3), some literals that are forced equal by the GNN must actually take opposite truth values in any satisfying assignment, making correct prediction impossible.

### Mechanism 2
Sequential solvers that set variables one by one do not overcome WL limits. For formulas indistinguishable by *k*‑WL (*k*≥4), any partial assignment of up to ⌊*k*/2⌋−1 variables can be mirrored so that the residual formulas remain WL‑indistinguishable (Lemma 5.4). Consequently, a WL‑bounded solver cannot differentiate a satisfiable residual from an unsatisfiable one, hindering branching decisions.

### Mechanism 3
Random SAT instances have literal‑incidence graphs with diverse degree patterns and neighborhoods that WL identifies quickly, leading to low *r*<sub>crit</sub>. Industrial instances often contain regular or community structures (e.g., from circuit encodings) that create persistent symmetries; these require higher‑order WL or remain “unsat”. Empirically, random families show low *r*<sub>crit</sub> while many competition families do not.

## Foundational Learning
- **Weisfeiler–Leman test**  
  *Why needed:* The theoretical yardstick for GNN expressive power; the paper uses its hierarchy to prove limitations and analyze instances.  
  *Quick check:* If two literals have the same WL color after convergence, what must be true about their representations in a GNN with equivalent power?

- **Graph Neural Networks (MPNNs) and expressive power**  
  *Why needed:* Standard Message Passing Neural Networks correspond to 1‑WL; understanding this mapping is key to interpreting results.  
  *Quick check:* What architectural changes (e.g., higher‑order messages, subgraph pooling) can push GNN expressivity beyond 1‑WL?

- **SAT formula graph representations (LCN)**  
  *Why needed:* The graph structure fed to the GNN determines what structural information is available; the LCN with negation edges is shown to be necessary.  
  *Quick check:* Why are literal‑literal negation edges essential to preserve formula semantics in the LCN?

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
CNF → LCN construction → GNN backbone (k‑WL equivalent) → Node predictor (MLP) → (Optional) Sequential decoupler (for iterative solving) → Satisfaction verifier (external)

### Critical Path
The GNN must separate literals sufficiently via WL iterations to allow the node predictor to assign correct truth values. For sequential solvers, the decoupler updates the graph after each assignment, but WL symmetry may persist in the residual.

### Design Tradeoffs
- **Expressivity vs. efficiency:** 1‑WL GNNs are linear‑time but fail on many industrial instances; 4‑WL can handle planar SAT but has *O(n*<sup>4</sup>)* complexity.
- **Symmetry breaking:** Adding handcrafted node features (e.g., variable degree) can help without changing WL power, but may harm generalization if not permutation‑invariant.
- **End‑to‑end vs. hybrid:** Pure GNN solvers face WL limits; hybrid solvers that invoke classical SAT solvers for branching may still rely on GNN heuristics that are WL‑limited.

### Failure Signatures
- High or “unsat” *r*<sub>crit</sub> in the WL‑constrained test.
- Many literals remain WL‑equivalent after convergence, especially within variable pairs or symmetric substructures.
- Poor accuracy on families like *heule‑nol*, *heule‑folkman*, or 3‑regular SAT.
- Significant drop in performance when moving from random to industrial benchmarks.

### First 3 Experiments
1. **WL‑constrained satisfiability test:** Compute WL partitions, add equality clauses per partition at each iteration *r*, and solve with a SAT solver to obtain *r*<sub>crit</sub> and *r*<sub>converged</sub>.
2. **Representation ablation:** Compare LCN (with negation edges) against LCG (without) on a diverse set; measure WL distinguishability and downstream GNN accuracy to quantify information loss.
3. **Scaling WL order:** For industrial instances failing 1‑WL, run 2‑WL, 3‑WL, 4‑WL (via higher‑order GNNs or direct simulation) to check if increased expressivity resolves them; record computational overhead.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- What minimal WL depth is needed to distinguish industrial SAT instances? The study measures convergence but does not pinpoint a threshold.
- How can we generate large‑scale, realistic industrial‑like SAT instances for training GNN solvers? Lack of scalable generators is a major obstacle.
- Do higher‑order GNNs (e.g., 3‑WL, 4‑WL) overcome WL limitations on industrial SAT without excessive computational cost? No empirical evaluation exists yet.

## Limitations
- Experimental details are underspecified: exact SAT solver(s), time/memory limits, initial WL coloring, CNF preprocessing, and random LIG generation algorithm are not documented.
- Handling of unsatisfiable formulas is ambiguous; competition results include “unsat” rows despite claims of all‑satisfiable data, making it unclear whether “unsat” *r*<sub>crit</sub> reflects WL limits or input unsatisfiability.

## Confidence
- **Theoretical limitations (Theorem 5.3, Corollary 5.5):** High. Proofs are rigorous and clearly show WL‑indistinguishable pairs with different satisfiability.
- **Random instance analysis (Theorem 6.3):** High. The probabilistic argument is solid and aligns with observed low *r*<sub>crit</sub> once randomization specifics are fixed.
- **Empirical industrial benchmark results (Tables 1–3):** Medium. The WL‑constrained test is well‑defined, but missing implementation specifics and dataset validation prevent definitive replication.

## Next Checks
1. **Dataset satisfiability audit:** Verify satisfiability of all competition instances with a strong solver (e.g., CaDiCaL, 300 s timeout). Remove any unsatisfiable formulas and re‑run the WL‑constrained test to ensure “unsat” outcomes arise from WL limitations, not pre‑existing unsatisfiability.
2. **Sensitivity to WL setup:** Replicate the WL‑constrained test while varying (a) initial node colors (uniform vs. literal/clause distinction) and (b) equality encoding (linear chain vs. all‑pairs). Report stability of *r*<sub>crit</sub> across choices.
3. **Higher‑order WL scalability:** For industrial families that remain “unsat” under 1‑WL, systematically run 2‑WL, 3‑WL, and 4‑WL. Record whether they become distinguishable and measure runtime growth to assess practicality.
