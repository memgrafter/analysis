# Whom to Query for What: Adaptive Group Elicitation via Multi-Turn LLM Interactions

## Quick Facts
*   **arXiv ID:** 2602.14279
*   **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14279
*   **Reference count:** 40
*   **Primary result:** A framework combining an LLM for query scoring and a GNN for response propagation achieves consistent accuracy gains on held-out opinion questions, with over 12% relative improvement on CES at a 10% respondent budget.

## Executive Summary
This paper tackles the problem of adaptive group elicitation, where an agent must decide both *what* question to ask and *whom* to ask within a group under a limited budget to best infer a latent group property. The proposed solution uses a meta-trained LLM to score candidate questions by their expected information gain (EIG) and a heterogeneous graph neural network (GNN) to propagate observed responses and demographic attributes across the group, imputing missing data. A greedy algorithm then selects a question and a diverse subset of respondents. Evaluated on three real-world opinion datasets, the method consistently outperforms baselines, demonstrating the value of adaptive query selection and GNN-based imputation.

## Method Summary
The core method is a multi-turn, adaptive loop. At each step, a fine-tuned LLM estimates the EIG for every candidate question based on the history of interaction. The question with the highest EIG is selected. A pre-trained heterogeneous GNN, operating on a graph of members, demographic bins, and question-choice nodes, then updates member embeddings from the current partial response graph. These embeddings are clustered to select a representative subset of `k` respondents. The selected question is posed to these respondents, their observed answers are added to the graph and history, and the loop repeats. The GNN's imputation capabilities allow the system to reason about unobserved members, improving the quality of the EIG estimates and respondent selection.

## Key Results
*   Consistent accuracy improvements on held-out questions across three real-world opinion datasets.
*   A 12%+ relative gain on the CES dataset at a 10% respondent budget compared to the strongest baseline.
*   Ablations confirm that both the adaptive EIG-based query selection and the GNN-based response imputation are critical contributors to performance.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1: LLM-based Expected Information Gain Scoring
*   **Claim:** Selecting queries that maximize expected information gain (EIG) on the latent group property efficiently reduces uncertainty.
*   **Mechanism:** A meta-trained LLM predicts individual responses to candidate questions given the interaction history; EIG is computed as the sum of individual entropy reductions. The query with the highest predicted EIG is selected.
*   **Core assumption:** The LLM’s predictive distribution \(p_\theta(Y|x, H)\) accurately estimates the true information gain, relying on its meta-training to capture the relationship between history and future responses via a de Finetti predictive perspective.
*   **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "LLM to score questions by expected information gain"; [section 3.2] "select the next question ... to maximize the expected information gain (EIG)".
*   **Break condition:** LLM predictive quality degrades for out-of-distribution queries or demographic subgroups not represented in meta-training data, causing EIG estimates to become misaligned with true utility.

### Mechanism 2: GNN-based Propagation and Imputation
*   **Claim:** A heterogeneous GNN imputes missing responses and refines respondent representations, enabling information propagation across the population.
*   **Mechanism:** The GNN operates on a heterogeneous graph with member, feature (demographic bins), and choice (query-response) nodes. Given partial observed edges (responses), message passing updates member embeddings; these embeddings predict unobserved responses via link prediction.
*   **Core assumption:** Population structure (demographics and response similarity) is sufficiently captured by the graph schema, and the GNN learning objective generalizes to cold-start respondents, assuming embedding similarity reflects true response similarity.
*   **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "heterogeneous GNN to propagate observed responses and attributes ... enabling imputation of missing responses"; [section 3.1] "predict the probability that \(v\) selects choice \(c\)" via Eq. (2).
*   **Break condition:** Graph structure is misspecified (e.g., demographic bins too coarse, omitted latent subgroups) or the observed response graph is too sparse for message passing to converge, leading to unreliable imputations.

### Mechanism 3: Greedy Joint Selection with Theoretical Guarantees
*   **Claim:** Greedy joint selection of (query, respondent subset) is near-optimal for maximizing cumulative information gain under budgets.
*   **Mechanism:** At each round, select the query maximizing EIG over all candidates, then select `k` respondents by clustering GNN embeddings to maximize coverage. Theoretical guarantee: under submodularity and monotonicity of the utility function `f` (EIG), greedy is within a constant factor of the optimal sequence.
*   **Core assumption:** The EIG utility function `f` is monotone (more data never hurts) and submodular (diminishing returns). The paper shows this holds approximately for their EIG definition.
*   **Evidence anchors:** [abstract] "theoretically grounded framework ... near-optimal guarantees for greedy joint selection"; [section 4] Theorem 4.1 and 4.2; Assumption A.1.
*   **Break condition:** Submodularity fails severely—e.g., if certain questions are only informative when asked of specific, non-overlapping subgroups, causing marginal gains to not diminish. Greedy could then be far from optimal.

## Foundational Learning
*   **Concept:** de Finetti’s predictive inference (exchangeability/martingale)
    *   **Why needed here:** Justifies using conditional entropy of held-out questions as a proxy for uncertainty about the latent group property. Enables the LLM to serve as a predictive machine without an explicit prior.
    *   **Quick check question:** If the sequence of group responses is exchangeable, what does de Finetti’s theorem imply about the existence of a latent entity governing responses, and how does that link to reducing predictive entropy?
*   **Concept:** Heterogeneous graph neural networks (RGCN)
    *   **Why needed here:** Handles multiple node/relation types (members, features, choices) and propagates information under partial observations. Critical for imputing missing responses in a sparse, structured group.
    *   **Quick check question:** Why is a homogeneous GNN insufficient for this task, and how do relation-specific transformations in an RGCN help aggregate demographic vs. response information?
*   **Concept:** Expected Information Gain (EIG) and submodular optimization
    *   **Why needed here:** EIG defines the utility for query selection; submodularity justifies the greedy algorithm’s near-optimality.
    *   **Quick check question:** What two properties must `f(S)` satisfy for the greedy algorithm’s (1-1/e) approximation guarantee to hold in standard submodular maximization, and how does the paper’s two-stage greedy differ?

## Architecture Onboarding
*   **Component map:** LLM predictor (scores candidate queries via EIG) -> GNN imputer (produces member embeddings & imputed responses) -> Respondent selector (clusters embeddings) -> Query execution (ask selected question to selected k respondents) -> History/Graph updater (adds observed responses, repeats).
*   **Critical path:** (1) Compute per-member uncertainty from LLM + history, (2) aggregate to group EIG for all candidate queries, (3) pick query, (4) run GNN on updated graph to get new member embeddings, (5) cluster to pick k respondents, (6) query those respondents, (7) add observed responses to graph and history, repeat. The GNN update (step 4) is critical for next-round imputation quality.
*   **Design tradeoffs:**
    *   *LLM vs. GNN role:* LLM provides flexible, history-aware prediction but is slow; GNN is fast and exploits relational structure but requires a fixed graph schema.
    *   *Greedy vs. multi-step planning:* Greedy is cheap and provably near-optimal; multi-step rollouts add minimal gain but high computational cost.
    *   *Budget split:* Trade-off between query budget (rounds) and respondent budget (`k` per round). More rounds accumulate more history; larger `k` gives more direct observations.
*   **Failure signatures:**
    1.  **Imputation collapse:** GNN predictions become uniform or majority-choice for many members, indicating insufficient signal or graph disconnection.
    2.  **EIG misranking:** Selected queries do not reduce held-out accuracy (flat learning curve), suggesting poor LLM EIG estimates.
    3.  **Respondent selection bias:** Chosen respondents are demographically homogeneous, failing to cover the embedding space—check clustering quality.
*   **First 3 experiments:**
    1.  **Component isolation:** Run with (a) random query + GNN imputation, (b) greedy query + no imputation, (c) greedy query + GNN imputation. Expect (c) > (a) shows EIG matters; (c) > (b) shows GNN imputation matters.
    2.  **Imputation quality stress test:** Artificially mask increasing percentages of observed responses (e.g., 30%, 60%, 90%) in validation and measure accuracy decay vs. a simpler imputer (e.g., matrix completion). Expect Ours to degrade more gracefully.
    3.  **GNN embedding sanity check:** Visualize UMAP of GNN member embeddings colored by known latent traits or sensitive subgroups. Expect clusters to align with meaningful demographics; if not, graph schema may be inadequate.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
*   **Question:** Why does LLM-only imputation fail in adaptive elicitation, and what relational capabilities does the GNN provide?
    *   **Basis in paper:** Meta-Greedy-Imp (LLM imputation) does not consistently beat Meta-Greedy, while GNN imputation yields substantial gains.
    *   **Why unresolved:** Understanding LLM imputation failure is necessary to assess the GNN's role and possible alternatives.
    *   **What evidence would resolve it:** Compare imputation accuracy; analyze LLM calibration; test prompting for relational modeling.
*   **Question:** What predicts respondent sensitivity to group-level propagation, and can it be estimated from minimal prior data?
    *   **Basis in paper:** Section 5.3 shows gains concentrate on high-sensitivity respondents but does not analyze predictors.
    *   **Why unresolved:** A sensitivity predictor would improve respondent selection in cold-start scenarios.
    *   **What evidence would resolve it:** Correlate sensitivity with demographics/graph features; train predictive models.
*   **Question:** Does the EIG utility satisfy submodularity and monotonicity as assumed for theoretical guarantees?
    *   **Basis in paper:** Theoretical results rely on Assumption A.1 without empirical validation for LLM-EIG.
    *   **Why unresolved:** If EIG violates submodularity, greedy selection may be suboptimal, questioning theoretical claims.
    *   **What evidence would resolve it:** Test diminishing returns of EIG; compare greedy to exhaustive optimal on small scales.

## Limitations
*   LLM-based EIG estimation may fail on out-of-distribution queries or demographic subgroups not covered in meta-training.
*   The GNN's graph schema (manual demographic bins) may miss latent subgroups or complex attribute interactions, limiting imputation quality.
*   The theoretical near-optimality guarantee for greedy selection relies on an approximate submodularity assumption that is not exhaustively validated for the LLM-EIG utility.

2.  **Graph Sparsity/Noise Robustness:** Systematically mask increasing percentages of observed responses (e.g., 30%, 60%, 90%) and measure accuracy decay vs. a simpler imputer (e.g., matrix completion). Check if propagation remains beneficial under extreme sparsity.
3.  **Submodularity Violation Probe:** Synthetically construct query sets where informativeness overlaps across disjoint subgroups (e.g., “immigration policy” only informative for foreign-born). Compare greedy to brute-force optimal (small scale) to quantify worst-case approximation gap.
