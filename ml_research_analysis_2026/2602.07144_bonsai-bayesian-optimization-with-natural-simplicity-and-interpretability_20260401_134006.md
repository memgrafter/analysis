# BONSAI: Bayesian Optimization with Natural Simplicity and Interpretability

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID**: 2602.07144
- **Source URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07144
- **Reference count**: 40
- **Primary result**: BONSAI adds a post-processing layer to any acquisition function that greedily resets components to default while controlling acquisition drop, achieving substantial reductions in non-default parameters with little overhead and preserving the no-regret property of GP-UCB.

## Executive Summary
Standard Bayesian optimization (BO) often produces recommendations far from a trusted default configuration, making them hard to interpret and potentially unstable in practice. BONSAI addresses this by introducing a simple, generic post-processing step: after maximizing an acquisition function, it greedily resets parameters to their default values as long as the acquisition value does not drop below a relative threshold. This explicitly trades off sparsity (number of deviations from default) against acquisition performance. Theoretically, when combined with GP-UCB and a decaying threshold, BONSAI retains the same asymptotic no-regret guarantees as vanilla GP-UCB. Empirically, across synthetic benchmarks (e.g., 50D Branin) and real-world high-dimensional tasks (e.g., 180D LassoDNA), BONSAI dramatically reduces the number of non-default parameters while maintaining competitive objective values and adding minimal computational overhead. Thus, BONSAI makes BO outputs more natural and interpretable for practitioners without sacrificing optimization power.

## Method Summary
BONSAI is a lightweight wrapper around any BO acquisition function. In each iteration, after computing the acquisition \( \alpha_t \) and finding its maximizer \( x^*_t \), BONSAI defines an incremental acquisition \( \tilde{\alpha}_t(x) = \alpha_t(x) - \max_{s<t} \alpha_t(x_s) \) to scale the gap meaningfully. It then greedily resets individual components of \( x^*_t \) to the default configuration \( x_{\text{def}} \) if the resulting acquisition gap \( \Delta_t(\tilde{x}) = \alpha_t(x^*_t) - \alpha_t(\tilde{x}) \) is at most \( \rho_t \tilde{\alpha}_t(x^*_t) \), always choosing the reset with the smallest gap. The threshold \( \rho_t \) may be fixed (default 0.2) or decayed (e.g., \( 1/t \)). The pruning loop runs in \( O(d^2) \) time (at most \( d^2 \) acquisition evaluations) and produces a pruned candidate \( \tilde{x}_t \) with fewer non-default parameters. The method requires no changes to the surrogate model and is compatible with any acquisition (e.g., LogNEI, LogNEHVI). An implementation exists in Ax using a MAP-SAAS GP surrogate and L-BFGS-B acquisition optimization.

## Key Results
- Substantially reduces the number of non‑default parameters in recommendations compared to standard BO.
- Maintains competitive objective values (or hypervolume) on synthetic and real‑world high‑dimensional problems (e.g., 50D Branin, 180D LassoDNA).
- Adds little overhead to candidate generation time and, with a decaying threshold, preserves the asymptotic no‑regret property of GP‑UCB.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)

### Mechanism 1
- **Claim**: Greedy pruning from the acquisition maximizer reduces the number of non-default parameters while preserving near-optimal acquisition value.
- **Mechanism**: Starting from the acquisition maximizer \(x^*_t\), BONSAI iteratively resets each component to its default value if the resulting acquisition gap (drop in acquisition) is below a relative threshold \(\rho_t\). The component with the smallest gap among feasible resets is chosen at each step, yielding a pruned candidate \(\tilde{x}_t\) with fewer deviations.
- **Core assumption**: The greedy selection of resets (choosing the smallest gap first) will produce a candidate with minimal \(\ell_0\) distance to the default among all candidates reachable by successive single-component resets that satisfy the gap rule. While not globally optimal, this approximation is effective in practice.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "it greedily resets components of the acquisition maximizer to default values while ensuring the acquisition gap stays below a threshold, explicitly trading off sparsity against acquisition value."
  - [section 4.2]: "greedily removes the components that incur the smallest drop in acquisition value at each step, often matching the exact combinatorial optimum in low dimensions."
  - [corpus]: Weak direct evidence; related work on sparse BO (e.g., SEBO Liu et al., 2023) also targets sparsity but via different mechanisms (regularization). BONSAI's post-hoc pruning is distinct.
- **Break condition**: If no single-component reset satisfies \(\Delta(\text{reset}) \le \rho_t \tilde{\alpha}_t(x^*_t)\), pruning stops prematurely, possibly retaining more non-default parameters than necessary. This can occur if the acquisition function is flat along many dimensions or if \(\rho_t\) is too small.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim**: Using an incremental acquisition value \(\tilde{\alpha}_t(x) = \alpha_t(x) - b_t\) (where \(b_t = \max_{s<t} \alpha_t(x_s)\)) makes the relative threshold \(\rho_t\) scale-invariant and meaningful across BO iterations.
- **Mechanism**: The acquisition gap \(\Delta_t(\tilde{x}) = \alpha_t(x^*_t) - \alpha_t(\tilde{x})\) is compared against \(\rho_t \tilde{\alpha}_t(x^*_t)\). Subtracting the baseline \(b_t\) ensures the threshold adapts to the current acquisition's "excess" over previously evaluated points, avoiding trivial thresholds when \(\alpha_t\) values are low.
- **Core assumption**: The baseline \(b_t\) is non-negative and represents a reasonable lower bound on acceptable acquisition values. This incremental formulation prevents accepting candidates with acquisition below the best-so-far.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "explicitly trading off sparsity against acquisition value" implies a normalized trade-off.
  - [section 4.2]: "we subtract the maximum acquisition value across the previously evaluated designs from the acquisition function... before applying the relative rule".
  - [corpus]: No direct evidence; the use of incremental acquisition is novel to BONSAI.
- **Break condition**: If the acquisition function can be negative (e.g., for some implementations of UCB) and the baseline \(b_t\) is negative, the relative threshold could become meaningless. The paper assumes \(\tilde{\alpha}_t(x^*_t) > 0\), which typically holds after the initial Sobol points.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim**: BONSAI's pruning induces a bounded acquisition inaccuracy, and under a decaying threshold schedule (e.g., \(\rho_t = c/t\)), it retains the asymptotic no-regret property of GP-UCB.
- **Mechanism**: The per-round acquisition accuracy \(\eta_t = \alpha_t(\tilde{x}_t)/\alpha_t(x^*_t)\) satisfies \(\eta_t \ge 1 - \rho_t\) (Lemma A.1). The accumulated inaccuracy \(M_T = \sum_{t=1}^T (1-\tilde{\eta}_t)\) is bounded by \(\sum_{t=1}^T \rho_t\). If \(\sum_{t=1}^T \rho_t = O(\sqrt{T})\) (e.g., \(\rho_t \propto 1/t\)), then the regret bound becomes \(R_T = O(\sqrt{\gamma_T T})\) plus lower-order terms, matching standard GP-UCB up to constants.
- **Core assumption**: The kernel satisfies regularity conditions (e.g., Matérn or SE) and the noise is conditionally sub-Gaussian. The analysis also assumes exact inner maximization of the acquisition or a lower bound on its accuracy.
- **Evidence anchors**:
  - [abstract]: "Theoretically, BONSAI with GP-UCB achieves the same no-regret bounds as vanilla GP-UCB up to additive gap-dependent penalties."
  - [section 5]: Theorem 5.1 and Corollary 5.2 explicitly state the regret bound and asymptotic no-regret conditions.
  - [corpus]: Builds on Kim et al. (2025) for inexact acquisition maximization; BONSAI's specific gap rule is a special case.
- **Break condition**: If \(\rho_t\) decays too slowly (e.g., constant \(\rho\)), then \(\sum \rho_t = O(T)\) and the regret bound degrades to \(O(T)\), losing no-regret. Also, if the acquisition function is not GP-UCB (e.g., EI), the theoretical guarantee does not directly apply, though empirical performance remains good.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept**: Bayesian optimization loop  
  **Why needed here**: BONSAI modifies the standard BO loop by adding a pruning step after acquisition maximization. Understanding the standard loop (surrogate model, acquisition optimization, evaluation) is essential to see where BONSAI fits.  
  **Quick check question**: In a typical BO iteration, what two main steps occur after the surrogate model is updated?

- **Concept**: Gaussian process posterior and UCB acquisition  
  **Why needed here**: The theoretical guarantees are for GP-UCB. The acquisition gap and relative threshold depend on the UCB form \(\mu_{t-1}(x) + \beta_t \sigma_{t-1}(x)\). Understanding GP mean and variance is crucial.  
  **Quick check question**: How does the GP posterior variance typically behave at points far from the training data?

- **Concept**: \(\ell_0\) sparsity and greedy approximation  
  **Why needed here**: BONSAI explicitly minimizes the \(\ell_0\) distance to the default, which is a combinatorial problem. The greedy algorithm is a heuristic for this NP-hard problem.  
  **Quick check question**: Why is exact \(\ell_0\) minimization intractable in high dimensions, and why might greedy be a reasonable approximation?

## Architecture Onboarding

- **Component map**:  
  Acquisition optimizer -> Baseline tracker -> Pruning engine -> Threshold scheduler

- **Critical path**:  
  At each BO iteration \(t\):  
  1. Optimize acquisition → \(x^*_t\).  
  2. Compute baseline \(b_t = \max_{s<t} \alpha_t(x_s)\).  
  3. Define \(\tilde{\alpha}_t(x) = \alpha_t(x) - b_t\).  
  4. Initialize \(\tilde{x}_t = x^*_t\), \(D = \{j : x^*_{t,j} \neq x_{\text{def},j}\}\).  
  5. While \(D\) non-empty:  
     - For each \(j \in D\), compute candidate \(x'\) by resetting \(j\) to default.  
     - Compute gap \(g_j = \alpha_t(x^*_t) - \alpha_t(x')\).  
     - If \(g_j \le \rho_t \tilde{\alpha}_t(x^*_t)\), mark feasible.  
     - If any feasible, pick \(j_{\text{best}} = \arg\min g_j\) among feasible, reset it in \(\tilde{x}_t\), update \(D\).  
     - Else break.  
  6. Query \(\tilde{x}_t\) and update surrogate.

- **Design tradeoffs**:  
  - **Greedy vs exact pruning**: Greedy is \(O(|A(x^*_t)|^2)\) per iteration (at most \(d^2\) acquisition evaluations), while exact is \(O(2^{|A(x^*_t|})\). Greedy is practical for high \(d\); exact only for low \(d\) (e.g., \(d < 15\)).  
  - **Threshold choice**: Smaller \(\rho_t\) yields sparser solutions but risks larger acquisition loss. Paper shows robustness across \(\rho \in [0.001, 0.3]\). Decaying \(\rho_t\) (e.g., \(1/t\)) improves asymptotic regret.  
  - **Batch extension**: For batch BO, pruning subsequent points uses incremental improvement over previously pruned batch points; this may affect diversity.

- **Failure signatures**:  
  - **Zero pruning**: If no components are ever pruned (\(\tilde{x}_t = x^*_t\) always), check that \(\rho_t\) is not too small and that acquisition gaps exist.  
  - **Excessive wall time**: If pruning overhead dominates, ensure acquisition optimization itself is efficient (e.g., using L-BFGS-B) and consider subsampling dimensions in \(D\) for very high \(d\).  
  - **Degraded objective**: If final objective is much worse than standard BO, \(\rho_t\) may be too aggressive; consider larger \(\rho\) or decaying schedule.

- **First 3 experiments** (to validate implementation):  
  1. **Synthetic 2D Branin**: Default at \([0.5, 0.5]\). Run standard BO vs BONSAI (\(\rho=0.2\)). Verify BONSAI produces points with near-zero ℓ0 distance (i.e., few changes) and similar best objective values after 50 iterations.  
  2. **Greedy vs exact on low-D problem**: On 6D Hartmann embedded in 20D, compare greedy and exact pruning on the same \(x^*_t\) for 100 iterations. Check that they produce identical \(\tilde{x}_t\) >95% of the time and that greedy is faster.  
  3. **Regret curves**: On a 1D synthetic function (e.g., \(f(x) = \sin(3x) + x^2\)), run GP-UCB with and without BONSAI using \(\rho_t = 1/t\). Plot cumulative regret; they should be similar after many iterations, confirming asymptotic no-regret.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out

### Open Question 1
- **Question**: Can we derive sublinear regret bounds for BONSAI when combined with acquisition functions that lack explicit confidence bounds, such as Expected Improvement (EI)?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] Section G explicitly states extending the analysis to EI as a key direction, noting the current proof relies on confidence bounds which hold for UCB but not for EI.
- **Why unresolved**: The existing theoretical framework hinges on the UCB structure; EI's probabilistic nature and lack of similar boundedness properties prevent a direct translation of the proof technique.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A new analysis that decomposes EI's behavior or introduces alternative concentration inequalities to bound the cumulative effect of pruning on regret, validated through corresponding theoretical guarantees and empirical alignment with the theory.

### Open Question 2
- **Question**: Under what conditions on the acquisition function or problem structure does greedy pruning achieve the same level of sparsity as exact combinatorial pruning, while still respecting the acquisition gap constraint?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] Appendix G calls for "better characterize[ing] when greedy pruning matches the combinatorial optimum" after noting empirically they perform similarly on low-dimensional problems.
- **Why unresolved**: The current understanding is limited to empirical observations and a simplified per-component gap model (Section A.2); general structural properties (e.g., submodularity or convexity of the acquisition gap in the active set) are unexplored.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A formal proof establishing conditions (e.g., acquisition functions with diminishing returns on resetting dimensions) under which the greedy algorithm is optimal or provides a bounded approximation factor relative to exact pruning.

### Open Question 3
- **Question**: How can BONSAI be extended to support weighted or structured sparsity objectives, where different parameters or groups of parameters incur different costs for deviation from the default configuration?
- **Basis in paper**: [explicit] Appendix G proposes integrating "more sophisticated models of user or operator preferences, for example allowing different components to have different 'weights' in the ℓ0 cost or incorporating structured defaults (such as grouped parameters)."
- **Why unresolved**: The current formulation uses a uniform ℓ0 count, which may not reflect real-world operational complexities where some changes are riskier or more impactful than others.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A generalized BONSAI algorithm that accepts a cost function over parameter deviations, along with theoretical bounds on the regret increase due to weighted pruning and empirical demonstrations that such extensions yield recommendations better aligned with domain-specific simplicity criteria.

### Open Question 4
- **Question**: What is an effective and theoretically justified strategy for setting the relative gap threshold ρ(t) to optimize the sparsity-performance trade-off across diverse Bayesian optimization problems?
- **Basis in paper**: [inferred] The paper uses fixed ρ=0.2 or a simple decaying schedule ρt=1/t without a comprehensive study of how ρ interacts with problem characteristics (dimension, kernel, acquisition) to influence sparsity and regret.
- **Why unresolved**: While Corollary A.2 shows certain decaying schedules preserve asymptotic no-regret, the finite-time behavior and sensitivity of the sparsity objective to ρ remain poorly understood; a fixed threshold may be suboptimal in many settings.
- **What evidence would resolve it**: A theoretical analysis linking the choice of ρ to the information gain or a problem-dependent constant, and/or an empirical evaluation across a wide range of benchmarks that identifies adaptive rules (e.g., based on progress monitoring or learned cost models) that consistently outperform fixed or simple schedules.

## Limitations
- Theoretical scope is limited to GP-UCB with decaying ρₜ; guarantees for other acquisitions (e.g., EI) or constant ρ are not provided.
- Greedy pruning is not globally optimal; its ℓ₀-distance approximation may vary with acquisition landscape and could retain more non-default parameters than necessary.
- Scalability for very high dimensions (e.g., d>100) is uncertain due to O(d²) pruning overhead; handling of mixed/discrete spaces may pose additional challenges.

## Confidence
- Mechanism effectiveness (pruning preserves acquisition): Medium
- Theoretical no-regret (GP-UCB + decaying ρₜ): High
- Empirical sparsity vs. objective trade-off: Medium
