# DeReason: A Difficulty-Aware Curriculum Improves Decoupled SFT-then-RL Training for General Reasoning

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2603.11193
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11193
- **Reference count:** 19
- **Primary result:** A difficulty-aware curriculum (SFT on easy problems, RL on hard) significantly improves 4B model performance on general STEM reasoning benchmarks, achieving state-of-the-art results for 4B models on GPQA-Diamond and BBEH.

## Executive Summary
The paper introduces DeReason, a method that addresses the inefficiency of reinforcement learning (RL) when applied directly to general STEM reasoning tasks. The authors find that supervised fine-tuning (SFT) with moderate-quality teacher responses consistently outperforms RL on the same data, making RL alone sample-inefficient. DeReason proposes a decoupled SFT-then-RL training pipeline guided by an LLM-based difficulty scorer. The scorer partitions problems into easy (scores 1–3) and hard (4–5). Easy problems are used for SFT to efficiently acquire broad knowledge, while hard problems are reserved for RL to explore complex reasoning. This curriculum significantly surpasses both pure SFT/RLL and random data-split baselines. On the Webinstruct-Verified dataset, DeReason achieves state-of-the-art results for 4B models, notably raising GPQA-Diamond pass@1 from 42.9 (SFT-only) and 47.8 (random SFT-RL) to 50.0, and BBEH from 15.8 to 16.7.

## Method Summary
DeReason operationalizes a difficulty-aware curriculum in three steps. First, an LLM-based scorer (Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507) evaluates each training problem and assigns a difficulty score from 1 to 5 using a fixed prompt. Second, training data is partitioned: problems with scores 1–3 form the SFT set (broad, knowledge-recall), while scores 4–5 form the RL set (reasoning-intensive). Third, sequential training is performed: the base model (Qwen3-4B-Base) is fine-tuned on the SFT set using teacher responses from Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 via standard supervised fine-tuning. Then, starting from this SFT checkpoint, reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (GRPO) is applied on the RL set. The reward is provided by a verifier (model-based or rule-based). This decoupled approach aligns the data characteristics with the strengths of each training stage. A random split of the data for sequential SFT-then-RL serves as a baseline; DeReason's principled partition consistently yields superior performance on challenging benchmarks like GPQA-Diamond and BBEH.

## Key Results
- On the Webinstruct-Verified dataset, DeReason increases GPQA-Diamond pass@1 from 42.9 (SFT-only) and 47.8 (random SFT-RL) to 50.0, and BBEH from 15.8 to 16.7 for 4B models.
- Similar gains are observed on mathematical benchmarks (AIME24, AIME25, MATH500) and the Webscale-RL dataset.
- Analysis confirms that the curriculum effectively separates broad, easier topics (history, biology) for SFT and concentrated, difficult math/physics problems for RL, matching training stage inductive biases.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1: SFT's efficiency for broad knowledge
**Claim:** Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) with moderate-quality teacher responses is more sample-efficient than RL for acquiring broad foundational domain knowledge in general STEM.
**Mechanism:** SFT directly distills correct reasoning patterns and factual knowledge from the teacher model into the student policy via maximum likelihood estimation. This provides a dense, high-signal learning gradient for knowledge recall and straightforward application, avoiding the noisy, sparse reward signals and exploratory inefficiency of RL from a randomly initialized base model.
**Core assumption:** The teacher model's responses, while not frontier-quality, are sufficiently correct and structured to serve as effective targets for knowledge acquisition on easier/broader problems.
**Evidence anchors:** The abstract states that SFT with moderate-quality responses consistently outperforms RL when trained on the same data. Section 2.1 and Figure 1 show controlled scaling experiments where SFT pass@1 increases steadily with data, while RL on the same data shows weaker or stagnant gains. Section 4.1 shows low-difficulty scores (1-3) correlate with broad, knowledge-oriented categories like History and Biology, aligning with SFT's strength in covering diverse factual recall.
**Break condition:** If the teacher model's responses are systematically incorrect or incoherent on the easy subset, SFT would consolidate poor knowledge, harming downstream performance.

### Mechanism 2: RL's role in refining complex reasoning
**Claim:** Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR), when applied after SFT to a focused set of high-difficulty problems, effectively explores novel reasoning paths beyond the teacher's demonstrations, pushing performance on the most challenging tasks.
**Mechanism:** RL's outcome-based reward signal incentivizes the policy to discover longer, more complex, or more efficient reasoning chains that were not present in the teacher's moderate-quality demonstrations. Starting from an SFT-initialized policy provides a stable, knowledgeable foundation, allowing RL's exploration to focus on reasoning refinement rather than basic knowledge acquisition.
**Core assumption:** The verifiable reward function accurately judges correctness for the targeted difficult problems, and the SFT-initialized policy has sufficient competence to generate plausible candidates for RL to improve upon.
**Evidence anchors:** The abstract notes that DeReason reserves a focused subset of difficult problems for RL to cultivate complex reasoning. Section 4.1 shows highest difficulty scores (4-5) are >78% Mathematics/Physics, domains requiring multi-step derivation. Section 4.3 and Figure 3 show RL starting from SFT on high-difficulty subsets (4&5) yields a positive reward slope, while RL from base on low-difficulty (1-2) shows little gain. Section 4.4 indicates RL on hard problems from the SFT checkpoint moderately shortens responses, suggesting efficiency gains.
**Break condition:** If the reward model is miscalibrated or sparse for the hardest problems, RL may optimize for spurious correlations or degenerate strategies.

### Mechanism 3: Decoupling over random split
**Claim:** The decoupled SFT-then-RL curriculum outperforms a random data split because it aligns the intrinsic characteristics of the training data (reasoning intensity) with the inductive biases of each training stage (SFT for knowledge distillation, RL for exploration/optimization).
**Mechanism:** Randomly mixing easy and hard problems in both stages dilutes the learning signal. SFT on hard problems may fail if the teacher's response is suboptimal. RL on easy problems wastes exploration budget on tasks already solved by SFT. The curriculum creates a staged learning trajectory: first build a broadly knowledgeable policy via efficient distillation on easier problems, then concentrate exploration resources on the residual hard problems where the teacher's knowledge is insufficient.
**Core assumption:** There exists a meaningful, score-able distinction between knowledge-recall and reasoning-intensive problems, and this distinction correlates with which training stage yields marginal improvement.
**Evidence anchors:** The abstract states that principled decoupling yields better performance than randomly splitting the data for sequential SFT and RL. Table 1 shows decoupled SFT-easy + RL-hard consistently beats "SFT then random RL" on GPQA-Diamond (50.0 vs 47.8) and BBEH (16.7 vs 15.8). Section 4.1 shows difficulty score correlates with category shift to math/physics, providing a concrete partitioning criterion. Section 4.3 demonstrates RL on only high-difficulty data from SFT start yields gains, while RL on mid/low difficulty does not.
**Break condition:** If the difficulty estimator is highly noisy or misaligned with true reasoning complexity, the partition could place reasoning-intensive problems into SFT or easy ones into RL, degrading performance relative to random.

## Architecture Onboarding
### Component Map
Base Model (Qwen3-4B-Base) → Difficulty Scorer (Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507) → Partitioning (threshold τ=4) → DSFT (easy) & DRL (hard) → SFT Trainer (Llama-Factory) → π_SFT → RL Trainer (VeRL, GRPO) + Verifier (model-based) → Final policy.

### Critical Path
1. Training problems are fed to the Difficulty Scorer to obtain 1–5 scores.
2. Scores are thresholded (τ=4) into DSFT (≤3) and DRL (≥4).
3. SFT Trainer fine-tunes the Base Model on DSFT using teacher-generated responses to produce π_SFT.
4. RL Trainer initializes from π_SFT and runs GRPO on DRL, using the Verifier to compute rewards.
5. The output is the final model.

### Design Tradeoffs
- Scorer choice: Using a same-size instruct model avoids dependency on stronger models but may limit scoring accuracy. A frontier model could be more reliable but introduces external dependency.
- Threshold τ: Fixed at 4 in the paper. Tuning on a validation set could optimize for target benchmarks but risks overfitting; a dynamic percentile-based threshold is unexplored.
- Verifier cost: Model-based verifier adds inference overhead compared to rule-based; its accuracy is critical for RL signal quality.

### Failure Signatures
- **SFT degradation:** π_SFT performance on easy benchmarks (e.g., MMLU-Pro) drops significantly vs. SFT-only baseline, indicating DSFT is too narrow or teacher responses are poor.
- **RL divergence:** During RL stage from π_SFT, reward or pass@1 on DRL validation set collapses, indicating reward hacking or too aggressive KL penalty.
- **No transfer gain:** Final model's performance on hard benchmarks (GPQA, BBEH) is not meaningfully above SFT-only baseline, suggesting DRL is not beneficial (wrong partition, bad verifier, insufficient RL steps).

### First 3 Experiments
1. **Validate Difficulty Partition:** Run the scorer on the full training set. Confirm the category distribution shift (Figure 2) by analyzing topic frequencies per score; manually inspect 50 random examples from score 1 vs. 5 to ensure high-score problems are reasoning-intensive.
2. **Ablate Data Split:** Train the full pipeline with: (a) Random 50/50 split for SFT then RL, (b) All data for SFT then RL on all data, (c) Proposed decoupled split. Compare on a hard benchmark (GPQA-Diamond) to isolate curriculum effect.
3. **Checkpoint Analysis:** From the SFT checkpoint (π_SFT), evaluate response length, entropy, and pass@1 by difficulty score level on a held-out set. This validates that π_SFT has learned the easy knowledge and sets a baseline before RL.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1: Optimal training data partition between SFT and RL for general STEM?
**Basis in paper:** The Introduction explicitly asks: "how should training data be allocated between the two stages in general domains?"
**Why unresolved:** The authors propose a difficulty-based split but do not explore other strategies; optimal allocation remains unknown.
**What evidence would resolve:** Systematic comparison of various criteria (difficulty thresholds, domain filters, learned partitions) across multiple datasets and model scales.

### Open Question 2: Reliability of the LLM-based difficulty scorer?
**Basis in paper:** DeReason uses an LLM to assign difficulty scores (Section 2.2) with no validation against human judgments.
**Why unresolved:** If scoring is biased, the curriculum could be suboptimal; better estimators might improve performance.
**What evidence would resolve:** Correlate LLM scores with human ratings of reasoning intensity; replace scorer with alternatives (solution length, model-predicted hardness) and measure impact on final performance.

### Open Question 3: How does model size affect the SFT vs. RL trade-off and the benefits of the decoupled curriculum?
**Basis in paper:** Experiments use only 4B models; the authors speculate SFT is essential for small models but leave scaling unexamined.
**Why unresolved:** Larger models may shift the balance, possibly making RL more effective and altering the optimal data split; the observed SFT>RL hierarchy may not generalize.
**What evidence would resolve:** Train models across a wide size range (e.g., 1B–70B) on fixed data; compare pure SFT, RL-only, and DeReason; track how relative performance changes with capacity.

## Limitations
- Verifier design details (model-based Vθ from Ma et al., 2025) are not described, making RL reward signal irreproducible.
- Key hyperparameters for GRPO (group size G, KL coefficient β, steps, sampling temperature) and SFT training duration (epochs, oversampling) are omitted, risking fragile replication.
- The curriculum hinges on an LLM scorer with no noise analysis or alternative partitioning (e.g., percentiles), so the partition may be brittle to scorer inaccuracies.

## Confidence
- **SFT > RL on same data:** High (supported by scaling experiments in Sec 2.1).
- **DeReason curriculum beats random split:** High (Table 1, Fig 3).
- **Mechanistic alignment (SFT for knowledge, RL for exploration):** Medium (correlational evidence from Sec 4.1–4.4).
- **Difficulty scorer validity:** Medium (category shift in Fig 2, but no human validation).

## Next Checks
1. **Human–scorer agreement:** Sample 200 problems (50 per score 1–5) and have domain experts rate reasoning intensity; compute correlation with LLM scores to validate the difficulty estimator.
2. **Threshold sensitivity:** Vary τ (e.g., 3, 3.5, 4, 4.5) on WebInstruct-Verified, re-run full pipeline, and measure performance variance on GPQA-Diamond to assess robustness to partitioning.
3. **Verifier ablation:** Replace the model-based verifier with a simple rule-based verifier (e.g., string match for MATH500) during RL; compare final pass@1 on math benchmarks to understand reward sensitivity.
