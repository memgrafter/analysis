# Evaluating Prompt Engineering Strategies for Sentiment Control in AI-Generated Texts

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.06692
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06692
- **Reference count:** 40
- **Primary result:** Few-Shot prompting with human-written examples achieves the highest emotion steering effectiveness (Emotion Score 0.785), outperforming Zero-Shot and fine-tuning while maintaining high output quality.

## Executive Summary
This study investigates prompt engineering as a resource-efficient alternative to fine-tuning for controlling sentiment in LLM-generated text. Using `gpt-3.5-turbo` and Ekman's six basic emotions, the authors systematically evaluate Zero-Shot, Chain-of-Thought, and Few-Shot prompting strategies. Effectiveness is measured via a classifier-based Emotion Score, with additional quality metrics for correctness, semantic similarity, and diversity. The key finding is that Few-Shot prompting, specifically using carefully curated human-written examples, significantly outperforms other prompt engineering techniques and full fine-tuning. Fine-tuning, while effective, produces repetitive, lower-quality outputs. The results establish prompt engineering—particularly Few-Shot—as a practical, cost-effective method for sentiment control, especially in data-constrained scenarios.

## Method Summary
The study compares prompt engineering strategies against full fine-tuning for steering the emotional tone of generated text. Experiments used 80 queries (40 factual, 40 subjective) and `gpt-3.5-turbo`. An iterative optimization process tested various prompt designs: baseline Vanilla, Zero-Shot (with delimiters and personas), Chain-of-Thought, and Few-Shot (varying example source, number, and order). Effectiveness was primarily measured by Emotion Score (weighted F1 from a DistilRoBERTa emotion classifier), supplemented by BERTScore, Distinct-n (diversity), Flesch Reading Ease, and correctness checks. The fine-tuning approach used the best Zero-Shot prompt format to generate 96 training examples for full parameter updating on `gpt-3.5-turbo`.

## Key Results
- Few-Shot prompting with human-written examples achieved the highest Emotion Score (0.785), significantly outperforming the best Zero-Shot prompt (0.739) and fine-tuning (0.743).
- Fine-tuning produced effective but repetitive, itemized outputs with lower lexical diversity compared to all prompt engineering techniques.
- All prompt engineering approaches maintained high response correctness (>0.975) and semantic similarity, preserving output quality while steering emotion.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
### Mechanism 1
- **Claim:** Few-Shot prompting with human-written, task-aligned examples most effectively steers emotion because it provides concrete, high-quality demonstrations of the target emotional style within the relevant query context.
- **Mechanism:** The model performs in-context learning by implicitly extracting and replicating the lexical, syntactic, and rhetorical patterns present in the provided examples. Human-written examples likely capture nuanced, context-appropriate emotional expression that generic or LLM-generated examples may miss or stylistically misalign (e.g., adding emojis).
- **Core assumption:** The model's ability to mimic style is highly sensitive to the specific linguistic characteristics of the examples, not just the emotional label.
- **Break condition:** Effectiveness degrades if examples are not human-written, from a mismatched domain/style, insufficiently varied, or ordered sub-optimally.

### Mechanism 2
- **Claim:** Zero-Shot prompting provides a baseline level of emotion steering by leveraging the model's pre-trained knowledge of emotional language, but its effectiveness is limited by inherent model biases and the lack of task-specific guidance.
- **Mechanism:** The instruction ("expressing {emotion}") activates latent representations associated with that emotion. Without concrete examples, the model falls back on its default, often neutral/joyful, tone or stereotypical associations.
- **Core assumption:** Pre-trained LLMs contain general knowledge of emotional lexicon and expression patterns, which can be accessed through direct instruction but are not optimally structured for precise, context-dependent generation.
- **Break condition:** Steering fails or reverses when the query type conflicts with the model's default bias, the instruction is ambiguous, or the model's internal representations for the target emotion are weak/stereotyped.

### Mechanism 3
- **Claim:** Fine-tuning for emotion steering is effective but produces lower-quality, repetitive outputs due to catastrophic forgetting or mode collapse induced by limited, homogeneous training data.
- **Mechanism:** Parameter updating on a small, narrow dataset causes the model to overfit to specific emotional phrasing patterns, reducing linguistic diversity and generalizability, leading to high repetition and simplified text.
- **Core assumption:** Fine-tuning requires a large, diverse dataset to maintain general language capabilities while injecting new behavior. A small dataset disproportionately harms output diversity.
- **Break condition:** Performance and quality degrade severely if the training dataset is small, lacks diversity in emotional expression styles, or does not cover the query distribution.

## Foundational Learning
- **Concept: In-Context Learning (Few-Shot Prompting)**
  - *Why needed here:* The core finding is that Few-Shot prompts with examples work best. Understanding that the model learns from the demonstration examples within the prompt, not from parameter updates, is fundamental.
  - *Quick check question:* Why do human-written examples outperform LLM-generated ones for emotion steering, even if both are "correct"?

- **Concept: Emotion Representation in LLMs (Ekman's Basic Emotions)**
  - *Why needed here:* The study operationalizes "emotion" using a fixed, discrete taxonomy (anger, disgust, fear, joy, sadness, surprise). Knowing this defines the target space and evaluation metric (classifier trained on similar labels) is crucial.
  - *Quick check question:* How might using a different emotion model (e.g., Plutchik's wheel, valence-arousal dimensions) change the prompt design and evaluation results?

- **Concept: Evaluation Metrics for Controlled Generation (Emotion Score, BERTScore, Distinct-n)**
  - *Why needed here:* The paper's conclusions rely on multi-faceted evaluation: primary effectiveness (Emotion Score vs. classifier) and quality (semantic similarity, diversity, correctness, readability). Misunderstanding these metrics could lead to incorrect interpretation.
  - *Quick check question:* Why is a high Emotion Score not sufficient to claim a "good" response? What does a drop in Distinct-n or Correctness tell you about the trade-off?

## Architecture Onboarding
- **Component map:**
  Base LLM (gpt-3.5-turbo) -> Prompt Composer -> Example Database -> LLM Generation -> Evaluation Pipeline (Emotion Classifier, Quality Metrics Module) -> Optimization Controller -> Next Prompt Variant

- **Critical path:**
  Prompt Design (template + examples) -> LLM Generation -> Emotion Classification -> Score Calculation -> Variant Comparison -> Selection -> Next Optimization Step

- **Design tradeoffs:**
  - Effectiveness vs. Cost/Latency: Few-Shot (high effectiveness) requires maintaining a high-quality example database and consumes more prompt tokens. Zero-Shot is cheaper but less effective.
  - Steering Strength vs. Output Quality: Fine-tuning may achieve strong steering but at a severe cost to diversity and correctness. Prompt engineering generally preserves quality better.
  - Generality vs. Specificity: A single "best" prompt (e.g., Human Few-Shot) may not generalize perfectly across all query types; performance shifts between factual and subjective queries.

- **Failure signatures:**
  - Zero-Shot CoT: Abnormally high Distinct-1 but low Distinct-2 and low Emotion Score, especially on factual queries. Indicates generation of single-word "answers" with incoherent emotional reasoning.
  - Fine-Tuning: Sharp drop in Distinct-n metrics, presence of repeated phrases or lists, and a drop in Correctness score. Indicates overfitting and loss of generative diversity.
  - LLM-Generated/Generic Examples (Few-Shot): Emotion Score significantly lower than the Human baseline. Indicates example-style misalignment (e.g., emojis, hashtags, informal language) being mimicked.

- **First 3 experiments:**
  1. Establish Baseline: Run Vanilla prompts for all 80 queries. Record default emotion distribution. Calculate baseline quality metrics.
  2. Zero-Shot Optimization: Test delimiter variants then persona variants. Identify the best Zero-Shot prompt (e.g., Persona Paul).
  3. Few-Shot Proof-of-Concept: Create a minimal Human Few-Shot prompt (1 example per emotion). Compare its Emotion Score against the best Zero-Shot prompt. If it outperforms, proceed to optimize example source, number, and order.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
### Open Question 1
- **Question:** How do prompt engineering strategies for emotion steering perform across languages with different linguistic and cultural emotional expression norms?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "our experiments were conducted solely in English. Extending our approach to other languages would provide valuable insights into the cross-linguistic applicability of emotional steering techniques."
- **Why unresolved:** Emotional expression is deeply influenced by language and culture; techniques effective in English may not transfer due to lexical, syntactic, or cultural differences in emotion conceptualization.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A multi-lingual replication of the study using the same prompting techniques and Ekman's emotions across typologically and culturally diverse languages, with evaluation by native-speaker classifiers and human raters.

### Open Question 2
- **Question:** To what extent do classifier-based Emotion Scores align with human perceptions of emotional tone and appropriateness in LLM-generated text?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "Our evaluation relied on a classifier-based metric. Human evaluation should complement future studies to ensure a human-centered assessment of emotional tone."
- **Why unresolved:** Automated classifiers may not capture nuances of authentic emotional expression, sarcasm, or contextual appropriateness that humans detect, potentially overestimating or misassigning emotional intent.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** A large-scale human evaluation study where raters assess the same model outputs used for the classifier, measuring inter-annotator agreement and correlating human judgments with the Emotion Score and other quality metrics.

### Open Question 3
- **Question:** Why do Few-Shot prompts improve emotion steering for factual queries but reduce it for subjective queries compared to Zero-Shot prompts, while Zero-Shot shows the opposite trend?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "Interestingly, prompts that include examples consistently show a shift in performance between factual and subjective queries... This phenomenon lacks a clear explanation, presenting an opportunity for further investigation."
- **Why unresolved:** The underlying mechanism—whether related to the model's internal handling of factuality vs. subjectivity, the nature of the training data, or interaction with example content—is unknown, limiting the ability to design prompts optimally for query types.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** Controlled experiments systematically varying query factuality/subjectivity, example content (emotional vs. factual focus), and model probing (e.g., analyzing attention patterns or latent representations) to identify the causal factor behind the differential effect.

### Open Question 4
- **Question:** Can parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods, such as LoRA, overcome the repetition and quality degradation seen in full fine-tuning for emotion steering while using similarly limited training data?
- **Basis in paper:** [explicit] "The fine-tuned model’s constrained performance might result from the very limited data availability... An insightful direction for follow-up research would be to apply our techniques to other LLMs, including open-source models. These models also allow for comparisons with alternative fine-tuning methods, such as parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT)."
- **Why unresolved:** Full fine-tuning induced catastrophic degeneration (repetition) likely due to overfitting on small datasets; PEFT methods update fewer parameters and may preserve general language abilities better, but their efficacy for precise emotion steering is unproven.
- **What evidence would resolve it:** An experiment fine-tuning an open-source LLM (e.g., Llama 2) with the same limited dataset using PEFT (e.g., LoRA) versus full fine-tuning, comparing Emotion Score, diversity metrics, and repetition rates against the prompt engineering baselines.

## Limitations
- **External validity:** Findings are tied to `gpt-3.5-turbo` and Ekman's six emotions. Generalizability to other LLMs or alternative emotion frameworks is unknown.
- **Reproducibility:** Exact 80 queries and the 12 curated human examples are not in the paper text; their availability in the GitHub repo is assumed but unverified. Minor prompt wording differences significantly impact results.
- **Evaluation metric dependency:** The Emotion Score relies on a specific classifier (`distilroberta-base-finetuned-emotion`). Using a different classifier or human evaluation could re-rank strategies.

## Confidence
- **Claim:** Prompt engineering (especially Few-Shot) is more effective and higher-quality than full fine-tuning for emotion steering with limited data. **Label:** Medium (based on one model, one small fine-tuning dataset).
- **Claim:** Human-written Few-Shot examples outperform LLM-generated or generic dataset examples. **Label:** High (directly evidenced in results).
- **Claim:** Zero-Shot prompting provides a weaker but viable baseline for emotion steering. **Label:** High (consistent across experiments).
- **Claim:** Fine-tuning with small datasets causes repetitive, low-diversity outputs. **Label:** High (quantified diversity metrics and qualitative analysis).
- **Claim:** Findings generalize broadly across LLMs and emotion taxonomies. **Label:** Low (external validity limited).
