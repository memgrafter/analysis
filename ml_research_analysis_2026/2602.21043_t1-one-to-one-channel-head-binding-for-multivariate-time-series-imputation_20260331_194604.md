# T1: One-to-One Channel-Head Binding for Multivariate Time-Series Imputation

## Quick Facts
- **arXiv ID:** 2602.21043  
- **Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21043  
- **Reference count:** 40  
- **Primary result:** Reduces mean squared error (MSE) by 46% on average compared to the best existing model and nearly halves MSE under 70% missing ratio.

## Executive Summary
T1 addresses multivariate time-series imputation under heavy and diverse missingness by introducing a CNN-Transformer hybrid with Channel-Head Binding. This one-to-one correspondence between CNN channels and attention heads enables selective information transfer: each channel extracts a distinct temporal feature, and its bound attention head adaptively down-weights unreliable features while preserving reliable cross-variable connections. Evaluated on 11 benchmarks, T1 achieves state-of-the-art performance, cutting average MSE by 46% versus PatchTST and nearly halving MSE at 70% missing. It maintains robust results with a single hyperparameter configuration across all datasets and generalizes to unseen missing patterns without retraining.

## Method Summary
T1 takes a multivariate series \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times T}\) and its observation mask \(\Omega\). Inputs are instance-normalized using only observed values, and the mask is concatenated as a second channel. A strided 1D convolution projects this to \(C=128\) channels, then a learnable variable-specific encoding is added. The core consists of repeated **T1 blocks**. Each block first applies **shared depthwise convolutions** (dual kernel sizes) across all variables to produce query, key, and value tensors per channel. **Channel-Head Attention** then treats each channel independently: for channel \(c\), attention is computed across the \(M\) variables using the corresponding \(Q_c\), \(K_c\), \(V_c\); outputs are concatenated across channels. A pointwise convolution with residual connection and a convolutional feed‑forward network (two pointwise convolutions with GeLU) follow. After two blocks, a downsampling layer halves the temporal resolution; two more blocks operate at the lower resolution. The final output is upsampled via PixelShuffle1D, projected to a single channel, and denormalized using stored per-variable statistics. Training is self‑supervised: a random mask covering 40% of originally observed values is generated per batch, and the model is trained with MSE loss only on the artificially masked positions. The same fixed hyperparameters work across all experiments.

## Key Results
- State-of-the-art on 11 benchmark datasets, reducing average MSE by 46% compared to the strongest baseline (PatchTST).  
- Under extreme sparsity (70% missing), T1’s MSE is nearly half that of the next best method.  
- Robust performance with a single consistent hyperparameter configuration; generalizes to unseen missing patterns without retraining.  
- Ablation confirms the necessity of Channel‑Head Binding: removing it or using grouped channels increases MSE by up to 16.86%.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The success of T1 rests on three tightly coupled mechanisms:

### 1. Channel‑Head Binding
A one‑to‑one mapping between CNN channels and attention heads gives each channel’s extracted temporal pattern a dedicated attention pathway. This enables *feature‑specific selective transfer*: if a channel captures a corrupted pattern (due to missingness), its bound head can down‑weight contributions from that channel across all variables without affecting other channels. Evidence includes the abstract’s claim, the design in Section 3.2, and representation analysis (Section 4.2.4). Ablation shows grouping channels (e.g., 32 per head) degrades performance, proving the necessity of fine‑grained binding.

### 2. Shared Depthwise Convolutions
By sharing DWConv weights across variables, each channel learns a consistent temporal filter (e.g., trend or seasonality detector) applied to every variable. This creates a *semantically aligned* feature space: the same channel index corresponds to the same pattern type across all variables, making the subsequent one‑to‑one binding meaningful. Section 3.2 explicitly states this alignment requirement; ablations on cross‑variable modeling (Section 4.2.5) show that removing cross‑variable transfer harms performance, but using pointwise convolutions (still with shared features) hurts less, highlighting that the selective transfer enabled by binding is key.

### 3. Mask‑Aware Embedding
The binary observation mask is concatenated with the normalized series as a second input channel, conditioning the first convolution on data reliability. This early injection helps the model distinguish true signals from sparsity artifacts. Section 3.1 describes the design; ablating the mask channel (Section 4.2.5) degrades MSE by 3.64%, confirming its contribution.

## Foundational Learning

**Depthwise Separable Convolutions for Time Series**  
*Why needed*: T1’s temporal extractor uses depthwise (DW) and pointwise (PW) convolutions. DWConv applies a 1‑D filter per channel independently, preserving separate channel maps; PWConv then mixes channels. This structure maintains the per‑channel features that later bind to attention heads.  
*Quick check*: If a DWConv with kernel size 5 and 128 channels processes a tensor of shape \([128, 96]\), what are the output dimensions, and what does each output channel represent?

**Multi‑Head Attention and Variable‑Axis Tokenization**  
*Why needed*: T1 repurposes attention across the *variable* dimension (each variable is a token), not time. The one‑to‑one binding means each head attends over all variables using only one channel of features—a non‑standard attention setup.  
*Quick check*: In standard MHA with \(\text{nh}=8\) and \(d_{\text{model}}=128\), the dimension per head is 16. In T1 with \(C=128\) and \(\text{nh}=128\), what is the dimension per head, and how does that compare?

**Imputation Loss Masking and Self‑Supervised Training**  
*Why needed*: Imputation models are trained by artificially masking observed values and computing loss only on those positions. This prevents memorization and forces learning from partial information. T1 follows this standard protocol.  
*Quick check*: Given an artificial mask \(\Psi\) and the original missing mask \(\Omega\), on which positions is the MSE loss computed? What would happen if loss were computed on *all* positions?

## Architecture Onboarding

**Component Map**  
Input \((X, \Omega)\) → Mask‑Aware Embedding → \([ \text{T1 Block} ] \times B\) → Reconstruction Upsampler → Denormalization → Output  
Inside a T1 Block: Temporal Conv QKV Projector → CHead Attention → Conv FFN  

**Critical Path**  
The mechanism‑critical flow is: Mask‑Aware Embedding (injects mask) → Temporal Conv Projections (extract aligned features) → **CHead Attention** (binds channel to head, performs selective cross‑variable fusion) → Conv FFN (refines) → (repeat) → Upsampler.

**Design Tradeoffs**  
- **Binding vs. Flexibility**: Strict one‑to‑one binding reduces expressive capacity but isolates corrupted features; the authors argue the constraint improves robustness.  
- **Shared vs. Per‑Variable Filters**: Shared convolutions enforce semantic alignment but may miss variable‑specific idiosyncrasies; the design assumes generic patterns suffice.  
- **Complexity**: Binding adds no extra FLOPs vs. grouped MHA but memory scales as \(O(M \cdot C \cdot L)\); for large \(M\), scalability becomes a concern.

**Failure Signatures**  
1. **Ablation – Grouped Channels**: Performance collapses when increasing channels‑per‑head (e.g., 16.86% MSE rise), proving the necessity of fine‑grained binding.  
2. **Attention Degradation Analysis**: If attention weights to a variable do *not* decrease with its missing ratio, or head‑specific patterns do not change with different masked features, the adaptive down‑weighting fails.  
3. **Training Mask Sensitivity**: If a model trained on one missing ratio (e.g., 0.1) fails catastrophically on another (e.g., 0.7), the reliability assessment enabled by binding is not learned. T1 shows robustness, so such sensitivity would indicate a breakdown.

**First Three Experiments**  
1. **Ablation Replication**: Train full T1 and a grouped‑channel variant (32 channels per head) on ETTh1 with 40% masking; expect the bound model to achieve lower MSE.  
2. **Visualize Attention Entropy per Head**: For a fixed test sample, compute entropy of the attention distribution (across variables) for each head. Heads bound to channels with reliable observed patterns should show lower entropy (more focused) than those with corrupted patterns.  
3. **Probe Shared Convolution Alignments**: Extract DWConv outputs \(Z[m,c,:]\) for a clean sample; for a fixed channel \(c\), compute pairwise correlations between variables. The hypothesis is that these correlations are meaningful and indicate semantic alignment; compare to a model with variable‑specific convolutions.

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
1. **Online streaming and active sensing** – Extending T1 to real‑time imputation with causal constraints and using active sensing to guide sensor placement under resource constraints is unexplored.  
2. **Theoretical explanation of binding** – Why strict one‑to‑one binding outperforms grouped or shared heads analytically remains open; current evidence is empirical.  
3. **Scaling to many variables** – T1’s attention is \(O(M^2)\); performance and memory for \(M > 1000\) are unknown; approximations or hierarchical modeling may be needed.  
4. **Cross‑domain generalization** – The claim of domain‑agnostic hyperparameters is based on similar datasets; transferring to vastly different domains (e.g., energy to healthcare) is untested.  
5. **Hybridization with diffusion models** – Combining T1’s fast single‑pass imputation with iterative diffusion refinement could improve quality under extreme sparsity but may increase latency.

## Limitations
- **Reproducibility**: Key details (padding, downsampling method, encoding placement) are missing, hindering faithful replication.  
- **Baseline fairness**: Baselines may not have been retrained under the same 40% masking protocol, potentially overstating T1’s gains.  
- **Mechanism validation**: The assumption that distinct CNN channels capture distinct temporal patterns is not directly tested; shared convolutions could be a bottleneck for highly heterogeneous series.  
- **Scalability & domain shift**: Testing is limited to \(M \leq 321\) and similar domains; applicability to thousands of variables or cross‑domain scenarios remains open.

## Next Checks
- **Ablation replication**: Train T1 and its 32‑channels‑per‑head variant on ETTh1 with 40% missing; expect the bound version to yield lower MSE.  
- **Head reliability probing**: Compute per‑head attention entropy; corrupt a channel by adding noise and verify that its bound head down‑weights contributions, reflected in lower entropy for reliable heads.  
- **Shared vs. variable‑specific convolutions**: Replace shared DWConv with variable‑specific kernels; compare performance. If the original shared version outperforms, it supports the alignment assumption; otherwise, it reveals a limitation.
