# Adversarial Vulnerabilities in Neural Operator Digital Twins: Gradient-Free Attacks on Nuclear Thermal-Hydraulic Surrogates
## Quick Facts
- arXiv ID: 2603.22525
- Source URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22525
- Reference count: 40
- Primary result: Neural operators used as digital twins for nuclear systems are acutely vulnerable to sparse, physically plausible adversarial attacks; minimal perturbations (<1% of inputs) increase prediction error from ~1.5% to 37–63% while evading standard validation metrics, with 100% of single‑point attacks passing z‑score detection.

## Executive Summary
Neural operators are emerging as efficient surrogates for high-fidelity physics simulations, with digital twin applications in nuclear systems. This paper reveals that such operators are acutely vulnerable to adversarial attacks that are sparse (involving less than 1% of input coordinates) and physically plausible. Using a gradient‑free differential evolution attacker, the authors demonstrate that minimal perturbations can inflate prediction errors from typical levels of 1.5% to as high as 37–63%, while remaining undetected by standard validation checks—all single‑point attacks evade z‑score detection. Through a two‑factor model combining effective perturbation dimension (deff) and Jacobian sensitivity magnitude, they explain the varying exploitability across four operator architectures (MIMONet, NOMAD, S‑DeepONet, POD‑DeepONet). S‑DeepONet, with moderate deff and high sensitivity magnitude, proves most susceptible, whereas POD‑DeepONet’s low‑rank output caps maximum error despite extreme sensitivity concentration. The findings underscore a fundamental tension between interpolation accuracy and robustness for safety‑critical deployments and suggest that robustness must be co‑designed with operator architecture rather than retrofitted.

## Method Summary
The study employs a thermal‑hydraulic heat exchanger benchmark comprising 1,546 CFD simulations (80/20 train/test split). Inputs consist of 102 physical parameters (inlet conditions and a 100‑point heat flux profile); outputs are four scalar fields (pressure, velocity components) on a 3,977‑node mesh. All models are trained to validation MSE < 0.014 using Adam (lr = 1e‑3, weight decay = 1e‑6) and modest dropout (10%). To probe vulnerability, the authors design a gradient‑free differential evolution (DE) attacker that optimizes k‑sparse perturbations under physical bounds (standardized inputs clipped to ±1σ). The attacker’s genome encodes k index–value pairs; DE evolves a population (size 20 × 2^k) via best‑1‑bin mutation and binomial crossover (CR = 1.0) for up to 150 generations. Success is defined as relative L2 error exceeding a threshold (τ ≥ 0.3). Additionally, the authors compute the input–output Jacobian to derive the effective perturbation dimension deff = (∑ ‖J·‖₂)² / ∑ ‖J·‖₂², which quantifies sensitivity concentration. The experiments reveal a strong correlation between deff, Jacobian magnitude, and the observed attack success rates across the four architectures.

## Key Results
- Sparse adversarial attacks (≤10 points) can inflate relative L2 error of neural operators from ~1.5% to 37–63% depending on architecture.
- All single‑point attacks on S‑DeepONet passed z‑score detection, demonstrating stealth.
- The effective perturbation dimension (deff) combined with Jacobian magnitude explains vulnerability; S‑DeepONet (deff≈4.4) is most exploitable, while POD‑DeepONet (deff≈1) is limited by its low‑rank output.

## Why This Works (Mechanism)
The vulnerability of neural operators to sparse attacks is explained by two primary factors: effective perturbation dimension and sensitivity magnitude. The effective perturbation dimension \(d_{\text{eff}} = (\sum_i \|J^{(i)}\|_2)^2 / \sum_i \|J^{(i)}\|_2^2\) measures how concentrated the input‑output Jacobian’s column norms are. A lower \(d_{\text{eff}}\) means fewer input coordinates need to be perturbed to trigger large output errors; for instance, POD‑DeepONet exhibits \(d_{\text{eff}} \approx 1\) while MIMONet/NOMAD have \(d_{\text{eff}} \approx 32\). However, sensitivity magnitude (the scale of the Jacobian norms) imposes a ceiling on achievable error, particularly for low‑rank outputs like POD‑DeepONet where the reconstruction subspace limits amplification. S‑DeepONet’s sequential GRU encoder further concentrates sensitivity at the end of the 100‑point heat‑flux sequence, making a three‑point attack achieve >90% success despite a moderate \(d_{\text{eff}} \approx 4.4\). This concentration arises from recurrent dynamics where late inputs have minimal forgetting, so perturbations at indices 99–100 propagate strongly to all output fields. The two‑factor model thus captures why some architectures are acutely exploitable even under tight physical bounds and black‑box constraints.

## Foundational Learning
- **Jacobian matrix and sensitivity analysis**  
  *Why needed*: Quantifies how input changes affect outputs; directly defines deff and sensitivity magnitude.  
  *Quick check*: If one column norm is 0.01 and others are \(10^{-6}\), that input coordinate dominates sparse attack effectiveness.

- **Effective perturbation dimension (deff)**  
  *Why needed*: Measures concentration of sensitivity; lower deff means fewer coordinates suffice for a damaging attack. Theorem 4 bounds the minimum fraction of dense‑attack damage achievable by a k‑sparse attack as \(1/\sqrt{d_{\text{eff}}}\).  
  *Quick check*: For \(d_{\text{eff}} = 4\), the best single‑point attack can achieve at most 50% of the damage of a dense attack.

- **Differential evolution for black‑box optimization**  
  *Why needed*: Enables gradient‑free search for sparse perturbations; genome encodes index–value pairs, and DE evolves a population to maximize error.  
  *Quick check*: DE uses a special discrete encoding to respect the ℓ0 budget; indices are rounded to integers and values clipped to physical bounds.

- **Neural operator architectures**  
  *Why needed*: Architectural differences (e.g., recurrent vs. feed‑forward, low‑rank output) lead to distinct deff and sensitivity profiles, explaining variable vulnerability.  
  *Quick check*: POD‑DeepONet reconstructs outputs via a low‑dimensional basis, while S‑DeepONet uses a GRU to process the heat‑flux sequence sequentially.

- **Sobolev universal approximation and accuracy–robustness trade‑off**  
  *Why needed*: Theorem 9 shows that any operator approximating well in the Sobolev sense inherits the true operator’s sensitivity, making robustness issues fundamental rather than incidental.  
  *Quick check*: If a neural operator’s Sobolev accuracy \(\varepsilon \to 0\), the worst‑case adversarial error for a fixed perturbation \(\epsilon > 0\) does not vanish; it approaches the true operator’s sensitivity‑limited error.

## Architecture Onboarding
The codebase consists of four modules: model training scripts (for MIMONet, NOMAD, S‑DeepONet, POD‑DeepONet), a Jacobian analysis utility (auto‑diff to compute deff), a differential evolution attack engine (black‑box optimizer with physics‑aware bounds), and an evaluation harness (relative L2, z‑score, per‑channel metrics). All share a standardized data pipeline (branch inputs normalized, trunk coordinates scaled).

- **Critical path**:
  1. Train each architecture to validation MSE < 0.014 (∼50 epochs, Adam, batch = 4).
  2. Load a trained model and a test sample.
  3. Compute the Jacobian at the test input; average column norms over 30 randomized projections to estimate deff.
  4. For sparsity \(k \in \{1,3,5,10\}\), run DE (population \(20 × 2^k\), 150 generations, best‑1‑bin mutation, CR = 1.0) until relative L2 > 0.30 or stall.
  5. Log successful attack genomes, error statistics, and vulnerability heatmaps.
  6. Repeat over all 310 test samples.

- **Design tradeoffs**:
  - Accuracy vs robustness: POD‑DeepONet’s low‑rank reconstruction naturally caps adversarial error but may sacrifice precision on sharp gradients; S‑DeepONet is most accurate yet most vulnerable.
  - Gradient‑free vs gradient‑based: DE avoids gradient issues and works under black‑box access, but is slower (~2 min/attack) than PGD; however, DE converges reliably even where PGD fails.
  - Physical constraints: Enforcing ±1σ bounds guarantees stealth but may limit maximum achievable error; relaxing bounds increases success rates but reduces realism.

- **Failure signatures**:
  - High‑risk profile: \(d_{\text{eff}} < 5\) and mean Jacobian norm > 5 × 10⁻⁴ (as in S‑DeepONet).
  - DE success >30% with \(L_0=1\) while passing z‑score detection indicates catastrophic blind spots.
  - Cross‑architecture transfer success >50% suggests a distribution‑driven rather than architecture‑specific vulnerability.

- **First 3 experiments**:
  1. **Baseline validation**: Train all four models; verify test relative L2 ∼1.3–1.5% (matches Table 12).
  2. **Jacobian diagnostics**: Compute mean deff and column‑norm spectra across 50 test samples; expect POD‑DeepONet ≈ 1.0, S‑DeepONet ≈ 4.4, others ≈ 32. Replicate Fig. 3.
  3. **Sparse attack benchmark**: Run DE with \(L_0=1\) and error threshold 30%; report success rates. Expect S‑DeepONet > 50% while MIMONet/NOMAD < 10% and POD‑DeepONet ∼20%. Validate that random perturbations achieve near‑zero success (Fig. 5).

## Open Questions the Paper Calls Out
- **Generalization across PDE families**  
  *Question*: Do adversarial vulnerabilities generalize beyond the thermal‑hydraulic benchmark?  
  *Basis*: The study uses only one benchmark; different PDE classes may exhibit different sensitivity structures.  
  *Why unresolved*: No systematic evaluation across PDE families has been performed.  
  *Resolving evidence*: Conduct adversarial evaluation on diverse PDE families (e.g., wave equations, electromagnetics) and compare deff and attack success rates.

- **Defense mechanisms**  
  *Question*: What defenses can mitigate sparse adversarial vulnerabilities while maintaining practical operator accuracy?  
  *Basis*: The paper characterizes attacks but does not evaluate defenses; robustness must be co‑designed.  
  *Why unresolved*: Trade‑offs between robustness and accuracy are unknown; architecture‑specific optimal strategies unclear.  
  *Resolving evidence*: Implement and compare proposed defenses (ensembles, physics‑based detectors, sensitivity regularization, certified verification) with robustness‑accuracy Pareto curves.

- **Physical constraint bounds**  
  *Question*: How does the physical constraint bound (±1σ vs. larger perturbations) affect attack success and detection probability trade‑offs?  
  *Basis*: Only ±1σ perturbations were studied; industrial attackers may accept higher detection risk.  
  *Why unresolved*: The effectiveness of anomaly detection under larger perturbations is unexplored.  
  *Resolving evidence*: Systematically extend attacks to ±2σ, ±3σ, … and evaluate detection by multivariate detectors, Mahalanobis distance, and physics consistency checks.

- **Formal verification for ℓ0‑constrained robustness**  
  *Question*: Can formal verification provide certified robustness guarantees under ℓ0‑constrained sparse perturbations?  
  *Basis*: No certification exists; ℓ0‑verification is NP‑hard; operator‑specific structure may enable custom algorithms.  
  *Why unresolved*: No algorithms tailored to neural operators have been developed.  
  *Resolving evidence*: Develop verification methods that bound worst‑case error under sparse perturbations (e.g., convex relaxations) and evaluate tightness on test operators.

## Limitations
- The thermal‑hydraulic CFD dataset is not public, blocking direct replication and limiting broader vulnerability assessment.
- Differential evolution implementation details (index rounding, mutation bounds, enforcement of “at least one inherited gene”) are underspecified, potentially affecting convergence and reported success rates.
- The two‑factor model relies on a first‑order Taylor approximation; its validity for larger perturbations or different architectures remains unverified.

## Confidence
- Effective perturbation dimension (deff) as the primary determinant of sparse attack success: **High**
- Output‑space constraints limiting maximum adversarial error: **Medium**
- Sequential encoder (GRU) amplifying endpoint sensitivity: **Medium**

## Next Checks
1. Replicate the DE attacks using a different gradient‑free optimizer (e.g., CMA‑ES or particle swarm) to confirm vulnerability is not an artifact of DE’s encoding.
2. Train the four architectures on a publicly available thermal‑hydraulic dataset (e.g., CROCUS benchmark) and verify that the deff–magnitude model predicts attack success across data distributions.
3. Ablate S‑DeepONet’s GRU branch by replacing it with a feed‑forward network while keeping other components fixed; recompute deff and attack success to isolate the sequential encoder’s contribution.
